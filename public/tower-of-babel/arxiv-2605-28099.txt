A computationally-tractable measure of global sensitivity for sampling-based Bayesian inference
Arina Odnoblyudova, Charita Dellaporta, François-Xavier Briol

Abstract
Bayesian inference can often be sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters of the prior or likelihood, yet defining and quantifying this sensitivity in a principled and computationally feasible way remains challenging in practice. Unfortunately, existing sensitivity methods are rarely applicable in modern Bayesian workflows due to their high computational cost and poor performance in moderate to high dimensions. To address these limitations, we introduce a new approach to global sensitivity analysis based on the Fisher divergence. Our method only requires a set of samples from a reference posterior and the ability to evaluate score functions, making it broadly computationally tractable. Under mild regularity conditions, it controls changes in the whole posterior, and provides a bound on the impact of perturbations on the first two moments. We demonstrate these strengths on challenging Bayesian inference problems which are practically out of reach of existing approaches, including generalised Bayesian inference for unnormalised models, inference in Bayesian models of time series, and neural simulation-based inference.

INTRODUCTION

Bayesian posterior distributions can be highly sensitive to modelling choices, particularly prior and likelihood hyperparameters. This sensitivity may substantially affect uncertainty quantification and predictive performance
(
11
;
82
;
58
;
74
)
, and may arise even when modelling choices vary within a plausible range. This challenge is further amplified in generalisations
(
17
;
59
)
or approximations
(
21
)
of Bayesian inference, which frequently introduce additional hyperparameters whose influence is hard to assess. Consequently, evaluating sensitivity is a key component of the Bayesian workflow
(
33
;
24
)
.

This task falls within the scope of Bayesian sensitivity analysis
(
13
;
82
)
, with existing methods generally categorised into local and global approaches, both of which analyse sensitivity relative to a reference prior/likelihood pair. The local approach
(
41
;
42
;
81
;
1
;
38
;
56
;
25
)
employs differential techniques to assess the effect of infinitesimal perturbations around this reference. It is well studied and often computationally tractable, but its interpretation can be less intuitive and its relevance limited to small perturbations. In contrast, global sensitivity analysis
(
9
;
73
;
61
;
34
;
46
)
, the main focus of this paper, aligns more closely with practical concerns: it evaluates the maximum change in the quantity of interest within a neighbourhood of the reference choice. Practitioners often have a sense of a plausible range of alternatives, and this approach directly quantifies how much their inferences could change within that realistic range.

Existing global sensitivity approaches can be categorised according to three main design choices: a measure used to quantify sensitivity, such as a posterior functional or a statistical divergence to a reference posterior, a neighbourhood in which sensitivity is measured, and a computational tool through which the method is implemented, typically combining an estimator for the sensitivity measure and a numerical optimiser. These algorithmic components interact, leading to significant trade-offs between three key desiderata: (i)
the strength
of the sensitivity measure, i.e. whether the measure controls changes in the entire posterior or only some functionals such as the mean, (ii) the
interpretability
of the measure, and (iii) the
computational tractability
of the method.

To date, no existing method has successfully balanced these desiderata in a way that is practically relevant for most modern Bayesian modelling problems, where the dimensionality of the posterior can be large, and posterior computation is expensive. The biggest limitation has been computational, with many existing methods either being limited to low dimensions
(
71
;
73
)
, requiring a conjugate posterior
(
10
)
, requiring a large number of runs of a sampling method
(
61
;
34
)
, or requiring the solution of challenging optimisation problems
(
95
;
63
)
.
Consequently, global sensitivity analysis is rarely applied in practice. Instead, many practitioners adopt an informal approach: they evaluate a few variants around a reference model and informally assess their impact on the posterior. While straightforward, this approach can easily overlook other plausible modelling choices that may lead to substantially different inferences.

Our goal in this paper is to address the computational challenges that have limited the practical use of global sensitivity analysis. To this end, we propose a new sensitivity measure based on the
Fisher divergence
(FD)
(
52
)
, a statistical divergence which controls changes in the entire posterior. This measure is both strong and interpretable: under regularity conditions, it provides a bound on the difference in the first two moments and on changes in total variation, Kullback-Leibler and Wasserstein distance. The key advantage of the FD, however, is its computational tractability. It can be estimated using only a single set of samples from the reference posterior, without requiring repeated posterior sampling for each perturbed model in the sensitivity neighbourhood. This is achieved through evaluations of the score of these perturbed posteriors, a key quantity used for many methods in Bayesian computation, including algorithms based on Langevin dynamics and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
(
47
;
6
;
29
)
. Furthermore, the FD has computational complexity which is linear in the number of posterior samples and in the dimensionality of the posterior, and can be approximated at a rate depending on the square root of the number of samples, which is dimension-free. This makes it much better suited for measuring sensitivity in high-dimensional posteriors. Armed with this sensitivity measure, we consider two settings. The first, most general, consists of performing global optimisation over the space of hyperparameters, allowing us to consider a broad range of problems of practical relevance. The second, more limited, case considers neighbourhoods constructed through hypercubes of hyperparameters in exponential family priors. The latter setting does however allow us to recast the optimisation problem in a tractable form which can be solved exactly in a finite number of evaluations of the FD.

We demonstrate the widespread applicability of this approach through extensive experiments on problems which are out of range of all existing methods. This includes Bayesian inference for heavy-tailed time-series, amortised neural simulation based inference for simulators of time-series in telecommunication engineering, and generalisations of Bayesian inference, which we consider in the context of challenging doubly-intractable models including the Ising model.

BACKGROUND

Notation

We write
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
for the set of probability measures on some set
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{Z}}}
. We denote by
L^{2}(P)
the Lebesgue space of functions
f:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with respect to some measure
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
such that
\int_{\mathcal{Z}}|f(z)|^{2}\,dP(z)<\infty
. Moreover, we write
C^{r}(\mathcal{Z})
for the set of
r
-times continuously differentiable functions
f:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. For any
f\in C^{1}(\mathcal{Z})
we denote its gradient by
\nabla f(z)=(\partial_{1}f(z),\dots,\partial_{D}f(z))^{\top}
, with
\partial_{i}=\partial/\partial z_{i}
.

We use
capital
letters (e.g.
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
) to denote probability measures and the corresponding
lowercase
letters (e.g.
p:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
) to denote the associated probability density or probability mass functions. Finally, for any density
p:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we define the support
\text{supp}(p)\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
as the domain where
p(z)>0
, and use
s_{p}:=\nabla_{z}\log p(z)
to denote the
score function
of
p
. Throughout, we use
\lambda\in\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Lambda}}
to denote hyperparameters of the inference procedure.

2.1
Bayesian Inference and Sensitivity Analysis

Consider some observed dataset
x_{1:n}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
consisting of (possibly dependent) realisations from some data-generating distribution
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
for some continuous or discrete domain
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}
. We posit a parametric family
\{P_{\theta}^{\lambda}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
with probability density or mass function
p_{\theta}(\cdot|\lambda):\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, where
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}}
denotes the parameters of the model and will be assumed to be continuous, and
\lambda\in\Lambda
are hyperparameters. To perform Bayesian inference, the modeller must select a prior distribution
\Pi^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
which represents initial beliefs about
\theta
, and whose density will be written
\pi(\cdot|\lambda):\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Given this choice of prior, inference is done through the posterior distribution with density

\tilde{\pi}_{\text{Bayes}}^{\lambda}(\theta|x_{1:n})\propto p_{\theta}(x_{1:n}|\lambda)\pi(\theta|\lambda),

(1)

where
\propto
denotes equality up to a multiplicative normalisation constant, and
p_{\theta}(x_{1:n}|\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{\theta}(x_{i}|\theta)
when the data is independent and identically distributed. Depending on the specific model and observed data, the posterior can be more or less impacted by changes in the hyperparameters
\lambda
. This is also the case for a plethora of approximations or generalisations of Bayesian inference, which we will refer to as posterior belief distributions. To cover all of these cases simultaneously, we will denote by
\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
any belief distribution constructed as a generalised (also called Gibbs) posterior
(
17
;
59
)
with density:

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda}(\theta|x_{1:n})\propto\exp\left(-L(\theta;x_{1:n},\lambda)\right)\pi(\theta|\lambda).

(2)

where
L(\theta;x_{1:n},\lambda)
is an empirical loss. A very common example is for this loss to take the form
L(\theta;x_{1:n},\lambda)=\lambda l(\theta;x_{1:n})
for some empirical loss
l:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, assumed to be bounded from below, and for the hyperparameter to be the learning rate
\lambda>0
, which balances the influence of the loss relative to the prior.
Our paper will focus on several important instances of this general framework. This includes standard Bayes, which is recovered through
L(\theta;x_{1:n})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{\theta}(x_{i})
. It also includes divergence-based posteriors
(
55
;
68
;
69
)
, where the loss is obtained through a statistical divergence between the model distribution and the empirical distribution of the data, and likelihood-free methods such as neural-likelihood estimation, which use a log-loss based on a neural surrogate of the likelihood
(
75
)
. However, the framework goes far beyond the examples considered in this paper, and also covers belief distributions such as power posteriors
(
39
;
48
)
, pseudo-likelihood or composite likelihood posteriors
(
79
)
and approximate Bayesian computation posteriors
(
86
)
.

Our aim in this paper is to measure the sensitivity of a posterior
\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}
to the choice of hyperparameters
\lambda\in\Lambda
. To formalise this, we follow the classical formulation of Bayesian global sensitivity analysis first proposed by
12
and later reviewed by
73
;
82
.
This requires the choice of a sensitivity measure, which is a functional

\rho:\mathcal{P}(\Theta)\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to which we will assess changes. Examples of sensitivity measures include posterior expectations, i.e.
\rho(\tilde{\Pi}):=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\tilde{\Pi}}[\tau(\theta)]
for some function
\tau:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}

(
64
;
73
;
46
)
, or the discrepancy between candidates
\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}
and some reference generalised posterior, i.e.
\rho(\tilde{\Pi}):=D(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}||\tilde{\Pi})
for some statistical divergence
D:\mathcal{P}(\Theta)\times\mathcal{P}(\Theta)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and reference distribution
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)

(
61
;
34
)
.

We also require a neighbourhood consisting of belief distributions corresponding to plausible combinations of hyperparameter values
\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda
, which will be denoted:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}:=\{\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta):\lambda\in\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda\}.

(3)

The global sensitivity of
\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}
over the neighbourhood
\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}
is then defined as the largest possible change in the sensitivity measure

\rho(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})
over hyperparameters in the neighbourhood
\lambda\in\Gamma
:

\displaystyle S(\Gamma):=\sup_{\tilde{\Pi}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}}\rho(\tilde{\Pi})-\inf_{\tilde{\Pi}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}}\rho(\tilde{\Pi})=\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\rho(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})-\inf_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\rho(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}).

(4)

From a computational viewpoint,
S(\Gamma)
cannot typically be computed exactly
and must be estimated through a statistical estimator
for the measure of sensitivity, and a numerical optimiser to solve the supremum and infimum problems.
Although a large number of methods have been proposed over the last four decades, these tend to suffer from the same three computational challenges:

•

Firstly, for a given candidate posterior
\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}
with fixed
\lambda\in\Lambda
, we typically need to estimate
\rho(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})
from samples, which can be computationally challenging. For instance, when
\rho
is defined through a statistical divergence, its estimation typically requires sampling from the reference
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}
and the candidate
\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}
. If the estimator converges slowly in the number of samples or is computationally expensive, this will quickly become prohibitive. For example,
(
34
;
70
)
quantify sensitivity through the Wasserstein distance and therefore suffer from the curse of dimensionality, with the most common estimator incurring a cost of
\mathcal{O}(m^{3})
and converging at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1/d_{\Theta}})
in the number of samples
m
when
d_{\Theta}\geq 2

(
31
)
.
Similarly,
61
use the Fisher-Rao distance, for which common sample-based estimators would also suffer from a large computational cost and curse of dimensionality due to the need to perform (nonparametric) density estimation.
For this reason, most Bayesian global sensitivity methods rely on simpler posterior functionals such as posterior moments
(
73
;
46
)
, which have a cost of
\mathcal{O}(m)
and convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1/2})
, but unfortunately do not necessarily capture changes in the entire posterior.

•

Secondly, beyond the difficulty of estimating
\rho
once, many existing methods require estimating it repeatedly for different candidates
\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda_{1}},\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda_{2}},\ldots
for
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots\in\Lambda
throughout the optimisation procedure. This often leads to the use of computationally expensive sampling schemes, with most existing methods requiring sampling from every candidate considered
(
104
;
61
;
35
;
34
)
. One alternative is to avoid this through importance sampling using samples from
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}
, or to use a few iterations of sequential Monte Carlo; see e.g.
46
. However, such approaches can lead to high-variance estimators of
\rho(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})
, especially for high-dimensional posteriors and when the reference has lighter tails than the candidates.

•

Thirdly, solving the optimisation problems in (
4
) can be very challenging, particularly when the optimisation objective is non-convex.
Several approaches have been proposed to tackle this issue; see for example the linearisation algorithms of
(
64
;
63
)
which turn sensitivity measures constructed through posterior expectations into the solution to a sequence of linear optimisation problems. However, most papers bypass the issue entirely by taking a neighbourhood with a finite number of elements, allowing for a direct solution to the supremum and infimum in
Equation

4

(
61
;
34
;
46
)
. This severely limits the applicability of these methods, and makes them much closer to the informal approach to sensitivity analysis.

Put together, these computational challenges mean that existing global sensitivity methods are not well-suited for modern Bayesian inference problems.

2.2
The Fisher Divergence

This paper will tackle some of the aforementioned computational limitations by introducing a sensitivity measure based on the
Fisher divergence (FD)

(
52
)
, also called the Hyvärinen or score-matching divergence. The FD measures how different two distributions are by comparing their score functions. Given two probability measures
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
with corresponding scores
s_{\pi_{1}},s_{\pi_{2}}
, the FD is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{FD}(\Pi_{1}\|\Pi_{2})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\Pi_{1}}\left[\|s_{\pi_{1}}(\theta)-s_{\pi_{2}}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}\right],

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm.
For the FD to be well-defined and a statistical divergence, that is,
\mathrm{FD}(\Pi_{1}\|\Pi_{2})=0\iff\Pi_{1}\equiv\Pi_{2}
for all
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, certain regularity conditions are required, which we discuss in detail in Section
3.1
.
As we will see in
Section

4
, the FD can be used to upper bound (under certain regularity conditions) the (1 or 2)-Wasserstein or total variation distances between
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
,
making it a particularly strong notion of discrepancy
(
51
)
. The FD is also computationally convenient as it can be expressed as an expectation and can therefore be estimated through a Monte Carlo estimator based on samples from
\Pi_{1}
.

The Fisher divergence has been widely used across statistics and machine learning, including for parameter estimation
(
52
;
53
;
5
;
101
;
85
)
, generalised Bayesian inference
(
3
;
2
)
, in the context of scoring rules
(
77
)
, and for score-based generative modelling
(
88
)
. In these settings, practitioners typically apply integration by parts when constructing Monte Carlo estimators of the FD, as this removes the dependence on the score of
\Pi_{1}
, an object that is usually unavailable. In our setting, the unnormalised posterior density is known up to a constant, so its score can be evaluated directly, and no integration‑by‑parts trick is required. Moreover, whereas most existing applications compute the FD between distributions on the data space
\mathcal{X}
, we apply it to distributions on the parameter space
\Theta
. This makes our use of the FD most closely related to its role in Bayesian model comparison and selection
(
22
;
87
;
54
)
.

3
METHODOLOGY

We are now ready to propose our novel measure of global sensitivity based on the FD, explore optimisation routines, and discuss interpretability of the measure.

3.1
The Fisher Divergence as a Sensitivity Measure

Let
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)

denote a reference belief distribution, which we will assume to be induced by a reference prior
\Pi_{\text{ref}}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
and loss function
L_{\text{ref}}:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
bounded from below so that

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}(\theta|x_{1:n})\propto\exp(-L_{\text{ref}}(\theta;x_{1:n}))\pi_{\text{ref}}(\theta).

(5)

We will take this reference distribution as corresponding to our default choice of prior and loss function, and will aim to study sensitivity relative to these choices. Throughout, we will assume the following conditions, which will be needed to ensure that the FD is a divergence when used with
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}
and elements of the neighbourhood
\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}
.

Assumption 1
.

•

The reference posterior
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}
has support,
\text{supp}(\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}})
, on an open connected subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}}
.

•

The functions
\pi_{\text{ref}},L_{\text{ref}}(\cdot;x_{1:n})\in C^{1}(\Theta)
and
s_{\pi_{\text{ref}}},\nabla_{\theta}L_{\text{ref}}(\cdot;x_{1:n})\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}})
.

•

The posterior neighbourhood
\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}
is a subset of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{FD}}(\Theta)
, the space of distributions for which the FD to
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}
exists:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{FD}}(\Theta):=\Big\{

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta):\tilde{\pi}(\theta|x_{1:n})\propto\exp(-L(\theta;x_{1:n}))\pi(\theta)\text{ with }\text{supp}(\tilde{\pi})=\text{supp}(\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}),

\displaystyle\ \quad\pi,L(\cdot;x_{1:n})\in C^{1}(\Theta)\text{ and }s_{\pi},\nabla L(\cdot;x_{1:n})\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}})\Big\}.

The assumptions are relatively mild and will be satisfied in all but one of our experiments. In fact, the assumption can be further simplified in a number of common settings; see
Appendix

B
for further discussion.
Given these, we now propose to use the FD as our measure of sensitivity:

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{FD}}(\tilde{\Pi}):=\mathrm{FD}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\|\tilde{\Pi})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}}\left[\big\|s_{\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}}(\theta)-s_{\tilde{\pi}}(\theta)\big\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(6)

As highlighted by the following proposition, this is a strong measure of sensitivity since it controls changes in the entire posterior and can distinguish distributions in
\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{FD}}(\Theta)
from
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}
.

Proposition 1

(The FD distinguishes posteriors)
.

Suppose
1
holds.
For
\tilde{\Pi}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{FD}}(\Theta)
,
\mathrm{FD}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\|\tilde{\Pi})=0\Leftrightarrow\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\equiv\tilde{\Pi}
.

The result follows from Theorem 1 in
103
and a proof is provided in
Section

A.1
. Following
Equation

4
, we define the FD-based global sensitivity analysis problem as

S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma):=\sup\limits_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\rho^{\mathrm{FD}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})-\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\rho^{\mathrm{FD}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}).

(7)

In particular, if the reference distribution is itself in the neighbourhood (i.e.
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}
, or equivalently if there exists a
\lambda_{\text{ref}}\in\Gamma
such that
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}=\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda_{\text{ref}}}
) then the infimum is
0
since
\mathrm{FD}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}||\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}})=0
following
Proposition

1
. However, we do not limit ourselves to this case for the remainder of this paper and therefore keep the presentation general. Note, that we are also able to do the local sensitivity analysis with the FD; see
Appendix

D
.

Before concluding, we note that using score functions for sensitivity analysis has a long history in Bayesian analysis. For example,
97
;
44
used scores to detect outliers and assess sensitivity of the posterior to individual data points. However, these methods focus mainly on local rather than global sensitivity, and do not use scores to construct a divergence.

3.2
Estimation of the Fisher Divergence Sensitivity Measure

Now that we have defined our measure of sensitivity, we discuss how to estimate it from samples. Since
\rho^{\mathrm{FD}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})
is defined as an expectation under the reference
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}
, it can be estimated through (possibly approximate) samples
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{m}
from
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}
, leading to a natural estimator for
S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{S}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\Gamma)=\sup\limits_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})-\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})

(8)

where we have

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})=\widehat{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\|\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|s_{\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}}(\theta_{i})-s_{\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda}}(\theta_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(9)

The use of i.i.d. samples would give a standard Monte Carlo estimator of the FD, but in our setting, it will often not be possible to obtain independent samples from
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}
. Instead, it will be more common to have samples obtained through a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler targeting the reference posterior.

The key advantage of this estimator is that it only relies on the score functions
s_{\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}}
and
s_{\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda}}
, and therefore does not require knowledge of the corresponding normalisation constants of
\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}
and
\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda}
, which are typically unavailable. This is in contrast with many other divergences used in sensitivity analysis, such as the Kullback-Leibler, Fisher-Rao
(
61
)
or Jensen-Shannon divergence
(
56
)
, who all require density evaluations. Assuming access to score functions is very mild; in fact, many MCMC samplers based on Langevin dynamics or Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
(
47
;
6
;
29
)
make use of scores to guide the Markov chain, and some of these quantities may therefore have already been pre-computed when obtaining the samples
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m}
.

Furthermore, another advantage of relying on the FD and scores is that we can estimate this divergence without ever sampling from
\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}
. This is in contrast with virtually all of the existing divergence-based methods for sensitivity, which would require MCMC sampling for every new candidate under consideration or the use of importance sampling methods which may have high variance.

This estimator of the FD is also very computationally attractive: the computational cost scales linearly in the number of samples
m
, with overall computational complexity
\mathcal{O}(md_{\Theta})
. When the samples are obtained from a geometrically ergodic and reversible Markov chain,
\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})
will satisfy a central limit theorem guaranteeing convergence to
\rho^{\mathrm{FD}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})
at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(m^{-\frac{1}{2}})
regardless of the dimension
d_{\Theta}

(
80
, Theorem 4)
. As we now show below, this result can be refined to a finite-sample complexity result:

Assumption 2
.

The samples
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are realisations from a Markov chain with invariant distribution
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}
, and there exists a Lyapunov function
V:\Theta\to[e,\infty)
such that:

(A2.1)

The Markov chain is
V
-uniformly geometrically ergodic.

(A2.2)

The score difference grows
at most like
\sqrt{V(\theta)}
over the neighbourhood, i.e.

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{\|s_{\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\theta)-s_{\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda}}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{V(\theta)}}<\infty.

Note that many commonly used MCMC methods satisfy geometric ergodicity; see, Theorem 2.1 in
65
and Theorem 9 in
27
for Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, and Theorem 16 from
28
for the No-U-Turn sampler. The growth control assumption will also be satisfied for a broad class of sensitivity problems under mild regularity conditions, such as when doing prior sensitivity with exponential-family priors, or when doing sensitivity analysis for the learning rate of generalised posteriors; see
Section

A.4
. Using
2
, we can now provide a finite sample result for our estimator of the measure of sensitivity.

Theorem 1

(Finite-sample complexity of the FD estimator under MCMC sampling)
.

Suppose
1
and
2
hold.
Then, for any
\lambda\in\Gamma
, there exists some constant
0<C^{\prime}<\infty
independent of
m
, such that
for all
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\big|\widehat{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\|\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})-\mathrm{FD}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\|\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})\big|\geq\delta\right)\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{m\delta^{2}}.

Furthermore, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)\leq\widehat{S}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\Gamma)+\frac{2\sqrt{C^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{m}\,\delta}.

A proof is provided in Appendix
A.2
. The first part of
Theorem

1
shows that the estimated FD is close to the exact FD with high probability. The second part of this result shows that the estimated sensitivity measure can upper bound
S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)
up to a term which decreases as
O(m^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, guaranteeing that we are not significantly over-estimating the sensitivity due to having only a finite number of MCMC samples. Most notably, and unlike most existing divergence-based sensitivity measures, we emphasise that this rate in
m
is independent of
d_{\Theta}
, the dimensionality of the parameter space. This will be essential to ensure that the method is widely applicable for Bayesian inference.

3.3
Numerical Optimisation

Although we now have an estimator for our sensitivity measure as given in
Equation

8
, this estimator still relies on an exact solution to two optimisation problems. This can be made explicit by writing

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{m}^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)=\sup\limits_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})-\inf\limits_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})=\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}\left(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda^{\text{sup}}_{m}}\right)-\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}\left(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda^{\text{inf}}_{m}}\right)

(10)

where
\lambda^{\text{sup}}_{m}\in\arg\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}),\lambda^{\text{inf}}_{m}\in\arg\inf_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})
.
The most direct approach is therefore to solve the two optimisation problems numerically with global optimisation algorithms. Given outputs
\lambda^{\text{sup}}_{m,t}
and
\lambda^{\text{inf}}_{m,t}
after
t\in\mathbb{N}
iterations of such an optimiser, we can estimate sensitivity with

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{m,t}^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma):=\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}\left(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda^{\text{sup}}_{m,t}}\right)-\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}\left(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda^{\text{inf}}_{m,t}}\right).

(11)

We can quantify the impact of the optimisation error through two uses of the triangle inequality combined with
Theorem

1
:
S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)\leq\widehat{S}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m,t}(\Gamma)+\frac{2\sqrt{C^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{m}\,\delta}+\varepsilon^{\mathrm{inf}}_{t}+\varepsilon^{\mathrm{sup}}_{t}

where
\varepsilon^{\mathrm{inf}}_{t}=|\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda^{\text{inf}}_{m}})-\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda^{\text{inf}}_{m,t}})|
and
\varepsilon^{\mathrm{sup}}_{t}=|\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda^{\text{sup}}_{m}})-\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda^{\text{sup}}_{m,t}})|
are the optimisation errors for the infimum and supremum problems respectively.
This decomposition makes clear that the accuracy of our estimator will depend on the difficulty of solving these two problems. Direct optimisation will only be feasible practically when
d_{\Lambda}
is relatively small and
\lambda\mapsto\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}
is a smooth map, in which case most non-convex optimisation methods such as Bayesian optimisation
(
32
)
or simulated annealing
(
23
)
should perform well. However, the approach may not be able to scale to large
d_{\Lambda}
due to the difficulty of performing high-dimensional non-convex optimisation.

Interestingly, our next result shows that in a broad range of scenarios relevant in Bayesian sensitivity analysis, the objective function becomes a convex quadratic. This makes the optimisation problem much more tractable, and we will see it can help ensure
\varepsilon^{\mathrm{sup}}_{t},\varepsilon^{\mathrm{inf}}_{t}\rightarrow 0
at a fast rate as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Assumption 3

(Linear loss and exponential family prior)
.

The candidate posteriors
\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}
have separable hyperparameters for the loss and prior so that
\Lambda=\Lambda_{L}\times\Lambda_{\pi}
, the loss function is linear in its hyperparameters, and the prior is in natural exponential family form. More formally, for some vector-valued function
l(\cdot;x_{1:n}):\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Lambda_{L}}}
, sufficient statistic
T:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Lambda_{\pi}}}
, base density
g:\Theta\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, and log-partition function
h:\Lambda_{\pi}:\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}:=\Big\{\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}:

\displaystyle\;\lambda=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{L}\\
\lambda_{\pi}\end{bmatrix}\in\Gamma=\Gamma_{L}\times\Gamma_{\pi}\text{ where }\Gamma_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{d_{{\Lambda}_{L}}}},\Gamma_{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{d_{{\Lambda}_{\pi}}}},\text{and}

\displaystyle L(\theta;x_{1:n},\lambda)=\lambda_{L}^{\top}l(\theta;x_{1:n})\quad\text{and}\quad\pi(\theta|\lambda)=\exp\left(\lambda_{\pi}^{\top}T(\theta)+\log g(\theta)-\log\left(h(\lambda_{\pi})\right)\right)\Big\}.

3
covers a very broad range of problems. For example, linearity of the loss is satisfied when measuring sensitivity of generalised posteriors to the learning rate/temperature parameter. It is also satisfied in standard Bayesian inference when considering sensitivity to hyperparameters of a natural exponential family likelihood, or sensitivity to the weights in weighted log-likelihood approaches. Exponential family priors are also extremely common, and include Gaussian, Gamma, and Beta priors amongst many others.

Proposition 2

(The FD as a quadratic form)
.

Suppose
3
holds. Then,

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})=\lambda^{\top}A\lambda+b^{\top}\lambda+c,

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant in
\lambda
, and we have

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}A_{LL}&A_{L\pi}\\
A_{\pi L}&A_{\pi\pi}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Lambda}\times d_{\Lambda}},\qquad b:=-\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}J(\theta_{i})^{\top}\big(s_{\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\theta_{i})-\nabla_{\theta}\log g(\theta_{i})\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Lambda}\times 1},

where
J(\theta):=\big[\,-\nabla_{\theta}l(\theta_{i};x_{1:n}),\;\nabla_{\theta}T(\theta)^{\top}\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}\times d_{\Lambda}}
and

\displaystyle A_{LL}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla_{\theta}l(\theta_{i};x_{1:n})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}l(\theta_{i};x_{1:n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Lambda_{L}}\times d_{\Lambda_{L}}},

\displaystyle A_{\pi\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla_{\theta}T(\theta_{i})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}T(\theta_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{d_{{\Lambda}_{\pi}}}\times d_{d_{{\Lambda}_{\pi}}}},

\displaystyle A_{L\pi}

\displaystyle=A_{\pi L}^{\top}=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla_{\theta}l(\theta_{i};x_{1:n})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}T(\theta_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{d_{{\Lambda}_{L}}}\times d_{d_{{\Lambda}_{\pi}}}}.

Furthermore,
A
is positive semi-definite, and thus the quadratic form is convex in
\lambda\in\Lambda
.

We omit the exact expression for
c
, but an interested reader can find it in the proof in
Section

A.3
. The complexity of computing the quadratic form is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(md_{\Lambda}^{2}d_{\Theta}\bigr)
.
Note that
\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})
remains a convex quadratic form if we consider it as a function of only a subset of the hyperparameters
\lambda
, such as when we are interested only in sensitivity to the prior parameters
\lambda_{\pi}
or to hyperparameters of the loss
\lambda_{L}
; see
Appendix

B
for details.

The main advantage of a convex objective is that, depending on the geometry of the neighbourhood
\Gamma
over which we optimise, both optimisation problems can be solved efficiently. A class of neighbourhoods for which this is the case is bounded convex polytopes
(
45
)
, such as hyperrectangles, simplices or
l_{1}
-balls. Bounded convex polytopes can be represented through the convex hull of a finite set of vertices:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{poly}}:=\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\lambda=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}v_{k}\text{ for }v_{1},\ldots,v_{K}\in\Lambda,\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K}\geq 0,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}=1\right\}

(12)

Since
\Gamma_{\text{poly}}
is convex and compact, we know by Bauer’s maximum principle that the supremum of
\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi})
will be achieved at an extreme point
(see e.g.
7
;
8
, Theorem 7.42)
. Thus, we can solve the supremum by evaluating the FD at the vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{K}
by simple enumeration
(see
49
, Section 2.1)
, meaning
\varepsilon^{\mathrm{sup}}_{t}=0
for any
t\geq K
and the computational cost is
\mathcal{O}(K)
. When
K
is very large, naively enumerating vertices may be prohibitively expensive, and gradient information from the FD divergence could be used to accelerate the optimisation.

Similarly, the infimum problem is a convex minimisation problem over a convex and compact set. One approach to solving this is to compute the unconstrained global minimiser

\lambda^{\mathrm{unc}}=-\frac{1}{2}A^{-\dagger}b
with complexity
\mathcal{O}(d_{\Lambda}^{3})
.
If
\lambda^{\mathrm{unc}}\in\Gamma_{\text{poly}}
, then the unconstrained minimiser is automatically the constrained global minimiser and we set
\lambda^{\text{inf}}_{m}=\lambda^{\mathrm{unc}}
. Otherwise, the problem reduces to a convex quadratic programme with linear inequality constraints. This can be tackled through projected gradient descent, where at each step a gradient descent update is performed followed by a projection onto
\Gamma_{\text{poly}}
to ensure feasibility.
The errors are
\varepsilon^{\mathrm{inf}}_{t}=\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{t})
for some
\kappa\in(0,1)
(see e.g. Section 7.3.3 from
98
) and the computational cost is driven by the maximum of
\mathcal{O}(d_{\Lambda}^{2})
, the cost of performing a gradient step, and the cost of the projection on
\Gamma_{\text{poly}}
.

A special case of a convex polytope relevant for Bayesian sensitivity analysis is when
\Gamma
is constructed through box-type constraints (also called hyperrectangles), where the boundaries for each parameter expressed through the vectors
\lambda^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime\prime}\in\Lambda
so that:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{box}}:=\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\lambda^{\prime}_{j}\leq\lambda_{j}\leq\lambda^{\prime\prime}_{j}\text{ for }j\in\{1,\ldots,d_{\Lambda}\}\right\}.

(13)

This is a bounded convex polytope where the constraints are placed separately on each hyperparameter. Since a box-constrained neighbourhood has at most
K=2^{d_{\Lambda}}
vertices, computing the supremum for box-constrained sensitivity analysis is computationally inexpensive for small to moderate
d_{\Lambda}
, but may be infeasible for large
d_{\Lambda}
. If the infimum is computed using projected gradient descent, the cost of the projection step onto
\Gamma_{\text{box}}
is
\mathcal{O}(d_{\Lambda})
, since it reduces to coordinate-wise clipping.

Before concluding, we emphasise that the choice of parametrisation is important here. For example, although natural exponential family priors lead to a convex quadratic form, this may not be the case for other parametrisations of an exponential family model. For exponential families in minimal form, this is not a problem as there always exists a smooth and invertible mapping between parametrisations (see e.g. Section 8.1 in
4
). However, specifying a neighbourhood in natural parameter space may be challenging, and it is not always possible to guarantee that a neighbourhood specified in the original parametrisation will remain a bounded convex polytope once mapped to the natural parametrisation. One exception is affine transformations, which maintain the required geometry of the domain.

4
STRENGTH AND INTERPRETABILITY

We have now proposed a measure of sensitivity which is highly tractable in the sense that it can be estimated without suffering from a curse of dimensionality, and where the optimisation problem can be solved efficiently in a broad range of settings relevant for Bayesian and generalised Bayesian inference. The remaining questions are whether the measure of sensitivity is meaningful and whether it is interpretable to the user.

4.1
Strength of the Measure of Sensitivity

We have already seen in
Proposition

1
that our measure is based on a statistical divergence, and therefore controls changes in the entire posterior. This makes it a much stronger measure than one based only on moments, or any other quantity which measures differences based on summaries. Below, we now show that it is in fact possible to relate our sensitivity measure to sensitivity measures based on alternative statistical divergences which are widely used. However, this is only possible with stronger assumptions on the neighbourhood.

Assumption 4

(Candidate posterior regularity)
.

We assume that

(A4.1)

For all
\lambda\in\Gamma
,

\log\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda}
has strongly concave tails, i.e. there exist constants
K_{\lambda}\geq 0
and
R\geq 0
, such that

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}\log\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda}(\theta)\succeq K_{\lambda}I_{d_{\Theta}}\qquad\text{for all }\theta\in\text{supp}(\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}),\|\theta\|_{2}\geq R

and
K_{\Gamma}:=\inf_{\lambda\in\Gamma}K_{\lambda}>0
.

(A4.2)

For
\tilde{\pi}_{\text{min}}^{R}:=\inf_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\inf_{\|\theta\|_{2}\leq 3R}\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda}(\theta)
and
\tilde{\pi}_{\text{max}}^{R}:=\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\sup_{\|\theta\|_{2}\leq 3R}\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda}(\theta)
, we have
0<\tilde{\pi}^{R}_{\text{min}}\leq\tilde{\pi}^{R}_{\text{max}}<\infty
.

The first assumption corresponds to requiring that the candidate posteriors are strongly log-concave
(
84
)
outside of some region near the origin. We note that the product of two functions is log-concave if and only if the functions are both log-concave, so sufficient conditions for this are for the loss function and prior to both be log-concave outside of some region near the origin. In particular, natural exponential family models are log-concave everywhere if and only if the base measure is log-concave and the sufficient statistic is affine. This case includes numerous examples, such as the Gaussian, exponential and Laplace distributions. There are also many examples where the distributions are not exponential family models but are still log-concave in the tails, such as for the Student-t and Gamma distributions. The second assumption simply requires the candidate posteriors to be bounded above and below on the centre of the distribution, which is very mild and will hold when the candidate priors and likelihoods are also bounded above and below on that interval. Under these assumptions, the FD sensitivity controls sensitivity in three widely studied divergences.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose
1
and
4
hold
and let
\text{W}_{p}
,
TV
and
KL
denote
the
p
-Wasserstein, total variation distance and Kullback-Leibler divergence respectively.
Then, for
p=1,2
there exist some constant
\alpha_{\Gamma}>0
depending on
K_{\Gamma}
,
R
,
\tilde{\pi}^{R}_{\text{min}}
and
\tilde{\pi}^{R}_{\text{max}}
such that:

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}W_{p}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}},\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}):=S^{\text{W}_{p}}(\Gamma)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{\Gamma}}\sqrt{S^{\text{FD}}(\Gamma)}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\text{TV}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\|\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}):=S^{\text{TV}}(\Gamma)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\alpha_{\Gamma}}}\sqrt{S^{\text{FD}}(\Gamma)}.

Moreover, if for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
, there exists
K^{\prime}_{\lambda}>0
such that
\log\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda}
is
K^{\prime}_{\lambda}
-strongly concave for all
\theta\in\Theta
and
\alpha^{\prime}_{\Gamma}:=\inf_{\lambda\in\Gamma}K^{\prime}_{\lambda}>0
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\text{KL}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\|\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}):=S^{\text{KL}}(\Gamma)\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha^{\prime}_{\Gamma}}S^{\text{FD}}(\Gamma).

The proof can be found in
Section

A.5
. This result demonstrates that the FD leads to a strong measure of sensitivity, meaning that small values of
S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)
guarantee small sensitivity with respect to many other notions of sensitivity.
Interestingly, under these same conditions,
S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)
becomes much more interpretable, in the sense that it controls differences in the first two moments of the reference and candidate posteriors. This is a direct corollary of the earlier result on control of the Wasserstein distance in
Proposition

3
.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
1
and
4
hold and for any
\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{FD}}(\Theta)
with finite second moments, denote by
\mu_{\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}},\Sigma_{\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}}
the corresponding mean vector and covariance matrix.
Then there exists some constant
\alpha_{\Gamma}>0
depending on
K_{\Gamma},R,\tilde{\pi}^{R}_{\text{min}}
and
\tilde{\pi}^{R}_{\text{max}}
such that:

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{mean}}(\Gamma):=\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\left\|\mu_{\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}}-\mu_{\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{\Gamma}}\sqrt{S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)}

and

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{cov}}(\Gamma):=\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\|\Sigma_{\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}}-\Sigma_{\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{3}{\alpha_{\Gamma}}\,\min\left(\sqrt{\|\Sigma_{\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}}\|_{2}},\ \sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\sqrt{\|\Sigma_{\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda}}\|_{2}}\right)\sqrt{S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)}+\frac{5.25}{\alpha_{\Gamma}^{2}}\,S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma).

The proof is in
Section

A.6
. There are two potential advantages to this result. The first is conceptual: our measure of sensitivity controls changes in the first two moments, which makes the measure more interpretable. The second is practical: in some cases, the value of
\alpha_{\Gamma}
can be obtained in closed-form and the result can be combined with
Theorem

1
to obtain a computable upper bound on the difference of moments, though this upper bound will not necessarily be tight.

4.2
Interpretability of the Measure of Sensitivity

Before concluding, we also consider the interpretability of our measure of sensitivity.
As for all divergence-based Bayesian global sensitivity measures, it is hard to interpret the raw value of
S^{\text{FD}}(\Gamma)
. Several approaches have been taken to mitigate this issue in the literature. For instance, when measuring sensitivity to the prior, one approach is to normalise the values by e.g. dividing by the value of the divergence evaluated at the worst-case prior in the neighbourhood
(
70
)
. This would be inconvenient in our case as the approach is limited to prior sensitivity and would require additional sampling from
\Pi_{\text{ref}}
.

Due to these well-known limitations, we instead propose to focus on relative comparisons. Given two neighbourhoods
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\subset\Lambda
, we can use
S^{\text{FD}}(\Gamma_{1})-S^{\text{FD}}(\Gamma_{2})
to measure the relative sensitivity to
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
. In addition, for a single neighbourhood, the sensitivity measure typically simplifies through various decompositions which can shed more light on what is being measured, including the relative sensitivity to the prior and loss function, or the relative sensitivity to different hyperparameters.

Decomposition 1: Impact of the loss/prior

Firstly, we can expand the squares in
Equation

6
:

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{FD}}(\tilde{\Pi})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}}\left[\left\|\nabla_{\theta}L_{\text{ref}}(\theta;x_{1:n})-\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta;x_{1:n})\right\|^{2}_{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}}\left[\left\|s_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}(\theta)-s_{\pi}(\theta)\right\|^{2}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}}\left[\left(\nabla_{\theta}L_{\text{ref}}(\theta;x_{1:n})-\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta;x_{1:n})\right)^{\top}\left(s_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}(\theta)-s_{\pi}(\theta)\right)\right]

(14)

This decomposition is particularly interpretable: the first term is non-negative and measures changes in the loss, the second term is also non-negative and measures changes in the prior, whereas the third term could be positive or negative and measures interactions between differences in the loss and prior. In particular, these three terms could be estimated separately through MCMC and used, for example, to compare the relative impact of changes in
\lambda
on the loss and prior.

Section

4.2
is particularly informative in several special cases. For example, if we assume the reference posterior is an element of the neighbourhood (i.e.
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}=\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda_{\text{ref}}}
for some
\lambda_{\text{ref}}\in\Gamma
) and consider only hyperparameters
\lambda
of the loss, then the last two terms are zero and the sensitivity simplifies to:

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)=\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}}\left[\left\|\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta;x_{1:n},\lambda_{\text{ref}})-\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta;x_{1:n},\lambda)\right\|^{2}_{2}\right].

(15)

Similarly, if we again assume the reference posterior is an element of the neighbourhood but only consider hyperparameters
\lambda
of the prior, then the first and last terms in
Section

4.2
are zero and our measure of sensitivity only considers changes in prior scores:

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)=\sup_{\lambda\in\Gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}}\left[\left\|s_{\pi(\cdot|\lambda_{\text{ref}})}(\theta)-s_{\pi(\cdot|\lambda)}(\theta)\right\|^{2}_{2}\right].

(16)

In both special cases, we note that the expression can be further reduced to a simplified quadratic form under
3
; see
Appendix

B
for more details.

Decomposition 2: Independence across dimensions

Secondly, suppose that both the reference and candidate posterior distributions factorise due to independence across subsets of dimensions; that is,
\Theta=\Theta_{1}\times\ldots\times\Theta_{J}
,
\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}(\theta)=\prod_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref},j}(\theta_{j})
and
\tilde{\pi}(\theta)=\prod_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{\pi}_{j}(\theta_{j})
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{FD}}(\tilde{\Pi})=\mathrm{FD}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\|\tilde{\Pi})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathrm{FD}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref},j}\|\tilde{\Pi}_{j}).

(17)

In particular, if the neighbourhood can also be written as
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\times\ldots\times\Gamma_{J}
with hyperparameter
\lambda_{j}\in\Gamma_{j}
only entering through the
j^{\text{th}}
factor
\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda_{j}}_{j}(\theta_{j})
so that
\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda}(\theta)=\prod_{j=1}^{J}\tilde{\pi}_{j}^{\lambda_{j}}(\theta_{j})
, then

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sup_{\lambda_{j}\in\Gamma_{j}}\mathrm{FD}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref},j}\|\tilde{\Pi}_{j})-\inf_{\lambda_{j}\in\Gamma_{j}}\mathrm{FD}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref},j}\|\tilde{\Pi}_{j})=:\sum_{j=1}^{J}S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma_{j}).

(18)

In other words, if the belief distributions factorise across dimensions with separate hyperparameters for each factor, then our measure of sensitivity becomes a sum of measures of sensitivity across dimensions, and we can examine individual terms to assess how much sensitivity depends on each of the subsets of dimensions. Though this situation is unlikely to be common, a special case where this could be useful is when measuring prior sensitivity for independent priors across parameters, each having their own hyperparameters. In this case, as we saw in
Equation

16
, the sensitivity measure depends on the difference of scores of the priors, and therefore we only need a factorisation of the prior but not of the likelihood/loss; i.e. if
\pi_{\text{ref}}(\theta)=\pi(\theta|\lambda_{\text{ref}})
and
\pi(\theta|\lambda)=\prod_{j=1}^{J}\pi_{j}(\theta_{j}|\lambda_{j})
, then

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sup_{\lambda_{j}\in\Gamma_{j}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{j}\sim\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref},j}}\left[\left\|s_{\pi_{j}(\cdot|\lambda_{\text{ref},j})}(\theta_{j})-s_{\pi_{j}(\cdot|\lambda_{j})}(\theta_{j})\right\|^{2}_{2}\right].

(19)

This type of decomposition across parameters is not only interesting for interpretability, but also for computation. Indeed, this allows us to compute our measure of sensitivity separately per dimension, which could significantly lower the dimensionality of each of the optimisation problems, and therefore accelerate convergence.

Strength of the FD measure.

We now extend our example to

Comparison with alternative sensitivity measures.

Having illustrated our method and discussed the importance of the strength of a sensitivity measure, we move on to computational considerations. In the following example, we further extend our analysis to multivariate Gaussians with
d_{\Theta}>2
and study the impact of the dimension of the posterior
d_{\Theta}
and the number of samples
m
on the estimation error and computational cost of different methods. We compare against three other measures of sensitivity: one based on the posterior mean, and two based on statistical divergences: the Wasserstein-2 distance and the KL divergence. Our results are given in
Figure

3
.

We first compare the FD and mean measures. These both have similar estimation errors of
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1/2})
, and relatively small computational cost of
O(md_{\Theta}^{2})
and
\mathcal{O}(md_{\Theta})
respectively. Although the FD will have a larger cost for large
d_{\Theta}
, this has to be balanced with the fact that it controls changes in the entire posterior. In contrast, the two other divergence-based measures are both able to control changes in the entire posterior, but as discussed below, have estimation errors and computational costs which are orders of magnitude larger than that of the FD.

Firstly, the KL divergence is one of many statistical divergences used for sensitivity analysis, such as the Fisher-Rao
(
61
)
and Jensen-Shannon divergences
(
56
)
, which require access to densities. This is typically not feasible for posterior distributions, where densities are only known up to normalisation constants. In those cases, it is first needed to obtain samples from both the reference and candidate posteriors, then to perform density estimation separately for the reference and candidate posteriors, and finally to plug-in these estimated densities to estimate the divergence through a Monte Carlo estimator. In
Figure

3
, this is achieved through kernel density estimation, which leads to a computational cost
O(m^{2}d_{\Theta})
and whose sample complexity is well known to suffer from the curse of dimensionality. This directly shows in the corresponding top row plot, where we do not observe convergence for
d_{\Theta}=25
and
d_{\Theta}=100
for
m<5000
.

Secondly, we consider the Wasserstein-
2
distance, as studied by
34
;
70
. Here, we must sample from both the reference and candidate posterior, then estimate the divergence by computing the Wasserstein distance through optimal transport between the corresponding empirical measures. The computational complexity of this procedure is
\mathcal{O}(m^{3}+m^{2}d_{\Theta})
whenever
d_{\Theta}>1
, which is significantly larger than all other alternative methods, and the sample complexity is
O(m^{-1/d_{\Theta}})
for
d_{\Theta}>2

(
31
)
, once again exhibiting a curse of dimensionality. We note that similar results would also hold for the Wasserstein-
1
distance.

5.2
Measuring Sensitivity to Learning Rate Estimation Methods

We are now ready to move on to more realistic sensitivity problem.
One of the main difficulties faced in generalised Bayesian inference is the choice of how to balance the loss function and the prior, as these may be defined on different scales. This is typically achieved through a scalar hyperparameter called learning rate or temperature, which controls the relative importance of these quantities. Despite the importance of this problem, there is no consensus on how to select this hyperparameter, with numerous approaches having been proposed in the literature; see e.g
99
. It is also well established that generalised posteriors can be highly sensitive to the value of this hyperparameter, and that some of these methods can be somewhat unstable in low data regimes. Interestingly, our proposed FD sensitivity measure can be used to verify this sensitivity numerically.

To illustrate this, we consider inference for an Ising model, following the setup of Section 4.2 in
69
. Despite only having one parameter, this is a challenging inference problem since the likelihood is only known in unnormalised form, which makes standard posteriors doubly intractable. The data in this experiment consist of binary configurations

x\in\{0,1\}^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}
defined on a
6\times 6
grid graph, so that
d_{\mathcal{X}}=36
, and
p_{\theta}(x)\propto\exp(\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathcal{X}}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}x_{i}x_{j})
where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mathcal{N}_{i}
denotes the neighbours of
x_{i}
on the graph.
We use a
\chi^{2}
prior with
3
degrees of freedom, and consider the dataset of size
n=1000
provided in this paper. We consider three generalised posteriors obtained through the pseudo-likelihood
(
14
)
, the discrete Fisher divergence (DFD;
69
), and the discrete kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD;
100
).
For each posterior, we used
m=5000
samples from a reference posterior obtained via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with
1000
burn-in samples, which takes approximately 7 seconds. We also verify

and
3
in
Section

.

We compare three learning rate selection methods by
91
,
66
and
69

\lambda_{\text{ref}}
, and measure sensitivity around it:
\Gamma=\{\lambda:|\lambda-\lambda_{\text{ref}}|\leq\epsilon\}

\epsilon>0
controls the size of the neighbourhood. This is particularly important because all three of these methods depend on algorithmic choices which can substantially affect the resulting estimate of the learning rate, such as a number of bootstrap replications or the number of steps of a numerical optimiser. The sensitivity measure should therefore tell us whether a given learning rate estimation method is stable for the problem at hand. The FD is particularly computationally convenient for this task; we see from
Equation

15
that for a loss of the form
L(\theta;x_{1:n},\lambda)=\lambda l(\theta;x_{1:n})

S^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma)=\epsilon^{2}\;\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}}\![\|\nabla_{\theta}l(\theta;x_{1:n})\|_{2}^{2}]

5.3
Autoregressive model of the temperature in Kilpisjärvi

For our next experiment, we return to the problem of prior sensitivity, but now consider the more challenging problem of Bayesian inference for time-series models, where sensitivity to hyperparameters is crucial
(
57
;
37
)
.
The aim of this experiment is twofold: to illustrate the method on a higher-dimensional posterior with real data, and to assess the performance of the FD sensitivity measure under different choices of optimisation routines.

We consider an autoregressive (AR) model for a univariate real-world time series of June temperatures recorded in Kilpisjärvi (Finland) starting in
1952
, with
n=62
observations; see
Figure

Bayesian time-series models are particularly useful here because the amount of data is limited and there is uncertainty about the underlying temporal dependence structure, leading practitioners to use relatively vague priors. Therefore, prior sensitivity is important since downstream conclusions are based on posterior predictive distributions, and sensitivity to plausible prior perturbations may substantially affect predictive reliability.
The likelihood for an AR model of order
S\in\mathbb{N}
is conditionally Gaussian and breaks down as follows:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x_{1:n}|x_{1:S})=\prod_{i=S}^{n}p_{\theta}(x_{i}|x_{i-1},\ldots,x_{i-S}),\quad p_{\theta}(x_{i}|x_{i-1},\ldots,x_{i-S})=\mathcal{N}\Big(x_{i};\alpha+\sum_{s=1}^{S}\beta_{s}\,x_{i-s},\sigma^{2}\Big)

(21)

and the parameters are
\theta=[\alpha,\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{S},\sigma]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}-1}\times(0,\infty)
. We reproduce the analysis of this dataset in the
posteriordb

(
67
)
package, and therefore use a reference prior for this model encodes independence across parameters:
\pi_{\text{ref}}(\alpha)=\mathcal{N}(\alpha;0,\,5^{2}),\pi_{\text{ref}}(\beta_{s})=\mathcal{N}(\beta_{s};0,\,5^{2})
for
s\in\{1,\ldots,S\}
, and
\pi_{\text{ref}}(\sigma)=\mathrm{HalfCauchy}(\sigma;0,\,1)
where
\sigma>0
is a scale parameter. For this dataset, we fix
S=5
, so that
d_{\Theta}=7
, and for each parameter we consider sensitivity to both hyperparameters, so that
d_{\Lambda}=14
. More precisely, we have

\displaystyle\pi(\theta|\lambda)=\pi(\sigma|\lambda_{\sigma})\,\pi(\alpha|\lambda_{\alpha})\,\prod_{s=1}^{S}\pi(\beta_{s}|\lambda_{\beta_{s}})

(22)

with
\lambda:=\lambda_{\pi}=[\lambda_{\alpha},\lambda_{\beta_{1}},\dots,\lambda_{\beta_{5}},\lambda_{\sigma}]

\pi(\alpha|\lambda_{\alpha})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha;\mu_{\alpha},s_{\alpha}^{2})

\pi(\beta_{s}|\lambda_{\beta_{s}})=\mathcal{N}(\beta_{s};\mu_{\beta_{s}},s_{\beta_{s}}^{2})
,

\pi(\sigma|\lambda_{\sigma})=\mathrm{InvGamma}(\sigma;a,b)
, and we construct a box-constrained neighbourhood by placing upper and lower bounds on each hyperparameter. We use the inverse Gamma family for
\sigma

\Gamma=\prod_{j=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\Gamma_{j}

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{m}^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma):=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\widehat{S}_{m}^{\mathrm{FD}}(\Gamma_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\Theta}}\sup_{\lambda_{j}\in\Gamma_{j}}\Big(\lambda_{j}^{\top}A_{j}\lambda_{j}+b_{j}^{\top}\lambda_{j}+c_{j}\Big)-\inf_{\lambda_{j}\in\Gamma_{j}}\Big(\lambda_{j}^{\top}A_{j}\lambda_{j}+b_{j}^{\top}\lambda_{j}+c_{j}\Big).

\inf_{\lambda_{\sigma}\in\Gamma_{\sigma}}\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\lambda_{\sigma})
only for
\sigma
.

m=5000

5

5.4
Simulation-based inference for calibrating radio propagation models

Our final example showcases our approach on a highly challenging Bayesian inference problem. We consider simulation-based inference (SBI) for the Turin radio propagation model
(
93
)
. The Turin model is an early stochastic radio channel model which is commonly used to help engineers evaluate communication systems across different propagation environments without collecting costly real-world measurements. Although it was first proposed in the 1970s, it remains widely used for modern millimetre-wave systems such as 5G
(
43
;
83
)
.

The Turin model is typically described through a simulator generating complex time-series observations and depending on
d_{\Theta}=4
parameters

\theta=[G_{0},T,\nu,\sigma_{W}^{2}]
. The observations take the form
x=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}X_{k}\exp(j2\pi k\Delta f\tau_{l})
, where
X_{k}=H_{k}+W_{k}
,
W_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{W}^{2})
is Gaussian observation noise, and

H_{k}=\sum_{l}\alpha_{l}\exp(-j2\pi k\Delta f\tau_{l})
is a transfer function with a frequency separation
\Delta f=\frac{B}{K-1}
and bandwidth
B
.
The delays
\tau_{l}
follow a Poisson point process with constant intensity
\nu
, and the gains
\alpha_{l}
are zero-mean complex Gaussians with exponentially decaying variance
\sigma^{2}(\tau_{l})=G_{0}/\nu\exp(\tau_{l}/T)

(
16
;
89
)

In this paper, we consider the setting in
50
, where we observe a single time-series (i.e.
n=1
) of length
K=801
with
B=4\text{GHz}
. We use reference uniform priors

G_{0}\sim\text{Unif}(10^{-9},10^{-8})
,

T\sim\text{Unif}(10^{-9},10^{-8})
,

\nu\sim\text{Unif}(10^{7},5\times 10^{9})
, and

\sigma_{W}^{2}\sim\text{Unif}(10^{-10},10^{-9})
, and perform SBI through neural likelihood estimation with a masked-autoregressive flow and a pre-trained summary network reducing the observation to a six-dimensional statistic. We first reparameterise the problem so that each parameter is on
(0,1)
, then generate
m=5000
posterior samples from
\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}
using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with
10
parallel chains, discarding the first
1000
iterations per chain as burn-in. This takes
\approx 180
seconds.

Although the majority of the literature
(
16
;
15
;
50
)
works with independent priors due to the simplicity of specifying upper and lower bounds per parameter, this may not be a good choice as some combination of parameters might be unrealistic from a physical perspective. For instance, when communication takes place in a room
(
78
)
, the Poisson arrival rate parameter
\nu
and the signal decay rate
T
get positively coupled: an increase in
\nu
corresponds to more reverberation of the signal inside the room, which suggests slower decay of the signal and hence larger
T
. Conversely,
\nu
and
G_{0}
are negatively correlated as a large signal variance
\sigma^{2}(\tau)
in the observations can be explained by either increasing
G_{0}
or decreasing
\nu
.

We therefore perform sensitivity analysis to the choice of prior for this problem. Given two parameters
\theta_{i},\theta_{j}
that we suspect of being correlated, we consider candidate priors of the form

\displaystyle\pi(\theta\mid\lambda)=\pi_{\text{ref}}(\theta\mid\lambda_{\mathrm{prior}})c_{\lambda_{c}}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j}),

where
\lambda:=[\lambda_{\mathrm{prior}},\lambda_{c}]
. This candidate family preserves the marginals of
\theta_{i}
and
\theta_{j}
while introducing dependence through a copula density which induces correlation through the magnitude of
\lambda_{c}
:

\displaystyle c_{\lambda_{c}}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\lambda_{c}^{2}}}\exp\!\left(\frac{2\lambda_{c}\Phi^{-1}(\theta_{i})\Phi^{-1}(\theta_{j})-\lambda_{c}^{2}((\Phi^{-1}(\theta_{i}))^{2}+(\Phi^{-1}(\theta_{j}))^{2})}{2(1-\lambda_{c}^{2})}\right).

We consider neighbourhoods of the form

CONCLUSION

This paper introduces a new computationally tractable method for global sensitivity analysis in Bayesian inference based on the Fisher divergence. By requiring only a single set of posterior samples and operating through score functions, our method directly addresses the key computational limitations that have hindered the practical use of Bayesian global sensitivity analysis. In many cases of practical relevance, we showed that the Fisher divergence is a convex quadratic form, which allows the associated optimisation problems to be solved efficiently. In addition, because the Fisher divergence considers discrepancies in the entire posterior, rather than merely summary statistics, it provides a strong measure of sensitivity which has high potential for challenging Bayesian inference problems.

We believe there are many possible and exciting extensions for the use of score-based divergences for Bayesian sensitivity analysis. The method could be readily applied across a wide range of Bayesian inference settings, including hierarchical models, as well as multi-task
(
40
)
, federated
(
102
;
19
)
, and transfer learning
(
90
)
settings. These settings are particularly relevant because model parameters are organised across levels (e.g., global and task-specific), and the FD objective can therefore be simplified under a per-level decomposition. This could enable sensitivity analysis of different levels of the model, allowing the practitioner to identify, for instance, whether posterior instability is driven by shared hyperparameters or task-specific components, or whether heterogeneity across tasks amplifies sensitivity.

Beyond this, several methodological extensions could be pursued in future work. A first direction would be to address settings involving multimodal posteriors with well-separated modes, a scenario for which score-based divergences are known to perform poorly
(
96
;
103
)
. One potential remedy is to construct the divergence using a tempered version of the reference posterior, thereby reducing the blindness to disjoint modes. A second direction would be to extend our measure beyond hyperparameter sensitivity. For instance,
9
;
72
;
61
;
46
study robustness under neighbourhoods defined by probability balls centred at a reference prior or likelihood; adapting our approach to these types of neighbourhoods could yield valuable insights. Finally, a third promising avenue would be to generalise the method beyond Euclidean parameter spaces to encompass posteriors defined over discrete domains, for which score functions are not naturally defined. In this case, the divergence proposed by
62
offers a principled alternative and also leads to a convex quadratic form. Such an extension would be particularly relevant for Bayesian model choice and variable selection problems involving discrete indices, as well as for hidden Markov models with discrete latent states.

Appendix B
Special Cases: Measuring Sensitivity to either Prior or Loss

In this section, we discuss how the FD sensitivity measure simplifies if we only consider prior sensitivity or loss sensitivity, but not joint sensitivity. In each case, we discuss how the standing assumptions can be simplified, and we derive simplified quadratic form representations of the FD.

B.1
Prior sensitivity

When measuring prior sensitivity only, we have already noted that the sensitivity measure simplifies as in
Equation

16
. Here, the scores of the loss function have been cancelled out, and we only look at differences in the prior scores. For this reason, the standing assumptions can be simplified to requiring only that
\pi,\pi_{\text{ref}}\in C^{1}(\Theta)
and
s_{\pi},s_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}})
, but we do not require
L,L_{\text{ref}}\in C^{1}(\Theta)
and
\nabla L,\nabla L_{\text{ref}}\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}})
in this setting.

The expression for the quadratic form in
Proposition

2
also simplifies. In particular, we have

\lambda_{L}=\lambda_{L_{\mathrm{ref}}}
, so the FD estimator becomes a function of the prior parameter

\lambda_{\pi}
only. Thus,
d_{\Lambda}=d_{{\Lambda}_{\pi}}
. Substituting
\lambda_{L_{\mathrm{ref}}}
into the quadratic form yields

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\|\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})=\lambda_{\pi}^{\top}A_{\pi}\lambda_{\pi}+b_{\pi}^{\top}\lambda_{\pi}+c_{\pi}

(49)

where the coefficients are given by

\displaystyle A_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla_{\theta}T(\theta_{i})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}T(\theta_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Lambda}\times d_{\Lambda}},

\displaystyle b_{\pi}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla_{\theta}T(\theta_{i})^{\top}\Big(s_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\theta_{i})-\nabla_{\theta}\log g(\theta_{i})\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Lambda}\times 1},

\displaystyle c_{\pi}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big\|s_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\theta_{i})-\nabla_{\theta}\log g(\theta_{i})\Big\|_{2}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

B.2
Loss sensitivity

Similarly to the above, the sensitivity measure simplifies when we only consider learning-rate sensitivity; see
Equation

15
. Here, the scores of the priors have been cancelled out, and we only look at differences in the scores of the losses. For this reason, the standing assumption can be simplified to requiring only that
L,L_{\text{ref}}\in C^{1}(\Theta)
and
\nabla L,\nabla L_{\text{ref}}\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}})
but we do not require
\pi,\pi_{\text{ref}}\in C^{1}(\Theta)
and
s_{\pi},s_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}})

The expression for the quadratic form in

2

the quadratic form reduces to a univariate quadratic function of the learning

\lambda_{L}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{FD}}_{m}(\tilde{\Pi}_{\text{ref}}\|\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda})=\lambda_{L}^{\top}A_{L}\lambda_{L}+b_{L}^{\top}\lambda_{L}+c_{L}.

C.3.1

In this experiment, sensitivity is analysed only with respect to the prior, so that

1

We first consider the reference posterior distribution

\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}-1}\times(0,\infty)

The set
\Theta

\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Theta}}

The autoregressive loss component is a standard log-likelihood which is finite

\theta\in\Theta

\displaystyle\alpha\sim\mathcal{N}(0,5^{2}),\qquad\beta_{1:S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,5^{2}),\qquad\sigma\sim\mathrm{HalfCauchy}(0,1),

\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}(\theta)>0

The reference prior density factorises as

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\theta)=\pi_{\mathrm{ref},\alpha}(\alpha)\prod_{s=1}^{S}\pi_{\mathrm{ref},\beta_{s}}(\beta_{s})\pi_{\mathrm{ref},\sigma}(\sigma),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\mathrm{ref}}}\mathbb{E}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\Big[\|s_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}\exp(-L_{\mathrm{ref}}(\theta;x_{1:n}))\Big]\leq\frac{1}{Z_{\mathrm{ref}}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[\|s_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\theta)\|_{2}^{4}]\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[\exp(-2L_{\mathrm{ref}}(\theta;x_{1:n}))]\Big)^{1/2}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[\exp(-2L_{\mathrm{ref}}(\theta;x_{1:n}))]

\sigma>0

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{S-n}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\sigma)C(\sigma)d\sigma

\sigma\to\infty

\sigma\to 0^{+}

\sigma\to\infty

Appendix D
Local sensitivity

We study the local sensitivity of the FD with respect to perturbations of the prior hyperparameters
\lambda\in\Lambda
. Consider a directional perturbation

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mu}=\lambda+\mu v,\qquad v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Lambda}},\ \|v\|_{2}=1.

The prior score function is
s_{\pi}(\theta|\lambda)=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi(\theta|\lambda)
.
Its directional derivative with respect to
\lambda
along
v
is given by

\displaystyle\dot{s}_{\pi}(\theta|\lambda):=\left.\frac{d}{d\mu}s_{\pi}(\theta|\lambda_{\mu})\right|_{\mu=0}=\frac{\partial s_{\pi}(\theta|\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}v=J_{s_{\pi}}(\theta|\lambda)v,

where
J_{s_{\pi}}(\theta|\lambda)
denotes the Jacobian of the prior score with respect to the hyperparameters. Recalling the estimated FD, its directional derivative at
\mu=0
is

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{d\mu}\,\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{FD}}(\tilde{\Pi}^{\lambda_{\mu}})\right|_{\mu=0}=-\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(s_{\tilde{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\theta_{i})-s_{\tilde{\pi}^{\lambda_{\mu}}}(\theta_{i})\big)^{\top}\dot{s}_{\pi}(\theta_{i}|\lambda).

This expression provides a closed-form and easily computable characterisation of the local sensitivity of the FD with respect to infinitesimal perturbations of the prior hyperparameters.
