Upfront unroofing of subepithelial lesions using cold snare in the upper and lower gastrointestinal tract: a retrospective study from Switzerland
Authors: Bernhard Morell, Jasmina El Hadad-Burla, Frans Olivier The, Fabian Reinisch, Christoph Gubler, Fritz Ruprecht Murray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsSubepithelial lesions (SELs) are frequently encountered in the upper and lower gastrointestinal (GI) tracts during routine endoscopy. As a significant proportion of SELs harbor malignant potential, a definitive diagnosis with tissue sampling should be sought in most patients. However, small SELs (<20 mm) represent a clinical challenge because endoscopic ultrasound-guided tissue acquisition may not be as efficient under these circumstances. Furthermore, mucosal incision biopsies require equipment that is not universally available.MethodsWe retrospectively investigated the diagnostic yield and safety of cold snare unroofing in an observational cohort.ResultsA total of 71 SELs (41 in the upper GI tract and 30 in the lower GI tract) with a median size of 10 mm (range, 3–60 mm) were included. Unroofing resulted in a histologic diagnosis in 85% (n=60) of cases, with no further diagnostic procedures or surveillance required in 88.3% (n=53/60). The final diagnoses consisted of 10 different etiologies, including three GI stromal tumors, one neuroendocrine tumor, and 44 lipomas. No significant complications, such as bleeding or perforation, were observed.ConclusionsCold snare unroofing represents an efficient and easy method for characterizing small SELs in the upper and lower GI tracts, offering a valuable alternative to mucosal incision-assisted biopsy (MIAB) and endoscopic ultrasound with fine-needle biopsy.

INTRODUCTION

Subepithelial lesions (SELs) of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract are masses or cysts that arise anywhere within the various wall layers of the gut and are covered by normal-appearing mucosa.1,2 The term SEL encompasses a heterogeneous group of entities that may be neoplastic (such as leiomyomas or gastrointestinal stromal tumors [GISTs]) or non-neoplastic (e.g., pancreatic rest, duplication cysts) in origin.1,3 Although most SELs are non-neoplastic lesions or benign neoplasms, approximately 20% are either malignant or harbor malignant potential, which need to be addressed.4

SELs are usually detected incidentally during routine endoscopy as rounded protuberances of various sizes. However, SELs occasionally become symptomatic when they cause hemorrhage or symptoms arising from luminal obstruction.5 The exact prevalence of SELs remains unknown.6 It is estimated that gastric SELs are found in approximately 1 in every 200 gastroscopies,7,8 and possibly in as many as 1 in 50 upper endoscopies.9

Except for lipomas, the diagnosis of solid SELs based on endoscopic or endosonographic appearance is not possible. Thus, guidelines recommend tissue acquisition with either endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided fine-needle biopsy (FNB) or EUS fine-needle aspiration, together with rapid on-site evaluation.1,2 In the case of SELs <20 mm, mucosal incision-assisted biopsy (MIAB) is an equal alternative to EUS-assisted tissue acquisition10 as three randomized controlled trials demonstrated no significant differences with respect to diagnostic yield and adverse events between the two methods.11-13 Alternatively, guidelines suggest surveillance of asymptomatic gastric SELs if a definitive histological diagnosis is not available.1-3 On the other hand, the ESGE recommends that an affirmative histological diagnosis be established for duodenal and colorectal SELs, as evidence in favor of surveillance is insufficient.2

However, EUS-assisted tissue acquisition has several disadvantages. The diagnostic yield may be considerably lower in SELs smaller than 20 mm, as studies have demonstrated success rates in the range of 53.9%–71.4%.11,14-16 In addition, linear echoendoscopes are not suitable for the examination of SELs distal to the duodenum and proximal to the sigmoid colon and are not uniformly available. Therefore, recent guidelines suggest MIAB as a valuable option for characterizing small SELs.1,2

MIAB encompasses several variants of endoscopic techniques that use unroofing before biopsy sampling of the SEL is performed2. In its original form, a tip knife is used to dissect the mucosa and, if needed, the submucosa,17 later referred to as single-incision needle-knife biopsy. Direct visualization of the SEL can be improved by injecting diluted epinephrine into the submucosa adjacent to the SEL.11,18 The most refined but simultaneously demanding method encompasses submucosal endoscopy with the mucosal flap method, in which a submucosal tunnel is created to reach the SEL.19

Recently, our group demonstrated that the unroofing principle can be safely applied using a cold snare,20 as originally described by Zimmer and Eltze.21 In this pragmatic study, we describe the efficacy and safety of cold snare unroofing in an extended real-world cohort of patients with SELs of the upper and lower GI tract.

METHODS

The investigation described herein was a retrospective, single-center cohort study. The patients considered for this analysis underwent endoscopy with cold snare unroofing of an SEL in the upper or lower GI tract between September 2022 and February 2025. The SELs evaluated in this study were either found incidentally at our clinic during routine endoscopy or further characterized upon referral. The indication to perform cold snare unroofing was established if the SEL etiology was unknown, upon request of the referring physician, or if the patient insisted on ruling out malignancy through tissue acquisition. The exclusion criteria for the analysis were age <18 years, missing informed consent, tissue sampling deemed unindicated based on clinical circumstances (e.g., limited life expectancy), portal hypertension, and suspected malignancy.

Description of the unroofing procedure

All patients presenting with SELs were evaluated according to predefined criteria. With respect to Zimmer and Eltze21 as well as our previous publication,20 the standard operating procedure for the diagnostic work-up of SELs at our institution was adapted to further investigate the diagnostic efficacy and safety of cold snare unroofing and to avoid potentially unnecessary further diagnostic work-ups, potentially cumbersome procedures (e.g., EUS-FNB for lesions <15 mm), and redundant surveillance procedures. Hence, although formally retrospective, the management and data acquisition were conducted as in prospective cohort studies. Following the routine protocol at our clinic, unroofing was performed for all SELs in the upper and lower GI tracts, regardless of whether they were diagnosed incidentally during routine endoscopy or after a specific referral. As stated above, SELs were excluded if the clinical index of suspicion was high for malignancy (e.g., in the case of an ulcerated, firm lesion in the proximal stomach or radiologic features favoring a malignant GIST). Furthermore, SELs in the antrum with sufficiently specific endoscopic features of the ectopic pancreas (location in the antrum and central umbilication) were not unroofed, or whenever the exact diagnosis was deemed clinically irrelevant. In cases of suspected lipomas, unroofing was conducted if a classical "pillow sign" was missing or when patients requested a histopathological diagnosis. All unroofing procedures were performed in the same manner, analogous to a previously described method.20 SELs were carefully examined endoscopically to evaluate the overlying mucosa and other macroscopic characteristics (e.g., central umbilication) that would point to a specific entity. The tip of the snare was used to probe lesion consistency. Lesion size was estimated using the diameter of the open snare. Next, the mucosa at the apex of each SEL was resected with a standard 10 mm cold snare (Fig. 1A, B). If required, unroofing was repeated until the underlying lesion was exposed. Subsequently, targeted biopsies (median, 3; range, 1–13) were performed using standard forceps (Fig. 1C). Finally, the unroofed site was observed to exclude bleeding. Prophylactic clips were placed at the discretion of the examiner.

EUS was performed prior to unroofing if a lesion with malignant potential was suspected, upon request by the referring physician, or to rule out relevant contraindications, such as gastric varices or duplication cysts. Notably, EUS procedures are not performed by residents in our clinic; however, they are encouraged to perform cold snare unroofing after identification of an SEL.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the diagnostic yield of cold snare unroofing based on representative tissue acquisition. The secondary endpoints were safety (complications), clinical relevance of the histopathological results, and relevance of unroofing, stratified according to SELs with external referral and prior inconclusive histology.

Statistics

All data were analyzed descriptively. Continuous variables with a skewed distribution or ordinal variables are summarized as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Ethical statement

All patients included in this analysis provided written consent for the evaluation of relevant data, and a competent ethics committee (Ethikkommission Kanton Zürich, BASEC No. 2024-02501) approved this study.

RESULTS

At the time of the analysis in February 2025, 89 SELs have been evaluated. In 10 of the 89 SELs, no unroofing was performed leading to exclusion from the analysis and the final diagnosis was made by endoscopic appearance (n=6 lipomas), MIAB (n=1 lipoma) and, forceps biopsy (n=1 lipoma). In addition to that, two leiomyomas were excluded from the analysis as in these cases the SELs were incidentally removed by cold snare and in another two patients, no biopsy was taken after successful unroofing (n=2 lipomas judged by the macroscopic appearance of fatty tissue). Lastly, 6 patients did not consent to participate in the study. As a result, 71 SELs (n=41 in the upper and n=30 in the lower GI tract) in 68 patients (three patients were diagnosed with two SELs; female, n=38 (53%); median age 67 years, range: 31-87) met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed, thereof 19 cases have already been described in our brief report.20 Patients’ clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1.

In general, the investigated SELs were small, with a median size of 10 mm; only 9 SELs (12.5%) measured ≥25 mm (7/9 resembling lipomas). Clips were placed in nine patients, all of whom were prophylactic. No perioperative or interventional bleeding was observed.

The final diagnoses consisted of 9 different etiologies, most of which were benign. However, there were 3 GISTs; n=2 in the upper and n=1 in the lower GI tract (median size 12 mm, range: 5–16) and one duodenal NET (15 mm). The etiology of the other three lesions remained unclear.

Clinical consequences of SELs with histology-proven diagnosis

Unroofing resulted in a final histological diagnosis in 84.7% (n=60), with no further diagnostic procedures or surveillance required in 88.3% (n=53/60).

Three patients underwent interventional therapy after histopathological confirmation of two GISTs and one NET. One of the GISTs, located in the gastric fundus, had previously been biopsied with inconclusive histology and was externally referred for further diagnosis. After final confirmation, the patient underwent an uncomplicated wedge resection. The other GIST was located in the cecum and was detected as a positron emission tomography-positive finding during follow-up of a known prostatic GIST. After histopathological diagnosis, the patient underwent a right hemicolectomy. The duodenal NET was discovered during an anemia work-up and was completely removed by full-thickness resection after histological confirmation following unroofing.

Three patients were included in the surveillance program despite a final non-malignant diagnosis. In two cases (leiomyoma and duplication cysts), the patients explicitly requested follow-up. In another case, a 54-year-old male patient was referred for a suspected colonic malignancy. After evaluation of radiological and external endoscopic images, the decision was made in favor of repeat colonoscopy. A 50 mm spherical, mobile lesion with an ulcerated apex was identified. Unroofing was performed, and biopsies ultimately revealed a lipoma. Owing to the lesion size and a medical history of recurrent colicky abdominal pain, repeat intussusception was suspected, and a non-oncologic right hemicolectomy was performed. Histological examination of the surgical specimen confirmed a lipoma.

Clinical consequences of SELs with inconclusive histology after unroofing

In 11 patients (15.3%), unroofing did not lead to a histologically confirmed diagnosis. In six of these cases (ectopic pancreas, n=4; duplication cyst, n=1; lipoma, n=1), the diagnosis was made based on characteristic EUS results and/or macroscopic appearance. One GIST (16 mm, located in the stomach antrum) with inconclusive histology after unroofing was finally diagnosed and successfully treated with full-thickness resection, and one duodenal duplication cyst was diagnosed after surgical resection (Whipple procedure for pancreatic cancer). Three lesions remained unclear (one patient will undergo further follow-up, which was refused by the second patient; in the case of the third patient, surveillance was deemed unnecessary because malignancy was ruled out).

Results upon external referral

Nineteen (26.8%) SELs located in the stomach (n=12), duodenum (n=3), or colon (n=4) were externally referred (Table 4). Unroofing confirmed the final histology in 13 (68.4%) patients, including lipoma (n=4), ectopic pancreas (n=2), GIST (n=1), leiomyoma (n=3), duplication cyst (n=1), and foreign bodies (n=2). One of the lipomas (10 mm, located in the colon) was explicitly referred for full-thickness resection, which was unnecessary after unroofing confirmed the final diagnosis. The remaining 6 SELs with inconclusive histology after unroofing were all diagnosed based on the characteristic EUS results and/or endoscopic appearance: lipoma (n=1), ectopic pancreas (n=4), and duplication cyst (n=1). Notably, half (n=14/27, 51.8%) of the investigated SELs with final non-lipoma diagnoses were referred externally.

Results after prior inconclusive forceps biopsy

Twelve SELs (16.9%) were investigated after prior inconclusive forceps biopsy, eight of which followed external referral (Table 4). Unroofing led to a final diagnosis in 9/12 cases (75.0%). Two of these previously unclear SELs were GISTs (external or internal referral 1/1). The remaining seven lesions were benign, consisting of n=3 lipomas (duodenum, n=2; colon, n=1), an ectopic pancreas (n=1), a leiomyoma (n=1), an duplication cyst (n=1) and a foreign body (n=1). Two SELs with inconclusive histology after unroofing were diagnosed based on characteristic EUS results and/or endoscopic appearance. In the remaining case, histology showed thickened mucosal/submucosal layer (n=1) with unspecific histological findings.

DISCUSSION

Management of small SELs represents a significant clinical challenge.22 As EUS-FNB yields only moderate sensitivity for SELs <20 mm,11,14-16 patients with small SELs often undergo repeat endoscopies to establish a histological diagnosis using different methods, such as MIAB, or are enrolled in a surveillance program. In addition, neither EUS nor MIAB equipment is readily available when SELs are discovered, thereby contributing to the considerable diagnostic effort required for these patients. In fact, 26.8% of the patients examined in this study were externally referred.

We were able to establish a firm diagnosis in 85% of patients, the majority of whom presented with small SELs with a median size of 10 mm. Moreover, in 88.3% of patients with a histologically confirmed diagnosis, no further interventions were necessary, underscoring the efficiency of SELs management. In contrast, in 9/12 patients with initially inconclusive histology, cold snare unroofing established the diagnosis. Hence, cold snare unroofing has proven to be an efficient and readily available method for diagnosing small SELs.

The diagnostic yield demonstrated in this study was lower than that reported in a meta-analysis23 that analyzed seven series evaluating the efficacy of MIAB, with an overall success rate of 89%. However, the pooled mean tumor size in this meta-analysis was 21 (95% confidence interval, 18.1–24.00) mm, which limits the comparability of the two studies. The reported success rate is, however, comparable to the results of a recent multicenter cohort study combining various unroofing techniques, with special emphasis on tumor exposure14 and to another study evaluating factors associated with diagnostic failure using mucosal cutting biopsy.24

In addition to providing further evidence for the application of cold snare unroofing in the upper GI tract, we demonstrated the usefulness of this method in the colorectum. SELs in the lower GI tract may present a particular challenge, as the use of EUS-FNB is limited owing to the reduced maneuverability of the linear echoendoscope and the low availability of miniprobes. In contrast, the ESGE guidelines suggest that for colorectal SELs, a definite diagnosis should be established, as evidence supporting surveillance is lacking.2 Albeit rare,25 GISTs arising in the colon commonly become malignant,26 supporting the notion of decisively obtaining a histological diagnosis. Herein, we histologically diagnosed 96.8% (29/30) of the SELs in the lower GI tract without any complications. By including the endoscopic appearance after unroofing as a criterion, we achieved a diagnostic yield of 100% in the lower GI tract.

The high prevalence of lipoma in this cohort warrants further investigation. Even though guidelines do not demand tissue sampling to establish the diagnosis of a lipoma, it needs to be kept in mind that the pillow sign has a modest sensitivity of 40%.27 In addition to ruling out other entities and justifying endoscopic or surgical resection, a definitive diagnosis of symptomatic lipoma should be attempted. Colonic lipomas are rare and can cause bleeding or intussusception.28 We opted for tissue sampling only if the pillow sign was lacking, prior investigations, such as sampling with forceps biopsy, were inconclusive, or if the lesion caused symptoms, such as bleeding, requiring further measures. EUS was additionally performed upon external referral or if cold snare unroofing was non-diagnostic. Whether cold snare unroofing offers additional benefits over forceps biopsy for the diagnosis of lipomas remains unclear. Of the 12 patients with prior inconclusive histology, three had a lipoma. The reason for inadequate tissue sampling using forceps biopsy in these cases remains unclear. Scarring of the overlying tissue layers or disintegration of soft fatty tissue during specimen processing may have contributed to this finding.

It should be emphasized that no major complications, such as bleeding or perforation, were observed. In nine cases, prophylactic clipping was performed. In addition to the self-explanatory efficacy and safety of the method described herein, the strengths of this study should be mentioned. It is important to note that cold snare unroofing was integrated into our daily clinical practice following a stringent procedural standard. In other words, wherever an SEL was found, and tissue sampling was indicated, cold snare unroofing was performed by both staff gastroenterologists and residents. Therefore, the circumstances of this cohort may be representative of small, incidentally detected SELs evaluated by gastroenterologists who were not uniformly trained in interventional endoscopy. Thus, it can be concluded that cold snare unroofing can be performed by endoscopists with different levels of experience, thereby establishing a firm diagnosis in a high proportion of patients.

With respect to the etiologies of SELs in this cohort, it is apparent that lesions of the 4th layer are underrepresented, as only 4/24 of the non-lipomatous SELs of the upper GI tract reside in the muscularis propria. In addition to chance, the fact that SELs with a high chance of being malignant were excluded might have contributed to this potential selection bias. Another limitation is that gastric or duodenal varices were not consistently ruled out before unroofing. Although these are rarely encountered in the absence of further endoscopic signs of portal hypertension, we suggest performing EUS in patients with suspected portal hypertension and SELs at the gastroesophageal junction or proximal stomach.

Lastly, further studies comparing “classic” MIAB using electrocautery or EUS-FNB with cold snare unroofing would be of great interest to clarify the exact role of our method in the diagnosis of SELs. The lack of a control group is an important shortcoming of the present study. Nevertheless, we regard cold snare unroofing as an additional tool in the early work-up of small SELs that may help prevent further endoscopies, but it lacks the potential to replace MIAB or EUS-FNB for larger and/or deeper buried lesions.

Taken together, this study confirms our previous finding20 that cold snare unroofing can be efficiently and safely applied in the upper and lower GI tracts for the characterization of small, incidentally found SELs. Its ease of application suggests that it can be performed upfront during routine endoscopy before referral to expert centers for EUS and MIAB. However, caution should be exercised in patients with potential portal hypertension, for whom EUS remains irreplaceable.
