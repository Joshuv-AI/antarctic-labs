Machine Learning for Two-Stage Graph Sparsification for the Travelling Salesman Problem
Bo-Cheng Lin, Yi Mei, Mengjie Zhang

Abstract
High-performance TSP solvers like LKH search within a sparsified candidate graph rather than over all possible edges. Graph sparsification is non-trivial: keep too many edges and the solver wastes time; cut too many and it loses edges that belong to the optimal tour. The two leading heuristic methods, α \alpha -Nearest and POPMUSIC, produce high-quality candidate graphs, but no single heuristic is both sparse and reliable across all instance sizes and distributions. Machine learning methods can potentially learn better sparsification models. However, existing approaches operate on the complete graph, which is expensive and mostly restricted to Euclidean distances. To address this issue, we propose a two-stage graph sparsification approach: Stage 1 takes the union of α \alpha -Nearest and POPMUSIC to maximise recall; Stage 2 trains a single model to reduce density. We conducted experiments across four TSPLIB distance types, five spatial distributions, and problem sizes from 50 to 500. The two-stage approach substantially reduces candidate-graph density while retaining high coverage, generalises across distance types and distributions, outperforms recent neural sparsification methods that are restricted to Euclidean distances, and becomes increasingly valuable at larger scales where single-stage heuristics degrade. 1 1 1 Source code: https://github.com/LinHuanli/ML_TSP_Reduction .



1
Introduction

High-performance travelling salesman problem (TSP) solvers do not try all possible edges. Instead, they work within a sparsified
candidate graph
, a small subset of edges that the solver is allowed to use. Heins et al.
[
12
]
showed that candidate-set design is a first-order determinant of LKH performance, and candidate graphs are equally important in other frameworks such as ant colony optimisation
[
28
]
. A good candidate graph must be sparse enough for fast search but must keep the edges that belong to high-quality tours. No existing method fully solves this trade-off.

The two most powerful heuristic methods for building candidate graphs
[
12
]
, both integrated in the state-of-the-art LKH solver
[
13
]
, are
\alpha
-Nearest and POPMUSIC. At small scales, POPMUSIC appears ideal: it is much sparser than
\alpha
-Nearest and loses fewer optimal-tour edges. However, its recall degrades significantly at larger scales and on hard spatial distributions.
\alpha
-Nearest is denser but more stable in recall across sizes. Neither method alone is both sparse and reliable across all scales and distributions. Their union maximises recall but at the cost of the highest density. The core question is:
how can we reduce the density of a high-recall candidate graph without losing the edges that matter?

We propose a
two-stage
approach: first maximise recall, then reduce density. In Stage 1, we take the union of
\alpha
-Nearest and POPMUSIC to build a candidate graph with near-perfect coverage. In Stage 2, we train a single model to score each edge and prune the lowest-scoring ones. The union creates a
source-provenance
signal that indicates whether an edge comes from one heuristic or both. This makes the learning problem easy: edges present in both heuristics are almost always optimal-tour edges, while single-source edges are candidates for removal. This order is essential; reversing it would force the ML model to work on all
N(N{-}1)/2
edges without structural guidance, and the heuristic applied afterwards could not recover edges the model already discarded. We design all features to be
distance-type agnostic
, so the method works for any TSPLIB distance convention. Following the multi-metric evaluation principle advocated by Kerschke et al.
[
18
]
, we report density, coverage, and downstream solver performance jointly.

Our contributions are:

•

We propose a two-stage graph sparsification pipeline that applies learned pruning to already-sparse heuristic candidate graphs rather than to the complete graph. A single model, trained once on a mixed dataset comprising 4 distance types and 5 instance distributions, reduces density by up to
47\%
while retaining
{\geq}99.69\%
of optimal-tour edges across all four distance types and five distributions. On Euclidean instances, the method also outperforms recent neural sparsification approaches that operate on the complete graph.

•

We show that fusing two heuristics (
\alpha
-Nearest
\cup
POPMUSIC) creates a source-provenance signal that makes learned pruning both effective and robust: even a simple logistic regression suffices, and the method transfers stably to instances five times larger than the training size.

•

We demonstrate that the two-stage method becomes increasingly valuable at larger scales: from
N{=}200
onward, union + learned pruning achieves higher coverage than POPMUSIC alone, even though POPMUSIC is the stronger single-stage heuristic at small sizes.

Background and Related Work

2.1
Problem Setting

We study the symmetric TSP. An instance is given by
N
cities with symmetric pairwise distances
d_{ij}\geq 0
; a feasible tour is a Hamiltonian cycle. An optimal tour
\tau^{\star}
has edge set
E^{\star}
with
|E^{\star}|=N
. A
candidate graph

G=(V,E_{c})
restricts which edges the solver may explore; edges outside
E_{c}
are never considered. We measure the quality of a candidate graph by its
coverage
: the fraction of optimal-tour edges it contains.

2.2
TSPLIB Distance Types

TSPLIB
[
29
]
defines several distance conventions. We use four in our benchmark (Table
1
), covering planar metrics, a rescaled variant, and spherical geometry. Methods that rely on coordinate geometry, such as Delaunay triangulation and most GNN approaches, are restricted to
EUC_2D
. Our features depend only on edge weights, so they work for all four types.

2.3
Heuristic Candidate Construction

The two leading heuristic candidate generators, both integrated in LKH
[
13
]
, are:

\alpha
-Nearest.
Ranks edges by their
\alpha
-values derived from minimum 1-trees. Smaller
\alpha_{e}
means the edge is structurally compatible with good tours. LKH keeps the five lowest-
\alpha
edges per node, producing graphs of
{\sim}5
–
6N
edges.

POPMUSIC

[
34
,
33
]
. Runs local tour improvement on overlapping subproblems and keeps every edge that appears in an improved local tour. This yields sparser graphs (
{\sim}1
–
2N
edges) but with less recall at large scales.

Both methods operate purely on edge weights, so they work for any TSPLIB distance convention. This sets them apart from Delaunay-based approaches
[
5
,
22
]
and other triangulation methods
[
23
,
35
]
, which require Euclidean coordinates.

Combinatorial rules can provably preserve optimal-tour edges
[
14
]
. Zhong
[
41
]
analysed such rules probabilistically, and Cook et al.
[
4
]
showed that local elimination can yield very sparse graphs on large instances. Fekete et al.
[
6
]
and Wang
[
38
]
studied geometric and frequency-based sparsification. These methods offer theoretical guarantees but are restricted to Euclidean distance and need long computation times.

2.4
Learning-Based TSP Methods

Machine learning has been widely applied to TSP. End-to-end neural solvers
[
3
,
20
,
16
,
1
]
learn to construct tours directly, while hybrid methods integrate learning with classical solvers: NeuroLKH predicts edge guidance for LKH
[
39
]
, Hudson et al. combine graph neural networks with guided local search
[
15
]
, Zheng et al. reinforce decisions inside LKH-style search
[
40
]
, and Wang et al. dynamically adapt candidate edges during search
[
37
]
. Learning has also been applied to exact-solver components such as branching and subtour-cut generation
[
9
,
36
]
.

The closest prior work applies learning to graph sparsification. GNN-based methods predict edge heatmaps
[
32
,
17
]
, and edge confidence scores can be viewed as implicit sparsifiers
[
24
,
25
]
. Sun et al.
[
31
,
30
]
introduced a machine-learning problem-reduction framework using statistical edge features and studied how such reductions generalise across TSP families. Fitzpatrick et al.
[
7
]
learn to sparsify for exact formulations, and Zhou et al.
[
42
]
dynamically reduce the search space during autoregressive solving. However, all these methods operate on the
complete graph
or dense subgraphs such as KNN-50 or KNN-100, which is expensive and scales poorly. They are also almost exclusively Euclidean. No prior work has applied learning to an already highly sparse candidate graph.

2.5
Features and Benchmarking

Our feature design extends from Sun et al.’s statistical edge features
[
31
,
30
]
and follows the TSP feature-engineering tradition
[
10
,
11
]
established for algorithm selection
[
21
,
19
]
. All our features depend only on edge weights, including ranks, ratios, and local statistics, rather than on coordinate geometry. This makes them distance-type agnostic.

3
Method

3.1
Pipeline Overview

Figure 1:
Single-stage vs. two-stage sparsification. Top: existing approaches apply heuristics or ML independently to the full graph. Bottom: our method first maximises recall via a heuristic union, then reduces density via learned pruning.

Figure
1
illustrates our two-stage approach. In
Stage 1
, a heuristic constructs a sparsified
base candidate graph

G_{\mathrm{b}}=(V,E_{\mathrm{b}})
with
|E_{\mathrm{b}}|\ll N(N{-}1)/2
. We use the union of
\alpha
-Nearest and POPMUSIC (Section
2.3
):

E_{\mathrm{union}}=E_{\alpha}\cup E_{\mathrm{pop}}.

(1)

This maximises recall and creates a
source-provenance
signal that Stage 2 can exploit, since whether each edge comes from one generator or both is a strong cue for its importance. In
Stage 2
, a learned model scores every edge in
E_{\mathrm{b}}
and removes the lowest-scoring ones, producing a
pruned candidate graph

\widetilde{G}=(V,\widetilde{E})
with
\widetilde{E}\subseteq E_{\mathrm{b}}
.

Training.
For each training instance, we run Stage 1 to obtain
E_{\mathrm{b}}
, compute the optimal tour with Concorde, and label each candidate edge as
y_{e}=\mathbf{1}[e\in E^{\star}]
. We extract a feature vector
x_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for each edge and train a scoring model
f_{\theta}
on these
(x_{e},y_{e})
pairs. At test time, the model scores all edges in a new base candidate graph, and a local pruning rule converts the scores into
\widetilde{G}
.

For each edge
e=\{i,j\}\in E_{\mathrm{b}}
, we compute a feature vector from six families (Table
2
). Our features extend those of Sun et al.
[
31
,
30
]
. Every feature is
distance-type agnostic
: we use ranks, ratios, and local statistics rather than coordinates, so the features work across all TSPLIB distance conventions.

Training Objective.

Each model is trained by weighted empirical risk minimisation on the labelled candidate edges to produce a scalar ranking score
s_{e}=f_{\theta}(x_{e})
per edge.

Logistic regression (LR).
Predicts
p_{\theta}(x_{e})=\sigma(w^{\top}x_{e}+b)
and minimises weighted regularised log-loss:

\min_{w,b}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|_{2}^{2}+C\sum_{e}w_{y_{e}}\bigl({-}y_{e}\log p_{\theta}-(1{-}y_{e})\log(1{-}p_{\theta})\bigr).

(2)

Linear SVM.
With
\tilde{y}_{e}=2y_{e}-1
and
f_{\theta}(x_{e})=w^{\top}x_{e}+b
:

\min_{w,b}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|_{2}^{2}+C\sum_{e}w_{y_{e}}\,\max\!\bigl(0,1-\tilde{y}_{e}f_{\theta}(x_{e})\bigr)^{2}.

(3)

XGBoost.
An additive ensemble of regression trees with score
F_{M}(x_{e})=\sum_{m}f_{m}(x_{e})
and
p_{\theta}=\sigma(F_{M})
. The objective is

\min_{\{f_{m}\}}\sum_{e}w_{y_{e}}\,\ell_{\log}(y_{e},p_{\theta}(x_{e}))+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Omega(f_{m}),

(4)

where
\ell_{\log}
is binary cross-entropy and
\Omega
is tree regularisation.

We deliberately use lightweight, edge-independent classifiers to isolate the contribution of features and base graph choice, and to enable the feature-importance analysis in Section
6
.

Score-to-Selection Rule.

A global score threshold works poorly because node degrees in
G_{\mathrm{b}}
vary widely. We instead use a
node-level
rule. For each node
i
, let
\delta(i)
be its incident candidate edges sorted by descending score. We define a local softmax distribution:

w_{i,e}=\mathrm{softmax}_{\,e\in\delta(i)}\!\left(\frac{s_{e}-s_{i}^{\max}}{T}\right)

(5)

where
s_{i}^{\max}=\max_{e\in\delta(i)}s_{e}
and
T>0
is a temperature. We keep the top edges by descending score until their cumulative softmax mass reaches a threshold
\eta\in(0,1]
, with a hard floor of
m_{\min}{=}2
edges per node to preserve tour feasibility. The threshold
\eta
controls the sparsity-coverage trade-off: smaller
\eta
retains fewer edges (sparser graph, lower coverage); larger
\eta
retains more. Nodes with spread-out scores keep more edges; nodes with a clear winner keep fewer. The final graph
\widetilde{G}
is the union of edges retained from both endpoints.

We fix the pruning temperature at
T{=}1
and select the cumulative-mass threshold
\eta
on the validation set: among all
\eta
values that achieve
{\geq}99\%
coverage on the validation instances, we choose the one that produces the fewest remaining edges (the most aggressive pruning within the coverage constraint).

Benchmark

We test the two-stage method on a benchmark of twenty TSP families: four TSPLIB distance types (

,

,

,

) crossed with five spatial distributions (Figure

We train a single aggregated model on all 20 families and evaluate three models (LR, SVM, XGBoost) for stage 2. The main experiments use the union base candidate graph; an ablation study compares all three Stage 1 generators (
\alpha

Labels and Parameter Settings

We obtain exact-tour labels with Concorde
[

]
, following standard practice
[
31
,
30
]
. Only edges in the candidate graph are labelled. Logistic regression and linear SVM use penalty
C{=}1.0
; XGBoost uses
M{=}96
rounds,
\texttt{max\_depth}{=}6
, and learning rate
\alpha_{\mathrm{lr}}{=}0.08
. We fix the pruning temperature at
T{=}1
and select the cumulative-mass threshold
\eta
(Section

) on the validation set. The pruned graphs are passed directly to LKH under its default configuration, without any solver-specific tuning. All times are reported as CPU time per instance on AMD EPYC 9634.

Downstream solver impact.
We evaluate the pruned graphs by passing them directly to LKH without re-tuning.

LKH solves faster with no loss of solution quality
(Table
4
). Union + LR consistently achieves
1.21
–
1.28\times
speedup across all four problem sizes. XGBoost achieves
1.20\times
at TSP100 but is less consistent at other sizes (
1.03
–

). SVM slows the solver at TSP100 to
0.89\times
, likely because its aggressive pruning removes edges that help LKH’s search trajectory. The optimality gap never increases: all pruned models slightly

the gap compared to the unpruned base (e.g.

At TSP500, LR removes

of edges yet achieves
1.20\times
speedup, while XGBoost removes
47\%
but only
1.07\times
. LKH uses candidate edges as search guidance, not just constraints, so statistically redundant edges can still aid navigation between local optima. LR’s conservative pruning preserves more of this connectivity.

5.2
Comparison with Neural Sparsification Methods

We compare our two-stage method against three neural sparsification approaches: DIFUSCO
[
32
]
, a diffusion-based model; AttGCN
[
8
]
, an attention-based graph convolutional network; and DIMES
[
27
]
, a differentiable meta-solver. These neural methods take city coordinates as input and produce edge heatmaps over the complete graph; they are trained exclusively on Euclidean instances. To ensure a fair comparison, we restrict the evaluation to

Further Analysis

To understand
why
the two-stage method works and
where
it breaks down, we examine which features drive the pruning decisions and what kinds of edges the models fail to retain.

Feature use.
Figure
4
shows the feature importance for the union base graph. Source provenance, i.e. whether an edge comes from one heuristic or both, is the dominant predictor across all three models and all twenty families. Edges present in both
\alpha
-Nearest and POPMUSIC are almost always optimal-tour edges; single-source edges are much more likely to be redundant. This is why union achieves the best pruning: the provenance signal gives the model an easy, reliable cue that the individual base graphs lack.

Figure 4:
Feature importance for the union base graph by instance family. Columns are the 20 TSP families; rows are LR, SVM, and XGBoost. Coloured segments show the share of each feature family. Source provenance dominates across all models and families.

Figure 5:
Source provenance of extra-lost optimal edges in union mode. Each bar shows the share of coverage losses from dual-source edges (both generators),
\alpha
-only edges, and POPMUSIC-only edges. Single-source edges, especially
\alpha
-only, are over-represented among losses.

Source provenance of lost edges.
Figure
5
breaks down the extra-lost optimal edges by their source provenance. Single-source edges, especially
\alpha
-only edges (present in
\alpha
-Nearest but not POPMUSIC), are over-represented among coverage losses relative to their share of the total edge set. Dual-source edges do appear among the losses, but at a lower rate than their prevalence in the candidate graph would suggest. This confirms that source provenance has genuine predictive power: edges endorsed by both generators are safer, though not immune to incorrect pruning.

Failure modes.
Figure
6
shows the feature profiles of union’s extra-lost optimal-tour edges. The coverage losses are overall rare and tend to occur on edges with higher distance rank and lower neighbourhood overlap. Combined with the provenance breakdown above, this shows that the model’s errors concentrate on single-source edges that also look locally atypical, the hardest cases for any feature set. Together, these analyses confirm that the source-provenance signal created by the heuristic union is both the primary driver of pruning effectiveness and the key to cross-size transfer stability.

7
Conclusions

We proposed a two-stage method for sparsifying TSP candidate graphs: Stage 1 takes the union of
\alpha
-Nearest and POPMUSIC to maximise recall; Stage 2 uses a learned model to reduce density. Three findings emerge.

C1: Effective and efficient two-stage pipeline.
The two-stage method reduces candidate-graph density by
37
–
47\%
while retaining
{\geq}99.69\%
of optimal-tour edges across all problem sizes (TSP50–500). It works across all four TSPLIB distance types with no distance-specific tuning. The pruning step adds under
83
ms per instance, and the sparser graphs give LKH a consistent
1.20
–
1.28\times
speedup (union + LR) without increasing the optimality gap; union + LR achieves the lowest gap among all candidate configurations tested, including the unpruned
\alpha
-Nearest, POPMUSIC, and union base graphs. On
EUC_2D
, the method achieves higher coverage than three recent neural sparsification approaches while operating at lower density and without requiring GPU.

C2: Source-provenance signal makes learning easy.
Fusing two heuristics creates a source-provenance signal that is the dominant predictor across all models and families. Even a simple logistic regression achieves effective pruning, and the method transfers stably to instances five times larger than the training size.

C3: Scaling advantage over single-stage heuristics.
POPMUSIC is the strongest single-stage heuristic at small sizes, but its coverage degrades at larger scales. From
N{=}200
onward, union + learned pruning achieves higher coverage than POPMUSIC alone (
99.82\%
vs.
99.72\%
at TSP200;
99.69\%
vs.
99.56\%
at TSP500).

Limitations.

Our study is empirical: we do not provide theoretical guarantees that learned pruning preserves all optimal-tour edges. The benchmark is synthetic, which enables controlled experiments but leaves open how the method handles classical TSPLIB instances. Training requires exact tours from Concorde, limiting scalability. We evaluate LKH as the downstream solver; since candidate graphs are used by many TSP solvers
[
13
,
26
]
, we expect the approach to generalise, but this remains to be verified.

Future directions.

Possible extensions include theoretical coverage guarantees for learned pruning, replacing hand-crafted features with graph neural networks that learn representations directly, and improving the sparsity-coverage trade-off through better training objectives or pruning rules.
