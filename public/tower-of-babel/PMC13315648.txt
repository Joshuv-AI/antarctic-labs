Spatiotemporal delivery of exosomes and alendronate orchestrates osteoblastic and osteoclastic activity for osteoporotic bone regeneration
Authors: Shabnam Anjum, Mohammad Saeed, Wenjing Zhang, Taozhao Yu, Zhihua Yang, Ping Liu, Sapna Sayed, Yuhui Kou, Baoguo Jiang, Yang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The treatment of bone defects in osteoporotic (OP) patients remain a significant clinical challenge. The most important and challenging task is to promote sufficient bone formation in the early stage and effectively inhibit bone resorption all period of bone healing. Unfortunately, the efficacy of current strategies falls short of meeting this requirement. In this study, we developed a dual-delivery system that allows spatiotemporal release of bone marrow stem cell-derived exosomes (BMSC-Exo) and alendronate (Aln) by core-shell nanofiber to match the spatiotemporal dynamics of bone healing and effectively treat OP bone defects. Core-shell polycaprolactone/polyvinyl alcohol (P/PVA) nanofibers were fabricated through coaxial electrospinning, incorporating Aln within the core layer and BMSC-Exo functionalized onto the surface of nanofiber via chemical modification with polyethyleneimine (PEI). Physicochemical characterization confirmed the successful fabrication of PEI@P/PVA-Aln nanofibers, which exhibited excellent mechanical strength (22.77 MPa), hydrophilicity (9.66°), and enabled the spatiotemporal release of BMSC-Exo and Aln. In vitro experiments showed that Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffold effectively promoted osteogenic differentiation by miR-486 in the BMSC-Exo through regulation of the PTEN/AKT signaling axis and significantly reduced osteoclastic differentiation. Under a rat OP cranial defect model, the Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffold demonstrated substantially enhanced bone repair efficiency after 4 and 10 weeks. To conclude, our results showed that the dual delivery of BMSC-Exo and Aln with spatiotemporal release orchestrates osteoblastic and osteoclastic activity, providing a potential strategy to facilitate the regeneration of OP bone defects.

Introduction

Osteoporosis affects approximately 200 million people worldwide, leading to significant bone loss, microstructural alterations, and an increased risk of fragility fractures [1]. As the global ageing population continues to grow, the number of osteoporotic fractures and bone defects has increased markedly, posing significant clinical challenges and severely impacting the quality of patient life [2,3]. The primary obstacles for the treatment of bone defects caused by osteoporosis are due to the insufficient bone formation and excessive bone resorption, disrupting the balance of bone remodeling [4,5]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop more effective strategies to promote osteogenic differentiation, inhibit excessive osteoclastic differentiation, and improve the local regenerative microenvironment for the treatment of osteoporotic bone defects.

Currently, a variety of therapeutic approaches are used to promote bone formation under osteoporotic conditions, including parathyroid hormone (PTH), growth factor therapies, and cell-based strategies [6,7]. Among these, bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), such as BMP-2 and BMP-7, have been most commonly used in clinical applications due to their strong pro-osteogenic properties. However, it has been associated with adverse effects, including inflammation, bone resorption, hematoma, and ectopic bone formation [8]. Therefore, the need for alternative osteoinductive factors has arisen. Notably, exosomes have been identified as a potential osteoinductive agent in recent years due to their low immunogenicity, off-the-shelf potential, immunomodulatory properties, and high content of pro-osteogenic molecules [9,10]. Particularly, exosomes secreted by bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs-Exo) demonstrated superior capacity to promote new bone formation due to their high content of osteogenesis-regulating proteins and miRNAs [[11], [12], [13]]. For instance, Zhang et al. [14] demonstrated that miR-935-enriched BMSC-derived exosomes significantly increased osteoblast activity and promoted bone formation via STAT1 downregulation.

In addition to promoting new bone formation, the inhibition of bone resorption is also urgently needed for the treatment of osteoporotic bone defects. The most common clinical treatments rely on antiresorptive drugs such as alendronate (Aln) [15,16]. However, the systemic administration of Aln at high doses has side effects, including jaw osteonecrosis, gastrointestinal complications, and impaired bone remodeling [[17], [18], [19]]. To solve these limitations, biomaterials-based local delivery systems for Aln have been proposed to inhibit osteoclast activity. Hydrogels, nanofibers, ceramics, microspheres, and nanoparticles have been developed to improve the bioavailability of Aln. However, a burst release frequently occurs and results in suboptimal effective concentration and poor targeting [20,21]. Therefore, it is important to achieve long-term, controlled release of Aln to continuously inhibit excessive osteoclast activity during the healing period of osteoporotic bone defects.

To overcome these challenges, core-shell nanofiber scaffolds have emerged as a potential strategy for controlled drug delivery due to their high surface area, mechanical strength, and ability to mimic the extracellular matrix. By properly designing the shell layer, drugs and proteins encapsulated within the core can be released in a sustained manner and remain effective for an extended period [[22], [23], [24]]. Thus, integrating osteoinductive exosomes with anti-resorptive Aln through a core-shell nanofibrous scaffold can provide an effective strategy to promote impaired bone regeneration under osteoporotic conditions. We hypothesized that sustained delivery of Aln could complement the pro-osteogenic functions of BMSC-Exo by suppressing excessive osteoclast activity of current bone biomaterials. To this end, we proposed a novel approach to achieve the spatiotemporal release of BMSC-Exo and Aln for treating osteoporotic bone defects. We prepared Aln-loaded core-shell nanofibrous scaffolds composed of polycaprolactone and poly (vinyl alcohol) (PCL/PVA-Aln) by co-axial electrospinning. Subsequently, the scaffold surface was modified with polyethyleneimine (PEI) to obtain PEI@P/PVA-Aln and successfully immobilized BMSCs-Exo via electrostatic interactions (Scheme 1). In particular, sustained release of Aln from the core and controlled release of BMSC-Exo from the nanofiber surface were expected to facilitate their respective roles at different stages of osteoporotic bone healing. The properties of the core-shell nanofibers, their release profiles, and their impact on in vitro macrophage polarization, osteogenic, and osteoclastic differentiation were investigated. Mechanistically, signaling pathways associated with immune regulation and bone remodeling were investigated, including miRNA-mediated osteogenic regulation by BMSC-Exo and the potential molecular mechanisms involved in Aln-mediated inhibition of osteoclasts. Additionally, an ovariectomized (OVX) model was established to dynamically evaluate the therapeutic effect of the dual delivery system (Exo-Aln) in osteoporotic bone defects from 2 weeks till 10 weeks. Collectively, this study provided a novel design to spatiotemporally deliver osteoinductive exosomes and anti-resorptive alendronate to orchestrate osteoblastic and osteoclastic activity for the treatment of osteoporotic bone defects.Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the core-shell nanofibrous scaffold (Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln) designed for osteoporotic bone defect repair. Materials and methods

Materials

Alendronate (Mw = 325.12 g/mol), Poly (ε-caprolactone) (PCL, Mn = 80 kDa), Poly (vinyl alcohol) (PVA, Mw: 80,000), 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (TFEA), N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethyl carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC.HCL), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), branched ethylene imine polymer (PEI, Mw = 70 kDa), and MES buffer (0.2 M, pH 5.5, and pH 6.5), 4 ′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and Rhodamine B were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co. Ltd. (P.R. China).

Isolation and characterization of BMSCs and BMSCs-Exo

Extraction and identification of BMSCs: BMSCs were isolated from the femurs and tibia of 4-week-old Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats according to the normative procedure. In brief, cells were isolated aseptically by flushing the femur and tibia with α-MEM (Bio-Channel Biotechnology, China) to extrude the bone marrow into a 50 mL sterile polypropylene tube. The bone marrow was gently homogenized with a pipette and filtered through a cell strainer. Cells were cultured in α-MEM with 10% FBS (Cellmax, SA101.01, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere at 37°C. The culture medium was replaced every two days. Passages 4-5 were used in this study. To evaluate the multilineage differentiation potential of BMSCs, BMSCs were stained with fluorescent-labeled CD29, CD45, and CD90 antibodies and identified by flow cytometry. Osteogenic differentiation was assessed by alkaline phosphatase staining and Alizarin red staining, and adipogenic differentiation was confirmed by Oil Red O staining.

Isolation of exosomes: Before the isolation of BMSCs-Exo, BMSCs were cultured in a serum-free medium for 48 h. Subsequently, supernatants were collected and centrifuged sequentially at 300 g for 10 min, at 2000 g for 15 min, and at 4500 g for 30 min at 4°C to remove cellular debris, and the supernatant was reserved each time (Fig. S1). The supernatant was filtered through 0.22 μm membrane, and then ultrafiltered tubes were used to concentrate the solution. The concentrated solution was passed through the qEV size exclusion column. Finally, the BMSC exosomes were stored at −80°C for further experiments.

The morphology of BMSC-Exo was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi, Japan). The zeta potential and particle size of exosomes were determined using nanoparticle tracking analysis. Western blotting was used to identify positive markers (CD81, Flotillin-1, and TSG101) for the exosomes. The protein concentration of exosomes was determined using a micro-BCA (bicinchoninic acid) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Exosomes labeling and cellular uptake assay: BMSC-Exo were labeled with PKH-26 dye (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, BMSC-Exo was mixed with PKH-26 dye and incubated at 37°C in the dark for 10 min. The labeled exosomes were then washed with PBS at 100,000 g for 1 h at 4°C. Finally, labeled exosomes were resuspended in PBS before use. For fluorescence tracking, BMSCs and osteoclasts were seeded in 48-well plates, and Dil-labeled BMSCs-Exo solution was added to each well. After 6 h, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100. The cells were stained with Phalloidin-FITC, and nuclei were stained with DAPI. The imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (Olympus, Japan).

Fabrication and characterization of exosome-functionalized core-shell scaffolds

Core-shell fibrous scaffold: The coaxial electrospinning (TL-BM-300; Shenzhen TONGLI Micro Nano Technology Co., China) set-up was used to prepare core-shell fibrous scaffolds. 12% PCL was dissolved in TFEA under magnetic stirring and transferred to the outer coaxial needle with a syringe pump. 8% PVA was dissolved in distilled water under continuous stirring, and 1% Aln was added and stirred to obtain a uniform solution, which was then transported to the inner needle. During electrospinning, the flow rates of the inner and outer solutions were 0.4 mL/h and 1.0 mL/h, respectively, with a tip-to-collector distance of 12 cm and an applied voltage of 20 kV.

PEI modification: The PEI coating was based on a previously published method with some improvements [25]. The core-shell fibrous scaffolds were placed in a mixture of 0.2 M NaOH and 75% ethanol (volume ratio 1:1) for 10 min with gentle stirring at room temperature (RT). The treatment aimed to enhance the hydrophilicity of the nanofibers and introduce carboxyl groups on their surface through hydrolysis. After washing with DI water, the carboxylated core-shell fibrous scaffolds were immersed in 10 mL of 0.2 M MES buffer (pH 5.5) containing 0.0446 g EDC and 0.0168 g NHS and stirred for 1 h. Then, 2.45 g (low), 4.90 g (medium), and 7.35 g (high) branched PEI was dissolved in 10 mL of 0.2 M MES buffer (pH 6.5) to prepare different PEI modification solutions, which was then added to the EDC/NHS reaction system for 1 h. The core-shell fibrous scaffolds were soaked and repeatedly washed with normal saline and DI water to remove unreacted PEI.

Immobilization of BMSCs-Exo: Before immobilization of exosomes, the core-shell scaffolds were rinsed with 75% ethanol solution for 30 min, washed with sterilized DI water three times, and air-dried in a biosafety cabinet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) and sterilized by exposure to ultraviolet radiation for another 30 min on each side. To immobilize BMSC-Exo on the surface of nanofibers, the scaffolds were immersed in 200 μL of exosome suspension and incubated for 3 h at 4°C on a rotator. To visualize BMSC-Exo on core-shell fibrous scaffolds, BMSC-Exo were stained with DiI and washed with PBS to remove excess dye. The Dil-labeled BMSC-Exo were loaded to the core-shell fibrous scaffold and imaged via CLSM.

The morphology of the core-shell fibrous scaffolds was observed using SEM (S4800; Hitachi Japan). The observation of the core-shell structure of the nanofibers was conducted through TEM. To better distinguish the core and shell layers in the coaxial construction fibers, the electrospun solution was mixed with traces of fluorescent agents (DAPI in the shell solution and Rhodamine B in the core solution). Subsequently, the core-shell nanofibers were directly deposited onto a glass coverslip for visualization via CLSM. The mechanical properties of the core-shell nanofibrous scaffolds were evaluated using a Universal Testing Machine (YG005A from Baien Instrument, China) with a 5 cN capacity load cell at a crosshead speed of 8 mm/min. The wettability of the fibrous scaffolds was measured using a water contact angle instrument (model JC2000C1, POWEREACH, China). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; AXIS Supra; Kratos UK) was used to analyze the surface chemical bonds of PEI-modified core-shell nanofibers. A solid surface zeta potential analyzer (SurPASS2, Anton Paar, Austria) was used to detect the surface electrical charge of the nanofibrous scaffolds before and after PEI modification. Scaffold samples (1 × 2 cm2) were mounted with the tested surface exposed and measured using 1 mmol/L KCl as the electrolyte. The measurement gap was adjusted to 95-115 μm. The pH was controlled using 0.1 mol/L HCl or 0.1 mol/L NaOH, and zeta potential values were obtained automatically from the streaming potential data.

The degradation of scaffolds

The degradation profile of the core-shell fibrous scaffolds was investigated by monitoring weight loss at different time intervals. Initially, scaffolds measuring 1 × 1 cm2 were precisely weighed (Wi) and then placed individually into 5 mL tubes containing 4 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After that, these tubes were placed in a shaking incubator at 37°C. At specific time intervals, the samples underwent drying until reaching a consistent weight (Wf). This process allowed for the assessment of changes in weight over time, indicating the degradation pattern of the nanofibers in the PBS solution.

The release of BMSC-Exo and Aln

To evaluate the exosome release from P/PVA and PEI@P/PVA, 200 μL of exosome suspension was added and incubated at 4°C for 3 h. The amount of exosomes in the supernatant was detected immediately after P/PVA and PEI@P/PVA were immersed in the medium. The total amount of exosomes minus the exosomes in the supernatant was the amount of loaded exosomes. Three repeat samples of Exo-P/PVA and Exo-PEI@P/PVA nanofibers were soaked in a tube containing 1 mL PBS and stored in an incubator at 37°C. At predetermined time points, the PBS was collected and replaced with fresh PBS. A Micro BCA protein assay kit was used to measure the amount of exosomes released in PBS.

For determining release profiles of Aln from core-shell nanofibers, the nanofiber samples (P/PVA-Aln and PEI@P/PVA-Aln) were immersed in 10 mL PBS at 37°C in a shaker. At predetermined time points, 2 mL of the solutions were collected, and an equal amount of fresh PBS was introduced. The collected supernatant was evaluated by UV-visible spectroscopy. Briefly, 1 mL of release medium was mixed with 2.5 mL of 0.2% ninhydrin and 0.5 mL of 0.05 M NaHCO3, heated in a 95 ± 5°C water bath for 35 min to yield a purple solution. The reaction mixture was then diluted to 5 mL with deionized water, and the absorbance was measured.

Biocompatibility assessment of scaffolds

Live/dead assay: MSCs were seeded on scaffolds at 5 × 103 cells/well and incubated for 3 days. The samples were rinsed with PBS and stained for 30 min in the dark with a staining solution containing 1 μM acetoxymethyl ester of calcein (calcein AM) and 1 μM propidium iodide (PI) (Solarbio, China). After 30 min, stained cells were washed with PBS to remove unbound dyes and imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Cell morphology: 5 × 103 cells/well were seeded onto scaffolds for 3 days. After 3 days, the culture media were removed, and cells were washed with PBS, fixed with PFA for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Again, washed with PBS, and stained with FITC-phalloidin for 30 min, and DAPI for 10 min in the dark. The micrographs were captured using a CLSM.

Subsequently, the bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well on the scaffolds and stimulated with 40 ng/mL LPS (Peprotech) or 20 ng/mL IL-4 (Peprotech). After 36 h, culture supernatants were collected for ELISA. The concentrations of TNF-α and CCL17 were quantified by using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. For immunofluorescence staining, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with a solution of 2% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. The cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies. Finally, the nuclei were stained, and the fluorescent images were observed with CLSM.

Flow Cytometry: The treated cells were washed with PBS, digested with 0.25% trypsin, centrifuged, and then resuspended in a medium containing 1% BSA to block non-specific binding sites. The cells were fluorescently labeled with antibodies against FITC anti-mouse F4/80 (BioLegend, USA), APC anti-mouse CD86 (BioLegend, USA), and PE anti-mouse CD206 (BioLegend, USA) were added to each group, followed by incubation in the dark at 4°C for 30 min. The cell suspension was centrifuged, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed and resuspended in PBS. The sample obtained was analyzed using a BD FACSCelest flow cytometer (Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA) and the Flowjo-v10.8.1 software (Ashland, Oregon, USA).

Apoptosis of MSCs: MSCs were seeded on the scaffolds at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. After culturing for 3 days, MSCs were treated with PI/Annexin V kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the vendor's protocol and detected using flow cytometry (FACSAria III, BD, USA).

Cytotoxicity assay: The proliferation of MSCs was evaluated by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, China) solution after 3 × 103 cells were cultured on each sample for 1, 3, and 5 days. Following the respective culture durations, the media were removed, and cells were washed with PBS. After this, 10 μL CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Subsequently, the absorbance was analyzed at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Biocompatibility evaluation in vivo: All animal surgeries were carried out in accordance with the Shenzhen University Animal Ethical Committee (No. 202500089). Eight-week-old female SD rats were assigned into five groups (control, P/PVA, P/PVA-Aln, PEI@P/PVA-Aln, and Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln), scaffolds (1 × 1 cm2) were implanted subcutaneously into the dorsal region of each rat. After post-operation for 4 weeks, the specimens, along with the surrounding tissue, were retrieved and major organs (liver, spleen, kidneys, heart) and blood were collected for evaluation of scaffolds biocompatibility. The collected specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The sections of 5-μm thickness were created and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). To evaluate hepatic and renal function, blood was centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected and stored at - 80◦C. Serum levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CREA) were measured by Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) using a Chemray 240 automatic biochemical analyzer (Shenzhen, Rituo Technology).

Osteogenic differentiation assays in vitro

Alkaline phosphatase activity (ALP) and Alizarin red staining (ARS): MSCs at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well were seeded on scaffolds for osteogenic differentiation. Next day, the medium was supplemented with osteogenic medium (α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, 0.1 mM ascorbic acid, 10 mM β-glycerolphosphate, and 10 nM dexamethasone). After 7 days of induction, cells were fixed with 4% PFA and stained using the BCIP/NBT ALP kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at RT for 20 min and imaged. For ALP activity, samples were subjected to two freeze-thaw cycles at −80°C to release intracellular ALP. The ALP activity of the samples was then measured using the p-nitrophenyl phosphate method. On day 14, cells were fixed and stained with alizarin red solution, and the stained mineral deposits were imaged under a microscope. For quantitative analysis of mineralization, the deposits were dissolved using 0.5 N acetic acid overnight, after which calcium content was quantified using the previously established OCPC method (Beyotime, China).

Osteogenic gene and protein expression assays: Real-time quantitative reverse transcription (RT-qPCR) was used to assess the osteogenesis-related gene markers (ALP, Runx2, Col-I, OCN). Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using Triquick reagent (R1100, Solarbio, China). Reverse transcription of RNA into cDNA was performed using the RT Plus Kit (SPARKJADE BioTech, China) and for miRNA First Strand Kit (SPARKJADE BioTech, China). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the SYBR Green qPCR Mix SPARKJADE BioTech, China) following the manufacturer's instructions, on a real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, China). The relative gene expression levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) for mRNA and U6 for miRNA, and the 2−ΔΔCT method was used to calculate expression levels. All gene primers were purchased commercially from Sangon Biotech. The primer sequences are listed in Table S1 and S2. In addition, osteogenesis-related protein (Col-I) expression was detected by immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. The cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies. Finally, the nuclei were stained, and the fluorescent images were observed with CLSM.

Osteoclasts differentiation assays in vitro

Cell actin and nucleus staining: BMDMs were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well on different scaffolds in complete medium containing α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. The cells were stimulated for 6 days with 30 ng/mL M-CSF and 50 ng/mL receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL) (PeproTech, Rocky Hill, USA). Next, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized, and nuclei and cytoskeleton were stained with DAPI and phalloidin, respectively. The stained cells were imaged using CLSM.

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) activity: BMDMs were cultured on different scaffolds at the density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2 in α-MEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 ng/mL RANKL, and 30 ng/mL M-CSF for 6 days. After 6 days, cells were fixed for TRAP staining (PH Biotechnology, CTCC-JD005, China). The supernatant was collected, and TRAP activity was performed using the pNPP method according to the manufacturer's instructions (Yuanye Bio-Technology, R22113, China).

Osteoclastic gene and protein expression assays: qRT-PCR was used to assess expression of the osteoclastogenesis-related genes NFATc1, TRAP, and cathepsin K (CTSK), and the 2−ΔΔCT method was used to calculate expression levels. GAPDH served as a control gene, with primers detailed in Table S1. To visualize osteoclastogenesis-related protein (CTSK) expression, immunofluorescence staining was performed. Briefly, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. The cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies. Finally, the nuclei were stained, and the fluorescent images were observed using CLSM.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (78446, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Subsequently, the proteins were mixed with loading buffer (Beyotime, China), denatured by boiling, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto 0.22-μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After washing three times with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Detailed information on the primary antibodies used is provided in Table S3.

miRNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

RNA isolation, library generation, cluster amplification, and sequencing were performed by ClimbBio (Guangzhou, China). Briefly, RNA was extracted from BMSC-Exo using the Maxwell RSC miRNA Plasma Kit. Libraries were generated using the SMARTer smRNA-seq Kit for Illumina (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) as described by the manufacturer. Sequencing of the libraries was performed using an Illumina HiSeq 2500 instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Transfection of miRNA mimics. MSCs were seeded at a density of 7 × 103 cells/cm2 in 6-well plates and cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, and maintained until reaching 80% confluence. miRNA mimics and negative controls were obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). To prepare the transfection complexes, miRNA mimics or negative controls were mixed with Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Scientific, USA) in OptiMEM reduced-serum medium and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. These complexes were then added to the MSCs and incubated at 37◦C for 8 h. Following this, the medium was replaced with fresh basic medium, and the cells were incubated for an additional 48 h. The transfected MSCs were subsequently harvested for subsequent experiments.

Evaluation on promoting osteoporotic bone regeneration in vivo

Critical-sized cranial bone defect model in osteoporotic rats: All animal surgeries were carried out in accordance with the Shenzhen University Animal Ethical Committee (No. 202500089). 12-week-old female SD rats were fed and underwent bilateral ovariectomy (OVX) owing to their resemblance to postmenopausal osteoporosis. Experimental rats underwent cranial defects after 4 weeks of OVX. In brief, a longitudinal 15-mm incision was made to elevate a full-thickness flap to expose the calvarium. Two defects (diameter: 5 mm) were created using a trephine bur on the skull. In the experimental group, P/PVA, P/PVA-Aln, and BMSC-Exo-loaded nanofiber (Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln) fibrous scaffolds with an 8-mm diameter were implanted to completely cover the defect region and ensure stable contact with the surrounding bone tissue during implantation. The defects remained empty without scaffold implantation in the control group. Notably, the fibrous scaffolds adequately covered the defect area. Cranial bones were collected at 2, 4, and 10 weeks after implantation and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for further analysis.

Micro-CT analysis: Bone regeneration in rat cranial samples was scanned using Micro-CT (PerkinElmer, USA). The scanning parameters were set to 90 kV voltage, 80 μA current, and 200 mGy dose, with a high-resolution scanning mode. After scanning, 3D photographs of each specimen were imported into the analysis software to calculate the new bone volume. The parameters of bone regeneration, including the percent of bone volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), within the region of interest were quantified using Analyze 14.0 software.

Histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis: After micro-CT scanning, the collected cranial samples were decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 1 month. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson trichrome, and TRAP staining were performed for bone histological analysis. To perform IHC staining, deparaffinized samples were rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking with goat-derived rabbit serum, tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4◦C with primary antibodies against Runx2 (ServiceBio, China), and OCN (ServiceBio, China). The sections were washed with PBS, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The staining signal was developed using a diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Subsequently, the stained sections were scanned by using a digital pathology scanner (Aperio CS2, Leica, Germany) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence (IF): IF staining was performed to identify M1 and M2 macrophages. Tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4◦C with primary antibodies against CD86, and CD206 (ServiceBio, China). Sections were then incubated with secondary donkey anti-rabbit IgG antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (Abcam, England), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min. Slides were then washed, mounted with anti-fade medium, and visualized using a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D). The data from all experiments were evaluated using Student's two-tailed t-test to compare data between two groups and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test, to identify significant differences between multiple groups. Statistical analysis was conducted using Origin 2019 and GraphPad Prism software. P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of BMSC-derived exosomes

The multidirectional differentiation potential of isolated BMSCs was confirmed through osteogenic and adipogenic assays (Fig. S2a–c) according to the Mesenchymal and Tissue Stem Cell Committee of the International Society for Cellular Therapy [26]. To further characterize BMSCs, flow cytometry analysis was performed, which showed positive expression of the surface markers CD29 (99.85%) and CD90 (94.56%), and negative expression of CD45 (1.03%) (Fig. S2d), indicating a highly homogeneous population of BMSCs. BMSC-Exo was isolated using a qEV size-exclusion column and further characterized by TEM, NTA, and Western blotting (Fig. 1a–c). BMSC-Exo showed the classical “cup-shaped” morphology and bilayer lipid membranes in TEM (Fig. 1a). NTA analysis further confirmed the diameter of BMSC-Exo was distributed around 139.3 nm (Fig. 1b). Preparation and characterization of core-shell nanofibrous scaffolds

We first characterized the surface morphology of the core-shell nanofiber scaffolds by SEM and TEM (Fig. 1e and g). The diameter of nanofiber scaffolds ranged from 200 to 400 nm and had a continuous and interconnected pore structure (Fig. 1f). To further verify the core-shell structure, CLSM imaging showed the blue dye around the shell PCL, while the red dye well-constrained within the core PVA, indicating a distinct core-shell structure of nanofibers (Fig. 1h).

The chemical modification on the surface of the core-shell nanofiber detected nitrogen element and amine groups corresponding to the conjugated PEI. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis confirmed the grafting of PEI on the surface of the nanofiber (Fig. 1i and j). The unmodified nanofiber (P/PVA-Aln) exhibited elemental peaks of C 1s and O 1s, and no nitrogen signal was detected (Fig. 1i). In contrast, a new nitrogen element (N 1s) peak appeared in the PEI-modified nanofiber (PEI@P/PVA-Aln), confirming the successful PEI modification (Fig. 1j). The hydrophilic properties of nanofibrous scaffolds were measured by static contact angle (Fig. 1k). After PEI modification, the contact angle of PEI@P/PVA-Aln was significantly reduced to 9.66° when compared to P/PVA (83.33°) and P/PVA-Aln (80.50°), indicating that PEI@P/PVA-Aln has a rough surface morphology and becomes more hydrophilic, which might add in cell adhesion and proliferation.

We evaluated mechanical characteristics, including tensile strength, elastic modulus, and elongation at break of the prepared nanofiber P/PVA, P/PVA-Aln, and PEI@P/PVA-Aln, calculated from the stress-strain curves (Fig. 1l). The scaffold P/PVA exhibited inferior tensile strength (11.42 MPa) and elastic modulus (263.46 MPa). In addition, the tensile strength was significantly higher in PEI@P/PVA-Aln (22.77 MPa) than in P/PVA (11.42 MPa) and P/PVA-Aln (14.26 MPa) (Fig. 1m). This confirms that PEI modification of the nanofiber can improve its mechanical. Moreover, PEI@P/PVA-Aln had the highest Young's modulus (361.35 MPa), surpassing both P/PVA (263.46 MPa) and P/PVA-Aln (281.76 MPa) (Fig. 1n). These results indicate that our core-shell nanofiber scaffold could provide excellent tensile properties as a scaffold. Changes in the electrical properties of the scaffold surface before and after PEI modification were detected using zeta potential. This chemical modification facilitated electrostatic interaction with negatively charged exosomes, enabling their tethering onto the core-shell fiber surface (Fig. 1o). Furthermore, the BMSC-Exo-containing solution was dripped onto the PEI-modified fibers, and fluorescence images show that uniform BMSC-Exo were deposited onto the surface of electrospun nanofibers (Fig. 1p).

To achieve spatiotemporal release, Aln was incorporated into the core of the nanofibers, and BMSC-Exo was attached onto the surface of the nanofibers. Aln was released in a controlled and sustained manner without an initial burst, primarily due to the barrier effect of the shell layer (PCL). The cumulative release of Aln from the PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffold reached approximately 18%, 37%, 50%, and 64% at days 4, 12, 16, and 24, respectively, corresponding to the cumulative released amount of approximately 9.0, 18.5, 25, and 31.9 μg/cm2 (Fig. 1q). We further systematically investigate the loading efficiency and release profile of BMSC-Exo from P/PVA-Aln and PEI@P/PVA-Aln. The total amount of grafted BMSC-Exo was approximately 75.72 μg/cm2 for the Exo@P/PVA-Aln scaffold and 168.36 μg/cm2 for the Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffold (Fig. 1r). Subsequently, we evaluated the exosomes release and found that the Exo-P/PVA-Aln scaffold without PEI modification exhibited a burst release of BMSC-Exo, reaching approximately 90% within 4 days, corresponding to a cumulative released amount of approximately 68.18 μg/cm2. Notably, the PEI-modified scaffold (Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln) could be electrostatically bound to exosomes and achieve the cumulative release equilibrium up to 16 days and a cumulative released amount of approximately 148.66 μg/cm2 (Fig. 1s). In addition, exosomes released from the Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffold could be efficiently internalized by BMSCs and macrophages, indicating that exosome immobilization process did not impair exosome integrity or cellular uptake capability (Fig. S5).

The degradation results indicated that PCL exhibited an excellent gradual biodegradation profile to maintain the structural integrity and stability of the fibrous membranes (Fig. S6a). After 12 days, SEM images showed that parts of the fibers fractured and their surfaces became rough (Fig. S6b). P/PVA scaffold exhibited the highest degradation rate, reaching nearly 80% by day 28, followed by P/PVA-Aln (∼72%) and PEI@P/PVA-Aln (∼60%).

Scaffold biocompatibility

Core-shell nanofiber scaffolds with different PEI grafting densities (PEI-L, PEI-M, and PEI-H) were first prepared and their effects on cell apoptosis were assessed (Fig. S7). MSCs cultured on these fibrous scaffolds did not exhibit increased apoptosis, indicating that grafted PEI had negligible cytotoxicity, as previous studies reported [25,28]. Therefore, PEI-L was selected for subsequent studies.

The biocompatibility of the prepared core-shell nanofiber scaffolds P/PVA, P/PVA-Aln, PEI@P/PVA-Aln, and Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln was further evaluated using CCK-8 assay, cell apoptosis, live/dead staining, cell morphology staining, and in vivo biocompatibility. Live/dead staining showed that all scaffolds have excellent biocompatibility (Fig. 2a). The proliferation of stem cells on various core-shell nanofibrous scaffolds revealed that exosomes had the capacity to promote cell proliferation (Fig. S8). The cytoskeleton and nuclear staining of MSCs on the P/PVA and P/PVA-Aln presented a near-spherical cellular shape with a few protrusions, implying poor cell adhesion (Fig. 2b). We also assessed the cytotoxicity of degraded P/PVA, P/PVA-Aln, PEI@P/PVA-Aln, and Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffolds using Annexin V/PI staining. Flow cytometry with Annexin V/PI staining revealed negligible apoptotic cells in the degraded scaffolds (Fig. 2c). To further evaluate biocompatibility in vivo, the scaffolds were subcutaneously implanted, and the major organs were harvested for histological analysis after 4 weeks. H&E staining revealed no obvious tissue damage, inflammatory response, or abnormal cellular infiltration around the implantation sites across all groups (Fig. 2d). Histological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, revealed normal tissue architecture, indicating favorable biosafety (Fig. S9). In addition, serum biochemical parameters associated with liver and kidney function, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine (CRE), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), remained within normal physiological ranges and showed no significant differences between the scaffold-treated and control groups (Fig. 2e). These findings suggest that the implanted scaffolds did not induce detectable hepatic or renal toxicity and exhibited superior systemic biocompatibility.

Pro-osteogenic ability of core-shell nanofibrous scaffolds

We first analyzed the pro-osteogenic potential of MSCs cultured with different scaffolds by assessing ALP staining and activity, calcium deposition, and the expression of osteogenic genes and proteins. Results of ALP staining and activity revealed that MSCs grown on the Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln group exhibited higher ALP expression levels compared to P/PVA-Aln, P/PVA, and control groups (Fig. 4a and b). Interestingly, the Aln-loaded (P/PVA-Aln) scaffold stimulated more effectively osteoblastic differentiation than the control and P/PVA groups. Additionally, ARS staining and calcium content demonstrated more mineralized nodules in Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln than in P/PVA-Aln, P/PVA, and control (Fig. 4c and d). Furthermore, we performed qRT-PCR to investigate the osteogenic activity of MSCs at the molecular level. Notably, the scaffold with BMSC-Exo significantly increased the expression of key osteogenic markers, including Runx2, ALP, Col I, and OCN (Fig. 4e–h). We further investigated the protein expression of Col I in MSCs by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 4i). MSCs in the Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffolds demonstrated the strongest Col I positive signals, indicating the potential role of BMSC-Exo in promoting the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs. Bioinformatic analysis was subsequently performed to explore the potential signaling pathways regulated by these candidate miRNAs. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the top 20 predicted target genes demonstrated enrichment in phosphatidylinositol signaling, TNF signaling, Wnt signaling, and mTOR signaling, suggesting potential involvement in osteogenesis and inflammatory regulation (Fig. 5b). GO enrichment analysis further demonstrated enrichment in biological processes related to positive regulation of protein kinase B signaling, osteoblast differentiation, and Wnt signaling. To identify the most potential osteogenesis-associated miRNAs, several highly abundant miRNAs (miR-486, miR-3596d, miR-26a-5p, miR-122-5p, miR-127-3p, miR-143-3p, miR-21-5p) were evaluated during osteogenesis of MSCs by qRT-PCR. Among these miRNAs, miR-486 exhibited the most significant upregulation during osteogenic induction (Fig. 5d). Based on its expression abundance and predicted pathway relevance, miR-486 was selected for subsequent analysis. MSCs were then transfected with negative control (NC) mimics and miR-486 mimics (Fig. 5e). RT-PCR analysis demonstrated that miR-486 expression increased by more than 6000-fold following mimic transfection. Previous studies have identified PTEN as a downstream target of miR-486 and a negative regulator of the Akt signaling pathway [31]. PTEN suppresses Akt activation by reducing phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3) levels, thereby limiting Akt phosphorylation and osteogenic differentiation of MSCs. In contrast, PTEN downregulation promotes Akt pathway activation and enhances osteogenesis. Based on this mechanism, we further investigated the relationship between miR-486 and the PTEN/Akt pathway. qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that miR-486 overexpression significantly reduced PTEN mRNA expression (Fig. 5f). Notably, Western blot analysis showed decreased PTEN protein expression and increased Akt phosphorylation following miR-486 overexpression (Fig. 5g–i), suggesting that miR-486 may promote osteogenic differentiation through activation of the PTEN/Akt signaling pathway.

To further explore the molecular mechanism underlying BMSC-Exo mediated osteogenesis, the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway was investigated. BMSC-Exo treatment markedly increased the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT in MSCs, indicating activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway following exosome uptake. Notably, treatment with the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 partially attenuated the pro-osteogenic effects of BMSC-Exo, confirming the involvement of this pathway in exosome-mediated osteogenesis (Fig. 5j).

Finally, the molecular mechanism underlying scaffold-mediated osteogenesis was further investigated (Fig. 5k and l). The scaffold with BMSC-Exo significantly increased the expression of p-PI3K, p-Akt, and Runx2, while PTEN expression was decreased. In contrast, total PI3K and Akt expression showed no obvious changes among the groups. Collectively, these findings suggest that BMSC-Exo enriched with miR-486 can modulate osteogenic differentiation through regulation of the PTEN/PI3K/AKT signaling axis.

Anti-osteoclastic differentiation ability of core-shell nanofibrous scaffolds

It is well known that osteoporosis is caused by an elevated number of osteoclasts that cause an imbalance in bone remodeling. Thus, in addition to promoting osteogenesis, the inhibition of osteoclastic differentiation is essential for restoring bone homeostasis and support osteoporotic bone repair. The effects of Aln on osteoclasts inhibition were evaluated through TRAP staining and activity, F-actin ring, as well as gene and protein expression of osteoclast markers. TRAP staining and activity assays demonstrated that both P/PVA-Aln and Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln groups exhibited a reduced number of TRAP-positive cells, followed by the P/PVA and control groups (Fig. 6a). Notably, the Aln-loaded group significantly inhibited osteoclast differentiation (Fig. 6b). To further validate the aforementioned findings, RT-qPCR analysis was conducted and revealed decreased expression of osteoclast gene markers CTSK, TRAP, and NFATc1. In particular, P/PVA-Aln and Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln groups showed a significant inhibition of these osteoclast genes compared with the P/PVA, and control groups (Fig. 6c–e). Next, the formation of F-actin ring, a typical actin structure essential for bone resorption by active osteoclasts, was visualized by CLSM. The multinucleated cells with well-defined F-actin rings were observed in control and P/PVA groups, whereas few F-actin rings were observed in both P/PVA-Aln and Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln groups (Fig. 6f). In addition, CTSK protein expression was further visualized using immunofluorescence staining. The results also showed lower expression of CTSK in the Aln-loaded P/PVA-Aln and Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln groups than in P/PVA and control groups (Fig. 6g). To further investigate the mechanism underlying osteoclast inhibition, the expression of osteoclastic differentiation-related proteins was evaluated by Western blot analysis (Fig. 6h). Compared with the control and P/PVA groups, the P/PVA-Aln and Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln groups exhibited markedly reduced expression of NFATc1, c-FOS, and CTSK, which are key regulators of osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption (Fig. 6i). Discussion

Osteoporotic bone defects present a significant clinical challenge due to impaired bone healing capacity and an increased risk of nonunion, making their treatment more complex than that of normal bone defects [32]. Effective osteoporotic bone healing requires precise temporal coordination between osteoblastic and osteoclastic activities. In this study, we successfully developed a core-shell nanofiber scaffold (Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln) capable of sequential and controlled release of BMSC-Exo and Aln for the treatment of osteoporotic bone defects. Our results suggested that the synergistic therapeutic effects of BMSC-Exo and Aln released from the scaffolds (Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln) promoted bone formation and inhibited bone resorption, which eventually effectively repaired osteoporotic bone defects.

The spatiotemporal delivery of therapeutic agents offers significant advantages for tissue regeneration by enabling the release of distinct bioactive cues at appropriate stages of the healing process [33,34]. To elucidate the temporal requirements of osteoporotic bone repair, we established a rat calvarial defect model and found that spontaneous healing remained limited even after 10 weeks. Dynamic analyses of inflammation, osteogenesis, and osteoclastogenesis from 2 to 10 weeks revealed pronounced inflammation and impaired osteogenesis during the early phase of healing, whereas elevated osteoclast activity persisted throughout the repair process. These observations suggest that osteoporotic bone regeneration may benefit from early immunomodulatory and pro-osteogenic interventions together with sustained suppression of osteoclastogenesis [35]. To address these stage-specific therapeutic demands, we developed core-shell nanofibrous scaffolds co-loaded with BMSC-derived exosomes (BMSC-Exo) and alendronate (Aln). BMSC-Exo were immobilized on the fiber surface and exhibited a relatively rapid yet controlled release profile, which was primarily attributed to the gradual weakening of electrostatic interactions under hydrated conditions. Notably, the majority of exosomes were released within the first 2 weeks, coinciding with the period during which anti-inflammatory and osteogenic stimulation may be most beneficial. In contrast, Aln was encapsulated within the fiber core and released at a substantially slower rate, primarily governed by the gradual degradation of the nanofibrous scaffold. Histological analysis revealed the persistence of scaffold remnants up to 10 weeks after implantation, indicating prolonged scaffold retention in vivo. Together with the sustained in vitro release profile of Aln, these findings suggest the potential for long-term anti-resorptive activity throughout the healing process. Collectively, the Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln nanofibrous scaffold provides a spatiotemporal delivery platform in which rapid exosome release is integrated with prolonged Aln release, thereby aligning therapeutic intervention with the dynamic biological processes of osteoporotic bone healing.

Scaffold degradation influences both the release of bioactive agents and subsequent bone ingrowth. However, the enhanced bone regeneration observed in the Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln group cannot be attributed solely to the conductive properties of the implanted scaffold. Notably, the P/PVA-Aln scaffold exhibited a slower degradation rate than the Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffold, yet resulted in substantially inferior bone regeneration. If scaffold conductivity were the primary determinant of bone repair, the slower-degrading P/PVA-Aln scaffold would be expected to provide comparable or superior outcomes. Furthermore, histological analysis revealed more extensive bone formation within the central region of defects treated with Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffolds rather than being restricted to the defect margins. This pattern is characteristic of osteoinductive stimulation and suggests a dominant contribution of BMSC-derived exosomes beyond the osteoconductive effects of the scaffold. Collectively, these findings indicate that the superior bone repair achieved by Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffolds arises from the synergistic actions of sustained Aln release and BMSC-Exo , rather than from the scaffold structure itself. Scaffolds implanted in bone defects must withstand the complex compressive and tensile forces generated by surrounding tissue, as these mechanical properties are essential for maintaining structural support and guiding tissue regeneration [36]. Our core-shell nanofiber scaffold showed that PEI modification improved both mechanical and hydrophilic properties. The favorable mechanical properties provided sufficient structural support for bone defect repair. Moreover, the enhanced hydrophilicity and surface roughness of the Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffold further supported cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation, and MSCs growth on the surface of nanofibers confirmed its biocompatibility. Notably, PEI modification contributes to these effects by introducing positively charged amine groups, which have been reported to enhance protein adsorption and promote integrin-mediated cell adhesion, thereby facilitating cell spreading and proliferation [[37], [38], [39]]. However, excessive positive charge may induce cytotoxicity, indicating that an optimized PEI modification is critical for balancing bioactivity and biocompatibility. Given this concern, we compared the cytotoxicity of different PEI graft densities (PEI-L, PEI-M, and PEI-H) and chose the PEI-L. Under this density, the scaffolds supported cell proliferation and showed negligible toxicity in vitro and in vivo.

In osteoporotic bone defects, elevated proinflammatory cytokines contribute to increased osteoclast activity, and decreased osteogenic differentiation [40]. Therefore, promoting anti-inflammatory signals is particularly beneficial for promoting bone regeneration. The reduced phosphorylation of P65 and IκBα observed in this work suggests that BMSC-Exo may attenuate inflammatory signaling through suppression of NF-κB activation. Consistent with these molecular findings, immunofluorescence analysis of scaffolds after implantation demonstrated decreased CD86 expression and increased CD206 expression at bone defect, indicating reduced M1 macrophage polarization and enhanced transition toward the reparative M2 phenotype. Such immunomodulatory effects, together with sustained inhibition of osteoclast activity by Aln and the osteogenic regulatory function of BMSC-Exo, contributed to establishing a favorable microenvironment for osteoporotic bone regeneration.

Previous studies have shown that a diminished osteogenic capacity of BMSCs is a principal factor in insufficient bone formation during osteoporosis [1,35]. Therefore, early enhanced osteogenic activity is essential for osteoporotic bone healing. Notably, our results demonstrated that the BMSC-Exo-functionalized (Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln) scaffold significantly enhanced osteogenic-related markers gene expression and matrix mineralization. To elucidate the mechanisms underlying therapeutic effects of BMSC-Exo, miRNA profiling was performed to identify regulatory miRNAs. Several highly abundant miRNAswere identified in BMSC-Exo, among which miR-486 showed relatively high expression. Functional analysis demonstrated that miR-486 overexpression reduced PTEN expression while enhancing AKT activation in MSCs, suggesting involvement of the PTEN/AKT signaling pathway. Since PTEN acts as a negative regulator of AKT signaling, its suppression may facilitate downstream signaling events associated with osteogenic differentiation through pathways involving mTOR, FOXO, and GSK3β [41]. However, it is noted that miR-486 was proved to be most potential component to promote osteogenesis here, other factors such as miR-21-5p, miR-26a-5p, and miR-143-3p might also be involved in this process.

Interestingly, both in vitro and in vivo results showed that the P/PVA-Aln group without BMSC-Exo also exhibited markedly enhanced osteogenic activity compared to the control and P/PVA groups. This improvement might be related to the pro-osteogenic effects of Aln released in a sustained manner. Previous studies have reported that a low dose of Aln can stimulate osteoblast differentiation. However, it is of note that high doses of Aln can inhibit osteoblast proliferation and mineralization [42,43]. Therefore, the application of Aln for bone repair should be utilized in a controlled manner as we adapted here. On the other hand, heightened osteoclastogenic activity contributes to abnormal bone remodeling and delays bone healing [44]. In the present study, incorporation of Aln into the core layer of the nanofiber enhanced its antiresorptive capacity and sustained release profile, leading to efficient and prolonged inhibition of osteoclast activity. Meanwhile, we found that the BMSC-Exo-functionalized scaffold (Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln) also has the capacity to inhibit osteoclast activity, suggesting that BMSC-Exo might contribute to the suppression of osteoclast maturation. This effect may be attributed to the ability of BMSC-Exo to regulate macrophage polarization, thereby reducing the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and ultimately inhibiting osteoclast formation [45,46].

Conclusions

In summary, a core-shell Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln nanofibrous scaffold was fabricated through coaxial electrospinning, allowing controlled spatial and temporal release of BMSC-derived exosomes and Aln. This strategy was designed to address a major obstacle in osteoporotic bone repair by resorting osteoblastic bone formation and osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. In vitro results demonstrated that the Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffold promoted osteogenic differentiation, through activation of the PTEN/AKT signaling pathway to support bone formation, whereas sustained release of Aln reduced bone resorption throughout the healing process. When implanted into osteoporotic cranial defects, the scaffold significantly improved the bone repair efficacy. These results demonstrate the potential for clinical use of the Exo-PEI@P/PVA-Aln scaffold in the repair of osteoporotic bone defects.
