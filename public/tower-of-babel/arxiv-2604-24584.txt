Pair-Dependent Drift of Kerr Neighboring-Overtone Gap Minima
Yuye Wu, Hong-Bo Jin

Abstract
Quasinormal modes (QNMs) underpin black-hole ringdown modeling and spectroscopy, where higher overtones can contribute at early times and neighboring modes can become locally organized in the complex-frequency plane. Motivated by this, we continuously track Kerr overtones along a spin scan and study a raw neighboring-overtone quantity: the complex-frequency gap between adjacent overtones. We find robust interior minima whose spin locations drift from pair to pair, even within the same ( s , ℓ , m ) (s,\ell,m) sector. We explain this by reformulating minimum setting as a local zero-setting problem for the complex separation between the two modes. Differentiating the squared gap yields a denominator-free real-projection diagnostic (the real part of the product between the complex separation, with conjugation, and its spin derivative). The sampled minimum is governed by an approximate local zero of this diagnostic, so minimum drift becomes the drift of its dominant zero crossing. This also yields a geometric picture: the minimum is a local radial turning event of the separation vector, while angular motion in the complex plane may persist. Finally, an expanded but deliberately restricted representative set (including same-family continuations, external positive transfers, and smooth no-trigger controls) supports this local picture for the triggered cases examined here. At the same time, the detailed local crossing environment remains intrinsically pair dependent.

Fixed-
n
object and local diagnostic.

We now define the object used throughout the paper. We continuously track each overtone as the Kerr spin
a
varies, and we keep its label fixed along the scan. For a neighboring pair
(n,n+1)
, we measure how far the two modes are separated in the complex-frequency plane. This is the absolute value of their complex-frequency difference:

g_{n,n+1}(a)=\left|\omega_{n}(a)-\omega_{n+1}(a)\right|.

(2)

The sampled spin location of the minimum gap is denoted by

a^{*}_{n,n+1}=\operatorname{argmin}_{a}g_{n,n+1}(a).

(3)

This raw neighboring-pair gap is the primary numerical input in what follows.

It is useful to package the same information into a single complex separation vector,

Z(a)\equiv\omega_{n_{2}}(a)-\omega_{n_{1}}(a),

(4)

where
(n_{1},n_{2})
denotes the neighboring pair. The gap is simply its magnitude:

g(a)=|Z(a)|,\qquad g^{2}(a)=|Z(a)|^{2}.

(5)

Because
g(a)\geq 0
, minimizing
g(a)
is equivalent to minimizing
g^{2}(a)
. Working with
g^{2}
is also cleaner. Differentiating
g(a)=|Z(a)|
introduces a denominator
1/|Z(a)|
. Differentiating
g^{2}(a)=|Z(a)|^{2}
avoids it.

The central local diagnostic in this work is

F(a)\equiv\Re\!\left(\overline{Z(a)}\,Z^{\prime}(a)\right).

(6)

This diagnostic is directly tied to how the gap changes with spin. From Eq. (
5
),

g^{2}(a)=|Z(a)|^{2}=Z(a)\,\overline{Z(a)}.

(7)

Differentiating with respect to
a
gives

\partial_{a}g^{2}(a)=Z^{\prime}(a)\,\overline{Z(a)}+Z(a)\,\overline{Z^{\prime}(a)}=2\,\Re\!\left(\overline{Z(a)}\,Z^{\prime}(a)\right).

(8)

\partial_{a}g^{2}(a)=2\,\Re\!\left(\overline{Z(a)}\,Z^{\prime}(a)\right)=2F(a).

(9)

Accordingly, a local minimum is characterized by the near-vanishing condition

F(a^{*})\approx 0.

(10)

This simple relation is the basic local statement used in the analysis below.

Local minimum-setting mechanism.

We now turn from the phenomenology to the local mechanism. The goal is to identify the local event in the complex-frequency plane that sets an interior minimum, and to explain why its spin location shifts from one neighboring pair to another. We proceed in three steps.

A. Cartesian balance near the minimum.

We now resolve the master relation in Eq. (
9
) in Cartesian form. In terms of the real and imaginary parts of the local separation vector, Eq. (
5
) becomes

g^{2}(a)=\Delta\omega_{R}(a)^{2}+\Delta\omega_{I}(a)^{2},

(14)

and therefore

\partial_{a}g^{2}(a)=2\Delta\omega_{R}(a)\,\partial_{a}\Delta\omega_{R}(a)+2\Delta\omega_{I}(a)\,\partial_{a}\Delta\omega_{I}(a).

(15)

We then define the two Cartesian contributions

\displaystyle C_{R}(a)

\displaystyle\equiv 2\Delta\omega_{R}(a)\,\partial_{a}\Delta\omega_{R}(a),

(16)

\displaystyle C_{I}(a)

\displaystyle\equiv 2\Delta\omega_{I}(a)\,\partial_{a}\Delta\omega_{I}(a),

(17)

so that

\partial_{a}g^{2}(a)=C_{R}(a)+C_{I}(a).

(18)

This split makes the local picture concrete. A minimum is not a mysterious dip in the scalar gap. It is the point where the two contributions compete and the sign of
\partial_{a}g^{2}
switches. The problem is therefore to understand how the real-part and imaginary-part pieces balance in the immediate neighborhood of the minimum.

Fig.
1
shows this balance near the minima in the mainline
(s,\ell,m)=(-2,2,2)
case. Both neighboring pairs share the same pattern. On the left,
\partial_{a}g^{2}<0
and the gap decreases. Near the minimum,
C_{R}
and
C_{I}
have opposite signs and nearly cancel, so that

\partial_{a}g^{2}(a)\approx 0.

(19)

On the right, the sum becomes positive and the gap turns upward. The minimum is therefore a local derivative-balance event.

Pair dependence enters through
how
the cancellation occurs. The same sign switch happens for both pairs, but the relative weights of
C_{R}
and
C_{I}
and the sharpness of the transition differ. This points to one mechanism, realized in different local complex-separation environments.

In this paper the Cartesian split is mainly diagnostic. It tells us which piece drives the sign switch, and it prepares a cleaner complex-plane statement.

B. Complex-plane reformulation.

The Cartesian statement is just the real/imaginary expansion of the complex relation. We now return to a compact complex-plane form. From Eq. (
9
), the same condition reads

\partial_{a}g^{2}(a)=2\,\Re\!\left(\overline{Z(a)}\,Z^{\prime}(a)\right)=2F(a).

(20)

To expose the geometry, we write the separation vector in polar form,

Z(a)=r(a)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(a)}.

(21)

Then

\overline{Z}Z^{\prime}=rr^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}r^{2}\theta^{\prime},

(22)

so that

\Re(\overline{Z}Z^{\prime})=rr^{\prime},\qquad\Im(\overline{Z}Z^{\prime})=r^{2}\theta^{\prime}.

(23)

The real part is the instantaneous radial projection. The imaginary part is the instantaneous angular rotation. The minimum is thus a radial turning event: radial compression stalls at the minimum, while rotation in the complex plane can continue.

\mathrm{pair}(5,6)

This complex-plane form sharpens the picture. The minimum is not only a cancellation in
\partial_{a}g^{2}
. It is a radial-projection event for the separation vector in the complex-frequency plane.

Global consistency and zero-crossing drift.

We now have a local picture of the minimum. Near
a^{*}
, the same balance can be written in Cartesian form or in the compact complex-plane form based on
F(a)=\Re(\overline{Z(a)}\,Z^{\prime}(a))
. The next step is to connect this local event to the full spin scan. We also want to understand why different pairs reach that event at different spins.

B. Pair-dependent zero-crossing drift.

With global consistency established, the drift statement becomes precise. The minimum is characterized by

F_{p}(a_{p}^{*})\approx 0,\qquad F_{p}(a)\equiv\Re\!\left(\overline{Z_{p}(a)}\,Z_{p}^{\prime}(a)\right),

so pair-dependent minimum drift is the drift of the dominant zero-crossing location of
F_{p}(a)
.

This is the same question in a cleaner form. Instead of asking where the gap curve dips, we ask where the radial projection of the separation dynamics changes sign. Different pairs reach that radial turning point at different spins.

Numerically, for both mainline pairs, the sampled minimum lies extremely close to the dominant zero crossing of
F_{p}(a)
. The offsets

a^{\rm zero}_{4,5}-a^{*}_{4,5}=-3.27\times 10^{-4},\qquad a^{\rm zero}_{5,6}-a^{*}_{5,6}=-8.9\times 10^{-5},

are far below the coarse grid spacing. The minimum is therefore anchored by the zero crossing. Drift of the minimum is drift of this dominant radial zero crossing.

The two pairs approach this event differently. The local slope of
F(a)
at the crossing is much smaller for
\mathrm{pair}(4,5)
than for
\mathrm{pair}(5,6)
,

\partial_{a}F|_{4,5}\approx 2.71,\qquad\partial_{a}F|_{5,6}\approx 28.36.

Thus
\mathrm{pair}(5,6)
crosses through zero sharply, while
\mathrm{pair}(4,5)
crosses gently. This difference in local crossing structure is part of what makes the minimum locations visibly pair dependent.

In this framework, drift is not a separate phenomenon. It is simply the pair dependence of the dominant zero-crossing location of
F_{p}(a)
.

C. Restricted representative validation.

We now ask how far this picture extends beyond the two mainline pairs. We therefore examine a small but deliberately chosen representative set of additional neighboring-pair cases. The goal is modest. We do not claim a family-wide law. We only test whether the same minimum-setting / dominant-zero-crossing picture transfers across a restricted but nontrivial set.

A first group consists of same-family continuations in
(s,\ell,m)=(-2,2,0)
. Besides
\mathrm{pair}(4,5)
, the pairs
\mathrm{pair}(5,6)
and
\mathrm{pair}(6,7)
also develop interior minima. The sampled locations are

a^{*}_{4,5}=0.84,\qquad a^{*}_{5,6}=0.80,\qquad a^{*}_{6,7}=0.78.

All three admit a dominant zero-crossing description. The first two are clean same-family transfers. The
\mathrm{pair}(6,7)
case is more distorted, so we treat it as an edge case rather than a flagship example.

A second group shows that transfer is not confined to one
(s,\ell,m)
family. We find clean positives and one moderate positive closer to the high-spin edge, where wording should be more cautious. Across all six positive cases, the sampled minimum and the dominant zero crossing remain aligned within one coarse scan step,

|a^{\rm zero}-a^{*}|<0.02.

The cleanest cases lie well inside this bound. Even the most distorted positive case,
({-2},2,0)
,
\mathrm{pair}(6,7)
, remains inside it:

|a^{\rm zero}-a^{*}|=0.012167<0.02.

Thus the transfer is not only qualitative. It preserves a quantitatively meaningful alignment between the sampled minimum and the dominant zero crossing.

At the same time, the set preserves selectivity. The smooth control case
({-2},2,1)
,
\mathrm{pair}(2,3)
, shows no robust interior minimum and does not trigger the same interpretation. This matters. The mechanism is not being imposed on an arbitrary gap curve.

The conclusion is therefore restricted but strong. The minimum-setting / dominant-zero-crossing picture is not a one-off description tied only to the original two-pair mainline. It transfers across a restricted validated class, and it remains selective against smooth controls. At the same time, the enlarged set still does not justify a family-wide law across the Kerr spectrum.

Conclusion.

We have shown that the pair-dependent drift of sampled gap minima in Kerr neighboring-overtone separations (with overtone labels held fixed along the spin scan) can be reformulated in a simple way. The minimum drift is the drift of the dominant zero crossing of

F(a)=\Re\!\bigl(\overline{Z(a)}\,Z^{\prime}(a)\bigr),

where
Z(a)
is the complex separation between the two neighboring modes. In this form, the minimum-setting event is not just a feature of a scalar gap curve. It is a local radial turning event of the separation vector in the complex-frequency plane.

Restricted validation shows that this local mechanism transfers across a small but nontrivial class of triggered cases while remaining selective against smooth no-trigger controls.
In this sense, our result is complementary to earlier studies of resonant excitation, quasinormal-mode resonances, excitation factors, and exceptional-point-related behavior
Motohashi 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
;
Lo et al. 2025
;
Cavalcante et al. 2024a
.
Rather than proposing a new global spectral class, we isolate a local minimum-setting mechanism that organizes how neighboring-pair gap minima drift along a one-parameter Kerr spin scan.
The conclusion is deliberately restricted but still strong: the observed drift is a structured local complex-spectral effect, not a numerical artifact, although we do not claim a family-wide law across the full Kerr spectrum.

This broader pattern supports the restricted language used in the main text. The minimum-setting / dominant-zero-crossing mechanism is a structured local explanation for triggered cases.
