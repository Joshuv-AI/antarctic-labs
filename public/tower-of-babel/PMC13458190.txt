Oral Embryonal Rhabdomyosarcoma and Its Management: A Rare Enigmatic Pediatric Case Report with Immunohistochemistry
Authors: Dominic Augustine, SV Sowmya, Rajanikanth B Rajaram, Kavitha Prasad, Kuntala Mondal, Harini Palanivelu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and backgroundMalignant tumors are rarely seen in children, of which lymphomas and sarcomas like rhabdomyosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma prevail. Histopathologically, these lesions show numerous round cells, which make it difficult to differentiate between one another. Molecular analysis such as immunohistochemistry has proved to be beneficial and cost-effective. The current report aims to highlight an enigmatic case of embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (ERMS), emphasizing the role of immunohistochemistry.Case descriptionA 3-year-old female patient presented to the dental outpatient department with a chief complaint of a progressively enlarging, diffuse swelling over the left cheek region, persisting for the past 2 weeks. Introduction

Head and neck cancers (HNC), though relatively rare, comprise 2–15% of all pediatric malignancies. Despite their rarity, cancer persists as a major contributor to mortality among children and adolescents on a global scale.1 Pediatric head and neck cancers (PHNC) encompass a diverse array of malignancies, with the principal entities including non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (NHL), Hodgkin's lymphoma (HL), rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS), nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), thyroid carcinoma (TC), neuroblastoma, and salivary gland carcinoma, each representing a critical facet of this oncological subset.2 Among them, RMS is an infrequent malignancy in the oral cavity, comprising merely 10–12% of all head and neck cases.3 Among intraoral sites, the tongue is the predominant location, followed by the palate and buccal mucosa, whereas gingival involvement is exceedingly rare.4 The embryonal variant represents the predominant histological subtype of RMS, accounting for approximately 49% of all cases. While it predominantly arises in children under the age of 10, its occurrence is also documented in adolescents and young adults.5 Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (ERMS) in the oral cavity may mimic infectious or inflammatory swellings, leading to diagnostic ambiguity and potential delays in timely therapeutic intervention. Early and precise diagnosis is crucial for maximizing survival outcomes, as it facilitates timely intervention and enhances prognostic efficacy. Histopathological analysis is paramount in the accurate diagnosis of ERMS of the jaw, facilitating a definitive characterization of the tumor's cellular architecture and histological composition, thereby distinguishing it from other pathologies.6 In pediatric patients, selecting an appropriate biopsy technique is fundamental, not only for ensuring an accurate and definitive diagnosis but also for mitigating procedural trauma and enhancing patient comfort. Tru-cut biopsy, by virtue of its minimally invasive nature, significantly attenuates patient morbidity in comparison to open surgical biopsies. This is particularly crucial for pediatric patients, who possess an increased vulnerability to the complications that often accompany more invasive surgical procedures.7 The diagnosis of RMS using conventional hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining is fraught with difficulty, owing to the substantial overlap in its histopathological features with a range of other neoplastic lesions exhibiting round and spindle-shaped cellular morphology.8 Immunohistochemistry is an indispensable tool in the diagnostic arsenal for ERMS. It not only confirms the myogenic origin of the tumor but also facilitates accurate differentiation from other malignancies, enabling precise diagnosis and guiding effective therapeutic interventions. ERMS exhibits a well-documented male predominance; however, the current case involved a pediatric female patient, thereby representing an uncommon demographic presentation for this malignancy.

This case report aims to underscore the critical importance of tru-cut biopsy and immunohistochemistry in navigating the intricate diagnostic challenges associated with ERMS in a pediatric patient.

Case Description

A 3-year-old female patient reported to the OPD of the Faculty of Dental Sciences, MS Ramaiah University of Applied Sciences, Bengaluru, Karnataka, India, with a chief complaint of diffuse extraoral swelling in the left cheek region for the past 2 weeks. An extraoral examination revealed a firm, nonindurated soft tissue swelling measuring approximately 5 × 6 cm. The swelling extended superoinferiorly from the infraorbital region to the lower border of the mandible and anteroposteriorly from the alar cartilage to the tragus of the ear. Intraoral examination demonstrated a diffuse swelling that resulted in obliteration of the vestibular space (Figs 1A to D).

(A) Extraoral view showing a nonindurated soft tissue swelling; (B) Lateral view showing the extent of the swelling; (C) Intraoral examination demonstrating a diffuse swelling; (D) The swelling exhibiting obliteration of the vestibular space

A tru-cut biopsy was executed for comprehensive histopathological evaluation. A provisional diagnosis of odontogenic myxoma was deduced through meticulous gross examination of the tissue specimen. Microscopic examination following staining by H&E revealed undifferentiated small round to oval cells with pleomorphism arranged in sheets. The majority of the cells showed hyperchromatic nuclei, and a few were vesicular in appearance. Some cells showed abundant cytoplasm with eccentrically placed nuclei, suggestive of strap cells or rhabdomyoblasts. The stroma showed varying-sized endothelial-lined blood vessels and loose myxoid stroma. A focal area showed normal skeletal muscle and bone (Figs 2A to D).

Photomicrographs of ERMS; (A) Sheets of round to oval-shaped tumor cells (H&E; ×40); (B) Loose, myxoid stroma showing bizarre tumor cells with pleomorphism and hyperchromatic nuclei (H&E; ×400); (C) PAS-positive strap cells or rhabdomyoblasts showing abundant cytoplasm and eccentrically placed nuclei (PAS; ×100); (D) Pleomorphic tumor cells with strap cells demonstrating glycogen (PAS; ×400)

The following differential diagnoses were considered: non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, malignant schwannoma, neuroblastoma, and ERMS. To achieve a conclusive diagnosis, a comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis was carried out. A panel was performed utilizing markers including desmin, vimentin, Ki-67, CK-19, and S-100 to enable a thorough diagnostic evaluation. The tumor cells demonstrated strong immunopositivity for desmin, vimentin, and Ki-67, alongside an absence of immunoreactivity for CK-19 and S-100.

The tumor cells exhibited strong cytoplasmic immunopositivity for desmin, indicative of myogenic differentiation, coupled with faint cytoplasmic reactivity for vimentin, reinforcing their mesenchymal lineage. The tumor cells revealed pronounced nuclear immunoreactivity for Ki-67, with a labeling index of 80%, underscoring a highly proliferative cellular phenotype. The assessment of positive cell count was carried out in strict accordance with the methodology established by Iamaroon et al. (2004).9 The tumor cells exhibited an absence of CK-19 expression, suggesting a nonodontogenic etiology, while the lack of S-100 immunoreactivity further corroborated a nonneurogenic origin (Figs 3A to J).

The application of an immunohistochemical panel was essential for distinguishing between differential diagnoses, particularly in ruling out odontogenic myxoma, neuroblastoma, and malignant schwannoma. This thorough diagnostic process ultimately culminated in the definitive diagnosis of ERMS.

Positron emission tomography and computed tomography (PET-CT) revealed a metabolically active soft tissue mass measuring 6.3 × 5.6 × 5.4 cm in the left infratemporal fossa, accompanied by scalloping and thinning of adjacent bony structures, as well as adenotonsillar enlargement. Additionally, the scan demonstrated multiple enlarged or prominent lymph nodes in the bilateral submandibular and upper jugular regions. The lesion exhibited scalloping and destruction of the left mandibular ramus, including the coronoid process, condylar process, and mandibular head, along with thinning of the adjacent skull base. Furthermore, there was scalloping of the maxillary alveolus, zygomatic arch, and pterygoid plate, with extension into the superior gingivobuccal sulcus and cheek. The mass collectively measured 6.3 × 5.6 × 5.4 cm and caused effacement and posteromedial displacement of the parapharyngeal fat pad. The CT scan further revealed marked adenotonsillar hypertrophy, left-sided serous otitis media, and multiple enlarged or prominent lymph nodes within the bilateral submandibular and upper jugular regions, with the largest node measuring about 2.1 × 1.0 cm (Fig. 4A).

(A) PET-CT revealed a metabolically active soft tissue mass accompanied by scalloping and thinning of adjacent bony structures; (B) Posttreatment assessment with PET-CT demonstrated pronounced attenuation in the tumor's size

As a therapeutic intervention, the patient was administered a two-cycle chemotherapy protocol comprising intravenous vincristine (0.48 mg), cyclophosphamide (700 mg in 250 mL of normal saline), mesna (140 mg), dexamethasone (2 mg), and dactinomycin (0.45 mg). Posttreatment assessment with PET-CT demonstrated a pronounced reduction in tumor size (Fig. 4B), indicative of a robust therapeutic response (Figs 5A and B). The patient was subsequently placed under structured clinical surveillance to monitor for disease progression or recurrence.

Discussion

Rhabdomyosarcomas stand as the most prevalent malignant neoplasms of mesenchymal origin affecting the musculoskeletal system in the pediatric and adolescent demographic. The prognosis of RMS is generally less favorable than that of other malignant lesions affecting the oral soft tissues. The most recent WHO classification delineates four principal subtypes: ERMS, alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma (ARMS), pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma (PMRMS), and spindle cell/sclerosing rhabdomyosarcoma (SpRMS),10 supplemented by descriptions of several newly identified variants.

The various histological subtypes of rhabdomyosarcoma, along with their associated prognostic outcomes, age of onset, and underlying genetic alterations, are comprehensively detailed in Table 1. Among the diverse histopathological variants, ERMS is the most frequently encountered subtype within the oral cavity, accounting for approximately 70–80% of documented cases.11 According to the literature, clinically ERMS manifests along a wide pathological continuum, from a discrete submucosal nodule to an aggressively expanding facial mass. While it may remain asymptomatic, certain cases present with associated symptoms such as pain, trismus, paresthesia, facial palsy, and nasal discharge, reflecting the tumor's potential for perineural invasion and regional extension.12

In the present case, a diffuse extraoral swelling was observed in the left cheek region, persisting for the past 2 weeks. A slight male predominance is observed in the occurrence of this malignancy. In this case, a 3-year-old female patient presented with a diffuse swelling localized to the left cheek region. A tru-cut biopsy was carried out to facilitate a comprehensive histopathological evaluation. Tru-cut biopsy is an invaluable diagnostic tool in pediatric populations, offering significant advantages due to its minimally invasive nature. By reducing the risks of complications, postoperative pain, and recovery time, it provides a safer alternative to traditional surgical biopsies. The technique ensures high diagnostic precision by obtaining a representative tissue sample for histopathological analysis, which is essential for diagnosis.13

According to the literature, microscopic analysis revealed the presence of primitive cells characterized by amphophilic cytoplasm and centrally located angular or oval nuclei. A few stellate cells were interspersed throughout the tissue. Notably, distinctive tadpole-shaped, strap-like, and spider cells were prominently discernible.14 Histopathologic analysis of this case revealed undifferentiated small round to oval cells exhibiting pleomorphism, predominantly organized in sheet-like patterns. The majority of cells displayed hyperchromatic nuclei, while a few exhibited vesicular nuclei. Some cells presented abundant cytoplasm with eccentrically located nuclei, indicative of strap cells or rhabdomyoblasts. The provisional differential diagnoses encompassed ERMS, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, malignant schwannoma, and neuroblastoma.

To achieve diagnostic certainty, a meticulous and comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis was performed. For diagnostic confirmation, an extensive panel of immunohistochemical markers was employed, including desmin, vimentin, Ki-67, CK-19, and S-100. Desmin is an intermediate filament protein residing in the cytoplasm, where it plays a vital role in preserving the structural integrity and proper alignment of the contractile apparatus within muscle cells. Desmin is regarded as one of the most sensitive markers for rhabdomyosarcoma, including its poorly differentiated forms. Research demonstrated that in desmin-positive cases, over 95% of tumor cells exhibit immunoreactivity, strongly supporting a diagnosis of RMS and indicating its muscle-derived origin.15

Vimentin, an intermediate filament protein predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of mesenchymal cells, is integral to maintaining cellular structural integrity and organizing the cytoskeletal framework. In cancer diagnostics, vimentin serves as a valuable immunohistochemical marker for identifying mesenchymal-origin tumors, including rhabdomyosarcoma, due to its characteristic expression in the cytoplasm of malignant mesenchymal cells.16

Ki-67, a nuclear protein, is extensively utilized to evaluate the proliferative activity of tumor cells. An elevated Ki-67 index reflects a higher proportion of actively proliferating cells, which is frequently associated with enhanced tumor aggressiveness and poorer prognostic implications.17 In this case, the labeling index was assessed at 80%, suggesting a highly aggressive proliferative cellular phenotype.

S-100 is a family of proteins typically expressed in the cytoplasm and nucleus of cells, particularly in neural and melanocytic cells. In the context of immunohistochemistry, S-100 is often used to identify neural differentiation.18 In this report, immunohistochemical profiling demonstrated the lack of S-100 expression, implying a nonneural origin for the tumor and excluding the possibility of malignant schwannoma and neuroblastoma.

CK-19 is a cytoplasmic marker commonly employed to delineate odontogenic epithelium, aiding in the identification of odontogenic lesions and tumors.19 In this case, the absence of CK-19 expression unequivocally eliminated the possibility of an odontogenic tumor.

Datta et al. (2016)5 reported a case of a 17-year-old female presenting with a mass on the left side of her mouth that had persisted for 1.5 months. Immunohistochemical evaluation was conducted using a comprehensive panel, including desmin, MYOD1, myogenin, muscle-specific actin, cytokeratin, epithelial membrane antigen, Mic-2, synaptophysin, CD31, CD34, and leukocyte common antigen. The results showed positive staining for desmin, MYOD1, myogenin, and muscle-specific actin, while cytokeratin, epithelial membrane antigen, Mic-2, synaptophysin, CD31, CD34, and leukocyte common antigen were negative. These findings culminated in a definitive diagnosis of ERMS.

The management of oral rhabdomyosarcoma generally involves radical surgical excision, complemented by a multi-agent chemotherapy regimen, typically consisting of vincristine, dactinomycin, and cyclophosphamide. In instances where complete resection is not feasible, adjuvant postoperative radiotherapy may be employed to enhance treatment efficacy.20 In this case, the patient received a two-cycle chemotherapy regimen consisting of intravenous vincristine (0.48 mg), cyclophosphamide (700 mg in 250 mL of normal saline), mesna (140 mg), dexamethasone (2 mg), and dactinomycin (0.45 mg). Posttreatment evaluation using PET-CT demonstrated a substantial reduction in tumor metabolic activity, signifying an effective response to therapy, and the patient remained under regular clinical follow-up thereafter.

Conclusion

This case report underscored the indispensable role of tru-cut biopsy in conjunction with immunohistochemical profiling in achieving a precise and expedient diagnosis of ERMS, thereby enabling the prompt initiation of targeted therapy.

Clinical Significance

The involvement of a pediatric female patient marked a distinctly uncommon demographic presentation for this neoplasm, accentuating its atypical clinical manifestation. The patient's favorable chemotherapeutic response further highlighted the critical importance of an integrated, multidisciplinary framework for optimizing therapeutic outcomes and ensuring robust long-term surveillance.
