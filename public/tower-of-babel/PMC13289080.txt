How the introduction of a new patient pathway impacts diagnosis and treatment of acute abdominal pain in the emergency department: a qualitative study based on expert interviews
Authors: Andreas Wagenknecht, Katharina Verleger, Martin Möckel, Britta Stier, Dörte Huscher, Anna Slagman, Liane Schenk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to explore (1) facilitating and inhibiting factors influencing the implementation of the Abdominal Pain Unit (APU) process, (2) physicians’ acceptance of the pathway and (3) typical experiential patterns and professional rationales emerging in clinical practice.DesignWithin a mixed-method framework, a qualitative evaluation study was conducted. Semi-structured expert interviews were conducted.Setting, participants36 physicians experienced in emergency care from 10 different emergency departments (EDs) involved in treating APU patients were interviewed. Years of work experience and professional status guided the selection.ResultsSeven major themes emerged: (1) physicians’ understanding of APU as a complex symptom associated with diagnostic uncertainties, (2) changes in clinical routines, (3) diagnostic certainty, (4) influence of professional experience, (5) the role of the digital APU application, (6) interdisciplinary cooperation and (7) obstacles to broad implementation. Overall, physicians perceived APU as beneficial for structuring clinical routines and standardising care, particularly for less experienced physicians. The pathway prompted more systematic documentation, repeated pain scoring and greater diagnostic reflection. The digital application was largely seen as intuitive, though its integration into existing IT systems and workflows posed challenges. No substantial changes were reported in interdisciplinary cooperation. Barriers to large-scale implementation included concerns about overdiagnosis, loss of clinical autonomy and additional documentation effort.ConclusionThe APU pathway supports the structured care of acute abdominal pain (AAP) in the ED. Its successful integration requires alignment with clinical routines, IT infrastructure and professional cultures. Balancing standardisation with clinical autonomy is key for sustainable implementation.Trial registrationDRKS00021052

Background

Among patients who present to the emergency department (ED), non-traumatic acute abdominal pain (AAP) is one of the most common complaints.1 2 Since AAP presents as a complex clinical phenomenon and can be caused by a variety of underlying diseases, among them severe ones, it constitutes a time-critical symptom. The demands on diagnostics and treatment are particularly high35 and dependent on qualifications and experiences of physicians as well as the resources available to EDs.6 Studies reveal a wide range of other challenges, such as limited resources, uncertainties and the limited usefulness of disease-related guidelines or guidelines that are limited to specific aspects of treatment, such as pain management.7 Studies observed a high portion of non-specific discharge diagnoses in abdominal pain patients as well as differing care quality and linked their findings to the lack of a structured management of non-traumatic AAP in EDs.8 The interdisciplinary collaboration between ED physicians and representatives from other specialist areas, as well as the multitude of possible differential diagnostic paths, necessitate a structured approach for the entire case, from the initial assessment, medical history and clinical examinations such as sonography and laboratory diagnostics to the decision on the type of further treatment—especially since symptom-oriented guidelines or standard operating procedures (SOPs) are not yet widely used or not generally agreed on beyond the local/regional level.9 Standardised processes and structured support for decision-making promise to assure quality and efficiency of care.

Due to their organisational structure and pressing issues such as high patient volumes and staff shortages, EDs would particularly benefit from process standards that include but are not limited to relevant points such as reaching a diagnosis, collaborating with imaging units and further treatment.10 Guidelines and evidence-based and expert knowledge included in such a pathway affect clinical as well as organisational processes, decision-making and patient-related outcomes. They reduce complexity, provide guidance and increase the quality of care.11 The Abdominal Pain Unit (APU) process was developed as a standardised treatment process—from symptomatic presentation to diagnosis and further treatment—to meet the challenges arising from the clinical complexity of AAP, with the aim of enabling efficient, targeted and safe diagnostics and thus improving the overall quality of care.12 13 APU provides a step-by-step care trajectory for patients with symptoms of non-traumatic AAP.

Elsewhere, measurable effects of the treatment pathway were demonstrated. While patient-reported pain and satisfaction decreased in the intervention group compared with the control group, the duration of treatment did not change significantly.13 This article, however, focuses on the process evaluation of the implementation of the treatment pathway. The specific objectives are as follows: First, to describe inhibiting and facilitating factors in the implementation of the APU; second, to evaluate the acceptance of the APU process by physicians and third, to reconstruct typical practical rationales, experiences and action patterns of physicians.

Methods

Study design

In order to examine the impact and effectiveness of the APU, its implementation was evaluated by means of a mixed-method study, which was essentially realised in a stepped-wedge cluster randomised controlled trial in 10 study centres and focused on relevant endpoints and parameters.13 14 As part of the mixed-method approach, qualitative methods were used to investigate and evaluate how the APU was implemented by ED staff in everyday clinical practice, what effects they have experienced15 and which conditions influenced the implementation.16 Therefore, the process evaluation can be understood as mutually complementary to the trial. The quantitative outcomes can only be integrated with the qualitative results to a limited extent. They were rarely discussed in the interviews, which instead addressed other aspects of implementing an intervention. The explicit goal of the process evaluation was not to explain the quantitative effects. Rather, the focus is on gaining a broader perspective on the implementation of the treatment pathway.

The qualitative evaluation focused on process quality from different perspectives, namely the key players in the APU process—ED staff—whose respective knowledge and experiences with the APU process were collected and analysed. Individual experiences, knowledge and behaviour of actors who implemented the APU pathway were—in addition to other factors such as clinical-medical, organisational and economic factors—relevant for the implementation of a new form of treatment.11 Therefore, physicians who were actively involved in the implementation process of the intervention by treating APU patients were interviewed. Acceptance of a new form of care by professionals is a prerequisite for its broad implementation. It is therefore necessary to understand the professionals’ perspective, their individual experiences, daily routines and stock of knowledge through conducting expert interviews were conducted.17 18

Data collection, participants, sampling

36 physicians who had treated patients according to the APU process in 10 EDs were interviewed based on interview guidelines.19 The interview guide was developed by the interdisciplinary APU team, which included—among others—sociologists and clinical experts, tested in two pilot interviews and extensively revised. In addition to general questions on professional experience in treating patients with AAP, the guide focused on implementing the treatment pathway, changes in treatment, advantages and disadvantages, risks and opportunities for improvement and technical implementation. The purposeful sampling aimed to provide a diverse picture of the physicians involved in the APU intervention in terms of professional experience and position (see online supplemental table 1). Altogether, 4 chief physicians (years of work experience between 12 and 22 years), 14 senior physicians (years of work experience between 3 and 14 years) and 17 assistant physicians (years of work experience between 0.5 and 2 years) were interviewed; most of them have a disciplinary background in internal medicine (with a few exceptions: anaesthesiology, surgery and neurology). Experts were recruited for the interviews via study nurses in the study centres. If potentially interested, the physicians received an invitation letter by email with information about the objectives of the study, data protection regulations and an explanation of the interview procedure. Physicians gave their written informed consent. All interviews were conducted by the first author (with a disciplinary background in sociology), whether by telephone or face-to-face, audio-recorded and transcribed. Most of the interviews took place during the physicians’ clinical working hours. Saturation was not discussed in the course of the research. Rather, the aim was to gather experience from all clinics. There was no acquaintance between interviewer and interviewee, and the interviewer introduced himself as part of the evaluation team, although not as a clinical expert.

Data analysis: documentary method

The expert interviews were interpreted using the Documentary Method.20 21 This interpretative method aims at reconstructing concrete experiences and implicit and explicit knowledge. The interpretation of the interview data took place in a multi-stage process. In a first step, the interview passages were subjected to a thematic fine structuring into main and sub-themes, and the statements were primarily described (formulating interpretation). This made it possible to trace dominant thematic structures and lines of argumentation. During the subsequent reflective interpretation, selected passages were interpreted individually in order to determine the framework in which the respective topics are dealt with. In other words, this step focuses on the ‘how?’ a specific issue is dealt with. Particular orientations, for example, economical or professional frameworks, were determined across cases in order to find typical patterns. A similar, common issue (the APU intervention) was thus examined for similarities and differences in its practical management. Given the large number of interviews, a point of thematic saturation was reached. In the final five interviews, the interviewees had not raised any significantly new topics. Data interpretation was primarily performed by the first author, regularly discussed within the evaluation team and clinical experts and intersubjectively validated within an interdisciplinary interpretation group. MAXQDA Plus 2022 was used for data analysis.

Funding, ethical considerations and data protection

The German Innovations Funds (01NVF19025) funded the APU study. The responsible institutional review boards reviewed and approved the study protocol (14). Extensive data protection policies were developed in cooperation with Charité’s data protection department and the responsible parties for data protection of the participating study centres. For the reporting of the methods of this study, the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) list served as an orientation.22

Results

In the interviews, seven relevant topics emerged: (1) the general understanding and experience with AAP in the ED, (2) the question of adaptation or translation of the new standard and what has actually changed in clinical routines, (3) diagnostic certainty, (4) the role of professional experience, (5) the role of an digital application as digital process support, (6) effects on interdisciplinary cooperation and finally (7) obstacles for a broad implementation of APU. In addition, overarching logics of action and reasoning patterns were identified that influenced the acceptance and feasibility of APU. These included a rather medical-clinical logic, a focus on questions of the profession, including the acquisition of professional knowledge and experience, and a focus on organisational issues and economic reasoning.

Abdominal pain is a complex symptom

In order to fully understand the significance of the APU pathway and its implementation, it is relevant to look at the basic understanding of the role of abdominal pain in everyday clinical practice in the ED and the specific challenges it poses for physicians. Primarily, many physicians gave a quantitative assessment, concluding that the proportion of patients with AAP is understood by the physicians to be relatively high in comparison to other symptoms. The general tenor was the same: abdominal pain makes up a “large proportion” (#1 - numbers indicate the quoted physician) and virtually “populates” (#20) the ED with “all sorts of real complaints” (#20), meaning serious and adequate symptoms. These complaints, physicians continued, need to be understood as serious and non-trivial and require appropriate attention and resources.

A second theme commonly described was uncertainty related to the fact that abdominal pain as a symptom eludes clear classification. The simple application of standard diagnostics resulting in a definite, secure diagnosis was described as quite unusual. Rather, abdominal pain was described as a symptom that usually represents a wide range of causes, of possible diseases or affected organs (#30), a variety of less problematic to serious diagnoses (#7, 10, 16, 25, 28) and “potential hazards” (#10, 11). AAP is a “diffuse symptom” (#4, 15) and described to be often associated with an atypical, ambiguous presentation. Other generators of uncertainty named were the difficulty for patients in localising their pain (#17, 24) and the subjectivity of pain perception (#26). In connection with the time economy (#20) and the scarce staffing or general overload (#22) of the ED, the following picture emerged in the interviews:

“The possibilities […] of […] diagnostics are […] very diverse. And you have to decide relatively quickly […] which path you want to take.” (#9).

In addition, the insufficiency of diagnostic technologies and procedures like sonography, ambiguous laboratory data as well as organisational challenges, like the assignment to specialist disciplines, were mentioned.

Changes in clinical routines introduced by APU

By asking physicians what had changed in their treatment routines as a result of the introduction of APU, the aim was to determine whether APU had led to adaptations of existing processes or rather to adjustments to the pathway. The general attitude regarding the question of whether APU had led to changes in clinical processes was that this was not the case. It was concluded that APU did not bring much qualitative change, if any. Most physicians shared this assessment.

“We already had a fairly similar process before the introduction of the APU. (…) We did sonographic clarification, CT if necessary and only then called in the surgical departments (…).” (#12)

“In fact, my impression was that there was not much difference, not at all, because our standard procedure for abdominal pain actually corresponds well with the APU study.” (#27)

Most of the physicians’ assessments had a specific focus. They referred to the ‘rough’ flow chart of APU, for example, anamnesis, laboratory, sonography, CT and consultation.12 Within this framework, the introduction of APU showed little to no difference to the previous procedure.

However, in the course of follow-up questions or in passing, “small things” (#16) that have changed were described by the physicians. The changes noted were framed as follows:

The entire treatment and diagnostics in the APU were described as—in comparison to common practice—more extensive and comprehensively structured. Diagnostic certainty

A central goal of the APU pathway was to increase the diagnostic certainty of the physicians.12 The respondents’ opinions on these issues revealed a variety of, sometimes contradictory, assessments of the extent to which the APU pathway had an influence on their perceived certainty.

First and foremost, some kind of negative essentialism was quite dominant. This means that a reliable diagnosis in patients with AAP was seen as an impossibility. A fully reliable diagnosis was seen as an unrealistic goal or something that other hospital departments were primarily aiming at and are responsible for.

“I think with the resources and also the time you have in the emergency department, it would be exaggerated to demand only safe diagnoses as the ultimate goal.” (#14)

“With many abdominal pain patients, you still end up with several diagnoses that could have been the cause. So, someone may simply have had constipation or gastritis, which can explain the symptoms. And you won't find that out with APU, because neither a lab, nor an ultrasound, nor a CT will find that out, so to speak, because the patient will then need a gastroscopy or a colonoscopy.” (#28)

In a similar fashion, many physicians (#16, 26, 27, 30) argued that there were two different classes of diagnosis to be assigned.

“I believe that everything that can be confirmed with sonography, laboratory tests and CT and, if necessary, with surgical exploration, has become more reliable [through APU]. But anything that requires endoscopic diagnostics that can't be performed urgently, um, endoscopic or further gastroenterological diagnostics, like irritable bowel syndrome or some other, I don't know, food intolerances, celiac disease, etc., has certainly not become any more certain. These remain only suspected diagnoses until I have confirmed them through the appropriate channels.” (#26)

Other interviewees made nuanced statements focusing on certain aspects in which they felt an increase in certainty through the use of APU. These aspects covered different forms and sources of security: procedural security, assignment security and security through experience:

The straightforwardness and simplicity of the path clearly laid out next steps and thus increased overall confidence.

Referring to questions of one’s own professionalism and motivation, the requirement of a reliable diagnosis was interpreted as an implicit (somewhat positive) ‘coercion’, encouraging self-assurance and the consideration of further measures. The potential for ‘just’ “waiting and keeping everything open” (#22) or that physicians “simply conclude unclear abdominal pain and sent the patient away” (#20) was reduced.

Physicians with more than 2 years of clinical experience in the ED described APU as a confirmation of their previous way of treating patients with AAP. Their assumption was that previous routines were now backed up by evident expert knowledge. They also shared the view that APU primarily helps young, inexperienced physicians.

“The study provided assistance, especially for young, inexperienced colleagues: Okay, have I thought of everything? Because it always asks explicitly: Have I done that now? Have I done that now? Do I have a working diagnosis, can I continue now or not? That was simply a great help.” (#21)

“It’s always the youngest who are sent to the emergency room. And so, you try to bring everyone up to the same level. The only factor that can never be replaced is experience. You can never achieve that. But when it comes to knowledge, you at least try to bring everyone up to the same level. And with (APU), you can do that wonderfully.” (#30)

Physicians with extensive experience (directors, department heads) had a third perspective on APU. They highlighted the dangers of overdiagnosis as well as inefficient and uneconomical clinical practice. Also, they provided nuanced criticism that APU is too undifferentiated, meaning that there is potential for more precise correlations between symptoms, data and parameters to be mapped in the pathway, leading to more specific diagnoses.

Digital application

In the following, the focus is on the digital application (APP). The APP itself is part of the standard, not only conveying it but also encountering existing practices and routines as a new element. When asked about the user-friendliness of the APP and how it was integrated into the work processes, the general tenor of the interviewees was that using the application—after a short period of familiarisation and through regular use—was unproblematic and not a time cost factor.

“After the first three patients, it’s actually quite manageable.” (#10)

Furthermore, working with the APP was described as simple, “intuitive” (#26) and “self-explanatory” (#11).

Criticism of the APP’s basic usability followed a certain logic. Everyday clinical practice (including the particular time economy of the ED) was used as a benchmark, and the APP was described as being in some ways contradictory. In this context, the excessive detail and the lack of specificity of the APP were noted:

“With the APP, I have to be honest, (…) it was just too detailed. For everyday clinical practice, I have to say that I always had to click through so much that in the end it was almost more work than it was worth.” (#4)

It was also noted that the APP does not correctly reflect the logic of practice, for example, in the question about a ‘safe diagnosis’.

“The only thing I stumbled across once was this certain diagnosis, where I then checked again and said: I wouldn't want to have any further diagnostics done because I think I've ruled out anything vital. But I still don't have a definite diagnosis.” (#26)

One of the noteworthy topics regarding APP use is the different attitudes when choosing the end device. Whether to use a tablet or desktop follows a different rationale and practical logic in each case, which, when reconstructed, sheds light on how APU is understood as a digitalisation project and which form of technical support is seen as useful. Whether the argument was in favour of one or the other end device, it was always about certain practical advantages for and routines of the user.

The desktop PC was said to have certain advantages:

The desktop represents a familiar environment. Clinical documentation is already carried out there. It was argued that devices should be used sparingly, whereby the PC with its multitude of additional functions (such as documentation and examination registration) is preferred. In addition, the room-specific atmosphere is emphasised as beneficial to working, which the tablet as a ‘device without a specific room’ does not have.

How APU affects cooperation with other departments

At the beginning of the study, it was assumed that the APU pathway had the potential to affect cooperation with other specialist departments beyond the ED and to enhance documentation within the ED. Although the latter takes place within the ED, it was not a direct component of the standardised pathway.

It was inquired whether and how APU affected cooperation between the ED and other specialist departments by focusing on consultations. The majority of interviewees stated that they had not experienced any changes with regard to cooperation between the ED and other specialist departments since the introduction of APU. Some of the reasons mentioned here were, for example, that the consultants were only interested in certain aspects of a case rather than an entire treatment process like APU, or that the relationship with other specialist departments was based on personal aspects, experience and trust, which APU had no effect on.

Other physicians addressed the conflict-filled nature of communication with other departments. Somewhat commonly, established findings from the ED were said to be devalued in the specialist department by repeated imaging examinations, especially CT scans. The effect of APU in this regard is described as a ‘stronger position’ for the ED:

“Based on the APU study, we were now able to show our colleagues that our ultrasound knowledge is good enough.” (#21).

Two physicians noted that APU followed a general trend with regard to the organisation of interdepartmental cooperation. In each case, they constructed a past/present comparison: In the past, patients may have been passed through a large number of specialist departments. In the present, treatment practice dictates that patients are seen centrally in the ED, diagnosed and only then assigned to a specialist department.

Obstacles for broad implementation

The statements from the interviews, which revolve around topics like criticism of APU, its disadvantages and risks and obstacles of a broad implementation, are multifold and can be summarised in various thematic frameworks.

In a clinical framework, that is, in terms of the purely clinical performance of APU and its ability to support clinical decision-making processes, a “lack of specificity” (#2) was noted. The aim of such specificity would be to present clear decision-making paths, like, for example:

“In these cases, you should always do an ultrasound, or: We do far too many ultrasounds, or here and there you need a CT scan right away. It would be great if, in the end, we learned a little bit about what diagnostics are actually necessary and what aren't.” (#4)

What is being called for is the further development of APU towards guidelines or an algorithmic tool that converts symptom/finding constellations into clinical decisions.

An initial criticism of APU arises in the context of reflecting on the physician’s own professionalism and the status of professional knowledge. In this framework, questions were raised about knowledge transfer and further training for new colleagues.

“Well, I think it can only be a good thing (…) but first you have to give people proper information about how it works before you do it.” (#25)

A more critical view was articulated by seeing the danger that APU will degenerate into a mere processing of schemata, pushing ‘clinical experience and expertise” (#7), individual case reflection and interpretation—the basis of medical authority and the foundation of acquired reflective knowledge—into the background.

“Well, I think that, generally speaking, with a structured approach (…) there is always a risk that you might end up being a little too formulaic and not taking an individual approach.” (#22)

“Depending on your level of experience or your tendency to rely on such things, an APP is an APP, and the patient is a patient. Just because the APP told me that doesn't mean it’s 100% certain that it has to be that way for the patient. (…) So, you also have to be able to question whether everything is correct.” (#31)

An implicit link to another, economical concern can be made. Too much focus on implementing the APU scheme for the sake of the scheme alone could result in overdiagnosis. Overdiagnosis refers to the performance of clinically unnecessary measures in order to fulfil the pathway.

“The risk of overdiagnosis. (…) We cannot do everything for everyone if we want to fulfill our legal mandate of providing necessary diagnostics in a resource-efficient manner. (…) That’s how it works at the APU: everyone gets everything first, and then we take a look. So, we do this scattergun diagnosis, and then we see which lab values pop up.” (#6)

“You can't just take any clinical picture and turn it into an SOP checklist that has to be followed in every case (…). In my opinion, that leads to significant overdiagnosis. And with scarce resources, this would then be lacking in other areas (…).” (#7)

As can be seen in the quote, overdiagnosis is linked to the time economy of the ED, where any excess directly leads to a shortage elsewhere or to the overloading of available (human, organisational, clinical) resources as well as an increase in economic costs (#8).

Purely technological challenges were also cited as obstacles to the future large-scale implementation of APU. This would depend on how APU is made “technically available” (#23) and integrated into existing operating systems, especially the documentation system. Complex and fault-prone extra devices tend to be viewed critically.

“It must be acknowledged that digitization occasionally results in a slight increase in the time required, although this ultimately improves quality. If documentation is then also carried out in two different ways, I am somewhat skeptical about its practicality and long-term usability.” (#22)

The reduction of documentation effort in particular, and technically induced extra work in general, should be achieved so that one does not have to “open yet another APP” (#1).

Discussion

This article presents an insight into the implementation process of a new, standardised care pathway for patients presenting with AAP (see for an overview online supplemental figure 1). Traditionally, evaluation studies in the context of emergency medicine, which have evaluated the introduction of various instruments and procedures,2326 have focused on the measurement of clinical or performance outcomes or focused on educational strategies, audit and feedback.27 The experiences of physicians, who actively shape the implementation process, are rarely studied, yet they play a crucial role in the success of an implementation. The aim of this study was to systematise the different motivations and attitudes that guide clinical action and to search for underlying factors.

The general challenges posed by AAP for ED physicians were mostly due to the diffuseness of the symptom. The resulting uncertainties in clinical diagnostics are exacerbated by sometimes insufficient diagnostic tools, organisational problems at the interface with other hospital departments and the general time pressure that is always present in the ED. The central starting point of a new treatment pathway is therefore the effective targeting of existing problems within clinical routines.

Every new standard relates to the pre-existing setting of procedures and habits. Improvements and successful implementations are associated with the level of change or adaption effort.28 The adaption effort can be described as how much needs to be changed when existing routines relate to the new APU path. As portrayed above, the introduction of APU is not associated with major and comprehensive changes in treatment procedures. The new standard was fundamentally understood as a continuation or even replication of the previous treatment routines. This enabled implementation without major friction and conflict, as the adaptation effort was rather low. Also, it is not uncommon for the relevant actors to have difficulties in noticing, reflecting and describing changes in their practical routines.29 Still, every implementation of a new standard leads to changes in the respective organisation. As can be seen, the changes listed are not marginal details from the perspective of APU standardisation, but are relevant for the entire process. However, the fact that the changes were perceived as described indicates that a certain understanding of the APU pathway prevailed: a core of relevant activities and a multitude of ‘little details’ around it.

Uncertainty in medical practice is a pressing and much-discussed topic.30 31 Questions of uncertainty reduction are relevant, either in the concrete treatment of clinical cases and decision-making or in relation to the organisation of interfaces with other departments. Complex diagnostics and organisation or protracted decision-making may become a problem within an environment where resources are scarce and time-critical. Recognising life-threatening and time-critical illnesses within the atypical presentation or diffuse symptoms that require immediate treatment is described as a particularly central challenge and objective of good care practice. This increases the complexity of treatment, establishing diagnoses is more difficult, and uncertainties permeate the decision-making process. This has been established in association with various diseases.3234 The evaluation of APU shows, on the one hand, physicians are making more diagnoses with a higher degree of certainty through APU. APU provides physicians with an increased sense of security and a structured approach in the face of a symptom that often presents atypically. On the other hand, there still are diagnoses that cannot be fully confirmed in the ED. For the latter, only suspected diagnoses can be made, which require further diagnostics beyond the APU and the ED. However, in general, the increased expectation of achieving a ‘definite diagnosis’ leads to changes in the motivational structure of physicians.

Our results show that there are different rationalities within an organisation with regard to an implemented standard. Different actors realise and accept the standard on the basis of different reasons and motivations. Younger doctors are more likely to focus on topics relating to their own clinical practice and professional expertise and how they can improve their clinical knowledge. The new standard simplifies career entry for assistant doctors. Inexperienced doctors can feel not adequately prepared in the highly demanding and stressful work environment of the ED, just like their treatment abilities have an influence on the overall organisation. Discussions showed the importance of learning skills and their adaption as well as of reducing the complexity of organisational aspects.35 Even though studies have found that the professional status or specialisation of assistant physicians has no influence on the quality of treatment for patients with AAP,36 this study shows the extent to which a new standardised process supports the practical work of physicians. It can be assumed that the issue is addressed by APU in two ways: on the one hand, APU acts as a learning tool and generator of professional knowledge for younger physicians; on the other hand, the standard, built on evidence-based knowledge and professional consensus, enables a reduction of uncertainty in the everyday treatment of patients with AAP.

Chief physicians, given their oversight of the entire ED as an economically efficient unit within the hospital, expressed some economic concerns. These concerns related neither to clinical evidence nor to professional benefit but primarily to potential economic disadvantages by an overuse or unnecessary use of diagnostic tools. This is a familiar topic. Avoidable financial costs and patient safety issues are at stake here.37 38 An economic evaluation of APU39 addressed if APU leads to an excessive performance of clinically unnecessary measures in order to fulfil the pathway. Results show that costs per patient were raised, mainly because more diagnostic measures (ECGs, urine examinations, ultrasounds, medical councils) were undertaken: patients in the intervention group received on average 3.0 procedures, while patients in the control group received 2.3 procedures. Yet, despite a small increase in ED-related costs, the intervention demonstrated favourable cost-effectiveness from the payer perspective. Regardless of financial costs, the main goal of APU was a standardised performance, which was consented by clinical experts to raise the quality of care. Post hoc, it cannot be determined whether any procedure was justified in every case. Nevertheless, it can be assumed that concerns about overdiagnosis and economic tension reflect a general concern in the German healthcare system about the financial resources of EDs40 and therefore must be part of the discourse when implementing a new care pathway.

Technical applications make it likely that a new treatment pathway runs well. They also ensure correct application. The acceptance of an APP depends on its user-friendliness: how much time does it cost to use the APP and does it help to save time? This is particularly true for the ED. Time economy is an important issue.4143 The fit between clinical practice (and its time economy) and technology was also the guiding structural moment when it came to the specific practical use of the APP on the tablet or desktop PC. Time scarcity as a structural feature of the ED and the acceleration potential of a digital infrastructure were in the foreground and pushed the potential service of the APP, its originally assumed function, into the background. This is in contrast to studies focusing on the effectiveness of digital innovations in the ED, for example, more effective patient assessment, patient experience, resource allocation and discharge.44 45 Other studies argue more in line with our findings that certain factors in everyday ED practice limit the benefits of digital solutions.46 In the case of APU, these are existing technology usage and related time-saving aspects. In summary, a digital APP for support is rated as subordinate to existing technical systems. As a way of efficient technical support of clinical care, the APP needs to ‘sink in’ existing systems, that is, get integrated. No major challenges are expected with this implementation, as the documentation systems used in emergency rooms are generally designed to map standardised diagnosis and treatment processes.

The interface of the ED with other departments of the hospital is a pressing organisational issue,47 especially regarding consultations from other hospital departments.48 The frequent and many times necessary interdisciplinary treatment of patients with AAP requires decisions about ‘Who is responsible?’ and when interdisciplinary help should be requested in the course of treatment. In most EDs that were part of this study, responsibilities and competencies were clearly defined beforehand, and asking for interdisciplinary consultations was (a) a well-established routine or (b) problematic, and APU did not change prevailing conflicts. Certain challenges that were described by the interviewed physicians originate from organisational structures going beyond the limited scope of an intervention such as APU. Also, it is important to note that not every other hospital department was informed about the APU care pathway being implemented. On the other hand, and more implicitly, it was noted that APU had an effect in the context of intra-professional conflicts, especially on the issue of CT scans. Here a notable change through the implementation of APU was noticed. It corresponds with the decreased use of CT scans,13 41 suggesting a stronger inter-professional relevance of sonography in the ED. Beyond this, it was noted that APU generally follows the idea of interdisciplinary thinking, which is being dominantly discussed with the focus on time economy.49 Concerning documentation, some kind of unintended effect was described. It is noticeable that APU has not directly led to more frequent and more complete documentation but that APU has changed treatment practices and diagnostics and that corresponding documentation is inevitably linked to this. Due to the precedence of practice over documentation, the documentation changes.

The threat to professionalism and professional knowledge posed by the implementation of standardisation measures is a well-known phenomenon. This conflict is balanced by the assessment that new clinical treatment pathways can also provide relief and security, particularly when used as a basis for resolving complex cases through high-quality and safe treatment decisions.11 When viewed in conjunction with the other statements made by doctors as presented in this article, the assessment suggests that the latter is more accurate.

The patterns outlined suggest different motivations, patterns of action and thinking among physicians. When implementing innovations or new standards of medical treatment, they should be reflected on in advance, in particular as some tend to act as facilitating and others as hindering factors, or they can have an ambivalent effect in both directions. However, further research in this regard—that is, into the relationship between thought and action schemes and the acceptance of changes in clinical practice—appears to be necessary.

Limitations

Limitations of the studies include the context-specific nature of the study. The results are limited to the German healthcare system and the participating study centres, which are located in large- to medium-sized German cities. Differences in hospital types, such as full-service providers and university hospitals, were also not taken into account. Such systemic and hospital-specific contexts influence the implementation and acceptance of a new form of care. Second, it would be useful to compare the findings with the experiences of patients, their perspective on APU, and their satisfaction with the care they received. This needs to be addressed in future publications, based on participant observations of the ED stay of patients receiving APU care. The results are also based on the reflections of physicians. The specifics of the concrete implementation practice in everyday hospital life were therefore not reconstructed directly, but on a quasi-secondary level, filtered through memory and reflection. Also, the interviewees only selectively referred to the status quo of their work routines before APU was implemented. A full comparison of the ED work before and after APU implementation is beyond the scope of the research design. Finally, the interview study was performed at the same site as the intervention and randomised controlled trial; there might be a probability of desirability informing the answers of physicians. We had the impression that this was not the case because the interviewer was neither part of the intervention team nor of clinical education. Also, part of the interviews were concrete narrations of particular APU cases, and it was the overall goal to generate narrations so implicit meanings and attitudes could be reconstructed.

Conclusion

To conclude, successful implementation requires developing a treatment pathway that incorporates expertise from across different institutions and positions and developing training programmes that address a variety of needs. Various contextual factors influence the successful implementation of the new form of care and must be considered as important components. In general, aspects like efficient and sustainable solutions of central clinical challenges, low adaptation effort, different professional backgrounds using the standard, functioning and reliable technical support, as well as effects beyond the implemented path, should be considered in future implementation projects. Broad implementation and further development of the path must be meaningfully integrated into existing routines of the medical personnel, considering and balancing technological, organisational and economic aspects while expanding rather than restricting autonomy of clinical action.
