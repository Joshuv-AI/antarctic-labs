C1 pedicle screw placement risks in patients with ponticulus posticus: a CTA retrospective simulation study
Authors: Ping Wang, Han Meng, Lifeng Yu, Ruipeng Zhang, Xinyu Song, Tiantian She, Dan Wang, Yuehua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPonticulus posticus (POPO) may distort C1 surface landmarks for C1 pedicle screw (CPS) placement, raising concern for vertebral artery compromise (VAC). We aimed to establish a CTA-based workflow to identify POPO anatomies potentially suitable for CPS placement.MethodsThis single-center retrospective CTA simulation study included 141 subjects: 46 bilateral POPO, 65 unilateral POPO, and 30 pure non-POPO patients. CPS trajectories were primarily simulated using PedicleScrewSimulator. D1 and D2 were analyzed as supra-arch and infra-arch localization variables. Ten morphometric parameters were measured; key safety metrics included D3 and D5. Simulated CPS feasibility and VAC were evaluated for 2.5-, 3.0-, 3.5-, and 4.0-mm screws. A side was considered geometrically feasible when D5 ≥ screw radius (r), D6/2 ≥ r, and D7/2 ≥ r. Simulated VAC was defined as violation of D5 or D7/2 clearance. Outcomes were analyzed using patient-clustered generalized estimating equations.ResultsD1 and the D1-D2 shift differed markedly among groups (p < 0.001). D5 was smaller in bilateral POPO (2.93 ± 0.99 mm vs. 3.44 ± 1.14 mm vs. 3.81 ± 1.13 mm; p < 0.001). D3 did not differ significantly (p = 0.211). At 3.5 mm, overall simulated feasibility was 96.0%. Actual POPO sides showed 93.5% simulated feasibility and 6.5% simulated VAC, versus 99.2% and 0.8% in regular sides. In actual POPO sides, increasing diameter from 2.5 to 4.0 mm reduced simulated feasibility from 98.7% to 85.6% and increased VAC from 1.3% to 14.4%. The exploratory D3/D5 rule (D3 ≥ 3.4 mm and D5 ≥ 2.0 mm) achieved 88.4% sensitivity, 100% specificity, and 100% positive predictive value for avoiding simulated 3.5-mm VAC. Reliability for the four remeasured variables ranged from 0.702 to 0.799.ConclusionsCTA-based D3 and D5 thresholds can identify POPO anatomies potentially suitable for CPS placement, but simulation results reflect planning feasibility rather than validated operative safety. The D3 ≥ 3.4 mm and D5 ≥ 2.0 mm rule may serve as a conservative rule-in screening tool, pending clinical validation. POPO should not automatically preclude consideration of CPS but should trigger side-specific CTA analysis and conservative screw-diameter selection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-07046-1.

Introduction

C1 pedicle screw (CPS) placement is challenging during posterior atlantoaxial stabilization, [1, 2] although it is one of the strongest methods of posterior fixation [3–5]. The placement of CPS is considered a risky procedure because the vertebral artery, spinal canal, and limited osseous corridors converge at the atlas [2, 6–8]. While C1 lateral mass screws remain predominant, CPS offer distinct advantages in selected situations, including high-riding vertebral arteries, deformity correction, and limited working space. [2, 9, 10] CPS provide the most rigid posterior fixation and can reduce hardware bulk and blood loss compared with alternatives.

However, the ponticulus posticus (POPO), a common dorsal arch variant in which the arcuate ossifies to form a partial or complete bony bridge over the vertebral artery groove (VAG) [8, 11–13], distorts surface landmarks and may narrow safe corridors, increasing vertebral artery compromise (VAC) risk. Several authors therefore recommend avoiding CPS when POPO is present. [6, 14, 15] POPO has a reported pooled prevalence of 4.4% to 30.2%, with higher incidence in African and females [11]. In addition, POPO has complete/partial and unilateral/bilateral morphology [8], which may influence operative decisions. Given this high prevalence and morphological patterns, surgeons would encounter POPO during preoperative planning. Current literature provides conflicting guidance, leading many surgeons to categorically avoid CPS in POPO cases regardless of severity [16]. However, the key gap is whether CT angiography (CTA) derived measurements can triage side-specific CPS feasibility in POPO, rather than treating POPO as a uniform contraindication.

Yet CPS may be advantageous or necessary in specific scenarios, and small case series report CPS placement could be also applied in POPO cases [17, 18]. Nevertheless, current practice relies on subjective, non-standardized CT inspection, and no validated preoperative algorithm exists to investigate which POPO patients can safely undergo CPS fixation. CTA offers thin-slice imaging with simultaneous visualization of bony anatomy and arterial structures and has been recommended as a routine preoperative examination prior to C1 instrumentation [8]. CTA data thus provide the information necessary to assess both bones and arteries [8, 9, 18, 19].

The aim of this study was to establish CTA-based morphometric criteria for CPS simulated placement triage in POPO patients, by comparing morphometric parameters between POPO and regular anatomy, evaluating how these parameters influence CPS feasibility and simulated VAC, and developing a preoperative triage algorithm.

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective CTA-based simulation study was approved by the institutional review board. The simulation approach was chosen because it allows systematic evaluation of screw placement across a large cohort without exposing patients to surgical risk, consistent with methodology used in prior imaging-based feasibility studies for spinal instrumentation.

Patient selection and imaging

We screened all head and neck CTA scans performed between January 2020 and December 2022. Exclusion criteria were (1) C1 fractures, tumors, or infections; (2) congenital malformations; (3) prior C1 surgery; and (4) age under 18 years. The CTA images were from GE Discovery CT750 HD and followed the parameters: slice thickness (0.625 mm), slice increment of 0.625 mm, and reconstruction using STANDARD kernel. Contrast enhanced head-and-neck CTA acquired in the arterial phase using a bolus-tracking trigger, and additional CTA scanning parameters could be referred from the previous studies [8, 19].

All eligible POPO cases with radiographic evidence were included, and a randomly selected control group of patients without POPO (n = 30), matched for age and sex, were also adopted for comparison. This enriched design supported morphometric comparison and rule development, but it distorts the underlying prevalence, event rates, and the resulting specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Accordingly, PPV and NPV are cohort dependent and should be interpreted as conditional on this enriched cohort rather than as clinical predictive values until tested in an unenriched, consecutive CTA cohort. After exclusions, a cohort of 111 POPO-involved subjects and 30 non-POPO controls were adopted for analysis.

POPO was classified on CTA according to morphology and laterality. Morphologically, POPO was categorized as complete or partial based on the presence of a fully ossified versus incompletely ossified posterior arch bridge [8]. At the patient level, cases were classified as bilateral POPO, unilateral POPO, or non-POPO. At the side level, each C1 side was evaluated independently according to the actual presence and morphology of POPO on that side. Therefore, the side-level comparisons were interpreted as anatomic morphometric comparisons rather than an independent case-control analysis.

CPS risk assessment

CPS placement risk was primarily assessed in 3D Slicer (v5.9.0) using the PedicleScrewSimulator extension [20]. First, the region of interest at C1 was selected (Fig. 1A), after which the screw model and insertion point were defined (Fig. 1B). The planned CPS trajectory was then iteratively adjusted by the evaluator in multiplanar views to identify the largest feasible C1 pedicle screw corridor (Fig. 1C). Trajectory optimization was performed by a trained radiologist (with dedicated imaging experience) using a standardized objective: maximize the screw to VAC while keeping the inner cortical boundary intact. The evaluator was blinded to the study hypothesis and to the side-level feasibility outcome during optimization but was necessarily aware of POPO status because it is visible on CTA. Multiple candidate trajectories were attempted per side, and the corridor yielding the largest clearance was retained. A1 and A2 were used as orientation references during trajectory adjustment. The reader who optimized the trajectory also measured D5 along the final path; to limit single-reader bias, the four osseous/VAG-height parameters were independently remeasured by additional readers for the reliability subset (see below), and broader multi-reader replanning is identified as future work.

Optimized CPS placement was based on two key criteria: (1) the vertebral artery must not be compromised (Fig. 1D), and (2) to protect the spinal cord, the inner cortex of the vertebral foramen had to remain intact. Inner-cortex violation was defined as simulated trajectory breach of the inner cortical boundary of the C1 posterior arch/pedicle corridor (Fig. 1E). Detailed step-by-step assessment using PedicleScrewSimulator could be found in the Supplementary file.

During the initial simulation analysis, three major risks (Fig. 1F-H) were frequently identified when the traditional 3-mm supra-arch localization approach was used in the presence of POPO anatomy (Fig. 2, red arrow). In this approach, the CPS entry point was measured from the superior margin of the VAG. To reduce the risk of VAC, an infra-arch approach was adopted (Fig. 2, green arrow), in which the CPS entry point was measured from the inferior margin of the VAG.

After the primary CPS insertion risk assessment, key parameters for CPS simulation were measured using Horos (v4.0.1). In the multiplanar reconstruction view, C1 was first identified, and the coronal plane was adjusted so that both C1 transverse processes were aligned at the same level before measurement.

To further quantify the risks of CPS insertion in POPO anatomy, ten parameters were measured per side, comprising eight linear distances and two angles (Fig. 3). D1 was the distance from the ideal entry point to the upper margin of the VAG; D2, distance from the ideal entry point to the lower margin of the VAG; D3, minimal height of the VAG; D4, planned screw trajectory length to the anterior cortex; D5, minimal distance from the planned trajectory centerline to the vertebral artery wall; D6, C1 pedicle width; D7, minimal width between the vertebral foramen and transverse foramen; D8, distance from the entry point to the midsagittal line; A1, medial (axial) insertion angle; and A2, cephalad (sagittal) insertion angle.

Screw placement assessment

To assess measurement reproducibility, three readers (two junior residents, one senior radiologist) independently measured pedicle width, pedicle height, VAG medial 1/3 height, and VAG lateral 1/3 height for all selected subjects in a blinded, randomized manner. Two readers repeated the measurements to assess intra-rater reliability. This reliability subset addressed selected osseous corridor and VAG height measurements, including D6 and D3-related VAG height assessment, but did not separately quantify D5, D7, A1, or A2 reproducibility. Because the proposed rule depends heavily on D5 and the planning discussion depends on D7, A1, and A2, the D3/D5 rule is presented as an exploratory planning rather than a validated screening test until formal inter-rater and intra-rater reliability for these decision-critical variables is established.

CPS feasibility was assessed using side-level morphometric measurements from preoperative CTA. For each simulated screw diameter, the screw radius (r) was used to define anatomic feasibility. A side was considered geometrically possible in idealized simulation when all three clearance criteria were satisfied: D5 ≥ r, D6/2 ≥ r, and D7/2 ≥ r. This definition assumes an ideal planned trajectory and does not incorporate cortical thickness variation, drill wobble, drill skive, navigation registration error, or surgeon-selected execution margins. To address this, we performed an execution margin sensitivity analysis at the 3.5-mm diameter by tightening each clearance criterion to D5 ≥ r + m, D6/2 ≥ r + m, and D7/2 ≥ r + m for execution margins of m = 0.5 mm and m = 1.0 mm.

D4 represented the planned screw trajectory length to the anterior cortex. Bicortical purchase at the anterior arch was allowed during planning when it could be achieved without compromising the vertebral artery corridor or inner cortical boundary, but it was not required at the expense of vascular clearance. D6 and D7 were supplementary corridor measurements assessed relative to the intended screw diameter. A1 was used as an angular guide during trajectory planning: more negative values indicated a more lateral direction, whereas larger positive values suggested a more medial trajectory and a higher likelihood of inner cortex breach. A1 was also considered when avoiding excessive lateral angulation near the transverse foramen region. Internal carotid artery and spinal cord compromise were considered qualitatively during planning, but they were not prespecified as independent quantitative endpoints in this retrospective CTA study. Therefore, the formal recorded endpoints were geometric feasibility and simulated VAC.

Simulated VAC was defined as violation of D5 clearance and was additionally flagged when the lateral osseous corridor (D7/2) was violated. D5 directly represented centerline to vertebral artery wall clearance. D7 represented the osseous corridor bounded medially by the vertebral foramen and laterally by the transverse foramen/vertebral artery region, because D7 did not include the vertebral artery lumen itself, a D7/2 violation is reported as an osseous corridor violation that serves as a conservative surrogate flag for proximity to the vertebral artery region, rather than as a direct VAC measurement. We simulated four screw diameters (2.5, 3.0, 3.5, and 4.0 mm), considering 3.5 mm the primary diameter of interest. The 3.0-mm diameter corresponds to commercially available small/rescue trajectories, the 2.5-mm diameter was included as a lower bound rescue/sensitivity option for severely constrained corridors anatomy, and the 4.0 mm diameter was modeled as an upper bound sensitivity test of maximal diameter rather than a routinely recommended choice. These simulated diameters are not presented as equally clinically acceptable; diameter selection should balance fixation strength against vascular clearance on a side-specific basis.

Decision rule development

To translate findings into a potential planning tool, D3/D5 screening thresholds were evaluated as exploratory, data-derived rules rather than prespecified clinical cutoffs. For the primary 3.5-mm analysis, the final conservative rule-in threshold was D3 ≥ 3.4 mm and D5 ≥ 2.0 mm. A stricter D3 threshold of 3.7 mm was retained as a sensitivity analysis. Sides that were geometrically feasible but failed the D3/D5 threshold were classified as borderline rather than automatically unsuitable.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were summarized as mean ± SD or median, and categorical variables as frequencies with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Inter-rater reliability was quantified using a two-way random-effects intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC (2,1)] for the four remeasured variables, with agreement further assessed via Bland-Altman analysis.

Because both sides could contribute data from the same patient, side-level continuous outcomes were compared among bilateral POPO, unilateral POPO, and pure non-POPO groups using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with patient ID as the clustering variable, an exchangeable working correlation structure, Gaussian family, identity link, and robust sandwich standard errors. D3 and D5 were prespecified as the primary morphometric endpoints; the remaining parameters (D1, D2, D4, D6, D7, D8, A1, and A2) were prespecified as exploratory. Pairwise contrasts were extracted from the GEE models and interpreted as exploratory because multiple morphometric outcomes were evaluated, with primary inference focused on D3 and D5. To address multiplicity among the exploratory contrasts, p values for the non-primary parameters were treated as hypothesis generating, exact p values with confidence intervals were reported for the key contrasts, and a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate perspective was adopted when interpreting the secondary comparisons. For binary outcomes, binomial-family GEE models with a logit link were used when model estimation was stable. When sparse or perfect cells were present, odds ratios were not estimated, and exact descriptive results were emphasized.

The D3/D5 decision rule was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value with exact 95% CI. Patient-level bootstrap resampling was used to assess the stability of rule performance estimates. Multivariable logistic regression was not performed because sparse events and perfect separation limited reliable model estimation. To address the concern that the D3/D5 rule was derived and tested in the same dataset, we added internal validation using leave-one-patient-out cross-validation and a patient clustered bootstrap optimism corrected estimate. All analyses were performed in Python 3.11.5, with two-sided p < 0.05 considered statistically significant for primary comparisons and exploratory for secondary morphometric contrasts.

Results

Cohort characteristics

POPO was present in 78.7% of patients, with 31.2% having bilateral and 47.5% having unilateral variants. At the side level, 92 sides were from bilateral POPO subjects, 130 from unilateral POPO subjects, and 60 from pure non-POPO subjects (Table 1). Age and sex distribution were comparable among the three groups.

POPO denominators and terminology were based on patient level groups (bilateral POPO, unilateral POPO, pure non-POPO); side-level groups comprising 157 actual POPO sides and 125 regular sides (65 contralateral regular sides from unilateral POPO patients plus 60 pure non-POPO sides); and broader POPO patient anatomy (all sides of POPO-involved patients).

Approach localization and morphometric comparison

The three principal risks associated with CPS placement are VAC, internal carotid artery compromise, and spinal cord compromise. Potential internal carotid artery and spinal cord risks were considered qualitatively during trajectory optimization through review of the screw path, inner cortical boundary, and insertion angles; however, they were not recorded as independent quantitative endpoints in this retrospective simulation. The formal quantitative endpoints of the present study were geometric feasibility and simulated VAC.

Diameter specific feasibility now uses complete data denominators. Primary exploratory assessment of the supra-arch approach performed across all 282 C1 sides using a 3.0 mm localization threshold and the same D5/D7-based clearance assumptions applied to the infra-arch analysis. Under these assumptions, the traditional supra-arch approach showed a 30.6% simulated prevalence of VAC. This exploratory finding motivated, but was kept separate from, the prespecified infra-arch trajectory algorithm that forms the basis of the feasibility and decision-rule analyses.

Supra-arch and infra-arch localization should be considered separately (Fig. 4). D1, representing supra-arch localization, differed markedly among groups, with the greatest value in bilateral POPO sides, followed by unilateral POPO and pure non-POPO sides (6.17 ± 2.40 mm vs. 4.02 ± 1.87 mm vs. 2.97 ± 1.12 mm; p < 0.001). In contrast, D2, representing infra-arch localization, showed only a small absolute difference among groups (1.86 ± 0.63 mm vs. 1.60 ± 0.48 mm vs. 1.59 ± 0.36 mm; p = 0.012). Consequently, the D1-D2 shift was significantly enlarged in POPO anatomy, especially in bilateral POPO sides (p < 0.001), supporting the need to distinguish supra-arch risk from infra-arch CPS planning.

Morphometric comparison

The reliability subset showed moderate to good agreement, with ICC(2,1) values ranging from 0.702 to 0.799 for the four remeasured variables. Bland-Altman mean bias (95% limits of agreement; Rater 2 minus Rater 1) was − 0.19 mm (-2.54 to 2.16) for pedicle width, 0.31 mm (-1.16 to 1.78) for pedicle height, 0.01 mm (-1.78 to 1.80) for VAG medial one-third height, and 0.12 mm (-1.70 to 1.94) for VAG lateral one-third height.

Among the infra-arch planning parameters, D5 remained the key clearance variable. D5 was significantly smaller in bilateral POPO sides than in unilateral POPO and pure non-POPO sides (2.93 ± 0.99 mm vs. 3.44 ± 1.14 mm vs. 3.81 ± 1.13 mm; p < 0.001, Fig. 4). In unilateral POPO patients, paired sensitivity analysis showed that actual POPO sides had smaller D5 than contralateral regular sides (3.25 ± 1.20 mm vs. 3.63 ± 1.05 mm, p = 0.018), whereas D3 was similar (4.74 ± 1.10 mm vs. 4.70 ± 1.02 mm, p = 0.708). When actual POPO sides were combined, D5 was also significantly reduced compared with pure non-POPO sides (3.24 ± 1.11 mm vs. 3.81 ± 1.13 mm; p = 0.004). D3 did not differ significantly as a continuous variable (p = 0.211), but actual POPO sides more frequently fell below the final D3/D5 rule threshold. The final D3 ≥ 3.4 mm and D5 ≥ 2.0 mm rule was satisfied in 77.7% of POPO sides, 87.7% of contralateral regular sides, and 95.0% of pure non-POPO sides.

Simulated CPS feasibility, safety, and potential decision rule

At the standard 3.5 mm diameter, overall simulated CPS feasibility was 96.0% (Table 1). Among actual POPO sides with complete 3.5-mm simulation data, feasibility was 93.5% (143/153) and simulated VAC was 6.5% (10/153). Among regular sides, including contralateral regular sides from unilateral POPO patients and pure non-POPO sides, feasibility was 99.2% (124/125) and simulated compromise was 0.8% (1/125). This terminology separates actual side-level POPO anatomy from broader POPO-patient anatomy. Applying a 0.5 mm margin reduced simulated feasibility from 96.0% to 82.4% overall, from 93.5% to 77.8% in actual POPO sides, and from 99.2% to 88.0% in regular sides; a 1.0-mm margin further reduced feasibility to 68.0% overall, 58.2% in actual POPO sides, and 80.0% in regular sides (Table 2). This confirms that idealized geometric feasibility overstates the corridor available once a realistic execution buffer is imposed, and that POPO sides are disproportionately sensitive to the applied margin (Fig. 5A).

Internal carotid artery and spinal cord compromise were not analyzed as formal event-count outcomes. These risks were considered qualitatively during trajectory planning, whereas the quantitative analysis focused on geometric feasibility and simulated VAC.

Screw diameter strongly influenced risk. In POPO anatomy, defined as both sides of bilateral POPO patients and both sides of unilateral POPO patients with complete data, feasibility decreased from 99.1% at 2.5 mm to 97.2% at 3.0 mm, 95.0% at 3.5 mm, and 88.1% at 4.0 mm (Fig. 5B). Over the same range, simulated VAC increased from 0.9% to 2.8%, 5.0%, and 11.9%, respectively. Pure non-POPO anatomy maintained 100.0% feasibility and zero compromise up to 3.5 mm. These findings indicate that POPO anatomy increases risk but does not justify categorical avoidance of CPS; rather, risk is diameter-dependent and can be assessed by CTA-based infra-arch planning.

The exploratory D3/D5 decision rule functioned as a conservative rule-in tool for simulated 3.5-mm CPS placement (Table 3). Using D3 ≥ 3.4 mm and D5 ≥ 2.0 mm, the rule achieved 88.4% sensitivity (95% CI, 83.9–92.0), 100% specificity (95% CI, 71.5–100), 100% positive predictive value (95% CI, 98.4–100), and 26.2% negative predictive value (95% CI, 13.9–42.0). The stricter D3 ≥ 3.7 mm and D5 ≥ 2.0 mm sensitivity analysis achieved 83.5% sensitivity (95% CI, 78.5–87.8), 100% specificity (95% CI, 71.5–100), 100% positive predictive value (95% CI, 98.4–100), and 20.0% negative predictive value (95% CI, 10.4–33.0). Perfect specificity and positive predictive value should be interpreted cautiously because simulated breach events were sparse.

Discussion

The placement of CPS in POPO anatomy requires great attention due to the surrounding neurovascular structures [6, 8, 15]. VAC could lead to life-threatening complications [21]. This study demonstrated that a CTA-based morphometric screening workflow can identify POPO patients who remain potentially suitable for CPS placement in simulation. Moreover, it showed that POPO should not be treated as a categorical contraindication to CPS. [17, 18]

Several studies have shown that the pullout strength for CPS is superior to lateral mass screws, and therefore they offer better fixation. [4, 22, 23] However, surgeons often prefer lateral mass screws because of the lower margin of error and lower risk of neurovascular damage [3, 21–24]. POPO has often been treated as a categorical contraindication to CPS, a conclusion derived from limited evidence [6]. Other recommendations merely advise surgeons to exercise caution when confronting POPO anatomy [15]. Our study systematically evaluated instrumentation related factors and found that D1, D2, the D1-D2 shift, and D5 differed among groups, whereas D3 did not differ significantly as a continuous variable. The key finding was that POPO reduces the distance between the vertebral artery and the optimized screw path, making D5 the principal clearance variable.

A supra-arch paradigm anchored to the upper VAG margin becomes unreliable in POPO anatomy because D1 is substantially altered. The lower VAG margin/D2 planning strategy may provide a more stable reference for identifying a posteroinferior entry point beneath the arch (infra-arch approach), but this remains a simulation-based planning inference rather than proof of superior clinical safety [25]. Additionally, the infra-arch approach offers the advantage of protecting the venous sinus plexus, which drains venous blood from intracranial, occipital, and spinal canal structures [8, 26–28], although clinical validation is required to confirm effects on bleeding, C2 nerve root irritation, medial breach, and VAC. Using this workflow in 3D Slicer, we identified the infra-arch trajectory and standardized measurement of D3 and D5.

Notably, screw placement may remain feasible even when D3 is relatively small. Huang et al. reported successful placement of 3.5 to 4.0 mm screws despite D3 measurements < 4.0 mm [29]. This feasibility may reflect the structural integrity of the cortical wall and the ability of meticulous drilling technique to maintain the planned corridor without compromising the vertebral artery wall. Taken together, these observations support the use of an infra-arch, measurement-guided protocol while emphasizing that morphometric thresholds should be interpreted in the context of surgical technique and local bony quality.

Treating POPO as a blanket contraindication oversimplifies the tradeoffs involved. Lateral mass screws, although widely accepted, can provoke C2 nerve root irritation [27, 30, 31] and may threaten a high-riding proximal V3 segment. By contrast, CPS placed in POPO anatomy interacts with the distal V3 segment. In this simulation study, actual POPO sides showed 93.5% feasibility at the standard 3.5-mm diameter, while broader POPO-patient anatomy showed 95.0% feasibility. These findings support smaller clinical series reporting CPS placement in carefully selected cases, but they should be interpreted as planning evidence rather than clinical proof of safety.

We observed a clear dose-response gradient. In POPO anatomy, reducing screw diameter from 3.5 to 3.0 mm preserved feasibility while reducing the simulated VAC rate from 5.0% to 2.8%. Conversely, increasing diameter to 4.0 mm reduced feasibility to 88.1% and increased compromise to 11.9%. In actual POPO sides, the corresponding 3.5-mm and 4.0-mm compromise rates were 6.5% and 14.4%, respectively. These findings challenge the reflex to maximize diameter for theoretical biomechanical advantage; the incremental fixation benefit of upsizing is frequently outweighed by the disproportionate escalation in vascular risk. Notably, moderate VAG breach may not invariably result in VAC. In 42.9% of C1 anatomies, the vertebral artery does not directly contact the bony groove, providing an inherent safety buffer [8].

With advances in drilling and insertion assistance technologies, image-guided navigation has improved instrumentation accuracy substantially. [26, 30, 32, 33] Robot-guided screw placement further increases accuracy [34], and virtual twin augmented reality guidance can achieve excellent accuracy without radiation exposure [35]. However, planning accuracy and execution accuracy should be analytically separated. Given these technological advances, more precise preoperative planning is now achievable, prompting reconsideration of C1 instrumentation in POPO patients. Moreover, preoperative planning and practice deviation was noticed [36, 37], which intraoperative modifications would be still necessary when confronting POPO anatomy. The present simulation evaluated idealized CTA-based planning, but navigation registration error, drill skive, and intraoperative modification may still change the final screw path. The safety-margin and borderline categories were intended to acknowledge this uncertainty, and recent iCT navigation [37] and upper cervical planning studies [36, 38–40] support separating preoperative planning error from intraoperative execution error.

The exploratory decision rule (D3 ≥ 3.4 mm AND D5 ≥ 2.0 mm) achieved 88.4% sensitivity and 100% specificity for avoiding simulated 3.5-mm VAC. This performance profile reflects an intentionally rule-in design: a positive result identifies anatomy that appears suitable in simulation, but a negative result should not automatically forbid CPS. Instead, failing either threshold should prompt adaptation, including smaller-diameter screws, trajectory modification, C1 lateral mass fixation, or enhanced intraoperative verification. Side-specific screening is essential because contralateral regular sides in unilateral POPO patients are not identical to either actual POPO sides or pure non-POPO controls.

Limitations

This retrospective CTA simulation lacks clinical outcomes; it evaluates planning feasibility only. Straight-line modeling ignores drill skive, venous plexus bleeding, and real-time adjustments. The intentionally enriched cohort was designed for morphometric comparison and rule development, not prevalence estimation. Single-center, all-Asian demographics may limit generalizability; thresholds may require recalibration in broader populations. Results regarding the complete/partial POPO morphology characteristics were not fully explained. VACs were sparse and confined to POPO, precluding multivariable adjustment and making perfect specificity/positive predictive value estimates uncertain. Internal carotid artery and spinal cord compromise were not separately adjudicated as quantitative simulated endpoints. Although these risks were considered during planning, a retrospective CTA-based simulation cannot reliably determine actual internal carotid artery or spinal cord injury risk, especially because limited inner cortex breach may still be clinically tolerated with an in-out-in technique [8, 41]. Reliability was quantified only for selected parameters and simulations-related; broader metric reproducibility and training curves remain to be studied. Further clinical validation is required before the D3/D5 rule can be used as anything more than a conservative screening aid.

Conclusions

Preoperative CTA-based morphometric assessment using D3 and D5 can help identify POPO patients who may potentially be suitable for CPS placement in simulation. The exploratory decision rule (D3 ≥ 3.4 mm AND D5 ≥ 2.0 mm) achieved 100% specificity and 100% positive predictive value for avoiding simulated 3.5-mm VAC, with wide CIs caused by sparse events. Although POPO anatomy imposes measurable constraints, particularly reduced D5 clearance, most actual POPO sides remained geometrically feasible at the standard 3.5-mm diameter.

These findings suggest that POPO should not automatically preclude consideration of CPS in preoperative planning but should trigger side-specific CTA analysis and conservative screw diameter selection rather than reflexive avoidance; because there were no operative screws, no fluoroscopy/navigation deviations, and no neurovascular outcomes, this remains a planning inference rather than proof of operative safety. Instead, side-specific CTA screening, D3/D5 threshold assessment, and conservative screw-diameter selection may guide individualized fixation strategy, pending clinical validation.
