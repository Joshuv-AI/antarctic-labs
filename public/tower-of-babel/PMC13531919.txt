Karacoline attenuates sepsis-induced acute lung injury by suppressing apoptosis via PPARγ-associated inhibition of JNK/ERK MAPK signaling
Authors: Zihan Lei, Yuhong He, Yuxuan Wang, Min Yuan, Xue Chen, Jingxue Qin, Xiaojing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis-induced acute lung injury (SALI) is a fatal complication of sepsis with limited therapeutic options. Karacoline, a diterpenoid alkaloid from Aconitum, shows potential protective effects, but its mechanism in SALI remains unclear.MethodsNetwork pharmacology and WGCNA identified core targets and pathways of Karacoline, with molecular docking confirming its binding to PPARγ. A cecal ligation and puncture mouse model was used to assess lung pathology, edema, barrier dysfunction, inflammatory cytokines, and apoptosis. In vitro, LPS-stimulated MH-S cells were used to evaluate apoptosis, ROS generation, mitochondrial function, and PPARγ/MAPK signaling. The PPARγ antagonist GW9662 was used to verify the involvement of PPARγ in the protective effects of Karacoline.ResultsKaracoline significantly alleviated lung injury, reduced the W/D ratio, decreased total protein and albumin levels in BALF, and suppressed IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. Mechanistically, Karacoline upregulated PPARγ expression and inhibited ERK1/2 and JNK phosphorylation, thereby contributing to the reduction of pulmonary cell apoptosis. In vitro, Karacoline limited ROS generation, maintained mitochondrial membrane potential, and reduced LPS-induced apoptosis in MH-S cells. Moreover, GW9662 weakened the anti-apoptotic effect of Karacoline and partially reversed its inhibition of JNK/ERK activation, further supporting the involvement of PPARγ in Karacoline-mediated inhibition of JNK/ERK MAPK signaling and apoptosis.ConclusionKaracoline protects against SALI by suppressing mitochondrial apoptosis, an effect closely associated with PPARγ-related inhibition of JNK/ERK MAPK signaling. These findings suggest that Karacoline may be a candidate compound worthy of further validation for sepsis-induced lung injury intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03767-3.Keywords: Karacoline, Sepsis-induced acute lung injury, PPARγ, MAPK signaling, Apoptosis, Network pharmacology

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening multi-organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Globally, nearly 50 million sepsis cases occur annually, with a mortality rate of ~ 26.7% [2, 3]. Recent studies have further revealed that sepsis has severe effects on multiple organs, including the liver, kidney, lung, and brain [4, 5]. The lung is one of the organs most susceptible to sepsis, and acute lung injury (ALI) often develops during the early phase of sepsis. SALI is characterized by disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier and excessive cytokine release, ultimately leading to respiratory failure [6], contributing to both high short-term mortality and long-term functional impairment [7, 8]. Current treatments mainly rely on antibiotics and mechanical ventilation, but the lack of effective targeted interventions remains a critical barrier to improved outcomes [9]. Hence, identifying new therapeutic agents for SALI is urgently needed [10].

Natural small molecules derived from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) have shown promise in mitigating SALI by suppressing inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune dysregulation [11, 12]. Aconitum carmichaelii (Fuzi), a processed root used in TCM for over 2,000 years, exhibits anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and immunomodulatory effects. Clinical studies suggest that Fuzi-containing formulations, such as Rhei and aconiti praeparatae decoction alleviate pulmonary edema and improve respiratory function in SALI patients, although the active components and precise mechanisms remain unclear [13]. Karacoline, a major diterpenoid alkaloid from Fuzi, has demonstrated anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects in other models, such as reducing extracellular matrix degradation via NF-κB signaling in intervertebral disc degeneration [14, 15]. Although Karacoline remains insufficiently studied in sepsis-related organ injury, its reported anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic activities suggest potential relevance to SALI, where inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis are key pathological features. Therefore, we investigated whether Karacoline could protect against SALI and explored its underlying mechanisms.

Excessive inflammation and apoptosis are central to SALI pathogenesis. Overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) during sepsis disrupts mitochondrial integrity, triggers cytochrome c and apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF) release, and exacerbates lung tissue damage [16, 17]. Suppressing apoptosis is therefore a promising strategy to mitigate lung injury. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) is a nuclear transcription factor with potent anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects [18]. Activation of PPARγ attenuates LPS-induced pulmonary inflammation, while its deficiency amplifies cytokine release [19, 20]. In contrast, aberrant activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway promotes inflammation and apoptosis, aggravating lung injury [21].

Network pharmacology, which integrates systems biology, polypharmacology, and bioinformatics, provides a systematic approach to deciphering drug–disease interactions [22]. By constructing “compound–target–pathway–disease” networks and combining molecular docking with experimental validation, it enables the discovery of multi-target therapeutic mechanisms [23]. For instance, hydroxysafflor yellow A was identified via network pharmacology as a protective agent against sepsis-induced multi-organ injury through the JAK2/STAT1 pathway [24].

In this study, we integrated network pharmacology, molecular docking, in vivo, and in vitro experiments to investigate the protective effects and mechanisms of Karacoline against SALI. We focused on its ability to modulate PPARγ and MAPK signaling to suppress apoptosis, providing theoretical and experimental evidence for its potential as a novel therapeutic agent. Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, weighing 20–25 g) were purchased from the Animal Experiment Center of Wuhan University. Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (12-h light/dark cycles, 23 ± 2 °C, and humidity of 45–55%) with free access to food and water. All animal care and experimental procedures complied with the ethical guidelines approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of Wuhan University.

Sepsis model and treatment

A classical cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) method was employed to induce sepsis. Briefly, male C57BL/6 mice were fasted for 12 h before surgery and anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg). Under aseptic conditions, a 1.5-cm incision was made along the midline of the abdomen to expose the cecum. The distal half of the cecum was gently ligated using a 4 − 0 silk suture and punctured once using a 22-gauge needle. A small amount of cecal content was extruded, and the cecum was then returned to the abdomen. The incision was closed in layers. Sham-operated mice underwent only laparotomy without ligation or puncture. Immediately following surgery, mice received a subcutaneous injection of pre-warmed saline (37 °C, 1 mL/mouse) for fluid resuscitation and were placed on a warming pad for recovery.

To determine the optimal in vivo dose of Karacoline, a preliminary dose-screening experiment was performed in CLP-induced septic mice. Mice were administered Karacoline by intragastric gavage at doses of 10, 20, or 40 mg/kg, and postoperative survival and treatment tolerance were monitored. Finally, 20 mg/kg was selected as the dose for subsequent in vivo experiments. Mice in the Karacoline treatment group received Karacoline (20 mg/kg) by intragastric administration at 1 h before and 12 h after surgery [14, 25]. Animal survival, behavior, and physiological indicators were monitored postoperatively.

Drug preparation

Karacoline (CAS: 39089-30-0, molecular formula C₂₂H₃₅NO₄, molecular weight 377.52 g/mol, purity > 98%) was purchased from Chengdu Alfa Biotechnology Co. Ltd (AB0974). Karacoline stock solution was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) immediately before use, ensuring a final DMSO concentration of less than 0.1%. All chemicals were of analytical grade, and purity was confirmed by the manufacturer’s analysis.

Screening active compounds and targets of Fuzi

Initially, 21 active components of Fuzi were identified from the TCMSP database (https://www.tcmsp-e.com), using criteria of oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug-likeness (DL) ≥ 0.18. Subsequently, SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch/) was employed to further refine these compounds, selecting 11 active ingredients based on high gastrointestinal absorption, drug-likeness (≥ 2 “Yes”), and blood-brain barrier permeability. Finally, potential molecular targets for these 11 compounds were predicted using the SwissTargetPrediction (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) and PharmMapper (https://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/) databases.

Identification of SALI-related therapeutic targets

Potential therapeutic targets for SALI were obtained by intersecting gene lists retrieved from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM (https://omim.org/), and DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org/) using the keywords “sepsis” and “acute lung injury.”

Differential gene expression analysis

The GSE243066 dataset, comprising 34 disease samples and 15 healthy controls, was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the “limma differential analysis tool” available from Sangerbox (http://sangerbox.com/home.html), with a screening criterion of |logFC| >1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was performed using R software. After hierarchical clustering to detect outlier samples, a soft threshold power (β = 28) was calculated using the pickSoftThreshold function. Gene clustering was conducted with a topological overlap matrix (TOM), and modules were delineated using the cutreeDynamic algorithm.

Enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of 70 overlapping DEGs and therapeutic targets were conducted using the Metascape database (https://metascape.org/). Results were visualized using the Bioinformatics online platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/).

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction

The PPI network of the 70 overlapping targets was constructed using STRING (https://cn.string-db.org) and visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10.1). Core genes were screened and visualized using Cytoscape plugins cytoHubba, cytoNCA, and MCODE.

Molecular docking

Protein structures were obtained from the Alphafold2 database (https://alphafold.com/) and compound structures from PubChem. Protein preprocessing was performed using PyMol (version 2.4), and docking files (PDBQT) were generated with AutoDock Tools (version 1.5.6). Docking simulations were conducted using AutoDock Vina (version 1.1.2), generating the 10 best docking poses, with the optimal pose selected based on lowest binding energy and highest cluster frequency. Docking results were expressed as mean binding energy ± standard deviation from three independent runs and visualized using PLIP (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/) and PyMol software.

Western blot

Protein extracts from lung tissues or MH-S were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with protein-free rapid blocking buffer for 1 h, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (PPARγ, cleaved-caspase3, Bcl-2, BAX, p-JNK, p-ERK, p-p38, GAPDH), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were visualized with ECL substrate and analyzed using ImageJ software [26, 27].

Bronchoalveolar lavage

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected 24 h post-surgery and centrifuged (3,000 rpm, 10 min at 4 °C). The concentrations of albumin, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in BALF were determined using ELISA kits (Jonlnbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. The total protein concentration in BALF was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Biosharp, China), with absorbance measured at 562 nm. Finally, cytokine or protein concentrations were calculated based on standard curves [28].

Histological analysis (HE Staining)

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Pathological changes, including alveolar structure, inflammatory infiltration, and interstitial edema, were scored semi-quantitatively by investigators blinded to the experimental groups.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections (4 μm) underwent antigen retrieval, endogenous peroxidase blocking, and incubation with primary antibodies (Bcl-2, BAX, PPARγ, diluted 1:100–1:200). Sections were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, visualized with DAB substrate, counterstained with hematoxylin, and analyzed using ImageJ by investigators blinded to the group allocation.

TUNEL staining

Apoptotic cells in lung tissues were detected using TUNEL fluorescence staining kits (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DAPI staining labeled nuclei. Fluorescence microscopy images were analyzed using ImageJ by investigators blinded to the experimental groups.

Cell culture

MH-S cells (purchased from ATCC CRL-2019) were cultured in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. Cells were stimulated with LPS (1 µg/mL) for 24 h, with or without Karacoline (1.6 µM).

Cell viability assay

MH-S cells were seeded into a 96-well plate. Following treatment, reagents were added according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Solarbio) and incubated for an additional 2 h. Subsequently, the optical density (OD) value of each well was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader, and statistical analysis was performed.

ROS staining

ROS levels in MH-S cells were detected using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates. Following treatment, reagents were added according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Solarbio). Observations and imaging were performed using a fluorescence microscope (FITC channel), and fluorescence intensity was analyzed semi-quantitatively using ImageJ software.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1) assay

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) in MH-S cells was measured using the JC-1 probe. Following treatment according to experimental groups, reagents were added and subsequent procedures performed per the supplier’s protocol (Solarbio). Images were acquired under a fluorescence microscope, and the red/green fluorescence ratio was calculated using ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry

After cell processing, collect adherent and suspension cells and proceed with subsequent steps according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Elabscience). Detect cells using flow cytometry. Early apoptotic cells are Annexin V⁺/PI⁻, while late apoptotic cells are Annexin V⁺/PI⁺. Experimental results were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized using reverse transcription premix (Vazyme, China). RT-qPCR was performed on a CFX384 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA), with GAPDH used as the internal control. The primer sequences were as follows: PPARG, forward:5′-CTCCAAGAATACCAAAGTGCGA-3′, reverse: 5′-GCCTGATGCTTTATCCCCACA-3′ [29, 30].

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked, then incubated overnight with anti-PPARγ antibody at 4 °C. After washing, a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody was applied, and nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were acquired by confocal microscopy and analyzed for PPARγ expression.

Inhibitor treatment

To further evaluate the involvement of PPARγ, MH-S cells were treated with PPARγ antagonist GW9662. Cells were divided into the following groups: Control, LPS, LPS + Karacoline, LPS + GW9662, and LPS + Karacoline + GW9662. GW9662 was administered at 10 µM for 1 h before LPS stimulation. Apoptosis-related proteins and MAPK pathway activation were assessed by Western blotting.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Two-group comparisons utilized independent t-tests, while multiple-group comparisons employed one-way ANOVA followed by LSD or Tukey tests. Survival analyses were performed by Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests. P-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Identification of potential therapeutic targets of Fuzi in SALI

To investigate the active compounds and potential therapeutic targets of Fuzi in sepsis-induced acute lung injury (SALI), we first screened the active ingredients using the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) database. Twenty-one candidate compounds were identified based on oral bioavailability (OB ≥ 30%) and drug-likeness (DL ≥ 0.18). These compounds were further evaluated using the SwissADME platform, narrowing the list down to 11 active components with high gastrointestinal (GI) absorption, drug-like properties (fulfilling at least two drug-likeness criteria), and blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability. These 11 core active compounds were ranked based on their degree values obtained by Cytoscape analysis (Table 1).

Screening of differentially expressed genes and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

To identify critical gene modules involved in SALI, we conducted differential expression analysis and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) using the GSE243066 dataset retrieved from the GEO database. Initially, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear distinction between the disease and control samples (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, differential expression analysis was performed utilizing the limma package, resulting in the identification of 4,701 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 2,565 upregulated and 2,136 downregulated genes, under the criteria of |log₂Fold Change| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05. Volcano plots and heatmaps were employed to visually illustrate the significant differences in gene expression profiles between the two groups (Fig. 3B–C).

Based on the identified DEGs, WGCNA was employed to explore gene-disease associations. The optimal soft-threshold power (β = 28) was selected based on scale-free topology criteria (Fig. 3D). All DEGs were then hierarchically clustered into five distinct co-expression modules according to gene expression correlations (Fig. 3E). The topological overlap matrix (TOM) heatmap revealed significant intramodule gene connectivity, indicating robust clustering (Fig. 3G). Moreover, module-trait correlation analysis was performed to examine relationships between each module and the SALI phenotype (Fig. 3F). Results indicated that the grey module (MEgrey) exhibited the strongest negative correlation with SALI (R = – 0.86, P < 0.001), containing a total of 142 genes, suggesting that genes within this module likely play crucial roles in SALI pathogenesis.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses reveal core signaling pathways

To further explore the molecular mechanisms underlying Fuzi-mediated treatment of SALI, we intersected the 262 overlapping targets between Fuzi-predicted targets and SALI-related disease targets with the 4,701 DEGs identified from the GSE243066 dataset. This analysis yielded 70 shared DEGs (Fig. 4A), which are potentially critical for the therapeutic effects of Fuzi in SALI. These 70 genes were then subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. GO analysis indicated that, in terms of biological processes (BP), the target genes were primarily enriched in “inflammatory response,” “immune response,” “regulation of apoptosis,” and “chemokine-mediated signaling pathways.” Regarding cellular components (CC), the enriched categories included “external side of the plasma membrane,” “membrane raft,” and “cell-cell junction.” For molecular functions (MF), genes were mainly involved in “chemokine activity” and “cytokine receptor binding” (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that the intersected genes play a central role in regulating inflammation, apoptosis, and immune responses.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that these genes were significantly enriched in pathways such as the “MAPK signaling pathway,” “TNF signaling pathway,” “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction,” “NF-κB signaling pathway,” “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway,” as well as apoptosis and necroptosis processes (Fig. 4C–D). The constructed pathway–gene chord diagram (Fig. 4E) visualized interactions between key genes and signaling pathways, supporting enrichment analysis results indicating a critical role of the MAPK pathway in Fuzi-mediated modulation of SALI. Furthermore, the Sankey diagram illustrated a common link between the biological process of apoptosis and the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 4F).

Collectively, these GO and KEGG enrichment analyses highlight that the regulation of inflammatory responses, cell apoptosis, and MAPK signaling constitute pivotal mechanisms through which Fuzi alleviates SALI.

Given these network pharmacology results and previous reports indicating Fuzi’s critical role in apoptosis regulation [31–33], we further searched for apoptosis-related targets in the GeneCards database and filtered these targets based on their p-value. Intersection analysis with the previously identified eight core targets highlighted five key therapeutic targets closely associated with apoptosis: PPARγ, MMP9, BCL2, MAPK3, and ICAM1 (Fig. 5F). Molecular docking analysis validating the binding affinity of Karacoline to key targets

To verify the direct interactions of the active compounds of Aconitum carmichaelii with the key therapeutic targets at the molecular level, molecular docking simulations were conducted between the 11 screened active compounds and the five core targets (PPARγ, MAPK3, MMP9, BCL2 and ICAM1). The binding energies were visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 6A), which indicated that most compounds exhibited favorable binding affinities toward these targets.

B–F Representative docking conformations of Karacoline with each core target, including PPARγ (B), MAPK3 (C), MMP9 (D), BCL2 (E), and ICAM1 (F)

Subsequently, PyMOL 2.4 was used to analyze the binding characteristics of Karacoline with the five key targets (Fig. 6B–F). The results demonstrated that Karacoline could form stable interactions with PPARγ, MAPK3, MMP9, BCL2, and ICAM1, suggesting strong binding potential. Among them, PPARγ showed the strongest binding affinity, forming four hydrophobic interactions (e.g., with PHE-226), two hydrogen bonds (GLU-343 and SER-342) and one salt bridge (GLU-295). In the interaction with BCL2, Karacoline established six hydrophobic interactions (e.g., with PHE-104) and two hydrogen bonds (TRP-144 and ARG-107). Docking with MAPK3 revealed three hydrophobic interactions, two hydrogen bonds, and one salt bridge (e.g., ASP-353). For MMP9, five hydrophobic interactions and four hydrogen bonds were observed. In contrast, ICAM1 exhibited the weakest binding activity, forming four hydrophobic interactions (e.g., TYR-180) and two hydrogen bonds.

To further evaluate binding strength, the binding free energy (ΔG), inhibition constant (Ki), and calculated pKi values were determined (Table 3). The results demonstrated that Karacoline exhibited the highest binding affinity to PPARγ, followed by BCL2, whereas its affinity for ICAM1 was considerably lower. Collectively, these findings suggest that Karacoline may exert its therapeutic effect in SALI through multi-target synergistic interactions, with PPARγ serving as the principal regulatory target.

Karacoline alleviates CLP-induced acute lung injury and improves survival in septic mice

To determine the appropriate in vivo dose of Karacoline, a preliminary dose-ranging survival experiment was first performed in CLP-induced septic mice. Mice were treated with Karacoline at doses of 10, 20, or 40 mg/kg, and 7-day survival was monitored (Fig. 7A–B). Survival analysis showed that the 7-day survival rate was markedly decreased in the CLP group. In contrast, Karacoline treatment improved the survival of septic mice, with the 20 mg/kg dose producing a clear survival benefit. However, increasing the dose to 40 mg/kg did not further improve survival compared with 20 mg/kg. Therefore, 20 mg/kg was selected as the working dose for subsequent in vivo experiments.

To further evaluate the protective effects of Karacoline in vivo, the CLP model was established in mice, and Karacoline was administered by oral gavage at 1 h before surgery and 12 h after surgery. Tissue and fluid samples were collected 24 h after CLP surgery (Fig. 7C). Pulmonary edema, as assessed by the lung wet-to-dry weight ratio (W/D), was significantly elevated after CLP but was notably reduced by Karacoline administration (Fig. 7D). Total protein and albumin levels in BALF were further measured to evaluate alveolar-capillary barrier permeability [34]. CLP substantially elevated total protein and albumin levels in BALF, while Karacoline treatment decreased both indicators (Fig. 7E–F), indicating that Karacoline alleviated CLP-induced pulmonary barrier dysfunction. Inflammatory cytokine analysis showed that IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were markedly increased in the CLP group, whereas Karacoline treatment significantly decreased the concentrations of all three cytokines (Fig. 7G–I), demonstrating its potent anti-inflammatory effects. Histopathological evaluation of lung tissues revealed that CLP induced severe alveolar disruption, marked interstitial edema, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration. Karacoline treatment markedly mitigated these pathological changes (Fig. 7K), which was consistent with a significant reduction in lung injury scores (Fig. 7J).

Karacoline protects against SALI by suppressing apoptosis

Apoptosis plays a pivotal role in the progression of sepsis-induced acute lung injury (SALI). Based on our network pharmacology results, we further explored whether inhibition of apoptosis contributes to the protective effect of Karacoline against SALI. Western blot analysis demonstrated that, compared with the CLP group, Karacoline treatment markedly reduced the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Cleaved-Caspase-3 (Fig. 8C) and significantly increased the Bcl-2/BAX ratio (Fig. 8B), indicating a pronounced anti-apoptotic effect (Fig. 8A). Immunohistochemistry further confirmed these findings. CLP markedly decreased Bcl-2 expression and increased BAX expression in lung tissues, whereas Karacoline treatment reversed these alterations (Fig. 8D–E and G). Consistently, TUNEL staining revealed a significant increase in TUNEL-positive cells in the CLP group, which was notably reduced after Karacoline administration, along with decreased fluorescence intensity (Fig. 8F, H). These results indicate that Karacoline alleviates sepsis-induced lung injury by suppressing pulmonary apoptosis.

Karacoline inhibits LPS-induced apoptosis by maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis

To further investigate the mechanism by which Karacoline regulates apoptosis, in vitro experiments were conducted using LPS-stimulated MH-S. Firstly, the cytotoxicity of Karacoline at various concentrations was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay (Fig. 9A). The results showed that Karacoline exhibited no significant cytotoxicity at concentrations ≤ 1.6 µM, and 1.6 µM, the highest non-cytotoxic concentration, was selected for subsequent mechanistic experiments. Western blot analysis of MH-S cells revealed that Karacoline attenuated LPS-induced apoptosis, as evidenced by a reduction in cleaved caspase-3 and an increased Bcl-2/BAX ratio, consistent with in vivo findings(Fig. 9B-D). Flow cytometric analysis using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining revealed that LPS markedly induced apoptosis in MH-S, whereas Karacoline treatment significantly reduced the proportion of apoptotic cells (Fig. 9E, H), indicating a pronounced anti-apoptotic effect. ROS detection showed that LPS stimulation substantially increased intracellular ROS levels, while Karacoline treatment attenuated ROS fluorescence intensity (Fig. 9F, I), suggesting that Karacoline alleviates LPS-induced oxidative stress. Furthermore, mitochondrial function was assessed by detecting mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) using the JC-1 fluorescent probe. LPS stimulation resulted in a significant decrease in MMP (manifested as reduced red fluorescence and enhanced green fluorescence), whereas Karacoline treatment reversed this effect and restored mitochondrial membrane potential stability (Fig. 9G, J), indicating that Karacoline contributes to the maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis.

Karacoline inhibits apoptosis by activating PPARγ and suppressing the JNK/ERK MAPK signaling pathway

Based on the above findings, we further investigated the molecular mechanism underlying the anti-apoptotic effect of Karacoline. Guided by the network pharmacology analysis, we focused on PPARγ and the MAPK signaling pathway. First, RT-qPCR was performed to examine the transcriptional changes in PPARγ. The results showed that PPARG mRNA expression was significantly decreased in the CLP group, whereas Karacoline treatment restored PPARG mRNA levels in lung tissues (Fig. 10A). Similar results were observed in MH-S cells (Fig. 10B). Furthermore, western blot analysis of mouse lung tissue and MH-S cells revealed that PPARγ expression was markedly downregulated in the model group, whereas Karacoline treatment significantly restored PPARγ expression to levels comparable to the control group (Fig. 10C-D, E, I). Consistent results were observed using immunohistochemistry of lung tissue and immunofluorescence staining in MH-S cells, which both confirmed that Karacoline markedly upregulated PPARγ expression compared with the model group (Fig. 10M-P). These results indicate that Karacoline may suppress apoptosis by activating PPARγ. Next, we evaluated the MAPK signaling pathway. Western blot analysis showed that phosphorylation of JNK, ERK, and p38 (p-JNK, p-ERK, p-p38) was markedly increased in the model group. Karacoline treatment suppressed the activation of p-JNK and p-ERK but had minimal effect on p-p38. These results were consistent in both in vitro and in vivo experiments (Fig. 10C-L), suggesting that Karacoline primarily exerts its anti-apoptotic effect via inhibition of the JNK/ERK MAPK pathway.

Given the known involvement of MMP9 in inflammatory injury and pulmonary barrier disruption, we further validated MMP9, one of the targets predicted by network pharmacology analysis, to provide supportive evidence for the protective effect of Karacoline against SALI. Western blot analysis showed that CLP markedly upregulated MMP9 expression in lung tissues, whereas Karacoline treatment significantly suppressed this increase (Fig. 10Q-T).

PPARγ antagonist GW9662 weakens the anti-apoptotic and JNK/ERK MAPK-inhibitory effects of Karacoline in LPS-stimulated MH-S cells

To further determine whether Karacoline exerts its protective effects through PPARγ activation, MH-S cells were pretreated with the PPARγ antagonist GW9662 before LPS and Karacoline stimulation. Western blot analysis showed that, compared with the LPS + Karacoline group, GW9662 pretreatment decreased the Bcl2/BAX ratio and increased the level of cleaved-caspase3 (Fig. 11A-C), indicating that the anti-apoptotic effect of Karacoline in LPS-stimulated MH-S cells was weakened by PPARγ inhibition.

We next examined whether GW9662 affected the regulatory effect of Karacoline on MAPK signaling. Karacoline markedly suppressed LPS-induced phosphorylation of JNK and ERK, whereas GW9662 pretreatment reversed these inhibitory effects. However, GW9662 did not further alter the phosphorylation level of p38 (Fig. 11D-G). These findings suggest that the anti-apoptotic effect of Karacoline and its inhibition of JNK/ERK signaling in MH-S cells are at least partially dependent on PPARγ activation.

Discussion

This study systematically explored the therapeutic effect and underlying molecular mechanism of Karacoline in alleviating sepsis-induced acute lung injury (SALI). As an important diterpene alkaloid derived from Aconitum (Fuzi), previous studies have indirectly suggested that Karacoline may exhibit anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties in several disease models [15, 35]. However, its precise molecular targets and mechanisms in SALI have never been investigated. To our knowledge, this is the first study to elucidate the protective effects and mechanisms of Karacoline in SALI, highlighting its potential as the principal bioactive compound of Aconitum. Using TCMSP and SwissADME databases for compound screening, combined with protein–protein interaction (PPI) network and molecular docking analysis, we identified Karacoline as the most critical bioactive compound for further investigation. Network pharmacology and bioinformatics analyses revealed that Karacoline may exert its therapeutic effects through specific functional modules and regulatory pathways in SALI. GO enrichment analysis indicated that Karacoline targets genes involved in inflammatory response, immune regulation, and apoptotic signaling, whereas KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted the MAPK signaling pathway and apoptosis as the principal pathways of action. Notably, through PPI network analysis and WGCNA, PPARγ was identified among the predicted targets of network pharmacology as a core node across multiple centrality parameters and showed the strongest correlation with the clinical phenotype of SALI. Molecular docking further confirmed that PPARγ exhibited the most stable binding affinity with Karacoline. These findings suggest that PPARγ may serve as the principal therapeutic target through which Karacoline exerts its anti-SALI effects.

To validate the mechanistic predictions, we experimentally investigated the role of Karacoline in PPARγ activation and MAPK pathway inhibition, focusing on apoptosis regulation. PPARγ, a ubiquitously expressed nuclear factor highly enriched in macrophages and epithelial cells, has been widely recognized for its trans-repressive effects in immune cells [36]. Activation of PPARγ has been shown to suppress LPS-induced pulmonary cytokine release and apoptosis [37]. Notably, the PPARγ agonist rosiglitazone protects against endotoxemia-induced lung injury by activating PPARγ and inhibiting NF-κB nuclear translocation [38], supporting PPARγ as a promising therapeutic target in SALI. Conversely, MAPK cascade activation, comprising p38, JNK, and ERK1/2, is a key signaling event in inflammation and apoptosis [39]. Pathologically, ROS can activate the ASK1-MKK3/6 cascade, enhancing p38 and JNK phosphorylation, promoting cytochrome c release, and triggering mitochondrial apoptosis [40]. Excessive ERK activation can facilitate mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) and caspase-9/3 cascade activation, accelerating apoptosis [41, 42].

In this study, Karacoline significantly reduced mortality in septic mice, alleviated pulmonary edema and barrier dysfunction, and suppressed inflammatory cytokine release. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that Karacoline upregulated PPARγ expression in CLP-induced lung tissue and LPS-stimulated MH-S cells. However, Karacoline inhibited the phosphorylation of JNK and ERK, but had no significant effect on p38 phosphorylation. Although JNK, ERK, and p38 all belong to the MAPK family, their activation can be differentially regulated depending on the type of stimulus, cellular context, and feedback regulatory mechanisms [43]. Moreover, the role of p38 in cytokine regulation and post-transcriptional control may be more context-dependent [44]. These findings suggest that the protective effects of Karacoline in SALI are associated with PPARγ activation and suppression of JNK/ERK MAPK signaling. In the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, BAX, a pro-apoptotic member of the Bcl-2 family, plays a central role in this process [45]. Our results showed that Karacoline suppressed BAX and cleaved caspase-3 expression while restoring Bcl-2 levels, supporting its anti-apoptotic effect. Furthermore, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are key triggers of apoptosis. In vitro, DCFH-DA and JC-1 assays confirmed that Karacoline reduced ROS generation and stabilized mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), thereby attenuating ROS-mediated mitochondria apoptosis. This result is consistent with previous studies on natural products such as oleuropein and Songorine, which maintain redox homeostasis and exert mitochondrial protective effects [46, 47]. In addition, the GW9662 intervention experiment further supported the involvement of PPARγ in the protective effects of Karacoline. After PPARγ inhibition, the regulatory effects of Karacoline on apoptosis-related proteins and JNK/ERK signaling were weakened, suggesting that PPARγ, at least to some extent, is involved in the upstream regulation of Karacoline-mediated inhibition of JNK/ERK MAPK signaling and apoptotic responses. Taken together, these findings indicate that Karacoline may exert protective effects against SALI by activating PPARγ and subsequently inhibiting JNK/ERK MAPK-mediated apoptotic signaling in septic mice and LPS-stimulated MH-S cells.

In addition to apoptosis, other forms of programmed cell death, such as pyroptosis and necroptosis, are also involved in the occurrence and progression of SALI. Previous studies have shown that caspase-11-mediated endothelial cell pyroptosis can disrupt the pulmonary vascular endothelial barrier and promote protein-rich pulmonary edema and inflammatory cell infiltration [48]. In addition, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages is also an important mechanism underlying inflammatory amplification in sepsis. TWIK2-mediated potassium efflux can trigger NLRP3 inflammasome activation and mature IL-1β release in macrophages [49], whereas IL-1β can suppress CREB-mediated VE-cadherin transcription in endothelial cells, thereby disrupting endothelial adherens junctions and aggravating SALI [28]. Therefore, pyroptosis may not only contribute to macrophage or endothelial cell injury as an inflammatory form of cell death, but may also further affect pulmonary vascular barrier function through inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, thereby cooperating with apoptosis to drive SALI progression. However, the present study mainly focused on the regulatory effect of Karacoline on the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, and pyroptosis-related markers, such as NLRP3, caspase-1, and GSDMD, were not examined. Therefore, it remains unclear whether Karacoline affects pyroptosis in MH-S cells or pulmonary vascular endothelial cells. Further studies are still needed to evaluate the effects of Karacoline on pyroptosis, apoptosis, and their crosstalk regulatory networks in macrophages and pulmonary vascular endothelial cells.

Prior to this study, no literature had reported the role of Karacoline in SALI, which represents one of the major innovations of this work. Previous studies on Karacoline have mainly focused on its anti-inflammatory effects and NF-κB-related mechanisms, whereas direct evidence regarding its involvement in PPARγ/MAPK signaling regulation remains lacking. Therefore, this study is not merely a simple extension of previous reports on the anti-inflammatory effects of Karacoline, but further expands its potential mechanism of action to the PPARγ-related JNK/ERK MAPK apoptotic signaling axis in the new disease context of SALI. Moreover, as a naturally derived small-molecule compound, Karacoline may possess potential advantages, such as multi-target regulatory activity and biocompatibility [50]. These findings provide new insights into the pharmacological effects of Aconitum-derived compounds in inflammatory lung injury and suggest that Karacoline may be a candidate compound worthy of further validation in SALI intervention strategies [51].

However, this study still has several limitations. First, although the GW9662 intervention experiment further supported the involvement of PPARγ in the regulatory effects of Karacoline on the JNK/ERK MAPK signaling pathway and apoptosis, the current evidence is still mainly based on pharmacological inhibition. Future studies should incorporate PPARγ knockdown or overexpression, as well as JNK/ERK pathway rescue experiments, to further clarify the direct causal relationship between PPARγ and JNK/ERK MAPK signaling. Second, the in vitro experiments in this study mainly used MH-S cells. Although this model can reflect alveolar macrophage-related inflammatory responses during the early stage of SALI, it cannot fully mimic alveolar epithelial cells, pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells, or their interactions. Therefore, further validation of the protective effects of Karacoline in other lung-related cells or co-culture models is still required. Finally, systematic pharmacokinetic, PK/PD, and safety evaluations have not yet been conducted in this study. Given that Karacoline is derived from Aconitum plants, its effective dose range, long-term safety, therapeutic window, and translational feasibility still need to be further clarified in subsequent preclinical studies [52, 53].

Conclusion

In summary, this study is the first to elucidate the protective role and potential mechanism of Karacoline in SALI. Karacoline alleviated sepsis-induced lung injury by suppressing mitochondrial apoptosis, an effect closely associated with PPARγ-related inhibition of JNK/ERK MAPK signaling (Fig. 12). These findings provide new experimental evidence for understanding the pharmacological effects of Karacoline in inflammatory lung injury and suggest that it may be a candidate compound for further investigation as a potential intervention for SALI.
