Gate Parameter Lee-Yang Zeros and Dynamical Phases in Quantum Circuits
Chang Liu a, Yu Wu b, Yunfeng Jiang a,c, Yang Zhang b,c,d

Abstract
We propose gate-parameter Lee-Yang zeros of Loschmidt amplitudes as probes of dynamical phases in finite quantum circuits. We illustrate this approach using a brickwork model, where the time evolution is generated by repeated application of a Floquet operator. The Loschmidt amplitude can be expressed as a rational function of the gate parameters. At fixed system size and large circuit depth, its zeros in one complexified gate parameter, with the other parameter held fixed, condense onto limiting curves. We show that these curves comprise a universal component governed by equimodular Floquet eigenvalues, as described by the Beraha–Kahane–Weiss theorem, together with state-dependent contributions controlled by the overlap of eigenstate of the Floquet operator with the initial state. As one of the parameters is varied, the set of zeros reorganizes abruptly, providing a finite-qubit diagnostic of a dynamical phase transition. This mechanism does not rely on integrability: while integrability enables an exact calculation of the Loschmidt amplitude, the condensation of zeros follows from spectral competition and local unitarity alone.

In a typical circuit experiment, one controls a finite number of qubits, prepares an initial

number of periods. This setting differs from conventional condensed-matter systems, where one
usually starts from a Hamiltonian and takes the thermodynamic limit. For finite circuits with
discrete time-translation symmetry, transition amplitudes are natural observables. A key
question is whether such amplitudes can diagnose sharp dynamical regimes without requiring a
thermodynamic limit.

We answer this question by proposing gate-parameter Lee-Yang zeros of transition amplitudes as probes of distinct dynamical phases. Interpreting a transition amplitude as a cylinder partition function, its zeros are analogs of the Lee-Yang zeros of a statistical partition function

The limiting curves can be analyzed by the

Discussions.
We have shown that gate-parameter Lee-Yang zeros of Loschmidt amplitudes provide a
sharp probe of dynamical phases in finite quantum circuits. In general, there are two types of limiting zero curves: universal curves fixed
by the Floquet spectrum and state-dependent curves fixed by overlaps with the initial state. For the brickwork model, the
universal curves reorganize at
\Delta=1
, producing a finite-size dynamical phase transition in
the large circuit-depth limit.

The origin of the universal curves is spectral rather than state specific. By the BKW theorem,
equimodular Floquet eigenvalues generate limiting zeros independently of the detailed overlap
coefficients, provided the relevant overlaps do not vanish identically. Local unitarity gives a
simple geometric characterization of these equimodular loci, explaining the unit circle in the
massive regime and the coordinate axes in the massless regime. This mechanism is not specific to the simple brickwork model and can be analyzed similarly to other models. In SM, we perform a similar analysis for another integrable quantum circuit. In fact, the mechanism does not even rely on integrability. Integrability is used here to compute the Loschmidt amplitude exactly
and to expose the structure cleanly; the same spectral mechanism should be testable in weakly nonintegrable or fully chaotic circuits.

It is useful to contrast this diagnostic with the standard DQPT use of Loschmidt zeros. Conventional DQPTs fix the dynamics and study zeros in complex time, with real-time nonanalyticities emerging after a thermodynamic limit. Our construction fixes one circuit parameter and studies zeros in the other gate parameter, with the sharp structure emerging in the large-depth limit at fixed finite
L
. The two approaches are complementary slices of the same broader analytic object, the Loschmidt amplitude as a function of time/depth and circuit parameters. Both are governed by competition between spectral contributions, but they diagnose different projections of that competition.

The diagnostic is naturally suited to present-day quantum processors. Loschmidt amplitudes can
be accessed through interferometric protocols or related echo measurements, and Lee-Yang zeros
have already been inferred experimentally from quantum coherence and full counting statistics

Wei2012LY
;
Peng2015LY
;
Brandner2017EDD
. Partition-function zeros have also been proposed as
a route to many-body thermodynamics on quantum computers
Francis2021PZQ
. Since the
transition studied here is formulated at fixed
L
and
n
, it does not require
extrapolation to a thermodynamic limit.

Several questions remain open. First, the finite-time
scaling of the zero density near
\Delta=1
should be developed into a quantitative critical
diagnostic, it would be interesting to see whether the exponents are universal and can be
determined analytically. Second, in this paper we have focused on the universal component of the
Lee-Yang zeros, this does not mean the state-dependent part is un-important. Instead, since they
encode rich information of the initial state, they can serve as useful probes for different kind of quantum states. A
systematic study of the state-dependent part of the GPLY zero and their potential applications
in quantum computation is a highly interesting task. Third, the robustness of the universal
component should be tested in nonintegrable and noisy circuits.

;
Izergin1987partition

Izergin1992

and for the spin-chain crosscap state

The product states used in the main text are

\displaystyle|\mathrm{DW}_{M}\rangle

(S10)

III
ANALYSIS OF A GENERALIZED STAGGERED MODEL

The model analyzed in the main text corresponds to a one-parameter slice of a more general integrable brickwork circuit. Here we consider this broader class of models.

The system remains a Yang–Baxter integrable circuit constructed from the six-vertex transfer matrix, as discussed in
PalettaDuhPozsgayZadnik2025
. The circuit geometry is determined by the ordering of inhomogeneities, while the local two-site gate depends only on their difference.

For a system with
L=2l
qubits, we define the inhomogeneous transfer matrix

{\mathcal{T}(u;\boldsymbol{\nu},\eta)={\rm Tr}_{a}\!\left[R_{La}(u-\nu_{L};\eta)\cdots R_{1a}(u-\nu_{1};\eta)\right],\qquad\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{L}).}

Here
a
is the auxiliary spin-
1/2
space,
u
is the auxiliary spectral parameter, and
\nu_{j}
is the inhomogeneity assigned to site
j
. The sequence
\boldsymbol{\nu}
is fixed once the circuit geometry is chosen. The anisotropy
\eta
is kept fixed throughout the transfer-matrix family. As in the main text, we set
q=\mathrm{e}^{\eta}
and
\Delta=(q+q^{-1})/2
.
In the two-inhomogeneity setup considered below, each site takes one of two values:

{\nu_{j}\in\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\}.}

The sequence
\boldsymbol{\nu}
encodes the circuit geometry. For a fixed sequence, we denote
\mathcal{T}(\theta_{i})=\mathcal{T}(u=\theta_{i};\boldsymbol{\nu},\eta)
for
i=1,2
, following the transfer-matrix convention used in
PalettaDuhPozsgayZadnik2025
.
Introduce two inhomogeneities
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
and define

\mathcal{U}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\mathcal{T}(\theta_{2})\,\mathcal{T}(\theta_{1})^{-1},

(S17)

Here
\mathcal{T}(\theta_{1})^{-1}
is the operator inverse of
\mathcal{T}(u=\theta_{1};\boldsymbol{\nu},\eta)
with the same inhomogeneity sequence.
The difference

\theta_{21}=\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}

(S18)

serves as the local gate parameter. This operator can be diagonalized via Bethe ansatz. For a Bethe state

|\mathbf{u}_{M}\rangle
, the Floquet eigenvalue is

\Lambda_{\rm gen}(\mathbf{u}_{M})=\frac{t(\theta_{2}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{M})}{t(\theta_{1}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{M})}=\prod_{k=1}^{M}\frac{\sinh(u_{k}-\theta_{2}+\eta)}{\sinh(u_{k}-\theta_{1}+\eta)}\frac{\sinh(u_{k}-\theta_{1})}{\sinh(u_{k}-\theta_{2})},

(S19)

where
t(u\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{M})
denotes the transfer-matrix eigenvalue.
Consequently, the Loschmidt amplitude again takes the finite spectral form

\mathcal{D}_{\Psi}(n;y)=\sum_{j}A_{j}(y)\,\Lambda_{j}(y)^{n},

(S20)

where
y
is the chosen complex gate parameter. By the Beraha–Kahane–Weiss mechanism, zeros at large
n
accumulate on loci where two or more dominant branches satisfy

|\Lambda_{i}(y)|=|\Lambda_{j}(y)|
, together with isolated points where the leading coefficient vanishes.

The explicit form of
\mathcal{U}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
depends on the specific arrangement of inhomogeneities. Two representative choices are illustrated in Fig.
S5
. The left panel uses the sequence
\{\theta_{1},\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{2},\ldots\}
, while the right panel uses the alternating period
\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots\}
.
In both panels, blue markers denote positions carrying
\theta_{1}
and red markers denote positions carrying
\theta_{2}
. Different orderings of the inhomogeneities do not affect the eigenvalue of the transfer matrices as it is symmetric with respect to the inhomogeneities. On the other hand, this will affect the overlap between the initial state and the Bethe state, which will enter the Loschmidt amplitude.

Figure S5:
Different transfer-matrix arrangements lead to the same eigenvalue ratios but different overlap weights in the Loschmidt amplitude.

The corresponding two-site gate can be written as

U_{ij}(\theta_{21})=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&\dfrac{\sinh\eta}{\sinh(\eta+\theta_{21})}&\dfrac{\sinh\theta_{21}}{\sinh(\eta+\theta_{21})}&0\\
0&\dfrac{\sinh\theta_{21}}{\sinh(\eta+\theta_{21})}&\dfrac{\sinh\eta}{\sinh(\eta+\theta_{21})}&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}_{ij}.

(S21)

The brickwork model studied in the main text is recovered by setting
\theta_{2}=\xi/2
and
\theta_{1}=-\xi/2
.
To compare with the unitary gate in (
S2
), we write

\frac{\sinh\eta}{\sinh(\eta+\theta_{21})}=\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}}\phi}\cos\alpha,\qquad\frac{\sinh\theta_{21}}{\sinh(\eta+\theta_{21})}={\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}}\phi}\sin\alpha.

(S22)

The reality of
\alpha
and
\phi
therefore gives two local-unitarity conditions:

\left|\frac{\sinh\eta}{\sinh(\eta+\theta_{21})}\right|^{2}+\left|\frac{\sinh\theta_{21}}{\sinh(\eta+\theta_{21})}\right|^{2}=1,

(S23)

and

\frac{\sinh\theta_{21}}{\sinh\eta}\in{\mathrm{i}}\mathbb{R}.

(S24)

III.1
Massive regime

In the massive regime we take
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and introduce

a=\mathrm{e}^{\theta_{1}},\qquad b=\mathrm{e}^{\theta_{2}},\qquad q=\mathrm{e}^{\eta}.

(S25)

The unitarity constraints in Eqs. (
S23
) and
(
S24
) give

\frac{b/a-a/b}{q-q^{-1}}\in{\mathrm{i}}\mathbb{R},\qquad\left|\frac{q-q^{-1}}{qb/a-q^{-1}a/b}\right|^{2}+\left|\frac{b/a-a/b}{qb/a-q^{-1}a/b}\right|^{2}=1.

(S26)

At fixed nonzero
b
, these equations imply
|a|=|b|
. After rescaling by
b
, this is the unit circle, which is the origin of the compact GPLY-zero locus in the massive regime.

III.2
Massless regime

In the massless regime let
q=\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}}\gamma}
, with real
\gamma
. We again set
a=\mathrm{e}^{\theta_{1}}
and
b=\mathrm{e}^{\theta_{2}}
. Excluding the special points with
\sin(2\gamma)=0
, the same unitarity constraints reduce to

\frac{b/a-a/b}{q-q^{-1}}\in{\mathrm{i}}\mathbb{R},

(S27)

and

\left|\frac{q-q^{-1}}{qb/a-q^{-1}a/b}\right|^{2}+\left|\frac{b/a-a/b}{qb/a-q^{-1}a/b}\right|^{2}=1.

(S28)

For fixed nonzero
b

a\in b\mathbb{R}

a

b
. This gives the extended radial GPLY-zero structure characteristic of the massless regime.
