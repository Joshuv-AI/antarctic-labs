1. Introduction

The DOE Nuclear Energy Advanced Reactor Technologies (ART) Fast Reactor Program (FRP)
has supported the development of several databases containing information on the safety
performance of fast reactors, components, and fuels [1]. This growing collection of legacy
experimental data, operating data, and analysis is available online to registered users.
Metallography data represents one of the most critical types of post-irradiation examination (PIE)
data being collected, organized, and archived in several ART Fast Reactor Databases
(https://frdb.ne.anl.gov), including the Metallic Fuels Irradiation & Physics Database (FIPD) [2-
4], Out-of-Pile Transient Database (OPTD) [5, 6], and TREAT (the Transient Reactor Test Facility)
Experimental Relational Database (TREXR) [7].

These databases contain three principal sets of metallography data. The first set comprises
metallography data from Experimental Breeder Reactor-II (EBR-II) and Fast Flux Test Facility
(FFTF) irradiated fuel pins examined in the Hot Fuel Examination Facility (HFEF). The second
set consists of metallography data from EBR-II irradiated fuel pins examined in the Alpha-Gamma
Hot Cell Facility (AGHCF). The third set includes metallography data from transient-tested fuel
pins (including both out-of-pile furnace tests and TREAT tests) examined in AGHCF. Since both
the second and third sets were generated in AGHCF, they are governed by identical specifications.
The metallography data in the databases consist of digital images scanned from either positive or
negative photographic films [8].

To analyze the microstructure of a fuel pin, a series of preparatory steps are required, including
sectioning, epoxy mounting, mechanical grinding and polishing, and etching. Following sample
preparation, specimens are transferred for metallographic examination. The AGHCF and HFEF
metallography data were generated using optical microscopes manufactured by Leitz and Bausch
and Lomb (B&L) [9]. Images were recorded on Polaroid film at preset magnifications.
Magnification verification for the Leitz and B&L metallographs was conducted every two months
prior to 1989 and at least every six months from 1989 through the conclusion of the IFR program
[10, 11]. Magnifications determined from imaging of microslide standards were compared to the
instrument settings for magnifications ranging from 50× to 500× [10-12]. If the magnifications
determined from standards deviated from the instrument settings, adjustments were made to the
bellows extension until agreement was achieved.

The specifications for AGHCF and HFEF legacy metallography data have been established based
on available hard-copy and digital records, most of which have been incorporated into the data
repositories associated with FIPD, OPTD, and TREXR. Detailed specifications including hard-
copy records, digitized records, cutting diagrams and sectioning schemes, high-magnification
photographs, photomosaics (composites), information tags, scale bars, and magnification
verification procedures can be found in a separate report [8].

Many metallography images provide magnification information but lack a scale bar, hindering
quantitative analysis of fuel pin microstructures. Scale bars are critical for reliable measurements
of grain sizes, porosity and bubble distributions, fuel-cladding chemical interaction (FCCI) layer
thicknesses, and other microstructural features essential for fuel performance analysis. High-
magnification images (e.g., 250× and 500×) typically lack scale bars, though some composite

 1

images contain embedded legacy scale bars. However, the original methodology used to determine
these legacy scale bars is unknown.

This work provides guidance for determining scale bars for recently digitized high-resolution
AGHCF metallography images of U(Pu)Zr fuel pins stored in the FIPD, OPTD, and TREXR data
repositories. The determined scale bars are validated by comparison with pixel sizes derived from
legacy scale bars and from the magnifications in ref. [9]. Given the substantial volume of available
metallography data, this report focuses exclusively on AGHCF metallography. Scale bar
determination for legacy metallography data generated in HFEF will be reported separately.

2. Metallography Data Description

 2.1. Hard-copy Data and Digitization
Original metallography data generated in AGHCF were preserved as positive photographs or
negative films. Most positive photographs (Polaroid pictures) are included in hard-copy AGHCF
databooks. These positive photographs fall into two main categories: (1) high-magnification
images (250× and 500×) and (2) photomosaics (composites)—large images assembled primarily
from positives, though occasionally from negatives [13].

Historical metallography data from the legacy IFR Materials Information System (IMIS) consisted
of low-resolution, black-and-white scans [14, 15] unsuitable for advanced analytical techniques
[16, 17]. To address this limitation, hard-copy metallography records were recently digitized using
an Epson Perfection V850 Pro scanner. Positive photographs were scanned at 600 dots per inch
(DPI) in 16-bit or 8-bit grayscale, while negative films were scanned at 2400 DPI in 16-bit
grayscale to capture finer details. All images were saved in Tag Image File Format (TIFF) [8].

3. Determination of Scale Bar of High-magnification Images

 3.1. Scale Bar Determination
Pixel sizes of high-magnification images are calculated from the image magnification and
scanning resolution. For photographs scanned at 600 DPI, pixel sizes are:
                        Pixel size (250×) = 1 inch / 600 pixel / 250 = 0.1693 µm/pixel,        (1)

                        Pixel size (500×) = 1 inch / 600 pixel / 500 = 0.0847 µm/pixel.        (2)
Using these pixel sizes, scale bar lengths in pixels are determined by dividing the physical scale
bar length by the corresponding pixel size. For example:
    • A 100 µm scale bar in a 250× image: 100 µm / (0.1693 µm/pixel) = 592 pixels
    • A 50 µm scale bar in a 500× image: 50 µm / (0.0847 µm/pixel) = 592 pixels
These calculated scale bars were added to the 250× and 500× magnification images in Figure 2,
with results shown in Figure 9.

 3.2. Scale Bar Validation
To validate the calculated scale bar and pixel size, cladding thickness was measured at five
locations in the 250× image (Figure 9(a)), yielding 363 ± 3 µm. This value is approximately 4.8%
lower than the nominal cladding thickness of 381 ± 13 µm. The discrepancy (~5%) may result
from: (1) non-uniform cladding thickness, (2) irradiation-induced densification, (3) irradiation
creep, or (4) polishing artifacts.

To examine the influence of polishing artifacts, cladding thickness was measured in the same
cross-section (437A7B) after surface etching. Measurements were taken from stitched 500×
images at locations marked by red lines in Figure 10, yielding 372 ± 1 µm – in good agreement
(~2% difference) with the nominal thickness.

As-built cladding thickness was also measured from a 250× image of etched cross-section 437A8
(Figure 11(a), measurement locations marked in red). The measured thickness of 383 ± 1 µm
agrees excellently with the nominal thickness of 381 ± 13 µm.

 3.3. Uncertainty Estimation
Although both optical microscopes (Leitz and B&L) were calibrated regularly, actual
magnifications may still deviate slightly from nominal values. Billone [9] verified the
magnifications using microslide standards for magnification settings ranging from 50× to 500× for
both systems. The verified magnifications are summarized in Table 2 along with the corresponding
values from the FBTA notebook. Magnification uncertainties were estimated based on deviations
between the measured and nominal magnifications (Table 2). The uncertainties derived from the
“Excel-determined Magnification” are larger than those from the “FBTA Notebook #2
Magnification.” Since scale bars are directly calculated from the magnifications, they inherit these
magnification uncertainties. Therefore, the larger uncertainties from the "Excel-determined
Magnification" were used to provide conservative estimates for scale bars added to high-
magnification images (250× and 500×). These estimated uncertainties are summarized in Table 3.

When the microscope system is unspecified, the larger uncertainty value between the Leitz and
B&L systems is used: 4.4% for 250× images and 4.0% for 500× images. This approach provides
conservative uncertainty estimates for pixel sizes in high-magnification photographs.

4. Determination of Pixel Size and Scale Bar of Composites

Most composite images were created by stitching individual images and then photographing the
assembled composite to produce hard-copy photographs. These include: (1) radial strip composites;
(2) axial strip composites; (3) whole radial cross-section composites; and (4) axial cross-section
composites (section 2.2). The only exception is the composites stitched from high-magnification
photographs for which scale bar can be determined using the same method described in section
3.1. Due to the multi-step process of composite image generation, the original magnifications
before stitching cannot be used to determine pixel sizes in the final composites. Instead, pixel sizes
of composite images are estimated using three references:

 1. Nominal cladding thickness
 2. Measured or nominal cladding outer diameter
 3. Distance between characteristic microstructural features (e.g., bubble spacing)
 measured from 250× or 500× images when these high-magnification images capture
 portions of the composite

 11

These references were applied to estimate pixel sizes and scale bars for different composite types,
including radial strip composites (spanning from fuel centerline to cladding outer diameter,
constructed from 250× images) and whole cross-section composites (constructed from 35× or 75×
images).

 4.1. Radial Strip Composites
Pixel sizes for the 250× radial strip composites of fuel pin DP11 were calibrated using two methods:
nominal cladding thickness and characteristic microstructural feature spacing.

Method 1: Using cladding thickness as reference. Figure 12 shows the 250× radial strip
composite of cross-section 437A8. Assuming negligible cladding thickness change after
irradiation, the cladding thickness values were measured at five locations (marked by red lines),
yielding 939 ± 5 pixels. This corresponds to a pixel size of 381 µm / (939 ± 5) pixels = 0.406 ±
0.002 µm/pixel. This calibrated value is approximately 9.4% larger than the pixel size of 0.371
µm/pixel derived from the legacy scale bar (whose determination method is not recovered).

Method 2: Using characteristic microstructural features as reference. Figure 13(a) shows two
small bubbles in a 250× image separated by 361 µm (marked by red line 1). The same two bubbles
in the 250× radial strip composite (Figure 13(b)) are separated by 952 pixels, yielding a pixel size
of 361 µm / 952 pixels = 0.379 µm/pixel. Using the same approach, additional four bubble pairs
(marked by red lines 2 to 5 in Figure 13) were analyzed to estimate the pixel size in the composite,
yielding an average pixel size of 0.380 ± 0.021 µm/pixel. This calibrated value is approximately
2.5% larger than the legacy scale bar-derived pixel size of 0.371 µm/pixel.

Table 4 compares the pixel sizes for the 250× radial strip composite of cross-section 437A8
calibrated using both methods.

Method 2, using characteristic microstructural features as a reference, is the recommended
approach for calibrating pixel sizes in radial strip composites. Using this method, a scale bar was
added to the 250× radial strip composite of cross-section 437A8, as shown in Figure 12.

This same approach (using characteristic microstructural features as a reference) was applied to a
different composite image of the same cross-section. As shown in Figure 14, the same bubble pair
identified in Figure 13(a) (marked by red line 1) is separated by 500 pixels in this composite,
yielding a pixel size of 0.722 µm/pixel. A corresponding scale bar was added to this radial strip

 4.2. Axial Strip Composites

Pixel sizes for the 250× axial strip composites of fuel pin DP11 were calibrated using two methods:
nominal cladding thickness and characteristic microstructural feature spacing.

Method 1: Using cladding thickness as reference. Assuming negligible cladding thickness
change after irradiation, the cladding thickness was measured at five locations (marked by red lines
in Figure 15), yielding 986 ± 3 pixels. This corresponds to a pixel size of 381 µm / (986 ± 3) pixels
= 0.386 ± 0.009 µm/pixel. This calibrated value is approximately 5.4% larger than the pixel size
of 0.367 µm/pixel derived from the legacy scale bar (whose determination method is not
recovered).

Method 2: Using characteristic microstructural features as reference. Figure 16 shows two
small bubbles in a 250× image separated by 258 µm (marked by red line 1). The same two bubbles
in the 250× axial strip composite are separated by 691 pixels, yielding a pixel size: 258 µm / 691
pixels = 0.374 µm/pixel. Using the same approach, an additional four bubble pairs (marked by red
lines 2 to 5 in Figure 13) were analyzed to estimate the pixel size in the composite, yielding an
average pixel size of 0.366 ± 0.015 µm/pixel. This calibrated value is approximately 0.1% smaller
than the legacy scale bar-derived pixel size of 0.367 µm/pixel.

 4.3. Whole Radial Cross-section Composites

Pixel size for the whole radial cross-section composites of fuel pin DP11 were calibrated using
two methods: nominal cladding thickness and cladding outer diameter measured by laser
profilometry. Figure 17 shows the laser profilometry data for the average outer diameter of fuel
pin DP11. The locations of the cross-sections are marked by red arrows in the figure. Billone [9]
measured the outer diameters of four radial cross-sections (437A2, 437A6, 437A7B, and 437A8),
reporting values of 5852, 5893, 5898, and 5923 µm, respectively. In this work, the outer diameter
of axial cross-section 437A4 was measured from the laser profilometry data, yielding an average
of 5846 ± 1 µm.

Method 1: Using cladding thickness as reference. As shown in Figure 18, the cladding thickness
of cross-section 437A8 was measured at ten locations, yielding 225 ± 7 pixels. Using the nominal
cladding thickness of 381 µm, the corresponding pixel size is 381 µm / (225 ± 7) pixels = 1.693 ±
0.050 µm/pixel. This calibrated value is approximately 3.5% larger than the pixel size of 1.636
µm/pixel derived from the legacy scale bar.

Method 2: Using cladding outer diameter as reference. As shown in Figure 18, the cladding
outer diameter of cross-section 437A8 was measured at five locations, yielding 3579 ± 28 pixels.
Using the measured outer diameter of 5923 µm, the corresponding pixel size is 5923 µm / (3579
± 28) pixels = 1.655 ± 0.013 µm/pixel. This calibrated value is approximately 1.1% larger than the
pixel size of 1.636 µm/pixel derived from the legacy scale bar pixel size of 1.655 ± 0.013 µm/pixel.

Both calibrated pixel sizes for cross-section 437A8 are compared to 1.636 µm/pixel derived the
legacy scale bar in Table 6.

 4.4. Axial Cross-section Composites

Pixel sizes for the 75× composite of axial cross-section 437A4 were calibrated using two methods:
nominal cladding thickness and laser profilometry-measured cladding outer diameter.

Method 1: Using cladding thickness as reference. As shown in Figure 21, the cladding thickness
was measured at ten locations, yielding 110 ± 3 pixels. Using the nominal cladding thickness of
381 µm, the corresponding pixel size is 381 µm / (110 ± 3 pixels) = 3.457 ± 0.089 µm/pixel.

Method 2: Using cladding outer diameter as reference. As shown in Figure 21, the cladding
outer diameter was measured at five locations, yielding 1773 ± 12 pixels. Using the measured outer
diameter of 5846 µm, the corresponding pixel size is 5846 µm / (1773 ± 12 pixels) = 3.296 ± 0.023
µm/pixel.

5. Recommended Procedures

A workflow chart for pixel size determination in different types of metallography images,
including high-magnification images and composites, is presented in Figure 23, with case numbers
used as examples in the green circles. Table 13 summarizes the recommended calibration methods,
estimated uncertainties, and relevant example cases for each image type. The recommended
procedures are organized by image classification as follows:

2. High-magnification images (250× or 500×): Direct conversion from the specified
 magnification is the recommended method. This yields pixel sizes of 0.1693 μm/pixel (250×)
 and 0.0847 μm/pixel (500×) with estimated uncertainties ranging from 2.6% to 4.4% (Cases
 1-6). This approach is applicable regardless of the imaging source (Leitz, BSL, or unspecified)
 and provides straightforward calibration when magnification metadata is available.
3. Composite images stitched from high-magnification images: When composites contain
 stitched high-magnification images captured at known magnifications (250× or 500×) without
 subsequent photographing of the assembled composite, these high-magnification images serve
 as internal calibration references. This achieves the same pixel sizes with uncertainties of 4.0%
   to 4.4% (Cases 7-8).
4. Composite images of strips or full cross-sections: For strips or full cross-section composites,
 three calibration methods are available:

 1. Characteristic microstructural feature calibration using identifiable features provides ~4.4%
 uncertainty for 250× strip composites (Cases 9, 12).
 2. Cladding thickness measurements provide 5.5% to 10% uncertainty for strip composites
          (Cases 10, 13).
 3. Cladding outer diameter measurements provide ~2.0% uncertainty for lower-magnification
 (75×) cross-section composites in both radial and axial orientations (Cases 11, 14).
The selection of the appropriate calibration method should be guided by: image classification,
availability of magnification metadata, presence of identifiable calibration features, the

 24

applicability of the assumptions needed to use the method, and the target measurement precision
required for subsequent quantitative metallographic analysis. Table 12 lists the calibration methods
with the prerequisite information needed and the assumptions that must be made to apply the
method. If one of the required assumptions cannot be justified or the uncertainty of the assumption
accounted for, the method should not be used. For example, in the case of some transient-tested
samples, the post-test cladding thickness may be expected to be significantly thinner than the pre-
test thickness, and therefore the cladding thickness method may not be used to determine image
magnification and scale bars.

A Subject Matter Expert (SME) providing scale bars must document the methodology used to
determine the scale for each processed image. For the composite images of strips or full cross-
sections, if a measured cladding diameter was used, the specific measurement technique (e.g., laser
profilometry, contact profilometry, or micrometer) must be specified. If no direct measurement is
available, the SME may use either the nominal (pre-irradiation) cladding diameter or a reference
cladding thickness. When using these alternatives, the SME must estimate the potential cladding

 25

swelling at the relevant burnup and temperature. This estimation should be based on data from a
sibling fuel pin in the same experiment or from a fuel pin in a different experiment with similar
irradiation conditions.

The estimated percentage of cladding swelling should then be factored into the uncertainty of the
scale bar. The final uncertainty value for the scale bar will be determined by an SME who performs
the data qualification for the metallography data.
