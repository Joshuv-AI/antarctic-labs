Assessment of climate variability impacts on water quality using SPI and GIS approaches in the Mubuku River catchment, Uganda
Authors: Abdirisak Mohamed Yousuf, Deepa Krishnan, Zubeda Ukundimana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Mubuku River catchment in western Uganda is highly sensitive to hydroclimatic variability, yet quantitative evidence linking rainfall anomalies to water quality degradation remains limited. This study presents a decadal (2014–2024) integrated assessment combining the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI), rainfall deviation analysis, GIS-based spatial interpolation, and laboratory evaluation of physicochemical and microbial parameters. Results reveal significant temporal and spatial deterioration in water quality, with Total Dissolved Solids increasing by ~35%, hardness rising from 300 to 400 mg/L, and nitrate concentrations exceeding 50 mg/L at multiple sites. Microbial contamination also intensified, with fecal coliform counts increasing from 50 to 75 CFU/100 mL, surpassing WHO permissible limits. SPI analysis identified recurrent drought years (2014, 2016, 2023, 2024) alongside high-runoff periods, demonstrating a dual vulnerability framework where both precipitation deficits and excesses exacerbate contamination. Spatial analysis highlighted critical hotspots (WS4, WS6, WS8), where pollutant concentrations were 40–60% higher than the catchment average. The findings further indicate that drought conditions enhance mineral dissolution and baseflow contamination, while wet periods accelerate runoff-driven pollutant transport from agricultural and urban sources. This study contributes a novel climate–water quality linkage framework for tropical catchments by integrating hydroclimatic indices with spatial water quality assessment. The results provide actionable insights for targeted interventions, including hotspot-based management, seasonal regulation strategies, and improved wastewater control, to enhance water security and climate resilience.

Introduction

Water quality constitutes a critical concern for public health, environmental sustainability, and the integrity of ecosystems, particularly in the context of a rapidly varying climate1–3. Variations in climatic patterns may influence aquatic systems through alterations in temperature, precipitation, and the frequency of extreme meteorological events4,5. Nonetheless, the repercussions on freshwater resources exhibit regional disparities. Generally, climatic variability pertains to fluctuations in climatic parameters, encompassing precipitation and droughts, that occur over short to medium temporal scales, ranging from several months to multiple years. Conversely, climate change, in stark contrast, signifies long-term systematic alterations in climatic statistical trends extending over decades or longer durations, typically evaluated utilizing extensive datasets of thirty years or more. The climate serves as a pivotal determinant of water conditions through its profound influence on the hydrological cycle. Fluctuations in climate patterns, such as rising global temperatures, alterations in precipitation patterns, and an increase in both the frequency and severity of extreme weather events, can have direct ramifications on both surface and groundwater systems6–8. Elevated temperatures influence water chemistry and the levels of dissolved oxygen9,10. Altered rainfall and flooding patterns modify watershed hydrology and influence runoff volume and water quality11–13. Warmer weather specifically tends to lower the amount of dissolved oxygen in the water, which can be detrimental to aquatic life14. Warmer air causes water to rise as well, which exacerbates eutrophication caused by nutrient loading. Increased turbidity, pathogens, and chemical contamination entering streams through surface runoff are associated with more heavy rainfall events15,16. On the other hand, protracted droughts reduce water levels and concentrate contaminants. However, not all regions experience climate change in the same way or to the same degree. The impacts on freshwater resources are also highly place-based depending on location factors like geology, elevation, land use and existing water stress levels17,18. Understanding site-specific dynamics is important for devising local adaptation responses to safeguard water security under future climatic conditions. The Mubuku River basin provides vital fresh water resources for communities in southwestern Uganda. Originating in the Ruwenzori Mountains, the Mubuku River flows eastward over 120 km before emptying into Lake Albert The basin covers approximately 256 km2, including tributaries like the Hokyo and Itara Rivers. Historically, the Mubuku basin experienced consistent rainfall patterns19–21. However, recent decades have seen declining precipitation trends that threaten to exacerbate pressures on water availability in the basin. Studies indicate reductions in annual rainfall of 23.8mm per decade since 1989 Concurrently, rises in temperature of 1.3°C since 1980 are elevating evapotranspiration losses19. These alterations in climatic parameters have already exerted detrimental effects on the surface and groundwater resources upon which communities depend. Gauge records indicate that the Mubuku River catchment has experienced flow reductions of 15% to 25%, particularly during dry seasons. Monitoring wells reveal that 40% are experiencing declines in groundwater levels ranging from 1 m to 3 m annually20,22. Furthermore, projections regarding future climatic conditions suggest that warming will persist, potentially increasing by 1.5°C –2.5°C by the middle of the century, alongside shifts in precipitation patterns and a rise in extreme events5,21. In the absence of implemented adaptation strategies, these trends are anticipated to exacerbate water scarcity and further compromise water quality23. Despite the escalating interest in hydroclimatic variability, there exists a paucity of studies that quantitatively correlate drought indices, such as the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI), with the dynamics of river water quality in equatorial mountainous catchments. This deficiency is particularly pronounced in the Mubuku River basin, where integrated spatial-statistical analyses are notably limited. In light of these emerging challenges, there exists an imperative need to evaluate the effects of climate variability on water resources within the Mubuku River Basin. This research endeavor aims to examine both current and projected impacts of water-climate variability through an integrated analysis of climatic and hydrological data. In this investigation, climate variability is quantified utilizing a ten-year rainfall dataset (2014–2024) employing the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) across multiple temporal scales of three, six, and twelve months, effectively capturing temporal deviations from normative precipitation patterns. By identifying critical trends, vulnerabilities, and interactions, this study seeks to enhance water security and resilience within the region.

Materials and methods

Study area - Geographical scope

The study is focused on the Mubuku River catchment, located in the Rwenzori Mountains region of western Uganda. This catchment represents a distinct hydrological unit, encompassing the main river, its tributaries, and associated water resources that sustain both ecological systems and human activities24. The spatial extent of the study area is delineated using the geographical coordinates of the Mubuku River basin, which approximately extend from 00°14′ 30” N to 00° 27′ 30” N latitude and 29° 57′ 00” E to 30°07′ 00” E longitude. Standard precipitation index analysis

In this study, the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) was applied to assess the influence of climate variability on water quality in the Mubuku River catchment, Uganda. The specific use of SPI is that it captures climate variability rather than long-term climate change trends. The other main reason to select SPI indices for drought assessment, particularly in regions with limited hydrometeorological data availability was its robustness, minimal data requirements, and global acceptance.

Data source and description

Monthly rainfall data for the period 2014–2024 were obtained from Uganda National Meteorological Authority (UNMA). A total of 4 meteorological stations located within and around the Mubuku River catchment were used in this study. The spatial distribution of these stations ensures adequate representation of rainfall variability across the catchment.

Data quality control and preprocessing

Prior to analysis, rainfall data spanning 2014–2024 were first subjected to rigorous quality control procedures, including, consistency and homogeneity checks, detection and removal of outliers using statistical thresholds and cross-validation with neighbouring stations. Missing data were treated inverse distance weighting, depending on station proximity and data availability. Stations with more than 10% missing records were excluded to maintain data reliability.

SPI computation procedure

Unlike a simple Z-score approximation, SPI was computed following the standard probabilistic approach recommended by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), by fitting a gamma probability distribution to the long-term precipitation record from each meteorological station, as the gamma distribution effectively models rainfall. Particularly, precipitation data were fitted to a two-parameter gamma distribution, which adequately represents the positively skewed nature of rainfall data.

Goodness-of-fit assessment

To ensure the suitability of the gamma distribution, a Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test was performed for each station and timescale. The results indicated that the gamma distribution adequately fits the precipitation data (p > 0.05), confirming its appropriateness for SPI computation.

Transformation to standard normal distribution

The cumulative probability was subsequently transformed into a standard normal distribution with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one, where negative SPI values denote drought conditions and positive values represent wet periods.

Timescale selection

SPI values were calculated at multiple timescales (3-, 6-, 9-, and 12-month) to capture both short-term and long-term precipitation anomalies. For correlation with water quality parameters, the 12-month SPI was primarily used, as it reflects long-term hydrological conditions influencing groundwater recharge and river baseflow. Also, shorter timescales (3- and 6-month SPI) were additionally analyzed to interpret seasonal variability. Classification of drought and wetness severity followed established thresholds: extreme drought (SPI ≤ –2.0), severe drought (–1.99 to –1.5), moderate drought (–1.49 to –1.0), and near-normal conditions (–0.99 to 0.99)12,25. Statistical procedures ensured reliable transformation of precipitation data into SPI values, enabling comprehensive detection of rainfall anomalies and trends. By analyzing these values, both drought and wet periods were identified, offering critical insights into precipitation variability and its implications for water quality in the catchment. Spatial analysis of water quality parameters

Geographic Information Systems (GIS) was used to evaluate the spatial distribution of water quality parameters and their linkage with climatic variability across the Mubuku River catchment of about 256 km2. Twelve water quality sampling sites (Fig. 2) were spatially referenced using high-accuracy GPS measurements and projected to the UTM Zone 36 N coordinate system. A total of ten key water quality indicators—pH, electrical conductivity (EC), total dissolved solids (TDS), calcium, magnesium, nitrates, sulphates, total hardness, total coliforms, and fecal coliforms—were analyzed and mapped for the years 2014 and 2024 to evaluate decadal-scale spatial variability. The GIS-based mapping procedure involved precise georeferencing of spatial datasets, implementation of the Inverse Distance Weighted (IDW) interpolation method for continuous surface generation, classification of thematic symbology, and preparation of final cartographic layouts within ArcGIS Pro. Interpolation method and justification

To generate continuous spatial surfaces, Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) interpolation was applied to ten core water quality parameters. IDW is a deterministic method that performs robustly to estimates unknown values based on the weighted influence of nearby observations, with closer points exerting greater influence, under sparse data conditions and avoids overfitting associated with poorly constrained variograms. The choice of IDW over geostatistical methods such as Kriging was based on the following technical considerations such as the dataset comprises a limited number of sampling points (n = 12), which is insufficient for reliable semi-variogram modeling required in Kriging. In such contexts, IDW is widely recognized as a robust alternative due to its computational simplicity, reduced parameter dependency, and lower susceptibility to overfitting.

Cross-validation and model performance

To ensure a robust and unbiased evaluation of interpolation accuracy under spatial dependence, a spatial cross-validation approach was implemented as an enhanced alternative to the conventional leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). Unlike standard LOOCV, which assumes independence among observations, Spatial CV explicitly accounts for spatial autocorrelation by incorporating spatial structure into the validation process. In this study, a spatially structured leave-one-out scheme was adopted, where each sampling location was iteratively excluded along with its surrounding neighbourhood defined by a specified spatial buffer distance. This buffer-based exclusion minimizes information leakage caused by nearby correlated observations, thereby providing a more realistic assessment of model generalization performance in spatial prediction tasks. For each iteration, the model was trained using the remaining spatially independent observations and used to predict the excluded location. Model performance was quantified using standard statistical metrics, including Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and coefficient of determination (R2). These metrics were computed across all validation folds and aggregated to evaluate overall model accuracy. The adoption of Spatial CV enhances the reliability of model validation by reducing over-optimistic bias commonly associated with traditional LOOCV in spatial datasets. This approach is particularly critical in geospatial analyses, such as hydrological and climatic modeling, where spatial autocorrelation can significantly influence predictive performance.

Spatial mapping and analysis

Separate GIS-based spatial distribution maps were generated for each parameter for both study years. These maps enabled identification of spatial patterns, including contamination hotspots, areas of improvement, and stable zones. Spatial overlay analysis was conducted by integrating water quality layers with land use data, rainfall variability (SPI), and topographic attributes derived from the Digital Elevation Model (DEM). This facilitated the identification of relationships between environmental drivers and observed pollution patterns26. Hotspot analysis using the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic was applied to detect statistically significant clusters of high and low values, providing further validation of spatial trends. Additionally, buffer zone analysis was used to assess anthropogenic influences, particularly the proximity of agricultural activities and settlements to sampling locations.

Model reliability and limitations

Although the interpolation results demonstrate acceptable accuracy, it is acknowledged that the relatively low sampling density introduces inherent uncertainty. Therefore, the generated maps should be interpreted as indicative spatial trends rather than exact representations. Field validation and consistency with known land use patterns were used to further support the reliability of the results27,25. Overall, this GIS-based framework provides a robust basis for linking spatial water quality patterns with climatic variability and anthropogenic influences, supporting informed decision-making for catchment-scale water resource management.

Water quality index assessment

One of the widely applied approaches for evaluating this vulnerability is the WQI, which integrates multiple physico-chemical-biological parameters into a single numerical value representing overall groundwater quality. The WQI method involves selecting relevant water quality parameters, assigning weights based on their relative importance to human health and water usability, and computing a weighted average. The final index categorizes groundwater into classes such as excellent, good, poor, very poor, or unsuitable for drinking. When applied to vulnerability assessment, WQI helps to identify pollution hotspots where groundwater is highly degraded, to quantify spatial and temporal variations in groundwater quality and further to link water quality to land use and anthropogenic activities, highlighting vulnerable zones. In this study, a 10-year rainfall dataset was analyzed to identify hydroclimatic variability using rainfall deviation and SPI-based assessment. The rainfall deviation results (Table 1) indicate pronounced interannual variability across the study period. Years such as 2014, 2016, 2023, and 2024 exhibited mild drought-induced vulnerability, while 2015, 2017, 2018, and 2019 showed moderate drought vulnerability characterized by negative rainfall deviations. In contrast, 2020, 2021, and 2022 were initially classified as having “no rainfall vulnerability” due to strong positive deviations. However, this classification has been refined to reflect low drought vulnerability but elevated runoff-induced (wet-condition) vulnerability, particularly in 2021, which recorded the highest positive deviation.

To resolve this ambiguity, the concept of hydroclimatic vulnerability is redefined to include both extremes of the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI):

(i) Negative SPI (dry conditions) associated with reduced dilution capacity, longer residence time, and increased concentration of dissolved constituents (e.g., TDS, hardness), and

(ii) Positive SPI (wet conditions) associated with intensified surface runoff, soil erosion, and mobilization of sediments, nutrients, and microbial contaminants. Thus, vulnerability in the Mubuku River basin is not unidirectional but occurs under both deficit and excess rainfall conditions. The SPI-derived meteorological maps (Fig. 3 and Fig. 4) reveal strong spatial heterogeneity in hydroclimatic vulnerability across the catchment. The western and eastern regions show higher susceptibility to both drought and runoff-driven impacts, while the northwestern zone remains relatively resilient. These spatial patterns reflect the combined influence of topography, land use, and rainfall distribution on groundwater recharge and surface hydrological processes.

Discussion - Hydroclimatic implications of rainfall variability

The observed variability in rainfall reflects increasing hydroclimatic instability in the Mubuku River basin, consistent with broader regional trends in East Africa5,21. Importantly, the results highlight that both increasing dry spells and episodic high-intensity rainfall events contribute to water quality degradation, albeit through different mechanisms. During drought periods, reduced recharge and streamflow limit dilution, leading to higher concentrations of dissolved ions11,20. Conversely, during wet periods, enhanced runoff accelerates the transport of sediments, nutrients, and microbial contaminants into water systems22. The spatial concentration of vulnerability in the eastern and western zones suggests strong topographic and land-use controls, influencing both runoff generation and infiltration processes10. These findings establish a more comprehensive linkage between SPI-derived precipitation anomalies and water quality dynamics, emphasizing the need to consider dual-mode vulnerability (drought and flood/runoff) in hydroclimatic risk assessments and water resource management strategies in the Mubuku River basin.

Physical water quality parameters

pH

The spatial analysis reveals a discernible transition towards heightened alkalinity, as evidenced by pH values ascending from 7.1–7.9 in the year 2014 to 7.6–8.3 by the year 2024. While these values remain within the permissible permissible thresholds, the upward trend implies a progressive chemical transformation of the riverine ecosystem. Fig. 5 (a) and Fig. 5(b) illustrate the spatial distribution of pH levels between 2014 and 2024. In the year 2014, the western sector displayed slightly acidic water characteristics, whereas the eastern region was characterized by more alkaline conditions. By 2024, the overall pH values experienced an increase from 7.1–7.9 to 7.6–8.3, underscoring a gradual shift towards alkalinity. Although these values still reside within the World Health Organization’s permissible range (6.5–8.5), the trend emphasizes the ramifications of climate variability, erosion, intensive agricultural practices, and industrial effluents29. This escalating alkalinity poses significant risks to aquatic ecosystems and the long-term Viability of water resources.

Electrical conductivity (EC)

The electrical conductivity measurements indicate a marked elevation in both midstream and downstream regions, signifying increased ionic concentrations and salinity levels. The spatial proliferation of areas with elevated electrical conductivity (EC) suggests an intensifying anthropogenic and hydroclimatic impact. Electrical Conductivity (EC), serving as an indicator of ionic load and salinity, has also demonstrated considerable spatial heterogeneity (Figs. 6(a) and Fig. 6(b)). A comparative analysis between the years 2014 and 2024 reveals substantial increases in EC within midstream and downstream regions, indicating escalating salinity stress associated with runoff and anthropogenic discharges30.

Discussion - physical parameters and climate–water quality linkages

The discerned augmentation in pH and electrical conductivity is indicative of intensifying hydro-chemical processes, which are propelled by climate variability and anthropogenic influences. Escalating temperatures facilitate mineral dissolution and biochemical reactions, thereby contributing to enhanced alkalinity31,32. Likewise, the elevated levels of electrical conductivity denote heightened salinity attributable to diminished water flows and increased evapo-concentration, a phenomenon extensively documented in riverine systems susceptible to drought6,8. The spatial concentration of elevated electrical conductivity in downstream regions further implies cumulative effects of pollution and land-use alterations, particularly emanating from agricultural activities and urban settlements.

Chemical water quality parameters

Total dissolved solids (TDS)

TDS concentrations increased significantly from 557.92–621.83 mg/L (2014) to 686.62–806.61 mg/L (2024), with approximately 70% of sampling locations exceeding the WHO guideline value of 500 mg/L, indicating substantial deterioration in water quality. Spatial comparison (Fig. 7(a) and Fig. 7(b)) shows that moderately elevated TDS levels in 2014, mainly confined to central agricultural zones, have expanded and intensified across the catchment by 2024.

While climatic variability plays a key role, the observed 45% rise in TDS is more accurately explained through a hydrogeochemical mechanism linked to the Rwenzori Mountain geology and flow regime shifts. During prolonged drought periods (2015–2018; SPI ≤ −1.5), river discharge is increasingly dominated by baseflow (groundwater contribution). Groundwater in the Mubuku catchment interacts extensively with mineral-rich lithologies of the Rwenzori Mountains, resulting in enhanced dissolution of rock-forming minerals13. This leads to elevated concentrations of dissolved ions, particularly calcium (Ca2⁺) and magnesium (Mg2⁺), thereby increasing TDS. Additionally, reduced surface runoff during dry periods lowers the dilution capacity of the river, further amplifying solute concentrations. In contrast, wet periods contribute secondary inputs through agricultural runoff, but the dominant control on elevated TDS is the increased proportion of mineralized groundwater under low-flow conditions. The spatial coincidence of high TDS zones with areas of drought vulnerability confirms this combined hydroclimatic–geological control.

Total hardness, calcium, and magnesium

Water hardness increased across the catchment, with values ranging from 220.16mg/L – 551.45 mg/L, classifying several as very hard to extremely hard water zones. Calcium and magnesium concentrations also showed significant upward trends, contributing to overall hardness. The distribution of water hardness in the Mubuku river catchment is visually represented using different shades of pink and purple presented in Fig. 8(a) and Fig. 8(b), categorizing the levels into five distinct groups. The data reveals that water hardness varies across different regions, with some areas showing significantly higher concentrations than others33 As an important factor influencing the suitability of water for residential, commercial, and agricultural use, hardness is measured in milligrams per liter. According to the research, the total hardness in River Mubuku catchment ranges from 220.16 mg/L to 551.45 mg/L, with certain areas exceeding 446 mg/L, classifying the water as extremely hard.

The calcium levels in the river catchment map have shown (Fig. 9(a) and Fig. 9(b)), a significant increase over the past decade. In earlier measurements, concentrations ranged from 54.86 mg/L to 81.44 mg/L, with deeper green regions indicating higher calcium levels and lighter areas reflecting lower amounts. Overall, water quality remained moderate, with calcium levels suitable for most household and environmental applications34. However, more recent data reveals a sharp rise, with concentrations now ranging from 104.96 mg/L to 171.01 mg/L. This increase is visually represented by a shift towards deeper green hues, signaling a notable rise in water hardness. The Mubuku River’s magnesium concentration depicted in Fig. 10(a) and Fig. 10(b), has significantly increased over the last ten years, as the study investigate, which might have an impact on the ecosystem’s health, water quality, and effectiveness. The data shows a significant increase in magnesium levels in catchment from 2014 to 2024, raising concerns about water quality and ecosystem health. While the exact cause of this rise remains uncertain, it is likely influenced by geological factors, industrial activities, and environmental changes23.

Sulphate concentrations increased both in magnitude and spatial extent. Nitrate levels showed substantial elevation, with several locations exceeding safe drinking thresholds (>39 mg/L). The sulfate levels in the Mubuku River catchment have shown a significant increase from 2014 to 2024, raising serious concerns about water quality. From, Fig. 11(a), it is observed that in 2014, sulfate concentrations ranged from 10.06 mg/L to 94.38 mg/L, with most of the river catchment falling within moderate levels. However, a few isolated areas already exhibited the highest concentrations, indicating localized pollution hotspots. By 2024 from Fig. 11(b), sulfate levels had risen to a range of 30.96 mg/L to 96.87 mg/L. The most striking change is the nearly fourfold increase in the minimum sulfate concentration, while the maximum levels have only slightly increased, suggesting that high pollution levels have persisted and spread. Geographic distribution maps reveal a larger dark blue area in 2024, indicating that sulfate pollution is now affecting more regions of the river basin.

Observations from Fig. 12(a) and Fig. 12(b), implicates, most of the locations in the catchment had nitrate values ranges between 13.89 mg/L and 18.15 mg/L, certain places have somewhat increased values (over 18 mg/L), indicating probable nitrate contamination. Lower values in certain locations fall under the acceptable drinking water guidelines (less than 15 mg/L). In comparison to 2014, nitrate levels are now significantly higher in all locations. Concentrations above 39 mg/L, which is dangerously high, are now present in a number of locations. Nitrate levels in most areas are more than 34 mg/L, which is an unacceptable threat to human health and water quality35. Elevated nitrate levels indicate heightened pollution, maybe resulting from sewage contamination, industrial discharge, or agricultural runoff. Compared to the maximum level seen in 2014, the lowest nitrate concentration in 2024 is far greater.

Discussion- chemical degradation and anthropogenic influence

The significant rise in TDS is a critical indicator of water quality deterioration linked to both climatic and human drivers. The observed 45% increase aligns with global studies demonstrating that drought conditions intensify solute concentrations due to reduced dilution capacity10,11. Additionally, agricultural intensification and fertilizer use likely contribute to elevated nutrient and ion concentrations, particularly nitrates and sulphates35,6. The spatial overlap between high TDS zones and rainfall vulnerability areas reinforces the combined effect of hydroclimatic stress and land-use practices.

The increase in hardness, calcium, and magnesium further suggests enhanced geochemical weathering and groundwater contributions, which are common under prolonged dry conditions34,36. These changes not only affect drinking water quality but also have implications for agricultural productivity and infrastructure. The abrupt rise in nitrate concentrations exceeding safe limits in multiple locations, indicates serious contamination risks, likely from agricultural runoff and wastewater discharge35. Similar patterns have been reported in intensively farmed catchments, where nutrient leaching is exacerbated by rainfall variability8. Elevated nitrate levels pose significant health risks, including methemoglobinemia and long-term chronic diseases, highlighting the urgent need for pollution control measures.

Biological water quality parameters

Total coliform

Over the past decade, the water quality of the river has taken a worrying turn. Total coliform levels (Fig. 13(a) and Fig. 13(b)), a key indicator of water contamination, have risen significantly from 2014 to 2024. Back in 2014, coliform concentrations ranged from 23 CFU/100 mL to 43 CFU/100 mL, with the highest levels found in the central section of the river catchment37. The outer areas were relatively cleaner, with the lowest recorded level at 23 CFU/100 mL. However, by 2024, the situation had worsened dramatically. Coliform levels now range from 38 CFU/100 mL to 68 CFU/100 mL, with the central region still being the most polluted, reaching a concerning 68 CFU/100 mL. Even the outer areas, which were once less affected, now show much higher contamination levels compared to a decade ago. This steady increase in coliform levels is a clear sign that the river’s water quality is deteriorating. Coliform bacteria, often linked to fecal contamination, pose serious risks to human health, including waterborne diseases like diarrhea and cholera. The rise in contamination is likely due to a combination of factors, including poor waste management, agricultural runoff, and inadequate sanitation infrastructure in the surrounding communities38.

Fecal coliform

Fecal coliform levels in the Mubuku River catchment Fig. 14(a) and Fig. 14(b), have alarmingly increased over the last ten years, indicating a deterioration in the water’s quality. The range of fecal coliform values in 2014 was 14 CFU/100 mL – 18 CFU/100 mL. The northern region had the lowest pollution, at 14 CFU/100 mL, while the southeast was most affected, with levels as high as 17 CFU/100 mL –18 CFU/100 mL. Throughout the river, moderate contamination levels (15 CFU/100 mL – 16 CFU/100 mL) were more common39. Nevertheless, things had gotten much worse by 2024. Today, fecal coliform levels vary from 19 CFU/100 mL to 26 CFU/100 mL, with the southern and southeastern regions having the highest contamination (24 CFU/100 mL – 26 CFU/100 mL). The northern region, which is still the least contaminated, noticed an increase to 19 CFU/100 mL, while the middle region primarily exhibits moderate contamination levels (21 CFU/100 mL – 23 CFU/100 mL).

Discussion

The increasing trends in total and fecal coliforms indicate worsening microbial water quality, strongly linked to inadequate sanitation, runoff, and climate-4driven hydrological changes37,38. Higher rainfall intensity can transport pathogens into water bodies, while low flows during drought reduce dilution, increasing contamination levels2. The persistent contamination in central and downstream regions suggests point-source pollution combined with diffuse agricultural inputs. These findings are consistent with studies in similar developing regions, where climate variability amplifies waterborne disease risks2,39. Overall, the results demonstrate a strong interaction between climate variability and anthropogenic pressures in driving water quality degradation. The Mubuku River basin is experiencing: Reduced dilution capacity due to drought, Increased pollutant loading from agriculture and settlements, Enhanced geochemical processes under warming conditions. These findings align with global assessments by the World Bank7 and UN Water40, which highlight climate change as a key driver of freshwater quality deterioration.

Discussion

The integrated WQI–GIS analysis reveals a progressive and spatially heterogeneous decline in water quality across the Mubuku River catchment over the past decade. The deterioration is most severe in downstream and anthropogenically influenced zones, driven by the combined effects of climate variability, agricultural intensification, and urban expansion. The dominance of nitrate and hardness-related parameters in the WQI further indicates a shift toward chemically degraded water systems, with significant implications for public health, agriculture, and ecosystem sustainability. Immediate implementation of integrated catchment management strategies, including pollution source control, climate-resilient water planning, and continuous monitoring, is essential to reverse the current trends.

Conclusion

The analysis of rainfall variability in Kasese and Fort Portal districts (2014–2024) reveals pronounced hydroclimatic instability characterized by both drought and wet extremes. SPI-based results indicate that 2014, 2016, 2023, and 2024 experienced drought-induced vulnerability, while years such as 2020 and 2021, although previously considered “normal,” are now interpreted as low drought but high runoff-induced vulnerability due to above-normal rainfall. This dual framework clarifies that water resource stress in the basin arises under both precipitation deficits and excess conditions. Drought periods reduce dilution capacity and increase baseflow dominance, whereas wet periods intensify surface runoff, erosion, and contaminant transport23.

Water quality trends show significant deterioration across physicochemical and biological parameters. The pH readings increased from 7.1 to 8.3, suggesting alkaline conditions from industrial and agricultural sources. The increase in TDS, electrical conductivity, and hardness is strongly linked to enhanced groundwater contributions during drought, where prolonged rock–water interaction in mineral-rich formations elevates Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ concentrations17. The nitrate content exceeded 50 mg/L at certain testing sites, which is attributed to agricultural intensification, wastewater inputs, and runoff mobilization during wet periods, particularly in identified hotspot locations. In terms of biological indicators, it was found that the fecal coliform levels rose from 50 CFU/100mL to 75 CFU/100mL, alongside elevated total coliform counts that surpassed WHO guidelines17.

Water Quality Index results confirm spatial degradation, with sites such as WS4 and WS6 deteriorating from ‘Very Poor’ to ‘Unsuitable’ status. Similarly, locations WS10 and WS11 dropped from ‘Good’ to ‘Poor’ class. GIS-based analysis further demonstrates a strong spatial correspondence between hydroclimatic variability and water quality decline4,9. Overall, the findings highlight that water quality degradation is driven by the combined effects of climate variability (drought and runoff extremes), geological controls, and land-use pressures. This underscores the need for integrated management strategies, including regulation of fertilizer use, protection of recharge zones, and targeted interventions in vulnerable hotspots to ensure sustainable water resource management.

The findings of this study underscore the need for targeted and operational water resource management strategies within the Mubuku River Catchment, particularly under increasing climate variability and spatially heterogeneous water quality conditions.
