Abstract
The analytic-synthetic distinction, from Kant to Carnap, Quine, Goodman and Davidson, remains tied to open questions
about necessity and the a priori. We argue that part of the difficulty arises because inquiry has focused on the products
of cognition (concepts, propositions, formal systems) rather than on the cognitive instrument that produces them. We
aimed to state the operational hypothesis, to test it against the literature published between 2021 and 2026 without
altering its content, and, in light of that literature, to sharpen its criteria, answer the main objections and derive empirical
predictions. Five lines of evidence converge: inference precedes logical words; new knowledge can arise without new
observation; geometric cognition works through compositional mental programs; arithmetic, although cultural, remains
constrained by a stable cognitive architecture; and current philosophy of logic regards both classical psychologism and
Fregean anti-psychologism as overstated. On this basis, we refine the concept of operational necessity with a noncontainment criterion, index necessity to the operation, distinguish it from Piaget's genetic epistemology, answer the
rule-following problem and locate normativity in the operation itself. We also propose a taxonomy of five sources of
necessity (definitional, conceptual, operational, formal and metaphysical) and derive four testable predictions, including
a clinical dissociation between definitional and operational necessity. The literature supports shifting the analysis from
the propositional product to the operational instrument and makes plausible a necessity grounded in the functional
invariants of cognitive operations, without a return to psychologism.
Keywords: Psychiatry; Cognition; Logic; Philosophy; Concept Formation; Mathematical Concepts.

1. Introduction
Since Kant, much of the discussion of necessary knowledge has turned on three concepts: the analytic, the synthetic and
the necessary. The Kantian formulation left a question that later philosophy never fully settled: how can a proposition extend
knowledge and still be necessary (Kant, 1998)? Analytic philosophy then moved the debate toward language, semantics and
formal systems. Carnap (1947) explicated analyticity through the rules of particular linguistic systems. Quine (1951) doubted
that the analytic-synthetic distinction could be drawn without circularity. Goodman (1983) showed that past experience is
compatible with different ways of projecting properties into the future. Davidson (1974), extending Quine's line of thought,
rejected the separation between a conceptual scheme and an empirical content given independently of it.
These contributions changed the problem for good, yet they share a starting point. By the time the question is asked, the
proposition has been formed, the concept exists, language has been constituted and the logical relation has been symbolized.
Only then does one ask whether the proposition is analytic or synthetic, necessary or contingent. Our hypothesis is that the
discussion begins too late in the cognitive process. Between the object in the world and the finished proposition stands a cognitive
instrument that works on objects, events, representations and concepts, and the problem of necessity has to be examined at that
level as well. We call this the operational hypothesis.
The hypothesis belongs to Cognitive Psychiatry, understood here as the study of the cognitive instruments through
which mental life is organized and of the ways in which these instruments shape both normal functioning and its disturbances.
Its model rests on three concepts. Praxization names the active process through which the organism explores, coordinates,
manipulates, compares, anticipates and relates what it encounters. At its originary level, logic is the relatively invariant structure
of the operations carried out in praxization, and formal logic is a later abstraction of these operations. The cognitive instrument
differs both from the object on which it acts and from the product it generates. Concepts, for their part, are not closed containers;
they carry multiple "theoretical hooks", possible relations that may stay latent until an operation activates them, so that cognitive
novelty can arise without new empirical information. Operational necessity, finally, is the necessity that arises from the
constitutive invariants of a cognitive operation. Table 1 gives working definitions of these terms, and Figure 1 shows the sequence
that links them.

Stated as an argument, the hypothesis has four premises and one conclusion:
P1. Every logical or mathematical relation expressed in a proposition presupposes cognitive operations that
distinguish, conserve and relate elements.
P2. Every cognitive operation has functional invariants without which it would cease to be the same operation.
P3. Some consequences depend only on these invariants, are absent from the explicit specification of the
operation and become available only through its execution.
P4. Such consequences cannot fail while the operation is preserved, and recognizing them requires no new
experience.
C. There is a modality of necessity, operational necessity, whose source is the operation and which functionally
precedes its conceptual, propositional and formal expression.

Sections 3.1, 3.3 and 3.5 examine P1 and P2; Section 3.4 examines P3, which introduces the non-containment criterion;
Sections 3.2 and 3.13 examine P4. Section 3.14 gathers the objections to the conclusion.

2. Methodology
A descriptive, critical, and qualitative study with limited systematization (Pereira et al., 2018; Risemberg et al., 2026a,
2026b) was conducted as a bibliographic research study (Snyder, 2019) in the form of a narrative review (Fernandes et al., 2023;
Ogassavara et al., 2025).
We carried out a critical narrative review of a theoretical and conceptual nature. The object of study is a theoretical
claim that crosses disciplines with distinct methods (philosophy of logic, developmental psychology, cognitive neuroscience and
philosophy of mathematics), so protocols designed for the quantitative synthesis of effects would not fit it. A narrative review
allows heterogeneous evidence to be gathered and weighed by its argumentative relevance to a well-defined conceptual question.
Searches covered PhilPapers, PubMed Central, SpringerLink, Cambridge Core, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar from
January 2021 to September 2026. We used the following English descriptors, alone and combined, together with their Portuguese
equivalents: analytic-synthetic distinction, synthetic a priori, psychologism, anti-exceptionalism about logic, logical reasoning
in infancy, disjunctive syllogism, learning by thinking, geometric cognition, language of thought, numerical cognition,
enactivism, projectibility, natural concepts, rule-following, modal cognition and semantic dementia. We included peer-reviewed
articles and academic books dealing with at least one of six axes: the cognitive origin of logical operations; knowledge acquisition
without new observation; geometric and arithmetical cognition; the relation between cognitive genesis and logical validity; the
naturalness and projectibility of concepts; and the organism-world relation in the formation of categories. We excluded texts
without peer review, purely technical work in formal logic and studies with no argumentative link to the premises.
The classical works of Kant, Frege, Husserl, Carnap, Quine, Goodman, Davidson, Piaget, Wittgenstein and Kripke
entered as primary sources regardless of date, because they are the direct interlocutors of the hypothesis. For the same reason,
two reference neuropsychological studies on the dissociation between semantic and arithmetical knowledge were included, given
their bearing on the predictions in Section 3.13. The final corpus comprised 33 references: 20 published between 2021 and 2026,
11 classical works and 2 clinical studies. The six methodological references cited at the opening of this section support the
classification of the study and are not part of the reviewed corpus. The analysis was thematic and deductive. We read each study
against the components of the model (operational level, theoretical hooks, geometric construction, functional invariance, nonpsychologism, rule-following and the relation with each classical interlocutor), recording points of agreement, complementarity
and objection.

3.1 The operational level: logic before logical language
According to P1, a system able to manipulate symbols such as P, Q and P → Q must already be able to identify,
differentiate, order, keep identities, relate antecedents to consequents and conserve relations during an operation. If P1 holds,
elementary logical operations should be observable before children master the words that name them. Recent developmental
research offers data on this point.
Cesana-Arlotti and Halberda (2024) found that children aged 2.3 to 3.2 years use disjunctive inference (A or B; not A;
therefore B) in very different situations, such as learning new words, learning facts about a person and identifying the winner of
a race, before any schooling and before they command the words that express logical meanings. The authors read the finding as
evidence of a general-purpose logical instrument, usable in any domain. What they describe is what our model calls a cognitive
instrument: a way of operating that stays the same across contents, not a verbal rule.
The empirical picture is contested, and the dispute is informative. In five studies with infants, Feiman et al. (2022)
examined the emerging ability to reason by exclusion with negation and argued that some very early successes admit explanations
that do not require full representations of disjunction and negation. The disagreement does not concern whether a pre-verbal
level of operation exists; it concerns the format of these operations and the age at which they stabilize. For the operational
hypothesis, the relevant question is which operation, with which invariants, produces the observed inferential behaviour, rather

recursively into mental programs supported by cortical circuits distinct from the classical language areas. Representational and
non-representational approaches disagree on much, but both place logical structure below verbal language, as Figure 1 assumes.

3.2 Theoretical hooks and intracognitive synthesis: novelty without new experience
We also hold that a proposition can be new to consciousness without resulting from new empirical input. Someone may
know that dogs and cats both have four legs without ever having set these facts side by side. On doing so, the person arrives at
the relation "both are, in this respect, quadrupeds" without having observed any new animal. The simple opposition between the
analytic as "nothing new" and the synthetic as "new information" misses this phenomenon, which the model calls intracognitive
synthesis.
Lombrozo's (2024) review of learning by thinking gives direct support to the idea. The author gathers evidence that
cognitive systems acquire new knowledge, without new observations, through explanation, simulation, analogy and reasoning.
Thought experiments are the classic case: Galileo and Einstein reached conclusions about the physical world without new data.
Lombrozo also shows that artificial systems display the same phenomenon, which suggests a functional property of certain
cognitive architectures rather than an accident of individual psychology.
For the model, the finding matters in two ways. It confirms that epistemic novelty is not the same as recent sensory
acquisition, which undoes the tacit equation between synthesis and new experience. And the mechanisms Lombrozo describes
are operations on contents already available, ways of activating latent theoretical hooks. Explanation links one fact to another,
analogy carries a structure from one domain to another and simulation runs a transformation internally. Each time, a relational
operation applied to contents already constituted yields a new cognitive configuration.

3.3 Geometric construction and operational necessity
Geometric construction is the central case for the model. When three segments are joined and the figure is closed, three
meeting points, three vertices and three angular relations necessarily appear. Nothing here had to be extracted from the word
"triangle" or learned by inspecting external triangles; the result comes from the structure of the operation performed. The original
formulation of operational necessity lists four criteria: an identifiable operation; the existence of invariants; the necessary
dependence of the result on the operation; and independence from new experience during the demonstration.
Work on the cognitive neuroscience of geometry published in the review period fits this description well. Sablé-Meyer
et al. (2021) compared the perception of quadrilaterals in humans of different cultures and ages and in baboons, using the same
intruder-detection task. Humans were markedly sensitive to geometric regularity (right angles, parallelism, symmetry), whereas
baboons were not, a contrast the authors interpreted as a possible signature of human cognitive singularity. Sablé-Meyer et al.
(2022) then proposed that humans have a language of thought for geometric shapes, able to produce line drawings as recursive
combinations of a small set of primitives. Formalized as a Logo-like programming language that combines discrete numbers and
continuous integration through repetition, concatenation and embedding, the model generates the basic geometric shapes with
its simplest programs, and minimal program length predicts how complex a shape seems to observers.

repeated) are consequences of the operation, neither empirical additions nor definitional content. A program that repeats that
instruction three times until the figure closes cannot run without producing three meeting points, and removing this outcome
requires a different program. In computational terms, this is the third criterion of operational necessity. What Sablé-Meyer et al.
call a mental program is, in the vocabulary of the model, a cognitive instrument of spatialization and composition.
The phenomenological tradition, read in light of the cognitive sciences, supplies the side of praxization. Halák (2022)
reconstructs Merleau-Ponty's claim that geometry and algebra are fields of motor enaction and argues that the structural
transformations producing geometric knowledge are carried out through motor intentionality. On this reading, building a figure
is not the contemplation of an essence but an oriented movement whose regularities become available as structure. The
computational approach of Dehaene's group and Halák's enactive approach start from different premises and reach a common
point: geometric necessity appears in the execution of a construction before any proposition records it.

3.4 Refining the concept: non-containment and conditional character
An obvious objection threatens the definition. If a consequence is necessary because its absence would turn the operation
into another one, one may reply that the necessity is contained in the description of the operation and amounts to analyticity
moved from the concept to the procedure. The three-vertex example is especially exposed, because "three closed segments"
already suggests three meeting points. To block the objection, we make explicit a fifth criterion, implicit in the original
formulation:
Non-containment. The consequence is absent from the explicit specification of the elements and rules of the operation.
It becomes available only through executing the operation, or inspecting that execution, and not through analysing the
description that defines it.
The sum of the interior angles of a triangle satisfies all five criteria, and Kant himself used it to characterize
mathematical construction (Kant, 1998). The specification "plane figure closed by three segments" says nothing about the angles
summing to two right angles. The result appears only when an additional operation is carried out, Euclid's construction in
Elements I.32: through one vertex, a line parallel to the opposite side is drawn; the alternate angles thus formed equal the two
base angles, and the three angles gathered at the vertex make a straight angle. No new triangles need to be observed, and no
analysis of the word "triangle" yields the result. The same holds for Kant's 7 + 5 = 12, obtained by adding five units to seven one
by one and not by decomposing the concepts "seven", "five" and "sum". The three-vertex case still serves as an introductory
illustration, but it sits on the border between definitional and operational necessity, whereas the angle sum and addition meet the
non-containment criterion without ambiguity.
The angle sum also raises a second objection. In non-Euclidean geometries the interior angles of a triangle do not sum
to two right angles; on a sphere, the sum is greater. Operational necessity survives, provided its conditional character is stated.
Operational necessity is always indexed to an operation: given the invariants of an operation O, the consequence C cannot fail
to occur. Euclid's construction depends on one specific invariant, the uniqueness of the parallel through an external point, and
that invariant produces the sum of two right angles. On a surface of positive curvature, drawing parallels lacks this invariant, so
the operation is a different one, with different necessary consequences. Far from weakening the operational hypothesis,
alternative geometries illustrate it: a different consequence required a different operation.
Indexing necessity to an operation brings a further benefit. Debates after Quine often run together two kinds of revision:
revising which operation to adopt and revising a consequence inside an operation already adopted. The first kind is real and well

3.5 Functional invariance, genesis and a cognitive reading of the a priori
The model limits carefully what it means for the cognitive instrument to be "fixed". The brain is not anatomically
immutable; the nervous system develops, changes with learning and varies between individuals. What the model requires is that
an operation keep enough stability to be identified as the same operation throughout its execution. From here follows the
distinction between genetic and functional priority: a structure may appear late in history and still be functionally prior to the
operations that depend on it. Having an origin does not make each later use contingent.
Pantsar's (2024) epistemology of arithmetic develops this distinction more systematically than any other recent work.
On the basis of an extensive review of numerical cognition, he argues that arithmetic is a human creation and a sophisticated
cultural product whose development is nonetheless constrained by an evolved cognitive architecture, grounded in protoarithmetical abilities present in infants and shared with other animals. Pantsar asks the philosophy of mathematics to pay more
attention to the origin of mathematical knowledge and less exclusively to its nature, and he argues that addition developed as a
direct continuation of counting, which in turn made the other operations possible. His account follows the left half of Figure 1,
from practice to a stable instrument, and illustrates what the model calls the instrument factory.
Pantsar does not, however, conclude that arithmetic is contingent. In a companion paper, he explains arithmetical
objectivity by the maximal intersubjectivity produced by the development of arithmetical cognition: the same operations, built
on the same architecture, give the same results to everyone who performs them correctly (Pantsar, 2023). Put differently,
historical genesis does not remove functional necessity. Zahidi (2021), writing from a radical enactivist position, reaches a
structurally similar result by another route, showing that numerical abilities can be explained through practices and interactions
without turning the regularity of their results into a psychological accident.
With these elements, the a priori can be reinterpreted while the Kantian problem is kept in view. Recent Kant
scholarship shows that explaining how synthetic a priori knowledge is possible remains a sensitive point for transcendental
idealism (Stern, 2022). Our hypothesis keeps Kant's insight that the mind organizes, synthesizes and constructs, and that
construction in intuition is central to mathematics (Kant, 1998), but it redirects the inquiry. Besides the transcendental conditions
of experience, we ask which instrument actually performs the constructions and which invariants of its functioning produce
necessary relations. On this reading, the a priori designates whatever already functions as a structural condition before a given
cognitive operation is performed. Piaget's genetic epistemology is the closest historical precedent for such a reading, and Section
3.6 compares the two.

3.6 Operational necessity and Piaget’s genetic epistemology
Piaget treated logical structures as abstractions from the coordination of actions and devoted a two-volume work to the
formation of necessity in cognitive development (Piaget, 1987). In his account, necessity is not given at the outset. Children pass
through phases of pseudo-necessity, in which the habitual is taken as obligatory, before reaching necessities grounded in the
composition of operations, in a process regulated by equilibration. Since Piaget and the operational hypothesis both place the
origin of necessity in operations rather than in propositions, the differences between them need to be spelled out.

(Cesana-Arlotti & Halberda, 2024; Cesana-Arlotti et al., 2025) has overturned the Piagetian timetable, which placed operational
logic in middle childhood. The same evidence weakens stage theory while leaving the operational hypothesis intact, because
operational necessity is not bound to a stage. It appears whenever an operation with defined invariants is executed, which explains
why it can be observed far earlier than Piaget expected.

3.7 Why the operational hypothesis is not psychologism
Any cognitive account of logic invites the charge of psychologism. In the form attacked by Frege (1980) and Husserl
(1970), psychologism reduces logical laws to facts about how people actually think. We reject that reduction. Psychological
regularity does not amount to logical necessity, and people make errors, reason inconsistently and draw invalid inferences. Two
questions have to be kept apart: what makes an inference formally valid, and which cognitive structures allow a subject to build,
recognize and use that relation. The second question does not replace the first, and the first gives no reason to drop the second.
Recent philosophy of logic makes room for this separation. Rudnicki and Łukowski (2024) note that contemporary
logicians have proposed weakened versions of psychologism, according to which logic applied in the cognitive sciences need
not tell us about the ontology or metaphysics of reasoning. They argue that both classical psychologism and Fregean antipsychologism were needlessly strong, since they placed logic wholly inside or wholly outside the psychological domain. Their
diagnosis matches a worry built into our model: in guarding against the confusion of logical truth with psychological occurrence,
part of the tradition stopped asking how an organism becomes able to perform logical operations at all.
The operational hypothesis goes further than these weakened versions. Its object is not individual psychological content
but the functional structure of an operation. A person may make a mistake when adding, and the mistake is a psychological fact;
it leaves unchanged what correct addition consists in. The relevant question is which conditions must hold for this operation to
remain recognizable as that operation, not what this person happened to think. Pantsar's (2023) account of objectivity as maximal
intersubjectivity supports the distinction, since the norm comes from the shared invariants of a correctly executed operation and
not from what one individual does. Logic and psychology are not identified; we claim only that the cognitive possibility of logic
depends on operational instruments that can be studied.
The source of normativity still needs to be named, and here the charge of psychologism usually returns. If a mistaken
sum leaves correct addition untouched, something must fix what "correct" means. Formal logic cannot play that role without
circularity, and the subject cannot play it without psychologism. We propose a third option: the norm is internal to the operation.
An operation with defined invariants has its own means of detecting deviations, independent both of an external formal system
and of the subject's idiosyncratic judgment. A sum can be checked by the inverse operation (12 − 5 = 7) or by another route (7
+ 3 + 2); a geometric result, by the agreement of different constructions. An error is an execution that violates an invariant, and
the operation itself can reveal it. When independent routes converge on the same result for everyone who performs the operation,
we have what Pantsar (2023) calls objectivity grounded in maximal intersubjectivity. Normativity is neither imported from
outside cognition nor reduced to an individual mental state.

3.8 The rule-following problem
The hardest philosophical objection comes not from classical anti-psychologism but from the rule-following problem.
Our definition depends on the phrase "while the identity of the operation is preserved". Kripke (1982), reading Wittgenstein,
argued that no fact about a subject's past behaviour or mental states settles whether the subject was adding or computing a
different function that agrees with addition in every case computed so far and diverges afterwards. If no fact fixes which operation
is being performed, constitutive invariants would seem to have nothing to rest on.
We answer with three considerations. First, the identity of an operation is fixed by the functional invariants of the
instrument that executes it, not by an interpretation of a rule that could itself be reinterpreted without end. Kripke raised two
complaints against dispositional answers: dispositions are finite, and they are not normative. The first complaint misses the
target, because a functional invariant is a structural relation (keeping units distinct and adding them one by one), not a list of
responses, and it applies to indefinitely many cases. The second is met by the internal norm described in Section 3.7, since the
operation contains the means to detect its own violation.
Second, Wittgenstein himself offers a description close to ours. In the Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics, a
proof turns an empirical proposition into a rule, converting a testable hypothesis into a paradigm against which experience is
then compared and judged (Wittgenstein, 1978). The model describes the same passage, from operational regularities formed in
practice to stable conditions for later operations. The two accounts part ways at one point: Wittgenstein treats the passage as a
grammatical fact, while we ask which cognitive instrument makes it possible.
Third, recent scholarship has sharpened a distinction that serves the hypothesis. Bold (2024) shows that the interlocutor's
question in Wittgenstein, whether the steps of a series are determined by the algebraic formula, comes from crossing two senses
of "determine": a behavioural sense, about what an agent or a machine actually does, and a mathematical sense, about what the
rule requires. The machine analogy Bold examines makes the point: a real machine can bend, jam or break, yet the machine
taken as a symbol of its mode of operation determines its movements. Our model draws the same line between the contingent
mental state, which can err, and the constitutive structure of the operation, which fixes its consequences. Operational necessity
belongs to the mathematical sense and psychological error to the behavioural one. Once the two senses are separated, the paradox
loses its grip, and something still fixes the identity of the operation.

3.9 Carnap, Quine and logical anti-exceptionalism
Carnap (1947) located much of analyticity in the rules of a constituted language: within a system L, certain propositions
are true by virtue of the rules of L. The project has great formal power but starts at an advanced point. Before anyone can build
a formal language, the organism must already distinguish symbols, keep them identical, order and substitute them, recognize
equivalences and follow rules. Carnap analyses the last stage of Figure 1 with great precision; we are interested in the stages that
make it possible.
Quine (1951) shook the traditional distinction by showing that synonymy and truth by virtue of meaning cannot be
explained without concepts just as problematic, and by treating beliefs as a web that faces experience as a whole. The most
influential heir of this line in the last decade is anti-exceptionalism about logic, the view that logic has no special epistemic status
relative to the sciences. Martin and Hjortland (2021) proposed logical predictivism, in which logical theories are chosen for their
predictive and explanatory success, as in the established sciences. The same authors later traced the path from naturalism to antiexceptionalism and set out its component claims (Martin & Hjortland, 2024), and Ferrari and Carrara (2025) examined in detail
the relations between logic and science the view presupposes.

Anti-exceptionalism might seem to threaten operational necessity, since it treats logical laws as revisable. On closer
inspection the two views work at different levels and complement each other. Anti-exceptionalism concerns the choice among
formal logical theories, systems that are already products of formalization. We can grant much of it: the boundary between
analytic and synthetic propositions may be blurred, and formal logical theories may be revisable. Nothing follows about the
invariants of the operations that make logical theorizing possible in the first place. In the terms of Figure 1, Quine and his
successors showed that no sharp boundary can be drawn at the level of propositions, which does not show that there are no
invariants at the level of operations. The Quinean critique of semantic analyticity does not, on its own, rule out operational
necessity.

3.10 Goodman and the cognitive architecture of categories
Goodman's new riddle of induction shows that the same past data can support divergent projections. All emeralds
observed so far are green, but they are also "grue", a predicate defined to coincide with "green" up to a given time (Goodman,
1983). What needs explaining is why some properties are cognitively favoured for generalization, since the observed data alone
do not decide. The model suggests going one level down and asking why the cognitive instrument tends to preserve continuity,
identity, similarity, stable properties and temporal regularity in some ways and not others.
Recent work on natural concepts takes this route. Scholz (2024) reconstructs Gärdenfors' attempt to solve the riddle
with the theory of conceptual spaces: projectible predicates refer to natural concepts, and naturalness is captured by similarity,
represented geometrically as distance, together with a convexity criterion that excludes predicates such as "grue". Scholz also
records Gärdenfors' methodological advice that logical analysis is not enough and that one must go below language. The
operational hypothesis says as much in its own terms: the problem of induction reaches down to the cognitive architecture of
categories.
Scholz (2024) nevertheless raises an objection worth absorbing, because it clarifies the reach of the hypothesis. Purely
cognitive criteria of naturalness, he argues, explain why we project certain predicates but do not justify those projections without
an ontological component, some reference to the structure of the world. The model escapes the objection through a defining
feature of praxization: operations form in the interaction between organism and world, not in an isolated mind. The instrument
that favours "green" over "grue" was built through action on objects whose properties stay stable, so the worldly component is
already part of how the instrument came to be. Goodman states the problem; the model suggests that part of the answer lies in
the architecture of categorizing operations, studied together with the world in which they were formed.

3.11 Davidson and the organism-world relation
Davidson (1974) rejected the picture of a raw empirical content later organized by an independent conceptual scheme.
The model is compatible with that critique. It does not posit a formless world on one side and a mind imposing arbitrary categories
on the other; praxization is relational, and the cognitive instrument forms and acts in interaction. Recent enactivist work
elaborates this relation for abstract abilities. Zahidi (2021) argues that numerical cognition, often thought to lie beyond nonrepresentational explanation, can be accounted for through practices and interactions, and Halák (2022) treats geometry as a field
of motor enaction.
To these contributions the model adds a claim they leave implicit: interaction between organism and world produces
functional instruments with invariants, and these invariants can become necessary conditions for later operations. Enactivism
shows where the instruments come from; operational necessity explains why, once formed, they yield results that cannot differ

The central claim can now be restated with the support of the reviewed literature. Philosophy often begins by declaring
a proposition necessary; the operational hypothesis asks first which operation produced the relation the proposition expresses.
The necessity observed at the propositional level may derive from a necessity already present at the level of the operation, as the
shaded box in Figure 1 indicates. In that case the proposition records a necessity it did not create. Methodologically, this amounts
to changing the frame from which a persistent problem is viewed: instead of classifying finished propositions, the analysis begins
with the operation that constitutes the relation.

3.13 Empirical predictions
A conceptual hypothesis gains weight when it yields predictions that could fail. Operational necessity yields at least
four.
Prediction 1 (development). Recognizing that a consequence cannot be otherwise should follow mastery of the
operation that produces it and precede, or be independent of, modal vocabulary. The available data are consistent with this.
Cesana-Arlotti et al. (2025) showed that children aged 34 to 65 months make decisions that respect the categories of necessity,
possibility and impossibility, doing better when choices cross these categories than when they differ only in probability, a pattern
that points to modal concepts in use before modal language. Leahy et al. (2022) found that three-year-olds facing several open
possibilities build an overly specific model of the situation and fail to separate mere possibility from necessity. Modal judgment
thus has a developmental course tied to the operations a child commands. A direct test would teach a new operation and check
whether necessity judgments about its consequences track mastery of the operation or merely time spent with examples.
Prediction 2 (clinical dissociation). If definitional and operational necessity have different sources, selective brain
lesions should separate them. Loss of semantic knowledge should impair the first while sparing the second, as long as the
operational instrument is intact; damage to the operational instrument should produce the reverse pattern. The neuropsychology
of semantic dementia supports the first half. Some patients lose the meaning of common words yet keep calculation and
conceptual knowledge of arithmetic, as in the patient described by Zamarian et al. (2006), who knew the result of 7 × 8 but not
the meaning of "elephant". Preservation varies across patients, and the anterior temporal atrophy typical of the disease tends to
spare the parietal lobes, which support calculation (Julien et al., 2008). The model accounts for the variation: calculation should
survive as long as the regions sustaining the operational instrument do. The reverse half has not been tested in these terms.
Patients with acalculia or constructional apraxia after parietal damage should keep definitional knowledge (a triangle has three
sides) while losing the recognition of the necessary consequences of construction (for example, that the angle sum does not vary).
Because of its closeness to clinical neuropsychiatry, Cognitive Psychiatry is well placed to carry out such studies.
Prediction 3 (independence from frequency). Properties that follow from the structure of a construction should be
judged necessary with more confidence, and should resist counterexamples better, than properties equally frequent in the
observed examples but not derived from the construction. The mental-program paradigms for geometric shapes (Sablé-Meyer et
al., 2022) allow a direct test, contrasting program-derived properties with properties only statistically associated with the figures.

3.15 Limitations and research agenda
Three limitations apply. The first is methodological: a narrative review does not aim at exhaustiveness, and the selection
of studies, though guided by explicit criteria, involves judgments of argumentative relevance. The second is empirical: part of
the evidence on early logical inference remains disputed as to the format of the representations involved (Feiman et al., 2022;
Cesana-Arlotti & Halberda, 2024), and language-of-thought models compete with non-representational enactive alternatives
(Zahidi, 2021). Both disputes concern the format of operations, not the existence of an operational level prior to formalization,
which is all the hypothesis needs. The third is conceptual: the non-containment criterion and the working definitions narrow the
room for circularity, but the invariants of each operation still have to be identified domain by domain, and the predictions in
Section 3.13 have yet to be tested directly.
These limitations suggest an agenda. Mathematics is the natural testing ground, because counting, grouping, ordering,
matching, establishing succession and conserving quantity can be studied as structured operations, and one can ask which of
their invariants yield the relations later formalized. The mental-program paradigms for geometric shapes (Sablé-Meyer et al.,
2022) already provide an experimental tool for checking whether properties derived from a construction are recognized as
necessary without further empirical inspection. Within Cognitive Psychiatry itself, the clinical dissociations described in Section

4. Conclusion
The review of work published between 2021 and 2026 found support for the operational hypothesis in fields that rarely
read one another. Developmental studies document inferential operations before children master logical words; research on
learning by thinking shows that cognitive novelty can arise without new external experience; the neuroscience of geometry
describes shapes as mental programs whose properties follow from how they are built; the epistemology of arithmetic separates
cultural genesis from functional objectivity; and current philosophy of logic regards both classical psychologism and Fregean
anti-psychologism as overstated. None of these studies formulates the concept of operational necessity, but together they make
it plausible and mark out where it applies.
Engaging this literature also made the concept sturdier. Non-containment separates operational necessity from
analyticity relocated to the description of a procedure. Indexing necessity to an operation accommodates non-Euclidean geometry
and Quinean revisability. The contrast between behavioural and mathematical determination answers the rule-following problem,
the internal norm of the operation answers the charge of psychologism, and the comparison with Piaget clarifies what is
distinctive about the hypothesis. The four predictions, on development, clinical dissociation, independence from frequency and
indexing to the operation, turn the hypothesis into a research programme open to empirical test.
Operational necessity is not psychological habit. It does not make something logically true because people tend to think
that way; it refers to the constitutive invariants of specific operations. The question it addresses survives the critique of
psychologism: how can a cognitive system produce and recognize necessary relations? Our answer starts from praxization and
follows the sequence in Figure 1, from action on the world to formal logic. Asking why a proposition is necessary thus leads to
an earlier question, which invariance of the cognitive instrument prevented the relation from being produced otherwise without
the operation ceasing to be what it is. Moving the analysis from product to instrument, from proposition to operation and from
formal to operational necessity is the central contribution of this article to Cognitive Psychiatry.
