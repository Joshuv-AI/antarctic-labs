Electronic and molecular dynamics mechanisms of wogonin adsorption on graphene nanocarriers
Authors: Xiong Li, Wenxu Yang, Tao Yang, Wei Hu, Zuojian Zhou, Chenjun Hu, Jian Zhang, Kongfa Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGraphene is a promising substrate for adsorbing bioactive molecules like wogonin. This study investigates their adsorption mechanism using density functional theory (DFT) and 20 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Results show adsorption significantly enhances wogonin’s planarity (MPP decreases from 0.244 to 0.173) and π-electron delocalization. Hirshfeld analysis confirms physical adsorption with negligible charge transfer. Crucially, binding energies calculated via B3LYP-D3 with gCP correction and high-level wB97X-D3 are − 149.45 and − 134.75 kJ/mol, respectively, while IGMH analysis visually confirms the dominance of π-π stacking. Furthermore, 20 ns MD trajectories demonstrate excellent dynamic stability in a biologically relevant aqueous environment without dissociation. This work provides a robust theoretical baseline for developing graphene-based drug delivery and biosensing platforms for traditional Chinese medicine components.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56435-3.Keywords: Graphene, Wogonin, DFT, Molecular structure, Electronic properties, MDSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Graphene, a two-dimensional carbon nanomaterial composed of carbon atoms in sp² hybrid orbitals forming a hexagonal honeycomb lattice, has garnered significant attention since its discovery. It possesses numerous exceptional properties, such as an ultra-high specific surface area, theoretically reaching up to 2630 m²/g, which provides ample active sites for molecular adsorption1–3. Its outstanding mechanical strength, with a Young’s modulus of approximately 1 TPa, ensures structural stability during adsorption processes, minimizing deformation1,3,4. Additionally, graphene exhibits excellent electrical properties, including high conductivity with electron mobility exceeding 15,000 cm²V⁻¹s⁻¹ at room temperature, and superior thermal conductivity, around 5000 Wm⁻¹K⁻¹5,6. These combined properties highlight graphene’s immense potential as a molecular adsorption substrate across various fields, including gas sensing, environmental pollutant adsorption and purification, and biomedical applications such as drug delivery. These properties collectively position graphene as a next-generation high-performance adsorption material.

In the biomedical field, many bioactive molecules from natural products hold significant medicinal value, with wogonin being one such molecule7,8. Wogonin, a flavonoid compound extracted from the traditional Chinese medicine Scutellaria Baicalensis, has been extensively studied for its broad range of biological activities9,10. These include anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, anti-tumor, and neuroprotective effects, demonstrating promising potential for the treatment of various diseases9,10. The 2D chemical structure of wogonin is shown in Scheme 1.

Wogonin has demonstrated significant application potential across three major therapeutic areas: oncology, inflammatory diseases, and metabolic disorder management. In the anti-tumor domain, its core mechanism involves inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in cancer cells by modulating key signaling pathways. For instance, wogonin promotes the nuclear translocation of the p53 tumor suppressor protein, thereby enhancing DNA repair capacity and inhibiting the proliferation of colon cancer cells. This mechanism significantly reduced tumor incidence in colorectal cancer models11. In breast cancer treatment, wogonin suppresses the PI3K/Akt pathway and downregulates the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein survivin, leading to a marked increase in the apoptosis rate of MCF-7 cells12. Regarding the inflammation-related tumor microenvironment, wogonin’s derivative, wogonoside, effectively inhibits inflammation-driven progression of colorectal precancerous lesions by blocking the PI3K/Akt-mediated activation of the NF-κB pathway. In an AOM/DSS-induced mouse model, wogonoside reduced the tumor burden by over 50%13. In the field of non-oncological inflammatory diseases, wogonin exhibits protective effects across multiple organ systems. In alcoholic liver disease (ALD), it activates the PPAR-γ receptor, significantly reducing the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 in the liver, thereby alleviating hepatocyte inflammatory injury14. In colitis models, wogonin restores the balance of innate lymphoid cell subsets (ILC3/ILC1) and repairs intestinal mucosal barrier function by activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway. Experimental results showed that the expression of the colonic mucin protein MUC-2 in the wogonin-treated group was nearly twice that of the control group15. Regarding neuroprotection, wogonin effectively inhibits the TLR4/NF-κB-mediated neuroinflammatory response. In an optic nerve injury model, it increased the survival rate of retinal ganglion cells by 35%, offering a new potential strategy for glaucoma treatment16. It is important to note that the application of wogonin still faces limitations, such as low oral bioavailability, which restricts its efficacy in systemic diseases17. Recent research is exploring strategies to overcome these challenges, including the use of nanocarrier delivery systems (e.g., framework nucleic acid complexes) to enhance its targeted accumulation in tissues affected by conditions like osteoarthritis18, and the use of microbial conversion technologies to improve the yield of wogonin from natural sources19.

More recently, graphene and graphene derivatives have been used as effective adsorbents and matrices for the detection and separation of wogonin and other bioactive flavonoids from traditional Chinese medicine herbs20–23. The adsorption of wogonin and other bioactive molecules on graphene-based materials can be leveraged to achieve highly sensitive and simultaneous detection of these compounds20–23. Furthermore, the adsorption of bioactive molecules on graphene can alter their molecular microenvironment and electronic structure, leading to changes in their bioactivity23–25. The adsorption of the enzyme haloalkane dehalogenase on graphene was found to modulate its enzymatic activity by altering the interfacial molecular interactions24. Moreover, the adsorption of bioactive molecules on graphene-based materials can improve their solubility and bioavailability, which in turn can enhance their therapeutic efficacy25,26. For instance, the adsorption of the anticancer drug doxorubicin on graphene oxide was found to improve its solubility and modulate its phototoxicity26. The adsorption of bioactive molecules on graphene-based materials can also be leveraged to achieve targeted delivery and controlled release of these compounds, further enhancing their therapeutic potential25. The adsorption of the acetylcholinesterase inhibitor on graphene oxide was shown to modulate its inhibitory activity and radical scavenging properties25. The modulation of bioactive molecule through adsorption on graphene-based materials has been demonstrated for a variety of compounds, including enzymes, drugs, and natural products like wogonin23–25,27. These findings provide a solid foundation for further exploring graphene-based materials for traditional Chinese medicine. While functionalized derivatives like graphene oxide (GO) or PEG-modified graphene are more commonly used as drug carriers due to their enhanced solubility, they introduce complex chemical environments. In contrast, this study focuses on pristine graphene to strictly isolate and characterize the pure π-π stacking mechanism, providing a fundamental ‘baseline’ for all graphene-based delivery systems.

Inspired by these works, this work is designed as a fundamental mechanism study. We employ DFT calculations and explicit-solvent MD simulations to systematically investigate the intrinsic physical adsorption between graphene and wogonin, aiming to provide a precise electronic and dynamic description of the drug-lattice interface. We optimized the adsorption orientation and position of wogonin molecules on the graphene substrate, and analyzed the molecular regulation through geometric and electronic structure characterization. Furthermore, the binding energy of the complex was precisely calculated, with the interaction types being thoroughly examined using energy decomposition based on molecular mechanics (MM) and interaction region indicator (IRI) descriptor. The conclusions drawn from this research provide crucial theoretical guidance for the enhanced utilization of active pharmaceutical ingredients in traditional Chinese medicine, such as wogonin. These findings are expected to pave new avenues in various applications, including the development of novel drug formulations, targeted drug delivery systems, and biosensing platforms for rapid detection of active components in traditional Chinese medicine.

Computational details

All calculations were performed using the DFT method through the ORCA code28. When constructing the graphene model, we considered the efficiency of computational resources and used a graphene flake containing 180 atoms, including 150 carbon atoms and 30 hydrogen atoms to saturate the edges. This graphene flake was designed to mimic the behavior of periodic graphene. Initially, the optimized wogonin molecule, which was free of imaginary frequencies, was positioned at the center of the graphene flake at an appropriate height above it. Given the obvious π-conjugated molecular orbital of graphene and the near-planarity of wogonin29, we selected the face-to-face orientation illustrated in the figures. Initially, the optimized wogonin molecule was positioned at the center of the graphene flake with an initial vertical distance of approximately 3.9 Å. A full geometry optimization without any constraints was then performed to determine the optimal adsorption structure. The system was allowed to relax completely until a local energy minimum was reached. The final equilibrium adsorption distance was found to be approximately 3.5 Å. This distance was quantified by measuring the separation between specific atoms whose interatomic vector is essentially perpendicular to the graphene plane; for instance, the distance between the O203 atom of wogonin (x = 24.296650; y = 29.982536; z = 14.932634) and the C26 atom of graphene (x = 23.238486; y = 32.203543; z = 8.870551) is 3.46 Å. This result confirms a gradual relaxation to a stable π-π stacking configuration during the optimization process.

In our subsequent analysis, we will demonstrate that the size of this graphene flake is sufficiently large to represent periodic graphene by examining the contributions of individual edge atoms to the binding energy of the complex. For the computational details, the DFT method with the B3LYP functional augmented by the D3 dispersion correction with Becke-Johnson (BJ) damping, and the Ahlrichs-type def2-SVP basis set was employed to optimize the geometry of the target wogonin@graphene complex in a vacuum environment. Regarding the choice of basis set, although def2-SVP is relatively modest, it was deliberately selected to ensure computational feasibility for this large-scale system containing 180 atoms. To rigorously address the basis set incompleteness error (BSIE) and basis set superposition error (BSSE), we implemented the geometrical counter-poise (gCP) method. For systems of this size, the standard counterpoise method or the use of excessively large basis sets with diffuse functions is often computationally prohibitive or technically problematic (e.g., linear dependence). The gCP scheme provides an efficient and robust alternative that has been extensively validated for accurately describing non-covalent interactions in large carbon-based nanomaterials. Furthermore, to explicitly respond to the need for characterizing the binding capability with higher accuracy thermodynamic parameter methods, high-level single-point energy calculations were additionally performed using the dispersion-corrected wB97X-D3 functional in conjunction with the def2-TZVP basis set based on the optimized geometries30. Before proceeding with the post-processing of electronic properties, we verified that the optimized complex was free of imaginary frequencies. Besides, the isolated wogonin was calculated at the same DFT level as the complex. The subsequent analyses of the complex mainly include: (1) characterizing the interactions within the complex using the IRI and IGMH descriptor; (2) demonstrating the contribution of each atom to the binding energy of the complex with energy-decomposition; (3) analyzing the delocalization pathways of π-electrons within the complex based on localized orbitals. Additionally, VMD was employed to generate high-quality visualizations of parts of results31.

To assess aqueous dynamic stability, a 20 ns MD simulation was performed in GROMACS using the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field and TIP3P water model. Topology parameters were generated via Sobtop (Version 2026.1.16)32. The complex was solvated in a cubic box with a 1.2 nm buffer. Following energy minimization, 100 ps NVT and NPT equilibrations were conducted at 298.15 K and 1.0 bar, utilizing V-rescale and Parrinello-Rahman schemes, respectively. Long-range electrostatics were handled by the PME method (1.2 nm cutoff), and H-bonds were constrained via LINCS. The 20 ns production run employed a 2 fs time step. Trajectory analysis, including RMSD, was performed using GROMACS utilities.

Results and discussion

Figure 1 displays the optimized structures of the isolated wogonin molecule and the wogonin@graphene complex (obtained at the same DFT level). The geometric structure of wogonin undergoes a notable change upon adsorption onto graphene, indicating a substantial interaction energy between the two entities. Conversely, the local planarity of the graphene substrate directly beneath the adsorbed wogonin is slightly reduced, further confirming the presence of mutual interaction. Importantly, the geometry of the graphene flake remains unaffected at its edges, which, from a structural perspective, validates that the chosen flake size is appropriate and that edge effects are effectively minimized.

The planarity of a molecule is a very important structural characteristic, and many issues are closely related to the planarity. For our typical π-conjugated system in this study, its degree of planarity will significantly affect the π-electron conjugation ability. Therefore, molecular planarity is closely related to several very important physicochemical properties, such as aromaticity, conductivity, and magnetic field response properties33–38. Moreover, molecular planarity will also affect the packing structures formed by the molecule itself and its ability to participate in intermolecular π-π interactions. The adjustment of the geometric structure of wogonin by graphene adsorption is very evident: the planarity of the wogonin molecular structure has been enhanced. To quantitatively demonstrate the effect of graphene on the planarity of wogonin, we introduced the molecular planarity parameter (MPP), which was put forward by Lu et al.33. The MPP = , = , and the coefficients A, B, C, and D are the parameters of the plane equation Ax+By+Cz+D = 0. After adsorption by graphene, the MPP of wogonin decreases from 0.244 to 0.173, indicating an enhancement in its planarity. In the color-coded diagram of = , atoms (excluding all hydrogen atoms) above and below the fitting plane are clearly displayed in red and blue, respectively, with deeper colors indicating greater deviation from the fitting plane (Fig. 2). We observed that the planarity of almost all carbon and oxygen atoms relative to the fitting plane has been enhanced. The MPP analysis and the color-coded deviation maps were generated using Multiwfn39.

Atomic charge is one of the simplest and most intuitive ways to describe the charge distribution in a chemical system. Specifically, an atom with a more negative charge, due to its strong attraction for the electron cloud, has a higher degree of electron enrichment and thus has a greater tendency to attract electrophilic reagents that are electron-deficient. Conversely, an atom with a more positive charge has a relatively lower electron cloud density and shows a stronger electron affinity, making it more likely to attract nucleophilic reagents rich in electrons. This interaction between charges and reagents is the key driving force for a series of chemical reactions. Table 1 provides the changes in Hirshfeld charges of each atom of wogonin before and after adsorption. In the isolated wogonin molecule, all oxygen atoms carry negative charges because of the high electronegativity of oxygen. According to the robust Hirshfeld population analysis, the O23 atom has a negative charge value of -0.259, which is the largest one among all atoms. This result clearly indicates that the O23 site is highly likely to be the primary target of electrophilic reagent attacks in chemical reactions. At the same time, some hydrogen atoms (e.g., H27 atom, with a charge of 0.172) in the system carry significant positive charges, suggesting these sites exhibit a certain degree of nucleophilicity.Compared with isolated wogonin, neither the sign nor the magnitude of the atomic charges changes significantly upon adsorption. The quantitative changes in Hirshfeld charges are negligible. For instance, the charge of the O23 atom only slightly shifts from − 0.259 to -0.260. These phenomena strongly suggest that there is no significant charge transfer between the wogonin and graphene. Significant charge transfer is usually an important indicator of the formation of strong interactions such as chemical bonds. Based on this robust observation, it can be reasonably inferred that the adsorption of wogonin on graphene is a typical physical adsorption. Furthermore, to verify this against basis-set dependence, traditional Mulliken charges were additionally calculated as a supplementary mapping (Table 2). Although the Mulliken analysis, due to its inherent basis-set dependence, assigns the largest negative population to the O28 atom rather than O23, it still exhibits a highly consistent trend regarding the overall negligible charge transfer upon complexation. Subsequently, we will further verify and clarify this conclusion through other descriptors.After the adsorption, according to the more robust Hirshfeld analysis, the O23 atom in the wogonin molecule still maintains the characteristic of having the largest negative charge site in the entire system. These stable sites with high negative charges play crucial roles in the process of wogonin exerting its biological activities, such as the antioxidant activity. This finding indicates that although the adsorption of graphene has an adjustment on the geometric structure of wogonin, it maximally preserves the sites with important chemical reaction activities in the original molecule. Evaluating from the perspective of fundamental physical interactions, graphene exhibits promising characteristics as a non-covalent adsorption substrate for bioactive molecules from traditional Chinese medicine, which lays a theoretical foundation for further solvent-inclusive studies in biologically relevant environments.

Since the planarity of wogonin is improved due to the adsorption of graphene, it is well expected that its electron delocalization will also be enhanced. In this work, we used the localized orbital locator function (LOL) to describe the electron delocalization40. In Fig. 3, the LOL-π map visually illustrates the delocalization pathways of π electrons within the complex system. Prior to complexation, the monomeric graphene, due to its two-dimensional structure, should possess a significant π molecular orbital. After complexation, it was observed that this characteristic was not destroyed. Figure 3 also displays the LOL-π distribution of the wogonin monomer at the same computational level. By comparing the distributions before and after complexation, it is observed that the LOL-π path of wogonin exhibits better integrity (under the same isovalue). This confirms that the graphene modulates the geometric configuration of wogonin, namely enhancing its planarity. Consequently, it can be anticipated that the aromaticity of wogonin is increased, which will facilitate its participation in π-π stacking and other interactions within biological systems, thereby better exerting its related biological activities.

Isosurface map of LOL-π with the isovalue of 0.2 for (left) the wogonin@graphene complex and (right) the optimized isolated wogonin (at the same DFT-level of calculation) for comparison.

Figure 4 depicts the intermolecular interactions between a graphene and wogonin molecule as characterized by IRI descriptor41. In each carbon C6 ring of the graphene flake, there is an IRI-red area at the center, reflecting that these regions possess significant steric hindrance, which is consistent with the steric characteristics of the C6-ring-systems. Additionally, extensive IRI-green regions are observed between the graphene and the wogonin molecule. According to the definition of IRI, these green areas correspond to van der Waals interaction zones. This result also intuitively reflects that the adsorption of wogonin molecules on graphene is a typical physical adsorption, without involving stronger chemical bonding interactions.

Isosurface map of IRI = 1 for wogonin@graphene complex grounded in the coloring settings explained by Lu et al.. In detail, the isosurface is colored according to the function: the sign corresponds to the sign of the second largest eigenvalue () of the electron density Hessian matrix; is the electron density. distinguishes different types of interactions: blue isosurfaces indicate strong, repulsive interactions (e.g., steric hindrance within covalent bonds and lone pairs). Green isosurfaces represent weak, attractive van der Waals interactions, dominant in the region between wogonin and the graphene surface, characteristic of π-π stacking. Red isosurfaces signify strong, non-bonded repulsive interactions (e.g., steric clashes).

This observation aligns with the enhanced planarity of the wogonin molecule when adsorbed onto the graphene, which facilitates π-π interactions with the graphene’s large π-conjugated molecular orbital. Furthermore, the presence of these extensive van der Waals interaction regions suggests a high binding energy when the graphene and wogonin form a complex. Furthermore, the covalent characteristics of adjacent carbon-carbon bonds in the graphene lattice and adjacent carbon-oxygen bonds within the wogonin molecule are distinctly highlighted by IRI-blue cylinders. All these interactions contribute to the formation and the final geometric structure of the produced graphene-wogonin complex. To further distinguish the intermolecular interactions from intramolecular covalent bonds, the IGMH analysis was performed (Fig. 5). As shown in Fig. 5, the IGMH isosurface exclusively highlights the π-π stacking regions at the interface by masking intramolecular contributions, which provides a more intuitive validation of the physical adsorption nature. The detailed distribution of IRI values across the complex, along with the consistent results from IGMH, provides a comprehensive understanding of the various types of interactions that contribute to the overall binding energy and stability of the wogonin@graphene complex.

To accurately calculate the binding energy of the large wogonin@graphene complex, a correction for the basis set superposition error (BSSE) is essential42. For systems of this size, the use of larger basis sets with diffuse functions or the rigorous but computationally demanding counterpoise method is not feasible due to either technical challenges (e.g., linear dependence) or prohibitive computational cost. Therefore, we employed the geometrical counter-poise (gCP) method as an efficient and reliable alternative44. The gCP scheme provides an approximate BSSE correction based on molecular geometry, making it particularly suitable for large systems where more exhaustive treatments are impractical45. This method has been successfully applied in numerous studies of non-covalent interactions involving carbon nanomaterials. The relative difference between the two methods is approximately 10%, demonstrating that the strong binding affinity of the wogonin@graphene system is highly robust and consistent across different theoretical levels.To contextualize the strength of this interaction, we compare it with a well-established experimental benchmark for pure π-π stacking: the adsorption of a single benzene molecule on graphene.Recent experimental studies using temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) have measured the desorption activation energy of benzene from a graphene surface to be 54 ∓ 3 kJ/mol43. This value serves as a fundamental reference for the interaction of a single aromatic ring with the graphene lattice. The binding energy obtained for our wogonin@graphene complex (-149.45 kJ/mol) is approximately three times this benchmark value.This is physically consistent with the molecular structure of wogonin, which consists of three fused/connected rings (A, B, and C rings), leading to a proportional increase in the π-π contact area and dispersion interactions compared to a single benzene ring46. This comparison demonstrates that the wogonin@graphene complex exhibits a robust and stable physical adsorption, with a binding affinity that scales logically with its polycyclic aromatic character.Given that most flavonoids molecules possess a core structure analogous to that of wogonin, it is reasonable to expect that these molecules will also have strong binding energies with graphene. This strongly confirm that graphene, with its large π-conjugated molecular orbitals, is highly suitable for adsorbing molecules like wogonin. Our multi-scale calculations—from electronic binding energy to aqueous-phase stability—confirm that the large π-conjugated orbitals of graphene offer a robust energetic support for such flavonoids, suggesting that pristine graphene segments can serve as a fundamental structural motif for bioactive molecule adsorption47.

The EDA-FF (energy decomposition analysis with force field) method proposed by Lu et al. was also applied to dissect the interactions between wogonin and graphene44,48. Specifically, the molecular force field utilized was the AMBER force field49,50, where atoms in graphene were assigned the CA atom type (corresponding to carbons in aromatic rings), edge carbon atoms were assigned the CM atom type, and hydrogen atoms were assigned the HA atom type. The binding energy calculated between the wogonin and graphene using EDA-FF was − 143.27 kJ/mol (with electrostatic interactions is -0.06 kJ/mol; exchange repulsion interactions at 155.59 kJ/mol; and dispersion attraction reaching − 298.81 kJ/mol). This aligns well with the reliable binding energy we previously calculated using the quantum chemical method, suggesting that this general force field is particularly effective in quantitatively describing this system with significant weak interactions. Moreover, this qualitative analysis via EDA-FF reveals that dispersion attraction overwhelmingly dominates the interaction between the wogonin and the graphene. In the future, our findings are highly likely to guide researchers toward directly utilizing ‘cost-effective’ classical force fields rather than ‘expensive’ quantum chemical methods to analyze the binding interactions between traditional Chinese medicine molecules and substrates.

In Fig. 6, atoms in the wogonin molecule that appear bluer (more negative) exert a stronger attractive effect, while those that appear redder (more positive) exert a stronger repulsive effect.;all other white atoms have essentially no effect on the interactions. Moreover, the B-ring of wogonin does not participate in the interactions, while the A and C rings are significantly involved. Both the A and C rings contain attractive blue atoms and repulsive red atoms, but in terms of quantity, there are more attractive blue atoms than repulsive red atoms, which also explains why the binding energy of the entire complex is negative. Interestingly, in this face-to-face arrangement, the methyl group, due to its carbon atom being farther from the graphene compared to other carbon atoms, is dominated by dispersion attraction and thus appears blue. As for the graphene, compared to the wogonin atoms, we find that the carbon atoms of graphene contribute little to the binding, essentially appearing white. Since the atoms at the edges of the graphene also show no significant interaction coloring, we further performed a quantitative validation using the EDA-FF method to rigorously confirm the adequacy of the flake size. By calculating and summing the interaction energy contributions of all 60 peripheral atoms (30 edge carbon atoms and 30 saturating hydrogen atoms), the cumulative edge contribution was determined to be -1.77 kJ/mol. Compared to the total binding energy of -151.32 kJ/mol, the overall edge contribution accounts for only 1.17%. This negligible proportion provides rigorous evidence that the edge effects are minimal, and thus it can be confirmed that the current graphene segment is sufficiently large. To further evaluate the stability of the wogonin@graphene complex under more biologically relevant conditions, we extended our investigation from static gas-phase calculations to dynamic aqueous-phase simulations. As illustrated by the representative snapshots in Fig. 7, wogonin remains firmly adsorbed on the graphene surface throughout the 20 ns explicit-solvent MD trajectory, consistently maintaining its parallel face-to-face orientation via persistent π-π stacking.

Representative snapshots of the wogonin@graphene complex solvated in an explicit water box at different time points during the molecular dynamics simulation.

The dynamic behavior was quantitatively assessed using Root-Mean-Square Deviation (RMSD) analysis (Fig. 8). The internal RMSD of the wogonin molecule (bottom panel) fluctuates around a remarkably low mean value of 0.34 ∓ 0.08 Å, demonstrating that the structural integrity and rigidity of the drug molecule are well-preserved in the aqueous medium. Meanwhile, the RMSD of wogonin relative to the graphene substrate (top panel) exhibits a mean value of 6.19 ∓ 1.88 Å. Although the molecule exhibits certain translational and rotational fluctuations on the smooth carbon lattice—characteristic of the non-covalent nature of the interface—no dissociation or significant structural detachment was observed. These results indicate that the π-π interactions identified in our DFT and IRI/IGMH analyses are sufficiently robust to withstand thermal fluctuations at room temperature in a solvent environment, further validating graphene’s potential as a stable carrier for wogonin delivery.

Conclusion and perspectives

We employed DFT calculations and 20 ns explicit-solvent MD simulations to systematically investigate the physical adsorption of wogonin on graphene. Our results demonstrate that the adsorption significantly enhances the molecular planarity of wogonin, as evidenced by the decrease in the MPP value, while maintaining a stable charge distribution. The LOL-π analysis further confirms that electron delocalization within wogonin is strengthened upon complexation. The calculated binding energy of − 149.45 kJ/mol reveals a robust physical adsorption affinity, which is logically consistent with the polycyclic aromatic character of the flavonoid core.

Crucially, the 20 ns MD trajectory and RMSD analysis provide direct evidence for the dynamic stability of the wogonin@graphene complex in a biologically relevant aqueous environment. Despite minor translational fluctuations characteristic of non-covalent interfaces, the persistent π-π stacking ensures that no dissociation occurs during the simulation. Furthermore, the high consistency between quantum chemical results and the AMBER force field suggests that classical molecular mechanics is a promising and cost-effective tool for analyzing the interactions between traditional Chinese medicine components and carbon-based substrates. These findings establish a fundamental structural and energetic baseline, paving the way for the future development of graphene-based targeted delivery systems and biosensing platforms for bioactive natural products.
