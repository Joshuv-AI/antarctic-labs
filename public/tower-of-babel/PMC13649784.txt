Single-cell catalogue of immune cells from Japanese individuals
Authors: Yukie Kashima, Kenichi Makishima, Genta Fujii, Masahide Seki, Yuuta Kuze, Patrick Reteng, Mamiko Sakata-Yanagimoto, Yutaka Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gene expression in immune cells varies greatly among healthy individuals and may be influenced by the genetic background, age and other factors. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying immune diversity at the single-cell level are not well understood. Here, we generate a single-cell multi-omics dataset of peripheral blood from 105 Japanese individuals. We focus on age-related changes in cellular populations and gene expression levels. In our dataset, age-associated population changes are most pronounced in naïve T cells, memory B cells, and CD56bright NK cells, which are associated with the initial immune responses of the respective cell lineages. Conversely, monocytes display prominent epigenomic changes that often precede those in the transcriptome, particularly among immune response genes. This dataset, which represents an underrepresented Japanese population in single-cell research, provides an invaluable resource for future studies.

Introduction

The immune landscape of an individual comprises diverse immune cells that are characterized by various gene expression, protein, and epigenetic profiles, representing the potential for complex immune responses that are important to maintain health and manage disease onset. Immune diversity is believed to be relatively limited at birth but expands progressively through continuous exposure to environment1. Consequently, a detailed understanding of the immune landscapes of healthy individuals could yield valuable insights into defining health status and estimating disease vulnerability2.

Recent attention has focused on age-associated variations in the immune landscape3. Immune senescence, defined as the progressive decline of immune function with age4, is a crucial target for optimizing care in an aging population. Studies have explored age-associated DNA methylation changes in humans and animal models, reporting that while such changes occur in healthy individuals, they often accelerate in autoimmune diseases5. Moreover, changes in DNA methylation correlate with disease progression and patient treatment outcomes, which have become particularly relevant in older patients with COVID-19, who exhibit more severe symptoms6 and diverse immune responses to SARS-CoV-2 infections. We have also previously reported that differences in B-cell maturation in older patients lead to variations in antibody production and hospitalization duration upon subsequent SARS-CoV-2 infection7.

Beyond general aging effects, immune landscapes are believed to be influenced by individual-specific features, such as genetic background and medical history. Despite strong indications that the immune landscapes diversity affects immune responses, a comprehensive analysis of immune landscapes is still lacking. Especially, those of a given ethnic background, such as those of Japanese older individuals, remains elusive. Recently launched International Asian Immune Diversity Atlas (AIDA) aims to delineate the diversity of the immune landscape across Asian populations8, in which infectious diseases remain prevalent, substantially influence immune diversity, and impact population-specific immune landscapes. Because AIDA is a pan-Asian project, it includes samples from Japanese donors. However, these samples are limited to young donors (147 of 149 Japanese donors are aged ≤50 years). Moreover, the datasets are based on single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and do not include other omics layers, such as single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin (scATAC). Another project on the Japanese population is the Osaka Atlas of Immune Cells (OASIS). This project includes omics datasets, such as scRNA-seq/single-cell V(D)J sequencing, shotgun metagenomic sequencing, whole genome sequencing, and the proteome. However, older individuals are also underrepresented in this project (only four healthy donors are aged ≥60 years)9. To the best of our knowledge, no project has included multi-omics datasets covering a wide age range with a sufficient number of individuals in the Japanese population to enable robust statistical inference.

In this study, we generated a multi-omics dataset from 105 Japanese donors, including scRNA-seq, scATAC-seq, DNA methylation, cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF), and Olink, using peripheral blood specimens collected from donors aged 20–90 years. We specifically focused on the aging population (≥50 years of age). In addition to creating a catalog of omics features, we demonstrated potential applications of the collected datasets by identifying age-associated changes in each omics method and comparing our results with those from previous studies, including AIDA8, UK biobank, and OneK1K10, using publicly available data. Our study sheds new light on the diverse immune landscapes of Japanese individuals.

Results and discussion

Sample collection and study design

In this study, we generated a multi-omics dataset of peripheral blood. Blood samples were collected from individuals living in a suburban city in Japan (Fig. 1A). The cohort included the following three sub-cohorts: Cohort 1 (older generation original cohort) included 51 older donors aged 50–89 years. Cohort 2 (younger generation cohort) included 26 donors aged 20–49 years. Cohort 3 (older generation additional cohort) included 28 donors aged 50–89 years (Fig. 1A). We assumed that Cohort 1 should be used for exploratory analysis and the initial characterization, while Cohorts 2 and 3 should be used for the validation of the observed age-span results and testing the robustness of the results, respectively. In our own analysis, all results shown in the figures were validated via cohort 3 (more details in Supplementary Data 1). The health status of all donors was confirmed via self-surveillance and by the blood tests upon sample collection. Detailed information on the health status of donors, i.e., basic information and medical history/conditions, is shown in Supplementary Data 1. Using the collected whole blood samples, plasma, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), the following analyses (in the order of appearance) were conducted: (i) Infinium MethylationEPIC array analysis11 on the Illumina platform; (ii) scMultiome analysis using 10x Genomics; (iii) CyTOF for single-cell-level cell surface marker protein analysis; and (iv) Olink multiple serum proteomic analysis (Fig. 1B). Owing to the cost limitations, all methods were performed for cohort 1, while only scMultiome was performed for cohorts 2 and 3 (Supplementary Data 1). Samples were collected at least twice over a 1-year interval from 38 of 51 (75%) individuals in cohort 1. Descriptive statistics for the datasets are available in Supplementary Data 2 and 3 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. With these datasets, we aimed to identify which immune features were associated with aging at the molecular, cellular, and blood phenotypic levels. Hereafter, we demonstrated that this collected database should provide a useful resource to profile age-related features of individuals of Japanese origin.

Initial characterization of the generated dataset regarding age-related changes

Using the generated dataset, we characterized immune cell profiles with increasing precision from bulk to single-cell levels. We specifically focused on age-related changes. We first characterized age-associated changes in cellular composition at the 12-cell-type resolution by deconvolution based on bulk-level DNA methylation analysis and according to previous studies12–14 (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Compared with other methods, methylation profiling is a bulk analysis. The experimental costs for this bulk-level analysis are low, but the results are based on predictions. Subsequently, mass cytometry analysis was used to obtain protein profiles at the single-cell level, albeit limited to 50 representative cell surface markers, which is suitable for identifying known cell types (Supplementary Figs. 3–5). In addition, this approach enables analysis of substantially larger cell numbers than other single-cell methods. Based on a previous study, natural killer (NK) cells can be grouped into two subtypes, late NK cells and early NK cells, based on CD57 expression15. Among the NK cell populations, we found the most significant aging-related changes in late-stage NK cells (Fig. 2C, D), which exhibited high CD57 expression following a decrease in NK cells. Using scMultiome data for cell type annotation, we provided a finer-resolution examination of immune cell populations (Fig. 2E–G, and Supplementary Figs. 6–13). We grouped cells into 19 detailed cell types. To further clarify the age span in which the observed changes occur, we compared two age groups: young (20–49 years of age: Cohort 2) and old (50–89 years of age: Cohort 1). The results revealed that the proportion of CD8+ naïve T cells, mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells, and B memory cells significantly decreased, while that of NK and CD8+ memory T cells showed an increasing trend, and that of naïve B cells showed a decreasing trend (non-significant) with age (Fig. 2F).

We also conducted a more detailed group comparison in seven age groups, especially focusing on the elderly individuals. CD8+ memory T cells significantly differed between individuals in their 50s and those in their 80s, with a consistent trend from the 50s to the 80s. By contrast, populations of naïve B cells, CD8+ naïve T cells, and MAIT cells declined (Fig. 2G). We also confirmed these age-associated trends using linear regression analysis (Supplementary Fig. 10). Changes in cellular populations, associated with the plasticity of the immune cell responses to novel stimuli or pathogens, typically decline with age, potentially explaining the weakened immune responses observed in older individuals. These results, which included individuals aged 50–89 years, are partly consistent with those of a previous study that recruited individuals aged 25–80 years, where the proportion of MAIT and CD8+ naïve T cells decreased with age14,17–19. All cell type comparisons via the different methods are summarized in Supplementary Figs. 1–13. We also performed comparisons using a logistic regression model and Tukey’s HSD method (Supplementary Data 4). These results further validated the age-associated differences indicated by linear regression.

We first performed the analyses on the aggregated bulk level. We expected that the general features beyond the single-cell level analysis could be captured by this approach. This approach also facilitated direct comparisons with results from previous studies. We identified 148 and 53 genes correlated with aging (Fig. 3B, C) based on gene expression and chromatin openness, respectively (Fig. 3D provides examples). The results of this bulk-level analysis mostly aligned with those obtained in a recent study20, indicating that diversity in expression diminished with aging.

Unexpectedly, we observed a relatively low overlap at a particularly clear parameter when comparing the scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq datasets (Fig. 3A–C). These results suggest that open chromatin formation may provide a “necessary” but “insufficient” condition for gene expression. Few previous papers have observed the same patterns21,22. For example, among the genes identified as “age-associated,” CD248 was included both in the scRNA and scATAC layers (Fig. 3C). This gene, previously reported as an age-associated gene, exhibited both gene expression (r = −0.52) and chromatin accessibility (r = −0.51; Fig. 3D). CD248 encodes endosialin, a transmembrane protein reported to regulate naïve CD8⁺ T cells23 (Fig. 2F, G). Although previous studies have reported age-related downregulation of CD24814,23,24, there is no evidence that this directly causes a decline in CD8⁺ naïve T cells. However, this downregulation suggests that CD248 may play a role in the CD8+ T naïve T cell population. Notably, the reduction of naïve T cells with aging is partly attributed to thymic involution25, but previous studies have also suggested that age-dependent changes in molecules and microenvironments supporting naïve T-cell homeostasis may contribute to this decline26. Another age-associated gene detected in this study, RALGPS2 (Supplementary Fig. 14), encodes a Ras-specific GTPase involved in regulating the cell cycle and cell survival through control of p27 and p21. This gene may be associated with age-associated changes in cellular populations27.

We performed the same analysis based on publicly available data in AIDA, which consists of datasets from five Asian countries, including Indonesia, South Korea, Singapore, Thailand, and Japan (95 out of 148 genes or 64%), with our current dataset compared with other countries (parameter R = 0.2). Interestingly, the dataset from South Korea shared the highest number of common genes. The Japanese dataset in AIDA showed a rather lower number of common genes than that from South Korea. This is because, compared with our cohort 1, the Japanese AIDA dataset is biased toward younger age, with only donors aged below 50 years, except for two donors between 60 and 70 years of age. These results suggest that age-associated genes reflect race/ethnicity and gene-specific patterns (Supplementary Fig. 14 for the case of CD248, where three out of the five countries appeared as “age-associated”).

We also analyzed the age- and sex-related features of gene expression. Previous studies have revealed that the immune system and responses differ in males and females. Sex hormones are thought to be major factors in these sex-based differences. Sex also has an effect on immune cell profiling, such as related to the immune cell transcriptome28. To acquire sex-dependent features, we selected 15 individuals using bootstrap selection, searched for features, and individually identified age-associated genes in males and females (Fig. 3E). Owing to the limited number of samples for each age group, we randomly selected 15 donors, i.e., five donors per age group, for the validation study. This approach enabled the generation of 100 combinations without including age- or sex-based biases. As a result, we identified several age- and sex-associated genes (Fig. 3F). For example, IL7R decreased in a male-specific and age-dependent manner, consistent with previous findings29. IL7R is an immunosenescence-related gene, and its expression level in blood is linked to familial longevity and healthy aging30,31. EFNA5, which plays a role in fertility, apoptosis, and steroidogenesis32,33, decreased in females and increased in males with aging.

Age-related gene expression at the single-cell level

We next conducted a similar analysis at the single cell-level based on the assumption that total expression levels can be determined by the gene expression at the single-cell level multiplied by the number of cells showing gene expression. Moreover, we assumed that the gene expression of different cell types has a distinct meaning. Figure 4A represents the total number of genes identified as age-associated based on gene expression and chromatin openness in monocytes, T cells, B cells, and NK cells. We observed that the most relevant aging-related changes were detected in monocytes in both the scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq analyses in cohort 1 (Fig. 4A), suggesting that monocytes are the cellular population most susceptible to age-associated influences. While recognizing the limitations of previous studies, several independent reports have described findings that are partly consistent with ours34,35. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these age-associated genes in monocytes implicated chromatin organization and immune response pathways, e.g., “chromatin organization” and “viral infection pathways” (Supplementary Fig. 15). We hypothesized that age-associated chromatin or cellular signaling changes could be associated with the occurrence of age-related blood cancer. We also conducted a similar GO analysis for age-associated differentially expressed genes in B and T cells. However, age-associated changes were less pronounced than those for monocytes (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). According to a previous study comparing groups of individuals aged 28–35 and 68–80 years, T cells were significantly enriched in genes related to inflammatory and immune responses36. These results of ours implied that changes in age-associated gene expression in immune-related systems in T and B cells in those aged 50 to 89 years are not as drastic as in those aged 28–80 years. These features are under the augmentation among previous studies. There are papers both consistent and inconsistent with our results29,37. These features may be particularly sensitive to the selection of the donors having a particular ethnic background or age range (for details, see the discussion section below).

We also found that the majority of genes associated with age were cell type-specific, except for only one gene, MAML2, which exhibited age-related changes across all four cell types (i.e., monocytes, T cells, B cells, and NK cells) at the RNA level (Fig. 4B, C, and Supplementary Data 5). MAML2 encodes a positive regulator of Notch signaling, which is pivotal in aging and inflammation onset38. It is possible that alterations in gene expression of key molecules serve as a foundation for fine-resolution, age-associated functional changes that are mediated by changes in genes in a more cell-type-specific manner.

Also here, the correlations between the scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq results were generally poor. A reason for this inconsistency may be that open chromatin may not solely be a decisive factor of the eventual RNA level. For example, interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF1) was marked as age-associated in the ATAC-seq analysis (as the closure of the chromatin with aging) but not in the RNA-seq data (as a decrease in mRNA) for monocytes (Fig. 4D). IRF1 is a crucial transcription factor activated by various innate immunostimulants and pivotal cytokines39. RNA induction occurred in some elderly individuals, albeit to a lesser extent than in younger individuals (Fig. 4D). We observed the similar chromatin closure among genes with related functions, including IRF1, IRF2, IRF7, and other “stimulation-inducible” genes. For those genes, the change in the chromatin status may become relevant when the induction signal arrives, and the mRNA induction actually occurs. Interestingly, such chromatin changes were also prevalent in genes associated with “chromatin organization,” which may be primarily responsible for the closed chromatin formations in monocytes (Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Data 5–7). We confirmed these results using AIDA datasets, i.e., age-associated genes were common, in at least one gene, to all datasets and cell types. Among them, monocytes showed the highest common gene expression compared with other cell types (Supplementary Data 8).

Next, we focused on NK cells, in which age-associated changes were most pronounced (Fig. 2). The second most significant change after those in cell populations chromatin levels, with a small number of alterations at the RNA level (Fig. 4A); these characteristics appeared in monocytes and B or T cells. The scRNA-seq data enabled identification of nine major subclusters using representative cell markers40,41: CD56bright NK cells, transitional NK cells, active NK cells, adaptive NK cells, mature NK cells, terminal NK cells, low ribosomal NK cells, NKT cells, and others (Fig. 4E, F; Supplementary Fig. 18). Among them, the most relevant age-associated changes occurred in CD56bright NK cells (Fig. 4F, right, p = 2.1e-03 based on Kruskal–Wallis test and p = 1.2e-02 for 50s vs. 70s, p = 1.1e-02 for 50s vs. 80s based on Dunn’s test, Supplementary Fig. 19). Using validation cohort databases, we confirmed this age-associated change not only in cohort 1 but also in cohort 2 (young cohort) and cohort 3 (old additional cohort) (Supplementary Fig. 19). This population, considered the most naïve among NK cells, matures into various NK lineages. The deteriorating population size of CD56bright NK cells with age may be associated with increasing vulnerability and weakened immune responses in older individuals. Here, again, in most instances, gene expression changes were not associated with changes in chromatin status. However, similar to monocytes, chromatin changes in NK cells resulted in RNA changes in a few cases (Fig. 4A). GO analysis indicated that genes involved in cellular stress and immune responses to pathogens were associated with age-related changes in chromatin openness and gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 20). Next, we investigated how the diverse profiles described above were orchestrated for phenotypic appearance at the individual level focusing on NK cells (Supplementary Note 1).

Proteomic analysis of plasma specimens

Next, to examine how diversity in cellular and genetic profiles was reflected in the serum proteome, we employed Olink Explorer 3072. For most individuals, analysis proceeded in a time-lapse manner. A total of 74 collected serum samples were subjected to the full panel analysis using 2944 proteins. For details regarding quality control and reproducibility, see Supplementary Fig. 21. Correlations with RNA expression levels were generally poor, as anticipated, although high correlations were observed for some genes, including GZMB, IFNGR1, and CCL26 (Supplementary Fig. 22). This finding emphasizes the importance of independent measurements of serum proteins, in addition to examining the epigenomes and transcriptomes of immune cells. When screening for age-associated proteins, we identified only nine that correlated with age (r < −0.5 or r > 0.5; Supplementary Fig. 22). This may indicate that protein expression profiles exhibit less diversity with age, as serum proteins directly influence the immune functions of individuals. Compensatory mechanisms may neutralize cellular or genetic changes; therefore, diversity observed at the cellular and molecular levels might not be apparent unless critical events occur. Nevertheless, eight out of the nine proteins, including TNFRSF11B, identified as age-associated have direct implications in inflammatory reactions (Supplementary Fig. 22). Thus, although immune landscapes appeared normal, changes in the expression of inflammatory factors might have already begun (Supplementary Fig. 22). A comparison with the results of a previous study for validation is shown in Supplementary Note 242.

Discussion

In this paper, we generated a single-cell multi-omics dataset of peripheral blood from 105 Japanese individuals. We specifically focused on age-related changes in cellular populations and gene expression levels. We believe this should provide a useful data resource to conduct the detailed profiling of aging immune cells, having a given ethnic background and an experimental condition. We further attempted to characterize the generated dataset. Our results, which focused on transcriptome and epigenetic alterations across various types of blood cells at the single-cell resolution, suggested that age-associated alterations occur not only in T cells, as previously reported, but also prominently in the myeloid cell lineage. Chromatin changes were most pronounced in NK cells, with cellular composition changes preceding changes in both chromatin and gene expression. Additionally, we observed that latent yet distinct features of immune cell landscapes exist even among general aging individuals with no substantial current disease symptoms. Our results also suggested that age range and ethnicity are important factors enabling a comprehensive understanding of immune cell changes with aging. Furthermore, age-associated changes in the monocytes and myeloid cells might reflect the diversity of health statuses among donors, including the levels of inflammation. In the future, detailed information on immune landscapes, potentially reflecting aspects of individuals’ medical histories, will be crucial for the implementation of personalized medicine.

This study has some limitations. First, the current dataset included only ~100 individuals, and each sample had a limited volume, e.g., only ~10,000 cells for single-cell RNA/ATAC-seq, which provided insufficient statistical power to generalize the results. Most individual-level findings should be interpreted as preliminary observations. Second, this analysis covered a short period of time, and a comprehensive understanding of the dynamics of aging and immunosenescence for a given individual was not possible. Third, we could not perform all combinations of cross-omics integration analyses. We believe that features at different omics layers are sometimes regulated independently. We mainly focused on old donors, in which inflammatory responses, many of which are initiated by the innate immune response, may be more strongly present than in younger donors. We could not distinguish whether these differences were due to methodological differences or the inherent nature of the different cohorts.

Nevertheless, we believe that the detailed omics descriptions provided here will serve as a valuable reference for larger studies, even though some studies have also provided PBMC atlases using scRNA-seq8,36. Our dataset includes Japanese donors aged above 70 years and provides multiple omics layer datasets. Although we focused on older individuals, we also included a dataset of younger individuals. We believe our publicly available datasets serve as a valuable foundation for future studies of the gene expression changes in aging immune cells.

Methods

Ethics approval

Peripheral blood cells were collected from 51, 26, and 28 donors in cohorts 1, 2, and 3, respectively. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Tokyo (20-351). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (age range: 20–90 years old). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Blood sample collection

Blood samples were collected from 105 donors (the samples used in the current study are summarized in Supplementary Data 1 and Fig. 1A) between 2021 and 2025. For 38 donors who attended the second sample collection, blood was collected twice (Supplementary Data 1, dataset with “Time point” 2). For the vaccination study shown in Supplementary Note, samples were taken from residents in the same city in which mass vaccination and sample collection were performed. Donors who participated in the study were vaccinated with the vaccine provided by Pfizer or Moderna targeting the Wuhan strain. As shown in Fig. 1, three types of specimens (i.e., whole blood, plasma, and PBMCs) were acquired from each individual. Whole blood was centrifuged, and plasma was acquired from the supernatant for Olink analysis. After plasma had been obtained, the remaining specimen was diluted with an equal volume of D-phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Cat#045-29795, FUJIFILM, Tokyo, Japan), loaded in SepMate-50 tubes (Cat#15460, STEMCELL, Vancouver, Canada) containing a density gradient medium (LymphoprepTM, Cat#1856, Serumwerk Bernburg, Bernburg, Germany), and centrifuged again (2000 rpm × 10 min, room temperature). The top layer was poured into a new tube and washed twice with PBS (1500 rpm × 5 min, 4 °C). Peripheral PBMCs were obtained as precipitates and resuspended in Cell Banker2 (Cat# CB031; Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo, Koriyama City, Japan).

Blood standard test

Standard blood tests were performed at a service provider (SRL, Tokyo, Japan). The tests included the following items: white blood cells (WBC, number of cells per microliter), red blood cells (RBC, number of cells per microliter), hemoglobin (Hb, volume [g] per deciliter), hematocrit (HT, percent), mean corpuscular volume (MCV, fL), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH, picogram), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC, percent) and Plt (number per microliter).

Construction of scMultiome libraries using 10× Genomics Chromium

For the scMultiome analysis, 10× Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression was used according to the manufacturer’s user guide (CG000365 Rev A, CG000337 Rev A, 10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Briefly, PBMCs were thawed and washed with 0.04% BSA -PBS. Washed cells were mixed with chilled lysis buffer and incubated for 3 min on ice. Isolated nuclei were washed with wash buffer thrice, and cell concentrations were measured. Cells, targeting a 10,000-cell output, and transposition mix were incubated at 37 °C for 60 min for transposition. After processing, nuclei, oil, and master mix were loaded onto the chip, and GEMs were generated using a Chromium controller. Individual GEMs contained barcoded DNA from transposed DNA and full-length cDNA. After amplification and indexing, two types of libraries, scATAC and scGEX, sharing the same 10× barcode, were acquired. The constructed libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA). Statistics representing these libraries are presented in Supplementary Data 2.

scMultiome data processing

The scMultiome datasets were first processed using Cell Ranger ARC (Cellranger-arc-2.0.0, [default, intron-include mode, ref = GRCh38-2020-A-2.0.0]). The BCL files were converted into fastq files using the command cellranger mkfastq (default parameters), and the fastq files were processed using the command cellranger count (default parameters). Reads were mapped to hg38 using STAR. Next, the output obtained from CellRanger was analyzed using the R packages Seurat version 4.1.143 and Signac version 1.7.044. Initial quality control was performed by creating a Seurat object for each sample. For the scRNA-seq data for each Seurat object, the following parameters were applied during initial quality control: the number of RNA molecules detected in a cell (nCount_RNA) was between 1000 and 25,000, and the percentage of reads aligned to the mitochondrial genome was <20%. For the scATAC-seq data, the initial filter was the number of ATAC fragment molecules detected in a cell (nCount_ATAC), the nucleosome signal was <2, and the transcription starting site enrichment was >2. A total of 744,974 cells passed through the initial quality control filter.

Cell type annotation of scMultiome datasets

Cell type annotation was performed using scRNA-seq data. For cohort 1 (n = 45), the raw scRNA-seq count data were extracted from each Seurat object and merged into a single Seurat object. This merged object was then processed using the following Seurat functions: NormalizeData (default parameters), FindVariableFeatures (default parameters), ScaleData (default parameters), and RunPCA (default parameters). Data integration was performed using Harmony (version 0.1.0) to minimize batch effects with the sample identity set as batch variable45 for dimension reduction and visualization. Cell clustering was performed in Seurat using the Harmony-corrected embedding. UMAP embedding was computed with dim = 1:30. Harmony-corrected data were used only for clustering and visualization and were not used for downstream gene expression analyses. Clusters were manually annotated using the canonical markers described in the Azimuth reference43. After cell type annotation, we confirmed the expression of representative cell markers.

After cell annotation of whole cell types, we created NK cell subsets, performed unbiased clustering, and identified differentially expressed genes in the clusters (Supplementary Fig. 17). Based on the canonical markers provided in previous studies40,41, we annotated eight out of nine clusters. The remaining cluster was small and did not show specifically high gene expression patterns; therefore, it was annotated as “other NK cells.” This cluster may present an artifact of the data processing.

For cohorts 2 (n = 26) and 3 (n = 28), cell annotation was performed using label transfer. Each sample was pre-processed using the following Seurat functions: NormalizeData (default parameters), FindVariableFeatures (selection.method = “vst,” nfeatures = 3000), ScaleData (default parameters), and RunPCA (default parameters). To identify the set of anchors between the reference and query datasets, the FindTransferAnchors function was used with dims = 1:30 and reference.reduction = “pca.” Then, the query data were mapped to reference data using the MapQuery function with reference.reduction = “pca” and reduction.model = “umap.”

Gene expression and chromatin openness at the sample and cell-type levels

To evaluate the association among age, chromatin openness, and gene expression, we calculated a chromatin openness score based on the promoter region and average gene and protein expression levels in each sample and cell type in each sample. (RNA: cohort 1 n = 45, cohort 2 n = 24, cohort 3 n = 26 ATAC: cohort 1 n = 45, cohort 2 n = 25, cohort 3 n = 28).

To acquire the averaged gene expression values, gene expression was first normalized using Seurat::NormalizeData with the default parameter. The normalized expression matrices were then extracted using Seurat::GetAssay, and the averaged expression per cell type was calculated by averaging the normalized scores across cells using the R function apply (mat, 1, mean).

To calculate chromatin openness, we defined approximately 1 kbp of each transcription start site as the promoter region and counted the number of tags in the promoter region for each cell in the ATAC data. The region with ≥2 tags was considered 2 tags, and the count data were divided by twice the total number of cells.

Gene expression and chromatin openness were aggregated at the sample and cell-type level to generate pseudobulk profiles by summing counts across all cells within each cell type for each sample.

Identification of age-associated genes

For the pseudobulk level, genes with a Pearson’s correlation coefficient >0.5 between age and averaged gene expression or chromatin openness were identified as “age-associated.” For cell-type-level age-associated characters, we employed an alternative approach. Initially, genes were excluded from the expression data in cases where four or less samples exhibited expression in approximately 10% of the cell population. Similarly, from the ATAC data, we excluded genes in cases where four or less samples showed openness scores in ≥10% of the cell population. After filtering, 22,301 genes remained unchanged. To assess age-associated genes, we applied smoothing of expression and openness scores using the LOESS method and the R stats package’s loess function (span = 0.75, method = “loess,” degree = 1). We calculated Δopeness, defined as the difference between the maximum and minimum of the smoothed scores in chromatin openness data. Subsequently, we extracted genes associated with age and chromatin openness by considering those with a Δopeness score of ≥0.1 (10%). Subsequently, we selected genes for which the difference between the maximum and minimum gene expression values after smoothing was more than two-fold, and the average expression levels in all specimens were >1.5. To further validate these findings, we performed a statistical analysis using generalized additive models (GAM) analysis in the R package mgcv46. Gene expression was modeled as a smooth function of age (s(age, k = 3), method = “REML”). P values obtained from GAM analysis were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. The list of statistical scores is provided in Supplementary Data 6 and 7 and Supplementary Table 4. Based on the identified age-associated features, we performed GO analysis. We employed Metascape (v3.5.20230623) at default settings.

Validation analysis for age- and sex-associated characters

For cross-validation and age- and sex-associated gene identification, cases were selected randomly from the whole cohort, so that the number of samples from gender or age intervals was equally 15. The test was repeated 100 times, and the distribution of Pearson’s correlation coefficient was examined. When p < 0.05 (based on Student’s t-test) for the deviation of the coefficient was compared to the original value of 0.5, the case was regarded as “validated.”

Sample collection and DNA extraction

A MagAttract HMW DNA Kit (Cat#67563; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used for DNA extraction from frozen blood samples. To obtain sufficient amounts for subsequent studies, we doubled the volume of samples and reagents and prolonged the elution time as indicated in the manufacturer’s guide (HB-1523-006). After DNA extraction, we confirmed the quality of samples using Qubit ds for a broad range of concentrations and a 4150 TapeStation (Cat#G2002AA, Cat#5067-5366, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) to determine the peak pattern.

Age-group comparison of cell types using boxplot

For the statistical analysis shown in Fig. 1C, E, H, significance was calculated using the Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon test; ns: 0.05 < p, *: 0.01 < p ≤ 0.05, **: 0.001 < p ≤ 0.01, ***: 1.0e-04 < p ≤ 0.001, ****: p ≤ 1.00e-04.

Analysis of age-associated differences in cell type ratios using logistic regression

For the scRNA-seq dataset, we constructed a logistic regression model that took the total number of cells into account. (RNA: cohort 1 n = 42, cohort 2 n = 24, cohort 3 n = 26 ATAC: cohort 1 n = 42, cohort 2 n = 25, cohort 3 n = 28) The number of cells of each cell type was counted for each sample and age group. Logistic regression was performed using the R glm function; glm (cbind (cell_count, diff_fromTotalCellCount) ~ age_group, data = df,family = “binomial”). We then used this model to compare the proportion of each cell type between age groups. Tukey’s HSD method was applied to multiple comparisons using the glht function (model, linfct = mcp (age_group = “Tukey”)) from the R package multcomp (version 1.4-26)47,48. Pairs with adjusted P values < 0.05 are shown in Supplementary Data 4.

Methylation microarray using Beadchip

For the methylation analysis, we isolated DNA from 500 µL of frozen whole blood using a MagAttract HWW DNA Kit and Quiagen Kit following the manufacturers’ protocols. The samples were processed using an Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC Beadchip array at Riken Genesis (Kanagawa, Japan).

Processing of DNA methylation profiles

To estimate the cell type proportion, we used the R package EpiDISH14 following the vignettes. We used the beta value of each sample as the input and estimated the proportion of cell types. We thus obtained annotation at the 12-cell-type resolution.

Mass cytometry analysis using CyTOF

For the mass cytometry analysis, we employed a Fluidigm CyTOF Helios Mass Cytometer (Fluidigm, South San Francisco, CA, USA, Cat#107001). PBMCs were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To characterize cell types in PBMCs, we applied the Maxpar® DirectTM Immune Profiling AssayTM (Fluidigm, Cat #201235), a commercial preset kit that included 30 markers, following the manufacturer’s guide (Quick Reference guide JPN_PN 400288 B1_001). Stained cells were analyzed using a Helios CyTOF system, and the acquired datasets were analyzed using a Maxpar Pathsetter (Fluidigm, Cat#401018). After Maxpar Pathsetter automatic processing, reports showing the cell count and displaying the data on a Cen-se′™ map were generated. For cell annotation, the gating parameters were set according to the default Pathsetter settings, which have been validated by the manufacturer. The details were as follows: CD8+ T cell (CD3+ CD66b CD19− CD8+CD4− CD161− TCRgd− CD123− CD11c−), CD4+ T cell (CD66b− CD3+CD4+CD14− TCRgd− CD11c−), gdT cell (CD66b−, CD3+, CD8dim/−, CD4−, CD14−, TCRgd dim/+), MAIT cell (CD66b− CD3+CD4− CD14− CD161 dim,+ TCRgd− CD28+CD16− CD161+), NK cell (CD14− CD3− CD123− CD66b− CD45RA+ CD56dim,+; early: CD57−/ late: CD57+), B cell (CD3− CD14− CD56− CD16 dim,− CD19+CD20+HLA−DR dim,+; naïve: CD27−/memory: CD27+), Plasmablast (CD3− CD14− CD16−,dim CD66b− CD20− CD19+CD56− CD38++CD27+), pDC (CD3− CD19− CD14− CD20− CD66b− HLA−DR dim,+ CD11c− CD123+), cDC (CD3− CD19− CD14− CD20− HLA−DR dim,+ CD11c dim,+ CD123− CD16 dim,− CD28 dim,+ CD294− HLA−DR++), monocytes (CD3− CD19− CD56− CD66b− HLA−DR+HLA−DR+ CD11c+; non-classical, transitional, classical: CD14 −>+, CD38 −>+), neutrophils (CD66b dim,+ CD16+HLA−DR−), eosinophils (CD14− CD3− CD19− HLA−DR− CD294+CD66b dim,+), basophils (HLA−DR− CD66b− CD123dim,+ CD38+CD294+).

Proximity extension assay using Olink

For proteomic analysis, we used the Olink proximity extension assay (Olink, Uppsala, Sweden). Plasma was processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The constructed libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq6000 platform (statistics are presented in Supplementary Table 2).

Processing of Olink datasets

Olink dataset analysis was conducted using NPX Manager, following the manufacturer’s instructions. After processing, normalized protein expression values were obtained for each protein and sample. Based on the NPX value, we calculated the correlation between age and protein level. For validation, we compared our datasets with the list of age-associated proteins provided in a previous paper42.

Validation of correlation in whole cohort datasets

We performed the analysis using CD248, which showed a high correlation for both ATAC and RNA (R = −0.69, R = −0.50, Supplementary Fig. 11), while RALGP2 showed a less correlation for RNA in the whole cohort datasets (R = −0.02 in GEX, R = −0.42 in ATAC, Supplementary Fig. 11), mostly due to the effects from Cohort 2 (a young population). Some of the age-related changes may not occur in a continuous manner but become relevant after a particular age (Fig. 3F).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed as described in the figure legends. Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison tests were used for comparisons among multiple groups (Figs. 1B, D, G and 4F), and Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon tests were used for two-group comparisons (Fig. 1F). Sample sizes varied across assays and cohorts depending on sample availability. For Fig. 2, n = 51, individual for Methylation, n = 44 individuals for CyTOF, and n = 69 for scMultiome. For Fig. 3E, the age group was divided by age 55 years old of age. Individual donors were treated as independent biological replicates unless otherwise specified. Boxplots display the median, interquartile range (Q1–Q3), and whiskers extending to 1.5 × IQR. Individual data points are overlaid where indicated.
