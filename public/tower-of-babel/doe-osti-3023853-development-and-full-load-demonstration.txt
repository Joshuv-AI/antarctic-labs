ABSTRACT
Sandia National Laboratories (SNL) and the German Aerospace Centre (DLR) are world leaders in
the joint and separate development of very high temperature next generation solar concentrator
systems using solid particles. This project “noLimits” is meant to provide the critical performance
information needed to commercialize DLR’s CentRec® solar receiver and ancillary particle systems
(conveying systems, storage vessels, flow control equipment etc.) developed at Sandia National
Laboratories (SNL). Performance information (at power levels closer to a small scale Concentrating
Solar Power (CSP) plant) will be obtained at full load conditions (2.5 MWt) and at 900 °C-1000 °C
particle outlet temperatures at the National Solar Thermal Test Facility (NSTTF) located at SNL.
Heliogen was proposed to be joining the project as a commercial partner to provide the
commercialization route for this receiver design, if successful, as Heliogen acquired the intellectual
property (IP) from DLR.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This project collaboration “noLimits” between Sandia National Laboratories and DLR (German
Aerospace Centre) to test a large-scale particle heating receiver was initiated in supporting the
commercialization of a high temperature particle heating receiver. Testing at the NSTTF in
Albuquerque New Mexico was proposed as the most desired test facility to support the experimental
campaign of a high temperature particle receiver at multi-MWth scale. This test campaign is meant to
provide the critical performance information needed to commercialize DLR’s centrifugal style
CentRec® receiver, provided by DLR as in-kind cost share to the project. The ancillary particle
systems like the vertical conveying systems, storage vessels, flow control equipment, high
temperature mass flow measurement and balance of plant hardware are developed at Sandia
National Laboratories. Performance information at power levels representative of a small scale
Concentrating Solar Power plant will be obtained at full load conditions. The full load thermal
power of 2.5 MWth and at 900 °C-1000 °C particle outlet temperatures was planned to be achieved
at the NSTTF located at Sandia National Laboratories.
After the initial systems engineering was completed to integrate the centrifugal receiver test unit with
the existing infrastructure at the NSTTF, it was identified that the available project budget at Sandia
and resources was lacking to achieve the full scope as planned by Sandia. The major cost items
identified by the project team were a particle heat rejection system that can reject the heat absorbed
in the receiver, and a particle conveyance system capable of lifting particles in the 25 kg/s range. The
sponsor of Sandia agreed to provide additional resource for supporting these auxiliary systems.
Auxiliary hardware was specified and procured to support receiver testing up to 2.5 MWth heat
rejection, making use of forced convection with air as the cooling fluid.
By the time the engineering was at an advanced stage for the test integration at the NSTTF, the
DLR completed a post-test inspection of the centrifugal receiver unit that was planned to be tested
at the NSTTF. During the hardware inspection, it was identified that the receiver unit sustained
significant damage related to thermal stresses during the previous on-sun test campaign from 2018 at
the test facility of the DLR in Jülich, Germany. The cost to repair the 2.5 MWth capable receiver unit
at DLR exceeded the available resources and the DLR team proposed to utilize a smaller version of
the receiver for testing at the NSTTF that was still part of an active test campaign in DLR’s Synlight
facility at the time. Upon inspection, this receiver was also declared unfit for future testing.
With the lack of a high temperature rated receiver test unit to be integrated at the NSTTF, the
project leads at both DLR and Sandia reached out to various entities that have interest in supporting
this effort going forward. General mining and mineral processing entities, as well as Heliogen (a CSP
developer at the time) were contacted. Heliogen expressed interest in joining the project.
Heliogen’s interest to commercialize the centrifugal style, high temperature rated particle receiver for
the CSP industry led to negotiations to have Heliogen join the project team. Due to the cost of
construction and structural modifications, the cost share contribution from Heliogen was significant.
The general proposed scope for Heliogen was to provide a receiver unit and contribute to the
structural modifications to accommodate the size of receiver they desired to test. However, the
addition of Heliogen to the Cooperative and Research And Development Agreement (CRADA)
could not be executed.

Therefore, the project team members between SNL and DLR completed all the closeout activities,
and the testing scope of this project at NSTTF was not realized.

1. PROJECT SUMMARY
The end state of this project is to provide the performance results of the solar particle receiver
systems needed to prove that commercialization of the final product will be successful. The primary
focus of the project is overcoming the testing and validation step needed during any
commercialization process. Licensing SNL’s particle receiver system components will be an end
goal. Providing performance results for the CentRec receiver will provide HelioHeat with results to
market the receiver they have licensed from DLR. An ideal commercial license will include a final
design needed for solar plant operation.
Technology Commercialization Funding (TCF) is meant to be supporting the commercialization
effort of technologies developed in the laboratory environments or potential licensing in the future.
A commercial project partner is usually very beneficial to have as part of the development team.

1.1. Project Objectives
The original project objectives are summarized as:

 • Modification of DLR’s existing CentRec® receiver, based on lessons learned from 2018 tests
 in Jülich, Germany. This is a product refinement step towards a better commercialized
 product. The modifications will result in a more refined TRL 6 receiver.
 • Planning and preparation of test infrastructure at SNL solar tower. This step ensures
 integration and collaboration between the German and US based research institutes.
 • Integration and commissioning of CentRec® receiver into SNL solar tower. This step will
 include the engineering and design of the ancillary systems such as particle transport,
 hoppers, particle cooler, and ducting/valves.
 • On-sun testing at various load conditions, up to 2.5 MWth and 1000 °C particle outlet
 temperature (this was later reduced to 900 °C).
 • Solar durability performance characterization during 15 test days of operation.

1.2. Target audience
Solar particle receiver systems are targeted for market penetration in the energy sector and is
mutually applicable for the industrial process heat applications as well. This includes markets in the
United States (U.S.), Germany and in other markets around the world. While electricity generation
from next-gen CSP is a major market, the combined technology package of SNL and DLR would
provide alternative thermal solutions to the large process heat demand in the U.S. and elsewhere.
This includes the cement industry which is a large polluting industry worldwide besides electricity
generation. A portfolio of very high temperature solar particle receiver technology also supplies the
emerging markets of hydrogen and sustainable fuels.

1.3. Communications and Outreach Strategy
Activities relating to licensing of a larger portfolio of particle technology will be ongoing as will
activities relating to exploring modes of commercialization, marketing and PR. Interactions with the
commercial entity, HelioHeat, will be on-going to ensure commercialization will be successful.

2. PROJECT ADMINISTRATION

2.1. Contract and CRADA Negotiations
The TCF project requires a CRADA be placed with any partners. SNL and DLR had an existing
CRADA contract in place, unfortunately it only applied for funding from DLR. The German
funding was from outside the DLR institution and thus a new CRADA needed to be placed. SNL
and DLR began negotiations in October 2019 and came to an agreement on terms in March 2020.
The CRADA was at DOE headquarters for final approval and had to obtain Sandia Field Office
approval. Finalization of the CRADA was reached in mid-May 2020 and the official start date of the
project was June 1st, 2020.The original project planning was based on testing the DLR-developed
solar receiver unit at the NSTTF at Sandia.
At the beginning of 2022 Heliogen Inc., acquired the German HelioHeat GmbH and then officially
expressed interest in joining the project, creating the need for a new tri-party CRADA in place
between NTESS, DLR and Heliogen.
Pursuing the CRADA between Sandia NTESS, Heliogen and DLR required significant coordination
and time from the Sandia team, since there is an international team player on the project. The
Statement of Work for this CRADA was agreed upon by all parties and was approved at the
National Nuclear Security Administration’s (NNSA) Sandia Field Office (SFO) for final approval.
This final approval was expected to be received in late July 2022 after which signatures from
representative from the three institutions was planned to be collected. This process was never
completed, since Heliogen withdrew from the project due to resource constraints and a mismatch in
the project schedule. The original CRADA is the only project agreement that got executed during
the project duration.

2.2. Project Delays
The delay in the original CRADA created project schedule issues that had a ripple effect in
downstream activities for both DLR and SNL. A mismatch in the human resources and project
scope was the main result. Additional Covid-19 pandemic related delays also caused unforeseen
schedule creep for both project partners.
Eventually, the project was discontinued due to the resource constraints mentioned above, in
addition to the current lack of a commercial project partner. After significant delays on the receiver
delivery to the NSTTF and the withdrawal of Heliogen from the CRADA negotiation, the
discontinuation of the project was announced by the PI from Sandia.

3.1.1. Particle Cooler Concept Selection
Shell and tube design – procured item from Solex Thermal Science
The concept for the cooling device has been selected based on the main parameters: cost space, air
as the cooling fluid and a universal usability for different thermal duties and a range of particle sizes.
This particle cooling device was bought for the purposes of this project. More information of the
selection of the particle cooling devices can be seen in Appendix A and in Figure 5 below.

 Figure 5: Solex design – shell and tube particle-to-air heat exchanger
The latest conceptual layout drawing of the Solex design can be seen in Figure 5. A high pressure
centrifugal blower will be used for this application that is installed on the cold side and rated for
ambient temperature operation to minimize the cost. Air exiting the cooler can be kept in the range
of 500 °C and an accompanying outlet stack is needed to be installed to redirect the hot air to a safe
zone. Due to budget and lead time limitations, the lowest cost option available high temperature air
ducting material options are considered. A vendor that can manufacture spiral wound SS304L air
ducting was identified.

Particle Cooler development (Sandia design – not pursued)
The initial intent according to the proposed project planning was to design a cooling mechanism at
Sandia. This route was not pursued due to resource constraints and reliability issues of a non-
commercial grade particle cooler unit. The particle cooling mechanism designed to reject the heat
absorbed during on-sun testing is a high priority design item to finalize early on in a project of this
magnitude.
The location of the receiver in the existing infrastructure is the main driving factor for the available
space to install the particle cooling mechanism. Heat rejection was planned to be achieved via a
combination of dry cooling options and potentially making use of the air-cooled water-glycol
cooling loop that is available at the NSTTF. The concept for this cooling device has been selected
based two main parameters, cost and space. The initial concepts based on indirect dry cooling heat
transfer, indicated that the cooling device would become prohibitively expensive and too large for
the available space on the module. The thermal design at this stage of the 2 MWth particle cooler was
then steered in a different direction, making use of direct cooling of the particles. Since the
preference of the project partner, DLR, was to have the particles return to the receiver at near
ambient temperature conditions, <60 °C, the cooling of the particles is a key design aspect to take
into account. With the Sandia developed design of the particle cooler, outlet temperatures can
conservatively be in the range of 100 °C to 300 °C, depending on the power of heat addition.

 Final Report noLimits/CentRec 18

 Figure 6: Section drawing of the direct cooled particle heat rejection device – Sandia design
An adaption of the design of the particle cooler to enhance the cooling capacity in the limited
amount of space can still include the 1 MW air cooled glycol cooling loop that is available at the
NSTTF. Since the planned particle elevator mass flow rate is above the 3 kg/s, that is the rated flow
rate of the current receiver, a recirculation loop on the cooler side is suggested to enhance the heat
rejection by simply doing a multi-pass through the cooler device. The latest conceptual layout
drawing of the SNL design can be seen in Figure 6. A high-pressure centrifugal blower is planned to
be used for this application that is installed on the cold side and rated for ambient temperature
operation to minimize the cost. Since this is a direct-contact air cooled design, the cooling air would
be directly in contact with the flowing particles. A need thus arises to prevent dust and for particles
from escaping the system. A dust separating cyclone that is commonly used in industry is proposed
to be used here to minimize R&D effort and to save time and resource. According to the thermal

3.3.1. Test Specifications
The aim of the prior tests of the CentRec particle receiver was to prove the high performance of the
receiver, which had previously only been calculated. At the beginning of the project, the CentRec500
with 1.13 m aperture diameter, i.e. 1.0 m² aperture area, which had previously been tested on the
Jülich solar tower, was intended for this purpose. Figure 17 shows a sectional view of a CentRec
receiver. Solar radiation from the heliostat field enters the receiver through the lower opening of the
rotating drum and is absorbed by the particle layer on the inner wall. The particles are fed through a
distributor at the top of the cylinder, which directs the particles to the inner wall. There, the particles
are held against the wall by centrifugal force. Under the influence of gravity, they gradually move
downward. The residence time depends on the adjustable rotational speed of the receiver. The
heated particles leave the rotating drum at the bottom edge and are collected by a stationary ring
(collecting ring) and discharged downwards.

 Figure 17: Sectional view CentRec receiver

 Final Report noLimits/CentRec 27

Within the scope of the tests, particle flow rates of 3-4 kg/s were envisaged. In commercial
applications, the goal is to achieve high particle outlet temperature of 900 to 1000 °C. The return
temperature depends strongly on the downstream thermodynamic configuration, i.e. the heat
exchangers. Typically, a return temperature of about 300 °C is expected.
These preliminary considerations are essential for the design of the particle handling system that was
needed at Sandia. There, 4 kg/s and return temperatures of 400°C were initially specified. The
cooler for the hot particles must be able to dissipate the >2 MW power absorbed by the receiver,
even in continuous operation. Water-cooled and air-cooled variants were considered for this
purpose.
Buffer vessels and particle mass flow control devices are also required to allow safe and continuous
operation of the receiver.
During operation, measurements of the mass flow, the particle temperature and the component
temperatures in the rotating receiver are required in particular in order to be able to characterize and
evaluate the operating behavior. For this purpose, the rotating receiver drum is equipped with radio
modules which, powered by batteries, transmit the measurement signals during operation to the data
acquisition system.

3.3.2. General Background
The analysis of the receiver condition after it was returned from Jülich was continued. The first
findings showed some repairs were needed. The inspection of the suspension of the inliner in the

3.3.3. Particle Film Characterization
DLR team is further investigating the behavior of the particle film inside the rotating drum of the
Centrec receiver. Two setups for cold testing are operated at DLR in Stuttgart. Technologies applied
until now for the film investigation are based on image processing. The particle front movement
speed is observed, or white tracer particles mixed into the dark bulk material are tracked. Small
cameras are used (GoPro cameras), but reach limits in resolution and optical quality. Such cameras
are mounted in the rotating drum, fixed in the drum and thus rotating with the drum. The
improvements in hardware and image processing are continuing to gain more detailed results.
Additional efforts are being made to improve the illumination of the dark particles inside the
rotating drum.

 Final Report noLimits/CentRec 28

Most recent work included pre-selection and initial tests with the supplier of a high-speed laser-
optical scanner device for particle flow speed analysis.
For proper analysis of the rotation speed and correlation of the camera images, new rotation angle
encoders based on codes for a magnetic sensor have been selected and implemented in the test
setup.
Further investigations apply an infrared camera in combination with radiative heaters on the
CentRec300S installation, see a photo of the test bench in Figure 18. This was in particular relevant
after having detected hot-spots during on-sun tests of the previous receivers in the DLR-Jülich
installations (solar tower and Synlight solar simulator).
The tests reveal the influence of the surface properties of the drum inliner. While the CentRec300S
has a wire-mesh welded to the inside, with the intention of increasing friction of the particles on the
drum, the CentRec300S test bench has a smooth surface, more slippery for the particles.
Experimental and model insights lead to the experience, that a structured surface is beneficial for the
particle film homogeneity. Heliogen followed this line in the manufacturing of their newest receiver
drum (milled surface).
In cooperation with the Materials Testing Institute Stuttgart (MPA), samples of the metal drum are
analyzed. The samples are from the drum having undergone high-temperature testing in the
previous project phase. The tests include crystal structure of the alloy as well as analyses of the
welding.
Extensive work on different designs of the feed nozzles for the particles from the static feed pipe
into the rotating drum has been executed on the test bench in Figure 18. The variants of the
“particle accelerator” include a double cone design with feed boards, the “double octopus” feed
pipes, and last-not-least the simpler single feed pipe in different diameters and for different ranges of
feed mass-flow. The latter has shown successful behavior in terms of evenness of the particle film
and is applied by Heliogen in the current CentRec receiver design. The risk of single particles
hopping out of the rotating particle film at the single feed location is considered low enough for the
current test plans. Analysis methods also include particle speed measurements at the nozzle outlet to
understand the mechanical situation at the upper end of the particle film in the rotating drum.

3.5.3. Particle accelerator

3.5.8. Other Adaption to US Environment for Heliogen
Instrumentation
The instrumentation of the receiver system is substantially a continuation of past instrumentation of
CentRec receivers including valve, motor, and pneumatic control, and temperature monitoring.
Further discussion with NSTTF is ongoing to review system process flow and to assure relevant
signals are also made available to the heliostat operating system to trigger automated emergency
defocus. This is to protect the receiver system in case of malfunction or transmitting too much
thermal power.
Radiation Shield
The radiation shield is required to be modified from past CentRec receiver applications due to
increased flux on aperture and thus increased spillage flux levels. We are currently reviewing
different design options to realize a radiation shield suitable for high radiation input. NSTTF is
supporting this activity with sharing their experience on materials and designs.
Estimates on Lead Time
Required materials are largely purchased and in delivery process to AT&F, the steel material for the
long lead-time items of the receiver shell and the inliner has arrived. The production of the receiver
system at AT&F has commenced with the receiver frame.
Auxiliary Components
The major pieces of system equipment that have been ordered are the particle flow control package
including the particle flow control valve that sits on the outlet of the particle cooler, as well as
associated temperature and level sensing instrumentation, and the main blower package including
the blower, 125 HP motor, VFD, and inlet filter box. The particle flow control package will be
shipped in 2 lots – instrumentation and valve – with the valve expected to arrive in mid-August
(instrumentation has started arriving on site). The current lead time on the blower has it arriving to
Sandia in mid-October.
We have identified a need for horizontal conveyance of “cold” (~450 °C) particles at the outlet of
the chiller. Commercial options have been explored.

4. LOGISTICS SUMMARY

4.1. Sandia Procurement (Bucket Elevator and Particle Cooler)
The Purchase Order with MHE for the bucket elevator was completed on May 19, 2022. Final
delivery of the elevator parts took place over the November 2022 to the February 2023 timeframe.
The particle cooler was ordered and the unit was delivered by the end of Feb 2023. The Sandia team
planned a visit to Solex (the subcontractor providing the cooler) to observe some of the tube
assembly, installation and welding in the final assembly. This visit was rescheduled to mid-2023, and
cancelled at a later stage.

4.2. Structural Support Modifications Update
The cost of the structural support modifications at Sandia contributed largely to the discontinuation
of the project as a whole. Resources that were committed to this activity in the very beginning of the
project planning (2019-2020) did not take into account many of the price increases of material and
labour that incurred in the following years. The scope of the structural modification was also not
clear at the time of the initial project planning and the statement of work did not capture all the
nuances that developed later on in the project. The overall scope of the auxiliary equipment
ultimately informed the support structure design and layout. Due to the multiple changes in choice
of the receiver unit to be tested at the NSTTF, the auxiliary system component engineering
specifications were changed to accommodate the latest receiver unit design specifications.
In the definition of the civil engineering design, Sandia Facilities was working closely with the
project team to provide design drawings (95% complete) suitable for construction and for a request
for quote (RFQ). Also, Sandia Facilities has assigned a Facilities Project Engineering Manager
(FPEM) who will facilitate the Rough Order of Magnitude (ROM) request and the interaction with
potential subcontractors once the RFQ is released. The FPEM typically oversee modifications and
installation from the approved subcontractor once selected.
The risk with this option (for Sandia Facilities to lead the modification) was identified to be a
delayed schedule and increased cost to get the work done. The PI asked for a ROM cost estimate
from Sandia Facilities, and received a cost estimate number at a later state. Heliogen has expressed
their concern in the timeframe to get a cost estimate from Sandia and the PI have informed
management of this concern.
As mentioned above, the ROM was not a final estimate of the work and will only assist the NSTTF
team and project partners to understand the level of costs to be incurred with more clarity and allow
to update cost and schedule plans. The three-point ROM cost estimate was presented to the project
team as; Pessimistic: $653,393; Total Project Cost (TPC): $435,595; Optimistic: $304,917.
The actual quote from the contractor came out to be: $1,168,795, which is significantly higher than
the initial ROM. The Sandia PI informed the project team about this cost estimate.

4.3. Timeline and Planning/Scheduling
The two receiver options that were proposed by DLR to be tested at the NSTTF were both
damaged in prior testing. After post-test inspection was completed at DLR on both of these units,
the resources required to repair either of these receiver units were beyond the available resources
available to the project team. Not only did the required resources exceed the budget that was
planned for repairs, but some unknown design adaptations were hard to identify based on the
existing failure modes.

 Final Report noLimits/CentRec 44

Heliogen proposed to provide a receiver for the continuation of the project in an effort to
commercialize the centrifugal receiver technology. This receiver was scheduled to arrive at the
NSTTF in October-November 2022. After the withdrawal of Heliogen from the project, there was
no reasonable means left of obtaining a receiver to continue with the project.
The original critical path identified on the side of SNL is the modification of the structure on the
NSTTF tower module unit to accommodate the CentRec® receiver and lifting the receiver in place
for installation. Therefore, significant time and resource of the project team was dedicated to this
effort, supporting the civil engineering team at Sandia that did the structural design.

4.4. Path Forward
Due to the latest developments in the past final quarters of the project, the scope of the project, the
budget and the schedule underwent revision. As mentioned above, with reference to the resource in
the project, it was decided by the project team not to continue with the project, but to steer the
project activities to an end. One of the main budgetary constraint factors is the cost for the
structural modifications. After the full structural modifications cost was known, a funding discussion
with the different project team members led to this decision.
The PI at Sandia informed the DOE technology manager as the project developments took place. A
schedule was provided by the PI to the DOE of all the close out activities that are needed to bring
this project to an end. The CRADA between Sandia and DLR was extended to accommodate the
close out activities for this project which include follow up by the PI on procurements that were
made for this project. The final procurement delivery was expected in the June/July 2023 timeframe.
Coordination to obtain the final project documentation input from DLR is completed and a
summary is included in this final project reporting.
Future project collaboration for testing a centrifugal style particle solar receiver for CSP application
is also being pursued by both DLR and Sandia. Test facility capabilities and good solar resource are
the main driving factors to test a receiver at the NSTTF in the future. Lessons learnt from this
project will also inform the project planning, scope definition and budgetary estimates for a potential
future project.

APPENDIX A. AUXILIARY COMPONENTS
As part of this project, a real time heat rejection device was chosen from a commercial vendor. In
general, the air-based shell and tube seems to make the most sense. However, it seems that the
installation of an evaporative cooler either at the inlet or just downstream of an initial bank of
air/particle surface might make a lot of sense. The water will absorb a lot of the heat, using it to
evaporate the liquid water into vapor. This has the added benefit of increasing the air density and
lowering the fan head (and auxiliary electrical power needs). The evaporation cooler will have a small
blowdown stream, but that might just be able to be discharged to a small evaporation pond, as this
facility will not be used continuously, and the New Mexico weather will evaporate the pond quickly.
