Patient experiences and perceptions of occupational therapy services in Chinese rehabilitation: a qualitative study
Authors: Xiaodan Wang, Yuhan Dai, Keli Mu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patient satisfaction is a critical determinant of rehabilitation outcomes in occupational therapy (OT). In non-Western contexts such as China, cultural norms including high power distance may profoundly shape the therapeutic relationship and patient experience. Few qualitative studies have explored the lived experiences of Chinese rehabilitation patients regarding OT services. This study aimed to explore the experiences and perceptions of Chinese rehabilitation patients regarding their OT services and to identify key factors that promote or hinder patient satisfaction from the patients’ perspective. A qualitative descriptive study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with ten participants purposively recruited from a rehabilitation department in China. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using qualitative descriptive thematic analysis, facilitated by NVivo software. The study adhered to the COREQ guidelines, and trustworthiness was ensured through triangulation, member checking, and reflexivity. Thematic analysis yielded four major themes: (1) Pathways to Initiation and Motivation for OT, characterized by passive entry via professional referral and trust in authority; (2) The Lived Experience of the Therapeutic Process, marked by passive compliance, monotonous activities, and a perceived lack of individualized, person-centered care; (3) Perceived Outcomes and Functional Gains, reflected patient-reported gains in physical function, daily living skills, and participation in community life; and (4) Facilitators and Barriers to Satisfaction, with therapist attentiveness and communication emerging as the strongest facilitators, while financial burden constituted the most significant barrier. This study reveals significant tensions between person-centered care ideals and standardized service delivery in Chinese OT practice. To enhance patient satisfaction with occupational therapy services in China, a multi-pronged approach is needed, encompassing strengthened public education and physician rehabilitation literacy to improve referrals, enhanced therapist training in clinical reasoning and individualized activity design, prioritized development of therapeutic communication skills, and integration of modern technology into treatment. Future research should develop culturally adapted shared decision-making models, design interventions to sustain motivation during functional plateaus, and evaluate the impact of policy reforms such as expanded insurance coverage on long-term outcomes. Introduction

Occupational therapy (OT) is one of the cornerstones of modern rehabilitation, dedicated to enabling individuals to participate meaningfully in the activities of their daily lives1.Patients satisfaction is inherently client centered and important in occupational therapy2, a multi-faceted construct that encompasses not only the perceived effectiveness of interventions but also the quality of therapeutic relationships and the overall experience of care3. High levels of patient satisfaction are critically linked to improved treatment adherence, better functional outcomes, and enhanced quality of life4,5.

Despite its recognized importance, a significant challenge persists in many healthcare systems: the tension between delivering standardized, efficient care and providing individualized, person-centered therapy6. Standardized treatment means therapeutic activities are administered uniformly rather than being tailored to the unique needs, interests, and goals of each patient7. This industrial model of care may risk overlooking the very principles of client-centeredness that form the ethical foundation of occupational therapy8.

To date, the understanding of patient satisfaction in OT has been largely shaped by quantitative surveys, which, while valuable for measuring predefined factors, often fail to capture the depth, nuance, and rich contextual reasons behind patients’ perceptions9,10. Furthermore, most research in this area has been conducted in Western contexts, where cultural norms around patient-provider communication—such as expectations for shared decision-making (SDM)—may differ significantly from those in other parts of the world11. In China, for instance, Confucian traditions that emphasize respect for authority may create a different dynamic in the therapeutic relationship, potentially leading patients to defer to the authority of doctors rather than to voice their preferences or dissatisfaction12. This cultural gap in the literature necessitates a deeper, qualitative exploration within the Chinese rehabilitation context.

Therefore, a clear knowledge gap exists. There is a lack of in-depth, qualitative research that explores the lived experiences of Chinese rehabilitation patients regarding their OT services, particularly how cultural norms interact with systemic pressures to shape their satisfaction and engagement.

To address this gap, this qualitative study aims to explore in depth the experiences and perceptions of rehabilitation patients regarding their occupational therapy services in China. The study is conceptually framed by Hofstede’s cultural dimensions theory, particularly the dimension of power distance, to examine how hierarchical norms shape therapist-patient interactions13. Furthermore, the Self-Determination Theory (SDT) informs our understanding of how the fulfillment (or thwarting) of patients’ basic psychological needs—for autonomy, competence, and relatedness—influences their motivation and engagement in therapy14.

Guided by these frameworks, this study seeks to answer the following research questions:

What are the key factors that promote or hinder patient satisfaction with OT service from the patients’ perspective?How do systemic and cultural factors, particularly those related to power distance and psychological needs, influence patients’ experiences and their decisions to continue or discontinue therapy?

By employing semi-structured interviews to give voice to the patient experience, this research seeks to move beyond numerical satisfaction scores. The findings are expected to provide a theoretically grounded, actionable insights for developing more culturally responsive, person-centered OT practices and to inform policy changes that can better support sustained patient engagement and improved rehabilitation outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

We employed a qualitative descriptive study design using semi-structured interviews to explore patients’ experiences and perceptions of occupational therapy (OT) services. This approach is well-suited to gain rich, detailed insights into complex phenomena from the participant’s perspective while staying close to the data and producing findings readily applicable to clinical practice15. Thematic analysis was conducted following the qualitative descriptive approach described by Sandelowski, which emphasizes descriptive summaries of participants’ experiences rather than highly interpretive or abstract theorizing16. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist17.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Creighton University Institutional Review Board in USA (IRB Approval No.: 2002971) when the first author of the study was a graduate student of Creighton University. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study prior to data collection. Participants were thoroughly informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, and their right to withdraw at any time without affecting their care. All data were anonymized and de-identified to ensure confidentiality.

Participant recruitment and characteristics

A purposive sampling strategy was used to recruit participants from the Rehabilitation Department of a Hospital in China Between April and July 2022.This sampling method ensured we captured a range of experiences relevant to the research question18.

Eligible participants were: (1) adults (aged ≥ 18 years); (2) had received at least two weeks of inpatient or outpatient occupational therapy for rehabilitation following [e.g., stroke, spinal cord injury, etc.]; (3) able to communicate in Mandarin Chinese; and (4) willing to provide informed consent. Participants with severe cognitive or communication impairments (with a score of less than 27 on the Mini-Mental State Examination and unable to engage in coherent interviews) were excluded.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted via online video chatting by the first author (a postgraduate student in Creighton University) to ensure consistency. The interviewer had received prior training in qualitative interviewing techniques and had no prior clinical relationship with the participants to minimize bias.

An interview guide (see Supplementary Material 1) was developed based on a review of the literature and consultation with expert supervisors. It included open-ended questions exploring: (a) how participants were introduced to OT; (b) their experiences and feelings during therapeutic activities; (c) perceived benefits; (d) factors influencing their acceptance and satisfaction; and (e) reasons for discontinuing therapy and suggestions for improvement.

All interviews were conducted privately in a quiet room in participants’ homes to ensure confidentiality. Each interview lasted between 30 and 60 min. With permission from all participants, interviews were audio-recorded digitally. Field notes were also taken to capture non-verbal cues and contextual observations. Data collection continued until data saturation was reached, where no new substantive themes emerged from subsequent interviews, which was achieved after the tenth interview.

Data analysis

The analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework for thematic analysis19,20, applied within a qualitative descriptive paradigm to produce findings that remain close to participants’ own language and experiences.

Familiarization. The interviewer, who is fluent in both Mandarin and English, transcribed the audio recordings verbatim into English. All transcripts were then checked for accuracy against the original recordings by a research assistant. Secondary data confirmation was performed by Dr Mu, who is bilingual in Chinese and English, to verify the accuracy of translation and transcription. Coding and analysis were conducted in English. The research team read and re-read the transcripts to immerse themselves in the data.

Generating initial codes. NVivo 12 software (QSR International) was used to systematically organize the data. Two researchers (XDW and YHD) independently coded three transcripts line-by-line to generate an initial set of codes (e.g., “Monotony and Lack of Variation,” “Increased Independence in ADLs,” " Infrequent Updates to Treatment Plans”). These initial codes were compared and discussed to develop a preliminary coding framework.

Searching for themes: The researchers then applied this coding framework to the remaining transcripts in NVivo, remaining open to new codes. All relevant data extracts were collated within each code. The codes were then sorted and grouped into potential themes (e.g., " Drivers for Acceptance,” " Perceived Quality of Therapeutic Activities”).

Reviewing themes. The potential themes were reviewed and refined in two stages. First, the coded data extracts for each theme were checked to ensure they formed a coherent pattern. Second, the entire dataset was re-read to ensure the themes accurately represented the meanings evident in the data as a whole. This process involved iterative discussions among the research team.

Defining and naming themes. Each theme was clearly defined and a concise, evocative name was chosen to capture its essence.

Producing the report. The final analysis was woven into a narrative, supported by compelling anonymized excerpts from the transcripts to illustrate the themes.

Node counts derived from NVivo reflect the total number of references coded to each theme and may include multiple mentions by the same participant. Accordingly, counts are used to indicate coding frequency within the dataset rather than to make claims about the prevalence or representativeness of themes. Throughout the Results, where comparative language is employed, it refers to observed patterns across the ten interviews.

Rigor and trustworthiness

The trustworthiness of the analysis was ensured through several strategies:

Triangulation. Multiple researchers (an OT graduate student, a senior rehabilitation therapist and a senior OT supervisor) were involved in the coding and thematic development process to provide multiple perspectives.

Member Checking. A summary of the preliminary findings was presented to three participants to check if the interpretations resonated with their experiences21.

Audit Trail. A detailed record of the research process, including interview guides, raw data, memos, and analytical decisions, was maintained in NVivo to allow for accountability.

Reflexivity. The first author of the study maintained a reflexive journal throughout the research process to bracket personal assumptions and biases as a [e.g., rehabilitation student] and to critically examine how their perspective might influence data collection and interpretation22.

Theme 1: Pathways to initiation and motivation for OT

This theme reveals that patients’ entry into occupational therapy service was predominantly facilitated through external channels rather than self-directed initiatives, establishing a passive care relationship from the outset.

Sub-theme 1.1: Sources of information and referral. Professional recommendation served as the primary gateway to occupational therapy services, with Professional Recommendation (11 nodes) emerging as the most frequent code. Multiple participants reported nearly identical pathways to OT. P3 stated: “During hospitalization, the rehabilitation doctor and team recommended it,” while P4 confirmed: “The rehabilitation doctor and rehabilitation team recommended it.” This pattern was consistently reported across interviews, with P1, P2, P6, P7, and P9 all citing medical professionals as their initial source of OT information. The dominance of this referral pattern highlights the crucial role of physicians as gatekeepers to OT services and suggests that strengthening interdisciplinary understanding of OT’s distinct value could significantly improve patient access and timely intervention23.

Sub-theme 1.2: Drivers for acceptance. Patients’ decisions to engage in OT service were motivated by two main factors: Trust in Professional Authority (16 nodes) and Desire for Functional Recovery (10 nodes) were coded more frequently than Goal of Regaining Independence in Daily Life (2 nodes). P1 articulated this dual motivation: “The main reason was that rehabilitation doctors recommended it, and we believed OT could help regain function.” Patients consistently expressed goals focused on specific physical improvements rather than broader life participation. P8 hoped OT would “improve my hand function, increase my hand dexterity, and reduce muscle tension,” while P2 sought simply to “improve right hand function.” Occupational therapy not only focuses on the patient’s functions, but also on their abilities, especially the ability to independently participate in daily life. Sometimes, even when there is no significant improvement in physical motor functions, occupational therapy can still help patients use various assistive devices and change their movement strategies, thereby enhancing their occupational performance and independence.

Theme 2: The lived experience of the therapeutic process

This theme describes the day-to-day reality of OT sessions, revealing a predominant pattern of passive engagement, a perceived disconnect between therapeutic activities and individual needs, and the factors that ultimately led to discontinuation of services.

Sub-theme 2.1: Dimensions of engagement. A significant finding was the prevalence of Passive Compliance and Reliance on Therapist (7 nodes). Many participants described their role as following directives without active input. P9 reported, “I did not have a sense of active participation; I primarily followed the therapist’s directives and rarely expressed my own ideas.” This was often rooted in a deep-seated respect for clinical authority, as P7 explained: “I mainly follow the occupational therapist’s opinions and instructions… because I believe the medical team is the most professional.” While Active Participation and Motivation (4 nodes) was present, it was often contingent on enjoying the activity; P1 noted he was “very excited and willing to participate” in activities he liked but “had no motivation” for disliked ones. A few participants also expressed initial Reluctance and Resistance (3 nodes), as P6 described feeling “resistant to doing occupational therapy” at the beginning of treatment. Overall, patient engagement was characterized predominantly by passive compliance rather than active collaboration, shaped by cultural deference to professional authority.

Sub-theme 2.2: Perceived quality of therapeutic activities. The content and delivery of therapy sessions were frequent sources of dissatisfaction. Participants reported Monotony and Lack of Variation (4 nodes), with P5 stating, “My occupational therapy activities didn’t change at all throughout my hospitalization,” and P7 similarly noting, “the therapeutic activities never changed, and I began to feel bored.” This was compounded by a Mismatch in Activity Difficulty (3 nodes), which could be demoralizing; P2 shared, “I felt it was too difficult and always wanted to give up on treatment.” A critical issue was the Perceived Lack of Purpose or Relevance (2 nodes), where participants engaged in tasks without understanding their therapeutic intent, as P1 noted, “I received occupational therapy without understanding the purpose of these therapeutic activities.” In summary, therapeutic activities were frequently perceived as monotonous, poorly matched to patient capabilities, and inadequately explained, thereby undermining engagement and therapeutic value. These accounts highlight critical areas for improvement in activity selection, gradation, and patient communication within occupational therapy practice.

Sub-theme 2.3: Person-centeredness and individualization of care. A strong and recurring critique was the perceived lack of tailored care. The most prominent code in this area was the Desire for Personalized and Goal-Oriented Plans (9 nodes). P10 articulated this need clearly: “I feel that my occupational rehabilitation treatment plan is the same as other patients’, not targeted enough, and not designed according to my own needs.” This desire stood in stark contrast to the reported Infrequent Updates to Treatment Plans (5 nodes), with P9 noting his plan “…never really changed.” The consequence was a Feeling of Assembly-Line Treatment (3 nodes), powerfully captured by P3: “I feel like the therapeutic activities other patients do are exactly the same as mine—it’s somewhat like an assembly line operation. My problems were not well addressed.”

Sub-theme 2.4: Factors leading to therapy discontinuation. The decision to stop OT was multifactorial, driven by a combination of systemic, functional, and personal factors. Discharge and Logistical Constraints (7 nodes) and a Plateau in Functional Gains or Goal Achievement (7 nodes) were the most frequently cited reasons. P1’s experience was typical: “My hospital stay was limited—I was discharged home and had to stop occupational therapy.” Others, like P5, discontinued due to “no hand function progress for a long time,” while P4 stopped because “all occupational therapy goals were achieved, and there was no further need.” Loss of Motivation or Interest (4 nodes) and External Circumstances (3 nodes), such as family matters (P6), a desire to return home for holidays (P7), or the COVID-19 pandemic limiting outpatient access (P7), also contributed to the cessation of services. Thus, therapy discontinuation was shaped as much by systemic and logistical barriers—such as discharge protocols and financial constraints—as by clinical considerations like functional plateaus or goal attainment.

Theme 3: Perceived outcomes and functional gains

Despite the challenges articulated in Theme 2, participants reported meaningful benefits from OT, primarily in the domains of physical function and daily living skills.

Sub-theme 3.1: Physical and functional improvements. The most commonly reported gain was Enhanced Motor Control and Dexterity (10 nodes). P10 celebrated, “My affected upper limb function has improved, my hand control has improved… I can write with my affected hand now.” P1 similarly noted that “hand movement control had improved.” Other improvements included Increased Strength and Reduced Spasticity (3 nodes), with P5 reporting “upper limb strength has improved” and P8 noting “muscle tension in my affected hand has decreased.” One participant (P7) also noted Improved Cognitive Function (1 node). In conclusion, tangible improvements in motor control and dexterity were the most commonly reported physical improvements, key validation factors for the efficacy of OT in patients, which are the basis for patient engagement.

Sub-theme 3.2: Empowerment in daily life and society. The translation of physical gains into meaningful occupational engagement was a key outcome. Increased Independence in ADLs (13 nodes) was the most highly valued benefit across the entire dataset. P2 shared the profound impact of regaining self-care skills: “I feel that occupational therapy has been helpful for my independence in daily life—for example, I can dress myself independently.” P6 reported, “my ADL ability has improved, and I can participate in work and recreational life. Now I am fully independent and can drive myself.” Several participants achieved Successful Community Reintegration (3 nodes), enabling a return to driving (P6), work (P6), and community activities with family supervision (P8). Ultimately, the most valued outcome of OT was the translation of functional gains into enhanced independence in daily activities and meaningful community participation, reaffirming the profession’s core focus on occupation-based outcomes.

Theme 4: Facilitators and barriers to satisfaction

Patient satisfaction was not monolithic but rather a dynamic state influenced by a constellation of factors related to the therapist, the therapy itself, and the broader contextual and policy environment.

Sub-theme 4.1: Therapy-related factors. The therapist’s interpersonal skills and professional competence were central to patient satisfaction. Therapist’s Attentiveness to Patient Needs (19 nodes) was the most frequently coded theme across the dataset, underscoring the critical importance of feeling seen and heard. P10 expressed a common wish: “I hope occupational therapists can communicate more with patients and pay more attention to our functional needs and opinions.” P6 similarly requested that therapists “pay attention to patients’ emotional changes and help us build rehabilitation confidence.” The Quality of Therapeutic Rapport and Communication (10 nodes) was also vital; P4 recommended, “I hope occupational therapists can communicate more with patients during treatment about goals and therapeutic activities.” Observable Functional Progress (8 nodes) served as a powerful motivator, with P4 stating that “when I felt my function improving, I felt excited and motivated.” Finally, Therapist Competence and Skill Level (6 nodes) was a clear expectation, with P1, P3, P5, P7, and P10 all citing it as a primary factor affecting their acceptance of therapy. In essence, therapist attentiveness and communication quality emerged as the most critical facilitators of satisfaction, outweighing even the technical competence of the therapist in shaping patients’ overall experience.

Sub-theme 4.2: Contextual and policy factors. External elements, particularly finances, weighed heavily on the therapeutic experience. Financial Burden (13 nodes) was a major concern. P2 plainly stated, “I pay for it myself and feel it’s a burden,” and for P6, this was a definitive reason for stopping: “I stopped because I had to pay for it myself.” P6 further advocated for “rehabilitation costs to be reduced or covered by outpatient medical insurance.” Other contextual factors included a desire for Modern Technology Equipment and Assistive Device Support (4 nodes), with P1 and P8 suggesting the use of “modern high-tech equipment such as transcranial magnetic therapy, virtual reality equipment” and P1 requesting “recommendations for assistive devices like functional chopsticks and bathing aids.” A recognized Need for Improved Rehabilitation Education and Awareness (2 nodes) was also voiced; P6 hoped “more rehabilitation knowledge can be disseminated so that people with rehabilitation needs can receive timely treatment.” Finally, there was Advocacy for Better Financial Support and Insurance (1 node), specifically for outpatient services to ensure continuity of care beyond hospitalization. Overall, financial burden and limited access to modern equipment and assistive devices constituted significant systemic barriers, highlighting the need for policy-level reforms to support equitable and sustained access to occupational therapy services.

Discussion

This qualitative study provides a nuanced, in-depth understanding of the experiences and perceptions of occupational therapy among rehabilitation patients in China, revealing a complex interplay between the profession’s client-centered ideals and the realities of a resource-constrained, culturally specific healthcare system. The thematic analysis of ten patient interviews yielded four overarching themes that collectively illuminate the critical factors shaping patient engagement, satisfaction, and functional outcomes throughout the rehabilitation journey.

The first major finding centers on the Pathways to Initiation and Motivation for OT, where professional recommendation emerged as the overwhelmingly dominant gateway to services. This finding underscores the pivotal role of physicians as gatekeepers to occupational therapy, but simultaneously raises a critical concern: the referral rate is directly contingent upon physicians’ knowledge and understanding of rehabilitation and occupational therapy23. If referring clinicians lack a comprehensive appreciation of OT’s distinct scope and value, patients with rehabilitation needs may never be identified or referred in a timely manner. Therefore, there is an urgent need to strengthen interdisciplinary education and communication, ensure the dissemination of rehabilitation knowledge among related specialties, and promote the implementation of the multidisciplinary team model in Chinese hospitals, which may have many challenges24. Furthermore, consistent with the high power distance characteristic of Chinese and broader Asian cultures, patients’ acceptance of OT was primarily anchored in trust in professional authority, often leading to a pattern of passive participation and compliance from the very outset of care11,25. While such trust facilitates initial engagement, it may inadvertently suppress patients’ active involvement in goal-setting and treatment planning, laying a fragile foundation for sustained intrinsic motivation14. The core contribution of this finding lies in empirically demonstrating that the referral pathway is not merely an administrative process but a critical leverage point: enhancing physicians’ rehabilitation literacy and fostering interdisciplinary collaboration are essential first steps to improving patient access and establishing a more active, collaborative care dynamic from the outset.

Moving beyond initiation, the study’s second theme illuminates the Lived Experience of the Therapeutic Process, revealing a significant disconnect between the profession’s client-centered philosophy and the day-to-day reality of standardized care delivery. A predominant pattern of passive compliance was evident, wherein patients deferred to therapist directives without voicing their own preferences or concerns, a dynamic encapsulated by the sentiment of not daring to question the expert team. This cultural deference, while respectful on the surface, effectively stifles the collaboration and shared decision-making advocated in contemporary rehabilitation, and a lack of patient-provider communication has been directly linked to poorer functional outcomes and diminished engagement26–28. The quality of therapeutic activities—the primary medium through which occupational therapists effect change—emerged as a critical concern. Patients frequently reported monotonous, unchanging activities that were poorly matched to their individual capabilities and delivered without adequate explanation of their therapeutic purpose, leading to a pervasive feeling of “assembly-line” treatment. These deficiencies point to a fundamental gap in clinical reasoning during the processes of activity analysis, grading, and adaptation, which are core competencies of the occupational therapy profession8. When activities are perceived as too difficult, irrelevant, or inexplicably repetitive, patients’ psychological needs for competence and autonomy are thwarted, directly eroding motivation and precipitating disengagement14. The issue of inadequate individualization was further compounded by infrequent updates to treatment plans, suggesting not only a lack of clinical responsiveness but also potential complacency in therapeutic practice. The “assembly-line” experience reported by participants highlights a disconnect between the client-centered principles foundational to occupational therapy and the realities of standardized service delivery8. This gap may reflect not only systemic pressures toward efficiency but also limitations in therapist training in activity analysis, grading, and the design of individualized interventions. Furthermore, the finding that patients focused narrowly on physical restoration rather than holistic life participation suggests that occupational therapists could more explicitly communicate how functional gains translate into meaningful daily occupations during initial goal-setting and throughout the treatment process. There are only five universities in mainland China that have carried out PT, OT sub-professional classification, and obtained WCPT and WFOT dual international certification, such as Capital Medical University, and most colleges and universities and higher vocational colleges and universities have not yet met the minimum standards of rehabilitation education in the alliance, and even have not yet enrolled students in rehabilitation therapy subtype majors29. This has resulted in a small number of professionally trained occupational therapists working in hospital department rehabilitation institutions in China. Thus, a significant proportion of occupational therapy services may be provided by professionals without specialized occupational therapy education, such as physiotherapists or nurses in dual roles, resulting in deficiencies in occupation-centered clinical reasoning. Consequently, therapy discontinuation was driven as much by these process-related failures—loss of interest and perceived lack of progress—as by systemic constraints such as rigid discharge protocols and financial burden. The key contribution of this finding is the elucidation of a “compliance trap,” wherein the confluence of cultural deference, non-individualized “assembly-line” activities, and insufficient therapist clinical reasoning suppresses patient voice, erodes intrinsic motivation, and ultimately leads to a mechanistic and frequently truncated therapy experience that falls short of the profession’s client-centered mandate.

Despite the significant process-related challenges, the third theme affirms that patients reported meaningful Perceived Outcomes and Functional Gains. The most frequently cited improvements were in motor control and dexterity, followed by increased strength and reduced spasticity, with cognitive improvements noted less frequently. In terms of daily life participation, the majority of gains were concentrated in basic activities of daily living (ADL), such as dressing and self-care, with some patients also achieving successful community reintegration, including returning to driving and work. These tangible improvements served as the most powerful validators of OT’s efficacy and the strongest motivators for continued engagement. However, a critical observation emerged: many patients in Chinese rehabilitation settings tend to focus narrowly on physical functional restoration, with comparatively less attention paid to how these gains translate into meaningful life participation. This phenomenon may be partly attributed to the collectivist and family-centered nature of Chinese culture, where patients often rely on family members for care which is associated with Confucian values of Chinese culture such as filial piety, thus reducing the sense of urgency to achieve full functional independence30,31. Yet, as China faces an increasingly aging population, enabling individuals to live independently not only alleviates the burden on families and society but also affirms personal dignity and self-worth. These findings point to a gap between functional improvement and its application in daily life, indicating that sustained motivation requires occupational therapists to link motor gains to meaningful occupations. The progress observed in patients who transitioned from a narrow focus on physical function to active participation in daily activities further underscores the value of occupation-centered guidance in bridging this gap. When recovery has stalled, increased use of assistive devices and changes in the way patients engage in activities also consistently increase their independence and ability to participate in life.

Finally, the fourth theme synthesizes the multifaceted Facilitators and Barriers to Satisfaction. The most striking and perhaps counterintuitive finding was that therapist attentiveness to patient needs and the quality of therapeutic communication emerged as the most frequently cited factors influencing satisfaction, surpassing even the traditional emphasis on therapist technical competence. This compellingly indicates that patients possess a deep-seated desire to be seen, heard, and understood within the therapeutic relationship. The evidence consistently demonstrates that patient-centered communication enhances the therapeutic alliance, improves treatment adherence and engagement, increases patient satisfaction, and leads to better overall health outcomes32. However, the question of how to conduct effective therapeutic communication—communication that not only conveys empathy but also educates patients about their condition, explains the purpose of activities, and collaboratively negotiates goals—remains a critical area requiring further attention and professional training. Observable functional progress served as a powerful facilitator of satisfaction, yet its counterpart, functional plateau, was a primary reason for discontinuation, underscoring the delicate balance therapists must strike between celebrating gains and managing expectations during periods of stagnation. On the contextual and policy level, financial burden emerged as the most formidable barrier to sustained engagement which is consistent with previous research33. Although medical insurance covers a portion of rehabilitation expenses, the majority of patients receiving rehabilitation are those with chronic conditions such as stroke, for whom the cumulative costs of long-term treatment combined with daily living expenses impose a substantial economic strain on families34. In this context, the development and uptake of commercial insurance may offer a viable risk-mitigation strategy. Additionally, the adequacy and modernization of rehabilitation facilities, including access to high-tech equipment such as transcranial magnetic stimulation and virtual reality, were identified as factors influencing patient participation and satisfaction, reflecting patients’ expectations for accelerated recovery through technological innovation. Finally, patients themselves recognized the critical importance of public education and awareness-raising about rehabilitation and occupational therapy, acknowledging that timely access to treatment can significantly mitigate long-term disability—a sentiment that aligns with China’s current national efforts to promote universal health literacy through various media platforms and community outreach initiatives35. The core contribution of this analysis is the identification of a clear hierarchy of factors influencing satisfaction: the therapeutic relationship—characterized by attentiveness and communication—has emerged as the single most powerful facilitator, surpassing even technical competence, while systemic financial barriers and limited access to modern technology constitute the most formidable obstacles, underscoring the necessity of addressing both the therapeutic relationship and systemic constraints.

Limitations and strengths

While this study offers rich, contextualized insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. The occupational therapy department of this hospital has three occupational therapists. There are 60 beds in the ward, and the occupational therapists would rotate according to the needs. The single-center sample may limit the transferability of findings to other geographical or institutional settings within China. Additionally, the study focused solely on patient experiences without incorporating therapist perspectives, which may limit a fuller understanding of the reported therapeutic dynamics. While interviews were conducted in Mandarin and transcribed into English with verification by a bilingual researcher, some nuances of participants’ expressions may have been lost in translation. Furthermore, the cultural context of high power distance profoundly shapes the reported experiences, and themes related to deference to authority should be interpreted within this specific framework. Potential for researcher bias was mitigated through reflexivity, member checking, and analyst triangulation. The study’s principal strengths lie in its in-depth qualitative design, which captured nuanced patient experiences often missed by quantitative surveys, and its successful amplification of the patient voice.

Conclusion and future research

In conclusion, to enhance patient satisfaction with occupational therapy services in China, a multi-pronged approach is warranted. First, rehabilitation knowledge dissemination and public education must be strengthened to improve referral rates and enable timely access to services, alongside enhancing the rehabilitation literacy of physicians across relevant specialties. Second, there is a pressing need to invest in the professional skills training of occupational therapists, particularly in activity analysis, clinical reasoning, and the design of individualized, graded therapeutic activities that prevent the “assembly-line” experience. Third, therapists must prioritize the cultivation of effective therapeutic communication skills, learning to attend to patients’ emotional needs, explain the purpose of interventions, and collaboratively set goals to foster a truly client-centered partnership. Fourth, integrating modern high-tech equipment into therapy can enhance engagement and accelerate the rehabilitation process. Future research should focus on developing and testing culturally adapted models of shared decision-making that empower Chinese patients within a high power distance context, designing interventions to sustain motivation through functional plateaus, and conducting longitudinal research to evaluate the impact of policy changes—such as expanded outpatient insurance coverage and public health education campaigns—on long-term rehabilitation outcomes. Ultimately, by heeding these patient voices and grounding improvements in a nuanced understanding of culture, system, and the primacy of the therapeutic relationship, occupational therapy in China can evolve from a standardized process into a truly collaborative, adaptive, and empowering journey toward recovery.
