Novel biomass-based cellulosic biofiller from Artocarpus heterophyllus fruit seed outer peel: Extraction and characterization
Authors: Gokulkumar Sivanantham, Divya Divakaran, Gurupranes Sivaraj Vijaya, Sivarao Subramonian, P Senthamaraikannan, Indran Suyambulingam, Naushad Edayadulla, Siva Avudaiappan, Palanisamy Bruntha Devi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the extraction and characterization of novel microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) from Artocarpus heterophyllus (jackfruit) seed outer peels and establish its suitability as a sustainable biomass source. MCC was obtained through a multi-step method consisting of alkaline treatment, acid hydrolysis, and oxidative bleaching. Structural and physicochemical features were analyzed using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), Ultraviolet-Visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy, X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA), Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC), Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX), and laser diffraction particle size analysis. FTIR analysis confirmed the removal of lignin and hemicellulose, while XRD revealed a high crystallinity index of 73.14%. UV-Vis analysis indicated the presence of chromophoric groups, suggesting possible relevance in biomaterial-related applications. TGA and DSC analyses demonstrated that the extracted MCC possessed thermal stability up to 256.6 °C along with distinct thermal transitions. AFM and SEM analyses revealed a fibrous surface morphology with rough texture and heterogeneous microparticle distribution. These findings confirm that MCC derived from jackfruit seed outer peel (JSOP) exhibits promising structural and thermal characteristics suitable for future bio-composite reinforcement applications.Keywords: Artocarpus heterophyllus, Cellulose extraction, Jackfruit seed outer peel (JSOP), Characterization, Thermal stability, CrystallinitySubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

The most abundant organic material on Earth is natural cellulose, which is found in minerals, plants, and animals. However, growing environmental concerns associated with synthetic polymeric materials derived from petrochemical sources have encouraged the development of sustainable, cellulose-based alternatives. As the ecological damage caused by synthetic materials increases, people have started showing interest in natural fiber composites because they are readily available, inexpensive, and cause minimal environmental damage1,2. Organic waste or biomass, which is typically discarded in landfills, can be utilized as reinforcements in materials3. Plant fibers can be harvested from stems4, bark5, leaves6, seeds7, and roots8. High-quality cellulose can be obtained using processes such as pretreatment, alkali treatment9, acid hydrolysis10, bleaching11, and purification12. These processes yield cellulosic materials such as fibers, micro, and nanocellulose, which depend on the plant, environment, and its geo-location13. This novel discovered cellulosic materials are characterized using techniques such as FTIR, XRD, SEM, TGA, and UV analysis to evaluate their physicochemical and structural attributes.

Recent studies14–22 on microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) extraction have demonstrated significant advancements in the utilization of various agricultural and industrial residues for sustainable composite applications. For instance, Millettia pinnata-derived cellulose exhibited a cellulose content of 54% and a tensile strength of 310 MPa, making it comparable to cotton and linen and suitable for textiles and biopolymer composites23. Similarly, Tabernaemontana divaricate stem fibers, extracted via microbial degradation, showed a crystallinity index of 62.9% and a cellulose content of 67.3%, with a remarkable tensile strength of 249.25 MPa, demonstrating their potential in polymer composite reinforcement for automotive and structural applications12. Similarly, neem oil cake-derived cellulose (NCC), extracted from Azadirachta indica agro-industrial residual waste, displayed 73.53% cellulose content, 66.23% crystallinity, thermal stability up to 335.71 °C, and a particle size of 17.93 μm, indicating its suitability as a bio-filler for eco-friendly composites24. Additionally, Syzygium cumini (Jamun) seed-derived cellulose, primarily investigated for its potential in polymer reinforcement, contains 43.28% cellulose and exhibits a crystallinity index of 42.63%, with thermal degradation resistance up to 316.5 °C, supporting its integration into biodegradable materials25.

Recent advancements in cellulose extraction have focused on utilizing agricultural waste26, particularly fruit peels, to develop high-performance microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) for sustainable and biodegradable applications. Among these, jackfruit27, citrus28, pineapple29, and Terminalia catappa peels30 have emerged as promising sources because of their high cellulose content and ease of processing. A study on jackfruit peel-derived MCC compared organic and inorganic acid hydrolysis, revealing that organic acids such as citric and oxalic yielded nanocellulose with higher crystallinity (above 70%) and better thermal stability than inorganic acids, which produced smaller particles (100–160 nm) but with slightly lower structural integrity27. Similarly, citrus peel-derived MCC exhibited a crystallinity index of 72.54%, a thermal degradation temperature of 308.17 °C, and a density of 1.413 g/cm3, demonstrating its suitability for polymer composite reinforcement28. In another study, pineapple peel MCC, obtained through enzymatic and mechanical treatments, significantly improved biodegradable film properties, with a tensile strength increase to 26.01 MPa and 96% degradation within a day, highlighting its potential for packaging applications. Meanwhile, Terminalia catappa peel-derived cellulose nanofibers achieved 82% crystallinity and a maximum degradation temperature of 372 °C, superior to many other natural sources, making it highly suitable for structural composites30. Beyond fruit peels, rice husk MCC demonstrated a 94.39% cellulose content, 77.8% crystallinity, and thermal stability up to 299.14 °C, further reinforcing its viability as a green composite material31. These findings collectively underscore the value of fruit peel-based MCC as an eco-friendly alternative to synthetic reinforcements, offering superior biodegradability, structural integrity, and mechanical performance, making it an ideal candidate for sustainable material development.

Beyond fruit peel-based MCC, numerous other biomass sources have been explored for cellulose extraction, each offering unique structural, thermal, and mechanical properties suitable for various applications. Corn straw-derived cellulose, for instance, has been extensively modified using UV/TiO2 photocatalytic oxidation, which effectively removes lignin and hemicellulose, leading to an increase in cellulose content and superior dispersion in polymer composites32. The pretreated cellulose composites exhibited enhanced mechanical strength, with a 90.27% weight loss after enzymatic degradation, making them promise for biodegradable applications. Similarly, mango leaf-derived MCC displayed a crystallinity index of 58.6% and a crystalline size of 20.28 nm, with high thermal stability in processing conditions between 180 and 200 °C, suggesting its suitability for high-performance bio-based composites33. Similarly, Ocimum tenuiflorum leaves, another lignocellulosic biomass, yielded MCC with a crystallinity index of 68.94% and thermal stability up to 187.83 °C, while also demonstrating a yield of 45.58% and a density of 1.398 g/cm3, indicating its potential in polymer reinforcement34. In a comparative study, reddish shell bean MCC extracted via ultrasound-assisted methods demonstrated a crystallinity index of 67.5% and superior adsorption capabilities, achieving 96.88% methylene blue dye removal efficiency, highlighting its potential for wastewater treatment35. Meanwhile, rice husk-derived MCC contained 94.39% cellulose, had a crystallinity of 77.8%, and exhibited thermal stability up to 299.14 °C, making it a robust bio-filler material for sustainable composites36.

Despite extensive research on cellulose/MCC/Nanocellulose extraction from various natural sources such as fruit peels, agricultural residues, and industrial byproducts, the utilization of outer peel of jackfruit seed as a MCC source remains unexplored. Prior studies have focused on MCC, demonstrating their potential in polymer composites. However, there is a significant research gap in investigating jackfruit seed outer peel (JSOP), a commonly discarded biowaste, as a cellulose-rich material for reinforcement applications. Given its high cellulose content (18% to 22%), biodegradability, and structural stability, exploring this underutilized biomass could provide a cost-effective, sustainable alternative to synthetic fillers. The scope of this study includes isolating and characterizing MCC from jackfruit seed outer peel (JSOP), evaluating its structural, thermal, and mechanical properties, and assessing its potential as a bio-filler for composite materials. This research aims to contribute to waste valorization, sustainable material development, and environmental conservation by introducing a novel cellulose source for biodegradable polymer reinforcement. The findings from this study will not only bridge the existing knowledge gap but also promote eco-friendly composite production, reducing dependence on non-renewable resources and mitigating environmental waste.

Materials and methods

Materials

The jackfruit (Artocarpus heterophyllus) seeds used in this study were obtained from a jackfruit processing firm located in Palakkad District, Kerala, India. The seeds were collected, washed with distilled water, and air-dried before the MCC extraction process. All chemicals required for the extraction process, namely sodium hydroxide (NaOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), acetic acid (CH3COOH), and sodium hypochlorite (NaClO2), were purchased from Scientific Chemicals, Coimbatore District, Tamil Nadu.

Isolation of MCC from jack fruit seed outer peel (JSOP)

Microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) was extracted from jackfruit seed outer peel (JSOP) using a standard chemical treatment method commonly employed for lignocellulosic biomass. Initially, the raw jackfruit seed outer peel was washed thoroughly with distilled water to remove adhering impurities, followed by air-drying and pulverization into a fine powder. Prior to alkali treatment, the powdered jackfruit seed outer peel (JSOP) was treated with dimethylformamide (DMF) and 3% ammonia solution at a solvent ratio of 3:1 (v/v) and heated at 70 °C for 2 h under continuous stirring at 500 rpm to facilitate the removal of waxes, pigments, and other residual impurities.

The treated material was subsequently washed thoroughly with distilled water before further processing was performed. Dewaxing and delignification were then performed using 5% sodium hydroxide (NaOH) treatment at 60 °C for 8 h to remove waxes, oils, hemicellulose, lignin, and other non-cellulosic components. The alkali-treated material was repeatedly washed with distilled water until a neutral pH was attained. Subsequently, acid hydrolysis was carried out using 5% hydrochloric acid (HCl) at 60 °C for 12 h to hydrolyze the amorphous regions of cellulose and promote the formation of microcrystalline cellulose (MCC). Further bleaching treatment was performed using a mixture of 5% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO2) and 5% acetic acid (CH3COOH) at 60 °C for 60 min to remove residual lignin and improve the purity of the extracted MCC. The obtained MCC was repeatedly washed with distilled water, oven-dried at 50 °C for 12 h, and finally ball-milled to obtain fine MCC powder. Based on previous literature reports, JSOP is estimated to contain approximately 45–55% cellulose, 20–30% hemicellulose, and 15–25% lignin content. Following chemical treatment and purification, the MCC recovery yield was estimated to be in the range of 77–85%, which is comparable to other agro-waste-derived MCC sources reported in the literature. Characterizations of MCC from jackfruit seed outer peel cellulose (JSOP)

FTIR analysis

The functional groups and chemical composition of the isolated MCC from JSOP were analyzed using FTIR spectroscopy (Jasco FT/IR-4000 Series). Samples were prepared by pressing 2 mg of powdered fiber into potassium bromide (KBr) pellets. Spectra were recorded in reflectance mode at 30 °C over a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1, with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans per minute.

UV-visible spectroscopy

The optical absorption properties of the isolated MCC from the JSOP were analyzed using a JASCO V-750 UV-visible spectrophotometer. For sample preparation, a homogeneous suspension was prepared by dissolving 2 g of MCC in a solvent at a 3:1 solvent-to-MCC ratio (w/v). The mixture was centrifuged to isolate the supernatant, which was then filtered to remove undissolved particulates. The clarified solution was then transferred to a quartz cuvette for spectral analysis. Absorbance measurements were conducted across the ultraviolet spectrum under controlled conditions: temperature maintained between 30 and 32 °C, operating frequency of 20 kHz, and amplitude set to 60 Hz. The instrument operated at a power consumption of 350 W during data acquisition.

XRD analysis

The MCC from JSOP was characterized using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD) (Rigaku SmartLab, Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The instrument was operated at 40 kV and 50 mA with a monochromatic Cu-Kα radiation source (wavelength: 1.5406 Å). The samples were mounted on the holder and analyzed in step-scan mode with a scan rate of 5.00°/min across a 2θ range of 5° to 70°. TGA analysis

The thermal stability and degradation behavior of the MCC extracted from the JSOP were evaluated using a Perkin Elmer TGA 4000 analyzer. Approximately 5–10 mg of the powdered MCC sample was heated in an aluminum crucible under a nitrogen atmosphere (flow rate: 20 mL/min) from ambient temperature to 600 °C at a heating rate of 20 °C/min. Mass loss (% weight) was recorded as a function of temperature, and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves were generated to identify the degradation stages.

Differential scanning calorimetric analysis (DSC)

Thermal transitions (endothermic/exothermic processes) in the extracted cellulose were characterized using a Mettler Toledo DSC 823e calorimeter. Samples (10–15 mg) were sealed in aluminum crucibles and subjected to three cycles under argon purge: Heating: -50 °C to 450 °C at 10 °C/min, Cooling: 450 °C to -50 °C at 10 °C/min, and Reheating: -50 °C to 450 °C at 10 °C/min. Each cycle included a 3-minute isothermal hold between phases to equilibrate the system.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis

The surface topography and roughness parameters of the extracted MCC from the JSOP were quantified using a Park Systems XE-100 AFM (USA). MCC samples were prepared by ultrasonically dispersing 0.01 g of powdered fiber in 20 mL of acetone for 20 min. High-resolution scans were performed using a 0.1 nm granularity probe. The key roughness metrics include average roughness (Ra), root mean square roughness (Rq), skewness (Rsk), and kurtosis (Rku).

SEM, EDX, and particle size analysis

The surface morphology and elemental composition of the extracted MCC from JSOP were analyzed using a Thermo Scientific™ Axia™ ChemiSEM™ Scanning Electron Microscope, which operates at 5–10 keV and was manufactured by Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA. The samples were gold-sputtered using an Edwards Sputter Coater (UK) to eliminate the charging effect prior to observation. Axia ChemiSEM enhances the SEM–EDS workflow by improving the collection, processing, and presentation of elemental data in real time. Elemental mapping of the MCC sample accurately identified the distributions of oxygen (O), carbon (C), chlorine (Cl), and sodium (Na). Five replicate EDX spectra were averaged to determine the weight and atomic percentages of each element, ensuring precise compositional analysis. The particle size distribution of the MCC was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53t) by analyzing high-resolution SEM images. The images were scaled (µm per pixel), and individual particles were isolated using a thresholding algorithm.

Roughness analysis

Surface roughness was evaluated using ImageJ software (version 1.53t). The SEM images were transformed into 8-bit grayscale, and the extracted 3D surface plot plugins yielded roughness parameters Ra, Rq, Rsk, and Rku for the entire scanned area. High-frequency noise was cut off using a Gaussian filter (σ = 2 μm), and to ensure reproducibility, five random regions (50 × 50 μm2) from each MCC sample were selected for analysis.

Results and discussions

FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectrum of the MCC extracted from the JSOP (Fig. 2) reveals the critical functional groups and chemical modifications induced during processing. The broad absorption band at 3328 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations, indicative of extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding within the cellulose fibrils37. These peaks underscore the hydrophilic nature of the extracted MCC, consistent with the hierarchical structure of cellulose Iβ polymorphs. The breadth of this band reflects strong hydrogen-bonding networks and a trace of crystalline MCC, which is consistent with the XRD crystallinity data. The asymmetric and symmetric C–H stretching vibrations of the methyl and methylene groups in cellulose and residual hemicellulose were observed at 2916 cm−138, respectively. The sharp peak at 1630 cm−1 arises from two overlapping phenomena: O–H bending of adsorbed water, suggesting hygroscopicity due to microfibril porosity, and conjugated C = O stretching from residual hemicellulose (xylan), implying partial retention of non-cellulosic polysaccharides despite alkaline treatment39.

FTIR analysis of extracted MCC from JSOP.

The absence of a distinct lignin signature (aromatic C=C at 1505–1600 cm−1) confirms effective delignification. The prominent peak at 1157 cm−1 is assigned to C–O–C antisymmetric stretching in β-(1→4)-glycosidic linkages40, a defining feature of the polysaccharide backbone of cellulose. This peak intensity corroborates the preservation of cellulose chain integrity post-extraction. The 1033 cm−1 band corresponds to C–O stretching in pyranose rings (cellulose/hemicellulose), while the weaker shoulder at 1103 cm−1 suggests residual acetyl groups (C–O–C) from hemicellulose, highlighting opportunities for further purification41. The band at 1323 cm−1, attributed to C–O stretching42, is ambiguous. However, the lack of definitive lignin markers (1265 cm−1 for guaiacyl units) reinforces the successful delignification. The 543 cm−1 band, although weak, likely arises from out-of-plane C–OH bending or skeletal vibrations, consistent with the β-glycosidic configuration of MCC43. These findings align with MCC extracted from other lignocellulosic sources (e.g., Bombax ceiba44 but are distinguished by the hemicellulose residuals at 1103 cm−1, suggesting that alkaline treatment (5% NaOH, 8 h) may require optimization for complete hemicellulose removal. The congruence of the spectrum with Punica granatum45 underscores the universality of MCC’s FTIR profiles across plant taxa but emphasizes species-specific nuances in peak intensities. The FTIR analysis of Ocimum tenuiflorum34 revealed peaks like those of MCC from JSOP, with O-H stretching observed at 3325.84 cm−1 and C-O stretching at 1018.61 cm−1. For Palm Fruit (PF-MCC)46, the peaks included O-H stretching at 3337.77 cm−1, C-H stretching at 2920.2 cm−1, and C-O stretching at 1027.61 cm−1. In the case of Martynia annua (MASF)47, peaks were observed at 3734 cm−1 for O-H stretching, 2387 cm−1 for C-H stretching, and 1230 cm−1 for C-O stretching. The FTIR spectrum of MCC from JSOP was in line with that of Ocimum tenuiflorum34 and palm fruit46 suggesting the existence of functional groups such as hydroxyl, methyl, and carbonyl groups. This is consistent with the successful purification of MCC, as the FTIR analysis demonstrated the predominance of characteristic cellulose functional groups along with substantial removal of lignin-associated peaks. However, minor residual non-cellulosic constituents may still remain in trace amounts, which is commonly observed in chemically treated lignocellulosic biomass-derived MCC materials. The peaks due to residual hemicellulose imply that some degree of retention of the amorphous regions could be retained, which would affect the mechanical characteristics in further uses. This resemblance illustrates that MCC from JSOP ought to be able to respond with polymeric matrices as other cellulose sources do, thus making it appropriate for similar uses in composite materials.

UV spectroscopy analysis

UV-Vis spectroscopic analysis (Fig. 3) revealed that the microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) derived from jackfruit seed outer peel (JSOP) exhibited a prominent absorbance peak at 286 nm, indicating the presence of chromophoric groups. This absorption peak suggests the presence of conjugated systems, such as residual lignin fragments, phenolic derivatives, or other UV-active functional groups associated with the lignocellulosic structure. Compared with MCC obtained from other fruit peel sources, such as orange peel-derived MCC, which exhibited an absorption peak at 283.54 nm, the slightly higher absorption wavelength observed for MCC from JSOP may be attributed to differences in the chemical composition and molecular arrangement of the residual functional groups28. The observed shift in absorbance suggests the possible presence of additional conjugated structures or altered intermolecular interactions that influence the optical characteristics of the extracted MCC. This optical behavior may be relevant for future applications of MCC from JSOP in UV-shielding biomaterials, optical coatings, and antioxidant-related material systems.

XRD analysis

The crystalline structure of the extracted microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) from jackfruit seed outer peel (JSOP) was analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD), and the obtained diffraction pattern is shown in Fig. 4. The XRD spectrum exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to the native cellulose I polymorph, which is commonly observed in plant-derived cellulose materials. A broad low-intensity diffraction peak observed at approximately 2θ = 15.67° corresponds to the overlapping contributions of the (1–10) and (110) crystallographic planes, representing the amorphous and less ordered regions of cellulose. The prominent high-intensity peak centered at 2θ = 22.25° is attributed to the (200) crystallographic plane of cellulose I, confirming the presence of an ordered crystalline cellulose structure.

XRD analysis of extracted MCC from JSOP.

The diffraction pattern obtained in the present study is consistent with previously reported MCCs derived from lignocellulosic biomass sources, such as citrus peel28, rice husk48, jackfruit peel27, and other agro-waste materials49. The absence of additional diffraction peaks associated with cellulose II or cellulose VI polymorphs indicates that the chemical treatment preserved the native cellulose I crystalline arrangement without significant polymorphic transformation. Generally, cellulose VI is produced through the specific ammonia treatment of cellulose III and is not commonly observed in naturally derived plant cellulose systems under conventional alkali-acid extraction conditions.

Using the Segal equation, the crystallinity index (CrI) of MCC from JSOP was calculated to be 73.14%, indicating a highly ordered, semi-crystalline structure. This is greater than that of Punica granatum (67.95%)45 and Azadirachta indica A. Juss (71%)24, but lower than Butea monosperma (88.3%)50 and Abutilon indicum (77.35%)51. The relatively high crystallinity may be attributed to the removal of amorphous constituents, such as hemicellulose, lignin, waxes, and other non-cellulosic components, during alkali treatment, acid hydrolysis, and bleaching processes. Increased crystallinity generally contributes to improved stiffness, thermal resistance, and structural stability of MCC materials, making them potentially suitable for reinforcement-related applications in sustainable composite systems.

TGA with DTG analysis

The thermal degradation behavior of the MCC extracted from JSOP was systematically analyzed using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and derivative thermogravimetry (DTG). The TGA curve revealed three distinct degradation stages (Fig. 5). The initial stage (up to 150 °C) corresponded to the evaporation of adsorbed moisture and volatile components, resulting in a weight loss of 11.61 wt%. This is consistent with the hygroscopic nature of cellulose, which is attributed to its hydrophilic nature of hydroxyl groups. The second stage, spanning 200–350 °C, exhibited a pronounced weight loss of 38.78%, which was attributed to the decomposition of hemicellulose and primary degradation of cellulose.

TGA with DTG analysis of extracted MCC from JSOP.

The DTG curve highlighted a peak degradation temperature of 256.6 °C, indicative of the cleavage of glycosidic bonds and depolymerization of cellulose chains. Notably, the final residue at 600 °C amounted to 23.78%, suggesting the presence of thermally stable carbonaceous residues and minor inorganic impurities retained after chemical treatment. In comparison, MCC extracted from Citrus x sinensis peel28 exhibited a higher degradation temperature (308.17 °C) and lower residue (3.70%), attributed to its higher crystallinity index (72.54%) and efficient removal of lignin during chemical treatment. Similarly, banana pseudostem cellulosic fillers52 demonstrated superior thermal stability, with a maximum degradation temperature of 370.94 °C, owing to their high cellulose content (59.22%) and compact crystalline structure53. The relatively low degradation temperature (256.6 °C) observed in this study may be associated with the presence of amorphous cellulose regions, trace residual non-cellulosic constituents, and thermally stable inorganic residues originating from the chemical treatment process. These factors may have contributed to the earlier thermal decomposition behavior compared to that of highly purified MCC systems.

These findings align with broader trends in lignocellulosic biomass valorization. For example, sugarcane bagasse-derived MCC54 degraded at 264.15 °C with 75% weight loss, while olive leaf derived MCC demonstrated stability up to 286.1 °C, emphasizing the role of fiber origin and processing methods55. The residual ash content (23.78%) in the present study suggests potential inorganic contaminants from chemical treatments, which could be mitigated through advanced purification methods.

DSC analysis

The differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) thermogram of MCC from JSOP (Fig. 6) revealed multiple thermal transitions associated with moisture evaporation and thermal degradation behavior of the cellulose structure. The first endothermic transition observed at 78.16 °C (onset: 50.07 °C, endset: 106.97 °C) corresponds to the evaporation of absorbed moisture and low-molecular-weight volatile compounds present within the MCC matrix. This behavior is characteristic of hydrophilic lignocellulosic materials owing to the abundance of hydroxyl functional groups capable of hydrogen bonding with water molecules. The second endothermic peak centered at 224.83 °C (onset: 210.54 °C, endset: 254.53 °C) is attributed to the initial thermal decomposition of amorphous cellulose regions and residual hemicellulosic constituents rather than melting of semi-crystalline cellulose. Unlike conventional thermoplastic polymers, cellulose does not exhibit true melting behavior because extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding leads to thermal degradation prior to melting. Therefore, the observed thermal event is associated with depolymerization, cleavage of glycosidic linkages, and degradation of less ordered cellulose domains. The final exothermic transition at 322.16 °C (onset: 300.83 °C, endset: 324.96 °C) corresponds to the major thermal degradation and pyrolytic decomposition of crystalline cellulose regions, involving rapid chain scission and carbonaceous char formation. The sharp exothermic behavior indicates the decomposition of the ordered cellulose structure and agrees well with previously reported MCCs derived from agro-waste biomass sources. The observed thermal transitions collectively confirm the semi-crystalline nature and thermal stability of MCC derived from JSOP.

DSC analysis of extracted MCC from JSOP.

Banana MCCs exhibit a higher degradation onset (~ 370 °C) because of their high cellulose content (59.22%) and dense crystalline structure53. Citrus x sinensis MCCs showed superior thermal stability (degradation peak at 308.17 °C), which was attributed to their higher crystallinity index (72.54%) and effective lignin removal28. In addition, MCC from Jackfruit peel27 demonstrated a sharper exotherm at 323 °C, correlating with its high purity and crystallinity (83.42%). The DSC profile underscores the influence of extraction methods and biomass sources on the thermal behavior. Residual non-cellulosic components (e.g., lignin and hemicellulose) and incomplete delignification likely contributed to the lower degradation temperatures observed in this study. Optimizing bleaching and alkali treatment can enhance the crystallinity and thermal stability of MCCs, thereby broadening their applications in high-temperature polymer composites56. These findings align with global efforts to tailor agro-waste MCCs to specific industrial needs, balancing sustainability and performance.

AFM analysis

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis of the MCC from JSOP (Fig. 7) revealed critical insights into its surface topography and nanoscale roughness, which are intrinsically linked to its structural and functional properties. AFM imaging was performed in non-contact mode (NC-AFM) to preserve the native morphology of the MCC, with two distinct scan scales (10 and 2.5 μm) to evaluate the scale-dependent surface characteristics.

AFM analysis of extracted MCC from JSOP: (a) 2D AFM topographical image, (b) 3D AFM surface morphology, (c) Surface roughness profile at 10 μm scan area, and (d) 2D AFM topographical image, (e) 3D AFM surface morphology, (f) Surface roughness profile at 2.5 μm scan area.

At the 10 μm scale, the MCC surface exhibited a root mean square roughness (Rq) of 8.842 nm and an average roughness (Ra) of 6.623 nm, indicating a relatively smooth topography. The negative skewness (Rsk = -1.237) suggests a surface dominated by valleys, likely resulting from the removal of non-cellulosic components (e.g., lignin and hemicellulose) during extraction, leaving behind porous regions. The kurtosis (Rku = 5.433) highlights a moderately peaked surface with sharper features compared to a Gaussian distribution. At 2.5 μm, higher-resolution imaging revealed increased roughness (Rq = 16.767 nm, Ra = 10.366 nm), emphasizing nanoscale heterogeneity. The pronounced negative skewness (Rsk = -3.337) and elevated kurtosis (Rku = 22.960) indicate deeper valleys and sharper peaks, which are characteristic of exposed cellulose microfibrils and residual amorphous regions. This aligns with chemical treatments (e.g., alkaline hydrolysis), which preferentially degrade amorphous cellulose, leaving crystalline domains and creating fibrillar aggregates. The negative skewness across both scales reflects a valley-dominated surface, consistent with nanopore formation during delignification. Such porosity enhances the surface area, which is beneficial for applications such as adsorption or composite reinforcement. The higher kurtosis at 2.5 μm signifies a leptokurtic surface (sharp peaks and heavy tails), likely due to the alignment of cellulose fibrils or localized aggregation of the fibrils. This heterogeneity is typical of biomass-derived MCCs, where the incomplete removal of hemicellulose or lignin residues creates irregular topographies.

The disparity in roughness values between the scan sizes underscores the scale-sensitive nature of the AFM measurements. Larger scans (10 μm) average out nanoscale features, yielding lower Ra/Rq values, whereas smaller scans (2.5 μm) resolve finer details, such as individual microfibrils (20–50 nm diameter) and inter-fibrillar gaps. This is consistent with studies on citrus peel MCC28, where fibril exposure after chemical treatment increased roughness at submicron scales. The Ra values (6.6–10.4 nm) were lower than those reported for sulfuric acid-hydrolyzed pineapple peel MCC (Ra = 15–30 nm), likely due to the milder extraction conditions preserving fibril integrity. However, the Rsk and Rku trends aligned with those of sodium chlorite-bleached pomegranate peel MCC45, where lignin removal created similar valley-dominated surfaces.

Roughness analysis

Roughness analysis of the MCC from the JSOP surface (Fig. 8) revealed a complex topography characterized by significant variations in height and texture. The root mean square (RMS) roughness (Rq = 39.0347 μm) and average roughness (Ra = 30.9427 μm) indicate a highly irregular surface, which is further supported by the peak-to-valley height (Rt = 257.816 μm). The positive skewness (Rsk = 0.8288) suggests a surface dominated by peaks rather than valleys, contrasting with the valley-dominated surfaces typically observed in AFM studies of MCC extracted from fruit peels28,57–59. This peak-dominated profile may arise from the aggregation of cellulose microfibrils or the incomplete removal of non-cellulosic components, such as lignin or hemicellulose, which can create protruding features. The kurtosis value (Rku = 1.1456) indicates a relatively flat surface with fewer sharp peaks than other cellulose systems, such as pomegranate peel MCC45, which often exhibits higher kurtosis owing to fibrillar aggregation.

Roughness analysis of extracted MCC from JSOP: (a) Surface topography image, (b) 3D surface roughness morphology, (c) Surface profile indicating peak-to-valley distribution, and (d) Quantitative roughness parameter analysis.

Compared to AFM data, which typically resolves roughness at the nanoscale (Rq ~ 8–17 nm), the macroscopic roughness values here (Rq ~ 39 μm) highlight the multiscale nature of MCC surfaces. For instance, the AFM analysis of the MCC from JSOP revealed a valley-dominated surface with negative skewness, in contrast to the peak-dominated profile observed here. This discrepancy underscores the influence of the measurement scale and resolution on the roughness characterization. At the macroscale, surface irregularities, such as fibril bundles or residual debris, become more pronounced, whereas AFM captures finer details such as individual microfibrils and nanopores.

Compared to other fruit peel MCCs, the MCC from JSOP exhibits unique roughness characteristics. For example, pineapple peel nanocellulose29 treated with sulfuric acid typically exhibits lower Ra values (~ 15–30 nm) owing to the formation of uniform fibrillar structures. Similarly, pomegranate peel MCC45 bleached with sodium chlorite often displays smoother surfaces (Ra ~ 6–10 nm) with negative skewness, reflecting effective delignification. The higher roughness and positive skewness observed here suggest a less refined extraction process, which may leave behind more non-cellulosic residues or induce fibril aggregation than other methods. The novelty of this MCC lies in its distinct roughness profile, which may be advantageous for certain applications. For instance, a peak-dominated surface may enhance mechanical interlocking in composite materials, whereas a high Rt value may increase the surface area for adsorption-based applications. However, the relatively flat kurtosis suggests limited sharp features, which may reduce its effectiveness in applications that require high surface energies, such as catalysis. By refining extraction protocols to reduce fibril aggregation and residual impurities, the surface properties can be further optimized for targeted industrial applications, bridging the gap between sustainable agro-waste valorization and high-performance material production.

Particle size analysis

The particle size analysis of the MCC from JSOP was conducted using SEM imaging and statistical distribution methods. The SEM micrograph (Fig. 9a) reveals irregularly shaped particles with varying sizes, and the corresponding intensity distribution graph indicates a dominant mode at 53 μm in size. The measured particle lengths ranged from 54.52 to 165.55 μm, with a mean length of 98.76 μm and a standard deviation of 33.97 μm (Fig. 9d). The statistical data (Fig. 9c) further support a heterogeneous distribution, which aligns with the histogram that follows a normal distribution pattern. Compared to MCC from other fruit peels, MCC from JSOP exhibited a finer and more uniformly distributed particle size. For instance, orange peel MCC28 showed a broader size distribution with a mean value of 112 μm, indicating larger aggregates, while pineapple peel MCC29 demonstrated larger fibrous structures with average sizes exceeding 150 μm. Similarly, the pomegranate peel MCC45 exhibited a wider dispersion with a non-uniform particle morphology. The relatively small particle size of MCC from JSOP enhances its surface area, making it potentially more effective for applications requiring high reactivity, such as biocomposites fillers, drug delivery carriers, or adsorbents. These results highlight the superior fineness and potential usability of these MCC compared with other peel-derived MCCs.

SEM analysis

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis of MCC from JSOP revealed significant morphological characteristics crucial for understanding its structural integrity and potential applications. The SEM micrographs (Fig. 10a–c) illustrate that the microparticles of these MCC exhibit irregular shapes with a polydisperse distribution, where the particles vary in size and possess rough surface textures. The presence of sharp edges and fragmented structures suggests breakage during processing, which may influence the mechanical properties and functional behaviors. Additionally, the surface debris observed in the SEM images indicated the partial degradation of the polymer composite matrix, likely owing to mechanical stress or environmental exposure. The fractured debris displayed heterogeneous structures, indicating variations in the crystallinity and amorphous content of the cellulose matrix. Moreover, the analysis of the rough surfaces of the filler highlighted the presence of porous and uneven textures, which could enhance mechanical interlocking and increase the surface area for potential applications such as reinforced biocomposites or adsorption materials. The high surface roughness and porous morphology suggest improved interaction capabilities, making MCC from JSOP a promising material for applications in biocomposites, sustainable packaging, and filtration systems. SEM analysis confirmed that the microparticles of this MCC possessed a rough, fragmented morphology with filler integration, which can enhance their mechanical stability and functional versatility in various industrial and environmental applications.

EDX analysis

Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis was performed to investigate the elemental composition of the extracted microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) derived from jackfruit seed outer peel (JSOP). The obtained spectrum along with quantitative elemental analysis is presented in Fig. 11. The EDX spectrum confirmed the predominance of carbon (C) and oxygen (O), which are the principal constituent elements of cellulose-based materials, thereby validating the successful extraction and purification of MCC from lignocellulosic biomass. Quantitative EDX analysis revealed that carbon and oxygen exhibited atomic percentages of 46.6% and 33.5%, respectively, with corresponding weight percentages of 36.2% and 34.6%. The high carbon and oxygen content is characteristic of cellulose-rich materials owing to the polysaccharide backbone composed of β-(1→4)-linked D-glucose units. The substantial oxygen content further confirms the abundance of hydroxyl (-OH) functional groups within the cellulose structure, which is consistent with the FTIR results showing prominent O–H stretching vibrations at approximately 3328 cm−1. These findings indicate that the extracted MCC retained the characteristic chemical composition expected for purified cellulose materials.

EDX Spectrum of MCC from JSOP.

In addition to carbon and oxygen, trace quantities of sodium (Na), chlorine (Cl), nitrogen (N), and silicon (Si) were detected. Sodium and chlorine were observed with atomic percentages of 7.1% and 4.9%, respectively, which may be attributed to residual chemical species originating from the alkali treatment and bleaching processes involving sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and sodium hypochlorite (NaClO2). The presence of these elements suggests that minor traces of processing chemicals remained adsorbed on the MCC surface even after repeated washing. Similarly, the small amount of nitrogen detected (7.6 atomic %) may have originated from the ammonia-assisted solvent treatment used during the purification stage. The detection of silicon in trace quantities (0.3 atomic %) could be associated with naturally occurring mineral impurities present in the biomass source or contamination introduced during sample handling and preparation.

The EDX spectrum also exhibited relatively minor peaks corresponding to elements such as calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), and nickel (Ni), which are commonly observed as trace mineral constituents in natural lignocellulosic biomass materials. The low intensities of these peaks indicate that the extraction and purification processes effectively reduced the concentration of inorganic impurities while preserving the structural integrity of the MCC.

Morphologically, the SEM-EDX coupled analysis revealed that the extracted MCC possessed irregular fragmented structures with rough and porous surfaces, which can enhance surface interactions and filler–matrix adhesion in composite systems. The elemental distribution pattern observed in the selected regions demonstrated a relatively homogeneous dispersion of carbon and oxygen throughout the MCC surface, confirming the uniformity of the extracted material composition.

Conclusions

The extraction and characterization of microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) derived from jackfruit seed outer peel (JSOP) demonstrated its promising structural, physicochemical, and thermal characteristics for sustainable material development. FTIR analysis confirmed the effective removal of hemicellulose and lignin, with characteristic MCC functional groups observed at 3328 cm−1 (O–H stretching) and 2916 cm−1 (C–H stretching), indicating successful purification of the lignocellulosic biomass. UV-Vis spectroscopy revealed a prominent absorbance peak at 286 nm, suggesting the presence of chromophoric functional groups associated with residual conjugated structures. XRD analysis demonstrated a crystallinity index of 73.14%, confirming the semi-crystalline nature and structural ordering of the extracted MCC. Thermal analyses through TGA and DSC indicated that MCC from JSOP exhibited thermal stability up to 256.6 °C along with characteristic thermal transition behavior associated with moisture evaporation, cellulose degradation, and char formation. AFM and SEM analyses revealed rough, fibrous, and heterogeneous surface morphologies with porous microstructural features, while particle size analysis confirmed a mean particle size of 98.76 ± 33.97 μm with relatively uniform distribution. The observed surface roughness, crystallinity, and thermal behavior suggest that MCC derived from JSOP possesses characteristics comparable to other agro-waste-derived MCC materials reported in the literature. Therefore, the present study demonstrates the successful valorization of jackfruit seed outer peel waste into MCC using chemical treatment methods, highlighting its potential relevance for future applications in sustainable biocomposites and biomass-derived material systems.
