C-reactive protein-to-lymphocyte ratio and stroke: associations with prevalence and subsequent mortality in NHANES 1999–2010
Authors: Yan Miao, Jianxin Chen, Zhiwei Zheng, Yunxiao Zhang, Ruiqi Liu, Junyi Chu, Sen Li, Ying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early risk detection is critical for timely intervention to reduce stroke morbidity and recurrence rate. This study systematically investigated the association between the CRP-to-lymphocyte ratio (CLR) and stroke prevalence, aiming to evaluate whether CLR may serve as a convenient and readily accessible inflammatory biomarker for stroke prediction. Data were analyzed from the 23,483 participants in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) between 1999 and 2010. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to assess the relationship between CLR and prevalent stroke. Among 789 individuals with a history of stroke, Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the association between CLR and all-cause as well as cause-specific mortality. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed to compare survival outcomes across CLR strata defined by an optimal cutoff. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was applied to explore potential nonlinear relationships. The predictive value of CLR was compared with that of established inflammatory markers using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. After adjusting for potential confounders, elevated CLR is significantly and independently associated with higher odds of stroke prevalence in the fully adjusted model (OR = 1.005, 95% CI: 1.002–1.007, P < 0.001). Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrates significantly higher mortality risks among stroke participants with CLR above the optimal threshold. RCS models showed no evidence of nonlinearity between the CLR and mortality risks. Furthermore, ROC analysis indicated that CLR exhibited superior predictive ability compared to the established inflammation markers, including the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and lymphocyte counts (all P < 0.05). These findings suggest that CLR is an independent and significant predictor of both the stroke prevalence and all-cause mortality in stroke patients, highlighting its potential utility as a readily accessible inflammatory biomarker for stroke prevalence stratification. Given its ease of derivation from routine examination data, CLR holds promise for broad clinical applicability. Further prospective studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and to validate its predictive value in diverse populations.Trial registration: This study is a secondary analysis of publicly available NHANES data and does not constitute a clinical trial; therefore, trial registration is not applicable.

Introduction

Stroke is a leading cerebrovascular disease characterized by high morbidity, mortality, and recurrence rates [1, 2]. Most patients remain unaware of their stroke risk prior to the symptom onset. Many individuals with minor stroke often fail to recognize the early signs and thus miss the optimal intervention opportunities until stroke recurrence or incidental detection during routine medical examinations. Timely intervention is critical to reduce stroke morbidity and recurrence rate, underscoring the importance of convenient routine screening tools for early risk detection [3–5]. Immune-inflammatory balance disorder represents a crucial pathological feature of post-stroke syndrome. Growing evidence also shows that stroke not only triggers local neuroinflammation, but also exerts profound effects on systemic immunity [6, 7]. The detectable nature of this balance disorder highlights its potential as an auxiliary early warning sign in routine patient monitoring.

Inflammation is a critical driver in the pathogenesis of acute stroke [8]. Cerebral ischemic leads to energy depletion and neuronal injury, subsequently evoking the robust inflammatory cascades [9]. This process includes the activation of resident microglia at the lesion site and the infiltration of peripheral leukocytes into brain tissue [10, 11]. The extent of the acute inflammatory response, followed by a phase of immunosuppression, plays a pivotal role in determining clinical outcomes. Excessive inflammation exacerbates neuronal damage, while systemic immunosuppression increases susceptibility to infection. Approximately, one-third of stroke patients develop pneumonia as a complication, which significantly contributes to elevated mortality and disability rates [12, 13]. Thus, the readily available hematological markers reflecting both of inflammation status and immune function may serve as early predictors for the risk of stroke and post-stroke mortality.

In clinical practice, systemic inflammatory status is commonly assessed through the measurement of white blood cells, neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, and C-reactive protein (CRP). Both CRP and lymphocyte count, as classical markers of inflammation, have been closely associated with poor outcomes and increased mortality in patients with ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke, as described in large numbers of meta-analytical studies [14]. However, the prognostic utility of these individual markers in predicting stroke risk is limited, given their sensitivity to a wide range of inflammatory conditions and infection [15, 16]. The aggregation of such markers into composite ratios, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), has been shown to offer enhanced prognostic and clinical value [17–19].

Recently, the C-reactive protein-to-lymphocyte ratio (CLR) has emerged as a promising biomarker that reflects the balance between inflammation and immune function. Accumulating evidence supports its prognostic and diagnostic utility across various pathological conditions. For instance, CLR has been shown to be negatively associated with the cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in patients with chronic heart failure and to retain independent prognostic significance after multivariable adjustment. Furthermore, CLR has demonstrated independent predictive capability in the in-hospital cardiac mortality in older patients with non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) [20] and has been strongly correlated with the morbidity in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [21].

Given the central role of inflammation and immune dysregulation in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, CLR as a composite marker integrating both inflammatory and immune status, may offer improved predictive capacity for stroke prevalence, particularly in the context of acute inflammatory events and subsequent stroke-associated immunosuppression. Therefore, the present study employed a combination of cross-sectional and longitudinal cohort study designs to evaluate the association between CLR and stroke prevalence and post-stroke mortality, using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). This work also aimed to validate the potential utility of CLR as a predictive biomarker for stroke in future clinical applications.

The NHANES study was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics’ Research Ethics Review Board. No further ethical clearance was required because NHANES data are accessible to the public.

Data for this analysis came from six waves of the NHANES, which ran from 1999 to 2010. Participants younger than 20 were not included in the analysis. Assessment of CLR

Peripheral blood lymphocyte counts and CRP concentrations were determined using automated blood analysis instruments. The CLR was computed with the following formula: (CRP [mg/dL] × 100) / lymphocyte count [cells/µL] [21].

Assessment of stroke

The self-reported diagnoses of participants’ in-person interviews were used to define stroke. We considered a person to have had a stroke if they said “Yes” when asked if a doctor or other health professional had ever informed them that they had a stroke [22, 23].

Covariates

Sociodemographic variables, lifestyle choices, and chronic medical disorders associated with stroke were the main types of confounders in this research. All participants were at least 20 years old. Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and Other Races were the five categories through which ethnicity was categorized. Marital status was categorized into six groups: married, widowed, divorced, separated, never married, and living with partner. Education level was categorized into five groups: less than 9th grade, 9th to 11th grade, high school graduate/General Education Development (GED) or equivalent, some college or associate degree, and college graduate or above. Tobacco use was classified as either smoker or non-smoker. Underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obese were the four categories used to classify BMI. Alcohol consumption was categorized based on self-report as non-drinkers or drinkers. Participants who answered “Yes” to the questions, “Has a doctor or health professional ever told you that you have diabetes?” or “Has a doctor or health professional ever told you that you have hypertension?”, were classified as having diabetes or hypertension, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Due to the collaborative multi-agency nature of NHANES data collection, the occurrence of missing data was unavoidable. Winsorization was utilized to handle extreme CLR values, adjusting values below the 1st percentile up to this percentile and those exceeding the 99th percentile down to the 99th percentile. Given NHANES’ sophisticated, multi-stage probability sampling design, baseline characteristics, logistic regression, Cox proportional hazards models, and restricted cubic spline analyses incorporated sampling strata, primary sampling units, and appropriate examination sample weights using the R survey package (version 4.2).

For pooled analysis across six NHANES cycles (1999–2010), mobile examination center (MEC) weights were recalculated following NHANES analytic guidelines. Differences in baseline characteristics between stroke and non-stroke groups were assessed using design-adjusted Wald tests for continuous variables and Rao-Scott chi-square tests for categorical variables. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range, IQR] for non-normally distributed data, while categorical variables are summarized as unweighted frequencies with survey percentages.

Weighted logistic regression models assessed associations between CLR and stroke prevalence. Model 1 had no adjustments. Model 2 included demographic and lifestyle variables: age, sex, ethnicity/race, education, PIR, marital status, smoking, alcohol consumption, and BMI. Model 3 further adjusted for self-reported physician-diagnosed diabetes and self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension.

For the survival analysis, a retrospective cohort was constructed from the 789 participants who reported a history of stroke at baseline. CLR was calculated from laboratory values obtained during the NHANES MEC visit, which served as the baseline measurement. Mortality status and follow-up duration were ascertained through linkage to the National Death Index (NDI) through December 31, 2019, with follow-up time defined as months from the MEC examination to death or censoring. The optimal CLR cutoff for survival stratification was determined using maximally selected rank statistics via the surv_cutpoint function in the survminer R package, with a minimum proportion of 10% required in each group.

Weighted restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was employed to examine potential nonlinear relationships between CLR and all-cause mortality among stroke participants. The accuracy of CLR as a predictor for all-cause mortality among stroke participants was evaluated via ROC curves and areas under the curve (AUC). Multivariate weighted Cox regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for the outcome associated with CLR. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses, grouped by CLR quintiles, visually represented survival differences.

To compare the discriminative ability of CLR with other established inflammatory markers, paired ROC curve comparisons were conducted using the DeLong test. The comparator markers and their definitions were as follows: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR = neutrophil count/lymphocyte count); systemic immune-inflammation index (SII = neutrophil count × platelet count/lymphocyte count); C-reactive protein; and lymphocyte count alone. All hematological parameters were obtained from the NHANES Complete Blood Count (CBC) module, and CRP from the Biochemistry Profile. AUC comparisons were performed using the pROC package in R with the roc.test function (method = “delong”).

Statistical analyses were executed with R software (version 4.4.3), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

Data from 23,483 participants in NHANES (1999–2010) were analyzed. Among these participants, 789 individuals reported experiencing a stroke (mean age: 66.98 ± 13.80 years), while 22,694 individuals did not (mean age: 48.84 ± 18.15 years). Significant statistical differences between stroke and non-stroke groups were observed for age, sex, ethnicity/race, education, PIR, marital status, smoking status, alcohol intake, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and CLR (Table 1). Most variables exhibited statistically significant differences (P < 0.001), while sex (P = 0.007) and ethnicity/race (P = 0.002) also reached statistical significance.

Associations between CLR and stroke

Weighted logistic regression demonstrated a substantial relationship between CLR and stroke prevalence. In the unadjusted model, higher CLR significantly correlated with elevated stroke prevalence (odds ratio [OR] = 1.010, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.008–1.011, P < 0.001). After demographic adjustments including age, sex, ethnicity/race, education, PIR, marital status, smoking, alcohol consumption, and BMI (Model 2), CLR maintained a significant correlation with stroke prevalence (OR = 1.005, 95% CI: 1.002–1.008, P < 0.001). Further adjustments for self-reported diabetes and hypertension (Model 3) confirmed the persistence of this significant positive correlation (OR = 1.005, 95% CI: 1.002–1.007, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Analysis of CLR and all-cause mortality in stroke

Fig. 3 shows that a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was employed to explore the connection between CLR levels and mortality among individuals diagnosed with stroke. Even after controlling for multiple potential confounders, CLR continued to exhibit a statistically significant positive association with increased mortality risk (HR = 1.003, 95% CI: 1.000–1.006, P = 0.023). These results underscore CLR’s potential utility as an independent prognostic biomarker for patients with stroke.

Fig. 3Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis of the association between CLR and all-cause mortality in stroke survivors

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis of the association between CLR and all-cause mortality in stroke survivors

Kaplan–Meier survival curves based on CLR quintiles (Q1–Q5, Fig. 4A) revealed no statistically significant difference (global log-rank test, P > 0.05). This result may be due to reduced event numbers in each stratified group, limiting statistical power. In contrast, a cutoff-based classification gave significance difference consistent with the notion that cut-off grouping is better on capturing risk differences. To enhance discriminative ability, an optimal CLR cutoff (approximately 64.12) was determined using the survminer package. Participants were subsequently categorized into low-CLR and high-CLR groups. Survival differences between these two groups were statistically significant (log-rank test, P = 0.034; Fig. 4B). Notably, the high-CLR group exhibited consistently lower survival probability compared with the low-CLR group throughout the follow-up period. These findings suggest that the cutoff-based grouping more sensitively reflects the association between CLR and mortality risk.

Given that Kaplan-Meier analysis does not account for potential confounders and that the cutoff selection may influence statistical power, Cox regression served as the primary analytical method. Additional validation included weighted RCS and weighted ROC analyses.

Fig. 5 illustrates the relationship between CLR and all-cause mortality (ACM) risk using RCS functions within a multivariable-adjusted, survey-weighted Cox proportional hazards model. This model incorporated demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and clinical factors, alongside sampling weights, strata, and clustering from the NHANES complex survey design. The weighted RCS analysis demonstrated a significant overall association, with no evdience of a nolinear relationship between CLR and ACM (P-overall = 0.038, P-nonlinear = 0.228). A pronounced increase in mortality risk was observed when CLR values exceeded approximately 64.12, suggesting a potential exploratory cutoff for early intervention.

Discussion

Through integrated cross-sectional and retrospective cohort analysis, the current investigation demonstrates that abnormally elevated CLR levels are correlated with increased stroke prevalence and ACM following stroke. After adjusting for multiple covariates, CLR exhibited superior predictive efficiency compared with commonly used inflammatory mediators including NLR, SII, CRP, and lymphocyte count. Weighted RCS analysis revealed a marked increase in mortality risk. These findings support the potential of CLR as a supplemental biomarker in predicting stroke prevalence and mortality.

Previous research has documented the associations between CLR and multiple chronic conditions. He et al. demonstrated that elevated CLR substantially increased the risk for myocardial infarction [24]. Analysis of the NHANES database reveal additional associations between hyper-CLR and COPD [21], chronic kidney disease [25], gallstones disease [26], and multiple hepatic conditions, including increased liver stiffness and metabolically dysfunctional steatotic liver disease [27, 28]. These reports suggest that abnormal CLR elevation reflects systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation, serving as both a trigger and consequence of multiple pathologies. Given that stroke pathogenesis involves chronic inflammation, autoimmune disorder, and atherosclerosis, these established associations warrants investigation into the role of CLR in stroke prevalence and prognosis. Our result corroborates prior findings and indicates CLR provides valuable information for stroke prevalence stratification and outcomes prediction. These results highlight its potential as a biomarker for cerebrovascular disease risk assessment, underscoring the need for further research into its mechanistic contributions to stroke pathogenesis.

CLR combines two essential inflammatory markers: CRP and lymphocyte count. CRP, as the prototypic acute-phase protein, contributes to stroke through the induction of endothelial dysfunction and acceleration of atherosclerosis. A positive correlation has been demonstrated between elevated CRP levels and increased stroke attack and adverse outcomes [29]. In contrast, lymphopenia represents defective immune regulation, leading to overactive inflammation and increased infectibility [30]. Reduced lymphocyte counts in stroke patients are correlated with poor functional recovery and elevated mortality [31]. By simultaneously capturing systemic inflammation and innate immune dysfunction, CLR provides a more comprehensive assessment of inflammation–immune disequilibrium compared to single markers (CRP or lymphocyte count) [28]. This dual-property mechanism may underlie its greater predictive capacity for stroke susceptibility. By integrating both dimensions into a single index, CLR may more comprehensively capture pathophysiological changes throughout the onset and progression of stroke than either component alone, thereby offering a reproducible and readily accessible tool for risk stratification and early intervention.

To our knowledge, this research is among the first to systematically evaluate the association between CLR and stroke prevalence and mortality by combining cross-sectional and cohort data. We utilized multivariable weighted Cox regression to estimate the association between CLR and mortality, while RCS models were applied to detect potential non-linear dose–response relationships and minimize information loss caused by categorical aggregation. Moreover, comparing with established inflammatory biomarkers, CLR demonstrates its relative strengths. Given that CLR is derived from the routine physical examination, it offers a cost-effective, simple, and clinically applicable alternative, underscoring its translational potential in stroke prevalence assessment [32].

Several limitations should be recognized. First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inference, and the retrospective cohort analysis remains susceptible to residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. Second, as the number of stroke cases was limited, the generalizability of our findings may be constrained. Third, the threshold value of 64.12 was data-driven and requires validation in larger, multicenter and prospective investigations. Lastly, stroke subtype stratification was not performed. Stroke history in NHANES was identified using a single self-reported questionnaire item, which only asks whether participants had ever been told by a physician that they had a stroke and does not distinguish ischemic from hemorrhagic stroke. In addition, public-use linked mortality files provide broad cause-of-death categories rather than detailed ICD-10 codes for reliable subtype classification. Therefore, subtype-specific analyses were not feasible in this public-use NHANES dataset. Future studies using restricted-use linked claims data or clinically confirmed cohorts are needed to evaluate whether CLR differs in predictive performance across stroke subtypes.

In summary, CLR demonstrates substantial value in stroke risk stratification and prognosis prediction. As a simple composite measure from routine hematological indices, CLR offers a universally applicable alternative. Future studies should explore the integration of CLR with current stroke predictive models and evaluate the clinical efficacy of CLR-based anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory interventions.
