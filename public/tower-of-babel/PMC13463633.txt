Delivery devices and container closure systems for co-formulated and co-administered biologics
Authors: Ming Zhou, Kevin Duffy, Chris Mitchener, Galen Huaiqiu Shi, David Collins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid evolution of antibody-based biologics is transforming the management of chronic conditions and driving innovation in drug-delivery devices and container closure systems (CCS). Combination therapy enabled through coformulation or coadministration offers the potential for synergistic outcomes that are not achievable with single-drug products. This review examines development pathways and emerging strategies for delivery devices and CCS that support antibody-biologic coformulations and coadministration, from clinical trials through commercialization. We evaluate established technologies with an emphasis on design and delivery mechanisms, and on key technical considerations, including compatibility with dual-molecule formulations, protein–protein interactions, sterilization approaches, container closure integrity, and human factors in CCS and device design. We also highlight emerging technologies and future opportunities for advancement. Overall, this review provides insights into the challenges and innovations shaping CCS and delivery systems for co-formulated or co-administered antibody biologics.

Introduction

In 2024, the US FDA authorized 16 biologics, including a record-breaking 13 monoclonal antibodies. The year also marked an all-time high of 18 biosimilar approvals by the FDA [1]. These milestones underscore the pharmaceutical industry’s continued surge in innovation and development across a diverse range of biologic modalities, such as peptide, mRNA, monoclonal antibody (mAb), fusion protein, adeno-associated virus vector, and gene therapy. These advancements are transforming disease management, driven by the rising prevalence of chronic conditions including diabetes, obesity, respiratory diseases, and cancer [2–23].

The majority of antibody biologics are currently administered through intravenous infusion in a clinical setting. However, subcutaneous injections are increasingly preferred over intravenous infusions due to reduced burden on healthcare providers, lower cost, more flexible self-injection by patients, and potentially lower risk of intravenous (IV)-related complications [24–36].

In addition to traditional monotherapy, combination therapy has gained continuously increased attention to achieve new therapeutic outcomes that monotherapy cannot achieve alone [37–45]. Combination therapy can be realized through copackaging, coformulation, or coadministration. Examples of approved combination therapies include Paxlovid, a copackage of nirmatrelvir and ritonavir for COVID-19 treatment [10, 46], Contrave® co-formulated bupropion and naltrexone for chronic weight management [47], and Airsupra inhalation aerosol, a co-formulated product of two FDA-approved active ingredients, albuterol and budesonide, for as-needed treatment or prevention of bronchoconstriction and to reduce the risk of asthma attacks in adults [48]. In addition, there are multiple combination antibody biologics that are in the pipeline and currently under development. For example, Eli Lilly is working on a combination of Ixekizumab and Tirzepatide for the treatment of psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis [49]. Regeneron is developing Linvoseltamab in combination with carfilzomib or bortezomib for the treatment of relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma [50]. Bristol Myers Squibb is creating a subcutaneous formulation of nivolumab (Opdivo) combined with relatlimab (Opdualag) for the treatment of melanoma and other solid tumors [51]. Roche is developing a copackaged mAb therapy, casirivimab and imdevimab (developed by Regeneron Pharmaceuticals), for the prevention and treatment of COVID-19 infections [52]. Additionally, Merck is working on a combination therapy of pembrolizumab and quavonlimab for various cancers, including melanoma, lung cancer, and renal cell carcinoma [53]. Additional information of these approved and pipeline combination therapies can be found in references [10, 46–53].

In 2013, Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) issued the first regulatory guidance for combination therapy development, “Codevelopment of Two or More New Investigational Drugs for Use in Combination” [54]. The guidance requires demonstration of each component’s contribution (per 21 CFR § 300.50), recommends factorial designs, and describes pathways for co-packaged and fixed-combination products. Similarly, EMA’s “Guideline on Clinical Development of Fixed Combination Medicinal Products” (EMA/CHMP/158268/2017; CHMP/EWP/240/95 Rev 2) [55] explicitly applies to biological products and defines three therapeutic scenarios (add-on, first-line, substitution) with corresponding data requirements; Section Container closure integrity for dual-chamber systems requires demonstration of similar pharmacokinetics (PK), usually through bioequivalence (BE), when transitioning from free combination to fixed-combination product, unless pivotal data were generated with the actual co-formulated product. A key practical distinction is that the FDA’s exclusion of CBER biologics means sponsors of co-formulated biologics (e.g. Phesgo) have relied on product-specific FDA advice, whereas the European Medicines Agency (EMA) guideline provides a more structured framework directly applicable to biologic co-formulations.

The rise of combination therapies is driving development of new drug delivery device technologies. In addition to conventional monotherapy platforms such as prefilled syringes (PFSs), autoinjectors, and on-body injectors, more advanced devices have been developed to support various combination therapy types. These include dual-chamber syringes and autoinjectors capable of delivering two biologics (i) together through a short-term premixing step, (ii) sequentially without premixing, or (iii) simultaneously and separately.

While FDA and EMA guidance establishes general principles for the contribution-of-components and regulatory pathways, it does not address the specific technical challenges of co-formulated injectable biologics, including protein–protein interaction challenges, and device design and development considerations for dual-chamber or co-administration delivery systems. This review addresses these gaps by first examining development strategy, biophysical characterization, and established container closure and delivery-device technologies. Then, we discuss key design considerations including sterilization, container closure integrity (CCI), and human factors for co-formulated and co-administered biologic combination products. Finally, we highlight emerging technologies and future opportunities for advancement.

Early phase (Phase 1) of clinical trial

For combination therapy, early-phase (Phase 1) clinical trials (CTs) aim to establish safety of each individual new investigational drug before combination dosing begins. As recommended in FDA’s 2013 codevelopment guidance [54], one acceptable design is sequential testing: subjects receive Drug A alone, then Drug B alone, then A + B together, allowing safety signals from the combination to be distinguished from individual drug effects. Dose-ranging identifies potential optimal doses for each drug in subsequent studies. At this stage, it is uncommon to introduce a commercial-representative delivery device or CCS, as formulations are still evolving. The standard practice is to use vials as the primary container and administer the two drugs separately via single-use syringes.

Compared to monotherapy, additional injection design considerations are needed to build a meaningful foundation for later coformulation development, including injection site, distance between injection sites, interval time, injection angle, speed, and needle design.

Late phase (Phase 2)

Phase 2 trials aim to develop and optimize a formulation and dosage to demonstrate safety and clinically meaningful efficacy. Vials and disposable syringes may remain the primary CCS and delivery device to allow formulation flexibility. During late Phase 2, once an optimum formulation and dosage range are established, the CCS and delivery device option may expand to two PFSs, or potentially a coformulation or coadministration delivery device. Consistency in needle design across delivery devices throughout CT studies is important to minimize later bridging risk.

A critical Phase 2 deliverable is qualifying a CCS and delivery device for the Phase 3 pivotal study. Associated activities include a CCS suitability assessment and qualification, risk management and design verification of the delivery device, and CCS-delivery device integration to support manufacturing and bridging strategies. Coformulation or coadministration can drive additional development activities such as protein–protein interaction assessment, elevated CCS and device performance requirements, unique manufacturing process development, and additional risk mitigation.

Late phase (Phase 3)

In Phase 3, CCS and delivery-device options include vial/syringe, two PFS, a coadministration or coformulation delivery device, depending on the maturity of dose selection, formulation development, and PFS/device readiness. If coformulation is not available at the beginning of Phase 3 but becomes ready during the study, it can be introduced via formulation and/or device-bridging studies to demonstrate PK BE, or PK and pharmacodynamic (PD) comparability, between the individual drug products and the co-formulated product. If coformulation is not available during Phase 3 study, or if coadministration is intended as a commercial administration route, a dual-chamber device or two PFS may be carried through submission and commercialization, with potential transition to coformulation as part of lifecycle management.

Decision roadmap for BE and PK bridging in combination therapy product development

As discussed in Section Late phase (Phase 3), combination therapy development commonly involves three representative bridging scenarios. These scenarios are defined by when a coformulation becomes available relative to Phase 3 and BLA submission. Below, we further discuss the corresponding BE and PK bridging strategies for the coformulation and/or delivery device changes.

Scenario A—Coformulation introduced pre-Phase 3: when coformulation precedes Phase 3, a formulation bridging study is conducted comparing the co-formulated product to the individual mono-products; the acceptance standard is PK BE [90% CI within 80%–125% for AUC (area under the concentration–time curve) and Cmax (maximum serum concentration), assessed independently for each drug]. If strict PK BE cannot be achieved, e.g. due to immunogenicity confounding or drug–drug interaction within the formulation, PK/PD comparability using clinically relevant endpoints may serve as an alternative, subject to FDA concurrence.

Scenario B—Coformulation introduced during Phase 3: when coformulation is not available before Phase 3, pivotal studies proceed using vials, two PFSs, or a dual-chamber coadministration device; a combined formulation and device bridging strategy is executed once the coformulation is available, using a sequential approach (formulation bridge first, then device bridge).

Step 1—Formulation bridge: A PK comparability study compares the co-formulated product to co-administration using the Phase 3 reference device (two PFS or dual-chamber device), with each drug’s PK assessed independently. For a dual-chamber coadministration device used as the Phase 3 reference, the formulation bridge must additionally account for any reconstitution or mixing step inherent to the device that could affect drug stability, delivered concentration, or injection performance, as these factors may confound the PK comparison between the dual-chamber reference and the coformulated product.

Step 2—Device bridge: Once formulation comparability is established, a separate PK comparability study supports the transition from PFS to the intended commercial delivery device (e.g. autoinjector). Injection depth and injection site have been identified as potential influencing factors on PK comparability outcomes for either device type, underscoring the importance of device parameter characterization prior to the bridging study.

Scenario C—Coformulation introduced post Phase 3 or submission: when coformulation is not available during Phase 3 or coadministration is an intended commercial administration route, the two-PFS or dual-chamber presentation is carried through Biologics License Application (BLA) submission, with device bridging (PFS to autoinjector or dual-chamber device) using PK comparability as the primary endpoint; transition to coformulation may be pursued as lifecycle management, at which point a formulation bridging strategy analogous to that described in Scenario B would apply.

Regardless of scenario, two cross-cutting scientific considerations apply to all bridging strategies. First, anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) can accelerate drug clearance, reducing AUC and Ctrough and confounding PK comparability assessments [56–60]. Neutralizing ADAs may also block pharmacological activity, affecting PD-based bridging endpoints. FDA guidance [56, 57] mandates that ADA-positive and ADA-negative subjects be analyzed separately and overall. For co-formulated products, each drug’s immunogenicity must be independently assessed and the impact of co-formulation characterized. The tiered assessment approach (screening, confirmatory, neutralizing antibody, characterization assays) is standard across regulatory jurisdictions. Second, model-informed drug development (MIDD) is transforming the bridging landscape [60–63]. Population PK modeling is now standard for IV-to-subcutaneous (SC) bridging, used to predict SC exposure, support dose selection, and evaluate whether PK differences fall within known inter-individual variability. The FDA MIDD Pilot Program demonstrated direct regulatory impact: dosing changes for ramucirumab and cetuximab were approved based on model-based evidence without additional CTs [60–62].

Challenge in coformulation development—protein–protein interaction and characterization

Co-formulated mAb biologics face a fundamental measurement gap: while single-mAb rheology and viscosity science have matured considerably, systematic quantitative characterization of cross-interactions and viscoelastic behavior in mAb mixtures remains remarkably sparse. When two mAbs are combined at high concentrations (e.g. 150–300 mg/ml total protein), the resulting viscosity and viscoelastic properties can deviate substantially from additive predictions due to the emergence of hetero-association networks superimposed on each molecule’s homo-association tendency.

Oscillatory shear rheology by measuring the storage modulus G′ (elastic energy storage) and loss modulus G″ (viscous energy dissipation) as functions of frequency provides a direct assessment of whether a concentrated protein solution behaves as a free-flowing liquid (G″ >> G′), a viscoelastic fluid, or an elastic gel (G′ > G″). Schermeyer et al. measured G′ and G″ across 50–200 mg/ml for IgG solutions and established the crossover angular frequency (ω_CO, where G′ = G″) as a colloidal stability predictor: solutions with ω_CO below ~1000 rad/s were aggregation-prone [64]. Sarangapani et al. demonstrated that at 120 mg/ml and pH 3.0, a mAb formed a viscoelastic gel with elastic yield stress, while the same molecule at pH 7.4 remained a viscous liquid, confirming that pH critically governs the sol–gel transition at formulation-relevant concentrations [65]. Singh et al. established the first predictive framework for hetero-interactions using a geometric mean relationship across 5–250 mM ionic strength [66], while Brudar et al. showed the Kirkwood–Buff integral G₂₂ outperforms B₂₂ as a viscosity predictor [67]. Woldeyes et al. cautioned that interaction parameters do not consistently predict viscosity rank order at 150 mg/ml [68]. Stability convergence, where co-formulated species drive each other toward intermediate degradation kinetics, represents an additional risk [69]. Although these studies examined single-mAb systems, they establish a framework directly applicable to co-formulations, where superposed homo- and hetero-association networks may shift the sol–gel transition to lower total protein concentrations.

When co-formulated solutions exceed the ~20 cP subcutaneous injectability threshold, excipient mitigation is necessary; arginine-based co-solutes have demonstrated viscosity reductions to target ranges at 220–250 mg/ml [70, 71]. Critically, no published study reports G′/G″ data or cross-Kirkwood–Buff integrals for defined two-mAb mixtures. Co-formulated products require characterization of long-term hetero-association governing shelf-life viscosity and injection force, while dual-chamber coadministration systems involve only transient mixing (seconds to minutes), a distinction with direct implications for device platform selection and deliverability risk assessment.

Device platform selection depends directly on closing these gaps. Co-formulated products require full characterization of long-term hetero-association governing shelf-life viscosity and injection force. Dual-chamber ready-to-mix systems confine inter-protein contact to seconds-to-minutes, making time-dependent viscosity evolution during this transient window the critical deliverability parameter. Characterizing hetero-association kinetics as a function of mixing duration thus represents a foundational need for rational device selection and injection force modeling across all combination biologic delivery modalities.

Established delivery device and CCS technology for combination therapy

Overview of combination therapy approaches

Two biologics or one biologic and its excipient can be delivered using one of four approaches: as a single injection (coformulation); via a premixing and dosing mechanism (ready-to-mix); separately without premixing (coadministration w/o premixing); or consecutively without premixing (sequential administration). These four types are illustrated in Fig. 2. A detailed comparison is provided in Supplementary Table A1.

Combination therapy and subsections discussed in this article.

In this article, coadministration refers to the simultaneous or sequential delivery of two or more therapeutic agents to a patient through a single integrated delivery system. It can be achieved by: (i) mixing separate drugs only at the point of injection to allow delivery of multiple drugs together through one injection (ready-to-mix coadministration); (ii) injecting multiple drugs separately and simultaneously through a single integrated device (coadministration w/o premixing); or (iii) injecting multiple drugs separately and sequentially through a single integrated device (sequential administration). In contrast, “two single injections” refers to the administration of two drugs via two independent, separate delivery devices (e.g. two PFSs), each administered as a distinct injection. Under this definition, sequential administration is indeed a form of coadministration, as both drugs are pre-integrated into a single delivery system and administered through one administration event, despite being delivered sequentially.

Coformulation

In a co-formulated drug product, all active ingredients and excipients are fully mixed and mutually compatible, with each drug remaining stable throughout shelf life and in-use period. Delivered as a single dose, coformulation is the preferred option for multi-component therapies due to patient convenience.

As discussed in Section Challenge in coformulation development—protein–protein interaction and characterization, protein–protein interactions in co-formulated biologics can significantly increase viscosity, often exceeding the limits of conventional PFS and standard autoinjectors. Both formulation and delivery device innovations have been pursued to address this challenge. On the formulation side, viscosity-reducing excipients (e.g. arginine or camphorsulfonic acid) can partially mitigate elevated viscosity but are often insufficient when both homo- and hetero-association contribute; microparticle suspension has emerged as a novel alternative that enables high-concentration delivery without proportionally increasing solution viscosity [72]. On the device side, selecting a PFS or autoinjector configured with a larger-gauge needle can be an effective mitigation for administering viscous coformulations. When viscosity remains prohibitively high despite these measures, lowering the protein concentration and increasing the delivery volume offer another path forward, one that requires large-volume injectors (LVIs) or on-body delivery systems (OBDS) capable of delivering volumes greater than 2 ml.

LVIs include large-volume disposable autoinjectors such as YpsoMate 5.5 (Ypsomed) [73] and Maggie 5.0 (SHL Medical) [74], which extend delivery volumes to ~5 ml. On-body delivery systems (OBDS) are wearable devices that deliver drug subcutaneously over several minutes; commercial examples include YpsoDose (2–10 ml) [75] and West SmartDose (3.5 and 10 ml) [76]. Large-volume autoinjectors are typically suitable for 2–5 ml deliveries, whereas OBDS platforms are preferred for volumes exceeding 5 ml or when extended, hands-free delivery is desired. Additional information of LVI and OBDS can be found in Supplementary Table A2.

Ready-to-mix coadministration

Ready-to-mix coadministration is suitable for two liquid solutions incompatible for extended mixing but stable for a very short period (seconds to minutes), or for lyophilized drug/liquid reconstitution. During shelf life, the two drug solutions remain separate until premixing at the point of injection. Three technology types achieve this purpose, varying by mixing activation mechanism, number of containers, and container design.

Dual-chamber PFS/cartridge with barrel bypass

A dual-chamber PFS or cartridge with a barrel bypass consists of two partitioned chambers within a single syringe or cartridge body. The distal (rear) chamber typically holds a liquid; the proximal (front) chamber contains a powder, lyophilized drug, or another liquid. The chambers are separated by a front plunger. A bypass on the outside of the barrel provides a fluid path between chambers. When the plunger rod is pressed, both plungers move forward; as the front plunger passes through the bypass, liquid from the rear chamber flows into the front for reconstitution or mixing. Shaking or inverting may be necessary to ensure homogeneous mixing, adding use complexity requiring human factors evaluation. Figure 3a illustrates this procedure.

Several commercial barrel bypass systems are available. Vetter [77, 78] supplies the Lyo-Ject® glass syringe (1, 2.5, and 5 ml) and the V-LK® glass cartridge (1.5 ml), originally for lyophilized drug/diluent reconstitution but also suitable for liquid/liquid combinations. Kaleo offers the Aerio™Mix autoinjector [79, 80] for lyophilized or liquid/liquid mixing, accommodating a 5 ml cartridge with 18–29-gauge needles and a gas cylinder drive system with automatic needle retraction and optional data connectivity. Ypsomed supplies the LyoTwist™ [81, 82], using a bypass glass cartridge activated by twisting the rear cap, with variable dose selection from 0.1 to 1 ml across eight settings. AstraZeneca and GlaxoSmithKline launched Bydureon® and Eperzan®/Tanzeum™ in LyoTwist™ devices for Type 2 diabetes in 2014 [83, 84].

A key commercialization hurdle for barrel bypass systems is the need for a new fill-finish process incorporating two sequential fill-plunger placement steps under aseptic conditions. Very limited CMOs currently have this capability. Figure 4 compares fill-finish processes for regular and dual-chamber PFS. Additional design considerations include: (a) achieving adequate mixing efficacy when two liquids differ significantly in viscosity or density; (b) developing effective CCI monitoring for each chamber independently; (c) ensuring standard barrel siliconization provides sufficient lubricity for simultaneous movement of two plungers; and (d) evaluating the more complex E&L profile from exposure of two plungers to two drug solutions.

Unlike barrel bypass systems, Credence offers a dual-chamber PFS creating a fluid bypass via a needle piercing through the front plunger [85, 86]. The pre-attached needle has two notches positioned so that both reside inside the partially filled front chamber before administration, reserving space for reconstitution or mixing. When the plunger rod is pressed, both plungers move forward until the needle pierces the front plunger, creating a bypass allowing liquid to flow from the rear to the front chamber. The primary advantage is leveraging the existing supply chain for standard barrel components and flexibility in customizing the delivery volume per chamber by adjusting the front plunger position during fill-finish without requiring new components. However, challenges persist: the dual-sequential fill-finish process, the requirement to hold the syringe upright during reconstitution, the need to prime the headspace bubble before injection (introducing human factors risk and limiting suitability for large-volume delivery), and particulate risk from needle piercing. Dual-chamber autoinjector with premixing

WindGap Medical offers a dual-chamber autoinjector that stores two drug solutions in side-by-side containers [87, 88]. When mixing is initiated, two septum-piercing needles puncture both cartridges simultaneously, creating a closed fluidic connection for reconstitution or mixing, with the solution then administered through a single needle. The compressed gas drive system handles viscosities exceeding 5000 cP. The side-by-side configuration utilizes standard container closure components, reducing development risk and offering high adaptability to drugs with varying volumes, viscosities, concentrations, excipients, and pH levels. Coadministration without premixing

When two drug solutions are incompatible even during brief mixing, simultaneous yet separate injection offers an effective alternative. This approach utilizes original container closures for each drug, minimizes new CCS qualification, eliminates the dual fill-finish process, and may yield PK/PD profiles comparable to monotherapy. Kaleo’s Aerio™ Duo autoinjector [79, 80] features two separate side-by-side syringes capable of automatically delivering up to 20 ml total (10 ml per syringe), with the two drug products completely isolated throughout shelf life and administration.

Sequential administration

Sequential delivery administers two liquids consecutively and separately through a single injection. Three technology types are available.

The Credence Companion Dual-Chamber Sequential Syringe [85] is similar in construction to the Reconstitution Syringe, with both liquids in a single syringe separated by a front plunger and a pre-attached needle. Key differences: (i) the front chamber is completely filled with no headspace; (ii) front chamber liquid is injected first, with the rear needle notch piercing the front plunger only after the front chamber is completely emptied. Figure 3d illustrates this procedure.

The DebioPass Dual-Chamber Syringe (DebioTech) [89] uses a front plunger with a built-in bypass that opens only when sufficient pressure is reached, expelling the front chamber liquid first and subsequently the rear chamber liquid through the same needle.

WindGap Medical’s DualFlo Sequential Delivery system [87, 88] uses two standard 5 ml cartridges with the two liquids never contacting each other, injected sequentially through the same needle via its compressed gas drive (up to 5000 cP). More details can be found in Table 1.

Sterilization for dual-chamber systems

Most biologic drug products cannot withstand terminal sterilization, necessitating a hybrid sterilization approach whereby device components are terminally sterilized prior to final assembly, while the drug product undergoes sterile filtration and aseptic fill. Dual-chamber systems differ from conventional single-chamber PFS in having a substantially greater drug contact surface area from additional elastomeric closures (front stopper, intermediate bypass stoppers, and in some configurations a tip cap), resulting in two to three times the cumulative elastomer-to-drug contact surface. This amplifies the risk that sterilization residues, persistent free radicals from gamma irradiation or reactive alkylating agents from ethylene oxide, will contact and degrade the biologic. Risk is further elevated with polymeric containers (cyclic olefin polymer (COP) or cyclic olefin copolymer (COC)) due to a greater propensity to generate and retain free radicals, and when two biologics are premixed prior to administration. A robust sterilization strategy must holistically incorporate drug product characteristics, container closure material and design, and an integrated process validation approach ensuring both sterility and drug product compatibility.

Container closure integrity for dual-chamber systems

CCI testing presents unique challenges for dual-chamber configurations. In a conventional single-chamber PFS, CCI testing (e.g. helium leak testing, high-voltage leak detection, vacuum decay, or dye ingress per USP <1207>) is performed across a single sealed system. In dual-chamber systems, integrity must be verified for each chamber independently, including the seal formed by the intermediate plunger. The bypass mechanism introduces additional potential leak paths that must be tested under representative storage, transport, and use conditions. For systems where the bypass is activated by plunger movement, CCI testing must confirm the bypass remains sealed throughout the shelf life and opens only at the intended activation pressure. Validated CCI methods specific to each dual-chamber configuration may require adaptation of standard approaches.

Human factors considerations in dual-chamber delivery devices

Co-administration and dual-chamber delivery devices present unique use-error risks. A critical risk is the priming step required after reconstitution or mixing: users must expel air before injection. Priming is a well-documented source of use errors, and FDA reviews [90] have cited incomplete mixing or improper priming as grounds for rejecting ready-to-mix products.

Architectural limitations compound these risks. Balson [91] identified that front-chamber headspace must accommodate the initial plunger stroke, both drug components after transfer, and mixing space, limiting reconstituted volume to ~4 ml. Venting to the atmosphere is typically required, meaning the device must be kept upright during mixing, introducing orientation-dependent errors. Real-world data confirm these concerns: the EMA Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee (PRAC) assessment [92] of leuprorelin depot medicines documented 169 medication errors for Eligard’s reconstitution system, with 32 at the mixing step, the largest error category, leading PRAC to require device replacement. Werk et al. [93] demonstrated that reconstitution in dual-chamber syringes requires meaningful wait times with active agitation and visual dissolution confirmation. In contrast, no-headspace sequential delivery systems such as the Credence Sequential Syringe eliminate priming entirely, reducing a significant dosing error source. For dual-chamber autoinjectors with manual mixing steps, human factors studies should evaluate mixing completeness in representative populations including dexterity-impaired users, identifying mitigations such as visual confirmation windows or automated mixing cycles [90, 94, 95].

Emerging combination therapy technology: microfluidic mixing-at-the-needle

Microfluidic “mixing-at-the-needle” keeps drug solutions separated during storage and mixes them at injection through embedded microfluidic channels. This addresses a key co-formulation challenge: mixing two antibodies creates emergent degradation pathways including heterogeneous aggregation and stability convergence unpredictable from individual component data [37, 69, 96]. By confining inter-protein contact to minutes rather than months, on-demand mixing eliminates long-term hetero-association risks while preserving each component in its optimized formulation.

Recent advances in additive manufacturing have enabled integrated microfluidic delivery devices. Coates et al. [97] demonstrated a monolithic microfluidic microneedle array patch fabricated at 5.4 μm resolution, capable of dual payload co-delivery and lyophilized biologic reconstitution, validated in vivo. Yeung et al. [98] pioneered 3D-printed hollow microneedles with a spiral mixing chamber for programmable multi-drug ratios. Park et al. [99] introduced a pumpless capillary-driven microfluidic microneedle system for dual-drug delivery without external power. These platforms remain in preclinical development but represent a promising future direction. The key remaining challenge is miniaturizing microfluidic mixing into conventional autoinjector form factors for high-concentration mAb formulations (100–200 mg/ml).

Conclusion and future outlook

Combination therapy leverages the synergistic efficacy of multiple drug products to address chronic diseases, necessitating both advanced CCS and delivery device technologies and a strategy for integrating devices into CTs at the appropriate stage.

Various delivery devices and CCS technologies have been developed, distinguished by factors including drug mixing during shelf life or administration, injection sequence, number of injections, number of container closures, and container design. Beyond safety and efficacy, supply chain complexity, manufacturing feasibility, and cost are indispensable considerations for technically and economically viable solutions.

For dual-chamber syringes with a bypass plunger, complexity extends beyond the dual fill-finish process. Residual volume within the bypass must be carefully accounted for in dose accuracy specifications. Vacuum insertion, while preferred to minimize headspace and improve dosing accuracy, may cause the bypass plunger to collapse, potentially leading to leakage or unintended plunger movement. This issue is particularly pronounced with Fluotech laminated plungers, where the laminate coating is susceptible to cracking under vacuum pressure. A comprehensive approach integrating bypass plunger design, plunger coating, barrel siliconization, and filling process development is essential.

Today, all existing dual-container side-by-side devices are single-use compressed-gas systems. While capable of handling a wide viscosity range, they generate significant product waste and have a large carbon footprint. A multi-use dual-container device would provide long-term cost and convenience benefits. Furthermore, a multi-use device with two independent programmable drive systems would accommodate scenarios where two formulations have different use profiles, e.g. one single-use, low-dosage, preservative-free drug could be paired with a multi-use, high-dosage, preserved drug with preserves, underscoring the need for independently controlled, multi-use delivery platforms.
