A universal method to approach the Poincaré center problem Thanks: The author is partially supported by a MICIN grant number PID2020-113758GB-I00 and an AGAUR grant number 2021SGR-01618.
Isaac A. García and Jaume Giné

Abstract
. We address the classical (degenerate or non-degenerate) center problem posed by Poincaré in the 19th century for monodromic singularities of analytic families of planar vector fields 𝒳 \mathcal{X} . We prove that every analytic center admits a Laurent inverse integrating factor V V in weighted polar coordinates. Moreover, we show that when 𝒳 \mathcal{X} has no local curves of zero angular speed, the Poincaré map is analytic, and if, in addition, V V has an essential singularity, then the singularity of 𝒳 \mathcal{X} is a center. Based on this result, we derive a theoretical procedure to determine parameter constraints within the family that characterize any center of a polynomial vector field. Applications to nontrivial families that have resisted other methods are also provided.

1.
Introduction and main results

In this work, we focus on families of planar real analytic vector fields

\mathcal{X}=P(x,y;\lambda)\partial_{x}+Q(x,y;\lambda)\partial_{y}

defined in a neighborhood of a monodromic singularity, which can be placed at the origin without loss of generality. Here
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the finite set of parameters of the family.

Recall that a monodromic singular point of
\mathcal{X}
is a singularity for which the associated flow rotates around it; consequently, a Poincaré map
\Pi
is well defined on a sufficiently small transversal section with endpoint at the singularity on the origin. Centers and foci are examples of monodromic singularities. We restrict our attention to the monodromic parameter space
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined as the subset of parameters for which the origin is a monodromic singularity of
\mathcal{X}
.

Several algorithmic procedures are available to determine the parameter restrictions that define
\Lambda
; see, for example,
[
9
,
3
,
1
,
2
,
18
]
. The center problem consists in determining the subsets of
\Lambda
corresponding to those
\mathcal{X}
such that the monodromic equilibrium point becomes a center, meaning that all nearby trajectories are closed orbits surrounding the equilibrium and therefore
\Pi
is the identity map. A key simplification occurs when the singular point has no characteristic directions, hence
\Pi
is analytic at the origin, although the computation of a jet may be intractable. Even in this simple case the center problem can be algebraically unsolvable, see
[
4
,
20
,
25
,
21
,
22
,
27
,
33
]
. The harder center problem with characteristic directions is still open and there the function inverse integrating factor has played a relevant role, see
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. A first step for the solution of the center problem consists in to determine the stability of the singularity at first order that corresponds to the computation of the linear part of
\Pi
, also studied in several works, see for instance
[
15
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
.

We consider the Newton diagram
\mathbf{N}(\mathcal{X})
of
\mathcal{X}
; see
[
5
,
1
]
. Each edge of
\mathbf{N}(\mathcal{X})
has slope
-p/q
, where
(p,q)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
are coprime. From now on, we denote by
W(\mathbf{N}(\mathcal{X}))\subset\mathbb{N}^{2}
the set of all such weights
(p,q)
.

Introducing the
weighted polar
coordinates
(x,y)\mapsto(\varphi,\rho)
given by
x=\rho^{p}\cos\varphi
,
y=\rho^{q}\sin\varphi
, and removing a suitable power of
\rho
appearing as a common factor,
\mathcal{X}
is transformed into the
polar vector field

\mathcal{Z}=\Theta(\varphi,\rho)\partial_{\varphi}+R(\varphi,\rho)\partial_{\rho}
, with

R(\varphi,0)=0
and
\Theta(\varphi,0)\geq 0
, assuming without loss of generality that the local flow has counterclockwise orientation.

The vector field
\mathcal{Z}
is defined on the cylinder
C
given by

(1)

C=\{(\varphi,\rho)\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}:0\leq\rho\ll 1\},

where
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\mathbb{R}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})
. The set of
characteristic directions
at the origin of
\mathcal{X}
is defined as

\Omega_{pq}=\{\varphi^{*}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}:\Theta(\varphi^{*},0)=0\}
. Thus, the set
\{\rho=0\}
is invariant under the flow of
\mathcal{Z}
and becomes either a periodic orbit or a polycycle, depending on whether
\Omega_{pq}=\emptyset
or
\Omega_{pq}\neq\emptyset
, respectively. This is just the reason why
\Pi
is analytic when
\Omega_{pq}=\emptyset
.

An inverse integrating factor
V(\varphi,\rho)
of the polar vector field
\mathcal{Z}
on
C
is a real-valued function of class
C^{1}(C\setminus\{\rho=0\})
that satisfies the linear partial differential equation

(2)

\mathcal{Z}(V)=V\;{\rm div}(\mathcal{Z}),

where
{\rm div}(\mathcal{Z})=\partial_{\varphi}\Theta+\partial_{\rho}R
denotes the divergence of
\mathcal{Z}
. Equivalently, (
2
) can be written as the divergence-free condition
{\rm div}(\mathcal{Z}/V)\equiv 0
off the zero-set
V^{-1}(0)
.

We say that
\mathcal{Z}
possesses a
Laurent inverse integrating factor
in
C
if it admits a Laurent series expansion about
\rho=0
of the form

(3)

V(\varphi,\rho)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}v_{j}(\varphi)\rho^{j},

whose coefficients, for fixed
\lambda
, are bounded functions
v_{j}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Two particular cases are the ascending expansions

(4)

V(\varphi,\rho)=\sum_{j\geq m}v_{j}(\varphi)\rho^{j},

and the descending expansions

(5)

V(\varphi,\rho)=\sum_{j\leq m}v_{j}(\varphi)\rho^{j},

both having a leading coefficient
v_{m}\not\equiv 0
with
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Therefore, for any fixed
\bar{\varphi}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the point
\rho=0
for the function
V(\bar{\varphi},\rho)
is either regular at
\rho=0
, or it has a pole or an essential singularity, depending on whether
m\geq 0
or
m<0
in (
4
), or whether there are infinitely many terms with negative exponents as in (
3
) or (
5
).

We emphasize that, in the step-by-step construction of the coefficients in (
4
) and (
5
), explained in Sections
2.1
and
2.2
, respectively, the functions
v_{j}
are indeed
C^{1}
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. In particular,
v_{j}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\Omega_{pq})
in the ascending case (
4
).

In
[
17
]
, it is proved that if the rescaled vector field
\mathcal{Z}/\Theta
admits a Laurent inverse integrating factor (allowing the coefficients
v_{j}
to be unbounded on
\Omega_{pq}
) without an essential singularity at
\rho=0
, that is, of the ascending form (
4
), then the origin of the restricted vector field
\mathcal{X}|_{\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{pq}}
is either a center or a focus of maximal order, where

(6)

\Lambda_{pq}=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\Theta^{-1}(0)\backslash\{\rho=0\}\neq\emptyset\}.

Here, we use the term
focus of maximal order
to denote a focus whose associated Poincaré map
\Pi
is close to the identity and differs from it only by a single nonvanishing Poincaré–Lyapunov quantity; see Remark
9
(i) for details.

Observe that
\mathcal{X}
has no local curves of
zero angular speed
when restricted to
\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{pq}
, although
\{\rho=0\}
may still be a polycycle. We refer the reader to the appendix of
[
17
]
for the computation of
\Lambda_{pq}
, using either branching theory based on the Newton–Puiseux algorithm or techniques from the singularity theory of maps.

In Proposition 5 of
[
19
]
, an example is given of a vector field
\mathcal{X}
with a maximal-order nilpotent focus at the origin, for which
\Omega_{pq}=\emptyset
(hence
\Lambda_{pq}=\emptyset
), and which does not admit a Laurent inverse integrating factor of the ascending form (
4
).

We show below that centers in the class
\Omega_{pq}=\emptyset
always admit a nonsingular ascending expansion of the form (
4
), that is, with
m\geq 0
.

Proposition 1
.

Any analytic center with
\Omega_{pq}=\emptyset
admits an analytic inverse integrating factor
V(\varphi,\rho)
of the form (
4
) with
m\geq 0
.

Remark 2
.

In contrast to what occurs in the coordinates
(\varphi,\rho)
, the analyticity of inverse integrating factors in Cartesian coordinates
(x,y)
is not guaranteed for centers with
\Omega_{pq}=\emptyset
. Let
\omega=P(x,y;\lambda),dy-Q(x,y;\lambda),dx
be the differential 1-form associated with
\mathcal{X}
. We say that a
C^{1}
real-valued function
v(x,y)
is an inverse integrating factor of
\mathcal{X}
if
\omega/v(x,y)
is closed in a neighborhood of the origin, except on the zero set
v^{-1}(0)
. We note that there exist polynomial vector fields
\mathcal{X}
with a center at the origin and
\Omega_{pq}=\emptyset
that do not admit any inverse integrating factor
v(x,y)
analytic at the origin. The reason for that is because sometimes any smooth
v(x,y)
is necessarily flat at the origin; see the proof of Theorem 2 in
[
23
]
.

Below we show that, in general, inverse integrating factors
V(\varphi,\rho)
are singular at
\rho=0
once the condition
\Omega_{pq}=\emptyset
is removed, as stated in Proposition
1
. The main results of this work are the following.

Theorem 3
.

Any analytic center admits a Laurent inverse integrating factor
V
of the form (
3
).

Theorem 4
.

Consider an analytic family of planar vector fields with a monodromic singular point, restricted to
\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{pq}
. If there exists a Laurent inverse integrating factor
V(\varphi,\rho)
with an essential singularity at the origin
\rho=0
, then the monodromic point is a center.

Remark 5
.

The vector field
\mathcal{X}=(-y+x(x^{2}+y^{2}))\partial_{x}+(x+y(x^{2}+y^{2}))\partial_{y}
has a focus at the origin, and its associated polar vector field
\mathcal{Z}=\partial_{\varphi}+\rho^{3}\partial_{\rho}
admits the inverse integrating factor
V_{1}(\varphi,\rho)=\rho^{3}\,\sin(2\theta+\rho^{-2})
, which is analytic in
C\setminus\{\rho=0\}
. We observe that
V_{1}
does not possess a Laurent expansion at
\rho=0
, although it is worth noting that
\mathcal{Z}
also has the Laurent-type inverse integrating factor
V_{2}(\varphi,\rho)=\rho^{3}
.

Remark 6
.

The vector field
\mathcal{X}=-y\partial_{x}+x\partial_{y}
has a center at the origin, and its associated polar vector field
\mathcal{Z}=\partial_{\varphi}
admits the inverse integrating factor
V_{1}(\varphi,\rho)=\sin(\rho^{-1})+\sin(\rho)
, which is analytic in
C\setminus\{\rho=0\}
and exhibits an essential singularity at
\rho=0
, with a Laurent expansion containing infinitely many terms involving both positive and negative exponents. We note that
\mathcal{Z}
also possesses the Laurent-type inverse integrating factor
V_{2}(\varphi,\rho)=\rho
.

An important consequence of the technique used in the proof of Theorem
3
concerns the structure of the Poincaré map. Following Il’yashenko’s work
[
24
]
, it follows that
\Pi
may even be non-differentiable at the origin. Specifically,
\Pi
is a semiregular map with a linear term, meaning it can be expressed as
\Pi(x)=\eta_{1}x+o(x)
, where the linear leading coefficient satisfies
\eta_{1}>0
. Using the analytic dependence of
\mathcal{X}
on
\lambda
, Medvedeva in
[
31
]
shows that
\Pi
admits a Dulac asymptotic expansion of the form

(7)

\Pi(x)=\eta_{1}x+\sum_{j}\mathcal{P}_{j}(\log x)\,x^{\nu_{j}},

where the exponents
\nu_{j}>1
are independent of
\lambda
and tend to infinity, while
\mathcal{P}_{j}
are polynomials whose coefficients depend analytically on the coefficients of
\mathcal{X}
.

It is well known that (
7
) contains no logarithmic terms and converges in a neighborhood of the origin (hence
\Pi
is analytic at the origin) when
\Omega_{pq}=\emptyset
, since in that case
\rho=0
becomes a periodic orbit of
\mathcal{Z}
. The following result generalizes the previous one, extending it to the case where the parameters lie in
\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{pq}
.

Theorem 7
.

The monodromic singular point at the origin of any analytic planar vector field restricted to
\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{pq}
has associated an analytic Poincaré map
\Pi
at the origin.

Restricting parameters to
\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{pq}
and assuming the existence of an ascending Laurent inverse integrating factor for
\mathcal{Z}
, it was shown in
[
17
]
that
\Pi
admits a formal expansion at the origin. In light of Theorem
7
, we now know that
\Pi
is in fact analytic at the origin. This represents a significant improvement over some results presented in Theorem 10 of
[
17
]
, where additional (and unnecessary) conditions were imposed to establish the analyticity of
\Pi
.

On the other hand, we wish to point out that even though
\Pi
is analytic at the origin, and therefore its expansion
\Pi(\rho_{0})=\sum_{j\geq 1}\eta_{j}\rho_{0}^{j}
is simpler than (
7
), the computation of the coefficients
\eta_{j}
(generalized Poincaré–Lyapunov quantities) remains an open problem. The difficulty arises from the fact that
\Pi(\rho_{0})=\Phi(2\pi;\rho_{0})
, where the solution
\Phi(\varphi;\rho_{0})
of the Cauchy problem given by
d\rho/d\varphi=R(\varphi,\rho)/\Theta(\varphi,\rho)
with initial condition
\Phi(0;\rho_{0})=\rho_{0}>0
sufficiently small is not analytic with respect to
\rho_{0}
at
\rho_{0}=0
. An obvious exceptional case where Bautin’s method can be applied to compute
\eta_{j}
occurs when
\Omega_{pq}=\emptyset
, in which case
\Phi(\varphi;\rho_{0})=\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}(\varphi)\rho_{0}^{j}
and
\eta_{j}=c_{j}(2\pi)
. Another (nontrivial) exceptional case where the
\eta_{j}
can be computed is given by those monodromic singularities in
\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{pq}
that possess an explicit increasing Laurent inverse integrating factor, as established in
[
17
]
(see Remark
9
).

2.
A procedure to solve generic center-focus problems

Based on Theorem
3
, we propose a method to derive necessary center conditions for analytic families of vector fields
\mathcal{X}
exhibiting a monodromic singularity. To this end, we use either polar coordinates or
(p,q)
-weighted polar coordinates
(\varphi,\rho)
with
(p,q)\in W(\mathbf{N}(\mathcal{X}))
.

The first step consists in proposing the initial terms of an ascending Laurent expansion (
4
) for a function
V
, where
m\in\mathbb{Z}
is arbitrary. By requiring that
V
satisfy equation (
2
), we obtain constraints on the parameters
\lambda
of the family
\mathcal{X}
, ensuring that an initial string of coefficients
v_{j}
with
j\geq m
in (
4
) are well-defined functions on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. At this stage, two possibilities arise: either
m
remains arbitrary or it becomes fixed.

If
m
becomes fixed, we have shown that no Laurent
V
with an essential singularity at
\rho=0
can exist, since such a singularity would necessarily appear in our construction (
4
) with
m
negative and unbounded. Moreover, if
m\leq 0
, then the singularity is a center by Theorem 4 in
[
14
]
. We continue in the case
m\geq 1
by computing further coefficients
v_{j}
until either a saturation occurs–meaning no additional parameter constraints arise–or an obstruction to the existence of expansion (
4
) for
V
is encountered. In the former situation, we suspect that a
V
of the form (
4
) exists. If we are able to obtain a closed-form expression for
V
, rather than merely the first terms of its expansion (
4
), we can apply the technique developed in
[
17
]
to derive a set of necessary and sufficient center conditions (see Remark
9
for details). In the latter situation, we have proven that no Laurent
V
of the form (
4
) exists, and consequently the monodromic singularity is a focus, by Theorem
3
.

If
m
remains arbitrary, it may happen that we encounter a further obstruction to the existence of expansion (
4
), or alternatively, that no such obstruction appears, but we are forced to let
m
decrease without bound in order to compute additional coefficients
v_{j}
. In either case, we have shown that no expansion (
4
) exists, implying that any Laurent inverse integrating factors must possess an essential singularity at
\rho=0
.

At this point, we repeat the procedure, this time seeking a
V
with a descending expansion (
5
) starting from a leading term. This search is feasible only when
\mathcal{X}
is a polynomial vector field. After computing the initial coefficients
v_{j}
, the same possible outcomes as before may occur.

Remark 8
.

Taking into account Theorems
3
and
4
in the above analysis, we emphasize that the only monodromic singular points of polynomial vector fields that lie outside the scope of our method are those that are nongeneric and admit a Laurent inverse integrating factor
V
with an essential singularity at
\rho=0
, with parameters belonging to
\Lambda_{pq}
.

Remark 9
.

Once we have determined a sufficiently long initial string of coefficients
v_{j}
that are well-defined on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
in the ascending expansion (
4
) of
V
, and we observe a possible stabilization in the sense that no further parameter conditions arise – then, assuming
V
exists according to the results in
[
17
]
, only two possibilities remain:

(i)

The singularity becomes a focus of maximal order, admitting a Laurent inverse integrating factor with leading exponent
m
. In this case, the Poincaré map has a formal power series expansion of the form
\Pi(\rho)=\eta_{1}\rho+\cdots
when
m=1
, or
\Pi(\rho)=\rho+\eta_{m}\rho^{m}+\cdots
when
m\geq 2
. Moreover,
\Pi(\rho)=\rho
holds only if
\eta_{1}=1
or
\eta_{m}=0
, respectively.

(ii)

The singularity becomes a center.

To distinguish between cases (i) and (ii) within the restricted monodromic parameter space
\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{pq}
, it is necessary to obtain the closed-form expression of
V
, rather than only the first terms of its expansion near
\rho=0
. In this context, setting
\mathcal{F}=R/\Theta
, we obtain that
\log\eta_{1}=\mathfrak{g}
when
m=1
, and
\eta_{m}=\mathfrak{g}
when
m>1
, where
\mathfrak{g}=G(r)
with

(8)

G(r)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathcal{F}(\varphi,r)}{V(\varphi,r)}\,d\varphi,

and this expression is independent of
r
for all sufficiently small
r>0
.

We note that there exist maximal-order foci that do not admit a Laurent inverse integrating factor of the form (
3
). This behavior is illustrated by the system
\dot{x}=y
,
\dot{y}=-x^{5}+ax^{4}y
, which has a maximal-order focus at the origin for
a\neq 0
, as can be verified by applying the Bautin method, given that
\Omega_{13}=\emptyset
. In Proposition 5 of
[
19
]
, it is shown that this family of foci does not possess an ascending Laurent inverse integrating factor. By Theorem
4
, we then conclude that there is no Laurent inverse integrating factor (
3
) when
a\neq 0
, since
\Lambda_{13}=\emptyset
.

Remark 10
.

In order to apply the results from
[
19
]
, instead of seeking an ascending Laurent inverse integrating factor
V
for
\mathcal{Z}
, we may equivalently look for a Laurent inverse integrating factor
V^{*}(\varphi,\rho)
for the vector field
\mathcal{Z}^{*}=\mathcal{Z}/\Theta=\partial_{\varphi}+\mathcal{F}(\varphi,\rho)\partial_{\rho}
defined on
C\setminus\Theta^{-1}(0)
. The relationship between
V
and
V^{*}
is simply
V^{*}=V/\Theta
. In this setting, we observe that the coefficients
v_{j}
in the Laurent expansions of
V^{*}
near
\rho=0
are functions defined only on
\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\Omega_{pq}
and may become unbounded near
\Omega_{pq}
.

2.1.
Ascending expansions

Putting the expansions
V(\varphi,\rho)=\sum_{j\geq m}v_{j}(\varphi)\rho^{j}
with arbitrary
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\Theta(\varphi,\rho)=\sum_{j\geq 0}G_{j}(\varphi)\rho^{j}
and
R(\varphi,\rho)=\sum_{j\geq 1}R_{j}(\varphi)\rho^{j}
into the partial differential equation (
2
) yields that the coefficients
v_{j}
of
V
are solutions of
2\pi
-periodic linear differential equation
\mathcal{L}^{a}_{j}(v_{j})+Q_{j}=0
where

\mathcal{L}^{a}_{m+j}(v_{m+j}):=G_{0}v^{\prime}_{m+j}+((m+j-1)R_{1}-G^{\prime}_{0})v_{m+j}\ \mbox{for }\ j\geq 0,

with
Q_{m}\equiv 0
and where
Q_{j}(\varphi)
with
j>m
is a function depending on the
v_{k}
with
k<j
.

The starting point of the method is to compute, if it exists, the quantity

(9)

\xi_{pq}=PV\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{R_{1}(\varphi)}{G_{0}(\varphi)}d\varphi,

where
PV
stands for the Cauchy principal value of the singular integral. It follows from Proposition 2 of
[
19
]
, see Remark
10
, that

(10)

(m-1)\xi_{pq}=0,

where
m
is the leading exponent of
V
in an ascending expansion. Therefore
m
is fixed to 1 in case that
\xi_{pq}\neq 0
. Indeed, (
10
) is also a consequence of Lemma
19
applied with
j=m
, hence with
P_{m}=(m-1)R_{1}-G^{\prime}_{0}
and
Q_{m}=0
.

2.2.
Descending expansions when
\mathcal{X}
is a polynomial

When
\mathcal{X}
is a polynomial vector field, we have that

\Theta(\varphi,\rho)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}G_{j}(\varphi)\rho^{j}
and

R(\varphi,\rho)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{j}(\varphi)\rho^{j}
for some fixed integer
n\geq 1
.
We substitute the expansion
V(\varphi,\rho)=\sum_{j\leq m}v_{j}(\varphi)\rho^{j}
, with arbitrary
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, and then equate the coefficients of like powers
\rho^{k}
with
k\leq m+n-1
.
As a consequence, the coefficients
v_{j}
satisfy recursive
2\pi
-periodic linear differential equations
\mathcal{L}^{d}_{j}(v_{j})=F_{j}
, where

(11)

\mathcal{L}^{d}_{j}(v_{j}):=G_{n-1}v_{j}^{\prime}+((j-n)R_{n}-G^{\prime}_{n-1})v_{j},\ \ \ j\leq m,

and each
F_{j}
is determined once the previous coefficients
v_{i}
with
i<j
are known.
We obtain that
F_{m}\equiv 0
, but in general
F_{j}
does not vanish identically for
j<m
.

Remark 11
.

There exist centers without an inverse integrating factor having a descending Laurent expansion (
5
). An example is given by the family

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(x^{6}+3y^{2})(-y+\mu x)+\lambda_{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})(y+Ax^{3}),

(12)

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(x^{6}+3y^{2})(x+\mu y)+\lambda_{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})(-x^{5}+3Ax^{2}y),

analyzed in Example 2 of
[
14
]
. It is proved there that the monodromic parameter set at the origin is

\Lambda=\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\mu,A)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:3\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}>0,\ \ \lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}>0\}
.
This family satisfies
W(\mathbf{N}(\mathcal{X}))=\{(1,1),(1,3)\}
and has the inverse integrating factor
v(x,y)=(x^{2}+y^{2})(x^{6}+3y^{2})
, which in polar coordinates
(\varphi,\rho)
yields the inverse integrating factor
V(\varphi,\rho)=v(\rho\cos\varphi,\rho\sin\varphi)/\rho^{3}
, having an ascending Laurent expansion with leading exponent
1
.

On the other hand, it is proved in
[
16
]
that
3\lambda_{1}\mu+\sqrt{3}A\lambda_{2}=0
is the unique center condition under some additional parameter restrictions (which do not imply
\lambda_{1}\mu=0
) ensuring that the system lies in
\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{11}
.
Here we prove that those centers at the origin of (
12
) restricted to
(\Lambda\setminus\Lambda_{11})\cap\{\lambda_{1}\mu\neq 0\}
have no inverse integrating factor with a descending Laurent expansion (
5
).
To show this, we substitute the expansion (
5
) into the partial differential equation (
2
), and we obtain that the leading coefficient
v_{m}
, solution of
\mathcal{L}^{d}_{m}(v_{m})=0
, is

v_{m}(\varphi)=\cos^{\alpha}\varphi\,\exp((m-5)\beta\varphi)

where
\beta=\lambda_{1}\mu/(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})
and
\alpha
depends on
m
and the parameters of (
12
).
Since
\beta\neq 0
,
v_{m}
is well defined on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
only when
m=5
, and in that case
v_{5}(\varphi)=\cos^{6}\varphi
.
The next coefficient
v_{4}(\varphi)
, solution of
\mathcal{L}^{d}_{4}(v_{4})=F_{4}
, is

v_{4}(\varphi)=\cos^{\gamma}\varphi\,\exp(-\beta\varphi)

for some
\gamma
depending on the parameters.
Therefore,
v_{4}
is not well defined on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, which completes the proof.

2.3.
How the method works in a toy example

We consider the linear vector field
\mathcal{X}=(-y+\lambda x)\partial_{x}+(x+\lambda y)\partial_{y}
and we are going to see that the origin is a center if and only if
\lambda=0
.

We have
W(\mathbf{N}(\mathcal{X}))=\{((1,1)\}
and taking polar coordinates

Lemma 17
.

Let
\Pi(z)=z+az^{m}+O(z^{m+1})
with
a\in\mathbb{C}
and
m\geq 2
be a parabolic holomorphic germ at
0\in\mathbb{C}
. For any attractor petal
P_{j}
and any
z\in P_{j}
the asymptotic behavior

(25)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pi^{n}(z)=0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}(\Pi^{n})^{\prime}(z)=0,

holds.

Proof.

Since
\Pi^{\prime}(0)=1
and
m\geq 2
, the point
z=0
is a
parabolic fixed point
of
\Pi
. By the classical Leau–Fatou flower theorem, when
a\neq 0
there exist open
m-1
open sectors
P_{1},\dots,P_{m-1}
(called
attracting petals
) such that for each
z\in P_{j}
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pi^{n}(z)=0
proving the first condition in (
25
), see for instance
[
6
]
.

We concentrate now in the proof of the second condition in (
25
). From the chain rule, and defining
\Pi^{0}(z)=z
, it follows by induction that

\left(\Pi^{n}\right)^{\prime}(z)=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\Pi^{\prime}\left(\Pi^{k}(z)\right).

Introducing that
\Pi^{\prime}(z)=1+maz^{m-1}+O(z^{m})
and
\Pi^{k}(z)=z+kaz^{m}+O(z^{m+1})
, we have that

\Pi^{\prime}\left(\Pi^{k}(z)\right)=1+maz^{m-1}+O(z^{m}).

On the other hand, using Fatou coordinates, there is an holomorphic map
\Psi:P_{j}\to\mathbb{C}
with
P_{j}
an attracting petal such that

(26)

\Psi\circ\Pi=\Psi+1.

Iterating (
26
) we get
\Psi(\Pi^{n}(z))=\Psi(z)+n
. Taking derivatives here we deduce
\Psi^{\prime}(\Pi^{n}(z))\,\left(\Pi^{n}\right)^{\prime}(z)=\Psi^{\prime}(z)
, hence isolating

(27)

\left(\Pi^{n}\right)^{\prime}(z)=\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(z)}{\Psi^{\prime}(\Pi^{n}(z))}.

To understand the limit when
n\to\infty
in the former right-hand-side we will use the following well-known (see for example the books
[
6
,
32
]
and
[
7
,
8
]
) asymptotic behavior of
\Psi
when
z\to 0
in
P_{j}
with dominant term

\Psi(z)\sim-\frac{1}{(m-1)az^{m-1}}.

Inserting this asymptotic together with
\Psi^{\prime}(z)\sim\frac{1}{az^{m}}
into (
27
) gives

\left(\Pi^{n}\right)^{\prime}(z)\sim\left(\frac{\Pi^{n}(z)}{z}\right)^{m}.

From this expression we finish the proof since, on
P_{j}
, we have
\Pi^{n}(z)/z\to 0
as
n\to\infty
so that,
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\Pi^{n})^{\prime}(z)=0
.
∎

Lemma 18
.

Let
F
be a holomorphic function defined on a punctured neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{C}
, and assume that
z=0
is an essential singularity of
F
. Suppose that there exists a holomorphic germ at
0
given by
\Pi(z)=z+az^{m}+O(z^{m+1})
with
a\in\mathbb{C}
and
m\geq 2
satisfying the differential equation
F(\Pi(z))=F(z)\,\Pi^{\prime}(z)
for all
z
sufficiently close to
0
with
z\neq 0
. Then
\Pi(z)\equiv z
holds.

Proof.

By Leau–Fatou flower theorem, there exist
m-1
open sectors
P_{1},\dots,P_{m-1}
(called
attracting petals
) such that for each
z\in P_{j}
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pi^{n}(z)=0
. Indeed, there also exist
m-1
open sectors (
repelling petals
) such that the union of all attracting and repelling petals forms a punctured neighborhood of
0
, arranged alternately around the origin.

Iterating equation
F(\Pi(z))=F(z)\,\Pi^{\prime}(z)
we have, for every integer
n\geq 1
,

(28)

F(\Pi^{n}(z))=F(z)\,(\Pi^{n})^{\prime}(z).

If now we fix
z_{0}\in P_{j}
in an attracting petal, then we know by (
25
) that
\Pi^{n}(z_{0})\to 0
and
(\Pi^{n})^{\prime}(z_{0})\to 0
as

.

On one hand, since
0
is an essential singularity of
F
, the Casorati–Weierstrass theorem (see
[
10
]
for example) asserts that the set

\{F(z):0<|z|\ll 1\}
is dense in
\mathbb{C}
. In particular, the sequence
F(\Pi^{n}(z_{0}))
, cannot converge to any finite limit in
\mathbb{C}
as
n\to\infty
.

On the other hand, as
n\to\infty
, the right-hand side of (
28
) at
z=z_{0}
satisfies

because

∎

5.1.
Periodic solutions of linear periodic differential equations

We state the following easy technical lemma that will be very useful in our context.

Lemma 19

(Periodic solutions of a linear singular first-order equation)
.

Let
G_{0}(\varphi)
,
P_{j}(\varphi)
, and
Q_{j}(\varphi)
be trigonometric polynomials, with
Q_{j}\not\equiv 0
, and let
\Omega_{pq}=G_{0}^{-1}(0)
be the finite set of singularities of the equation

(31)

G_{0}(\varphi)v_{j}^{\prime}(\varphi)+P_{j}(\varphi)v_{j}(\varphi)+Q_{j}(\varphi)=0.

Assume that

\mu_{j}(\varphi)=\exp\left(PV\int_{0}^{\varphi}\frac{P_{j}(s)}{G_{0}(s)}\,ds\right),\ \ \zeta_{j}(\varphi)=PV\int_{0}^{\varphi}\frac{Q_{j}(s)}{G_{0}(s)}\mu_{j}(s)\,ds

exist and
\mu_{j}
is non-vanishing on
\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\Omega_{pq}
. Then the following holds:

(i)

If
\mu_{j}(2\pi)\neq 1
then (
31
) admits a unique solution defined in
\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\Omega_{pq}
with

v_{j}(0)=\frac{\zeta_{j}(2\pi)}{1-\mu_{j}(2\pi)}.

(ii)

In the case
\mu_{j}(2\pi)=1
, either all solutions of (
31
) are defined in
\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\Omega_{pq}
or none according to whether
\zeta_{j}(2\pi)
is zero or not, respectively.

Proof.

It is straightforward imposing the condition
v_{j}(2\pi)=v_{j}(0)
to the general solution of the equation (
31
) on
\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\Omega_{pq}
given by the (generalized version via principal values) variation of constants formula

v_{j}(\varphi)=\mu_{j}(\varphi)^{-1}\left(v_{j}(0)-PV\int_{0}^{\varphi}\frac{Q_{j}(s)}{G_{0}(s)}\mu_{j}(s)\,ds\right),

where
v_{j}(0)\in\mathbb{R}
is arbitrary and all the involved principal values are assumed to exist for any
\varphi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\Omega_{pq}
. Case (i) corresponds to an equation
v_{j}(2\pi)=v_{j}(0)
having a unique solution
v_{j}(0)
while case (ii) has infinitely many solutions for
v_{j}(0)
.
∎

5.2.
Computing Cauchy principal values via residues

We mention that some principal value can be computed using residues as follows. First we express
W_{j}(\varphi)=\tilde{W}_{j}(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)
and next we perform the complex change
z=\exp(i\varphi)
with
i^{2}=-1
so that
\cos\varphi=(z+z^{-1})/2
,
\sin\varphi=(z-z^{-1})/(2i)
and
d\varphi=dz/(iz)
. This procedure yields

PV\int_{0}^{2\pi}W_{j}(\varphi)\,d\varphi=PV\oint_{|z|=1}f_{j}(z)dz,\quad\mbox{where }f_{j}(z)=\frac{\tilde{W}_{j}\left(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2},\frac{z-z^{-1}}{2i}\right)}{iz}.

Let
z_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
be the poles of
f_{j}
and assume the poles on the contour
|z_{k}|=1
are simple. Then

(32)

PV\int_{0}^{2\pi}W_{j}(\varphi)\,d\varphi=2\pi i\left(\sum_{|z_{k}|<1}{\rm Res}(f_{j},z_{k})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{|z_{k}|=1}{\rm Res}(f_{j},z_{k})\right).
