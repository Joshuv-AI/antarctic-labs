Effects of L-BAIBA supplementation with and without grains of paradise on changes in resting metabolic rate, body composition, and cardiometabolic risk factors
Authors: Leah E Allen, Paige J Sutton, Alex Schrautemeier, Kevin Holley, Connor J Gaige, Joesi M Krieger, Anthony M Hagele, Karenina Antequera Gutierrez, Petey W Mumford, Chad M Kerksick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStrategies to augment weight loss, fitness, and body composition are in high demand. The myokine beta-amino-isobutyric acid (BAIBA) has been shown to increase fat loss, while grains of paradise (GOP), a member of the ginger family, may aid weight loss by reducing appetite and increasing thermogenesis.Purpose To evaluate the impact of 56 days of supplementing with L-BAIBA or L-BAIBA + GOP (BGOP) on resting metabolic rate, body composition, perceptual indicators of mood and affect, and cardiometabolic markers in overweight and obese men and women. Methods Healthy men and women (43.2 ± 11.4 years, 169.4 ± 7.6 cm, 90.3 ± 15.6 kg, 31.4 ± 4.4 kg∙m-2) completed a weekly exercise program and followed a calorie-restrictive diet and were randomly assigned in a double-blind and blocked manner according to sex and DXA lean mass to consume daily either a placebo (PLA) (n=12 [11F, 1M]), 500 mg L-BAIBA (L-BAIBA: MitoBurn™, NNB Nutrition) (n=13 [9F, 4M]), or 500 mg L-BAIBA + 40 mg GOP (BGOP: CaloriBurn™, NNB Nutrition) (n=12 [8F, 4M]). After 0, 14, 28, and 56 days of supplementation, participants were evaluated for changes in body mass, body composition using dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA), resting metabolic rate, perceptual indicators of hunger, appetite, energy, focus, attention, and concentration using 100-mm anchored visual analog scales (VAS) and mood using the Profile of Mood States along with evaluation of selected cardiometabolic markers. Dietary intake was evaluated using 3-day dietary recalls using ASA24 throughout the study. Mixed factorial ANOVAs with repeated measures assessed the main and interaction effects. A p-value of 0.05 was used to determine statistical outcomes. Results No statistically significant group × time interactions (p>0.05) were found for body mass, resting metabolic rate, DXA fat mass, DXA fat-free mass, DXA % fat, VAS ratings for appetite, energy, focus, attention, and concentration, resting heart rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, glucose, or lipid panel component. A significant group × time interaction (p = 0.011) for hunger VAS ratings was observed. Compared with PLA, BAIBA reported reduced hunger ratings after 14, 28, and 56 days of supplementation, while BGOP reported reduced hunger ratings after 28 days of supplementation. Conclusion Despite no accompanying changes in body composition, resting metabolic rate, or dietary intake, supplementation with BAIBA and BGOP reduced perceived hunger compared to PLA during the diet and exercise program.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-026-01349-z.Keywords: L-BAIBA, Grains of Paradise, Metabolic, Body Composition, Metabolism, Obesity, Weight loss, Supplementation

Introduction

Obesity remains a critical public health issue, contributing significantly to the global burden of disease and chronic conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, metabolic syndrome, and Type 2 diabetes. In individuals with obesity, disruptions in energy homeostasis and metabolic regulation often manifest as impaired glucose regulation, systemic inflammation, and unfavorable lipid profiles. Accordingly, effective treatment strategies require a multifaceted approach, with lifestyle interventions centered on manipulations of diet and exercise [1]. Importantly, the success of these interventions depends not only on physiological adaptations but also on perceptual factors such as hunger, satiety, and appetite regulation, which influence dietary adherence [2, 3].

Lifestyle interventions, including energy restriction and physical activity, are fundamental aspects for promoting weight loss and managing obesity as well as its metabolic consequences. Energy intake restriction improves glucose homeostasis and metabolic flexibility, partly mediated by changes in bioactive metabolites, such as β-aminoisobutyric acid (BAIBA) [4]. BAIBA, a myokine secreted during skeletal muscle activity, has garnered attention in pre-clinical models for its role in enhancing fatty acid oxidation, improving insulin sensitivity, and reducing adiposity through its actions on carbohydrate and lipid metabolism [5]. Emerging evidence also suggests that metabolic and inflammatory signaling can interact with central appetite-regulating pathways, raising the possibility that BAIBA may influence perceptual hunger and satiety in addition to its peripheral effects [6]. Recent findings underscore the potential of BAIBA as a therapeutic adjunct in obesity management. Among those with obesity, elevated plasma BAIBA concentrations correlate with improved glucose regulation, independent of exercise, following low-energy diets [1]. Preclinical models also reveal that BAIBA prevents diet-induced obesity by modulating leptin pathways and energy balance [7]. Furthermore, oral supplementation of L-enantiomers of BAIBA (L-BAIBA) in humans demonstrates dose-response absorption kinetics, suggesting its feasibility as a dietary intervention [8].

In addition to its metabolic effects, BAIBA modulates inflammatory processes by inhibiting hypothalamic inflammation and microglia activation, mechanisms implicated in obesity-related neuroinflammation [9]. Because hypothalamic inflammation is linked to impaired leptin and insulin signaling as well as altered food intake, these effects provide a plausible pathway through which BAIBA may also affect subjective hunger and satiety responses during energy restriction [10]. Safety assessments further support the therapeutic viability of BAIBA, as sub-chronic toxicity studies demonstrate no adverse effects at clinically relevant doses [8, 11].

Beyond metabolic health, BAIBA may also affect other physiological systems, including bone and muscle. Recent research suggests distinct roles for the L- and D-enantiomers of BAIBA in modulating bone mineral density, body mass index, and physical performance [12]. These findings expand the scope of BAIBA’s applications, underscoring its relevance to whole-body energy redistribution and homeostasis during physical activity and energy restriction [13]. Similarly, grains of paradise (GOP; Guinea pepper) a botanical native to West Africa has demonstrated thermogenic properties. For centuries, GOP has been used as a food additive to remedy various ailments. Sugita and colleagues [14] demonstrated in 19 healthy women the ability of GOP to increase energy expenditure and reduce fat tissue in the thorax after four weeks of supplementation. Another study by Sugita et al. [15] in 19 healthy men supplemented with GOP for 4 weeks and demonstrated its ability to increase energy expenditure. However, whether GOP influences perceptual hunger, satiety, or appetite-related behaviors remains unclear.

While this preliminary human data on BAIBA and GOP is encouraging, longer studies integrating dietary and exercise interventions are needed to assess whether supplementation with BAIBA or in combination with GOP can stimulate greater decreases in body fat, improve markers of cardiometabolic health, increase metabolism, and influence perceptual response to energy restriction. Given that subjective hunger and satiety are key determinants of adherence and long-term weight loss success, evaluating these perceptual outcomes alongside physiological measures is essential. For this reason, by integrating dietary and exercise interventions coupled with supplement ingestion, the purpose of this study is to evaluate the potential impact of 56 days of supplementing with L-BAIBA or L-BAIBA + GOP (BGOP) on resting metabolic rate, body composition, weight loss, perceptual ratings, and cardiometabolic risk factors in overweight and obese men and women.

Methods

Overview of research design

This study was completed as a prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study with parallel groups of overweight and obese but otherwise healthy men and women. Prior to commencing the study, all participants signed an Institutional Review Board (IRB)-approved informed consent document (Lindenwood University: IRB-23-28, Approval Date 28 November 2023) and completed a health history questionnaire to determine eligibility. This study protocol and design was prospectively registered on Clinicaltrials.gov on 15 May 2023 as NCT05775003.

As seen in Table 1 below, study participants completed five laboratory visits (pre-screening and after 0, 14, 28, and 56 days of supplementation) at approximately the same time between 0600 and 1000 h. For each laboratory visit, participants arrived after completing an overnight (8-hrs) fast and avoiding alcohol, nicotine, and caffeine in addition to abstinence from non-programmed exercise for 24-hrs. Upon arrival, anthropometric (height [visit 1 only], weight, body mass index (BMI), hydration status (urine specific gravity), and hemodynamics (resting heart rate and blood pressure) measurements were taken. During the first laboratory visit (pre-screening), prospective participants signed an IRB-approved consent form and had their eligibility finalized through a medical and health history review, medication and dietary supplement check, and a peak VO2 assessment using a motorized treadmill and metabolic cart.

Participants deemed eligible were informed of the 56-day protocol and were scheduled to complete all laboratory visits. Visits 2–5 followed the same protocol, where participants submitted a 3-day food log (Visits 2, 4, and 5), completed a review of medications and dietary supplements, and had DXA, bioelectrical impedance spectroscopy (BIS), and waist circumference assessments completed. Participants were then asked to consume a minimum of eight fluid ounces of water followed by venipuncture for blood collection, two resting metabolic rate (RMR) measurements (beginning of visit, and 60 min after supplement dose), a second venipuncture for blood collection immediately after the second RMR, the completion of the profile of mood states questionnaire, and received updated macronutrient goals. Furthermore, a count of the remaining supplement pills was completed at the end of the visit and redispersed to the participant. Upon the completion of Visit 2, participants were given the exercise training schedule and their randomized supplementation with ingestion directions. Weekly check-ins evaluated compliance with the exercise program, 24-hr dietary recalls, and supplementation while assessing the presence of any adverse events. Participants who completed at least 80% of their assigned workouts, dietary recalls, and supplementation were considered compliant. Adherence to the predefined threshold was verified by the study coordinator through review of digital exercise logs and photos, confirmation of attendance at supervised in-person sessions, and evaluation of weekly dietary assessments for compliance with prescribed caloric and macronutrient targets. Completion of dietary recalls using ASA24 was confirmed before each laboratory visit. Supplement adherence was assessed via digital tracking logs and verified by capsule counts at each in-person visit. Upon processing, all blood samples were stored at -80 °C.

Study participants

A total of 37 healthy men and women completed the 56-day study protocol (Table 2). To be eligible, participants must have been between 18 and 60 years old, have a BMI between > 25.0 to ≤ 39.9 kg∙m− 2, free-living and independent, in good health with no signs or symptoms of cardiovascular, respiratory, metabolic, immune, psychiatric, or musculoskeletal disease or disorders, willing to maintain consistent sleep duration throughout the study, willing and able to agree to the requirements and restrictions of the study and carry out all study-related procedures, and able to complete a peak oxygen consumption exercise test with no contraindications in accordance to the American College of Sports Medicine [16]. Alternatively, participants were excluded if they: had a positive medical history or were currently being treated for some form of heart or cardiovascular disease; kidney disease, renal failure, or had dialysis performed on regular intervals; had liver disease or some form of clinically diagnosed hepatic impairment; were diagnosed with having Type I or Type II diabetes, thyroid disease; major affective disorder or other psychiatric disorder that required hospitalization in the prior year; immune disorder (i.e., HIV/AIDS); had a history of cancer (except localized skin cancer without metastases or in situ cervical cancer within five years prior to screening visit); had an abnormality or obstruction of the gastrointestinal tract precluding swallowing (e.g., dysphagia) and digestion (e.g., known intestinal malabsorption, celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic pancreatitis, steatorrhea); had a positive medical history for any neurological condition or neurological disease; were currently prescribed a statin drug (i.e., Lipitor, Livalo, Crestor, Zocor, etc.) or any hypertension medications (i.e., Beta-blockers, ACE Inhibitors, Alpha blockers, Vasodilators, etc.); were a current smoker or used any form a nicotine (average of > 1 pack per day within the past 3 months), or had quit within the past six months; took any drugs (prescribed or over the counter) or dietary supplements that are known or are purported to influence weight loss (such as thermogenics, hydroxycitric acid, ephedra, capsaicin, etc.); were lactating; pregnant or planning to become pregnant; had a known sensitivity or allergy to any of the study products; had a history of alcohol or substance abuse in the year prior to screening; had received or used an investigational product in another research study within 30 days of beginning the study protocol; had any planned major changes in lifestyle (i.e., dieting, exercise level, travel, etc.) during the study; had a recent history (< 3 months) of exercise training or weight loss (> 5%); had any orthopedic limitation that would prevent participation in a general fitness program; and any condition or abnormality that, in the opinion of the investigator, would compromise the safety of the participant or the quality of the study data. Procedures

Standing height and body mass

Standing height was measured using a wall-mounted stadiometer, with participants barefoot, standing fully erect, and maintaining a neutral head position. Measurements were recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Participants had their body mass determined in their bare feet with a t-shirt and athletic shorts on a leveled, self-calibrating digital scale (Tanita BWB-627A, Tokyo, Japan). All recorded body masses were recorded to the nearest ± 0.1 kg upon arrival to each study visit. Body mass index was computed at baseline for eligibility and demographic purposes.

Peak VO2 assessment

For descriptive purposes, research study participants completed a VO2Peak assessment using a metabolic cart (True Max 2400 Metabolic Measurement System, ParvoMedics, Sandy, UT, USA) calibrated to within 2% of the previous day’s calibration value and a motorized treadmill (Trackmaster, Salina, KS). A graded, staged treadmill protocol following the methods of Bruce et al. (12) was employed to assess peak oxygen consumption. This test began with a two-minute warm-up at 2.0 mph and 1.0% grade and progressed through three-minute stages using standardized increases in treadmill and grade (%) until volitional fatigue was reached. VO2Peak was achieved if two of the following three events occurred: the participant achieved a respiratory exchange ratio of > 1.10; recorded exercise heart rates were within 10 beats of age-predicted maximal heart rate (Max HR = 210 – [0.7 x age]), and VO2 changed less than 250 mL O2/min for two consecutive sampling points.

Urine specific gravity

Prior to each body composition measurement, participants donated a mid-stream urine sample for determination of urine specific gravity and hydration status. Using a transfer pipette, approximately 500µL of fresh urine was transferred onto a handheld refractometer and compared against a standardized evaluation scale. A value ≥ 1.020 was considered dehydrated, and when achieved, participants were required to consume tap water and produce additional urine samples until a urine specific gravity of ≤ 1.020 was achieved.

Hemodynamic procedures

Resting heart rate and blood pressure were assessed after the participant had been resting quietly for ten minutes. Measurements were obtained using an automated blood pressure monitor (Omron 3 Series Upper Arm Blood Pressure Monitor, BP7150; Omron Healthcare Inc., Lake Forest, IL, USA), which detects blood pressure via oscillometric measurement of the brachial artery. Resting heart rate was recorded as part of this assessment.

Dietary intake

Dietary analysis was completed by having participants log their dietary intake over three consecutive days immediately before 0, 28, and 56 days of following the supplementation, diet, and exercise programs. For each dietary recall, participants were asked to enter all food and fluid they consumed prior to each study visit into an electronic log located in the Automated Self-Administered 24-Hour (ASA24®; National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD, USA) or a paper record that was later entered into ASA24. All food and fluid intake were analyzed for energy and macronutrient content. Weekly, MyFitnessPal logs were reviewed by a research team member to evaluate prescribed caloric and macronutrient targets.

Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA)

Body composition was assessed using a Hologic QDR Discovery A (Waltham, MA, USA) dual-energy x-ray absorptiometer after 0, 14, 28, and 56 days of supplementation. Participants were required to observe an overnight fast from both food and fluid to facilitate an accurate determination of body composition. Prior to each morning of testing, the device was calibrated according to manufacturer guidelines, and all scans were completed according to device specifications. Reference to fat-free mass (FFM) throughout the manuscript is in reference to the lean + bone mineral content (DXA Lean + DXA BMC = DXA FFM), and statistical outcomes related to fat mass, lean mass, fat-free mass, and percent body fat were made using DXA data.

Waist circumference

At the level of the umbilicus, participants had their waist circumference assessed with three separate measurements using a flexible measuring tape. All measurements were taken directly over the skin, and a standardized amount of pressure was applied prior to each measurement to ensure consistent measurement. The average measurement was recorded.

Bioelectrical impedance spectroscopy (BIS)

Body water [total, intracellular, and extracellular] was assessed using bioelectrical impedance spectroscopy. Prior to each assessment, participants were required to observe an overnight food and fluid fast. The Impedimed SFB7 (Impedimed, Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA) deployed 256 different frequencies between 3 − 1,000 kHz and used Cole modeling and Hanai mixture theory to quantify body water and soft tissue masses. Before this testing, participants had their left wrist and ankle prepared for electrode attachment using isopropyl alcohol and light abrasion of the skin. After the skin was properly prepped, two measurements were completed. Each morning of testing, the device was calibrated, and all assessments were completed according to manufacturer guidelines.

Resting metabolic rate (RMR)

Resting metabolic rate (RMR) was determined in a thermoneutral laboratory environment eight times throughout the study protocol (two measurements during each visit after 0, 14, 28, and 56 days of supplementation). During each visit, two RMR visits were completed. The first RMR for each visit was completed after an overnight fast, while the second RMR was completed 60 min after ingestion of the daily dose of their assigned supplement. This measurement pattern was followed to help provide insight as to the acute changes that occurred in each supplementation group throughout the entire 8-week study protocol. Acute ingestion responses and the evaluation of any extent to which acute changes adjust throughout several weeks of supplementation are rarely evaluated in dietary supplementation and have not been evaluated for BAIBA or GOP supplementation. To complete each assessment, participants rested supine with a blanket placed over them in an environment free of auditory and visual stimuli. A clear, hard plastic shield with a transparent plastic drape was placed over the participant’s head and shoulders and connected to the metabolic cart to collect all expired gases (oxygen and carbon dioxide). All expired gases were analyzed using indirect calorimetry (TrueMax 2400 Canopy System, ParvoMedics, Sandy, UT, USA) and computed to determine rates of energy expenditure and rates of carbohydrate and fat oxidation. Each day, the metabolic cart was calibrated to less than 2% of the previous day’s calibration factor. For each RMM assessment, the participant remained motionless without going to sleep for 30 min. Each assessment took place for a minimum of 20 min, and a five-minute period where rates of oxygen uptake changed < 5% were averaged and used for data analysis.

Venous blood collection and processing

Venous blood was collected using standard phlebotomy techniques via the forearm vein into serum and EDTA-coated Vacutainer tubes. Serum samples were collected into serum-separator tubes (SST) and, upon collection, gently inverted ten times. Whole blood samples used for safety analysis were collected into EDTA, and upon collection, gently inverted ten times. All serum and plasma samples used for safety analysis remained at room temperature, were allowed to clot, and were sent to a commercial diagnostic laboratory (Quest Diagnostics) for same-day analysis.

Complete blood count

Whole blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes and sent unprocessed to a commercial diagnostic laboratory (Quest Diagnostics) for analysis. The following hematological parameters were measured to evaluate supplement safety: red blood cell count, white blood cell count, platelet count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, red blood cell dimension width (RDW), mean corpuscle volume (MCV), mean corpuscle hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscle hemoglobin content (MCHC), differential white blood cell counts (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils) as both percentages and absolute counts.

Comprehensive metabolic panel

Serum samples were collected in SSTs, allowed to clot for 30 min at room temperature, and centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 15 min at room temperature using a Drucker Diagnostics Model 642E Quest Centrifuge (Drucker Diagnostics, Philipsburg, PA, USA) to separate the serum. The isolated serum was sent to a commercial diagnostic laboratory (Quest Diagnostics) for analysis of a comprehensive metabolic panel (albumin, globulin, albumin: globulin ratio (calculated), total bilirubin, total protein, alkaline phosphatase, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, BUN: creatinine ratio (calculated), glomerular filtration rate, glucose, potassium, sodium, calcium, carbon dioxide, and chloride) to evaluate safety of supplement ingestion.

Lipid panel

Serum samples were processed identically to the comprehensive metabolic panel before analysis of the lipid panel (triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, and LDL cholesterol) at a commercial diagnostic laboratory (Quest Diagnostics).

Mood state and visual analog scales

Participants completed electronic versions of the Profile of Mood States (POMS) questionnaire and visual analog (VAS) scale to evaluate perceptual responses. The POMS is a 65-item validated scale that uses a 5-point Likert scale, with each individual area being scored and summed into profiles for tension, anger, vigor, fatigue, depression, and confusion [17]. A total mood disturbance score was calculated by summing all categories and subtracting the vigor score. Each VAS scale was completed using a 100-mm line anchored by “Lowest Possible” and “Highest Possible” to assess subjective ratings of hunger, appetite, energy, focus, attention, and concentration. The validity and reliability of VAS assessing fatigue, appetite, and energy have been previously established [15–20].

Supplementation protocol

In a double-blind, placebo-controlled fashion, participants were randomly assigned to one of three supplementation groups. Participants were stratified in a blocked fashion by sex and DXA lean mass (rounded to the nearest kilogram) ranges and randomized to either the intervention or placebo group by a blinded research team member who did not perform any data analysis. One group was assigned to ingest 500 mg of resistance dextrin as a placebo (PLA). A second and third group were assigned to ingest either 500 mg of L-beta-amino-iso-butyric acid (L-BAIBA) (Mitoburn™, NNB Nutrition, Nanjing, China) or 500 mg of L-beta-amino-iso-butyric acid (L-BAIBA) (Mitoburn™) + 40 mg of Grains of Paradise (BGOP) (Caloriburn™, NNB Nutrition, Nanjing, China). Assigned supplements were ingested with food at approximately the same time each day for 56 days. Participants were instructed to consume one dose per day with 12 fluid ounces of cold water and were required to complete a supplementation log (on paper and digital) to document when each dose was consumed. All test materials were prepared into gelatin capsules of identical size, color, shape, and transparency, with no distinguishable smells. Intervention and placebo capsules were dispensed in identical opaque white high-density polyethylene bottles to maintain blinding from participants and the research team. Compliance with the supplementation protocol and pre-testing guidelines was assessed through communication with each study participant before beginning each visit to confirm fasting was observed, and caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, and exercise were refrained from as required.

Standardized hypoenergetic diet approach

According to the approach employed by Layman et al. [21], participants were instructed and guided to follow a restricted energy intake diet. Daily energy prescription was achieved by multiplying the measured baseline RMR by a physical activity factor of 1.3 and then subtracting 500. In addition, all participants were instructed on strategies to increase their protein intake to a minimum of 1.6 g∙kg− 1∙day−1and no greater than 2.2 g∙kg− 1∙day− 1. Approximately 30% of total daily calories came from fat. Daily macronutrient goals spanning two weeks at a time were provided to aid participants in achieving their energy and macronutrient recommendations. To facilitate real-time adherence to the study’s dietary guidelines, participants completed weekly 24-hour dietary recall check-ins, entering all the food and fluid they consumed into MyFitnessPal (MyFitnessPal Inc., Baltimore, MD, USA). Participants were provided with detailed instructions and visual portion-size comparators to improve the accuracy of their dietary records. Participants who failed to meet the dietary recommendations for two consecutive weeks were removed from the study. The data from MyFitnessPal was only used to aid in adherence to the diet and was not analyzed.

Physical activity and exercise prescription

Independent of the supplementation group, all study participants followed a 56-day structured exercise program administered through the TeamBuildr application (TeamBuildr, LLC, Silver Spring, MD, USA) that documented loading progression and compliance with the program. For aerobic activity, participants were prescribed to walk 30 min per day or accumulate a minimum of 8,000 steps per day. Participants were given the flexibility to accumulate these amounts in any manner that they desired. As a result, the intensity and duration of each aerobic exercise session were not systematically programmed. In addition, participants completed a 3-day per week resistance training program that consisted of ten machine exercises that emphasized all major muscle groups. Each participant was required to complete two sets of ten repetitions at a load that achieves muscular failure before the 12th completed repetition on the third set. If more than 12 repetitions were completed, the load was increased for the subsequent session (Table 3). Each person followed their prescribed program for the entire 56-day study protocol. For each participant, a research team member supervised two of the three resistance training sessions during the first week and one of the three resistance training sessions per week for the following five weeks. Supervision was not required for the final two weeks of the protocol. Participants were given the option to request additional workout supervision for subsequent workouts. All participants were required to complete at least 80% of the prescribed workouts over the eight weeks.

Adverse event reporting

The occurrence of adverse events was recorded throughout the entire duration of the study through spontaneous reporting by the study participants, clinical evaluation, or interaction of a research team member with a study participant, or through the participant’s study research file. All recorded events were systematically categorized using MedDRA system organ class and lowest level terms (LLT) before being graded using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), v5.0, US Dept Health & Human Services (published: November 27, 2017).

Statistical procedures

An a priori power analysis was completed using body mass outcomes from previous research [22, 23] revealed that an effect size (d) of 0.20–0.25 was achieved with an alpha of 0.05, 3 groups, 4 repeated measurements, and 12 people per group (total sample size of 36), a power of 0.684–0.889 would be achieved (G*Power). All analyses were completed using Microsoft Excel and the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (v23; SPSS Inc., Chicago IL). Before any statistical tests were performed, data was analyzed for normality, skewness, and kurtosis. All non-normal data was log-transformed before analysis. Data was considered statistically significant for all statistical tests when the probability of a type I error was 0.05 or less. The primary endpoints for this investigation were resting metabolic rate and changes in DXA fat mass. 3 × 4 mixed factorial (group x time) ANOVA with repeated measures on time was used to determine statistically significant differences for time and group main effects and group × time interaction effects. Statistically significant interaction terms were further explored by calculating delta values and analyzing between-group differences using one-way ANOVAs. All data are presented as means (standard deviation) with p-values for group, time, and group × time interaction effects for all ANOVA outcomes and p-values, Cohen’s d, and 95% confidence intervals reported for pairwise comparisons using the procedures of Tukey. Statistical trends were discussed when p-values were between 0.051 and 0.10.

Results

Protocol compliance

A total of nine days of food records were required for each participant. MyFitnessPal logs were reviewed by the study coordinator on a weekly basis to confirm broad alignment with the energetic and macronutrient targets. Dietary recalls using ASA24 were scheduled to be completed before each study visit; compliance was calculated at 88.6%. Workout participation was supervised by research assistants, and compliance with the workouts was calculated at 88.1% for the study cohort. Supplement compliance was assessed through remaining pill counts and was calculated to be at 99.2%.

Post-supplementation blinding questionnaire

Of the 37 participants who completed the study, 28 (75.7%) completed the post-supplementation blinding questionnaire. Six participants in the BGOP completed the questionnaire; one answered correctly (16.7%), five answered incorrectly or indicated they didn’t know (83.3%). Changes in absolute eosinophils were not statistically significant after 14 (p = 0.37), 28 (p = 0.33), and 56 days (p = 0.16) of supplementation. The percentage of eosinophils in our collected whole-blood samples was not statistically significant after 14 (p = 0.12), 28 (p = 0.26), and 56 days (p = 0.62) of supplementation. Changes in mean platelet volume experienced statistically significant changes between the groups on day 14 (p = 0.007, η2 = 0.256), 28 (p = 0.007, η2 = 0.254), and day 56 (p = 0.005, η2 = 0.266) of supplementation. Tukey HSD post hoc comparisons were completed using Tukey HSD which indicated that the changes in PLA were different than L-BAIBA on day 14 (p = 0.005, 95% CI: 0.159–0.969 fL), day 28 (p = 0.005, 95% CI: 0.202–1.261 fL), and day 56 (p = 0.005, 95% CI: 0.196–1.246 fL). Finally, serum chloride levels experienced statistically significant changes between the groups at day 14 (p = 0.05, η2 = 0.162) and day 56 (p = 0.004, η2 = 0.278), but not after 28 days (p = 0.73) of supplementation. No statistically significant pairwise comparisons were observed on day 14 and day 28, but on day 56, changes in chloride in BGOP and L-BAIBA were statistically different (p = 0.003, 95% CI: 0.989–5.434 mEq/L). Regardless of statistical outcomes, all recorded variables stayed within clinically accepted normative values for each variable.

Discussion

The present study examined the efficacy of supplementing with L-BAIBA or L-BAIBA + grains of paradise (BGOP) for 56 days in overweight and obese men and women undergoing a structured diet and exercise intervention. The primary finding of this study was that supplementation with L-BAIBA or BGOP did not augment changes in body composition or resting metabolic rate (RMR). No between-group differences were observed for fat mass, lean mass, or overall body mass (Table 7), and RMR remained unchanged across all conditions (Table 8). These findings do not support a physiological role for L-BAIBA or BGOP in increasing metabolic activity or improving body composition within this context. While pre-clinical work has suggested that BAIBA may enhance fatty acid oxidation, improve insulin sensitivity, and reduce adiposity, these mechanistic effects did not translate to measurable changes in human body composition or metabolic rate in the present trial. Similarly, despite prior human studies reporting reductions in body fat or increases in energy expenditure with grains of paradise (GOP) supplementation, no effects were observed. Differences in participant characteristics (e.g., age, adiposity status), as well as the inclusion of a structured diet and exercise program in the present study, may have attenuated or masked any independent effects of supplementation.

Consistent with best-practice weight loss strategies [24], all groups experienced significant reductions in body mass, body mass index, and waist circumference, indicating that the prescribed hypoenergetic diet and exercise program was effective independent of supplementation. In considering our body composition outcomes, no previous research, to our knowledge, has longitudinally administered L-BAIBA to humans for any outcome. Changes in body mass and body composition were of interest in the present study based on previously published data in cell culture and animal models that indicated BAIBA may impact insulin sensitivity or fatty acid metabolism [2, 4, 23]. Alternatively, longitudinal studies in humans are available that have provided GOP supplementation. The first study by Sugita et al. [14] reported statistically significant reductions in visceral fat in 19 young, healthy women when 30 milligrams of GOP was supplemented each day for four weeks, when compared to placebo. Additionally, a study by Yoneshiro and colleagues [25] in nine healthy men, who were supplemented with either 40 milligrams of GOP or a placebo for five weeks, reported significantly greater reductions in percent body fat, but no changes in body mass or fat-free mass. While the absolute dose of GOP was similar between these studies and the present study, participants in the present study were required to supplement for 3–4 weeks longer. Age differences were evident between these studies, which may have explained the discordance in findings. Another important difference in study design was the energy-restricted diet and regular exercise program, best practice considerations for weight loss [24], which participants in the present study were required to follow and may have overpowered any impact of supplementation.

Examining the ability of supplementation to augment energy expenditure rates was another key investigative area in the present study. Results from this study failed to highlight any ability of L-BAIBA or BGOP to augment energy expenditure. As mentioned before, no published human data is available for BAIBA to offer comparisons; however, these results do conflict with previous studies involving GOP. Sugita et al. [15] reported that acute ingestion of 40 milligrams of GOP increased whole-body energy expenditure rates two hours after consumption, but only in those participants found to have positive brown adipose tissue activity. A follow-up four-week study that had 19 young, healthy, non-obese women supplement for four weeks in a single-blind fashion with either a placebo or 40 milligrams of GOP also reported statistically significant increases in whole-body energy expenditure when compared to placebo [14]. And these results align with the significant increase in cold-induced thermogenesis reported by Yoneshiro et al. in a 2021 study that had nine healthy men supplement with 40 mg of GOP extract per day or a placebo for five weeks [25]. The observed discordance in outcomes could be due to differences in the study population employed, as the present study included non-diseased overweight and obese men and women aged 18–60 years, while the Sugita study [14] was comprised of healthy, young women, and the Yoneshiro study [25] was comprised of young, healthy men.

In line with the null findings for body composition and RMR, no effect of supplementation was observed on cardiometabolic biomarkers. Circulating glucose, triglycerides, and cholesterol fractions (total, HDL, LDL, non-HDL) were unchanged with L-BAIBA or BGOP supplementation. This investigative direction was driven by previous work by Roberts and colleagues [26] who reported that L-BAIBA improves glucose homeostasis in mice and increases expression of beta-oxidation in liver cells, while, in humans, circulating BAIBA was inversely associated with metabolic risk factors such as insulin, HOMA-IR, triglycerides, and total cholesterol. In addition, Park et al. [9] reported that BAIBA may positively impact inflammation and microglia activation. These findings contrast with prior work that suggests BAIBA is associated with improved glucose homeostasis and reduced metabolic risk, as well as potential anti-inflammatory effects. The lack of observed changes may reflect the relatively short intervention duration, modest effect sizes, or the dominant influence of the energy-restricted diet and exercise program.

In contrast to these physiological outcomes, supplementation did influence perceptual measures. The novel finding of this study was that L-BAIBA supplementation reduced subjective hunger ratings at 14, 28, and 56 days compared to placebo, while BGOP reduced hunger at 28 days only (Table 9; Fig. 2). With only one published human clinical trial examining oral administration of L-BAIBA using an acute, crossover design, Krieger and colleagues [27] demonstrated that oral supplementation increases circulating L-BAIBA levels in a dose-response fashion, the present findings were unexpected given the lack of pre-clinical or clinical evidence indicating that L-BAIBA may influence perceptual hunger ratings. Although BAIBA has been implicated in metabolic signaling pathways and may interact with leptin and energy balance, direct evidence connecting L-BAIBA to hunger perception is limited. While previous data from cell culture and animal studies have highlighted the potential for BAIBA to impact various aspects of metabolism, limited mechanistic studies exist to connect L-BAIBA to appetite and hunger signaling. Pre-clinical evidence does connect BAIBA to potential mechanistic angles where it may support metabolic signaling or nutrient partitioning. For example, in its role as a myokine, BAIBA gets released from contracting skeletal muscle [28] and its appearance is connected to improvements in fatty acid oxidation and insulin sensitivity as well as reduced adiposity in animal models [5, 7, 11, 29], potentially through augmenting leptin signaling and energy balance [7]. Additionally, previous pre-clinical work involving L-BAIBA and GOP support metabolic and blood glucose changes while potentially aiding digestion, energy balance, and inflammation [4, 30]. To this point, circulating BAIBA levels in obese adults following a low-calorie diet correlate with improved glucose regulation and leptin levels, independent of exercise [4]. Due to leptin’s known ability to influence energy intake [31], perceptual ratings of hunger, appetite, energy, focus, attention, and concentration were completed along with the Profile of Mood States to explore the ability of either supplementation regimen to influence these outcomes.

Despite reductions in perceived hunger, these changes did not translate into meaningful behavioral or physiological outcomes. No between-group differences were observed in dietary intake (Table 6), body mass, or body composition, indicating that lower hunger ratings did not lead to reduced energy intake or enhanced weight loss. Importantly, participants were prescribed individualized energy and macronutrient targets and were instructed to adhere to these goals throughout the intervention, which may have constrained spontaneous reductions in energy intake that could otherwise result from decreased hunger. This disconnect suggests that the effects of L-BAIBA may be primarily perceptual rather than physiological within the context of this intervention.

Additional analyses further contextualized these findings. Although between-group differences in hunger were not observed (p = 0.117), baseline hunger ratings were numerically lower in the placebo group (47.8 ± 23.1) compared to L-BAIBA (66.3 ± 20.6) and BGOP (61.4 ± 23.1). Furthermore, within-group changes over time were not statistically significant for L-BAIBA (p = 0.400) or BGOP (p = 0.112), whereas the placebo group did exhibit a significant change (p = 0.020). Adjusting for baseline hunger via ANCOVA attenuated group differences (p = 0.203), suggesting that baseline variability may partially explain the observed effects. While L-BAIBA and BGOP may help stabilize hunger perceptions during a hypoenergetic diet, the practical significance of this effect is limited given the absence of downstream changes in dietary intake or body composition.

Collectively, these findings suggest that L-BAIBA does not exert meaningful effects on resting metabolic rate, body composition, or cardiometabolic health in overweight and obese adults undergoing a diet and exercise intervention. However, BAIBA availability may influence subjective perceptions of hunger. This dissociation between perceptual and physiological outcomes highlights a potentially limited or context-dependent role for L-BAIBA and GOP in human metabolism. The null results observed in our study could likely have been driven by a relatively short investigative duration of eight weeks, quantitatively small effects driven by supplementation, or the possibility that any potential treatment effect may have been overshadowed by the overall impact of the hypoenergetic diet and exercise program we prescribed participants to follow. In addition, a possibility remains that our inability to report on any metabolic changes in the GOP-containing group could be due to competing influences between BAIBA and GOP. Longer supplementation periods, different dosing strategies, or interventions independent of caloric restriction may be required to detect physiological effects. Future research should also aim to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the observed changes in hunger perception and determine whether these effects can translate into meaningful behavioral or clinical outcomes over time.

Strength and limitations

Strengths of this study include the prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study design employed along with energy restriction and exercise. In addition, this study also included a mixed-gender cohort and integrated measurements that spanned several areas where L-BAIBA has been implicated in scientific literature. Another key strength lies in the multiple assessments of acute responses to supplementation throughout the 8-week supplementation protocol. A primary limitation of this study relates to the short duration of the study protocol. It is acknowledged that the study duration (56 days) is shorter than the recommended study durations needed to explore changes in body mass and body composition [24]. This short duration, combined with small effects being offered by supplementation above and beyond the influence of the reduced energy diet and exercise program are likely reasons for our null outcomes. However, a key investigative direction of the present study was to explore the potential for L-BAIBA and the combination of L-BAIBA + GOP to augment changes in RMR, and the study duration employed in the present study was longer than previous research [10, 22]. It is acknowledged that underreporting is widely reported in studies using the energy intake methods we employed and that this may have impacted the magnitude of body mass and body composition changes in the present study. In reviewing this data, it appears that all groups were able to successfully reduce their energy, fat, and carbohydrate intake, but protein intake was not impacted. Finally, the reported perceptual changes in hunger require more investigation. The lack of any concomitant change in energy intake, body mass, or body composition raises questions as to the actual impact of this finding and as a result it requires both careful interpretation and follow-up investigation. Future studies should explore longer supplementation periods, different doses, or how well supplementation changes our measured outcomes without any overlying diet or exercise program.

Conclusion

The results of this 56-day prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study revealed that resting metabolic rate, body mass, and body composition remained unchanged, with no corresponding alteration in energy intake, indicating no detectable effects of supplementation on metabolic activity or anthropometric outcomes. Consistent with these findings, no changes were observed in resting energy expenditure or markers of cardiometabolic health, suggesting minimal impact of the protocol on systemic metabolic function. Despite this, perceptual hunger ratings were reduced in individuals supplementing with L-BAIBA after 14, 28, and 56 days, whereas participants receiving BGOP reported reductions only after 28 days. Furthermore, supplementation was well tolerated, with no adverse events or clinically meaningful changes in safety markers observed across groups.
