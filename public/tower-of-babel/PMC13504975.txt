Temporal changes of oxidative stress and ferroptosis-associated biomarkers in STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI and their correlation with myocardial reperfusion injury
Authors: Mahdis Sharifikia, Maryam Mehrpooya, Akram Ranjbar, Sajad Naghdi, Afsaneh Familmotaghi, Seyed Saman Talebi, Seyed Kianoosh Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury in ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) involves complex mechanisms, including oxidative stress and ferroptosis, but the timing and relationship of these processes with cardiac injury markers remain unclear. This study investigated how oxidative stress and ferroptosis-associated biomarkers change over time and their correlations with cardiac injury markers in STEMI patients undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).MethodsA prospective cohort of 25 STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI was studied. Serum levels of cardiac injury biomarkers (Troponin I, CK-MB) and oxidative stress/ferroptosis markers (superoxide dismutase [SOD], total antioxidant capacity [TAC], glutathione peroxidase [GPx], lipid peroxidation products [LPO], serum iron, and ferritin) were measured at pre-PCI, and at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-PCI. Temporal trends and Pearson correlations between these biomarkers were analyzed.ResultsMarkers of oxidative stress and ferroptosis-associated biomarkers demonstrated distinct time-dependent patterns post-reperfusion: SOD and TAC peaked early (6–12 h), while GPx initially decreased. LPO levels peaked at 6 h. Serum iron transiently declined, and ferritin showed a temporary increase at 6–12 h. Activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, GPx, TAC) showed inverse correlations with peak and cumulative cardiac injury markers in this pilot cohort, whereas higher LPO and iron showed positive correlations with greater myocardial damage observed here. CK-MB associations were stronger than those with Troponin I in this pilot cohort.ConclusionIn this pilot cohort, oxidative stress and ferroptosis-associated biomarkers showed rapid early changes after reperfusion in STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI, correlating with myocardial injury extent (stronger correlations with CK-MB than troponin I). These findings suggest early oxidative stress and iron dysregulation as potential therapeutic targets for reperfusion injury, warranting confirmation in larger studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06066-3.

Introduction

ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, despite significant advances in reperfusion therapies such as primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [1]. While timely reperfusion is critical to salvage ischemic myocardium, the restoration of blood flow [2], paradoxically induces additional myocardial injury, known as ischemia-reperfusion injury [3]. This reperfusion injury significantly contributes to infarct size expansion and adverse cardiac remodeling, ultimately impacting clinical outcomes [4].

The pathophysiology of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury is complex and multifactorial, involving oxidative stress, inflammation, calcium overload, and various forms of regulated cell death [5]. Among these mechanisms, early oxidative stress plays a pivotal role. The sudden restoration of oxygen upon reperfusion triggers an immediate and intense burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which rapidly overwhelms the heart’s antioxidant defenses, including enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx). This early oxidative burst causes extensive cellular damage through lipid peroxidation, DNA oxidation, protein modification, and membrane disruption [6]. Such oxidative injury not only directly damages cardiomyocytes but also triggers downstream processes including apoptosis, autophagy dysregulation, and inflammation, which further exacerbate myocardial injury [7].

Recently, ferroptosis has been recognized as a distinct and significant form of regulated cell death contributing to myocardial injury during ischemia-reperfusion [8]. Unlike apoptosis or necrosis, ferroptosis is driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, resulting in the accumulation of toxic lipid hydroperoxides that disrupt cellular membrane integrity and lead to cardiomyocyte death [9]. Iron plays a crucial role by catalyzing the Fenton reaction, producing highly reactive hydroxyl radicals that intensify lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage. During reperfusion, several mechanisms contribute to iron accumulation in the myocardium, including increased iron uptake from circulating transferrin, mobilization of intracellular iron stores, and dysregulation of iron storage proteins such as ferritin. This iron overload creates a pro-ferroptotic environment that not only intensifies oxidative stress but also overwhelms cellular antioxidant systems, particularly glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPx4), a key enzyme that detoxifies lipid hydroperoxides and prevents ferroptotic cell death [10, 11]. The interplay between iron metabolism dysregulation and oxidative stress thus forms a vicious cycle that promotes ferroptotic cell death, exacerbating myocardial injury following reperfusion [12, 13].

A growing number of experimental and clinical studies have extensively evaluated the relationship between oxidative stress and ferroptosis and clinical outcomes in myocardial reperfusion injury. These investigations have consistently shown that elevated markers of oxidative damage, coupled with decreased activities of antioxidant enzymes, are associated with larger infarct size, impaired left ventricular function, and adverse cardiac remodeling [14–17]. Recent work by Jia et al. demonstrated nicorandil-modulated changes in ferroptosis biomarkers, and serum iron at baseline and 6 h post-pPCI in STEMI patients, linking these to improved TIMI flow and reduced CK-MB [18]. However, natural temporal dynamics beyond 6 h and direct correlations with peak/cumulative cardiac injury markers (Troponin I/CK-MB) remain uncharacterized, limiting identification of therapeutic windows. This study was conducted to evaluate granular kinetics (pre-PCI, 6/12/24/48 h post-PCI) of oxidative stress/ ferroptosis-associated biomarkers in untreated STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI and evaluate their correlations with cardiac injury markers. These temporal profiles could identify critical windows for antioxidant/anti-ferroptotic interventions to minimize myocardial injury and improve clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-center, prospective pilot study was conducted at the Coronary Care Unit (CCU) of Farshchian Cardiovascular Center, affiliated with Hamadan University of Medical Sciences, from September 2024 to March 2025. The study was approved by the local Research Ethics Committee (approval code: IR.UMSHA.REC.1402.598) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion.

Patient selection

Adult patients aged 18 years or older, presenting with STEMI as defined by current guidelines [2], and scheduled for primary PCI within 12 h of symptom onset, were considered for enrollment. Excluded were candidates for emergency coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) instead of PCI; patients with a history of prior myocardial infarction or prior PCI procedures; recipients of thrombolytic therapy; those with cardiogenic shock (defined as systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg despite adequate intravenous fluid resuscitation); individuals with chronic heart failure or reduced left ventricular ejection fraction prior to the index event; patients with significant renal impairment (eGFR ≤ 60 ml/min/1.73 m², calculated by the CKD-EPI formula); those with advanced liver disease or cirrhosis; patients with known autoimmune or chronic inflammatory diseases; users of antioxidant supplements or medications beyond standard prescribed therapy; pregnant or lactating women; and any individuals who refused or were unable to provide informed consent.

Demographic data, clinical history, and cardiovascular risk factors (including age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and family history of premature coronary artery disease) were systematically collected for all enrolled patients. Additional information regarding symptoms on arrival, medication history, and laboratory results was recorded at hospital admission.

Blood sampling, processing, and storage

Venous blood (10 mL) was collected before primary PCI (baseline) in the emergency department, and at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-procedure in the cardiac care unit (CCU) or, if the patient was transferred, in the respective hospital ward. This approach was used to capture early and sustained changes in oxidative stress and ferroptosis biomarkers, based on prior studies [18, 19]. Samples were drawn into clot-activator tubes, allowed to clot at room temperature, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and serum was stored at − 80 °C until analysis. All assays were performed in duplicate, with the mean values used for analysis. Assay precision was monitored by intra-assay coefficient of variation, accepting values below 10%.

Measurement of oxidative stress biomarkers

All assays were performed using commercial kits from Kiazist (Tehran, Iran). Assays followed manufacturer protocols. Serum total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was measured using the Cuprac (Cupric Reducing Antioxidant Capacity) assay kit (Cat. No. KTAC-96) [20]. This method quantifies the reduction of neocuproine-Cu²⁺ complex to a stable orange-colored Cu⁺-neocuproine complex by antioxidants in serum, measured spectrophotometrically at 450 nm. Results are expressed as mmol/L Trolox equivalents (LLOQ: 1 mmol/L; intra-CV: 7.8%, inter-CV: 10.5%). SOD activity was measured spectrophotometrically using the SOD assay kit (Cat. No. KSOD-96) and the modified nitrite method, as previously described by Oyanagui [21]. This assay quantifies SOD’s ability to inhibit the formation of nitrite from superoxide radicals, measuring total SOD activity (all isoforms: cytosolic SOD1, mitochondrial SOD2, extracellular SOD3) without isoform separation, with results expressed in units per milliliter (U/mL) (LLOQ: 0.5 U/mL; intra-CV: 8.6%, inter-CV: 12.2%). GPx activity was measured spectrophotometrically using the GPx assay kit (Cat. No. KGPX-96) and the method described by Flohe and Günzler, which monitors the rate of NADPH oxidation coupled to the reduction of hydrogen peroxide by GPx [22], primarily reflecting serum GPx3 (extracellular isoform). Results were expressed in U/mL (LLOQ: 0.2U/mL; intra-CV: 9.2%, inter-CV: 13.1%). Serum LPO concentration were determined using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay kit (Cat. No. KMDA-96), based on the reaction of LPO with thiobarbituric acid to form a colored complex measurable spectrophotometrically at 532 nm [23]. TBARS measures TBARS-reactive substances (non-specific for MDA/LPO) and is susceptible to artifacts from sucrose/amino acids; (LLOQ: 1 µmol/L; intra-CV: 8.1%, inter-CV: 10.4%). Results were expressed in micromoles per liter (µmol/L).

Determination of serum ferritin and iron levels

Serum iron levels were quantified using a Ferene-based photometric assay kit (Pars Azmoon. Cat. No. PT.FER-96). This method involves releasing Fe³⁺ ions from transferrin in an acidic buffer, reducing them to Fe²⁺ by ascorbic acid, and forming a blue-colored complex with Ferene reagent. The absorbance of this complex was measured spectrophotometrically at 600 nm. Visual inspection excluded grossly hemolyzed samples (pink/red discoloration) to minimize artifactual iron elevation. Results were expressed in micrograms per deciliter (µg/dL). Serum ferritin concentration was measured using a sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Pishtaz Teb. Cat. No. PT.FER-96), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Ferritin binds to specific antibodies coated on microplate wells, followed by detection with an HRP-conjugated antibody and color development measured at 450 nm. Results were expressed in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL) (LLOQ: 0.5 ng/mL; intra-CV: 5.4%, inter-CV: 9.3%).

Measurement of cardiac injury markers

Serum CK-MB and conventional Troponin I concentrations were measured using commercial sandwich ELISA kits (CK-MB: DIALAB, Cat. No. DT-1018; Troponin I: PHARMED BEHIN AZMA, Cat. No. TRO-402) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. CK-MB provides earlier peak kinetics (6–24 h) for acute reperfusion assessment, while conventional Troponin I offers high cardiac specificity. Colorimetric detection was performed at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Concentrations were calculated from standard curves and expressed in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL) (CK-MB: LLOQ: 0.2 ng/mL; intra-CV: <8%, inter-CV: <10%; Troponin I: LLOQ: 0.1 ng/mL; intra-CV: <8%, inter-CV: <10%).

Primary and secondary clinical outcomes

The primary outcome was the temporal change in serum levels of oxidative stress and ferroptosis biomarkers (SOD, GPx, TAC, LPO, serum iron, and ferritin) over 48 h following primary PCI in STEMI patients. Secondary outcomes included assessing correlations between these biomarkers and myocardial injury markers at their peak levels (12 h post-PCI) as well as their cumulative exposure, quantified by the area under the curve (AUC).

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 [24] for a two-tailed Pearson correlation test. Based on an anticipated **large effect size** (r = 0.5) between LPO and troponin levels, with 80% power and α = 0.05, the analysis yielded a minimum sample size of 29 participants. Due to practical constraints such as limited patient recruitment and available resources in this single-center pilot study, the final sample size was set at 25 participants.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Exploratory Pearson correlation analyses were performed to identify potential associations between biomarkers and myocardial injury markers. Given the hypothesis-generating nature of this pilot study, no formal correction for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni or FDR) was applied. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Demographics and baseline characteristics

Figure 1 presents the participant flow diagram. Of 64 patients evaluated for eligibility, 34 did not meet inclusion criteria and 5 declined participation, leaving 25 patients finally included. The baseline clinical characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors, and pharmacological treatments of the 25 enrolled patients are summarized in Table 1. The cohort had a mean age of 64.2 years, with a predominance of males (72%). Approximately half of the patients were smokers and hypertensive, while diabetes and hypercholesterolemia were present in 28% and 60% of the cohort, respectively. The majority of culprit lesions involved the left anterior descending artery (48%) and right coronary artery (44%). Radial artery access was the predominant vascular approach (88%). Most patients (72%) had single-vessel disease, and pre-PCI TIMI flow was predominantly grade 0 (68%), consistent with acute vessel occlusion. Pre-PCI biochemical assessments revealed elevated CK-MB and Troponin I levels, indicative of acute myocardial injury. Post-procedural outcomes demonstrated successful reperfusion, with 80% of patients achieving TIMI grade 3 flow and complete ST-segment resolution. All patients received guideline-directed medical therapy during hospitalization.

Temporal changes in cardiac injury biomarkers

Figure 2a shows that serum troponin I levels increased from 5.31 ± 4.82 ng/mL at pre-PCI to 23.98 ± 17.28 ng/mL at 6 h, reaching 43.87 ± 29.97 ng/mL at 12 h (rmANOVA time effect F = 270.164, p < 0.001, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected). After this 12-hour value, troponin I levels gradually declined over the following 36 h. Similarly, Fig. 2b depicts CK-MB concentrations rising from 52.84 ± 7.43 ng/mL at pre-PCI to 127.48 ± 25.55 ng/mL at 6 h and reaching 204.28 ± 45.42 ng/mL at 12 h (per-patient peak occurred at varying times) (rmANOVA time effect F = 50.917, p < 0.001, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected). CK-MB levels then progressively decreased through 24 and 48 h.

Temporal changes in oxidative stress and ferroptosis biomarkers

Serum SOD activity increased from 3.57 ± 0.25 U/mL at pre-PCI to 4.41 ± 0.47 U/mL at 6 h and remained higher at 12 h (4.03 ± 0.37 U/mL) before declining at 24 and 48 h (rmANOVA time effect F = 69.503, p < 0.001, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected) (Fig. 3a). Similarly, TAC rose from 1.91 ± 0.30 mmol/L at pre-PCI to 2.24 ± 0.43 mmol/L at 6 h and 2.02 ± 0.25 mmol/L at 12 h, then decreased by 24 h and partially recovered at 48 h (rmANOVA time effect F = 60.955, p < 0.001, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected) (Fig. 3b). In contrast, GPx activity decreased from 0.80 ± 0.08 U/mL at pre-PCI to 0.63 ± 0.11 U/mL at 6 h and further to 0.57 ± 0.06 U/mL at 12 h, followed by a gradual recovery at 24 and 48 h (rmANOVA time effect F = 9.033, p < 0.001, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected) (Fig. 3c).

Serum LPO levels increased from 2.29 ± 0.17 µmol/L at pre-PCI to 3.11 ± 0.18 µmol/L at 6 h and 2.83 ± 0.15 µmol/L at 12 h, before declining toward baseline by 48 h (rmANOVA time effect F = 214.311, p < 0.001, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected) (Fig. 3d). Serum iron concentrations decreased from 65.88 ± 5.91 µg/dL at pre-PCI to 59.79 ± 5.63 µg/dL at 6 h and 57.88 ± 5.28 µg/dL at 12 h, then gradually returned to near baseline by 48 h (rmANOVA time effect F = 69.503, p < 0.001, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected) (Fig. 2e). Ferritin levels transiently increased from 220.86 ± 19.36 ng/mL at pre-PCI to 251.76 ± 19.34 ng/mL at 6 h and 231.65 ± 16.31 ng/mL at 12 h, followed by a decline at 24 and 48 h (rmANOVA time effect F = 257.192, p < 0.001, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected) (Fig. 3f).

These biomarkers showed dynamic temporal patterns, most prominent at 6 and 12 h post-reperfusion (all rmANOVA time effects p < 0.001, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected).

Discussion

This study examined temporal changes in oxidative stress and ferroptosis-associated biomarkers and their correlations with myocardial injury in STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI. In this pilot cohort, these biomarkers showed dynamic alterations with the most significant changes occurring within the first 12 h post-reperfusion. Specifically, serum LPO increased at 6 h post-PCI, remained elevated at 12 h, and gradually declined toward pre-PCI by 48 h. This temporal pattern aligns with previous evidence demonstrating an early surge ROS generation immediately after reperfusion, which drives lipid peroxidation and oxidative cellular damage [25]. The burst of ROS during reperfusion is primarily attributed to activation of xanthine oxidase, mitochondrial electron transport chain dysfunction, and NADPH oxidase activity in inflammatory cells. During ischemia, ATP degradation leads to hypoxanthine accumulation, which upon reperfusion is oxidized by xanthine oxidase to xanthine and uric acid, generating superoxide radicals as by-products and contributing significantly to the oxidative burst [5]. Additionally, ischemia-induced mitochondrial electron transport chain impairment causes electron leakage upon reperfusion, producing ROS such as superoxide and hydrogen peroxide that exacerbate lipid peroxidation and cellular injury [26]. Furthermore, activated inflammatory cells infiltrating the reperfused myocardium express NADPH oxidase, an enzyme complex catalyzing superoxide radical production, thereby amplifying oxidative stress [27]. These mechanisms collectively contribute to reperfusion injury and underscore the importance of timely antioxidant defenses in limiting myocardial damage.

In this cohort study, concurrent antioxidant enzymes, SOD and GPx, exhibited inverse temporal trends following reperfusion, with an early increase in SOD activity accompanied by an initial decrease in GPx activity. Both enzymes are critical components of the endogenous antioxidant defense system, where SOD catalyzes the dismutation of superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide, and GPx subsequently reduces hydrogen peroxide to water, preventing further oxidative damage [28]. The early rise in SOD likely represents a compensatory response to the rapid surge of superoxide radicals generated during reperfusion, consistent with findings in STEMI patients showing elevated SOD activity correlating with reperfusion [29–31]. In contrast, the transient decrease in GPx activity may reflect its consumption or inactivation due to overwhelming oxidative stress and depletion of its cofactor glutathione during the acute phase of reperfusion injury [32, 33]. This suggests that while SOD activity ramps up quickly to neutralize superoxide radicals, GPx activity may temporarily lag because of substrate exhaustion or oxidative modification [34]. This differential response underscores the complexity of antioxidant enzyme regulation during myocardial reperfusion injury and highlights potential therapeutic windows for antioxidant interventions.

Concurrent with changes in SOD and GPx, serum TAC exhibited an early increase following reperfusion. TAC represents the cumulative effect of all antioxidants present in serum, including enzymatic and non-enzymatic components, providing a global measure of antioxidant status [35]. The observed rise in TAC shortly after PCI may indicate a compensatory systemic response aimed at neutralizing the surge of ROS generated during reperfusion. However, the magnitude and duration of TAC elevation may be limited by the overwhelming oxidative stress, as observed by its gradual decline toward pre-PCI by 24 to 48 h post-PCI. This decline in antioxidant capacity correlates with poorer myocardial recovery and increased oxidative damage [36], suggesting that supporting antioxidant defenses exogenously (e.g., via antioxidant supplementation) may improve outcomes by replenishing depleted antioxidants and mitigating reperfusion injury [37]. However, clinical trials investigating antioxidant therapies in acute myocardial infarction and reperfusion injury have yielded variable and often conflicting results.

In our cohort study, serum iron concentrations exhibited a transient decrease during the early reperfusion period, consistent with hepcidin-mediated hypoferremia of inflammation, reaching their lowest levels within the first 12 h post-PCI before gradually returning toward pre-PCI by 48 h. In contrast, ferritin levels showed a transient increase shortly after reperfusion, highest around 6 to 12 h as an acute-phase response, followed by a decline back to near pre-PCI levels thereafter. The observed pattern reflects systemic iron regulation rather than myocardial iron release or export. Hepcidin, rapidly induced by reperfusion inflammation (IL-6/TNF-α), downregulates ferroportin on macrophages/hepatocytes, trapping iron intracellularly and causing hypoferremia to limit Fenton chemistry and bacterial growth [38]. Concurrently, ferritin rises as an acute-phase reactant via cytokine induction, safely sequestering iron [39]. This indicates that the changes in serum iron and ferritin predominantly mirror systemic iron sequestration in response to inflammation and oxidative stress, not specific myocardial iron mobilization. Free iron is a potent catalyst of the Fenton reaction, which generates highly reactive hydroxyl radicals contributing to lipid peroxidation and cellular injury [40]. To mitigate this, the body transiently sequesters free iron, reducing its circulating levels to limit its availability for harmful redox reactions. This sequestration is often mediated by increased intracellular uptake and storage of iron within ferritin, the primary iron storage protein [41]. Correspondingly, the transient increase in serum ferritin levels shortly after reperfusion suggests enhanced iron storage activity [42]. Ferritin binds free iron in a non-reactive form, thus serving as a protective buffer against iron-induced oxidative stress [43]. The peak in ferritin levels around 6 to 12 h post-reperfusion may reflect both increased synthesis and release from cells responding to oxidative stress and inflammation. Elevated ferritin also acts as an acute-phase reactant, rising in response to inflammatory cytokines released during reperfusion injury [39]. The opposing temporal trends—decreased serum iron and increased ferritin—highlight a tightly regulated and dynamic iron homeostasis during myocardial reperfusion. Importantly, the gradual normalization of both serum iron and ferritin levels by 48 h suggests a resolution phase where iron metabolism stabilizes as oxidative stress diminishes and tissue repair processes commence. These temporal biomarker profiles collectively highlight the complex interplay between oxidative stress and ferroptosis pathways during early myocardial reperfusion. Clinically, monitoring these biomarkers may provide insights into the extent of oxidative stress and iron-mediated damage during reperfusion and help identify patients who might benefit from targeted therapies such as iron chelators or antioxidants to mitigate reperfusion injury [44, 45].

Our correlation analyses evaluated relationships between oxidative stress and ferroptosis-associated biomarkers with both 12-hour values and AUC values of myocardial injury markers CK-MB and Troponin I. In this pilot cohort, the inverse correlations of antioxidant enzymes SOD and GPx with both CK-MB and Troponin I suggest a potential protective role of endogenous antioxidant defenses during reperfusion [46]. Elevated activities of these enzymes suggest a potential capacity to neutralize ROS and limit oxidative damage to cardiomyocytes [47]. This aligns with previous clinical and experimental studies where higher SOD and GPx activities were associated with reduced infarct size, improved myocardial salvage, and better clinical outcomes [48–50]. Understanding these relationships may aid in identifying patients at higher risk of reperfusion injury and guide antioxidant-based therapeutic interventions.

In this pilot cohort, LPO and serum iron showed significant positive correlations with myocardial injury markers CK-MB and Troponin I, suggesting a potential role of LPO and iron-catalyzed oxidative stress in reperfusion-related myocardial damage observed here. LPO is initiated by ROS attacking polyunsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes [51]. This oxidative membrane damage compromises cardiomyocyte integrity, leading to leakage of intracellular enzymes such as CK-MB and Troponin I [52]. Elevated LPO levels thus reflect the extent of oxidative injury sustained by myocardial cells. Serum iron exacerbates this injury by catalyzing the Fenton reaction, where ferrous iron reacts with hydrogen peroxide to produce highly reactive hydroxyl radicals. These radicals intensify lipid peroxidation and promote ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by overwhelming lipid peroxide accumulation [53, 54]. Ferroptosis contributes to cardiomyocyte loss and worsens myocardial dysfunction during reperfusion injury. Therefore, the interplay between elevated serum iron and LPO not only amplifies oxidative damage but also activates ferroptotic pathways [53, 55], underscoring the potential therapeutic value of iron chelators and lipid peroxidation inhibitors in mitigating reperfusion-induced myocardial injury.

Interestingly, TAC and serum ferritin showed weaker or fewer correlations with myocardial injury markers in our study, potentially reflecting their systemic rather than localized nature. TAC measures the overall antioxidant potential of plasma, encompassing various enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants, but may lack sensitivity to detect the rapid and localized oxidative bursts occurring within the myocardium during reperfusion [56, 57]. Similarly, ferritin serves primarily as an iron storage protein and an acute-phase reactant that rises in response to systemic inflammation and cellular injury, but its levels may not directly correspond to the acute severity of myocardial necrosis [58]. Factors such as chronic inflammation, comorbidities, and dynamic iron release from ferritin complicate its relationship with myocardial injury [39]. Therefore, while TAC and ferritin provide valuable information on systemic redox balance and iron status, their limited specificity reduces their utility as markers of acute myocardial injury severity during reperfusion, highlighting the need for more targeted biomarkers to accurately reflect oxidative and ferroptotic damage in this context. Notably, in cardiovascular diseases, ferritinophagy—the autophagic degradation of ferritin—plays a crucial role in inducing ferroptosis driven by lipid peroxidation. This process is tightly regulated by intracellular iron homeostasis and ROS production. Studies have demonstrated that ferritinophagy promotes ferroptosis by increasing intracellular iron levels, thereby exacerbating oxidative damage. Therefore, early detection of ferritin levels, maintenance of iron homeostasis, prevention of iron overload, and further exploration of the relationship between ferritinophagy and heart diseases may provide novel insights for the prevention and treatment of myocardial injury [59].

In this pilot cohort, CK-MB showed stronger correlations with oxidative/ ferroptosis-associated biomarkers than Troponin I. CK-MB, primarily localized in cardiac muscle, rises rapidly after myocardial injury, peaking within 4 to 6 h, making it a sensitive marker of acute necrosis during early reperfusion when oxidative stress and ferroptosis peak [60]. In contrast, Troponin I, though highly specific and sensitive, remains elevated for 7–10 days due to slower clearance, which may weaken its correlation with acute oxidative biomarkers measured within 24 to 48 h post-reperfusion [61]. These findings suggest that CK-MB may be more sensitive for detecting acute reperfusion injury; however, the relatively small sample size may have limited the detection of significant correlations with troponin I.

We also observed that oxidative stress and ferroptosis-associated biomarkers correlate significantly with both the 12-hour values and AUC of CK-MB and Troponin I, suggesting a potential mechanistic link between oxidative damage, ferroptotic processes, and myocardial injury. These 12-hour values reflect the acute extent of injury reflect, while the AUC represents cumulative myocardial damage over time [62]. These correlations suggest the sustained role of LPO and iron-mediated oxidative injury throughout reperfusion, indicating potential value in early therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways to reduce myocardial injury and improve outcomes [63].

In this pilot cohort, correlations between oxidative stress/ ferroptosis-associated biomarkers and myocardial injury markers were most significant at 6- and 12-hours post-reperfusion. This finding aligns with the known pathophysiology of ischemia-reperfusion injury, wherein the early reperfusion phase is characterized by a surge in ROS generation and activation of ferroptotic pathways, leading to maximal myocardial damage [5, 64]. The temporal concordance of biomarker elevations at these time points highlights a critical window for both diagnostic assessment and therapeutic intervention. Monitoring oxidative and ferroptosis-associated biomarkers during this period may enable more accurate prediction of injury severity and guide targeted treatments to attenuate reperfusion injury [64].

Recent intervention trials, such as Jia et al., demonstrated nicorandil-modulated changes in select ferroptosis/oxidative stress biomarkers (SIRT1, HIF-1α, GPX4, FTH1, FSP1, 4-HNE, serum iron) at baseline and 6 h post-pPCI, associating these with improved TIMI flow, reduced CK-MB/NTproBNP, and lower rates of unstable angina/heart failure at 12 weeks [18]. Our findings show distinct temporal patterns, with antioxidants inversely correlated to injury extent. These early profiles (most prominent 6–12 h post-PCI) identify therapeutic windows for targeted interventions (e.g., SOD mimetics, GPx inducers, iron chelators) to blunt reperfusion injury, reduce infarct size, preserve LV function, and improve MACE.

Clinical implications and future directions

This study underscores the pivotal role of oxidative stress and ferroptosis in myocardial injury following primary PCI in STEMI patients. The significant correlations between antioxidant enzyme activities and reduced cardiac injury, alongside the association of lipid peroxidation and iron dysregulation with increased damage, suggest these pathways as promising therapeutic targets. Clinically, monitoring these biomarkers could improve risk stratification and guide personalized interventions to mitigate reperfusion injury. Future research should validate these findings in larger cohorts and explore antioxidant and ferroptosis-modulating therapies to enhance cardioprotection and improve long-term outcomes.

Additionally, ferritinophagy has been shown to play an important role in the formation and progression of cardiac diseases by regulating iron homeostasis and ferroptosis. Future studies focused on elucidating the specific mechanisms of ferritinophagy could lead to the development of novel therapeutic strategies for cardiovascular diseases.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the modest sample size (n = 25) reduced power, increased type II error risk, and limited generalizability—despite significant findings signaling for larger trials. Second, multiple Pearson correlations across biomarkers/timepoints lacked within-patient dependence modeling or multiplicity correction (Bonferroni/FDR), inflating type I error; linear mixed-effects models recommended for confirmation. Third, the observational design precludes causality, with unadjusted confounders (infarct covariates like TIMI flow/symptom-to-wire, patient factors like age/diabetes/renal function, comorbidities/medications) potentially biasing biomarker-injury links; multivariate models with Δ-scores needed. Fourth, systemic serum biomarkers may not reflect localized myocardial activity, and total SOD/GPx assays lack isoform specificity (SOD1/2/3 combined; serum GPx3 vs. intracellular GPX4), limiting compartment/ferroptosis attribution. Fifth, the biomarker panel captures oxidative/iron perturbations compatible with—but not proving—ferroptosis (lacks GPX4/ACSL4/4-HNE/tissue analyses); serum iron, ferritin, and LPO/TBARS are not specific to ferroptosis and may reflect general oxidative stress and inflammation, so the observed patterns should be interpreted as suggestive rather than conclusive evidence of ferroptotic cell death. Finally, no therapeutic interventions were tested, warranting future antioxidant/ferroptosis-targeted trials.

Conclusion

This prospective cohort study in STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI observed oxidative stress and ferroptosis-associated biomarkers showing dynamic early reperfusion changes that correlated with myocardial injury extent (stronger correlations with CK-MB than troponin I). Higher oxidative damage and iron showed positive correlations with injury, while higher antioxidant activities showed inverse correlations. These associations provide hypothesis-generating leads for future mechanistic work, suggesting early oxidative/ferroptotic processes as potential therapeutic targets warranting confirmation in larger trials.
