The effect of fall-prevention education on fall-related behaviors in older adults
Authors: Betül Esra Çevik, Hümeyra Hançer Tok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aimed to examine the effect of a structured fall-prevention education program on safe fall-related behaviors among older adults.MethodThe study was conducted using a pretest–posttest parallel-group randomized controlled design. A total of 66 older adults who attended a family health center in the Central Anatolia region of Türkiye and were classified as high risk according to the Morse Fall Risk Scale were included. The intervention group received an education program consisting of two sessions delivered through interactive presentations and question–answer discussions, while the control group received an informational brochure only. Data were collected using the Personal Information Form, the Morse Fall Risk Scale, and the Fall Behaviors Scale for Older Adults.ResultsA statistically significant increase was observed in fall behavior scores in the intervention group between the pretest and posttest assessments. In contrast, no significant difference was found between pretest and posttest scores in the control group. Between-group comparisons revealed that posttest scores of the intervention group were significantly higher than those of the control group.ConclusionThe structured and interactive education program was found to be effective in improving safe fall-related behaviors among older adults. Passive information-based approaches were insufficient to produce behavioral change, whereas nursing-based educational interventions that promote active participation were more effective.Trial registrationNCT07499895- 2026/03/30- Retrospectively registered.Keywords: Older adults, Falls, Education program, Nursing, Behavior change

Introduction

With the increase in the older adult population worldwide, health problems experienced by older individuals have become a major public health issue at both societal and individual levels. In this period—characterized not only by age-related changes but also by declining physical capacity and increasing dependency—falls represent one of the most common and serious problems faced by older adults. Falls cause anxiety and panic, diminish quality of life, and may lead to loss of independence. The World Health Organization (WHO) identifies falls as one of the four most significant problems of old age [14, 19]. Falls constitute a substantial proportion of healthcare visits among older adults [15]. It is estimated that approximately one-third of individuals aged 65 years and older experience at least one fall annually, and some older adults may fall more than once within a year [17]. Falls in older adults can result in serious consequences, including fractures, head injuries, reduced mobility, prolonged hospitalization or the need for long-term care, and even death [1, 7, 24]. A severe fall can substantially impair quality of life and often necessitates a prolonged recovery period. Because falls in older adults may require hospital treatment, including surgical intervention for fractures, they also impose a considerable economic burden on healthcare systems. Preventing falls can benefit older adults by reducing physical problems such as disability and dependency, as well as psychosocial consequences,at the same time, fall prevention contributes to healthcare systems by lowering costs and reducing the burden of care [14, 17]. For these reasons, falls are recognized as a major public health problem [14, 27]. Fall prevention in older adults is a critical issue that requires careful attention due to its impact on health and overall well-being. The role of nursing in fall prevention is of vital importance in ensuring the safety and care of older indivals (Dahlke & Hunter, [8]; [19]. Nurses also play a key role in raising awareness of fall-prevention strategies and promoting behavioral change by providing education and counseling to older adults and their families (Reis da Silva, [23]), [12]. Falls are not an inevitable consequence of aging. Through early recognition and appropriate management of age-related changes, education of older adults at high risk of falls and their caregivers, and the implementation of age-friendly modifications in living environments, falls can be prevented (Reis da Silva, [23]) [12]. Preventing falls among older adults is a critical component of healthcare services that requires the active involvement of nursing professionals. In this context, the present study aims to examine the effect of fall-prevention education on fall-related behaviors among older adults, thereby contributing to the existing literature and providing practical guidance for healthcare professionals. Although previous studies in Turkiye have examined fall risk, fear of falling, and fall-related behaviors among older adults, interventional randomized controlled studies evaluating the effectiveness of structured fall-prevention education programs on fall-related behaviors remain limited. In particular, evidence regarding nurse-led, interactive, and structured educational interventions delivered in primary healthcare settings is insufficient. Therefore, this randomized controlled trial was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured fall-prevention education program on fall-related behaviors among older adults at high risk of falls.

Method

Study design

This study was designed as a pretest–posttest, parallel-group randomized controlled trial.

Study population and sample

The study was conducted with older adults who applied to a family health center in the Central Anatolia region of Türkiye. The Family Health Center is a primary healthcare institution that provides preventive, promotive, and basic curative healthcare services to individuals and families within the community. It also plays an important role in health education, chronic disease management, and preventive health programs for older adults.

The sample size was calculated based on a previously conducted study [2], assuming an effect size of 0.73, a margin of error of 0.05, a power of 90%, and a one-tailed test. Accordingly, a minimum of 66 participants was required, with 33 individuals in the intervention group and 33 in the control group. Inclusion criteria were: being a native Turkish speaker, aged 65 years or older, having a high score on the Morse Fall Risk Scale, being literate in Turkish, and consenting to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included not being a native Turkish speaker, having communication problems, scoring low on the Morse Fall Risk Scale, having previously received fall-related education, and declining to participate in the study. Furthermore, individuals were excluded if they met specific medical, motor, cognitive, or medication-related criteria that could confound the results or interfere with training sessions. Specifically, individuals with severe cognitive impairment, severe hearing or visual problems, or acute medical conditions requiring urgent treatment (medical history) were excluded. To account for motor and physical constraints, those with severe mobility limitations preventing attendance were also excluded. Additionally, patients taking high-risk medications known to significantly alter alertness or balance (e.g., sedatives, hypnotics) were excluded to maintain sample homogeneity regarding baseline fall risks.

Data collection ınstruments

Data were collected using the Personal Information Form, the Morse Fall Risk Scale, and the Fall Behaviors Scale for Older Adults, administered as pretest and posttest measures.

Personal information form

The form consisted of 11 items assessing participants’ age, sex, educational level, income status, social security coverage, presence of chronic disease, life satisfaction, sleep problems, regular physical activity, prior fall-related education, and history of previous falls. The items were developed based on the literature [2, 4], MacKay et al., [18]; Morse [16], ;[21, 22], Topaz et al., [25]; [29]. Life satisfaction was assessed using a single self-report question. Participants were asked to evaluate their overall life satisfaction and select one of three response options: good, moderate, or poor.

Fall behaviors scale for older adults

The Turkish validity and reliability of the scale were established by Uymaz and Nahcivan [26]. The scale aims to assess behaviors exhibited by older adults to protect themselves from potential falls. The content validity index was r = 0.94, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was α = 0.90, item–total score correlations ranged from r = 0.23 to r = 0.70 (p = 0.01), and the two-week test–retest correlation was r = 0.96. The instrument is suitable for self-report or interview administration and is limited to older adults’ perceptions of their own behaviors. The scale consists of 30 items across 10 subdimensions and is scored on a four-point Likert scale: never (1 point), sometimes (2 points), often (3 points), and always (4 points). Each item is scored between 1 and 4. The total scale score is calculated by averaging the item scores, with higher scores indicating safer fall-related behaviors. Items 7, 8, 9, 10, 19, and 23 are reverse-coded. Subscale scores are calculated similarly by summing the relevant item scores and dividing by the number of items (Ekşi- [26].

Morse fall risk scale

The Morse Fall Risk Scale was developed by Morse in 1985 and adapted into Turkish by Yılmaz Demir [31]. The scale consists of six criteria used to assess fall risk: history of falls, presence of a secondary diagnosis, use of ambulatory aids, presence of an intravenous line or heparin therapy, gait/transfer status, and mental status. Scores below 25 indicate a low risk of falls, scores between 25 and 50 indicate a moderate risk, and scores of 51 or higher indicate a high risk of falls [9].

Randomization and blinding

A total of 66 older adults who met the inclusion criteria and volunteered to participate were randomly assigned to the intervention or control groups using a simple randomization method. The study was completed with 33 participants in the intervention group and 33 in the control group. Randomization was performed after obtaining informed consent from the participants, and assignment of the first participant to either group was determined by drawing lots. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants was not possible. Participants who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled by the researchers. Until the start of the intervention, participants were not informed of their group allocation. Sample ordering and data analysis were performed by an independent statistician, thereby ensuring blinding during the statistical analysis. Both the intervention and control groups were recruited from the same Family Health Center and participated under similar environmental conditions. The only difference between the groups was the type of intervention received. Potential participant attrition, as well as incomplete or incorrectly filled forms, were taken into account during the data collection process; invalid forms were excluded, and data collection sustained until the minimum sample size targeted by the power analysis was successfully reached.

Pilot study

A pilot study was conducted to assess the appropriateness of the education duration and the estimated time required to complete the data collection instruments (Personal Information Form, Morse Fall Risk Scale, and Fall Behaviors Scale for Older Adults). The pilot study was carried out with four individuals who met the inclusion criteria. During the pilot, the time required to complete the pretest questionnaires was measured and found to be approximately 15–20 min. The education program was planned to consist of two sessions, each lasting approximately 25 min, with a 10-min break between sessions, confirming a total education duration of 50 min (Fig. 1).

Implementation of the Study

The pilot results indicated that the educational content was understandable, the duration was appropriate for older adults, and the data collection instruments could be completed without difficulty. Based on these findings, no modifications were required, and the main study proceeded using the same structure and procedures established during the pilot phase.

Control group

Participants assigned to the control group were invited to a meeting room to receive information about the study and to assess their fall risk. Older adults identified as having a high risk of falls completed the Personal Information Form and the Fall Behaviors Scale for Older Adults as a pretest. Following the pretest, brochures prepared by the researchers in line with the literature were distributed to participants [2, 4], MacKay et al., [18]; Morse, [16]; [21, 22], Topaz et al., [25]; [29]. Participants were given approximately 45 min to review the brochures. The brochure provided to the control group included information on common risk factors for falls, home safety precautions, environmental modifications to reduce fall hazards, the importance of appropriate footwear, adequate lighting, regular physical activity, balance-enhancing activities, and general recommendations for fall prevention in older adults. During this period, the researcher remained present to answer participants’ questions. The posttest assessment was conducted 20 days after receiving the brochure using the Fall Behaviors Scale for Older Adults.

Intervention group

Older adults who applied to the family health center and scored high on the Morse Fall Risk Scale were assigned to the intervention group. After being informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, participants gathered in the institution’s meeting room. The educational intervention was delivered in small groups in the meeting room of the Family Health Center.

First, participants completed the Personal Information Form and the Fall Behaviors Scale for Older Adults as a pretest. Following the pretest, an education program developed by the researchers and strengthened through content validity assessment by five experts was implemented [2, 4], MacKay et al., [18]; Morse, [16]; [21, 22], Topaz et al., [25]; [29]. Expert opinions were obtained using the Davis technique, with content validity indices ranging between 0.80 and 1.00.

The education program consisted of two sessions lasting a total of 50 min (25 + 25 min), with a 10-min break between sessions. The first session (25 min) addressed the topics of what fall risk is, how fall risk affects older adults and their caregivers, and risk factors for falls. Individual/intrinsic factors (e.g., muscle weakness, balance and gait problems, visual impairment, chronic diseases, polypharmacy and sedative medication use, inappropriate footwear) and environmental/extrinsic factors (e.g., inadequate lighting, slippery floors, loose rugs, thresholds and cables, lack of safety features on stairs and in bathrooms) were discussed using practical examples.

The second session (25 min) focused on measures to reduce fall risk, including home environmental modifications (e.g., improving lighting, securing rugs, using non-slip mats, installing grab bars in stairways and bathrooms), safe behaviors (e.g., standing up slowly, choosing appropriate footwear, proper use of assistive devices), and health-related measures (e.g., medication review with a physician, balance and strengthening exercises, adequate hydration and nutrition). The session concluded with a question-and-answer segment in which participants’ individual concerns were addressed (Table 1). Following completion of the intervention, participants were contacted again and the posttest assessment was conducted 20 days after the educational program using the Fall Behaviors Scale for Older Adults.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis of the data obtained in the study was performed using the SPSS 23.0 software package. To assess the homogeneity of the intervention and control groups in terms of age and other sociodemographic variables, the chi-square test and the independent samples t-test were used. These analyses examined whether the groups were comparable with respect to age, sex, educational level, income level, social security status, presence of chronic disease, life satisfaction, sleep problems, level of physical activity, prior fall-related education, and history of falls. To evaluate changes between pretest and posttest fall behavior scores within each group, paired samples t-tests were conducted separately for the intervention and control groups. In addition, independent samples t-tests were used to compare pretest and posttest score differences between the intervention and control groups. In all analyses, a significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Sivas Cumhuriyet University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2024–07/32; Date: 18 July 2024) and written permission was received from the Provincial Directorate of Health (Date: 01 November 2024; Reference No: E-73192166–044–258248763). Older adults were provided with verbal information about the study by the researchers, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Only individuals who agreed to participate were included in the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Across all sociodemographic and health-related variables—including sex, education, income level, social security, presence of chronic disease, life satisfaction, sleep problems, physical activity level, prior fall education, and history of falls—no statistically significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups (p > 0.05). These findings indicate that the groups were homogeneous at baseline.

Based on the results presented in Table 3, the mean fall-related behavior score of older adults in the intervention group increased from 71.81 ± 2.40 at pretest to 78.45 ± 2.46 at posttest. Statistical analysis indicated that this increase was significant (t = 10.503, df = 32, p < 0.01), demonstrating that the intervention led to a meaningful improvement in fall-related behavior scores among older adults. In contrast, the control group showed no significant change in fall-related behavior scores, with mean values of 70.57 ± 3.13 at pretest and 70.75 ± 3.35 at posttest (t = 0.566, df = 32, p = 0.575). Between-group comparisons revealed no significant difference at baseline (t = 1.807, df = 64, p = 0.075), indicating comparable initial levels between the groups. However, a significant difference emerged at posttest (t = 10.624, df = 64, p < 0.01), favoring the intervention group. These findings indicate that the fall-prevention education implemented in the intervention group was effective in improving fall-related behavior scores and achieved a significantly greater improvement compared with the control group.

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate that the education program implemented in the intervention group significantly increased older adults’ levels of safe fall-related behaviors. This result is consistent with findings reported in the existing literature. Indeed, fall-prevention interventions among older adults have been shown to reduce fall incidence by increasing individuals’ behavior and adoption of protective behaviors [19]. Similarly, Tiago Horta [11] emphasizes that individualized education provided by nurses is effective in raising fall-related behavior and promoting behavioral change among older adults (Tiago [12]. In addition, studies conducted by Clemson et al. [6] and Yardley et al. [28] have reported that education-based interventions increase behavior levels and lead to positive fall-related behavioral changes in older individuals (Clemson et al., [5], Yardley et al., [30]). Studies carried out in Türkiye also support these findings, indicating that educational interventions contribute substantially to recognizing risk factors, developing preventive behaviors, and enhancing perceptions of safety among older adults [13]. Accordingly, the findings of the present study are consistent with the literature and highlight education programs as an important tool for increasing fall behavior in older adults.

The absence of a significant difference between pretest and posttest scores in the control group, despite the provision of brochures, underscores the limited effectiveness of passive information transfer in enhancing fall behavior. Consistent with this finding, the literature suggests that interventions based solely on written materials or brochure distribution are insufficient to promote behavioral change among older adults. For example, Colón-Emeric et al. (201) reported that brochures and informational materials alone are not effective in reducing fall risk, but yield more positive outcomes when supported by multi-component and interactive educational approaches [7]. Yardley et al. [28] similarly found that older adults have difficulty translating information into practice when fall-prevention advice is delivered only through written sources, whereas interactive education increases both behavior and motivation (Yardley et al., [30]). These findings support the lack of statistically significant change observed in the control group in the present study. Comparisons with the literature indicate that one-way information delivery methods, such as brochures, are limited in raising behavior and inadequate for producing behavioral change,in contrast, face-to-face education, group discussions, and nurse-led individualized interventions—such as those applied in the intervention group—are more effective (Latkin et al., [16], [19].

Identifying risk factors, assessing environmental hazards, implementing necessary modifications, informing the community, promoting physical activity and exercise, and providing balance training are essential components of fall prevention among older adults [19]. From this perspective, the educational content applied in the present study may have contributed to increased behavior among participants. In the control group, only informational brochures were distributed, and no active learning methods were employed,consequently, passive delivery of educational content proved insufficient to facilitate behavioral change. The literature consistently emphasizes that effective health education requires methods that actively engage learners (Yardley et al., [30]). The inclusion of interactive presentations and question–answer sessions in the educational program likely supported older adults’ internalization of learning. Indeed, systematic reviews indicate that education delivered solely through passive methods, such as brochures, is ineffective in reducing fall risk, whereas multi-component interventions incorporating exercise, environmental modifications, and interactive education are more effective [10]. In this context, the two-session structured education program implemented in this study may be considered an effective approach for promoting behavioral change.

From a nursing practice perspective, this study highlights the critical role of nurses in fostering protective health behaviors related to fall risk among older adults. Nurses can contribute directly to fall prevention not only through therapeutic care but also by actively fulfilling their educational and counseling roles. In this regard, it is particularly important for nurses working in primary healthcare settings to develop and implement fall-prevention programs through one-to-one engagement with older adults.

Strengths of the study

A major strength of this study is its randomized controlled trial design, which provides a robust methodological framework for evaluating the effectiveness of fall-prevention education among older adults. The inclusion of both intervention and control groups enabled a direct comparison of outcomes and strengthened the internal validity of the findings. Another important strength is that the educational content was developed based on the literature and underwent expert evaluation, with content validity indices ranging from 0.80 to 1.00, ensuring the scientific quality and relevance of the intervention. Furthermore, the study specifically targeted older adults identified as being at high risk of falls according to the Morse Fall Risk Scale, thereby focusing on a population most likely to benefit from preventive interventions. These strengths enhance the reliability of the findings and contribute valuable evidence regarding the effectiveness of nurse-led fall-prevention education programs in primary healthcare settings.

Limitations of the study

The primary limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the study was conducted in a single geographic region, which may limit the applicability of the results to older adults with different sociocultural characteristics. The education program was short-term, and long-term effects were not evaluated. Moreover, providing only brochures to the control group limited comparisons with other passive educational methods. For these reasons, the results should be interpreted with caution and supported by future studies involving larger samples, diverse settings, and longer follow-up periods.

Conclusion

This study found that a structured and interactive education program was effective in increasing fall behavior among older adults. While a significant improvement in behavior levels was observed in the intervention group following the education, no meaningful change occurred in the control group despite the provision of brochures. These findings indicate that education methods based on active participation are more effective than passive information delivery in enhancing fall behavior. Implementing fall-prevention education programs in a regular and systematic manner within primary healthcare services is therefore of great importance. By effectively utilizing their educational and counseling roles and expanding individualized and interactive education approaches, nurses can contribute substantially to the development of behavior and protective behaviors among older adults. Furthermore, follow-up studies are needed to assess the long-term effects of such educational interventions. Similar studies conducted in different regions with larger samples would enhance the generalizability of the findings and facilitate the integration of the results into national health policies and nursing practice.
