Light Alignment Improves LLM Safety via Model Self-Reflection with a Single Neuron
Sicheng Shen, Mingyang Lv, Han Shen, Jialin Wu, Binghao Wang, Zhou Yang, Guobin Shen, Dongcheng Zhao, Feifei Zhao, Yi Zeng

Abstract
The safety of large language models (LLMs) has increasingly emerged as a fundamental aspect of their development. Existing safety alignment for LLMs is predominantly achieved through post-training methods, which are computationally expensive and often fail to generalize well across different models. A small number of lightweight alignment approaches either rely heavily on prior-computed safety injections or depend excessively on the model’s own capabilities, resulting in limited generalization and degraded efficiency and usability during generation. In this work, we propose a safety-aware decoding method that requires only low-cost training of an expert model and employs a single neuron as a gating mechanism . By effectively balancing the model’s intrinsic capabilities with external guidance , our approach simultaneously preserves utility and enhances output safety. It demonstrates clear advantages in training overhead and generalization across model scales, offering a new perspective on lightweight alignment for the safe and practical deployment of large language models. Introduction

As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in real-world applications, the safety of their generated outputs has become a central dimension for evaluating overall model capability. The continued scaling of model size and expressive power enables flexible generation under complex instructions, but simultaneously amplifies the risk of producing unsafe, harmful, or value-misaligned content
(
26
)
. Ensuring that LLMs reliably adhere to safety constraints during generation without substantially compromising utility—has therefore emerged as a key challenge in contemporary language model research
(
27
;
20
)
. Importantly, this challenge is no longer confined to adversarial or edge-case inputs; safety violations increasingly arise in open-ended and seemingly benign usage scenarios, where risks are harder to anticipate and control.

Most existing work formulates this challenge as an alignment problem, aiming to align model behavior with human values, usage norms, and safety boundaries. The dominant solutions rely on post-training paradigms, such as supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning-based methods, which explicitly encode safety into model parameters
(
44
)
. These approaches have proven effective in practice, particularly in reducing overtly harmful behaviors (e.g., RLHF and DPO)
(
34
;
31
)
. However, post-training alignment is computationally expensive, heavily dependent on large-scale, carefully curated human preference data, and typically requires full or partial fine-tuning for each target model or checkpoint
(
41
;
23
)
. As a result, alignment becomes tightly coupled with costly retraining pipelines, limiting scalability and hindering efficient reuse across model families
(
10
;
19
)
.

To mitigate these limitations, a growing line of research has shifted attention to inference-time safety mechanisms, which intervene directly during decoding without modifying model parameters. From a design perspective, existing approaches can be broadly categorized into three groups. The first group relies on external intervention, injecting safety constraints by steering hidden representations along predefined safety-related directions or by modifying output probabilities using expert models or safety vectors
(
35
;
3
;
42
)
. The second group formulates safe generation as a constrained search problem, employing handcrafted rules, rollback strategies, or tree-based exploration to identify token sequences that satisfy safety constraints
(
21
;
40
)
. In parallel, a third line of work relies almost entirely on the model’s intrinsic capabilities, prompting the model to reflect, self-examine, or proactively refuse unsafe requests during generation
(
54
;
28
)
.
Existing inference-time safety methods face fundamental limitations. Approaches based on external intervention or search often avoid model post-training and incur minimal inference-time overhead; however, they typically require substantial model-specific pre-computation, such as constructing auxiliary datasets or deriving safety vectors for each target model, which introduces non-trivial deployment overhead and poor cross-model generalization. In contrast, methods relying solely on model self-reflection are lightweight at both training and inference time, but depend on unreliable and highly model-specific safety awareness. More fundamentally, current external intervention methods fail to utilize the target model’s intrinsic safety capabilities learned during training, resulting in redundant and inefficient interventions even when inputs are already recognized as safe
(
17
;
15
)
.

) that explicitly couples intrinsic model awareness with minimal external control. Our approach requires training only the smallest model in a model family as a safety expert, substantially reducing alignment cost while enabling efficient transfer to larger, related models. At the core of our method, we introduce a
single-neuron-based gating mechanism
that exposes the model’s internal risk signals and adaptively regulates safety intervention during decoding. As a result, safety control is selectively activated only when elevated risk is detected, rather than being uniformly imposed. This design preserves model utility on benign inputs while maintaining robust safety behavior, achieving an effective balance between intrinsic model capability and external guidance. Related Work

2.1
Jailbreak Attack

Jailbreak attacks aim to bypass the safety alignment mechanisms of large language models to elicit unsafe or harmful outputs. Early studies mainly rely on manually crafted adversarial prompts that exploit conflicts between safety and helpfulness objectives or reveal generalization failures under adversarial input distributions
(
25
;
51
)
. While effective, such approaches are limited in scalability. More recent work shifts toward automated, optimization-based jailbreak generation. Gradient-based methods, such as GCG
(
55
)
, optimize adversarial prefixes to identify trigger tokens that induce unsafe responses, whereas search- or evolution-based approaches like AutoDAN
(
24
)
explore the discrete prompt space without gradient access. In parallel, red-teaming frameworks introduce auxiliary language models to iteratively generate and refine jailbreak prompts through multi-round interactions, as exemplified by PAIR
(
5
)
and GPTFuzzer
(
50
)
. These advances motivate the need for alignment methods specifically designed to defend against increasingly automated jailbreak attacks.

2.2
Decoding-time Safety Methods

Early alignment methods predominantly operate at the
post-training stage
, where model weights are fine-tuned on safety datasets aligned with human preferences. InstructGPT
(
26
)
combines supervised fine-tuning with reinforcement learning from human feedback, while later approaches such as DPO
(
31
)
, PITA
(
4
)
, and DRPO
(
36
)
reduce annotation dependence or introduce preference-based and prompt-aware optimization. Despite their effectiveness, these methods incur high training costs that scale with model size and require model-specific alignment, limiting generalization.

In contrast,
decoding-time alignment
methods enforce safety during generation. Approaches such as Jailbreak Antidote
(
35
)
, SafeInfer
(
3
)
, and InferAligner
(
42
)
inject safety-related guidance into model representations, whereas RAIN
(
21
;
40
)
frames safe generation as a constrained token-level search problem. Although these methods reduce training overhead, they often introduce additional computation at inference time, degrade utility, and still exhibit limited cross-model generalization.

2.3
Leveraging Intrinsic Model Capabilities for Safety

In safety-aware generation, the model’s internal risk-awareness capability is relatively underexplored. CARE
(
16
)
and Bidirectional Intention
(
37
)
enable periodic self-checks during decoding to detect and roll back unsafe tokens. ICD
(
45
)
, Self-Reminder
(
47
)
, and Self-Exam
(
28
)
further prompt the model to explicitly recognize harmful intent or re-evaluate its own outputs during generation. In a related vein, Persona-Judge
(
53
)
and PAD
(
6
)
assign the model dual roles as generator and judge to suppress unsafe tokens via prompt-based or learning-based mechanisms.

3.1
Safety-Aware Decoding

As formulated in Eq.
1
, SafeDecoding
(
48
)
ensures output safety by jointly decoding a base model and a safety expert at inference time. The safety expert is obtained via LoRA-based fine-tuning of the base model and therefore shares the same architecture and model scale. At each decoding step
t
, the conditional distribution over the next token
y_{t}
is adjusted by linearly interpolating the predictions of the base model
p_{b}
and the expert model
p_{e}
. The interpolation coefficient
\alpha
controls the strength of the safety guidance.

\displaystyle P(y_{t}

\displaystyle\mid y_{<t},x)=p_{b}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},x)

(1)

\displaystyle+\alpha\Big(p_{e}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},x)-p_{b}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},x)\Big)

Building upon this framework, Speculative Safety-Aware Decoding (SSD)
(
43
)
introduces a lightweight safety expert and constructs either intersection-based or union-based candidate token sets between the base and expert models at each decoding step. Instead of operating on probabilities, SSD directly performs safety-aware adjustments on token log-probabilities within the selected token set. Furthermore, SSD leverages historical decoding signals to periodically adapt the coefficient
\alpha
and dynamically switch between different decoding modes, achieving a better balance among safety, efficiency, and utility.

3.2
Nonlinear Neuron as Temporal Gating Mechanism

We propose a neuron-inspired unit (Eq.
2
) designed to act as a gating mechanism in the temporal domain.

\tau_{m}\frac{dV(t)}{dt}=-\big(V(t)-V_{\text{rest}}\big)+RI(t),

(2)

where
V(t)
denotes the membrane potential,
I(t)
is the input signal, and
\tau_{m}
controls the temporal integration scale. This dynamics allows the neuron to retain historical information by accumulating both past and current inputs.
A spike is generated when the membrane potential exceeds a predefined threshold,

S(t)=\Theta\big(V(t)-V_{\text{th}}\big),

(3)

where
\Theta(\cdot)
denotes the Heaviside step function, which outputs
1
for positive inputs and
0
otherwise. The resulting binary output
S(t)\in\{0,1\}
can be naturally interpreted as a gating signal.

In NGSD, the distributional discrepancy between the target model and the expert model is injected into the neuron as the input signal
I(t)
. Owing to its nonlinearity, the neuron simultaneously supports the accumulation of historical signals and rapid responses to strong immediate signals. The experimental results prove neurons effective for gating.

Methodology

4.1
Overview

We propose
N
euron-
G
uided
S
afe
D
ecoding (
NGSD
), a lightweight inference-time alignment framework (Fig.
1
). NGSD aims to provide robust safety guarantees under unsafe inputs while preserving the base model’s utility capability on the benign inputs, without modifying model parameters or introducing significant inference-time overhead.

Before decoding, NGSD performs a
one-time prompt-level self-reflection
, in which the base model evaluates the input prompt along multiple risk dimensions and the algorithm selects a fixed safety guidance strength for the generation process. This design allows the method to adapt safety intensity to the input risk level. During decoding, NGSD employs a
neuron-guided gating mechanism
to monitor the distributional discrepancy between the base model and a lightweight safety expert, accumulating this signal over time and triggering external safety intervention only when sustained risk is detected. When the gate is activated, NGSD applies
SafeDecoding-style logit correction
at the current decoding step to combine predictions from the base and expert models; otherwise, decoding proceeds using the base model alone. Through this selective intervention strategy, NGSD enforces safety constraints at high-risk positions while minimizing interference with normal generation.

4.2
Neuron-Guided Decoding

NGSD introduces a lightweight safety expert model
M_{e}
at decoding time to provide external safety guidance for a base model
M_{b}
when necessary. We select the smallest model from the same model family as the base model as the expert, ensuring a shared tokenizer and a compatible output space. The expert model is trained with lightweight safety augmentation and can be directly transferred to larger models within the same family.

Distributional Discrepancy as Risk Signal.

At decoding step
t
, given the input prompt
x
and the previously generated tokens
y_{<t}
, we compute the next-token probability distributions of the base and expert models:

p_{b}=\mathrm{softmax}(M_{b}(x,y_{<t})),\quad p_{e}=\mathrm{softmax}(M_{e}(x,y_{<t})).

We measure the discrepancy between the two distributions using the
\ell_{1}
norm,

I(t)=\frac{1}{2}\lVert p_{b}-p_{e}\rVert_{1},

and treat
I(t)
as a scalar risk signal at the current decoding step. Intuitively, a large discrepancy indicates strong disagreement between the base model and the safety expert, suggesting an increased likelihood of unsafe generation.

Neuron-Inspired Gating.

Rather than triggering intervention based on instantaneous discrepancies, NGSD employs a neuron-inspired gating mechanism that takes the distributional discrepancy
I_{t}
as its input and integrates this signal over time. The gate is activated only when sustained risk is detected, enabling NGSD to distinguish persistent tendencies from fluctuations. This design allows safety intervention to be applied selectively, avoiding unnecessary interference at low-risk decoding steps.

Selective SafeDecoding Intervention.

When the neuron gate is activated, NGSD applies SafeDecoding-style logit correction at the current decoding step. Specifically, we construct a candidate token set

C=\mathrm{TopK}(p_{b})\cup\mathrm{TopK}(p_{e})

(4)

and compute the corrected distribution

\tilde{p}(y)=p_{b}(y)+\alpha\bigl(p_{e}(y)-p_{b}(y)\bigr),\quad x\in C

(5)

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
denotes the safety guidance strength determined by prompt-level self-reflection (Sec.
4.3
). The next token is selected from
\tilde{p}
. If the gate is not activated, decoding proceeds using the base model without modification.

By activating external guidance only at decoding steps associated with sustained risk, NGSD enforces safety constraints at critical positions while preserving the base model’s original generation behavior in the majority of cases.

4.3
Prompt-Level Self-Reflection

As described in Sec.
4.1
, NGSD jointly balances and leverages internal and external signals to assess risk and orchestrate effective defense actions.
In this subsection, we focus on how the strength of the safety guidance
\alpha
–which determines the influence of the safety expert when intervention is triggered–is selected prior to decoding. Larger values of
\alpha
enforce stronger safety constraints but may degrade generation quality, while smaller values better preserve the base model’s utility at the cost of weaker safety guarantees. Selecting an appropriate value of
\alpha
is therefore critical to balancing safety and utility in decoding-time alignment.

To avoid unstable and computationally expensive token-level parameter adjustments, NGSD adopts a
prompt-level self-reflection
strategy that determines the safety guidance strength once before decoding begins. Specifically, prior to generation, the base model evaluates the input prompt along multiple predefined risk dimensions that capture
objective aspects of risk
, rather than being designed for any particular attack method or attack class.

Let
S,A,E,T\in[0,10]
denote the scores for
severity
,
actionability
,
evasion
, and
targeting
, respectively. To obtain an overall risk estimate, we first sort the set
P=\{A,E,T\}
in descending order and denote the two largest values as
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
. The aggregated risk score
r
is then computed as

r=\max\!\left(S,\;\frac{1}{2}S+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{P_{1}+P_{2}}{2}\right)

(6)

which is then clipped to range
[0,10]
.

Based on the resulting risk score, NGSD selects a fixed safety guidance strength for the entire decoding procss:
\alpha
in Eq.
5
is set to
0.9
for high-risk prompts (
r>5
) and to
0.1
for low-risk prompts (
r\leq 5
). Once decoding starts,
\alpha
remains constant throughout generation.
Self-reflection avoids periodic parameter updates and additional inference overhead required by prior methods such as SSD, while still allowing the strength of safety intervention to adapt to the input prompt. 5.2
Experiment Result

5.2.1
Safety Robustness and Generalization

As shown in Tab.
1
, our method demonstrates stable and consistent safety performance across a wide range of model–attack combinations, outperforming existing baselines in most settings. Even when it does not exceed the strongest baseline under certain configurations, it remains competitive and consistently outperforms SafeDecoding-based defenses on average.

On weakly or moderately aligned models such as Vicuna and Llama2 (Appendix
B.3
), self-reflection–based methods exhibit clear performance limitations, as they rely heavily on the model’s intrinsic safety awareness, which is insufficient under high-risk or adversarial inputs. In contrast, our approach introduces explicit and selective external control, enabling robust safety enforcement beyond the model’s internal capabilities.
For highly aligned models such as Qwen3,
baseline safety performance is already near saturation across multiple attack scenarios
, placing these settings in a clear marginal-gain regime where additional decoding-time interventions yield limited observable improvements. This behavior reflects a ceiling effect rather than a limitation of our method, which remains effective as a low-interference safety fallback. Overall, our approach demonstrates strong robustness and transferability across models with diverse alignment characteristics.

Finally, since SafeDecoding relies on expert models fine-tuned on self-collected datasets, we conduct additional controlled experiments to rule out the possibility that the observed safety gains stem from dataset differences. Specifically, we replace the expert model in SafeDecoding with our expert model, as well as with a new expert model trained via LoRA on DeepAlign dataset, denoted as SD
TinyExpert
and SD
DaExpert
, respectively. The results show that SafeDecoding exhibits severe performance degradation and extreme instability under these settings, providing strong evidence that the safety improvements of our approach arise from the algorithmic design itself rather than from the expert training procedure or the underlying dataset.

7
Conclusion

This paper presents
NGSD
for improving the safety of large language models through selective and adaptive alignment. Instead of enforcing safety constraints uniformly during generation, the proposed approach applies minimal intervention only when potential risks are detected, aiming to preserve the model’s general capabilities while enhancing safety.

Experimental results across different models and evaluation settings demonstrate that this strategy consistently reduces unsafe behaviors
without significantly degrading performance on benign tasks
. The framework also exhibits robustness to a range of attack scenarios and shows encouraging generalization across model scales, indicating that effective safety signals can be learned and transferred with limited overhead.

Overall, our study suggests that safety alignment does not necessarily require extensive retraining or continuous control.
Lightweight and targeted mechanisms can provide meaningful safety improvements while remaining efficient and scalable
, offering a practical direction for deploying safer large language models.

Limitations and Future Work

This work evaluates selective alignment primarily under controlled safety benchmarks, and its effectiveness in more open-ended or long-horizon settings remains unclear. Moreover, while the method transfers within a model family, differences in tokenization limit its generalization across families. Future work may explore adaptive risk estimation and more general safety alignment strategies, including cross-family evaluation under diverse generation regimes.

Impact Statements

This work proposes a lightweight alignment algorithm designed to improve the balance between model safety and usability, and we do not believe the method itself introduces new ethical risks beyond those already well studied in the alignment literature. All reproduced attack strategies are used solely in controlled evaluation settings to assess robustness, and are neither deployed nor intended for real-world misuse. By focusing on selective and interpretable alignment mechanisms, our approach aims to support safer and more practical deployment of machine learning models.

Appendix A
Over Alignment

A.1
Decoding Dynamics under Over-Alignment

We observe a consistent form of over-alignment across two classes of inference-time defense mechanisms:
injection-based alignment
and
token-level logit manipulation
. In both cases, models exhibit degenerate generation behaviors, including repetitive low-information outputs or the repeated emission of semantically equivalent rejection statements, even when inputs do not strictly violate safety constraints. Notably, this form of over-alignment manifests not only as a degradation in task utility, but also as a failure to produce well-formed and informative refusals when encountering genuinely risky inputs
(
2
;
13
;
46
)
.

We attribute this behavior to the fact that both defenses directly intervene in the decoding process, thereby deforming the effective decision boundary in the token probability space. Injection-based methods introduce strong priors toward safety-related sequences via contextual prompts, while logit-level adjustments explicitly suppress large regions of the vocabulary and concentrate probability mass on a small set of high-frequency refusal tokens. As a result, probability mass collapses onto locally optimal trajectories that are safe in appearance but semantically unproductive.

Importantly, such boundary deformation not only restricts content generation but also disrupts termination dynamics. By systematically altering the relative likelihood between continuation tokens and the termination token (⟨EOS⟩), the model often fails to naturally decode ⟨EOS⟩ and instead enters a low-entropy attractor characterized by repetitive, safety-aligned outputs. Consequently, even in cases where refusal is appropriate, the model may fail to terminate with a concise and coherent rejection, suggesting that this form of over-alignment arises from structural interference with decoding dynamics rather than purely semantic misalignment.

B.1
Self Reflection Template

We assess the safety risk of each user request along four complementary dimensions, each capturing a distinct aspect of potential real-world harm. Together, these dimensions provide a structured and interpretable risk representation for downstream safety control.

The
severity
score captures the inherent danger or sensitivity of the core domain involved in a user request. It reflects how harmful the underlying activity could be if carried out in the real world, independent of narrative framing or virtual settings. Requests associated with domains such as physical violence, weapons, terrorism, large-scale crime, or biological and chemical threats are assigned higher severity, whereas everyday and benign topics receive low scores.

The
actionability
score measures the extent to which a request seeks concrete, operational, or directly executable guidance. This dimension focuses on whether the request includes or implies step-by-step instructions, procedures, parameters, tools, or other actionable details that could enable real-world execution. High actionability indicates that the information could be readily acted upon with minimal additional inference.

The
evasion
score reflects attempts to bypass or weaken safety constraints through prompt engineering strategies. This includes reframing harmful requests as hypothetical, fictional, educational, or research-oriented, as well as the use of role-playing or expert personas to legitimize unsafe behavior. Higher evasion scores indicate stronger signals of intentional boundary probing or circumvention.

The
targeting
score evaluates the degree of real-world specificity present in a request. It accounts for whether the request references identifiable individuals or groups, concrete locations, times, or environments, or includes details that substantially increase real-world feasibility. Requests with explicit or realistic targets receive higher scores, while abstract or non-specific prompts are assigned lower values.

B.2
Rationale for Risk Dimensions and Aggregation

We emphasize that the four risk dimensions considered in our framework are
not optimized for any specific attack category or threat pattern
. Instead, they are designed to capture objective and broadly applicable attributes of risk, which are shared across a wide range of potentially harmful inputs. Each dimension reflects a distinct aspect of how risk may arise, allowing the model to reason about safety in a structured and attack-agnostic manner, rather than relying on predefined heuristics or scenario-specific rules.

At the conceptual level, the proposed dimensions are intentionally designed to be
semantically distinct and approximately orthogonal
. In particular, Severity and Actionability characterize fundamentally different properties of an input. Severity measures the intrinsic sensitivity of the behavior itself, independent of how the request is framed or whether it can be readily executed, whereas Actionability captures the extent to which the input provides concrete, executable details. As a result, highly severe behaviors are not necessarily actionable, and highly actionable requests may correspond to relatively low-risk behaviors. Explicitly separating these dimensions prevents the conflation of risk magnitude with feasibility and improves the resolution of risk assessment.

In the final risk aggregation, we assign
primary importance to Severity
, as it directly reflects the inherent risk level of the underlying behavior and effectively determines an upper bound on potential harm. Even in the absence of explicit instructions, targeting information, or evasion signals, inputs involving intrinsically high-risk behaviors should not be assigned a low overall risk score. Accordingly, Severity is treated as a dominant component in the computation of the final risk.

For the remaining three dimensions, rather than aggregating all signals uniformly, we consider only the top-two highest scores on a per-sample basis. This design choice is motivated by the observation that factors which materially amplify risk tend to concentrate in a small subset of dimensions, while the others often introduce weak or noisy signals. By restricting aggregation to the top-2 dimensions, we reduce the influence of incidental fluctuations and improve the robustness of the final risk estimate, without sacrificing sensitivity to strong risk-inducing cues.

Overall, the four-dimensional scoring framework and its corresponding aggregation rule are not heuristic constructions tailored to particular attacks, but instead reflect principled design choices aimed at generalizability, orthogonality, and robustness. This structure enables consistent risk estimation across diverse safety scenarios while maintaining interpretability and stability in the presence of heterogeneous inputs.

This observation indicates that Self-Reminder–style methods heavily rely on the intrinsic alignment capability of the underlying model, thereby limiting their cross-model generalization. In contrast, our method demonstrates more robust and consistent safety performance across multiple models.

C.1
Neuron Robustness

Figure 3
:
Neuron is robust among gating mechanism against attack methods.

Neuron exhibits strong robustness to hyperparameter choices and is more hardware-friendly in practice. Owing to its distinctive mechanism, Neuron can continuously accumulate historical information through the membrane potential, while still providing clear and timely responses to emerging risks. When intervention is no longer necessary, the membrane potential can rapidly decay, effectively preventing unnecessary influence on the model.

We conduct experiments on Vicuna by applying three different gating mechanisms under three attack scenarios with identical inputs, and compare their behaviors over the first 150 tokens. The results show that all three gating methods preserve broadly consistent trends. However, SMG- and EMA-based gating strategies appear significantly more “smooth.” (Fig.
3
) This excessive smoothness makes them less responsive to sudden risks and, at the same time, causes unnecessary interference from expert models when intervention is not required. These observations clearly demonstrate the advantages of Neuron as a gating mechanism, achieving a more favorable balance between responsiveness and stability.

We further analyze different distributional discrepancy measures from a methodological perspective. Compared to Jensen–Shannon divergence and cosine similarity, the L1-norm is better suited for neuron-level gating, as it operates directly on neuron-wise activation differences without logarithmic, trigonometric, or normalization operations.
