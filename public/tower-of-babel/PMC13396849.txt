Double-kissing double-crush technique for an iliac bifurcation lesion involving the internal iliac artery: A case report
Authors: Alexandre Araujo Pereira, Vinicius Fornari Fernandes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Iliac bifurcation lesions involving the internal iliac artery present a technical challenge in peripheral arterial disease, with important implications for pelvic perfusion. We report the case of a 46-year-old man with lifestyle-limiting claudication caused by a heavily calcified iliac bifurcation lesion involving the common, internal, and external iliac arteries. The lesion was treated using the double-kissing double-crush technique, a strategy well established in coronary interventions but not previously reported in peripheral arteries. At 12-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with durable stent patency.

Endovascular therapy has become an established and effective treatment option for patients with symptomatic peripheral arterial disease (PAD). As life expectancy increases and the prevalence of chronic inflammatory and atherogenic conditions grows, patients increasingly present with complex arterial anatomy, posing new challenges for endovascular intervention. Among these challenges, bifurcation lesions remain particularly demanding and require advanced technical expertise, with suboptimal outcomes still frequently reported.

The iliac arterial axis is especially prone to bifurcation disease, often involving the internal iliac artery (IIA). Compromise of the IIA is a well-recognized cause of buttock and thigh claudication, pelvic ischemia, and erectile dysfunction. Despite its clinical relevance, the true prevalence of IIA involvement is uncertain, and standardized endovascular treatment strategies remain limited.1

In this report, we describe the case of severe symptomatic PAD due to a complex iliac bifurcation lesion involving the IIA, successfully treated using the double-kissing double-crush (DK-crush) technique. Although extensively studied in coronary bifurcation interventions, this technique has not previously been reported in peripheral arterial applications.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Case report

A 46-year-old man, a former smoker, presented with lifestyle-limiting left lower-extremity claudication (Rutherford category III) refractory to optimized medical therapy, including cilostazol and a supervised exercise program. Ankle-brachial index was 0.78, and computed tomography angiography of the aortoiliac segment demonstrated extensive arterial calcification with critical stenosis involving the distal left common iliac artery (CIA), the ostium of the IIA, and the ostium of the external iliac artery (EIA) (Fig 1). The procedure was performed under local anesthesia using bilateral ultrasound-guided retrograde common femoral artery access. A 7F × 11-cm sheath was placed on the ipsilateral (left) side, and a 7F × 55-cm sheath was advanced from the contralateral (right) side to provide additional support. Initial angiography confirmed severe stenosis of the distal CIA, IIA, and EIA (Fig 2).Fig 2Diagnostic angiography confirming critical stenosis at the iliac bifurcation involving the internal and external iliac arteries.

Diagnostic angiography confirming critical stenosis at the iliac bifurcation involving the internal and external iliac arteries.

Recanalization of the CIA and EIA was performed retrogradely from the left side using a V-18 guidewire (Boston Scientific) and a 5F vertebral catheter. The IIA was selectively catheterized from the contralateral access. Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) study using an OptiCross 18 catheter (Boston Scientific) confirmed true lumen guidewire positioning and demonstrated a heavily fibrocalcific plaque burden at the bifurcation.

An uncovered balloon-expandable Dynamic stent (7 × 38 mm; Biotronik) was deployed in the IIA. The first crush was performed using a 7 × 40 mm semicompliant balloon inflated to 8 atm within the CIA/EIA segment. The IIA was subsequently rewired, and the first kissing balloon inflation was performed using 7 × 38 and 7 × 40 mm semicompliant balloons at 8 atm in the IIA and CIA/EIA, respectively.

A second balloon-expandable Dynamic stent (7 × 56 mm; Biotronik) was then deployed from the distal CIA into the EIA. The IIA was rewired through the main stent struts, followed by balloon dilation using a Pacific balloon (3 × 20 mm; Medtronic). A second kissing balloon inflation was performed using 7 × 40 and 7 × 56 mm semicompliant balloons at 8 atm (Fig 3).Fig 3Intraoperative angiography illustrating the double-kissing double-crush technique.

Intraoperative angiography illustrating the double-kissing double-crush technique.

Proximal optimization was achieved with an Omnilink balloon-expandable stent (9 × 39 mm; Abbott Vascular). Final angiography demonstrated excellent stent expansion with no residual stenosis or flow limitation (Fig 4).Fig 4Final angiography showing optimal stent expansion with preserved patency of the common, internal, and external iliac arteries.

Final angiography showing optimal stent expansion with preserved patency of the common, internal, and external iliac arteries.

IVUS study confirmed adequate stent apposition and preserved patency of all treated segments. Vascular access sites were closed using Perclose ProGlide devices (Abbott Vascular). No periprocedural complications occurred.

At 12-month follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of claudication symptoms, full peripheral pulses and an ankle-brachial index of 0.96. Discussion

This case demonstrates the feasibility and safety of applying the DK-crush technique to a complex iliac bifurcation lesion involving the IIA. To our knowledge, this is the first reported use of this coronary-derived bifurcation strategy in peripheral endovascular intervention.

Incomplete stent expansion and inadequate ostial coverage are major contributors to restenosis and thrombosis in bifurcation lesions. Although the kissing balloon technique is commonly used to optimize bifurcation geometry, reaccessing the side branch through the main stent struts may be technically challenging, particularly in heavily calcified lesions.

The DK-crush technique was originally described in 2005 to address these limitations in coronary bifurcation interventions.2 Subsequent randomized trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated superior outcomes compared with provisional stenting strategies, particularly in complex bifurcation anatomy.3, 4, 5 Despite anatomical and biomechanical differences between coronary and iliac arteries, bifurcation-related challenges remain fundamentally similar across vascular territories.

Alternative strategies for the management of iliac bifurcation disease should also be considered. The sandwich technique has been described as a viable option to preserve IIA flow, particularly in the context of aortoiliac aneurysmal disease, although it may be associated with risks of gutter-related endoleaks, device competition, and increased procedural complexity. Isolated IIA angioplasty and/or stenting represents a less complex approach and may be appropriate in selected cases; however, it may result in suboptimal ostial coverage and does not fully address bifurcation geometry, potentially predisposing to restenosis. Conversely, intentional IIA embolization remains a commonly used strategy but is associated with pelvic ischemic complications, including buttock claudication and erectile dysfunction, something of particular concern in such a young patient.

Although covered stents may offer advantages in heavily calcified lesions by reducing the risk of vessel rupture during high-pressure balloon inflations, their use in this case was carefully weighed against potential limitations. Specifically, covered stents may hinder access to the side branch and compromise pelvic collateral circulation, which is particularly relevant when preserving IIA patency. An uncovered balloon-expandable strategy was therefore favored to allow precise ostial reconstruction, maintain access for DK-crush optimization steps, and preserve physiological flow dynamics across the bifurcation.

Covered stents were considered in light of the heavily calcified anatomy and the potential risk of vessel rupture during high-pressure balloon inflations. However, an uncovered balloon-expandable strategy was preferred to preserve access to the side branch, allow precise ostial reconstruction with the DK-crush technique, and maintain pelvic collateral flow. This approach was felt to better balance procedural safety with the need for accurate bifurcation scaffolding in this setting.

A potential limitation of the DK-crush technique in the iliac circulation is the presence of a double-metal layer at the bifurcation, which may increase the risk of stent fracture or accelerated neointimal hyperplasia. Although the iliac arteries are exposed to lower biomechanical stress compared with the femoropopliteal segment, the bifurcation remains a dynamic zone, particularly in calcified or tortuous anatomy. Increased metal burden may also affect flow dynamics and promote restenosis. Careful stent selection and optimization with intravascular imaging, such as IVUS study, may help mitigate these risks, although long-term data in this setting remain limited.

Conclusions

This case report describes a novel application of the DK-crush technique for the treatment of a complex iliac bifurcation lesion involving the IIA. The technique proved technically feasible and resulted in excellent short- and midterm clinical and imaging outcomes. Although technically demanding, DK-crush may represent a valuable strategy for selected peripheral bifurcation lesions. Further experience and dedicated studies are needed to better define its role in PAD.
