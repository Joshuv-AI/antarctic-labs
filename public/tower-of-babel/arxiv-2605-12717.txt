The End Justifies the Mean: A Linear Ranking Rule for Proportional Sequential Decisions
Carmel Baharav, Niclas Boehmer, Bailey Flanigan, Maximilian T. Wittmann

Abstract
Abstract AI alignment and participatory design motivate a new democratic design problem: how to collectively choose a decision rule to use repeatedly . We study this problem for linear ranking rules , which repeatedly rank items x j x_{j} within batches X = ( x 1 , … , x m ) ∈ ( ℝ d ) m X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{m} , where each item’s ranking is dictated by its score ⟨ θ ∗ , x j ⟩ \langle\theta^{*},x_{j}\rangle according to a fixed scoring vector θ ∗ \theta^{*} . Given voters’ preferred scoring vectors θ ( 1 ) , … , θ ( n ) \theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(n)} and their population fractions α ( 1 ) , … , α ( n ) \alpha^{(1)},\dots,\alpha^{(n)} , we ask how to choose a collective vector θ ∗ \theta^{*} satisfying individual proportionality (IP) : every voter type i i should agree with the resulting rankings to an α ( i ) \alpha^{(i)} -proportional degree, either on average over time ( long-run IP ) or even within each batch ( per-batch IP ). The default rule, the arithmetic mean of the θ ( i ) \theta^{(i)} , has been shown to be severely majoritarian; more generally, it is not clear that any fixed linear rule can balance many voters’ disparate opinions. Our main result is that, surprisingly, there is a simple rule that does satisfy long-run IP: the angular mean , the spherical analog of the arithmetic mean. We then show that exact per-batch IP is impossible for fixed linear rules, but that the gap between per-batch and long-run IP shrinks quickly with batch size. Experiments on three real-world preference datasets show that all rules perform similarly when voters’ preferences are homogeneous, while the angular mean substantially improves proportionality in high-disagreement regimes.



1
Introduction

In AI alignment and participatory design, there is growing interest in the democratic design of
decision rules
: mappings that are repeatedly used to map inputs
X\in\mathcal{X}
to outputs
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
over the course of repeated decision-making. Examples include collectively choosing objective functions
[
Shirali et al. 2024
]
, allocation rules for settings such as kidney exchange
[
Freedman et al. 2020
]
and food donation
[
Lee et al. 2019
]
, and reward or scoring functions used to guide the alignment of language models
[
Ge et al. 2024
]
.

Collectively deciding decision rules requires two steps:
eliciting
individuals’ preferences, and
aggregating
them into a single decision rule. The literature uses a variety of approaches to this process: classical LLM alignment pipelines pool individual evaluators’ raw elicitation data and then fit one collective model
[
Ge et al. 2024
,
Ouyang et al. 2022
]
; virtual democracy learns a model for every individual agent, has the models vote, and then aggregates these votes with a voting rule
[
Kahng et al. 2019
]
; still other work learns an individual model for each agent and then aggregates these models directly into a final model. In order, these approaches utilize increasingly richer information about individuals’ beliefs.

We study the aggregation step in the third approach. We focus on the aggregation step because it is comparatively under-studied: there is extensive existing work on eliciting
individuals’
decision rules
[
Freedman et al. 2020
,
Lee et al. 2019
,
Boerstler et al. 2024
,
Keswani et al. 2026
,
Keswani et al. 2024
,
Mohsin et al. 2022
]
, but even in the few papers that consider how to directly aggregate them, the aggregation method is not the main object of analysis, and they default to taking the arithmetic mean of the parameters
[
Noothigattu et al. 2018
,
Shirali et al. 2024
]
. We focus on the third approach because it is a best-case benchmark: if high-quality aggregation is impossible even with known individual rules, then lower-information pipelines cannot be expected to recover it; if it
is
possible, this approach will identify the structure those pipelines should preserve.

We specifically consider aggregating over
linear ranking
decision rules, related to the paradigm of linear social choice
[
Ge et al. 2024
]
. The input to linear ranking rules is a batch of
m
items
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
, where each item
x\in X
is described by a feature vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A rule, parameterized by a score vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, assigns each item the score
s_{\theta}(x)=\langle\theta,x\rangle
and returns the ranking
\succ_{\theta}^{X}
induced by these scores. Linear ranking rules are a natural first case: they are interpretable, technically tractable, and capture the score-then-rank structure underlying many prioritization systems for, e.g., resource allocation, recipient selection, and preference-based ranking of language model outputs.

For linear ranking rules, the aggregation task is then: given
n
voter types, their respective fractional shares of the population
\alpha^{(1)},\dots,\alpha^{(n)}\in[0,1]
, and their respective individual scoring vectors
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we want to choose a scoring vector
\theta^{*}
. This is a non-standard voting problem because the object being chosen is
reusable
: a decision rule is not evaluated only by whether it reflects the electorate at the moment of aggregation, but by how it treats voters across the sequence of decisions on which it is applied. The central worry is therefore cumulative: a fixed rule may repeatedly agree with some viewpoints and repeatedly override others, producing representational inequality over time.
In fact,
Feffer et al. 2023
document this very problem for the literature’s default aggregation approach, the arithmetic mean
\theta_{\mathrm{arith}}=\sum_{i}\alpha^{(i)}\theta^{(i)}
: with just two voter types and two alternatives, the
arithmetic mean can be inherently majoritarian, in some cases producing rankings that almost never agree with the preferred ranking of the minority group.

This observation motivates the design of
individually proportional
aggregation mechanisms: mechanisms that produce a
\theta^{*}
such that if voter type
i
constitutes an

\alpha^{(i)}
fraction of the electorate, the rankings produced by
s_{\theta^{*}}
agree with
i
’s preferred ranking on at least an
\alpha^{(i)}
fraction of item pairs. From the outset, however, it is not at all obvious that
any
fixed scoring rule
\theta^{*}
should be able to satisfy such a requirement: because a fixed rule must prioritize the same features in the same way across all decision instances, it could easily fail to balance so many competing viewpoints. This leads to our research question below; surprisingly, we find that its answer is
yes
.

Research Question:

Does there exist an aggregation mechanism that, given any
(\theta^{(i)},\alpha^{(i)})_{i\in[n]}
, produces a
\theta^{*}
that ranks items proportionally to all voter types
i
over the long-term?

1.1
Approach, Contributions, and Related Work

In
Section

2
, we set up the formal problem and define
long-run
individual proportionality (IP). This notion is measured
in expectation over batches of items
X
, where
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
is repeatedly drawn from some fixed absolutely continuous distribution
\mathcal{D}^{m}
. For a given
X
, voter type
i
’s level of agreement with the induced ranking
\succ_{\theta^{*}}^{X}

is measured by the Kendall-Tau (KT) similarity (i.e., the number of pairwise agreements) between
\succ_{\theta^{*}}^{X}

and
i
’s own ranking
\succ_{\theta^{(i)}}^{X}
. Voter
i
’s proportional agreement entitlement, on average over
X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}
, is
\alpha^{(i)}{m\choose 2}
, so achieving long-run IP means that the following quantity is at least 1, i.e., all voters receive at least their proportional agreement entitlement.

\text{Long-Run IP}(\theta^{*})=\min_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}_{X\in\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\text{ KT similarity of }\succ_{\theta^{*}}^{X}\text{ and }\succ_{\theta^{(i)}}^{X}\right]\,/\,\alpha^{(i)}\tbinom{m}{2}.

We then formally re-establish
Feffer et al. 2023
’s computationally-derived finding that the arithmetic mean
\theta_{\mathrm{arith}}
severely violates Long-Run IP. This motivates our first contribution, a new aggregation mechanism.

Contribution 1: The
angular mean
satisfies long-run proportionality.

Our main result is a proof that for generic
(\theta^{(i)},\alpha^{(i)})_{i\in[n]}
, there is a surprisingly interpretable linear ranking rule that is guaranteed to satisfy long-run IP (
Theorem

3.1
): the
angular mean

\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
. It is the angular analog of the arithmetic mean
\theta_{\mathrm{arith}}
, as illustrated by their analogous defining optimization problems (see
Appendix

C
for proof that the arithmetic mean can indeed be formulated this way):

\theta_{\mathrm{arith}}:=\text{argmin}_{\theta}\ \ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\alpha^{(i)}\|\theta-\theta^{(i)}\|^{2}\qquad\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}:=\text{argmin}_{\theta}\ \ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\alpha^{(i)}\angle(\theta,\theta^{(i)})^{2}

We prove
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
satisfies long-run individual proportionality specifically for
spherically symmetric
distributions
\mathcal{D}
, the distribution class also analyzed by
[
Feffer et al. 2023
]
; a restriction we interpret in
Section

2
.

The main challenge of this result is that the angular mean does not have a known closed-form representation nor is its optimization problem necessarily convex, so reasoning about it is non-trivial. The proof relies on a new key theorem (
Theorem

3.3
), which establishes a fundamental property of the angular mean in
d
dimensions. This property may be of independent interest, as it generalizes a similar bound in 2 dimensions from the math literature
[
Hotz and Huckemann 2015
]
. To our knowledge, this generalization to higher dimensions — and even its possibility — was an open question.

As a bonus, we examine when
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
’s proportionality is
approximable
by other scoring vectors. This leads to a general robustness result: as
\theta
approaches
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
in angular distance, its long-run proportionality accordingly converges to that of
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
(
Remark

3.4
,
Proposition

D.7
).

Contribution 2: The possibility of
per-batch
proportionality.

We have so far taken as granted that, given
\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(n)}
, we need to choose a
fixed
and
linear
ranking rule to be applied consistently across inputs
X
. These two restrictions are well-motivated, serving the interpretability of the decision rule by making it explicit which weights will be applied in every case. However, one may worry that this restriction has major costs to achieving proportionality
per-batch
: i.e., instead of making sure that no group is shortchanged on average in the long run, we might want to ensure that within a given batch, we should not expect
any
group to agree too little with the resulting ranking. Technically speaking, we are simply strengthening the definition above by re-ordering the minimum and the expectation in the above definition of Long-Run IP to define
\text{Per-Batch IP}(\theta^{*})
.

In contrast to Long-Run IP, we find that even on spherically symmetric distributions,
no
fixed linear ranking rule can achieve Per-Batch IP of at least 1 (
Theorem

4.3
). However, we show that for any
\theta^{*}
and
any
distribution satisfying minimal conditions, Per-Batch IP rapidly approaches Long-Run IP with increasing batch size (
Theorem

4.5
). Hence, our commitment to a consistent and interpretable rule results in surprisingly little degradation of our ability to achieve per-batch proportionality.

Contribution 3: Empirical Results.

We empirically compare five aggregation mechanisms on three real-world preference datasets
[
Awad et al. 2018
,
Keswani et al. 2026
,
Lee et al. 2019
]
: the angular mean, arithmetic mean, geometric median, Borda count, and Proportional Sequential Borda (PSB). We find that on the original datasets, voters’ scoring vectors are fairly homogeneous, and all rules easily satisfy individual proportionality. We formalize this finding for
\theta_{\mathrm{arith}}
: as voters’ scoring vectors converge, the arithmetic mean converges to the angular mean, and thus enjoys near-optimal proportionality in such cases (
Proposition

F.1
).

We then induce greater voter heterogeneity by subsampling and restricting these datasets. Here, the rules separate sharply: the angular mean substantially improves long-run IP over the arithmetic mean, geometric median, and often Borda. PSB performs best due to its ability to output rankings not inducible by any scoring vector and to adjust its behavior per-batch, outperforming the angular mean and demonstrating a loss associated with restricting to fixed, linear ranking rules. We also demonstrate robustness of the angular mean to non-spherical batch distributions and confirm empirically that each rule’s long-run IP and per-batch IP converges quickly as the batch size grows.

Related Work.

In addition to the work cited above, the most closely-related research is on proportionality in repeated decision-making, including work introducing the PSB rule
[
Lackner 2020
,
Kozachinskiy et al. 2025
,
Chandak et al. 2024
,
Bulteau et al. 2021
,
Lackner and Maly 2023
,
Phillips et al. 2025
]
. We draw our conceptual notion of proportionality from these papers (i.e., “an
\alpha
fraction of the population deserves an
\alpha
fraction of agreement”)
[
Chandak et al. 2024
,
Lederer 2025
]
, but they differ fundamentally in that they typically allow the use of non-fixed, non-linear voting rules. Also from this literature comes the objective
Squared Kemeny,
which was shown to have strong proportionality in repeated voting
Chandak et al. 2024
; interestingly, we show in
Section

D.4
that optimizing this objective converges to the same behavior as
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
as the batch size
m
grows, but it is potentially more majoritarian at small
m
with respect to long-run IP.
We give a broader related work overview in
Appendix

B
.

Model & Preliminaries

Let there be
d\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
dimensions, and let
S^{d-1}
be the
d
-dimensional unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For a vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
j\in[d]
, we let
z_{j}
be the
j
-th entry of
z
.
The
angular distance
between two vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the (minimal) angle between them,
d_{\measuredangle}(x,y):=\cos^{-1}(\langle x,y\rangle/(\|x\|_{2}\|y\|_{2}))
.

Items and Batches.

Let an
item

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a covariate vector, and let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the set of feasible covariate vectors.
A
batch
of items consists of
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
items, represented as a tuple
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathcal{X}^{m}
.
We assume that batches
X
are formed by sampling
m
items i.i.d. from an
item distribution

\mathcal{D}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
that is absolutely continuous, and has PDF
f_{\mathcal{D}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
; we denote the induced
batch distribution
as
\mathcal{D}^{m}
. Additionally, we assume that
f_{\mathcal{D}}
is strictly positive and continuous on some open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For some results, we will additionally assume that
\mathcal{D}
is spherically symmetric, which we will emphasize notationally as
\mathcal{D}_{\circ}
. The PDF
f_{D_{\circ}}
of a spherically symmetric distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\circ}
is rotationally invariant and hence can be written as a function solely of an item’s Euclidean norm, so
f_{D_{\circ}}(x)=g(\|x\|)
for some function
g\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, i.e., the PDF assigns the same probability to any two items with the same Euclidean norm, regardless of their rotation in space. This assumption is natural in many practical settings; for instance, items representing population percentiles of independent covariates are inherently spherically symmetric.

Scoring Vectors and Rankings.

A
scoring vector

\theta\in S^{d-1}
is a vector of linear weights to be placed on the covariates in
x
. Accordingly, the
score
of
x
according to
\theta
is
s_{\theta}(x):=\langle x,\theta\rangle
. These scores naturally induce a ranking over the elements of
\mathcal{X}
: Let
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, and fix some arbitrary tie-breaking ranking over
\mathcal{X}
denoted as
\succ_{\text{tiebreak}}
. Then, the ranking over
\mathcal{X}
induced by scoring vector
\theta\in S^{d-1}
, denoted as
\succ_{\theta}
, is such that for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
(where
x\succ y
denotes that
x
is preferred to
y
),

x\succ_{\theta}y\iff s_{\theta}(x)>s_{\theta}(y)\lor(s_{\theta}(x)=s_{\theta}(y)\land x\succ_{\text{tiebreak}}y).

For any
\theta\in S^{d-1}
and
X\in\mathcal{X}^{m}
,
\succ_{\theta}^{X}
denotes the restriction of the ranking
\succ_{\theta}
to the items in
X
.

Voter Types and Aggregation Mechanisms.

There are
n\geq 2
distinct
types
of voters
1,\dots,n
, where
i
has an ideal scoring vector
\theta^{(i)}\in S^{d-1}
and constitutes an
\alpha^{(i)}\in(0,1]
fraction of the population (so
\sum_{i\in[n]}\alpha^{(i)}=1
). We let
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta^{(i)})_{i\in[n]}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha^{(i)})_{i\in[n]}
, and we call
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
a
profile
.
Let
P
be the set of all possible profiles, and
P_{n,d}\subseteq P
be all those with
n
voter types in
d
dimensions.

An
aggregation mechanism
is a mapping that takes in a profile
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and outputs some
\theta\in S^{d-1}
. A generic aggregation mechanism is denoted as
\theta_{f}
, so
\theta_{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
is the scoring vector produced by some underlying function
f
.

We remark that requiring the output vector to be normalized (i.e., in
S^{d-1}
) is without loss of generality, since rescaling a scoring vector does not change the rankings it induces.

Given a profile
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in P_{n,d}
, the two aggregation mechanisms we focus on — the (normalized)
arithmetic mean

\theta_{\mathrm{arith}}
and
angular mean

\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
— are respectively defined as

\theta_{\textrm{arith}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\argmin_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}\|\theta-\theta^{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\theta_{\textrm{ang}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\argmin_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}d_{\measuredangle}(\theta,\theta^{(i)})^{2}.

We show that this formulation of
\theta_{\mathrm{arith}}
is equivalent to the direct arithmetic mean formulation in
Proposition

. If there are multiple global minimizers for either function, we assume some fixed tie-breaking scheme is applied.

Remark 2.1

(
Computability of Angular Mean
)
.

How to compute the angular mean is a well-studied problem
[
Buss and Fillmore 2001
,
Afsari et al. 2013
,
Charlier 2013
,
Cazals et al. 2021
]
. Because it has no known closed-form representation, computation generally relies on gradient-based approximation schemes. The objective is not necessarily convex so convergence is not guaranteed
[
Buss and Fillmore 2001
,
Afsari et al. 2013
]
; however, in practice, this is often addressed by the fact that the arithmetic mean often serves as a good initial point for optimization (cf.
Appendix

F
), in which case there exist strong theoretical guarantees that gradient-based methods will converge
[
Buss and Fillmore 2001
,
Afsari et al. 2013
]
.

Long-Run
Individual Proportionality.

In words, an aggregation mechanism
\theta_{f}
is
long-run
individually proportional if, over many random draws of
X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}
, voter type
i
“agrees with” at least an
\alpha^{(i)}
fraction
of pairwise comparisons in the collective ranking
\succ_{\theta_{f}}^{X}
.
“Agreement” is formalized as Kendall-Tau (KT) agreement. For two scoring vectors
\theta,\psi\in S^{d-1}
, their induced rankings on a batch
X\in\mathcal{X}^{m}
have
KT-agreement

a_{KT}(\theta,\psi,X):=|\{x,y\}\in\binom{X}{2}\colon(x\succ_{\theta}y\land x\succ_{\psi}y)\lor(y\succ_{\theta}x\land y\succ_{\psi}x)|
.

An individual’s proportional
entitlement
of KT-agreement is then equal to
\alpha^{(i)}\binom{m}{2}
, i.e., an
\alpha^{(i)}
fraction of all
{m\choose 2}
pairwise comparisons in a ranking over
m
items.

For an aggregation mechanism
\theta_{f}
, a profile
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in P_{n,d}
, a batch
X\in\mathcal{X}^{m}
, and voter type
i\in[n]
,
i
’s individual proportionality (IP) level
is

\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})):=\frac{a_{KT}(\theta^{(i)},\theta_{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),X)}{\alpha^{(i)}\binom{m}{2}}.

Then, the
Long-Run IP Level
(
Long-IP
) is the minimum
expected
individual proportionality level received by any voter type.

Definition 2.2

(
Long-Run IP Level
)
.

For any batch size
m
, item distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and profile
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in P_{n,d}
, an aggregation mechanism
\theta_{f}
has

Long-Run IP Level

\displaystyle\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}(\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})):=\min_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))\right]

and
Worst-Case Long-Run IP Level

\displaystyle\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}^{*}(\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m}):=\inf_{(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in P}\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}(\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})).

Given that we aim to bound
\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}^{*}
, we now characterize what is possible: we show that the most proportional aggregation mechanism can achieve
\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}^{*}
of no more than 1, and the least can achieve
\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}^{*}
no less than 0. The lower bound is by nonnegativity of
\textsc{IP}_{i}
; see
Appendix

C
for proof of the upper bound. Accordingly, we say that
\theta_{f}

satisfies

Long-IP
iff
\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}^{*}(\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m})=1.
This means that in every profile,
\theta_{f}
gives every voter type at least their proportional entitlement.

[Proof]

Proposition 2.3
.

For any
\theta_{f},m,
and
\mathcal{D}
,
\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}^{*}(\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m})\in[0,1]
.

Geometric interpretation of agreement

In addition to this critique of the arithmetic mean,
Feffer et al. 2023
also observe a strong relation between two scoring vectors’ angular distance and their expected KT-agreement on batches drawn from spherically symmetric distributions when
m=2
:

Proposition 2.5

(Proposition 1,
Feffer et al. 2023
)
.

For any spherically-symmetric item distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\circ}
and any
\theta,\psi\in S^{d-1}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}_{\circ}^{2}}[a_{KT}(\theta,\psi,X)]=\nicefrac{{\pi-d_{\measuredangle}(\theta,\psi)}}{{\pi}}.

The intuition for the argument will be useful, so we summarize it here. Let
X=(x_{1},x_{2})
and define the difference vector
\tilde{x}=x_{1}-x_{2}
. Note that
\succ_{\theta}
and
\succ_{\psi}
agree on the ranking of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
iff
\sign(\langle\theta,\tilde{x}\rangle)=\sign(\langle\psi,\tilde{x}\rangle)
. Geometrically (see
Figure

1(b)
), note that the set
H_{>}^{\theta}=\{y:\sign(\langle\theta,y\rangle)>0\}
forms a halfspace, and so does the similarly defined
H_{>}^{\psi}
. The intersection
H_{>}^{\theta}\cap H_{>}^{\psi}
then forms a cone representing the set of
\tilde{x}
for which
\theta
and
\psi
agree that
x_{1}\succ x_{2}
. Via the symmetric argument for when
\theta,\psi
agree that
x_{2}\succ x_{1}
, it follows that the set of all
\tilde{x}
on which
\theta
and
\psi
agree forms a double-wedge. The intersection of that double-wedge with any sphere centered at
0
takes up a
\nicefrac{{\pi-d_{\measuredangle}(\theta,\psi)}}{{\pi}}
fraction of the sphere’s total surface area. Finally, observe that the expected agreement between
\theta
and
\psi
is equal to the probability of
\tilde{x}
landing in the double-wedge. By spherical symmetry,
\tilde{x}
itself is drawn from a spherically symmetric distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\circ}
, and this probability is thus equal to the size of the double-wedge, concluding the proof.

The Angular Mean Satisfies Long-Run Individual Proportionality

(

\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}^{*}
in terms of
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}_{\circ}^{m}}\big[a_{KT}(\theta,\psi,X)\big]
,
we deduce that it suffices to show that
d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta_{\mathrm{ang}})\leq(1-\alpha^{(i)})\pi
for all
i\in[n]
. We now show that this fundamental property of the angular mean holds, i.e., the angular mean is guaranteed to simultaneously lie within a
(1-\alpha^{(i)})\pi
angle from any
\theta^{(i)}
. To our knowledge, this was unknown, and generalizes the known version on
S^{1}

[
Hotz and Huckemann 2015
]
. The full proof is located in
Appendix

D
.

\marginline

[Proof]

Theorem 3.3

(
Fundamental Property of Angular Mean
)
.

For any profile
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in P_{n,d}
,

d_{\measuredangle}(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\theta^{(i)})\leq(1-\alpha^{(i)})\pi\ \ \text{ for all }i\in[n].

Proof Sketch.

Let
F(\theta):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}d_{\measuredangle}(\theta,\theta^{(i)})^{2}
be the value of the angular mean objective for a generic
\theta
. Suppose for contradiction that there exists
i\in[n]
such that
d_{\measuredangle}(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}},\theta^{(i)})>(1-\alpha^{(i)})\pi
. We will find another score vector
\theta^{\prime}
such that
F(\theta^{\prime})<F(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}})
, contradicting the optimality of
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
.

We illustrate the intuition with
Figure

2
. To construct
\theta^{\prime}
, let
G_{i}
be the unique great circle passing through
\theta^{(i)}
and
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
(left image). Then, let
\theta^{\prime}
be positioned on the great circle
G_{i}
, but swooped around to be within a
(1-\alpha_{i})\pi
distance of
\theta^{(i)}
but on the other side. We want to show that

\textstyle F(\theta^{\prime})-F(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}})=\sum_{j\in[n]}\alpha^{(j)}(d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{\prime},\theta^{(j)})^{2}-d_{\measuredangle}(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}},\theta^{(j)})^{2})<0.

The
j=i
term of this sum will be handled at the end.
We first bound
d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{\prime},\theta^{(j)})^{2}
for each
j\neq i
, corresponding to bounding the angular length of the yellow arc (left image).
We do this by applying Toponogov’s inequality and the Euclidean law of cosines, which together give a spherical analog of the Pythagorean Theorem showing that the yellow arc is upper bounded by its Euclidean counterpart when the other two sides of the triangle are preserved (middle image).
As will be important in the next step, one term of this bound tracks whether the move from
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
toward
\theta^{\prime}
initially moves toward or away from
\theta^{(j)}
, as captured by
\cos(\gamma_{j})
(see diagram for
\gamma_{j}
).

With a bound on
d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{\prime},\theta^{(j)})^{2}
for each
j
, we then take the final weighted sum over voters to bound
F(\theta^{\prime})-F(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}})
as above. Here we add one final key ingredient: the local optimality of
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
. Because
F
has zero derivative at
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
in the direction of the move from
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
to
\theta^{\prime}
, the weighted sum of the directional terms involving
\cos(\gamma_{j})
, as carried through our bound above, sum to 0 (right image). The remaining terms then reduce to an expression involving only the move length
d_{\measuredangle}(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}},\theta^{\prime})
, the original distance
d_{\measuredangle}(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}},\theta^{(i)})
, and
\alpha^{(i)}
, which is negative by the choice of
\theta^{\prime}
and the assumed violation
d_{\measuredangle}(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}},\theta^{(i)})>(1-\alpha^{(i)})\pi
. We conclude

F(\theta^{\prime})<F(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}})
, our contradiction.
∎

Remark 3.4

(
Robustness
)
.

A natural question is whether a score vector
close to

\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
will exhibit similar proportionality. The answer is yes: if a score vector is within angular distance
\gamma
of
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
, each
i
’s IP level can decrease (additively) by at most
\gamma/(\pi\alpha^{(i)})
(

).

4
The Possibility of Per-Batch Individual Proportionality

So far, we have shown that
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
ensures that voters will receive their proportional entitlement
in expectation over batches
, and thus on average over sufficient rounds by the Law of Large Numbers.
However, one may also want a stronger guarantee: proportionality on
every item batch
. The strongest notion would guarantee that for any
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
with
n
voter types and any
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
,
\theta_{f}
is such that

\textstyle\min_{X\in\mathcal{X}^{m}}\min_{i\in[n]}\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))\geq 1.

One can immediately see that this too strong for simple divisibility reasons: e.g., consider the Antipodal Construction with
m=2
, where any deterministic rule must output the same ranking each time, thereby giving one group 0 agreement.
A similar construction can be used to show that this issue can occur even without divisibility issues (i.e., even when all voters’ entitlements
\alpha_{i}\binom{m}{2}
are integral): simply let
m
be large but make the items in the batch collinear, and so again, one group in the Antipodal Construction must end up with 0 agreement (
Example

E.1
).

In light of this impossibility, we pursue a weakened version of per-batch proportionality, which takes the
expectation
over item batches instead of the
minimum
:

Definition 4.1
.

(
Per-Batch IP Level
)
For any batch size
m
, item distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and profile
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in P_{n,d}
, an aggregation function
\theta_{f}
has
per-batch proportionality level
of

\textstyle\textsc{batch-IP}(\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})):=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\min_{i\in[n]}\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))\right].

The expectation allows us to avoid over-indexing on the worst-case item batches, while moving the minimum over
i\in[n]
inside the expectation ensures that no voter type is shortchanged too frequently within each batch (rather than just on average over batches).
The following proposition, proved in
Section

E.2
, formalizes this intuition that
batch-IP
is a strict strengthening of
long
-
IP
.

\marginline

[Proof]

Proposition 4.2
.

For any
\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
,
\textsc{batch-IP}(\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))\leq\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}(\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))
.

Also note that this result — and all in this section unless otherwise stated — holds for
all
item distributions (meeting the regularity conditions in
Section

2
), not just spherically symmetric ones.

Unfortunately, unlike
long
-
IP
, we show that there exists
no
fixed linear ranking rule that can perfectly satisfy
batch-IP
. This can be shown trivially via divisibility issues, but we prove a stronger impossibility requiring that in the constructed profile, all voter type entitlements are integral:

\marginline

[Proof]

Theorem 4.3

(
Impossibility of
batch-IP
)
.

For any batch size
m\geq 3
2
2

2

Note that for
m=2
, there is no profile satisfying the integral proportionality entitlement condition.
, there exists a profile
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in P_{n,d}
with
\alpha^{(i)}\binom{m}{2}\in\mathbb{Z}

\forall i\in[n]
such that, for any
\theta_{f}
and any
\mathcal{D}
,
\textsc{batch-IP}(\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))<1.

We prove this result in
Section

E.3
via a similar intuition as used above for
Example

E.1
: we show that on batches
X
that are
nearly
collinear (and thus occur with nonzero probability), at least one voter type receives
\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))=0
. We then upper-bound this type’s individual proportionality level in all other batches, demonstrating the bound.

Remark 4.4
.

This impossibility is universal only over fixed linear ranking rules: the Proportional Sequential Borda voting rule introduced by
Lederer 2025
is guaranteed to achieve
\textsc{batch-IP}=1
on all profiles with integer entitlements.

The bad news is that the above remark concretely establishes a proportionality cost of restricting to fixed linear ranking rules. The good news, which we show next, is that this cost is surprisingly small and diminishes quickly in the batch size
m
. Intuitively, the
batch-IP
improves in
m
because as
m
grows, the likelihood that all
x\in X
are collinear decreases, causing
batch-IP
to approach
long
-
IP
. The full proof with explicit constants is in
Section

E.4
.

\marginline

[Proof]

Theorem 4.5

(
Main Result
)
.

For all
\theta_{f},m,\mathcal{D}
, and
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in P_{n,d}
,

\textstyle\textsc{batch-IP}(\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))\geq\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}(\theta_{f},\mathcal{D}^{m},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))-\frac{1}{\alpha_{min}}\cdot O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\min\{\log(n),d\log(m/d)\}}{m}}\right).

Proof Sketch.

The fundamental goal in this proof is to show that
\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))
concentrates around its expectation over
X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}
as
m
grows, in which case by definition
batch-IP
converges to
long
-
IP
. We do this by first rewriting
i
’s IP level as

\textstyle\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))=\frac{1}{\alpha^{(i)}\binom{m}{2}}\sum_{1\leq j<\ell\leq m}\mathbb{I}\left(\text{sign}(\langle\theta^{(i)},x_{j}-x_{\ell}\rangle)=\text{sign}(\langle\theta_{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),x_{j}-x_{\ell}\rangle)\right),

the intuition being that agreement between
\theta^{(i)}
and
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
on a pairwise comparison of items
x_{j},x_{\ell}
just comes down to whether the two scoring vectors have the same signed dot product with the item difference vector. This is now the sum of indicators, which is ripe for a concentration bound except that these indicators have a particular kind of dependence: while the
x_{j}
are individually independent, their
differences
are not. To handle this dependence, we reframe individual proportionality level as a U-statistic: we rewrite this sum as the average of
m!
sums of indicators acting on
\lfloor m/2\rfloor
mutually exclusive pairs of items, which lets us show that the sum is sub-Gaussian with variance inversely proportional to
\lfloor m/2\rfloor
. We use this setup to get both convergence rates in the minimum: The
\log(n)
rate comes from taking the maximum over the
n
sub-Gaussian random variables corresponding to
\textsc{IP}_{i}
for each
i\in[n]
, and the
d
-dependent bound is via the Rademacher complexity, the intuition being that in low dimensions, even an arbitrary number of voters must be “close.”
∎

As a byproduct of this proof, we are also able to give tail bounds showing that each
i
’s IP level is close to its expectation with high probability (
Corollary

E.5
). We can additionally apply this bound to show that
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
converges to the optimal
batch-IP
as
m
grows, noting that by
Proposition

4.2
,
batch-IP
of 1 is the best we can hope for.

Corollary 4.6
.

For spherically symmetric
\mathcal{D}_{\circ}
,
Theorem

3.1
gives that
\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}},\mathcal{D}^{m}_{\circ},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))=1
;
Theorem

4.5
then immediately implies that

\textsc{batch-IP}(\theta_{\mathrm{ang}},\mathcal{D}^{m}_{\circ},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))\xrightarrow{m\to\infty}1\quad\text{{ (with a known convergence rate for finite $m$)}}.

6
Discussion

Zooming out, there are many ways to collectively decide a fixed linear ranking rule: one could pool raw elicitation data to fit a collective model, as in alignment; learn noisy
\hat{\theta}^{(i)}
that are correct up to rankings, and then simulate a vote, as in virtual democracy; directly aggregate noisy
\hat{\theta}^{(i)}
with robust methods; or collect enough individual data that more precise
\theta^{(i)}
can be learned and aggregated, as we study here. Given our finding that there exists a linear ranking rule
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
that achieves
long
-
IP
and quickly converges in
m
to achieving
batch-IP
, a natural next question is how well linear ranking rules learned through each of these less information-rich regimes can approximate these notions of proportionality. Many applications may also merit extending these ideas to nonlinear fixed rules and downstream decision tasks other than ranking.

General-Use Appendix Results

In this portion of the Appendix, we put examples and small technical results that we reference throughout the remainder of the Appendix.

A.1
Tied Scores

Proposition A.1
.

(Score ties occur with probability 0)
For any item distribution
\mathcal{D}
, finite
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
, and finite set of scoring vectors
V

\Pr_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left(\exists i,j\in[m],i\neq j\text{ and }\theta\in V\colon s_{\theta}(x_{i})=s_{\theta}(x_{j})\right)=0

Proof.

We can union bound the expression and get the following:

\displaystyle\Pr_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left(\exists i,j\in[m],i\neq j\text{ and }\theta\in V\colon s_{\theta}(x_{i})=s_{\theta}(x_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\sum_{\theta\in V}\Pr_{x_{i},x_{j}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}}(s_{\theta}(x_{i})=s_{\theta}(x_{j}))

As the total number of terms,
\binom{m}{2}\cdot|V|
, is finite by our specification of finite
m,|V|
, it suffices to show that for any
\theta\in V
,
\Pr_{x,y\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}}(s_{\theta}(x)=s_{\theta}(y))=0
. Equivalently,
\Pr_{x_{i},x_{j}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}}(\langle\theta,x_{i}\rangle=\langle\theta,x_{j}\rangle)=0
. Fix some
\theta\in V
. By conditioning on a specific realization of
x_{j}
, we can rewrite our expression as

\Pr_{x_{i},x_{j}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}}(\langle\theta,x_{i}\rangle=\langle\theta,x_{j}\rangle)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Pr_{x_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}}(\langle\theta,x_{i}\rangle=\langle\theta,c\rangle|x_{j}=c)f_{\mathcal{D}}(c)dc

Now observe that for
\langle\theta,x_{i}\rangle
to be equal to
\langle\theta,c\rangle
for some fixed
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it must be that
x_{i}
is in a specific hyperplane
H_{\theta,c}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\langle\theta,z\rangle=\langle\theta,c\rangle\}
.
H_{\theta,c}
an affine subspace of dimension
d-1
, and hence has
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure 0:
\lambda^{d}(H_{\theta,c})=0
. The item distribution
\mathcal{D}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, which tells us that sets with Lebesgue measure 0 have probability measure 0. Putting it all together

\Pr_{x_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}}(\langle\theta,x_{i}\rangle=\langle\theta,c\rangle|y=c)=\Pr_{x_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}}(x_{i}\in H_{\theta,c})=0

Plugging back into our integral, we get that

Appendix B
Extended Related Work

We discuss related work on incorporating public input into the design of decision rules. Existing work differs mainly along three dimensions. First, the elicited input may take the form of a scoring vector for each voter, or of ordinal preference information such as pairwise comparisons or rankings. Second, the aggregation procedure may output a single collective scoring vector that is applied repeatedly, or it may aggregate preferences separately for each decision instance, thereby allowing decisions that are not necessarily induced by the same rule across rounds. Third, the downstream decision task may be to rank or to select candidates.

Virtual democracy and participatory design.

The most closely related literature is
virtual democracy
, a
paradigm that uses models of each voter’s preferences to make
collective decisions on their behalf at decision time

Kahng et al. 2019
.
Closest to our work,

Noothigattu et al. 2018
and
Feffer et al. 2023

study the problem of aggregating individual scoring vectors into a
collective one.

Freedman et al. 2020
take an alternative approach: they learn
a collective scoring vector directly by pooling pairwise comparisons
across voters before fitting a single linear model.

Kahng et al. 2019
and
Lee et al. 2019
pursue
a third paradigm in which, at every decision instance, each voter’s
preferences over the current alternatives are inferred from a learned
model and aggregated via a single-winner voting rule, producing decisions
that need not be consistent across instances.

Aggregating scoring vectors in social choice.

Aggregating voters’ scoring vectors into a single collective vector
has been studied extensively from a different perspective in divisible
participatory budgeting, also known as portioning, where each voter
proposes a budget split over projects

[
Elkind et al. 2026
,
Freeman et al. 2021
,
de Berg et al. 2024
,
Caragiannis et al. 2024
,
Brandt et al. 2024
,
Freeman and Schmidt-Kraepelin 2024
]
.
In this literature, the arithmetic mean is the canonical proportional
aggregator, and the central concern is its lack of strategyproofness.
In contrast to our work, however, the downstream task is a one-shot
cardinal task rather than a repeated ordinal one; under the
proportionality notion we study, the arithmetic mean no longer retains
the same guarantees.

Proportionality in repeated decision making.

A separate line of work studies individual and group-level proportional
fairness in repeated decision making

[
Lackner 2020
,
Kozachinskiy et al. 2025
,
Chandak et al. 2024
,
Bulteau et al. 2021
,
Lackner and Maly 2023
,
Phillips et al. 2025
]
.
These works typically allow the use of a different decision rule at
each decision step, and often retain memory across rounds to balance
representation over time. Moreover, the downstream task is usually to
select one or several candidates rather than to output a ranking induced
by a fixed scoring vector.

Proportional rank aggregation.

Individual and group-level proportionality in rank aggregation, i.e.,
the task of aggregating a given set of rankings into a single collective
one, has recently been studied by
Lederer et al. 2024
and

Lederer 2025
. In particular,

Lederer 2025
introduces the Proportional
Sequential Borda (PSB) rule, which guarantees that any

\alpha
-fraction of the electorate agrees with roughly an

\alpha
-fraction of the pairwise comparisons in the collective
ranking. PSB can be applied to our setting per batch, but the
resulting decisions are neither linear nor consistent across batches.
We benchmark against PSB empirically in
Section

5
.

RLHF and alignment from a social-choice perspective.

In reinforcement learning from human feedback, the reward model plays
the role of a typically nonlinear scoring rule, and standard practice
pools annotator data before fitting

[
Christiano et al. 2017
,
Ouyang et al. 2022
,
Rafailov et al. 2023
]
.
A growing line of work studies alignment as a collective
decision-making problem

[
Ge et al. 2024
,
Hollender and Kraiczy 2026
,
Siththaranjan et al. 2024
,
Procaccia et al. 2025
,
Maura-Rivero et al. 2025
,
Sorensen et al. 2024
,
Conitzer et al. 2024
]
.
Closest to us,
Ge et al. 2024
give an axiomatic
analysis of rules that aggregate individual rankings into a collective
weight vector under a linear functional form for the reward model;
their axioms, however, target majoritarian and efficiency properties
rather than proportionality.

Shirali et al. 2025
argue that the arithmetic mean of per-type
reward functions is a principled aggregator when the downstream task
uses cardinal scores. Our angular mean offers a viable alternative in
the ordinal regime, and can also be applied to combine the linear heads
of multi-objective reward models that capture diverse user preferences
or objectives

[
Wang et al. 2024a
,
Wang et al. 2024b
]
.

d_{i}(\theta)=d_{\measuredangle}(\theta,\theta^{(i)})
is differentiable everywhere other than
-\theta^{(i)}
, where only the directional derivatives exist. So suppose that
\theta^{*}
is antipodal to some of the
\theta^{(i)}
. Let
I=\{i\in[n]:\theta^{(i)}=-\theta^{*}\}
be the set of indices of these antipodal
\theta^{(i)}
.

Let
x:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to G_{i}
be a unit-speed parametrization of
G_{i}
such that
x(0)=\theta^{*}
. We will decompose
F
into a singular and non-singular part as
F(\theta)=F_{\text{sin}}(\theta)+F_{\text{diff}}(\theta)
, with

F_{\text{sin}}=\sum_{i\in I}\alpha^{(i)}d_{\measuredangle}(\theta,\theta^{(i)})\quad\text{ and }\quad F_{\text{diff}}=\sum_{i\notin I}\alpha^{(i)}d_{\measuredangle}(\theta,\theta^{(i)})

The right-sided derivative of
F(x(\varphi))
at
0
is then given by

\left.\frac{d}{d\varphi}F(x(\varphi))\right|_{\varphi=0^{+}}=\left(\left.\frac{d}{d\varphi}F_{\text{sin}}(x(\varphi))\right|_{\varphi=0^{+}}\right)+F_{\text{diff}}(x(\varphi))^{\prime}=F_{\text{sin}}^{+}+F_{\text{diff}}(x(\varphi))^{\prime},

while the left-sided derivative is given by

\left.\frac{d}{d\varphi}F(x(\varphi))\right|_{\varphi=0^{-}}=\left(\left.\frac{d}{d\varphi}F_{\text{sin}}(x(\varphi))\right|_{\varphi=0^{-}}\right)+F_{\text{diff}}(x(\varphi))^{\prime}=F_{\text{sin}}^{-}+F_{\text{diff}}(x(\varphi))^{\prime}.

Now, because
\theta^{(i)}
is antipodal to
\theta^{*}=x(0)
for all
i\in I
, we get that
F_{\text{sin}}(\varphi)
=
F_{\text{sin}}(-\varphi)
and hence the left and right-sided derivative of
F_{\text{sin}}(x(\varphi))
at
0
are identical up to their sign, i.e.
F_{\text{sin}}^{+}=-F_{\text{sin}}^{-}
. In particular
F_{\text{sin}}^{+}<0
and
F_{\text{sin}}^{-}>0
, as
F_{\text{sin}}
only decreases when moving away from
x(0)
.
x(0)
can only be a minimum when the right-side derivative non-negative and the left-sided derivative is non-positive, so

\displaystyle 0\leq F_{\text{sin}}^{+}+F_{\text{diff}}(x(0))^{\prime}

\displaystyle\implies-F_{\text{diff}}(x(0))^{\prime}\leq F_{\text{sin}}^{+}

\displaystyle 0\geq F_{\text{sin}}^{-}+F_{\text{diff}}(x(0))^{\prime}

\displaystyle\implies F_{\text{diff}}(x(0))^{\prime}\leq-F_{\text{sin}}^{-}

Combining these two inequalities then yields

0<F_{\text{sin}}^{-}\leq F_{\text{diff}}(x(0))^{\prime}\leq-F_{\text{sin}}^{-}<0,

a contradiction. Therefore
\theta^{*}
cannot be antipodal to
\theta^{(i)}
.

∎

Next, we will establish how we upper-bound spherical distances in the proof. Let us give a quick introduction to the aforementioned Toponogov’s Inequality. Intuitively it states that spherical triangles are "tighter" than flat euclidean triangles, because the edges can curve towards each other.
More formally, we compare a spherical triangle and a euclidean triangle that are both constructed using the same two side lengths and one angle. Toponogov’s Inequality then states that the last side of the spherical triangle is shorter than that of the euclidean triangle.

Theorem D.3
.

[
Toponogov 1964
,
Meyer 1989
]
Let
M
be an
m
-dimensional Riemannian manifold with sectional curvature
K
satisfying
K\geq\delta
. Let
pqr
be a geodesic triangle, i.e. a triangle whose sides are geodesics, in
M
, such that the geodesic
pq
is minimal and if
\delta>0
, the length of the side
pr
is less than
\pi/\sqrt{\delta}
. Let
p^{\prime}q^{\prime}r^{\prime}
be a geodesic triangle in the model space
M_{\delta}
, i.e. the simply connected space of constant curvature
\delta
, such that the lengths of sides
p^{\prime}q^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime}r^{\prime}
are equal to that of
pq
and
pr
respectively and the angle at
p^{\prime}
is equal to that at
p
. Then

d(q,r)\leq d(q^{\prime},r^{\prime}).

We can combine this theorem with the euclidean law of cosines to bound the side lengths of spherical triangles.

Corollary D.4
.

Consider three points
p,q,r\in S^{n}
forming a geodesic spherical triangle. Then

d_{\measuredangle}(q,r)^{2}\leq d_{\measuredangle}(p,q)^{2}+d_{\measuredangle}(p,r)^{2}-2d_{\measuredangle}(p,q)d_{\measuredangle}(p,r)\cdot\cos(\gamma),

where
\gamma
is the angle between
\overline{pq}
, and
\overline{pr}
at
p
.

Proof.

We use the law of cosines in conjunction with Toponogov’s theorem. First, note that the sectional curvature
K
of the unit sphere is constantly equal to
1
.
Applying Toponogov’s theorem with lower curvature bound
\delta=1
, we obtain
d(q,r)\leq d(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
, where
p^{\prime}q^{\prime}r^{\prime}
is the comparison triangle in the model space
M_{1}=S^{n}
with the same two side lengths and included angle
\gamma
at
p^{\prime}
. Applying the Euclidean law of cosines to this Euclidean triangle then yields

d(q,r)^{2}\leq d(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})^{2}=d(p,q)^{2}+d(p,r)^{2}-2d(p,q)d(p,r)\cos(\gamma).

∎

The specific bound we use in our main proof is related, but slightly stronger. The distance bound should also apply in case we do not consider strictly geodesic triangles. The precise formulation is as follows.

Lemma D.5
.

Let
\theta^{*},\theta^{(j)}\in S^{d}
with spherical distance
l_{j}=d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{*},\theta^{(j)})
. Let
x:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to S^{d}
be a unit-speed geodesic with
x(0)=\theta^{*}
, and let
\gamma_{j}
be the angle between the geodesic
\overline{\theta^{*}\theta^{(j)}}
and the tangent
x^{\prime}(0)
in the positive direction of
x
.

For any
\varphi\in[-2\pi,0]
,
x_{j}(\varphi)^{2}=d_{\measuredangle}(x(\varphi),\theta^{(j)})^{2}
satisfies

x_{j}(\varphi)^{2}\leq l_{j}^{2}+\varphi^{2}-2l_{j}\varphi\cos(\gamma_{j}).

Proof.

Since
\varphi\leq 0
,
\varphi
can be seen to travel backward when compared to the forward facing tangent
x^{\prime}(0)
.
We consider two cases for
\varphi
:

Case 1:
\varphi\in(-\pi,0]
.

In this case the geodesic
\overline{\theta^{*}x(\varphi)}
runs directly opposite to
x^{\prime}(0)
, so the angle between the geodesics
\overline{x(\varphi)\theta^{*}}
and
\overline{\theta^{*}\theta^{(j)}}
is equal to
\pi-\gamma_{j}
. The lengths of these geodesics are
d_{\measuredangle}(x(\varphi),\theta^{*})=|\varphi|=-\varphi
and
d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{*},\theta^{(j)})=l_{j}
, so by applying
Corollary

D.4
to these two geodesics with the angle
\pi-\gamma_{j}
we obtain

\displaystyle x_{j}(\varphi^{2})

\displaystyle\leq l_{j}^{2}+(-\varphi)^{2}-2l_{j}(-\varphi)\cos(\pi-\gamma_{j})

\displaystyle=l_{j}^{2}+\varphi^{2}-2l_{j}(-\varphi)(-\cos(\gamma_{j}))

\displaystyle=l_{j}^{2}+\varphi^{2}-2l_{j}\varphi\cos(\gamma_{j}),

which is exactly the desired bound for
\varphi\in[-\pi,0)
.

Case 2:
\varphi\in(-\pi,-2\pi]
.

Here, we employ
Corollary

D.4
for a different triangle. We replace the vertex
\theta^{*}
by
-\theta^{*}
when compared to the spherical triangle in case 1. So we will consider the two geodesics
\overline{-\theta^{*}x(\varphi)}
and
\overline{-\theta^{*}\theta^{(j)}}
. Note that the angle between them is also
\gamma_{j}
, because it sits at the unique other intersection between the great circle parametrized by
x
and the geodesic
\overline{\theta^{*},\theta^{(j)}}
.
The lengths are then
d_{\measuredangle}(-\theta^{*},x(\varphi))=-\varphi-\pi
and
d_{\measuredangle}(-\theta^{*},\theta^{(j)})=\pi-l_{j}
. Applying
Corollary

D.4

\displaystyle x_{j}(\varphi^{2})

\displaystyle\leq(\pi-l_{j})^{2}+(-\varphi-\pi)^{2}-2(\pi-l_{j})(-\varphi-\pi)\cos(\gamma_{j})

\displaystyle\leq(\pi-l_{j})^{2}+(-\varphi-\pi)^{2}+2(\pi-l_{j})(\varphi+\pi)\cos(\gamma_{j})

\displaystyle=(\underbrace{\pi^{2}-2\pi l_{j}}_{(B)}+\underbrace{l_{j}^{2}}_{(A)})+(\underbrace{\varphi^{2}}_{(A)}+\underbrace{2\pi\varphi+\pi^{2}}_{(B)})+2(\underbrace{\pi^{2}-l_{j}\pi+\varphi\pi}_{(C)}\underbrace{-l_{j}\varphi}_{(A)})\cos(\gamma_{j})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{l_{j}^{2}+\varphi^{2}-2l_{j}\varphi\cos(\gamma_{j})}_{(A)}+\underbrace{2\pi^{2}-2\pi l_{j}+2\pi\varphi}_{(B)}+2(\underbrace{pi^{2}-l_{j}\pi+\varphi\pi)}_{(C)}\cos(\gamma_{j})

\displaystyle=(l_{j}^{2}+\varphi^{2}-2l_{j}\varphi\cos(\gamma_{j}))+2\pi(\pi-l_{j}+\varphi+\cos(\gamma_{j})(\pi-l_{j}+\varphi))

The expression in the first parentheses is what we want to obtain as an upper bound, so the statement is proven, if we can show the remainder is at most
0
. We can rewrite this remainder via

2\pi(\pi-l_{j}+\varphi+\cos(\gamma_{j})(\pi-l_{j}+\varphi))=2\pi(\pi-l_{j}+\varphi)(\cos(\gamma_{j})+1).

Note that
\cos(\gamma_{j})+1\geq 0
, so we only need to certify that
\pi-l_{j}+\varphi\leq 0
. This is true, since
\varphi<-\pi
and
l_{j}\geq 0
by definition.
∎

Having collected all the ingredients we can now get into the full proof of
Lemma

D.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

D.1
.

Let
\theta^{*}
be a global minimizer of
F_{|G_{i}}
.
Because
\theta^{(i)}\in G_{i}
, we can define a unit-speed parametrization
x:\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\to G_{i}
of
G_{i}
such that
x(0)=\theta^{*}
and there is a unique
l_{i}\in[0,\pi]
such that
x(l_{i})=\theta^{(i)}
. Since
G_{i}
is a geodesic, for
\varphi
in a neighborhood of
0
we have
d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{*},x(\varphi))=|\varphi|
, and in particular
d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{*},\theta^{(i)})=l_{i}
.

To reason about the minima of
F(x(\varphi))
, we first want to analyze the derivative of
F(x(\varphi))
at
0
. By
Lemma

D.2
, there is no point antipodal to
\theta^{*}
, hence the derivative of
F(x(\varphi))
at
0
is well defined.
If we write
l_{j}\coloneq d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{*},\theta^{(j)})
and define
x_{j}(\varphi)=d_{\measuredangle}(x(\varphi),\theta^{(j)})
, then the spherical law of cosines states that

\cos(x_{j}(\varphi))=\cos(l_{j})\cos(\varphi)+\sin(l_{j})\sin(\varphi)\cos(\gamma_{j}),

where
\gamma_{j}
is the angle between the two geodesics
\overline{\theta^{*}\theta^{(j)}}
and
\overline{\theta^{*}x(\varphi)}
. Taking the derivative on both sides gives

\displaystyle-\sin(x_{j}(\varphi))\cdot x_{j}(\varphi)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\cos(l_{j})\sin(\varphi)+\sin(l_{j})\cos(\varphi)\cos(\gamma_{j})

\displaystyle\implies x_{j}(\varphi)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos(l_{j})\sin(\varphi)-\sin(l_{j})\cos(\varphi)\cos(\gamma_{j})}{\sin(x_{j}(\varphi))}.

Since
l_{j}=x_{j}(0)
by definition, plugging in
\varphi=0
yields
x_{j}^{\prime}(0)=-\cos(\gamma_{j})
. Furthermore, because
\theta^{*}=x(0)
is a global optimum of
F
along
G_{i}
, first-order optimality implies

0=\frac{1}{2}F^{\prime}(x(0))=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{(j)}x_{j}(0)x_{j}^{\prime}(0)=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{(j)}l_{j}\cos(\gamma_{j}),

and hence

0=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{(j)}l_{j}\cos(\gamma_{j})=\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha^{(j)}l_{j}\cos(\gamma_{j})+\alpha^{(i)}l_{i},

where the last equality follows from
\gamma_{i}=0
so
\cos(\gamma_{i})=1
.

In addition, if we apply
Lemma

D.5
to
\theta^{*}
,
x(\varphi)
, and
\theta^{(j)}
then we obtain

x_{j}(\varphi)^{2}\leq l_{j}^{2}+\varphi^{2}-2l_{j}\varphi\cos(\gamma_{j}).

For
x_{i}(\varphi)
specifically, we know that both
\theta^{(i)}
and
x(\varphi)
are on the circle
G_{i}
, thus the distance is given by
x_{i}(\varphi)=d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},x(\varphi))=\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|(l_{i}-\varphi-2k\pi)|
. Thus the squared distance
x_{i}(\varphi)^{2}
can be bounded by

x_{i}(\varphi)^{2}=\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(l_{i}-\varphi-2k\pi)^{2}\leq(l_{i}-\varphi-2\pi)^{2}.

With all these bounds established, we will now derive a contradiction. Comparing the objective value of
F(x(0))
to that of another proposed solution
F(x(\varphi))
results in

\displaystyle F(x(\varphi))-F(x(0))

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha^{(j)}x_{j}(\varphi)^{2}+\alpha^{(i)}x_{i}(\varphi)^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{(j)}l_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha^{(j)}\left(l_{j}^{2}+\varphi^{2}-2l_{j}\varphi\cos(\gamma_{j})\right)+\alpha^{(i)}(l_{i}-\varphi-2\pi)^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{(j)}l_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha^{(j)}\Bigl(\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}l_{j}^{2}}_{(A)}+\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\varphi^{2}}_{(B)}-\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}2l_{j}\varphi\cos(\gamma_{j})}_{(C)}\Bigr)+\alpha^{(i)}\Bigl(\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}l_{i}^{2}}_{(A)}+\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\varphi^{2}}_{(B)}+\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}4\pi^{2}}_{(D)}-\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}2l_{i}\varphi}_{(C)}-\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}4\pi l_{i}+4\pi\varphi}_{(D)}\Bigr)-\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{(j)}l_{j}^{2}}_{(A)}

\displaystyle\quad=\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{(j)}\left(l_{j}^{2}-l_{j}^{2}\right)}_{(A)}+\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\varphi^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{(j)}}_{(B)}-2\varphi\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}\alpha^{(j)}l_{j}\cos(\gamma_{j})+\alpha^{(i)}l_{i}\right)}_{(C)}+\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\alpha^{(i)}\left(4\pi^{2}+4\pi\varphi-4\pi l_{i}\right)}_{(D)}

Using
l_{j}^{2}-l_{j}^{2}=0
,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha^{(j)}=1
, and the local optimality condition
\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha^{(j)}l_{j}\cos(\gamma_{j})+\alpha^{(i)}l_{i}=0
, this reduces to

F(x(\varphi))-F(x(0))=\varphi^{2}+\alpha^{(i)}4\pi(\pi+\varphi-l_{i}).

By using the assumption that
l_{i}>(1-\alpha^{(i)})\pi
and setting
\varphi=-2\pi\alpha^{(i)}
, we then get

\displaystyle F(x(\varphi))-F(x(0))

\displaystyle=\varphi^{2}+\alpha^{(i)}4\pi(\pi+\varphi-l_{i})

\displaystyle<4\pi^{2}(\alpha^{(i)})^{2}+\alpha^{(i)}4\pi(\pi-2\pi\alpha^{(i)}-(1-\alpha^{(i)})\pi)

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}(\alpha^{(i)})^{2}+4\pi\alpha^{(i)}(-\alpha^{(i)}\pi)

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}(\alpha^{(i)})^{2}-4\pi^{2}(\alpha^{(i)})^{2}=0.

But this means that
F(x(\varphi))<F(x(0))=F(\theta^{*})
, thus contradicting our initial assumption that
\theta^{*}
minimizes
F
on
G_{i}
.
∎

Approximation

D.4
Squared Kemeny Objective

Chandak et al. 2024
present the Squared Kemeny rule as a means to obtaining a proportional ranking. Their setting is different — they receive voter rankings on every batch, and directly aggregate the rankings themselves. That is, given a profile
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
, and batch
X\in\mathcal{X}^{m}
, their rule would output
\succ_{SqK}
as follows

\succ_{\mathrm{sqK}}^{X}=\argmin_{\succ^{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}\cdot\text{KT-dist}(\succ^{X},\succ_{\theta^{(i)}}^{X})^{2}

where KT-dist
(\succ^{X},\succ_{\theta^{(i)}}^{X})=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq m}\mathbb{I}((x_{j}\succ x_{k}\land x_{k}\succ_{\theta^{(i)}}^{X}x_{j})\lor(x_{k}\succ x_{j}\land x_{j}\succ_{\theta^{(i)}}^{X}x_{k}))
is the Kendall-Tau distance between
\succ^{X}
and
\succ_{\theta^{(i)}}^{X}
.

As we are looking to optimize for a similar notion of proportionality, what if we select our linear scoring rule based off of minimizing the expected Squared Kemeny objective?

If we optimize for the Squared Kemeny objective on a known item distribution
\mathcal{D}
, we have

\theta_{\mathrm{sqK}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\coloneqq\argmin_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}\left(\binom{m}{2}-a_{KT}(\theta^{(i)},\theta,X)\right)^{2}\right]

Interestingly, we find that on spherically symmetric item distributions,
\theta_{\mathrm{sqK}}
approaches
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
for sufficiently large batch sizes.

Theorem D.6
.

For spherically symmetric
\mathcal{D}_{\circ}^{m}
, we have that

\theta_{\mathrm{sqK}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\argmin_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\Theta(m^{2})\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)+\Theta(m^{3})c(\theta)+\Theta(m^{4})\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)^{2}\right)

where
c(\theta)
is a value that does not depend on
m
and is upper bounded by
\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)
. Importantly this means that as
m
increases,
\theta_{\mathrm{sqK}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\rightarrow\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
.

Proof.

We simply rewrite the objective that
\theta_{\mathrm{sqK}}
minimizes. All expectations are with respect to
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{\circ}^{m}
. First for any
i\in[n]
, and
j,k\in[m]
such that
j<k
, let
D_{jk}
be the event that
\theta^{(i)}
and
\theta
disagree on the ranking of
x_{j}
and
x_{k}
, i.e.
D_{jk}=\mathbb{I}\{(x_{j}\succ_{\theta^{(i)}}^{X}x_{k}\land x_{k}\succ_{\theta}^{X}x_{j})\lor(x_{k}\succ_{\theta^{(i)}}^{X}x_{j}\land x_{j}\succ_{\theta}^{X}x_{x})\}
. Then observe that for any
X\in\mathcal{X}^{m}
,

\binom{m}{2}-a_{KT}(\theta^{(i)},\theta,X)=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq m}D_{jk}

Substituting this into our objective, we have that
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}\left(\binom{m}{2}-a_{KT}(\theta^{(i)},\theta,X)\right)^{2}\right]
:

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}\left(\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq m}D_{jk}\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha^{(i)}\left(\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq m}D_{jk}\right)^{2}\right]

When we expand this squared term, there are 3 different types of terms: those in which there is an indicator variable squared, those in which the two indicator variables being multiplied together share exactly one index, and those in which the indicator variables refer to disjoint sets of items. Informally we can write this in the following way:

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j,k}D_{jk}^{2}+\sum_{j,k,\ell}D_{jk}D_{k\ell}+\sum_{j,k,\ell,p}D_{jk}D_{\ell p}\right]

How many of each term is there? There are
\binom{m}{2}
of the first type, and then
\binom{m}{3}\cdot 3\cdot 2
of the second type (picking three distinct indices, picking which is repeated, and picking which set of two corresponds to the first indicator), and
\binom{m}{4}\cdot\binom{4}{2}
of the last type (picking four distinct indices, and picking which correspond to the first indicator). As the
x_{i}
are drawn iid, the indicators do not depend on the specific choice of indices (e.g.
D_{jk}
is the same for any choice of
j\neq k
). So by linearity of expectation, for some choice of distinct
j,k,\ell,p
:

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}\left(\binom{m}{2}\mathbb{E}[D_{jk}^{2}]+6\binom{m}{3}\mathbb{E}[D_{jk}D_{k\ell}]+6\binom{m}{4}\mathbb{E}[D_{jk}D_{\ell p}]\right)

Observe that as
D_{jk}
is an indicator variable,
\mathbb{E}[D_{jk}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[D_{jk}]
. This is just
1-\{
the probability that
\theta^{(i)}
and
\theta
agree on a pairwise ranking
\}
, so
1-\frac{\pi-d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)}{\pi}
by
Proposition

2.5
. Hence
\mathbb{E}[D_{jk}]=\frac{d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)}{\pi}
. Additionally,
D_{jk}
is independent of
A_{\ell p}
when
\{j,k\}
and
\{\ell,p\}
are disjoint, so
\mathbb{E}[A_{jk}A_{\ell p}]=\mathbb{E}[D_{jk}]\mathbb{E}[D_{\ell p}]=\frac{d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)^{2}}{\pi^{2}}
. So we have that

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}\binom{m}{2}\frac{d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)}{\pi}\right)+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}6\binom{m}{3}\mathbb{E}[D_{jk}D_{k\ell}]\right)+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}6\binom{m}{4}\frac{d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\right)

Let
c(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}\mathbb{E}[D_{jk}D_{k\ell}]
and substitute in to get the final expression:

\displaystyle=\binom{m}{2}\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)\right)+6\binom{m}{3}c(\theta)+\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\binom{m}{4}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)^{2}\right)

Note that for
m=2
, we have that
\theta_{\mathrm{sqK}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\argmin_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)
. Let
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
be the Antipodal Construction instantiated with
\alpha^{(1)}>0.5
and
\alpha^{(2)}=1-\alpha^{(1)}<0.5
. Minimizing the weighted sum of
unsquared
angular distances recovers exactly the majority vector (in this case
\theta^{(1)}
). Hence,
\theta_{\mathrm{sqK}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
gives voter type 2 individual proportionality level of 0.

However, for large
m
, the final term dominates, and minimizing this objective is equivalent to finding
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
. We know then that in the regime of large
m
,
\theta_{\mathrm{sqK}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\approx\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
. By
Proposition

D.7
, this tells us that
\theta_{\mathrm{sqK}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
inherits the good proportionality guarantees of
\theta_{\mathrm{ang}}
for spherically symmetrical item distributions.

Interestingly, this points to a potential “phase shift” where minimizing the expected Squared Kemeny for small batch sizes yields an overly majoritarian scoring vector, but doing so for large batch sizes yields a vector that gives strong proportionality guarantees.

∎

D.5
Robustness

Proposition D.7
.

For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, any spherically symmetric batch distribution
D_{\circ}^{m}
, any profile
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
,
\theta,\psi\in S^{d}
such that
d_{\measuredangle}(\theta,\psi)\leq\gamma
,

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}_{\circ}^{m}}\left[\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta,X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim D_{\circ}^{m}}[\textsc{IP}_{i}(\psi,X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))]-\gamma/(\pi\alpha^{(i)})\qquad\forall i\in[n].

Proof.

By using the identity established in
Lemma

3.2
and the reverse triangle inequality we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta,X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\frac{a_{KT}(\theta^{(i)},\theta,X)}{\alpha^{(i)}\binom{m}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha^{(i)}\binom{m}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[a_{KT}(\theta^{(i)},\theta,X)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha^{(i)}\binom{m}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\frac{\pi-d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\theta)}{\pi}\cdot\binom{m}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\alpha^{(i)}\binom{m}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\frac{\pi-d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\psi)-\gamma}{\pi}\cdot\binom{m}{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha^{(i)}\binom{m}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\frac{\pi-d_{\measuredangle}(\theta^{(i)},\psi)}{\pi}\cdot\binom{m}{2}\right]-\frac{\gamma}{\pi\alpha^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[\textsc{IP}_{i}(\psi,X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))]-\frac{\gamma}{\pi\alpha^{(i)}}.

∎

First, define a “deviation quantity” for voter type

W_{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),X)\right]-\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),X).

The

\min_{i\in[n]}\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\mathcal{D})
in terms of expected normalized individual agreement minus this deviation term as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{i\in[n]}\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),X)

\displaystyle=\min_{i\in[n]}\left(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),X)]-W_{i}\right)

Taking the expectation of both sides, applying linearity of expectation, and observing that
\mathbb{E}[\min_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}[\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),X)]]=\min_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}[\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),X)]

\displaystyle\textsc{batch-IP}(\theta_{f},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\mathcal{D}^{m})

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[\max_{i\in[n]}W_{i}]
. We show two different upper bounds, one that has a dependence on
n
and the other that has a dependence on
d
, which results in the minimum term in the final expression.

Both upper bounds rely on reformulating normalized individual agreement as a U-statistic (with an addition
\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}

\textsc{IP}_{i}(\theta_{f},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),X)=\frac{a_{KT}(\theta^{(i)},\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))}{\alpha_{i}\binom{m}{2}}

As
\theta_{f}
and
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
are fixed, we omit the function inputs in the rest of this proof for concision, and just write
\textsc{IP}_{i}
to refer to the quantity above.
Define a kernel function
h_{\theta^{(i)}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
that takes in a difference vector (between two items) and indicates whether
\theta^{(i)}
and
\theta_{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
have the same sign of dot product with this difference vector

h_{\theta^{(i)}}(z)\coloneqq\mathbb{I}\{\text{sign}(\langle\theta^{(i)},z\rangle)=\text{sign}(\langle\theta_{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),z\rangle)\}

Recalling that agreement between scoring vectors on a pairwise comparison between items just comes down to whether the two scoring vectors have the same signed dot product with the item difference vector, we can rewrite the
\textsc{IP}_{i}

\textsc{IP}_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}\binom{m}{2}}\sum_{1\leq j<\ell\leq m}h_{\theta^{(i)}}(x_{j}-x_{\ell})

For all permutations
\sigma\in\Pi_{m}

V_{i,\sigma}=\frac{1}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\left(h_{\theta^{(i)}}(x_{\sigma(1)}-x_{\sigma(2)})+\dots+h_{\theta^{(i)}}(x_{\sigma(2\lfloor m/2\rfloor-1)}-x_{\sigma(2\lfloor m/2\rfloor)})\right)

Then we can rewrite
\textsc{IP}_{i}
again, this time as a function of the
V_{i,\sigma}

\textsc{IP}_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}\cdot m!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Pi_{m}}V_{i,\sigma}

The key observation is that every
V_{i,\sigma}
is the sum of
\lfloor m/2\rfloor

independent
variables, because each term is the application of the kernel function to a disjoint pair of items. Therefore, we can more easily apply standard results to show that the all of the
V_{i,\sigma}
concentrate around their expectation quickly, and translate that into bounds on the deviation of
\textsc{IP}_{i}
from its expectation (
W_{i}
).

Now the two upper bound methods diverge. For the first bound, resulting in a dependence on
n
, we use the reformulation of
\textsc{IP}_{i}
to show that the
W_{i}
are all sub-Gaussian in

E.2
. Then, we apply a known bound on the maximum of sub-Gaussian random variables in
Lemma

E.3
.

The second method, found in
Lemma

E.4
, bounds the maximum
W_{i}
by taking the supremum over the deviation quantity for
any
scoring vector in
S^{d-1}
and bounding this using Rademacher complexity. This bound supports the intuition that even if there are many different voter types, the diversity among scoring vectors is limited by the dimension. Scoring vectors that are close together in space have positively correlated deviation quantities — on any given batch, they are likely experience similar deviations from their expected individual proportionality.

Lemma E.2
.

For all
i\in[n]
,
W_{i}=\mathbb{E}[\textsc{IP}_{i}]-\textsc{IP}_{i}
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{4\lfloor m/2\rfloor\alpha_{i}^{2}}
.

Proof.

In order to show sub-Gaussianity, we want to show that
\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(W_{i}-\mathbb{E}[W_{i}])}]\leq e^{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}/2}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Observe that
\mathbb{E}[W_{i}]=0
.
To bound the MGF,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(W_{i}-\mathbb{E}[W_{i}])}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(\mathbb{E}[\textsc{IP}_{i}]-\textsc{IP}_{i})}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\lambda}{\alpha_{i}}\sum_{\sigma\in\Pi_{m}}\frac{1}{m!}(\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}[\textsc{IP}_{i}]-V_{i,\sigma})\right)\right]

By Jensen’s inequality:

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\sigma\in\Pi_{m}}\frac{1}{m!}\exp\left(\frac{\lambda}{\alpha_{i}}(\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}[\textsc{IP}_{i}]-V_{i,\sigma})\right)\right]

Then by linearity of expectation and the observation that all
V_{i,\sigma}
have the same distribution (so the statement holds for any permutation
\sigma
):

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\lambda}{\alpha_{i}}(\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}[\textsc{IP}_{i}]-V_{i,\sigma})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{-\lambda}{\alpha_{i}}(V_{i,\sigma}-\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}[\textsc{IP}_{i}])\right)\right]

Note that
\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}}[\textsc{IP}_{i}]=\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},x_{2}}[h_{\theta^{(i)}}(x_{1},x_{2})]=\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}}[V_{i,\sigma}]
. So the expression
V_{i,\sigma}-\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}[\textsc{IP}_{i}]
can be rewritten as
V_{i,\sigma}-\mathbb{E}[V_{i,\sigma}]
. Now we can see that the task has come down to bounding the MGF of
V_{i,\sigma}
. Recalling the definition of
V_{i,\sigma}
and plugging in yields:

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{-\lambda}{\alpha_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{h_{\theta^{(i)}}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)}-x_{\sigma(2j)})}{k}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[V_{i,\sigma}]}{k}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}\exp\left(\frac{-\lambda}{\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{h_{\theta^{(i)}}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)}-x_{\sigma(2j)})}{k}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[V_{i,\sigma}]}{k}\right)\right)\right]

Now we can utilize the independence of
h_{\theta^{(i)}}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)},x_{\sigma(2j)})
for different values of
j
. Recall that these are independent because each of the
\lfloor m/2\rfloor
terms are functions of disjoint subsets of the randomly drawn
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
.

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{-\lambda}{\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{h_{\theta^{(i)}}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)}-x_{\sigma(2j)})}{k}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[V_{i,\sigma}]}{k}\right)\right)\right]

Moving the factor of
1/k
outside and recalling that
\mathbb{E}[V_{i,\sigma}]=\mathbb{E}\left[h_{\theta^{(i)}}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)},x_{\sigma(2j)})\right]
gives us:

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{-\lambda}{\alpha_{i}k}\left(h_{\theta^{(i)}}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)}-x_{\sigma(2j)})-\mathbb{E}[V_{i,\sigma}]\right)\right)\right]

Then, we can apply Hoeffding’s lemma to each of these
k
terms, which states that for a random variable
X
that is bounded in
[a,b]
w.p. 1, we have that
\mathbb{E}[\exp(c(X-\mathbb{E}[X]))]\leq e^{c^{2}(b-a)^{2}/8}
for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Observe that
h_{\theta^{(i)}}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)}-x_{\sigma(2j)})\in[0,1]
, hence:

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}e^{\lambda^{2}/(8\alpha_{i}^{2}k^{2})}=e^{\lambda^{2}/(8\alpha_{i}^{2}k)}

Putting it altogether and plugging in for
k
, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(W_{i}-\mathbb{E}[W_{i}])}]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8\alpha_{i}^{2}\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}

So,
W_{i}
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{4\alpha_{i}^{2}\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
.
∎

Lemma E.3
.

We can bound the expected max fluctuation in individual proportionality as follows:

\mathbb{E}_{X}[\max_{i\in[n]}W_{i}]=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}\left(\mathbb{E}[\textsc{IP}_{i}]-\textsc{IP}_{i}\right)\right]\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{min}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(n)}{2\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}

Proof.

In combination with our result showing that the individual
W_{i}
are sub-Gaussian in
Lemma

E.2
, we can directly apply a known bound on the maximum of (not necessarily independent) sub-Gaussian RVs (e.g.
Rigollet and Hütter 2023
Thm 1.14). We can upper bound the variance parameter of all of these individual variables by
\max_{i\in[n]}\sigma^{2}_{i}\leq\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{4\lfloor m/2\rfloor\alpha_{i}^{2}}=\frac{1}{4\lfloor m/2\rfloor\alpha_{min}^{2}}
. So we have that

\mathbb{E}_{X}[\max_{i\in[n]}W_{i}]\leq\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}\log(n)}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{min}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(n)}{2\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{a_{KT}(\theta,\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))}{\alpha_{min}\binom{m}{2}}\right]-\frac{a_{KT}(\theta,\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))}{\alpha_{min}\binom{m}{2}}\right)\right]

We can use our method of rewriting individual proportionality for an arbitrary vector
\theta\in S^{d-1}
:

\frac{a_{KT}(\theta^{(i)},\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))}{\binom{m}{2}}=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Pi_{m}}\frac{1}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}h_{\theta}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)}-x_{\sigma(2j)})

recalling that
h_{\theta}(x-y)
is the kernel function, and is an indicator function for whether
\theta
and
\theta_{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
agree on the ranking of
x
and
y
. Observe that all of the
x_{i}
are iid, so for any permutation
\sigma
, all of the difference vectors
x_{\sigma(2j-1)}-x_{\sigma(2j)}
are also identically distributed. Then, by linearity of expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}h_{\theta}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)}-x_{\sigma(2j)})\right]=\mathbb{E}[h_{\theta}(x_{1}-x_{2})]
. We can continue in our bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{i\in[n]}W_{i}\right]
follows:

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{min}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{a_{KT}(\theta,\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))}{\binom{m}{2}}\right]-\frac{a_{KT}(\theta,\theta_{f},X,(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))}{\binom{m}{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{min}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Pi_{m}}\left(\mathbb{E}[h_{\theta}(x_{1}-x_{2})]-\frac{1}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}h_{\theta}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)}-x_{\sigma(2j)})\right)\right]

The permutation average can be thought of as the expectation over a permutation drawn uniformly at random. Then, by the convexity of supremum and Jensen’s inequality, we can upper bound this expression by pulling the supremum inside the permutation average:

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{min}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\sigma\in\Pi_{m}}\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\mathbb{E}[h_{\theta}(x_{1}-x_{2})]-\frac{1}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}h_{\theta}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)}-x_{\sigma(2j)})\right)\right]

Note that this supremum is the same for all permutations, as all of them share the same distribution. So, we can fix any permutation
\sigma
, and write our expression in terms of it:

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{min}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\mathbb{E}[h_{\theta}(x_{1}-x_{2})]-\frac{1}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}h_{\theta}(x_{\sigma(2j-1)}-x_{\sigma(2j)})\right)\right]

If we let
\mathcal{H}=\{h_{\theta}\colon\theta\in S^{d-1}\}=\{z\mapsto\mathbb{I}(\text{sign}(\langle z,\theta\rangle)=\text{sign}(\langle z,\theta_{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\rangle)\colon\theta\in S^{d-1}\}
be our function class, then it is a known result that we can upper bound this expected supremum by twice the Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{H}
(e.g.
[
Wainwright 2019
]
Prop 4.11).

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\alpha_{min}}\mathcal{R}_{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}(\mathcal{H})

In order to bound
\mathcal{R}_{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}(\mathcal{H})
, we first bound the VC-dimension of
\mathcal{H}
and then use a known connection between the two.

Consider the class of linear halfspaces that pass through the origin,
\mathcal{F}=\{z\mapsto\text{sign}(\langle\theta,z\rangle)\mid\theta\in S^{d-1}\}
. Because our function class
\mathcal{H}
is formed by checking equality against a fixed boolean label pre-assigned by
\theta_{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
,
\mathcal{H}
cannot shatter any set of points that
\mathcal{F}
cannot shatter. While the VC dimension of general affine halfspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
d+1
(
[
Mohri et al. 2018
]
, Example 3.12 and Thm 3.13), our class
\mathcal{F}
has the additional restriction that the halfspaces must pass through the origin, making it geometrically isomorphic to the class of general affine halfspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. Therefore,
\text{VCdim}(\mathcal{F})=d
. Thus,
\text{VCdim}(\mathcal{H})\leq\text{VCdim}(\mathcal{F})=d
.

Finally, combining Massart’s lemma and Sauer’s lemma (and their implications in
[
Mohri et al. 2018
]
Corollaries 3.8 and 3.18), we get that

\frac{2}{\alpha_{min}}\mathcal{R}_{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}(\mathcal{H})\leq\frac{2}{\alpha_{min}}\sqrt{\frac{2\log((e\lfloor m/2\rfloor/d)^{d})}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{min}}\sqrt{\frac{d\cdot 8\log(e\lfloor m/2\rfloor/d)}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}

∎

Returning to the proof of
Theorem

4.5
, we can now plug in our upper bounds on
\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\in[n]}W_{i}]
from
Lemma

E.2
and
Lemma

E.4
, and recover the desired bound:

\textsc{batch-IP}(\theta_{f},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\mathcal{D}^{m})\geq\textsc{long}\text{-}\textsc{IP}(\theta_{f},(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\mathcal{D}^{m})-\frac{1}{\alpha_{min}}\sqrt{\frac{\min(\log(n),d\cdot 16\log(e\lfloor m/2\rfloor/d))}{2\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}

F.4.2

The proportionality guarantee for the angular mean
(
Theorem

3.1
) holds under spherically symmetric batch
distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\circ}
. In this section we probe what
happens when this assumption is dropped, by drawing items from an

angular central Gaussian
(ACG) distribution

[
Tyler 1987
]
on
S^{d-1}
parameterized by a
concentration axis
v\in S^{d-1}
and an anisotropy parameter

\lambda\in(0,1]

z\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\;\lambda\cdot I+(1-\lambda)\,vv^{\top}\bigr),\qquad x=z/\|z\|.

1
along
v
and
\lambda
along every
direction orthogonal to
v

\lambda=1
recovers the isotropic
Gaussian (and hence the uniform distribution on
S^{d-1}
), while

\lambda\to 0
collapses items onto
\{+v,-v\}
. We probe two
settings: a synthetic two-voter setup and the real-world 2D
electorates.
