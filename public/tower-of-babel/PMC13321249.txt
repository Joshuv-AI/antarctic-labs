A randomized double-blind sham-controlled preliminary study of a 5-week protocol of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) combined with cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for gambling disorder in Indonesia
Authors: Kristiana Siste, Lee Thung Sen, Belinda Julivia Murtani, Enjeline Hanafi, Kevin Surya Kusuma, Astria Aryani, Christopher Tan, Fitri Dona Nainggolan, Nur Aida, Yunyun Setiawan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gambling disorder (GD) has increased in prevalence particularly in Indonesia where online gambling predominates. Gambling harm may involve not only individuals with GD but also their significant others. This preliminary study aims to investigate the efficacy of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) in conjunction with cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) compared to sham rTMS with CBT in treating individuals with GD. Twenty seven participants diagnosed with GD were randomly assigned to receive either active or sham rTMS over five weeks, alongside a standardized CBT program. Outcomes were assessed at multiple time points using various standardized measures. This protocol was safe and well-tolerated, with minimal adverse events (adverse event rate: 7.2%). Both groups displayed significant reductions in gambling symptoms measured by the Gambling Symptom Assessment Scale. However, no significant differences were observed between the active and sham rTMS groups. The active rTMS group demonstrated faster reductions in gambling craving [B: 2.63 (95% CI 0.55 to 4.70), p: 0.015] and gambling-related cognition, particularly in the domains of gambling expectancies [B: 1.84 (95% CI 0.98 to 2.71), p ≤ 0.001] and the perceived inability to stop gambling [B: 1.70 (95% CI 0.36 to 3.04), p: 0.014]. The results suggest that rTMS may enhance CBT outcomes in treating GD by modulating craving and cognitive biases associated with gambling. Further research is warranted to optimize intervention parameters and assess the long-term effects of rTMS in GD treatment.

Introduction

Gambling Disorder (GD) has become an increasing concern for global mental health. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), GD is defined as a persistent or recurrent pattern of problematic gambling behavior, occurring online or offline, that results in clinically significant distress or impairment for at least 12 months (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; WHO, 2025). A recent global meta-analysis estimated that 46.2% of adults gambled in the past 12 months, with higher rates among men (Tran et al., 2024; WHO, 2024). Although gambling is legally prohibited in Indonesia, online gambling remains accessible with prevalence of GD estimated as high as 2% of the population (Sinambela, 2022). In 2024, reports indicated that online gambling transactions in Indonesia reached approximately 359.8 trillion IDR (Indonesian Rupiahs), equivalent to nearly 20.5 billion USD. This marked a 30-fold increase since 2017, underscoring the scale of potential harm (PPATK, 2024).

Neurobiologically, GD is linked to dysregulation within the brain reward circuitry and impaired top-down control, implicating fronto-striatal circuitry, particularly in the striatum and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (Meng et al., 2014; Sghaier, Ben Haouala, Zariat, Romdhane, & Ben Garouia, 2025). Multiple neurotransmitters have been linked to GD, including norepinephrine, serotonin, dopamine, opioid and glutamate (Potenza, 2013). In pathological gamblers specifically, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) shows significantly greater activation during exposure to gambling cues (Crockford, Goodyear, Edwards, Quickfall, & el-Guebaly, N., 2005; Limbrick-Oldfield et al., 2017; Raimo, Cropano, Trojano, & Santangelo, 2021). These mechanisms provide a rationale for the neuromodulation approach targeting prefrontal control circuits.

Craving may be conceptualized as an intense urge or desire to gamble (Mallorquí-Bagué, Mestre-Bach, & Testa, 2023). The evidence base indicates that it plays an important role in the development and maintenance of GD and is elicited by gambling-related cues. Craving is also associated with clinically relevant outcomes including relapse risk and treatment attrition. Gambling cue-induced craving has been linked to activity in reward and salience-related regions such as the insula and ventral striatum, together with altered prefrontal regulation. Accordingly, craving represents a clinically meaningful and mechanistically relevant target for interventions aimed at strengthening top-down control over gambling behavior (Limbrick-Oldfield et al., 2017; Mallorquí-Bagué et al., 2023; Potenza, 2014; Raimo et al., 2021).

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a non-invasive brain stimulation treatment in which time-varying magnetic fields induce electrical currents in the superficial cortex. Its effects depend on the stimulation protocol, including the frequency, pattern, dose, and target stimulation site, with international safety guidance supporting its tolerability (Rossi et al., 2021). Reviews of neuroimaging studies have implicated broader prefrontal and salience-related regions, including the DLPFC, insula, and anterior cingulate cortex, in cue-reactivity, decision-making, and craving in GD, supporting the DLPFC as one plausible neuromodulation target within a distributed executive-control network (Crockford et al., 2005; Limbrick-Oldfield et al., 2017). Consistent with this DLPFC-focused rationale in GD, Gay et al. (2017) reported that a single session of rTMS over the left DLPFC significantly reduced cue-induced craving (F: 4.87, p: 0.04). Additionally, other modes of neurostimulation such as transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), which also targeted the DLPFC, strengthened frontoparietal functional connectivity associated with cognitive control for patients with GD (Bouchard et al., 2021).

A recent systematic review concluded that excitatory stimulation over left prefrontal targets has shown promising effects on craving, whereas inhibitory stimulation over the right DLPFC showed no clear benefit (Concerto et al., 2023). The meta-analysis by Del Mauro et al. (2023) further reported significant pre-post reductions in craving across non-invasive brain stimulation studies (SMCC: -0.69 (95% CI -1.2 to −0.2), while emphasizing the limitations in the evidence base, including small samples, few sham-controlled trials and inconsistent behavioral outcome measurement. In conclusion, these findings support DLPFC as a plausible target for modulating gambling-related craving and highlight a key gap. As far as the authors are aware, neuromodulation studies in GD have rarely been tested as an adjunct to standardized clinical treatment such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).

CBT remains the evidence-based cornerstone treatment option for GD, demonstrating significant reductions in gambling severity and psychological distress (Pfund et al., 2023). However, response rate is highly variable, with meaningful dropout and relapse risk, particularly in the context of high-cue, high-access online gambling environments where cravings are repeatedly induced (Granero et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2022; Lucas et al., 2023; Mallorquí-Bagué et al., 2023; Tessier, Romo, & Zerhouni, 2021; Wardle et al., 2024). We therefore hypothesized that adjunctive rTMS could help CBT by facilitating earlier reductions in craving and gambling-related cognitions that can interfere with behavioral change. The primary objective of this preliminary study is to evaluate the safety and preliminary effectiveness of active rTMS in conjunction with CBT compared to CBT with sham rTMS among individuals with GD in Indonesia.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a preliminary double-blind, sham-controlled, RCT with two parallel arms at Dr. Cipto Mangunkusumo National Referral General Hospital, Jakarta, Indonesia from March 2024 to May 2025. Both the participants and the outcome assessors were blinded to the allocation process. Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either active rTMS or sham rTMS arms and both groups also received a standardized CBT program. The stimulation protocol followed our previous rTMS procedures (Siste et al., 2025). This study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov with registration number: NCT06182137. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethical Committee of Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Indonesia (approval number: KET-1499/UN2·F1/ETIK/PPM.00.02/2023). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. This study was written according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines (Hopewell et al., 2025).

Recruitment and eligibility criteria

About 50 participants from the Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic in Dr. Cipto Mangunkusumo National Referral General Hospital, Jakarta were initially screened with the South Oaks Gambling Screen (SOGS) to identify pathological gambling between January to March 2024. We used the Indonesian version of the SOGS which has undergone validity and reliability testing, demonstrating good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.879) (Siste et al., 2025). Those who scored ≥5 on the SOGS, were contacted by the research team and further clinically interviewed by two psychiatrists. A total of 30 participants fulfilled the gambling disorder diagnosis based on the ICD-11 criteria. Around 3 participants were uncontactable and did not come in for subsequent visits after the baseline survey. In total, 27 participants were recruited for randomization. Eligible participants fulfilled the inclusion criteria mirroring in accordance to the previously published protocol (Siste et al., 2025): (1) screened with SOGS (score ≥ 5); (2) fulfilled the ICD-11 criteria for gambling disorder; (3) aged 18–74 years old; (4) fluent in Bahasa Indonesia; (5) provided written informed consent to participate and receive treatment. Exclusion criteria included: (1) participants with lifetime history of psychotic and personality disorder according to ICD-11; (2) severe neurological comorbidities, (e.g., seizure or loss of consciousness); (3) intellectual disability; (4) history of receiving neuromodulation; (5) history of implanted medical devices; (6) is currently or expecting pregnancy; and (7) a diagnosis of substance use disorder or any form of substance misuse within the previous six months. Concomitant pharmacological medications were not modified and could be consumed by the participants throughout the entire research process, with the exception of benzodiazepine cessation 24 h prior to the rTMS session, which occurred under close supervision by the researchers. We documented the concomitant medications for all participants, including drug name, dosage, and frequency (see Supplementary Table S1). Participants may withdraw from the study at any time. Drop out criteria include missing two consecutive rTMS sessions. An appointed research associate was tasked to send reminder text messages to each participant one day prior and the morning of the day of their scheduled therapy to further enhance participant adherence.

Randomization, allocation, and blinding

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the active or sham rTMS group using a computer-generated random allocation sequence prepared by a research associate. Participants were consecutively assigned to either active or sham rTMS according to the order of this pre-generated sequence as they were enrolled. Participants were blinded to their treatment allocation. The operator administering the rTMS procedure was aware of the group allocation but did not participate in assessment or data analysis. Sham rTMS have identical appearance, sound and contact sensation with identical session count and duration, but produced no effective magnetic field. Both arms received identical CBT schedules. The appointed addiction psychiatrists are in charge of administering the CBT schedules and as investigators were blinded towards the allocation.

rTMS intervention

The rTMS protocol was developed by the Department of Psychiatry, Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Indonesia, Indonesia in collaboration with the Faculty of Medical Sciences, Radboud University Medical Center, Netherlands (Siste et al., 2025). We selected the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) as the target stimulation site, based on its well-established role in the pathophysiology process of behavioral addictions through prior neuroimaging and functional connectivity studies (Siste et al., 2022). Studies showed reduced DLPFC activation during response inhibition and hypoactivation during gambling cue exposure and reversal learning task (Brevers, Noël, He, Melrose, & Bechara, 2016; Fujimoto et al., 2017; Goudriaan, De Ruiter, Van Den Brink, Oosterlaan, & Veltman, 2010; Moccia et al., 2017; Spagnolo, Gómez Pérez, Terraneo, Gallimberti, & Bonci, 2019; Van Holst, Van Holstein, Van Den Brink, Veltman, & Goudriaan, 2012).

The Beam F3 method was used to localize the left DLPFC and the target site was marked on a personalized headcap to ensure consistent coil placement across sessions (Beam, Borckardt, Reeves, & George, 2009). Stimulation was delivered with a Neurosoft Neuro-MS/D system using a 100-mm figure-8 coil positioned tangentially to the scalp with the handle oriented posterior-laterally at a 45-degree angle from midline (Ueno & Sekino, 2021). Stimulation intensity was 120% of the resting motor threshold (RMT), with RMT determined visually as the minimal stimulus producing ≥5 visible twitches of the right abductor pollicis brevis in 10 consecutive stimuli (Badran et al., 2019). The stimulation protocol used 10 Hz trains of 4 s duration with 11-s intertrain intervals, totalling 75 trains and 3000 pulses per session. These parameters followed safety recommendations for rTMS and within the effective range of previously reported protocols (Rossi et al., 2021).

All rTMS sessions were outpatient, operated by two trained medical doctors, administered once daily, three days per week for five weeks (15 sessions total). Sham control used a Neurosoft sham coil with identical appearance and similar sounds but produced negligible neuromodulatory effects. However, the sham coil differs in its tapping sensation on the targeted scalp area (Gomes, Brasil-Neto, Allam, & Rodrigues De Souza, 2012). To preserve blinding, participants with prior rTMS experience were excluded and active and sham groups were scheduled separately.

To ensure safety and monitor adverse events, all participants were screened with a 16-item safety checklist adapted from Radboud University's internal rTMS safety protocol, adjusted with prior rTMS guidelines (Rossi et al., 2021). The checklist evaluated contraindication and potential risk factors (e.g. sleep deprivation, metal implants, consumption of substances which can lower the seizure threshold) and was re-assessed before each session. During and after each session, participants were evaluated for any discomfort or adverse events using structured safety assessments. All reported events were documented in detail, from pain (onset, location, quality, duration, severity), tingling sensation, muscle twitching, scalp redness, tinnitus, hearing changes, mood changes and other adverse events (Rossi et al., 2021).

CBT intervention

All participants received 12 individualized CBT sessions for Gambling Disorder, adapted from the Indonesia Drug Addiction and Relapse Prevention Program (Indo-DARPP) CBT module originally designed for substance use disorders and modified to suit gambling-specific frameworks (Battersby, Oakes, Tolchard, Forbes, & Pols, 2008; Higuchi, Matsushita, & Furuno, 2021; Oakes, Gardiner, McLaughlin, & Battersby, 2012; Tolchard & Battersby, 2013; Yamada et al., 2021). Each session spans 30–45 min and was delivered by trained addiction psychiatrists. Sessions were scheduled three times during the first week, immediately after the participant's rTMS session, followed by twice weekly for the next four weeks. The final CBT session (relapse prevention planning) was completed on the 6th week ensuring all participants completed the full 12 session CBT program.

This CBT module emphasized GD-relevant topics, structured into twelve sessions; (1) psychoeducation on the definition, symptoms and neurobehavioral underlying mechanisms of GD; (2) identification of individualized gambling-related harms; (3) exploring motivations to stop gambling; (4) recognition of the stages of change; (5) identification and management of gambling triggers; (6) understanding the gambling cycle; (7) gambling craving and extinction strategies; (8) development of alternative, non-gambling daily routines; (9) financial management skills; (10) restoring interpersonal relationships disrupted by gambling behavior; (11) emotional regulation strategies; and (12) relapse prevention planning.

Outcome measurement and assessment

Assessments were performed at five time points: baseline (T1), interim after the seventh rTMS session (T2), post-treatment after completing the fifteenth rTMS session and the last CBT session (T3), at 3-month follow up (T4), and at 6-month follow up (T5). After screening using the SOGS, at baseline (T1) all eligible participants undergo a complete psychiatric evaluation, cognitive assessment, a demographic survey, followed by completion of six standardized psychometric instruments, consisting of three gambling-related instruments; the Gambling Symptom Assessment Scale (G-SAS), Gambling Urge Scale (GUS) and Gambling Related Cognition Scale (GRCS), two instruments to evaluate mental health disorder; the Self-Reporting Questionnaire-20 Item (SRQ-20) and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 Item (PHQ-9), and one clinician rated instrument: Clinical Global Impression – Severity Scale (CGI—S). At mid intervention (T2) only G-SAS, GUS and CGI-S were evaluated to monitor early clinical improvements; whereas T3, T4 and T5 re-evaluated all six instruments. Potential neurocognitive changes and side effects were assessed at T1 and T3 using a neurocognitive battery (Creyos, Canada) tailored to assess visuospatial memory, working memory, response inhibition, executive planning, attention span, and verbal short-term memory (Cole et al., 2020; Sobreira, Aleixo, Moreia, & Oliveira, 2016). The details of each test are available on the provider's website: https://creyos.com/. All sessions and assessments at T1, T2 and T3 were conducted in person at Dr. Cipto Mangunkusumo National Referral General Hospital, while T4 and T5 assessments were conducted either on-site in outpatient clinics or via secure online platforms to accommodate participant availability and maximize participant retention.

Instruments

Primary outcomes

Gambling symptom assessment scale (G-SAS)

Developed by Kim, Grant, Potenza, Blanco, and Hollander (2009), the G-SAS is a 12-item self-rated instrument measuring the severity of gambling symptoms during the past week. Each item is rated from 0 to 4, resulting in a total score of 0 to 48. Higher scores indicate greater gambling symptom severity: 8–20 (mild), 21–30 (moderate), 31–40 (severe), and > 40 (extreme). The G-SAS has good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.89) and is sensitive to change, making it widely used to evaluate gambling symptom severity and monitor treatment progress in GD (Kim et al., 2009). The G-SAS has also been recommended for measurement of response rate, with a reduction of 35% in G-SAS scores equalling a treatment response (Grant, Ioannidis, & Chamberlain, 2024).

Gambling urge scale (GUS)

The GUS is a 6-item self-report instrument measuring the intensity of current gambling urges. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 6 to 42. Higher scores indicate stronger gambling urges. The GUS has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.93) and has been widely used in other clinical studies to evaluate craving fluctuations (Smith, Pols, Battersby, & Harvey, 2013). Participants were not provided with external stimuli to induce craving; thus, in this study, the GUS measured internally motivated, or deprivation-based, tonic craving (Tiffany & Wray, 2012).

Secondary outcomes

Gambling related cognitions scale (GRCS)

The GRCS, developed by Raylu and Oei (2004), is a 23-item instrument designed to assess five domains of cognitive distortions related to gambling: gambling expectancies (GRCS-GE), illusion of control (GRCS-IC), predictive control (GRCS-PC), inability to stop gambling (GRCS-ISG), and interpretative bias (GRCS-IB). Responses are rated on a 7-point Likert scale. The domain with the highest score reflects the most dominant cognitive distortion present in the individual (Raylu & Oei, 2004).

Self-reporting questionnaire-20 (SRQ-20)

The SRQ-20, developed by the World Health Organization, is a 20-item screening tool to identify common mental health disorders, particularly depression and anxiety, in primary care and community settings. Each item consists of a yes/no question, focusing on symptoms experienced in the past 30 days. Each “yes” answer scores 1 point, while “no” scores 0, with scores totalling 0 to 20. Higher scores indicate greater psychological distress, with a cutoff score of ≥6 to indicate probable psychological distress. The SRQ-20 Indonesian version has been widely used in psychiatric research (Prasetio, Triwahyuni, & Prathama, 2022).

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 is a 9-item self-report scale based on DSM-IV diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder. Each item is rated from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), with a total score of 0 to 27. Severity cutoffs are categorized as: 5–9 (mild), 10–14 (moderate), 15–19 (moderately severe), and ≥ 20 (severe depression). The PHQ-9 has been validated in multiple languages, including Bahasa Indonesia, and is commonly used to detect changes in depressive symptoms over time (Dian, Effendy, & Amin, 2022).

Clinical global impression – severity (CGI—S)

The CGI-S is a clinician-rated instrument consisting of two components: the Severity (CGI—S) and Improvement (CGI—I) scales. The CGI-S rates overall illness severity from 1 (“normal, not at all ill”) to 7 (“among the most extremely ill patients”). The CGI-S provides a clinical judgment of gambling severity and insights towards the treatment progress and efficacy based on the treating physicians' assessment (Busner & Targum, 2007).

Sample size estimation

Because prior trials evaluating the combined effect of rTMS and CBT in gambling disorder are limited, and because existing effect estimates derive from different outcome domains, we mimicked the trial sample size of previous studies (Cardullo et al., 2019; Gay et al., 2017; Pettorruso et al., 2020; Sauvaget et al., 2018) which ranged from 7 to 30 participants; thus, this preliminary study was also designed to employ 30 participants. Afterwards, we performed a post-hoc analysis to quantify the study's power. The final sample size utilized for analysis in this study was 27. The regression coefficients from the group by time analyses (of GUS and GRCS) yielded high effect sizes (Hedge's g) ranging from 0.91 to 1.48 (estimated using the calculator from https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/calculator/). However, a previous meta-analysis of brain stimulation aggregate effect on craving displayed modest reduction of standardized mean change −0.49 (Del Mauro et al., 2023). Other individual studies indicated similar modest reduction for gambling severity after 5 days (t: 5.61, p < 0.001) and after 30 days (t: 5.06, p < 0.001) (Cardullo et al., 2019; Gay et al., 2017). Thus, to minimize bias from overfitting and misestimated effect size, we selected a more conservative medium effect size assumption (f: 0.25, approximately equivalent to d/g = 0.50) for a post hoc F-test of a within-between interaction (repeated measures) model using G*Power (version 3.1). This study had four repeated measurements, with two groups, and utilized default nonsphericity correction at 1 and correlation between measurements at 0.5. This resulted in F (3, 75): 2.72 and a statistical power of 86.5%. Accordingly, this study should be interpreted as a preliminary trial.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 30.0 (IBM, NY) and R version 4.5.1, with a significance level of p: 0.05. The main analysis followed the intention-to-treat principle, including all randomized participants. Descriptive baseline characteristics were summarized as means (standard deviations) or medians (interquartile ranges) for continuous variables and as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Chi-Square and Independent t-test were used to assess statistically significant differences in the demographic data between the two groups and to verify the validity of our randomization. Nearly all outcomes displayed floor effects in the final sample after intervention. As such, we utilized the generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) for longitudinal changes in our primary and secondary outcomes with fixed effects for group, time, and by time effects using the glmmTMB version 1.1.13 package in R. Initial data screening demonstrated statistically significant differences between GUS, GRCS-GE, and GRCS-ISG baseline scores among the two groups. As such models included age, sex, onset age of gambling, and baseline scores of GUS, GRCS-GE, and GRCS-ISG as covariates with residuals inspected graphically to verify distributional assumptions (residual QQ plot, overdispersion, and deviation tests). GLMMs are inherently robust to violations of distributional assumptions (Schielzeth et al., 2020). The models included a random intercept for subject as the grouping variable, used Satterthwaite approximation of degrees of freedom and employed bootstrapping with 2000 iterations (boot package version 1.3–31) to introduce robust estimation of coefficients thus handling violations of model assumptions. For the GUS and GRCS-GE and GRCS-ISG domains, parametric bootstrapping with 2000 iterations was used to derive 95% bootstrap confidence intervals for estimated marginal means. All statistical tests were two-sided. For the primary GLMM analyses, statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05 based on the model coefficients and Type III Wald tests. Post-hoc analyses were conducted using the Holm-Bonferroni correction in the emmeans package (version 2.0.0). Exact p-values are reported whenever possible; values smaller than 0.001 are reported as p ≤ 0.001.

Results

Sociodemographics and baseline correlates

A total of 27 participants completed the rTMS treatment protocol, with 13 allocated to the intervention arm and 14 to the control arm. During the three-month follow-up (T4), three participants (all from the active rTMS group) were lost to follow-up due to changes in contact information and address, and an additional three participants (two from the active rTMS and 1 from sham rTMS group) were lost to follow-up during the six-month follow-up period (T5) for similar reasons. All participants have a dominant right hand.

Overall, there were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups in baseline sociodemographic characteristics, including age or sex distribution. All participants performed their gambling online. The majority of participants in both groups reported gambling primarily for a sense of achievement or entertainment. Approximately 54.5% of participants in the intervention group and 45.5% in the control group engaged in more than one type of gambling activity. However, a significantly higher proportion of participants in the control group reported engaging in slot-machine gambling compared with the intervention group (p: 0.03). Safety and adverse events

Mild adverse events were observed in 6 out of 13 subjects (46%) of the active rTMS group and none from the sham rTMS group. Of the 6 subjects experiencing adverse effects, 3 subjects (23%) reported headache symptoms, 1 subject (8%) reported tingling sensation, and 2 subjects (15%) reported both headache and tingling sensation (Table 3). The total adverse event rate observed was 7.2% (29 events out of all 405 active and sham rTMS sessions). All adverse events were self-limiting and only required a brief observation period. All participants experiencing discomfort were allowed to rest, they gradually recovered and none required medication. Reports of discomfort decreased over the course of treatment; and none led to discontinuation of treatment sessions. Neurocognitive batteries demonstrated stable scores over time across both groups (pre-intervention, T1 vs. post-intervention, T3). No negative side effects were observed following active rTMS stimulation. Performance improved significantly for working memory in the intervention group compared to control (B: 6.11, p: 0.036) even after Holms-Bonferroni correction. Gambling symptoms and craving

The GLMM model demonstrated a significant time effect (B: −4.44, p ≤ 0.001) on G-SAS scores. However, the group (p: 0.41) and group by time interaction (p: 0.42) were not significant predictors. Transforming the G-SAS to response rate categorization (a reduction of at least 35% from baseline score), indicated that group (p: 0.84) and group by time interaction (p: 0.94) remained non-significant between the active and sham rTMS. Only time was a significant predictor, B: 0.40 and p: 0.003, for changes in G-SAS response rate. At the end of treatment, 84.6% (n = 11/13) from the intervention groups met criteria for G-SAS response compared to 78.6% (n = 11/14) in the control group. During the 6-month follow-up, all participants in the active rTMS group (n = 8) and sham rTMS group (n = 13) maintained G-SAS response compared to their baseline (T1) scores (see Supplementary Table S3 for G-SAS response rate).

The group effect was significant (B: −6.71, p: 0.035) for GUS scores in the primary model analysis. Further post-hoc analysis demonstrated significant differences between groups occurred only during baseline (B: 5.26, p: 0.033), and group differences were not significant at any other time point. There was, however, a steeper reduction trend, particularly between T2 (interim assessment) and T3 (post-treatment assessment), in GUS scores in the intervention group (ΔT2-T3: 4.06) compared to the control group (ΔT2-T3: 0.046). The time effect was also significant (B: −6.91, p ≤ 0.001) in the primary model analysis and in all timepoints (T2, T3, T4, T5) pairwise comparisons to baseline (T1) in both groups, surviving the post-hoc analyses (p < 0.05). However, at no other time point were pairwise comparisons significant in the post hoc analyses. The group by time interaction (primary model analysis) was a significant predictor of GUS reduction, with a slope difference of B: 2.63 (95% CI 0.55 to 4.70), p: 0.015 (See Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S4). Gambling cognitions

As shown in Fig. 3, the active rTMS group had significantly higher GRCS-GE scores (B: −5.66, p ≤ 0.001) than the control group in the primary model analysis. While group post-hoc analyses indicated significant differences for active and sham rTMS during T1 (B: 4.62, p ≤ 0.001) and T4 (B: −2.58, p: 0.04) for GRCS-GE scores. The GRCS-GE scores also significantly diminished over time in the primary model (B: −4.95, p ≤ 0.001). The significant time effect for GRCS-GE was followed with post-hoc analyses which demonstrated significance for T1 compared to T3, T4, and T5 (all p ≤ 0.001), but not other pairwise comparisons (see Supplementary Table S5). Additionally, group by time interaction (primary model analysis) also reached statistical significance (B: 1.84, p ≤ 0.001) and demonstrated a steeper decrease of GRCS-GE score for active rTMS by 1.84 points (95% CI 0.98 to 2.71) compared to CBT and sham rTMS group.

There was also a significant group (B: −4.79, p: 0.05), time (B: −6.50, p ≤ 0.001), and group by time effect (B: 1.70, p: 0.014, 95% CI 0.36 to 3.04) for reduction in GRCS-ISG scores during primary model analysis. The intervention group had higher reduction of GRCS-ISG than the control group, especially from T1 to T3 (refer to Fig. 3 and see Supplementary Table S5). All timepoints (T3, T4, and T5) displayed significant differences (p ≤ 0.001) on GRCS-ISG scores compared to baseline (T1) during post-hoc analyses. Additionally, post-hoc analyses on group effects also revealed that only baseline differences reached statistical significance (B: 4.35, p: 0.012).

The overall model for GRCS-IC was not significantly predicted by group (p: 0.49) or group by time interaction (p: 0.71). However, the GRCS-IC scores improved significantly over time, B: −2.07, p ≤ 0.001. There was also a significant time effect on GRCS-PC (B: −4.43, p ≤ 0.001) and GRCS-IB (B: −3.08, p ≤ 0.001). No group or group by time interaction effect was statistically significant for GRCS-PC and GRCS-IB.

Clinical correlates

Time was the only significant predictor for SRQ-20 scores, B: −2.39 and p: 0.003. Similarly, group and group by time predictors were not significant for PHQ-9 scores. There was a trend towards significance of time effect for PHQ-9 (B: −2.29, p: 0.058). Similarly, the time predictor demonstrated a trend nearing significance for CGI scores (B: −0.65, p: 0.051). No group or group by time interaction effect was statistically significant for CGI scores.

Discussion

This preliminary study aims to investigate the safety, feasibility, and preliminary efficacy of a 5-week active rTMS protocol combined with CBT compared to sham rTMS and CBT. There were no serious adverse events noted during our rTMS protocol. Participants reported mild adverse events in about 7.2% of the sessions overall, for example tingling sensation around stimulation area and headache. This rate is acceptable and comparable to those reported in other rTMS studies (Caulfield, Fleischmann, George, & McTeague, 2022; Overvliet et al., 2021). All symptoms were self-limiting and did not lead to treatment termination, leading to completion of treatment among all participants. Both intervention arms were able to achieve response (compared to baseline) even at the 6-month follow up. In this study, active rTMS combined with CBT did not significantly reduce gambling symptoms compared with CBT with sham rTMS. However, the active rTMS and CBT combination showed early evidence of reducing gambling urge and gambling-related cognitive errors (gambling expectancies and inability to stop gambling). A significant reduction of gambling symptom severity assessed by G-SAS was observed in the intervention and control groups. Although, no statistically significant difference was found between the two arms. Prior studies have found that rTMS did not improve the severity of gambling symptoms. On the other hand, a rigorous umbrella meta-analysis has showcased a high effect size (g: −0.91) of CBT to gambling severity (Pfund et al., 2023). Interestingly, we demonstrated a persistent (up to 6 months) response efficacy from both intervention and control group based on G-SAS scores. Notably, rTMS has the potential to induce sustained alterations in neurotransmitter levels, particularly glutamate, aspartate, and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) (Moretti & Rodger, 2022). These changes may lead to long-term epigenetic modifications, such as histone acetylation, promote the upregulation of neurotrophic factors, and activate glial and microglial cells (Chervyakov, Chernyavsky, Sinitsyn, & Piradov, 2015; Etiévant et al., 2015). However, the long-term maintenance of this effect or the superiority of rTMS in preventing relapse cannot be determined in this study. Dosing has been a concerning issue of rTMS for addictive disorders. A meta-analysis of rTMS use in SUD patients found a significant positive association between the total number of stimulation pulses (dose) and the effect size of craving reduction with excitatory rTMS over the left DLPFC (Zhang, Fong, Ouyang, Siu, & Kranz, 2019). Several reports have recommended that optimization on the number of sessions, amount of pulse per session, involvement of novel sites, such as the frontopolar region, and utilizing novel deliveries (e.g., intermittent theta-burst stimulation, iTBS) may lead to greater efficacy (Chang, Liou, Liu, Lu, & Chen, 2022; Mehta et al., 2024; Soleimani et al., 2025).

Significant group by time interaction effects were observed in the GUS, GRCS-GE, and GRCS-ISG domains between the two groups, with the active rTMS group demonstrating a more rapid reduction in scores compared to the sham rTMS group. This indicates a potential adjunctive effect of rTMS in combination with CBT for treating GD, particularly in harm reduction efforts for individuals with severe manifestations of the condition. In this study, we specifically evaluate tonic craving, the persistent and stable internal drive that arises independently of cues and is associated with the individual's background state, different from phasic (cue-induced) craving (Ray, Courtney, Bacio, & MacKillop, 2013). Tonic craving is clinically relevant, as elevated craving intensity has been shown to predict relapse, even after a long period of abstinence, this shows its role in the maintenance of addictive behaviors, including GD (D. P. Smith et al., 2013). A previous study using mixed-effects binary logistic regression analyses had found that gambling-related cravings can significantly predict gambling episodes (OR: 2.23, 95% CI: 1.61, 3.08) (Hawker, Merkouris, Youssef, & Dowling, 2021). Consistently, another study has demonstrated a significant association between gambling urges and relapse risk (OR: 1.29, 95% CI: 1.12, 1.49), strengthening the relevance of continuous craving assessment in relapse prevention (D. P. Smith et al., 2015). A recent systematic review and meta analysis emphasized that one of the major limitations of previous neuromodulation studies in GD is their reliance solely on pre- and post-treatment craving measurement, which fail to capture tonic craving, a factor crucial for predicting relapse (Del Mauro et al., 2023). We addressed this limitation by repeatedly assessing tonic craving using the GUS scale at multiple time points over a 5-week period, with additional measurements during follow-up. Our findings suggested that active rTMS combined with CBT reduces tonic craving faster compared to CBT alone, which could translate into less gambling engagement, faster harm reduction, and reduced relapse risk, although direct evidence linking rTMS-induced craving reduction and relapse remains limited (Hawker et al., 2021).

Individuals with GD often exhibit cognitive distortions that reinforce their gambling activity. The GRCS, along with its subdomains, likely reflects the interplay of various neural mechanisms (Zheng et al., 2025). The GRCS-GE domain indicates that these individuals hold unrealistic beliefs about gambling's ability to enhance their happiness, which is associated with the subjective value of rewarding stimuli and dysfunctions in the prefrontal cortex, insula and striatum (Ruiz de Lara & Perales, 2020; D. Smith, Woodman, Drummond, & Battersby, 2016). Dysfunction within these structures are also linked to depression and anxiety disorders, both of which are prevalent comorbid conditions in GD, further contributing to the expectation that gambling might provide an escape from negative emotional states (Motzkin, Philippi, Wolf, Baskaya, & Koenigs, 2015; Neophytou, Theodorou, Artemi, Theodorou, & Panayiotou, 2023). Individuals with GD often rationalize their belief that gambling is the only means to relieve negative emotions, evoke excitement, and reduce boredom. Consequently, they may perceive the cessation of gambling as a deprivation of satisfaction and a threat to their well-being and functioning (Raylu & Oei, 2004). The CBT module aims to address these issues by identifying individualized motivations to stop gambling, gambling-related harms, gambling triggers, the development of alternative non-gambling routines, and emotional regulation strategies (Siste et al., 2025).

As with other addictive behaviors, impulsivity and also compulsivity have also been identified as core constructs in GD and may lead to a sense of helplessness in controlling gambling behavior (Lee, Hoppenbrouwers, & Franken, 2019). The GRCS-ISG domain reflects the perceived inability of individuals with GD to stop gambling, particularly after recognizing their problems. Such beliefs diminish the likelihood of self-control attempts, reinforcing feelings of helplessness that may contribute to relapses and are associated with depression (Raylu & Oei, 2004). GRCS-ISG has been associated with impairment in the DLPFC, anterior cingulate cortex, and amygdala, as well as correlated to craving measures (Ruiz de Lara & Perales, 2020). Another study also demonstrated that the GRCS-ISG domain is a significant predictor of relapse, particularly among individuals engaged in strategic gambling, as these individuals tend to exhibit more pronounced cognitive distortions that operate as maintaining mechanisms and craving induction (Mallorquí-Bagué et al., 2019; Ruiz de Lara & Perales, 2020). Furthermore, self-efficacy has been identified as a moderator in the relationship between craving and gambling behavior. Self-efficacy refers to an individual's belief in their ability to cope effectively with a situation, which in gambling includes craving self-efficacy (the ability to resist urges to gamble) and gambling self-efficacy (the ability to refrain from gambling behavior). Lower levels of self-efficacy have been associated with an increased risk of relapse, as individuals may lack effective coping strategies to manage gambling urges or prevent gambling episodes. (Hawker et al., 2021; Hodgins, Peden, & Makarchuk, 2004). This underscores the potential of CBT to enhance balanced gambling self-efficacy. While high-frequency rTMS applied to the DLPFC may improve decision-making and cognitive flexibility, through frontoparietal and salience network connections, and reduce insula hyperactivity thereby alleviating cravings (Li et al., 2017; Wiehler & Peters, 2015; Winfree, Ginley, Whelan, & Meyers, 2015).

The limitation of this study includes the relatively small sample size, although we have incorporated a sample size calculation considering both error types, the effect size estimation might still be too large in hindsight. Future studies might consider using a small effect size estimation even for combination of CBT and rTMS, and recruit larger sample sizes for a more robust RCT. Additionally, the attrition bias particularly during follow-up for treatment and control groups. However, the missingness, as in many clinical studies, appears to be completely at random, since it was due to changes in contact numbers. The mixed model analysis is known to be more robust to bias from missing data. However, the model still carries a risk of overfitting due to the smaller sample size compared to the number of covariates, particularly additional covariates such as the baseline scores of GRCS-GE, GRCS-ISG, and GUS. Notably, they were added as covariates to control the effect on the overall model, but the effect cannot be fully eliminated. Secondly, we did not utilize any focal electrical stimulation to mimic the stimulation sensation of active rTMS. The sham rTMS might still impart placebo effect through the sham coil. Thirdly, there might be the Hawthorne effect, where participants improve because they realize they are being monitored. Many of the instruments used were participant-administered thus carrying self-report bias. We also observed floor effects for nearly all the clinical measurements, thus having other variables in distinct scoring format may aid in detecting the difference of treatment effects. For example, gauging the number of past-month gambling episodes and evaluating gambling harm impact. Our study did not evaluate cue-induced craving and future research should attempt to incorporate both tonic and cue-induced craving assessments to better explore the neurobehavioral effects of rTMS in GD. Subsequently, a more frequent measurement (e.g., weekly) of the psychiatric correlates could lend deeper insights to the inception and trend of the rTMS effect on gambling symptoms. Lastly, a longer observation period might be required to determine the potential of rTMS for relapse prevention.

Conclusion

Overall, this study did not find a significant effect of rTMS combined with CBT, compared to CBT alone, to reduce gambling severity scores. The rTMS treatment provides steeper amelioration in craving and gambling-related cognition scores, particularly domains of inability to stop gambling and gambling expectancies. The designed protocol is also safe, feasible and tolerable with a high rate of response up to 6 months follow-up. The present study can provide the founding blocks for future multiple-session rTMS in GD, particularly compared to mainstay psychological therapy. Future protocol iteration should consider optimizing stimulation parameters, particularly adopting an accelerated form (e.g., iTBS), the number of pulse doses, or multisite involvement.
