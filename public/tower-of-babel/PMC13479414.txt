Experiences and perceptions of unrecognised vision loss in the lives of community-dwelling older adults in Denmark: a qualitative study
Authors: Andrea Nedergaard Jensen, Ida Vega Thulesen, Torben Lykke Sørensen, Birte Bay, Maria Kristiansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTimely detection of vision loss and eye disease is essential, as impaired vision can challenge everyday life and independence, particularly in later life. Nonetheless, vision loss frequently remains unrecognised and underdiagnosed. While novel home-based detection initiatives may offer a promising approach, little is known about how older adults themselves perceive their vision. The aim of this study is to explore the meaning of vision loss in the context of biography and everyday life among older adults aged ≥ 75 years who had lived with unrecognised vision loss.MethodsA qualitative study using ethnographic methods was conducted among older adults receiving home care services who were identified with vision loss during a novel home-based detection initiative in a Danish provincial area. Participants detected with vision loss (Snellen decimal visual acuity ≤ 0.63) were subsequently invited to hospital-based ophthalmic assessment. Participant observations during clinical encounters (n = 13) and in-depth home-based interviews (n = 15) were conducted with 15 consecutively recruited older adults and six relatives. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using a narrative-thematic approach.ResultsAlthough all participants were clinically identified with vision loss, often related to underlying eye disease, their subjective experiences did not necessarily align with ophthalmic assessments. Narratives of the past, present, and envisioned future revealed that vision was embedded within participants’ biographies. Past and present complex life circumstances, including accumulated hardships, declining health, and urgent health- or caregiving-related demands, shaped how participants made sense of their vision and, consequently, whether they recognised, prioritised, or responded to their vision loss.ConclusionsOlder adults’ perceptions of vision are deeply connected to their biographies. To ensure person-centred and tailored eye care for more care-dependent older adults, this study highlights the importance of considering not only biomedical aspects when designing initiatives to detect and address vision loss, but also biographical dimensions that may otherwise overshadow vision-related challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07788-x.

Background

Globally, more than 2.2 billion people live with vision loss, eye disease, or blindness, and this number is expected to increase with population ageing [1, 2]. Vision loss affects not only the ability to see but can also have substantial negative consequences for everyday functioning, quality of life, mobility, independence, social life, and mental health [3–7]. However, failure to recognise vision loss and to seek eye care is common among older adults, particularly those aged ≥ 75 years, resulting in many cases remaining undiagnosed [1, 8, 9]. Consequently, eye conditions such as refractive error, glaucoma, cataracts, and age-related macular degeneration (AMD) frequently go undetected, despite being readily detectable and, in many cases, treatable [8–15]. This is partly due to the stealthy progression of several eye conditions, as well as beliefs that poor vision is a natural part of ageing [1, 7, 14, 16]. Such belief may reflect dominant cultural narratives [17] of ageing that portray older age as decline [18, 19], albeit many cases of vision loss among older adults can be prevented, reduced, or treated through timely detection and access to eye care services [1, 3, 8]. Globally, novel efforts to promote timely detection of vision loss among underserved older adults have been initiated, including community-based interventions [14, 15]. While these initiatives have demonstrated to hold some potential to identify older adults living with vision loss, several barriers have been identified, including poor uptake and loss to follow-up among individuals with poor general health, frailty, and dependency [14, 15]. Consequently, a recent systematic review on community-based interventions aimed at detecting visual impairment among older adults has called for qualitative studies to develop a more comprehensive understanding of the lived experiences of underserved older populations, with the aim to increase reach and effectiveness of future interventions [14].

To understand experiences of vision loss in later life, narratives that capture both biographical trajectories and everyday life contexts are needed. Biography may be understood as a mosaic of multiple, contextual, and sporadic experiences unfolding across the lifetime, encompassing past, present, and future [20], and can be particularly useful for understanding how functional decline and chronic illness are understood and managed in older age [17]. The concept of ‘biographical disruption’ introduced by Bury [21], emphasises the importance of biography in relation to chronic illness. Bury suggested that the progression of chronic illness acts as a disruptive experience in everyday life due to the sudden exacerbations and bodily changes. This argument was based on his work with younger individuals living with rheumatoid arthritis, a condition typically associated with older age, which appeared to play an important role in illness being experienced as disruptive [21]. Conversely, research focusing on older adults has demonstrated that long-term conditions are not per se experienced as biographical disruption, particularly when illness is perceived as a normal part of ageing [22]. Rather, Faircloth et al. [22] propose the concept ‘biographical flow’ to better unfold how illness experiences are integrated into ageing. Within biographical flow, illness becomes embedded within older adults’ ongoing biography, alongside other health concerns and demands of everyday life [22].

While the notion of long-term conditions as biographical flow has been studied in other illnesses [22–25], to our knowledge this concept has not been applied to understand vision loss among older adults who have not recognised or prioritised their vision loss. By applying the concept of biographical flow, this study aims to explore the meaning of vision loss in the context of biography and everyday life among older adults aged ≥ 75 years who have lived with unrecognised vision loss. Such insights may deepen our understanding of vision in later life and inform the development of more person-centred and tailored approaches to eye care and timely detection that better reflect what matters to underserved older adults [14].

Methods

Design and setting

This Danish qualitative study using ethnographic methods was nested within a larger interdisciplinary Scandinavian research-innovation project aiming to improve timely detection of vision loss among community-dwelling older adults [56]. In the Danish context, a novel home-based detection initiative to identify unrecognised vision loss was conducted among older adults receiving home care services and residing in a provincial municipality in Region Zealand. During the detection phase, novel strategies were employed, including mobile technology and simple, non-invasive methods such as assessments of visual function and fundus photography. Older adults detected with vision loss, defined as presenting visual acuity of ≤ 0.63 (Snellen decimal) in the better-seeing eye, were invited to a full ophthalmic assessment at an ophthalmology department at a university hospital in Region Zealand, Denmark. The qualitative study reported here was conducted alongside the Danish detection phase and included older adults invited to the ophthalmic assessment following home-based detection.

In Denmark, healthcare is tax funded and free of charge for all residents. Eye care is accessed through multiple entry points [26]. Optometrists typically operate in privately owned stores outside the public healthcare system and provide visual acuity testing as well as selected diagnostic examinations. Due to the widespread availability of optician stores, optometrists may constitute the first point of contact for individuals experiencing visual changes [27]. However, they are not permitted to make medical diagnoses or refer patients to secondary care. General ophthalmologists function as independent, tax-funded providers in primary care and are accessible without referral [26], whereas hospital-based ophthalmology services require referral from a general ophthalmologist or a general practitioner. The provincial municipality in Region Zealand where the study was conducted, covers both urban and rural areas. The municipality is characterized by a lower density of ophthalmologist than the national average [26], potentially limiting timely access to ophthalmic care due to geographical distance and longer waiting times.

Participant recruitment

Participant recruitment took place between June 2024 and April 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 75 years, and (2) Snellen decimal visual acuity ≤ 0.63, identified during the home-based detection phase. Exclusion criteria were: (1) contact with an ophthalmologist within the preceding two years; (2) a diagnosis of dementia and/or Alzheimer’s disease, or (3) declining participation.

A participant information package, tailored to individuals with vision loss, was provided during the home-based detection phase and again at the ophthalmic assessment and/or in-depth interview.

In total, 15 older adults were consecutively recruited by ANJ upon presentation to the department (n = 13) or via telephone contact (n = 2). At participants’ request, six interviews were conducted as dyadic interviews with a relative participating. Data collection

For participants who attended the ophthalmic assessment, fieldwork began with participant observation, with an experienced qualitative researcher, ANJ, accompanying participants throughout assessments and imaging procedures. In addition, informal interviews were conducted between assessments, consisting of conversations about vision, vision experiences, and everyday life [28]. The aim of the observations and informal interviews was to gain insight into clinical encounters alongside building rapport with participants. Subsequently, in-depth interviews were conducted by ANJ in participants’ homes: nine individual interviews and six dyadic interviews (participant-relative dyads). Interviews lasted a mean of 1.22 h (range 33 min-2.07 h). A flexible interview guide was used, covering the topics: introduction (health and everyday life), retrospective and present vision experiences, vision-related challenges (social, functional, mental), unmet needs, and future aspects. Observations of the physical home environment were conducted to gain a deeper understanding of everyday life, including the interaction between vision loss and the physical context of the home, such as interior layout, lighting conditions, and visual aids. Study design

Data analysis

Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Inspired by Riessman [29], an abductive narrative-thematic analysis was carried out using an iterative approach. Initially, all transcripts and fieldnotes were read and re-read. Subsequently, ANJ conducted a narrative analysis by chronologically organising the transcripts into condensed individual narratives, keeping participants’ stories as a coherent whole, and focussing on the “what” and “when”. Next, during an analysis workshop involving the interdisciplinary author group (researchers, an ophthalmologist, an optometrist, and a medical student), the “what” and “why” were explored using a more thematic analytical approach, identifying recurring patterns across narratives while engaging in interpretation. The analytical process enabled the development of a rich ethnographic understanding of the individual narrative while also shedding light on patterns across participants. Data were managed using NVivo (version 14). In accordance with the narrative analytical approach [29], we aimed to provide nuanced insights into the complex data material by presenting rich data from eight participant narratives to exemplify general patterns across stories, while acknowledging that each participant has a unique biography. Findings are reported in accordance with the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ) (Appendix 1).

Ethics

All participants received written and verbal information during recruitment and again before the interview which included a pamphlet conveying essential information on vision loss, common eye diseases, and available support. Written informed consent was obtained. During the interview debriefing, participants were informed that they could contact the first author by telephone at any time should they have further questions and concerns. Further, as part of the larger study, an advisory board was established to provide expert input. The board comprised relevant patient organizations, experts, and a local vision rehabilitation centre. The centre offers guidance, counselling, and access to assistive devices. Participants who could benefit from these services were informed about this opportunity. The study is registered in the Region of Zealand's Research Register (REG-021-2023). The Danish Data Protection Agency (2023-212-4736) and the Ethics Committee for the Region of Zealand (EMS-2023-06002) were consulted and deemed that formal ethical approval was not necessary according to Danish research guidelines, as the study did not involve data sharing or biological material. The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, Danish Data Protection Authority's guidelines, the Danish Code of Conduct and the General Data Protection Regulation [30].

Key findings

Based on existing vision research, we expected the narratives to mainly centre on vision-related social, functional, and mental challenges in everyday life. Interestingly, vision did not emerge as a central concern in the narratives of most, though not all, participants. Rather, participants provided extensive accounts of past and present experiences that initially appeared unrelated to vision. However, as their narratives unfolded, these accounts revealed how biography shaped their experiences of vision loss, and that participants’ vision loss was often difficult for them to recognise. In addition, participants oscillated between narratives of the past, present, and future, with vision woven into these temporal layers. Consequently, participants embedded their often unrecognised vision loss within an ongoing biographical flow, through which vision was understood in relation to older age, past experiences, present life circumstances, and future expectations. These themes are elaborated below.

The ageing context

Most participants perceived their vision as good. In their narratives, vision occupied a less prominent position than expected, and participants did not describe vision-related challenges as salient in their everyday life. Upon closer examination, however, accounts of everyday vision-related challenges emerged: reading, watching television, driving, recognising acquaintances in public spaces, and gardening. Thus, despite being backgrounded, vision loss did affect participants’ daily activities. This was reflected in accounts of compensatory strategies, such as enlarging text on tablets or mobile phones or using magnifying glasses or spectacles. Nonetheless, vision loss often remained ‘hidden’ to themselves, and they frequently referred to vision loss as a natural part of ageing.

This was exemplified by Alma, a woman in her early 80s living alone. Her vision loss stemmed from undiagnosed early/intermediate AMD, retinal vein occlusion, macular hole, and cataracts. Although she had noticed declining vision, she explained that this was nothing out of the ordinary given her age: “(…) but that’s the thing about thinking ‘Oh well, you’re getting older’, right. So, I’ve heard of so many people with declined vision, so that was just it, right.”. Similar notions were expressed by David, a man in his early 80s living with his wife, Jane, in a rural area. David’s vision loss arose from undiagnosed cataracts, and he had been referred for surgery. Like Alma, he had experienced vision-related challenges, particularly when reading, but narrated: “I blame that on age too. Some things work worse with age”. By placing their vison loss within the context of ageing, participants normalised vision loss by weaving it into an ongoing age-related biographical flow, so that it became nothing out of the ordinary given their age.

Past hardships

Besides situating their vision loss within an age-related biographical flow, participants drew on their past life stories, including hardships accumulated across the lifespan. Narratives of complex and often traumatic life situations are illustrated by John, a man in his late 70s living alone in a spacious house in a rural area. Although clinically identified with undiagnosed early/intermediate AMD, cataracts, and pigment epithelial detachment (PED), a serious condition that can cause distortion or central vision loss, John described his vision as good and offered rather brief descriptions of vision and visual challenges. Instead, his stories centred on recounts of past hardships beginning in childhood: “I was kicked out of school [lower secondary education]”, he narrated. He subsequently went against his parents’ wishes and pursued a different life path than his father, which strained family relations. After starting his own family, they encountered several episodic hardships of alcohol misuse, financial struggles, and a divorce. Later, both his adult daughter and his former wife, with whom he continued to live, died from cancer: “We have four [children]… or three… Yes, we had four, but one died… [pause]. You can't do anything about that. It was also cancer. (…). So, her [daughter] and my wife, they died of cancer”. Especially the death of his former wife and the financial aftermath more recently resulted in estrangement from two of his adult sons: “The boys, I’m on bad terms with them. (…) I don’t know where they live, those two hustlers [sons]. (…) I haven’t seen them since they sold the house. I was supposed to get [sales proceeds], and I was supposed to get their mother’s share too, because we had been living together while we were married”.

Accumulated hardships featured the narratives of many participants, including Robert, a man in his late 70s living alone in a residential neighbourhood. Robert’s vision loss arose from cataracts, and he was additionally diagnosed with entropion (inward-turning eyelid), for which he was referred for surgery. Like John, Robert had encountered various hardships. Robert narrated the loss of his mother in early childhood and explained that he never knew his father: “My mother died. At that time, I lived in [the city with her]. And then I moved… I moved to my grandparents (…) and they took me in foster care at that time.”. During childhood, he developed absence seizures that worsened during adolescence, leading to residential care for young people with epilepsy. His epilepsy shaped his entire life trajectory, and although he completed high school, it prevented him from completing an apprenticeship and later from entering the workforce: “I haven’t really done much. I can’t say that. It’s because of my illness”. As a result, he relied on social benefits throughout adulthood. Despite one of Robert’s eyes appearing visibly irritated and showing signs of corneal damage, he expressed that he had no vision or eye problems apart from morning irritation:”It [eye] doesn’t always cooperate”.

The cases of John and Robert illustrate how participants’ vision loss must be understood within biographies marked by experiences such as alcohol misuse, financial struggles, bereavement, chronic and acute illness, unemployment, and family estrangement. These accumulated hardships served as a framework for evaluating new challenges. When interpreted within this framework, newly recognised vision loss and/or eye disease(s) appeared less disruptive or problematic and was instead meaningfully embedded within already difficult life trajectories.

Present long-term conditions

In addition to drawing on past accumulated hardships, participants embedded their vision within a complex web of long-term conditions that emerged from childhood onwards and shaped their present everyday lives. These conditions spanned neurological, mental, metabolic, cardiovascular, autoimmune, and oncological diseases. At the time of the interview, managing symptoms and treatments was often considered a full-time job. This was expressed by Jane, the spouse of David (introduced earlier): “[Besides vision] There have been so many other things to juggle, and when will I have time for it all? I can’t get everything done.”. Struggling with declining health, David lived with two chronic neurological conditions and had experienced multiple strokes. Both David and Jane acknowledged that Jane almost solely carried the caregiving burden, while simultaneously managing her own health and household responsibilities. The metaphor of “juggling” was common and was used to describe prioritising among multiple, often competing, demands.

For most participants, long-term conditions had accelerated in recent years, with often immediate negative consequences and bodily implications that disrupted and restricted everyday life. This was reflected in Maria’s narrative, a woman in her mid 70s who was identified with cataracts. Although her vision loss mainly stemmed from hereditary myopia, and she was one of the few participants for whom vision had been salient throughout life, her narrative centred primarily on declining health. Her stories illustrated a working life characterised by hard manual labour, which resulted in physical deterioration with osteoarthritis affecting her lower back, ankles, hips, and knees, and ultimately led to the granting of a disability pension. Less than five years prior to the interview, Maria had suffered a severe stroke. Her illness trajectory and subsequent unsuccessful rehabilitation were the first aspects she narrated upon the initial meeting. The stroke had major consequences and led to extensive care needs and a requirement for assistance both day and night. As a result, Maria had moved to a care home:“(…) I can’t do anything myself. I can sit here [in an electric wheelchair]. I can use my arm to some extent, but vacuuming and stuff like that… the toilet… and it pains me… I tell you! My upstairs neighbour is only 50 years old. He has multiple sclerosis. We often talk about how it irritates us that you can’t get up and go out and make coffee yourself. You’re completely dependent on them [staff] to make the coffee for you. (…) I can’t... I don’t cook for myself either. But I would like to be able to do just some things myself.”.

Maria’s narrative of substantial physical decline resembled those of most participants. Beyond visible physical limitations, some narratives reflected the burden of managing more urgent matters. These urgent matters often demanded participants’ immediate attention and emotional energy and thus became foregrounded in everyday life. Conversely, the often asymptomatic and gradually progressing nature of vision loss allowed it to naturally flow more seamlessly into their biographies, slipping into the background and consequently receiving lower priority. For example, some participants lived with continuing bodily symptoms and at the time of the interview, underwent diagnostic evaluations for suspected cancer, dementia, or hearing loss. These processes caused distress and were mentally exhausting.

This was illustrated by Lola, a woman in her mid 70s living alone in an apartment close to the city centre, who was undergoing a diagnostic evaluation for suspected cancer. She narrated stories of her past uterine cancer trajectory in the early 2000s, which led to a hysterectomy. This was followed by several endoscopic examinations due to constipation and rectal bleeding. Luckily, at that time, the bleeding was attributed to polyps, she narrated. Now experiencing new symptoms, she explained:“And now there's blood again. I believe it is a bit different than the other times, because now the faeces are thin. (…) But I just wanted to have it checked. And... I was down there [general practitioner] with a faeces sample on Tuesday... well, that's a week ago. [Only] Half an hour passed, then the doctor called: 'Yes, there's blood. You need a colonoscopy'. (…). And… I believe that it feels a little different than the three previous times. That’s why I said, ‘It’s cancer!’, and then my daughter said, ‘Well, colon cancer needs to be diagnosed as soon as possible’.”.

Although she experienced that the new spectacles were insufficient, her vision-related struggles were backgrounded in relation to the cancer evaluation process, which she experienced as a biographically disruptive event.

Urgent matters also related to caregiving responsibilities, requiring participants to direct mental and emotionally energy towards caring for critically ill family members. This was the case for both Lola and Maria, who at the time of the interview were deeply concerned about the terminal illness of their son-in-law and adult son, respectively. With grief, Lola narrated:“(…) [Lola starts crying] … Oh my... The crying has nothing to do with me... It’s my son-in-law, he has [neurological disease] [sobs]. Lola directed emotional energy towards her son-in-law, imagining activities and trips they might still have been able to share. Particularly his visible physical decline and her experiencing him becoming unable to perform certain tasks were foregrounded. Similarly, Maria devoted much of her mental capacity to concerns about her son. Although Maria’s restricted mobility limited her to care for her son’s practical and physical needs, her narratives emphasised the emotional support she provided.

Across the narratives, navigating disruptive concerns, such as bodily changes, acute illness, and care responsibilities, emerged as participants’ top priority. In contrast, vision loss, subtle and slowly progressing, was often backgrounded, leading some participants to struggle to notice or acknowledge their vision loss.

Visualising the future

Most narratives did not include reflections on how vision might feature in the future. However, when prompted, expectations for the coming four to six months spanned on a continuum from vision would improve to vision would worsen. The three participants who had been referred for cataract surgery anticipated improvement, particularly regarding reading. Only Lola feared deterioration, as she had already noticed declining vision prior to the interview. Nevertheless, the majority expressed no clear expectations or hoped that their vision would remain stable. Some participants described that vision was far from their biggest concern and felt unable to relate to their future vision; some therefore expected the unexpected. This was exemplified by Olivia, a woman in her early 80s living with a progressive neurological disease and using antidepressant medication. Her vision loss stemmed from early/intermediate AMD, geographic atrophy, and chronic blepharitis. Due to her neurological disease, her mobility was restricted, and she spent several hours weekly exercising to manage her symptoms. When asked about her future vision, she said: “I haven’t thought about my vision. I must admit that. Because what worries me the most is whether my mobility gets worse… I haven’t thought about my vision”. She was particularly concerned about becoming “more helpless” and needing assistance “to go to the toilet and wash yourself and things like that, right.”.

These notions were further voiced by Charlie, a man in his mid 80s whose vision loss was related to undiagnosed cataracts and who was additionally identified with ectropion (outward-turning eyelids). Over the past decade, he had experienced two transient ischemic attacks, which he described in vivid detail. Nonetheless, everything is fine now, he told, while also stating that: “You must admit it’s going backwards now [health decline]. But that’s how it is…”. Charlie further expressed that death was inevitable and perhaps near, and he said with a smile: “(…) … then you suddenly stop breathing”. As illustrated by Olivia and Charlie, many participants found it difficult to envision their future vision. Instead, they anticipated general health decline, expressed no specific expectations, or were uncertain how long a future itself might be. Consequently, vision loss was rarely given a distinct or prioritised future orientation.

Discussion

In this qualitative study, we explored the meaning of vision loss in the context of biography and everyday life among older adults aged ≥ 75 years who have lived with unrecognised vision loss. Our findings illustrate that, while all participants were clinically identified with vision loss, often stemming from underlying eye disease, their experiences of vision were frequently misaligned with the clinical findings. Rather, we show how vision loss among these older adults meaningfully flows into their pre-existing biographies. In their narratives participants drew on their entire biography and oscillated between stories of the past, present, and future, which appeared to provide a framework for how they made sense of their vision in the present, even after becoming aware of their vision loss. Consequently, the identification of their vision loss was not experienced as a disruption [21], but rather normalised and placed within the ongoing biographical flow [22] shaped by their entire lived experiences, which unfolded in various ways.

First, we found that contextualising vision loss within narratives of older age enabled participants to perceive vision loss as nothing out of the ordinary, but rather as a chronological given. These findings mirror previous studies showing that poor vision is often perceived as an inevitable part og ageing and that disability tends to be attributed less meaning in later life [1, 31–33]. We further found that some participants embedded their vision experiences within narratives of accumulated past hardships, which appeared to provide a framework or lens through which challenges were interpretation as more or less important and manageable [17]. Similar patterns have been identified across other diseases [23, 34–36], demonstrating how difficult life trajectories may shape illness experiences and enable some older adults to embed new conditions or symptoms into their biographies already marked by hardships [22, 34, 36]. As argued by Kleinman [17], illness cannot be understood solely as a biological phenomenon but is further shaped by individuals’ biographical context and enacted through narrative sense-making processes that inform present experiences. In our study, vision loss similarly became part of a ‘continuing ebb and flow’ [34] of participants’ lives in which crises were not unusual. By drawing on past experiences, participants engaged in narrative and sense-making processes [17], and we argue that, for some, experiences of vision loss were meaningfully integrated into their biographies as a way of adapting to and managing their unrecognised vision loss. Moreover, the participants were often already living with multiple long-term conditions that influenced how vision loss was (not) experienced and managed [23]. These conditions frequently had immediate negative bodily consequences and were thus ‘now-visible disease’ [21]. The visible physical features of other illnesses that had led to the body no longer working as it used to were frequently experienced as more disruptive [21, 37] compared to vision loss, that appeared more hidden. Managing more urgent matters, such as undergoing a diagnostic evaluation or coping with terminal illness among close family members, required participants’ immediate emotional and mental energy. This ‘juggling’, as described by participants, between urgent matters, long-term conditions, and attending to tasks, appointments, and responsibilities, resulted in shifting priorities, whereby conditions were foregrounded, while vision loss, with gradual deterioration and nondisruptive nature, were backgrounded. Similar findings of shifting perspectives of foregrounding and backgrounding [38] have likewise been reported in a study of osteoarthritis and comorbidity [39]. Other research similarly suggests that vision is not top-priority [14, 33, 40–42], and that prioritising is dynamic and may be a way to make everyday management easier or focusing on conditions that at present have a very negative impact [43] or is perceived as more urgent, or unpredictable [39]. Finally, our findings highlight the importance of how participants envision the future. Many narrated fears of future ill health and decline or expressed that mortality was inevitable, by which their future horizon had shrunken markedly. In line with previous research, the notions of ‘time is running out’ [44] shaped what was experienced as meaningful in the present [45], making it difficult for participants to visualise their future vision.

Our finding that complex health-related and social concerns came to surface during proactive detection of unrecognised vision loss among care-dependent older adults carries clinical relevance. Specifically, it highlights the need for health and social care to move beyond a siloed, single-disease paradigm [46] toward a more person-centred approach to eye care [1]. Such an approach should attend to the whole person, including biography, social networks, and complex health and social conditions, while recognising the importance of relational care and the need to foster trust [47]. Our findings further suggest that home-based detection initiatives should reflect the diversity of older adults’ lives and address vision loss in relation to individual needs, priorities, and preferences. In practice, this may include maintaining as much assessment and diagnostic activities as possible within community-based settings to facilitate participation and improve accessibility. Integrating biomedical assessments with an understanding of individuals’ biographies and boarder life circumstances [17] may help overcome the lack of prioritisation of eye care and potentially reduce the risk of disease progression and irreversible vision loss [48]. In line with World Health Organization recommendations [1], vision care may advantageously be integrated into multidisciplinary care models to support older adults and their relatives in recognising and acknowledging vision loss. This may involve interdisciplinary teams of ophthalmologists, optometrists, nurses, and social and care professionals [33].

This is particular important, as progression into severe vision impairment can exacerbate existing health and social challenges faced by more care-dependent older adults [3–7]. Addressing these challenges requires tailored approaches to identification, recruitment, and care provision that consider how vision loss fits within the wider often-complex puzzle of concerns that older adults try to fit together in everyday life [33, 49]. Such a person-centred approach has the potential to drive innovation within eye care and promote more inclusive clinical and social practices leading to more meaningful and tailored care [1, 50, 51]. Engaging in narrative inquiry to explore the intersection of ageing and vision loss [52] may further support clinicians in identifying and assisting those most in need. To advance person-centred care, future research should explore provider perspectives and the potential for co-creating flexible detection strategies that incorporate perspectives across patients, providers, and other stakeholders. Such approaches may strengthen home-based detection initiatives, particularly among care-dependent older populations who may be reluctant to engage due to perceived good vision and therefore do not acknowledge or accept unmet needs, or who face challenges engaging with initiatives developed primarily from a provider perspective, potentially limiting relevance and uptake.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study was the qualitative design encompassing comprehensive ethnographic data. This enabled insights into tacit and taken-for-granted knowledge and facilitated a nuanced and contextualised understanding of how older adults detected with unrecognised vision loss experienced their vision [28, 29] Further, the approach allowed for great flexibility, and although not originally intended, six interviews applied a dyadic design. This contributed to detailed descriptions and dual perspectives, which at times uncovered differing experiences of vision loss in everyday life [53]. In addition, the involvement of an interdisciplinary author group during the analysis workshop ensured multiple perspectives on data, supported reflections, intercoder reliability, and contributed to nuancing the findings [54]. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First and foremost, participants’ level of vision loss was generally mild in the better-seeing eye and therefore may not have substantially affected functional task performance. This may partly explain why vision loss was not perceived as a major priority by participants, and should be considered when interpreting the results. Second, although participants were recruited consecutively, the included participants were relatively homogeneous: all participants were born in Denmark and had similar educational backgrounds, indicating limited representation of older adults with more diverse backgrounds. However, as this study formed part of a larger project aimed at identifying undetected vision loss, the educational homogeneity may not be unexpected. Previous research has shown that higher socioeconomic status (SES), including higher educational attainment and household income, is associated with a an increased likelihood of receiving a diagnosis of glaucoma and cataract within the Danish population [55]. This may reflect greater symptom awareness and higher tendency to consult ophthalmic services among individuals with higher SES [55].

Conclusion

This qualitative study explored the meaning of vision loss in the context of biography and everyday life among older adults aged ≥ 75 years receiving home care services who were identified with unrecognised vision loss through a home-based detection initiative. Our findings highlight the need for a deeper understanding of how older adults’ perceptions of vision are deeply connected to their biographical context and broader health and social circumstances. To ensure person-centred and tailored eye care that reflects what matters to more care-dependent older adults, our study highlights the importance of considering not only biomedical aspects when designing initiatives to detect and address vision loss, but also biographical dimensions. These biographical dimensions may otherwise hinder prioritisation of eye care and potentially contribute to disease progression and irreversible vision loss.
