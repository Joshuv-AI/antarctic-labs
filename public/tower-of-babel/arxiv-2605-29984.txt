Compactly Supported Gabor Orthonormal Bases
Lukas Liehr

Abstract
. We characterize all lattices Λ ⊂ ℝ 2 \Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2} and all compactly supported functions g ∈ L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}) for which the Gabor system { e 2 ​ π ​ i ​ s ​ x ​ g ​ ( x − t ) : ( t , s ) ∈ Λ } \left\{e^{2\pi isx}g(x-t):(t,s)\in\Lambda\right\} forms an orthonormal basis for L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}) . The characterization is given in geometric terms through translation tilings and discreteness properties of lattice projections. In particular, this resolves a conjecture of Han and Wang on the non-existence of Gabor bases along specific irrational lattices. Finally, we construct Gabor bases that cannot be realized by any product set, answering a problem of Iosevich and Mayeli.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.

Let
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
and let
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a countable set such as a full-rank lattice, i.e.,
\Lambda=A{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:=\{Az:z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\}
for an invertible matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}
. The Gabor system of
g
along
\Lambda
is given by the system
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)\subset{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
which is defined by

\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda):=\left\{e^{2\pi isx}g(x-t):(t,s)\in\Lambda\right\}.

The function
g
is called the window-function of the system
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
.
The most classical example of a Gabor system with compactly supported window function that forms an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
is given by
\mathbf{G}(\mathds{1}_{[0,1]},{\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
where
\mathds{1}_{S}
denotes the characteristic function of a measurable set
S
. The property of
\mathbf{G}(\mathds{1}_{[0,1]},{\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
being an orthonormal basis follows directly from the fact that the exponential system
\{e^{2\pi inx}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(0,1)
. The question of which other compactly supported functions
g
have the property that
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis was investigated by various authors
[
23
,
1
,
6
,
14
,
12
]
and is sometimes referred to as the Fuglede–Gabor problem, in analogy with the classical Fuglede problem on bases of exponentials
[
9
,
27
,
21
,
11
]
.
This question has been studied from several different perspectives in the literature. In particular, the problem has been investigated in connection with tiling and rigidity properties of the set
\Lambda

[
14
,
15
,
25
,
20
,
1
,
6
,
10
,
12
]
, as well as in higher-dimensional and convex-geometric settings
[
10
,
4
,
5
,
19
]
. Related questions have also been considered in discrete settings
[
28
]
, over locally compact Abelian groups and fields
[
18
,
26
]
, and in the context of Schauder bases
[
2
,
16
,
22
]
.

1.2.

In the present paper we investigate the following problem: for what lattices
\Lambda
and compactly supported functions
g
does
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
form an orthonormal basis? While the existence of
some
function
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
yielding an orthonormal basis is settled by a result of Bekka
[
3
]
, the existence of a compactly supported function constitutes an open problem. This problem is further motivated by a conjecture of Han and Wang
[
14
,
15
,
20
]
as well as a question raised by Iosevich and Mayeli
[
19
]
(see the discussion below for the precise conjecture and question). In the following we give a complete answer to this problem. To do so, we denote for a set
Q\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
the projection onto the first coordinate by
\pi_{1}(Q)
,

\pi_{1}(Q)=\{a\in{\mathbb{R}}:\exists b\in{\mathbb{R}}\ \,\text{such that}\ \,(a,b)\in Q\}.

Moreover,
D(\Lambda)
denotes the density of a lattice
\Lambda
, i.e., the reciprocal of the volume of a fundamental domain of
\Lambda
. We start by characterizing all lattices for which there exists a compactly supported window function generating a Gabor orthonormal basis.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every lattice
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

there exists a compactly supported
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
such that
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}

(2)

D(\Lambda)=1
and
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)=a{\mathbb{Z}}
for some
a>0
.

The previous theorem yields a proof of the Han-Wang conjecture. Han and Wang conjectured (implicitly in
[
14
]
and explicitly in
[
15
, p. 186]
; see also
[
20
, p. 3095]
) that if
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}
, then for the lattice
\Lambda_{\alpha}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
defined by

\Lambda_{\alpha}=A{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

(1.1)

there exists
no
compactly supported
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
such that
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda_{\alpha})
is an orthonormal basis. Theorem
1.1
gives an immediate affirmation of this conjecture: since
\alpha
is irrational it follows that
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)={\mathbb{Z}}+\alpha{\mathbb{Z}}
is dense in
{\mathbb{R}}
. Hence, there exists no
a>0
such that
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)=a{\mathbb{Z}}
.

1.3.

Now suppose that
\Lambda
is a lattice with
D(\Lambda)=1
and
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)=a{\mathbb{Z}}
for some
a>0
. According to Theorem
1.1
we can find a compactly supported
g
such that
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
. The next theorem gives a characterization of all such
g
that yield a basis. Hence, in combination with Theorem
1.1
, we obtain a complete picture of when a basis exists and what windows generate the basis.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a lattice with density
D(\Lambda)=1
and
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)=a{\mathbb{Z}}
for some
a>0
. Further, let
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
be compactly supported. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis

(2)

|g|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\mathds{1}_{\Omega}
for a bounded measurable set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}
that tiles the real line with
a{\mathbb{Z}}
-translates.

1.4.

If
g
is compactly supported and
\Lambda
is a lattice with density
D(\Lambda)=1
and projection
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)=a{\mathbb{Z}}
, then from the previous statements one can further obtain that if
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis, then also
\mathbf{G}(g,A\times B)
is an orthonormal basis where
A
and
B
are the arithmetic progressions
A=a{\mathbb{Z}}
and
B=a^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Hence, although the lattice
\Lambda
need not itself have a product structure, we are able to find a product set
A\times B
so that the same window
g
generates an orthonormal basis along
A\times B
. Iosevich and Mayeli asked in
[
19
, Question 4.4]
whether there exist a function
g
and a set
\Lambda
such that
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
, while
\mathbf{G}(g,A\times B)
fails to be an orthonormal basis for every choice of sets
A,B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
. The preceding discussion shows that compactly supported windows cannot provide such an example in the lattice setting. However, the next theorem highlights that by replacing the compactly supported function
g
with a function of unbounded support, one obtains an affirmative answer. Here, we denote by
R_{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}
the rotation matrix with angle
\theta
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Lambda=R_{\theta}{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
with
\theta\not\in\frac{\pi}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then there exists
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
such that
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
but
\mathbf{G}(g,A\times B)
is not an orthonormal basis for any
A,B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
.

2.
Proof of the Han-Wang conjecture

This section is devoted to proving the Han-Wang conjecture, which we will use to prove Theorem
1.1
. Defining
T_{t}g(x)=g(x-t)
and
M_{s}g(x)=e^{2\pi isx}g(x)
, the Han-Wang conjecture can be stated as follows: if
\alpha
is irrational, then there does not exist a compactly supported
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
such that
\left\{M_{n}T_{m+\alpha n}g:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
is an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
.

2.1.
A weighted Zak transform

In the following we use the Fourier transform convention

{\mathcal{F}}g(\xi)=\widehat{g}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)e^{-2\pi ix\xi}\,dx.

We start by defining a function
D
on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}}
that can be regarded as a weighted Zak-transform of
\widehat{g}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
and let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\left\{M_{n}T_{m+\alpha n}g:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
is an orthonormal system in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Define a sequence
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
by
\gamma_{k}=e^{\pi i\alpha k(k-1)}
. Moreover, define for a.e.
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
,

D(\omega,\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{k}\widehat{g}(k+\omega)e^{2\pi ik\theta}

where the series is interpreted as an
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
-Fourier series in
\theta
. Then

D(\omega+1,\theta)=e^{2\pi i(\alpha-\theta)}D(\omega,\theta-\alpha)

(2.1)

for a.e.
(\omega,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{T}}
.
Moreover,
|D(\omega,\theta)|=1
for a.e.
(\omega,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}\times{\mathbb{T}}
.

Proof.

Define the sequences
h(\omega)=\{h_{k}(\omega)\}_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and
b(\omega)=\{b_{k}(\omega)\}_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
by

h_{k}(\omega)=\widehat{g}(k+\omega),\quad b_{k}(\omega)=\gamma_{k}h_{k}(\omega).

Since
\widehat{g}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, it follows that for a.e.
\omega\in[0,1]
, the sequence
h(\omega)
belongs to
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
. Since
|\gamma_{k}|=1
, the same is true for
b(\omega)
.
For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, define

r_{n}(\omega)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}b_{k-n}(\omega)\overline{b_{k}(\omega)}.

This series is absolutely convergent for a.e.
\omega\in[0,1]
. Indeed, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|b_{k-n}(\omega)||b_{k}(\omega)|\leq\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|b_{k-n}(\omega)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|b_{k}(\omega)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|b_{k}(\omega)|^{2}.

Since
|b_{k}(\omega)|=|h_{k}(\omega)|
, it follows from the estimate

\int_{0}^{1}|r_{n}(\omega)|\,d\omega\leq\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|h_{k}(\omega)|^{2}\,d\omega=\|g\|^{2}

that
r_{n}\in L^{1}(0,1)
. The identity

{\mathcal{F}}M_{n}T_{m+\alpha n}g(\xi)=e^{-2\pi i(m+\alpha n)(\xi-n)}{\mathcal{F}}g(\xi-n),

in combination with orthonormality and Plancherel’s theorem gives

\delta_{m,0}\delta_{n,0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi i(m+\alpha n)(\xi-n)}\widehat{g}(\xi-n)\overline{\widehat{g}(\xi)}\,d\xi.

Equivalently, we have

\delta_{m,0}\delta_{n,0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi i(m+\alpha n)\xi}\widehat{g}(\xi-n)\overline{\widehat{g}(\xi)}\,d\xi.

Periodization of the integral and using the definition of

h(\omega)
, we get

\delta_{m,0}\delta_{n,0}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi im\omega}e^{-2\pi i\alpha n\omega}e^{2\pi i\alpha n^{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi i\alpha nk}h_{k-n}(\omega)\overline{h_{k}(\omega)}\,d\omega.

The elementary identity
\gamma_{k-n}\overline{\gamma_{k}}=e^{-2\pi i\alpha nk}e^{\pi i\alpha n(n+1)}
shows that the series under the previous integral satisfies the relation

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2\pi i\alpha nk}h_{k-n}(\omega)\overline{h_{k}(\omega)}=e^{-\pi i\alpha n(n+1)}r_{n}(\omega).

Consequently,

\delta_{m,0}\delta_{n,0}=e^{\pi i\alpha n(n-1)}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi im\omega}e^{-2\pi i\alpha n\omega}r_{n}(\omega)\,d\omega.

Since the prefactor is nonzero, we obtain

\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi im\omega}\bigl(e^{-2\pi i\alpha n\omega}r_{n}(\omega)\bigr)\,d\omega=\delta_{m,0}\delta_{n,0},\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}.

We have shown above that
r_{n}\in L^{1}(0,1)
. Therefore the function

e^{-2\pi i\alpha n\omega}r_{n}(\omega)

belongs to
L^{1}(0,1)
. Hence, by uniqueness of Fourier coefficients,

e^{-2\pi i\alpha n\omega}r_{n}(\omega)=\delta_{n,0}

for a.e.
\omega\in[0,1]
. Thus, after removing one null set independent of
n
,

r_{n}(\omega)=\delta_{n,0},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

for a.e.
\omega\in[0,1]
.
For such an
\omega
, define

D(\omega,\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}b_{k}(\omega)e^{2\pi ik\theta}

as an
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
-Fourier series. Then
|D(\omega,\cdot)|^{2}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
. Its Fourier coefficients are the autocorrelations of
b(\omega)
,

\int_{0}^{1}|D(\omega,\theta)|^{2}e^{2\pi in\theta}\,d\theta=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}b_{k-n}(\omega)\overline{b_{k}(\omega)}=r_{n}(\omega)=\delta_{n,0}.

Thus all Fourier coefficients of the
L^{1}
-function
|D(\omega,\cdot)|^{2}
agree with those of the constant function
1
. By uniqueness of Fourier coefficients,
|D(\omega,\theta)|^{2}=1
for a.e.
\theta\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Consequently,
|D(\omega,\theta)|=1
for a.e.
(\omega,\theta)\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{T}}
.
Further, we have

\displaystyle D(\omega+1,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{k}\widehat{g}(k+\omega+1)e^{2\pi ik\theta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{j-1}\widehat{g}(j+\omega)e^{2\pi i(j-1)\theta}

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i(\alpha-\theta)}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{j}\widehat{g}(j+\omega)e^{2\pi ij(\theta-\alpha)}

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi i(\alpha-\theta)}D(\omega,\theta-\alpha).

Finally, since the factor
e^{2\pi i(\alpha-\theta)}
has modulus one, this identity transfers
|D|=1
from
[0,1]\times{\mathbb{T}}
to every shift
(r+[0,1])\times{\mathbb{T}}
with
r\in\mathbb{Z}
, implying that
|D(\omega,\theta)|=1
for a.e.
(\omega,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}\times{\mathbb{T}}
.
∎

For a measurable function
F:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
with polynomial growth, we denote by
T_{F}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}})
the tempered distribution induced by
F
through the pairing

\langle T_{F},\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(t)\varphi(t)\,dt,\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),

where
\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}})
denotes the Schwartz space on the real line.
Moreover, we denote by
{\mathcal{F}}:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}})\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}})
the distributional Fourier transform, which for
V\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}})
is defined by the action

\langle{\mathcal{F}}V,\varphi\rangle=\langle V,\widehat{\varphi}\rangle.

Recall that if
U\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is an open set and
V\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}})
, then
V
vanishes on
U
if for every
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
we have
\langle V,\varphi\rangle=0
. The support of
V
is denoted by
\operatorname{supp}(V)
and is defined as the complement of the set

\bigcup\left\{U\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}:U\,\text{open},\ V\ \text{vanishes on}\ U\right\}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be compact,
g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
with
\operatorname{supp}(g)\subseteq K
and let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Suppose that
\left\{M_{n}T_{m+\alpha n}g:m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\right\}
is orthonormal and let
D
be defined as in Lemma
2.1
. Then, for almost every
\theta\in{\mathbb{T}}
, the distributional Fourier transform of
\omega\mapsto D(\omega,\theta)
is supported in
-K
.

Proof.

For almost every
\theta
, the function
\omega\mapsto D(\omega,\theta)
defines a tempered distribution. Call this distribution
T_{\theta}
.
We show that
\operatorname{supp}({\mathcal{F}}T_{\theta})\subseteq-K

for a.e.
\theta
. To do so, let

U=\mathbb{R}\setminus(-K).

It suffices to prove that, for a.e.
\theta
,

\langle{\mathcal{F}}T_{\theta},\psi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\psi}(\omega)D(\omega,\theta)\,d\omega=0\qquad\text{for every }\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U).

For
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
define
I_{\psi}
via

I_{\psi}(\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\psi}(\omega)D(\omega,\theta)\,d\omega.

Since
|D|=1
almost everywhere by Lemma
2.1

and
\widehat{\psi}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, the integral defining
I_{\psi}

is absolutely convergent for almost every
\theta
, and

|I_{\psi}(\theta)|\leq\|\widehat{\psi}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}.

Hence
I_{\psi}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. Next, we determine the Fourier coefficients
\{a_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
of
I_{\psi}
.
For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, Fubini’s theorem gives

\displaystyle a_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}I_{\psi}(\theta)e^{-2\pi ik\theta}\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\psi}(\omega)\left(\int_{0}^{1}D(\omega,\theta)e^{-2\pi ik\theta}\,d\theta\right)d\omega

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\psi}(\omega)\widehat{g}(k+\omega)\,d\omega.

Here we used that, for almost every
\omega
,
D(\omega,\cdot)
is the

L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
-Fourier series whose
k
-th Fourier coefficient is

\gamma_{k}\widehat{g}(k+\omega)
.
A change of variables yields

a_{k}=\gamma_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\psi}(\omega-k)\widehat{g}(\omega)d\omega.

and by Plancherel, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\psi}(\omega-k)\widehat{g}(\omega)d\omega=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)e^{-2\pi ikx}\psi(-x)\,dx.

Since
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
, the supports of
g
and
\psi(-\cdot)
are disjoint and therefore
a_{k}=0
. Since
k
was arbitrary, we have
I_{\psi}=0
. Hence for each
fixed

\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
we have

\langle\widehat{T_{\theta}},\psi\rangle=0

for almost every
\theta
. In other words, for every
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
there exists a set
\Omega(\psi)\subseteq{\mathbb{T}}
of full Lebesgue measure such that

\langle\widehat{T_{\theta}},\psi\rangle=0,\quad\theta\in\Omega(\psi).

It remains to remove the dependence of the full measure set on
\psi
.

To do so, we argue as follows:
since
U
is open and
\sigma
-compact, choose compact sets

L_{j}=\{x\in U:|x|\leq j,\ \operatorname{dist}(x,-K)\geq 1/j\},\qquad j\in\mathbb{N},

so that every compact subset of
U
is contained in some
L_{j}
. For each

j
, the space

C_{L_{j}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}):=\{\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}):\operatorname{supp}\psi\subseteq L_{j}\}

is separable in its usual Fréchet topology. Choose a countable dense subset

\mathcal{A}_{j}\subset C_{L_{j}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, and put

\mathcal{A}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{A}_{j}.

Since countable intersections of sets of full measure are of full measure, the preceding argument shows that there exists a single
full-measure set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that

\langle\widehat{T_{\theta}},\psi\rangle=0

for every
\psi\in\mathcal{A}
and every
\theta\in\Omega
.

Fix
\theta\in\Omega
. Let
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
. Then

\operatorname{supp}\psi\subseteq L_{j}
for some
j
. Choose

\psi_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{j}
with
\psi_{n}\to\psi
in

C_{L_{j}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
{\mathcal{F}}T_{\theta}
is a distribution,
it is continuous on
C_{L_{j}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore

\langle{\mathcal{F}}T_{\theta},\psi\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle{\mathcal{F}}T_{\theta},\psi_{n}\rangle=0.

Hence
{\mathcal{F}}T_{\theta}
vanishes on
C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
. Therefore
\operatorname{supp}({\mathcal{F}}T_{\theta})\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus U=-K
, which proves the claim.
∎

2.2.
A complex analysis argument

We now invoke an argument from complex analysis, where we make use of the following well-known fact which is a direct consequence of the Hadamard factorization theorem. Recall that an entire function
H:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is said to be of exponential type, if there exists
C,c>0
such that

|H(z)|\leq Ce^{c|z|},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
H
be an entire function of exponential type with no zeros in
\mathbb{C}
. Then there exist
C\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
a\in\mathbb{C}
such that
H(z)=Ce^{az}
.

Using the previous result we obtain the following statement.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
F\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
|F(t)|=1
for almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that the distributional Fourier
transform
\widehat{F}
has compact support. Then there exist

\lambda\in\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{F})
and
c\in\mathbb{C}
, with
|c|=1
, such that
F(t)=ce^{2\pi i\lambda t}
for almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
T_{F}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}})
be the tempered distribution induced by
F
.
Let
U={\mathcal{F}}T_{F}
be the Fourier transform of
T_{F}
.
By assumption,
U
is compactly supported. Choose
\sigma\geq 0
such that

\operatorname{supp}U\subset[-\sigma,\sigma].

By the Paley-Wiener-Schwartz theorem for compactly supported distributions

[
17
, Theorem 7.3.1]
, the inverse Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}^{-1}U
is represented on
\mathbb{R}
by the restriction of an entire function

f(z):=\big\langle U(\xi),e^{2\pi iz\xi}\big\rangle,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Moreover, Paley-Wiener-Schwartz gives constants
C_{0}>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such
that

|f(z)|\leq C_{0}(1+|z|)^{N}e^{2\pi\sigma|\operatorname{Im}z|},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

In particular,
f
is an entire function of exponential type.
Since
\mathcal{F}^{-1}U=T_{F}
, the restriction of
f
to
\mathbb{R}

represents
F
as a distribution. Hence
F=f
almost everywhere. Since

|f(t)|=|F(t)|=1
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
and since
f
is continuous, the last identity improves to
|f(t)|=1
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Now define

G:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}},\quad G(z):=f(z)\overline{f(\overline{z})}.

Then
G
is an entire function and for every real
t
we have

G(t)=f(t)\overline{f(t)}=|f(t)|^{2}=1.

By the identity theorem for holomorphic functions, we have
G(z)=1
for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
In particular,
f
has no zeros in
\mathbb{C}
.
We may therefore apply Lemma
2.3
to
f
. Hence
there exist
C\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
a\in\mathbb{C}
such that

f(z)=Ce^{az},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Using
|f(t)|=1
for every real
t
, we get

1=|f(t)|=|C|e^{\operatorname{Re}(a)t},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Taking
t=0
gives
|C|=1
. Therefore,

e^{\operatorname{Re}(a)t}=1,\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

which forces
\operatorname{Re}(a)=0
. Hence
a=i\alpha
for some

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Writing
\alpha=2\pi\lambda
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and setting
c=C
, we obtain

f(t)=ce^{2\pi i\lambda t},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

with
|c|=1
. Since
F=f
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
, it follows
that

F(t)=ce^{2\pi i\lambda t}

for almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Clearly we have
\lambda\in\operatorname{supp}({\mathcal{F}}F)
.
∎

2.3.
Proof of the Han-Wang conjecture

Up to this point, all results in this section have been established for an arbitrary
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
; in particular, no irrationality assumption has been imposed. To prove the Han–Wang conjecture, we now combine the preceding results with the following observation, in which the irrationality of
\alpha
plays a crucial role.

Lemma 2.5
.

Assume
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}
.

(1)

If
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
satisfies
f(\theta)=f(\theta-\alpha)
almost everywhere, then
f
is constant almost everywhere.

(2)

If
c\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
satisfies

c(\theta+\alpha)=\kappa e^{-2\pi i\theta}c(\theta)

(2.3)

almost everywhere for some
|\kappa|=1
, then
c=0
.

Proof.

The first part of the statement is well-known, see for example the proof of
[
7
, Proposition 2.16]
.
For the second statement, let
c\in L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
and denote by
c_{m}
its
m
-th Fourier coefficient,

c_{m}=\int_{0}^{1}c(x)e^{-2\pi imx}\,dx.

Then the equality in (
2.3
) implies that the Fourier coefficients of
c
satisfy the relation

e^{2\pi im\alpha}c_{m}=\kappa c_{m+1},\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}.

Since
|\kappa|=1
, it follows that

|c_{m+1}|=|c_{m}|,\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}.

Thus, if one Fourier coefficient
c_{m}
were nonzero, then all Fourier
coefficients would have the same positive modulus, contradicting

\{c_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Hence all
c_{m}
vanish and the uniqueness theorem for Fourier coefficients implies
c=0
.
∎

In the next theorem we prove the Han-Wang conjecture.

Theorem 2.6
.

If
\alpha
is irrational then there exists no compactly supported
g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
such that
\left\{M_{n}T_{m+\alpha n}g:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.

Proof.

Suppose by contradiction that
\left\{M_{n}T_{m+\alpha n}g:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
is an orthonormal basis for some
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
with compact support. Let
K=\operatorname{supp}(g)
.

By Lemma
2.2
, for almost every
\theta
, the
function
\omega\mapsto D(\omega,\theta)
has distributional Fourier transform
supported in the compact set
-K
. Lemma
2.4

therefore implies that, for almost every
\theta
, there exist

c(\theta)\in{\mathbb{C}},\quad\lambda(\theta)\in-K,

with
|c(\theta)|=1
, such that

D(\omega,\theta)=c(\theta)e^{2\pi i\lambda(\theta)\omega}

(2.4)

for almost every
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
. This gives two functions
c(\theta)
and
\lambda(\theta)
which are defined for almost every
\theta\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

We show that
c(\theta)
and
\lambda(\theta)
are measurable, considered as functions in
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})
. Since
-K
is compact, choose
\tau>0
so
small that the map

x\mapsto e^{2\pi i\tau x}

is injective on
-K
. From (
2.4
) we obtain the relation

\int_{0}^{1}D(\omega+\tau,\theta)\overline{D(\omega,\theta)}\,d\omega=e^{2\pi i\tau\lambda(\theta)}.

The left-hand side is measurable in
\theta
. Since the exponential map above
is injective on
-K
and since
\lambda(\theta)\in-K
for almost every
\theta\in{\mathbb{T}}
, it follows that
\lambda(\theta)
is measurable. Then

c(\theta)
is measurable as well, since it can be represented in the form

c(\theta)=\int_{0}^{1}D(\omega,\theta)e^{-2\pi i\lambda(\theta)\omega}\,d\omega.

Now use the relation (
2.1
) which says that

D(\omega+1,\theta)=e^{2\pi i(\alpha-\theta)}D(\omega,\theta-\alpha).

Substituting (
2.4
), and restricting to a
full-measure set of
\theta
’s on which both
\theta
and
\theta-\alpha
have
the representation (
2.4
), gives

c(\theta)e^{2\pi i\lambda(\theta)(\omega+1)}=e^{2\pi i(\alpha-\theta)}c(\theta-\alpha)e^{2\pi i\lambda(\theta-\alpha)\omega}

(2.5)

for almost every
\omega
. Define
A_{\theta}
and
B_{\theta}
via

A_{\theta}=c(\theta)e^{2\pi i\lambda(\theta)},\quad B_{\theta}=e^{2\pi i(\alpha-\theta)}c(\theta-\alpha).

Since
|c(\theta)|=|c(\theta-\alpha)|=1
, we have
|A_{\theta}|=|B_{\theta}|=1
and

\frac{A_{\theta}}{B_{\theta}}e^{2\pi i(\lambda(\theta)-\lambda(\theta-\alpha))\omega}=1

for a.e.
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since complex exponentials are linearly independent, it follows that

\lambda(\theta)=\lambda(\theta-\alpha)

and therefore
\lambda(\theta)=\lambda(\theta-\alpha)
for a.e.
\theta\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Since
\lambda
is a measurable and bounded function, it follows from Lemma
2.5
(i) that
\lambda
is a constant function. Write
\lambda(\theta)=\lambda_{0}
for almost every
\theta
. Then (
2.5
) gives

c(\theta)e^{2\pi i\lambda_{0}}=e^{2\pi i(\alpha-\theta)}c(\theta-\alpha).

Replacing
\theta
by
\theta+\alpha
, we obtain

c(\theta+\alpha)=\kappa e^{-2\pi i\theta}c(\theta),\quad\kappa=e^{-2\pi i\lambda_{0}}.

By Lemma
2.5
(ii), the only
L^{2}
-solution
is
c=0
. This contradicts
|c(\theta)|=1
almost everywhere.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2

We begin with a structural invariance property that will be used repeatedly throughout this section (see, for instance,
[
8
]
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let the matrix
S\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}
be defined by

S=\begin{pmatrix}\mu&0\\
\nu&\mu^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mu,\nu\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad\mu\neq 0.

Moreover, let
U_{S}:{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\to{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
be the unitary operator

(U_{S}f)(t)=|\mu|^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{\pi i(\nu/\mu)t^{2}}f(t/\mu).

Then
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis if and only if
\mathbf{G}(U_{S}g,S\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis where
S\Lambda:=\{S\lambda:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
.

The previous lemma, in combination with the solution to the Han-Wang conjecture, gives the following statement.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a lattice with density
D(\Lambda)=1
. If
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)
is not discrete then there exists no compactly supported
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
such that
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
.

Proof.

Since
D(\Lambda)=1
, there exists
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}
with
\det A=1
such that
\Lambda=A{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. Suppose that
A
is given by

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}.

The projection of
\Lambda
onto the first coordinate is given by
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)=a{\mathbb{Z}}+b{\mathbb{Z}}
. This subgroup of
{\mathbb{R}}
is not discrete if and only if
a\neq 0
and
\alpha\coloneqq\frac{b}{a}\not\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. With this choice of
\alpha
we have the factorization

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
c&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Defining

L=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
c&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

it follows that
\Lambda=L\Lambda_{\alpha}
where
\Lambda_{\alpha}
is given as in equation (
1.1
). Now suppose towards a contradiction that there exists a compactly supported function
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
such that
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
. Define
S:=L^{-1}
. Then
S
is given by

S=\begin{pmatrix}a^{-1}&0\\
-c&a\end{pmatrix}.

Applying Lemma
3.1
with
\mu=a^{-1}
and
\nu=-c
it follows that
\mathbf{G}(U_{S}g,SL\Lambda_{\alpha})=\mathbf{G}(U_{S}g,\Lambda_{\alpha})
is an orthonormal basis. Since
U_{S}g
is compactly supported if and only if
g
is compactly supported, we obtain a contradiction to Theorem
2.6
.
∎

Having ruled out the non-discrete case, we now consider lattices for which
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)
is discrete. The next lemma shows that such lattices can always be represented, after a suitable change of basis, by a lower triangular matrix.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a lattice with density
D(\Lambda)=1
. If
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)
is discrete then there exists a lower triangular matrix
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}
of the form

L=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
c&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\quad a>0,

so that
\Lambda
can be represented by
\Lambda=L{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.

Proof.

Define
H=\pi_{1}(\Lambda)
. Since
\Lambda
is an additive subgroup of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, it follows that
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)
is a discrete additive subgroup of
{\mathbb{R}}
. Since every discrete additive subgroup of
{\mathbb{R}}
that contains zero is of the form
\tau{\mathbb{Z}}
, it follows that
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)=\tau{\mathbb{Z}}
for some
\tau>0
.
Now assume that
\Lambda=A{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
where

A=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}.

Since
D(\Lambda)=1
, we may choose the lattice basis matrix
A
so that
\det A=1
.
Since
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)=\alpha{\mathbb{Z}}+\beta{\mathbb{Z}}=\tau{\mathbb{Z}}
, there exist integers
m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

\alpha=\tau m,\quad\beta=\tau n.

We therefore have

\alpha{\mathbb{Z}}+\beta{\mathbb{Z}}=\tau(m{\mathbb{Z}}+n{\mathbb{Z}})=\tau\,\mathrm{gcd}(m,n){\mathbb{Z}}=\tau{\mathbb{Z}}

which implies that
\mathrm{gcd}(m,n)=1
. Since every integer can be written as a linear combination of two coprime integers, it follows that there exist
r,s\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

mr+ns=1.

Now define the matrix
U\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2\times 2}
by

U=\begin{pmatrix}r&-n\\
s&m\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\det U=1
and therefore
U
leaves
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
invariant, i.e.,
U{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}={\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. A direct calculation shows that

AU=\begin{pmatrix}\tau&0\\
\gamma r+\delta s&-\gamma n+\delta m\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\det(AU)=\det A\det U=1
and the upper-left entry is
\tau>0
,
we must have

-\gamma n+\delta m=\tau^{-1}.

Thus
L=AU
has the required form with
a=\tau
and

c=\gamma r+\delta s
.
∎

To complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we appeal to the following characterization due to Liu
[
24
]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
be compactly supported. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathbf{G}(g,{\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
is an orthonormal basis

(2)

|g|=\mathds{1}_{E}
for a bounded measurable set
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}
that tiles the real line with integer translates.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
be an orthonormal basis. By Lemma
3.3
, there exists a matrix

L=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
c&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

such that
\Lambda=L{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. Let
S=L^{-1}
, i.e.,

S=\begin{pmatrix}a^{-1}&0\\
-c&a\end{pmatrix}.

By Lemma
3.1
with
\mu=a^{-1}
and
\nu=-c
we have that
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis if and only if
\mathbf{G}(U_{S}g,{\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
is an orthonormal basis. By Theorem
3.4
, the latter holds if and only if
|U_{S}g|=\mathds{1}_{E}
for a bounded measurable set
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}
that tiles the real line with integer translates. We have that

|U_{S}g(t)|=|\mu|^{-\frac{1}{2}}|g(t/\mu)|=|a|^{\frac{1}{2}}|g(at)|

Hence, the condition that
|U_{S}g|=\mathds{1}_{E}
for a bounded measurable set
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}
that tiles the real line with integer translates is equivalent to the condition that

|g|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\mathds{1}_{\Omega}

where
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}
is a bounded measurable set that tiles the real line by
a{\mathbb{Z}}
-translates.
∎

Theorem
1.1
follows at once from the results in this section.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Suppose that
\Lambda
is a lattice and
g
is a compactly supported function such that
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis. It is well-known that in this case the density of
\Lambda
satisfies
D(\Lambda)=1
(see for example
[
19
]
). Moreover,
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)
must be discrete by Theorem
3.2
. Since
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)
is a discrete subgroup containing zero, we have
\Lambda=a{\mathbb{Z}}
for some
a>0
.

On the other hand, if
D(\Lambda)=1
and
\pi_{1}(\Lambda)=a{\mathbb{Z}}
for some
a>0
, then Theorem
1.2
implies that the choice
g=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\mathds{1}_{[0,a]}
gives an orthonormal basis
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

4.1.
Tilings of the unit cube

We begin with a result of Gabardo, Lai, and Wang characterizing Gabor orthonormal bases generated by characteristic functions of unit intervals
[
10
, Theorem 1.2]
. This theorem relates the orthonormal basis property to a geometric tiling condition in the time-frequency plane. For sets
S,T\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we say that
S+T
is a tiling if

\bigcup_{t\in T}(S+t)={\mathbb{R}}^{2}

and
(S+t)\cap(S+t^{\prime})
has Lebesgue measure zero for all
t,t^{\prime}\in T
with
t\neq t^{\prime}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Then
\mathbf{G}(\mathds{1}_{[0,1]},\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
if and only if
\mathbf{G}(\mathds{1}_{[0,1]},\Lambda)
is an orthogonal set and
[0,1]^{2}+\Lambda
is a tiling of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

The second theorem provides a structural description of all tilings of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by translates of the unit cube, see
[
10
, Proposition 3.2]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. If
[0,1]^{2}+\Lambda
is a tiling of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, then there exist
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and a sequence
\{s_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\subseteq[0,1)
with
s_{0}=0
such that
\Lambda\in\{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}\}
where

\Lambda_{1}=z+\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}({\mathbb{Z}}+s_{k})\times\{k\},\quad\Lambda_{2}=z+\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\{k\}\times({\mathbb{Z}}+s_{k}).

4.2.
Rotations of the time-frequency plane

We next discuss the behavior of Gabor systems under rotations of the time-frequency plane, where rotations are represented by the matrices

R_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\quad\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(4.1)

The corresponding action of a rotation on functions is implemented by the fractional Fourier transform. We briefly recall its definition and basic properties.

Let
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\subset{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
be the normalized Hermite basis. The fractional Fourier transform of angle
\theta
is the unitary operator defined by

{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}:{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}\to{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})},\quad\mathcal{F}_{\theta}f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-in\theta}\langle f,h_{n}\rangle h_{n},

(4.2)

where the series converges in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The operator
{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}
generalizes the ordinary Fourier transform: if
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
, then (
4.2
) is the spectral decomposition of the Fourier operator and therefore
{\mathcal{F}}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}={\mathcal{F}}
.
Moreover, we have

\mathcal{F}_{0}=I,\quad\mathcal{F}_{\pi}=\mathcal{R},\quad\mathcal{F}_{\theta+\phi}=\mathcal{F}_{\theta}\mathcal{F}_{\phi},

where
\theta,\phi\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathcal{R}f(x)=f(-x)
denotes the reflection operator.
The following lemma expresses the invariance of the orthonormal basis property under simultaneous rotation of the lattice and application of the fractional Fourier transform. For a proof of this statement we refer to
[
8
]
(see also
[
13
, Chapter 9]
).

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
g\in{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
, let
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and let
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis if and only if
\mathbf{G}(\mathcal{F}_{\theta}g,R_{-\theta}\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis.

4.3.
The Iosevich-Mayeli problem

For a set
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we define its upper Beurling density via

D^{+}(S)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\#\bigl(S\cap\mathbb{B}(x,r)\bigr)}{|\mathbb{B}(x,r)|},

where
\mathbb{B}(x,r)=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:|x-y|\leq r\}
denotes the Euclidean ball of radius
r\geq 0
around
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\theta\not\in\frac{\pi}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Further, define
g:={\mathcal{F}}_{-\theta}\mathds{1}_{[0,1]}
and
\Lambda:=R_{\theta}{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. Then the following holds:

(1)

\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
,

(2)

\mathbf{G}(g,A\times B)
is not an orthonormal basis for any
A,B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{G}(\mathds{1}_{[0,1]},{\mathbb{Z}}^{2})
is an orthonormal basis, it follows from Lemma
4.3
, that
\mathbf{G}({\mathcal{F}}_{-\theta}\mathds{1}_{[0,1]},R_{\theta}{\mathbb{Z}}^{2})=\mathbf{G}(g,\Lambda)
is an orthonormal basis.

Let
A,B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
be two arbitrary subsets of the real line. We need to show that
\mathbf{G}(g,A\times B)
is not an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
.
Assume the contrary, i.e.,
\mathbf{G}(g,A\times B)
is an orthonormal basis.
Applying the fractional Fourier transform of order
\theta
, and using Lemma
4.3
, we obtain that

\mathbf{G}(\mathds{1}_{[0,1]},R_{-\theta}(A\times B))

is an orthonormal basis for
{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}
.

According to Theorem
4.1
,
[0,1]^{2}+R_{-\theta}(A\times B)
must be a tiling for
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Theorem
4.2
implies that
R_{-\theta}(A\times B)
is of the form
\Lambda_{1}
or
\Lambda_{2}
. Suppose that it is of the form
\Lambda_{1}
(the case where it is of the form
\Lambda_{2}
can be treated in a similar way), i.e.,

R_{-\theta}(A\times B)=z+\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}({\mathbb{Z}}+s_{k})\times\{k\}

(4.3)

where
z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
\{s_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\subseteq[0,1)
with
s_{0}=0
. It follows that for every
a\in A
and
b\in B
we have

-a\sin\theta+b\cos\theta\in z_{2}+{\mathbb{Z}}.

Fixing some
b_{0}\in B
we obtain from the previous relation that the set
A
satisfies the inclusion

A\subseteq\frac{-z_{2}+b_{0}\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}{\mathbb{Z}}.

Here we used the assumption
\theta\not\in\frac{\pi}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
which gives
\sin\theta\neq 0
and
\cos\theta\neq 0
. Hence,
A
is contained in an arithmetic progression with spacing
\frac{1}{|\sin\theta|}
. An analogous argument shows that
B
is contained in an arithmetic progression with spacing
\frac{1}{|\cos\theta|}
. From this, we obtain that the upper Beurling density of
A\times B
satisfies the bound

D^{+}(A\times B)\leq|\sin\theta\cdot\cos\theta|\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Since the upper Beurling density is rotation invariant, we obtain
D^{+}(R_{-\theta}(A\times B))\leq\frac{1}{2}
. But
D^{+}(\Lambda_{1})=1
, which gives a contradiction to the set-equality (
4.3
). Therefore,
\mathbf{G}(g,A\times B)
cannot be an orthonormal basis. Since
A,B
were arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

According to the discussion in the introduction, the function
g
in Theorem
4.4
cannot be compactly supported. This can be seen directly, when working with the integral representation of
{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}
, which says that if
\theta\not\in\frac{\pi}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
then for every
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
one has

{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}f(\xi)=\gamma_{\theta}|\sin\theta|^{-1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\pi i\left(\frac{\xi^{2}+y^{2}}{\tan\theta}-\frac{2\xi y}{\sin\theta}\right)}f(y)\,dy.

(4.4)

This integral formula and related representations can be found, for instance, in Folland’s book
[
8
]
.
From (
4.4
), we see that
{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}f(\xi)
is, up to a dilation and multiplication with a non-vanishing function, the Fourier transform of the compactly supported function

y\mapsto\mathds{1}_{[0,1]}(y)e^{\frac{\pi iy^{2}}{\tan\theta}}

which arises as a product of
\mathds{1}_{[0,1]}
and a quadratic exponential. Hence,
{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}\mathds{1}_{[0,1]}
has unbounded support whenever
\theta\not\in\frac{\pi}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
.
