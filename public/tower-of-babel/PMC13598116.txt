A high-resolution, two-millennium monthly downscaled climate dataset for North America
Authors: P. R. Guaita, P. Crippa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long-term regional climate projections are critical for validating and implementing regional impact assessments and for capturing slow processes in ecological models. Earth System Model (ESM) simulations are the primary source of climate data, but their coarse resolution, systematic biases, and limited representation of uncertainty restrict their applicability. Here, we present DPastCliM-NA, a bias-corrected, high-resolution (0.2° × 0.2°) monthly reconstruction of temperature and precipitation for North America over the past 2000 years, with explicit uncertainty estimates. The dataset is derived by statistically downscaling MPI-ESM1.2-LR simulations against the GHCN-m observational dataset for 1875–2014. Downscaling is performed using Principal Component Regression, and incorporates a model selection strategy to prevent overfitting. Spatiotemporal dependencies in the residuals are captured through spatial error models and autoregressive–moving-average processes. The resulting reconstructions show substantial improvements over raw ESM output, while preserving the long-term climate trends and variability represented by the ESM.

Background & Summary

Temperature and precipitation are fundamental drivers of both human and natural systems. Their variability has been extensively documented through direct and remotely sensed measurements since the industrial era, providing crucial insights into modern climate change1,2. To understand long-term pre-industrial climate dynamics and provide a baseline for anthropogenic warming, paleoclimate proxies have been instrumental in reconstructing climate records over past centuries3–5. These reconstructions, notably those covering the last two millennia, have provided essential evidence for the rapid, human-driven global warming observed over the last 150 years6,7.

While Earth System Models (ESMs) are crucial for projecting future climate, they face significant challenges. The models are often calibrated using relatively short modern datasets, limiting their ability to accurately simulate, for example, long-term ecosystem shifts that unfold over centuries8. In this sense, paleoclimate simulations are essential for testing model performance beyond the scientific measurements era, as coordinated by the Paleoclimate Modeling Intercomparison Project (PMIP)9. Despite these advances, the ESMs used in these experiments are typically characterized by coarse grid spacing (100–200 km horizontal resolution), often contain systematic biases10, and do not have uncertainty estimates. These features preclude their direct applicability in regional climate impact assessment studies and their integration into ecological models, as the relevant processes (e.g., droughts, ecotone shifts) happen at much finer scales and could be significantly affected by biases11,12.

To overcome these limitations, downscaling techniques are employed to refine ESM output to finer spatial resolutions and correct systematic biases. Downscaling methods can be broadly categorized as dynamical or statistical approaches13. While dynamical downscaling provides physically based, high-resolution simulations, its computational expense limits its application to short time periods. In contrast, statistical downscaling is computationally efficient, establishing empirical relationships between large-scale variables and local-scale observations to refine climate model outputs. Most statistical downscaling methods define an empirical link (transfer function) between a set of predictors (modeled data to be downscaled) and a set of predictands (usually observed data), and they can be classified under different categories depending on the employed methodology14. Transfer function methods range from simple linear regression to more advanced machine-learning approaches15,16. These methods are often applied in the context of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 5 and 6 (CMIP5 and CMIP6) ensembles to correct systematic biases and enhance spatial resolution for regional climate impact studies17,18.

At the time of writing, two long-term, high-resolution global paleoclimate reconstructions are publicly available: CHELSA-TraCE21k, which uses a statistical downscaling method based on topography and ice cover19,20, and PaleoClim, which uses the delta method21,22. Both products offer multi-year climatological averages rather than continuous high-frequency time series. While these products offer very high spatial resolution, the resulting large data volumes limit their temporal resolution. Furthermore, these models do not directly provide uncertainty estimates due to their inherently deterministic methodology. Typically, other regional downscaled products available at the paleoclimatic scale exhibit similar limitations, providing outputs at decadal to millennial temporal resolutions and at a spatial resolution of 0.5° × 0.5°23–25.

This study presents a set of statistically downscaled monthly mean reconstructions of 2m air temperature (tas) and precipitation (pr) for the past 2000 years over North America26, generated by combining ESM simulations and observational datasets. A key feature of our product is the explicit inclusion of uncertainty estimates. Reconstructions are provided at individual measurement stations, allowing end-users to regrid the data to a resolution of their choice. A gridded product is also available; to limit storage requirements for continuous monthly reconstructions, it is provided at a coarser spatial resolution (0.2° × 0.2°) than some existing products, but retains sufficient detail for a wide range of applications.

Methods

The Principal Component Regression (PCR)27 is a type of Model Output Statistics13 approach. This technique is not only a spatial downscaling method - i.e., it refines an initial product from a coarse spatial resolution to a finer target resolution - but also corrects systematic biases in the mean climatology and variability of ESM simulations by directly linking simulations to observational data. Once calibrated over present times, the PCR can then be applied to climate simulations spanning past centuries.

PCR employs first a principal component analysis27 on the climate model output. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) decomposes the model output into eigenvectors (spatial patterns, or spatial modes) and scores (temporal coefficients), which are then used in a regression model against the observed data. However, not all eigenvectors and scores need to be included in the downscaling model. In this study, a model selection process is employed to retain only those modes that contribute to a reduced Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE). The PCR is calibrated locally, on a station-by-station basis, and separately for each month. Calibration is conducted at the station level because long-term, homogeneous gridded observational datasets at monthly resolution are often either regionally restricted or cover shorter time periods than station observations (1875–2014 in this study). Furthermore, using station data allows direct use of quality-controlled observations without introducing artifacts from prior spatial aggregation; interpolation to the target grid is performed only after downscaling. As a result, a given ESM is associated with (12 × number of stations) distinct downscaling models. These models are then applied to the selected time periods using ESM simulation data specific to each station location, producing downscaled ESM time series at each station. Finally, a gridded downscaled product over the entire domain is obtained by spatially interpolating these time series onto the target grid. Datasets

GHCN-m observations

Surface air temperature and precipitation from the Global Historical Climatology Network-monthly (GHCN-m) dataset version 4 are used as predictors in the proposed downscaling approach28,29. These datasets contain thousands of land station timeseries worldwide, which are processed via automated pairwise homogenization and undergo extensive quality assurance29–31. For the purposes of this study, the dataset is filtered over North America (lon = [−175°, −50°], lat = [5°,72°]) and for the years between 1875 and 2014 (Fig. 2). Furthermore, timeseries shorter than 20 years for both temperature and precipitation are removed entirely. The filtered dataset is divided into calibration, model selection, and testing subsets. Initially, of the 140 years in the record, 50 years are randomly assigned to model selection, 15 years to testing, and the remaining 75 years to calibration. However, to maintain reliable calibration statistics, if a station’s calibration period is shorter than 30 years, the entire time series of that station is assigned to testing. ESM data: MPI-ESM1.2-LR

The simulations considered in this study are from the experiments past2k32 and historical33 from the model MPI-ESM1.2-LR34,35, which is part of the PMIP49, one of the components of CMIP633. MPI-ESM1.2-LR includes coupled atmosphere, ocean, sea-ice, and land-surface processes. Its atmospheric component has a horizontal resolution of 1.875° × 1.8496° (~150–200km) and 47 vertical levels extending to the top of the atmosphere (~0.01hPa). The experiment past2k covers 0–1849 CE32, and is a transient simulation incorporating various forcings derived from reconstructed or observed datasets, including volcanic aerosols, greenhouse gas variability, land-use changes, and orbital variations, with a focus on key climate periods such as the Medieval Climate Anomaly, the Little Ice Age, and the Roman Warm Period. Other than the past2k experiment, the historical experiment (covering 1850–2014 CE) is included in this study in order to calibrate the downscaling model over the historical period. Temperature and precipitation fields for both experiments were obtained at monthly resolutions. MPI-ESM1.2-LR is selected because it provides a continuous, internally consistent monthly simulation spanning both the PMIP4 past2k (0–1849 CE) and historical (1850–2014 CE) experiments, allowing calibration and reconstruction within a unified modeling framework. At present, among PMIP4 models, only MPI-ESM1.2-LR provides monthly-resolved output covering the full two-millennium period required by our PCR-based downscaling framework.

Downscaling with PCR

In this work, we define a target downscaling grid over North America with a spacing of 0.20° × 0.20° (626 × 336 grid points, longitude × latitude). This horizontal resolution is compatible with the goal of a fine-scaled product with a monthly timestep, while retaining computational feasibility and manageable storage dimension. Henceforth we define Nt as the number of timesteps, Ns as the number of stations (6, 889 for tas and 11, 546 for pr), Ng as the number of nodes in the target grid (626 ⋅ 336 = 210, 336), and Ne as the number of selected eigenvectors/scores. The data value at a given timestep t and location x is denoted by M(x, t) or O(x, t) depending on whether it originates from the Earth System Model (ESM) or the observational dataset, respectively. The indexes are sometimes omitted for brevity: M(x) and O(x) refer to the full time series at location x. When both the time and location indices are omitted, M and O represent the entire dataset.

The first step of the PCR (henceforth denoted as PCR-downscaling) consists in regridding the ESM maps from the historical experiment to the target downscaling resolution via spline interpolation to ensure consistency. More specifically, an Akima cubic Hermite interpolation is used, modified to reduce undulations by assigning greater weight to the side with the slope closer to zero in the local derivative estimates36,37. This spline-based interpolation is used at this stage to preserve large-scale gradients and ensure smooth transitions in the regridded ESM fields prior to PCA decomposition. By definition, each row of the anomaly matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\bf{M}}}^{{\prime} }$$\end{document}M′ sums to zero. Consequently, the maximum rank of this matrix is Nt − 1, and decomposition through PCA can lead to identifying up to Nt − 1 eigenvectors. Usually, only a subset of them is selected, and a common approach consists in choosing the leading modes in terms of explained variance. However, selecting the eigenvectors according only to their explained variance does not necessarily correspond to a general improvement in model performances27. For this reason, in this study, the eigenvectors/scores selection is guided by a criterion based on root mean square error (RMSE), rather than just on explained variance. Initially, all modes explaining at least 10% of the total variance are included. Iteratively, for each mode with explained variance above 0.001% that was not included, a new model with the new mode adjoint is calibrated again, and a mean RMSE is computed by averaging the RMSE from the evaluation of 50 generated realizations (new samples) against the model selection dataset. The number 50 is chosen to balance computational efficiency with estimate reliability. At the end of every iteration, each mode is thus associated with one RMSE value. The eigenvector/score associated with the lowest final RMSE is added to the set of the selected modes if it leads to a RMSE reduction by at least 1%. Otherwise, the iterative process stops. This methods allows up to a total of 16 modes to be selected.

After calibration and model selection, the PCR models are applied to the ESM outputs from the past2k simulations to generate downscaled climate anomaly fields at each station location (Eq. (5)). These downscaled anomalies are then adjusted to reflect the long-term climatic trends simulated by the ESM. In particular, the mean values from the downscaled estimates are corrected using the difference (for temperature; Eq. (6)) or the ratio (for precipitation; Eq. (7)) between the ESM’s 30-year moving mean and its mean over the calibration period.

To obtain individual realizations at each timestep, the term u(x) needs to be explicitly modeled to provide spatiotemporal coherence in the downscaled series, as the residuals display spatial and temporal dependencies. First, their spatial correlation is modeled using a Spatial Error Model (SEM; Eq. (10)), which captures the spatial autoregressive structure that the residuals at nearby stations display. The resulting residuals are then further modeled in time using an Autoregressive Moving Average model (ARMA; Eq. (11)). This model is used to generate continuous time series of temporally correlated residuals extending across the full two-millennia reconstruction period. These temporal error series are then transformed back through the spatial error model, reintroducing the appropriate spatial correlation structure to produce timeseries with a spatiotemporal structure inferred from observations.

After modeling the residuals, the downscaled values at individual stations are interpolated onto the 0.20° × 0.20° target grid. Natural neighbor interpolation is used to perform this step, as it is computationally efficient, locally adaptive, and well suited for interpolating irregularly spaced station data to a regular grid without imposing excessive smoothing. Because interpolation tends to smooth spatial variability, the resulting gridded fields generally exhibit lower variance than the original ESM fields. To correct this, the anomalies of the downscaled variables (relative to a 30-year moving mean) are rescaled to match the moving standard deviation of the ESM over the same time window (Eq. (12)). Finally, a land mask (rescaled to the target resolution39) is applied to the final downscaled products.

Prediction intervals (PIs) are defined here in the statistical sense as interval estimates for a single realization of the PCR-downscaled variable. They are constructed via Monte Carlo simulations of the regression residuals, assuming Gaussian errors with variance equal to the model’s mean squared error. For each time step, random residuals are added to the fitted regression values. The resulting values are then transformed back to the physical variable scale, and the empirical 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the simulated distribution are taken as the bounds of the 95% PI.

As with all transfer-function–based statistical downscaling approaches, the PCR framework assumes that the relationship between large-scale model modes and local-scale anomalies estimated during the calibration period (1875–2014 CE) remains valid when applied to earlier centuries. In this study, the regression is performed on anomaly fields rather than absolute values, and long-term changes in mean and variance simulated by the ESM are explicitly reintroduced through the moving mean adjustment (Eqs. (5)–(8)) and variance rescaling (Eq. (12)). Consequently, the assumption concerns the stability of the relationship between large-scale circulation modes and local anomalies, rather than stationarity of absolute climate statistics.

Validation metrics

Other than the PCR, a linear regression (LR) defined by using raw temperature and precipitation as predictors is also used for downscaling (LR-downscaling) for benchmarking. The performance of the PCR-downscaled and LR-downscaled products are evaluated across multiple aggregation levels. Timestep-level metrics are computed by comparing the individual monthly timesteps of the downscaled products with observations from the GHCN-m testing dataset. The metrics falling within this category are: mean bias (MB; Eq. (13)), which measures whether the model underestimates or overestimates the observations; mean absolute error (MAE; Eq. (15)), which provides a measure of the overall model accuracy; Kling-Gupta efficiency (KGE; Eq. (17)), which summarizes correlation, bias, and variability into a single index and ranges between − ∞ and 1, with perfect prediction at 1. Station-level metrics are computed by first averaging both model outputs and GHCN-m observations at each station over the testing dataset, and then comparing these station-level means. The metrics in this category are: MB (Eq. (14)); MAE (Eq. (16)); Pearson correlation (r; Eq. (18)), which quantifies the spatial correlation between the station-averaged model outputs and observations. Both timestep- and station-level metrics are calculated for the full year and for individual seasons (MAM, JJA, SON, DJF). For comparison purposes, we also evaluate CHELSEA-TraCE21k19,20 using station-level metrics. This product provides only monthly climatologies averaged over centennial periods; therefore, we selected the most recent available climatology (1900–1990) and evaluated temperature and precipitation over the GHCN-m testing dataset.

For each timestep-level and station-level metric, we test the null hypothesis of no difference between PCR and LR. Specifically, for the timestep-level metrics, we implemented a space–time block bootstrap procedure in order to account for both spatial and temporal dependence in the data. In this approach, observations are resampled in blocks defined by spatial clusters and temporal windows, and the difference in metrics between PCR and LR is recomputed for each bootstrap sample. Then, we estimate the two-sided bootstrap p-values for the differences in MB, MAE, and KGE between the two downscaling methods. For both temperature and precipitation, we use 1,000 bootstrap samples, 10 spatial blocks identified through k-means on the station coordinates, and temporal blocks of the size of 24 months. Instead, for the station-level metrics, statistical significance is assessed using parametric tests. For MB and MAE, we perform paired two-sided t-tests on the station-level errors (and absolute errors for MAE) obtained from PCR and LR, testing whether their mean difference is significantly different from zero. For the Pearson correlation coefficient, differences between PCR and LR are assessed using the Fisher r-to-z transformation.

Finally, we calculate domain-level metrics, i.e., metrics that are computed either from gridded fields, or from spatially aggregated domain averages. Namely, we evaluate first the MB (Eq. (19)). Then, extremes in temperature and precipitation are evaluated by calculating the 10th and 90th percentiles for the modeled and the observed gridded products, and then the differences between the percentiles are computed (hereafter denoted Δ10th and Δ90th perc.). Finally, the structural similarity index metric (SSIM) is calculated between the mean modeled and observed gridded fields. SSIM is a widely used metric, originally developed for image quality assessment and more recently applied in climatic studies. For the mathematical formulation of this metric we refer to 40,41. SSIM evaluates the similarity between two images in terms of luminance, contrast and structural similarity, and ranges from 0 (no similarity) to 1 (perfect similarity).

To summarize the comparative performance of the PCR- and LR-downscaling methods relative to the original ESM, we compute skill scores42. For each accuracy metric A, the skill score SSA expresses the fractional improvement (in percent) of the downscaled product relative to the ESM baseline, normalized by the difference between the ESM and a hypothetical perfect prediction. Positive values indicate improvement over the ESM, while negative values indicate degradation. Because the various performance metrics differ in their scales and units, they are combined into a Mean Skill Score (MSS; Eq. (21)) using a weighted average. Each metric is weighted according to the mean of its absolute values across all seasons and the annual evaluation, ensuring balanced contributions from all metrics. The MSS results in a percentage ranging between −∞ and 100%, which represents the percentage improvements (or degradations, when negative) that the PCR- and the LR-downscaled products achieved with respect to the total possible theoretical improvement between the original ESM and a perfect predictor. The MSS is calculated for timestep-level metrics first, and then jointly for station-level metrics and SSIM.

Residual Modeling

After defining the deterministic component of the downscaling model, we next describe the stochastic residual modeling framework used to generate spatiotemporally coherent realizations. In order to explicitly model the space-time dependency of the error term u(x), residuals are first sampled at the location x of every calibration station: 9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bf{r}}({\bf{x}},t)=g{({\bf{O}}({\bf{x}},t))}^{{\rm{{\prime} }}}-{\mathbb{E}}(g{(\hat{{\bf{O}}}({\bf{x}},t))}^{{\rm{{\prime} }}})$$\end{document}r(x,t)=g(O(x,t))′−E(g(O^(x,t))′)Sampled residuals over calibration stations contain gaps in their timeseries. To fill these gaps, timeseries are gapfilled from other available calibration stations by thin-plate spline interpolation43. Thin-plate splines are employed here because they provide a smooth surface representation suitable for irregularly spaced station data while minimizing artificial local oscillations. As the observational network becomes sparser further back in time, complete timeseries were reconstructed using this method for the years 1915-2014, instead of the whole period 1875-2014.

The error timeseries ε(x) at each calibration station are modeled in time using an Autoregressive Moving Average model with p autoregressive and q moving average terms (ARMA(p, q)): 11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon ({\bf{x}},t)=\mathop{\sum }\limits_{i=1}^{p}{\varphi }_{i}\,\varepsilon ({\bf{x}},t-i)+\eta ({\bf{x}},t)+\mathop{\sum }\limits_{j=1}^{q}{\theta }_{j}\,\eta ({\bf{x}},t-j)$$\end{document}ε(x,t)=∑i=1pφiε(x,t−i)+η(x,t)+∑j=1qθjη(x,t−j)with φi and θj the autoregressive and moving average coefficients, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta ({\bf{x}},t) \sim {\mathcal{N}}\left(0,{\sigma }^{2}({\bf{x}})\right)$$\end{document}η(x,t)~N0,σ2(x) the i.i.d. Gaussian innovation at grid node x and timestep t. Predictions for each calibration station location for the past are initialized assuming ε(x, 0) = 0 and η(x, 0) = 0. Finally, the ε(x, t) values are transformed back through the SEM to obtain the modeled residuals across the two millennia for every calibration station. For temperature, p = 1 and q = 2, while for precipitation p = 1 and q = 1. These values were determined by identifying the lag at which the median partial autocorrelation and autocorrelation across timeseries ε(x) crossed the 0.05 threshold.

A skill score for a certain accuracy metric A42 can be defined as: 20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S{S}_{A}=100\cdot \frac{{A}_{t}-{A}_{r}}{{A}_{p}-{A}_{r}}$$\end{document}SSA=100⋅At−ArAp−Ar where At is the accuracy of the target prediction (PCR- or LR-downscaled product), Ar is the accuracy of the reference prediction (ESM), and Ap is the accuracy of a perfect prediction.

The weight (wA) for each metric A is given by the mean of all absolute values of that metric across the full year and the four seasons. The MSS results in a percentage ranging between −∞ and 100%, representing the percentage improvements (or degradations, when negative) that the PCR- and LR-downscaled products achieved with respect to the total possible theoretical improvement between a perfect predictor and the original ESM.

The DPastCliM-NA product shows consistent improvements across all metrics and for both variables compared to the original ESM. For temperature, the largest improvements are observed in the MB. In particular, the MB reaches negligible values between − 0.27 and 0.38 °C, depending on the season, whereas the ESM shows MB values between −1.74 and −0.48 °C. This demonstrates the ability of the PCR method to correct the systematic cold bias of the ESM, leading to nearly unbiased temperatures on average. MAE and KGE are also improved (1.83–2.26 °C and 0.66–0.95), though to a lesser extent. For precipitation, improvements are also evident, although the original ESM already performs reasonably well in many aspects. KGE appears to benefit the most from PCR-downscaling, followed by MB and MAE. On the annual scale, KGE improves by 0.25, and MB is reduced by 0.28 mm day−1. Seasonally, PCR shows consistent performance across all four seasons, with no single season dominating. However, DPastCliM-NA temperature performs particularly well during DJF and MAM. In winter, KGE is substantially improved compared to the ESM, indicating that both mean values and variability align more closely with observations. Precipitation also improves consistently across seasons, with the exception of JJA, which exhibits a slightly wetter MB compared to MPI-ESM1.2-LR.

At this aggregation level, the DPastCliM-NA product shows even larger improvements relative to the ESM. This is expected, as comparisons at the timestep level are more affected by temporal mismatches between model and observations due to internal climate variability, whereas station-level averages emphasize spatial patterns and long-term climatological agreement. For temperature, the PCR-downscaled product achieves r > 0.99 annually and across all seasons. Consistent with the timestep-level results, the station-level MB is reduced to near-zero values. In contrast to the timestep-level MAE, substantial improvements are also observed in station-level MAE, which decreases from 1.7–2.3 °C to a much narrower range of 0.3–0.6 °C. Similar behavior is found for precipitation, with marked improvements in spatial correlation, increasing from 0.54–0.66 to 0.95–0.98. Also for this variable, both MB and MAE are strongly reduced and become nearly negligible. Table 2 also reports the SSIM, which, rather than being a station-level metric, is more appropriately interpreted as a domain-level (gridded) metric. In this case as well, substantial improvements over the ESM are observed for both variables and across all seasons, with the exception of JJA for temperature and DJF for precipitation, where improvements are more limited.

The differences between the two approaches are further assessed by testing whether the corresponding metrics from PCR and LR differ significantly (Table 1). At the timestep level, the two downscaling methods generally do not differ significantly; even when statistical significance is detected, the magnitude of the differences remains small and of limited physical relevance. In contrast, clearer differences emerge at the station level. For temperature, the PCR approach performs significantly better than LR annually and across all seasons in terms of r and MAE, while MB shows significant improvements in DJF and JJA. For precipitation, differences are less robust: although some metrics indicate statistically significant differences, their magnitude remains modest.

Figures 6a,b and 7a,b present the mean bias (MB) for the PCR-downscaled product and the original MPI-ESM1.2-LR output over the testing dataset. The results show an improved MB for both temperature and precipitation across the continent (Figs. 6a, 7a), especially in data-rich areas such as the US and Canada, while improvements are less evident in the southern regions. The MB reaches uniformly low values for both temperature (within 1°C) and precipitation (within 0.5 mm day−1). The bias correction is particularly notable along the western coast, where the original ESM shows the largest biases. Here, temperature MB is reduced after PCR-downscaling, although some areas still exhibit a larger, mostly warm bias. Although the downscaling procedure did not explicitly target extremes, improvements in distributional percentiles are observed when comparing DPastCliM-NA and MPI-ESM1.2-LR with GHCN-m. For temperature, DPastCliM-NA fields inherit the percentile structure of the original ESM but with reduced discrepancies and more accurate estimates (Fig. 6c–e). Similar improvements occur for precipitation, with percentile differences generally ranging between − 0.5 and 0.5 mm day−1 (Fig. 7c–e). Despite these improvements, the North American Cordillera remains a region of systematic bias in the extremes for both variables. Particularly, the cold and warm biases inherited from the original ESM are predominantly found along this mountain range. For precipitation, dry extremes are represented correctly on average in the ESM, while DPastCliM-NA carries slightly drier estimates for low-precipitation months in this region. On the other hand, the substantial overestimation of the 90th percentile precipitation in the ESM is effectively corrected in DPastCliM-NA across the continent.

A limitation inherent to this approach is the assumption that the statistical relationships calibrated over the instrumental period remain valid under different background climate states. While the anomaly-based formulation and explicit preservation of ESM-simulated changes in mean and variance reduce sensitivity to shifts in baseline climate, potential changes in large-scale circulation dynamics over the past two millennia could introduce non-stationarities that are not explicitly modeled. This assumption should be considered when interpreting long-term reconstructions.

Evaluation Against ERA5 and Assessment of Spatial Structure

To further assess the spatial completeness of DPastCliM in regions with sparse station coverage–particularly at high latitudes, in the southern part of the continent, and in topographically complex areas–we conduct an additional comparison against the ERA5 reanalysis over the modern reference period. ERA5 provides spatially continuous fields and therefore offers an independent benchmark for evaluating large-scale structure and regional biases.

For near-surface temperature, DPastCliM-NA shows an overall positive bias relative to ERA5 (Fig. 8a), with an annual mean bias of about +0.5°C (Table 6). The warm bias exhibits a clear seasonal cycle, being strongest in SON (+0.7°C) and DJF (+0.6°C) and weakest in JJA (+0.2°C). The largest positive biases occur over topographically complex terrain in correspondence to the North America Cordillera, particularly in the northwestern part of the continent. For precipitation, DPastCliM-NA exhibits an overall modest dry bias across the study domain (Fig. 8b), with an annual mean bias of approximately −0.3 mm day−1. The dry tendency strengthens during the warm season. At the seasonal scale, the bias is weakest in DJF (about −0.1 mm day−1) and increases in JJA and SON (about −0.4 mm day−1). Spatially, negative precipitation biases are widespread over northwestern North America and, more locally, across the southern part of the continent. Positive biases are mostly present in the Gulf of Mexico area.

For both temperature and precipitation, spatial variability substantially exceeds the magnitude of the domain-mean bias, indicating that regional variability is relatively large. Thus, DPastCliM-NA maintains realistic large-scale spatial patterns even in areas with limited station density, but systematic discrepancies are amplified in mountainous regions, where strong elevation gradients and localized precipitation processes are difficult to resolve. While the PCR framework implicitly incorporates elevation-dependent signals through station-based calibration and spatial predictors, no explicit lapse-rate correction or orographic adjustment is applied beyond what is learned statistically from the training data. Consequently, residual biases over complex terrain likely reflect both station sparsity and inherent limitations of large-scale predictors.
