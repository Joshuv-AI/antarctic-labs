Metabolism of citrate-mediated PI3K-Akt signaling and primary cilium regulates spatiotemporal craniofacial mineralization
Authors: Jiaying Zhao, Sijia Hao, Ning Gao, Hao Zhang, Yiqian Yu, Yani Chen, Bicong Gao, Junhong Lai, Zhikang Wang, Zili Gao, Yueqi Ni, Jinglun Zhang, Lujiao Zhang, Wenjin Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe repair of craniofacial bones requires high-quality mineralization. The key regulator involved in the difference in mineralization of craniofacial bones remains unknown. As the main component of the bone, citrate regulates hydroxyapatite crystallization. We found that the regional accumulation of citrate caused differences in mineralization. Transcriptome analysis showed that the expression of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and primary cilia was significantly different between mineralized and unmineralized areas. Citrate specifically activated the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway to form a positive feedback loop and strengthened the metabolic activity of osteoblasts. The cooperation between citrate and primary cilia improved osteogenesis and stabilized the structure of amorphous calcium phosphate clusters. Our research reveals a new citrate-mediated metabolic-signaling-structure pattern. It explains the regional differences in mineralization of craniofacial bones and offers a theoretical basis for bone development mechanisms and regeneration strategies.Keywords: Citrate, Craniofacial bone, Metabolic regulation, Mineralization, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, Primary cilia

Introduction

Craniofacial bones are vital for maintaining physiological function and facial form [1]. Their complicated anatomical structure and special functions demand high-quality and highly efficient mineralization [2,3]. Craniofacial bones develop primarily through intramembranous ossification and exhibit pronounced regional differences in mineralization, accompanied by dynamic endogenous citrate accumulation during the onset of mineralization [4]. Although current repair strategies, such as massive autologous bone grafting or exogenous growth factor delivery, have achieved encouraging regenerative outcomes, achieving a balance between rapid bone formation and highly organized mineral maturation remains a major challenge, particularly in large or irregular defects [5,6]. This imbalance often culminates in mechanically inferior fibrous tissue formation or misaligned mineral architecture [1]. Therefore, determining the mechanism of highly efficient mineralization of craniomaxillofacial bones plays an important role in exploiting regeneration therapy (see Scheme 1).

A citrate-centered "metabolism-signaling-structure" regulatory network.

Citrate activates the PI3K-Akt signaling and synergizes with primary cilium-mediated pathways to promote osteogenesis. In extracellular matrix, citrate stabilizes amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) and guides craniofacial bone mineralization.

The mineralization of craniofacial bones shows significant regional differences. This distinction implies the involvement of various regulators. Previous studies have shown that reprogramming of osteoblasts influences their differentiation and mineralization [7,8]. Therefore, energy metabolism and key metabolites are of essential importance. We speculated that different regional metabolic mechanisms might cause the difference in mineralization. Therefore, identifying the key metabolic pathway offers new targets for regeneration of craniofacial bones.

Citrate is the main element in bone that chelates calcium and combines with hydroxyapatite to control crystal growth and enhance mechanical properties and mineralization stability [9]. Although the physicochemical influence of exogenous citrate on mineralization has been documented, the spatiotemporal dynamics of endogenous citrate production and distribution within craniofacial regions remain unexplored [10].

This study reveals a new citrate-mediated mechanism for ordered and efficient craniofacial mineralization. Transcriptome analysis showed spatial differences in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and primary cilia. Citrate participates as an energy source in synthesis and specifically activates the PI3K-Akt pathway. It also influences the mineralization fate of cells with primary cilia. Therefore, we investigated the synergy between citrate accumulation, primary cilia, and classical osteogenic pathways during early mineralization. In summary, these findings advance our understanding of craniofacial bone regeneration and its repair mechanisms. This study provides the basis for designing next-generation smart biomaterials for precise craniofacial reconstruction by shifting bone tissue engineering from passive structural scaffolds to active endogenous metabolic microenvironmental regulation.

Results

Region- and time-dependent cranial mineralization

Embryonic day 14.5 (E14.5) represents a critical developmental transition during mouse embryogenesis [11]. Studies have shown that before E14.5, embryos undergo a phase of rapid development. However, from E14.5, the skeletal system, including the cranial bones, enter an active stage of ossification (Fig. 1A). To verify this developmental shift, we isolated the supraorbital tissues from E10.5 to E14.5 mouse embryos for RNA sequencing. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct transcriptional profiles between the samples from E10.5, E12.5, E13.5, and E14.5. This indicated that cranial development is a highly coordinated and continuous process accompanied by extensive transcriptional reprogramming. Notably, the most dramatic transcriptomic change occurred between E13.5 and E14.5, as observed by the clear separation of these two time points in the PCA plot (Fig. 1B).

Further analysis confirmed that numerous genes were specifically activated or upregulated at E14.5, forming a distinct expression pattern compared to the earlier stages. The expression of a key transcription factor governing osteogenic differentiation, Sp7 (osterix), also increased sharply. This was accompanied by coordinated activation of functional downstream genes such as Sparc and Dmp1 (bone matrix formation and mineralization), Gja1 (cell junction), Slc13a5 (citrate transport), and OPG (bone homeostasis) (Fig. 1C). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that these activated genes were highly enriched in biological processes central to skeletal formation, including osteoblast differentiation, bone mineralization, and extracellular matrix (ECM) organization (Fig. 1D). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed that this transition was driven by the activation of key signaling pathways, particularly the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and the ECM-receptor interaction pathway. These pathways are essential for cell proliferation, differentiation, and skeletal tissue formation (Fig. 1E). Collectively, these results confirmed the temporal differences during the development of craniofacial bones.

In addition to temporal dynamics, the development of craniofacial bones exhibited significant spatial heterogeneity. Previous studies have shown that mesenchymal cell condensation first occurs in the supraorbital domain at approximately E11.5. Partial mineralization is evident in this region by E14.5 [12,13]. Consistently, our alizarin blue and alcian red S staining results revealed that at E14.5, the cranial region contained both mineralized and unmineralized regions. These different regions were defined as anterior (mineralized) and posterior (unmineralized) (Fig. 1F). Staining confirmed that mature mineralized bone tissue had formed in the supraorbital area by E14.5, whereas the posterior region such as the occipital bone remained unmineralized. The mineralization of posterior region began after E16.5 (Fig. S1A). These findings indicate that cranial mesenchymal cells in the anterior region initiate ossification earlier and mineralize more rapidly, revealing a clear spatiotemporal heterogeneity in craniofacial bone development.

Region-specific osteogenic capacity of cranial mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)

We further validated the above findings through in vitro experiments. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were isolated from both mineralized and unmineralized regions of E14.5 mice cranial bones(Fig. 2A). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed the identity of MSCs. Hematopoietic markers CD34 and CD45 were negative, and the MSC markers CD44, CD73, CD29, and CD90 were positive (Fig. S1B).

Notably, even before osteogenic induction, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining revealed intrinsic differences in ALP activity between cells from the two regions. This suggested that the osteogenic potential correlated with in vivo developmental timing (Fig. 2B). After osteogenic induction, ALP staining on day 7 and alizarin red S (ARS) staining on day 21 demonstrated that cells from the mineralized anterior region exhibited significantly stronger mineralization capacity than those from the unmineralized posterior region (Fig. 2B and C).

To further corroborate these functional differences, we analyzed the expression of key osteogenic markers at both the gene and protein levels. Quantitative PCR showed that cells from the mineralized region expressed higher levels of osteogenic genes, including Ocn, Alp, Osx, Runx2, and Col1a1 (Fig. 2D–H). Similarly, Western blot analysis confirmed that the protein expression of these markers was also increased (Fig. 2I and J).

Collectively, these in vitro results reinforced the notion that MSCs from the anterior and posterior cranial regions possessed different osteogenic capabilities. This finding is consistent with the temporally staggered mineralization pattern observed during in vivo craniofacial development.

Metabolic heterogeneity shaping regional mineralization

We further explored the mechanisms underlying craniofacial mineralization heterogeneity. As shown in Fig. 1, the craniofacial bone formation exhibited significant temporal specificity. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that from E10.5 to E14.5 nearly all mitochondrial genes showed a consistent increase in expression (Fig. 3A). These results indicated a systemic enhancement of mitochondrial function and associated metabolic pathways, rather than the activation of individual genes. This suggested a shift in cellular energy metabolism to support increased protein synthesis and ECM secretion.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further demonstrated that E14.5 embryos displayed significant downregulation of cell cycle and mitotic pathways compared with earlier stages. In contrast, genes involved in collagen formation and ECM organization were markedly upregulated (Fig. S1C). These findings indicated that the structural components essential for bone scaffold formation are actively synthesized and assembled, providing a foundation for matrix maturation and mineralization. Moreover, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway was activated at E14.5 (Fig. 3B). This pathway plays a key role in the regulation of cell growth, survival, and anabolic metabolism. Collectively, these results showed that at E14.5, cranial cells underwent a developmental shift from proliferation to functional differentiation, which was potentially influenced by changes in energy metabolism.

During the period from E10.5 to E14.5, there is a metabolic reprogramming from glycolysis to OXPHOS, accompanied by enhanced mineral ion transport. The expression of central osteogenic markers, including the transcription factors Runx2 and Sp7, and downstream matrix proteins Sparc and Col1a1, was progressively and significantly increased throughout development. The increase in the levels of osteogenic markers provided direct evidence of rapid bone formation (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the expression of key glycolytic enzymes (Hk1, Pgam1, and Ldha) decreased sharply, suggesting reduced dependence on less efficient energy production (Fig. 3D).

Meanwhile, the expression of genes responsible for mitochondrial fuel uptake and mineral ion transport was significantly upregulated (Fig. 3F and Figure S2, A and B), indicating the involvement of more efficient oxidative metabolism in order to support high biosynthetic activity. Together, these changes provide a metabolic foundation for sustained ATP production and matrix mineralization.

Although a global shift from glycolysis to OXPHOS was observed during osteoblast maturation, spatial analysis revealed a distinct metabolic divergence at E14.5. The glycolytic and anabolic pathways are reactivated at the mineralized region to meet the high energy and biosynthetic demands of matrix mineralization. GSEA confirmed significant activation of ribosomal pathways in the mineralized region, which could support protein synthesis. Enhanced glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathways provide the energy and precursors required for matrix production (Fig. 3F). Metabolic heatmaps further revealed the upregulation of metal ion transporters (Mt1, Mt3, and Trpv6), thereby promoting local mineral accumulation. The unmineralized region showed high expression of metabolic inhibitors such as Deptor and Txnip, suggesting two distinct and mutually exclusive metabolic states (Fig. 3G).

Based on these transcriptomic findings, we further validated whether these molecular differences translated into measurable metabolic phenotypes. Cells in the mineralized anterior region showed significantly higher ATP levels, indicating higher energy activity (Fig. 3H). Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) analysis confirmed increased glycolytic activity (Fig. 3I and J). In addition, the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was also increased concurrently, indicating enhanced mitochondrial respiration (Fig. 3K and L). The simultaneous elevation of OCR and ECAR revealed that cells in the mineralized region engaged in both glycolysis and OXPHOS to satisfy the high energy and anabolic demands of bone mineralization.

In summary, these results demonstrated that spatiotemporal differences in craniofacial mineralization are closely associated with distinct metabolic states. Stem and progenitor cells from the mineralized region exhibit higher metabolic activity. This metabolic pre-activation in the mineralized region correlates strongly with superior osteogenic and mineralization capacities upon induction. Therefore, we concluded that during craniofacial bone development, the metabolic state of cells is a decisive determinant of their mineralization potential.

Citrate metabolism regulates mineralization

To investigate the key metabolic pathways leading to spatial differences in craniofacial bone mineralization, we performed an integrated analysis across temporal and spatial dimensions. In the temporal dimension, as described above, transcriptomic analysis revealed that at E14.5, compared with earlier developmental stages (E10.5-E13.5), genes associated with citrate metabolism and transport were markedly activated. Upregulation of Slc13a5, Slc1a5, and Cd36 enhanced mitochondrial substrate import, whereas increased expression of Magt1, Slc41a2, and Slc39a2 provided essential metal ion cofactors for oxidative enzyme activity (Figure S2, A and B). These findings suggested that, as a central intermediate of the TCA cycle, citrate might play a central regulatory role during osteogenic differentiation and craniofacial mineralization.

Further analysis revealed coordinated upregulation of Ank, Slc25a1, Slc13a5, and Slc13a3 at E14.5 (Fig. 4A). These genes form a fine-tuned regulatory network thereby facilitating citrate metabolism and transport. Slc25a1 exports citrate from the mitochondria to the cytosol. Extracellular release of Slc13a5 regulates matrix stability and crystal growth. Ank removes the mineralization inhibitor pyrophosphate (PPi) to promote calcification and Slc13a3 might function in feedback regulation[[14], [15], [16]].

To characterize the metabolic distinctions between mineralized and unmineralized regions at E14.5, untargeted large-scale metabolomic profiling was performed using cells isolated from both regions. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering based on 68 identified metabolites revealed a clear segregation between the two groups (Fig. 4B and Fig. S2C), indicating distinct metabolic signatures associated with the mineralization state. The volcano plot further demonstrated that Aldoc was upregulated, whereas Txnip was downregulated in the mineralized region, reflecting enhanced glycolytic and anabolic activity (Fig. 4C)[[17], [18], [19]]. In parallel, the elevated expression of metallothioneins (Mt1 and Mt3) suggested increased intracellular zinc sequestration, which could alleviate zinc-mediated inhibition of pyruvate dehydrogenase, thereby redirecting acetyl-CoA flux toward citrate synthesis. Collectively, these findings indicated that the onset of mineralization was accompanied by profound metabolic remodeling. Among the altered pathways, a notable accumulation of citrate was detected in mineralized tissues (Fig. 4D), highlighting its potential role in regulating osteogenic metabolism. Citrate, a key intermediate metabolite in the TCA cycle, is crucial for bone formation. Therefore, further examination of extracellular citrate levels in mineralized and unmineralized cells revealed higher levels of citrate accumulation in cells with higher mineralization capacity (Fig. 4E).

Functional validation confirmed that supplementation with 200 μM exogenous citrate significantly enhanced mineral nodule formation in both calvarial cells and bone mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) (Fig. 4F, Fig. S2D). After seven days of induction, qPCR analysis revealed significant upregulation of osteogenic marker genes such as Runx2 (Figure S3, A-C). Western blotting further confirmed increased levels of osteogenic proteins (Fig. 4G and H, Figure S3, D and E).

In summary, transcriptomic analysis identified temporal activation of citrate metabolism at E14.5, whereas metabolomic and functional assays demonstrated its spatially enriched and pro-osteogenic role. By enhancing substrate supply, ion transport, and extracellular accumulation, citrate not only supports energetic and precursor metabolism, but also directly contributes to mineralized matrix formation, thus driving efficient craniofacial development.

Involvement of primary cilia in metabolic activity and osteogenesis

To evaluate temporal transcriptomic dynamics, we compared cells at E14.5 with those from earlier developmental stages. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that genes related to cilia assembly were significantly upregulated at E14.5 (Fig. 5A). Having established a temporal association between ciliogenesis and developmental progression, we next sought to determine whether this ciliary signature also exhibited spatial heterogeneity. Comparing cells isolated from mineralized and unmineralized craniofacial regions, heatmap analysis demonstrated that genes associated with osteogenic differentiation (Runx2 and Ibsp), primary cilia biogenesis (Foxj1, Mcidas, and Dnah family), and Wnt pathway activators (Wnt8b and Rspo3) were coordinately upregulated in the mineralized region (Fig. 5B).

To determine whether these transcriptional changes translated into structural differences, we performed immunofluorescence staining. Consistent with these transcriptional profiles, this confirmed a higher abundance of primary cilia in cells from the mineralized region. Furthermore, citrate treatment directly augmented primary cilia assembly (Fig. 5C), suggesting a regulatory link between citrate and ciliogenesis. To test this possibility, we applied a targeted blockade of citrate transport, which significantly diminished the expression of the primary cilia marker Ift88 (Fig. S4A). These data indicate that citrate positively regulates primary cilia biogenesis.

To further evaluate the functional role of primary cilia in osteogenesis, we silenced primary cilia using small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting Ift88. The knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qPCR (Fig. 5D) and immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5E). Following primary cilia knockdown, intracellular ATP production was severely impaired (Fig. 5F). Concurrently, the expression of osteogenic markers was markedly downregulated at both the protein (Fig. 5G) and mRNA levels (Fig. 5H, Fig. S4B). These loss-of-function results demonstrate that the primary cilium is required for maintaining cellular metabolic activity and subsequent osteogenic differentiation.

The role of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway in citrate-induced osteogenesis

Guided by the observed enrichment of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway at E14.5 in our spatiotemporal transcriptomic data (Fig. 3B), we investigated its specific regulatory role during this developmental process. We first evaluated the interplay between citrate and this pathway. Western blot analysis demonstrated that citrate treatment robustly enhanced PI3K-Akt activation in cells from both the mineralized and unmineralized regions (Fig. 6A). Conversely, targeted blockade of citrate transport significantly suppressed this cascade (Fig. S4C). We also observed a reciprocal effect: pharmacological inhibition of PI3K using LY294002 led to a significant decrease in cellular citrate levels across both cell populations (Fig. 6B). These results suggest a tight positive feedback loop between intracellular citrate accumulation and PI3K-Akt signaling.

Having demonstrated that citrate directly augments ciliary assembly (Fig. 5C), we next investigated whether primary cilia serve as the upstream mediators for citrate-induced PI3K-Akt signaling by knocking down Ift88. In these cells, citrate-induced PI3K-Akt activation was markedly suppressed (Fig. 6C). This indicates that the primary cilium functions upstream to positively regulate PI3K-Akt signaling in response to citrate.

Finally, to determine whether this cilia-dependent PI3K-Akt signaling acts as the functional bridge linking citrate metabolism to osteogenic differentiation, we examined the phenotypic consequences of PI3K inhibition. In citrate-treated cells, co-treatment with LY294002 effectively reversed the pro-osteogenic benefits: intracellular ATP production was severely impaired (Fig. 6D), and the expression of terminal osteogenic markers was significantly downregulated at both the mRNA (Fig. 6E, Figure S4D) and protein levels (Fig. 6F). Together, these results demonstrate that primary cilia-dependent activation of PI3K-Akt signaling, sustained by a positive feedback loop with citrate, is required for citrate-driven osteogenic differentiation.

Citrate modulates calcium phosphate nucleation

Based on the previously discovered molecular foundation of coordinated citrate transport mediated by Slc25a1 and pmCiC, we further hypothesized that this transport system might stimulate extracellular citrate accumulation, thereby driving high-quality biomineralization by promoting the more orderly and energy-efficient deposition of amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) clusters within the ECM (theoretical model, Fig. 7A).

(D-E) The radial distribution function of HPO42− around Ca2+ with (E) or without (D) citrate. (F) Evolution of the number of clusters formed during 10 ns simulation. (G) Evolutions of the size of clusters formed during 10 ns simulation.

To systematically validate this hypothesis, we first conducted a multifaceted investigation at the gene expression level. Gene co-expression network analysis revealed the specific activation of a highly specialized C2 module at the critical time point of E14.5. This module was significantly enriched in ion channels and passive transport functions. Its key genes, Grin2a and Grin2d, revealed substantial enhancement in calcium ion transport and channel activity in cells from the mineralized region at this stage. This provided crucial ionic support for subsequent mineral deposition (Fig. 7B). Notably, initiation of the mineralization program is a complex process involving multiple genes. In addition to enhanced ion transport system, our analysis of the expression dynamics of osteogenic genes revealed that several key genes, including Mepe, Dmp1, Ibsp, Alpl, Enpp1, and Anxa5, were upregulated from E10.5 to E14.5 (Fig. S5A). The coordinated activation of these genes clearly demonstrated the dynamic process of osteoblasts gradually differentiating, maturing and initiating the mineralization program. This provides supporting evidence from another dimension for citrate-driven orderly mineralization hypothesis.

To clarify the specific regulatory role of citrate in the nucleation of calcium phosphate at the molecular level, we used molecular dynamics to simulate its pre-nucleation within a molecular system containing calcium and phosphate ions (Fig. 7C). The system reached an equilibrium after 10 ns (Fig. S5B). The radial distribution function results showed that there were about 2.9 HPO42− distributed around the Ca2+ at a distance of 2.15 Å under the crystallization system without citrate (Fig. 7D). Once citrate was present, there was a competition between citrate and HPO42− around the Ca2+. When the distance around Ca2+ was 2.15 Å, there were about 2.68 HPO42− and about 0.65 citrate groups (Fig. 7E and Fig. S5C). However, there was no clear water layer around these clusters. As the 10 ns simulation progressed, the system gradually reached equilibrium. At the same time, the number of Ca2+ gradually decreased and the ACP cluster size gradually increased, suggesting the formation of ACP clusters that act as precursors to crystallization nucleation (Fig. 7F and G). Overall, citrate bound to Ca2+ and occupied part of the binding space between HPO42− and Ca2+, allowing ACP to form small clusters with a more uniform distribution, and bridging the remaining clusters through citrate to form larger Ca-P clusters.

In addition, we simulated citrate-promoted nucleation in the ECM after vesicle rupture (Fig. S5D). When the simulation system lacked citrate, approximately 2.9 HPO42− were distributed around the Ca2+ at a distance of 2.55 Å, and approximately 3.38 HPO42− were distributed at the second peak at 3.45 Å. Similarly, when citrate was added to the system, approximately 2.31 HPO42− were distributed around the Ca2+ at a distance of 2.55 Å, and approximately 2.7 HPO42− were distributed at 3.45 Å. Moreover, approximately 0.89 citrates were distributed at a distance of 2.55 Å from Ca2+. Citrate and HPO42− had a certain degree of competitive attraction and repulsion around Ca2+ (Figure S5, E and F). Furthermore, although for simple Can(HPO4)m, the number of clusters increased slightly after adding citrate, the average size of the clusters did not differ significantly (Figs. S5, G, and H).

Citrate-directed mineralization accelerates bone repair

We conducted pre-nucleation experiments on Ca-P clusters in vitro to verify the observations of molecular dynamics simulations (Fig. 8A). We found that after the addition of citrate, the ACP clusters were larger than those in the control group. Combined with the results of the molecular dynamics simulations, citrate could bridge more Ca2+. However, as shown by SAED, the control group exhibited preliminary crystallization at 6 h, which was earlier than that in the citrate-treated group at 12 h. Therefore, citrate might stabilize ACP and control its aggregation into larger ACP clusters before crystallization. Taken together, the pre-nucleation time of ACPs treated with citrate was prolonged, but the clusters were larger.

Additionally, to examine the effect of citrate on intrafibrillar mineralization, it was used to pretreat collagen, the mineralization solution, or both (Fig. 8B). The SAED results showed that citrate-treated mineralization solution had a greater effect on promoting mineralization than collagen treatment alone. Pretreatment of both collagen and mineralization solution with citrate also yielded a superior mineralization effect. Therefore, citrate had a greater effect on ACP clusters than collagen.

Having clarified the fundamental mechanism by which citrate regulates mineral nucleation and assembly in vitro, we next investigated whether these favorable properties could translate into enhanced bone regeneration. To evaluate the osteogenic potential of citrate in vivo, we constructed multiple groups of collagen scaffolds for in vitro induction of osteogenesis. The experimental groups included scaffold-only group, scaffolds loaded with cells from mineralized regions, scaffolds loaded with mineralized-region-derived cells and citrate, scaffolds loaded with cells from unmineralized regions, and scaffolds loaded with unmineralized-region-derived cells and citrate. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) results (Fig. 8C) showed that citrate treatment transformed the mineral crystal morphology from spherical to a more plate-like structure resembling that of natural bone tissue. Furthermore, ARS staining and quantitative analysis confirmed that citrate significantly enhanced the formation of mineralized nodules (Figure S6, A and B). Analysis of calcium content and Ca/P ratio revealed that the citrate-treated groups exhibited Ca/P ratios close to 1.67, which is the ratio observed in natural bone. This effect was most significant in cells from the mineralized region (Figure S6, C and D). These in vitro results indicated that citrate not only promoted mineral deposition but also guided the formation of hydroxyapatite crystals with a more mature morphology and chemical composition.

To further verify the bone repair efficacy of citrate, we implanted the aforementioned scaffolds into calvarial defects in mice. Samples were collected after eight weeks for histological evaluation. Micro-CT analysis showed that regardless of whether the scaffolds were loaded with cells from mineralized or unmineralized regions, the bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV) ratio was higher in the citrate-treated groups than in the untreated groups (Fig. 8D and E). Histological sections (HE and Masson staining) similarly demonstrated a significant increase in new bone formation and improved healing in the defective areas of citrate-treated groups (Fig. 8F and Fig. S6E).

In conclusion, these in vitro and in vivo results demonstrate that citrate directs the formation of mineral crystals by stabilizing ACP. It also accelerates bone regeneration and improves bone structure.

Discussion

High-quality biomineralization is the foundation of craniofacial bone development and the development of clinical biomaterials [20]. However, the endogenous signals that determine its unique spatiotemporal heterogeneity are still under investigation. Traditional bone tissue engineering typically uses high doses of growth factors as signaling molecules [21]. In this study, we found that citrate acts as a key metabolic osteogenic signaling molecule, which is recognized by upstream primary cilia and synergistically triggers the intracellular PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. Our findings further revealed that citrate increased intracellular osteogenic transcription and stabilized extracellular ACP clusters through physicochemical interactions, thereby guiding ordered mineral deposition within fibers. Taken together, our study establishes a citrate-centered metabolism-signaling-structure paradigm for craniofacial biomineralization, revealing how a single endogenous metabolite simultaneously coordinates intracellular osteogenic signaling and extracellular mineral organization to drive high-quality bone formation. Importantly, this citrate-centered regulatory mechanism provides biological design principles for future biomaterials. In this study, citrate-loaded biomimetic collagen scaffolds recapitulated the endogenous metabolic microenvironment to promote high-quality craniofacial bone regeneration in vivo.

Previous studies have established citrate as an important metabolic regulator of osteoblast differentiation and matrix mineralization10,[22], [23], [24]. Our findings extend this view by demonstrating that citrate also functions as a physicochemical regulator of extracellular mineral organization. This dual role may be particularly important in craniofacial bones, since studies have shown that the concentration of citrate is several times higher than that in the muscle tissue [25]. The craniofacial bone is formed primarily through intramembranous ossification, a specialized process that relies directly on rapid deposition and mineralization of the local matrix [26]. In addition, collagen maturity, mineral crystallinity, and Young's modulus of the jawbone are all significantly higher than that of the femur [27]. These differences suggest that craniofacial bones have more mineralization quality requirements, and their high citrate content may be precisely to meet this specific need. Citrate in the microenvironment forms strong electrostatic complexes with pre-nucleation clusters of ACP owing to the chelating ability of carboxyl groups[[28], [29], [30]]. This mechanism maintains ACP in a highly hydrated polymer-induced liquid-phase precursor (PILP) state [31]. It temporarily inhibits the premature and rapid deposition of hydroxyapatite [32]. Our molecular dynamics simulations and experimental validation revealed that citrate bridged Ca2+ and HPO42− ions to stabilize ACP pre-nucleation clusters, delayed premature crystallization, and ultimately guided ordered hydroxyapatite formation. Taken together, these findings reveal a previously unrecognized extracellular mechanism wherein citrate coordinates biomineralization beyond its established intracellular metabolic functions.

Citrate has recently emerged as a signaling metabolite capable of regulating cell fate through metabolic reprogramming [23,33]. Previous studies have shown that intracellular citrate enhances mitochondrial metabolism, ATP production, and epigenetic regulation after uptake via citrate transporters, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation23,[34], [35], [36], [37]. Our findings further demonstrated that citrate activates the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway to drive metabolic reprogramming toward an osteogenic state. This metabolic shift not only supports cellular bioenergetics, but also amplifies osteogenic transcription, highlighting PI3K-Akt as a key downstream effector linking citrate metabolism to osteoblast differentiation.

Importantly, our study identified a previously unrecognized role of primary cilia as signaling hubs that integrate citrate-derived metabolic cues with PI3K-Akt activation during craniofacial biomineralization. Our spatiotemporal transcriptomic and co-expression analyses revealed significant enrichment of primary cilia-related genes in mineralized regions, while citrate treatment further promoted ciliogenesis together with PI3K-Akt activation. Previous studies have primarily characterized primary cilia as mechanosensory organelles that transduce extracellular physical stimuli into osteogenic signaling through PI3K-Akt, Wnt/β-catenin, and Hedgehog pathways[[38], [39], [40]]. The perception of mechanical and physical characteristics of ECM by cells depends on primary cilium. Mechanical stimuli such as fluid shear stress and matrix stiffness can regulate the length, structure, and signal transduction capacity of primary cilia[[41], [42], [43]]. In bone tissue, pulsed electromagnetic fields and fluid shear stress activate the PI3K-Akt pathway via primary cilia on the surface of osteoblasts, thereby promoting osteogenic activity and differentiation [39,42]. In addition, cholesterol metabolism disorders alter the activity of the Wnt/β-catenin and Hedgehog signaling pathways by affecting primary ciliogenesis, leading to abnormalities in cranial bone formation [38]. ACP pre-nucleation clusters can infiltrate collagen fibers and alter the local stiffness, ionic microenvironment, and nanotopology of the ECM[[44], [45], [46]]. Thus, we can speculate that ACP prenucleation clusters in the extracellular matrix may alter local physicochemical properties, which are then detected by primary cilia. Our findings extend this paradigm by demonstrating that primary cilia function as metabolic integrators linking citrate metabolism to osteogenic differentiation. These results position primary cilia as a mechanistic interface that translates metabolic signals into osteogenic responses for craniofacial bone mineralization.

Despite these advances, several questions remain. Recent findings reveal that osteoblasts channel glycolytic carbon into citrate synthesis and export citrate via Slc25a1 for hydroxyapatite mineralization [22,24]. Future studies integrating isotope-tracing with craniofacial models are required to define citrate carbon flux dynamics during mineralization. Moreover, since the current evidence relies primarily on pharmacological inhibition and a small in vivo cohort, genetic validation using conditional Slc13a5 or Slc25a1 knockout models is needed to establish the physiological relevance of this regulatory network. Finally, scaffold evaluation in large-animal models will be critical for assessing long-term efficacy, safety, and translational potential. Scaffold composition and citrate delivery strategies will also be optimized to further improve regenerative efficacy.

The regulatory network involving citrate, primary cilia, and PI3K-Akt, revealed in this study, is not limited to craniofacial development. It also has broader implications for systemic metabolic homeostasis. Dysregulation of citrate metabolism, primary ciliary function, and PI3K-Akt signaling has been implicated in a variety of metabolic and degenerative bone diseases, including osteoporosis and osteoarthritis [47,48]. Our research established a metabolism-signaling-structure regulatory framework, which makes citrate an attractive candidate for integration into tissue-engineering materials. Unlike traditional protein-based growth factors, which are limited by high manufacturing costs, rapid degradation, and potential off-target risks, citrate is a highly stable, low-cost, and biocompatible endogenous molecule [10,21,49]. It can be readily incorporated into biodegradable polymer networks or composite scaffolds to enable metabolite-driven, localized, and sustained release, thereby modulating the local regenerative microenvironment [22,50]. Specifically, our findings help establish biological design principles, highlighting the importance of temporally programmed metabolite delivery, primary cilia as metabolic signal integrators capable of regulating cellular signaling competence, and hierarchical control of mineral organization rather than simply increasing mineral deposition. Our study not only broadens the fundamental understanding of biomineralization, but also provides a design strategy with translational potential for the development of next-generation smart bioactive materials for craniofacial reconstruction.

Materials and methods

Mice embryo collection

The research agreement was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (No. ZJU20250917). Eight-week-old female and nine-week-old male C57BL/6 mice used for mating were purchased from Vital River (China). The observed plug date is denoted as E0.5. Pregnant female mice were sacrificed by an overdose of anesthetic at presupposed time points. The collected embryos were then subjected to corresponding experiments. All experimental procedures have been approved by Zhejiang University and conducted following laboratory animal care and usage guidelines.

Whole-mount Alcian Blue and alizarin red staining

Staged embryos were harvested and dissected in PBS, and their skin and internal soft organs were removed. Following fixation in 95% ethanol overnight at room temperature, skeletons were incubated with 0.01% Alcian Blue (Sigma, A5268, USA), 0.005% Alizarin Red (Sigma, A5533, USA), and 5% glacial acetic acid in 70% ethanol for 72 h at 37°C incubator. The skeletons were then washed quickly in 95% ethanol, rinsed with 1% KOH, and subsequently in increasing concentrations of glycerol in KOH until reaching 80% glycerol.

Cell culture, treatments, and ALP and ARS staining

To disperse skull cells, collagenase type II (1 mg mL−1, 17101015, Gibco, Life Technologies Corporation, USA) was used. To collect BMSCs, femurs and tibias of female 7-week-old C57BL/6 mice were obtained and cleared. The bone marrow contents were flushed using alpha Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (α-MEM, HyClone, USA). The primary cranial cells and BMSCs were all cultured under a standard environment in α-MEM containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA). After further purification, the cells in passages 3-6 were used in corresponding experiments.

The osteogenic-inducing medium was composed of 10% FBS, 10 nM dexamethasone (D4902, Sigma, USA), 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (G9422, Sigma, USA), and 5 mM L-ascorbic acid (A4403, Sigma, USA) in αMEM. The osteogenic-inducing medium of each well was replaced with fresh medium every other day. Citrate (200 μM, C0706, Sigma, USA) was added into the osteogenic-inducing medium.

Induced cells for ALP and ARS staining were fixed after 7 and 21 days of incubation, respectively. The ALP staining kit was purchased from Beyotime (C3206, China). 0.1% Alizarin red S (A5533, Sigma, USA) solution (pH = 4.6) was used to stain. After pictures were obtained, the Alizarin red was dissolved in 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (C9002, Sigma, USA) at room temperature overnight away from light, and the absorbance value (OD value) was examined at 562 nm.

Citrate production assays

Cell supernatant, isolated mitochondria, cytoplasm, and EVs were collected, respectively. The method for detecting citrate content has been optimized according to the manufacturer's agreement (Solarbio, China). Quantify using the manufacturer's process and repeat the experiment 3 times.

Mice cranial defect model and micro-CT analysis

Each defect was implanted with nothing (Con), collagen scaffold (CS), collagen scaffold with mineralized cells (CS + Mine), collagen scaffold with mineralized cells treated by citrate (CS + Mine + Citrate), collagen scaffold with unmineralized cells (CS + Unmine), collagen scaffold with unmineralized cells treated by citrate (CS + Unmine + Citrate).

Cranial bone tissues were collected after 8 weeks, followed by fixing in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h at 4°C and scanning with a micro-CT scanner d using a high-resolution micro-computed tomography scanner (SkyScan 1172, Kontich, Belgium). The scanner was set at 80 kV, 100 mA, and 6 mm per pixel resolution. To determine the volume of interest, a 5 mm diameter circular contour based was drawn around the center of each defect, which encompassed the entire original defect volume. A 3D analysis of the trabecular bone volume per tissue volume (BV/TV) was performed using CTAn (Brucker).

Western blot

After loading for electrophoresis by SDS-PAGE, proteins were carefully transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. The membrane was blocked by 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by primary antibodies incubating overnight at 4°C. Rinsed membranes were treated with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. WesternBright ECL HRP substrate kit (Advansta, USA) and ChemiDoc™ Imaging System (#12003153, Bio-rad, USA) were used to visualize protein bands. Primary antibodies used were as follows: COL1A1 (1:1000, A16891, ABclonal, USA), β-actin (1:5000, AC004, ABclonal, USA), RUNX2 (1:1000, #12556, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), Osterix (1: 500, Abcam, ab209484, UK), Osteocalcin (1: 500, Abcam, ab93876, UK).

ACP nucleation and collagen mineralization

The calcium stock was stirred gently at 200 r.p.m. before adding the P solution. The reaction was followed under stirring for typically 1-12h. For citrate treatment, an appropriate citrate stock solution (in TBS buffer, pH = 7.4) was rapidly added into the ACP solution. The working solution of citrate was 200 μM. At each time point, the Ni grids were immersed in the solution, quickly dipped 5 times, washed with anhydrous ethanol, and vacuum-dried for observation.

The intrafibrillarly mineralized collagen (IMC) was generated as previously reported. Briefly, a single-layer rat tail collagen was crosslinked on the formvar-and-carbon-coated Ni TEM grids, which were then floated upside down on a 200 μL drop of a mineralization solution (MS) under 100% humidity incubator. The working solution of contents in the mineralization solution was 2.1 mM Na2HPO4, 4.5 mM CaCl2.2H2O and 0.5 g ml−1 polyacrylic acid (PAA). In the Cit-col-IMC group, Ni grids loaded collagen fibrils were pretreated 1 h with 200 μM citrate, followed by floating upside down on MS. In Cit-ACP-IMC group, 200 μM citrate was added into MS (without PAA) for 6 h, and then Ni grids loaded collagen fibrils were incubated with citrate-MS (with PAA). In Cit-IMC group, collagen fibrils and MS (without PAA) were pretreated, respectively. Total mineralization was performed at 37°C, and the duration was 72 h.

Finally, each grid was observed with FEI TECNAL G2 F30 S-TWIN TEM (FEI, USA) at 100 kV. The SAED coupled to TEM was used to determine the degree of crystallinity.

Flow cytometry

The tissue was then placed in a solution of type II collagenase (2 mg ml−1) (Sigma Aldrich, USA) at 37°C for 1 h. The digestive part contains 100 U mL−1 streptomycin (Hyclone, USA), 100 U ml−1 penicillin (Hyclone, USA), and 10% FBS in αMEM and start the experiment after the third generation. Mesenchymal stem cells exhibit the following phenotypes: CD34− (1:100, BioLegend, USA), CD45− (1:200, BioLegend, USA), CD44+ (1:100, BioLegend, USA), CD29+ (1:200, Bio Legend, USA), CD73+ (1:200, BioLegend, USA), and CD90+ (1:200, Bio Legend, USA). After the cells are digested into single cells, they are stained with mesenchymal stem cell-labeled antibodies at 4°C for 30 min. Then, the labeled cells were collected and analyzed using FACScan flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson). The data was analyzed using FlowJo software.

Seahorse metabolic analysis

Cells (2 × 104 cells per well) were inoculated into the XF24 cell culture plate (Seahorse Bioscience), and the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) were measured for each well. After measuring the baseline concentration, glucose (10 mM) and oligomycin (1.5 μM). Add 2-DG (50 mM) to each hole in sequence for ECAR measurement. Oligomycin (1.5 μM), FCCP (1 μM), and rotenone/antifungal A (1 μM) were injected in each hole sequentially for OCR measurement. Analyze the data using Seahorse Wave software 2.6.1. Normalize the data using protein content.

Metabolomics analysis

Inoculate cells (40 × 104 cells per well) in a 6-well plate, and after 24 h, add 1 mL of precooled 80% methanol to each well. Centrifuge at 14000×g for 20 min to obtain the supernatant and then measure its protein concentration. The supernatant was dried using a Speedvac instrument. Use the metabolomics platform of Tsinghua University for metabolite measurement. Peak detection and integration of intracellular metabolites were performed using Agilent MassHunter quantitative software (B.07.01).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

The 3D structures of citrate and HPO42− were obtained from the PubChem (CID: 31348; CID: 3681305). The basic model was originally built in a cubic box with a size of 5 × 5 × 5 nm3, and the simulation box was solvated with plenty of SPC water molecules. The Ca2+ and HPO42− ions were added and the concentration of Ca2+ and HPO42− were set to 1.5 M. For the simulation of a system containing citrate, citrate ions were added to the system, and the concentration of citrate was set to 200 μM. Na+ and Cl− ions were added to neutralize the systems. The steepest descent method was adopted for energy minimization. Systems were heated to 323 K using the V-rescale thermostat in the NVT ensemble for 100 ps and equilibrated for 1 ns in NPT. Then, a 10 ns molecular dynamics simulation for ion interactions was performed. For collagen simulations, the 3D structures of collagen were obtained from literature and the forcefield used was Amber99. The simulation system size was 20 × 20 × 68.43160 nm3 and the simulation box was solvated with plenty of SPC water molecules. 295 Ca2+ and 750 HPO42− ions were added to the system. Na+ and Cl− ions were added to neutralize the systems with a concentration of 0.15 nM. Then, a 10 ns molecular dynamics simulation was performed. For the simulation of system containing citrate, 100 citrate ions were added to the system and the system was first made to pre-equilibrate the citrate and collagen for 5 ns and then simulated for 10 ns. The radial distribution function of ion pairs were obtained to analyze the interaction. The number and size of clusters were obtained to evaluate the progress of crystalline nucleation. The root mean square deviations (RMSDs) of systems were calculated to determine the simulation stability. VMD software was used for molecular dynamics simulations trajectory visualization and analysis.

Collagen scaffolds synthesis

Tropocollagen solution (#354236, Corning, USA) were diluted to 0.1 mg mL−1 with 0.1 M NaOH (in PBS, sterile, pH = 9.2). 100 μL collagen solution was added to 48-well plate to form proper-sized collagen scaffolds at 37°C and 100% humidity incubator for 24 h. Then collagen was crosslinked by 100 μL sterile 0.3 M 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-carbodiimide (EDC)/0.06 M N-hydroxy-sulpho-succinimide sodium salt (NHS) for 4 h at 37°C incubator. Gelatinous collagen scaffolds are approximately 3 mm in thickness and 6 mm in diameter.
