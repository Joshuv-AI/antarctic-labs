Association between smoking and periodontitis severity in patients attending a periodontology clinic: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Omer Ogutcen, Nursema Demir, Dilek Ozkan Sen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate the association of cigarette smoking on the development and severity of periodontitis and to examine the association of this relationship with demographic and behavioral factors.MethodsMedical and dental histories, smoking characteristics, systemic parameters, and periodontal findings of all patients presenting to the periodontology clinic were routinely recorded. A total of 781 volunteers aged 18 years and older were included. Smoking status was further categorized based on daily cigarette consumption (non-smoker, ≤ 10 cigarettes/day, and > 10 cigarettes/day). Only conventional cigarette smoking was considered, and the use of alternative smoking devices (e.g., e-cigarettes or heated tobacco products) was not included. Periodontal diagnosis, staging, and grading were performed according to standard clinical criteria. Statistical analyses included Chi-square tests for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis tests for comparisons of periodontal parameters.ResultsA significant association was observed between daily cigarette consumption and periodontitis stage severity (p < 0.001). The proportion of Stage IV periodontitis was higher among individuals smoking > 10 cigarettes/day than among non-smokers. Smoking status was also significantly associated with probing depth values (p = 0.012), suggesting greater periodontal destruction with increasing cigarette consumption. In the adjusted ordinal logistic regression model, smoking > 10 cigarettes/day remained significantly associated with higher periodontitis stage severity. Toothbrushing ≥ 2 times/day was associated with lower odds of severe periodontitis, whereas dental visit frequency and education level were not significantly associated with stage severity.ConclusionsSmoking was significantly associated with increased severity and more advanced stages of periodontitis. Behavioral and demographic factors were also associated with periodontal disease severity.Trial RegistrationThis study did not involve a health care intervention or a randomized clinical trial. It is a cross-sectional observational study based on anonymized clinical records; therefore, prospective trial registration was not required.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by the disruption of host–biofilm homeostasis and the progressive destruction of periodontal tissues [1]. It is widely recognized as a significant public health concern, affecting nearly half of the global adult population and imposing substantial burdens on both individual well-being and healthcare systems [2].

The progression of periodontitis is influenced by microbiological, behavioral, systemic, and demographic risk factors [3]. Among the modifiable determinants, smoking is one of the most powerful environmental factors. It suppresses immune function, alters vascular integrity, delays wound healing, and diminishes the response to periodontal therapy [4, 5]. Tobacco exposure also induces microvascular constriction, gingival fibrosis, and increased keratinization, which may mask early inflammatory signs [6, 7].

Previous studies have reported a clear dose–response relationship between cigarette consumption and periodontal destruction [8, 9]. Heavy smokers exhibit higher rates of advanced disease and poorer treatment outcomes [10]. Additionally, oral hygiene practices, educational level, gender, and regular dental attendance significantly influence the clinical course of periodontitis, underscoring its multifactorial nature [11].

Despite extensive research, data on how smoking affects the staging and grading of periodontitis remain limited. In addition, clinic-based records derived from routine patient data may not fully capture the true distribution of disease among smokers and non-smokers. Furthermore, the influence of demographic and behavioral factors requires more comprehensive evaluation [12, 13]. Moreover, previous studies often relied on smaller or non-clinical samples, limiting the generalizability of existing findings. A more robust, clinic-based dataset is therefore essential to clarify these associations.

Therefore, this study aims to assess the prevalence of periodontitis and the effects of smoking on its stage and grade among individuals presenting to the Periodontology Clinic of Necmettin Erbakan University, and to examine the associations of demographic and behavioral characteristics with periodontal disease severity.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted using anonymized clinical records of individuals aged 18 years and older who presented to the Department of Periodontology, Faculty of Dentistry, Necmettin Erbakan University.

The minimum required sample size was calculated as 700 participants using G*Power 3.1 software (Heinrich Heine Universität, Düsseldorf, Germany), with α = 0.05, power (1–β) = 0.80, and a medium effect size (d = 0.3). A total of patients presenting to the clinic during the study period were assessed for eligibility. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, eligible participants were included in the study, and 781 individuals were analyzed.

Inclusion criteria: (i) age ≥ 18 years, (ii) presentation to the periodontology clinic for any reason, (iii) provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: (i) refusal to participate, (ii) presence of psychiatric, mental, or physical disability, (iii) history of systemic diseases known to affect periodontal status, including diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular diseases, immunological disorders, and osteoporosis, (iv) pregnancy or lactation, (v) any history of previous periodontal treatment, regardless of the time elapsed since treatment. Information regarding systemic diseases and the presence of psychiatric, mental, or physical conditions was obtained from patient self-reports recorded in medical history forms and confirmed through available clinical records. Information regarding smoking duration and pack-years was not available in the clinical records and was therefore not included in the analysis. Smoking status was further categorized based on daily cigarette consumption (non-smoker, ≤ 10 cigarettes/day, and > 10 cigarettes/day). Only conventional cigarette smoking was considered, and the use of alternative smoking devices (e.g., e-cigarettes or heated tobacco products) was not included. The study population consisted of patients who presented to the clinic between January 2024 and December 2024.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, personal data were anonymized, and all information was used solely for scientific purposes. Ethical approval was granted by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Necmettin Erbakan University on 20.02.2025 (Approval No: 2025/565). This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Periodontal examination

All participants underwent a comprehensive periodontal examination. Plaque Index (PI), Gingival Index (GI), pocket depth (PD), bleeding on probing (BOP), and gingival recession (GR) were recorded for all teeth [14–16]. Clinical measurements were obtained from six sites per tooth (mesial-buccal, mid-buccal, distal-buccal, mesial-lingual, mid-lingual, and distal-lingual) using a Williams periodontal probe (Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA).

Measurement reliability was verified in 10 participants selected from the study population. These participants were also included in the final study sample. PD and clinical attachment level (CAL) measurements were recorded twice by the same examiner with a 48-hour interval under similar clinical conditions. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), which demonstrated excellent agreement (ICC = 0.91) [13–15].

Periodontal diagnoses were established according to the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions (AAP/EFP). Periodontal diagnoses were established according to widely accepted criteria. Participants with PD < 3 mm, no clinical attachment loss, and BOP < 10% were considered periodontally healthy, whereas those with BOP ≥ 10% and no attachment loss were diagnosed with gingivitis. Participants diagnosed with gingivitis were limited to those with an intact periodontium. Individuals with gingival inflammation on a reduced periodontium were excluded in order to avoid confounding effects related to previous periodontal attachment loss and to ensure homogeneity among study groups. Periodontitis was defined as interdental CAL at ≥ 2 non-adjacent teeth or buccal/oral CAL ≥ 3 mm with PD > 3 mm at ≥ 2 teeth. Staging and grading were determined based on clinical parameters (PD, CAL) and radiographic bone loss (RBL) assessed from digital panoramic radiographs. Radiographic bone loss (RBL) was evaluated from digital panoramic radiographs by the same calibrated periodontist. Measurements were performed using the distance from the cemento-enamel junction to the alveolar bone crest as a standardized reference point. RBL was assessed using a semi-quantitative approach based on visual estimation and categorized according to the 2017 classification guidelines. CAL values of 1–2 mm, 3–4 mm, and ≥ 5 mm were classified as Stage I, Stage II, and Stage III, respectively. Stage IV was defined according to the 2017 World Workshop classification, considering additional complexity factors such as severe tooth loss, masticatory dysfunction, and the need for complex rehabilitation. Grading was determined according to disease progression rate, where Grade A represented slow progression, Grade B moderate progression, and Grade C rapid progression. Smoking status (≥ 10 cigarettes/day) and other risk factors were considered in assigning Grade C. Generalized periodontitis was diagnosed when more than 30% of teeth exhibited attachment loss. Complexity factors, including furcation involvement and tooth mobility, were incorporated into the final staging [1].

Outcome definition

The primary outcome of this study was periodontitis stage severity according to the 2017 World Workshop Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions. Periodontitis stages (Stage I–IV), determined as described above, were analyzed as an ordinal outcome variable, with higher stages representing greater disease severity.

Data collection

Following ethics approval, the medical and dental records of patients presenting to the clinic were reviewed. Information regarding smoking status (current smoker or non-smoker), demographic characteristics (age, sex and general health conditions), and periodontal status—including periodontal diagnosis and clinical periodontal parameters such as bleeding on probing, probing depth, clinical attachment levels, and plaque scores—was collected. The prevalence and distribution of periodontitis severity were compared between smokers and non-smokers. In addition, periodontal disease presence, absence, and severity indicators (including probing depth, tooth loss, and radiographic bone loss) were evaluated according to smoking status.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. The distribution of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with normal distribution were compared using the Independent Samples t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons involving more than two groups. Differences between categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-square (χ²) test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The primary outcome variable was periodontitis stage severity (Stage I–IV), which was treated as an ordinal variable. To evaluate the association between smoking status and periodontitis severity, ordinal logistic regression analysis was performed. Smoking status was categorized as non-smoker, ≤ 10 cigarettes/day, and > 10 cigarettes/day. Age, sex, education level, toothbrushing frequency, and dental visit frequency were included in the adjusted model as potential confounding variables based on a directed acyclic graph (DAG) and previous literature.

Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated. Prior to model fitting, the proportional odds assumption was assessed using the Test of Parallel Lines. Multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using variance inflation factor (VIF) values. Model fit was assessed using Pearson and Deviance goodness-of-fit statistics, and Nagelkerke pseudo-R² values were calculated to estimate the explanatory power of the model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The mean age of the 781 individuals included in the study was 52.4 ± 9.3 years, with 60.7% females and 39.3% males. The age distribution of the participants indicated a higher proportion of individuals in older age groups, suggesting a concentration above middle age. The study population consisted predominantly of individuals diagnosed with periodontitis. A small proportion of participants were classified as periodontally healthy (n = 3) or with gingivitis (n = 3), and these individuals were excluded from the grading analysis.

Regarding educational status, 26.9% of the participants had primary school education, 47.4% were high school graduates, and 25.7% had university-level education or higher. Among the participants, 37.1% were non-smokers, 31.4% smoked ≤ 10 cigarettes per day, and 31.5% smoked > 10 cigarettes per day. In terms of oral hygiene practices, 61.4% of the participants brushed their teeth once a day or less, whereas 38.6% brushed twice a day or more (Table 1).

Analyses examining the effects of smoking on periodontal disease severity revealed statistically significant results. The relationship between periodontitis stage and smoking level was significant (p < 0.001). Among non-smokers, Stage II periodontitis was the most prevalent stage (50.3%), whereas Stage III periodontitis predominated among individuals smoking > 10 cigarettes/day (64.2%). Stage IV periodontitis was also more frequent among heavy smokers (14.7%) than among non-smokers (11.1%) and individuals smoking ≤ 10 cigarettes/day (2.9%). The effect size analysis (Cramer’s V = 0.130) demonstrated a weak association between smoking status and periodontal disease stage. Furthermore, the Kendall’s tau-c coefficient of 0.086 supports a progressive increase in disease severity parallel to higher levels of cigarette consumption. Smoking status was also significantly associated with probing depth values (Kruskal–Wallis H = 8.925, p = 0.012).

Significant associations were also observed between periodontal disease grade and demographic and behavioral variables. The higher prevalence of Grade C among males (p = 0.015, Cramer’s V = 0.104) suggests a possible sex-related influence on disease severity. Decreased toothbrushing frequency demonstrated a strong and inverse relationship with periodontal disease severity (p < 0.001, Cramer’s V = 0.226, Kendall’s tau-c = − 0.226). The association between educational level and disease grade was not statistically significant (p = 0.085). No statistically significant association was found between dental visit frequency and periodontal disease severity (p = 0.362) (Table 2). The prevalence of Grade C periodontitis was 64.5% among individuals who smoked more than 10 cigarettes per day, compared with 20.3% in non-smokers.

Discussion

This study was conducted to evaluate the associations of cigarette consumption on the clinical severity and stage of periodontitis, as well as the potential associations of this relationship with demographic and behavioral determinants. The relatively high mean age of the study population may reflect the tendency of older individuals to seek periodontal care at more advanced stages of disease.

In our study, a higher proportion of Stage IV periodontitis was observed among individuals who smoked ≥ 10 cigarettes per day compared with non-smokers and those who smoked ≤ 10 cigarettes per day. This finding indicates that smoking was associated with increased severity and more advanced stages of periodontitis in this cross-sectional study. Notably, the 2017 classification identifies smoking (≥ 10 cigarettes/day) as a determinant for Grade C. In our study, the higher prevalence of Grade C among heavy smokers is therefore consistent with the expected classification framework rather than representing an independent association [1]. Similarly, Chatzopoulos et al. reported that smoking is significantly associated with advanced periodontitis (Stage III–IV) and Grade C [17, 18].

However, the effect size calculated in our study (Cramer’s V = 0.130) and the low ordinal association coefficient (Kendall’s tau-c = 0.086) indicate that the progression of periodontitis cannot be explained by smoking alone. As emphasized by Kinane et al., individual variations in immune response, genetic predisposition, and oral hygiene behaviors are critical determinants in the course of periodontal disease [7]. Based on these findings, incorporating structured smoking-cessation counseling and closer clinical monitoring of smokers may be considered an important strategy to control disease progression. Adjusted regression analysis showed that smoking remained independently associated with increased periodontal disease severity after controlling for potential confounders. The significance of age and oral hygiene behaviors further supports the multifactorial nature of periodontitis. In addition, the DAG-based framework strengthened the methodological validity of the study by clarifying the relationships between smoking, periodontal severity, and confounding variables.

When the relationship between smoking level and mean PD was examined, a statistically significant difference was found (p = 0.012). This observation is consistent with numerous studies reporting significantly higher PD and CAL in smokers compared with non-smokers [18–20].

Regarding demographic variables, males exhibited higher periodontal disease severity. Several prior studies have also indicated that periodontal disease prevalence is greater among men [18, 21]. Albandar et al. attributed this difference primarily to higher smoking rates among males [22].

In the evaluation of behavioral factors, higher toothbrushing frequency was associated with reduced periodontitis severity, consistent with previous findings in the literature [23–27]. Although the association between educational level and periodontitis severity was not statistically significant, earlier studies have shown that higher education levels are linked with lower periodontal disease risk [28, 29] .

Although dental visit frequency was not significantly associated with disease severity, the higher proportion of Grade A among individuals who attended a dental check-up within the last 6 months may indicate an association between regular professional care and less severe periodontal conditions. The literature similarly indicates that early diagnosis, effective plaque control, and behavioral motivation can reduce periodontal disease progression [30] .

Adjusted regression analysis demonstrated that increasing age was independently associated with greater periodontitis stage severity. Immunosenescence, the accumulation of systemic diseases, and heightened destructive processes within periodontal tissues are known contributors to this pattern [31]. Therefore, the interplay between age, smoking, education level, and oral hygiene habits underscores the multifactorial nature of periodontitis progression. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that no single risk factor dominates the pathogenesis of periodontal disease; rather, a complex interaction among environmental, behavioral, and biological determinants is evident. The findings suggest that smoking cessation counseling and comprehensive oral hygiene education may be considered important components of periodontal treatment planning. Furthermore, incorporating community-based smoking cessation programs into primary healthcare services may contribute to improved periodontal health outcomes as well as other tobacco-related systemic conditions, thereby reducing the burden on healthcare systems and improving overall quality of life.

This study has certain limitations. Although the association between smoking and periodontal disease is well established, the present study provides additional insight by evaluating this relationship within a clinical population while considering demographic and behavioral factors. However, due to its single-center design and inclusion of individuals seeking dental care, the findings may have limited generalizability. In addition, age may have acted as a confounding factor in the observed association between smoking and periodontal disease severity. The lack of a significant association between dental visit frequency and periodontal disease severity may be due to unmeasured factors such as variability in oral hygiene practices or irregular attendance patterns. Additionally, the relatively small number of participants with higher education levels may have reduced the statistical power of certain subgroup analyses. The absence of detailed information regarding smoking duration, age of smoking initiation, pack-years, and smoking cessation status restricted the assessment to daily cigarette consumption alone. Previous studies have shown that smoking duration and cessation history are important determinants of periodontitis progression.

Future research should incorporate smoking intensity and cessation history to better elucidate the associations with periodontal disease severity, and studies with multicenter samples are recommended to enhance generalizability. Furthermore, evaluating biochemical markers and inflammatory profiles in periodontal tissues may contribute to a better understanding of disease mechanisms and strengthen the clinical relevance of the findings.

Conclusion

Smoking was significantly associated with increased periodontitis stage severity, particularly among individuals who consumed ≥ 10 cigarettes per day. Increasing age was also associated with greater disease severity, whereas more frequent toothbrushing was associated with lower odds of severe periodontitis. These findings support the importance of addressing behavioral risk factors in the prevention and management of periodontitis.
