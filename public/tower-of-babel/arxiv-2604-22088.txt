Zero-inflated modeling with smoothing on counting tensors
Elena Tuzhilina, Yaoming Zhen

Abstract
We propose a unified probabilistic framework for sparse count tensors with excess zeros, motivated by single-cell Hi-C data. The observed data are naturally represented as a three-way tensor indexed by genomic loci pairs and cells, exhibiting pronounced sparsity, zero inflation, and cell-to-cell heterogeneity. We introduce a zero-inflated Poisson tensor model that integrates low-rank CP structure, cluster-specific latent embeddings, and smoothness along ordered genomic loci, thereby jointly capturing multiway dependence, heterogeneity, and structured variation. We develop a Bayes-optimal procedure for distinguishing structural from technical zeros, enabling principled inference and uncertainty quantification. We establish identifiability of the model parameters and derive consistency rates for the proposed estimators in a high-dimensional regime. Simulation studies and analyses of single-cell Hi-C data demonstrate improved performance in zero detection, latent structure recovery, and downstream tasks such as clustering and 3D chromatin organization inference. The proposed framework provides a flexible approach for multiway count data with excess zeros and structured dependencies, and suggests several directions for future work, including mixture-based modeling of cell populations and scalable computation for large-scale applications.



Keywords:
Counting Tensors, Zero-inflation, False Zero Detection, Imputation

1
Introduction

High-dimensional count data with excess zeros arise in many modern applications, including single-cell genomics
(
Nagano et al., 2017
;
Pierson and Yau, 2015
)
, ecology
(
Martin et al., 2005
;
Zuur et al., 2009
)
, and recommender systems with implicit feedback
(
Hu et al., 2008
)
. Understanding the mechanisms generating excess zeros is essential for recovering the underlying signal, since observed zeros may arise from multiple sources, including true structural absence, technical limitations, detection failure, and measurement noise
(
Hicks et al., 2018
)
.

A substantial literature has addressed zero inflation in lower-dimensional settings through hurdle and zero-inflated models that explicitly separate structural and sampling zeros
(
Mullahy, 1986
;
Lambert, 1992
;
Cameron and Trivedi, 2013
)
. These approaches are well established for univariate and low-dimensional count data. In parallel, tensor methodology has emerged as a flexible framework for modeling multiway data via low-rank representations
(
Kolda and Bader, 2009
;
De Lathauwer et al., 2000
)
. However, existing tensor decomposition methods typically do not accommodate excess zeros, while classical zero-inflated models do not naturally extend to tensor-valued observations. Bridging these two literatures is therefore essential for analyzing modern multiway count data.

In this paper, we study inference for sparse count tensors under zero inflation as a primary modeling challenge. We propose a zero-inflated Poisson tensor model in which both the intensity and masking probability tensors admit low-rank structure. Beyond zero inflation, the proposed framework accommodates additional structures commonly encountered in multiway data. In particular, different modes may exhibit distinct forms of organization: one mode may index heterogeneous units with latent group structure, while another may correspond to an ordered domain along which the underlying signal varies smoothly. These components are incorporated through structured latent representations that jointly capture heterogeneity across units and smooth variation along ordered modes, yielding a flexible model for complex tensor-valued count data.

A key challenge is that existing approaches address these components in isolation. Zero-inflated regression models accommodate excess zeros but assume independent observations and do not extend to tensor-valued data
(
Mullahy, 1986
;
Lambert, 1992
)
. Tensor decomposition methods capture low-rank multiway dependence but typically rely on standard likelihood models that do not account for zero inflation
(
Kolda and Bader, 2009
)
. At the same time, probabilistic tensor models generally impose a shared distributional form across slices or modes, limiting their ability to capture heterogeneity across subpopulations
(
Hoff, 2011
)
. Smoothing methods for ordered or spatial domains are well developed in classical regression settings
(
Wood, 2017
)
, but are largely disconnected from tensor factorization and zero-inflated frameworks. Consequently, a unified approach that simultaneously accommodates zero inflation, low-rank multiway dependence, heterogeneity, and smooth structure remains lacking.

Motivating application

A key application motivating this work is single-cell chromatin contact data obtained via Hi-C technology
(
Lieberman-Aiden et al., 2009
;
Rao et al., 2014
)
. Hi-C is a genomic assay that measures the frequency of spatial proximity between pairs of genomic loci and is widely used to reconstruct three-dimensional (3D) chromatin organization (
Duan et al., 2010
;
Hu et al., 2013
and, more recently,
Tuzhilina et al., 2022
;
Tuzhilina et al., 2024
), with broad applications to gene regulation, chromosome architecture, and disease studies. The output of Hi-C is a symmetric, integer-valued contact matrix whose entries record the observed interaction frequencies between pairs of genomic loci, with higher values typically interpreted as indicating closer spatial proximity.

Early work focused on bulk Hi-C data, which aggregate signals across many cells into a single contact matrix whose entries represent interaction frequencies at the population level. More recently, single-cell Hi-C technologies enable measurements at the level of individual cells, yielding a collection of contact matrices that capture cell-to-cell variability in chromatin organization
(
Nagano et al., 2017
;
Stevens et al., 2017
;
Ramani et al., 2017
)
. These data naturally form a three-way tensor (genomic loci
\times
genomic loci
\times
cell). While enabling characterization of cell-to-cell heterogeneity in chromatin organization, single-cell measurements also introduce substantial statistical challenges.

1.

Extreme sparsity:
Single-cell contact matrices are highly sparse due to both biological absence of contacts and technical limitations such as low sequencing depth and incomplete capture efficiency. Disentangling
structural zeros
(true absence of contact) from
technical zeros
(dropout events) is essential for downstream tasks such as 3D reconstruction. Failure to distinguish these sources can bias inference, while treating all zeros as missing discards informative structural signal.

2.

Cell-to-cell heterogeneity:
Chromatin organization exhibits variability across cells. Statistical methods must therefore accommodate population heterogeneity while borrowing strength across cells through shared structure.

3.

Smoothness along the genome:
Genomic loci are ordered along chromosomes, and neighboring loci tend to exhibit similar interaction patterns due to the continuous nature of chromatin. Leveraging this smoothness can improve signal recovery.

Existing methods address these challenges in isolation. HiCImpute
(
Xie et al., 2022
)
distinguishes structural zeros from technical dropouts via a Bayesian hierarchical model. scHiCSRS
(
Xu et al., 2022
)
combines self-representation smoothing with mixture modeling for zero identification. Higashi
(
Zhang et al., 2022
)
employs hypergraph neural networks to learn cell embeddings and impute contact maps by leveraging correlations across cells. However, these approaches lack a unified probabilistic framework that exploits the inherent tensor structure of the data and jointly models zero inflation, cell-to-cell heterogeneity, and smoothness along the genomic loci.

Proposed framework and main contributions.

We propose
Zero-Inflated Tensor model with Smoothing (
ZITS
)
, a likelihood-based framework for sparse count tensors integrating three components: (i) zero-inflated Poisson model with entry-specific masking probabilities to explicitly separate technical and structural zeros; (ii) low-rank CP tensor decomposition with heterogeneous structure across one mode via latent grouping; and (iii) functional smoothing along ordered modes via basis expansion.

1.

Theoretical contributions:
We establish identifiability conditions for the model parameters and derive consistency rates for the maximum likelihood estimator. Notably, despite
O(K)
parameter growth with sample size, consistent estimation is achievable through the block structure constraint, providing theoretical justification for the model design.

2.

Methodological contributions:
We develop a likelihood-based estimation algorithm and propose a Bayes-optimal classifier for distinguishing structural from technical zeros, enabling principled zero-inflation detection with posterior uncertainty quantification. The unified probabilistic framework allows joint inference over all model components.

3.

Empirical validation:
We demonstrate effectiveness through simulation studies and real single-cell Hi-C data, showing improved signal recovery, enhanced zero detection, and more accurate latent representations compared to existing approaches.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We begin in Section
2
by introducing the proposed model, followed by Section
3
, which develops the estimation and inference procedures. The theoretical properties are studied in Section
4
, where we establish identifiability and consistency and introduce the zero classification and imputation method. We then assess the performance of the approach through simulation studies in Section
5
and illustrate its practical utility on real single-cell Hi-C data in Section
6
. Section
7
discusses limitations and extensions.

2.2
Model

Let
\bm{\mathcal{C}}=(C_{i,j,k})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times K}
be a Hi-C contact tensor on
N
genomic loci among
K
cells. Precisely,
C_{i,j,k}
records the contact counts between genomic loci
i
and
j
in cell
k
, for any
i,j\in[N]
, and
k\in[K]
. In practice, due to insufficient sequencing depth and measurement errors, many contact counts will be inevitably and randomly masked by zeros, leading to a great among of false zero contact counts and hence a sparse pattern of the observational Hi-C tensor
(
Xie et al., 2022
)
. Moreover, heterogeneity is a ubiquitous phenomenon for Hi-C data. For example, the chromatin conformations could be substantially different from cells in one tissue. These two unique phenomena render the challenges of intensity estimation and downstream analysis for the Hi-C data. To circumvent these difficulties, we propose a data-driven zero-inflated tensor model with smoothing (
ZITS
) for the Hi-C tensor in several steps.

First of all, we employ the zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP) distribution to accommodate the potential false zero observations. Specifically, we assume that for any
i<j\in[N]
, and
k\in[K]
the contact counts
C_{i,j,k}
are random variables independently following

\mathbb{P}\left(C_{i,j,k}=C_{j,i,k}=c\right)=\begin{cases}p_{i,j,k}+(1-p_{i,j,k})e^{-\lambda_{i,j,k}},\text{ if }c=0;\\
(1-p_{i,j,k})\lambda_{i,j,k}^{c}e^{-\lambda_{i,j,k}}/c!,\text{ if }c\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.\end{cases}

(1)

Herein,
\bm{\Lambda}=(\lambda_{i,j,k})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times K}
with
\lambda_{i,j,k}>0
being the underlying Poisson intensity tensor and
\bm{\mathcal{P}}=(p_{i,j,k})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times K}
with
0\leq p_{i,j,k}<1
being a masking probability tensor.

The proposed zero-inflated Poisson model, denoted as
C_{i,j,k}\sim
ZIP
(p_{i,j,k},\lambda_{i,j,k})
, can be understood as follows. Originally, the true underlying contact counts between genomic loci
i
and
j
in the cell
k
, denoted as
\widetilde{C}_{i,j,k}
, is generated by a Poisson distribution with intensity
\lambda_{i,j,k}
. Subsequently, it will be masked as zero with probability
p_{i,j,k}
, which may induce a false zero unless
\widetilde{C}_{i,j,k}=0
. Therefore, we can factorize
C_{i,j,k}
as the product of
\widetilde{C}_{i,j,k}\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_{i,j,k})
and
B_{i,j,k}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1-p_{i,j,k})
, i.e.
C_{i,j,k}=B_{i,j,k}\widetilde{C}_{i,j,k}
. Herein,
B_{i,j,k}
is independent with
\widetilde{C}_{i,j,k}
, implying that the masking scheme is randomly implemented given the probability
p_{i,j,k}
, regardless of the actual value of
C_{i,j,k}
, for any
i\leq j\in[n]
and
k\in[K]
. Therefore, a zero-inflated Poisson distribution is simply a mixture distribution of a Poisson distribution with a degenerate distribution putting all the probability mass on
0
. Moreover, the averaged scale of the
p_{i,j,k}
’s controls the overall sparsity of the Hi-C tensor. Specifically, the Hi-C tensor would be relatively sparse if the masking probabilities
p_{i,j,k}
’s are quite close to
1
, while the Hi-C tensor would be dense if
p_{i,j,k}
’s are close to
0
. Clearly, when
p_{i,j,k}=0
, the ZIP distribution will reduce to the usual Poisson distribution, showing that the ZIP distribution includes Poisson distribution as a special case. When
p_{i,j,k}=1
or
\lambda_{i,j,k}=0
, the ZIP will become a degenerated distribution putting all the probability mass to
0
, which is trivial, and we thus rule out these two cases in the definition. More discussions on the ZIP distribution are relegated to Appendix D.

Second, denote
\bm{\eta}=(\eta_{i,j,k})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times K}
and
\bm{\Theta}=(\theta_{i,j,k})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times K}
as an entry-wise logarithm transformation of
\bm{\Lambda}
and an entry-wise logit transformation of
\bm{\mathcal{J}}-\bm{\mathcal{P}}
, respectively, where
\bm{\mathcal{J}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times K}
is the tensor of all ones. Precisely,
\eta_{i,j,k}=\log\lambda_{i,j,k}
, and
\theta_{i,j,k}=\log\frac{1-p_{i,j,k}}{p_{i,j,k}}
, for
i,j\in[N]
and
k\in[K]
. Such logarithm transformations are computationally convenient and favorable as they avoid the positive or
[0,1]
fixed interval constraint in terms of optimization and could simplify the zero-inflated Poisson likelihood to certain extent. We remark that
\theta_{i,j,k}=\text{logit}(1-p_{i,j,k})
is also consistent with the common logit transformation in logistic regression and binary network data analysis
(
Zhang et al., 2020
;
Ma et al., 2020
;
Lyu et al., 2023
)
. This is because under the decomposition of the ZIP distribution mentioned above,
1-p_{i,j,k}
is the successful probability of the Bernoulli distribution; that is,
P(B_{i,j,k}=1)=1-p_{i,j,k}
.

. Denote
\bm{Z}=(Z_{k,r})\in\{0,1\}^{K\times R}

\bm{\eta}

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{\alpha}\times_{2}\bm{\alpha}\times_{3}\bm{Z}\bm{\beta},\text{ and }\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{\alpha}\times_{2}\bm{\alpha}\times_{3}\bm{Z}\bm{\xi},

(2)

where

,

, and

if and only if

.

Further denoting

, and

, we can rewrite (

\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}{\bm{\alpha}}\times_{2}\bm{\alpha}\times_{3}\tilde{\bm{\xi}}
. We now provide several insights and interpretation of the above doubly heterogeneous CP decomposition on
\bm{\eta}
and
\bm{\Theta}
. Specifically,
\bm{\alpha}_{i}
can be regarded as the embedding of genomic locus
i
across all cells, and the cluster structures among the cells have been captured by

and
\tilde{\bm{\xi}}
with the same block structures after appropriate permutation of the rows, and they can also regarded as the embeddings of the cells. Note that the doubly tensor decomposition also models the fact that, due to measurement errors, the genomic locus pairs with low contact intensities are likely to induce false zeros, while slightly changes on the contact counts of those pairs with high contact intensities will generally not lead to false zeros. For example, when all
\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}
and
\bm{\xi}
are non-negative, large magnitude of
\bm{\alpha}
yields large magnitudes of
\bm{\eta}
and
\bm{\Theta}
given

and

, leading to high intensities and low zero-inflated probabilities.

We also remark that when

is block diagonal, we can model different chromatin embeddings of different cell clusters. For example, setting

for some integer

, we denote
\bm{\alpha}=[\bm{\alpha}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\alpha}^{(R)}]

, for

can be regarded as the global embedding of genomic locus

across all cells while

is the local embedding of gonomic locus
i
for the cells inside the

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}[\bm{\alpha}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\alpha}^{(R)}]\times_{2}[\bm{\alpha}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\alpha}^{(R)}]\times_{3}\bm{Z}\odot^{\top}\bm{\beta},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}[\bm{\alpha}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\alpha}^{(R)}]\times_{2}[\bm{\alpha}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\alpha}^{(R)}]\times_{3}\bm{Z}\odot^{\top}\bm{\xi}.

(3)

For the sake of consistent estimation, we further impose the block structure in
\bm{\beta}
and
\bm{\xi}
as in the mixture multi-layer stochastic block model (MMSBM) for network data

)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}=\bm{Z}\overline{\bm{\beta}},\text{ and }\bm{\xi}=\bm{Z}\overline{\bm{\xi}},\text{ for }\overline{\bm{\beta}},\overline{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times L},

where

Finally, different from most tabular tensor data analysis, the Hi-C tensor resembles a functional tensor with consecutive smoothness
(
Dolgov et al., 2021
;
Luo et al., 2023
;
Ni et al., 2023
;
Han et al., 2024
)
. One important feature of the genomic loci is that they are sequentially adjacency to one after another, which forms part of the chromatin. Geometrically, a chromatin resembles a one-dimensional principle curve in a three-dimensional space. Though the chromatin can be folded, twisted, and banded, its smoothness as a curve should be preserved. This further motivates us to model
\{\bm{\alpha}_{i,.}\}_{i=1}^{N}
as a sequence of points locating in a smooth curve. In this paper, we use basis expansion to model the smoothness. Specifically, we choose some orthonormal basis functions
\{h_{q}(t):t\in[0,T]\}_{q=1}^{Q}
for some bandwidth
T>0
, and approximate any smooth function by a linear combination of the

’s. Denote
\bm{H}=(H_{i,q})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times Q}
such that
H_{i,q}=h_{q}(iT/N)
is the realization of the basis functions in the grid
iT/N
, for
i\in[N]
. The embedding of the genomic loci for cells in the
r

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}^{(r)}=\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}^{(r)},

for
r\in[R]
, where
\bm{\Gamma}^{(r)}\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times L}
is the corresponding coefficient matrix. Although binning typically results in evenly spaced genomic loci, it still serves as a good approximation of the actual distances between genomic loci, given the high density of loci along the chromatin. Irregular spacing could be handle by binning in a non-uniform way, while it will introduce another layer of complexity. Collectively, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}=\bm{H}[\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\Gamma}^{(R)}]:=\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}.

(4)

Plugging (

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}\times_{2}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}\times_{3}\widetilde{\bm{\beta}},\text{ and }\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}\times_{2}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}\times_{3}\tilde{\bm{\xi}}.

(5)

Therefore, the underlying parameters for the model are
\bm{\Gamma},\overline{\bm{\beta}},\overline{\bm{\xi}}
and
\bm{Z}
.

To summarize, the proposed data-driven tensor decomposition approach includes the following interesting and important features. It employs a zero-inflated Poisson distribution to address the noisy sparsity of the contact counts among the genomic loci pairs and introduces cluster structure to accommodate the heterogeneity among the cells. It is then followed by a doubly tensor decomposition of the transformed Poisson intensity tensor and the transformed masking probability tensor with homogeneous patterns of cells within the same cluster, which greatly reduces the number of parameters and makes estimation feasible. The latent space embeddings of the genomic loci are further imposed smooth curve-shape structure as chromatin. Taking all the components into consideration, we term the proposed model as zero-inflated tensor model with smoothing (
ZITS
).

4
Theory

Under the proposed SZIBM model, we can write the Hi-C tensor as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{E}\bm{\mathcal{C}}+\left(\bm{\mathcal{C}}-\mathbb{E}\bm{\mathcal{C}}\right)=(\bm{\mathcal{J}}-\bm{\mathcal{P}})*\bm{\Lambda}+\left[\bm{\mathcal{C}}-(\bm{\mathcal{J}}-\bm{\mathcal{P}})*\bm{\Lambda}\right],

where
(\bm{\mathcal{J}}-\bm{\mathcal{P}})*\bm{\Lambda}
represents the low-dimensional signal of the Hi-C data while
[\bm{\mathcal{C}}-(\bm{\mathcal{J}}-\bm{\mathcal{P}})*\bm{\Lambda}]
corresponds to the noise. The key to the success of the model lies in the identifiability of the model and controlling the noise. This is because identifiability of the ZIP distribution allows us to distinguish the true zeros from the false ones probabilistically and identifiability of the low-rank tensor decomposition allows us to estimate the latent embedding of the chromatin and the clusters of the cells correctly, while controlling the noise provides possibility to consistently estimate these signals from the data. In this section, we will provide identifiability guarantees in Section
4.1
, tail and concentration behaviors of zero-inflated Poisson random variables in Section
4.2
, consistency theory for our penalized likelihood estimator in Section
4.3
, and discussion on false zero imputation from a Bayes optimal classifier perspective in Section
4.4
.

4.1
Identifiability

The identifiability of the probabilistic model (
1
) is foundamental. Real-life Hi-C tensors are usually sparse in the sense that it contains many zero contact observations. Only when the probabilistic model (
1
) is identifiable, can we correctly estimate the unique probabilities that the zero observations are indeed no contacts and that non-zero contacts are accidentally masked by zeros. With identifiability, we can further impute those zero observations with high probabilities being missing values by the corresponding Poisson intensities, which could provide better knowledge on the data structures and for downstream analysis. The following proposition states the identifiability of zero-inflated Poisson distribution.

Proposition 1
.

(Identifiability of zero-inflated Poisson distribution) Suppose there are two sets of parameters
\{(p_{i,j,k},\lambda_{i,j,k}):i\leq j\in[n],k\in[K]\}
and
\{(p^{\prime}_{i,j,k},\lambda^{\prime}_{i,j,k}):i\leq j\in[n],k\in[K]\}
parametrize the same distribution (
1
), we have
p_{i,j,k}=p^{\prime}_{i,j,k}
and
\lambda_{i,j,k}=\lambda^{\prime}_{i,j,k}
, for
i\leq j\in[n]
and
k\in[K]
.

We next turn to investigate the identifiability of the doubly curve-oriented tensor decomposition. Once the SZIBM is identifiable, the clustering memberships of the cells and the latent embeddings for the genomic loci for cells in every cluster will be uniquely and well defined. We begin with some useful notations. Let
\kappa_{\bm{A}}
be the Kruskal’s k-rank of a matrix
\bm{A}
, which is the maximal number
\kappa
such that any
\kappa
columns of
\bm{A}
are linearly independent. Clearly,
\kappa_{\bm{A}}
equals the rank of
\bm{A}
if
\bm{A}
has full column rank, and
\kappa_{\bm{A}}
could be smaller than the rank of
\bm{A}
in general. For ease of notation, denote
\psi^{-1}(r)=\{k:\psi_{k}=r\}
be the index set of the cells belongs to the
r
-th cluster and
K_{r}=|\psi^{-1}(r)|
be its cardinality. In addition, denote
\bm{\beta}_{\psi^{-1}(r),\cdot}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{r}\times L}
as a sub-matrix of
\bm{\beta}
containing the rows in
\psi^{-1}(r)
only. The following lemma provides a sufficient condition for model identifiability.

Proposition 2
.

(Identifiability of doubly tensor decomposition) Suppose
\kappa_{\bm{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top},\bm{\xi}^{\top})^{\top}}\geq RL+1
and there is no zero row in the matrix
[\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi}]
, the cluster membership
\bm{Z}
will be identifiable up to relabeling (column permutation). Given a particular order of the labels for the clusters, the model parameters
\bm{\beta}
and
\bm{\Gamma}
are identifiable up to some simultaneous column permutations and scalar multiplication of
\bm{\Gamma}^{(r)}
,
\bm{\beta}_{\psi^{-1}(r),.}
and
\bm{\xi}_{\psi^{-1}(r),.}
, for
r\in[R]
.

Clearly, the cluster membership matrix
\bm{Z}
could be directly identified from the the proposed SZIBM under mild condition. This is a better result than many latent space models for tensor data. For example, in the hypergraph embedding model
(
Zhen and Wang, 2023
)
, only the latent embedding of the vertices in a hypergraph network are identifiable, while identifying their cluster memberships requires more refined structure on the embedding centers among the clusters. The reason that we can identify the cluster membership without additional structures is because the proposed model incorporates
\bm{Z}
directly in the model via the Khatri-Rao product, and the row-wise block structure
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}
allows us to determine the cluster membership for every cell. In addition, scalar multiplication issue among
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
does not change the cluster structure. Besides, the permutation issue is only restrict to inside a cluster instead of arbitrary, yielding the same interpretation for the genomic loci embeddings of the cells belonging to any particular cluster. All of these neater identifiability results showcase the proposed doubly tensor decomposition is non-trivial.

We remark that the sufficient condition
\kappa_{\bm{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{\top})^{\top}}\geq RL+1
is mild and easy to satisfy. This is because the columns of both
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}
are sequentially organized into different clusters. Any column from one cluster in
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
or
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}
will be orthogonal to any column from another cluster. Hence, concatenating
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}
vertically will process the same orthogonal property, and the Kruskal’s
K
-rank of
(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{\top})^{\top}
will be no less than that of
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
or
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}
, showing that it is easier for the proposed doubly tensor decomposition to have identifiability guarantee compared to the conventional single tensor decomposition. In the simple case that both
\bm{\alpha}
and
(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{\top})^{\top}
have full column ranks, the sufficient condition reduces to
RL\geq 2
.

Before closing this section, we finally remark that, on one hand, as the chromatin are in the
3
-dimensional space, we could take
L=3
. However, this is not necessary. If the 3D structure of the chromatin is too complicated, it might require
Q
to be rather large to obtain accurate function approximations, which leads to heavy computational burden. This phenomenon could be alleviated when embedding the chromatin into a slightly higher dimensional space. On the other hand, if the
L
is too large and
Q
is quiet small, the tensor decomposition is not guaranteed to be identifiable as the sufficient condition in Lemma
2
will not satisfy since
\kappa_{\alpha}\leq\min\{N,Q,RL\}
according to the smoothness constraint of
\bm{\alpha}
in (
4
). In principle, we suggest to choose
L\in\{3,4,5\}
and
Q\geq L
.

4.2
Tail behaviors of zero-inflated Poisson distribution

In this subsection, we study the tail behaviors and concentration property of the zero-inflated Poisson random variable, which forms an important building block of the consistency results in the next subsection. Intuitively, a zero-inflated Poisson random variable is supposed to have slightly lighter tail than the Poisson random variable with the same intensity parameter, which is sub-exponential. Recall that a random variable is sub-exponential if and only if its
\psi_{1}
-Orlicz norm is finite. The following proposition characterizes the sub-exponential property of the zero-inflated Poisson random variable.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose
X\sim
Poisson
(\lambda)
,
C\sim
ZIP
(p,\lambda)
, we have

\displaystyle\|X\|_{\psi_{1}}=\left(\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log 2\right)\right)^{-1},\text{ and }\|C\|_{\psi_{1}}=\left(\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\frac{2-p}{1-p}\right)\right)^{-1},

where
||\cdot||_{\psi_{1}}=\inf\{t>0:\mathbb{E}e^{\frac{|\cdot|}{t}}\leq 2\}
is the
\psi_{1}
-Orlicz norm.

Since
\frac{2-p}{1-p}\geq 2
and the equality holds only when
p=0
, we have
\|C\|_{\psi_{1}}<\|X\|_{\psi_{1}}
, showing that
C
has lighter tail behaviors as expected. This is also reflated on the fact that
C=B\tilde{C}
for
B\sim
Bernoulli
(1-p)
and
\tilde{C}\sim
Poisson
(\lambda)
which is independent with
B
. Therefore, for any
u>0
, by the law of total probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(C\geq u)=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{C}>u|B=1)\mathbb{P}(B=1)=(1-p)\mathbb{P}(X>u)<\mathbb{P}(X>u).

An immediate result from Proposition 3 is that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(C>u)\leq 2\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\frac{2-p}{1-p}\right)^{-u},

(6)

showing that the tail distribution of
C
decays exponentially. Yet,
6
does not provide a two-sided concentration bound for
C
and it can not directly generalize to a sharp tail bound for the average of independent ZIP random variables. Toward this end, we need other characterizations of the centered moment generating function of the ZIP random variables, leading to the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
C\sim ZIP(p,\lambda)
, then its centered moment generating function satisfies

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}e^{t\left(C-(1-p)\lambda\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda[1+p(1-p)\lambda]t^{2}}{1-\max\{1,\lambda\}t},\text{ for }|t|<\frac{1}{\max\{1,\lambda\}}.

Moreover, let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
be independent zero-inflated Poisson random variables with parameters
(p_{i},\lambda_{i})
, respectively. For any
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
M>0
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}[C_{i}-(1-p_{i})\lambda_{i}]\geq M\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}M^{2}}{2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}[1+p_{i}(1-p_{i})\lambda_{i}]+nM\max\{\|\bm{a}\|_{\infty},\|\bm{a}*\bm{\lambda}\|_{\infty}\}\right)}\right),

where
\bm{a}*\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
(\bm{a}*\bm{\lambda})_{i}=a_{i}\lambda_{i}
.

The first part of Theorem
1
provides another characterization of the sub-exponential property of the ZIP random variable, and the second part quantifying the concentration behaviors of a linear combination of independent ZIP random variables directly follows from the Bernstein’s inequality. These sub-exponential tail property and concentration behavior play a crucial role in deriving the consistency result in the next subsection.

4.3
Consistency

In this subsection, we establish the asymptotic consistency of our penalized negative log-likelihood estimator. To do this, we first define the parameter space of interest

\displaystyle\Omega=\{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi}):\|\bm{\Gamma}_{.,l}\|=1,\text{ for }l\in[RL],\bm{Z}\in\Delta,\|\bm{\beta}\|_{\infty}\leq\beta_{\max},\|\bm{\xi}\|_{\infty}\leq\xi_{\max}\},

where
\Delta
is the set of all possible clustering assignment matrices, and
\beta_{\max}
together with
\xi_{\max}
quantify the boundary of the parameter space. Since the doubly tensor decomposition on
\bm{\eta}
and
\bm{\Theta}
is identifiable up to scaling, we fix the columns of
\bm{\Gamma}
to have unit norm. Note that these imply that there exists some quantities
\lambda_{\min}
and
\lambda_{\max}
depending on
\xi_{\max}
, such that

\lambda_{\min}\leq\lambda_{i,j,k}\leq\lambda_{\max}
, for any
i\neq j\in[N]
and
k\in[K]
. Similarly, the range of
p_{i,j,k}
is determined by
\beta_{\max}
. As a result, if we want the Hi-C tensor to be sparse, or equivalently many
1-p_{i,j,k}
’s vanish at some rate, we can allow
\beta_{\max}
to diverge accordingly. Without loss of generality, we can further assume
\lambda_{\max},\xi_{\max},\beta_{\max}>1
to simplify our theoretical development.

Next, denote
\bm{\Gamma}^{*}
,
\bm{Z}^{*}
,
\bm{\beta}^{*},\bm{\xi}^{*}
as the underlying true parameters with
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*}=\left((\bm{Z}^{*})^{\top}\odot(\bm{\beta}^{*})^{\top}\right)^{\top}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*}=\left((\bm{Z}^{*})^{\top}\odot(\bm{\xi}^{*})^{\top}\right)^{\top}
. According to the definition of
KL
-divergence or Jensen’s inequality, we have
(\bm{\Gamma}^{*},\bm{Z}^{*},\bm{\beta}^{*},\bm{\xi}^{*})=\arg\min_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in\Omega}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\Gamma},(\bm{Z}^{\top}\odot\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top},(\bm{Z}^{\top}\odot\bm{\xi}^{\top})^{\top};\bm{\mathcal{C}}\right)
, implying that
(\bm{\Gamma}^{*},\bm{Z}^{*},\bm{\beta}^{*},\bm{\xi}^{*})
is the population level maximum likelihood estimator and the empirical counterpart should be close to it. As such, we consider any penalized negative log-likelihood estimator
(\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}},\widehat{\bm{Z}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\widehat{\bm{\xi}})\in\Omega
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}},\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}},\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}};\bm{\mathcal{C}})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mu}(\bm{\Gamma}^{*},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*};\bm{\mathcal{C}})+\epsilon_{N,K}=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Gamma}^{*},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*};\bm{\mathcal{C}})+\epsilon_{N,K},

(7)

for some vanishing quantity
\epsilon_{N,K}>0
depending on
N
and
K
, whose asymptotic order will be determined later. Herein,
\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}}=(\widehat{\bm{Z}}^{\top}\odot\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}
, and
\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}}=(\widehat{\bm{Z}}^{\top}\odot\widehat{\bm{\xi}}^{\top})^{\top}
. Clearly,
(\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}},\widehat{\bm{Z}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\widehat{\bm{\xi}})
is not necessary to be the global minimizer of the penalized negative log-likelihood as (
7
) just requires the objective value given by
(\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}},\widehat{\bm{Z}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\widehat{\bm{\xi}})
is no less than that given by the underlying true parameter with an additive error term
\epsilon_{N,K}
accounting for the computation error. When
\epsilon_{N,K}
approaches zero, the estimator will be refined as a better estimator in terms of minimizing the objective function. We are thus interested in investigating the asymptotic property of such an estimator. For simplicity, we further denote

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}^{*}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}^{*}\times_{2}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}^{*}\times_{3}\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*},\bm{\Theta}^{*}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}^{*}\times_{2}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}^{*}\times_{3}\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*},

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\eta}}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{H}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}\times_{2}\bm{H}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}\times_{3}\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}},\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{H}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}\times_{2}\bm{H}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}\times_{3}\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}},

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}=e^{\eta_{i,j,k}^{*}},\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}=e^{\widehat{\eta}_{i,j,k}},p^{*}_{i,j,k}=\frac{1}{e^{\theta^{*}_{i,j,k}}+1},\text{ and }\widehat{p}_{i,j,k}=\frac{1}{e^{\widehat{\theta}_{i,j,k}}+1},

for any
i,j\in[N]
and
k\in[K]
. The following large deviation inequality quantifies the closeness between the ZIP distributions of the Hi-C tensor parametrized by
(\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}},\widehat{\bm{Z}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\widehat{\bm{\xi}})
and
(\bm{\Gamma}^{*},\bm{Z}^{*},\bm{\beta}^{*},\bm{\xi}^{*})
in terms of KL-divergence.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
1-p_{i,j,k}\geq s_{N,K}
for some possibly vanishing sparsity threshold
s_{N,K}
depending on
N
and
K
, for any
i,j\in[N]
and
k\in[K]
, then for any estimator
(\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}},\widehat{\bm{Z}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\widehat{\bm{\xi}})
satisfying (
7
), there exit some universal constants
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
and
M_{3}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}KL_{\text{ZIP}}(p_{i,j,k}^{*},\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}||\widehat{p}_{i,j,k},\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k})\geq 4\epsilon_{N,K}\right)\leq M_{1}\exp\left(-M_{2}\epsilon_{N,K}\varphi_{N,K}s_{N,K}\right).

where

\displaystyle\epsilon_{N,K}=\frac{M_{3}(QRL+KR+2KL)\log\varphi_{N,K}}{\varphi_{N,K}s_{N,K}}.

The proof of the Theorem
2
is non-trivial. It highly explores and exploits the sub-exponential properties of the zero-inflated Poisson distribution and its hurdle Poisson representation whose KL divergence can be tackled easier. Note that every ZIP distribution can be reparametrized as a hurdle Poisson distribution, while the other way is not true. Detail discussion of the relationships between ZIP distribution and Hurdle Poisson distribution are relegated to Appendix
D
and the expression of the KL-divergence between two hurdle Poisson distribution is provided in Lemma
5
of Appendix
E
.

Similarly to multi-layer network analysis
(
Xu et al., 2023
;
Zhen et al., 2026
)
, the sparsity threshold
s_{N,K}
essentially quantities the signal of the Hi-C tensor and thus appears in the denominator of
\epsilon_{N,K}
. Besides, the term
QRL+KR+2KL
in the numerator of
\epsilon_{N,K}
counts the number of parameters in the doubly tensor decomposition while
\varphi_{N,K}
in the denominator counts the number of independent entries, or samples, of the Hi-C contact counts. As a theoretical example, when
s_{N,K},R,L
are of constant order, we have
\epsilon_{N,K}=\widetilde{O}_{p}(Q/N^{2}K)
when
Q
diverges faster than
K
and
\epsilon_{N,K}=\widetilde{O}_{p}(1/N^{2})

when
Q
diverges slower than
K
. Herein, we use the notation
\widetilde{O}_{p}(\cdot)
to hide the logarithm factor in
O_{p}(\cdot)
for clearer illustration.

Based on the large deviation inequality in Theorem
2
and a sequence of innovation inequalities we develop from Lemma
1
to Lemma
4
in Appendix

Theorem 3
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2
, we further denote
\lambda_{\min}
,
\lambda_{\max}
to be the uniform lower and upper bounds of
\lambda_{i,j,k}
’s for any

and
k\in[K]
, which can be defined in terms of
\xi_{\max}
. With probability at least
1-M_{2}\exp\left(-M_{3}\epsilon_{N,K}\varphi_{N,K}s_{N,K}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\left(\|\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}-\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}-\bm{\Lambda}^{*}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{32}{(1-e^{-\lambda_{\min}})^{2}}\min\left\{\frac{1}{1-s_{N,K}+s_{N,K}e^{-\lambda_{\min}}},\frac{s_{N,K}(1-e^{-\lambda_{\min}})}{16(\lambda_{\max}+1)}\right\}^{-1}\epsilon_{N,K}.

Theorem
3
establishes the asymptotic consistency in terms of Frobenius norm for both the estimation of the zero-inflated probability tensor
\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}
and that of the intensity tensor
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}
. Particularly, when
\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}
are of constant order and
s_{N,K}
vanishes toward
0
as
N
or
K
increase, we have
\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\left(\|\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}-\bm{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}-\bm{\Lambda}^{*}\|_{F}^{2}\right)\leq M_{4}\epsilon_{N,K}
for some constant
M_{4}
with high probability.

4.4
False zero detection and imputation

Recall that we use zero-inflated Poisson distribution to accommodate false zeros in the Hi-C contact counts. Specifically, a zero-inflated Poison random variable
C
can be decomposed as
C=B\tilde{C}
, where
\tilde{C}
is a Poisson random variable modeling the exact contact counts with intensity
\lambda
and
B
is a noisy Bernoulli random variable with successful probability
1-p
, which potentially masks the counts. Therefore, when
C\neq 0
, we can easily conclude that
\tilde{C}=C
and
B=1
. However,
C=0
refers to no contact count only when
\tilde{C}=0
, and it is a false zero when
\tilde{C}\neq 0
but
B=0
. Since we can not get access to the oracle data
\tilde{C}
directly, false zero detection is needed to distinguish the zero observations.

Statistically, we can convert the false zero detection problem into a binary classification problem. Let
Y
be the binary label such that
Y=1
if
C=0
is a false zero and
Y=0
if
C=0
is a true zero. Clearly, the successful probability of
Y
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y=1|C=0)=\frac{p(1-e^{-\lambda})}{p(1-e^{-\lambda})+e^{-\lambda}}.

Next, for any zero observation
C=0
and classifier
\Psi
, we define the classification risk and the corresponding Bayes optimal classifier as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\Psi)=\mathbb{P}\left(\Psi(C)\neq Y|C=0\right),\text{ and }\Psi^{*}=\arg\min_{\Psi}\mathcal{R}(\Psi).

The following theorem provides the expression of
\Psi^{*}
and the excess risk of any classifier
\Psi
.

Theorem 4
.

For a zero-inflated Poisson random variable
C\sim ZIP(p,\lambda)
, when
C=0
, the Bayes optimal classifer for false zero detection is

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}(0)=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }p>(e^{\lambda}-1)^{-1},\\
0\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Moreover, for any classifier
\Psi
, its excess risk is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\Psi)-\mathcal{R}(\Psi^{*})=\frac{\left|p(1-e^{-\lambda})-e^{-\lambda}\right|\ind\left(\Psi(0)\neq\Psi^{*}(0)\right)}{p(1-e^{-\lambda})+e^{-\lambda}}.

Motivated from Theorem
4
and our consistent estimator
\bm{\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}
and
\bm{\widehat{\Lambda}}
, we propose to classify
C_{i,j,k}
as a false zero if and only if
\widehat{p}_{i,j,k}>(e^{\lambda_{i,j,k}}-1)^{-1}
. Moreover, once
C_{i,j,k}
is labeled as a false zero, we can further impute it by the corresponding estimated Poisson intensity
\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}
, for any
i,j\in[n]
and
k\in[K]
.

Simulation studies

In this section, we evaluate the numerical performance of the proposed
ZITS
method. We begin by describing the data generation procedure in Section 5.1. In Section 5.2, we propose several initialization approaches for the model parameters. In Section 5.3, we study the numerical performance of various initialization approaches, and the accuracy of false-zero detection in one cell clusters. In section 5.4, we turn to study the advantages of false-zero imputation for clustering when multiple cell clusters appear.

5.1
Data generation

We impose a piece-wise smooth structure on the genomic sequences through its embedding matrix
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times L}
. Firstly, we define a mean embedding matrix
\overline{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
with
\mu_{\alpha}
in the diagonals and
\frac{\mu_{\alpha}}{L}
in the off-diagonals, where
\mu_{\alpha}
is a parameter to be chosen. Secondly, the
N
genomic loci are continuously cut into
L
segments of equal size
\lfloor\frac{N}{L}\rfloor
and the last segment could be longer as it also includes the reminding genomic loci when
N
is not a multiple of
L
, where
\lfloor x\rfloor
represents the largest integer not exceeding
x
. Thirdly, for loci
i
belonging to segment
j
; that is,

(j-1)\lfloor\frac{N}{L}\rfloor<i\leq j\lfloor\frac{N}{L}\rfloor,
for
j=1,\ldots,L-1
, or
(L-1)\lfloor\frac{N}{L}\rfloor<i\leq N
when
j=L
, the embedding vector is generated by perturbing the corresponding segment center with uniform noise:
\alpha_{i,\ell}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\overline{\alpha}_{j,\ell}+\mathrm{Unif}\bigl([0,\sigma_{\alpha}]\bigr)
,
for
\ell\in[L]
. This construction produces
L
clusters of genomic embeddings, with within-cluster variability controlled by
\sigma_{\alpha}
, a parameter to be chosen.

The cell embedding matrices
\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times L}
are generated analogously, but without adding noise to the block constant structure. The
K
cells are partitioned into
R
equal-sized blocks. Specifically, cell
k
in block
r
if

(r-1)\lfloor\frac{K}{R}\rfloor<k\leq r\lfloor\frac{K}{R}\rfloor
when
r=1,\ldots,R-1
or
(R-1)\lfloor\frac{K}{R}\rfloor<k\leq K
when
r=R
. We first generate block-center matrices

\overline{\bm{\beta}},\overline{\bm{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times L}
with entries

\overline{\beta}_{r,\ell}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}([\mu_{\beta},\mu_{\beta}+\sigma_{\beta}])
and
\overline{\xi}_{r,\ell}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}([\mu_{\xi},\mu_{\xi}+\sigma_{\xi}])
, for
r\in[R],\ \ell\in[L]
, where
\mu_{\beta},\mu_{\xi},\sigma_{\beta},\sigma_{\xi}
are parameters to be chosen.
We then use the corresponding block-center vectors as the cell embedding; that is,

\beta_{k,\ell}=\overline{\beta}_{r,\ell},\xi_{k,\ell}=\overline{\xi}_{r,\ell}
if cell
k
belongs to block
r
, for
\ell\in[L]
. This construction yields cluster-wise block embeddings, with heterogeneity arising exclusively across blocks rather than within blocks.

Given
\bm{\alpha}
,
\bm{\beta}
, and
\bm{\xi}
, we compute the parameter tensors
\bm{\eta}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{\alpha}\times_{2}\bm{\alpha}\times_{3}\bm{\beta}
and
\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{\alpha}\times_{2}\bm{\alpha}\times_{3}\bm{\xi}.

The contact counts
C_{i,j,k}
’s are then generated as

C_{i,j,k}=\widetilde{C}_{i,j,k}\cdot B_{i,j,k},

where
\widetilde{C}_{i,j,k}
and
B_{i,j,k}
are independently generated as

\widetilde{C}_{i,j,k}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_{i,j,k})
and
B_{i,j,k}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1-p_{i,j,k})
,
with
\lambda_{i,j,k}=e^{\eta}_{i,j,k}
and
p_{i,j,k}=\frac{1}{e^{\theta_{i,j,k}}+1}
.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we developed a unified probabilistic framework for sparse count tensors with excess zeros. The proposed approach integrates a zero-inflated Poisson observation model, low-rank CP tensor decomposition, cluster-specific latent embeddings to capture heterogeneity, and functional smoothing along ordered modes. This integration provides a flexible framework for addressing key challenges in modern multiway count data.

We develop a Bayes-optimal classifier for distinguishing structural from technical zeros, enabling principled inference on zero inflation and uncertainty quantification. We establish identifiability of the model parameters and derive consistency rates for the proposed estimators. Simulation studies and analyses of single-cell Hi-C data demonstrate improved performance in signal recovery, zero detection, and latent structure inference compared to existing methods.

Although motivated by chromatin contact data, the proposed framework extends naturally to other multiway count data with zero inflation, heterogeneity, and smooth structure, such as ecological data indexed by species, location, time, and environmental conditions.

Several directions remain for future work. First, while heterogeneity is currently captured via latent embeddings and post hoc clustering, a more principled approach would model cluster membership directly through mixture models for tensor-valued data, enabling explicit probabilistic characterization of subpopulations. Second, scaling the method to ultra-high-dimensional settings such as high-resolution Hi-C data may require distributed optimization and parallel computation. Finally, extending the framework to higher-order tensors and developing data-driven procedures for selecting model complexity, such as the number of latent factors, are important directions for future investigation.

Appendix B
Derivations

B.1
The negative log-likelihood

For a zero-inflated Poisson random variable
C
whose probability mass function is

\mathbb{P}\left(C=c\right)=\begin{cases}p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda},\text{ if }c=0;\\
(1-p)\frac{\lambda^{c}e^{-\lambda}}{c!},\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

where
1-p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\theta}}
and
\lambda=e^{\eta}
. The negative log-likelihood of
C
reads

\displaystyle\quad-\log\left(\left[(1-p)\frac{\lambda^{C}e^{-\lambda}}{C!}\right]^{\ind(C\neq 0)}\left[p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda}\right]^{\ind(C=0)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\ind(C\neq 0)\log\left(\left[(1-p)\frac{\lambda^{C}e^{-\lambda}}{C!}\right]\right)-\left(1-\ind\left(C\neq 0\right)\right)\log\left[p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda}\right]

\displaystyle=\ind(C\neq 0)\log\left(\frac{C!\left[p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda}\right]}{(1-p)\lambda^{C}e^{-\lambda}}\right)-\log\left[p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda}\right]

\displaystyle=\ind(C\neq 0)\left(\log\left(e^{-\lambda}+\frac{p}{1-p}\right)+\lambda-C\log\lambda+\log C!\right)-\log(1-p)-\log(e^{-\lambda}+\frac{p}{1-p}).

Under the logit transformation of
1-p
, we have
\frac{p}{1-p}=e^{-\theta}
and
-\log(1-p)=\log(1+e^{-\theta})
. This leads to

\displaystyle\quad-\log\left(\left[(1-p)\frac{\lambda^{C}e^{-\lambda}}{C!}\right]^{\ind(C\neq 0)}\left[p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda}\right]^{\ind(C=0)}\right)

\displaystyle=\ind(C\neq 0)\left(\log\left(e^{-\lambda}+e^{-\theta}\right)+\lambda-C\log\lambda+\log C!\right)+\log(1+e^{-\theta})-\log(e^{-\lambda}+e^{-\theta})

\displaystyle=\ind(C\neq 0)\left(\log\left(1+e^{\lambda-\theta}\right)-C\log\lambda+\log C!\right)+\log(1+e^{-\theta})+\lambda-\log(1+e^{\lambda-\theta})

\displaystyle=\ind(C\neq 0)\left(\log\left(1+e^{e^{\eta}-\theta}\right)-C\eta+\log C!\right)+\log(1+e^{-\theta})+e^{\eta}-\log(1+e^{e^{\eta}-\theta}).

Note that the term
\ind(C\neq 0)\log C!
only depends on the data but not the model parameters. Therefore, minimizing the negative log-likelihood is equivalent to minimizing

\displaystyle\ind(C\neq 0)\left(\log\left(1+e^{e^{\eta}-\theta}\right)-C\eta\right)+\log(1+e^{-\theta})+e^{\eta}-\log(1+e^{e^{\eta}-\theta}).

It then follows that the negative log-likelihood of the Hi-C tensor, up to an affine transformation is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\Theta},\bm{\eta};\bm{\mathcal{C}}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{N(N+1)K}\sum_{i\leq j\in[n],k\in[K]}\left[\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)\left(\log\left(1+e^{e^{\eta_{i,j,k}-\theta_{i,j,k}}}\right)-C_{i,j,k}\eta_{i,j,k}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\log(1+e^{-\theta_{i,j,k}})+e^{\eta_{i,j,k}}-\log(1+e^{e^{\eta_{i,j,k}-\theta_{i,j,k}}})\right].

B.2
Derivatives in homogeneous case

In homogeneous case that all the cells are from the same clusters, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{\alpha}\times_{2}\bm{\alpha}\times_{3}\bm{\beta}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}\times_{2}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}\times_{3}\bm{\beta},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\bm{\bm{\Theta}}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{\alpha}\times_{2}\bm{\alpha}\times_{3}\bm{\xi}=\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}\times_{2}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}\times_{3}\bm{\xi}.

Therefore, by the chain rule and the symmetry of

Appendix D
More on zero-inflated Poisson distribution

Definition 1
.

A counting random variable
C
follows zero-inflated Poisson distribution with parameter
(p,\lambda)
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(C=c)=\begin{cases}p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda},\text{ if }c=0,\text{ and }\\
(1-p)\frac{\lambda^{c}e^{-\lambda}}{c!},\text{ if }c\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.\end{cases}

Since we can decompose
C=B\widetilde{C}
, where
B
is a Bernoulli random variable with successful probability
1-p
and
\widetilde{C}
independent with
B
is a Poisson random variable with intensity
\lambda
. The expectation of
C
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}C=\mathbb{E}B\widetilde{C}=\mathbb{E}B\mathbb{E}\widetilde{C}=(1-p)\lambda.

By the law of total variance, the variance of
C
is

\displaystyle\text{Var}(C)

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(B\widetilde{C})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\text{Var}(B\widetilde{C}|\widetilde{C})\right]+\text{Var}\left[\mathbb{E}\left(B\widetilde{C}|\widetilde{C}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=p(1-p)\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{C}^{2})+\text{Var}\left[(1-p)\widetilde{C}\right]

\displaystyle=p(1-p)\lambda(1+\lambda)+(1-p)^{2}\lambda

\displaystyle=\lambda(1-p)(p\lambda+1).

Clearly, the variance of
C
is greater than
\lambda
if
\lambda>\frac{1}{1-p}
and smaller than
\lambda

\lambda<\frac{1}{1-p}
.

Proof of Proposition
3

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{|X|/t}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}e^{k/t}\frac{\lambda^{k}e^{-\lambda}}{k!}=\exp\left(\lambda(e^{1/t}-1)]\right),

which is increasing with

\displaystyle t=\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\log 2+1\right)\right)^{-1},

which is the exact

.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{|C|/t}

\displaystyle=p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda}+(1-p)\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}e^{k/t}\frac{\lambda^{k}e^{-\lambda}}{k!}

\displaystyle=p+(1-p)\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}e^{k/t}\frac{\lambda^{k}e^{-\lambda}}{k!}

\displaystyle=p+(1-p)\exp\left(\lambda(e^{1/t}-1)\right),

which is also increasing with

. Setting

\displaystyle t=\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\frac{2-p}{1-p}+1\right)\right)^{-1},

Definition 2
.

A counting random variable
C
follows Hurdle Poisson distribution with parameter
(p,\lambda)
,
p\neq 1
, if

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(C=c)=\begin{cases}p,\text{ if }c=0,\text{ and }\\
\frac{1-p}{1-e^{-\lambda}}\frac{\lambda^{c}e^{-\lambda}}{c!},\text{ if }c\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.\end{cases}

According to the above two definitions, a Hurdle Poisson distribution with parameter
(p,\lambda)
is a zero-inflated Poisson distribution with parameter
\left((p-e^{-\lambda})/(1-e^{-\lambda}),\lambda\right)
, provided that
e^{-\lambda}\leq p<1
. This is because

\displaystyle p=\frac{p-e^{-\lambda}}{1-e^{-\lambda}}+\left(1-\frac{p-e^{-\lambda}}{1-e^{-\lambda}}\right)e^{-\lambda},\text{ and }\frac{1-p}{1-e^{-\lambda}}=1-\frac{p-e^{-\lambda}}{1-e^{-\lambda}}.

Conversely, a zero-inflated Poisson distribution with parameter
(p,\lambda)
is a Hurdle Poisson distribution with parameter
\left(p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda},\lambda\right)
. This is because

\displaystyle 1-p=\frac{1-\left(p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda}\right)}{1-e^{-\lambda}}.

Because
0<e^{-\lambda}\leq p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda}<p+(1-p)=1
, any zero-inflated Poisson distribution can be reparametrized as Hurdle Poisson distribution. However, not any Hurdle Poisson distribution can be reparametrized as zero-inflated Poisson distribution, implying that the family of zero-inflated Poisson distributions is a special subset of the family of Hurdle Poisson distributions.

Proof of Theorem
1
The centered moment generating function of
C
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{t\left(C-(1-p)\lambda\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=1}^{+\infty}(1-p)\frac{\lambda^{c}e^{-\lambda}}{c!}e^{t\left(c-(1-p)\lambda\right)}+\left(p+(1-p)e^{-\lambda}\right)e^{-t(1-p)\lambda}

\displaystyle=(1-p)e^{p\lambda t+\lambda(e^{t}-t-1)}+pe^{-(1-p)\lambda t}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\lambda(e^{t}-t-1)}\left[(1-p)e^{p\lambda t}+pe^{-(1-p)\lambda t}\right]

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda(e^{t}-t-1)}\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda t[B-(1-p)]},

for any
t>0
, where the inequality comes from the fact that
e^{t}-t-1>0
, and
B
is a Bernoulli random variable whose successful probability is
1-p
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda t[B-(1-p)]}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=2}^{+\infty}\frac{(\lambda t)^{k}}{k!}\mathbb{E}[B-(1-p)]^{k}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=2}^{+\infty}\frac{(\lambda t)^{k}}{k!}\{(1-p)p^{k}+p[-(1-p)]^{k}\}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{p(1-p)\lambda^{2}t^{2}}{2}+2p(1-p)\sum_{k=3}^{+\infty}\frac{(\lambda t)^{k}}{6}[p\vee(1-p)]^{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{p(1-p)\lambda^{2}t^{2}}{2}+\frac{p(1-p)\lambda^{3}t^{3}[p\vee(1-p)]^{2}}{2(1-[p\vee(1-p)]\lambda t)}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{p(1-p)\lambda^{2}t^{2}}{2(1-[p\vee(1-p)]\lambda t)}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{p(1-p)\lambda^{2}t^{2}}{2(1-[p\vee(1-p)]\lambda t)}},

where
p\vee(1-p):=\max\{p,1-p\}
and the last three inequalities holds when
t<\frac{1}{[p\vee(1-p)]\lambda}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle e^{t}-t-1=\sum_{k=2}^{+\infty}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2(1-t)},

when
t<1
, leading to

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}e^{t\left(C-(1-p)\lambda\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda t^{2}}{2(1-t)}+\frac{p(1-p)\lambda^{2}t^{2}}{2(1-[p\vee(1-p)]\lambda t)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda[1+p(1-p)\lambda]t^{2}}{2(1-\{1\vee[p\vee(1-p)]\lambda\}t)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda[1+p(1-p)\lambda]t^{2}}{1-\max\{1,\lambda\}t},

where the first two inequalities hold for any
0<t<\frac{1}{\max\{1,[p\vee(1-p)]\lambda\}}
and the last inequality holds for any
0<t<\frac{1}{\max\{1,\lambda\}}
. It is not hard to verify that the above display also holds when
-\max\{1,\lambda\}<t\leq 0
. This implies that

is a sub-exponential random variable with parameter
\left(\lambda[1+p(1-p)\lambda],1\vee[p\vee(1-p)]\lambda\right)
, or simply a sub-exponential random variable with parameter
\left(\lambda[1+p(1-p)\lambda],\max\{1,\lambda\}\right)
.

Since
C_{i}\sim\text{subE}\left(\lambda_{i}[1+p_{i}(1-p_{i})\lambda_{i}],\max\{1,\lambda_{i}\}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}C_{i}\sim\textbf{subE}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}[1+p_{i}(1-p_{i})\lambda_{i}],\max_{i}\max\{|a_{i}|,|a_{i}|\lambda_{i}\}\right).

By the Bernstein’ inequality,

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}[C_{i}-(1-p_{i})\lambda_{i}]\geq M\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}[C_{i}-(1-p_{i})\lambda_{i}]\geq nM\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}M^{2}}{2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}[1+p_{i}(1-p_{i})\lambda_{i}]+nM\max_{i}\max\{|a_{i}|,|a_{i}|\lambda_{i}\}\right)}\right).\qed

Lemma 1
.

For any

, we have

\displaystyle a-b-b\log\frac{a}{b}\geq(\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b})^{2}.

Proof of Lemma

.

\displaystyle a-b-b\log\frac{a}{b}

Lemma 2
.

For any

, we have

\displaystyle\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}\log\frac{x}{y}+\log\frac{e^{y}-1}{e^{x}-1}\geq\left(\sqrt{\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}}-\sqrt{\frac{y}{1-e^{-y}}}\right)^{2}.

Proof of Lemma
2
.
According to Lemma
1

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}}-\sqrt{\frac{y}{1-e^{-y}}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{y}{1-e^{-y}}-\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}-\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}\left(\log\frac{y}{x}+\log\frac{1-e^{-x}}{1-e^{-y}}\right).

It then suffices to show that

\displaystyle\frac{y}{1-e^{-y}}-\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}-\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}\log\frac{1-e^{-x}}{1-e^{-y}}\leq\log\frac{e^{y}-1}{e^{x}-1},

or equivalently the function

for any

and

, where

\displaystyle f_{x}(y)

\displaystyle=\log(e^{y}-1)-\frac{y}{1-e^{-y}}-\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}\log(1-e^{-y})

\displaystyle-\log(e^{x}-1)+\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}+\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}\log(1-e^{-x}).

In fact,

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{x}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{y}}{e^{y}-1}-\frac{1-e^{-y}-ye^{-y}}{(1-e^{-y})^{2}}-\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}\cdot\frac{e^{-y}}{1-e^{-y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-e^{-y}-(1-e^{-y})+ye^{-y}-\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}\cdot e^{-y}(1-e^{-y})}{(1-e^{-y})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-y}\left(\frac{y}{1-e^{-y}}-\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}\right)}{1-e^{-y}}.

Clearly,

and

since

, and the sign of

depends on

only. Let

\displaystyle g(y)=\frac{y}{1-e^{-y}}=\frac{ye^{y}}{e^{y}-1},

for

. The first order derivative of

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(y)=\frac{(e^{y}+ye^{y})(e^{y}-1)-ye^{2y}}{(e^{y}-1)^{2}}=\frac{e^{y}(e^{y}-y-1)}{(e^{y}-1)^{2}}=\frac{e^{y}\sum_{k=2}^{+\infty}y^{k}}{(e^{y}-1)^{2}}>0,

showing that

is a strictly increasing function for

. Consequently,
f_{x}^{\prime}(y)<0
for

,
f_{x}^{\prime}(y)=0
for
y=x
, and
f_{x}^{\prime}(y)>0
for

. This implies that the continuous function
f_{x}(y)
strictly monotone decreases for
y\in(0,x]
and strictly monotone increases for

. Therefore,

, for any

p_{i,j,k}^{*}=\frac{\pi_{i,j,k}^{*}-e^{-\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}}}{1-e^{-\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}}}
and
1-p_{i,j,k}^{*}=\frac{1-\pi_{i,j,k}^{*}}{1-e^{-\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}}}
, similarly for
\widehat{p}_{i,j,k}
and
1-\widehat{p}_{i,j,k}
. Further denote
\varphi_{N,K}=N(N-1)K
be the number of independent zero-inflated Poisson random variables in the Hi-C tensor.

By the definitions of
\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left[\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)\log\left(\frac{C_{i,j,k}!\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}(1-e^{-\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}})}{(1-\widehat{\pi}_{ij,k})\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}^{C_{i,j,k}}e^{-\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}}}\right)-\log\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}\right]+\mu J(\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}},\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left[\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)\log\left(\frac{C_{i,j,k}!\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}(1-e^{-\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}})}{(1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k})(\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k})^{C_{i,j,k}}e^{-\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}}}\right)-\log\pi_{i,j,k}^{*}\right]+\mu J(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*})+\epsilon_{N,K}.

Reorganizing the terms yields that

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{i,jk}}{\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)\left(\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}{\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{1-\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}}{1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{e^{\lambda^{*}}_{i,j,k}-1}{e^{\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}}-1}+C_{i,j,k}\log\frac{\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}}{\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mu J(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*})-\mu J(\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}},\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}})+\epsilon_{N,K}.

(11)

Note that

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)\left(\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}{\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{1-\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}}{1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{e^{\lambda^{*}}_{i,j,k}-1}{e^{\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}}-1}+C_{i,j,k}\log\frac{\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}}{\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}(1-\pi_{i,j,k}^{*})\left[\left(\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}{\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{1-\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}}{1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{e^{\lambda^{*}}_{i,j,k}-1}{e^{\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}}-1}\right)+\frac{\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}}{1-e^{-\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}}}\log\frac{\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}}{\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}\right].

Subtracting this expectation on both sides of the inequality (
F
), we obtain

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}KL_{\text{Hurdle}}(\pi_{i,j,k}^{*},\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}||\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k},\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k})\leq\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left[\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)-(1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k})\right]

\displaystyle\times\left(\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}{\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{1-\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k}}{1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{e^{\lambda^{*}}_{i,j,k}-1}{e^{\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}}-1}\right)+\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left(C_{i,j,k}-\frac{(1-\pi_{i,j,k}^{*})\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}{1-e^{-\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}}}\right)\log\frac{\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k}}{\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\left[J(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*})-J(\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}},\widehat{\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}})\right]+\epsilon_{N,K}.

(12)

Herein, we use the fact that
\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)C_{i,j,k}=C_{i,j,k}
.

Second, define

S=\left\{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi}):\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}KL_{\text{Hurdle}}\big(\pi_{i,j,k}^{*},\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}||\pi_{i,j,k},\lambda_{i,j,k})\geq 4\epsilon_{N,K}\right\},\text{ and }

S_{u}=\left\{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi}):2^{u+1}\epsilon_{N,K}\leq\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}KL_{\text{Hurdle}}\big(\pi_{i,j,k}^{*},\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}||\pi_{i,j,k},\lambda_{i,j,k})\leq 2^{u+2}\epsilon_{N,K}\right\},

for
u=1,2,\ldots
, where
\pi_{i,j,k}
and
\lambda_{i,j,k}
are functions of
(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})
. Clearly,
S=\bigcup_{u=1}^{\infty}S_{u}
.
As such, we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}KL_{\text{Hurdle}}(\pi_{i,j,k}^{*},\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}||\widehat{\pi}_{i,j,k},\widehat{\lambda}_{i,j,k})\geq 4\epsilon_{N,K}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left[\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)-(1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k})\right]\left(\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}{\pi_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{1-\pi_{i,j,k}}{1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{e^{\lambda^{*}}_{i,j,k}-1}{e^{\lambda_{i,j,k}}-1}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad+\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left(C_{i,j,k}-\frac{(1-\pi_{i,j,k}^{*})\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}{1-e^{-\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}}}\right)\log\frac{\lambda_{i,j,k}}{\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}KL_{\text{Hurdle}}(\pi_{i,j,k}^{*},\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}||\pi_{i,j,k},\lambda_{i,j,k})\geq-2\epsilon_{N,K}\right\}\leq\sum_{u=1}^{+\infty}I_{u},

where the inequality comes from (
F
), the fact that
\mu J(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}})\geq 0
, and the assumption that
\mu J(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*},\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*})\leq\epsilon_{N,K}
. Herein,
I_{u}
is defined as

\displaystyle I_{u}=\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\sup_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left[\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)-1+\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}\right]\left(\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}{\pi_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{1-\pi_{i,j,k}}{1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{e^{\lambda^{*}}_{i,j,k}-1}{e^{\lambda_{i,j,k}}-1}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left[\left(C_{i,j,k}-\frac{(1-\pi_{i,j,k}^{*})\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}{1-e^{-\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}}}\right)\log\frac{\lambda_{i,j,k}}{\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}-KL_{\text{Hurdle}}(\pi_{i,j,k}^{*},\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}||\pi_{i,j,k},\lambda_{i,j,k})\right]\geq-2\epsilon_{N,K}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\sup_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left[\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)-1+\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}\right]\left(\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}{\pi_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{1-\pi_{i,j,k}}{1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{e^{\lambda^{*}}_{i,j,k}-1}{e^{\lambda_{i,j,k}}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left(C_{i,j,k}-\frac{(1-\pi_{i,j,k}^{*})\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}{1-e^{-\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}}}\right)\log\frac{\lambda_{i,j,k}}{\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}

\displaystyle\quad\geq\inf_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}KL_{\text{Hurdle}}(\pi_{i,j,k}^{*},\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}||\pi_{i,j,k},\lambda_{i,j,k})-2\epsilon_{N,K}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\sup_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left[\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)-1+\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}\right]\left(\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}{\pi_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{1-\pi_{i,j,k}}{1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{e^{\lambda^{*}}_{i,j,k}-1}{e^{\lambda_{i,j,k}}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\left(C_{i,j,k}-\frac{(1-\pi_{i,j,k}^{*})\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}{1-e^{-\lambda^{*}_{i,j,k}}}\right)\log\frac{\lambda_{i,j,k}}{\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}}\geq 2^{u}\epsilon_{N,K}\Bigg\},

where the last inequality comes from the definition of
S_{u}
and the inequality that
2^{u+1}-2\geq 2^{u}
for
u\geq 1
.

To simplify notations, we denote the following

\displaystyle a_{i,j,k}=\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}{\pi_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{1-\pi_{i,j,k}}{1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{e^{\lambda^{*}}_{i,j,k}-1}{e^{\lambda_{i,j,k}}-1},\quad b_{i,j,k}=\log\frac{\lambda_{i,j,k}}{\lambda_{i,j,k}^{*}},

\displaystyle f_{i,j,k}(C_{i,j,k})=a_{i,,j,k}\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)+b_{i,j,k}C_{i,j,k},\text{ and }Y_{i,j,k}=f_{i,j,k}(C_{i,j,k})-\mathbb{E}f_{i,j,k}(C_{i,j,k}).

It then follows from the standard symmetrization argument that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}Y_{i,j,k}\leq 2\mathbb{E}\sup_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}|\sum_{i\leq j,k}f_{i,j,k}(C_{i,j,k})\varepsilon_{i,j,k}|,

where
\varepsilon_{i,j,k}
’s are independent Redemacher variables, which are also independent with
C_{i,j,k}
’s. This leads to

\displaystyle I_{u}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}Y_{i,j,k}\geq 2^{u}\epsilon_{N,K}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\sup_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}Y_{i,j,k}-(1+\nu)\mathbb{E}\sup_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}Y_{i,j,k}

\displaystyle\quad\geq 2^{u}\epsilon_{N,K}-2(1+\nu)\mathbb{E}\sup_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}|\sum_{i\leq j,k}f_{i,j,k}(C_{i,j,k})\varepsilon_{i,j,k}|\Bigg).

for any
0<\nu\leq 1
.

Third, since
S_{u}
is a compact set,
0<\lambda_{\min}\leq\lambda_{i,j,k}\leq\lambda_{\max}<+\infty
,
e^{-\lambda_{\max}}\leq\pi_{i,j,k}\leq 1-s_{N,K}+s_{N,K}e^{-\lambda_{\min}}
, and
a_{i,j,k}
and
b_{i,j,k}
are continues functions of the model parameters, there exists a parameter tuple
(\bm{\Gamma}^{\dagger},\bm{Z}^{\dagger},\bm{\beta}^{\dagger},\bm{\xi}^{\dagger})\in S_{u}
such that

\displaystyle|f_{i,j,k}(C_{i,j,k})|

\displaystyle\leq|a_{i,j,k}^{\dagger}|\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)+|b_{i,j,k}^{\dagger}|C_{i,j,k}

\displaystyle\leq\left(|\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}{\pi^{\dagger}_{i,j,k}}+\log\frac{1-\pi^{\dagger}_{i,j,k}}{1-\pi^{*}_{i,j,k}}|+|\log\frac{e^{\lambda^{*}}_{i,j,k}-1}{e^{\lambda^{\dagger}_{i,j,k}}-1}|\right)\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)+|\log\frac{\lambda^{\dagger}_{i,j,k}}{\lambda_{i,,k}^{*}}|C_{i,j,k}

\displaystyle:=F_{i,j,k}(C_{i,j,k}),

for any random tensor
\bm{\mathcal{C}}
and any tensor value function
\bm{f}=(f_{i,j,k})
parametrized by
(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}
. Therefore,
\bm{F}=(F_{i,j,k})
could serve as an envelop of the function class parametrized by
S_{u}
, which we further denoted as
\bm{f}_{u}
. It then follows from the uniform entropy bound that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{(\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Z},\bm{\beta},\bm{\xi})\in S_{u}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}|\sum_{i\leq j,k}f_{i,j,k}(C_{i,j,k})\varepsilon_{i,j,k}|\leq m_{1}\varphi_{N,K}^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{1}\sup_{Q}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}\left(\epsilon||\bm{F}||_{Q,2},\bm{f}_{u},L_{2}(Q)\right)}d\epsilon||\bm{F}||_{P,2},

for some universal constant
m_{1}
, where

\displaystyle\|\bm{F}\|_{Q,2}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i\leq j,k}\mathbb{E}_{Q}F_{i,j,k}(C_{i,j,k})^{2}},

the supremum in the right hand side is taken over all distributions parametrized by
S_{u}
,
P
corresponds to the underlying true distribution, and
\mathcal{N}
denotes the covering number in terms of the
L_{2}(Q)
distance. For any
(\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)},\bm{Z}^{(1)},\bm{\beta}^{(1)},\bm{\xi}^{(1)}),(\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)},\bm{Z}^{(2)},\bm{\beta}^{(2)},\bm{\xi}^{(2)})\in S_{u}
parametrized
\bm{f}^{(1)}
and
\bm{f}^{(2)}
, we can upper bound their
L_{2}(Q)
distance as

\displaystyle\quad L_{2}(Q)(\bm{f}^{(1)},\bm{f}^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i,j,k}\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[(a^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-a^{(2)}_{i,j,k})\ind(C_{i,j,k}\neq 0)+(b^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-b^{(2)}_{i,j,k})C_{i,j,k}\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i,j,k}\sum_{c=1}^{+\infty}\left[(a^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-a^{(2)}_{i,j,k})+(b^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-b^{(2)}_{i,j,k})c\right]^{2}(1-p_{i,j,k}^{(Q)})\frac{(\lambda^{(Q)})^{c}e^{-\lambda^{(Q)}}}{c!}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i,j,k}\left[(a^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-a^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}(1-e^{-\lambda_{i,j,k}^{(Q)}})+(b^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-b^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}\lambda_{i,j,k}^{(Q)}(1+\lambda_{i,j,k}^{(Q)})\right](1-p_{i,j,k}^{(Q)})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i,j,k}\left[(a^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-a^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}(1-e^{-\lambda_{i,j,k}^{(Q)}})+(\eta^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-\eta^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}\lambda_{i,j,k}^{(Q)}(1+\lambda_{i,j,k}^{(Q)})\right](1-p_{i,j,k}^{(Q)})}.

Note that

\displaystyle|\frac{\partial a_{i,j,k}}{\partial\theta_{i,j,k}}|=|\frac{\partial a_{i,j,k}}{\partial\pi_{i,j,k}}\cdot\frac{\partial\pi_{i,j,k}}{\partial(1-p_{i,j,k})}\cdot\frac{\partial(1-p_{i,j,k})}{\partial\theta_{i,j,k}}|\leq\frac{1-e^{-\lambda_{i,j,k}}}{4\pi_{i,j,k}(1-\pi_{i,j,k})}=\frac{1}{4\pi_{i,j,k}(1-p_{i,j,k})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4s_{N,K}(1-s_{N,K}+s_{N,K}e^{-\lambda_{\max}})},\text{ and }

\displaystyle|\frac{\partial a_{i,j,k}}{\partial\eta_{i,j,k}}|=|\frac{\partial a_{i,j,k}}{\partial\pi_{i,j,k}}\cdot\frac{\partial\pi_{i,j,k}}{\partial\lambda_{i,j,k}}\cdot\frac{\partial\lambda_{i,j,k}}{\partial\eta_{i,j,k}}-\frac{\lambda_{i,j,k}e^{\lambda_{i,j,k}}}{e^{\lambda_{i,jk}}-1}|=|\frac{\lambda_{i,j,k}e^{-\lambda_{i,j,k}}}{\pi_{i,j,k}(1-e^{-\lambda_{i,j,k}})}-\frac{\lambda_{i,j,k}e^{\lambda_{i,j,k}}}{e^{\lambda_{i,jk}}-1}|

\displaystyle\leq(\lambda_{i,j,k}+1)|\frac{e^{-\lambda_{i,j,k}}}{\pi_{i,j,k}}-1|\leq\lambda_{i,j,k}+1\leq\lambda_{\max}+1,

where the inequality in the second line follows from the monotonicity, and the first inequality in the forth line follows from the proof of Lemma
4
.

Therefore, by the mean value theorem

\displaystyle(a^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-a^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}\leq\frac{(\theta^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-\theta_{i,j,k}^{(2)})^{2}}{2s_{N,K}^{2}(1-s_{N,K}+s_{N,K}e^{-\lambda_{\max}})^{2}}+2(\lambda_{\max}+1)^{2}(\eta_{i,j,k}^{(1)}-\eta^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}.

It then follows that

\displaystyle\quad L_{2}(Q)(\bm{f}^{(1)},\bm{f}^{(2)})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i,j,k}\left[(a^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-a^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}(1-e^{-\lambda_{i,j,k}^{(Q)}})+(\eta^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-\eta^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}\lambda_{i,j,k}^{(Q)}(1+\lambda_{i,j,k}^{(Q)})\right](1-p_{i,j,k}^{(Q)})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i,j,k}\left[(a^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-a^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}(1-\pi^{(Q)}_{i,j,k})+(\eta^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-\eta^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}\lambda_{i,j,k}^{(Q)}(1+\lambda_{i,j,k}^{(Q)})(1-p_{i,j,k}^{(Q)})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i,j,k}\left[\frac{(1-\pi^{(Q)}_{i,j,k})(\theta^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-\theta_{i,j,k}^{(2)})^{2}}{2s_{N,K}^{2}(1-s_{N,K}+s_{N,K}e^{-\lambda_{\max}})^{2}}+(\lambda_{\max}+1)^{2}(3-2\pi^{(Q)}_{i,j,k}-p_{i,j,k}^{(Q)})(\eta_{i,j,k}^{(1)}-\eta^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\varphi_{N,K}}\sum_{i,j,k}\left[\frac{(\theta^{(1)}_{i,j,k}-\theta_{i,j,k}^{(2)})^{2}}{2s_{N,K}^{2}(1-s_{N,K}+s_{N,K}e^{-\lambda_{\max}})^{2}}+3(\lambda_{\max}+1)^{2}(\eta_{i,j,k}^{(1)}-\eta^{(2)}_{i,j,k})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varphi_{N,K}s_{N,K}^{2}(1-s_{N,K}+s_{N,K}e^{-\lambda_{\max}})^{2}}\|\bm{\Theta}^{(1)}-\bm{\Theta}^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{6(\lambda_{\max}+1)^{2}}{\varphi_{N,K}}\|\bm{\eta}^{(1)}-\bm{\eta}^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}.

Taking the low-rank structures of
\bm{\eta}^{(1)},\bm{\eta}^{(2)},\bm{\Theta}^{(1)}
, and
\bm{\Theta}^{(2)}
into consideration, we have

\displaystyle\quad\|\bm{\eta}^{(1)}-\bm{\eta}^{(2)}\|_{F}\leq\|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{H}(\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)}-\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)})\times_{2}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)}\times_{3}\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(1)}\|_{F}

\displaystyle+\|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)}\times_{2}\bm{H}(\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)}-\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)})\times_{3}\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(1)}\|_{F}+\|\bm{\mathcal{I}}\times_{1}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)}\times_{2}\bm{H}\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)}\times_{3}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(1)}-\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(2)})\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)}-\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)}\|_{F}\|\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)}\|_{F}\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(1)}\|_{F}+\|\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)}\|_{F}\|\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)}-\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)}\|_{F}\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(1)}\|_{F}+\|\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)}\|_{F}^{2}\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(1)}-\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(2)}\|_{F},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that the columns of
\bm{H}
are certain realizations of orthonormal function basis. Without loss of generality, we can assume the columns of both
\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)}
and
\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)}
have unit
l_{2}
-norm; otherwise we can rescaled the factors in the last inequality such that
\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)}
and
\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)}
fall into the parameter space
\Omega
. Since
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(1)}=((\bm{Z}^{(1)})^{\top}\odot(\bm{\beta}^{(1)})^{\top})^{\top}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(2)}=((\bm{Z}^{(2)})^{\top}\odot(\bm{\beta}^{(2)})^{\top})^{\top}
, we have
\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(1)}\|_{F}=\|\bm{\beta}^{(1)}\|_{F}
,
\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(2)}\|_{F}=\|\bm{\beta}^{(2)}\|_{F}
, and

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(1)}-\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(2)}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{Z}^{(1)}\odot^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{(1)}-\bm{Z}^{(2)}\odot^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{(1)}\|_{F}+\|\bm{Z}^{(2)}\odot^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{(1)}-\bm{Z}^{(2)}\odot^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{(2)}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{L}\beta_{\max}\|\bm{Z}^{(1)}-\bm{Z}^{(2)}\|_{F}+\|\bm{\beta}^{(1)}-\bm{\beta}^{(2)}\|_{F},

where we use the notation
\odot^{\top}
to denote row-wise Kronecker product to simplify notation. These leads to

\displaystyle\quad\|\bm{\eta}^{(1)}-\bm{\eta}^{(2)}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq 2L\sqrt{RK}\beta_{\max}\|\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)}-\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)}\|_{F}+RL\sqrt{L}\beta_{\max}\|\bm{Z}^{(1)}-\bm{Z}^{(2)}\|_{F}+RL\|\bm{\beta}^{(1)}-\bm{\beta}^{(2)}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=2RL\sqrt{KL}\beta_{\max}\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{RL}}(\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)}-\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)})\|_{F}+RL\sqrt{KL}\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}(\bm{Z}^{(1)}-\bm{Z}^{(2)})\|_{F}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+RL\sqrt{KL}\beta_{\max}\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{KL}\beta_{\max}}(\bm{\beta}^{(1)}-\bm{\beta}^{(2)})\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq 2RL\sqrt{KL}\beta_{\max}\left(\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{RL}}(\bm{\Gamma}^{(1)}-\bm{\Gamma}^{(2)})\|_{F}+\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}(\bm{Z}^{(1)}-\bm{Z}^{(2)})\|_{F}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\left.+\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{KL}\beta_{\max}}(\bm{\beta}^{(1)}-\bm{\beta}^{(2)})\|_{F}\right),

whre we use the assumption that
\beta_{\max}>1
.
