Nuclear Gluon Gravitational Form Factors and Neutron Skins at the Electron–Ion Collider
Lei Wang

Abstract
Coherent quarkonium production at the Electron–Ion Collider can image the average small- x x gluon radius of nuclei, providing a reaction mechanism complementary to parity-violating electron scattering. We develop a calibrated radius-sum-rule framework that connects this gluonic radius to neutron skins and quantify the leading limitations from finite-dipole saturation, nuclear opacity, and instrumental resolution. The central result is that coherent J / ψ J/\psi production contains sufficient shape information for a competitive neutron-skin program, but its precision is not limited by luminosity alone. It is instead controlled by the calibration of the nuclear small- x x gluon density, while the cleaner Υ \Upsilon channel remains statistically limited at early EIC luminosities. This framework identifies what an EIC neutron-skin measurement can robustly add to symmetry-energy studies and which theoretical and experimental controls are required before such a measurement can be interpreted as precision nuclear-structure information.

II
Radius Sum Rule and Calibrated Skin Extraction

For coherent vector-meson production the average amplitude may be written schematically as

{\cal A}(\bm{\Delta})\propto{\cal F}_{g}(\bm{\Delta})=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{b}\,e^{-i\bm{\Delta}\cdot\bm{b}}T_{g}(\bm{b}),\qquad t=-\bm{\Delta}^{2},

(1)

where
T_{g}(\bm{b})
is the configuration-averaged gluon thickness.
The coherent and incoherent Good–Walker moments are

\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\rm coh}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{|\langle{\cal A}\rangle|^{2}}{16\pi},\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\rm inc}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\langle|{\cal A}|^{2}\rangle-|\langle{\cal A}\rangle|^{2}}{16\pi}.

(2)

Thus the coherent slope and diffractive structure constrain the average transverse gluon radius,

\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{g}=-4\left.\frac{\partial\ln{\cal F}_{g}(\bm{\Delta})}{\partial\Delta^{2}}\right|_{\Delta^{2}=0}.

(3)

At leading moment the nuclear gluon thickness can be written as an isoscalar-weighted nucleon sum,

T_{g}(b)=(1-W)T_{p}(b)+WT_{n}(b),\qquad W=\frac{\kappa N}{Z+\kappa N},

(4)

where
T_{p}
and
T_{n}
are the proton and neutron center thicknesses, and
\kappa=g_{n}/g_{p}
is the neutron-to-proton gluon charge ratio.
Including a common intrinsic gluon radius of a bound nucleon gives

\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{g}^{A}=\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{\rm int}+(1-W)\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{p}+W\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{n}.

(5)

Here
\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{g}^{A}
is the nuclear gluon radius extracted from the coherent spectrum,
\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{\rm int}
is the common intrinsic nucleon-gluon contribution, and
\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{p},\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{n}
are the transverse mean-square radii of the proton and neutron center distributions.
Equation (
5
) is not a direct decoding of the raw coherent slope; it is a calibrated relation after the common intrinsic offset has been fixed or subtracted.
An isovector intrinsic difference is a genuine residual systematic.

For the nearly spherical nuclei considered here,
\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle=2\langle r^{2}\rangle/3
.
Defining the intrinsic-subtracted gluon moment
\bar{\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle}_{g}=\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{g}^{A}-\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{\rm int}
, the neutron radius follows from

\displaystyle\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{p}+\frac{\bar{\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle}_{g}-\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{p}}{W},

(6)

\displaystyle\Delta r_{np}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{n}}-\sqrt{\langle r^{2}\rangle_{p}}.

(7)

Equivalently, with
R_{g}^{2}=(3/2)\bar{\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle}_{g}
and
R_{p}^{2}=\langle r^{2}\rangle_{p}
,

\Delta r_{np}=\left[\frac{Z+\kappa N}{\kappa N}R_{g}^{2}-\frac{Z}{\kappa N}R_{p}^{2}\right]^{1/2}-R_{p}.

(8)

This closed-form expression is valid under calibrated common intrinsic gluon radius; it is not an exact model-independent inversion.

The dependence on
\kappa
is structurally suppressed.
Writing
\Delta B_{n}=\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{n}-\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{p}=(\bar{\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle}_{g}-\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{p})/W
gives

\frac{\delta(\Delta B_{n})}{\Delta B_{n}}=-(1-W)\frac{\delta\kappa}{\kappa}.

(9)

For
{}^{208}\mathrm{Pb}
and
\kappa=1
,
W=N/A\simeq 0.61
.
A few-percent prior on
\kappa
therefore contributes a subleading skin uncertainty.
The more serious question is not
\kappa
, but whether finite-dipole saturation changes the radius relation by a common offset or by an isovector distortion.

IV
Finite-Dipole Saturation Correction

In a dipole picture the coherent amplitude contains the factor

\displaystyle{\cal S}(r,b)

\displaystyle=1-\exp[-\Omega(r,b)],

(12)

\displaystyle\Omega(r,b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2N_{c}}r^{2}\alpha_{s}(\mu^{2})\,xg(x,\mu^{2})\,T_{A}(b),

(13)

\displaystyle\mu^{2}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}^{2}+\frac{C}{r^{2}}.

The leading-twist additive limit corresponds to expanding Eq. (
12
) in
\Omega
.
At finite effective dipole size, the exponential suppresses the dense interior and shifts the coherent radius outward.
We define the saturation-induced radius offset

\Delta_{\rm sh}\equiv\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{\rm coh}-\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{T_{A}},

(14)

where
\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{\rm coh}
is computed from the impact-parameter profile of the dipole amplitude folded with the vector-meson overlap, and
\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{T_{A}}
is the leading-moment radius of the nuclear thickness.

The finite-dipole calculation uses boosted-Gaussian
J/\psi
and
\Upsilon
overlaps, an IP-Sat opacity with RSVK-type parameters, and leading-order gluon-only DGLAP evolution from

xg(x,\mu_{0}^{2})=A_{g}x^{-\lambda_{g}}(1-x)^{5.6}
.
Its purpose is not to replace a global exclusive-production analysis, but to quantify the correction that the original Fisher forecast treated as a small shadowing residual.

For
{}^{208}\mathrm{Pb}
at
x=10^{-3}
, the correction is large for
J/\psi

\Delta_{\rm sh}(J/\psi)\simeq 1.14~{\rm fm}^{2},

(15)

about
5.4\%
of the additive radius and comparable to the neutron-skin signal in
\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{n}-\langle b_{\perp}^{2}\rangle_{p}
.
At
Q^{2}=10~{\rm GeV}^{2}
it decreases to about
0.80~{\rm fm}^{2}
, and for
\Upsilon
it falls to about
0.27~{\rm fm}^{2}
.
These three values correspond to fractional shifts of approximately
5.4\%

3.8\%

1.3\%

The additive-radius interpretation of coherent

VI
Relation to the Symmetry Energy

The gluonic measurement constrains the neutron skin

\Delta r_{np}=\sqrt{\langle r^{2}\rangle_{n}}-\sqrt{\langle r^{2}\rangle_{p}},

(17)

where
\langle r^{2}\rangle_{n}
and
\langle r^{2}\rangle_{p}
are the neutron and proton mean-square radii.
It does not directly measure the symmetry-energy slope,

L=3n_{0}\left.\frac{\partial S(n)}{\partial n}\right|_{n_{0}},

(18)

but can be mapped to
L
only through an external nuclear-structure correlation.
The final step uses an external EDF correlation,

\Delta r_{np}(A,Z)=c_{1}(A,Z)L+c_{0}(A,Z),

(19)

where
L
is in MeV and radii are in fm.
For the illustrative mapping in Fig.
5

(c_{1},c_{0})=(0.00107,0.103)
for
{}^{208}\mathrm{Pb}

(0.00090,0.115)

, and
(0.000546,0.138)
for
{}^{48}\mathrm{Ca}

The

Other EDF ensembles shift the absolute mapping, so comparisons with PREX-II and CREX should be interpreted as internal pulls within a common correlation scheme, not as a model-independent determination of

The connection to neutron-star phenomenology is correspondingly indirect.
The measured skin constrains the symmetry pressure near saturation,
P_{\rm sym}(n_{0})\simeq n_{0}L/3
, not a stellar radius by itself.
The value of the gluonic measurement is that it supplies a third reaction mechanism in the chain from finite nuclei to dense matter.

VII
Discussion

The analysis separates three levels of claim.
First, the radius sum rule is structurally robust at leading moment once the common intrinsic nucleon-gluon radius is calibrated.
The dependence on the neutron-to-proton gluon charge is suppressed by Eq. (
9
), and the coherent channel is the correct Good–Walker moment for an average radius observable.
Second, the coherent
J/\psi
spectrum contains enough shape information, after realistic
t
smearing, to support a Fisher-level skin reach near
0.02
fm.
This is the content of Fig.
2
and the original multi-nucleus projections.
Third, the finite-dipole correction is large enough that the extraction should not be presented as an uncalibrated precision measurement.
The IP-Sat opacity calculation changes the interpretation from luminosity-limited precision to opacity-limited calibration.

This reframing also clarifies why the earlier deformation route was less promising.
Incoherent observables are sensitive to Good–Walker fluctuations, subnucleonic structure, and hot-spot variance.
Those effects are important EIC physics, but they obscure a clean extraction of a small deformation or quadrupole skin.
The average neutron skin is better conditioned because it enters the coherent one-body density and is protected by the leading-moment radius relation.

Several limitations remain.
The finite-dipole calculation uses a smooth optical nuclear thickness and an IP-Sat opacity with leading-order gluon evolution.
Subnucleonic fluctuations, full exclusive-event reconstruction, detector acceptance, nuclear breakup selection, and correlated global fits of proton, deuteron, and nuclear data are required for a full experimental proposal.
The treatment of
\kappa\simeq 1
should also be connected to global nuclear parton information.
These limitations do not erase the channel trade-off or the isoscalar nature of the saturation offset, but they define the defensible level of the claim.

VIII
Conclusions

Coherent gluon imaging at the EIC can provide an independent constraint on nuclear neutron skins, but the achievable precision is controlled by the calibration of the gluon-to-matter-radius map.
The leading-moment sum rule gives a transparent connection between the coherent gluon radius and the neutron radius after a common intrinsic nucleon-gluon size is calibrated.
The original coherent
J/\psi
Fisher forecast is strong, with
0.02
fm-level statistical and instrumental reach for
{}^{208}\mathrm{Pb}
,
{}^{124}\mathrm{Sn}
, and
{}^{48}\mathrm{Ca}
.
The finite-dipole calculation shows why this should not be presented as an uncalibrated precision claim: the
J/\psi
saturation offset in
{}^{208}\mathrm{Pb}
is
\Delta_{\rm sh}\simeq 1.1~{\rm fm}^{2}
at
x=10^{-3}
.
Because this offset is nearly isoscalar, it can be calibrated; because it is large, the residual opacity uncertainty sets the floor.

With
10\%
opacity control, the
J/\psi
channel gives a
{}^{208}\mathrm{Pb}
skin uncertainty near
0.03
fm.
The
\Upsilon
channel is cleaner theoretically but statistics limited at first-generation luminosity.
The resulting PRD-level message is therefore constructive and bounded: coherent gluon imaging is a viable neutron-skin program, complementary to PREX/CREX and useful for symmetry-energy studies, provided that the nuclear small-
x
opacity is constrained with sufficient precision.

The expected yield in the

\mu_{i}=\mathcal{L}\,\epsilon\,\Delta t_{i}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\rm coh}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)_{i},

changes the relative proton and neutron weights in an isoscalar sum, whereas the skin is extracted from a difference of radii.
This structural suppression is important because it prevents the extraction from being dominated by a poorly known overall neutron gluon charge.
It does not remove the need for a prior on
\kappa
; rather, it shows that a modest prior is adequate compared with the larger opacity-calibration uncertainty discussed in the main text.

Appendix C
Born-Level Diffractive Pattern

Figure
8
shows the unsmeared coherent spectra corresponding to Fig.
2
.
The sharp diffractive minima move with the neutron skin and explain the origin of the Fisher information.
In the Born limit, the minima are mathematically clean and the ratio of two spectra can become very large near a zero.
That feature is useful for diagnosing where the sensitivity originates, but it is not a realistic observable.
Momentum-transfer resolution, bin migration, and incoherent leakage fill the minima and convert the formal zero into a finite shape distortion.
The experimentally relevant information is therefore not the singular Born ratio but the low-
|t|
curvature and the residual depth and position of the smeared minima.
For this reason the main text uses the smeared spectrum as the forecast observable while retaining the Born figure only as a diagnostic.

Appendix F
Good–Walker Coherent/Incoherent Separation

Figure
10
shows a Good–Walker decomposition for spherical
{}^{208}\mathrm{Pb}
including nucleon-position, hot-spot, and color-charge fluctuations.
The coherent component retains the diffractive structure needed for a radius measurement.
The incoherent component is smoother and becomes important near the coherent minima, where it acts as a background rather than as a precision skin observable.
This supports the choice of the coherent channel as the primary observable.
The reason is not that incoherent diffraction is uninteresting; on the contrary, it is sensitive to fluctuations of the nuclear wave function and subnucleonic gluon structure.
Those fluctuations, however, are not the degrees of freedom that define an average one-body neutron skin.
They dilute the clean radius interpretation and make deformation-sensitive observables substantially more dependent on fluctuation modeling.
The coherent channel projects onto the event-averaged density and is therefore the natural channel for the leading-moment skin program considered here.
