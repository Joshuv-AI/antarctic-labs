Before new variants in genes associated with hematuria or proteinuria can be classified as causative, their pathogenicity must be demonstrated
Authors: Sounira Mehri, Josef Finsterer
Published: 2026

We read with interest the article by Oh et al. on the diagnostic utility of whole exome sequencing (WES) combined with mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) analysis for elucidating the genetic background of 77 pediatric patients with hematuria or proteinuria and a corresponding positive family history [1]. The diagnostic yield of WES was 55%, with COL4A-associated nephropathy being the most frequent diagnosis [1]. An asymptomatic carrier of the m.3243A>G variant in the MT-TL1 gene was identified [1]. The study is promising but requires further discussion.

Firstly, the pathogenicity of a new variant was assessed using in silico methods (computer-aided predictive models) rather than by correlation with the phenotype, population frequency data, segregation analyses, or functional or biochemical methods. Although in silico methods are frequently used to estimate the pathogenicity of a variant, they have several drawbacks. Most tools predict pathogenicity based on sequence conservation, protein structure, or statistical models, rather than using direct biological evidence. Results from different tools often diverge. Sensitivity and specificity are low. Accuracy is low for splice site variants, non-coding/regulatory variants, structural variants, and insertions/deletions (indels). Accuracy depends on the training of the datasets. Often, there is no connection to the clinical context. Many tools are based on assumptions about protein structure or function that may not fully reflect biological reality [2].

The second point is that the patient with mitochondrial nephropathy was not systematically examined for multisystem disease. Carriers of the m.3243A>G variant typically exhibit multisystem involvement, either at disease onset or during the course of the disease [3]. This multisystem involvement is often subclinical, particularly in the early stages of the disease. Therefore, it is crucial to evaluate these patients clinically and with appropriate instrumental investigations for subclinical multisystem involvement. Besides kidneys, the most frequently affected organs include the brain, eyes, ears, endocrine organs, heart, and gastrointestinal tract. Phenotypic features of m.3243A>G carriers that are frequently overlooked or not attributed to the m.3243A>G variant, in addition to heart disease, structural brain disease, and hearing loss, include short stature, low weight, facial deformities, migraine, ptosis, diplopia, hypothyroidism, hepatopathy, lactic acidosis, gastrointestinal disease, or behavioral and cognitive disturbances [3]. Did the m.3243A>G carrier present with one or more of these features?

Thirdly, the low heteroplasmy rates in the mother and siblings of the m.3243A>G carrier do not prevent the development of symptoms. Since heteroplasmy rates were determined in the blood and no clinical abnormalities were found, it is conceivable that they, too, may develop kidney disease over time. The mother already exhibited microhematuria [1]. Therefore, regular follow-up examinations are necessary in these patients to avoid overlooking clinical manifestations of mitochondrial disease.

In conclusion, the pathogenicity of novel variants in genes associated with proteinuria or hematuria should be confirmed by reliable tests, and patients with mitochondrial nephropathy should be prospectively screened for subclinical multisystem involvement.
