Extensions of the Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem via the arithmetic level- d d inequality
Carlo Francisco E. Adajar, Rishika Agrawal, Mukul Rai Choudhuri, Chian Yeong Chuah, Steve Fan, Swaroop Hegde, Andrew Lott, Krishnamohan Nandakumar, Nagendar Reddy Ponagandla

Abstract
Very recently, Green and Sawhney obtained a quasipolynomial bound in the Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem for square differences by proving an “arithmetic level- d d ” inequality, thereby yielding a greatly improved density increment scheme. We adapt their method to general intersective polynomials h ∈ ℤ ⁡ [ x ] h\in\mathbb{Z}[x] and obtain an analogous quasipolynomial upper bound for the largest subset of { 1 , 2 , … , X } \{1,2,\dots,X\} whose difference set contains no nonzero element of the form h ⁡ ( n ) h(n) with n ∈ ℤ n\in\mathbb{Z} . This is the best quantitative upper bound presently known for sets lacking intersective polynomial differences. In contrast to the square case, extending the method to general intersective polynomials requires performing a density increment iteration in which the underlying polynomial changes at each step; a key contribution of this paper is to show that the arithmetic level- d d inequality remains effective uniformly across all auxiliary polynomials arising in the iteration. We also develop smoothly weighted versions of the exponential sum estimates of Rice.

A natural generalization of the Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem is to replace squares by the image of a polynomial.
If

Theorem 1

Let

In
[
9
]
, Green and Sawhney remark that their method should extend beyond the square case to more general polynomials. We confirm this observation: the arithmetic level-
d
inequality yields a density increment scheme once sufficient Fourier mass is detected on appropriate major arcs, and this technique is not intrinsically tied to the monomial
n^{2}
. However, implementing this strategy for general intersective polynomials presents additional technical difficulties. In contrast to the square case, where the forbidden difference set is stable under dilation so that the relevant polynomial remains fixed throughout the iteration, the natural iteration for intersective polynomials necessarily introduces a family of auxiliary polynomials whose coefficients evolve from step to step. As a result, one must establish circle method estimates uniformly across all auxiliary polynomials that arise, while simultaneously controlling the growth of the auxiliary polynomial coefficients in order to ensure that these estimates remain valid at every stage of the iteration.

In an earlier draft of the project, we used classical polynomial exponential sum estimates to prove a weaker version of theorem

A sketch of the proof

We outline the proof of Theorem
1
, which we hope will serve as a road map for the reader. Let
h
be the intersective polynomial under consideration, and suppose that
A\subseteq[X]
avoids differences of the form
h(n)
. Our first goal is to show that this restriction forces
A
to exhibit a Fourier “bias”.

Write
\textbf{1}_{A}
for the characteristic function of
A
.
If
\textbf{1}_{A}
were equidistributed among residue classes modulo
q
, then for coprime
a,q
the exponential sum
\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}(a/q)
would display strong cancellation (heuristically, it resembles a near-complete sum of
q
th roots of unity with roughly equal weights), and hence should be small. By contrast, the circle method shows that the absence of differences
h(n)
forces
\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}
to have substantial
L^{2}
-mass on suitable major arcs. Pigeonholing then yields a large Fourier coefficient at a frequency close to some rational
a/q
with small denominator
q
. At an intuitive level, such a large coefficient indicates that
A
is not uniformly distributed across congruence classes modulo
q
, which leads to a density increment for
A
on a long subprogression of
[X]
with step size comparable to
q
.

More precisely, the circle method yields initial Fourier mass estimate of the form

\sum_{2\leq q\leq C\alpha^{-2-\epsilon}}q^{-1/(2+\epsilon)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\bigl|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}(a/q+\xi)\bigr|^{2}\gg\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

Here
\xi
is a small real number (see (
8.1
) for the precise constraint on
\xi
), and the derivation of this estimate is carried out in Sections
7
and
8
.

Up to this point, the argument is classical, and parallels Sárközy’s original density increment method for square differences
[
23
]
, as well as Lucier’s extension to intersective polynomials. There are, however, important technical refinements: combining techniques of Green-Sawnhney
[
9
]
and Rice
[
21
]
, we introduce a smooth weight on the image of the polynomial over a sifted set. In this way, we achieve near square root cancellation in the Gauss sum estimates, and we take the major arcs to be much narrower than in classical arguments. In the classical approach, one would now pigeonhole the initial Fourier mass to obtain a single large Fourier coefficient and then deduce a density increment of the type
(\ast)
in Proposition
5.1
. Iterating this increment leads to bounds of essentially Sárközy/Lucier strength. This is the point at which our approach diverges from the classical argument.

In Sections
10
and
11
, we apply the arithmetic level-
d
inequality of Green and Sawhney (Theorem
A
) twice to the initial Fourier mass estimate (
8.1
), with the goal of localizing the Fourier mass to much smaller denominators. Through a case analysis (similar in spirit to
[
9
]
, Version 1), we show that either the hypotheses of Theorem
A
are satisfied, or else we already have a quasipolynomial bound on the density of
A
. If the hypotheses of Theorem
A
are satisfied, then either we obtain a strong density increment (type
(2)
in Proposition
5.1
), or we obtain the strengthened Fourier mass estimate

\sum_{2\leq q\leq C^{\prime}\log(1/\alpha)^{(2+\epsilon)^{2}}}q^{-1/(2+\epsilon)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\bigl|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}(a/q+\xi)\bigr|^{2}\gg\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

In particular, the range for
q
is localized from
2\leq q\leq C\alpha^{-(2+\epsilon)}
to

2\leq q\leq C^{\prime}\log(1/\alpha)^{(2+\epsilon)^{2}}
, which is a substantial improvement. Consequently, a pigeonhole argument produces a strong density increment (type
(3)
in Proposition
5.1
). Overall, the case analysis yields one of three possible density increments, recorded as
(\ast),(2),(3)
in Proposition
5.1
.

Our density increment statement is Proposition
5.1
. In Section
5
we show inductively how Proposition
5.1
implies Theorem
1
. As noted in the introduction, after each density increment step one naturally reformulates the problem in terms of a new auxiliary polynomial (defined in Section
4
) governing the forbidden differences on the rescaled set. A potential obstruction is that the coefficients of these auxiliary polynomials may grow with the iteration, which could in principle render the circle method estimates ineffective. We show, via calculation and a careful treatment of the three increment scenarios in Proposition
5.1
, that this growth remains controlled throughout the iteration, and hence the circle method estimates continue to apply at every step of the iteration.

c_{0}\left(F(X)-F(X^{\prime})\right)\leq\log(1+c_{h}^{C_{h}-1})\leq\log R
.

Case
(2)
.

Let
j
be the parameter from option (2). Then
\log R\geq j\log 2
.
We assume that

\alpha>e^{-c_{h}F(X)}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad L<c_{h}F(X).

(5.4)

Option (2) gives
y\ll_{k}j\,L^{k+1}(\log L)^{2}
. Using (
5.3
) we obtain

c_{0}\left(F(X)-F(X^{\prime})\right)\ \ll_{k}\ c_{0}\,j\,L^{3+\epsilon}(\log L)^{2}\,(\log X)^{d_{\epsilon}-1}\bigl(\log(3+\log X)\bigr)^{-1/2}.

Now we have the bound
L^{3+\epsilon}\leq(cF(X))^{3+\epsilon}\ll_{h}F(X)^{3+\epsilon}
, and note that

F(X)^{3+\epsilon}=\left(\frac{(\log X)^{d_{\epsilon}}}{\left(\log(3+\log X)\right)^{1/2}}\right)^{3+\epsilon}=(\log X)^{d_{\epsilon}(3+\epsilon)}\bigl(\log(3+\log X)\bigr)^{-\frac{3+\epsilon}{2}}.

Substituting this yields

c_{0}\left(F(X)-F(X^{\prime})\right)\ \ll_{h}\ c_{0}\,j\,(\log L)^{2}\,(\log X)^{d_{\epsilon}(4+\epsilon)-1}\bigl(\log(3+\log X)\bigr)^{-\frac{4+\epsilon}{2}}.

We now check that the factor multiplying
j
is
\leq\log 2
for
X
large (after shrinking
c_{0}
if necessary).
First, with
d_{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)+2}
we have

d_{\epsilon}(4+\epsilon)-1=\frac{3+\epsilon}{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)+2}-1=\frac{-(2+\epsilon)^{2}+2(2+\epsilon)}{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)+2}\leq 0,

with equality only when
k=2
.
Also, we have
L<c_{h}F(X)
, hence
\log L\ll_{h}\log\log X
for
X
large, so

(\log L)^{2}\ \ll_{h}\ (\log\log X)^{2}.

Therefore the factor multiplying
j
is

\ll_{h}\ c_{0}\,(\log\log X)^{2}\,(\log X)^{d_{\epsilon}(4+\epsilon)-1}\bigl(\log(3+\log X)\bigr)^{-\frac{4+\epsilon}{2}}.

If
k\geq 3
then
d_{\epsilon}(k+2)-1<0
, so the
(\log X)
–power already tends to
0
, and hence the whole factor
tends to
0
as
X\to\infty
.
If
k=2
then
d_{2}(4)-1=0
, so the factor multiplying
j
is

\ll_{h}\ c_{0}\,\frac{(\log L)^{2}}{(\log(3+\log X))^{2}}\ \ll_{h}\ c_{0},

since
\log L\ll\log\log X
for
X
large. Choosing
c_{0}
sufficiently small (depending only on
c_{h}
) makes this factor

\leq\log 2
for all
X\geq X_{\min}
.
In either case, for
X\geq X_{\text{min}}
we may ensure this factor is at most
\log 2
, and thus

c_{0}\left(F(X)-F(X^{\prime})\right)\leq j\log 2\leq\log R,

proving (
5.2
) in case (2).

Case
(3)
.

We assume that

\alpha>e^{-c_{h}F(X)}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad L<c_{h}F(X).

(5.5)

Option (3) gives a length bound
X^{\prime}\geq X\,L^{-C_{h}}
, hence

y=\log\left(\frac{X}{X^{\prime}}\right)\leq\ C_{h}\log L

It also gives a density gain
R\geq 1+c_{h}\,L^{-(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}
. Since in option (3) we have
\alpha\leq c_{h}
,
it follows that
L=\log(1/\alpha)\geq\log(1/c_{h})
and therefore
cL^{-(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}\leq c<1
. Since
R\geq 1+c_{h}L^{-(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}
and
0<c_{h}L^{-(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}\leq c_{h}<1/10
, we have

\log R\ \geq\ \log\bigl(1+c_{h}L^{-(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}\bigr)\ \geq\ \frac{c_{h}}{2}L^{-(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}.

(5.6)

so in particular
\log R\gg L^{-(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}
.
Using (
5.3
) and
y\leq C_{h}\log L
, we have

\displaystyle c_{0}\left(F(X)-F(X^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\,y\,(\log X)^{d_{\epsilon}-1}\bigl(\log(3+\log X)\bigr)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\ll c_{0}\,(\log L)\,(\log X)^{d_{\epsilon}-1}\bigl(\log(3+\log X)\bigr)^{-1/2}.

(5.7)

Dividing (
5.7
) by (
5.6
) gives

\frac{c_{0}\left(F(X)-F(X^{\prime})\right)}{\log R}\ \ll\ c_{0}\,(\log L)\,(\log X)^{d_{\epsilon}-1}\bigl(\log(3+\log X)\bigr)^{-1/2}\,L^{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}.

(5.8)

Since
L<c_{h}F(X)
, we have
L^{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}\ll_{h}F(X)^{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}
, and also

\log L\ll_{h}\log\log X
for
X
large. Moreover,

F(X)^{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}=\left(\frac{(\log X)^{d_{\epsilon}}}{\left(\log(3+\log X)\right)^{1/2}}\right)^{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}=(\log X)^{d_{\epsilon}(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}\bigl(\log(3+\log X)\bigr)^{-\frac{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}{2}}.

(5.9)

Substituting these into (
5.8
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}\left(F(X)-F(X^{\prime})\right)}{\log R}

\displaystyle\ll_{h}c_{0}\,(\log\log X)\,(\log X)^{d_{\epsilon}-1+d_{\epsilon}(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}\bigl(\log(3+\log X)\bigr)^{-\frac{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)+1}{2}}.

(5.10)

Finally,

d_{\epsilon}-1+d_{\epsilon}(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)=d_{\epsilon}(1+(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon))-1=\frac{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)+1}{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)+2}-1=-\frac{1}{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)+2}\ <\ 0,

so the right-hand side of (
5.10
) tends to
0
as
X\to\infty
.
Thus, after shrinking
c_{0}
if necessary and increasing
X_{\text{min}}
, we have

c_{0}(F(X)-F(X^{\prime}))\leq\log R
for all
X\geq X_{\text{min}}
, proving (
5.2
) in case (3).

This proves (
5.2
) in all cases, hence
\alpha\leq e^{-c_{0}F(X)}
.
∎

(5.13)

) and (

Thus, our goal reduces to showing that both

are small. Notice that

measures how much smaller

is than

. To bound

, we bound the number of times each increment option can be applied before reaching a contradiction (here, a contradiction would be

), and to bound

Now we bound

. Write

•

Option
(
\ast
)
.

When (

and
\alpha_{n+1}\geq\alpha_{n}+\alpha_{n}^{C_{h}}\geq\alpha_{n}+c_{h}^{C_{h}}

Since

•

Options
(2)

Each such step multiplies density by at least

In particular

•

Option
(3)
.

When (3) occurs, Proposition

\alpha_{n+1}\ \geq\ \alpha_{n}\Bigl(1+c_{h}\,L_{n}^{-(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}\Bigr).

\displaystyle L_{n+1}=\log(1/\alpha_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq\log\Bigl(\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}(1+c_{h}L_{n}^{-(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)})}\Bigr)=L_{n}-\log\bigl(1+c_{h}L_{n}^{-(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}\bigr).

Also

Then from (

\displaystyle L_{n+1}^{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)+1}

(

Option
(

In this section we record the sieve definitions and

[

. Fix an intersective polynomial

with

to be the

the derivative

is
not
identically zero modulo

Let

modulo

W_{\ell}(U)\ :=\ \Bigl\{\,n\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\not\equiv 0\pmod{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\ \text{ for every prime }p\leq U\Bigr\}.

Further, for any
q\in\mathbb{N}
, define

W_{\ell}^{q}(U)\ :=\ \Bigl\{\,n\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\not\equiv 0\pmod{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\ \text{ for every prime }p\leq U\text{ with }p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\mid q\Bigr\}.

Now we record the following estimate, which gives the sum of the derivative
h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)
over the sieved set
W_{\ell}(U)
restricted to a congruence class
b\pmod{q}
. This comes from a standard Brun sieve calculation and partial summation. The exact statement of this estimate can be found in the proof of Lemma 4.1 in
[
21
]
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
q,b,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and let
h_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, and let
t,U,Y>0
with
1\leq t\leq Y
. Suppose further
b\not\in W_{\ell}^{q}(U)
and

c\log(Y/q)\geq\log U\log\log U,

for a constant
c>0
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq t\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\\
n\equiv b\bmod q\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)=\frac{h_{\ell}(t)}{q}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\nmid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)+O_{h}\!\Big(\frac{Y}{q}\,e^{-c\frac{\log(Y/q)}{\log U}}\Big).

(6.1)

Lemma 6.1
.

If
h\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
is intersective with
\deg(h)=k
, then for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and all
U\geq 2
,

\prod_{p\leq U}\Bigl(1-\frac{j_{d}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{d}(p)}}\Bigr)\ \gg_{h}\ \prod_{k\leq p\leq U}\Bigl(1-\frac{k-1}{p}\Bigr)\ \gg\ (\log U)^{1-k}.

Theorem B

(Rice)
.

Let
\ell
be a positive integer and consider the auxiliary polynomial
h_{\ell}=b_{0}+b_{1}n+\cdots b_{k}n^{k}
of degree
k\geq 2
.

(i)

(
Square root cancellation
)
If
(a,q)=1
, then

\Biggl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s=0\\
s\in W_{\ell}^{q}(U)\end{subarray}}^{q-1}e^{2\pi ih_{\ell}(s)a/q}\Biggr|\ \ll_{h,\epsilon}\ \begin{cases}q^{1/2+\epsilon},&\text{if }q\leq U,\\[4.0pt]
q^{1-1/k+\epsilon},&\text{if }q\geq U.\end{cases}

(ii)

(
Weyl-type inequality
)
If
N,U,Z\geq 2
,
UZ\leq N
,
(a,q)=1
and
|\alpha-a/q|<q^{-2}
. Write
K=2^{k}
. Then,

\Biggl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq N\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}e\bigl(\alpha\,h_{\ell}(n)\bigr)\Biggr|\ \ll_{h}\ N(\log U)^{ek}\Biggl(e^{-\frac{\log Z}{\log U}}+\left(b_{k}\log^{k^{2}}(b_{k}qN)\Bigl(q^{-1}+\frac{Z}{N}+\frac{qZ^{k}}{b_{k}N^{k}}\Bigr)\right)^{1/K}\Biggr).

Remark
.

Our version of Theorem
B
follows immediately from the estimates in
[
21
]
using
\text{cont}(h_{\ell})\ll_{h}1
and the standard divisor bound
\omega(q)\ll\frac{\log q}{\log\log q}
. We also used different letters for the variables so that our
U
is his
Y
and our
N
is his
X
.

7
Smoothly weighted exponential sum estimates

Recall that
h
(and consequently
h_{\ell}
) are positive, increasing, and concave up on
[1,\infty)
. Also, define

I(h_{\ell}):=\{h_{\ell}(n):n\in W_{\ell}(U)\},

where
U>0
is a parameter to be chosen late. We define a smooth weight on
I(h_{\ell})
in the style of
[
9
]
. We record the following, which can be obtained by applying simple horizontal transformations to the function found in Lemma
5.1
of
[
9
]
.

Lemma 7.1
.

There exists a smooth function
w:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
, supported on
[0,1]
, such that
w(x)\geq\frac{1}{2}
for
x\in[\frac{1}{4},\frac{3}{4}]
and satisfying the Fourier estimate
|\widehat{w}(t)|\ll e^{-\sqrt{|t|/2}}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
w
be given as by the lemma above. For the auxiliary polynomial
h_{\ell}
, define
g_{X,h_{\ell}}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
by

g_{X,h_{\ell}}(x):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }|x|\notin I(h_{\ell}),\\
J_{U,\ell}\cdot h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)w\left(\frac{h_{\ell}(n)}{X}\right)&\text{if }|x|=h_{\ell}(n)\in I(h_{\ell}),\end{cases}

where

J_{U,\ell}:=\prod_{p\leq U}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)^{-1}.

We know that
h_{\ell}(n)
is positive and increasing for
n\in\mathbb{N}
by (
4.4
), so
g_{X,h_{\ell}}
is well-defined. Notice also that
g
is even and supported on
\pm I(h_{\ell})
.

We fix a large ambient parameter
X
and make the global choices

U:=\exp(\sqrt{\log X}),\qquad Z:=\exp\big((\log X)^{7/8}\big).

(7.1)

We also choose
Y
so that
h_{\ell}(Y)=X.

In the application we will apply Theorem
B
(ii) for integers
N
satisfying

X^{1/k-\eta}\ \ll\ N\ \ll\ X^{1/k},

(7.2)

where
0<\eta<1/k
is constant and with
U,Z
defined as above.

We have
\log N\asymp\log X
throughout the range (
7.2
).
Consequently, any quasipolynomial factor of the form
\exp((\log X)^{d})
is also
N^{o(1)}
on this range.
In particular, with
U,Z
as in (
7.1
) we have

UZ\leq N\quad,\qquad Z^{k}=\exp\big(k(\log X)^{7/8}\big)=N^{o(1)},\qquad e^{-\frac{\log Z}{\log U}}=\exp\big(-(\log X)^{3/8}\big)=N^{-o(1)}.

(7.3)

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
X
be sufficiently large, fix
U,Z
as in (
7.1
), and let
N
satisfy (
7.2
).
Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
and suppose

\Bigl|N^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq N\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}e\bigl(h_{\ell}(n)\theta\bigr)\Bigr|\ \geq\ \delta.

(7.4)

Assume also that

\delta\ \geq\ 8(\log X)^{ek/2}\Bigl(e^{-(\log X)^{3/8}}+\Bigl(\log^{k^{2}}(b_{k}N^{k+1})\frac{b_{k}Z}{N}\Bigr)^{1/K}\Bigr).

(7.5)

Then there exists an integer
q\geq 1
such that

q\ \ll_{h}T^{K}\delta^{-K}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|q\theta\|_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\ \ll_{h}T^{K}N^{-k+o_{h}(1)}\,\delta^{-K},

where

T:=(\log X)^{ek/2}\left(\log^{k^{2}}(b_{k}N^{k+1})b_{k}\right)^{1/K}

Proof.

Write

S(\theta)\ :=\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq N\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}e\bigl(h_{\ell}(n)\theta\bigr),\qquad\text{so that}\qquad|S(\theta)|\ \geq\ \delta N

by (
7.4
).
Define

Q\ :=\ \frac{1}{8^{K}}\cdot\frac{\delta^{K}N^{k}}{T^{K}Z^{k}}.

(7.6)

By Dirichlet’s theorem there exist coprime integers
a,q
with
1\leq q\leq Q
such that

\Bigl|\theta-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr|\ \leq\ \frac{1}{qQ}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{q^{2}}.

By (
7.3
) we have
UZ\leq N
, so we may therefore apply Proposition
B
(ii).
Dividing by
N
and using
(x+y+z)^{1/K}\leq x^{1/K}+y^{1/K}+z^{1/K}
for
x,y,z\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta\leq\ \frac{|S(\theta)|}{N}\ll_{h}(\log X)^{ek/2}e^{-(\log X)^{3/8}}+Tq^{-1/K}+T\Bigl(\frac{Z}{N}\Bigr)^{1/K}+Tq^{1/K}\Bigl(\frac{Z^{k}}{b_{k}N^{k}}\Bigr)^{1/K},

(7.7)

where we also used the fact that
q\leq Q<N^{k}
. Using the fact that
b_{k}\geq 1
the right-most term is,

\leq q^{1/K}T\Bigl(\frac{Z^{k}}{N^{k}}\Bigr)^{1/K}\leq Q^{1/K}T\Bigl(\frac{Z^{k}}{N^{k}}\Bigr)^{1/K}=\frac{\delta}{8}

by definition (
7.6
).
Combining this with (
7.5
), we see that
\frac{3\delta}{4}\ \leq\ T\,q^{-1/K},
and hence
q\ \ll\ T^{K}\delta^{-K}.
Finally, from
\|q\theta\|_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{Q}
and
Z^{k}=N^{o(1)}
we have
\|q\theta\|_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\ \ll_{h}T^{K}N^{-k+o_{h}(1)}\,\delta^{-K}.

∎

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
X
be large.
Assume
\ell\leq X^{\rho}
. Let
0<\delta<1
satisfy

\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}\gg_{h}(\log X)^{ek/2}\exp\!\Big(-(\log X)^{3/8}\Big).

If
\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
and
|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\theta)|\geq\delta X
, then there exists an integer
q\geq 1
such that

q\ll_{h}\delta^{-K}X^{(k-1)\rho+o_{h}(1)},\qquad\|q\theta\|_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\ll_{h}\delta^{-K-k}\,X^{-1+2(k-1)\rho+o_{h}(1)}.

Proof.

Let
C
be a positive constant depending on
w
but independent of all other parameters.
Let
Y
be such that
h_{\ell}(Y)=X
. Since
w
is supported on
[0,1]
, the definition of
g_{X,h_{\ell}}
gives

\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\theta)=2J_{U,\ell}\,\Re\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\,w\!\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(n)}{X}\Big)\,e(-\theta h_{\ell}(n)).

From
|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\theta)|\geq\delta X
and
|\Re z|\leq|z|
, we obtain

\Bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\,w\!\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(n)}{X}\Big)\,e(-\theta h_{\ell}(n))\Bigg|\ \geq\ \frac{\delta X}{2J_{U,\ell}}.

Expanding
w
using the inverse Fourier transform and using the triangle inequality gives

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\widehat{w}(\xi)|\,\Bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\,e\!\Big(\Big(-\theta+\frac{\xi}{X}\Big)h_{\ell}(n)\Big)\Bigg|\,d\xi\ \gg_{h}\ \frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}X.

We have the trivial bound on the inner sum using Markov Brothers’ inequality (see e.g. equation (1.1) in
[
9
]
):

\Bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\,e\!\Big(\Big(-\theta+\frac{\xi}{X}\Big)h_{\ell}(n)\Big)\Bigg|\ \leq\ \sum_{1\leq n\leq Y}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\ \ll_{h}\ Y\max_{1\leq n\leq Y}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\ \ll_{h}\ Y\cdot\frac{X}{Y}\ \ll_{h}\ X.

(7.8)

Using this and the Fourier decay
|\widehat{w}(\xi)|\ll\exp(-\sqrt{|\xi|/2})
, the contribution of
|\xi|\geq C/\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}

to the outer integral is
o(\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}X)
provided
C
is chosen large enough.
Thus there exists some
\xi
with
|\xi|\leq C/\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}
such that

\Bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\,e\!\Big(\Big(-\theta+\frac{\xi}{X}\Big)h_{\ell}(n)\Big)\Bigg|\ \gg_{h}\ \frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}X.

(7.9)

Write
\beta:=-\theta+\xi/X
. Changing the order of summation and letting
t(u)
denote a function of
u
such that

h_{\ell}^{\prime}(t(u))=u
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)e(\beta h_{\ell}(n))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)}e(\beta h_{\ell}(n))\,du=\int_{0}^{h_{\ell}^{\prime}(Y)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t(u)\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}e(\beta h_{\ell}(n))\,du.

Combining this with (
7.9
) and substituting
u=yh_{\ell}^{\prime}(Y)
gives

\Bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t(yh_{\ell}^{\prime}(Y))\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}e(\beta h_{\ell}(n))\,dy\Bigg|\ \gg_{h}\ \frac{\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}X}{h_{\ell}^{\prime}(Y)}.

By Markov Brothers’ inequality again,
h_{\ell}^{\prime}(Y)\ll_{h}X/Y
, so we can rewrite this as

\Bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t(yh_{\ell}^{\prime}(Y))\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}e(\beta h_{\ell}(n))\,dy\Bigg|\ \gg_{h}\ \frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}Y.

Then for some
y\in[0,1]
we have

\Bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t(yh_{\ell}^{\prime}(Y))\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}e(\beta h_{\ell}(n))\Bigg|\ \gg_{h}\ \frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}Y.

(7.10)

Let
M:=\lceil t(yh_{\ell}^{\prime}(Y))\rceil
and write

S(N):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq N\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}e(\beta h_{\ell}(n)).

Then (
7.10
) is
|S(Y)-S(M-1)|\gg_{h}\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}Y
, so for some
N\leq Y
we have

|S(N)|\gg_{h}\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}Y
, so that

\Bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq N\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}e(\beta h_{\ell}(n))\Bigg|\ \gg_{h}\ \frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}.

(7.11)

By the triangle inequality, this also gives
N\gg_{h}\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}Y
.

Our goal now is to apply Lemma
7.2
to
\beta
with the correlation parameter comparable to
\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}
.
By Lemma
4.1
we have
Y=(X/b_{k})^{1/k}+O_{h}(1)
, and since
\ell\leq X^{\rho}
we have

b_{k}\ll_{h}\ell^{k-1}\leq X^{(k-1)\rho}
. In particular
N\ll Y\ll X^{1/k}
and
N\gg_{h}\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}Y
,
so
N
satisfies (
7.2
) (with
\delta
replaced by a constant multiple of
\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}
) for
X
large.
Moreover, the hypothesis

\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}\gg_{h}(\log X)^{ek/2}\exp(-(\log X)^{3/8})
ensures the condition (
7.5
)
in Lemma
7.2
(the remaining term in (
7.5
) is
o(\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}})
for
X
large, using

N\gg_{h}\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}Y
and
b_{k}\ll_{h}X^{(k-1)\rho}
).
Thus Lemma
7.2
yields an integer
q\geq 1
such that

q\ll_{h}T^{K}J_{U,\ell}^{K}\,\delta^{-K},\qquad\|q\beta\|_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\ll_{h}T^{K}J_{U,\ell}^{K}N^{-k+o_{h}(1)}\,\delta^{-K},

where
T
is as in Lemma
7.2
. Since
\log N\asymp\log X
and
b_{k}\ll_{h}X^{(k-1)\rho}
, we have

T^{K}=(\log N)^{ekK/2}\log^{k^{2}}(b_{k}N^{k+1})\,b_{k}\ll_{h}X^{(k-1)\rho+o_{h}(1)}
. By Lemma
6.1
, we have
J_{U,\ell}\ll(\log U)^{k-1}=X^{o(1)}
, and therefore

q\ll_{h}X^{(k-1)\rho+o_{h}(1)}\delta^{-K}

Also
N\gg_{h}\frac{\delta}{J_{U,\ell}}Y
implies

, and

by Lemma

,
so

\|q\beta\|_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\ll_{h}\delta^{-K}\,T^{K}\delta^{-k}\frac{b_{k}}{X}\,X^{o_{h}(1)}\ll_{h}\delta^{-K-k}\,\frac{b_{k}^{2}}{X}\,X^{o_{h}(1)}\ll_{h}\delta^{-K-k}\,X^{-1+2(k-1)\rho+o_{h}(1)}.

Finally, since
\beta=-\theta+\xi/X
and

, we have

\|q\theta\|_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\leq\|q\beta\|_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}+\frac{|q\xi|}{X}\ll_{h}\delta^{-K-k}\,X^{-1+2(k-1)\rho+o_{h}(1)},

which completes the proof.
∎

Next, we estimate
\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)
in the case where
\xi
is close to a rational number
a/q
with small denominator.

Lemma 7.4

(Major arc estimate)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
. Let
q
be a positive integer with
q<X^{1/8k}
and let
(a,q)=1
. Assume
c\log(Y/q)\geq\log U\log\log U
for some
c>0
. Assume
|\theta|\leq X^{-(1-1/8k)}
and

.
Then

\Bigl|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}\Bigl(\frac{a}{q}+\theta\Bigr)\Bigr|\ \ll_{h,\epsilon}\ X^{\frac{3}{2k}}+\,Xe^{-\sqrt{|\theta|X/2}}\cdot\begin{cases}q^{-1/(2+\epsilon)}&\text{ if }q\leq U\\
q^{-1/(k+\epsilon)}&\text{ if }q\geq U\end{cases}.

(7.12)

Proof.

Recall that
Y
is defined so that
h_{\ell}(Y)=X
. We will need, for each
b\in\{0,1,\dots,q-1\}
, an asymptotic for the following:

S_{b}(\theta):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq Y\\
n\equiv b\pmod{q}\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\,w\!\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(n)}{X}\Big)\,e\!\big(-\theta\,h_{\ell}(n)\big).

(7.13)

Notice
S_{b}(\theta)=0
whenever
b\notin W_{\ell}^{q}(U)
. So for now on we assume
b\in W_{\ell}^{q}(U)
.

Define

H_{b}(t):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq t\\
n\equiv b\pmod{q}\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)

By Lemma
6.1
, we have

H_{b}(t)=\frac{h_{\ell}(t)}{q}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\nmid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)\;+E_{0}(t)

(7.14)

where

\displaystyle E_{0}(t)=O_{h}\!\Big(\frac{Y}{q}\,e^{-c\frac{\log(Y/q)}{\log U}}\Big),

uniformly for
1\leq t\leq Y
. Now set
\xi:=\theta X,
and
F(u):=w(u)\,e(-\xi u).
Then
S_{b}(\theta)
becomes

S_{b}(\theta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq Y\\
n\equiv b\pmod{q}\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\,F\!\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(n)}{X}\Big).

Abel summation yields

S_{b}(\theta)=-\int_{1}^{Y}H_{b}(t)\,d\!\Big(F\!\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(t)}{X}\Big)\Big).

(7.15)

(The boundary terms vanish since the support of
w
is
[0,1]
.) Next compute the differential:

d\!\Big(F\!\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(t)}{X}\Big)\Big)=\frac{h_{\ell}^{\prime}(t)}{X}\,F^{\prime}\!\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(t)}{X}\Big)\,dt.

So
S_{b}(\theta)
becomes

S_{b}(\theta)=-\int_{1}^{Y}H_{b}(t)\,\frac{h_{\ell}^{\prime}(t)}{X}\,F^{\prime}\!\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(t)}{X}\Big)\,dt.

(7.16)

Insert (
7.14
) into (
7.16
):

\displaystyle S_{b}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{q}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\nmid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)\int_{1}^{Y}h_{\ell}(t)\,\frac{h_{\ell}^{\prime}(t)}{X}\,F^{\prime}\!\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(t)}{X}\Big)\,dt\;+E_{1}(\theta)

(7.17)

where

E_{1}(\theta):=-\int_{1}^{Y}E_{0}(t)\,\frac{h_{\ell}^{\prime}(t)}{X}\,F^{\prime}\!\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(t)}{X}\Big)\,dt.

Make the substitution

u=\frac{h_{\ell}(t)}{X},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad du=\frac{h_{\ell}^{\prime}(t)}{X}\,dt\quad\text{and}\quad h_{\ell}(t)=Xu.

Notice that since
h_{\ell}(Y)=X
, the

–range is
u\in[h_{\ell}(1)/X,\,1]
. Also note that
h_{\ell}(1)\ll_{h}\ell^{k-1}\leq X^{(k-1)\rho}

Thus the main integral becomes

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{q}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\nmid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)\int_{h_{\ell}(1)/X}^{1}(Xu)\,F^{\prime}(u)\,du

\displaystyle=-\frac{X}{q}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\nmid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)\int_{h_{\ell}(1)/X}^{1}u\,F^{\prime}(u)\,du.

Integrate by parts in
u
:

\int u\,F^{\prime}(u)\,du=uF(u)-\int F(u)\,du.

Hence

\displaystyle-\int_{h_{\ell}(1)/X}^{1}uF^{\prime}(u)\,du

\displaystyle=-\Big[uF(u)\Big]_{h_{\ell}(1)/X}^{1}+\int_{h_{\ell}(1)/X}^{1}F(u)\,du.

Now
F(1)=w(1)e(-\xi)=0
, and
|uF(u)|\leq|F(u)|\leq 1
, so the boundary contribution at
u=h_{\ell}(1)/X
is

\Big|\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(1)}{X}\Big)F\!\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(1)}{X}\Big)\Big|\ll_{h}\frac{X^{(k-1)\rho}}{X}.

Multiplying by
X/q
gives a total boundary contribution
O_{h}(X^{(k-1)\rho}/q)
.

Therefore the estimate for
S_{b}(\theta)
is

\displaystyle\frac{X}{q}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\nmid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)\int_{h_{\ell}(1)/X}^{1}F(u)\,du\;+\;O_{h}\!\Big(\frac{X^{(k-1)\rho}}{q}\Big)+E_{1}(\theta),

(7.18)

Where we also extended the lower limit from
h_{\ell}(1)/X
to
0
, introducing another error of the same shape
O_{h}\!\Big(\frac{X^{(k-1)\rho}}{q}\Big)
.

But

\int_{0}^{1}F(u)\,du=\int_{0}^{1}w(u)\,e(-\xi u)\,du=\widehat{w}(\xi)\quad\text{(since $w$ is supported on $[0,1]$)}.

Recalling
\xi=\theta X
, we obtain

S_{b}(\theta)=\frac{X}{q}\,\widehat{w}(\theta X)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\nmid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)+O_{h}\!\Big(\frac{X^{(k-1)\rho}}{q}\Big)+E_{1}(\theta)

Next we bound error from the sieve remainder,
E_{1}(\theta)
. First, note the trivial uniform bounds for
F
and
F^{\prime}
:
since
w
is fixed smooth compactly supported, we have
\|F\|_{\infty}\leq\|w\|_{\infty}\ll 1,
and

F^{\prime}(u)=w^{\prime}(u)e(-\xi u)+(-2\pi i\xi)\,w(u)e(-\xi u),

so

\|F^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\ll 1+|\xi|=1+|\theta|X,

(7.19)

where the implied constant depends on the choice of
w
. Also,

\int_{1}^{Y}\frac{h_{\ell}^{\prime}(t)}{X}\,dt=\frac{h_{\ell}(Y)-h_{\ell}(1)}{X}=1+O_{h}(X^{(k-1)\rho}/X)\ll_{h}1.

Then, by the definition of
E_{1}
and the bound on
E_{0}(t)
, we have

|E_{1}(\theta)|\ll_{h}\frac{Y}{q}\,e^{-c\frac{\log(Y/q)}{\log U}}\int_{1}^{Y}\frac{h_{\ell}^{\prime}(t)}{X}\,dt\;\Big\|F^{\prime}\Big\|_{\infty}\ll_{h}\frac{Y}{q}\,e^{-c\frac{\log(Y/q)}{\log U}}\,(1+|\theta|X).

Combining all of this, we have

S_{b}(\theta)=\frac{X}{q}\,\widehat{w}(\theta X)\,\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\nmid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)\,\mathbf{1}_{b\in W_{\ell}^{q}(U)}\;+\;O_{h}\!\Big(\frac{X^{(k-1)\rho}}{q}\Big)\;+\;O_{h}\!\Big(\frac{Y}{q}\,e^{-c\frac{\log(Y/q)}{\log U}}\,(1+|\theta|X)\Big).

(7.20)

Finally, writing out
\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}
and splitting into residue classes
b\pmod{q}
we have

\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}\!\left(\frac{a}{q}+\theta\right)=2J_{U,\ell}\cdot\Re\sum_{b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)}e\!\left(-\frac{a\,h_{\ell}(b)}{q}\right)\,S_{b}(\theta).

(7.21)

Inserting our estimate for
S_{b}(\theta)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}\!\left(\frac{a}{q}+\theta\right)

\displaystyle=J_{U,\ell}\cdot\frac{2X}{q}\,\widehat{w}(\theta X)\,\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\nmid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)\\
b\in W_{\ell}^{q}(U)\end{subarray}}e\!\left(-\frac{a\,h_{\ell}(b)}{q}\right)+O_{h}\!\big(X^{\frac{3}{2k}}\big),

(7.22)

using
q\leq X^{1/8k}
,
J_{U,\ell}\ll\log(X)^{c}
,
\rho=2^{-10k},\theta<X^{-(1-1/(8k))}
, and
Y\ll X^{1/k}
.
Thus, the bound on
\widehat{w}
and Theorem
B
(i) yield the lemma. Here, we also need

\displaystyle J_{U,\ell}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\nmid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\mid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{j_{\ell}(p)}{p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}}\Big)^{-1}\ll_{h}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leq p\leq U\\
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}\mid q\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{k-1}{p}\Big)^{-1}\ll 2^{\omega(q)}\ll q^{o(1)}

where we used the standard bound
\omega(q)=O(\log q/\log\log q)
and the fact that a degree
k-1
polynomial which does not identically vanish modulo
p
has at most
k-1
roots modulo
p
which lift to at most
(k-1)p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)-1}
roots modulo
p^{\gamma_{\ell}(p)}
.

∎

8
The classical Fourier mass

Suppose
A\subset[X]
and
(A-A)\cap I(h_{\ell})=\emptyset
. Also, set
\alpha=|A|/X
. Suppose
\ell\leq X^{\rho}
and let
\alpha
satisfy

\alpha\gg_{h}(\log X)^{ek}\exp\!\Big(-(\log X)^{3/8}\Big).

Our goal in this section is to show that either
A
enjoys one of the density increments in Proposition

5.1
or
\widehat{1_{A}}
has
L^{2}
Fourier mass on suitably chosen major arcs, following the classical ideas of Sárközy. To this end, we must define the major and minor arcs. Recall that
L=\log(1/\alpha)
, and define

\tau=C_{1}L^{2}X^{-1},

where
C_{1}
is a positive constant to be determined later. Now, define the
major arcs

\mathfrak{M}\subset\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
by

\mathfrak{M}:=\bigcup_{1\leq q\leq C_{1}\alpha^{-2-\epsilon}}\ \bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\Big\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}:\ \Big\|\xi-\frac{a}{q}\Big\|_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\leq\tau\Big\}.

The
minor arcs
are given by
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{M}^{C}
.
The goal of this section is to prove the following

Lemma 8.1
.

Suppose
A\subset[X]
and
(A-A)\cap I(h_{\ell})=\emptyset
. Also, set
\alpha=|A|/X
. Suppose
\ell\leq X^{\rho}
and let
\alpha
satisfy

\alpha\gg_{h}(\log X)^{ek}\exp\!\Big(-(\log X)^{3/8}\Big).

Either there exists
\xi\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
such that
|\xi|<\tau
and

\sum_{2\leq q\leq C_{1}\alpha^{-2-\epsilon}}q^{-1/(2+\epsilon)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}(a/q+\xi)|^{2}\gg\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

(8.1)

or
A
satisfies one of the density increment option
(2)
or
(3)
in Proposition
5.1
.

Before proving Lemma
8.1
, we need to prove two estimates. First, we have the following minor arc estimate, which in words says that
\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}
is uniformly small on
\mathfrak{m}
.

Lemma 8.2

(Minor arc estimate)
.

Suppose
\ell\leq X^{\rho}
and let
0<\alpha<1
satisfy

\alpha\gg_{h}(\log X)^{ek}\exp\!\Big(-(\log X)^{3/8}\Big).

Then, we have
\sup_{\xi\in\mathfrak{M}^{c}}|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)|\leq 2^{-9}\alpha X
.

Proof.

Suppose
|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)|>2^{-9}\alpha X.
We show
\xi\in\mathfrak{M}
. By Lemma
7.3
, there exists a positive integer
q
such that

q\ll_{h}\alpha^{-K}X^{(k-1)\rho+o_{h}(1)},\qquad\|q\xi\|_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\ll_{h}\alpha^{-K-k}\,X^{-1+2(k-1)\rho+o_{h}(1)}.

By the second equation, there exists an integer
a
such that we can write
\xi=\theta+a/q
with

|\theta|\ll_{h}X^{-1+o_{h}(1)+2(k-1)\rho}\alpha^{-2^{k}-k}.

Using our lower bound assumption for
\alpha
we conclude

q<X^{1/8k}
and
|\theta|\leq X^{-(1-1/8k)}
, which are the conditions required to apply Lemma
7.4
. Thus, we have

\Bigl|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}\Bigl(\frac{a}{q}+\theta\Bigr)\Bigr|\ \ll_{h,\epsilon}\ X^{\frac{3}{2k}}+\,Xe^{-\sqrt{|\theta|X/2}}\cdot\begin{cases}q^{-1/(2+\epsilon)}&\text{ if }q\leq U\\
q^{-1/(k+\epsilon)}&\text{ if }q\geq U\end{cases}

Then, we have

\alpha X\ll_{h}\Bigl|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}\Bigl(\frac{a}{q}+\theta\Bigr)\Bigr|\ \ll_{h,\epsilon}\ X^{\frac{3}{2k}}+\,Xe^{-\sqrt{|\theta|X/2}}\cdot\begin{cases}q^{-1/(2+\epsilon)}&\text{ if }q\leq U\\
q^{-1/(k+\epsilon)}&\text{ if }q\geq U\end{cases}

Using the fact that
\alpha>X^{-\rho}
, we can ignore the term
X^{\frac{3}{2k}}
and obtain

\alpha\ll_{h}e^{-\sqrt{|\theta|X/2}}\cdot\begin{cases}q^{-1/(2+\epsilon)}&\text{ if }q\leq U\\
q^{-1/(k+\epsilon)}&\text{ if }q\geq U\end{cases}

Either way, we have at least
\alpha\ll_{h}e^{-\sqrt{|\theta|X/2}}q^{-1/(k+\epsilon)}.
By rearranging, we have both
|\theta|\ll_{h}L^{2}X^{-1}
and
q\ll_{h}\alpha^{-k-\epsilon}
. We choose
C_{1}
(in the definition of
\mathfrak{M}
) to be the implicit constant in the inequality
q\ll_{h}\alpha^{-k-\epsilon}
. By assumption, we also have

\alpha\gg_{h}(\log X)^{ek}\exp\!\Big(-(\log X)^{3/8}\Big).

which implies
q\leq U
for sufficiently large
X
. Therefore,

\alpha\ll_{h}e^{-\sqrt{|\theta|X/2}}q^{-1/(2+\epsilon)},

and rearranging yields
q\leq C_{1}\alpha^{-2-\epsilon}
. Thus,
\xi\in\mathfrak{M}
, as needed.
∎

Next, we prove the following important physical space estimate.

Lemma 8.3
.

Either

\sum_{x,y}\textbf{1}_{A}(x)\textbf{1}_{[X]}(y)\,g_{X,h_{\ell}}(x-y)\geq 2^{-8}\alpha X^{2}.

(8.2)

or
A
enjoys a density increment superior to any of the density increment options in Proposition
5.1
.

Proof.

Suppose that

\sum_{x,y}\textbf{1}_{A}(x)\textbf{1}_{[X]}(y)\,g_{X,h_{\ell}}(x-y)\leq 2^{-8}\alpha X^{2}.

(8.3)

We will show
A
has a strong density increment.

Let
Y>0
be defined by
h_{\ell}(Y)=X
. Write
h_{\ell}(t)=b_{k}t^{k}+b_{k-1}t^{k-1}+\cdots+b_{0}.
(Here, of course the coefficients
b_{k},...,b_{0}
depend on
\ell
, but we suppress this dependence in the notation.)

Since
g_{X,h_{\ell}}\geq 0
, from (
8.3
) we may discard the contributions coming from

y=x-h_{\ell}(n)
and keep only those coming from
y=x+h_{\ell}(n)
, obtaining

\displaystyle 2^{-8}\alpha X^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{x,y}\textbf{1}_{A}(x)\textbf{1}_{[X]}(y)\,g_{X,h_{\ell}}(x-y)

\displaystyle\geq J_{U,\ell}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{n\in W_{\ell}(U)}\textbf{1}_{A}(x)\textbf{1}_{[X]}(x+h_{\ell}(n))\,h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\,w\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(n)}{X}\Big).

(8.4)

Restricting to
1\leq n\leq Y
(so that
h_{\ell}(n)\leq h_{\ell}(Y)=X
) and swapping the order of summation gives

J_{U,\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq Y\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\,w\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(n)}{X}\Big)\,|A\cap[X-h_{\ell}(n)]|\leq 2^{-8}\alpha X^{2}.

(8.5)

Using that
h_{\ell}
is increasing on
\mathbb{N}
, we define
Y_{1},Y_{2}
by
h_{\ell}(Y_{1})=\frac{X}{4}
and
h_{\ell}(Y_{2})=\frac{3X}{4}
, and then
1/4\leq h_{\ell}(n)/X<3/4
for
Y_{1}\leq n\leq Y_{2}
. Thus, the support properties of
w
give
w\Big(\frac{h_{\ell}(n)}{X}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{2}
for
Y_{1}\leq n\leq Y_{2}
. Therefore (
8.5
) implies

J_{U,\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y_{1}\leq n\leq Y_{2}\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\,|A\cap[X-h_{\ell}(n)]|\leq 2^{-7}\alpha X^{2}.

(8.6)

By Lemma
6.1

J_{U,\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y_{1}\leq n\leq Y_{2}\\
n\in W_{\ell}(U)\end{subarray}}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)=3X/4-X/4+o(X)=X/2+o(X)

(8.7)

Combining (
8.6
) and (
8.7
), the pigeonhole principle gives
n\in[Y_{1},Y_{2}]\cap W_{\ell}(U)
such that

|A\cap[X-h_{\ell}(n)]|\leq\frac{2^{-7}\alpha X^{2}}{X/2}=\alpha X/64

(8.8)

We also have
h_{\ell}(n)\leq 3X/4
, and therefore
\alpha X\leq 4\alpha(X-h_{\ell}(n)).

Thus (
8.8
) implies

|A\cap[X-h_{\ell}(n)]|\leq\alpha X/64\leq\frac{1}{16}\,\alpha(X-h_{\ell}(n)).

Since
|A|=\alpha X
, using the above facts and the inequality
h_{\ell}(n)\leq 3X/4
we deduce a density increment on the final interval
(X-h_{\ell}(n),X]
:

|A\cap(X-h_{\ell}(n),X]|\geq\frac{21}{16}\alpha h_{\ell}(n)

Since also
h_{\ell}(n)\geq X/4
, this is a very strong density increment on an interval of
length
\gg X
, and better than any of the increments in Proposition
5.1
, so we would be done in this case.
∎

Finally, we may prove Lemma
8.1
.

Proof of Lemma
8.1
.

By Lemma
8.3
, we may assume (
8.2
). In addition, using the fact that
(A-A)\cap I(h_{\ell})=\emptyset
and the definition of
g_{X,h_{\ell}}
, we have

\sum_{x,y}\textbf{1}_{A}(x)\textbf{1}_{A}(y)\,g_{X,h_{\ell}}(x-y)=0.

(8.9)

Combining (
8.9
) and (
8.2
), we have

\bigg|\sum_{x,y}\textbf{1}_{A}(x)f_{A}(y)g_{x,h_{\ell}}(x-y)\bigg|\geq 2^{-8}\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

Finally, we can convert this inequality into information in Fourier-space by applying the orthogonality relation. Indeed, this inequality becomes

\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}(\xi)\widehat{f_{A}}(-\xi)\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)d\xi\bigg|\geq 2^{-8}\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

(8.10)

By Cauchy–Schwarz, Parseval, and Lemma
8.2
(the minor arc estimate), we have

\Bigl|\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\,\widehat{f_{A}}(-\xi)\,\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\,d\xi\Bigr|\leq\sup_{\xi\in\mathfrak{m}}|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)|\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\widehat{f_{A}}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\Bigr)^{1/2}\leq 2^{-9}\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

Combining this with (
8.10
) yields

\Bigl|\int_{\mathfrak{M}}\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\,\widehat{f_{A}}(-\xi)\,\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\,d\xi\Bigr|\geq 2^{-9}\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

(8.11)

Write

\mathfrak{M}=[-\tau,\tau]\ \cup\ \mathfrak{M}^{\prime},\qquad\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq q\leq C_{1}\alpha^{-2-\epsilon}\end{subarray}}\ \bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\Bigl[\frac{a}{q}-\tau,\ \frac{a}{q}+\tau\Bigr],

where
\tau
is as in the definition of the major arcs. From (
8.11
) we have either

\Bigl|\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\,\widehat{f_{A}}(-\xi)\,\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\,d\xi\Bigr|\geq 2^{-10}\alpha^{2}X^{2},

(8.12)

or

\Bigl|\int_{\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\,\widehat{f_{A}}(-\xi)\,\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\,d\xi\Bigr|\geq 2^{-10}\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

(8.13)

We handle these two cases differently.

Case 1: (
8.12
) holds.

From (
8.12
) and the triangle inequality,

2^{-10}\alpha^{2}X^{2}\leq\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\bigl|\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\bigr|\,\bigl|\widehat{f_{A}}(-\xi)\bigr|\,\bigl|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\bigr|\,d\xi.

Using the trivial bound
\bigl|\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\bigr|\leq|A|=\alpha X
, we obtain

2^{-10}\alpha^{2}X^{2}\leq\alpha X\Bigl(\sup_{|\xi|\leq\tau}\bigl|\widehat{f_{A}}(\xi)\bigr|\Bigr)\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\bigl|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\bigr|\,d\xi,

and hence

\Bigl(\sup_{|\xi|\leq\tau}\bigl|\widehat{f_{A}}(\xi)\bigr|\Bigr)\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\bigl|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\bigr|\,d\xi\ \geq\ 2^{-10}\alpha X.

(8.14)

We now bound the integral. By Lemma
7.4
with
(a,q)=(0,1)
and
\theta=\xi
.
(For
X
large the conditions to apply Lemma
7.4
are clearly satisfied due to the definition of
\tau
and the assumed lower bound on
\alpha
.)
Thus for all
|\xi|\leq\tau
,

\bigl|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\bigr|\ll_{h}Xe^{-|\xi X|^{1/2}}+X^{\frac{3}{2k}}.

Integrating and using the definition of
\tau
gives

\displaystyle\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\bigl|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\bigr|\,d\xi

\displaystyle\ll_{h}1.

Returning to (
8.14
), we conclude that

\sup_{|\xi|\leq\tau}\bigl|\widehat{f_{A}}(\xi)\bigr|\gg_{h}\alpha X.

Applying Lemma
5.3
to yields a density increment
(as good as (
\ast
) or (3) in Proposition
5.1
).

Case 2: (
8.13
) holds.

To prepare for the next section, it is convenient to replace the balanced function
f_{A}
by
1_{A}
on
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}
.
Since
f_{A}=1_{A}-\alpha 1_{[X]}
, we have

\sup_{\xi\in\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}\bigl|\widehat{f_{A}}(\xi)-\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\bigr|=\alpha\sup_{\xi\in\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}\bigl|\widehat{1_{[X]}}(\xi)\bigr|.

(8.15)

Since
\xi\in\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}
, by definition there exist coprime integers
(a,q)=1
with

2\leq q\leq C_{1}\alpha^{-2-\epsilon}
and some
\eta\in[-\tau,\tau]
such that

\xi=\frac{a}{q}+\eta,\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad\Bigl\|\xi-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\tau.

Moreover, for
X
sufficiently large we have
q\tau\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, and hence

\|\xi\|_{\mathbb{T}}\geq\Bigl\|\frac{a}{q}\Bigr\|_{\mathbb{T}}-\|\eta\|_{\mathbb{T}}\geq\frac{1}{q}-\tau\geq\frac{1}{2q}\gg\alpha^{2+\epsilon}.

Evaluating
\widehat{1_{[X]}}(\xi)=\sum_{1\leq n\leq X}e(\xi n)
by summing a geometric series yields

|\widehat{1_{[X]}}(\xi)|\ll\|\xi\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{-1}
, and hence

\sup_{\xi\in\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}\bigl|\widehat{f_{A}}(\xi)-\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\bigr|\ll\alpha^{1-(2+\epsilon)}\leq X^{(2+\epsilon)\rho},

using
\alpha^{-1}\leq X^{\rho}
.

By the triangle inequality and extending the
\xi
-integral to
\mathbb{T}
,

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\widehat{f_{A}}(-\xi)\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\,d\xi-\int_{\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\widehat{1_{A}}(-\xi)\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\,d\xi\Bigr|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sup_{\xi\in\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}\bigl|\widehat{f_{A}}(\xi)-\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\bigr|\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)|\,|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)|\,d\xi.

By Cauchy–Schwarz and Parseval,

\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)|\,|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)|\,d\xi\leq\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\Bigr)^{1/2}=|A|^{1/2}\,\|g_{X,h_{\ell}}\|_{2}.

Moreover,

\|g_{X,h_{\ell}}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{n}|g_{X,h_{\ell}}(n)|^{2}\leq\Bigl(\sup_{n}|g_{X,h_{\ell}}(n)|\Bigr)\sum_{n}|g_{X,h_{\ell}}(n)|.

Since

\sum_{n}|g_{X,h_{\ell}}(n)|=\sum_{1\leq n\leq Y}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(n)\,w(h_{\ell}(n)/X)\ll\int_{0}^{Y}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(t)\,dt=h_{\ell}(Y)=X,

and by Lemma
4.1
one has

\sup_{1\leq n\leq Y}h_{\ell}^{\prime}(m)\ll_{h}b_{k}Y^{k-1}\ll_{h}X^{1-\frac{1}{k}}\ell^{\frac{k-1}{k}}\leq X^{1-\frac{1}{k}+\rho}.

It follows that

\|g_{X,h_{\ell}}\|_{2}\ll X^{1-\frac{1}{2k}+\rho/2}.

Consequently the replacement error is

\ll X^{k\rho}\,(\alpha X)^{1/2}\,X^{1-\frac{1}{2k}+\rho}=o(\alpha^{2}X^{2})

for
X
sufficiently large (since
\alpha^{-1}\leq X^{\rho}
and
\rho=2^{-10k}
is small).
Thus, from (
8.13
) we may replace
f_{A}
by
1_{A}
on
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}
at negligible cost, obtaining

\Bigl|\int_{\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)\widehat{1_{A}}(-\xi)\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)\,d\xi\Bigr|\geq 2^{-11}\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

Since
\widehat{1_{A}}(-\xi)=\overline{\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)}
, this implies

\int_{\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}}|\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)|^{2}\,|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)|\,d\xi\geq 2^{-11}\alpha^{2}X^{2}.

(8.16)

We now estimate
|\widehat{g_{X,h_{\ell}}}(\xi)|
on each major arc interval using Lemma
7.4
.
Splitting
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}
into the constituent intervals
[\frac{a}{q}-\tau,\frac{a}{q}+\tau]
and writing
\xi=\frac{a}{q}+\eta
, we obtain

\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq q\leq C_{1}\alpha^{-2-\epsilon}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\Bigl|\widehat{1_{A}}\Bigl(\frac{a}{q}+\eta\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}\Bigl(q^{-1/(2+\epsilon)}Xe^{-|\eta X|^{1/2}}+X^{1-\frac{1}{2k}}\Bigr)\,d\eta\ \gg\ \alpha^{2}X^{2}.

The contribution of the
X^{1-\frac{1}{2k}}
term is

\ll X^{1-\frac{1}{2k}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\widehat{1_{A}}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi=X^{1-\frac{1}{2k}}|A|=\alpha X^{2-\frac{1}{2k}}=o(\alpha^{2}X^{2}),

again using
\alpha^{-1}\leq X^{\rho}
with
\rho<1/(2k)
.
Finally, since
\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}Xe^{-|\eta X|^{1/2}}\,d\eta\ll 1
, it follows that there exists some
\xi
with
|\xi|\leq\tau
such that (
8.1
) holds, and we are done.
∎

9
The classical density increment

Throughout the rest of the paper, suppose
A\subset[X]
and
(A-A)\cap I(h_{\ell})=\emptyset
. Set
\alpha=|A|/X
, and assume
\ell<X^{\rho}
. Let
C_{h}>0
be a large constant to be determined later. Also, assume
0\leq\alpha\leq 2/3
and
X>C_{h}
. The goal for this section is to establish the
(\ast)
increment in Proposition

5.1
.

We first make a harmless reduction. If
X>C_{h}
is sufficiently large and

\alpha\ll_{h}(\log X)^{ek}\exp\!\Big(-(\log X)^{3/8}\Big)

then we clearly have

\alpha\leq\exp(-c_{h}F(X)),

so conclusion
5.1
(1) holds. Therefore, for the remainder of the argument we may
assume

\alpha\gg_{h}(\log X)^{ek}\exp\!\Big(-(\log X)^{3/8}\Big),

(9.1)

so that the condition for Lemma
8.1
is satisfied.

Now suppose that the density–increment conclusion in Lemma
8.1
does not occur.
Then there exists
\xi\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
with

C_{3}^{-\frac{d-1}{2+\epsilon}}\,L^{-\frac{(2+\epsilon+1)(d-1)}{2+\epsilon}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{2}\\
|S|=d\end{subarray}}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ R_{S})\\
m\nmid b\ \text{for every }m\in S\end{subarray}}\left|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}\left(\frac{b}{R_{S}}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}.

Applying (
11.2
) gives

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq q\leq C_{2}L^{(2+\epsilon)\lfloor\log L\rfloor}\\
l_{2}(q)=d\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}\ \leq\ C_{3}^{-\frac{d-1}{2+\epsilon}}\,\alpha^{2}X^{2}\,L^{\frac{2+\epsilon-d+1}{2+\epsilon}}\left(\frac{2^{12}}{d}\right)^{d}.

(11.4)

Now sum (
11.4
) over
2+\epsilon+1\leq d\leq\lfloor L/2^{7}\rfloor
. Since
d\geq 2+\epsilon+1
implies

C_{3}^{-\frac{d-1}{2+\epsilon}}\leq C_{3}^{-1}
and
L^{\frac{2+\epsilon-d+1}{2+\epsilon}}\leq 1
, we obtain

\sum_{d=2+\epsilon+1}^{\lfloor L/2^{7}\rfloor}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq q\leq C_{2}L^{(2+\epsilon)\lfloor\log L\rfloor}\\
l_{2}(q)=d\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{\alpha^{2}X^{2}}{C_{3}}\sum_{d=2+\epsilon+1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2^{12}}{d}\right)^{d}.

Choosing
C_{3}
sufficiently large makes the right-hand side at most

\tfrac{1}{2}\,\alpha^{2}X^{2}
. Combining this with (
11.1
) yields

\alpha^{2}X^{2}\ \ll\ \sum_{d=1}^{2+\epsilon}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq q\leq C_{2}L^{(2+\epsilon)\lfloor\log L\rfloor}\\
l_{2}(q)=d\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}.

(11.5)

Fix
C_{4}>0
. If
q>C_{4}L^{(2+\epsilon)^{2}}
then
q^{-\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}\leq C_{4}^{-\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}L^{-(2+\epsilon)}
. Repeating the same two-step argument
used to prove (
11.4
) (namely: bound
q^{-\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}
by its maximum on the range, then use
Lemma
12.5
and (
11.2
)) we obtain, for each
1\leq d\leq 2+\epsilon
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{4}L^{(2+\epsilon)^{2}}<q\leq C_{2}L^{(2+\epsilon)\lfloor\log L\rfloor}\\
l_{2}(q)=d\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{\alpha^{2}X^{2}}{C_{4}^{\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}}\,L^{d-(2+\epsilon)}\left(\frac{2^{12}}{d}\right)^{d}.

Since
d\leq 2+\epsilon
we have
L^{d-(2+\epsilon)}\leq 1
, and summing over
1\leq d\leq 2+\epsilon
gives

\sum_{d=1}^{2+\epsilon}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{4}L^{(2+\epsilon)^{2}}<q\leq C_{2}L^{(2+\epsilon)\lfloor\log L\rfloor}\\
l_{2}(q)=d\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}\ \ll\ \frac{\alpha^{2}X^{2}}{C_{4}^{\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}}\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2^{12}}{d}\right)^{d}.

Taking
C_{4}
sufficiently large makes this at most
\tfrac{1}{2}\,\alpha^{2}X^{2}
.
Combining with (
11.5
) yields

\alpha^{2}X^{2}\ \ll\ \sum_{2\leq q\leq C_{4}L^{(2+\epsilon)^{2}}}\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}.

(11.6)

From (
11.6
) and the bound

\sum_{2\leq q\leq C_{4}L^{(2+\epsilon)^{2}}}\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}}\ll(C_{4}L^{(2+\epsilon)^{2}})^{1-\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}}\ \ll L^{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)},

there exists an integer
2\leq q\leq C_{4}L^{(2+\epsilon)^{2}}
such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}\ \gg\ \frac{\alpha^{2}X^{2}}{L^{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}}.

(11.7)

We now pass from
\textbf{1}_{A}
to the balanced function
f_{A}
:

\widehat{f_{A}}(\theta)=\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}(\theta)-\alpha\,\widehat{\textbf{1}_{[X]}}(\theta).

For
(a,q)=1
we have
\|a/q\|\geq 1/q
, and since
\|\xi\|\leq C_{1}L^{2}/X
and
q\ll L^{k^{2}}
,
for
X
sufficiently large we have
\|a/q+\xi\|\geq 1/(2q)
. Hence the geometric sum bound gives

|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{[X]}}(a/q+\xi)|\ll q
. Using
|u-v|^{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}|u|^{2}-|v|^{2}
, we obtain

\left|\widehat{f_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2}\left|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}\ -\ \alpha^{2}\left|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{[X]}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}.

Summing over
(a,q)=1
gives

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\widehat{f_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\widehat{\textbf{1}_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}\ -\ O(\alpha^{2}q^{3}).

Since
q\ll L^{(2+\epsilon)^{2}}
and
X
is sufficiently large, the error term
O(\alpha^{2}q^{3})
is negligible compared
to the lower bound in (
11.7
). Therefore

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left|\widehat{f_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}\ \gg\ \frac{\alpha^{2}X^{2}}{L^{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}}.

In particular (since the sum over
a\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)
dominates the sum over
(a,q)=1
),

\sum_{a\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)}\left|\widehat{f_{A}}\left(\frac{a}{q}+\xi\right)\right|^{2}\ \gg\ \frac{\alpha^{2}X^{2}}{L^{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}}.

Also,
q\ll L^{(2+\epsilon)^{2}}
implies
3q\leq X
for
X
sufficiently large. We may therefore apply
Lemma
5.3
(ii) with
\eta\asymp L^{-(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}
and denominator
q
. Writing

T:=\max(1,\|\xi\|X)\ll L^{2}
and using
\lambda(q)\leq q^{k}\ll L^{k^{3}}
, we obtain a progression

P\subseteq[X]
with common difference
\lambda(q)
such that

\frac{|A\cap P|}{|P|}\ \geq\ \left(1+\frac{c_{h}}{L^{(2+\epsilon)(1+\epsilon)}}\right)\alpha

and
|P|\geq\ \frac{X}{L^{C_{h}}}.

(Here, we also choose
c_{h}
sufficiently small and
C_{h}
sufficiently large). This is Proposition
5.1
(3).

This completes the second application of Theorem
A
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{1},\mathcal{Q}_{2},l_{1},l_{2}
be defined as in Sections
10
and
11
.
For
i\in\{1,2\}
and
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
write
R_{S}:=\prod_{m\in S}m.

Also set

H_{1}:=C_{1}\alpha^{-(2+\epsilon)},\qquad Y_{1}:=L^{2+\epsilon},\qquad\qquad H_{2}:=C_{2}L^{(2+\epsilon)\lfloor\log L\rfloor},\qquad Y_{2}:=C_{3}L^{3+\epsilon}.

Lemma 12.1
.

Let
i\in\{1,2\}
. If
l_{i}(r)<\infty
, then
l_{i}(r)\leq\omega(r)
, where
\omega(r)
is the number of distinct
prime divisors of
r
.

Proof.

Let
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
be a set of minimal cardinality such that
r\mid\prod_{q\in S}q
, so
|S|=l_{i}(r)
.
If some
q\in S
satisfied
(q,r)=1
, then removing
q
would still leave a product divisible by
r
, contradicting
minimality. Hence
(q,r)>1
for every
q\in S
.

For each
q\in S
choose a prime
p_{q}\mid(q,r)
. If
q\neq q^{\prime}
, then
\gcd(q,q^{\prime})=1
(pairwise coprime), so

p_{q}\neq p_{q^{\prime}}
. Thus
q\mapsto p_{q}
is an injection from
S
into the set of prime divisors of
r
, and hence

|S|\leq\omega(r)
. Since
|S|=l_{i}(r)
, this proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 12.2
.

Let
i\in\{1,2\}
. If
1\leq q\leq H_{i}
, then
l_{i}(q)<\infty
.

Proof.

Write the prime factorization
q=\prod_{p}p^{\nu_{p}}
and split the primes into
p\leq Y_{i}
and
p>Y_{i}
.

By construction of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, the distinguished element
q_{i,1}\in\mathcal{Q}_{i}
contains a large power of
every prime
p\leq Y_{i}
. For such a prime,

2^{\nu_{p}}\leq p^{\nu_{p}}\leq q\leq H_{i},

so
\nu_{p}\leq\log_{2}(H_{i})
. In the first application one has
\log_{2}(H_{1})\leq\lceil 2(2+\epsilon)L\rceil
for
\alpha

sufficiently small, and in the second application one has
\log_{2}(H_{2})\leq\lceil 2(2+\epsilon)(\log L)^{2}\rceil
for
L

sufficiently large; in either case this ensures
\prod_{p\leq Y_{i}}p^{\nu_{p}}\mid q_{i,1}
. (Here we can guarantee
\alpha
is sufficiently small and
L
sufficiently large by simply choosing
c_{h}
to be sufficiently small.)

Now let
p>Y_{i}
with
p^{\nu_{p}}\mid q
. Since
q\leq H_{i}
and
H_{i}\leq P_{i,\max}
by definition of
P_{i,\max}
,
we have
p^{\nu_{p}}\leq P_{i,\max}
. By construction of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, the maximal prime power
p^{b}\leq P_{i,\max}

appears as some element of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, and then
p^{\nu_{p}}\mid p^{b}
.

Therefore every prime-power divisor of
q
divides some element of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, so
q
divides the product of
finitely many elements of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
. Hence
l_{i}(q)<\infty
.
∎

Lemma 12.3
.

For
\alpha
sufficiently small (equivalently,
L
sufficiently large), one has

\max_{1\leq q\leq H_{i}}l_{i}(q)\ \leq\ \frac{L}{2^{7}}\qquad\text{for each }i\in\{1,2\}.

Proof.

Fix
i\in\{1,2\}
. By Lemma
12.2
we have
l_{i}(q)<\infty
for all
q\leq H_{i}
, and then
Lemma
12.1
gives
l_{i}(q)\leq\omega(q)
. For
q\geq 3
we have the standard bound

\omega(q)\ \leq\ \frac{2\log q}{\log\log q}.

Since
q\leq H_{i}
, it follows that

l_{i}(q)\ \leq\ \omega(q)\ \leq\ \frac{2\log Q_{i}}{\log\log Q_{i}}.

For
i=1
we have
\log H_{1}=\log C_{1}+(2+\epsilon)L
, and for
i=2
we have
\log H_{2}=\log C_{2}+(2+\epsilon)\lfloor\log L\rfloor\log L
;
in both cases the right-hand side is
o(L)
as
L\to\infty
. Thus for
L
sufficiently large it is at most
L/2^{7}
.
∎

Lemma 12.4
.

Let
i\in\{1,2\}
and let
2\leq q\leq H_{i}
. Write
d:=l_{i}(q)
. Then

q\geq Y_{i}^{\,d-1},\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad q^{-1/(2+\epsilon)}\leq Y_{i}^{-(d-1)/(2+\epsilon)}.

(In particular, for
i=1
this reads
q\geq L^{(2+\epsilon)(d-1)}
, and for
i=2
it reads
q\geq(C_{3}L^{3+\epsilon})^{d-1}
.)

Proof.

Choose
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
of minimal cardinality such that
q\mid R_{S}
, so
|S|=d
.
By construction of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, at most one element of
S
can contain primes
\leq Y_{i}
(namely
q_{i,1}
),
and every element of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}\setminus\{q_{i,1}\}
is a power of a prime
>Y_{i}
.

Since the elements of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
are pairwise coprime, distinct primes
>Y_{i}
dividing
q
must come from
distinct elements of
S
. Therefore
q
has at least
d-1
distinct prime divisors exceeding
Y_{i}
, and hence

q\ \geq\ \prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid q\\
p>Y_{i}\end{subarray}}p\ \geq\ Y_{i}^{\,d-1}.

The bound on
q^{-1/(2+\epsilon)}
follows immediately.
∎

Lemma 12.5
.

Let
i\in\{1,2\}
. Let
2\leq q\leq H_{i}
and suppose
l_{i}(q)=d
. Let
1\leq a<q
with
(a,q)=1
.
Then there exist a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
with
|S|=d
and an integer
b
with
1\leq b<R_{S}
such that

\frac{a}{q}=\frac{b}{R_{S}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad m\nmid b\ \ \text{for every }m\in S.

Proof.

Choose
S\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
minimal such that
q\mid R_{S}
, so
|S|=d
, and write
R_{S}=q\,u
with
u\in\mathbb{N}
.
Set
b:=au
. Then
a/q=b/R_{S}
.

Fix
m\in S
. By minimality of
S
we have
(m,q)>1
, so choose a prime
p\mid(m,q)
.
Because the elements of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
are pairwise coprime, all powers of
p
appearing in
R_{S}
come from
m
,
so
v_{p}(R_{S})=v_{p}(m)
. Since
p\mid q
, we have
v_{p}(q)\geq 1
, and therefore

v_{p}(u)=v_{p}(R_{S})-v_{p}(q)=v_{p}(m)-v_{p}(q)<v_{p}(m).

Also
p\nmid a
since
(a,q)=1
, so
v_{p}(b)=v_{p}(au)=v_{p}(u)<v_{p}(m)
, and hence
m\nmid b
.
∎
