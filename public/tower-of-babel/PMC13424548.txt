Lack of behavioral response of a native crayfish to the invasive predatory swamp eel
Authors: Brandon A. Güell, Nathan J. Dorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Invasive predators are globally pervasive and frequently drive ecological disruption, yet the mechanisms underlying extreme prey vulnerability often remain unclear. Prey that fail to recognize or appropriately respond to novel predators may experience disproportionality high mortality. In the Florida Everglades, the recent invasion of the Asian Swamp eel (Monopterus albus/javanensis) has been associated with rapid collapse of slough crayfish (Procambarus fallax), and other prey of nesting wading birds. We assessed the behaviors of slough crayfish exposed to either a common native predator, warmouth (Lepomis gulosus) or invasive Asian swamp eels. Consistent with established antipredator responses of aquatic invertebrates facing visually oriented fish predators, crayfish reduced their movement, increased spatial avoidance, and decreased feeding in the presence of warmouth. In contrast, crayfish exhibited limited or no response to swamp eels. Naïve crayfish from invaded and uninvaded wetlands showed similar responses to the different fish predators, suggesting limited recognition/responsiveness despite > 10 generations of exposure to eels. Laboratory observations of predation by swamp eels suggest that slough crayfish may face a behavioral trade-off, with native predators selecting for cryptic lifestyles while coexistence with swamp eels favors more movement and active escape responses. Our results provide an initial assessment of behavioral mechanisms that may help explain crayfish population collapses following swamp eel invasion in the Everglades, are consistent with a hypothesis of prey naiveté, and suggest that historical selective pressures and/or constraints from conflicting predation modes may represent an overlooked mechanism in studies of predator-prey dynamics involving invasive species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00442-026-05941-2.

Introduction

Invasive predators are major drivers of population declines and extinctions, often causing rapid, severe reductions of native species and altering community structure (Chapin et al. III 2000; Clavero and García-Berthou 2005; Doherty et al. 2016; Gherardi 2007). Yet, the mechanisms responsible for such strong predator-prey interactions are not always well understood. Some invasive species can become particularly impactful because they possess novel predator archetypes—morphological and behavioral traits used to obtain prey not present among native predators (sensu Cox and Lima 2006)—that allow them to exploit prey effectively and avoid constraints faced by native predators (e.g., Lawson and Hill 2022). These novel predator-prey interactions can be exceptionally strong if native prey fail to recognize or appropriately respond to the invader, amplifying predation pressure and driving rapid population collapse (Sih et al. 2010). Thus, understanding whether declining native prey perceive and respond to invasive predators is important for conservation and management efforts aimed at predicting long-term prey dynamics and/or mitigating the negative impacts of invasive predators on native biodiversity (Britton 2022).

Freshwater ecosystems are particularly vulnerable to invasive predators, which can decimate prey populations and reshape community dynamics (Britton 2022; Cucherousset and Olden 2011; Mills et al. 2004). Behavioral responses are often the first line of defense for prey (Weis and Sol 2016), influencing encounter rates, survival, and ultimately population persistence without the need for evolutionary change (Gherardi et al. 2012; Mery and Burns 2010; Peacor et al. 2006; Snell-Rood 2013; West-Eberhard 2003; Wiedenmayer 2009). Prey that persist with their predators are presumably under strong selection to assess predation risk (Lima and Dill 1990) and they may be able to recognize and respond to non-native predators through generalization (Davis et al. 2012; Ferrari et al. 2007; Melotto et al. 2021; Wilson et al. 2018). Crayfishes are among the best-studied aquatic prey and demonstrate anti-predator behavioral adjustments including reduced activity (Blake and Hart 1993; Garvey et al. 1994; Hamrin 1987; Keller and Moore 2000; Stein and Magnuson 1976), increased shelter use (Beattie and Moore 2018; Blake and Hart 1993; Garvey et al. 1994; Martin 2014), and decreased foraging (Musil et al. 2023; Resetarits Jr 1991; Stein and Magnuson 1976). These responses presumably increase individual fitness by reducing attack rates through decreased detection, and importantly, are dependent on accurate predator recognition (Brodie Jr et al. 1991). For instance, low activity levels (i.e., plastic responses or slow cryptic lifestyles) promote coexistence with fish in soft-bottom permanent habitats where fast-moving visual fish predators are common (Dorn and Mittelbach 1999; Elkin and Baker 2000; Kellogg and Dorn 2012; Lima and Dill 1990; McPeek 1990a, b; Wellborn et al. 1996; Werner and Anholt 1993). Other behaviors include post-encounter responses such as chelae displays and rapid tail-flips that can reduce attack success and increase survival (Herberholz and Marquart 2012; Herberholz et al. 2004). Differences in crayfish behavioral traits affect vulnerability to native fish predators, and in turn affect assemblage structure, in freshwater ecosystems (Fortino and Creed 2007; Kellogg and Dorn 2012).

The Asian swamp eel (Monopterus albus/javanensis; hereafter ‘swamp eels’) provides a striking example of a predator capable of generating exceptionally strong prey population declines. Its invasion to the wetlands of the southern Everglades has been associated with a 95–99% collapse of crayfish populations (Procambarus fallax and its congener P. alleni) and significant density reductions of several small fishes (Pintar et al. 2023). Unlike most diurnal, visually-oriented native fish predators (mostly Centrarchidae; e.g., Lepomis spp.) plus common nonnative predators such as cichlids (e.g., Mayaheros urophthalmus), swamp eels are largely nocturnal predators that rely on sit-and-wait hunting tactics to capture their prey; video recordings of predation on crayfish suggest that their visual perception of prey is limited, though they can orient towards and slowly approach moving prey in small enclosures (Güell 2024; Shafland et al. 2010). Their elongated eel-like body shape, drought tolerance, and novel foraging behavior (Chew et al. 2005; Collins et al. 2002; Liem 1987; Schofield and Nico 2009; Wu and Liu 1940; Wu and Kung 1940) constitute a predator archetype absent in native fish communities of Florida, and their drought tolerance enables them to exert strong effects on prey during the early wet-season when predator densities would typically be lower; most fish predators must recolonize the topographically flat landscape from distance (Dorn 2008; Dorn and Cook 2015; Dorn and Trexler 2007; Ruetz et al. 2005; Trexler et al. 2005). Behavioral response to the nocturnal ambushing eel predator may be limited among native prey as well.

Given the severity of crayfish population declines and the novelty of the predator, we questioned whether crayfish were perceiving or responding to the new predator risk. Because individuals used in this study were reared under standardized, predator-free laboratory conditions prior to testing, all crayfish were experimentally naïve to predators at the time of behavioral assays. Our goal was to determine whether behavioral misperception could be exacerbating the predator-prey interaction with eels. Specifically, we assessed responses of slough crayfish (P. fallax) from invaded and uninvaded wetlands to both a known, important, and widespread native predator (warmouth, L. gulosus; Bransky and Dorn 2013; Kellogg and Dorn 2012) and to swamp eels. We expected individuals from both invaded and uninvaded wetlands to respond to native warmouth but anticipated that crayfish from the uninvaded wetland would show limited or no response to swamp eels. Failure of crayfish from both populations to respond to swamp eels would support level 1 naïveté (complete lack of recognition; sensu Banks and Dickman 2007). Given that crayfish in the invaded wetland have experienced approximately 10–12 generations of exposure to swamp eels, lack of response by this population would suggest that this duration of exposure has not been sufficient to improve predator recognition or elicit effective responses.

Materials and methods

Experimental animal collection and maintenance

We collected P. fallax from two wetlands within the greater Everglades ecosystem: one previously invaded by swamp eels and one uninvaded. The invasion status of each wetland was known from ongoing aquatic monitoring studies (Pintar et al. 2024). Crayfish from the invaded wetland were collected from multiple sites spaced 950–1700 m apart within Water Conservation Area 3B (hereafter ‘WCA 3B’), where swamp eels had been established for at least six years (Fig. 1). Crayfish from the uninvaded wetland were collected from two isolated 8-ha macrocosms at the Loxahatchee Impoundment Landscape Assessment (hereafter ‘LILA’) facility in Boynton Beach, where swamp eels had never been detected (Fig. 1). All crayfish were collected in November 2023.

Unlike its congener P. alleni (Everglades crayfish), Procambarus fallax is a small (max size ~ 40 mm carapace length, CL), slow-moving, and cryptic procambarid that coexists with fish predators in the Everglades (Dorn and Cook 2015; Dorn and Trexler 2007; Dorn and Volin 2009). Movements of tracked individuals over short periods (21 days) suggest they move in circuitous paths over small home ranges (< 0.1 ha; van der Heiden 2012). The invaded and uninvaded wetlands, separated by levees, canals and > 80 km represent reproductively isolated populations, while our sampling locations within the wetlands likely included multiple subpopulations given the small home ranges and dispersal-limiting features of deep water and levees (Fig. 1). Crayfish used for testing were pooled across all sampling locations within each wetland. However, within each wetland, we collected individuals from at least two distinct subpopulations to ensure broader representation of the wetland population. Thus, the tested crayfish likely provided representative samples from the invaded and uninvaded wetland populations. The behavioral observations for each wetland might therefore be considered preliminary. Crayfish were collected using mesh seines and throw traps, while fish predators were collected from WCA 3B using an airboat-mounted electrofisher (Chick et al. 1999).

Densities of P. fallax were relatively high (> 4/m2) in the LILA impounded wetland but low (< 1/m2) in WCA 3B (Pintar et al. 2023; unpublished data), making it difficult to collect all the individuals necessary for our experiment. For WCA 3B we collected 9 mature individuals from WCA 3B, bred them in the laboratory, and used offspring from five broods (each from a different female) for the experiments. Consequently, test-crayfish from the invaded wetland (WCA 3B) were ontogenetically (i.e., experientially in the wild), but not evolutionarily, naïve to all predators (sensu Cox and Lima 2006), while those from the uninvaded wetland (LILA) were naïve to swamp eels in both senses. The short lifespans of P. fallax (< 1.5 years; van der Heiden 2012) and phenology of the population mean the crayfish from the uninvaded range (8.7–17.0 mm CL) had been free living for ≤ 4 months at the time of collection. We assumed crayfish responses to swamp eels therefore reflected innate behaviors. Because test-crayfish from the uninvaded wetland were wild-caught, it is possible they may have had previous encounters with native fish predators, which could limit our ability to separate learned behavior from innate responses to the warmouth. We cannot conclusively rule out the possibility that prior exposure to native predators might have influenced responses to swamp eels in our experiment. However, crayfish memory fades within one to three weeks (Hazlett et al. 2002; Kubec et al. 2019) and the individuals we tested were reared for three months (November to February) in predator-free laboratory conditions, undergoing several molts before testing, making it unlikely they retained information about their native environment.

All animals were kept in 75 L stock aquaria filled with dechlorinated tap water at 21 °C and under a 12 L:12 D photoperiod at the Aquatic Ecology Lab at Florida International University until testing. Crayfish were acclimated to laboratory conditions for 3–4 months (November to February) prior to testing. Crayfish and swamp eels were fed commercial sinking shrimp pellets at least three times per week. Warmouth were fed a combination of crayfish and grass shrimp at least three times per week. Swamp eels were additionally fed crayfish and dragonfly naiads to supplement their diet at least once a week. We measured crayfish carapace length (CL) and fish total lengths (TL) to the nearest 1 mm. We only tested crayfish with complete and intact appendages and fully hardened exoskeletons; individuals that molted during testing were excluded from analyses, and we only used individual crayfish once for trials with predators. We used male and female medium-sized juvenile crayfish (12.43 ± 1.54 mm CL, range = 8.7–17 mm) within the range of individuals found in swamp eel guts (13.3 ± 6.2 mm CL, range = 2.94–32.4 mm, N = 149; Herrera et al. unpublished data). We used two warmouth (12.7 and 15.1 cm TL) and four swamp eels (54.8 ± 6.6 cm TL, mean ± SD here and throughout text, range = 45–59 cm) for experiments; warmouth above 7 cm TL are known to feed heavily on crayfish, shrimp, and small fishes in the Everglades (Bransky and Dorn 2013). Swamp eels > 30 cm TL consume crayfish when they are available (Herrera et al. unpublished, Shafland et al. 2010). All fish were starved for at least 12 h prior to use in the experiments.

Experimental arena and design

We assessed the behavioral response of P. fallax to warmouth and swamp eels from 14 February to 9 April 2024. We implemented a 2 × 3 experimental design in which juvenile P. fallax from invaded and uninvaded wetlands were exposed to one of three different predator treatments: control water, a native predator (warmouth), and an invasive predator (swamp eel). We performed 15 replicates per population per treatment (N = 15 per population per treatment; 90 trials total) in four identical 75 L experimental aquaria (76.2 × 30.48 × 30.48 cm) filled with 48 L of continuously aerated dechlorinated tap water (21 °C). Sex ratios were approximately balanced across predator treatments for crayfish from both wetlands: LILA (uninvaded) – 7 M: 8 F for all treatments; WCA 3B (invaded) – 8 M: 7 F for all treatments. Crayfish sex had no effect on any of our response variables (results not shown); we therefore pooled data across crayfish sex for all analyses. Aquaria were divided in half, at approximatively 38 cm from each side, using a perforated transparent plexiglass divider that allowed for visual and waterborne cues between predator and prey (Fig. 2). A 2 cm layer of sand was provided on the side with the crayfish to facilitate natural movements (e.g., crawling and digging) and to serve as a contrasting background so crayfish positions and behaviors would be easily visible in recordings. We externally marked the crayfish side of each aquarium to delineate three equally-sized ‘zones’ crayfish could occupy: the third closest to the divider, the middle third, and the third furthest from the divider (Fig. 2). We wrapped aquaria with black plastic on three sides to limit shadows and eliminate visual cues or perturbations between aquaria but left the front and top of the aquaria unobstructed for video recording of behavior and introductions of animals.

Each trial consisted of the observation of three behavioral responses of individual crayfish: (1) time spent active before and after exposure to predator treatments, (2) arena space use (as a measure of predator avoidance) over longer-term predator exposure, and (3) feeding response after prolonged predator exposure. To begin a trial, we stocked an aquarium with one crayfish at approximately 14:00 h, allowed it to acclimate for 45 min, then began video recording for a total of 10 min. Several studies indicate that 30 min is sufficient to acclimate crayfish to experimental conditions for behavioral research (e.g., Acquistapace et al. 2004; Gherardi et al. 2011). After 5 min (i.e., the “before” trial period), we introduced either a single warmouth, a single swamp eel, or 1 L of control water into the opposite side of the aquarium. Predators were gently added to the arena with ~ 1 L of water from their holding aquaria to minimize disturbance to all experimental animals. Control water was introduced to arenas similarly by gently and gradually pouring it into the arena using the same container, motion, and technique used to add predators. We recorded video continuously at 30 frames s− 1. Trial periods were before (5 min) and after (5 min) exposure to predator treatments. Next, we measured crayfish position within the arena over longer-term exposure to predator treatments using a time-lapse series of photographs taken every 20 min from 15:00 to 09:00 h the following day (i.e., 18 h; 55 photographs). Light levels were substantially decreased (from ~ 237 to 22 lx; measured using a HOBO light meter where experimental aquaria were placed) by turning off half the overhead lights during this period to encourage crepuscular and nocturnal behaviors of predators and prey. In the morning, we assessed the feeding response and the latency to feed of crayfish during a 2-hour feeding trial by placing a single sinking food pellet in the center of the crayfish side of the arena and beginning a time-lapse series of photographs taken every 2 min from 09:00 to 11:00 h (i.e., 2 h: 60 photographs total). Aquaria, dividers, and sand substrate were rinsed thoroughly between trials.

Video analysis of crayfish behavioral responses to predator treatments

We quantified the proportion of time crayfish spent active with 1/30 s (single frame) accuracy from video recordings and compared them across predator treatments, trial periods (i.e., before and after exposure to predator treatments), and crayfish populations. We considered all movements by crayfish including walking, swimming, crawling, digging, probing, and grooming as activity (see Stein and Magnuson 1976 for behavioral definitions), and combined their durations in our analyses of crayfish activity before and after adding predators to aquaria. We assessed crayfish activity under the assumption that encounters with visually-oriented fish predators would be decreased by risk-induced reduction in activity consistent with other prey from soft-bottom habitats (Boys et al. 2025; Peterson et al. 2021; Strobbe et al. 2011) and the hypotheses by Kellogg and Dorn (2012), which suggested that low baseline activity and reduced movement can reduce detection and predation risk from visually hunting fish predators (McPeek 1990a, b; Wellborn et al. 1996).

We quantified predator avoidance (i.e., horizontal space use) of P. fallax in response to predator treatments by recording which of the three zones (furthest, middle, or closest third to the divider; Fig. 2) in the arena crayfish occupied in each frame of the first time-lapse. Since the proportion of time spent in each zone was not independent because crayfish always had to be in one of the three zones, we assigned the presence of crayfish in each zone a numerical value (i.e., furthest zone: 3, middle: 2, and closest: 1) and calculated the position score for each crayfish by summing these values. Thus, position scores represented the relative time spent in each zone, with higher scores representing crayfish that spent more time in the furthest zone from the predator. We assumed that occupancy of the furthest zone represented maximum threat-sensitive predator avoidance (Helfman 1989) and simulated natural spatial changes in prey distribution (i.e., predator avoidance) that reduces detection and encounters with predators in nature (Ahmed et al. 2011; Lima 1998; Lima and Dill 1990; Sih 2005; Thaker et al. 2011). Preliminary analyses only analyzing the proportion of time spent in the zone furthest from the arena divider produced similar results as using position scores.

Finally, we assessed the feeding response and the feeding latency of crayfish after long-term exposure (18 h) to predator treatments by recording if and when crayfish first began feeding during the two-hour feeding trial. Because we assessed the feeding response after chronic exposure to high risk (as opposed to a “brief pulse of high risk”; see Lima and Bednekoff 1999), we considered the binomial feeding response and feeding latency to be reasonably accurate measures of crayfish risk assessment and expected that they should reduce foraging in contexts perceived as high risk, consistent with predictions of optimal foraging theory (Lima and Bednekoff 1999; Lima and Dill 1990; Strobbe et al. 2011).

Statistical analysis

We used mixed models (‘lme4’ package; Bates et al. 2015) followed by likelihood ratio tests of nested models to analyze the behavioral responses of crayfish to predator treatments. We used a linear mixed model (LMM) to determine the effects of predator treatment, trial period, crayfish population, and their interactions (i.e., predator treatment × trial period × crayfish population three-way interaction) on the proportion of time crayfish were active in response to predators. Predator treatments were exposure to native warmouth, invasive Asian swamp eels, or control water; trial periods were before and after exposure to predator treatments; and populations represent crayfish from wetlands that were invaded (WCA 3B) or uninvaded (LILA) by the swamp eel at the time of this study. We included individual crayfish as a random effect to account for the non-independence of observations on the same animal before and after predator exposure and treatment-specific variance structures to account for heteroscedasticity.

We used LMMs to determine the effects of predator treatment, crayfish population, and their interaction (i.e. predator treatment × crayfish population two-way interaction) on the position score and latency to feed, respectively; the response variables in these analyses were the position scores and the number of minutes until crayfish interacted with the food item respectively. We ran the model to assess latency to feed on log-transformed values to satisfy normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions for parametric analyses. We then used comparisons of estimated marginal means (EMMs) with Bonferroni corrections (‘emmeans’ package; Lenth 2023) to determine effects within and across treatments, trial periods, and populations.

We compared the proportion of crayfish that fed in each predator treatment by calculating odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and assessed statistical significant using chi-squared tests (‘epi R’ package; Stevenson and Sergeant 2025). Finally, a subset of the warmouth (n = 26) defecated while in the experiment and we conducted a preliminary analysis of all three responses to warmouth replicates with and without defecation to look for responses to diet cues (i.e., defecation indicating recent digestion), but we did not find any variation in the responses (all P > 0.29) and did not report further on this in the results. All statistical analyses were performed in the R statistical environment using RStudio v. 4.1.1 (R Core Development Team 2021).

Exposure to warmouth reduced crayfish activity by an average of 50% (pre- vs. post-exposure: P < 0.001; Fig. 3A), resulting in a significant difference in post-exposure activity when compared to swamp eel and control treatments (both pairwise comparisons P < 0.001). Activity also decreased slightly (from 67 to 51%) in response to swamp eels (P = 0.001), but post-exposure activity in the presence of swamp eels was similar to the post-exposure activity of crayfish in response to the control treatment (P = 0.983). Crayfish did not alter their activity in response to control water (pre- vs. post-exposure: P = 0.746).

Across all predator treatments and populations, crayfish occupied the zone furthest from the divider more than other zones (64 ± 28% vs. 31 ± 27% and 4 ± 5% in the closest and middle zones, respectively). Crayfish position score (i.e., the relative time spent in each zone) varied with predator treatment, but not across crayfish populations or the predator treatment × crayfish population two-way interaction (Table 1). Position scores of crayfish exposed to swamp eel and control treatments did not differ (P = 0.986) but were both significantly lower than those of crayfish exposed to the warmouth treatment (both pairwise comparisons P < 0.02). Crayfish had the highest positions scores when exposed to warmouth, indicating they were most likely to occupy the furthest zone in response to this predator treatment and did so 20% more of the time compared to when exposed to swamp eel and control treatments (Table 1; Fig. 3B).

The probability of feeding was a function of predator treatment (Fig. 3C). All but one crayfish (97%) in the control treatment fed within two hours, while crayfish exposed to warmouth were 92.3 times less likely to feed (warmouth vs. control: OR = 0.01, 95% CI: 0.00–0.08; χ2 = 36.274, P < 0.001). The probability of feeding was only slightly reduced by swamp eels; it was 10.5 times less likely than in the control treatment (swamp eel vs. control: OR = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.01–0.82; χ2 = 4.706, P = 0.03). When likelihood of feeding was compared directly the crayfish were 11 times less likely to feed when exposed to warmouth than swamp eels (warmouth vs. swamp eel: OR = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.03–0.30; χ2 = 17.143, P < 0.001; Fig. 3C). For crayfish that did feed, the latency to feed varied with predator treatment, but not crayfish population or their interaction (Table 1; Fig. 3D). Crayfish exposed to warmouth took > 2 times longer (52%) to begin feeding compared to those in swamp eel or control treatments (Fig. 3D).

Discussion

Juvenile slough crayfish exhibited clear threat-sensitive antipredator responses to native warmouth, reducing their activity, maximizing spatial avoidance, and suppressing feeding— behaviors consistent with classic risk responses of aquatic prey vulnerable to visually oriented fish predators (Wellborn et al. 1996). These responses align particularly well with longstanding predator–prey dynamics in Everglades wetlands, where P. fallax have coexisted with centrarchids and is under persistent selection to mitigate predation risk by active visual predators (Dorn and Mittelbach 1999; Kellogg and Dorn 2012; Knorp and Dorn 2014). In contrast, crayfish showed only weak or undetectable antipredator behaviors when exposed to Asian swamp eels. The weak reductions in activity and feeding observed in the presence of swamp eels may reflect generalized responses to an unfamiliar and moving stimulus rather than recognition of specific predator cues, consistent with cautious behavior toward novel organisms. This stark divergence suggests that swamp eels—whose foraging mode, morphology, and sensory ecology differ markedly from native predators—act as a novel predator archetype to which crayfish do not exhibit appropriate threat-sensitive responses.

The rapid and severe declines of crayfish and small fishes in eel-invaded wetlands (Pintar et al. 2023) have generally been attributed to the swamp eels’ persistence across the hydroperiod gradient and their ability to exploit early wet-season periods when native predator densities are naturally low. Our findings suggest a second mechanism that could be contributing to high prey vulnerability and population collapse; crayfish appear behaviorally naïve to swamp eels, failing to recognize and/or appropriately respond to the predatory threat, thereby increasing encounter rates and mortality. If crayfish fail to recognize swamp eels as dangerous, they may maintain baseline levels of activity, remain spatially proximate to eels, and thereby continue foraging in high-risk conditions—behaviors that would elevate encounter rates and vulnerability.

As expected, crayfish from both invaded and uninvaded wetlands had strong antipredator and predator avoidance responses when exposed to warmouth due to their long-shared evolutionary history and the widespread abundance of the sunfish predator (Parkos et al. 2011). Previous work by Kellogg and Dorn (2012) suggested that lower baseline activity allows P. fallax to better coexist with higher abundances of native predatory fishes than the congeneric P. alleni. Here, we provide the first empirical evidence that P. fallax also exhibits plastic, threat-sensitive behavioral adjustments when directly exposed to predators, demonstrating that this species relies not only on low baseline activity, but also on inducible antipredator responses to mitigate predation risk. The substantial reduction in activity (50%) and feeding (77%), alongside a significant change in spatial distribution (20%) (i.e., predator avoidance), reflects highly adaptive responses to this specific predator in its native habitat. Warmouth are large-bodied, fast-moving, visually-oriented, diurnal, and voracious predators known to reduce crayfish abundance (e.g., Dorn and Mittelbach 1999; Kellogg and Dorn 2012; Knorp and Dorn 2014; Stein 1977). Through the perforated divider in our experiment, crayfish were likely exposed to multiple cue types, including visual, mechanosensory, and chemical cues, all of which may contribute to predator recognition.

We did not design this study to focus on the identity of the risk cues, but acknowledge that slight differences in predator diets in holding tanks (i.e., warmouth consumed more crayfish) could have produced different diet cues and affected the responses. For several reasons we do not find this a good explanation for our results. First, all predators were starved for at least 12 h which should have diminished the exudates as there was no recent foraging by the predator (Large and Smee 2010; Scherer and Smee 2016). Second, the rapid responses (within seconds) to the warmouth additions suggests visual cues were the source of primary risk information. Third, the replicates in which warmouth defecated, indicating recent digestion, produced similar behavioral responses as replicates in which the warmouth did not defecate (note in methods). Finally, diet cues produced by conspecific ingestion are not generally considered reliable cues or primary sources of information about risk from generalist predators (Diebboll 2024; Scherer and Smee 2016), and both of our predators were generalists (Bransky and Dorn 2013; Herrera et al. unpublished). While we do not consider diet cues a likely mechanism driving our results, we do recognize that further work should be done on the risk cues used by crayfish to identify both native fish and non-native predators.

The combined cues immediately triggered crayfish to freeze and/or hide (Güell 2024) in our study. When directly exposed to warmouth, some crayfish also turned and oriented towards the perceived threat, raised and spread their chelipeds in a defensive position (defined as ‘chelae display’ in Stein and Magnuson 1976) (Fig. 4A). The hunting modality of warmouth and other visually-oriented fish predators contrasts sharply with that of swamp eels, which appear to be largely non-visual, bottom-dwelling, ambush predators that are primarily active at night and only move quickly during their ram-suction feeding attacks (Table S1; see Güell 2024 for video recordings of attacks). Because both predator types moved within experimental arenas during all parts of the experiment and crayfish must respond rapidly when threatened in the wild at any point in time, differences in diel activity patterns of the predators in our experiment are unlikely to explain the lack of response to swamp eels. Observations from predation trials suggest that swamp eels perceive prey using olfactory and mechanosensory cues (see Supplementary Information). Swamp eels readily oriented towards and consumed motionless food items, and they used both ambush and active (albeit slow approach) hunting strategies equally to attack both moving and motionless crayfish (Table S1). These observations suggest that the freezing response crayfish use when encountering warmouth would likely be ineffective against swamp eels, even if triggered. But crayfish exposed to swamp eels also showed no signs of predator spatial avoidance over the night-time hours in the experiments when eels were expected to be most active. In our observations of crayfish placed with lethal eels they regularly approached, climbed on, and interacted with swamp eels as if they were inanimate objects (Fig. 4B). This lack of response across both wetland populations is consistent with either a widespread lack of predator recognition or an inability to mount an effective antipredator strategy.

We found no behavioral differences between crayfish from invaded and uninvaded wetlands, providing no evidence for selection favoring enhanced recognition. This result may be unsurprising as the invasion of the swamp eel is still relatively recent at our study site (~ 6 years and 12 crayfish generations) and population-level responses to novel predators may take upwards of 200 generations of exposure to new ecological pressures to evolve (Anton et al. 2020; Carthey and Banks 2016). The drastic effects of the swamp eel on local prey populations could be attenuated through local adaptation, and strong predation pressure could select for the rapid evolution of predator recognition and/or a new behavioral response that reduces predation (Ruland and Jeschke 2020).

The appropriate and effective antipredator response to swamp eels remains unclear even if the risk were perceived by crayfish. Immediate reductions in activity within the proximity of swamp eels seems ineffective from preliminary observations (Güell 2024; Table S1) and in nature would likely incur increased predation risk. Retreating to shelters can be effective against large, visually-oriented fish predators (Beattie and Moore 2018; Blake and Hart 1993; Garvey et al. 1994; Martin 2014); however, most soft-bottom habitats of Everglades wetlands where P. fallax are found lack permanent refuges. Moreover, swamp eels can navigate highly vegetated habitats and even follow crayfish into burrows more easily than native predators, likely rendering shelter use ineffective. Tail flipping, however, is a highly effective and general crayfish escape behavior that can increase survival in attacks by a range of predators (e.g., Herberholz and Marquart 2012; Herberholz et al. 2004). Crayfish successfully escaped attacks by swamp eels and warmouth using tail flips in preliminary feeding trials (Güell 2024; Table S1). Tail-flipping is an immediate antipredator response to imminent danger that a determined predator may be able to overcome with persistent chasing; nevertheless, tail flipping could create enough space between crayfish and swamp eels to deter further attacks. Our observations of some crayfish escaping attacks with little-to-no subsequent chase by swamp eels in small enclosures (Güell 2024) suggests eels do not rapidly pursue prey and that tail flipping may be the only promising response to this novel predator at close range (i.e., < 15 cm). At further distances, crayfish may reduce encounter rates with swamp eels, if they recognized their approach, by frequently redistributing. Procambarus fallax may be stuck between opposing predator modalities (Peckarsky and McIntosh 1998), with visual native predators selecting for slow, cryptic lifestyles, and swamp eels selecting for active predator avoidance and/or escape responses.

Whether native prey can acquire recognition and adapt to invasive predators quickly may largely determine whether local populations are driven to extinction. Our results are concerning for the future of P. fallax populations in south Florida and the success of one of the primary goals of Everglades restoration: to restore historic wading bird populations by increasing nesting success in Everglades National Park (Doren et al. 2009; Frederick et al. 2009). Procambarus fallax, along with its congener the Everglades crayfish (P. alleni), are essential components of the Everglades trophic web (Frederick and Spalding 1994) and particularly important prey for wading bird populations (Boyle et al. 2012, 2014; Cocoves et al. 2021). In the initially invaded wetlands of the Everglades, the wading bird prey biomass has been reduced by 80% (Pintar and Dorn 2025). If the rampant invasion and spread of swamp eels permanently limits prey populations, the trophic functions of the wetlands to provision nesting wading bird populations could also be affected despite enormous efforts at hydro-restoration of the Everglades. How P. alleni responds behaviorally to swamp eels is unknown; however they are larger, faster-growing, more active, and outcompete P. fallax for space and food (Dorn and Trexler 2007), despite living in seasonally drying (low fish) wetlands. Active life-history traits typically make crayfish more susceptible to visual fish predators (Dorn and Mittelbach 1999; Fortino and Creed 2007; Garvey et al. 1994; Hill and Lodge 1999), but could prove useful in surviving with swamp eels if the novel predator is perceived as risky. The collapse of P. alleni populations in invaded wetlands (Pintar et al. 2023) indicates the predation on P. alleni is intense and that both species are highly vulnerable to eel predation regardless of their contrasting life histories.

A broader implication of our findings is that prey may face conflicting selection pressures in systems where native and invasive predators exploit fundamentally different foraging modalities. For instance, slough crayfish (P. fallax) are well adapted to coexist with fast-moving, visually oriented native predators by maintaining low activity and relying on crypsis, but these same traits appear maladaptive against mechanosensory, nocturnal ambush predators such as swamp eels. Such trait mismatches can constrain the ability of prey populations to employ behavioral strategies that minimize predation risk across predator assemblages. This tension may limit the potential for rapid behavioral adjustment and create demographic bottlenecks when a novel predator imposes strong, opposing selective pressures. Similar constraints have been proposed for prey exposed to divergent predator hunting modes, where movement, refuge use, or vigilance that reduces encounters with one predator can elevate vulnerability to another (Peckarsky and McIntosh 1998; Relyea 2003; Sih et al. 1998). In the case of swamp eels, selection may favor increased movement and active escape, but such responses would also elevate detection by native fish predators, producing an unstable adaptive landscape. This conflict provides a population-level mechanism that may help explain why crayfish declines have been so severe and persistent in eel-invaded wetlands. Such behavioral constraints may represent an underrecognized mechanism shaping prey population dynamics following introductions of non-native predators with novel predatory phenotypes. Understanding how prey populations navigate and adapt to opposing predation regimes will be essential for predicting long-term coexistence and for anticipating the broader ecological consequences of invasive predators with novel foraging phenotypes.
