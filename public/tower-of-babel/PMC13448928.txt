Nucleation-Accelerated Epitaxy at Large-Area (100) β‑Ga2O3 Terraces
Authors: Suji Im, Byeongcheol Choe, Huiwon Kim, Gwangcheol Ji, MyeongJun Kang, Haeyong Kang, Hyoungjeen Jeen, Andrej Kuznetsov, Sungkyun Park, Jong Mok Ok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Even though epitaxy is synonymous with order, its practical realization often depends on the availability of surface sites that locally break ideal flatness, such as steps and kinks, where adatoms can be efficiently incorporated. This challenge is well-known for homoepitaxial growth of thermodynamically stable monoclinic gallium oxide (β-Ga2O3) on (100) substrates, which in the as-cleaved exhibit large-area, atomically flat terraces with a low density of step edges. To address this problem, we propose a self-organized process triggered by annealing that subdivides large-area terraces into narrower domains, with substeps corresponding to approximately half the unit cell height. Using such substep-templated substrates, we achieved a high growth rate of ∼2.83 μm h–1, exceeding values reported for (100) β-Ga2O3 homoepitaxy in the literature, while maintaining reasonable structural quality and electronic functionality. By placing this substep-templating approach in the context of other nucleation-enhancing strategies for β-Ga2O3 homoepitaxy, we conclude that it provides a viable route to mitigate limitations associated with higher surface-energy planes or off-axis miscuts. Altogether, these results demonstrate a practical strategy for improving β-Ga2O3 homoepitaxy for advanced device applications.

Introduction

Recently, gallium oxide (Ga2O3) has emerged as a promising ultrawide bandgap semiconductor for next-generation power electronics, UV-photonics, etc. − For all these applications, device performance strongly depends on the availability of high-quality epitaxial β-Ga2O3 films. Particular attention has been attracted to the homoepitaxy of the thermodynamically stable monoclinic Ga2O3 polymorph, typically labeled as β-Ga2O3 in the literature. , Notably, among other orientations of β-Ga2O3, (100) stands out as a natural cleavage plane with the lowest surface energy, offering a range of potential advantages for homoepitaxy, such as ease of the surface preparation, atomic flatness, and compatibility with standard fabrication processes, etc. , However, despite these benefits, epitaxial growth along the (100) orientation remains challenging, primarily due to the inherently low density of step-edges on on-axis β-Ga2O3 (100) surfaces. As-cleaved β-Ga2O3 (100) surfaces are characterized by wide terraces and a low density of surface steps. The resulting low density of step edges limits the number of energetically favorable incorporation sites available for migrating adatoms, increasing the probability of terrace nucleation during film growth. Consequently, step-mediated incorporation becomes less efficient, particularly at high growth rates, making it more difficult to maintain uniform epitaxial growth on on-axis β-Ga2O3 (100) surfaces.

There is a conventional approach to deal with this challenge: intentionally introducing a 4–6° miscut angle from the nominal (100) plane, narrowing the terrace width, and resulting in a higher number of step edges, thereby promoting the step-flow growth. , Such miscut approaches are not unique to β-Ga2O3 and have been widely employed in other semiconductor materials such as Si, SiC and GaN to stabilize step-flow epitaxy. − However, despite their effectiveness in promoting nucleation and suppressing island-type growth, miscut substrates inherently introduce several limitations. The fabrication of miscut wafers requires additional material removal during wafer processing, increasing material consumption and wafer cost. In addition, the step-bunching behavior commonly observed on off-axis surfaces degrades surface morphology and film-thickness uniformity, particularly over large-area wafers. ,− Additional wafer processing can also introduce polishing-induced lattice damage, residual strain, and other crystallographic defects in the near-surface region, leading to deterioration of the near-surface crystal quality and requiring further surface preparation before epitaxy. − Miscut orientations tend to facilitate dislocation replication and complicate device processing steps that are optimized for on-axis configurations. ,− These drawbacks collectively may limit the scalability and process compatibility of miscut β-Ga2O3 substrates for practical device technologies.

Alternatively, as demonstrated in this work, thermal annealing can be used to modify the surface morphology of large-area, flat β-Ga2O3 (100) terraces into a more step- or kink-rich surface while preserving the on-axis substrate orientation. In this work, we investigate the effect of annealing on β-Ga2O3 (100) surfaces and its influence on subsequent homoepitaxial growth. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements show that annealing subdivides large-area terraces into narrower domains and forms substeps with heights corresponding to approximately one-half of the β-Ga2O3 unit cell height, which is comparable to the step height typically observed on β-Ga2O3 (100) surfaces. As a result, the reconstructed surface provides a higher density of step-edge sites for subsequent growth. Using these reconstructed surfaces as growth templates, homoepitaxial β-Ga2O3 films were grown at rates exceeding previously reported values for on-axis β-Ga2O3 (100) homoepitaxy while maintaining good structural and electronic properties.

Results and Discussion

Generation of Substeps on Large-Area (100) β-Ga2O3 Terraces

Figure shows AFM topography images of (a) an as-cleaved (100) β-Ga2O3 surface, and (b) the same surface after annealing for 6 h at 1100 °C, revealing significant surface reconstruction following annealing. The as-cleaved (100) β-Ga2O3 surface exhibits flat, large-area, regularly stepped terraces, with a root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of ∼0.134 nm measured within a single terrace area, as shown in Figure a. The height profile below the AFM image in Figure a shows the terrace height measured along the red dotted line in the AFM image. The surface consists of terraces several tens of micrometres wide, separated by steps with a height of ∼0.59 nm, consistent with (a/2) cos β, indicating that the substrate has a negligible miscut angle.

Surface morphology of the β-Ga2O 3 (100) substrate revealed by AFM. (a) As-cleaved surface showing atomically flat terraces several tens of micrometers wide. The accompanying height profile extracted along the red dashed line shows a step height of approximately 0.59 nm. (b) Surface morphology after annealing at 1100 °C for 6 h in air. The enlarged AFM image of the selected area and the accompanying height profile extracted along the red line show the newly generated substeps.

In contrast, Figure b shows a reconstructed surface morphology after annealing. Moreover, the insets in Figure b provide an enlarged AFM view of the selected area and an example of the height profile for the newly generated square-shaped voids. These features, defined as substeps in the rest of the paper, subdivide the original large-area terraces into narrower domains, resulting in an increased density of step edges. The height profile shows that the substeps have a characteristic height of approximately one-half of the β-Ga2O3 unit-cell height, comparable to the characteristic step height of the (100) surface. This observation is consistent with the bc-plane orientation exposed by cutting along (100), indicating that the reconstruction is governed by crystallographic stability and orientation selectivity.

To further quantify the geometric effect of the reconstructed morphology, the representative AFM image shown in Figure b was analyzed by extracting the boundaries of the thermally generated substeps. The total step-edge length within the 5 × 5 μm2 AFM image was approximately 95 μm, corresponding to a representative step-edge density of ∼3.8 μm–1. Compared with the as-cleaved β-Ga2O3 (100) surface, where terrace widths exceeding 40 μm give an estimated step-edge density of ≤0.025 μm–1, the annealed surface therefore exhibits at least ∼150-fold increase in step-edge density. This reconstruction was not limited to a local region but was observed across a relatively wide area of the substrate. Supporting Figure 1 shows that the substep reconstruction extends over a wide surface area exceeding 100 × 150 μm2, indicating that terrace subdivision occurs over a broad surface region beyond that shown in Figure b.

The observed reconstruction is likely driven by thermally activated surface mass redistribution across the wide (100) terraces. Surface atoms migrate during annealing, leading to local depletion or accumulation of material and thereby forming void-like morphologies in the main figures, while island-like features are more clearly observed in Supporting Figure 2. This behavior should be distinguished from negative growth typically observed under strongly oxygen-deficient conditions, where surface recession is associated with oxide decomposition and the loss of volatile Ga suboxide species. − In contrast, the present reconstruction occurs under ambient-pressure annealing and produces both void-like and island-like features, indicating local redistribution of terrace material rather than simple evaporation-driven material loss. The spatial separation of these morphologies further suggests that they are alternative outcomes of the same terrace-reorganization process. We note that this process is also distinct from conventional step bunching: because the on-axis β-Ga2O3 (100) substrate has a negligible miscut angle and very few pre-existing step edges, the reconstruction is unlikely to arise from step aggregation and instead occurs within the interior of the initially wide terraces.

The evolution of the reconstructed surface morphology under different annealing conditions is presented in Supporting Figure 2. Substep formation appears to require an appropriate annealing window: 900 °C was insufficient to induce terrace subdivision, whereas annealing at 1100 °C initiated substep formation and a 6 h treatment produced well-defined square-shaped substeps. In contrast, annealing at 1300 °C for 1 h yielded only a limited number of substeps, suggesting that excessive surface mobility may promote smoothing or coarsening rather than stable substep formation. Together with the presence of both void-like and island-like features, this annealing-window dependence supports that the observed reconstruction arises from surface mass redistribution rather than simple negative growth driven by net material loss.

Nucleation-Enhancement Strategies for β-Ga2O3 Homoepitaxy

Figure compares the present thermal-annealing approach with previously reported nucleation-enhancement strategies for β-Ga2O3 homoepitaxy. Because the as-cleaved (100) β-Ga2O3 surface exhibits the lowest surface energy, it forms large atomically flat terraces with a low density of step- and kink-related incorporation sites, making it unsuitable for high growth rate conditions, as schematically shown in Figure . More specifically, using molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), Sasaki et al. reported a growth rate as low as ∼10 nm h–1 growth rate on as-cleaved (100) planes. Moreover, even when applying processes that typically exhibit high growth rates, such as metal–organic vapor phase epitaxy (MOVPE) or metal–organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD), the growth rates on the as-cleaved (100) surface were reported in the range of 0.85–1 μm h–1 , at maximum.

Schematic comparison of representative nucleation-enhancing strategies for β-Ga2O3 homoepitaxy, including the thermally generated substep-templated on-axis β-Ga2O3 (100) surface proposed in this work (bottom panel).

To accelerate epitaxial growth, increasing the density of step edges on the substrate surface is essential because adatoms preferentially incorporate at step sites. Two classical approaches have been widely used to achieve this: introducing a miscut angle on the (100) substrate or, more radically, switching to a different crystallographic plane such as (010), as illustrated in Figure . In the former case, the increased step density promotes the step-assisted incorporation reported in the literature. ,, Nevertheless, even on miscut surfaces, adatoms deposited away from step edges must diffuse across a finite terrace width before incorporation, so terrace nucleation can still compete with step-edge incorporation under high-flux or high-growth-rate conditions. − In the latter case, growth on the (010) plane naturally provides a higher step density. However, the steps originate from thermodynamically stabilized subnanometer facets composed of the (110) and (1̅10) planes. − The resulting facets are inclined by less than ∼14° with respect to the (010) plane and form elongated ridge-and-trench structures across the surface. , Consequently, the surface exhibits a jagged morphology characterized by discontinuous and irregular step edges, which may complicate the formation of atomically flat epitaxial layers. A related but more severe limitation is encountered for step-terrace morphologies that have also occasionally been observed on nominally on-axis (100) substrates after CMP treatment or subsequent high-temperature annealing. In this case, however, these steps typically originate from a small inherent crystal misorientation (effective miscut ∼0.3–0.4°). Consequently, adatom incorporation still relies on diffusion to step edges across wide terraces, and the fundamental limitation associated with widely spaced step edges remains.

In contrast, our approach generates additional incorporation sites on the true on-axis β-Ga2O3 (100) surface solely through thermal annealing, without introducing a macroscopic substrate miscut. As shown in Figure , thermally induced square-shaped void-like or island-like features form within the wide terraces and act as substeps. These substeps first provide a geometric benefit by redistributing step-edge sites within the terrace interior, thereby reducing the effective migration distance required for adatom incorporation. The representative step-edge density of ∼3.8 μm–1 observed in our sample provides a characteristic lateral scale of ∼0.26 μm for the distribution of local incorporation sites. This submicrometer scale is comparable to the island spacing observed in MOCVD-grown β-Ga2O3 (100) homoepitaxial layers, where submicrometer to several-micrometer separations suggest that adatom transport over these length scales is feasible under suitable growth conditions.

In addition to this geometric effect, the reconstructed substeps may also provide energetically favorable incorporation sites. The square-shaped geometry of the substeps introduces corner sites with kink-like character, where lower atomic coordination and a higher density of unsatisfied bonds may increase adatom binding energy and reduce the barrier for stable nucleus formation. , A further contribution may arise from the side facets of the substeps. Previous first-principles calculations suggest that (2̅01)-type side facets provide favorable bonding sites owing to their relatively high surface energy and high dangling-bond density. Although the side-facet orientation was not directly determined in the present work, the reconstructed sidewalls may expose related energetically favorable facets. Taken together, these geometric and energetic considerations suggest that thermally generated substeps can provide local incorporation sites within true on-axis β-Ga2O3 (100) terraces, distinct from conventional miscut approaches that rely primarily on macroscopic terrace-width reduction.

Proof of Concept for Nucleation-Accelerated Epitaxy

To investigate the impact of the morphological evolution observed in Figure and discussed in the broader context of Figure , we performed a comparative pulsed laser deposition (PLD) of Si-doped β-Ga2O3 thin films on as-cleaved and annealed (100) substrates, corresponding to the surfaces shown in Figure a,b, respectively. The as-cleaved substrate was used as a reference surface with large-area terraces and a low density of step-edge sites, while the annealed substrate represented the substep-reconstructed surface. Growth on the annealed substrate was first optimized by varying the PLD repetition rate, laser fluence, and number of pulses, and the optimized condition was then applied to the as-cleaved reference substrate for direct comparison.

Figure summarizes the data on the PLD growth optimization as a function of (a) repetition rate, (b) laser fluence, and (c) number of pulses, as well as benchmarking the progress in comparison with literature in panel (d). Notably, the growth temperature and oxygen partial pressure were maintained at 650 °C and 0.5 mTorr, respectively. Specifically, the effect of the repetition rate on the growth was investigated by varying the repetition rate from 5 to 20 Hz, keeping the rest of the growth conditions fixed (0.4 J cm–2, 5000 pulses), as shown in Figure a. The increasing trend in Figure a is consistent with expectations, resulting in the highest growth rate of 0.79 μm h–1 at 20 Hz, the upper limit of our equipment. It is worth noting that this value is already comparable to the maximum growth rate range reported in the literature, − making it a good starting point for further optimization. Indeed, having the repetition rate fixed at 20 Hz, the laser fluence was varied from 0.4 to 1.6 J cm–2, revealing an increasing trend as shown in Figure b. As a result, within the output range of our equipment, the maximum growth rate of ∼2.83 μm h–1 was achieved at a laser fluence of 1.6 J cm–2.

Summary of the PLD growth optimization results as a function of (a) repetition rate, (b) laser fluence, and (c) number of pulses. (d) Comparison of the maximum growth rate achieved in this work with representative literature values for β-Ga 2 O3 (100) homoepitaxy. The inset in panel (d) shows a cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of the 2.36 μm-thick film corresponding to 60,000 PLD pulses, confirming the film integrity and uniformity.

Further, the process was investigated as a function of the total number of PLD pulses, as shown in Figure c, with samples labeled S1, S2, and S3 corresponding to 10,000, 30,000, and 60,000 pulses and resulting in film thicknesses of 0.33, 1.09, and 2.36 μm, respectively. The growth rate remains fairly constant (∼2.83 μm h–1) with increasing pulse counts, indicating that the film thickness varies linearly as a function of the number of pulses. This linearity suggests that neither the ablation rate of the target nor the sticking probability of adatoms on the substrate surface changes during the process. Thus, the optimized process exhibits excellent reproducibility, maintaining a stable growth rate even for thicker films and demonstrating that nucleation-accelerated epitaxy of β-Ga2O3 is achieved using the substrates shown in Figure b.

To directly compare the optimized substep-reconstructed surface with the as-cleaved reference surface, an additional film was grown on an as-cleaved β-Ga2O3 (100) substrate under the same optimized PLD conditions (20 Hz, 1.6 J cm–2, 60,000 pulses). Consistent with previous reports, ,, growth on the as-cleaved substrate was slow and accompanied by pronounced three-dimensional island formation, as shown in Supporting Figure 3. Because of this highly nonuniform islanded morphology, a representative film thickness could not be reliably defined. Therefore, the apparent growth rate was conservatively estimated from the relatively continuous background region that covers most of the surface, excluding the locally protruding three-dimensional islands, yielding a value of approximately 0.58 μm h–1. This value is physically reasonable for a high-flux deposition process such as PLD, considering that a reported β-Ga2O3 PLD growth rate of 6 nm/min at 5 Hz corresponds to a 20 Hz-equivalent value of ∼1.4 μm h–1 if linear scaling with repetition rate is assumed. As a broader oxide-PLD reference, SrTiO3 homoepitaxy can reach 20 Hz-equivalent growth rates of ∼1–4 μm h–1 depending on the laser spot size and growth-per-pulse condition. , Thus, the apparent background growth rate of ∼0.58 μm h–1 on the as-cleaved β-Ga2O3 (100) substrate is modest for PLD and, together with its highly nonuniform islanded morphology, should not be regarded as a representative rate for uniform film formation. In contrast, the annealed, substep-reconstructed substrate achieved ∼2.83 μm h–1 with a comparatively uniform morphology, demonstrating a growth rate that is competitive even by oxide-PLD standards.

To this end, Figure d compares representative growth rate data achieved by MBE, HVPE, MOVPE, and PLD (this work). The apparent growth rate obtained on the as-cleaved β-Ga2O3 (100) substrate falls within the representative range reported in the literature, whereas the annealed, substep-reconstructed substrate shows a substantially higher growth rate of ∼2.83 μm h–1. Notably, the inset in Figure d shows a cross-sectional SEM image of the 2.36 μm-thick film (corresponding to 60,000 PLD pulses), grown for 50 min under the optimized conditions, confirming the film thickness and overall uniformity. This comparison indicates that the enhanced growth rate is closely associated with the thermally induced substep reconstruction.

Assessment of Films Quality

Figure summarizes the structural and electrical characterization of the homoepitaxial films grown on substeps-templated β-Ga2O3 (100) on-axis substrates. Specifically, Figure a displays the X-ray diffraction (XRD) 2θ–ω scans revealing identical (400), (600), and (800) diffraction peaks in the substrate and in the film, confirming homoepitaxy. The rocking curve of the film exhibits a full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of 0.146°, which is moderately broader than that of the substrate (0.079°) as shown in Figure b. This increase in FWHM indicates some broadening after film growth. However, considering that the in-house-grown substrate already exhibits a relatively broad rocking-curve FWHM, the film still retains reasonably good crystalline quality. ,,− To further verify the in-plane crystallographic alignment, φ-scans of the β-Ga2O3 (510) reflection were performed, as shown in Supporting Figure 4. The φ-scan exhibits the expected 2-fold symmetry, with the two diffraction peaks separated by 180°. No additional diffraction peaks were observed over the full 360° rotation, indicating that no secondary in-plane orientations were detected within the measurement resolution. Importantly, the reciprocal space map (RSM) in Figure c shows a single streak-like β-Ga2O3 (403) reflection without any distinguishable separation between the film and substrate peaks. Since the substrate peak serves as a reference for the unstrained lattice parameter, the complete overlap of the film and substrate reflections in Figure c is direct evidence of strain-free and coherently aligned homoepitaxial growth. −

Additionally, Figure d–f show AFM images of samples S1, S2, and S3 defined in Figure c, with film thicknesses of 0.33, 1.09, and 2.36 μm, respectively. Regardless of film thickness, all samples exhibit smooth surfaces. The thinner samples exhibit atomically smooth surfaces with a roughness below 0.3 nm, and even the thickest film (2.36 μm) maintains a surface roughness at only a few-nanometers, indicating that smooth surface morphology is maintained even at high growth rates.

To gain insight into the early stage of film growth, we examined the surface morphology of relatively thin films, as shown in Supporting Figure 5. The substrate step-terrace morphology remains visible at the film surface, indicating that the initial substrate morphology is transferred during the early stage of epitaxial growth. This supports the role of thermally generated substeps as additional step-edge sites for step-assisted nucleation and step-mediated adatom incorporation. As the film becomes thicker, the initial substep morphology becomes less distinguishable, reflecting thickness-dependent surface evolution rather than a transition to island-dominated growth.

Finally, Figure g plots the Hall effect measurements data for samples S1, S2, and S3. All samples exhibit consistent electron concentrations in the mid 1019 cm–3 range, as well as relatively high electron mobility, reaching 36.2 cm2 V–1 s–1, which is comparable to or even higher than the values typically reported for Si-doped β-Ga2O3 thin films. − The electrical resistivity, electron concentration, and Hall mobility values for each sample are summarized in Supporting Table 1. This consistency in electrical properties independent of the film thickness indicates that the Si dopants are effectively activated, supporting successful homoepitaxial growth without apparent surface segregation or compositional deviation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have demonstrated a miscut-free surface modification strategy for β-Ga2O3 (100) substrates based on high-temperature annealing. This approach induces the self-organized formation of void- or island-like substeps within wide terraces, increasing the step edge density while preserving the on-axis substrate orientation. Using these sub step templated substrate, we achieved growth rates exceeding previously reported values for on-axis β-Ga2O3 (100) homoepitaxy, while maintaining reasonably good crystalline quality and electronic properties. These results suggest that thermal annealing provides an alternative means of increasing the density of incorporation sites while retaining the intrinsic advantages of the β-Ga2O3 (100) surface, including its atomically flat morphology and low surface energy. Overall, this strategy provides a practical route to alleviate the growth limitations associated with the wide terraces of β-Ga2O3 (100), thereby enabling high-rate homoepitaxial growth without requiring intentional substrate miscut or alternative crystallographic orientations.

Experimental Section/Methods

Substrate Growth

Unintentionally doped (UID) (100)-oriented β-Ga2O3 single crystal substrates with an electron concentration in the low 1017 cm–3 range were grown using the zone-melting growth technique. The (100) substrates were subsequently cleaved from the bulk crystal, producing wide, atomically flat step-terrace surfaces. This surface served as the starting surface for the subsequent annealing treatment and homoepitaxial growth. The cleaved substrates were used for deposition without any postgrowth treatment.

Substrate Surface Sub Step Patterning

Thermal annealing was performed in a box-type muffle furnace (SH-FU-11MS1700, SH Scientific, Korea). To promote substep formation, the substrates were annealed in ambient air at 1100 °C for 6 h. The annealing conditions facilitated the thermodynamic reorganization of surface atoms, leading to the spontaneous formation of void- and island-like substeps within wide terraces.

Growth of Homoepitaxial Thin Film

Si-doped β-Ga2O3 thin films were deposited by pulsed laser deposition system (D1108-PSD6, Dada) on as-cleaved and substep-patterned substrates. A KrF excimer laser (COMPex pro 102F, Coherent, λ = 248 nm) was utilized with a repetition rate ranging from 5 to 20 Hz and a laser fluence within the range of 0.4 to 1.6 J cm–2. The target-to-substrate distance was fixed at 4 cm to ensure reproducibility. The base pressure was below 1.0 × 10–6 Torr, and growth was carried out under a working oxygen pressure of 0.5 mTorr and a substrate temperature of 650 °C. The PLD target was prepared by mixing 99.999% pure β-Ga2O3 powder with SiO2 powder to obtain a Si concentration of 0.5 atom %. The mixture was subsequently sintered in air at 1450 °C, yielding a single-phase ceramic target suitable for deposition.

Structural, Chemical, and Electrical Characterization

The thin films were characterized using the instruments available at the Quantum Matter Core Facility of Pusan National University. The crystallographic structure of the deposited films was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 DISCOVER diffractometer with Cu Kα1 radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), operated at 40 kV and 40 mA, employing 2θ-ω scans, rocking curve measurements and φ scans. The surface morphology and roughness were evaluated using atomic force microscopy (AFM, Park Systems NX7) operated in noncontact mode (NCM). The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the β-Ga2O3 films were examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, TESCAN MIRA LMS) equipped with an ELPHY quantum nanopattern lithography system at the Department of Physics, Pusan National University, operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV using a secondary electron (SE) detector. The film thickness was further verified by cross-correlating AFM surface images with SEM cross-sectional observations, ensuring accurate evaluation of the deposited layer thickness. The electrical properties were characterized by current–voltage (I–V) measurements using a microprobe station (MPS-PT, Nextron) to assess contact behavior and linearity. Hall effect measurements were conducted using a Teslatron PT system (Oxford Instruments) to extract carrier concentration, mobility, and resistivity. The electrodes for Hall measurements were fabricated in the form of a bilayer thin film consisting of titanium (50 nm) and gold (100 nm), sequentially deposited using a DC magnetron sputter coater (K575XD, Emitech).
