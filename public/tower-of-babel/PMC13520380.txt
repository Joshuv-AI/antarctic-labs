‘Put me off getting tested for years and years’: a qualitative study of gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men’s experiences of homophobia in primary healthcare in Aotearoa New Zealand
Authors: Thomas B. Swinburn, Mataroria P. Lyndon, Ruth Allen, Peter J. W. Saxton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (GBM) are disproportionately impacted by HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Recent biomedical innovations such as HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis, STI vaccines and testing offer new prevention tools. However, realising these benefits hinges on GBM disclosing their sexuality and receiving appropriate support from healthcare providers (HCPs). We aimed to understand GBM’s experiences of sexual healthcare in Aotearoa New Zealand.MethodsWe conducted an inductive thematic analysis of free-text responses to the Sex and Prevention of Transmission Study, a large and diverse national cross-sectional behavioural surveillance survey of GBM in 2022. Data from 422 participants reporting negative experiences when discussing their sexuality and sexual health with HCPs were examined.ResultsOverall, 19.1% reported a negative experience discussing their sexuality with an HCP. Six themes were derived from participants’ experiences. Negative reactions to GBM’s sexuality and sexual health needs centred around four themes: (1) judgement and prejudice, (2) disrespect, (3) discrimination, and (4) inexperience and skill deficits. Additionally, two themes related to: (5) participants’ views about how their HCP’s demographic characteristics (e.g. age, gender, religion, sexual orientation) might underpin their behaviour, and (6) consequences of GBM’s negative experiences (changing HCPs, forgoing sexual health services, suffering poor mental and physical health).ConclusionIn the era of biomedical HIV/STI prevention, GBM’s ability to communicate about their sexuality and sexual health will be a key antecedent to eliminating HIV and STI transmission. Strategies spanning health workforce training and targeted service expansion are needed to improve experiences of sexual healthcare services for GBM. Heterosexist environments that underpin judgement and prejudice, disrespect, discrimination and lack of skill must be addressed; cultural competence and safety must support diverse HCPs to improve delivery; and the consequences for those whose care is compromised by negative healthcare experiences must be mitigated.

Background

Globally, gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (GBM) are disproportionately impacted by HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has warned that increasing uptake of prevention and testing tools among key populations, including GBM, is critical to eliminating HIV transmission by 2030 [3]. Several effective biomedical HIV/STI prevention and testing options now exist, including HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) [4, 5], “Doxy-PEP” [6], and vaccination for gonorrhoea, human papillomavirus, and mpox [7–9]. However, stigma still surrounds same-sex sexuality and GBM’s sexual behaviours, meaning GBM often experience discrimination and must therefore manage how they present to others [10]. Realising the personal and public health benefits for HIV/STI prevention hinges on GBM having positive experiences with general practitioners (GPs) and other healthcare providers (HCPs) when discussing their sexual healthcare needs in clinical settings.

Disclosure of sexuality to HCPs is an important antecedent for GBM to access relevant sexual healthcare, with the balance of evidence pointing to an association between sexuality disclosure and increased HIV/STI prevention and testing interventions [11-14]. Yet, disclosure of sexuality to HCPs ranges from 8.6% to 87.8% depending on the population, clinical and national context [11, 12, 15-18]. In Aotearoa New Zealand (NZ), a country with a relatively progressive human rights reputation on Rainbow issues [19], only 62.1% of participants of a large, diverse national HIV behavioural surveillance survey in 2022 were ‘out’ to their GP (that is, they indicated that their doctor was aware that they were gay, bisexual, takatāpui [an indigenous Māori term for same-sex attracted individuals], pansexual, queer, or had sex with men), with several reporting negative experiences [20].

Negative experiences are concerning given access to vital HIV/STI prevention tools relies on trusting relationships between GBM and their HCPs [21, 22]. Often these interactions need to be frequent, such as PrEP guidelines which specify three-monthly clinical follow-up [23]. In NZ as in other countries, relationships between GBM and their GPs are particularly critical, as primary care has assumed a significant sexual health workload [24]. However, NZ data on the nature of negative healthcare experiences in the era of biomedical prevention are lacking. With PrEP uptake accelerating and a rapidly evolving landscape of pathogens and new biomedical prevention tools, GPs will be instrumental in delivering clinical HIV/STI interventions.

Understanding how negative experiences impact effective sexual healthcare is therefore critical. The aim of this paper is to examine the nature of GBM’s negative experiences of sexual healthcare, addressing the research question, “What negative experiences have GBM had discussing their sexuality and sexual health with HCPs?” We sought to achieve this aim by analysing free-text responses from a large and diverse national sample of GBM. We approached the research question by drawing on multiple positionalities, including insider sexual minority (GBM) expertise, Indigenous and ethnic minority status, clinical healthcare service delivery and medical training.

Methods

We examined data from the 2022 round of the Sex and Prevention of Transmission Study (SPOTS), a repeat, cross-sectional behavioural survey of GBM informed by WHO guidelines for second-generation HIV surveillance [25, 26]. The questionnaire consisted of 11 sections (demographics, sexual partnering, sexual and preventive behaviour with casual and regular male partners, HIV testing, STI screening, PrEP, healthcare experiences and vaccination, substance use, blood donation, attitudes and knowledge to HIV and safe sex, dried blood spot sampling). Participation was self-completed, online, anonymous, voluntary, and non-renumerated. Eligible participants were those aged 16 years or older living in NZ who were either GBM, or transgender or non-binary individuals who had had sex with GBM in the previous five years.

A multimodal campaign publicised the study across mainstream media channels, community organisations, strategic locations such as sexual health clinics and gay nightclubs, and online spaces including social networking sites and dating apps. Interested individuals were shown a participant information sheet and had to indicate consent before proceeding. The study received ethics approval from NZ’s Health and Disability Ethics Committee (HDEC 2021 11450). SPOTS was led by the University of Auckland in collaboration with the University of Otago, Burnett Foundation Aotearoa, Body Positive, Te Whāriki Takapou, and the New Zealand Blood Service. Further information on the study’s methods can be found in the overview report [25].

Nested in a section about sexuality disclosure and healthcare experiences, participants were asked, “Has a healthcare provider ever reacted negatively to things you’ve asked them about your sexuality or sexual health needs?”. If the response was “Yes”, they were asked, “What was the issue that triggered the negative reaction?”, followed by a freeform textbox. These responses comprise the data presented in this paper.

Data analysis

We analysed the data using a ‘general inductive approach’ to thematic analysis as outlined by David Thomas [27]. Thomas’s approach was selected for its suitability in analysing shorter pieces of text. The method follows an iterative, inductive process of preparing the data, familiarising with the text, coding to create categories, dealing with overlapping and uncoded text, and revising and refining the categories to produce a ‘model’ of the key categories [27].

The first author led the analysis, firstly reading over the responses repeatedly to deeply engage with the data. Guided by the research question, the process of inductive coding included making free annotations and highlights to generate coding labels and descriptions. Multiple codes were allocated to some responses and others were uncoded according to their perceived relevance. After 50 responses had been coded, the research team reviewed and discussed this subset. As the analysis continued, existing codes were assigned to the remainder of the data whilst generating and applying new codes to the entire dataset as distinct concepts were identified. We then used a manual mind-mapping process to synthesise the codes into themes and subthemes. Some codes that were too small to justify or too heterogeneous to describe were incorporated under other codes where appropriate. Finally, the team distilled and defined the themes and subthemes.

Results

Overall, 533 out of 2796 participants (19.1%) reported that an HCP had ever reacted negatively to their sexuality or sexual health needs. Of the subsample of participants reporting negative experiences, 37% were aged under 30 years old, 76% identified as European, 13% as Māori and 7% as an Asian ethnicity. Over half (58%) identified as gay, 8% as bisexual, and 35% reported another sexual, gender or cultural identity.

Of the 533 GBM reporting a negative experience, 458 participants (85.9%) responded to the subsequent free-text prompt, “What was the issue that triggered the negative reaction?” Thirty-six responses were uninterpretable, such as where a participant had written “NA” or left the question blank, leaving 422 valid responses. Entries ranged from straightforward one-word answers to more detailed descriptions of up to 50 words.

Six overarching themes were derived from participants’ experiences (Table 1). Negative reactions to GBM’s sexuality and sexual health needs centred around four themes: (1) judgement and prejudice, (2) disrespect, (3) discrimination, and (4) inexperience and skill deficits; within each of these themes, further subthemes were identified and are described. Additionally, two themes related to: (5) participants’ views about how their HCP’s demographic characteristics (e.g. age, gender, religion, sexual orientation) might underpin their behaviour, and (6) consequences of GBM’s negative experiences (e.g. changing GPs, forgoing sexual health services, suffering poor mental and physical health). Judgement and prejudice

Participants described judgement and prejudice spanning all aspects of their sexualities and sexual health needs. Subthemes included judgement, scorn, paternalism, pathologising, and prejudice.

Judgement

Participants frequently named HCPs as “judgemental” or described “being judged”. Reasons for the perceived judgement included “sexual behaviour that was not heteronormative”, “subtle slut shaming”, “unprotected sex”, “multiple partners”, and the “mere existence of [a] same-sex partner”. In terms of sexual health, participants felt judged for seeking an “STI check” or simply asking “sexual health related questions”, for “going on PrEP”, or for “getting chlamydia” or other STIs. At times, clinicians’ moralistic remarks were subtle and insidious.They asked me about how many partners I have had in the past three months and raised their eyebrow and said, ‘That’s a lot’. I laid a complaint.

They asked me about how many partners I have had in the past three months and raised their eyebrow and said, ‘That’s a lot’. I laid a complaint.

Scorn

Sometimes, judgement could be overtly scornful. For example, one participant presenting with an anal fissure described a doctor who “made negative assumptions and told me I was ‘doing that gay nonsense too much’”. Another described an HCP who “shamed me for being promiscuous”. In several instances, HCPs’ contempt related to participants’ decisions around condom use, sometimes in relation to seeking PrEP:Consultation at sexual health clinic to obtain PrEP, was sneered at by doctor when I answered that there was huge peer pressure these days not to use condoms. His answer ‘And that’s it? You won’t use condoms because of peer pressure?’

Consultation at sexual health clinic to obtain PrEP, was sneered at by doctor when I answered that there was huge peer pressure these days not to use condoms. His answer ‘And that’s it? You won’t use condoms because of peer pressure?’

As well as verbal remarks, scorn could be conveyed in other ways. For example, a participant recalled “discussing unprotected sex which I was honest about [and] she screwed her face up like she was about to gag”.

Paternalism

Numerous GBM reported judgemental remarks with paternalistic overtones, particularly during sexual health counselling. Participants were “told off for contracting gonorrhoea” and “lectured to about my choices”. One “asked for an STI test after a risk episode and was scolded by the doctor, even though it was a very normal sexual encounter and we hadn’t done anything unsafe for HIV”. Other clinicians gave sexual health advice that was unrealistic or belittling, such as advising participants about “abstaining” or “changing my lifestyle”. One participant “asked for an HIV test and was told I should consider making different life choices”.

Pathologising

A few participants described HCPs who made remarks that extended to pathologising GBM sexuality and sexual health, such as making medically misinformed links between homosexuality and disease. For example, one participant recalled a doctor saying “I was sick because I was homosexual”, while another was “told by [the] doctor that homosexuals have more neurotic tendencies”. While seeking PrEP, a participant was told that they were “a habitual user”, which made PrEP “feel like an illicit drug rather than prescribed medication”.

Prejudice

Prejudiced HCPs conflated stereotypes in the absence of complete information to draw negative conclusions, such as one who “assumed I had multiple partners because I was gay”. Others made assumptions that the cause of a participant’s symptoms was attributable to their sexuality or sexual behaviours, as exemplified by one participant who described that “at age 17 my former GP thought a rash in my throat was HIV because I was gay”. Likewise, another reported a prejudiced doctor made insensitive suggestions based on their sexuality:During a travel medicine consultation, a doctor suggested I take additional vaccinations/medications after hearing about my sexuality, assuming that I would be sleeping with local men (despite being told otherwise).

During a travel medicine consultation, a doctor suggested I take additional vaccinations/medications after hearing about my sexuality, assuming that I would be sleeping with local men (despite being told otherwise).

Several participants described other instances where, despite attending for something unrelated, HCPs initiated STI screening after learning inadvertently about their sexuality. Although these actions could be perceived as benign and proactive, they were nonetheless based on stereotypes.I went to a new GP and asked for a check on my ears. He added an HIV test to blood tests which was not related to the medical issue, once he heard I had a boyfriend.

I went to a new GP and asked for a check on my ears. He added an HIV test to blood tests which was not related to the medical issue, once he heard I had a boyfriend.

Disrespect

Many participants felt that their HCPs acted disrespectfully. Disrespect took various forms, including ridiculing comments, disrespectfully raising or responding to sexuality, indifferent and minimising attitudes, discomfort, and unprofessional conduct.

Ridicule

Multiple participants shared experiences of ridicule or misguided attempts at humour. Comments often involved disparaging references to clothing or appearance:It was as simple as having painted nails and they made an indirect derogatory joke about it.I was wearing boxers – the doctor said only gays wore these.

It was as simple as having painted nails and they made an indirect derogatory joke about it.

I was wearing boxers – the doctor said only gays wore these.

There were even insulting and mocking remarks made about participants’ genitals, such as one participant who reported an HCP saying “my testicles were small – do I like boys!”. Similarly, a nurse doing a sexual health check was reported as saying, “‘Oh god, this has seen better days’, referring to an anal swab”.

The inappropriate timing of a derisive comment could compound its disrespectful nature, as expressed by a participant whose HCP had “a laugh at me being gay, while they were diagnosing me with depression at a tough time in my life that required medication”.

Disrespectfully raising or reacting to sexuality

Sometimes, HCPs raised matters of sexuality and sexual health disrespectfully. Participants shared examples of being asked irrelevant or “invasive” questions, or partners being misgendered:My current GP struggles to acknowledge I am married to a man and is often asking about my ‘wife’. [The GP] is unable to communicate about my sexual health in a respectful manner.

My current GP struggles to acknowledge I am married to a man and is often asking about my ‘wife’. [The GP] is unable to communicate about my sexual health in a respectful manner.

Equally, participants described HCPs whose reactions to their sexuality showed “surprise that I am gay”, and responses to sexual health needs that lacked sensitivity:I raised the point of PrEP – to which they stopped all eye contact and were very standoffish, after shaking my hand on entering the doctor’s office, they refused to shake my hand on the way out.

I raised the point of PrEP – to which they stopped all eye contact and were very standoffish, after shaking my hand on entering the doctor’s office, they refused to shake my hand on the way out.

Indifferent and minimising attitudes

Disrespect was demonstrated perniciously by HCPs who were described as acting indifferently when providing care or who actively minimised participants’ experiences or needs. For instance, some HCPs referred to homosexuality as a “lifestyle”, and one participant encountered a GP who “downplayed an issue I had with anal fissures for several years. I had to seek a gay GP until I could get a referral to a specialist for this issue.”

Others appeared disinterested, exemplified by one participant who felt that their HCP “didn’t seem interested in my needs and flaked me off”. Participants stated that clinicians were “reluctant” to provide sexual health services. Of these, some HCPs reportedly used sexual health clinic referrals to disengage from care:Couldn’t prescribe PrEP and referred me to sexual health clinic rather than upskilling to ensure it’s provided in my small town. There is one person one hour’s drive away who will do this in [a rural region of NZ].

Couldn’t prescribe PrEP and referred me to sexual health clinic rather than upskilling to ensure it’s provided in my small town. There is one person one hour’s drive away who will do this in [a rural region of NZ].

Discomfort

Several participants perceived that their HCP was uncomfortable discussing sexuality and sexual health. Simply expressing one’s sexual orientation made some HCPs uncomfortable, as one participant described: “[I] almost felt that my sexuality made the older male doctor uncomfortable/awkward.” In other encounters, specific topics such as requesting a sexual health check or having open relationships triggered the discomfort:GP was uncomfortable talking about open relationship–seemed to have attitudes of a certain generation.

GP was uncomfortable talking about open relationship–seemed to have attitudes of a certain generation.

Unprofessional conduct

Some participants reported HCPs conducting themselves unprofessionally. For example, one HCP failed to ensure “proper privacy to discuss (curtain between the waiting room and myself after saying I felt uncomfortable and embarrassed)”, while another wilfully ignored the participant’s privacy by “telling other staff loudly” about them “having sex with men without condoms”. Others overstepped professional boundaries by making inappropriate comments, such as making “personal statements re his [religious] beliefs” when providing sexual health services.

Discrimination

Some GBM reported experiencing overt discrimination when accessing sexual healthcare. HCPs were discriminatory when their behaviour changed after learning about a participant’s sexuality, sexual health needs, or presumed HIV status. Many participants called out variations of “homophobia” or “homophobic comments”. For others, discrimination manifested as HCPs who refused to provide services, assaulted participants, or enacted HIV stigma.

Refusal to provide services

HCPs refusing to offer services was the most frequently mentioned form of discrimination, generally occurring in the context of PrEP. Participants described HCPs who “wouldn’t prescribe PrEP” or “didn’t want to be able to prescribe it”. One GBM described an encounter with a doctor who “refused to discuss or promote PrEP” and “suggested that I would need to change doctors if I wanted to discuss/seek PrEP”.

A smaller subset believed their HCP withheld relevant STI tests, illustrated by one participant who felt that their HCP was “not offering me the correct tests I know I needed”. In a few cases, HCPs ended the consultation or clinical relationship after learning about the participant’s sexuality in seemingly overtly discriminatory ways:Asking for advice regarding my secret gay life at the time. The doctor lost the plot completely and asked that I find another medical centre for future treatment.[My] previous GP was completely homophobic, and once I came out, refused to have me as a patient.

Asking for advice regarding my secret gay life at the time. The doctor lost the plot completely and asked that I find another medical centre for future treatment.

[My] previous GP was completely homophobic, and once I came out, refused to have me as a patient.

Assault

Two participants shared experiences of being physically assaulted after relaying information about their sexual health needs. On both occasions, the GBM experienced harm from an HCP using a needle while carrying out an otherwise legitimate clinical activity:A nurse assaulted me and seriously injured my arm when taking blood, after I told her I was there to renew my PrEP prescription. She forcefully jammed the needle into my arm and dug it around while drawing blood. I complained but no action was taken.

A nurse assaulted me and seriously injured my arm when taking blood, after I told her I was there to renew my PrEP prescription. She forcefully jammed the needle into my arm and dug it around while drawing blood. I complained but no action was taken.

HIV-related discrimination

One participant described being stigmatised by a clinician who presumed they had HIV because they had had counselling at a centre that provided wide-ranging support, including–but not limited to–people living with HIV.Because I mentioned I was receiving counselling at [specific named centre], they came to the conclusion that I had HIV and visibly recoiled from me. They tried to touch me as little as possible during the consult. It was literally their job to know better!

Because I mentioned I was receiving counselling at [specific named centre], they came to the conclusion that I had HIV and visibly recoiled from me. They tried to touch me as little as possible during the consult. It was literally their job to know better!

Inexperience and skill deficits

Many participants’ negative experiences seemed to relate to HCPs’ lack of skill and experience interacting with GBM. One participant summed this up aptly, saying “the gay sexual culture differs from heterosexuality and there’s a general misunderstanding or inability to relate to common gay sexual experiences.” Participants spoke of HCPs who were ignorant of their lives and needs, lacked adequate training–sometimes to the point of being medically misinformed–and used language unskilfully.

Ignorance

Numerous participants suggested that HCPs’ ignorance of their sexuality and sexual health needs led to a negative experience. Ignorance was mainly raised in the context of PrEP. Many HCPs “had never heard of PrEP” or “needed to be told what PrEP was”. Additionally, some HCPs misunderstood the nature of participant’s relationships. For example, one participant “discussing having a friend with benefits” was faced with an HCP who “couldn’t understand why I wouldn’t want to be in a relationship with them”. Ignorance sometimes overlapped with discomfort, as illustrated by one participant who “asked a student health GP for PrEP and he clearly had very little understanding about bisexuality and PrEP and was visibly uncomfortable”.

Inadequate training

Some participants attributed their negative experiences to HCPs’ inadequate training, such as “inexperience in how to prescribe PrEP”. Another said, “I live in a rural area and I feel like they don’t have much exposure to gay people and are even less aware of how important sexual health testing is.” Some GBM felt that HCPs’ lack of training or experience directly impacted the quality of care they received, such as one who described an HCP who “didn’t screen for sexual health risk and so wasn’t testing for sexual health”. Similarly, another HCP showed “poor handling of topics around sexual health”, leaving the participant feeling that they were given “no advice or discussion about preventive measures I could take etc”.

The role of training was, however, sometimes contested. Among two participants referring to issues for transgender healthcare users, one attributed their negative experience to an HCP’s “lack of education”. In contrast, the other said, “Of the dumb encounters with people who don’t understand trans issues, worst is the ones who consider themselves extremely knowledgeable because they went to some talk.”

Medical misinformation

A few GBM described HCPs who lacked knowledge to the point of being medically misinformed. Most accounts described HCPs who believed PrEP was for treating rather than preventing HIV, as outlined by one participant: “I saw an ophthalmologist … and told her I was on PrEP at the time. She had no idea what that was and asked me what my T-cell count was.” However, medical misinformation took other forms. One participant trying to access post-exposure prophylaxis encountered a hospital nurse who “claimed no such thing existed and suggested I review my sexual practice in the future”. Another described an HCP who “still believed that you can be HIV positive for years before it showed on an HIV test”. In some instances, participants had to assume the role of educator; for example, having to “constantly explain” that PrEP was an HIV prevention medication, while one GBM seeking PrEP was “sent to do my own research (which I’d already done)”.

Unskilled use of language

Unskilled use of language could hinder effective communication about sexuality and sexual health. Participants shared accounts of HCPs using insensitive or inaccurate language. Examples include HCPs who used “poor descriptors around queer sex” and had “poor choice of words in response to finding out about [their sexuality]”. For some, HCPs’ ignorance of GBM-relevant language revealed larger knowledge gaps in participants’ lives:It was a kōrero [conversation] around having children and her misunderstanding of what queer life can look like, especially when they don’t know what the word queer means.

It was a kōrero [conversation] around having children and her misunderstanding of what queer life can look like, especially when they don’t know what the word queer means.

GBM’s negative experiences and HCPs’ demographic characteristics

Several participants associated their negative experiences with a demographic characteristic of their HCP. Participants referred explicitly to features such as age, gender, ethnicity, or religion; for example, HCPs who were “older and seemed pretty uncomfortable talking about sexual things” or were “of a particular race and religious group who thought I shouldn’t be on [PrEP]”. Two stated that their HCP was a “conservative Christian”. Sometimes, negative experiences were attributed to a combination of traits, such as one participant who encountered “just the typical self-imposed religious Pākehā [NZ European] being homophobic”.

A few mentioned their HCP’s heterosexual orientation, such as one participant who “asked about PrEP and they (cis straight man) didn’t know anything about it”, and another who described a “female straight GP [who] did not respond well to the fact that I had an open relationship, and was very uncomfortable in her handling my issue at the time”. Conversely, two participants specifically stated their HCP’s sexual orientation as a “gay man”, appearing to suggest that even HCPs with lived experience as GBM could act in negative, internalised homophobic ways:I was judged/mocked by a sexual health nurse (a gay man) not long after I came out for expressing support for monogamy in the relationship I was in at the time.

I was judged/mocked by a sexual health nurse (a gay man) not long after I came out for expressing support for monogamy in the relationship I was in at the time.

Consequences of HCPs’ negative reactions

Some participants elaborated on the impact of their negative experiences, including forgoing necessary sexual health services, suffering poor mental and physical health and changing or avoiding GPs.

HCPs’ negative reactions led to participants forgoing relevant prevention and testing, such as one participant who sought a sexual health check-up after “unsafe sex” and found the “doctor was dismissive and judgemental… [and I] never got the test or PrEP”. For another, their HCP’s ignorance left them with no options to access PrEP in their community:Asking for a PrEP prescription and the practitioner had no clue what it was, hence why I haven’t been on PrEP in the last 12 months as the town I currently live in can’t prescribe it, even the sexual health clinic!

Asking for a PrEP prescription and the practitioner had no clue what it was, hence why I haven’t been on PrEP in the last 12 months as the town I currently live in can’t prescribe it, even the sexual health clinic!

Negative experiences were detrimental to participants’ mental health and self-esteem, such as where a participant reported, “They made me feel dirty and guilty and it negatively affected my mental health.” Concerningly, this led to some stating that they avoided health services entirely, potentially compromising their wellbeing:Had an issue with tight foreskin which was causing me mental and physical pain, my usual GP (male) was not available so was seen by a female GP who proceeded to laugh at my situation which destroyed me mentally and I have never been back to a GP since.

Had an issue with tight foreskin which was causing me mental and physical pain, my usual GP (male) was not available so was seen by a female GP who proceeded to laugh at my situation which destroyed me mentally and I have never been back to a GP since.

Those whose HCPs acted (or failed to act) on prejudiced conclusions experienced delayed diagnoses and serious complications of medical conditions that were unrelated to STIs:I received a delayed diagnosis with an autoimmune inflammatory arthritis for five years. Every time I saw my GP with joint pain and they found out I was gay I was sent to the [sexual health] clinic rather than a rheumatologist.Throat infection, [Dr] assumed gonorrhoea. Actually ended up in ICU, could have been picked up sooner if he didn’t assume it was an STI, despite advising I was in a monogamous relationship.

I received a delayed diagnosis with an autoimmune inflammatory arthritis for five years. Every time I saw my GP with joint pain and they found out I was gay I was sent to the [sexual health] clinic rather than a rheumatologist.

Common responses included laying complaints and changing HCPs, including looking “specifically for an LGBTQIA+ doctor to go to instead”. This could be beneficial, as shown by one participant who “found a grounded more accommodating doctor”.

Discussion

Gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men in our study reported 422 negative healthcare experiences in relation to their sexuality or sexual health needs. They encountered clinicians who were judgemental and prejudiced, disrespectful, discriminatory, inexperienced and lacking skill. Following such encounters, some GBM tried to make sense of the behaviour as being linked to the demographic characteristics of the HCP. For many, these experiences had negative personal impacts, including delaying access to prevention and testing.

These accounts are a concern for equitable access to healthcare, system quality and safety, healthcare delivery, and the health workforce. Excellent healthcare services (including, for example, preventative PrEP delivery) require person-centred care and access, within supportive environments provided by evidence-informed policies, systems and workforce [28]. Participants’ experiences showed all these functions were compromised. In the following discussion, we refer to relevant research about homophobic environments underpinning discrimination and prejudice; issues with ignorance and inexperience in healthcare that compromise PrEP delivery in particular; and reflections on how culture at all levels of sexual healthcare may affect the ways healthcare professionals behave. Recommendations are included in each section.

Homophobic environments underpin discrimination and non-disclosure

As our participants demonstrated, their heightened vulnerability to HIV/STIs occurred within a homophobic environment. Other research has shown that within health services, GBM and other sexual minorities experience multiple inequities and barriers, compounded by stigma and discrimination [10, 29]. A social-ecological model usefully articulates the overlying social and structural determinants of HIV control, including where homophobic attitudes, public policy, and laws continue to shape the epidemic’s global trajectory [30]. These determinants can negatively shape individual interactions with HCPs—including an unwillingness to disclose sexuality—leading to suboptimal healthcare [30], as our participants experienced.

Such culturally unsafe experiences of judgement and prejudice, discrimination and disrespect provide insight as to why some patients do not raise issues relevant to their sexuality or sexual health. A UK literature review identified that fear of discrimination, negative reactions from HCPs, and feelings of embarrassment and humiliation were reasons LGBT people do not disclose their sexuality [22]. Local studies have shown that young Rainbow people both anticipate discrimination and then experience it in primary care and mental health services [31, 32]. Concerningly, some SPOTS participants likewise had experienced discrimination. Experts believe that homophobic prejudice from previous healthcare encounters can lead to an unwillingness to disclose to one’s current healthcare provider [33]; such prejudice was another common experience for GBM in this study. Although SPOTS was not expressly designed to examine the relationship between negative experiences and GBM’s disclosure and comfort, these findings nonetheless mirror the wider literature that shows how negative healthcare experiences can negatively affect sexual orientation disclosure (and thus reduce uptake of effective prevention tools such as PrEP and STI screening).

Our findings contribute to the growing literature on the suboptimal state of Rainbow health in many countries. For example, Australian researchers examined the role that received and anticipated stigma had on healthcare experiences of younger and rural GBM [34]. In other NZ studies, primary care staff expressed feelings of discomfort, awkwardness, and a lack of knowledge and training when providing healthcare to gender and sexual minority youth [35], and a study of NZ gay men found that most pursued a “strategy of minimal disclosure”, sometimes due to anticipating negative responses from HCPs [36, 37].

Improve disclosure safely

Healthcare environments need to be safe, inclusive places where people feel able to disclose their sexuality and discuss their sexual health needs, especially in the context of HIV testing and prevention [28, 38]. This is particularly important for GP settings, given that most GBM, like the general population, go to GPs first for most health issues [36, 39]. The literature suggests several ways to facilitate disclosure. Routinely asking about sexual identity, normalising sexual health, and assuring confidentiality are commonly cited [22, 38, 40, 41]. In clinical spaces, iconography and resources that affirm Rainbow communities, such as Rainbow symbols and information brochures, convey cues about the safety of these environments [22, 34, 42]. These materials should use inclusive, gender-neutral language and, where appropriate, be tailored specifically to GBM [22, 42]. Critically, the behaviour of all staff in the healthcare setting needs to be congruent with these signals [11, 22, 31]. Finally, clinicians who are Rainbow, Rainbow-friendly, or experienced in Rainbow health could consider actively promoting their services [36, 42].

Dedicated services

Some participants sought out Rainbow HCPs after their negative experiences and may feel more comfortable disclosing their identities and accessing prevention and testing in dedicated services. For example, a qualitative US study evaluating an LGBTQ-specific health service found that some LGBTQ people were more willing to disclose in these settings [43]. However, they also found that those who were satisfied with their current HCP were less likely to seek out the dedicated service [43], suggesting that Rainbow-specific services may fill a gap primarily for those who are not comfortable accessing mainstream healthcare.

In NZ, a 2015 study of GBM showed that almost half had attended their GP for STI screening [44]. As in other countries, sexual health delivery in primary care is complemented by publicly funded sexual health services and peer-based community organisations. While some experts have contested the current model of sexual healthcare [24], GPs are currently essential in providing such care. More research is needed on which service-users access dedicated services [45], and how developing innovative, alternative access points to sexual healthcare may be warranted.

Inexperience and ignorance compromise preventative healthcare measures like PrEP

Survey participants encountered clinicians who were inexperienced and unskilled, highlighting the need for workforce training. These experiences triangulate with studies of HCPs from NZ, Ireland, and the UK, which identified ignorance and inexperience as barriers to effectively engaging with Rainbow patients [46, 47]. PrEP was consistently raised as an area of deficit by our participants, and international studies have shown PrEP awareness among GPs and other HCPs remains low [48–50].

The inexperienced and unskilled nature of HCPs as exemplified by knowledge gaps in PrEP likely speaks to the lack of priority placed on Rainbow health in clinical education, despite one in 20 patients likely belonging to a Rainbow identity [51]. In a study of primary care staff, clinicians expressed a desire to improve their competence in delivering healthcare to Rainbow young people [35]; our research shows that needs to be extended across all ages.

Cultural competence

Health workforce education should train clinicians to deliver culturally competent sexual healthcare. Cultural competence emphasises a clinician’s awareness of practices, language and norms of particular communities so that they can deliver healthcare that respects these cultural differences [52]. For example, a culturally competent HCP would use accurate and sensitive language when discussing sexual practices during a PrEP consultation. Foundational cultural competence training should be included in undergraduate clinical curricula. Non-government organisations have produced learning modules about PrEP, STI management, and GBM culture that is tailored for GPs and counts towards their continuing professional development [53]. These educational resources therefore need to be promoted more widely and proactively to the primary care workforce.

Culture at all levels of sexual healthcare

Most HCPs would not expect (nor want) a sizeable minority of their patients to feel judged, disrespected, and subject to discrimination and prejudice by them, nor to feel that, as clinicians, they were practising with a demonstrable ignorance and lack of skill. Relating these outcomes to their own demographic characteristics, and exploring the negative outcomes longer term for those seeking healthcare who were let down, calls for HCPs to reflect deeply on what needs to change—but also for the systems within which they work to be examined. There are healthcare service delivery impacts of concern if one in five GBM wanting to engage with effective preventative health measures like PrEP and STI testing face negative experiences and barriers to doing so, especially as new tools continue to develop. In current US implementation studies of innovative long-acting PrEP, having trust in an HCP to engage in ‘non-judgmental conversations about sex and life experiences’ was critical to engagement with effective HIV prevention [56]. Furthermore, a Canadian study concluded that HCPs needed to recognise how their own underlying heteronormative values shaped the care they provided to LGBTQ+ people [21]. The cultural competence and cultural safety recommendations above would also foster such recognition.

Considered through a regulatory lens, some of our participants’ reports of HCP conduct meet the Medical Council’s definition of unprofessional behaviour and even sexual impropriety [57]. In a legal context, their accounts mirror breaches of NZ’s Code of Health and Disability Services Consumers’ Rights, as demonstrated by a verdict in December 2023 [58]. The totality of this evidence builds a picture in which Rainbow people in NZ continue to experience subpar, culturally unsafe healthcare across a range of health services. The naming of GBM as a priority population in the HIV Action Plan must ensure that training and service delivery interventions lead to real improvements for GBM and other Rainbow communities across all aspects of health [59].

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. SPOTS was the largest-ever HIV behavioural surveillance survey in NZ, providing critical data to inform epidemic management. The diverse sample of survey participants in terms of age, ethnicity, residence, and sexual identity reflected the research team’s extensive community consultation and marketing. These efforts ensured SPOTS was relevant and accessible for a broad range of members of a hard-to-sample population in healthcare services research [26]. The qualitative analysis was conducted with the supervision of both subject matter and methodological experts. The majority (85.9%) of the 533 participants who reported a negative experience provided open-text responses, suggesting these negatives experiences left an enduring impression.

Our study also has several limitations. SPOTS uses non-random convenience sampling so data may not be generalisable to all GBM. The survey was only available in English, meaning the findings may not represent the healthcare experiences of GBM with limited English literacy, such as migrant GBM. Survey completion required access to the internet, privacy and a device (e.g. smartphone, computer), which may have prevented participation by GBM without access to these. The nature of the survey and questions did not allow for responses to be probed or clarified, and participants generally left short accounts of their experiences. Our findings may therefore be best geared towards identifying the broad nature of GBM’s negative interactions rather than providing in-depth accounts of individuals’ experiences.

Future directions

The findings reported in this paper are topical, given the WHO’s call to increase uptake of biomedical prevention and screening to meet the 2030 goals. Giving voice to the negative healthcare experiences and barriers to care of almost 20% of the sample, distilled into rigorously analysed themes, clearly calls for action.

Whilst the negative experiences described in this paper show that improvement is needed, it is encouraging that most SPOTS participants did not report a negative experience when interacting with their HCP about sexual health. In future, it would be valuable to understand participants’ positive experiences discussing sexuality and sexual health with HCPs. Exploring positive outcomes could help us better understand how their values, non-discriminatory behaviour, training and experience might be developed in other settings.

Conclusions

In the era of biomedical HIV/STI prevention, many GBM continue to face barriers to safe and effective sexual healthcare. Strategies spanning health workforce training and targeted service expansion are needed to improve experiences of sexual healthcare services. The homophobic environment in public healthcare services that underpins judgement and prejudice, disrespect, discrimination and lack of skill must be addressed; cultural competence and safety must support diverse HCPs to do better; and the consequences for those whose care is compromised by negative healthcare experiences must be mitigated.
