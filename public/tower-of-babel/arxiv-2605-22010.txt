Abstract

Introduction

Feature Learning in Shallow NNs:

The defining characteristic of neural networks is their ability to automatically learn useful representations out of high-dimensional data, which can then be transferred to downstream tasks. The simplest instance is given by one hidden-layer neural networks, which construct function approximations
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form

f(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma(w_{i}^{\top}x)\penalty\ ,\penalty\ w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\in\mathcal{S},

(1.1)

where
\sigma
is a non-linear activation function, and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

While (
1.1
) is an idealized model, far from the bleeding edge of modern architectures, its non-asymptotic learning guarantees under gradient descent remain largely open. As expected, the difficulty comes from the non-convexity of the loss in this model:

\mathcal{L}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\mathcal{D}}[(f(x)-y)^{2}]
.
Thanks to the permutation symmetry of neurons in (
1.1
), the gradient-flow training dynamics admit an Eulerian description in terms of the empirical measure
\hat{\rho}^{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{w_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
. In
[
13
,
35
,
43
,
44
]
, the resulting dynamics are shown to be a Wasserstein Gradient Flow (WGF) for the associated functional

\mathcal{L}(\rho):=\mathbb{E}_{x}[(f_{\rho}(x)-y)^{2}]
, where
f_{\rho}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\rho}[\sigma(w^{\top}x)]
. By leveraging the convexity of this objective in
\rho
, global asymptotic convergence to minimizers was established under appropriate conditions
[
13
,
40
]
in the
mean-field
over-parametrized limit
m\to\infty
.
1
1

1

We remark that these results are qualitatively different from the overparameterized neural tangent kernel (NTK) limit
[
28
,
22
,
4
,
53
]
, where global convergence is also attained, but there is no feature learning.
More recently, quantitative
\operatorname{poly}(1/\epsilon)
local convergence rates were attained for mean-field ReLU networks, under assumptions on the smoothness of the measure minimizing the loss
[
14
]
.

The key question is then to understand
under which conditions can one bring these mean-field convergence guarantees to a finite-width network
, bypassing known negative results (eg
[
25
]
). This naturally raises the question of stability of the above WGF dynamics to particle discretization (i.e. finite width), which is the main focus of this work.

Propagation-of-Chaos, Coupling, and Gronwall’s Inequality:

Given a probability measure
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
from which the neurons are initialized, the Monte-Carlo iid discretization
\hat{\rho}^{m}_{0}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\leq m}\delta_{w_{i}}
, with
w_{i}\sim\rho_{0}
, satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\rho}^{m}_{0}\sim\rho_{0}^{\otimes m}}\|f_{\hat{\rho}^{m}_{0}}-f_{\rho_{0}}\|^{2}\lesssim 1/m
, where here
\|f\|^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}(f(x)^{2})
.
A key question — known as the Propagation-of-Chaos (PoC)
[
48
]
— is to understand how the initial ‘chaos’ or particle independence evolves under the WGF dynamics. That is, letting
\hat{\rho}^{m}_{t}
denote the empirical distribution of the
m
particles at training time
t
, and
\rho_{t}^{\textsc{MF}}
the mean-field distribution at time
t
, we seek to understand how close the joint law of any
k
-tuple of particles from
\hat{\rho}^{m}_{t}
is to
{\rho_{t}^{\textsc{MF}}}^{\otimes k}
. More relevant to the neural network setting is the notion of
weak
PoC,
which only focuses on the convergence of certain observables. In our case, we consider evolution of the function error
\mathcal{E}(\rho_{t}^{\textsc{MF}},\hat{\rho}^{m}_{t}):=\|f_{\hat{\rho}^{m}_{t}}-f_{\rho_{t}^{\textsc{MF}}}\|^{2}
as a function of
t
and
m
.

A standard approach to establishing PoC is to consider an appropriate coupling
between the mean-field and the empirical evolution
[
35
,
34
,
19
]
.
Specifically, we can decompose the error
\mathcal{E}(\rho_{t}^{\textsc{MF}},\hat{\rho}^{m}_{t})\leq 2\mathcal{E}(\rho_{t}^{\textsc{MF}},\bar{\rho}^{m}_{t})+2\mathcal{E}(\bar{\rho}^{m}_{t},\hat{\rho}^{m}_{t})
in terms of an auxiliary model
\bar{\rho}^{m}_{t}
which
first
evolves along the mean-field dynamics (starting from
\rho_{0}
) and
then
discretizes using an
m
-particle iid empirical measure. As a result, the first term is at the Monte-Carlo scale
O(1/m)
, while the second term captures the commutation error between sampling a measure and evolving it along the WGF dynamics. This commutation error can then be controlled by coupling the dynamics of
\hat{\rho}^{m}_{t}
and
\bar{\rho}^{m}_{t}
, which we describe in detail in Section
2.2
.
Grönwall’s inequality is then used to establish a bound of the form
\mathcal{E}(\bar{\rho}^{m}_{t},\hat{\rho}^{m}_{t})\leq\exp(Lt)O(\text{poly}(d)/m)
, where
L
is a uniform Lipschitz bound of the gradient.

Towards uniform-in-time estimates

Because of the exponential dependence on
t
, the direct Grönwall estimate is inherently limited to short time-horizons,
and leaves open the question of obtaining PoC guarantees for longer timescales. Longer timescales are important for several reasons. First, high dimensional problems often exhibit long “burn-in” times to escape saddles
[
8
,
18
,
2
,
9
]
. Second, convergence to small thresholds
\epsilon
may require time polynomial in
1/\epsilon
, e.g. in problems with flat landscapes near the optimum
[
6
]
or when the minimizing measure lies on a continuous manifold
[
5
,
14
]
. The latter question of small-
\epsilon
convergence is the main motivation of this work.

A common strategy to attain PoC at longer timescales is to add noise in the dynamics, leading to the so-called Mean-Field Langevin dynamics
[
27
,
38
,
15
]
. The diffusion term creates a contraction in the Wasserstein metric, quantified via a uniform logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI), leading to
uniform-in-time propagation of chaos

[
10
,
46
,
30
,
39
]
. However, the LSI assumption often transfers the exponential dependency to the runtime
[
47
,
52
,
37
,
49
]
.

In this work, we take an alternative route towards obtaining uniform weak PoC guarantees. Our approach builds from
[
24
]
, which developed a dedicated PoC analysis beginning
with an ODE describing the evolution of the fluctuation
\Delta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
(see (
2.7
)) which tracks the differences between the
m
coupled neurons in
\hat{\rho}^{m}_{t}
and
\bar{\rho}^{m}_{t}
:

\frac{d}{dt}\Delta_{t}=D_{t}\odot\Delta_{t}-H_{t}\Delta_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}+O(\|\Delta_{t}\|^{2}),

(
\star
)

Here
D_{t}(\cdot)
is a non-interacting diagonal term that amplifies fluctuations whenever a neuron is visiting non-convex regions of the landscape, and
H_{t}
is a PSD interaction kernel that dissipates fluctuations across the neurons (see Defn.
1
). Finally, the source term
\varepsilon_{t}
‘pumps’ error at the Monte-Carlo scale
O(1/\sqrt{m})
.
The focus of
[
24
]
was on obtaining non-asymptotic bounds on
\|\Delta_{t}\|
for problems with long burn-in times that improved upon the Grönwall estimate, specifically targeting systems where the required timescale to achieve small error is
O(\text{poly}(d))
, e.g. single-index models (SIMs) with large information exponent.

In this work, under a suitable decay of the loss in the mean field system, we attain PoC guarantees that hold uniformly in time. This allows us to transfer mean-field convergence guarantees at arbitrary convergence thresholds
\epsilon
to the finite-width setting. In Lemma
2

, the short-time Grönwall bound
\exp(Lt)O(\text{poly}(d)/m)
can be extended to a
uniform-in-

bound. We state an informal version of this main result below.

.

Suppose

and Assumption

\displaystyle\|f_{\hat{\rho}^{m}_{t}}-f_{\rho_{t}^{\textsc{MF}}}\|^{2}\lesssim\begin{cases}\exp(2C_{\text{reg}}S)\frac{\operatorname{poly}(C_{\text{reg}}d)}{m^{1/3}}&\text{always}\penalty\ ,\\

(1.2)

A key implication of this theorem is that whenever the mean-field dynamics has a convergence rate faster than
1/t^{2}
(possibly after a burn-in time of order

), we can attain a loss of
\epsilon
in a network with only
\text{poly}(d)/\epsilon

Our proof of PoC can be viewed as a stability analysis of the mean-field dynamics, and thus our uniform-in-time result can also be extended to other sources of discretization error, beyond just finite neurons. Under the same assumptions, the formal version of this theorem gives a uniform-in-time bound that decays polynomially in the smallest of the width
m
, the number of training samples
n
, and in
\eta^{-1}
, the learning rate. Thus whenever the mean-field convergence rate of gradient flow on the population loss is faster than
1/t^{2}
, we can attain
\epsilon
loss with
\text{poly}(d)/\epsilon
neurons, training samples, and gradient descent steps. Finally, we empirically verify our convergence rate assumption on several synthetic examples in Section

Remark 1
.

In the case that the ground truth
f^{*}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\mathcal{D}}[y|x]

\rho^{*}

Related Work

General Uniform-in-Time Propagation-of-Chaos:

There is a rich and developing literature on uniform-in-time propagation of chaos in general interacting particle systems.
[
20
]
establish
O(1/m)
weak PoC uniformly in time for weakly interacting diffusions under regularity assumptions.
[
31
]
obtain sharp uniform-in-time PoC rates for interacting diffusions with convex potentials and small torus interactions, while
[
36
]
derive time-uniform log-Sobolev inequalities as a tool for uniform propagation of chaos, including sharp marginal estimates in smooth cases; see also
[
21
,
26
]
. These results all rely on contraction mechanisms, where the noise in the dynamics plays an instrumental role. In contrast, our results exploit a different structural property (the decay of the energy functional), enabling uniform-in-time guarantees in the determinisitc setting.

Mean-Field Langevin dynamics:

[
15
,
27
,
38
]
studied the effect of adding a diffusive term in the Wasserstein Gradient Flow dynamics arising from Shallow NNs; in particular, by leveraging the aforementioned log-sobolev contraction tools,
[
10
]
prove uniform-in-time propagation of chaos for mean-field Langevin dynamics under functional convexity, with bounds in Wasserstein and relative entropy.
[
45
]
prove a quantitative weak propagation-of-chaos result for mean-field gradient Langevin dynamics, with
O(1/m)
finite-particle discretization error uniformly over time, explicitly motivated by infinite-width two-layer neural networks; see also
[
52
,
37
,
49
,
39
]
.

Most of this work on uniform-in-time PoC exists in settings where there is a unique and stable invariant measure, and the local convergence to this measure is exponentially fast. However, there are several rotatable exceptions: in
[
20
]
, weak PoC is shown for the super-critical Kumamoto model which has continuum of invariant measures,
and
[
42
]
attain a uniform-in-time PoC guarantee in a singular-interaction setting where the mean-field object converges at an inversely polynomial rate. We emphasize that in the context of shallow NN feature learning, one typically needs to anneal the diffusive dynamics, leading to exponential runtime
[
49
,

]
.

Setting and Preliminaries

2.1
Projected Gradient Dynamics on Neural Networks

Consider a neural network to be parameterized by some distribution
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
, such that

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\rho}\sigma(x;w),

for some activation function
\sigma
. We will be primarily interested in the case where
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

A supervised regression problem is parameterized by an initial distribution for the network weights,
\rho_{0}
, and a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over datapoints
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
. Given
(\rho_{0},\mathcal{D})
, we define
f^{*}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[y|x]
.
We will train the neural network to minimize the excess squared loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\rho):=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}(f_{\rho}(x)-y)^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}(f^{*}(x)-y)^{2}\penalty\ .

(2.1)

We study the (projected) gradient flow dynamics of
\rho
induced by moving each particle
w\sim\rho
in the (negative) direction of the gradient of the loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)
, and then optionally projecting the particle back to
\mathcal{S}
. Let
P_{w}
denote the orthogonal projection on the tangent space
T_{w}\mathcal{S}
. Thus when
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
P_{w}=I-ww^{\top}
; when
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have
P_{w}=I
.

In our two settings of interest, we have the dynamics
\frac{d}{dt}w=P_{w}\nu_{\mathcal{D}}(w,\rho)
, where:

\displaystyle\nu_{\mathcal{D}}(w,\rho):=\nabla_{w}F_{\mathcal{D}}(w)-\nabla_{w}\mathbb{E}_{w^{\prime}\sim\rho}K_{\mathcal{D}}(w,w^{\prime}),

(2.2)

and

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{D}}(w):=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}y\sigma(x;w)\qquad\text{and}\qquad K_{\mathcal{D}}(w,w^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\sigma(x;w)\sigma(x;w^{\prime}).

(2.3)

When the data distribution
\mathcal{D}
is clear from context, we will often abbreviate and drop the
\mathcal{D}
subscript on
\nu_{\mathcal{D}}(w,\rho)
,
K_{\mathcal{D}}
,
F_{\mathcal{D}}
. Further, whenever an expectation over
x
appears in this paper without explicit distribution, it should be interpreted being drawn from the
x

\mathcal{D}
.

2.3

Description of the Dynamics of
\Delta_{t}

As described in (

⋆

‣
1
),
[
24
, Lemma 5]
gave a first-order approximation to the ODE describing the dynamics of
\Delta_{t}
, with a source term on the scale
1/\sqrt{m}
. In Lemma
2
below, we obtain a refinement of this result, which improves over
[
24
, Lemma 5]
in two ways. First, under appropriate regularity assumptions, we extend beyond the spherical setting they studied and to the case where
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Second, we refine the source term by separating it into two terms: (1) a term on the scale
1/\sqrt{m}
that becomes constant convergence, and (2) a smaller source term on the scale
1/m
. This refinement of the source term is key to attaining the
1/m
rate in our uniform-in-time guarantee.

Before stating Lemma
2
, we review from
[
24
]
the two key quantities governing the dynamics of
\Delta_{t}
: a self-interaction term, and an interaction term. The self-interaction term is described by what we call the local Hessian, the derivative of a particle’s velocity with respect to that particle’s position. The part of the dynamics driven by the other

Lemma 3

For all

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Delta_{t}=D_{t}\odot\Delta_{t}-H_{t}\Delta_{t}+\bm{e}_{t},

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{t}\|\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{\text{reg}}S}}\right)\int_{s=0}^{t}\|\bm{e}_{s}\|ds\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{\text{reg}}S}}\right)t\epsilon_{m}\approx\frac{t}{\sqrt{m}}.

. However, our refined dynamics in Lemma

Conclusions and Future Work

The Mean-field Wasserstein gradient flow dynamics offer one of the clearest analytic windows into the training of overparameterized shallow neural networks, but their practical relevance ultimately depends on how accurately finite-width networks track this continuum limit. In this work we
studied the long-time behavior of fluctuations
introduced by several forms of discretization of these idealized dynamics, such as finite neurons or data samples. Our main contribution is a novel uniform-in-time PoC bound that, focusing on function error, exploits the rate of convergence of the mean-field dynamics, overcoming the short-time barriers of standard Grönwall estimates. As a takeaway, whenever
\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{t}}dt=O(\log d)
, one can attain population loss
\epsilon
using
\text{poly}(d)/\epsilon
resources, including neurons, training samples and gradient steps.

Our result thus reinforces the powerful role of mean-field descriptions in providing novel algorithmic guarantees.
While applicable on a variety of idealized learning scenarios, our work leaves several interesting avenues for future research.
Two concrete questions are to understand (i) whether other functionals, besides the loss functional, enjoy the same uniform-in-time PoC under our same assumptions, and (ii) the necessity of our assumption on the MF convergence rate — by either weakening it to slower rates
\mathcal{L}_{t}\lesssim t^{-c}
,
c<2
, or else by finding a counter-example.
In that respect, as exhibited by empirical scaling laws
[
29
,
7
]
, many tasks do not enjoy such fast convergence rates
\mathcal{L}_{t}\lesssim t^{-2}
, and further, many problems exhibit dimension-dependent burn-in times to escape saddles. Both of these cases require novel technical tools to avoid exponential dependencies.
Thinking bigger, another tantalizing question is whether our tools can be extended to other NN models where mean-field formulations have proven useful, such as Resnets

Lemma 17

(Covering Numbers)

(x,y)

\displaystyle\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\epsilon,\mathcal{\bar{\mathcal{G}}},L_{2}(P)}}\right)}}\right)\leq\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Cd\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\kappa_{x}Cd/\epsilon}}\right)}}\right),

where

in the net with

is

-subguassian, then with probability

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\bar{\mathcal{G}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}g(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}g(x)}}\right|\leq\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C\kappa_{x}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{Cd\log^{2}(d/\delta)}{n}}}}\right)..

Proof.

First we prove that
\mathcal{G}:=\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
is a VC subgraph of VC dimension
\Theta(d)
. It suffices to bound the VC dimension of the subgraph class

\operatorname{subgraph}(\mathcal{G}):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}:t<g(x):g\in\mathcal{G}}}\right\}.

Fix points
((x_{i},y_{i}),t_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\dots,n
. For fixed
parameters
(u,w,s)
, the label of
(x_{i},t_{i})
is determined by whether

t_{i}<y_{i}(u^{\top}x_{i})\mathbf{1}\{w^{\top}x_{i}\geq s\}.

which occurs if and only if

\bigl(w^{\top}x_{i}-s\geq 0\ \text{and}\ y_{i}u^{\top}x_{i}-t_{i}>0\bigr)\quad\text{or}\quad\bigl(w^{\top}x_{i}-s<0\ \text{and}\ t_{i}<0\bigr).

Thus, once the sample points are fixed, the induced labeling is determined by
the signs of the
2n
affine functions

P_{i}(u,w,s):=w^{\top}x_{i}-s,\qquad Q_{i}(u,w,s):=u^{\top}y_{i}x_{i}-t_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

These are affine functions of the
2d+1
real parameters
(u,w,s)
. Since
relaxing the constraint
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
to
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
can only increase

possible labelings of the
n
fixed points is at most

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{8en}{2d+1}}}\right)^{2d+1}

provided
2n\geq 2d+1
; the remaining case is trivial. If the
n
points were shattered, then all
2^{n}
labelings would be realized.
Therefore

2^{n}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{8en}{2d+1}}}\right)^{2d+1}.

Writing
k=2d+1
and

2^{r}\leq 8er
.
The latter inequality fails for all
r\geq 7.5
. Hence
n<7.5(2d+1)\leq 24d

. Thus no set of more than
24d
points can be shattered, and so

\operatorname{VCsubgraph}(\mathcal{G})\leq 24d

\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d\log(\kappa_{x}Cd/\epsilon)}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\|\nu(\hat{\xi}_{t}(w_{i}),\hat{\rho}^{m}_{t})-\nu(\hat{\xi}_{t_{\eta}}(w_{i}),\hat{\rho}_{t_{\eta}}^{m})\|\leq 2C_{\text{reg}}(\eta\kappa_{t}C_{\text{reg}}+(\eta\kappa_{t}C_{\text{reg}})^{2})\leq\kappa_{t}^{2}\epsilon_{\eta}.

(A.95)

Fifth term:
\nu(\hat{\xi}_{t_{\eta}}(w_{i}),\hat{\rho}_{t_{\eta}}^{m})-\nu_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}(\hat{\xi}_{t_{\eta}}(w_{i}),\hat{\rho}_{t_{\eta}}^{m})

By Lemma
16
, we have with high probability:

\displaystyle\|\nu(\hat{\xi}_{t_{\eta}}(w_{i}),\hat{\rho}_{t_{\eta}}^{m})-\nu_{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}(\hat{\xi}_{t_{\eta}}(w_{i}),\hat{\rho}_{t_{\eta}}^{m})\|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{t_{\eta}}\epsilon_{n}.

(A.96)

Final result.

Putting together Equations (
A.77
), (
A.83
), (
A.91
), (
A.95
), and (
A.96
) or their spherically corrected counterparts, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Delta_{t}(i)

\displaystyle=D_{t}(i)\Delta_{t}(i)-\mathbb{E}_{j\sim[m],j\neq i}H_{t}(i,j)\Delta_{t}(j)+P_{\xi_{t}(w_{i})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}_{j\sim[m]}\nabla K_{t}(i,j)-\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\rho_{0}}\nabla K_{t}(w_{i},w)}}\right)+{\bm{\epsilon}},

(A.97)

where

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\epsilon}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{n}+\kappa_{t}^{2}\epsilon_{\eta}+\kappa_{t}\epsilon_{m}\|\Delta_{t}(i)\|+C_{\text{reg}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1.5\|\Delta_{t}(i)\|^{2}+\|\Delta_{t}(i)\|\mathbb{E}_{j}\|\Delta_{t}(j)\|+1.5\mathbb{E}_{j}\|\Delta_{t}(j)\|^{2}}}\right)

(A.98)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{n}+\kappa_{t}^{2}\epsilon_{\eta}+\kappa_{t}\epsilon_{m}\log(t+1)\|\Delta_{t}(i)\|+2C_{\text{reg}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\Delta_{t}(i)\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{j}\|\Delta_{t}(j)\|^{2}}}\right).

(A.99)

Using the alternative concentration approach to the first term in the decomposition, we also have the simpler result:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Delta_{t}(i)

\displaystyle=D_{t}(i)\Delta_{t}(i)-\mathbb{E}_{j\sim[m],j\neq i}H_{t}(i,j)\Delta_{t}(j)+{\bm{\epsilon}},

(A.100)

with

\displaystyle\|{\bm{\epsilon}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{n}+\kappa_{t}^{2}\epsilon_{\eta}+2\kappa_{t}\log(t+1)\epsilon_{m}+2C_{\text{reg}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\Delta_{t}(i)\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{j}\|\Delta_{j}\|^{2}}}\right).

(A.101)

For
\gamma\in\{1,2,4,8\}

n=1024
data points
(x_{i},f^{\gamma}(x_{i}))
. For the best approximation of the population loss, when
d=2

x_{i}
evenly spaced around
\mathbb{S}^{1}
; otherwise we choose the
x_{i}
randomly on
\sqrt{d-1}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

[-1,1]^{d}
. We use a step size of
\eta=0.1
and a width of
m=32768
to approximate the mean field gradient flow dynamics on the population loss. We chose these values of
n,m,\eta
because with twice as much granularity (choosing
n,m
to be two times larger, or
\eta
to be two times smaller), the results were very similar.

We plot the results for
d=2
and
d=128
.

For
x\in[-1,1]^{d}
, let

\displaystyle f^{*}(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}^{\infty}\hat{f}_{k}\cos(k\arccos(x_{1}))

(B.1)

Define
f^{\gamma}
by

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\gamma}_{k}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}&k=0\\
\frac{1}{4}&k\pm 1\\
\frac{1}{2}(k/\sqrt{2})^{-(\gamma+2.5)}&|k|>1,\operatorname{even}\end{cases}

(B.2)

21

Observation 21

(Representation on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
)
.

Let
d=2
, and let
\theta(x)=\arccos(x_{1})
. Let
F(\theta)=\sum_{k}\widehat{f}_{k}\cos(k\theta)
, and assume that
\widehat{f}_{k}=0
for all odd
k>1
, and that for some

\eta>0
,

|\widehat{f}_{k}|\leq Ck^{-\gamma-2.5-\eta}\qquad\text{for all even }k\geq 2.

Then there exists a finite signed measure
\rho^{*}
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
whose
density has bounded
\gamma
-Sobolev norm such that

F(\arccos(x_{1}))=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\operatorname{ReLU}(\langle x,w\rangle)\,d\rho^{*}(w).

Proof.

Let
\phi(t):=\operatorname{ReLU}(\cos t)
. We use the complex Fourier convention

h(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{h}_{k}e^{ikt},\qquad\widehat{h}_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(t)e^{-ikt}\,dt.

The Fourier coefficients of
\phi
are

\widehat{\phi}_{k}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\pi},&k=0,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{4},&k=\pm 1,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{(-1)^{m-1}}{\pi(4m^{2}-1)},&k=\pm 2m,\ m\geq 1,\\[6.0pt]
0,&|k|>1\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

Let
\mu
be a finite signed measure on
[0,2\pi)
, and define

g_{\mu}(\theta):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\phi(\theta-\omega)\,d\mu(\omega).

Writing
\widehat{\mu}_{k}:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-ik\omega}\,d\mu(\omega)
, we have

\phi(\theta-\omega)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\phi}_{k}e^{ik\theta}e^{-ik\omega}.

Therefore

g_{\mu}(\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\phi}_{k}\widehat{\mu}_{k}e^{ik\theta}.

Thus the Fourier coefficients of
g_{\mu}
are
\widehat{g}_{\mu,k}=\widehat{\phi}_{k}\widehat{\mu}_{k}
.

Now we will construct
\rho^{*}
to be the distribution of
(\cos(\omega),\sin(\omega))
for
\omega\sim\mu^{*}
for some measure
\mu^{*}
on
[0,2\pi)
. Define the Fourier coefficients of
\mu^{*}
by

\widehat{\mu}^{*}_{k}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\widehat{f}_{k}}{\widehat{\phi}_{k}}&\widehat{\phi}_{k}\neq 0\\
0&\widehat{\phi}_{k}=0.\end{cases}

By the assumed decay,

|\widehat{\mu}^{*}_{\pm k}|\lesssim k^{-\gamma-0.5-\eta}.

Therefore

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+k^{2})^{\gamma}|\widehat{\mu}^{*}_{k}|^{2}<\infty.

Hence
\mu^{*}
has bounded
\gamma
-Sobolev norm. Now

g_{\mu^{*}}(\theta)=F(\theta),

Thus with
x=(\cos(\theta(x)),\sin(\theta(x)))
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\operatorname{ReLU}(\langle x,w\rangle)d\rho^{*}(w)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mu^{*}}\operatorname{ReLU}(\cos(\theta(x)-w))=g_{\mu^{*}}(\theta(x))=F(\theta(x)).

(B.3)

But since cosine is an even function
F(\theta(x))=F(-\theta(x))
, so
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\operatorname{ReLU}(\langle x,w\rangle)d\rho^{*}(w)=F(\arccos(x_{1}))
.
∎
