Diabetes Priorities Questionnaire (DPQ): a codesigned preclinic priority setting tool to enable patient-centred care for people with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Sumathy Ravi, Julie Ayre, Jaybee Serrano, Glen Maberly, Kirsten McCaffery, Carissa Bonner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo codesign a preclinic Diabetes Priorities Questionnaire (DPQ) tool for people with type 2 diabetes and healthcare professionals to identify patient needs in advance and enable efficient, patient-centred consultations for diabetes management.DesignParticipatory approach using codesign focus groups to explore perspectives and develop solutions.SettingThis study was conducted in Western Sydney, Australia, an area with high socioeconomic disadvantage and cultural diversity.Participants10 adults with type 2 diabetes and 8 healthcare providers including specialists, diabetes educators, general practitioners, dietitians and pharmacists were recruited from a public hospital-based outpatient diabetes service and community-based providers in Western Sydney.MethodsThe codesign process was employed to iteratively develop and refine the DPQ. Focus groups were conducted in a hybrid format including in person and online, using semistructured discussions on four key topics: (1) understanding the purpose of the tool, (2) usability of the tool, (3) perceived benefits of completing the tool and (4) asking the right questions (content). Participants were invited to review and provide feedback on the initial set of questions, with the aim of identifying key challenges and potential solutions to enhance the content and uptake of the tool. Feedback was collected through audio recordings and field notes. These insights informed successive revisions of the DPQ following each round of focus groups.ResultsFive focus groups involving 18 participants identified opportunities to improve the initial questions by restructuring their order, simplifying the content and incorporating concerns about diabetes complications, including mental health. Participants highlighted the importance of holistic care and the need for both digital and paper-based formats to enhance accessibility.ConclusionsThe final DPQ was digitised within a patient care portal and integrated with tailored diabetes educational content. By enabling preconsultation screening, the codesigned DPQ supports multidisciplinary teams in delivering patient-centred care. Further research is needed to assess the tool’s impact on clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction and healthcare efficiency.Keywords: Diabetes Mellitus, Type 2; Delivery of Health Care, Integrated; Digital Technology; Patient-Centered Care; Community-Based Participatory Research

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a rapidly increasing chronic metabolic disorder, currently affecting 537 million individuals and projected to affect 783 million by 2045 worldwide.1 Diabetes often remains asymptomatic or unrecognised for a long period of time, but it is estimated to claim 3.4 million lives globally annually.2 Affected individuals face twice the risk of serious complications including heart failure, kidney disease, stroke, heart attack, amputation and blindness compared with healthy individuals.3 This rising burden strains healthcare systems, making it increasingly challenging to optimise diabetes care using traditional and fragmented models of care.4

Over the past decade, integrated care has emerged as a key strategy to reduce fragmented healthcare by advocating coordinated multidisciplinary care that holistically addresses a person’s health needs across physical, mental and social health needs.5 Central to this approach is patient-centred care (PCC) that necessitates more effective engagement and collaboration with patients and their support networks to develop care plans focusing on individual preferences, values and goals.6 Especially, PCC plays a crucial role in the self-management of type 2 diabetes, where personalised support can significantly improve outcomes. Evidence shows that integrated, people-centred care provides more effective management of type 2 diabetes and multiple comorbidities by aligning services around the needs of the individual.7–9

Clinician understanding of and timely access to each patient’s priorities, concerns and clinical information before a consultation can support PCC in diabetes care. Preclinic priority setting tools have been proposed as a practical solution for gathering and organising patient-reported information ahead of the consultation, allowing clinicians to focus attention on discussing and responding to what matters most to the patient.10 Such tools are distinct from validated outcome measurement instruments, such as health-related quality of life,11 12 the Problem Areas in Diabetes scale (PAID)13 and the Diabetes Distress Scale (DDS),14 which are designed to assess clinical endpoints rather than to facilitate agenda-setting. These questionnaires can be lengthy and time-consuming, and may be impractical for routine clinical use, increasing burden on patients15 and reducing data quality and response rates.16 17

In Western Sydney, a preclinic questionnaire was introduced as part of a broader model of care that sought to address fragmented care through multidisciplinary engagement and digital solutions.18 However, the uptake of this initial preclinic questionnaire was very low19 indicating a need for a co-designed tool. In addition, advances in digital delivery of healthcare offered an opportunity to change the format for the questionnaire. Digitally administered preclinic tools may reduce administrative burden, enable real-time data integration across care settings and support consistent communication between patients and providers.20

This study therefore aimed to codesign a more efficient and useful preclinic priority setting tool with patients and healthcare professionals, to improve engagement and uptake. The tool was created to enable patients to raise their current concerns and education needs, as well as confirming critical clinical information for health professionals. It was designed as a conversation starter to bridge information gaps and support more collaborative, person-centred care in type 2 diabetes management.

Methods

Integrated care model

The initial preclinic priority setting tool was embedded within an existing integrated care model for type 2 diabetes in Western Sydney, Australia. This model leverages digital technologies including Continuous Glucose Monitoring, virtual multidisciplinary diabetes case conferencing, and more than 180 short educational plain-language videos. The goal of this model of care (figure 1) is to enhance coordination between primary and specialist care and to empower general practitioners (GPs) with the tools and knowledge to manage diabetes more effectively.18 As part of this model, a preclinic priority setting tool had been developed to capture individual patient concerns and clinical information ahead of each consultation. However, uptake of the tool was very low. The original tool consisted of 47 questions across 5 printed pages (see online supplemental appendix 1). It incorporated validated patient-reported outcome measures, including items from the PAID-20 scale13 and the DDS,14 alongside additional questions to verify current diabetes management status, information that was frequently absent from patient records and referral letters. This original tool served as the starting point for the codesign process described below.

Study design

This study used a codesign participatory framework21 to develop and improve a preclinic priority setting tool, exploring its acceptability with patients and healthcare providers through iterative focus groups. This involved five rounds of structured discussions with diabetes services staff, community health providers, and people with lived experience. Separate focus groups were conducted for patients and providers.

Sample and setting

Western Sydney is one of Australia’s most ethnically and linguistically diverse regions, with a large proportion of residents born overseas and significant representation from South Asian, East Asian, Middle Eastern, Pacific Islander, Aboriginal and Torres Strait communities.22 The area has higher rates of diabetes-related complications and socioeconomic disadvantage compared with metropolitan averages,23 making it a particularly relevant setting for developing accessible, person-centred diabetes care tools. WSD delivers a hybrid model of integrated care (figure 1) addressing the complex needs of type 2 diabetes patients. For the patient group, adult individuals with type 2 diabetes who were able to speak, read and write in English were eligible. Patients referred to WSD complex diabetes clinic for consultation were invited to participate by the research team. A researcher (SR) met these patients in the clinic’s waiting room, provided participant information sheet, consent form and recruitment flyers, and explained the study and codesign process. For the healthcare professionals’ group, the WSD specialist team, including endocrinologists, diabetes educators, dietitians and nurses, were invited by the research team to participate. Community healthcare providers including GPs and allied health professionals who had been referring patients to the complex diabetes outpatient service were also invited. All participants provided written informed consent to participate and received a gift card in compensation for their travel costs and time.

Codesign procedure

Three rounds focus groups were conducted iteratively, alternating between patient and healthcare provider groups, with findings from each round informing the next. Each focus group consisted of three to five participants and lasted approximately 2 hours. Sessions were held in a meeting room at Blacktown Hospital campus, Western Sydney Local Health District, with online participation via Zoom or MS Teams available for those participants unable to attend in person. Three focus groups were held with patients with type 2 diabetes, and two focus groups were held with healthcare professionals: GPs, endocrinologists, diabetes specialist nurses, dietitians and pharmacists.

The discussions were facilitated by SR, supported by JS and moderated by CB and JA using a semistructured discussion guide (see online supplemental appendix 2). At the start of each session, participants were provided with an overview of the study, the WSD model of care and the purpose of the preclinic priority setting tool. In round 1 (FG1 for patients and FG2 for HCPs), participants reviewed the PAID-20, DDS and the paper format of the original Diabetes Priorities Questionnaire (DPQ), discussing the relevance, clarity and burden of each question item. Round 2 (FG3 for patients) focused on refining question content and structure based on round 1 feedback, with participants reviewing a reduced draft by identifying missing or irrelevant items. The revised paper version of the tool was reviewed with attention to layout, language and overall usability. Round 3 (FG4 for patients and FG5 for HCPs) presented the near-final digital version, allowing participants to interact with the tool, to provide feedback on digital usability and format, with participants indicating whether outstanding concerns had been resolved. Major changes about the content (eg, questions or response options) were no longer suggested during this round.

From focus groups 4 and 5 onwards, the tool was administered digitally via GoShare platform—a web portal that has been used for hosting the educational bundles for WSD. The digital format was designed to be accessible on both desktop and mobile devices and was structured to present one question at a time to reduce cognitive burden. Usability of the digital tool was evaluated within the focus group discussions using a set of structured prompts addressing ease of navigation, clarity of instructions, accessibility of language and the overall experience of completing the tool digitally.

Data collection

Data collection included audio-recordings and transcripts from all focus groups, along with field notes documenting all suggestions for improvement (recorded by CB, JA and SR). After each round, feedback was collated and a list of potential changes was generated by the research team. The tool was updated with changes that were feasible to implement, considered important by both patients and providers and/or reduced participant burden.

Data analysis

The codesign discussions included four key topics: (1) understanding the purpose of the tool, (2) usability of the tool, (3) perceived benefits of completing the tool and (4) asking the right questions (content). Specific issues and suggestions in field notes were grouped into categories and transcript excerpts were selected to illustrate key themes. Data were analysed and managed using Microsoft Word and Microsoft Excel. Data from each focus group were discussed by the research team and used to update the tool for the following round of focus group until no major changes were suggested by either patient or provider groups. The initial and final versions of the tool are provided as supplemental files.

Public and patient involvement

The codesign focus groups involved people with lived experience or type 2 diabetes and their carer, whose perspectives were used for the redesign and implementation of the DPQ. All participants provided informed written consent prior to their participation.

Key issues and solutions

Key issues and solutions raised in the discussions are summarised under the categories such as purpose and benefits, usability and content. Table 3 provides specific examples with illustrative participant quotes.

Purpose and benefits

A key benefit of the tool was its capacity to encourage patients to reflect on their self-management, health concerns and priorities. Patients found it valuable to use a structured tool that not only facilitated their understanding of diabetes but also helped them prepare for specialist consultations. Based on participant suggestions, prompts were added to the digital version to provide patients with a choice to request educational videos on key diabetes topics (eg, managing hypoglycaemia). Providers saw the tool’s purpose as streamlining processes so that the consultation could be focused on addressing key needs rather than going over medical record data that was already in the system. They raised the importance of ensuring the tool reduced time burden rather than increasing it, through integration with clinical systems and workflows, and by reducing duplication of information in medical records.

Usability

The initial focus groups highlighted the need for a simple and intuitive tool format. In response to participant feedback, the tool transitioned from a paper-based format in focus groups 1–3 to a digital platform in focus groups 4–5. This transition allowed for a more streamlined experience, incorporating features such as dynamic question skipping and conditional question display, thereby tailoring the tool to individual responses. Some participants commented on the additional opportunity of integrating tailored educational videos to meet individual patient needs. Health professionals perceived this as empowering patients to personalise their learning experience, making the tool not only a data collection instrument but also a resource for patient education and support. Healthcare providers also suggested incorporating a preregistered account system to simplify the user experience. Accounts could be created in advance for each patient by the healthcare team, rather than asking them to sign up themselves. This would reduce participation barriers and enable only authorised patients to access the tool through the unique link sent to them.

Concerns about survey fatigue were acknowledged, particularly given the number of questionnaires patients are often asked to complete. To address this, the codesign process focused on identifying and removing redundant or non-essential questions, especially those that could be sourced from medical records (eg, questions about pathology results were removed). Patients described difficulty recalling or interpreting such information from their medical documents and providers perceived that reliance on patient memory for medical details could compromise response rate and accuracy.

In the later workshops where the digital format was delivered by SMS, participants expressed concerns that the initial text message and tool introduction lacked clear sender identification, making it appear as spam and potentially resulting in low uptake. To build trust and authenticity, participants suggested the message begin with the name of the diabetes service, that is, ‘Hello from Western Sydney Diabetes’, and to include the name and logo to the introduction of the tool. Participants recommended automated reminder messages to prompt timely completion before the appointments, as well as QR code access points in waiting areas like pharmacies and clinics, making it convenient for patients to complete the tool while waiting.

Content

Content was optimised by reducing the total number of questions, removing less relevant questions, revising general questions and adding targeted questions about mental health. For example, general questions about health professionals were reworded to reference diabetes specifically (eg, ‘Do you have a GP you would like us to talk to about your diabetes?’). The transition to a digital format enabled patients to skip non-mandatory questions and receive follow-up questions only where relevant based on previous responses. Many patients talked about experiencing mental health challenges but reported not raising these in clinical settings about diabetes management. Some patients were hesitant to bring up emotional or psychological struggles unless prompted. To address this, the tool incorporated open-ended questions about mental health, as well as relevant questions from validated scales.

Patients highlighted the need to simplify complex medical questions, particularly about hypoglycaemia and medications, which many patients found confusing or difficult to answer accurately. For example, questions about managing hypoglycaemia were flagged as relevant for those in their early stages of diagnosis. Similarly, instead of presenting a complicated matrix of medication types or insulin regimens, a more intuitive approach was recommended by providing a simplified, selectable list of both commonly used medications and injectable treatments. This would make it easier for patients to identify what they were taking without the need to recall or search for the drug names.

Health literacy was raised by providers as a key challenge, particularly around medication identification. Some patients had limited understanding of diabetes terminology and medications, often referring to their treatments by colour, shape, or timing rather than by name, which could contribute to medication errors especially when seeing different providers. Further, one patient was unaware that insulin is used to treat type 2 diabetes, while others did not know about severe complications such as risk of amputation. To address this, medical jargon was simplified throughout the tool, such as ‘diabetes in pregnancy’ instead of gestational diabetes, ‘low sugar’ instead of ‘hypo’, ‘diabetes specialist’ instead of ‘endocrinologist’, ‘heart specialist’ instead of ‘cardiologist’ and ‘kidney specialist’ for nephrologist. Other suggestions included providing pictures of common medications to help recognition, language translations and artificial intelligence (AI) supported voice calls, but these were not feasible to implement within the scope of the current study.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study used an iterative codesign approach to develop the DPQ, a preclinic priority setting tool that integrates clinical, educational and emotional needs. Patients and healthcare professionals collaborated across multiple phases to refine the tool, to clarify the purpose, improve usability and ensure benefits by addressing both patient and provider needs with the right questions. Changes included focusing on simplicity, language clarity and relevance to real-world practice. The DPQ can be used as a structured prompt for discussion between people living with type 2 diabetes and their healthcare providers. The tool is designed to identify key patient needs and serve as a conversation starter in consultations, enabling PCC including discussion of sensitive mental health issues between patients and providers. It aims to support a holistic and proactive approach for personalised and timely diabetes care, with the aim of improving patient outcomes and provider satisfaction.

Within the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour framework,24 which identifies Capability, Opportunity and Motivation as the key drivers of Bhaviour, the DPQ may assist health services to overcome capability and opportunity barriers to PCC. The tool enables patient capability by helping them express their needs and concerns more clearly to health professionals. It also creates a structured opportunity for conversation about key needs during clinical encounters, addressing social barriers for patients (eg, stigma or perception that mental health cannot be raised in a diabetes consultation) and time constraints for health professionals (eg, by identifying current needs before the consultation, enabling more time for shared decision making about the key issue).

Comparison to prior research

Compared with longer and more complex questionnaires,25 the DPQ offers a simplified and user-friendly format to increase feasibility and relevance for routine clinical diabetes care. Distinctively, the DPQ combines clinical priority-setting, emotional well-being screening and patient education needs within a single tool. This integration is not commonly seen in existing diabetes questionnaires, which tend to focus on a single aspect such as distress measurement or quality of life assessment. The digital format with conditional logic further differentiates the DPQ by reducing question burden and tailoring the experience to the individual, rather than presenting a fixed set of items to all patients regardless of relevance.

Other studies have shown that while integrating user-friendly digital tools can enhance patient engagement, they must be carefully designed for the specific context to meet diverse needs.26 The codesign method used in this study demonstrates a practical and effective approach to achieving this, which could be replicated in other health settings. Co-designing health tools with patients and providers has been shown to improve acceptability, relevance and uptake across a range of chronic disease settings, including cardiovascular disease, mental health and cancer care.27 However, evidence of co-designed preclinic priority setting tools specifically in diabetes care remains limited, highlighting the novelty of this work and its potential contribution to the field.

Broader research shows that emotional concerns often go unaddressed in consultations,28 leading to unmet needs. The DPQ was perceived by both patients and clinicians as a practical solution for early identification and proactive care.29 Diabetes distress and burnout are common, affecting up to 36% of people with type 2 diabetes.30 The ongoing demand coupled with fear of complications are linked to poorer glycaemic control and inadequate engagement in self-care,31 yet they are rarely screened for in routine practice due to various barriers.32

Practical implications

The DPQ offers a scalable solution for improving the quality and efficiency of diabetes care by ensuring that patient priorities, including mental health, are systematically addressed at every consultation. It is now being used in clinical practice to enable more targeted and meaningful diabetes consultations, reducing time spent on data collection during visits and enabling more personalised care. It promotes proactive identification of complications and emotional distress, potentially preventing downstream health issues. The initial evaluation has shown increased uptake and completion of the tool,33 and 1-year outcomes will be reported in a future paper.

Designed for digital delivery, the DPQ has the potential to be implemented beyond its current setting, including public and private diabetes services and community health settings where digital infrastructure is available. The tool’s format and principles could also be adopted for use in other countries, particularly those with similar integrated care models and high diabetes burden. However, successful implementation in different health systems would require consideration of local factors including digital infrastructure capacity, health literacy levels, cultural attitudes toward self-reporting of emotional concerns and available languages. Future integration of the tool with electronic referral systems and medical records is essential to minimise duplication and streamline clinical workflows. By enabling seamless data sharing across platforms, such integration can reduce time burden on clinicians, support more efficient decision-making and ensure that patient information is consistently updated across systems. Adaptation for other chronic conditions, where similar barriers to person-centred care exist, is also worth exploring in future research.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is its robust codesign methodology, involving both healthcare providers and patients to ensure the tool supports PCC and proactive care for diabetes. The tool’s simplicity and digital format allow for efficient preconsultation preparation and adaptable integration into any digitally enabled health system. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study involved a small number of participants across focus groups and the sample including 7 males out of 10 patient participants may not reflect the broader population of people living with type 2 diabetes. Future research should seek larger and representative samples. The tool is currently available in English only, which may restrict accessibility for people with limited English proficiency, a significant issue given Australia’s high rates of low health literacy.34 These limitations might have influenced recruitment, lead to biases in identifying problems and solutions related to the design and use of DPQ, potentially excluding specific perspectives and needs unaddressed from those of underrepresented populations. Translation into different languages could address these gaps and improve equitable access.35 Future research could also explore uptake and use of the tool in clinical practice and examine which specific parts may be most influential in supporting self-management behaviour change and improved outcomes.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates how a codesign process led to the development of a more acceptable preclinic priority setting tool for diabetes consultations, using digital formats to reduce burden on both patients and providers. The resulting DPQ is a scalable solution to support integrated person-centred diabetes care and has the potential to be adopted across different outpatient diabetes services and primary care settings within digitally enabled health systems. With appropriate adaptation for language, culture and local workflows, it could also be applied to the management of other chronic conditions where fragmented care and unmet patient priorities remain a challenge.
