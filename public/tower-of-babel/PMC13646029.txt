Dietary management and feeding considerations of pediatric aerodigestive and upper gastrointestinal motility disorders
Authors: Khalil El‐Chammas, Kirsten B Jones, Joan Arvedson, Mary Boruta, Erick Toro‐Monjaraz, Anita Sicolo, Anil Darbari, Rachel Rosen, Julie Khlevner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePediatric aerodigestive and upper gastrointestinal (UGI) motility disorders are common and may be associated with compromised nutritional status and decreased quality of life. Traditional dietary recommendations for aerodigestive and UGI motility disorders have been rudimentary and largely based upon experience and common sense rather than scientific evidence.MethodsThis position paper on the role of dietary therapy in pediatric aerodigestive and UGI motility disorders was developed by an expert group from the North American Society of Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (NASPGHAN) Neurogastroenterology and Motility Committee that performed an evidence‐based search of the literature on pediatric aerodigestive and UGI motility disorders. The literature was summarized and expert recommendations developed. The authorship met to discuss the evidence and statements. A consensus of at least 70% was required to approve a recommendation. Areas requiring further research were identified.ResultsThe literature on nutrition in pediatric aerodigestive and UGI motility disorders is limited. These patients benefit from interdisciplinary collaboration involving dietitians for ongoing assessment for growth and nutritional deficiencies as a result of their condition.ConclusionThis position paper provides the first set of expert recommendations on dietary considerations for pediatric patients with aerodigestive and UGI motility disorders. Further research is needed to bridge the gaps in knowledge as identified in this paper.Keywords: esophageal dysmotility, gastric dysmotility, oropharyngeal dysphagia

INTRODUCTION

Pediatric aerodigestive and upper gastrointestinal (UGI) motility disorders are common and may be associated with compromised nutritional status and decreased quality of life. 1 One of the primary goals in the treatment of these disorders is to maintain an optimal nutritional status as well as support the patients' nutritional needs and growth while managing symptoms and preventing lung damage. Dietary intervention is an important non‐pharmacologic treatment of aerodigestive and UGI motility disorders. A position paper on the role of dietary therapy in pediatric aerodigestive and UGI motility disorders will be a valuable resource for clinicians and patients and provide evidence‐based recommendations, grounded in current literature and expert opinion, aimed at enhancing treatment strategies and optimizing patient outcomes.

METHODS

The authors involved in the development of this position paper included nine members of the North American Society of Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (NASPGHAN), eight of whom are members of the NASPGHAN Neurogastroenterology and Motility committee. Literature searches were conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE/EMBASE databases through June 2024; search terms included aerodigestive, aspiration, achalasia, diet, dysphagia, esophageal atresia (EA), formula, reflux, gastroparesis, lung transplant, motility disorders, nutrition, oropharyngeal, pediatric, and thickener. Non‐English literature was excluded. When pediatric (≤18 years of age) data were limited, adult studies were used and disclosed in their respective sections. Recommendation statements expressed in each section are derived from the available published evidence and the expert opinion of the nine authors. Authors anonymously voted on position statements using an online survey to indicate “agree,” “disagree,” or “want to reword the statement before voting.” A statement vote for “want to reword the statement before voting” prompted further discussion and modification. The edited statements were subsequently voted on again in an online survey, with the statement's final wording determined by the highest number of votes. “Recommendation” statements were defined by at least 70% of authors in agreement. Email correspondence and teleconferences between the authors were used to vote on, reach consensus, and modify the recommendations of this position paper. Of note, pharmacotherapy is beyond the scope of this position paper and will not be discussed.

ROLE OF NUTRITION

Dietary management of pediatric patients with aerodigestive and UGI motility disorders typically starts with a careful review of symptoms and feeding tolerance by a pediatric gastroenterologist and registered dietitian (RD). A formal nutrition evaluation by an RD who has experience in treating patients with these disorders can be helpful. This evaluation should include changes in nutritional parameters (weight, height, body mass index, mid‐upper arm circumference), signs of malnutrition on physical examination (fat/muscle wasting, skinfold thickness), and changes in dietary patterns (textures, volumes) over the short and long term. 1 , 2 If there is concern for malnutrition, focused laboratory testing should be considered. 1

OROPHARYNGEAL DYSPHAGIA (OPD)

The mainstay of OPD management is based upon understanding the underlying pathophysiology. Thickening of liquids has been shown to improve the number and percentage of safe swallows in some adults and children. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 In pediatrics, thickening has also been shown to improve outcomes such as hospitalizations, pulmonary and gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms, and weight percentiles. 7 Despite this, there are no consensus guidelines on the most appropriate and safe ways to thicken fluids. Dietary management and feeding considerations with OPD

The dietary management and feeding considerations of children with OPD involve the balance of risks and benefits to provide nutrition, advance oral skills, and maintain lung health. Risks for aspiration or penetration with oral feeding must be considered in all decision‐making by families, caregivers, and professional team members. Over the last 5 years, there has been increasing evidence that untreated OPD (either penetration and/or aspiration) is associated with persistent symptoms, recurrent respiratory infections, elevations in pulmonary inflammatory markers, and even bronchiectasis. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Therefore, evidence of aspiration or penetration should be treated. Because >80% of pediatric aspiration is silent, the presence of symptoms at the time of an aspiration event should not be the determining factor when considering thickening. 10 , 12 Relevant definitions of the terms used in this section are available in Table 1.

Management decisions of nutrition must consider individual differences in the ability of infants and children to take oral feeding safely. These decisions may involve, in addition to consideration of the safe vehicles (e.g., type of cup, bottle, pouch), determination of safe textures with the possibility of thickening fluids and safe positioning (e.g., side lying).

Texture considerations

Fluids can be thickened with a variety of different additives depending on the patient's age and comorbidities. In infants younger than 1 year, the standard thickener used is cereal or purées, though, in some patients without comorbidities, carob bean gum can be safely used to thicken breast milk or infant formula. 13 , 14 For children older than 1 year, options for thickening are likely to include food‐based thickeners (e.g., single grain cereals, strained baby foods, other smooth foods like yogurt) as well as commercial thickeners. High‐risk patients include patients with a history of NEC or NEC watches, patients with gastrointestinal dysmotility, patients with a history of poor perfusion to the gastrointestinal tract, patients with a history of pneumatosis, and patients with a history of gastrointestinal surgery.

The use and choice of thickeners is not a static decision. Most infants with OPD and aspiration experience improvements in swallow function over time, and thickeners/delivery vehicles can be weaned or changed as swallow function, palate, and caloric needs change. Antegrade aspiration is unlikely to be seen in neurodevelopmentally normal children older than 2 years of age exhibiting persistent lower respiratory tract infections 15 ; for patients over the age of 2 years with persistent symptoms or aspiration or penetration on videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS), an anatomic or neurologic cause for OPD is more likely and the likelihood of spontaneous improvement becomes lower hence, while their swallow may be safe on thickened feeds, further evaluation is needed. As patients age, differences in swallowing efficiency with liquids, purées, and solids can be noted on VFSS, for example, nipple, spoon, cup, or syringe. Concurrent with the need for testing, changes in swallow safety may be noted, and so changes in feeding methods may be needed.

As mentioned in Table 2, there are some thickeners that are preferred in certain populations, serving a dual role to manage swallowing issues and underlying medical conditions. For patients with weight gain difficulty, food‐based thickeners that add calories are preferred. For patients with seizure disorders on a ketogenic diet, commercial thickeners are often used due to their lack of carbohydrates. For patients with hypoglycemia, corn starch thickeners are typically preferred due to the sustained glucose release. However, most patients can safely thicken with food, and many can thicken with commercial thickeners.

Just as there are some thickeners that are preferred for certain patient populations, there are others that should not be used, used with extreme caution, or used in limited quantities. Published case series highlighted the risk of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) in preterm infants given xanthan gum. 16 This report required a re‐evaluation of thickening practices for patients at risk for microbiome disturbances 17 , 18 , 19 or mucosal injury, including patients with a history of prematurity, NEC “watches” or confirmed NEC. Given the high risk of death in this case series of infants with NEC, commercial thickeners should be avoided and used with extreme caution in any patient at risk for ischemic bowel disease such as cardiac disease, GI stasis, GI dysmotility, immune deficiencies, or other significant comorbidities. In addition, commercial thickeners contain added electrolytes and fiber, which may have a significant impact on medically fragile patients such as those at risk for electrolyte disturbances (e.g., children with renal disease or those taking diuretics). Similarly, with food‐based thickeners, allergies need to be considered, and the impact of certain foods on the digestive tract. For example, patients with dairy allergies need to use dairy‐free yogurts and infants with food protein‐induced enterocolitis syndrome may need to avoid cereal thickeners.

There are many misconceptions about thickening that have recently been addressed. For example, studies have shown that dehydration and constipation are unlikely risks of thickening since orally ingested thickening agents do not significantly alter the absorption rate of water from the gut. 20 , 21 Another common concern is that it is more harmful to aspirate thickened liquids than thin liquids; however, there is limited clinical data to support this. A preclinical study found increased lung inflammation when xanthan gum‐based nectar‐thick water was repeatedly instilled into the lungs of anesthetized mice at a volume of 0.1 mg/kg compared to a comparable volume of water instillation. 22 In a study of rabbits who had saline, breast milk, or formula instilled in the trachea, both breast milk and formula triggered a significant drop in lung compliance, highlighting that the goal is to reduce all fluid from entering the lung, which is only possible with thickening. 23 , 24 Finally, pediatric studies have shown significant improvements in symptoms with thickening, suggesting that the benefit of intervention outweighs the risk of aspirating thickened liquids (provided the liquid is thickened appropriately as informed by VFSS). 3 , 4 A recent study in infants confirmed this, showing that infants who aspirated on VFSS and continued to breastfeed had more lung inflammation than babies who received breast milk thickened by bottle, again highlighting that the goal is to stop any fluid from entering the lungs. 9 Finally, while there is adult literature about potential risks of pharyngeal residue in the oropharynx, there is no evidence that residue is a significant problem in pediatrics, likely because most aspirations are due to developmental or anatomic causes rather than neurologic ones. 5 , 25 , 26 , 27 However, even in the adult literature, the risk of any adverse event from thickening is 1%, which is very low given the risk of developing aspiration‐related complications from not thickening. 27

While thickening of oral feedings has become the mainstay of OPD treatment, thickening of gastrostomy tube feeds has also become an important tool to manage feeding intolerance and gastroesophageal reflux disease. Studies in both adults and children have shown that thickening gastrostomy tube feeds either with commercial thickeners or food improves GI and pulmonary outcomes and reduces the number of gastroesophageal reflux events without prolonging gastric emptying. 28 , 29 , 30

The International Dysphagia Diet Standardization Initiative (IDDSI) has developed a standard to objectively describe the characteristics of foods and drinks so that they can be classified into one of eight “levels.” 31 The decision to use thickeners should be a collaboration among speech‐language pathologists or occupational therapists, gastroenterologists, and RDs to prevent the use of inappropriate thickeners in the context of underlying medical risk factors and to ensure adequate nutrition while thickening feeds.

ESOPHAGEAL DYSMOTILITY

Esophageal dysmotility can occur when there is impaired function of any part of the swallowing process, including the upper esophageal sphincter (UES), esophageal body (EB), and lower esophageal sphincter (LES). Esophageal dysmotility can have serious prognostic implications for high‐risk patients such as lung transplant recipients who are at increased risk of chronic lung allograft dysfunction. 32

Dietary management and feeding considerations with esophageal dysmotility

Patients with esophageal dysmotility are at risk for nutritional deficiencies due to decreased ability to tolerate oral intake. The risk for malnutrition varies based on the age of the patient as well as the etiology for impaired esophageal function. For example, patients with achalasia have a higher prevalence of weight loss (61%) than patients with eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE, 10%–24%). 33 , 34

Esophageal dysphagia, including conditions such as achalasia, is typically worse with solid food compared to liquids early in the disease course. If a patient tolerates a specific texture like liquids or purées, nutritional support with that texture should be optimized while evaluating and managing the underlying etiology for reported symptoms. If solids cannot be tolerated, common strategies used include use of a liquid or blenderized/puréed food diet, with each swallow followed by liquid intake to help clear the esophagus. 1 Other strategies involve frequent, small meals containing low fiber with high fluid content. 35 In cases where patients are intolerant to all oral textures, nasogastric, nasoduodenal, gastrostomy, or gastrojejunostomy tubes can be considered depending on the underlying disease process and expected duration of need for nutritional supplementation. Parenteral nutrition is not indicated in the majority of cases and should only be reserved for patients who cannot tolerate post‐pyloric feedings or have contraindications to enteral feedings. 1

Special considerations

Gastroesophageal reflux disease

Gastroesophageal reflux (GER), the physiological retrograde movement of gastric contents into the esophagus, occurs across all age groups and is typically asymptomatic and self‐limiting. In contrast, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) refers to reflux associated with troublesome symptoms or complications. 36 GERD is more prevalent in pediatric populations with prematurity, congenital anomalies (i.e., EA), pulmonary disease, developmental delay, neuromuscular disorders (e.g., cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy), and Down syndrome. 37 , 38

Initial management of GERD focuses on positional and feeding modifications. Although left lateral, prone, and head‐elevated positions reduce reflux episodes, 39 these are contraindicated in infants due to the risk of sudden infant death syndrome and airway compromise, 40 though they may be considered in older children. 36 Infants may benefit from smaller, more frequent feedings based on age and weight while maintaining an appropriate total daily calories and volume with careful avoidance of overfeeding. 36 Thickening of feeds can reduce overt symptoms such as vomiting but does not decrease reflux events. 41 In infants with regurgitation and vomiting, a trial of maternal cows' milk elimination, extensively hydrolyzed or amino‐acid based formulas (in more severe cases with intractable symptoms) may be appropriate. 42 In older children, dietary modifications include avoidance of trigger foods that promote esophageal irritation (e.g., acidic or spicy foods), gastric distension (carbonated beverages, large or calorie‐dense meals), or lower LES tone (chocolate, peppermint, caffeine). 43 If conservative measures fail, enteral nutrition—including post‐pyloric feeding—may be indicated. In children receiving tube feeds, a blenderized diet, characterized by a higher viscosity, has demonstrated reduced GI symptoms (including GER‐related symptoms) and hospitalizations when compared to conventional formulas. 30

Achalasia

Achalasia is one of the most well‐known motor disorders of the esophagus. 44 In adults, the incidence of achalasia in the United States is estimated at 2.9/100,000 annually. 45 It is much more rare in children, with an incidence of 0.11/100,000 annually. 46 Impaired esophageal function can lead to poor growth (31%) and weight loss (61%). 34 In adults, weight loss is seen in 51% of patients. 47

Diagnosis of achalasia is typically confirmed with high‐resolution esophageal manometry (HRM) 48 as well as EndoFLIP, a newer technology that has been shown to allow the diagnosis of achalasia in adults and children during endoscopy. 49 HRM allows classification into different achalasia subtypes based on Chicago Classification 4.0. 48 In adult patients, achalasia type II had the highest risk of weight loss (63%) and type III the lowest risk (27%). 47 In that same study, 43% of patients did not regain weight after achalasia intervention, suggesting that EB and LES dysfunction alone are not resulting in the impaired nutritional state and hypothesizing a higher catabolic state in certain achalasia subtypes. 47 Untimely diagnosis can further predispose to malnutrition, with one study showing that only 20% of patients were able to tolerate a normal diet by the time the diagnosis was made and 26% of patients had weight loss before the diagnosis. 50

There are no clear guidelines on the management of feedings in pediatric or adult patients with achalasia. Dysphagia initially occurs with solid foods in achalasia. As the condition progresses, dysphagia becomes more pronounced, with liquids eventually resulting in symptoms as well. 51 Nutritional management should be dictated by the patient's tolerances on presentation, with attention made to avoid foods with a high risk for esophageal obstruction or esophageal irritation. 52 Patients should be encouraged to eat in an upright position and to minimize eating for several hours before bedtime. 53 A liquid or purée may be required with the guidance of a dietitian to ensure adequate nutritional intake while waiting for the diagnosis and definitive management of achalasia as well as following surgical intervention, depending on the patient's symptoms and nutritional status. This diet should be based on liquids and softer and/or puréed foods, and if solids are tolerated with the use of liquid chasers after each swallow. 1 Nasogastric‐tube feeds may be indicated in patients with severe esophageal dysphagia. 1

Esophageal atresia

EA is a rare structural defect of the esophagus with an incidence of 1 in 2500 to 4500. 54 At birth, low weight is commonly present in patients with EA, as these patients are subjected to polyhydramnios and intrauterine growth restriction compared to patients without EA, with 20% having mild malnutrition, 11% having moderate malnutrition, and 9% having severe malnutrition. 55 Furthermore, patients with EA commonly have difficulties with oral feedings—dysphagia is reported in 50.3% of children with a history of EA, 56 with 13%–58% of patients requiring gastrostomy or gastrojejunostomy feedings after the first year of life. 54 , 55 These difficulties persist following surgical repair and may be due to multiple factors including esophageal dysmotility, late exposure to solids, and feed‐associated symptoms such as choking, dysphagia, slow eating, and oral aversion. 57 Only 28% of children with EA are consuming age‐appropriate food textures within the first 2 years of life, with that improving to 70% of children over 5 years of age. 54 Family‐reported challenging mealtime behaviors, such as extreme food selectivity and food refusal, were reported to be present in 29% of children with EA. 54 Due to difficulties with feedings, it is estimated that 9% of EA patients are malnourished within the first 16 years of life. 54 Weight appears to improve in patients with EA as they age; the percentage of patients having a weight less than the 25th percentile decreases from 70% in those less than 1 year of age to 49% at 5 years of age, and to 31%–41% at 10 years of age. 54 , 58 , 59

There is little data and no published guidelines on the role of different dietary changes in the management of patients with EA. It is generally recommended that children with GERD refrain from eating within 3 h of going to sleep. Based on studies in infants with GERD, aspiration and OPD, thickening formula may help those patients with EA who have OPD. 60 Dietary texture modification may also help those patients with EA who have dysphagia due to esophageal dysmotility based on HRM findings. 61

Many infants with EA require gastrostomy tube placement because they cannot safely tolerate oral feeding or meet caloric requirements, especially during staged repair of EA and/or subsequent anastomotic dilations. Post‐pyloric feeding may be considered as a fundoplication alternative for concerning symptoms such as aspiration (from below), GERD, retching, gastrostomy leakage, distention, intolerance to gastric feeding, recurrent pneumonias, or cardiopulmonary events. 60

In children with long gap EA who have delayed surgery, sham feedings can be considered to promote swallowing through the development of pharyngeal and esophageal function and coordination. 62 Sham feedings involve the awake infant orally consuming milk (by breast or bottle feeds) or solid food; the food is then extracted via a syringe connected to a tube in the upper pouch of the esophagus and reinserted via gastrostomy. Sham feeds typically follow the infant or child's hunger cues to further allow for satiety regulation and overall have a positive impact on the family. 63

Although fundoplication is recommended in EA in the setting of recurrent anastomotic strictures, poorly controlled GERD, long‐term dependency on transpyloric feedings, and recurrent cyanotic episodes, 64 patients with EA who underwent fundoplication have been shown to have worsened nutritional status compared to those without fundoplication. 54 In patients with retching, significant GER, or aspiration, transpyloric feedings with a gastrojejunostomy tube can be considered. 60 Post‐pyloric feeds are considered an optimal alternative option to fundoplication with respect to esophageal dysmotility, as fundoplication may potentially lead to stasis of food and secretions in the esophagus.

As patients with EA grow older, self‐reported symptoms remain prominent. Beyond feeding difficulties, patients with a history of EA have been reported to be at higher risk for GERD (87%), EoE (38%), esophageal strictures (27%), and aspiration (11%–24%), which may further limit optimal nutritional intake. 54 , 65 A prospective cohort study showed that the proportion of caregivers of patients with EA reporting feeding difficulties was approximately one‐third and unchanged over both time points of the study (where median patient age was 1.6 and 4.2 years) 66 ; these feeding difficulties can impact the well‐being of families, with one study showing 34% of parents experiencing mealtime anxiety. 65 These families report stress elicited by meals outside the home, concern for aspiration and lack of the normal transition between feeding stages. 67 In a systematic review and meta‐proportional analysis, quantitative and qualitative data relating to swallow impairment, use of mealtime adaptations and eating and drinking‐related quality of life were studied. 65 This study highlighted that 25% of caregivers report challenging mealtime behaviors, and identified three patient‐reported mealtime adaptations that included the need for water when eating (49%), eating slowly (37%), and modifying textures (28%). When advancing oral feeds, IDDSI guidelines suggest progressing through textures as complementary feeding starts. (https://iddsi.org/Resources/Patient-Handouts) IDDSI Levels 3 and 4 are recommended as the appropriate first‐line texture (considered liquidized and moderately thick substances with no lumps or solid pieces). 31 The rate of food texture progression in patients with EA is delayed compared to other children without EA, as most patients with EA are not able to manage more textured foods until 18–24 months of age, and they have increased prevalence of swallowing difficulties in the first 4 years of life. 68 The psychosocial impacts identified by caregivers of these patients with EA include fear and trauma associated with eating and drinking, as well as isolation and a lack of support. 65 A multi‐disciplinary team that includes a dietitian and speech language therapist can provide guidance to families and children to navigate these barriers and concerns related to feeding and feeding advancement.

GASTRIC DYSMOTILITY

Gastroparesis is a syndrome of objectively delayed gastric emptying in the absence of a mechanical obstruction and with cardinal symptoms including nausea, vomiting, early satiety, bloating, and abdominal pain. Most pediatric cases are idiopathic or post‐viral, with less common causes being drug‐induced or post‐surgical. 69 , 70 Food intake can be limited with gastric dysmotility due to symptoms including early satiety, fullness, abdominal distension, nausea, and vomiting. Nutrition is further stunted by the decreased grinding of food by the antrum and the decreased emptying of gastric contents into the duodenum, reducing the absorption of nutrients. 71

Dietary management and feeding considerations with gastroparesis

Patients with moderate to severe gastroparesis are at risk of dehydration and malnutrition complicated by micronutrient deficiencies (calcium, iron, magnesium, potassium, folate, vitamin D, vitamin E, vitamin K), the absorption of which can be decreased with chronic proton pump inhibitor administration. 72

While dietary modification is often considered first‐line therapy in patients with mild gastroparesis, it often results in suboptimal improvement in symptoms in clinical practice. 73 Nevertheless, dietary interventions play a crucial role in the management of gastroparesis in pediatric patients. Pediatric clinical trials or comparative studies are lacking; however, there are few adult studies (discussed in this section) that evaluate dietary interventions in patients with gastroparesis.

An initial approach for patients with gastroparesis includes adhering to an oral diet with an optimal meal structure, food composition, and particle size that is well tolerated. Small, frequent meals and maintaining a post‐prandial upright position may be better tolerated. 74 , 75 Liquids typically empty from the stomach faster than solids 76 ; therefore, increasing liquid calories and switching from solid to liquid calories later in the day may help patients with their symptoms.

Fatty, acidic, spicy, and high‐fiber foods can exacerbate symptoms in patients with gastroparesis as they delay gastric emptying, exacerbate pain, and promote release of cholecystokinin. 77 , 78 Diets low in fat and insoluble fiber are generally better tolerated in patients with gastroparesis. Soluble fiber, especially when cooked or homogenized to reduce particle size, may be better tolerated, except in severe cases. In a study by Homko et al., gastroparesis patients experienced greater symptoms after consuming high‐fat solid meals compared to high‐fat liquids, indicating that solid fats worsen symptoms more than liquid fats. 78 A low‐fiber diet is better tolerated 79 as fiber can delay gastric emptying and prolong satiety via mechanisms such as extended cholecystokinin activity. 80

Small‐particle diets, consisting of puréed, blended, or finely ground foods, facilitate gastric emptying by reducing mechanical workload on the stomach. These diets include soft fruits, cooked vegetables, tender meats, and processed foods of reduced particle size. In a randomized trial, compared to large‐particle meals, small‐particle diets have been shown to improve gastric emptying and reduce symptoms such as nausea, bloating, and abdominal discomfort in adults with gastroparesis. 81 , 82 , 83 Additionally, they may alleviate anxiety symptoms. 83 Modified‐consistency diets (chopped, ground, or puréed foods) similarly benefit patients, particularly those with diabetic gastroparesis. 84 In adults with gastroparesis, low‐fat diets, small‐particle diets, isoflavone‐containing diets, and bland, starchy, sweet, or salty foods were not associated with symptom exacerbation. Small‐particle and isoflavone‐rich diets demonstrated improved gastric emptying times. 82 Investigational dietary interventions with promotility effects include soy germ pasta and Pistacia atlantica kurdica gum. Soy germ pasta, rich in isoflavones, significantly enhanced gastric emptying compared to conventional pasta. 85 While these are not commercially available, similar isoflavone sources include soy protein, soy flour, tempeh, and tofu, although these have not been formally studied. P. atlantica kurdica gum significantly reduced symptoms versus placebo but remains investigational. 86 , 87

Despite dietary modifications, tolerance varies among patients with gastroparesis, necessitating individualized monitoring by a pediatric gastroenterologist and RD. In cases of inadequate oral intake risking malnutrition, nasogastric or nasojejunal feeding or, for long‐term needs, gastrostomy or jejunostomy may improve nutritional status. 72 The choice of enteral formula—polymeric (i.e., intact macronutrients), semi‐elemental (peptide based) or elemental (amino acid based)—should be patient‐specific; in resource‐limited settings, nutritionally balanced homemade feeds may be appropriate. 75 Pediatric studies have shown enhanced gastric emptying with medium‐chain triglycerides as compared to long‐chain triglycerides, 88 enhanced gastric emptying with whey‐based hydrolysate formulas compared to casein‐predominant formulas, 89 and enhanced gastric emptying with extensively hydrolyzed formula compare to intact or partially hydrolyzed formula. 90 Blenderized tube feeds, despite higher viscosity mostly due to fiber, have demonstrated comparable gastric emptying to standard formulas and are associated with reduced GI symptoms and hospitalizations. 30 , 91 Parenteral nutrition remains a last‐line option due to its risks and should be reserved for those intolerant to enteral support. Caution is advised when initiating enteral or parenteral nutrition in patients with gastroparesis given the significant overlap with functional dyspepsia and increased prevalence of feeding intolerance in adolescents with disorders of gut–brain interaction. 92 Given the high incidence of eating disorders in patients with disorders of gut–brain interaction, careful screening for eating behaviors is necessary to avoid unnecessary procedures or high‐risk interventions. 93 , 94

Dietary management and feeding considerations with gastric dysmotility in pediatric aerodigestive patients

UGI dysfunction and pulmonary aspiration in children with aerodigestive disorders are important causes of chronic cough, frequent hospitalizations, recurrent pneumonia, and progressive lung injury leading to respiratory disability. This can be a cause for concern in high‐risk patients such as lung transplant recipients. Adult data have shown that lung transplant patients with delayed gastric emptying pretransplant had a higher incidence of acute cellular rejection. 95 Gastric dysmotility is related to allograft dysfunction in pediatric lung transplant patients. 96 Several risk factors have been implicated, though an exact mechanism is not fully understood. More attention has focused on the growing body of literature supporting the role of gastric dysmotility with and without GERD as a more significant driver of pulmonary complications in aerodigestive patients.

Several treatment options for this unique patient population have been explored, with dietary interventions showing promising results. In the setting of pharmacotherapy‐refractory symptoms of gastroparesis, surgical techniques are often employed. Intrapyloric Botulinum toxin injection administered for patients with feeding disorders and associated GI symptoms proved to be safe and effective, with patients on enteral tube feeds showing improvement in their oral feeding and decreased need for post‐pyloric tube feeds. 97 Gastrostomy and/or jejunostomy tube placement in aerodigestive patients with upper gut dysmotility and feeding intolerance provides access to decompress the stomach and provide nutrition when oral feeding is inadequate to sustain appropriate growth and symptom control.

The benefits of blenderized tube feedings (BTFs), commercially available or homemade via gastrostomy or post‐pyloric tubes, have received a lot of attention for patients with aerodigestive disorders. The American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition recently published detailed practice recommendations providing guidance to medical providers and family pertaining to the use of BTFs regardless of their indication. 98 It is hypothesized that higher viscosity tube feedings progress more quickly to the antrum, which may reduce GER‐ and reflux‐associated GI and respiratory symptoms. 99 , 100 The direct implication of BTFs on gastric emptying was recently explored in a retrospective cohort study. The authors found that although the percentage of tube feed residual at 1 h was significantly higher in patients receiving blends compared with formula, the number of patients with delayed gastric emptying did not differ, and although the mean percentage of gastric residual was higher with blends, this may be explained by higher bolus volumes administered. 91 Additional benefits of BTF compared to commercial formula in medically complex children include decrease in emesis, retching, and the use of acid suppressive medication, improved caregiver satisfaction and increase in intestinal microbial diversity, 101 decreased health care utilization (decreased total and respiratory admissions and emergency room visits) and improved quality of life measures. 30

A recent retrospective study investigating the impact of additional thin liquids in medically complex children with pulmonary comorbidities including aspiration found that patients who were receiving higher proportions of thin liquids were more likely to undergo chest x‐rays in the year after starting BTFs. 102 It remains unclear whether continuous or bolus feeding is superior in this patient population.

Despite the reported benefits of BTFs, there is potential for an increased risk of inadequate nutrition, poor weight status, electrolyte imbalances, and equipment malfunction. 103 To mitigate the potential risks, dietary management requires an individualized care plan to optimize growth and nutrition and to improve overall health outcomes. Therefore, close follow‐up by the medical team, including an RD, is paramount to ensure safety and efficacy.

CONCLUSIONS

This document represents the first set of expert opinion recommendations on the dietary management of pediatric patients with aerodigestive and UGI motility disorders. The involvement of an RD as part of the interdisciplinary team is crucial for the evaluation and monitoring of the nutritional status of this patient cohort, as they are at risk of suboptimal nutrition and malnutrition. Decisions regarding optimal delivery of nutrition/hydration for infants and children in many instances require extensive evaluation by multiple professionals with primary caregiver input that includes cultural environment, along with safety, access, and optimal quality of life. The scarcity of published research available on this subject matter represents a limitation that led to most of the recommendations being expert recommendations versus evidence‐based and hopefully promotes future research to provide evidence for the specific dietary interventions these patients need.
