Integrating Polchinski’s equation by convergent binary tree expansions
Paul Obernolte, Manfred Salmhofer

Abstract
. We give a solution to Polchinski’s equation for the Wilsonian effective action in terms of an expansion in binary trees, and prove that this expansion converges in fermionic field theories, provided the fermionic covariance has finite determinant and decay constants. A novel element of the proof are detailed combinatorial estimates for the number of leaf trees associated to binary trees. The method can be used on standard models of fermionic quantum field theory and quantum statistical mechanics.



1.
Introduction

Introduced in statistical mechanics in the study of critical phenomena, the Kadanoff-Wilson-Wegner renormalization group (RG)
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
has become a standard in the theory of quantum fields and quantum many-body systems, both in theoretical physics and mathematics. It sets up a flow in a space of action functionals or Hamiltonians, which is parametrized by a scale, such as a length or an energy. The basic idea is that averaging over local fluctuations at a certain length scale defines a scale-dependent
effective action
, which contains all information about correlations at larger length scales, and that by varying the scale parameter one can understand how fundamental or ‘microscopic’ models and emergent or ‘macroscopic’ models are related. A specific model to be studied then corresponds to an initial condition, starting from which the flow tends to effective actions that characterize the long-distance correlations of the model. If, as is the case in many examples, the flow tends to an attractor, typically a fixed point, the corresponding fixed-point effective action determines the emergent model and its properties.

Mathematically, the local averaging corresponds to a smoothing operation which also gives a natural way of regularizing quantum field theoretical models, and field-theoretic renormalization can be understood as a way of adjusting the initial condition of a Wilsonian RG flow in order to get a finite limit at the end of the flow. A particularly nice way of doing this in perturbative QFT was given by Polchinski
[
10
]
. Following his seminal work, the method was simplified and extended to general renormalization conditions
[
11
]
, and it has subsequently been applied to a large variety of models of QFT, both related to high-energy physics
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
and to condensed-matter physics
[
16
]
.

The averaging operation has been implemented in technically diverse ways, from the block spin transformation originally introduced by Kadanoff
[
1
]
to momentum and phase space variants. Mathematical proofs have mostly been done using discrete scale sequences, where the scale parameter (such as the above-mentioned length) is increased in steps, e.g. in a geometric progression. Formal theory and approximate calculations have very often been done using a continuous variation of the scale parameter. The mathematical proofs with discrete RG sequences include pioneering results like the construction of infrared
\phi^{4}
theory in four dimensions
[
5
,
6
]
, the first constructions of the two-dimensional Gross-Neveu model
[
7
,
8
]
, and the construction of a two-dimensional Fermi liquid at zero temperature
[
9
]
.

In particular, Polchinski’s equation has been studied in many cases and in different representations. Given a continuous flow, one can of course also derive from it a discrete “flow”, i.e. a sequence of actions, simply as the values of the flowing effective action at a sequence of scales. In their seminal work, Brydges and Kennedy analyzed Polchinski’s equation by PDE methods and derived an explicit solution as an expansion labelled by trees. They used it to give a new proof of convergence of Mayer expansions
[
17
]
. This was subsequently applied to fermions by Brydges and Wright
[
18
]
. Abdesselam and Rivasseau gave important generalizations and combinatorial perspectives in
[
19
]
. Disertori and Rivasseau used the integrated flow equation to construct the Gross-Neveu model
[
20
]
and the rotationally symmetric two-dimensional many-fermion system
[
21
,
22
]
.

In this paper we integrate Polchinski’s equation by a binary tree expansion, which arises naturally because the nonlinearity in the differential equation is quadratic. The form it takes is a bit different from the Brydges-Kennedy formula, and indeed one can think of it as a continuous version of the Gallavotti-Nicolò expansion, which was introduced and used in the context of discrete scale decompositions and applied to proofs of perturbative renormalizability of four-dimensional scalar field theories
[
23
]
, and further developed and applied to quantum electrodynamics in
[
24
]
.

For the case of fermionic field theories with regular covariances, we prove that our binary-tree expansion converges. Regularity of the covariance means that both its determinant bound and its decay constant are finite, and bounded independently of the volume. Tree expansions have been used to analyze fermionic theories in many works, in particular in the context of many-fermion systems
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
. For a prototypical development of the technique see, e.g.
[
28
]
; bounds on determinant constants especially suitable for time-ordered covariances were given in
[
29
]
. One novel aspect of our proof are combinatorial estimates for “leaf trees” that are induced by the binary trees arising in the iterative solution of the Polchinski equation, and by the action of Laplacians in field space in that context.

2.1.
Grassmann algebra setup

We will consider a fermionic QFT on a finite lattice. Take the lattice to be the torus

2.2.
Polchinski’s equation

In the framework of renormalization theory, it is natural to integrate degrees of freedom associated to different length or energy scales successively. Mathematically, this is done by decomposing a covariance (which, in absence of regularizations, typically has an integral kernel with singularities) into many regular pieces. The decomposition can be discrete or continuous; we are interested in the latter and write

(13)

C(s)=\int_{0}^{s}{\rm d}{r}\;\dot{C}(r),

where
s\in\mathbb{R}
plays the role of the
flow parameter
. Assume that
C(s)
is a smooth function of
s
and that every
\dot{C}(r)
is skew-symmetric. Note that a dot will always represent a derivative with respect to the flow parameter. We will frequently use the following notation for
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
:

(14)

C_{t,s}=\int_{s}^{t}{\rm d}{r}\;\dot{C}(r).

Now, we can compute the Wilsonian effective potential based on the covariance matrix
C(s)
. This way, the Wilsonian effective potential also gains a dependence on the flow parameter
s
. We write
\mathcal{G}(s,\Psi)=\mathcal{G}(\Psi,C(s),\mathcal{V})
for short; sometimes, we even write
\mathcal{G}(s)=\mathcal{G}(s,\Psi)
.
By taking the derivative of
\mathcal{G}(s)
with respect to
s
, one gets Polchinski’s equation
[
10
]

(15)

\dot{\mathcal{G}}(s,\Psi)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\dot{C}(s)}\mathcal{G}(s,\Psi)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathcal{G}(s,\Psi),\dot{C}(s)\nabla\mathcal{G}(s,\Psi)\right).

Polchinski’s equation is a first order ordinary differential equation (ODE) in the flow parameter
s
on the finite-dimensional space
\bigwedge(V)
. Given an initial condition, it has a unique solution because Gaussian convolution is well-defined on
\bigwedge(V)
whenever
C(s)
is well-defined.

We define
C:\ [0,1]\to M_{N}(\mathbb{R})
in such a way that
C(0)=0
. Then
\mathcal{G}(0,\Psi)=\mathcal{V}(\Psi)
where
\mathcal{V}
is the initial interaction of our model. Hence, our goal will be to find the Wilsonian effective potential as a solution of Polchinski’s equation with this initial condition.

To derive our expansion, we start from the following integral version of Polchinski’s initial value problem (see also
[
30
, Appendix A]
).

Lemma 3
.

Polchinski’s Equation (
15
) with initial condition
\mathcal{G}(0,\Psi)=\mathcal{V}(\Psi)
is equivalent to the integrated Polchinski equation

(16)

\mathcal{G}(t,\Psi)=\mathcal{G}_{0}(t,\Psi)-\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}{s}\;e^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{C_{t,s}}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathcal{G}(s,\Psi),\dot{C}(s)\nabla\mathcal{G}(s,\Psi)\right),

where

(17)

\mathcal{G}_{0}(t,\Psi)=e^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{C_{t,0}}}\mathcal{V}(\Psi).

Proof.

Because
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\Delta_{C_{t,s}}=-\Delta_{\dot{C}(s)}
and
\Delta_{C_{t,s}}
commutes with
\Delta_{\dot{C}(s)}
,

(18)

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}(t)-{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{C_{t,0}}}\mathcal{G}(0)&=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\;\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\left[{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{C_{t,s}}}\mathcal{G}(s)\right]\\
&=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\;{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{C_{t,s}}}\left[-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\Delta_{\dot{C}(s)}\mathcal{G}(s)+\dot{\mathcal{G}}(s)\right]\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\;{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{C_{t,s}}}\;(\nabla\mathcal{G}(s),\dot{C}(s)\nabla\mathcal{G}(s))\;.\end{split}

In the last step we have used (
15
).
∎

h^{\prime}=h+\left(\sqrt{2e}+1\right)\gamma_{C}
(and

Moreover, if

(36)

{\left\|\mathcal{G}(t=1)\right\|}_{h}\leq\frac{2{\left\|\mathcal{V}\right\|}_{h^{\prime}}}{1-4e\;\omega_{C}\;{\left\|\mathcal{V}\right\|}_{h^{\prime}}}.

In particular, the formal power series

is convergent in this case. For

, we have in this case:

(37)

{\left\|\mathcal{G}(t=1)-\sum_{l=1}^{P-1}\mathcal{G}_{l}(t=1)\right\|}_{h}\leq\frac{2}{4e\;\omega_{C}}\;\frac{\big(4e\;\omega_{C}\;{\left\|\mathcal{V}\right\|}_{h^{\prime}}\big)^{P}}{1-4e\;\omega_{C}\;{\left\|\mathcal{V}\right\|}_{h^{\prime}}}.

If the determinant bound
\gamma_{C}
and the decay constant

of the covariance matrix

can be chosen independent of the lattice spacing
\epsilon
and the system size

, then the tree expansion for the Wilsonian effective potential converges in a disk that is uniform in

and

Theorem
6
is the central result of this paper. It will be proven in Section

3.
Combinatorics of graphs

3.1.
Short review

Graph expansions are a common tool in quantum field theory. Simple notions of graph theory are reviewed in Appendix
A
. To bound graph expansions, one has to know about the combinatorics of the specific type of graphs used. The simplest expansions of the Wilsonian (effective) potential that one can obtain are expansions in general (connected) graphs. Unfortunately, these have quite a bad growth behaviour in the number of vertices:

Lemma 7

(Number of (connected) graphs on
n
vertices)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(i)

The number of graphs on
n
vertices is

(38)

{\left|\mathfrak{G}([n])\right|}=2^{\binom{n}{2}}=2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}.

(ii)

For
n\to\infty
, the number of connected graphs on
n
vertices is

(39)

{\left|\mathfrak{G}_{c}([n])\right|}\sim 2^{\binom{n}{2}}=2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}.

Proof.

Part (i) is clear. To prove part (ii), we use from
[
33
, Section 3.11]
that
d_{n}
, the number of labelled connected graphs on
n
vertices, satisfies

n\;2^{{n\choose 2}}=\sum_{k\geq 1}{n\choose k}k\;d_{k}\;2^{{n-k\choose 2}}.

Define
\delta_{k}=d_{k}\;2^{-{k\choose 2}}
. Then by (i),
\delta_{k}\leq 1
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

1=\delta_{n}+(n-1)\delta_{n-1}2^{1-n}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}{n\choose k}\frac{k}{n}\delta_{k}\;2^{-k(n-\frac{k-1}{2})}\;.

The last sum is bounded by

\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}{n\choose k}2^{-k(n-\frac{k-1}{2})}\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}{n\choose k}2^{-kn/2}=(1+2^{-n/2})^{n}-1\leq{\rm e}^{n2^{-n/2}}-1.

Thus

\delta_{n}\geq 1-(n-1)2^{1-n}-({\rm e}^{n2^{-n/2}}-1)\;,

so for
n\geq 10
,
\delta_{n}\geq 1-n2^{-n/2}
.
∎

For this reason, expansions in general (connected) graphs usually behave badly in the sense that estimates for individual graphs are not sufficient to prove convergence of the series. Expansions in tree graphs behave a lot better because the number of trees on
n
vertices exhibits a much smaller growth rate:

Lemma 8

(Cayley’s formula
[
31
, Chapter 2]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\geq 1
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=2(n-1)
.

(i)

We have:

(40)

{\left|\mathfrak{T}_{n}\right|}=n^{n-2}.

(ii)

Define

(41)

\mathfrak{T}_{n}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})=\left\{T\in\mathfrak{T}_{n}:d_{T}(i)=d_{i}\ \forall i\in[n]\right\}.

Then:

(42)

{\left|\mathfrak{T}_{n}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})\right|}=\frac{(n-2)!}{(d_{1}-1)!\dots(d_{n}-1)!}.

We have
n^{n-2}\leq n!e^{n}
; the factorial is usually cancelled by an inverse factor

in according tree expansions.

The expansion presented in this paper is in binary trees. Their number can be obtained by Catalan family considerations:

Lemma 9

(Number of binary trees
[
31
, Example 14.9]
)
.

For
l\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
{\left|\mathfrak{B}_{l}\right|}=\frac{1}{l}\binom{2(l-1)}{l-1}
.

Hence,
{\left|\mathfrak{B}_{l}\right|}<(2e)^{l-1}
; the growth rate of binary trees is non-factorial, hence significantly better than even that of normal trees. On the other hand, it turns out that our expansion in binary trees gives rise to a sum over yet another structure: Leaf trees of binary trees. These are defined and analysed in the next subsection.

4.1.
Role of determinants in fermionic QFT

The following lemma shows how determinants naturally arise when applying exponentiated laplacians to monomials.

Lemma 16
.

Let
D\subset\mathcal{X}
and
\mathbb{M}\in M_{N}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, there exist
\varepsilon_{D}^{A,A^{\prime}}\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}
for all

(58)

e^{\Delta_{\mathbb{M}}}\prod_{X\in D}\psi(X)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A,A^{\prime}\subset D\\
{\left|A\right|}={\left|A^{\prime}\right|}\\

Here,
\mathbb{M}_{A,A^{\prime}}
denotes a quadratic minor of the matrix
\mathbb{M}
.

This is an adapted version of
[
28
, Lemma 5]
. Although the proof remains similar, we give it explicitly in Appendix

The preceding lemma shows the importance of bounding determinants. The basis for these bounds is Gram’s inequality:

Lemma 17

(Gram’s inequality
[
35
, Lemma B.13]
)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

(59)

(f,g)=\det((\langle f_{i},g_{j}\rangle)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}).

Then:

(60)

|(f,g)|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lVert f_{i}\rVert\cdot\lVert g_{i}\rVert.

The proof can be found in
[
35
, Appendix B]
.
Next, we define what we mean by a matrix to have a certain Gram constant:

Definition 18

(Gram constant)
.

Let

. We say that

and
\lVert g_{i}\rVert\leq\gamma

of

corresponding to the Gram constant

With this definition, the following statement follows directly from Lemma

Corollary 19

Let

(63)

5.
Discussion and Outlook

The main difference to the proof of convergence of the tree expansion in fermionic quantum field theories, as for instance in
[
28
]
), is that two types of trees appear here. The iterative solution of the integrated Polchinski equation produces a sum over binary trees, the number of which does not grow factorially as a function of the iteration depth. However, the action of the Laplacians coming from the tree and from the exponential that creates loops does create another kind of tree which we call here the leaf tree. In analogy to the nomenclature of
[
39
]
, the binary trees would be called the vertical trees of our expansion (which record how the iterative expansion proceeds) and the leaf tree would be called the horizontal tree (which forms the backbone of the connected Feynman graphs that are resummed into the action of the exponential of the Laplacian). Controlling the combinatorics of the latter is crucial for proving convergence. An essential combinatorial lemma that we have proven in the paper can be seen as a variant of Cayley’s formula, as follows. Cayley’s formula gives the number of labeled trees on
p
vertices with incidence numbers
d_{1},\ldots,d_{p}
as

(89)

\frac{(p-2)!}{(d_{1}-1)!\ldots(d_{p}-1)!}\;.

In tree expansions based on
[
28
,
37
]
or on the standard Brydges-Kennedy formula (see also
[
38
]
), the factorials in the denominator are used to cancel the ones arising from the differentiation of the vertex functions. The factorial in the numerator is then cancelled by an overall
1/p!
in front of the sum over all trees with
p
vertices (see, e.g.
[
28
]
).

Leaf trees are of course just special cases of general labelled trees, but our formula relates the number of leaf trees to the way they arise from the binary tree: we recall from Lemma
12
that

{\left|\mathfrak{L}(T,d)\right|}=\frac{\prod_{v\in V_{\mathrm{fork}}(T)\setminus\left\{*\right\}}\left(\sum_{w\in\mathcal{L}_{T}(v)}(d_{w}-2)+2\right)}{\prod_{w\in V_{\mathrm{leaf}}(T)}(d_{w}-1)!}.

and from Lemma
13
the bound

(90)

{\left|\mathfrak{L}(T,d)\right|}\leq\frac{\prod_{v\in V(T)\setminus\left\{*\right\}}{\left|\mathcal{L}_{T}(v)\right|}}{\prod_{w\in V_{\mathrm{leaf}}(T)}(d_{w}-1)!}

When the tree in Cayley’s formula is identified with our leaf tree, the denominators in these formulas are identical. It is the product in the numerator of (
90
) that encodes information about the binary tree that induces the leaf tree. While the cancellation of the factorials
d_{L}(w)!
in (
75
) by the ones in the denominators is similar to the one in other tree expansions, the cancellation of the product in the numerator of (
75
) against factors arising in the integral over the
s
-variables fits specifically to the way our expansion is built up.

Theorem
5
is valid for a general (differentiable) dependence of the covariance
C
on the parameter
s
, while Theorem
6
includes the hypothesis that
C(s)
depends linearly on
s
. When using RG as a multiscale method, this is still useful for controlling a single step in an iteration for discrete times. Specifically, suppose that
C(\sigma)=\int_{0}^{\sigma}\dot{C}(\tau)\;{\rm d}\tau
(where
C(\tau)
does not depend linearly on
\tau
) and the Wilsonian effective action
\mathcal{G}(\sigma)=\mathcal{G}(\Psi,C(\sigma),\mathcal{V})
is defined by the Polchinski equation. If the limit of interest is
\sigma\to\infty
, one can try to take this limit as the limit of the discrete sequence
\mathcal{G}_{j}(\Psi)=\mathcal{G}(\Psi,C(\sigma_{j}),\mathcal{V})
, where the flow times
\sigma_{j}
form an increasing sequence that tends to infinity for
j\to\infty
. The sequence starts at
\mathcal{G}_{0}=\mathcal{V}
and for
j\geq 1
,
\mathcal{G}_{j}
is obtained from
\mathcal{G}_{j-1}
by

(91)

\mathcal{G}_{j}=\mathcal{G}(\sigma_{j})=\mathcal{G}\left(\Psi,C_{j},\mathcal{G}_{j-1}\right)\;.

where
C_{j}=C(\sigma_{j})-C(\sigma_{j-1})
.
By Theorem
6
, the linear interpolation

with

implies that
\mathcal{G}_{j}

\mathcal{G}_{j-1}

\sigma_{j}

Still, it would be nice to have a method that does not involve the discrete sequence of
\sigma_{j}
. It is possible to generalize Theorem
6
to a general

-dependence of
C(s)

[
41
]
. Moreover, it is in general necessary to include renormalization effects, in particular, separate out the flow of RG-relevant terms in
\mathcal{G}
. This can be done for two-point terms (which are usually the most relevant ones in that they grow fastest in the flow) by a modification of Polchinski’s equation in which terms that are quadratic in
\Psi

\mathcal{G}
are continuously absorbed in the covariance during the flow
[
40
]
. This modifies the Polchinski equation by additional terms that are linear in

are under investigation
[
41
]
.

&\qquad\cdot\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta\psi(X_{1})}\dots\frac{\delta}{\delta\psi(X_{k})}\prod_{X\in A}\psi(X)\right)\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta\psi(X^{\prime}_{1})}\dots\frac{\delta}{\delta\psi(X^{\prime}_{k})}\prod_{X\in A^{\prime}}\psi(X)\right)\\
=&-\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in S_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\;\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\;\mathbb{M}_{a_{\sigma(1)}a^{\prime}_{\tau(1)}}\dots\mathbb{M}_{a_{\sigma(k)}a^{\prime}_{\tau(k)}}\\
=&-\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\;\mathbb{M}_{a_{1}a^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)}}\dots\mathbb{M}_{a_{k}a^{\prime}_{\sigma(k)}}=-\det(\mathbb{M}_{A,A^{\prime}}).\end{split}

This proves the lemma.
∎
