Lifestyle Medicine 3.0: From Disease Prevention to Human Health Optimization
Authors: In Deok Kong
Published: 2026

More than a decade ago, Yeh and Kong [1] described the advent of lifestyle medicine as a new medical paradigm arising from the increasing burden of chronic diseases and the recognition that lifestyle is deeply involved in their pathogenesis. Since then, lifestyle medicine has evolved from an emerging concept emphasizing healthy behaviors into an increasingly established clinical discipline. At the same time, the concept of health itself has expanded beyond the mere absence of disease toward broader dimensions of well-being and human functioning [2]. Lifestyle medicine has consequently developed through evidence-based clinical practice, professional education, multidisciplinary care, and defined core competencies [3,4].

Today, however, medicine is entering another period of transformation. Artificial intelligence (AI), wearable technologies, digital health, precision health, and advances in healthy aging are changing how health can be measured, understood, and promoted. At the same time, population aging, health inequities, mental health challenges, and environmental changes are forcing us to reconsider a fundamental question: What should be the ultimate goal of medicine?

Against this background, I propose “Lifestyle Medicine 3.0” as a conceptual framework—not an established classification—for the next evolution of lifestyle medicine: from disease prevention and evidence-based intervention toward human health optimization across the lifespan (Fig. 1).

FROM LIFESTYLE MEDICINE 1.0 TO 2.0

The early development of lifestyle medicine can be viewed conceptually as Lifestyle Medicine 1.0, centered primarily on disease prevention and risk-factor reduction. Smoking cessation, healthy nutrition, physical activity, weight management, and other behavioral interventions became increasingly recognized as fundamental strategies for preventing chronic disease.

The subsequent evolution toward Lifestyle Medicine 2.0 has emphasized evidence-based clinical intervention. Lifestyle medicine is increasingly incorporated into primary care and chronic disease management through structured programs, behavior-change strategies, multidisciplinary teams, and shared decision-making. Recent expert consensus has recognized lifestyle medicine as an important component of primary care and emphasized its integration into clinical practice [3].

The 2022 update of lifestyle medicine core competencies further reflects the maturation of the field [4]. Lifestyle medicine is no longer simply a collection of recommendations about healthy behaviors; it is becoming an integrated clinical discipline with defined competencies and professional standards.

WHY LIFESTYLE MEDICINE 3.0?

The next challenge is not simply to develop more lifestyle interventions. It is to make those interventions more personalized, continuous, scalable, equitable, and sustainable.

Precision lifestyle medicine provides one important direction. Individual responses to lifestyle interventions vary according to biological, behavioral, social, and environmental factors [5]. Yet precision should not be reduced to genomics or technology alone. A holistic and person-centered approach should remain at the heart of lifestyle medicine [6].

Digital technologies provide another major opportunity. Digital Lifestyle Medicine can deliver lifestyle support in the environments where people actually live, work, eat, sleep, and exercise, while enabling continuous engagement and scalable behavioral support [7]. More recently, AI has created opportunities to analyze lifestyle and wearable data, personalize counseling, support clinical decision-making, and reduce the administrative burden on clinicians while preserving the human connection at the center of care [8].

These technologies should therefore not replace clinicians or human relationships. Rather, they should serve as tools that enable more precise understanding, earlier intervention, and sustained behavior change.

Perhaps the most important conceptual shift in Lifestyle Medicine 3.0 is the transition from a disease-centered endpoint to a health-centered endpoint.

Traditional medicine often asks: What disease does this person have, and how should we treat it?

Lifestyle Medicine 3.0 should increasingly ask: How can this person achieve and maintain the highest attainable level of health and function throughout life?

This distinction is more than semantic. A person may be free from a diagnosed disease yet have poor physical fitness, inadequate sleep, social isolation, psychological distress, or declining functional capacity. Conversely, an individual living with a chronic disease may achieve excellent functional health and quality of life through effective medical and lifestyle management.

The concept of positive health provides an important foundation for this broader perspective. Seligman [2] proposed positive health as a state extending beyond the mere absence of disease and suggested that biological, subjective, and functional measures could be used to characterize it. Similarly, the World Health Organization’s healthy-aging framework emphasizes functional ability and well-being rather than simply the absence of disease [9].

Thus, the ultimate outcomes of lifestyle medicine should extend beyond disease prevention or treatment to include healthspan, functional capacity, well-being, resilience, and quality of life.

Lifestyle Medicine 3.0 must also recognize that human health cannot be separated from the environments in which people live. Food systems, urban environments, climate change, pollution, and social determinants of health influence both individual and population health. The EAT-Lancet Commission provided an important framework linking healthy diets with sustainable food systems and planetary health [10].

A NEW ROLE FOR THE JOURNAL OF LIFESTYLE MEDICINE

The Journal of Lifestyle Medicine has a unique opportunity to help define this next stage.

In 2013, the journal published The Advent of Lifestyle Medicine, describing lifestyle medicine as an emerging medical paradigm and anticipating its increasing importance in future medicine [1]. More than a decade later, we are again at an important inflection point.

I therefore propose Lifestyle Medicine 3.0 as an invitation to discussion, research, education, and international collaboration—not as a fixed definition, but as a framework that can evolve with emerging evidence.

Lifestyle Medicine 3.0 should integrate four complementary dimensions: precision and personalization; digital health and AI; systems and population approaches; and planetary sustainability. Its ultimate goal should be human health optimization—maximizing physical, mental, and social well-being across the lifespan while maintaining harmony with the environments in which we live.

The Journal of Lifestyle Medicine should help define, test, and disseminate this new paradigm. We should provide a platform connecting basic science, clinical medicine, behavioral science, digital innovation, public health, education, and health policy. We should encourage research that asks not only whether a lifestyle intervention prevents disease, but also for whom it works, why it works, how it can be sustained, how it can be scaled, and how it can improve healthspan and quality of life.

The advent of lifestyle medicine was driven by the recognition that lifestyle matters to health. The next chapter should be driven by an even broader recognition: health itself is the ultimate outcome.

Lifestyle Medicine 3.0 is an invitation to move beyond preventing disease toward optimizing human health—for individuals, communities, and future generations.
