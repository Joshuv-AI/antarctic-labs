 Abstract
 The Department of Energy’s Advanced Sensors and Instrumentation program seeks to develop and qualify advanced
 sensors for the nuclear industry. Reliable high temperature and high radiation sensors for detection, characterization,
 and monitoring of structural flaws in pipes, vessels, and structurally critical components are a weakness for both
 conventional light water reactors with coolant T-hot approaching 350°C, and for advanced reactors with T-hot
 temperatures in the range of 500 to 800°C and potentially higher. Magnetostrictive Electromagnetic Acoustic
 Transducers (EMATs) using a cold spray cobalt coating have been proposed as a sensor design that can withstand
 these temperatures and serve as online sensors to detect flaws before cracks, pits, or erosion/corrosion damage
 progresses to through-wall failures. This project investigates cold spray cobalt (Co) as part of a magnetostrictive
 EMAT sensor for high temperature service. Co is known to have strong magnetostrictive properties; however, the
 effect of cold spray application is not well studied. Surprisingly, cold sprayed Co exhibited little or no magnetostrictive
 behavior until thermally annealed. Following annealing to 650°C however, cold spray Co did exhibit a
 magnetostrictive response, albeit with lower amplitude and higher acoustic noise than either Ni or FeCo. Prior work
 showed that Co magnetostrictive EMAT was successfully tested to 400°C with an AlNiCo permanent magnet. This
 report reviews the permanent magnet tests and extends the investigation to consider a Hyperco® 50 core electromagnet
 EMAT to >700°C. The electromagnet sensor failed at 889oC. A second electromagnet sensor was held at 700oC for 7
 days in an argon environment and another 3 days in an air environment with no degradation in performance.
 Examinations of the sensors after heating did show some degradation and provided guidance for adjustments to
 improve the design but generally the feasibility for a long-term ultrasound monitoring sensor that can operate to and
 above 700oC is confirmed. Planned continuation of this work will include other magnetostrictive materials, and
 incorporation of lessons learned to guide sensor design adjustments.

 Summary
 Magnetostrictive EMATs have been shown to produce guided wave ultrasound that can sense flaws well
 away from the sensor. The EMAT sensor consists of a magnet to produce a bias field, and a coil through
 which a high current flows to produce a change in the magnetic field. When the changing magnetic field
 acts on a magnetostrictive material, a stress wave is produced. The wave mode is governed by the specific
 coil and magnet configuration. Normally the magnetostrictive material is the substrate under test (like steel),
 or an adhesively applied FeCo or Ni foil that transmits the acoustic wave into the substrate. Nuclear reactors
 with both high temperatures and high radiation levels are ill-suited for adhesively applied magnetostrictive
 foils. An alternative way to apply Ni or Co is to use cold spray. Magnetostrictive EMATs using cold spray
 Ni have been shown to be an effective magnetostrictive material that can sense flaws well away from the
 sensor. The magnetostrictive behavior of Ni however goes to zero as it approaches its Curie temperature
 of ~ 320oC. Cobalt is known to have even stronger magnetostrictive behavior and a Curie temperature of
 ~1115oC. An initial evaluation in 2024 (Glass, Good, et al. 2024) showed that indeed, a cobalt
 magnetostrictive EMAT with an AlNiCo permanent magnet showed a strong response to 400oC. A 100
 mm x 525 mm x 12.5 mm plate was prepared with a 100 mm square coating on the plate end. The Co was
 applied by an automated cold spray process in a robotic cold spray booth. The cold spray coating was
 controlled to between 0.2-0.5 mm. The EMAT attaches magnetically to the magnetic cobalt coating, so
 no fixture screws are required. We did however find that a clamp pressing the coils to more closely couple
 to the ms material helped the response. The response was judged based on the plate end response.
 Suprisingly the magnetostrictive cobalt showed virtually no response until it was thermally annealed at
 600oC. Thereafter the magnetostrictive EMAT showed a strong response to the maximum temperature
 tested. The response increased to several times the room temperature response up to ~ 300oC thereafter the
 response fell but the signal was still clear with good signal-to- noise behavior and an amplitude response of
 46% of the room temperature signal at the maximum test temperature of 400oC.

 The non-linear magnetostrictive response and the need to thermally anneal the cobalt material was
 micrographically evaluated with X-ray diffraction (XRD) and electron beam backscatter diffraction
 (EBSD). The high residual strain from the cold spray application may have placed the Cobalt coating into
 saturation such that the magnetostrictive response was inhibited. Once the thermal annealing and
 corresponding stress relief occurred, the coating could respond to the magnetic field change to produce the
 ultrasound stress wave. Co is a complex material that manifests in two allotropic forms; a hexagonal-
 close-packed form (HCP), which is stable at temperatures below about 400°C (673 K), and a face-centered
 cubic form (FCC) that is stable at higher temperatures up to its melting point. At atmospheric pressure, Co
 undergoes its phase transformation from HCP to FCC at ~ 450-47oC. The magnetostrictive coefficient
 ranges from ~ -50 to -70 at 20oC; to ~ 0 at 400oC; to ~+50 at 550oC. The phase transformation is suspected
 to cause the nonlinear magnetostrictive response.

 Continuing this work as described in this paper, the permanent AlNiCo magnet was replaced with an
 electromagnet. The electromagnet field strength was not as strong as with the permanent magnet. but the
 room temperature plate end response was 24% of the permanent magnet on the test sample. The
 electromagnet assembly was inserted into a test oven similarly to the permanent magnet test setup with
 wires coming out so that the EMAT response could be monitored as the test assembly was exposed to higher
 temperatures. The assembly was cycled between room temperature and 600oC, 700oC, then 800oC. The
 EMAT system failed at 889oC and on disassembly, the electromagnet was discovered to have a broken lead
 as the wire entered the electromagnet coil region. Some degradation was also noted on the wafer on which
 the EMAT coils were wound. The lead wires of a new EMAT assembly were reinforced and then the
 EMAT and the Co test plate were inserted into the oven which was ramped up to 700oC for 7 days in argon
 and held for an additional 3 days in air. At the end of the long-term test, the room temperature sensor
 response was the same amplitude as before the 10 day test.

Summary vi

 One conclusion is that a high temperature EMAT can be designed and built to withstand temperatures
 to at least 700oC for an extended period as may be required for online monitoring of nuclear reactor
 components.

 Appreciating the difficulty of using Cobalt in high radiation and particularly Neutron environments that
 will tend to transform the material to Co-60 which is extremely hazardous to personnel working in close
 proximity, and appreciating the highly nonlinear Co response, continuing work will examine these or
 similar EMAT coils on other cold spray magnetostrictive materials like FeCo, and Galfenol.

 1.0 Introduction
 Ultrasound is well understood as an effective way to interrogate nuclear power structural components for
 cracks, pits, wall thinning, and other anomalies indicative of structural damage. Piezoelectric sensors are
 widely used for periodic manual or robotic inspection to identify and resolve small defects or features of
 interest in a wide variety of materials including metals, concrete, plastics, and for medical applications.
 These piezoelectric sensors, however, are difficult to apply to high temperature components that may need
 to be inspected. Most high sensitivity piezoelectric materials degrade their performance or cease to work
 above ~ 350oC and long-term high temperature coupling between piezoelectric material and component is
 challenging even at lower temperatures.

 Electromagnetic Acoustic Transducers (EMATs) can also generate and sense ultrasonic waves in structural
 components plus they provide some advantages over piezoelectric technologies. In particular, EMAT
 sensors can couple to the structural component electromagnetically with no need for either fluid or adhesive
 coupling between the sensor and the substrate. Also, EMAT sensors may be made of materials that can
 survive to much higher temperatures than most piezoelectric materials or piezoelectric couplings.

 1.1 Lorenz Force EMATs
 Traditional Lorentz force EMATs are governed by the Lorentz force equations (Figure 1) (Nave 2017)
 named after the Dutch physicist Hendrik A. Lorentz (1853-1928). The EMAT can be configured in a
 number of different ways to direct sound in different modes and in different directions but with a Lorentz
 force EMAT, the force is generated by the coil induced eddy currents generated within the substrate
 crossed with the magnetic field to generate the ultrasonic stress wave in the substrate material (Figure
       2)(Zhang et al. 2021) (Wenze et al. 2021).

 1.2 Magnetostrictive EMATs
 Magnetostrictive EMATs (msEMAT) do not rely on these Lorentz forces discussed above. Rather, they
 rely on the magnetostrictive behavior of either the substrate material or a thin magnetostrictive coating layer
 between the EMAT coil and the substrate material under test. A magnetostrictive material changes shape
 in response to an imposition or change of the magnetic field (Joule 1842). As magnetic domains within the
 material reorient themselves to align with the magnetic field, this reorientation causes a strain in the atomic
 lattice, which leads to a measurable change in its physical dimensions. The material property that governs

Introduction 2

 magnetostriction is called the magnetostrictive coefficient (normally shown as λ) and is defined as change
 in length / nominal length (Δl/l) in response to imposition of the magnetic field. The configuration of the
 EMAT magnet and coil governs how the change in magnetic field induces the magnetostrictive stress that
 leads to propagation of the stress wave in the material. The coil current can be oscillated at the ultrasonic
 frequency of interest leading to the desired ultrasonic strain wave of interest.

 An effective ultrasonic sensor must also sense any reflected wave or in the case of a transmit/receive pair,
 it must sense changes in the transmitted ultrasonic wave. This is possible due to the reciprocal
 magnetostrictive behavior of msEMAT sensors also referred to as the Villari effect (Villari 1865). When
 mechanical stress is applied to the magnetostrictive material, it alters the alignment of magnetic domains
 within the material which may be sensed as an impedance change of the EMAT coil.

 Generally the amplitude response of a msEMAT where it may be applied is stronger and more sensitive for
 NDE detection of flaws than Lorentz approaches. (Rose 2014) asserted that magnetostrictive materials
 have a higher magneto-mechanical coupling coefficient, which results in more efficient energy conversion
 and stronger ultrasonic signals compared to those generated by Lorentz force mechanisms. (Ribichini,
 Nagy, and Ogi 2012) found that magnetostrictive EMATs deliver higher signal amplitudes and better
 sensitivity especially at lower frequencies, where Lorentz force EMATs are typically less effective. These
 references collectively support the assertion that Magnetostrictive EMATs exhibit higher sensitivity
 compared to Lorentz EMATs, making them more suitable for certain applications in non-destructive testing
 and evaluation.

 The concept of magnetostrictive ultrasound in general and magnetostrictive guided-wave ultrasound in
 particular is well understood by practitioners of the art and has been addressed by numerous researchers in
 the literature (Ali, Lopez, and Weinmeister 2017, Burrows, Fan, and Dixon 2014). One principal
 configuration of patch magnetostrictive sensing is shown in Figure 3. The sensor system consists of an
 electrically conductive meander shape, referred to as the coil, placed over the magnetostrictive layer (either
 a FeCo adhesive strip or a magnetostrictive cold-spray patch for the purpose of this publication). A
 perturbation in the magnetic field acting on the magnetostrictive material with its magnetic domains
 directionally aligned gives rise to a shear-horizontal stress wave. The acoustic wave is generated in the

 Flaw response amplitude varies as a function of the excitation angle and flaw distance from the EMAT.
 Higher angles (relative to the surface-normal) have fewer bounces and propagate longer distances along the
 plate surface, while lower angles have more reflections and must propagate longer distances within the
 plate for a similar surface propagation. Although the amplitude response generally falls off with distance,
 due to wave bouncing between the top and bottom surface, this trend may not be monotonic and may depend
 on flaws initiating from the top or bottom surface. The wave-angle, frequency (f) dependence feature was
 exploited to produce multiple measurements with the same coil by simply changing the instrument
 excitation frequency. The multiple measurements serve to either corroborate or dismiss apparent
 indications, thereby improving the probability of a correct detection and reducing the probability of a false
 detection.

 The SH0 fundamental shear wave travels at constant velocity, C, and satisfies following bulk wave
 relationship for θ=90 deg.

 f = C/(λ sinθ )

 The fundamental wave excited at 90 degrees still travels within the plate by bouncing within the top and
 bottom surfaces due to its associated beam spread. If frequency is changed causing a change in the angle of
 excitation for the SH0 wave, the wave bouncing between the walls of the plate produces higher and lower
 energy concentrations due to half and full wave skips bouncing from the far wall to the near wall.
 Significantly higher frequencies can generate a dispersive higher order SH wave mode. The preference is
 always to generate the fundamental mode rather than higher order modes for the simplicity of interpretation
 and analysis of the results.

 Any mode in the SH family can be considered as the superposition of up- and down-reflecting bulk shear
 waves polarized along the horizontal (y-plane) direction, with wave vectors lying along the vertical (z-
 plane) and axial (x-plane) directions and inclined at such an angle that the system of waves satisfies traction
 free boundary conditions on the surface of the layer (Rose 2014). The SH0 mode can be excited at multiple
 angles as long as the EMAT wavelength is greater than or on the order of thickness of the plate. However,
 in case the wavelength of the EMAT coil is less than the thickness of the plate, the higher order SH wave
 modes will be excited with its dispersive behavior limiting the range of propagation. The magnetic field
 orientation to generate both fundamental (SH0) and higher order shear horizontal waves must be parallel to
 the EMAT meander wires as explained in Figure 3.

 2.0 Magnetic Field Effect on Magnetostrictive Behavior of
 Magnetic Materials
 The magnetostrictive behavior of magnetic materials is maximized by having the magnetic bias field below
 the material’s saturation point. The degree of magnetostriction is directly proportional to the applied
 magnetic field strength up to the saturation point. Beyond this point, the material's response plateaus and
 further increases in the magnetic field do not result in additional strain. This behavior is crucial for designing
 devices that rely on magnetostrictive materials, as it defines the operational limits and efficiency of these
 devices. In this project we found that our first selection of SmCo magnets were saturating the Cobalt cold
 spray coatings and so found that the magnetostrictive response was maximized by increasing the distance
 between the magnet and the coating by as much as 25mm.

 2.1 Magnetostrictive Materials
 One of the essential components of a magnetostrictive EMAT sensor is the magnetostrictive material. This
 can either be the substrate material under test situated directly below the EMAT coil, or it can be a thin
 layer of magnetostrictive material that can couple ultrasonic waves into the substrate material under test.
 The temperature response of these materials is critical because their magnetostrictive properties can vary
 significantly with temperature. Generally speaking, the magnetostrictive response is reduced with
 increasing temperature and radiation. The room temperature magnetostrictive coefficient and Curie
 temperatures for candidate magnetostrictive sensor materials are shown in (Table 2) and are discussed in
 the following sections.

 Co is a complex material that manifests in two allotropic forms; a hexagonal-close-packed form (HCP),
 which is stable at temperatures below about 400°C (673 K), and a face-centered cubic form (FCC) that is
 stable at higher temperatures up to its melting point. At atmospheric pressure, Co undergoes its phase
 transformation from HCP to FCC at ~ 450-470oC. The magnetostrictive coefficient ranges from ~ -50 to -
 70 at 20oC; to ~ 0 at 400oC; to ~+50 at 550oC (Betteridge 1980)(Figure 7). Although the specific
 magnetostrictive response is strongly temperature dependent, Co has the highest absolute magnetostrictive
 response of all natural elements (Kim and Yang 2018). For the EMAT sensor response, the sign of the
 magnetostrictive response is unimportant; it is only the absolute value of the magnetostrictive coefficient
 that is important for the EMAT response. Above ~ 600oC, Co’s magnetostrictive coefficient decreases with
 increasing temperature back to zero at its Curie temperature of ~1121°C.

 Co is normally not manufactured as a foil or tape to be adhesively adhered to a substrate for a
 magnetostrictive sensor, but it is possible to cold spray cobalt powder (10-100 microns) onto various
 substrates including aluminum and stainless steel. The cold spray powder configuration introduces
 additional complications for producing a magnetostrictive response. Our experience is that the
 magnetostrictive reaction was very weak until the cold spay coating was thermally annealed. This may
 have relieved some of the cold-work strain within the cobalt layer and fused grain boundary separations to
 facilitate the ms response, but it may also relate to the allotrope phase modes. It is noted that thermal
 annealing was not required in Ni, so there may be other factors influencing the magnetostrictive behavior
 of Co.

 In high energy neutron and gamma radiation fields, there is also a concern for the normal isotope of Co
 (Co-59) to transform to Co-60 which constitutes a significant radiation hazard to personnel in close
 proximity.

 2.1.3 Iron Cobalt (FeCo) Alloy

 FeCo alloys are used for computer hard drives, electric motors and transformers, and electromagnetic
 sensors leveraging their useful magnetostrictive and magnetic properties. Depending on the specific
 formulation, the material may have a magnetostrictive coefficient of 70-90ppm (Nolte, Severin, and
 Hoffmann 2000) and Curie temperature from 977 to 1227oC (MacLaren et al. 1999) depending on the
 specific material distribution. The material may be produced as a tape or foil layer that may be suitable for
 magnetostrictive EMAT sensors to adhesively attach the foil to a substrate. This approach is actively used
 by EMAT sensor vendors for sensor applications where adhesives may be used. One problem however is
 that adhesives do not work well for long term high temperature or high radiation applications.

 3.1.4 Galfenol Alloy

 Galfenol is known for its excellent magnetostrictive properties and temperature stability. It has a lower
 temperature dependence compared to many other magnetostrictive materials and a Curie temperature
 around 700°C. Galfenol is an Iron-Gallium alloy with a typical ratio of 85-90% Fe and 10-15% Ga. The
 addition of Ga however increases the magnetostrictive response relative to pure iron by up to 10 times.

 2.2 Magnets
 The magnetostriction response benefits from the application of a bias magnetic field. When a magnetic
 material undergoes magnetostriction, its shape or dimensions change in response to the application of a
 magnetic field. The application of a bias magnetic field can help align the magnetic domains in the material,
 leading to a more pronounced and consistent magnetostrictive effect so long as the field strength does not
 push the material into saturation. Magnets are chosen based on the target field strength, target temperature,
 and to a lesser degree – radiation tolerance. Material choices are limited primarily to Neodymium Iron
 Boron (NdFeB), Samarium Cobalt (SmCo), and Aluminum Nickel, Cobalt (AlNiCo) permanent magnets
 and electromagnets. Relative merits of magnet choices are discussed below.

 2.2.1 Neodymium Iron Boron

 NdFeB permanent magnets have the strongest field strength, but the strength fades quickly as temperatures
 increase above 200oC. They are also more susceptible to radiation damage than SmCo or AlNiCo (Brown
 and Cost 1990). After high temperature exposure, limited recovery in magnetic properties may be seen if
 subjected to temperatures above maximum operating temperature but below the Curie temperature of 310
 – 410oC. Above the Curie temperature, demagnetization is permanent and magnetic properties will not
 return. NdFeB materials are difficult to machine, fragile, and suciptible to impact, handling damage, and
 corrosion.

 2.2.2 Samarium Cobalt

 SmCo permanent magnets have a relatively strong field strength and can operate to temperatures of 300 –
 350oC. They are less susceptible to radiation damage, corrosion, and degradation than NdFeB materials
 and may be a strong choice for msEMAT sensors. One caution is that SmCo materials are difficult to
 machine or shape, fragile, and susceptible to impact and handling damage.

 2.2.3 Aluminum Nickel Cobalt

 AlNiCo permanent magnets have the weakest field strength of the three materials considered. They can
 operate to higher temperatures with a limited reduction in performance to 500oC. They also are less
 susceptible to radiation damage than either SmCo or NdFeB. AlNiCo can be machined with conventional
 machining tools, and the finished pieces are tougher and can be more easily handled without fear of cracking
 or fracture. The field strength of AlNiCo magnets may be too weak to firmly attach an EMAT sensor to a
 magnetostrictive coating. The AlNiCo magnets can be supplemented by adjacent peripheral SmCo magnets
 to attach to the magnetostrictive layer or by mechanical straps over the AlNiCo sensor. An AlNiCo magnet
 was used for practically all of the tests for this project.

 2.2.4 Electromagnets

 Electromagnets are manufactured by winding a wire coil around a core with a high magnetic permeability
 then passing a current through the wire to generate the magnetic field. The strongest electromagnet cores
 are typically made from an alloy of Iron, Cobalt, and Vanadium. The field is proportional to the core
 magnetic permeability, the number or wire turns, and the current. Electromagnet designs can produce
 higher fields than permanent magnets within a similar space volume but the logistics of managing the wire
 and connection to a current supply make the EMAT more complex to manage. The electromagnet does
 offer an advantage for a scanning or moving sensor in that the magnet may be turned off while moving then
 energized when it is desired to attach the magnet to the magnetostrictive substrate and make a measurement.
 If the wire has a polymer or shellac insulation, then the wire insulation becomes the temperature limiting

Magnetic Field Effect on Magnetostrictive Behavior of Magnetic Materials 12

 component. In this application however, we have used a ceramic insulation nickel magnet wire that can
 function to 800oC or higher. The magnetic field strength will diminish as the temperature is elevated due to
 decrease in the core permeability, increase in the wire resistivity, and corresponding reduction in coil
 current but these variables can be managed, and the electromagnet should still have significant field strength
 at temperatures up to 800oC.

 5.0 Permanent Magnet msEMAT Performance to 400oC
 The permanent magnet EMAT configuration was initially tested with a samarium cobalt (SmCo)
 permanent magnet with a very high field strength that produced quite poor results. We noted
 that responses improved significantly by elevating the magnet more than 10cm above the coil.
 This indicated that the EMAT was trying to operate in the saturated range of the BxH curve.
 The AlNiCo magnet (with a field strength of 71 mT) produced much better results plus it was
 easier to handle. The AlNiCo magnet was also less likely to impact and fracture as it attached

 degradation of the magnetic field strength. As shown in Figure 15, the 27oC 2nd end-plate

 8.0 Plans for sensor improvements and continued research
 The EMAT design and performance was reviewed by the PNNL staff and also with external
 experts skilled in high temperature EMAT fabrication (T-axel 2016) and with msEMAT
 instrumentation expertise (INNERSPEC 2020). Recommendations for improved sensor
 response were:

 • The EMAT coil should be wound for a push/pull excitation taking advantage of the
 +,ground,- connection on the VOLTA EMAT instrument.

 • The degraded mica wafer might be expected for exposure above 800oC. For long term
 operation, the wafer and coil form might be machined from Aluminum Oxide to better
 withstand the higher temperatures.

 • The Ni wire is subject to oxidation and for extended operation (10 years or more), Ni
 alloys or platinum that may be a little higher in resistivity but would resist oxidation
 better. If the environment is argon, then oxidation will not be a problem but in air,
 oxidation will significantly influence wire resistance over a prolonged exposure (like 10
 years or more).

 • Wire insulation degradation was observed. This can be mitigated by including an
 alumina fiber into the insulation. This will have a drawback though of slightly increasing
 the wire diameter thereby limiting the number of coils that can be incorporated into the
 sensor.

 The Cobalt magnetostrictive material did not have the expected magnetostrictive response plus
 the substantially nonlinear behavior as a function of temperature may complicate signal
 interpretation under different temperature conditions. Other magnetostrictive materials are
 planned to be investigated – primarily FeCo and Galfenol.

 9.0 Observations and Conclusions
 High Temperature Magnetostrictive EMAT does seem feasible up to 400oC with an AlNiCo permanent
 magnet/ Co cold sprayed sensor. The AlNiCo magnets are known to undergo irreversible reduction in
 magnetic strength at temperatures above 400oC and so were not tested to higher levels. The magnetic field
 strength at room temperature remained unchanged despite exposure from multiple data acquisition runs to
 400oC.

 The permanent magnet sensor response more than doubled between 25 and 250oC. Between 250 and 300oC,
 the response dropped but then continued to rise as the temperature approached 400oC. The sensor system
 at 400oC showed a persistent plate end response of 49% of the room temperature response. The 400oC
 temperature signal quality was also quite similar to or even slightly improved from the room temperature
 response. These behaviors further support feasibility of a Co msEMAT sensor up to 400oC.

 Surprising differences between cold spray Ni and cold spray Co were observed. In particular, the as-sprayed
 Ni produced a strong magnetostrictive response but the Co (which – according to the literature should have
 demonstrated a stronger response) only showed a magnetostrictive response only after thermal annealing.

 The two phase modes of Co (HCP and FCC) were noted by XRD in 500oC annealed, N2 sprayed Co. Only
 HCP mode was observed by XRD in the 650oC, H2 sprayed Co. XRD tests of unannealed samples were
 inconclusive. The magnitude of the ms. coefficient is known to vary widely between the two modes, and
 this may have a bearing on sensor response, but further study is required to understand phase mode
 influences on the sensor response.

 The electromagnet sensor configuration should be able to retain a significant magnetic field well above
 400oC. The electromagnet was not as strong as the permanent AlNiCo magnet but there was still a strong
 EMAT response up to the failure point at 889oC. Autopsy of the failed sensor showed that the lead wires
 to the electromagnet failed but there was also degradation of the ceramic insulation and the mica coil
 support wafer.

 A new electromagnet sensor was re-tested for 7 days in an argon gas environment at 700oC and for an
 additional 3 days in air with virtually no change in the sensor response. After the long-term test at 700oC,
 visual inspection of the assembly showed oxidation of the Ni wire and some insulation cracking and
 embrittlement. Neither the mica degradation nor any lead-wire failures (after lead-wire strain-relief
 protection) as noted following the 889oC test-to-failure were observed. Several potential improvements to
 the sensor were identified but generally the tests performed confirmed the feasibility of using a cold spray
 Co msEMAT for long term monitoring.
