Bayesian Thinking in the Intensive Care Unit: From Statistical Theory to Clinical Practice
Authors: Marcus J Schultz, Prashant Nasa, Swagata Tripathy, Tonny Veenith, Ary Serpa Neto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCritical care medicine operates in an environment of profound uncertainty, where clinicians must make high-stakes decisions based on incomplete, evolving, and often conflicting information. Despite this, most critical care research is frequentist-based, relying on static thresholds, dichotomous interpretations of evidence, and delayed incorporation of new data. This paradigm may not fully align with the dynamic and probabilistic nature of critical illness. Bayesian approaches offer an alternative framework that explicitly incorporates prior knowledge, continuously updates probabilities as new data emerge, and supports real-time, individualized decision-making. Rather than asking whether an intervention “works” in a binary sense, Bayesian methods estimate the probability of benefit or harm in a given clinical context, thereby aligning more closely with bedside reasoning. Importantly, such approaches are no longer theoretical. Adaptive platform trials have demonstrated the feasibility of Bayesian methodologies at scale, enabling continuous learning, dynamic treatment allocation, and simultaneous evaluation of multiple interventions. In this viewpoint, we explore how Bayesian decision-making could extend beyond research into routine intensive care practice. We discuss its potential to enhance clinical judgment, personalize therapy, and integrate heterogeneous data streams into coherent probabilistic estimates. The question is no longer whether Bayesian methods can be implemented, but how quickly and effectively they can be embedded into everyday critical care practice.Keywords: Bayesian method, Bayesian theorem, Intensive care unit, Methods, Research design, Research methodology

Introduction

Picture this: You are reviewing the latest acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) ventilation guideline, and something does not quite add up.1 The evidence synthesis shows no clear benefit for low tidal volumes, yet the guidelines still recommend them. Meanwhile, high positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) demonstrated no clear benefit, and in this instance, guideline recommendations were more cautious. Such differences highlight that guideline decisions are influenced by multiple considerations beyond statistical significance alone, including certainty of evidence, magnitude of effect, risk–benefit balance, and evidence-to-decision frameworks. Rather than reflecting on the limitations of methodology itself (frequentist methodology in this case), these examples illustrate the challenges in interpreting and translating statistical findings into clinical recommendations.

It is 2026, and we are still making clinical decisions as if it were 1990. While we have made tremendous advances in critical care, our statistical approaches to evidence synthesis have not always kept pace with the complexity of modern practice. Of course, frequentist statistics have brought enormous progress to critical care, enabling us to test interventions rigorously and move beyond anecdote or tradition. These methods remain invaluable. Yet, their limitations become apparent when applied to the complex, uncertain decisions of modern intensive care unit (ICU) practice. Meanwhile, there is a 250-year-old mathematical framework that could revolutionize how we practice medicine, if only we had given the reverend Thomas Bayes a seat at the clinical decision-making table.

The Current State of Affairs—Evidence in Isolation

In critical care research, randomized clinical trials are frequently interpreted through statistical metrics, such as p values and confidence intervals (CIs), which are the foundation of the frequentist approach (Table 1). Although the evidence-based medicine appropriately incorporates multiple factors, including effect size, study quality, biological plausibility, and clinical context, interpretation in practice may sometimes become overly influenced by dichotomous thresholds of statistical significance. Such reliance on binary interpretations may contribute to the oversimplification of uncertainty and treatment effects.

Frequentist statistics is not a single unified framework and has evolved through several schools of thought. The Fisherian approach focuses on p-values as a measure of evidence against a null hypothesis. For example, in a trial evaluating corticosteroids in septic shock, a p-value of < 0.05 may be interpreted as evidence against the null hypothesis of no treatment effect. In contrast, the Neyman–Pearson framework emphasizes predefined hypotheses, type I and type II error rates, and CIs to guide decision-making. For example, a 95% CI around a mortality risk ratio in a fluid resuscitation trial helps determine whether a clinically meaningful effect can be excluded. A third approach, the likelihood framework, focuses on comparing the relative support that observed data provide for competing hypotheses. For instance, likelihood ratios are routinely applied in clinical diagnosis, such as estimating the probability of pulmonary embolism after D-dimer testing. This approach treats each study as if it exists in a vacuum.

Take the ECMO to rescue lung injury in severe ARDS (EOLIA) trial on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) for severe ARDS.2 The frequentist analysis showed no statistically significant benefit (p = 0.09). Case closed, right? Not quite. When the same data underwent Bayesian reanalysis, it suggested a meaningful probability of benefit.3 Another example is the TracMan trial—A large randomized controlled trial (RCT) from the United Kingdom found no significant mortality benefit from early tracheostomy.4 However, a Bayesian meta-analysis synthesizing 19 RCTs and 3,508 patients demonstrated a 99% posterior probability of at least some mortality benefit, along with high probabilities of reduced ventilator-associated pneumonia, as well as shorter ventilation ICU and stay.4 The frequentist result was not wrong; it was simply uninformative when viewed in isolation. Bayesian synthesis revealed what the single trial could not: The direction and magnitude of benefit supported by the totality of evidence. Yet, we largely ignored this insight because it did not fit our familiar statistical framework.

This pattern repeats constantly. We conduct expensive, time-consuming studies and then analyze them using methods that often fail to capture the full story. It is like having a sophisticated diagnostic tool but only reading half the results.

The Meta-analysis Mirage

Meta-analyses were supposed to solve this problem by combining multiple studies to give us more reliable answers. Often, evidence accumulates across multiple studies and is synthesized through meta-analyses in an attempt to refine estimates and reduce uncertainty surrounding treatment effects Sometimes, they work brilliantly. Other times, as we have observed with ventilation strategies, they create new puzzles.

The recent ESICM guidelines for ARDS perfectly illustrate this paradox.1 For low tidal volumes, meta-analyses failed to show a clear benefit, yet the guidelines maintained strong recommendations. The guideline committee wrote, “Although the mortality summary estimate did not achieve statistical significance, in developing our recommendation statements, we considered the extremely strong physiologic rationale underpinning the use of low tidal volume ventilation based on animal and human studies.” However, for high PEEP, meta-analyses also showed no clear benefit, but this time the guidelines translated this into weaker recommendations, writing they were “unable to make a recommendation for or against PEEP titration to reduce mortality in patients with ARDS.” Although similar statistical approaches were used to synthesize evidence, the resulting recommendations differed because guideline development incorporates broader considerations beyond statistical findings alone, including biological plausibility, certainty of evidence, and evidence-to-decision frameworks. This approach may appear pragmatic, but it also highlights and acknowledges a fundamental problem: Frequentist statistical methods often leave us uncertain even when we have substantial evidence. We are forced to make binary decisions (“yes” or “no,” “recommend” or “not recommend”) based on tools designed for hypothesis testing, not clinical decision-making.

Enter the Bayesian World

Bayesian statistics offers a fundamentally different approach. This approach involves inverse probability, which means estimating the likelihood of a hypothesis based on the observed data. Unlike the frequentist approach, which evaluates the probability of observing data under a null hypothesis, Bayesian methods estimate the probability that a treatment effect or hypothesis is true after considering available evidence. This approach is often appealing to clinicians because it more closely aligns with bedside reasoning.

Consider how different the EOLIA story might have been with routine Bayesian analysis.2,3 Clinicians typically wish to know: “Given the available evidence, what is the probability that a treatment will benefit my patient?” rather than “Assuming no treatment effect, how likely are the observed data?” Instead of relying solely on a binary statistically significant/non-significant interpretation, Bayesian analyses provide probabilistic estimates, such as “There is a 92% probability that ECMO reduces mortality by at least 2% in this population.” Such outputs do not eliminate the need for clinical decisions; rather, they provide richer information that can be integrated with decision thresholds, expected utility, patient preferences, and risk–benefit considerations. Whether a 2% mortality reduction justifies intervention ultimately depends on the clinical context and values underlying decision-making.

This framework naturally incorporates prior knowledge. When evaluating a new ventilation strategy, we never start from “zero”: We have decades of physiological understanding, animal studies, and clinical experience. Bayesian analysis lets us combine this background knowledge with new trial data to reach more nuanced conclusions (Table 2).5

From a philosophical perspective, frequentist and Bayesian approaches differ in their underlying modes of reasoning. Frequentist inference traditionally follows a deductive framework in which predefined hypotheses are tested against observed data under fixed assumptions. In contrast, Bayesian reasoning is more closely aligned with induction, where prior knowledge is updated with accumulating evidence to refine estimates and beliefs. Although these distinctions arise from the philosophy of science, they may also influence how evidence is interpreted and applied in clinical decision-making.

Bayesian inference is not a single unified framework and has evolved through different philosophical traditions. The subjective Bayesian perspective, associated with de Finetti and Savage, interprets probability as a degree of belief that may incorporate prior knowledge or expert opinion. In contrast, Jeffreys proposed a more objective Bayesian approach using formal mathematical principles to derive priors that minimize subjective influence. Although these distinctions arise from statistical philosophy, they influence approaches to prior specification and interpretation in clinical research.6,7

Bayesian reasoning already exists in everyday ICU practice. Intensivists frequently revise diagnostic and therapeutic decisions by integrating prior knowledge with evolving clinical information, such as hemodynamic response, imaging findings, laboratory results, and treatment trajectories. This iterative process resembles Bayesian updating and may complement formal statistical applications in critical care research.

For example, an intensivist evaluating a critically ill patient with sudden hypoxemia and tachycardia may initially estimate a moderate probability of pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE) based on clinical assessment. A normal D-dimer result in a low-risk patient substantially lowers the probability of PTE, whereas elevated D-dimer levels in an appropriate clinical context increase diagnostic suspicion. Similarly, in a patient with chest pain and hemodynamic instability, clinicians continuously revise the likelihood of myocardial infarction based on pre-test probability and serial troponin measurements. This process of updating prior beliefs with new information closely resembles Bayesian inference and reflects how clinicians routinely make decisions under uncertainty.

Why are We Not Yet Bayesian?

If Bayesian methods are so superior, why are we not using them routinely? Several barriers exist:

Educational inertia—Most clinicians learned frequentist statistics in medical school; Bayesian thinking requires a conceptual shift that feels foreign initially;

Regulatory conservatism—Drug regulators have traditionally preferred frequentist approaches, although this is slowly changing;

Computational complexity—Bayesian analyses can be more complex to perform, although modern software has largely solved this problem;

Cultural resistance—The medical establishment is notoriously slow to adopt new methodologies, even beneficial ones; and

Fear of subjectivity—While Bayesian approaches offer several conceptual and practical advantages, they are not without limitations. Results can be sensitive to the choice of prior distributions and model assumptions, and transparency in their specification is essential to ensure interpretability and trust. For example, in the Bayesian analysis of the EOLIA trial, the posterior probability for ECMO benefit depends strongly on the prior used, and this is not always made explicit.3 Any Bayesian interpretation of EOLIA should therefore acknowledge both the dependence on prior assumptions and the weight of the extended outcome data, which together give a more accurate and transparent account of the evidence.

A Day in Bayes’ ICU

Imagine if Bayesian thinking became standard in critical care, here is what might change:

Precision medicine—The treatment effects in critical care may not be uniform across patients, centers, or care environments. Large multicenter trials often summarize outcomes as average treatment effects, which may obscure meaningful heterogeneity arising from differences in case-mix, local practices, and implementation. In such settings, Bayesian approaches, particularly hierarchical models, may offer an advantage by explicitly accounting for this variability rather than collapsing it into a single summary estimate. For example, an elderly patient with multiple comorbidities would not automatically be assumed to benefit to the same extent as a young, otherwise healthy patient with the same diagnosis. Thus, Bayesian methods provide the natural statistical language for precision medicine in the ICU. They allow us to ask, for a specific heterogeneous patient at a specific moment, the probability of benefit, given the individual's clinical profile. This mirrors how clinicians already reason at the bedside.

Trial interpretation—Every new study would be interpreted in the context of existing evidence; for example, a trial showing “no significant effect” might still be valuable if it shifts our probability estimates in a meaningful direction, while a trial showing “statistical significance” might be less convincing if its small effect size contradicts substantial prior evidence suggesting no benefit.

Continuous physiological variables are frequently encountered in ICU practice and may provide intuitive examples of Bayesian reasoning. For example, an intensivist evaluating fluid responsiveness may integrate prior knowledge regarding expected cardiac output changes with observed hemodynamic measurements from echocardiography or pulse contour analysis. Unlike simple discrete examples, continuous variables generate probability distributions rather than binary outcomes. Bayesian inference requires combining prior distributions with observed data to derive posterior distributions, often involving integration across complex probability functions. In practical clinical problems with multiple variables and parameters, these integrations may become mathematically intractable. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods address this challenge by repeatedly sampling from the posterior distribution and approximating it numerically, allowing estimation of clinically meaningful probabilities without requiring direct analytical solutions.

Guidelines with nuance—Instead of blanket recommendations, guidelines would provide probability-based guidance, such as “for patients with these characteristics, there is an XX% chance this intervention provides meaningful benefit.”

Adaptive learning—As new evidence emerges, our probability estimates would continuously update; we would not need to wait for the next meta-analysis to incorporate new information.

Resource allocation in research—The randomized embedded multifactorial adaptive platform for community-acquired pneumonia (REMAP-CAP) trial provides a compelling real-world demonstration of Bayesian adaptive decision-making in critical care research.8 This platform utilizes Bayesian statistical methods to continuously update posterior probabilities of treatment effectiveness as data accrue, enabling dynamic allocation of patients to the most promising interventions.5 Importantly, it operates across multiple therapeutic domains simultaneously, allowing efficient evaluation of complex, multifactorial treatment strategies within a single trial infrastructure.

This approach illustrates that Bayesian decision support is not merely theoretical or aspirational. Rather, it is actively being implemented to guide treatment allocation in real time while iteratively refining estimates of treatment effect.

The Uncomfortable Truth

Here is what Bayes would tell us if he ran the ICU: “We are already Bayesian thinkers, whether we admit it or not. Every time we consider a patient's clinical context when applying study results, we are updating our prior beliefs with new evidence. Every time we are more convinced by consistent findings across multiple studies than by a single large trial, we are Thinking like a Bayesian!”

The difference is that we are doing this informally and inconsistently. Formal Bayesian methods would make this process transparent, reproducible, and more accurate.

The Way Forward

Adopting Bayesian methods in critical care will not happen overnight, but we can start immediately:

Education—Medical schools and training programs need to teach Bayesian concepts alongside traditional statistics.

Research culture—Journals should encourage Bayesian analyses alongside frequentist analyses.

Guideline methodology—Guideline committees should explicitly incorporate Bayesian thinking into their deliberations.

Software tools—User-friendly Bayesian analysis tools need wider dissemination.

Pilot projects—Forward-thinking ICUs could begin applying Bayesian decision-making to specific clinical scenarios; this should not be limited to personalized tidal volume and PEEP titrations, but also neuromuscular blockade decisions and prone positioning choices, to name a few; such approaches could naturally extend beyond ventilation to other ICU decisions involving uncertainty and individualized risk–benefit calculations.

Conclusion

Better decisions, better outcomes! Clinical decision-making in the ICU involves constructing and evaluating competing hypotheses while integrating evidence, uncertainty, physiological reasoning, and contextual factors. Although statistical methods provide important tools for hypothesis testing and inference, translating evidence into clinical action requires probabilistic reasoning and judgment under uncertainty. Bayesian approaches may complement this process by explicitly quantifying uncertainty and updating beliefs as new evidence emerges. It is time for critical care to embrace its inner Bayesian. Our patients deserve decisions based on all available evidence, not just the subset that fits our preferred statistical framework. After all, in the ICU, every decision is a bet on uncertainty. Our patients deserve us to place those bets with the best possible odds (Fig. 1).
