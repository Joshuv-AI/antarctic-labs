How do large language models answer ADHD-related questions? A comparative study of ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek
Authors: Berrin Bilgiç, Serkan Turan, Sibelnur Avcil, Aynur Akay Pekcanlar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveLarge language models (LLMs) are increasingly used by patients and caregivers as sources of health information. However, their performance in addressing attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)–related questions has not been systematically compared. This study aimed to evaluate and compare the accuracy, reproducibility, quality, usefulness, and reliability of responses generated by ChatGPT (GPT-4o), Gemini, and DeepSeek R1.MethodsIn this cross-sectional comparative study, 22 commonly asked ADHD-related questions identified from publicly available digital sources were categorized into four domains: basic knowledge, diagnosis and assessment, treatment and medication, and long-term outcomes. Each question was presented to all three models using the same standardized prompts in separate chat sessions. The generated responses were independently evaluated by two specialists in child and adolescent psychiatry. Reproducibility was examined by repeating the same queries on different days. Descriptive statistics and non-parametric repeated-measures analyses were used to compare model performance.ResultsAll models showed high overall accuracy, with mean scores of 91% for ChatGPT (GPT-4o), 89% for Gemini, and 87% for DeepSeek R1. Reproducibility followed a similar pattern (89%, 86%, and 84%, respectively). Gemini and DeepSeek performed relatively better in basic knowledge and diagnostic domains, whereas ChatGPT (GPT-4o) showed stronger performance in treatment and long-term outcome–related questions. Significant differences were observed in quality, usefulness, and reliability across models, with ChatGPT (GPT-4o) achieving the highest overall expert-rated scores.Conclusion:Although large language models generally provided accurate responses to ADHD-related questions, notable differences were observed in the depth, clarity, and clinical usefulness of the information across models. These systems may serve as supportive sources of information for patients and caregivers; however, their responses should be interpreted with caution and should not replace professional clinical evaluation or medical advice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08280-x.

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by developmentally inappropriate levels of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that arise in early life and impair functioning across multiple domains [1]. Although ADHD was historically considered primarily a childhood condition, a growing body of research demonstrates that symptoms frequently persist into adolescence and adulthood. Current global estimates suggest a prevalence of approximately 7% in children, 5.6% in adolescents, and between 2.5 and 6% in adults [2]. Given its high prevalence and long-term functional impact, ADHD represents a major public health concern affecting patients, families, and healthcare systems worldwide.

Because ADHD affects academic performance, family relationships, and psychosocial functioning, patients and caregivers frequently seek information regarding symptoms, diagnosis, treatment options, and long-term outcomes. Traditionally, such information was obtained from healthcare professionals, printed materials, or institutional guidance. However, the rapid expansion of digital technologies and widespread internet Access has significantly transformed health information–seeking behaviors. Increasingly, patients and caregivers turn to online environments to obtain medical information, share experiences, and seek support. Digital platforms provide immediate access to diverse health resources, contributing to the growing reliance on online and interactive sources for health-related information [3–5].

Despite the accessibility of online health information, the quality and reliability of information disseminated across digital platforms can vary considerably. Previous studies have demonstrated that internet-based health resources may simultaneously offer valuable educational opportunities while also posing risks of misinformation that can influence health-related decision-making [6, 7]. These concerns are particularly relevant in the context of ADHD, where parents frequently rely on digital resources when making decisions regarding assessment, treatment options, and long-term management strategies for their children [8, 9].

In recent years, rapid advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have further transformed the production and accessibility of digital health information. Among these developments, large language models (LLMs) based on the Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) architecture have gained substantial attention. Trained on extensive textual datasets, these models are capable of generating coherent natural language responses and addressing complex user queries. ChatGPT represents one of the most widely recognized applications of this technology and has attracted considerable interest in both public and academic contexts [10]. Emerging research suggests that LLM-based systems may provide potential benefits in educational and clinical environments by summarizing medical information, explaining clinical concepts, and responding to patient questions [11]. Consequently, AI-powered conversational systems are increasingly being explored as tools that may support patient education and health information seeking in digital settings [12, 13].

In addition to general-purpose models such as ChatGPT, several new large language models developed by different technology companies have emerged in recent years. Among these are Gemini, developed by Google, and DeepSeek, which has recently attracted considerable attention. These models aim to improve performance across various knowledge domains through the use of larger training datasets, advanced training strategies, and, in some cases, multimodal processing capabilities. The ability to integrate and analyze multiple data types—including text, images, and other modalities—may further expand the potential applications of these systems in healthcare contexts, particularly in areas such as medical information retrieval, health-related content generation, and clinical decision support [14, 15].

Nevertheless, the use of large language models in healthcare also raises important limitations and safety considerations. These systems may generate inaccurate or misleading information, reflect biases present in their training data, or demonstrate limitations in reasoning when addressing complex clinical questions [16, 17].

For instance, previous studies evaluating ChatGPT in psychiatric contexts have reported generally relevant responses while highlighting limitations in areas requiring detailed domain-specific knowledge, such as pharmacological information [18]. Because many LLMs are trained primarily on large-scale web-based data rather than curated academic sources, systematic evaluations of their reliability and clinical appropriateness are increasingly necessary.

Building on this growing need for systematic evaluation, a number of recent studies have investigated the performance of large language models across various medical and clinical contexts. For example, research in clinical psychiatry has shown that ChatGPT can generate generally relevant responses to psychiatric questions, although limitations remain regarding the depth and clinical reliability of certain answers [18]. Similarly, comparative studies assessing ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek in answering medical questions have demonstrated that these models can provide generally informative responses, while also revealing variability in accuracy, readability, and overall response quality across systems [19]. Beyond psychiatry-focused evaluations, recent investigations have also explored the performance of large language models in broader clinical decision-support scenarios. For instance, studies assessing ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek in emergency department triage simulations based on real-world clinical conversations have demonstrated moderate to high accuracy in classifying patient urgency [20]. Similarly, benchmark analyses examining the capabilities of DeepSeek models in clinical decision-making tasks have reported promising performance while emphasizing that accuracy may vary depending on the complexity of the clinical scenario [21]. In addition to clinician-oriented applications, an increasing number of studies have examined how large language models respond to patient-oriented health questions. For example, comparative evaluations of ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek addressing autism-related questions from parents reported generally informative responses without statistically significant differences between models [22]. Comparable findings have been reported in studies focusing on learning disorders such as dyslexia and dyscalculia, where overall response quality appeared similar across models, although reliability varied depending on the clinical topic [23].

However, despite the growing number of studies evaluating large language models across medical and mental health domains, comparative evidence examining how these systems respond to ADHD-related questions remains limited. Given the high prevalence of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and the increasing reliance of patients and caregivers on digital sources of health information, understanding the reliability and educational value of AI-generated responses in this context is particularly important. To our knowledge, no previous study has systematically compared the performance of multiple large language models in answering ADHD-related questions posed by patients and caregivers. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate whether widely used large language models can provide accurate, useful, and reliable information when responding to common ADHD-related questions and to systematically compare the performance of three widely used systems—ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek.

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design to evaluate the performance of large language models (LLMs) in responding to commonly asked patient-related questions about attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Three widely used AI-based conversational systems—ChatGPT (GPT-4o), Google Gemini, and DeepSeek-R1—were systematically assessed.

The primary objective was to examine the accuracy, usefulness, reliability, and overall quality of responses generated by these models when addressing questions commonly asked by patients, parents, and caregivers regarding ADHD. Because the study analyzed AI-generated textual responses and did not involve human participants, personal data, or patient records, institutional ethics approval was not required.

Question identification and selection

To develop the question set, a semi-structured search strategy was implemented to identify frequently asked questions related to ADHD. A multi-platform content exploration approach was conducted between February and March 2026 using search terms reflecting common parental concerns, including “ADHD symptoms in children,” “Does my child have ADHD?” “ADHD treatment,” “ADHD medication side effects,” “How is ADHD diagnosed?”, and “long-term outcomes of ADHD.”

Questions were identified through content exploration of publicly available digital sources and user-generated online content, including Google search trends, online parenting forums, social media discussions, and child and adolescent mental health information platforms. For search engine queries, the first 2–3 pages of results were reviewed, as these pages are more likely to reflect the information commonly accessed by the general public. Additional sources included publicly accessible ADHD-related mental health and parenting resources, including the Children and Adults with Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (CHADD) and American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry (AACAP) websites, from which additional question themes were derived.

Platform selection was guided by the aim of capturing authentic parental information-seeking behavior across diverse digital environments. These sources were examined to capture commonly expressed concerns and information needs among patients, parents, and caregivers regarding ADHD symptoms, diagnostic processes, treatment options, and long-term outcomes.

Following the initial collection process, candidate questions were independently reviewed by two clinicians specializing in child and adolescent psychiatry. The selection process aimed to capture commonly recurring themes and clinically relevant concerns expressed across multiple digital sources rather than to establish a quantitative ranking of question frequency. Questions that appeared repeatedly across multiple sources or reflected commonly expressed parental concerns were prioritized for inclusion. Questions were selected based on their clinical relevance, recurrence across online discussions, and representativeness of key domains of ADHD assessment and management. Repetitive or highly similar questions were combined and refined for clarity and clinical relevance. Through this process, a final set of 22 questions was compiled. The final number of 22 questions was considered sufficient because the questions were selected to ensure representation of all predefined conceptual domains rather than to exhaustively capture every possible ADHD-related query. The question set covered the most frequently recurring and clinically relevant themes identified across the reviewed sources, including symptoms, diagnostic assessment, treatment, medication-related concerns, long-term outcomes, and functional adaptation. This scope was considered appropriate for a comparative evaluation of model performance while maintaining feasibility for a detailed expert-based assessment.

The selected questions were subsequently categorized into four conceptual domains:Basic knowledge about ADHD (e.g., definition, core symptoms, common misconceptions)Diagnostic recognition and assessment (e.g., age of diagnosis, assessment methods, differentiation from typical childhood behavior)Treatment and medication (e.g., stimulant safety, behavioral interventions, medication side effects)Long-term outcomes and functional adaptation (e.g., academic functioning, persistence into adulthood, family and school support strategies)

AI response generation

Model prompt standardization

To maintain methodological consistency and reduce potential bias related to prompting, a uniform prompt protocol was applied across all models. Each question was submitted in a separate, newly initiated chat session without any prior interaction history. Default settings were retained for all models, and no advanced reasoning or search configurations (e.g., Thinking or Deep Search modes) were activated during the evaluation process. All questions were presented in English using identical phrasing to ensure comparability. Additionally, the sequence in which models were queried was randomized to minimize possible temporal or system-related influences. All queries were conducted in March 2026 using the freely accessible versions of ChatGPT (OpenAI), Gemini (Google), and DeepSeek. Internet browsing, memory, and personalization features were not used during the evaluation process, and all models were assessed under their default safety settings. The complete set of standardized prompts used during model evaluation is presented in Supplementary File 1. To improve methodological transparency and reproducibility, the full original responses generated by ChatGPT (GPT-4o), Gemini, and DeepSeek R1 for all evaluated questions are provided in Supplementary File 2.

Response generation procedure

Each of the 22 questions was submitted to three large language models: ChatGPT (GPT-4o), Google Gemini, and DeepSeek-R1. All interactions were conducted using the default system settings of each model. The same standardized prompts were used for all three models to ensure comparability across responses. Responses generated by the models were subsequently collected for analysis. No plug-ins, external tools, or additional system instructions were used during the interactions. To evaluate response stability, each question was submitted again in a separate session on a different day using the same procedure. This repeated prompting approach enabled the assessment of response reproducibility by examining whether the core informational content of the responses remained consistent across independent prompting sessions.

AI response collection and dataset preparation

All responses generated by the language models were recorded in their original form immediately upon completion, without any modification or post-processing. The collected responses were subsequently compiled into a structured dataset and prepared for analysis. To ensure objectivity, all responses were anonymized prior to evaluation by assigning randomly generated identification codes, and any information that could reveal the originating model was removed.

Response evaluation

To reduce the risk of evaluation bias, all responses were anonymized prior to assessment. Each response was assigned a randomly generated identification label (e.g., Model A, Model B, Model C), and any information indicating the source model was removed. In addition, the sequence of responses was randomized before evaluation. The reviewers remained unaware of the originating model throughout the entire assessment process.

Accuracy assessment

Response accuracy was evaluated using an expert-derived reference framework developed on the basis of established clinical guidelines and evidence-based literature related to ADHD. The reference framework incorporated diagnostic criteria from the DSM-5-TR as well as recommendations from evidence-based ADHD clinical practice guidelines, including guidelines published by the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry (AACAP) and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE).

The expert-derived framework was designed to assess whether responses provided clinically accurate, guideline-aligned, and developmentally appropriate information regarding ADHD symptoms, diagnostic assessment, treatment approaches, medication-related issues, and long-term outcomes. Two independent specialists in child and adolescent psychiatry evaluated each response to determine the degree to which the information aligned with current clinical knowledge. Both evaluators had more than 10 years of clinical experience in ADHD assessment and management. Responses were rated according to their level of agreement with guideline-consistent information.

Accuracy assessment was conducted on an item-by-item basis using a categorical expert-rating framework developed for the present study. Each response was independently evaluated by two child and adolescent psychiatry specialists according to its consistency with current evidence-based ADHD knowledge and guideline-aligned clinical information.

Responses were scored using a 3-point rubric:

0 = inaccurate or clinically misleading information,

1 = partially accurate or incomplete information,

2 = clinically accurate and guideline-consistent information.

For each model, overall accuracy percentages were calculated by dividing the total obtained accuracy score by the maximum possible score across all evaluated responses and multiplying by 100. Higher percentages therefore reflected greater overall alignment with evidence-based clinical information and established ADHD guidelines.

Inter-rater agreement between the evaluators was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. When discrepancies occurred, responses were re-evaluated and consensus was reached through discussion.

Quality, usefulness, and reliability assessment

The overall quality of responses was evaluated using a structured expert rating framework adapted from previous studies assessing AI-generated medical information. The modified Global Quality Scale (GQS), which has been widely used to evaluate the quality of online health information, was employed for this purpose. Quality assessment focused on several key dimensions, including clarity, completeness, educational value, and clinical relevance of the information provided in response to ADHD-related questions commonly asked by patients, parents, and caregivers. Reliability assessment also considered whether responses included appropriate cautionary statements regarding diagnosis, medication-related issues, and the need for professional clinical evaluation in potentially high-risk or clinically sensitive situations.

All responses were independently evaluated under blinded conditions by two specialists child and adolescent psychiatrists with more than 10 years of clinical experience in the assessment and management of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD).

The GQS is a 5-point scale, with higher scores indicating higher quality information. In addition to overall quality ratings, responses were also evaluated for usefulness and reliability using a 7-point Likert scale. Usefulness reflected the extent to which the response provided understandable, practical, and actionable information for patients and caregivers seeking guidance about ADHD. Reliability was evaluated based on the perceived trustworthiness of the information, including factual accuracy, internal consistency, and the absence of misleading or unsupported claims. For both dimensions, scores ranged from 1 (very poor) to 7 (excellent).

All responses were rated independently by both evaluators. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ = 0.82), reflecting overall agreement across the blinded expert evaluations of accuracy, quality, usefulness, reliability, and reproducibility dimensions, indicating excellent agreement. In cases of disagreement, responses were re-evaluated and consensus was reached through discussion.

Reproducibility assessment

Response reproducibility was assessed by comparing the informational content of initial outputs with those generated through repeated prompts conducted in separate sessions on different days. Repeated prompts were conducted using the same standardized procedure and identical question wording across independent chat sessions. Reproducibility was operationalized as the consistency of core informational content across repeated responses. Core informational consistency referred to the preservation of key clinical and educational content across repeated responses, including (i) similar explanations regarding ADHD symptoms and diagnosis, (ii) comparable treatment- or management-related guidance, and (iii) the absence of major contradictions in the overall clinical message. Minor variations in wording, phrasing, or level of detail were not considered indicative of inconsistency if the overall clinical meaning remained unchanged. Responses were classified as reproducible when the primary informational elements and key clinical recommendations remained stable across iterations. Reproducibility assessments were performed independently and under blinded conditions by the same two child and adolescent psychiatry specialists involved in the primary evaluation process. The reviewers were unaware of the originating model during reproducibility assessment. In cases of disagreement, responses were re-evaluated and final decisions were reached through consensus discussion. For each model, a reproducibility rate (%) was calculated based on the proportion of responses demonstrating consistent core content. This approach allowed for the evaluation of response stability across independent prompting sessions, which is a critical consideration for the reliability of large language models as sources of health information. Representative examples of reproducible and partially non-reproducible responses are provided in Supplementary Table 3 to improve the transparency and interpretability of the reproducibility assessment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics for quality, usefulness, and reliability scores were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), given the ordinal nature of the rating scales. Because the same 22 ADHD-related questions were evaluated across all three large language models, comparisons were conducted using the Friedman test as a non-parametric repeated-measures approach that accounts for clustering by question. When significant overall differences were identified, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction. Exact p-values were reported. Effect sizes were calculated using Kendall’s W for Friedman tests and r values for pairwise comparisons, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals where applicable. Inter-rater agreement between evaluators was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. All statistical tests were two-tailed. Exact p-values were reported, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

The evaluated large language models demonstrated high levels of informational accuracy when responding to ADHD-related questions. Reproducibility of responses

Reproducibility analysis showed that model responses remained generally consistent across repeated prompts conducted on separate days. The reproducibility rate was highest for ChatGPT (GPT-4o) (89%), followed by Gemini (86%) and DeepSeek R1 (84%) (Fig. 1).

At the item level, reproducibility rates varied across individual questions (Fig. 3). ChatGPT (GPT-4o) demonstrated slightly higher reproducibility across several items, although reproducibility levels were broadly comparable across the three models. Domain-Level Performance

Domain-level analysis revealed variability in model performance across different ADHD-related domains (Fig. 4). In the basic knowledge domain, Gemini (90%) and DeepSeek R1 (91%) achieved slightly higher accuracy than ChatGPT (GPT-4o) (87%). In the diagnosis and assessment domain, Gemini demonstrated the highest accuracy (87%), followed by ChatGPT (GPT-4o) (85%) and DeepSeek R1 (80%). In contrast, ChatGPT (GPT-4o) demonstrated higher accuracy in the treatment and long-term outcomes domains. In the treatment domain, ChatGPT (GPT-4o) achieved an accuracy of 88%, compared with 83% for Gemini and 85% for DeepSeek R1. Similarly, in the long-term outcomes domain, ChatGPT (GPT-4o) achieved the highest accuracy (96%), whereas Gemini and DeepSeek R1 achieved lower accuracies of 83 and 80%, respectively. Discussion

The present study provides one of the first systematic comparisons of large language models in responding to ADHD-related questions commonly asked by patients and caregivers. Overall, the evaluated models demonstrated high levels of informational accuracy and response stability. However, meaningful differences were observed between models in terms of response quality, usefulness, reliability, and domain-specific performance. Although overall accuracy rates were relatively comparable, ChatGPT (GPT-4o) achieved consistently higher expert-rated scores across several evaluation domains, particularly in treatment-related and long-term outcome questions. These findings suggest that factual accuracy alone may not fully capture the clinical adequacy or practical usefulness of AI-generated mental health information. In addition, the observed differences across question domains may reflect differences in how models communicate and structure information in clinically nuanced topics, such as treatment planning and long-term outcomes, compared with more straightforward informational questions. Nevertheless, the present study was not specifically designed to directly evaluate reasoning processes or underlying model mechanisms.

Clinically, questions regarding treatment and long-term outcomes may be especially important, as the information provided in these areas could influence caregiver expectations, treatment adherence, and help-seeking behavior. In contrast, questions related to basic knowledge or diagnostic recognition may rely more heavily on direct informational content, whereas treatment- and outcome-related questions may require more detailed communication and organization of clinical information. From a clinical safety perspective, appropriate cautionary language regarding diagnosis, medication use, and the need for professional evaluation may represent an important component of responsible AI-generated health communication [24]. In the present study, most models generally avoided definitive diagnostic statements and frequently encouraged consultation with healthcare professionals, although variability in the strength and clarity of these warnings was observed across responses.

In this context, ChatGPT demonstrated relatively stronger performance in treatment- and long-term outcome–related questions within the evaluated dataset. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the limited number of questions included in the study may not fully represent the broader spectrum of ADHD-related clinical scenarios and patient concerns. Previous studies have similarly reported that large language models may differ not only in informational accuracy, but also in the clarity, depth, and practical usefulness of the information they provide to users [25, 26]. In this context, the observed differences in quality, usefulness, and reliability scores in the present study suggest that evaluation of large language models in healthcare settings may benefit from incorporating broader measures of clinical communication in addition to factual correctness.

Beyond differences in model performance, the findings of the present study also highlight the potential role of large language models in facilitating access to mental health information. In neurodevelopmental conditions such as ADHD, families often seek clear and understandable guidance while facing challenges related to stigma, psychosocial burden, and limited access to specialized services [27]. This need may be even more pronounced within the broader context of global inequalities in mental health care, where substantial treatment gaps persist, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [28]. In such settings, large language model–based systems may serve as complementary informational tools by presenting complex clinical concepts in more accessible language and supporting the psychoeducational needs of patients and caregivers [29].

Similar observations have been reported in previous studies evaluating the performance of large language models across different medical contexts. For example, a study examining responses to patient questions about type 1 diabetes in children found that ChatGPT, Gemini, and other large language models generally produced highly accurate responses, although differences were observed in terms of clarity and practical usefulness [30]. Likewise, a study assessing ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek in clinical case evaluations within internal medicine reported that the models generated correct responses in most cases, while showing variability in the depth of clinical interpretation [31]. More recently, a comparative evaluation of large language models responding to parent-centered questions about anorexia nervosa similarly reported domain-specific variability and clinically relevant omissions across models [32]. Previous research has also emphasized that LLM performance may vary depending on factors such as model architecture, training data, and task complexity [16, 33]. Overall, these findings suggest that large language models may help support basic informational needs in healthcare settings, although differences in response quality and clinical usefulness should also be considered.

Comparable patterns have also been reported in studies examining the question–answer performance of large language models in mental health contexts. For instance, evaluations of responses to parent questions about autism have shown that models such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek are generally able to provide accurate and understandable information [22]. Similarly, a recent comparative study evaluating ChatGPT and DeepSeek in parent-centered autism-related questions also reported variability across clinical domains and response characteristics [34]. Likewise, research focusing on learning disorders such as dyslexia and dyscalculia has reported high levels of response quality and usefulness across different models, although some variability in reliability has been observed [23]. Comparable results have also been reported in studies examining responses to questions related to depression, where large language models produced informative answers but differed in the depth and scope of clinical explanations [35]. Overall, these findings suggest that differences between large language models may be better recognized when evaluations include clinically oriented expert assessments in addition to factual accuracy.

While the present findings are broadly consistent with the growing body of literature evaluating large language models in healthcare contexts, the current study also provides several additional insights. Previous research has primarily focused on performance dimensions such as accuracy, readability, and overall response quality, whereas the present study additionally examined the reproducibility of responses across separate prompting sessions. Furthermore, the domain-level analyses in the present study demonstrated that even when overall accuracy rates appeared relatively similar across models, more distinct differences emerged in expert-rated measures such as usefulness and reliability. These findings suggest that clinically oriented evaluation approaches may provide additional insight into model performance beyond overall accuracy measures alone.

Beyond these methodological contributions, the present findings also have several clinical and ethical implications regarding the potential use of large language models in healthcare. AI-based information systems may serve as complementary tools for psychoeducation and patient education, particularly in neurodevelopmental conditions such as ADHD, where parents and caregivers frequently seek accessible information. Rapid access to clear and understandable explanations may help increase awareness about the disorder and facilitate more informed communication with healthcare professionals. However, the potential role of large language models in patient education should be distinguished from their use in clinical decision-making, particularly in complex psychiatric presentations requiring individualized assessment and professional judgment.

At the same time, several important concerns regarding the clinical safety and reliability of AI-generated mental health information should also be considered. Previous studies have identified important limitations in AI-generated mental health information, including inaccurate or incomplete responses, limited source transparency, insufficient developmental contextualization, and readability concerns that may reduce accessibility for caregivers [36, 37]. In addition, GPT-4o has been reported to generate fabricated references in mental health literature review tasks [38]. More broadly, medical hallucinations—defined as outputs that are factually incorrect or unsupported by scientific evidence—remain a significant concern in foundation models, particularly in healthcare settings where factual inaccuracies may compromise patient safety [39]. These findings highlight the importance of cautious interpretation of LLM-generated health information, particularly in clinically complex developmental conditions such as ADHD. Therefore, AI-generated information should be considered a complementary informational resource rather than a substitute for comprehensive professional clinical evaluation [40].

In addition to these broader implications, the relatively high reproducibility observed across models indicates that large language models largely preserve core informational content when responding to repeated ADHD-related queries. Nevertheless, minor variations in wording and explanatory emphasis were observed across repeated outputs. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies showing that large language model responses to medical queries tend to maintain stable core information while varying in presentation style [41]. Comparative analyses have also reported differences in reproducibility across systems. For example, an evaluation of patient-oriented ophthalmology questions found that ChatGPT and DeepSeek demonstrated higher levels of response reproducibility across repeated prompts, whereas Gemini showed relatively lower reproducibility rates [42]. Taken together, these findings suggest that reproducibility represents an important dimension of LLM performance when evaluating their potential use as sources of health information. In the present study, some variability in explanatory emphasis and the scope of clinical information was observed across repeated responses. From a clinical perspective, such variability may be relevant because inconsistencies in treatment-related information and long-term outcome discussions could influence caregiver understanding and expectations.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of the present study. First, large language models are dynamic systems whose performance may change over time due to ongoing updates in model architecture, training data, and system-level modifications. Therefore, the findings of the present study reflect model performance at a single time point and may not fully represent future model behavior. Second, although the question set was designed to reflect common patient-related inquiries about attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), the number of evaluated questions was necessarily limited and may not capture the full range of concerns expressed by patients, parents, and caregivers in real-world settings. Future studies including larger and more diverse question sets may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of AI-generated responses. Third, the questions were presented only in English, which may limit the generalizability of the findings across different linguistic and cultural contexts. This limitation is particularly important given the global need for accessible ADHD-related information among patients, families, and caregivers. Previous studies have also suggested that the quality and consistency of LLM-generated responses may vary across languages [43]. Separate inter-rater reliability coefficients were not calculated for individual evaluation dimensions. In addition, although Cohen’s kappa demonstrated excellent overall inter-rater agreement, this statistic may not fully capture the ordinal structure of Likert-type evaluation scales. Alternative agreement measures, such as weighted kappa or intraclass correlation coefficients, may provide more methodologically precise estimates for ordinal rating data in future studies. Finally, the analyses were conducted under controlled prompting conditions. In real-world settings, user interactions with large language models are typically more dynamic and may involve variations in prompts, conversational context, and follow-up questions, which could influence the consistency of AI-generated health information.

Conclusion

Large language models such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek were generally capable of generating responses that were largely consistent with current clinical information for commonly asked ADHD-related questions. Although response reproducibility was relatively high across models, meaningful differences were observed in expert-rated measures such as quality, usefulness, reliability, and domain-level performance. In particular, more distinct differences were observed in treatment- and long-term outcome–related questions. Overall, these findings suggest that large language models may have potential as supportive tools in ADHD-related psychoeducation. However, given the risks of variability, incomplete information, and potential medical hallucinations, AI-generated responses should be interpreted cautiously and considered complementary to professional clinical evaluation rather than replacements for clinical assessment or treatment guidance. Further research is needed before large language models can be considered for broader clinical applications.
