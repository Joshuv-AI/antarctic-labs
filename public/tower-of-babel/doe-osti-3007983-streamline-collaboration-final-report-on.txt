1 Abstract
The DOE-funded STREAMLINE1 collaboration pushed the frontiers of theoretical and computa-
tional research on the nuclear many-body problem using machine learning (ML). By leveraging
ML techniques, STREAMLINE advanced large-scale nuclear-physics computations, substantially
enhancing predictive power and improving our understanding of nuclear structure and dynamics,
bulk nucleonic matter, and emergent many-body phenomena.
 This final report focuses on the achievements made by the supported graduate student at Ohio
State University, advised by the PI Richard Furnstahl, and their STREAMLINE collaborators. The
research advanced nuclear theory through two complementary projects that improved the accuracy
and efficiency of nuclear theory predictions.
 First, OSU graduate student Simon Sundberg developed and applied software tools for a criti-
cality analysis of artificial neural networks in nuclear physics. This work is aimed at improving the
understanding of artificial neural networks (ANNs) using an effective field theory and renormaliza-
tion group approach (neural network field theory, or NNFT). The prototype application was to an
ANN that learns and predicts nuclear masses from the AME2020 dataset. Simon’s results show
that critically tuned networks outperform the non-critical equivalents and suggest many further
avenues of research.
 Second, together with STREAMLINE colleagues at Ohio University and Michigan State Uni-
versity (plus several outside collaborators), we addressed the computational bottleneck in two- and
three-body scattering calculations by introducing reduced-order emulators, or surrogate models,
equipped with active learning for training and error estimation. These projection-based emulators
significantly lowered the cost of solving scattering equations in the parameter space of nuclear inter-
actions while maintaining accuracy and providing reliable error estimates. The developed approach
applies to both two-body and three-body scattering and supports Bayesian parameter estimation,
allowing for uncertainty quantification in complex nuclear scattering and reaction processes. This
development is critical in light of recent leadership campaigns in nuclear experiment throughout
the United States.
 Led by PI Dean Lee at Michigan State University (MSU), STREAMLINE brought together
leading researchers in theoretical nuclear physics and ML. The co-PIs included Christian Drischler
from Ohio University, Kévin Fossez from Florida State University (FSU), Richard Furnstahl from
Ohio State University (OSU), Gaute Hagen from Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), Morten
Hjorth-Jensen from Oslo/MSU, Sebastian König from North Carolina State University (NCSU),
Alessandro Lovato from Argonne National Laboratory (ANL), Witold Nazarewicz from MSU,
Noemi Rocco from Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory (FNAL), Jorge Piekarewicz from FSU,
and Thomas Papenbrock from the University of Tennessee (UT), Knoxville.

2.1 Criticality analysis of nuclear binding energy neural networks
Machine learning methods, in particular deep learning methods such as artificial neural networks
(ANNs) with many layers, have become widespread and useful tools in nuclear physics. How-
ever, these ANNs are typically treated as “black boxes”, with their architecture (width, depth,
and weight/bias initialization) and the training algorithm and parameters chosen empirically by
optimizing learning based on limited exploration.
 We have been testing a non-empirical approach to understanding and optimizing nuclear physics
ANNs by adapting a criticality analysis based on renormalization group flows in terms of the hy-
perparameters for weight/bias initialization, training rates, and the ratio of depth to width [3].
This treatment utilizes the statistical properties of neural network initialization to find a gener-
ating functional for network outputs at any layer, allowing for a path integral formulation of the
ANN outputs as a Euclidean statistical field theory. We used a prototypical example to test the
applicability of this approach: a simple ANN for nuclear binding energies. We found that with
training using a stochastic gradient descent optimizer, the predicted criticality behavior is realized,
and optimal performance is found with critical tuning.
 We first confronted ANNFT predictions of initialization distribution behavior. This is pre-
training; we simply collect statistics from repeated random initializations of the weights and biases.
We found the expected approach to Gaussian distributions for the final layer output distributions
with fixed depth but increasing width. Within small fluctuations, normal distributions were seen
by a width of 120 neurons for both ReLU and Softplus activations and for every other activation
function we have tried. The correlations between pairs of input values with a width of 120 and
increasing depths showed Gaussian process (GP) correlations with the predicted kernel at depth 1
and increasing correlations with additional hidden layers.
 A comparison of measured variance and excess kurtosis (difference of the fourth moment from
Gaussian expectations) from many initializations for a fixed width of 240 and increasing depths
validates the theoretical predictions of criticality. In particular, for the ReLU activation function
the variance grows or shrinks with depth if the initialization hyperparameters are tuned above
or below the predicted critical value. At criticality the variance is flat. In contrast, the Softplus
activation function does not have a critical fixed point for initialization. In accordance with theory,
the variance either grows or asymptotes to a constant value with depth. Non-zero excess kurtosis

 Next we considered training of the ANN. We followed the mean absolute error (MAE) loss for a
large number of epochs with several different depths, using combinations of critical and non-critical
initialization and training. The networks with critical initialization and critical training (CICT)
reached the lowest loss in all cases both for ReLU and Tanh activation functions, with particular
contrast to when non-critical hyperparameters were used for both initialization and training.
 Most practitioners do not encounter these issues even though they rarely pay attention to details
of initialization and training. This is because the critically tuned networks are still outperformed
by adaptive optimizers. Nevertheless, the results are comparable, and differ by a much smaller
factor than when untuned.
 Of particular interest is the prediction by ANNFT of different training regimes, characterized
by the ratio of depth to width r. The predictive analyses of ANNFT are based on the validity
of a Taylor series expansion about the initialization. By considering the mean hidden-layer rms
deviation and Frobenius norms of the weight matrices before and after training, we can see that the
conditions of small deviations from initialization are achieved with sufficiently small r. Different
ANN learning regimes associated with the value r were identified (see [3]). The different learning
regimes are manifested by looking at the rms deviation of the binding energy data and ANN outputs
as a function of r, achieved by varying the width at fixed depth, and through heatmaps of global
residuals across all N and Z for four different r values.
 When r ≥ 1, correlations between neurons are too strong to learn features, and the networks
can be considered “overly deep”. When r ≪ 1, the networks are “effectively deep”, and can learn.
When r → 0, the neuron correlations turn off, and features cannot be learned. This implies an
optimal r (which will be denoted r∗ below). The behavior with r is illustrated in Fig. 1.

the distributions become entirely centered around 72 MeV.
 Our results validated that ANNFT offers insight into [3, 4]

 • why networks that are wider than they are deep are preferred, as they will have correlations
 in their output distribution attenuated by the larger width;

 • the success of activation functions such as ReLU or Tanh compared to Softplus or sigmoid;

 • the success of certain initialization schemes (He initialization is reproduced by ReLU’s critical
 initialization).

We restricted the training protocol in this work to facilitate a clean analysis of criticality, but the
door is open to explore heuristically successful approaches to network training in the context of
ANNFT. However, the use of an adaptive learning algorithm leads to somewhat superior results
without concern for tuning and thus obscures the analysis. Nevertheless, the criticality analysis
offers a way to look within the black box of ANNs, which is a first step towards potential improve-
ments in network performance beyond using adaptive optimizers.
 In future work in STREAMLINE-2, we will continue to investigate criticality in binding-energy
fitting with more features/inputs and work toward extending criticality to other training algo-
rithms/hyperparameters. To improve network performance without spoiling criticality conditions,
we will explore mini-batch training as opposed to the full-batch training. For full-batch training,
the loss gradients for every data point in the training set are computed and averaged over, and a
single gradient is performed per epoch. Mini-batch training performs this same process for mini-
batches of size B, resulting in multiple gradient updates for a single epoch equal to the size of
the training set divided by the batch size B. The increased number of gradient updates averaged
from smaller datasets results in more gradient noise as the network is trained, resulting in more
explorative behavior when searching for a minimum of the loss function. This can result in faster
convergence to a minimum, or convergence to a better minimum than what full-batch training is
able to reach.
 Mini-batching is an ideal next step, as it allows us to keep our critical prescriptions for both
initialization and training. Mini-batch training affects the way gradient updates are applied, but
does not alter anything about our initialization or how we establish our learning rates. The effect
of mini-batching should appear only in the neural tangent kernel as a result of its impact on
the gradients. Given that full-batch network dynamics are well understood, batch-size can be
gradually decreased to see how the neural tangent kernel and other quantities of interest change,
and we can begin to construct an ANNFT understanding of mini-batch training. At the same
time, we will explore more nuclear systems with critical networks to further understand learning
behavior in different problems. The field-theory-based framework offers the possibility of applying
approximations and techniques from many-body field theory to extend ANNFT beyond simple
perturbation theory. Finally, we will consider an alternative formulation of ANNFT that organizes

2.2 Active learning emulators for nuclear scattering
Nuclear scattering experiments yield invaluable data to test, validate, and improve theoretical
models such as chiral EFT, which has been the method of choice for deriving microscopic nuclear
interactions at low energies. However, there are competing formulations of chiral EFT, with open
questions regarding EFT power counting, sensitivity to regulator artifacts, and differing predictions
for medium-mass atomic nuclei. Low-energy nuclear scattering data, combined with rigorous statis-
tical methods such as Bayesian parameter estimation, model comparison, and sensitivity analysis,
applied to chiral EFT predictions, can provide essential insights to address these issues.
 However, to fully exploit the available data with such statistical methods, fast and accurate
predictions across a wide range of model parameters are required. While, in principle, the scattering
equations can be solved accurately in few-body systems, doing so is prohibitively slow for statistical
analyses of three- and higher-body scattering, and even for two-body scattering, more efficient
alternatives are appealing.

 6

Figure 3: Flowchart depicting the process of constructing an emulator basis using the developed
greedy algorithm. See also the illustration of the greedy algorithm in Fig. 2. The greedy algorithm
iteratively refines an initial emulator basis during the offline (or training) stage. Once the greedy
algorithm terminates, the emulator can be used in the online (or evaluation) stage, analogous to
the POD, although with a different emulator basis. The acronym FOM stands for full-order model,
and θ denotes the vector of free parameters. The figure was taken from Ref. [2].

 In the second project [2], we developed, implemented, and tested two model-driven emulators
based on Galerkin and Petrov-Galerkin projection using the prototypical test case of two-body
scattering with the Minnesota potential and a more realistic local chiral potential. The high-
fidelity scattering equations were solved with the matrix Numerov method, a reformulation of the
popular Numerov recurrence relation for solving special second-order differential equations as a
linear system of coupled equations. These reduced-basis method (RBM) emulators allow one to
approximate scattering solutions in the parameter space of the chiral interactions θ, characterized
by the NN contact interactions.
 A novel error estimator based on reduced-space residuals was applied to an active-learning ap-
proach (a so-called greedy algorithm) to select training samples (“snapshots”) for the emulator,
and was contrasted with the standard, although naı̈ve, proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)
approach. Figure 2 shows an illustrative comparison between the POD and greedy approaches.
During emulator training, the greedy algorithm iteratively updates the emulator basis, placing
snapshots in the interaction’s parameter space where the emulator’s error is estimated to be max-
imal. Figure 3 provides a flowchart of this greedy iteration process. Both approaches enable
computationally efficient offline-online decompositions, but the greedy approach required substan-
tially fewer snapshot calculations, thereby enabling emulator training in applications that would be
otherwise computationally too expensive, such as three-body scattering. Furthermore, the greedy
algorithm provided emulator error estimates for emulated wave functions and associated phase
shifts, which was a critical development, as studying emulator errors was in its infancy in nuclear
physics, and has wide-ranging applications for emulating solutions to large linear systems.
 This project was led by STREAMLINE-supported graduate student Joshua Maldonado (Ohio
University), working with STREAMLINE PIs C. Drischler (Ohio University) and Furnstahl. Mal-
donado presented this research at the “2024 Ohio University Student Research and Creative Activity
Expo,” for which he received a poster award. Soon after, he completed and defended his master’s
thesis on this project [5] and graduated from Ohio University in the fall of 2024. The research
project was later published in Ref. [2].
 Maldonado was succeeded by Abhinav Giri, the second graduate student supported by STREAM-
LINE at Ohio University. He has been working with Drischler and Furnstahl, with additional assis-
tance from postdoctoral researcher Jane Kim (Ohio University), on extending this active learning
approach in the following essential ways:

An advanced draft of a manuscript is currently a work in progress and is expected to be submitted
in a few weeks. Giri successfully presented his work at the 2025 Ohio University Student Research
and Creative Activity Expo. He plans to present his Ph.D. prospectus in the spring of 2026,
with ongoing support from the STREAMLINE successor, the DOE-funded STREAMLINE2. In
September 2025, Kim transitioned to her second postdoctoral position at ANL, where she has
continued to work with STREAMLINE2.
 Together with the STREAMLINE members Xilin Zhang (FRIB/MSU) and Drischler, Furn-
stahl has been collaborating with Alex Gnech (Old Dominion University) and the Pisa group to
develop active learning emulators for nucleon-deuteron (Nd) scattering above the deuteron-breakup
threshold. The Pisa group has unique expertise in few-body scattering calculations based on the
Kohn variational principle (KVP) and hyperspherical harmonics, which provide high-fidelity cal-
culations for training scattering emulators. The efficient emulator training is facilitated by the
active learning approach developed in Refs. [2, 5]. In a proof-of-principle calculation [6, 7], we
demonstrated that these three-body emulators enable the accurate and efficient emulation of the
R-matrix for proton-deuteron scattering in the parameter space of the two 3N low-energy couplings
at N2 LO, cD and cE . The STREAMLINE collaboration significantly contributed to the develop-
ment of these three-body emulators, and they were subsequently submitted to the preprint server
arXiv upon STREAMLINE’s conclusion. The continued development will be supported by the
STREAMLINE2 collaboration.
 These developments laid the groundwork for emulating scattering observables using chiral NN
and 3N interactions, as well as optical models in the FRIB era, where computational speedups are
necessary for Bayesian uncertainty quantification of scattering processes. The source code for the
two-body scattering emulators has been made publicly available on GitHub, enabling practitioners
to use and extend our emulators in their research.
