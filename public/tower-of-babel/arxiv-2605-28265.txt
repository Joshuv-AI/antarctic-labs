Abstract
We study the robustness of Bayesian persuasion to uncertainty about the receiver’s preferences. We analyze two conceptually distinct notions: continuity , in which only the modeler lacks precise knowledge, but where the model’s predictions are nonetheless accurate; and robustness , in which the sender also lacks precise knowledge, but where the outcome is insensitive to this ignorance. We model preference uncertainty as infinitesimally small, non-probabilistic (Knightian) uncertainty, and the sender’s behavior as either minimizing the regret or maximizing the minimum utility. We show that continuity holds if and only if robustness holds, and that both notions are generic. Thus, while some instances of Bayesian persuasion are fragile, typical instances are both continuous and robust with respect to a small amount of ignorance.



1
Introduction

Bayesian persuasion, introduced by Kamenica and Gentzkow
[
15
]
, is one of the most widely studied models in microeconomics. This is most likely due to its simplicity and its applicability to various domains in economics, law, political science and more
[
14
]
. In its basic form, there are two agents: one agent, the ‘sender’, has access to information about a state of the world, but takes no action. Instead, the sender shares his information, partially and strategically, with a second agent, the ‘receiver’. The receiver then takes an action that determines the utilities of both agents. A main assumption in Bayesian persuasion is that the sender can devise and commit to his information-sharing procedure before he observes the realized state. The central observation, dating back to work by Aumann et al.
[
3
]
on repeated zero-sum games with incomplete information on one side, is that the maximal utility of the sender is equal to the value of the concavification of his indirect utility function—the implied utility function given that the receiver best-replies to his updated belief.

How robust is this observation, and to what extent does it depend on strong modeling assumptions? In the so-called Wilson Doctrine
[
26
]
, Wilson
argues that

“Game theory has a great advantage in explicitly analyzing the consequences of trading rules that presumably are really common knowledge; it is deficient to the extent it assumes other features to be common knowledge, such as one agent’s probability assessment about another’s preferences or information.”

However, the Bayesian persuasion model does assume that the receiver’s preferences are common knowledge.
In this paper we ask whether this assumption is crucial for the model’s main observation to hold, or whether the model is robust.

More specifically, we consider two conceptually distinct notions of robustness. The first notion, which we call
continuity
, supposes that the sender and receiver do have complete knowledge of all features of their interaction, but that the analyst or modeler lacks some knowledge. The model is
continuous
if, despite the lack of complete knowledge, the modeler can nonetheless accurately predict the outcome of the interaction
[
13
]
.

The second notion, which we henceforth refer to as
robustness
, is more along the lines of the Wilson Doctrine, and supposes that the sender also does not have complete knowledge. The model is
robust
if, despite the sender’s lack of complete knowledge, the outcome of the interaction is nonetheless close to the benchmark in which all features are common knowledge.

Formally, we ask whether the concavification result is continuous and robust when the precise preferences of the receiver are no longer common knowledge. We assume that the lack of common knowledge manifests as infinitesimal ignorance—specifically, that for any action and state, the receiver’s utility is only known to be within some small interval, without any distributional assumptions (e.g., ambiguity or Knightian uncertainty). The Bayesian persuasion model is continuous if the maximal utility attained by the sender—the concavification value—can be (approximately) predicted by the modeler even if the modeler only knows the receiver’s utilities to within such small intervals.

In order to study robustness, we first elaborate on the sender’s strategy in light of his ignorance.
To model this strategy we invoke two paradigms from decision theory
[
22
]
, max-min utility and regret minimization. A max-min sender chooses a strategy that maximizes her worst-case utility. The sender’s regret is the difference in utility between the actual decision made and what would have been the optimal decision in hindsight. Regret minimization is the behavioral assumption that the sender chooses her signal policy in order to minimize the worst-case regret. Under these two paradigms, the Bayesian persuasion model is then robust if the maximal utility attained by the sender—the concavification value—is also (close to) what she obtains when she only knows the receiver’s utilities to within small intervals, and when she chooses her strategy to either maximize worst-case utility or to minimize regret.

Whether or not a particular instantiation of a Bayesian persuasion model is continuous or robust depends on the parameters of the instance. For illustration, in Section
1.2
we analyze two examples, one of which is both continuous and robust and the other of which is neither continuous nor robust.

The examples illustrate that continuity and robustness need not hold. They also illustrate that, although the two notions are conceptually distinct, there appears to be a tight connection between them. Our first result is that this is always the case: in Theorem
1
we show that continuity holds if and only if robustness holds. Furthermore, in Theorem
2
we show that continuity and robustness hold generically.

1.1
Related literature

The motivation for this work can be traced back to the literature on the robustness of equilibria in incomplete information games
[
13
,
25
,
21
]
. In this line of papers, the authors study whether the introduction of a small amount of uncertainty about players’ higher-order beliefs changes the modeler’s predictions about the equilibrium—what we call continuity. Continuity with respect to various model assumptions has also been studied in communication games, and specifically in cheap talk games
[
6
,
7
,
2
,
24
]
. In our study, we focus on a different form of communication game, Bayesian persuasion
[
3
,
15
,
14
]
.

Since its introduction, many variants of the Bayesian persuasion setting have been studied, including ones where the assumption that the sender’s knowledge is superior to that of the receiver is relaxed.
Several contributions can be interpreted as studying the
continuity
and
robustness
of Bayesian persuasion with respect to different relaxations, including variations in the receiver’s private information, the receiver’s prior, the receiver’s level of rationality, the sender’s credibility, and the receiver’s utilities.

For example, Matysková and Montes
[
20
]
study an environment in which the receiver can acquire private information at a cost.
The information structure of the receiver’s private signal is governed by a cost parameter which is known to the sender.
The authors show that as the cost tends to infinity—and so the receiver becomes almost uninformed—the sender can attain the same expected utility as in the baseline Bayesian persuasion model.
This provides a continuity result with respect to the receiver’s private information.
In Hu and Weng
[
11
]
, the receiver is privately informed about a binary state, but the sender does not know the receiver’s information structure.
They characterize the optimal information structure for an ambiguity-averse sender with a max-min expected utility function.
They show that, when the receiver’s private information becomes arbitrarily weak, the sender can guarantee an expected utility arbitrarily close to the baseline Bayesian persuasion model.
This can be interpreted as a robustness result with respect to the receiver’s private information. Finally, Gan et al.
[
10
]
study the robustness of Stackelberg games (which are closely related to Bayesian persuasion) to slightly sub-optimal receiver behavior, as well as algorithms for computing robust sender strategies. Relatedly, Yang and Zhang
[
27
]
study the computational aspects of Bayesian persuasion under such slightly sub-optimal receiver behavior.

Other papers also study variations of Bayesian persuasion in which the sender lacks some information about the receiver. Dworczak and Pavan
[
8
]
assume that the sender is concerned that her belief about the environment may be false. Specifically, the sender is uncertain about the exogenous sources of information the receiver may learn from, and about strategy selection.
In Kosterina
[
16
]
the sender is ignorant about the receiver’s prior, and takes the adversarial approach to evaluate each information structure by assuming the worst case of the receiver’s prior.
Zu et al.
[
28
]
study a repeated setting where both the sender and receiver do not know the distribution over the payoff-relevant state. Instead, the sender learns this distribution over time by observing the state realizations.

A different perspective on continuity and robustness in the Bayesian persuasion setting is taken by Feng et al.
[
9
]
, who depart from the fully rational paradigm.
The authors first show that, when the receiver is almost fully rational, an optimal policy for the fully rational benchmark remains approximately optimal.
They then study robust signaling schemes when the sender is uncertain about the receiver’s rationality level. Another related paper is that of Lin and Chen
[
17
]
, in which the authors study generalized principal-agent problems (which generalize Bayesian persuasion) and where the receiver uses no-regret learning algorithms to respond to the sender.

In another related paper, Lipnowski et al.
[
19
]
study the robustness of persuasion models to the tie-breaking rule.
In addition, the authors show that their model can also be applied to environments in which the sender has imperfect commitment power (Lipnowski et al.
[
18
]
).
The latter can be viewed as a continuity analysis of Bayesian persuasion with respect to the sender’s credibility.

Finally, our work is related to two papers that also relax the assumption that the sender knows the receiver’s utility function, and where the sender uses regret minimization in choosing his strategy: Babichenko et al.
[
4
]
and Castiglioni et al.
[
5
]
. Specifically, Babichenko et al.
[
4
]
study a setting in which the receiver has two actions and there are multiple states. The authors show that the sender’s complete ignorance, and use of a regret-minimizing strategy, leads to some utility loss. Castiglioni et al.
[
5
]
focus on an online learning setting where one of finitely many types of receivers is repeatedly and adversarially chosen. The authors characterize an algorithm to minimize the regret, and study its corresponding complexity. In contrast to these two papers, we are interested in whether an infinitesimal lack of information results in substantial changes in the conclusions.

The example above motivates the study of two potential properties of Bayesian persuasion models:

Model

A Bayesian persuasion model (BP model) is a tuple,
G=\{\Omega,A,\mu_{0},u,v\}
, where

\Omega
is a finite set of states, and a state is realized according to the full support prior,
\mu_{0}\in\Delta(\Omega)
.

2

The full support assumption is without loss of generality.
The notation

\Delta(\Omega):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\sum_{j\leq N}x(j)=1;~~~\forall j\leq N,x(j)\geq 0\}
, where
N=|\Omega|
.

A
is a finite set of actions available to the receiver (
he

Let
u:A\times\Omega\rightarrow R
denote the utility function of the receiver,
and
v:A\times\Omega\rightarrow R
that of the sender. We assume that utilities are bounded and nonnegative,

0\leq u(a,\omega),v(a,\omega)\leq 1\ \forall a,\omega
.

The game proceeds as follows. A sender (
she

\omega
.

Then the state is realized and a message is publicly announced according to that signal policy, after which the receiver takes an action and both utilities are realized.

It follows from the celebrated Aumann-Maschler splitting lemma

that a signal policy is equivalent to a distribution over a finite set of posterior beliefs for the receiver, for which the average is equal to the prior. Formally, a signal policy,
\pi\in\Delta(\Delta(\Omega))

\{\mu_{i}^{\pi}\}_{i\leq I}\ \subset\Delta(\Omega)

\{\alpha_{i}^{\pi}\}_{i\leq I}
, which satisfy

\mu_{0}=\sum_{i\leq I}\alpha_{i}^{\pi}\mu_{i}^{\pi}~~\,\,\mathrm{and}\,\,~~\sum_{i\leq I}\alpha_{i}^{\pi}=1
.
We denote the set of all policies under this representation by
\Pi\subset\Delta(\Delta(\Omega))
.

{\mathrm{opt}A}_{\theta}(\mu)=\{a\in A:\forall b\in A,Eu_{\theta}(a,\mu)\geq Eu_{\theta}(b,\mu)\}.

Let

2.2
Structure of the Type Space

In order to capture a non-Bayesian model where no distributional information on the type of the receiver is assumed but where the uncertainty is negligible, we add the following structural assumption to our model: for any realization of state
\omega

A collection of BP models

\{\{u_{\theta}(a,\omega)\}_{(a,\omega)}:{\theta\in\Theta}\}=\times_{(a,\omega)}[\underline{u_{a,\omega}},\overline{u_{a,\omega}}],

\delta

A BP model

Continuity of a model indicates that the model predictions are accurate even if the modeler is infinitesimally ignorant regarding the receiver’s utility function. It justifies disregarding this ignorance if it is small. However, it assumes that the sender is familiar with the utility function. But what if the sender is also (infinitesimally) ignorant? To cope with this, we make the pervasive assumption that an ignorant sender, having no distributional assumptions on the type of the receiver, behaves according to one of the following two criteria:

1.

max-min
: she chooses a signal policy that maximizes her worst-case expected utility.

2.

regret-minimizing
: she chooses a strategy that minimizes her worst-case regret.

This motivates the following definition:

Definition 5
.

A signal policy
\pi^{\prime}
is a
max-min
policy and a signal policy
\pi^{*}
is a
regret-minimizing
policy if, for any signal policy
\pi\in\Pi
,

\min_{\theta\in\Theta}Ev_{\theta}(\pi^{\prime})\geq\min_{\theta\in\Theta}Ev_{\theta}(\pi),\quad\mathrm{Regret}(\pi^{*})\leq\mathrm{Regret}(\pi).

We can now define the robustness of a BP model to minor ignorance of the sender with respect to the receiver’s utility:

A BP model
G=\{\Omega,A,\mu_{0},u_{\theta_{0}},v\}

robust when the sender is max-min
if for all
\epsilon>0

\delta_{0}>0
such that, for any
\delta<\delta_{0}
, any
\delta

G
, and any type
\theta

|Ev_{\theta}(\pi^{\prime})-Ev_{\theta_{0}}(\mathrm{opt}\Pi(\theta_{0}))|<\epsilon
.

Definition 7

Further Discussion of The Adjustment Signal Policy

In contrast with the two-dimensional setting where the weights on the posterior beliefs can be easily calculated, in high-dimensional settings this is more difficult (and, in particular, requires a matrix inverse).
For example, in Figure

Furthermore, notice that the required changes would be dependent on the specific combination of posterior beliefs. And finally, the initial signal policy may be supported on fewer posterior beliefs than the number of states. For example, in Figure

, almost every slight change will cause there not to exist a feasible signal policy supported on only two posterior beliefs.

Motivation for the adjustment signal policy

In contrast, the adjustment signal policy guarantees its performance without further optimization, and also without dependence on the specific values of posterior beliefs used in the optimal signal policy.

In addition to these benefits, we now show that even when adjusting the beliefs and weights without adding a counterweighting posterior is possible, the adjustment signal policy may possibly still perform better.
Let us consider the following example. The state space is binary, the prior is
\mu_{0}=0.5
, and at types
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
the indirect utility functions are

v(\mu,\theta_{1})=\begin{cases}1,\,0\leq\mu\leq 0.485\\
0.97,\,0.485<\mu\leq 0.505\\
0,\,0.505<\mu\end{cases}\,\,v(\mu,\theta_{2})=\begin{cases}1,\,0\leq\mu\leq 0.49\\
0.97,\,0.49<\mu\leq 0.501\\
0,\,0.501<\mu\end{cases}

Suppose the sender does the concavification for type
\theta_{1}
by optimally splitting
\mu_{0}
into
\mu_{1}=0.485
with weight
\alpha_{1}=\frac{1}{4}
, and
\mu_{2}=0.505
with weight
\alpha_{2}=\frac{3}{4}
. His expected utility here is
\frac{1}{4}\times 1+\frac{3}{4}\times 0.97=0.9775
. Now he knows that the receiver is indeed of
\theta_{2}
, and he knows that there exist
\mu_{1}^{\prime}=0.49
and
\mu_{2}^{\prime}=0.501
, which are very close to
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, and satisfy
v(\mu_{i},\theta)=v(\mu_{i},\theta_{2})
,
\forall i\leq 2
. If he neither chooses to deduce the new optimal signal policy, nor applies the adjustment signal policy, but just updates the weights for these two new posteriors (so that
0.5=\frac{1}{11}\times 0.49+\frac{10}{11}\times 0.501
), he will get expected utility
\frac{1}{11}\times 1+\frac{10}{11}\times 0.97\approx 0.9727
. But by considering the adjustment signal policy, which is given by
0.5=\frac{2000}{2007}(\frac{1}{4}\times 0.49+\frac{3}{4}\times 0.501)+\frac{7}{2007}\times 1
, he will get expected utility
\frac{2000}{2007}\times 0.9775\approx 0.9741
. Thus, the adjustment signal policy yields higher utility than simple updating of the weights. The underlying logic is that for type
\theta_{2}
the optimal splitting is no longer
\mu_{1}^{\prime}
and
\mu_{2}^{\prime}
. However, the adjustment signal policy can bridge different optimal signal policies corresponding to different types without dependence on the optimal concavification.
