Evaluating the acceptability of a portable handwashing intervention (the Spatap) in three Fijian communities, a mixed-methods study
Authors: Rose Hosking, Alice Richardson, Darren Gray, Simon Hales, Suliasi Batikawai, Aparna Lal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWater, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions in resource-constrained communities can improve health outcomes, but uptake and sustainability are influenced by acceptability. We evaluated the acceptability of the Spatap, a portable silicone tap attached to a bottle, as a household handwashing intervention in Fijian communities.MethodsWe distributed Spataps in three communities and conducted a survey two to seven days later, assessing sociodemographic factors, WASH access, and Spatap acceptability (n = 207). We applied a novel quantitative approach, calculating a total acceptability score from seven Likert-scale questions aligned with the component constructs of the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (with a maximum score of 35) and asked open-ended questions about barriers and enablers to Spatap use. We analysed scores and used multiple linear models to examine predictors. Five months later, we conducted three focus group discussions (FGD) (n = 22) to supplement the survey data and performed thematic analysis using NVivo (ver 12).ResultsSpatap acceptability scores ranged from 18 to 35 (median 29), indicating high overall acceptability. Burden and opportunity cost scored lower than other component constructs. Sociodemographic factors were not meaningfully associated with acceptability scores. Few barriers were reported: ease of use, water saving, and convenience were key enablers. FGDs largely supported the survey findings and additionally highlighted ease of use for children and perceived illness reduction as contributors to acceptability. Time spent refilling bottles was burdensome in some larger households, the cost of larger bottles was prohibitive for some, and minor leakage issues were reported.ConclusionsThe Spatap was broadly acceptable in the study communities, particularly where its portability, ease of use, and water-saving potential addressed locally important needs. These findings suggest the Spatap may have value as a household WASH intervention in resource-constrained settings, though its appropriateness relative to lower-cost alternatives should be considered. Future implementations should assess the cost-effectiveness and availability of suitable bottles, provide local language and pictorial instructions, and consider targeted engagement with children. The acceptability score method showed potential as a rapid evaluation tool but requires refinement before reapplication.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28142-1.Keywords: Acceptability, Handwashing, Hygiene, WASH, Qualitative, Quantitative, Enabling technology, Pacific Islands

Introduction

Handwashing with soap and water, safe drinking water, and toilet facilities where excreta are separated from human contact are pillars of human health [1]. Increased water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) access is a priority in the Pacific Island Countries and Territories (PICTs), which have a high burden of WASH-associated diseases [2]. WASH coverage varies within and between islands in the PICTs, but rural areas and some resource-constrained coastal communities have lower levels of access than urban areas [3]. Delivering WASH infrastructure to remote or otherwise isolated communities is costly and a key challenge for WASH improvement in the PICTs [4, 5]. WASH interventions, including the provision of WASH infrastructure (e.g. latrines, handwashing stations, water sources) and promotion of accompanying behaviours, are a vital component of achieving universal access, reducing water insecurity, and improving health outcomes in resource-constrained settings [5, 6].

The Spatap is a portable silicone tap that attaches to bottles of various sizes, transforming them into a handwashing station (Fig. 1). The intervention is lightweight, easily transportable, and does not require connection to mains water, mitigating many of the costs associated with delivering WASH infrastructure to remote or difficult-to-access communities. The volume of water dispensed from the bottle can also be carefully controlled. During humanitarian distributions led by the device developers, up to 60 handwashes have reportedly been achieved from a 1.5 L bottle (~ 25 mL per handwash); however, this has not been independently or formally evaluated. Water volumes used by other portable handwashing interventions vary (e.g. tippy taps have been reported to use ~ 50 mL per handwash), but are generally lower than those of conventional taps, which flow continuously during lathering, or of basins, which require a larger volume of water [7, 8]. Spataps have been distributed in many communities throughout the PICTs and Africa [8]. There has been one evaluation with acceptability components in resource-constrained households in Tanzania, but no in-depth evaluations or household trials to date [9].

Beliefs, attitudes, perceptions, and cultural norms influence whether people adopt and use interventions over time, regardless of their effect on health [10]. Because of this, the acceptability of an intervention to the intended users affects adoption and sustained use [11, 12]. Acceptability evaluations are increasingly common across the WASH and broader healthcare sectors [13, 14]. Acceptability of WASH interventions has most commonly been defined as feelings toward an intervention (positive or negative, satisfaction), motivations/barriers, alignment with sociocultural norms, willingness to use or purchase, or has been conflated with adoption or adherence measures [13]. Previous WASH acceptability evaluations have drawn on frameworks such as the Integrated Behavioural Model for Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (IBM-WASH) and the Health Belief Model. However, many evaluations have lacked a consistent definition of acceptability that has a theoretical underpinning [13].

The Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA) developed by Sekhon and colleagues comprises seven component constructs (affective attitude, burden, ethicality, intervention coherence, opportunity costs, perceived effectiveness, self-efficacy) that interact to affect overall acceptability [14]. We applied the TFA to this evaluation and defined acceptability using the definition developed by Sekhon et al. as “a multi-faceted construct that reflects the extent to which people delivering or receiving a healthcare intervention consider it to be appropriate, based on anticipated or experienced cognitive and emotional responses to the intervention.” While this is one of the first applications of the TFA to a WASH intervention since its development, it has been successfully applied to assess a variety of broader health interventions [15–17].

Focus group discussions (FGDs) or interviews are common methods for obtaining acceptability information in WASH studies [13]. However, many WASH implementations operate under resource constraints and collecting, preparing, and analysing qualitative data can be resource-intensive, especially in cross-cultural contexts where translation is required [18, 19]. In some implementations, incorporating acceptability questions into cross-sectional surveys may be a more achievable way to obtain acceptability data.

We aimed to conduct an acceptability evaluation of the Spatap handwashing intervention in three Fijian communities. Secondarily, we aimed to understand the utility of a novel score-based method for evaluating the acceptability of a handwashing intervention.

Methods

This study was a mixed-methods acceptability evaluation of a portable handwashing intervention (Spatap) implemented in three Fijian communities. We provided Spataps to three communities and conducted separate surveys for adults and children. Five months later, we returned to the communities and conducted FGDs with adult participants.

Study setting

We conducted this study in Fiji, one of the PICTs. Fiji has distinctive wet (November to April) and dry (May to October) seasons. During the dry season, parts of the main island receive very limited rainfall [20]. Fiji is an upper-middle-income economy; however, there are disparities in development across the country [21, 22]. Water availability in Fiji communities depends on socioeconomic status, geographic location, climate, and use practices [2]. Forty per cent of the Fijian population lives in rural areas, including villages, settlements and smaller, more remote islands, where water access is intermittent or of lower quality than in urban areas [23].

The Fijian government has made continual efforts to improve WASH access, primarily by investing in infrastructure and delivering behaviour change campaigns in schools and communities [24]. However, delivering and maintaining WASH infrastructure in remote or otherwise isolated communities is costly and a key challenge for WASH improvement [4]. Although further testing of Spatap water use is needed, the device is designed to limit flow while still enabling a complete handwash, suggesting potential utility in settings with limited hygiene access or with water availability challenges.

Site selection

We developed site selection criteria in consultation with the Fijian Ministry of Health and Medical Services (MOHMS), who partnered with us to conduct this project. Criteria included population size (up to 100 households, to ensure there were enough to distribute two Spataps per household), accessibility, and existing WASH access levels (limited). Selection was driven by MOHMS’s existing relationships and deep knowledge of the communities. To limit the burden on recipient communities, we also restricted selection for this project to communities that had recently engaged with the MOHMS in the Ba district in the North of the Western Division of Viti Levu (Fiji) through latrine distributions or requests for water tanks (Fig. 2).

Map of Fiji showing Ba Province, Northwestern Viti Levu, Fiji’s largest island. Inset: location of Ba Province in Viti Levu (Fiji). Created using ArcGIS Online (Baselayer: ESRI 2023)

MOHMS selected three communities in Ba, which are referred to hereafter as “Dua”, “Rua” and “Tolu” (one, two, three in iTaukei). The Ba district receives very little rainfall in the dry season [20]. Dua, the largest community (population ~ 828, 108 households), is a low-lying coastal village between Ba and Lautoka. Water is supplied to Dua from a dam, located several kilometres away, connected to the community by pipes. Both Rua (population ~ 43, 26 households) and Tolu (population ~ 17, 3 households) are villages located in the rural highland interior of Ba. Rua has a dam connected by pipes, located 30–45 min away on foot. Tolu is supplied by a borehole approximately 1 km away, which is connected by a single pipe.

Intervention delivery

In June 2022, the study team visited the communities to provide the Spataps and information about the study. We obtained permission to enter the communities from the village heads. All community members were invited to attend a meeting about the Spatap. At the meetings, researchers and health workers demonstrated the use of the Spatap in English and iTaukei. Community heads were then provided with enough Spataps to distribute two per household. Each Spatap was accompanied by written English instructions on how and where to hang them.

Survey

Recruitment and eligibility criteria

The field team, consisting of three bilingual (English & iTaukei) field assistants trained in community surveys and one researcher from an Australian university, visited every household in the community two to seven days after distribution of the Spataps. Everyone in the household who was eligible at the time of the visit was invited to complete an individual survey. We conducted separate surveys for adults and children.

Community members were eligible to participate in the adult survey if they (i) were aged 18 years or older, (ii) lived in a household that received Spataps, and (iii) resided permanently in the community. Community members were eligible to participate in the child survey if they were aged 10 to 17 years. The survey was administered using an electronic device: the surveyor read the questions and response options aloud, and the participant indicated a response. Open-response questions were recorded verbatim in the language of the response.

Survey content

The adult survey consisted of 39 questions covering sociodemographic factors (age, gender, education level, employment type, household size, and number of children < 5 living in the household), sanitation and hygiene access, water sources, and questions on acceptability. The 20-question child survey was a simplified version, with sociodemographic factors (age, gender, current school level, household size, and number of children < 5 living in the household), hygiene access and acceptability questions.

Water sources, sanitation and hygiene access

WASH access questions were adapted from the Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) core household WASH questionnaire [25]. For water, we asked about sources for handwashing and toilets. We included an additional question about water availability, “Are there times of year when water (for all purposes — drinking, handwashing, toilet, domestic purposes) is less available to you?” Response options were “Yes, every year,” “Yes, some years,” or “Never.” We asked questions to obtain the JMP classifications for sanitation access (open defecation, unimproved, limited, basic, safely managed) and hygiene access (none, limited, basic). We did not ask children about sanitation facilities. Additional questions asked respondents to rate satisfaction with their current handwashing facility and whether they had a facility close to the toilet1 in addition to the JMP questions about the handwashing facility used at home. Children rated satisfaction with their current handwashing facility on a 5-point smiley scale, which is useful for assessing children’s attitudes, especially when reading is not yet fluent [26, 27].

Acceptability

Acceptability score

We developed a novel quantitative approach, the acceptability score, to evaluate acceptability using a survey. We asked seven Likert scale questions corresponding to the seven components of the TFA, each on a five-point scale (Fig. 3). For example, the “self-efficacy” question asked: “How confident are you to use the Spatap without help?” The authors’ application of the TFA component constructs as they relate to the Spatap are in Table 1. We scored the response options from 1 (considered highly unacceptable) to 5 (considered highly acceptable). The questions and scale were the same for children, except the affective attitude component was scored on a 5-point smiley scale.

The minimum total acceptability score was 7 (a score of 1 for each of the 7 components), and the maximum was 35 (a score of 5 for each of the 7 components) accordingly (Fig. 3). We categorised total acceptability scores into unacceptable [7–14], partially acceptable [15–21], acceptable [22–28], and highly acceptable [29–35]. We asked an additional question on what the recommended purpose of the Spatap was, with options: filtering/cleaning water, storing water, handwashing, other (open response) or “I do not know”. We also included two open-response questions on barriers and enablers: “What would help you to use the Spatap instead of your current facility?” and “What would stop you using the Spatap instead of your current facility?”

Survey data processing and analysis

We created tabulated summaries of the survey data and conducted descriptive data analysis in Microsoft Excel (version 2212). All further statistical analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software (version 4.2.1) [28].

Child and adult data were analysed separately. For adults, total acceptability scores (range 7–35) were modelled as the outcome with predictor variables listed in Additional File 1 Table A1-1. We initially fit a multilevel multiple linear model to account for clustering; however, the estimated random effects variance was zero because there were insufficient degrees of freedom to precisely estimate between-community differences, and the model did not converge due to the distribution of individuals across the households, therefore no results are reported. We then proceeded with single-level models.

We analysed acceptability score as a continuous and binary outcome (acceptability score ≥ 32 or < 32 based on minimum density point, see Additional File 1). For the binary outcome, we used Chi-squared tests for bivariate associations and multivariate logistic regression. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis to determine the effects of analysing independent individuals, by randomly selecting one participant from each household, reducing the sample size from 166 to 101 (“reduced model”). We then repeated the steps of the above analyses. Further details of the statistical analysis are available in Additional File 1.

All child data were analysed together, treating observations as independent, given the small sample size. Analyses followed the same procedures as for adults. Open survey responses in iTaukei were translated to English by the bilingual field assistant. We analysed these using an inductive approach, recurring keywords were identified, tallied, and presented as a proportion of total responses [29].

We used the STROBE reporting checklist for cross-sectional studies when editing the survey methods and results, available as Additional File 2 [30].

Focus group discussions

Five months after the surveys, we returned to the communities and conducted one FGD in each. We designed the FGD component of this study in line with the Relevance, Appropriateness, Transparency and Soundness qualitative research guidelines [31], and edited in line with the COREQ guidelines, available as Additional File 3 [32].

Recruitment and eligibility criteria

Recruitment for the FGDs was via volunteer convenience sampling. We first contacted the village head, who spread the word about the FGDs and recruited volunteers. Community members were eligible to participate in the FGD if they (i) were aged over 18, (ii) had received Spataps five months earlier and (iii) provided informed consent. Participants were informed that participation in the FGD was voluntary, would not affect whether they could keep the Spatap, and that they did not have to provide a positive opinion to receive the incentive ($25 FJD local supermarket voucher).

A bilingual female moderator who had conducted the earlier surveys and one female researcher, an Australian doctoral student (RH), ran the FGDs. During the FGDs, the researcher operated the audio recording equipment and made field notes. The moderator asked ten questions, excluding additional probes, and facilitated discussion (Additional File 4). The FGD were conducted in communal areas and only the participants, moderator and researcher attended.

FGD content

The content of the questions was informed by the survey outcomes and research aims; however, questions were broad and theory-informed rather than based on specific barriers and enablers identified in the survey. The acceptability questions corresponded to the seven components of the TFA, as well as a question about overall acceptability [33]. We also included questions on how the Spatap was used (who, what, when, and where) (Additional File 4).

Translation

The question guide was developed in English. The bilingual moderator translated the questions into iTaukei. The iTaukei questions were then back-translated by another Fijian researcher who had not seen the English version. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion between one researcher and the two translators. After the FGD, the moderator transcribed all of the audio recordings verbatim in iTaukei. The iTaukei script was then translated into English by the moderator and a second bilingual health worker.

FGD data analysis

We analysed the FGD data using NVivo software (Version 12, QSR International, Melbourne, Australia) using a combination of deductive and inductive approaches [34]. We conducted a content analysis by applying predefined codes based on the seven TFA component constructs. We also used inductive coding to identify unexpected themes emerging from the data. To determine intracoder agreement, one researcher coded each transcript twice at separate time points (two months apart). A researcher who was not involved in the data collection then independently coded a 50% sample of the scripts. Intracoder and intercoder agreement, calculated by percentage agreement, were both high. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion. We selected quotes for each theme by considering the context, frequency, and internal consistency of the quote, and whether it was succinct, illustrative, and representative of the patterns in the data [35]. For an example, see Additional File 4 Table A4-1.

Any contextual clarifications are denoted with square brackets. English grammar was corrected in the translation for clarity, but the original words and meaning of the quote are retained wherever possible.

Reasons for non-participation were not recorded; however, most who were present at the time of data collection chose to participate.

Handwashing

Almost everyone (99%) had somewhere to wash their hands, usually a plastic or metal basin filled from a central tap (Table 3). However, 39% reported that soap or water was not always available to them and were therefore classified as having limited hygiene access under JMP categories. Two-thirds (68%) of the adults did not consider their handwashing facility to be “close” to their home toilet, 40% were completely satisfied with their existing facility, and fewer than 5% were somewhat or completely dissatisfied (Table 3). A higher proportion of children than adults (83% vs. 68%) reported that their home handwashing facility was not close to their toilet. A greater proportion of children were dissatisfied with their existing facility (29% of children vs. 4% of adults) (Table 3).

Sanitation

Three-quarters of respondents had access to basic sanitation facilities with an improved toilet type, as classified by JMP (Table 3). The proportion of respondents using unimproved sanitation facilities was four times that of the general Fijian population (8% vs. 2%) (Table 3).

Acceptability: survey

Total acceptability score

The median adult acceptability score was 29 out of 35 (range 18–35). Almost all adult total acceptability scores fell into the acceptable [22–28] and highly acceptable [29–35] ranges (Fig. 4). Due to an enumerator error in which the smiley scale slide was used incorrectly, only 15 of 41 children responded to the affective attitude construct question; therefore, this construct was removed and the total acceptability score for children is out of 30 (Fig. 4). The median child acceptability score was 25 out of 30 (range 18–30). No respondents had total acceptability scores in the unacceptable ranges (7–14 adults or 6–12 children).

Component constructs

Some component constructs scored more highly than others. Confidence (self-efficacy), ethicality, and perceived effectiveness of the intervention were all scored as highly acceptable (5 on the Likert scale) by 84–90% of adults (Fig. 5), and 73–80% of children (Fig. 6). Affective attitude was scored as completely acceptable by 89% of adults. Most adults (71%) and children (85%) reported they mostly or completely understood the purpose of the Spatap. Additionally, 97% of adults correctly selected the purpose of the Spatap was for handwashing. The median was 5 for affective attitude and intervention coherence in adults and children.

Burden and opportunity cost had a higher proportion of low-score responses than the other component constructs. In adults, 62% scored burden and 61% scored opportunity cost three or lower (Fig. 5). The median score was three for burden and two for opportunity cost in adults. In children, 53% scored burden and 48% scored opportunity costs as three or lower (Fig. 6). The median score for children was three for burden and four for opportunity costs.

Total acceptability score predictor variables

Adults

Employment and water availability were significant predictors of acceptability score across all valid models, the direction of effect varied (Additional file 1). In the reduced model with one participant per household, being employed was associated with lower acceptability scores than being unemployed (Table 4). Participants who always had water available had lower acceptability scores than those who reported having water unavailable every year (β = − 3.74, p = 0.01). Satisfaction with existing facility was also significant in some models (Additional file 1). In the reduced model, those who reported being “somewhat satisfied” had higher acceptability scores than those who were “completely dissatisfied” (β = 6.30, p = 0.009), while the other satisfaction categories were not significant (Table 4).

Barriers and enablers to use of the Spatap from the survey

In response to the open questions, respondents cited ease of use (54%), water saving (42%), and convenience (18%) (including proximity to the toilet) as key enablers to using the Spatap (Additional File 5, Table A5-1). Few respondents reported anticipated barriers in the survey. These included water leaking (< 3%), limited water availability (< 3%), and potential damage (< 3%). Children reported similar barriers and enablers to those reported by adults (Additional File 5, Table A5-2).

Focus group discussions

FGD participants

We conducted three FGDs with a total of 22 participants: ten in Dua, nine in Rua and three in Tolu. There were no non-participants present. As FGDs were conducted using volunteer convenience sampling, a response rate and reasons for non-participation were not available. There was an approximately equal proportion of male and female participants, and most were aged 25–54 years (Additional File 5, Table A5-3). The head of the community participated in the discussion in Dua and Rua but not in Tolu. The discussions on the Spatap lasted 20–30 min.

FGD thematic analysis

Use of the Spatap

At follow-up, all groups reported that the Spataps were still used by all family members. Children were described as especially enthusiastic users. The devices were mainly placed near toilets, front doors, or kitchens. No one reported using the Spatap for functions other than handwashing (e.g. bathing). Maintenance was largely the responsibility of adults, with several women noting that mothers were primarily responsible.

“We are all still using it.” (Multiple participants)

“The kids really like it.” (Male, Dua)

“It is us, the mothers [who are responsible].” (Female, Tolu)

Acceptability

Affective attitude

Attitudes towards the Spatap were largely positive at follow-up. Reasons participants gave included making life easier, saving water, and helping children to wash their hands. The Spatap was recommended for use in similar communities, and participants expressed a desire to see it provided for their neighbours.

“We all really like it.” (Male, Rua)

“We just really appreciate it because it has made our work easier.” (Female, Rua)

“Thank you for bringing [it] in a difficult time during COVID-19 and when we have a lack of water.” (Female, Dua)

“It has really helped, especially for our kids for washing hands.” (Female, Rua)

Intervention coherence and self-efficacy

All participants understood the function of the Spatap as a portable handwashing device. Participants highlighted that it was easy enough for children to use independently. Most people knew which bottles to use and where to place them to prevent leakage. However, a small number of participants mentioned problems with bottles leaking.

“Whenever we touch something dirty, we use it.” (Female, Dua)

“It is easy for [the children] to just open the [nozzle] and use it.” (Female, Dua)

“Even when we adults are not at home, the kids use it when they return from playing or the washroom.” (Female, Dua)

“Some bottles we use, the water keeps on leaking.” (Female, Tolu)

Burden and opportunity costs

Using or maintaining the Spatap was generally not perceived as a burden. Instead, participants noted that it saved time compared with their previous setup, with proximity to toilets and kitchens, and required minimal maintenance. However, some people with larger households found small bottles inconvenient because they required frequent refills. While no one mentioned difficulty obtaining bottles in general, one group discussed that the cost of purchasing larger bottles was prohibitive for some families. As part of this discussion of costs, participants in that community requested that bottles be provided.

“Before we had to take a long walk to the tap to wash hands, but now it’s just near the washroom, it is easier and convenient.” (Male, Rua)

“It does not take much effort… whenever the bottle is empty, we refill it, and there is a spare bottle ready.” (Female, Dua)

“The bottle is very small for the families of five… some people have ten in their families… and it takes time to change it every hour.” (Female, Dua)

“There are four litre bottles, but we have to buy them…it’s very expensive.” (Female, Dua)

Perceived effectiveness and ethicality

We did not emphasise the benefits of handwashing for reducing infectious illness when the Spatap was distributed. However, several participants in each group linked handwashing with reduced disease and perceived the Spatap as effective for limiting illness when discussing its usefulness. Some mentioned overall changes in attitudes towards handwashing due to the Spatap. Several participants noted that the Spatap did not waste much water. When we asked whether a different handwashing intervention would have been more helpful, few respondents had much to say. However, participants across all communities requested water storage tanks.

“[The Spatap is useful for] Minimising the spread of disease.” (Male, Tolu)

‘‘When this device was brought to our community, it really taught the kids and us adults the importance of our hygiene.” (Male, Dua)

“It does not waste a lot of water.” (Female, Dua)

“This is the best model.” (Male, Rua)

Discussion

We conducted a mixed-methods study in three Fijian communities to evaluate the acceptability of the Spatap, applying a novel quantitative approach alongside qualitative methods. This study is one of the first acceptability evaluations of the Spatap and contributes to understanding its potential as a WASH intervention in resource-constrained settings. Although several methodological constraints limited the depth of evaluation, both the survey and FGD findings suggest that the Spatap was an acceptable intervention in the study communities. Participants particularly valued its proximity to toilets and kitchens, ease of use, and potential to conserve water and reduce illness. Practical considerations for future implementations include upfront costs and replacement, the affordability of large-volume bottles for bigger households, occasional leakage, and the broader challenge of water availability. The acceptability score demonstrated some potential as a rapid, scalable evaluation tool, but several refinements are required before future reapplication.

Acceptability of the Spatap

Overall acceptability

Total acceptability scores were high among both adults and children, and the FGD supported overall acceptability, suggesting acceptance of the Spatap. While some issues were raised, they did not outweigh overall positive attitudes, and participants reported that the Spatap was used by everyone. However, supplementing the FGDs with direct observations of use in the communities would strengthen this finding.

Acceptability components

WASH interventions implemented in resource-constrained settings can be ineffective at improving health if they require high levels of effort for adherence [38, 39]. Although burden scored lower than other constructs, representing lower acceptability, one-fifth of participants identified “convenience” as a key enabler in the open responses. This was supported by the FGDs, in which participants reported that placing Spatap close to toilets and kitchens saved time. Consistent with this, most respondents did not consider their existing handwashing facility close to the toilet. The importance of conveniently located handwashing facilities has been demonstrated in several studies [40–42]. In communities without a handwashing facility near the toilet or kitchen, emphasising the portability of the Spatap may enhance acceptability.

While most agreed that the Spatap was convenient, some people with larger households found frequent refilling of small bottles burdensome. Similarly, a household study in Tanzania found lower acceptability of the Spatap than other mobile handwashing interventions due to perceived small capacity [9]. Using larger-volume bottles may reduce refilling frequency; however, the feasibility of this approach depends on bottle availability and affordability.

The cost of obtaining larger bottles was prohibitive for some households in this study. The importance of access to suitable bottles has been highlighted for similar interventions [43, 44]. For example, soapy water bottles were feasible in urban slums in Bangladesh, where reclaimed bottles were cheap and readily available, but less feasible in rural Africa and Asia, where households reported difficulty accessing plastic bottles [43–45]. Providing larger-volume bottles or distributing devices on a per-person rather than household basis may reduce burden and address cost issues; however, research is needed to determine the appropriate Spataps-to-person ratio, and these strategies would increase implementation costs.

Participants did not bear the intervention costs, but opportunity cost scored lower than other acceptability components. We did not systematically explore participants’ willingness to pay, an important factor influencing sustainability and scalability, which may have impacted acceptability [46, 47]. At AUD 40 (USD 28) or AUD 15 (USD 10.50) per unit for humanitarian projects, Spataps are more expensive than some low- or no-cost alternatives [8]. In resource-constrained settings where materials are readily available, and households have the capacity to construct and maintain handwashing devices, low- or no-cost handwashing stations may be more appropriate. For example, formative research in rural Bangladesh described homemade handwashing stations with minimal cost, reflecting the availability of locally sourced materials [48]. While they are recyclable and durable in the environment, Spataps cannot be recreated from locally available materials if they are damaged. As a purpose-designed device offering consistent functionality and durability, Spataps may be better suited to contexts where durability, standardisation, or rapid deployment are prioritised, or where materials or capacity to produce homemade devices are limited (e.g. humanitarian or disaster settings). These trade-offs should be explicitly considered in future implementation planning, particularly where users bear direct costs.

Participants generally understood how the Spatap works and felt confident using it, indicating high intervention coherence and self-efficacy, though some leakage challenges were reported by a small number of survey respondents (open response on barriers) and in FGDs. Leaking can result from incorrect assembly or from using bottles made from thin plastic material [8]. Incorrect bottle selection may have been due to limited availability of suitable bottles or a misunderstanding of the instructions. In future implementations, providing written instructions in the local language, with accompanying pictures, may be more accessible to people with varying literacy levels and reduce the potential for misunderstanding [10].

Acceptability scores were higher among participants who reported experiencing water availability challenges, and saving water emerged as an enabler in both open responses and FGDs. Most respondents considered saving water during handwashing important, reflecting the frequent experience of limited or intermittent water supply. These findings suggest that Spatap is likely to be most acceptable in settings where water efficiency, compared to existing facilities, directly addresses a salient need. In a Fijian settlement with hand washing facilities similar to those in the study communities, the belief that handwashing requires a lot of water discouraged the practice, suggesting that water-saving interventions may help overcome this barrier [49]. For Spatap implementation, explicitly demonstrating the small volume of water required for effective handwashing, rather than assuming this benefit is self-evident, may support both acceptability and behaviour change [40]. Participants also perceived the Spatap as effective in reducing illness, despite health benefits not being emphasised during distribution. In addition to discussing Spatap, all FGD groups requested water tanks. Linking Spatap use to locally meaningful outcomes such as water conservation and minimising illness may have enhanced overall acceptability but highlights that a holistic approach to addressing water and hygiene is required to achieve WASH for all and improve health outcomes.

Sociodemographic predictors

Children’s and adults’ scores were not directly comparable; however, all FGD groups consistently identified children as particularly enthusiastic Spatap users, and a small number of survey respondents identified children as an enabler. Similar patterns have been observed for other mobile handwashing interventions, such as tippy taps, which children in Africa found humorous and easy to use [7]. There is some evidence that children’s acceptance can influence household and community-level uptake [50, 51]. In this study, a higher proportion of children than adults were dissatisfied with their existing facilities. The satisfaction question did not explicitly specify the home handwashing facility but all data collection was framed around household practices and conducted in participants’ homes. However, children may have interpreted the question as referring to facilities in other settings, such as schools.

A higher proportion of children also reported that they did not have a handwashing facility near their toilet, which may have increased the appeal of a portable handwashing device. However, this finding may also reflect differences in how children interact with sanitation environments. Children’s behaviours may be shaped by context-specific routines and infrastructure which may differ from household arrangements (e.g. school-based facilities). ln future Spatap implementations, targeted promotion among children may increase overall acceptability.

Adult acceptability scores did not vary significantly by age or gender. However, participants in all three FGDs suggested that mothers were primarily responsible for maintaining the Spataps, potentially reflecting gendered divisions of household labour in iTaukei Fijian communities [52]. Implementation strategies should recognise potential gendered roles in Spatap maintenance and ensure that training and support are accessible to those responsible for upkeep. The design of this study, which involved mixed-gender FGD and lacked gender-specific probes, limited deeper exploration of gendered experiences. Future evaluations using gender-sensitive methods would strengthen understanding and align with broader calls for inclusive WASH programming [53, 54]. Further, the age categories were unevenly distributed: few respondents were aged over 75, and none of the FGD participants were in this age group. This reflects the Fijian population distribution, where only 1.6% are over 75, but also indicates that perspectives from older adults were limited in this evaluation [55]. Evidence from the broader WASH literature suggests age can shape behaviours and interaction with interventions, so future evaluations should aim to include older people wherever feasible [56].

Other sociodemographic predictors showed inconsistent associations with acceptability scores, limiting meaningful interpretation in context. Overall, these findings suggest that Spatap acceptability in this study was more strongly determined by contextual and functional factors, such as portability, ease of use, and convenience, than by demographic characteristics. For future implementations, this suggests that targeting deployment based on contextual needs and usage patterns rather than demographic segmentation alone is likely to be more effective.

Reflections on the acceptability score method

While using a quantitative acceptability score had some analytical advantages, these should be interpreted alongside important limitations, and we do not recommend reapplying the tool without modification.

Survey-based data collection was efficient relative to the size of the study team, and treating acceptability as a quantitative outcome enabled analysis of significant predictors, as well as comparisons between communities and across component constructs. However, these analytical advantages were constrained by the uneven distribution of participants across communities in this study, with the largest community contributing a disproportionate number of participants. Consequently, a multilevel model could not be fit to account for clustering and between-community differences could not be estimated. The acceptability scores represent individual-level associations within an unbalanced sample rather than robust comparisons across the groups. Additionally, uneven age distribution and different scales for children and adults limited direct comparability across age groups. Furthermore, using a single question per component construct simplified the survey but prevented assessment of participants’ understanding of each construct, which is particularly important in cross-cultural research [57].

The study was originally designed to include baseline and follow-up measurements of the total acceptability score for comparison of acceptability over time. However, repeat surveys were not feasible due to COVID-19 and project constraints, and FGDs were conducted instead. As a result, temporal comparisons and longitudinal assessment of acceptability were not possible, and the analysis is restricted to a single cross-sectional time point. This also limited the ability to assess stability and reliability of the acceptability score over time.

Although the quantitative approach enabled structured analysis, its usefulness in this study was limited by these methodological constraints, which reduced the scope for more detailed statistical analysis. Strengthening the acceptability score as a quantitative tool requires more purposive or evenly distributed sampling, as well as consistent questions for adults and children. Repeated measurements over time would enable analysis of change and reliability. The use of multiple questions per construct, combined with cognitive testing, would also enable correlation-based measures to assess comprehension [58, 59]. In addition, varying the order of response categories could help to reduce satisficing.

More broadly, quantifying acceptability reduced the complexity of a concept that is inherently multidimensional [60]. While the scoring approach provided a simplified summary measure and allowed structured analysis, it did not fully capture the contextual and interpretive dimensions of acceptability. To retain the benefits of quantification while improving depth, future studies should supplement score data with qualitative explanations of responses (e.g. by asking participants to explain their ratings in open-ended questions). Opportunity costs questions should also explicitly explore willingness to pay for the intervention.

Finally, the theoretical underpinning also warrants consideration. The TFA captured a broad suite of factors that may have influenced acceptability and supported alignment between the survey and FGD findings. However, some context-specific influences, including environmental considerations (e.g. water availability) and the accessibility of all intervention components (i.e. bottles), were not well captured by the framework, despite their importance in other WASH intervention studies [59]. Development of a WASH-specific acceptability framework, potentially integrated with established frameworks like IBM-WASH, is an area for future research [61].

Limitations

There are several considerations for interpreting the findings beyond the limitations of the sampling and scoring methods discussed above.

High acceptability scores may be due in part to satisficing, where respondents select the same response option for each question to reduce the mental effort of completing a survey [62]. However, scores were still high among those who varied their responses, so it is unlikely to be the only reason for high scores. Future studies could reorder the response categories for acceptability questions to minimise satisficing.

The ethicality component, intended to capture alignment between the intervention and community values, was represented in the survey by a single question asking how important conserving water while handwashing was to the respondent, rather than ethical alignment with handwashing more broadly. This approach assumed that Spatap saves water compared with existing facilities, although this has not been formally evaluated. As a result, the ethicality measure provides a higher-level, partial representation of the construct, with other dimensions of ethicality informed by survey questions and FGDs. Emphasising water saving in this way may have biased findings towards it as a perceived enabler; however, water saving also emerged independently in open survey responses and FGDs, suggesting that its importance was not solely an artefact of survey design.

Only one FGD was conducted per community, they were short in duration and with few participants in each, so it is unlikely that thematic saturation was achieved. The results should be viewed as illustrative of participant perspectives rather than a comprehensive representation of all possible views. FGD participants were also recruited through convenience sampling; those who were less accepting of the Spatap may have chosen not to participate, potentially biasing the results toward positive outcomes. While the FGDs generated some useful insights, they were also conducted in a manner closer to a group interview, and little additional data was generated from interactions, an important aspect of FGD methodology [63]. The formal, semi-structured style of FGDs may not align with different cultural approaches to knowledge sharing [64]. In Fiji and other PICTs, the Talanoa methodology, which involves informal, reciprocal knowledge sharing and storytelling, may be an appropriate alternative for future evaluations [65]. Additionally, direct observation of use status at follow-up, FGD, and triangulation with key informant interviews could supplement attitudinal information and provide a more in-depth understanding of acceptability.

Position

Researcher paradigms influenced the development of the research questions, study design, and interpretation of findings. The primary investigators were academics from diverse disciplinary backgrounds at Australian and New Zealand universities, while the broader team included staff from the Fijian MOHMS. The FGDs were facilitated by a Fijian community health worker known to the participants and fluent in the local iTaukei dialect. This combination of international academics and local practitioners was a strength, integrating contextual knowledge and research expertise. The involvement of Fijian MOHMS staff and a local moderator may have enhanced cultural appropriateness, participant trust, and the validity of community perspectives. However, the primary investigators’ status as external academics influenced which questions were prioritised and how data were interpreted, potentially emphasising theoretical constructs over locally embedded meanings. These dynamics highlight the importance of reflexivity in cross-cultural WASH research and the need to balance external scientific frameworks with local ways of knowing [66].

Conclusions

This study provides one of the first acceptability evaluations of the Spatap as a household WASH intervention in a resource-constrained Fijian community context. Overall, findings from both the survey and FGDs indicate that the Spatap was acceptable to participants, particularly when portability, proximity to toilets and kitchens, ease of use, and water-saving potential addressed salient contextual needs. Acceptability was shaped more by functional and environmental factors than by sociodemographic characteristics in this study, although children were identified as enthusiastic users, suggesting a potential entry point for household and community uptake. Practical considerations for future implementation include bottle size and availability, leakage, maintenance burden and cost, and the need to position the Spatap appropriately relative to lower-cost, locally constructed alternatives. These findings indicate that the Spatap may be most suitable in contexts where durability, standardisation, rapid deployment, or water efficiency are prioritised, and where it forms part of an integrated approach to addressing water availability and hygiene.

The study also contributes methodological insights into the use of a quantitative acceptability score. Treating acceptability as a quantitative outcome enabled efficient data collection, comparison across constructs, and identification of predictors, offering some advantages in settings where resources constrain extensive qualitative work. The alignment between score patterns and FGD findings suggests that the approach is conceptually promising. However, limitations related to construct depth, sampling, and cultural interpretation mean that the score, as applied here, should not be reapplied without modification. With refinement, including using multiple items per construct, cognitive testing, complementary open-ended questions, repeated measurement and a more robust sampling approach, the acceptability score could become a valuable tool for rapid evaluation and for tracking acceptability over time in future WASH implementations.

Overall, this study underscores the importance of grounding WASH interventions in local contexts and user priorities to enhance acceptability. Ensuring both intervention acceptability and methodological appropriateness will be essential for interventions to contribute meaningfully to achieving sustained WASH for all.
