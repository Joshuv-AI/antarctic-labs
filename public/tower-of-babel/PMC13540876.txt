EMD-HVG: a normalization-independent method for highly variable gene selection based on Earth mover’s distance
Authors: Chunfang Peng, Guobin Li, Jiamiao Wu, Feng Tan, Xiaobo Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdentifying highly variable genes (HVGs) is a critical step in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) analyses. Conventional approaches typically rely on normalization to adjust for library size differences and estimate variability under predefined distributional assumptions. However, normalization procedures can inadvertently mask true biological variability, and the assumed distributions often fail to adequately capture the sparsity and noise inherent in scRNA-seq and ST data. To address these limitations, this study aims to develop a normalization-independent and nonparametric method for robust HVG identification in scRNA-seq and ST data.ResultsWe propose Earth Mover’s Distance based Highly Variable Gene identification method (EMD-HVG), a normalization-free and nonparametric framework for HVG identification. EMD-HVG models gene-specific expression patterns using a mixture distribution across cells or spatial locations, thereby preserving native biological heterogeneity without requiring library size normalization. To measure expression variability, EMD-HVG employs Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD), a robust, nonparametric metric that avoids reliance on any specific distributional form.ConclusionExtensive benchmarking on both real and simulated datasets demonstrates that EMD-HVG consistently outperforms existing HVG detection methods. It achieved top performance in 26 out of 30 evaluation scenarios across various accuracy metrics. Furthermore, HVGs identified by EMD-HVG significantly enhance the quality of downstream clustering, facilitating more precise cell-type delineation across diverse biological systems. Introduction

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) have facilitated high-resolution characterization of gene expression at both the cellular and spatial levels [1]. However, the high dimensionality and sparsity of these data introduce analytical challenges, particularly the “curse of dimensionality” and an abundance of zero counts that can obscure biological signals [2, 3]. A common approach to addressing these challenges is to select highly variable genes (HVGs), which display substantial expression variability across cells or spatial regions [4]. By reducing dimensionality and preserving key biological signals, HVG selection alleviates data sparsity issues and enhances clustering, trajectory analysis, and other downstream applications [5]. Typically, HVG selection methods consist of two main steps: normalization and quantification of gene variability.

Normalization is essential for addressing library size differences among cells or spatial spots due to capture efficiency, sequencing depth, and other technical confounders [3, 6]. Global-scaling normalization methods, initially developed for bulk RNA-seq data analysis, are commonly applied to scRNA-seq, such as DESeq [7] and trimmed mean of M values (TMM) normalization [8]. These methods adjust expression levels within each sequencing unit (cell or spot) by a constant factor, aiming to equalize total mRNA content across units. However, this approach may be inadequate when substantial heterogeneity exists across cell types, potentially masking actual biological signals. To address these limitations, several normalization methods tailored for scRNA-seq, such as BASiCS [9] and scran [3], have been developed. Nevertheless, these methods may still encounter limitations when the number or fold change of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the cell population surpasses certain thresholds [6]. Studies have shown that HVG selection results can vary substantially depending on the normalization method, underscoring the need for more robust approaches that reduce reliance on normalization strategies [10].

After normalization, various statistical models are employed to quantify the variability in gene expression and identify highly variable genes (HVGs). Existing methods, such as Seurat [11] and BASiCS, rely on strong assumptions about the distributions of gene expression. However, these assumptions are often insufficient to accurately represent the characteristics of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) data. Gene expression profiles in these datasets exhibit high sparsity and are dominated by near-zero values. This sparsity challenges traditional variance models in accurately assessing gene variability, potentially leading to biased estimates of gene variability. Moreover, conventional models assume a single-dimensional distribution, limiting their capacity to account for the intricate expression patterns and high variability across diverse cell types [6].

To address these limitations, this study aims to develop a normalization-independent and nonparametric method for robust HVG identification in scRNA-seq and ST data. Accordingly, we propose Earth Mover’s Distance based Highly Variable Gene identification method (EMD-HVG), which leverages Earth Mover’s Distance to quantify gene expression variability without relying on predefined distributional assumptions or library size normalization.

Material and methods

The EMD-HVG framework comprises two key components designed to overcome challenges in gene expression analysis (Fig. 1). First, a gene-specific mixture model is developed to represent gene expression distributions across varying library sizes, eliminating the need for traditional normalization methods that adjust for technical biases. Second, the Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD) is employed to quantify gene expression variability, and the top k genes with the highest EMD score are identified as HVGs. EMD is a robust, nonparametric approach that avoids assumptions about underlying distributions and can effectively measure differences between the high-dimensional distributions generated by gene-specific mixture models [12, 13].

This approach reduces biases associated with conventional normalization methods, providing a more precise and comprehensive representation of gene expression variability.

Quantify gene expression variability based on the Earth Mover’s distance

To identify highly variable genes (HVGs), we quantify gene expression variability using the Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD), which measures the dissimilarity between the distribution of a target gene and that of a reference gene set with similar average expression levels [12, 14]. Low EMD scores indicate stable expression, whereas high EMD scores highlight HVGs, assuming that genes with similar average expression levels should exhibit comparable distributions unless they are highly variable. We apply a pre-filtering procedure to reduce the risk of reference contamination by extreme or distributionally discordant genes. Specifically, candidate genes are pre-screened to remove extreme variance outliers (variance larger than 99.9th percentile). For each target gene, we form an initial pool of 16 mean-matched candidates and discard the four candidates with the largest average Kullback-Leibler Divergence, retaining about 12 reference genes. Comparative analysis of HVG selection methods

To assess the performance of EMD-HVG, we applied it to six real datasets and 216 simulated datasets, comparing its HVG selection accuracy with that of Seurat [11, 15], Scran [3], Monocle [16], and BASiCS. The evaluation was conducted based on two key criteria: (1) HVG detection accuracy, measured against reference HVG sets generated using Seurat’s FindMarkers function [17], and (2) clustering accuracy, evaluated using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI). Before applying Seurat’s FindMarkers function to construct the reference HVG sets, the data were normalized using Seurat’s NormalizeData function. This ensures that the reference HVG sets were derived under standard preprocessing, avoiding any bias in favor of normalization-free methods such as EMD-HVG. Each method ranked genes based on its default HVG selection strategy. We evaluated HVG selection accuracy using standard performance metrics, including the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), precision, sensitivity, specificity, and Jaccard similarity. The top 2000 HVGs identified by each method were evaluated using Seurat’s clustering pipeline, with ARI computed relative to ground truth annotations (See Supplementary for details.)

Accuracy of HVG selection

Our comparative analysis across six datasets and five performance metrics demonstrates that EMD-HVG consistently outperforms existing methods in identifying HVGs. EMD-HVG achieved the top rank in 87% of comparisons (26 out of 30), surpassing Seurat, Scran, Monocle, and BASiCS in HVG detection (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Table S2). Among the other methods, Scran ranked second in overall performance, achieving the highest score in 17% of evaluations (4 out of 30).

EMD-HVG achieved the highest mean AUC (0.907) and demonstrated superior performance over all other methods across datasets (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. S2). In comparison, Monocle, Scran, Seurat, and BASiCS exhibited lower AUC values of 0.749, 0.648, 0.628, and 0.573, respectively. To assess performance more comprehensively, we compared the top 2000 genes identified by each method against the reference HVG set across four additional metrics. EMD-HVG consistently ranked first in all datasets except for Mouse_Embryos, where it placed second. We found that the Mouse_Embryos dataset is based on the Stereo-seq platform and exhibited the lowest sparsity and the highest mean gene expression among all datasets (Supplementary Table S1), suggesting that the advantage of EMD-HVG may be less pronounced in such contexts.

Specifically, it achieved the highest mean sensitivity (0.546) and specificity (0.930). While specificity differences among methods were relatively small, ranging from 0.899 to 0.926, sensitivity differences were more pronounced, varying between 0.214 and 0.458. The superior sensitivity of EMD-HVG was particularly evident in datasets with low gene expression levels. In the DLPFC dataset, which had the lowest average gene expression, EMD-HVG outperformed the second-ranked Scran by 0.237. These findings highlight the robustness of EMD-HVG in detecting HVGs in datasets characterized by low gene expression.

Precision and Jaccard similarity exhibited substantial variation across datasets due to differences in reference HVG set sizes. Impact of HVG selection on clustering accuracy

We evaluated clustering accuracy on ST datasets with ground truth annotations using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI). We observed significant differences in ARI across methods (Supplementary Table S3). EMD-HVG achieved the highest clustering accuracy in the DLPFC (ARI = 0.381) and Mouse_Embryos datasets (ARI = 0.487). However, in the ST_PDAC dataset, which contains only 428 spots, EMD-HVG yielded a lower ARI (0.359), ranking below Seurat (0.493), Scran (0.413), and BASiCS (0.367). These results suggest that dataset characteristics, particularly the number of spatial spots, may influence clustering performance.

To assess the impact of HVG selection accuracy on clustering performance, we evaluated the HVG selection methods based on both HVG selection accuracy and clustering accuracy, and then compared their consistency. As shown in Fig. 3c, the ranking of HVG selection accuracy between methods was highly consistent with their ranking in clustering performance. Among all methods, EMD-HVG and Scran demonstrated superior performance, consistently ranking highest in both HVG selection accuracy and clustering performance (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Detailed analysis of the DLPFC dataset

To comprehensively evaluate HVG selection and clustering performance, we focused on the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) dataset from the spatialLIBD resource (https://spatial.libd.org/spatialLIBD/) [18]. This dataset serves as a robust benchmark for comparing HVG detection methods with manually curated cell types and known marker genes.

To evaluate the influence of the number of selected HVGs on method performance, we analyzed the DLPFC dataset with k = 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, 2000, and 5000. For HVG detection accuracy, EMD-HVG performed less favorably at smaller k values (≤ 250), ranking behind Scran and Monocle. Its performance began to catch up from k = 500, and for larger sets (k ≥ 1000), it consistently achieved the highest accuracy (Supplementary Table S4). Clustering performance showed a similar dependence on k: accuracy generally increased with the number of HVGs, peaking at k = 2000 (ARI = 0.296) before slightly declining at k = 5000 (ARI = 0.215) (Supplementary Table S5). At very small k values (50 and 100), Monocle yielded the best clustering results, but its accuracy did not improve as k increased. In contrast, EMD-HVG and Seurat improved steadily from k ≥ 250, consistently ranking among the top performers. When k ≥ 1000, EMD-HVG consistently ranked first in both HVG detection and clustering accuracy, highlighting its robustness for larger gene sets. These results indicate that Scran and Monocle are preferable for small-scale HVG selection, while EMD-HVG is the optimal choice for larger-scale analyses.

To evaluate how well different methods prioritize biologically relevant genes, we compared the recall rates and rankings of 13 known marker genes (Supplementary Table S6). EMD-HVG, Seurat, and Scran exhibited superior recall rates, ranking 50% marker genes at positions 255, 243, and 536, respectively. We further categorized marker genes into high-expression (greater than 1), mid-expression (ranging from 0.1 to 1), and low-expression (less than 0.1) groups. Seurat, Scran, and Monocle tended to prioritize high-expression genes, with all three high-expression markers ranked within the top 200. For mid-expression genes, EMD-HVG and Seurat performed similarly, achieving median ranks of 240 and 250, respectively, outperforming Scran and Monocle, which had median ranks of 536 and 567. EMD-HVG demonstrated a remarkable ability to identify low-expression markers within the top 500, outperforming Seurat, which ranked these markers at 762 and 2897. Scran and Monocle failed to identify marker genes with expression levels below 0.15.

We further examined the expression patterns of the top 10 HVGs selected by each method. As illustrated in Fig. 2f–j, EMD-HVG prioritized cell-type-specific genes, whereas Scran and Monocle tended to choose more broadly expressed HVGs. These findings suggest that EMD-HVG is particularly effective at capturing low-abundance markers and enhancing cellular resolution, thereby addressing limitations observed in other methods.

We further compared the computational cost of all methods under identical conditions. Seurat was the fastest method (a few seconds), followed by Scran and Monocle (around one minute). In contrast, BASiCS and EMD-HVG required several minutes, reflecting their more complex modeling strategies (Supplementary Table S7). To further evaluate robustness, we conducted two stability analyses. First, runtime stability was assessed by performing five independent runs per sample; variation across runs was negligible, confirming consistent computational performance (Supplementary Table S8). Second, we assessed reproducibility of EMD scores across repeated runs, which were identical in all cases, demonstrating that EMD-HVG is fully deterministic and produces reproducible results (Supplementary Table S9).

Further analysis revealed that clustering accuracy, as measured by the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), was influenced by key dataset characteristics, including library size and the proportion of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Discussion

In both ST and scRNA-seq real datasets, EMD-HVG consistently achieves strong performance across multiple HVG selection accuracy metrics. Specifically, it ranked first in 26 out of 30 comparisons across five performance metrics and six real datasets. Similarly, EMD-HVG achieved high accuracy in simulation datasets, maintaining optimal performance under various simulated conditions. These results highlight EMD-HVG’s effectiveness in identifying genes essential for cellular heterogeneity, a key aspect of complex biological systems.

Furthermore, we examined the impact of HVG selection on clustering accuracy. EMD-HVG achieved the highest ARI value in both DLPFC and Mouse_Embryos datasets. EMD-HVG’s performance improved with increased sequencing units and cellular diversity, highlighting its robustness and adaptability in complex biological contexts. In our simulation design, the number of cells increased concurrently with the number of cell types, thereby raising the complexity of the clustering task. This led to a decline in performance for other methods (ARI decreased from 0.246 to 0.194), whereas EMD-HVG benefited from the larger sample size per group, enabling more accurate estimation of the underlying probability distributions and resulting in enhanced clustering robustness (ARI increased from 0.319 to 0.502).

EMD-HVG also showed a clear advantage in identifying low-expression marker genes. Although low-expression genes comprise 58.58% of the dataset, only 15.38% the known marker genes belong to this category, underscoring the challenge of detecting biologically relevant low-abundance markers. EMD-HVG successfully ranked the low-expressing genes FREM3 and TRABD2A at positions 301 and 171, significantly outperforming comparative methods.

Despite its strong performance, EMD-HVG has limitations when applied to datasets with extremely low sequencing coverage. For example, its performance in the ST_PDAC dataset was suboptimal, likely due to the limited number of spots (428 spots), which may provide insufficient observations for accurately modeling gene-specific distributions. Additionally, its effectiveness depends on having sufficient genes with comparable expression levels to enable meaningful variance estimation. Another limitation lies in computational efficiency. Compared with Seurat (fastest) and methods such as Scran and Monocle, both EMD-HVG and BASiCS require longer runtime due to their more complex modeling strategies. In particular, the increased cost of EMD-HVG arises from two components: the construction of multivariate expression distributions for each gene and the optimal transport computation inherent to Earth Mover’s Distance. While this trade-off is consistent with the algorithmic design, future work may consider optimization strategies such as approximation of optimal transport or parallelization to improve computational efficiency without sacrificing accuracy.

Beyond scRNA-seq and ST data, EMD-HVG’s adaptable framework holds promise for broader applications in omics research, including methylomics, proteomics, and single-cell ATAC-seq. With methodological refinements and the incorporation of additional biological priors, EMD-HVG can potentially become a versatile tool for studying gene expression variability and cellular heterogeneity across diverse biological systems.

Conclusion

EMD-HVG provides a novel framework for identifying highly variable genes (HVGs) in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) datasets. By combining a gene-specific mixture model with the Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD), EMD-HVG accurately quantifies gene expression variability without relying on distributional assumptions or normalization procedures that may obscure biological signals. It also enhances downstream clustering, facilitates the effective detection of low-expression marker genes, and maintains performance under varying levels of cellular complexity. Hence, EMD-HVG holds substantial promise as a broadly applicable tool for dissecting gene expression variability and cellular heterogeneity in complex biological systems.
