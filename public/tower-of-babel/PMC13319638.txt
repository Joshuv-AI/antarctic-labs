Generating real-world evidence via partnerships between implementing non-governmental organisations and research institutions

The AIRE project (Améliorer l’Identification des détresses Respiratoires chez l’Enfant) demonstrated that a simple device—a pulse oximeter—can contribute to improving the management of severely ill children at primary care level in resource-limited settings. The data presented in this BMJ Global Health supplement confirm its usefulness and acceptability in detecting hypoxaemia, improving early recognition of severe cases and strengthening the quality of clinical decision-making.1 2 These findings are further informed by a realist evaluation conducted as part of the project, which explored how contextual and implementation factors shaped pulse oximetry adoption.3

The project was implemented by a consortium of non-governmental organisations (NGOs): Terre des hommes (Tdh), Solthis and the Alliance for International Medical Action (ALIMA). Acting in partnership with research institutions, the Institut National de la Santé et de la Recherche Médicale (INSERM) and the Institut de Recherche pour le Développement (IRD), the project generated robust evidence from four West African countries, Burkina Faso, Guinea, Mali and Niger. The project benefited greatly from the NGOs’ long-standing presence and trusted relationships with Ministries of Health and local communities—a major asset for conducting research4 embedded in routine health services.

Importantly, the introduction of pulse oximetry did not occur in isolation. It was implemented as part of a broader package of interventions, ranging from community-level activities to strengthening referral systems, hospital-based care, district health governance and national policy dialogue. The evidence presented in this Supplement therefore reflects not only the performance of a device but also the conditions required—in fragile health systems—for its appropriate use.

Revitalising integrated management of childhood illness: the first condition for appropriate use of pulse oximetry

One of AIRE’s major contributions was to reveal persistent limitations in implementation of Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI)5—a WHO strategy developed in the 1990s for simplified diagnosis and management of childhood illnesses at primary healthcare level in resource-limited settings. Although included in national guidelines, IMCI has often been applied inconsistently in practice, hindered by limited refresher training, insufficient supportive supervision and heavy workloads, as documented in multiple settings.68 Within AIRE, the introduction of pulse oximetry served as a catalyst to reinvigorate IMCI by updating national recommendations, reinstating IMCI registers and strengthening the skills of hundreds of health workers—activities supported by implementing NGOs.

While AIRE supported IMCI strengthening, it also highlighted persistent implementation gaps. The clinical value of pulse oximetry depends on consistent application of IMCI decision and referral pathways. AIRE’s findings nonetheless showed that, despite implementation support, a substantial number of children classified as severe cases were not referred according to IMCI recommendations.9

Digital decision-support tools: a lever for strengthening guideline implementation

Evidence from several African settings suggests that digital clinical decision-support tools can strengthen adherence to IMCI, reduce classification errors and support health workers operating under demanding conditions.10 Within AIRE, the digital IMCI platform IeDA (Integrated e-Diagnostic Approach) implemented by Tdh in Burkina Faso and parts of Mali was associated with improved adherence to pulse oximetry use in routine care.4 While not formally demonstrated as an outcome driver in AIRE, IMCI digitisation appears to be a promising implementation lever.

From hypoxaemia detection to lives saved in fragile contexts

The importance of investing in training, supervision, and coaching

Costed national scale-up plans developed as part of the AIRE project highlighted a commonly underestimated reality: while pulse oximeters themselves are relatively inexpensive, the system required to ensure their appropriate use is costly.

Initial and refresher training, regular supportive supervision, coaching and mentoring, and device maintenance constitute the bulk of the investment. These needs are consistent with findings reported in this Supplement, where variability in quality of care points to broader gaps in workforce capacity.

Within AIRE, experience from Niger illustrates both opportunities and challenges: building on the project’s advocacy, national scale-up was enabled through the integration of pulse oximetry into Global Fund and USAID (United States Agency for International Development) supported programmes, but the broader challenge persists—ensuring sustainable financing for ongoing training, supervision and maintenance.

Other essential health system enablers

As highlighted in other commentaries and articles in this Supplement, major gaps persist in referral9 and management of severe childhood illness with hypoxaemia,1 limiting the impact of improved detection tools. Community engagement and early care-seeking11 are also critical, as diagnostic improvements only translate into benefit when children reach care in time.1 Finally, financial accessibility of services—including reduced or absent out-of-pocket expenses—appears to be an important enabling factor for timely care-seeking and referral uptake.1 9 12 13

Conclusion

Following its pilot introduction, the four AIRE countries have now integrated pulse oximetry into national IMCI guidelines—a major achievement enabled through close collaboration with national authorities and effective translation of research findings into policy and practice.

Lessons from AIRE show that health innovations deliver sustainable impact only when systems are prepared to absorb them. Scaling up pulse oximetry should therefore not be viewed merely as expanding access to a device, but as integrating a technology into a comprehensive package of primary healthcare interventions. Pulse oximetry is an essential tool—but outcomes ultimately depend on the healthcare system as a whole. Other trials such as TIMCI (Tools for Integrated Management of Childhood Illness) did not demonstrate significant impact of pulse oximetry introduction on severe clinical outcome.14 AIRE was not designed as a mortality impact trial; however, converging findings suggest that system constraints may limit the measurable effect of diagnostic innovations alone.
