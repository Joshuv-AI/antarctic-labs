The Distribution of Xenobiotics of Terrigenous Origin in the Nearshore Waters of a Caribbean Island
Authors: Lisa Schellenberg, Vesna Bertoncelj, Virginia Sánchez Barranco, Meine D. Boer, Daniel Petras, Craig E. Nelson, Benjamin Mueller, Mark J. A. Vermeij, Furu Mienis, Corina P. D. Brussaard, Andreas F. Haas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anthropogenic pollutants known as xenobiotics, such as pharmaceuticals, pesticides, or human metabolites, primarily originate on land and accumulate near populated coasts, where rainfall further increases their transport and harmful impacts on marine ecosystems. However, empirical evidence for such dynamics remains limited. Here, we investigate spatial and seasonal patterns of terrigenous xenobiotics along the land–sea continuum of Curaçao. Using solid-phase extraction and untargeted LC-HR-MS/MS metabolomics, we detected approximately 30,000 molecular features, including 1,394 xenobiotics that were most abundant in terrestrial waters, declined offshore, and correlated strongly with other terrestrial input indicators (fDOM). Results from three cross-seasonal campaigns (2021–2022) revealed that rainfall and increased water residence times were dominant drivers for xenobiotic abundances along the shore, with 3.5 times higher abundances during the 2022 wet season compared to drier periods. Additional factors were local landscape features, distance to fluvial runoff, hydrodynamic connectivity, and, for specific substance groups (e.g., human metabolites or personal care products), human activities in proximity to sampling locations. Our findings show that terrigenous xenobiotics spread island-wide, making them a broader concern beyond discharge sites. This first island-wide, seasonally resolved assessment provides a baseline for managing emerging contaminants and understanding their wider impact on Caribbean coastal ecosystems.

Introduction

Chemicals are a part of our everyday life and are used, e.g., as pharmaceuticals, pesticides, personal care items, or food additives. However, chemical pollution from sewage, industrial waste, and agricultural runoff introduces potentially harmful substances into the marine environment. − While over 2,000 new chemicals are introduced annually, their individual, cumulative, and sublethal effects on reef organisms remain unknown and make the task of defining which substances contribute foremost to marine pollution an urgent one, especially as chronic pollution likely exacerbates the effects of climate change.

Pollutants enter nearshore marine environments through surface runoff, wastewater effluent, subsurface groundwater discharge (SGD), or as aerosols, and are often dependent on climatological factors. Intense rainfall increases surface runoff and results in higher seaward SGD fluxes. ,− These increased freshwater inputs can translate to increased terrigenous organic matter fluxes, which may carry increasing pollutant loads. For example, organic matter composition along the coastline of southern California changed significantly after rainfall, with increased concentrations of pollutants from anthropogenic sources. The input of pollutants from terrestrial sources further involves complex hydrological and chemical processes such as adsorption, desorption, sediment transport, and chemical transformations. − These processes are influenced not only by rainfall but also by multiple other factors such as vegetation and antecedent dry periods, making accurate modeling and prediction of pollutant transport and fate difficult. Moreover, the diverse nature of pollutant sources, ranging from industrial effluents to pollutants from agricultural and urban sources, adds additional complexity as contaminants vary in their spatial distribution and environmental persistence. When pollutants arrive in nearshore waters, local and regional hydrodynamics shape exposure levels in the ecosystem. ,

The influx of xenobioticschemical substances foreign to a biological system or organism, i.e., anthropogenic organic molecules (such as pesticides and endocrine disruptors)is of growing concern as they can impair growth, reproduction, and resilience to stress in many marine organisms. , Xenobiotic mixtures exhibit more complex and often more severe environmental impacts than single compounds in marine systems, with individual pollutants affecting fewer than 5% of marine organisms compared to up to 15.2% of coastal species under mixture exposure. , Once introduced, xenobiotics often become absorbed by organisms and, as such, enter the marine food web, where they accumulate, especially in organisms at higher trophic levels. Some xenobiotics have been reported to disrupt key ecosystem processes, resulting in increased disease and decreased local biodiversity, ,, such as the herbicide diuron, which impairs symbiosis and resilience of reef-building corals.

Near-shore ecosystems, like coral reefs, are especially vulnerable to xenobiotics, such as herbicides or sewage effluent, which exacerbate the effects of other stressors, including ocean warming and acidification. , While coral reefs are often a focal point of concern due to their ecological and economic importance, xenobiotics pose a broader threat to marine life development, impairing fertilization and early developmental processes in fish, corals, vertebrates, and others. , Estuarine and near-shore coastal areas, where many marine species reproduce, can therefore become zones of heightened biological impact due to the accumulation of xenobiotics. The review by Ouédraogo et al. (2021) examined 7,937 ecotoxicological studies on tropical coral reefs. Despite the large number of studies, there remains a significant gap in in situ research and a general lack of knowledge about the actual presence and consequences of xenobiotics on coral reef communities under natural conditions. Although the impact of xenobiotics on coral reefs depends on site-specific factors, such as pollution sources, rainfall, and oceanographic characteristics, the increasing terrestrial and anthropogenic input driven by industrialization, land-use changes, and more frequent severe weather poses a growing general threat to coral reef ecosystems globally. Despite this overwhelming evidence of hazardous effects on coral reef ecosystems, current methods to monitor pollution focus primarily on nutrients and targeted compounds, leaving many xenobiotics and their potential impacts unaccounted for. ,

The chemical diversity of xenobioticsmany of which are complex organic molecules that can exist in multiple forms or derivativesposes a certain challenge. Untargeted metabolomics is increasingly used to study known and unknown metabolites in marine ecosystems, including xenobiotics. , Using solid-phase extraction (SPE) and liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS/MS), untargeted metabolomics of dissolved organic matter (DOM) enables the analysis of a wide range of small molecules in organismal tissues or the environment without prior knowledge of their chemical composition. ,

It is commonly postulated that xenobiotics primarily originate from terrestrial sources, increase in concentration during rainfall events, and tend to be more severe near urban areas. Here, we empirically evaluate these assumptions by assessing xenobiotic abundance both spatially and temporally across 3 sampling periods over multiple locations inland, nearshore, and offshore of the coastline of Curaçao. We used information on precipitation patterns, land use, and ocean currents to assess the relative importance of terrestrial inputs (e.g., groundwater, runoff) and oceanic mixing on the spatial distribution of a wide range of xenobiotics. To achieve this, we integrated DOM data from both land and offshore samples. Dissolved inorganic macronutrients were measured to trace nutrient enrichment associated with terrestrial inputs and runoff, while microbial abundances provided insight into biological responses to nutrient and organic matter loading. Fluorescent dissolved organic matter (fDOM) was used to characterize the source and composition of dissolved organic matter, tracing terrestrial inputs. In parallel, untargeted metabolomic analyses using solid-phase extraction coupled to LC-HRMS/MS enabled the detection and comparison of diverse xenobiotic compounds across the land–sea continuum. Together, these complementary measurements allowed us to link biogeochemical responses and the distribution patterns of terrigenous xenobiotics moving through a Caribbean land–sea continuum.

Materials and Methods

Study Site and Sample Collection

Fieldwork was conducted on and around the Caribbean island of Curaçao during three seasons (Figure , Table S1): late October to mid-November 2021 (wet season; 64 mm precipitation per month), the end of April to the beginning of June 2022 (dry season; 37 mm precipitation per month), and late October to mid-November 2022 (wet season; 144 mm precipitation per month). All precipitation data were obtained from the Meteorological Department of Curaçao, and the reported monthly averages were calculated from data recorded during the respective fieldwork periods. Curaçao is located in the southern Caribbean Sea (12°10′N, 68°59′W) with a resident population of 155,826 inhabitants in 2023 (Central Bureau of Statistics Curaçao, 2025) over 444 km2. It has intense rain periods during the wet season in October to December (Meteorological Department of Curaçao). The southwest coast (leeward side, dominated by the Caribbean Current), sheltered from steady northeast trade winds, encompasses most urban development, including the capital Willemstad. In contrast, the northeast coast (windward side) is exposed to strong swells, with rocky cliffs and is less developed. Two surface ocean flow patterns recurrently influence the (leeward) waters around Curaçao: the strong northwest-directed Caribbean Current and weaker cyclonic eddies moving predominantly in the opposite direction (Figure S9A).

Our study focused on assessing terrestrial input to nearshore waters, using offshore waters as a reference point. A total of 197 metabolomic samples were collected across multiple locations (Figure S1, Table S2), including 29 from terrestrial sources (groundwater, surface runoff and wastewater) and 53 offshore samples, collected at locations seaward of the reef edge at three depths: surface (10 m), the deep chlorophyll maximum (DCM), and a deeper depth above the seafloor, depending on bottom depth. One location (17) was sampled on the island’s wave-exposed windward shore, devoid of substantial coral reef development (benthic communities in this area are dominated by the brown seaweed Sargassum). The remaining 115 samples were from 17 nearshore sampling locations (wet season 2021: n = 42; dry season 2022: n = 36; wet season 2022: n = 37). Locations on the leeward side of Curaçao (1–16), from east to west, encompassed varying levels of anthropogenic impacts such as tourism and urban development (Figure ). Each location was sampled during at least two seasons (one wet and one dry season), and 2–3 different sampling sites were visited per day, with a maximum of 9 samples in the morning hours, including one field blank per day. Water samples were collected in 2 L polycarbonate bottles that had been acid-washed (0.12 M HCl) and rinsed with ultrapure water. Nearshore samples were hand-dipped and flushed three times with sample water from the respective depth: in the surf zone at around 0.10 m water depth, at 0.5 m (surface), around 1.5 m water depth nearshore, reef water at surface (0.5–1 m), and above the reef (∼0.5 m above the reef; bottom depth 8–10 m; Figure C). In the 2021 wet season, sampling focused on the surf zone, reef, and reef surface waters; in the 2022 dry season, reef and reef surface were prioritized, with additional nearshore surface samples; and in the 2022 wet season, sampling was largely limited to nearshore surface due to logistical constraints. After collection, water samples were kept in the dark and on ice for 2–4 h during transport until further processing in the lab at the CARMABI field station.

Laboratory Processing of Samples after Collection

In the lab, aliquots (1 mL) for microbial abundances were fixed with EM-grade glutaraldehyde (0.5% final concentration) for 30 min at 4 °C, followed by snap-freezing in liquid nitrogen and storage at −80 °C. Subsamples for fluorescent dissolved organic matter (fDOM), DOC, and inorganic macronutrients were prefiltered using a 0.2 μm filter (polyethersulfone filters, Sterivex, Millipore, USA). The fDOM samples (80 mL) were subsequently stored at 4 °C in the dark until further analysis, while approximately 80 mL of the filtrate was collected in precombusted (450 °C) amber glass vials with Teflon-lined septa caps, and acidified with 5 drops of 36% HCl to pH 2 for dissolved organic carbon (DOC) measurements, and stored at 4 °C. Furthermore, 5 mL of filtrate was collected in 6 mL high-density polyethylene pony vials (PerkinElmer, USA) for dissolved inorganic macronutrient analysis (PO4, NH4, NO3, NO2) and stored at −20 °C. The metabolomics sample comprised 1.3 L of 0.2 μm filtrate, which was acidified and kept at 4 °C in the dark, between 30 min and 3 h, until further processing.

Dissolved Organic Carbon (DOC) Measurements

Analyses were performed according to the standard protocol with a Shimadzu TOC-L Total Organic Carbon Analyzer and TNM-L Total Nitrogen measuring unit under the following conditions: combustion temperature of 720 °C, UHP oxygen carrier gas (150 mL min–1), 500 mL min–1 ozone flow generated by a Whatman TOC Gas Generator, 6-min sample sparge time, and 100 μL injection volume. Separate 1000 ppm stock solutions (83.33 mM carbon and 71.43 mM nitrogen) were prepared to create a 6-point calibration curves ranging from 0 to 300 μM for carbon and 0–50 μM for nitrogen. A small amount of concentrated hydrochloric acid was added to the standards to account for dilution during acidification. The average analytical variation of the instrument was <3.5%.

Dissolved Inorganic Macronutrient Measurements

Dissolved inorganic macronutrient concentrations were measured using colorimetric analysis with a Bran & Luebbe TrAAcs 800 continuous flow autoanalyzer, following established procedures for PO4, NH4, and NO3 and NO2. Detection limits were approximately 0.02 μM for dissolved inorganic phosphate (PO4), 0.05 μM for ammonia (NH4), 0.1 μM for nitrate (NO3), and 0.01 μM for nitrite (NO2). Each run included a standard containing all macronutrients to monitor the performance of the analysis, and calibration was performed using standards diluted in low-nutrient seawater (LNSW). A freshly diluted mixed-nutrient standard was added to each run for performance monitoring. Precision and accuracy were better than 3% and 5%, respectively, based on international standards (CRM).

Microbial Abundance Measurements

Microbial abundances are only available for the wet seasons (2021 and 2022) due to the loss of dry season samples. Flow cytometric enumeration of prokaryotes (including bacteria and archaea) and viruses was performed at the NIOZ according to the protocol by Brussaard et al. (2010) and using a TE buffer at pH 8.2. In short, samples were diluted in TE buffer and stained with the nucleic acid-specific green fluorescence dye SYBRGreen-I (10–5 dilution of commercial stock, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 15 min at room temperature for prokaryotes and for 10 min at 80 °C for viruses. Samples were then analyzed using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer with a 488 nm laser (BD Biosciences, USA). Prokaryote and virus populations were discriminated (Figure S2) based on green fluorescence versus side scatter, and raw data were analyzed using FCS Express 5 (De Novo Software, USA).

Fluorescent Dissolved Organic Matter Measurements

Samples for fluorescent dissolved organic matter (fDOM) were collected in the wet and dry seasons of 2022. As the instrument available to us did not support excitation–emission matrix (EEM) measurements, we restricted our analysis to the most promising fluorescence parameters. We targeted a total of 9 fDOM compound groups (Table S3), based on literature research and substances detected in selected test samples across sample types. Prior to the measurement of fDOM on a Hitachi F-2500 fluorescence spectrophotometer, the samples were equilibrated to room temperature for approximately 20 min. Measurements were conducted in batches of five, along with an additional blank sample consisting of ultrapure water (PURELAB Classic, Elga, UK). The blank sample was used for blank subtraction and Raman normalization. Raman normalization makes measured values independent of variations in instrumental settings, time, and location. The Raman scattering was measured at an excitation wavelength of 350 nm, with emission recorded between 371 and 428 nm, in accordance with the method described by Lawaetz and Stedmon (2009).

Dissolved Organic Matter Processing and Extraction

The metabolomics samples were processed using solid-phase extraction (SPE) of DOM. , In summary, samples were filtered through a polyethersulfone 0.22 μm Sterivex filter (Millipore, USA) at a flow rate of approximately 1 L h–1. The first 200 mL of filtrate was flushed, and the following 1.1 L of filtrate was collected, adjusted to pH 2 with 12 N HCl (Supelco, USA), and then extracted using 200 mg Bond Elut-PPL resin (styrene-divinylbenzene polymer cartridges) in a 3 mL polypropylene cartridge (Agilent 12105005, USA). The PPL cartridges were prepared before the field campaign with a 1x filling of LC-MS grade methanol (MeOH; Honeywell, Riedel-de Haën, USA) and soaked overnight with MeOH. Afterward, they were washed with 2x fillings of dH2O followed by a 1x filling of MeOH, and then dried. Immediately prior to sample processing, the PPL cartridges were activated with 1x methanol and 2x pH 2 water. Filtered samples were extracted using a peristaltic multichannel pump with a flow rate of roughly 8 mL min–1 through acid-leached (with 0.1 M HCl) and (100–200 mL) sample flushed tubing. The cartridges were desalinated with 3x fillings of pH 2 water and dried under N2 gas. The cartridges were then stored at −20 °C. Prior to LC-HRMS/MS analysis, the DOM was eluted from the cartridges with 2 mL of LC-MS grade methanol into combusted (450 °C for 4 h) 1.5 mL LC glass vials (BGB Analytik AG, Switzerland). The extract was dried in a vacuum centrifuge (Eppendorf Concentrator 5301) at 240 × g (1,400 rpm) overnight, redissolved in 100 μL of LC-MS grade MeOH, and transferred into a combusted glass insert.

UHPLC-HRMS/MS Analysis

An established nontargeted LC-MS/MS method, originally developed for seawater analysis ,,, , was used in this study. Five microliters of solid-phase extracted sample, dissolved with ≥99.9% LC-MS grade methanol (Honeywell, Riedel-de Haën, USA), were injected into an ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system (UHPLC) (Agilent 1290 Infinity II, USA), equipped with a thermostated autoinjector and column compartment, coupled to a Q-Exactive Plus Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) equipped with an Ion Max source with a HESI (Heated Electrospray Ionization) probe, according to Wegley Kelly et al., 2022. LC–HRMS analyses were performed using ESI in positive ionization mode, with chromatographic separation on a C18 core–shell column using water (0.1% formic acid) and acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid) as mobile phases A and B, respectively; full-scan data were acquired over m/z 150–1500 at a resolution of 70,000 fwhm (at m/z 200), with data-dependent MS/MS collected at 17,500 fwhm (at m/z 200). All instrument settings and quality-control measures, including the LC–MS gradient, ion-source parameters, MS/MS acquisition strategy, and QC injection scheme, are detailed in Supporting Table S4. The solid-phase extraction using PPL resin, coupled with the nontargeted tandem mass spectrometry method used in this study, was previously validated for the analysis of xenobiotics in seawater by Cancelada et al., 2022. The mean recovery at pH 2 in positive ionization mode was 37%, which falls within the typical range for hydrophobic PPL resin, generally reported to capture between 35% and 40% of dissolved organic carbon.

Mass Spectral Data Analysis

The data from liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry were processed in MZmine3 (version 3.9.0), followed by spectral matching with GNPS (https://gnps.ucsd.edu) using the feature-based molecular networking workflow (FBMN). Details of the settings are disclosed in Supporting Table S4. MS/MS spectra were queried against the GNPS spectral libraries using both direct library and analogue matching. Library matches were assigned as putative identifications (Schymanski level 2; MSI level 2), while analogue matches were interpreted as structurally related tentative candidates (Schymanski level 3; MSI level 3). , Consequently, all GNPS-based annotations were limited to confidence levels 2–3 according to both classification schemes.

The data cleanup and normalization were performed using a statistical pipeline designed specifically for feature-based molecular networks from nontargeted metabolomics data by Pakkir Shah et al. (2024). In summary, features are removed if their field blank-to-sample average intensity ratio exceeds the 0.3 cutoff, allowing up to 30% field blank contribution relative to 70% from the sample. Additional steps are taken to ensure statistical analyses can be performed by imputing missing values in the data set using the blank-removed feature table to assess frequencies across relative intensities and create random values between 0 and the minimum value in our blank-removed table, and randomly replacing all 0s with these values. Raw peak areas were used for downstream analyses to retain absolute quantitative variation, whereas TIC-normalized data were used for ordination (PCoA) and associated statistical tests to account for differences in the total signal across samples and focus on relative metabolite composition.

Curated Terrigenous Xenobiotics

We identified potential xenobiotics using the NORMAN Suspect List Exchange (NORMAN-SLE), which is a “living database” for screening chemicals of interest. The database comprises the following subcategories of xenobiotics: biocides, drinking water chemicals, drugs of abuse, food contact chemicals, human metabolites, human neurotoxins, indoor environment substances, natural toxins, persistent, mobile, and toxic substances, personal care products, pharmaceuticals, plant protection products, plastic additives, smoke compounds, surfactants.

The InChIKey, a 27-character condensed form of the International Chemical Identifier, was used to match hits between the NORMAN database and annotated features derived from feature-based molecular networking (FBMN). We aimed to identify the potential xenobiotics that are evidently coming from land-based sources into the marine environment. Xenobiotic databases like NORMAN are not marine-specific and thus may encompass compounds classified as xenobiotics in both marine or terrestrial ecosystems. We aim to avoid labeling naturally occurring substances as xenobiotics, which could overshadow terrestrial inputs due to their higher abundance. To improve our methodology for identifying true xenobiotics, which are not part of the natural marine metabolome, we required features to be at least twice as abundant on average in terrestrial samples compared to nearshore water samples, using the mean of the sample types based on raw extracted ion chromatogram (XIC). To further validate these features, we excluded any whose average offshore raw XIC abundance exceeded 30% of their average nearshore abundance, ensuring that they were not prevalent in offshore waters, in line with the blank removal criteria used to filter out background signals.

Statistics and Graphics

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, https://www.R-project.org/, for details, see Supporting Information). The majority of data, including metabolomic features, nutrients, fDOM, and microbial abundances, were not normally distributed based on the Shapiro–Wilk test (p < 0.05). We therefore used nonparametric tests for all comparisons, applying the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s pairwise tests when appropriate, except for the linear model analysis, where a parametric linear model was employed on log-transformed data as it provides interpretable parameter estimates and effect sizes. Additional justification, including residual diagnostics, is provided in the next section. Maps displaying sampling locations were created using QGIS (QGIS.org, 2024–2025. Linear Modeling of Environmental Predictors of Xenobiotic Distribution

To explore how environmental parameters predict the spatial distribution of curated terrigenous xenobiotic subgroups, we fitted linear models to each xenobiotic subgroup using R (version 4.3.0). For each xenobiotic subgroup, we fit a multiple linear regression model using the lm­() function in R, with log10-transformed xenobiotic abundance as the response variable and the measured environmental predictors as explanatory variables. If the Pearson correlation between any two predictors exceeded p > 0.5, one of the two correlated predictors was removed from subsequent analyses. Each model was fitted using ordinary least squares via the lm­() function, followed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) to identify variance among predictors. We further calculated omega squared (ω2) to estimate the variance explained by each predictor while accounting for residual error. Omega squared (ω2) is an effect size statistic that quantifies the proportion of total variance in a response variable attributable to a given factor and is commonly used in ANOVA-type analyses. This procedure was applied iteratively across all xenobiotic subgroups to ensure a consistent model structure while treating each subgroup independently. Residuals from all linear models (n = 17) were evaluated visually using quantile–quantile comparisons, confirming Gaussian distributions to meet model assumptions. Predictor variables included log-transformed sampling depth, catchment area, distance to the nearest fluvial runoff, land cover type, island zone (the west coast of the island was divided into zones, congruent to the Waitt Institute report, 2017; Figure ), coastal connectivity derived from ocean flow patterns, population density, and average rain per month, as well as the prevailing current type. Catchment areas and distances to the nearest fluvial runoff were calculated in QGIS using high-resolution elevation models to delineate catchment boundaries and determine the contributing area for each sampling location. Stream networks were identified based on Strahler order 6 to represent major streams, and distances from sampling sites to the nearest mapped stream were measured in QGIS. Catchment areas correspond to the total upstream area hydrologically connected to each point, as derived from elevation and stream network analysis. Average ocean surface flow patterns were simulated using the SCARIBOS hydrodynamic model, which is based on the Coastal and Regional Ocean Community Model (CROCO) and described in detail by Bertoncelj et al. (2025). Surface currents around Curaçao were averaged across the three fieldwork periods. Analyses of coastal connectivity and surface visitation hotspots were adapted from Bertoncelj et al. (2025) and applied to the specific fieldwork periods in this study. Hotspots are defined as areas where substances are more likely to accumulate relative to other areas, while coastal connectivity refers to the connectivity between the coastal zones. In all analyses, particles were advected by surface currents, simulating positively buoyant particles that represent substances such as nutrients, pollutants, and xenobiotics.

Results

Land-to-Sea Variability in fDOM

To better understand the land-to-sea transition of organic compounds, we compared the distribution of fluorescent dissolved organic matter (fDOM) across terrestrial, nearshore, and offshore environments. All measured fDOM components showed a significant decrease in abundance from terrestrial to marine ecosystems (Figure S3, Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.005; see statistics Table S6). As described previously, of all fDOM categories, fDOMc (“Component C” in Coble 1996) best indicates terrestrial influx. We observed a decrease in fDOMc concentrations from land to offshore, i.e., a 59-fold decrease from land to nearshore waters and a 90-fold decline when comparing land and offshore waters (Figure A). From nearshore to offshore waters, we only saw a 1.5-fold decrease in average fDOMc concentration (Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001; Dunn’s tests p < 0.002; Figure A).

Building on these observations, we conservatively identified terrigenous xenobiotics as those substances with at least a 2-fold higher abundance in terrestrial samples compared to nearshore waters, while also excluding those whose average offshore raw XIC abundance exceeded 30% of their nearshore abundance. This reduced the number of candidate xenobiotics from 5,328 to a curated data set of 1,394 terrigenous xenobiotics, from an initial pool of ∼30,000 molecular features identified across the samples. To validate our approach, we compared uncurated and curated xenobiotics data sets against fDOMc, a proxy for terrigenous humic compounds, − and examined their trends to determine if curation enhances alignment with terrigenous signals. The positive correlation between fDOMc and curated xenobiotics (r2 = 0.136, p = 0.004) was significant and stronger than that for the uncurated xenobiotics data, which was not significant (r2 = 0.007, p = 0.521, Figure B).

Environmental Setting of Nearshore Study Sites

For our main sampling locations (nearshore water sites, sampled during the 2021, 2022 wet seasons, and 2022 dry season), several significant correlations were observed among individual variables, such as macronutrients (PO4, NH4, NO3, NO2), DOC, and microbial abundances (Table , Table S6). The most pronounced seasonal differences for macronutrients were observed for NO3, PO4, and NO2 (all Kruskal–Wallis, p < 0.001; Table S5A), with the highest concentrations in the 2022 wet season. The average NO3 concentration (1.31 ± 1.41 μM) differed by 4-fold compared to the other two seasons. The PO4 concentrations (0.089 ± 0.075 μM) differed by 2.5–5-fold, and NO2 concentrations (0.080 ± 0.109 μM) differed by 2.9-fold and 1.4-fold relative to the wet season of 2021 and the dry season of 2022, respectively (Table S5A).

Concerning spatial variation, the locations within the greater Willemstad region (urban center) had, on average, higher macronutrient concentrations compared to the other four spatial zones (Figure ) during all three sampling campaigns (Table S5B, Figure S4, Table S6). In the 2021 wet season specifically, average PO4 and NH4 concentrations were higher near Willemstad (0.048 and 1.25 μM, respectively) than all the other zones (0.014 and 0.39 μM, respectively). During the 2022 dry season, NO3 and NO2 concentrations peaked in the nearshore waters near Willemstad, i.e., 0.42 μM and 0.079 μM compared to 0.18 μM and 0.026 μM for the rest of the island, respectively, with the exception of the North Shore, where levels were higher. In the 2022 wet season, NO3 concentrations were overall high, reaching up to 1.00 μM for the zones other than the greater Willemstad region (1.69 μM). This season’s sampling campaign was also characterized by extreme rainfall across the island (144 mm, i.e., 2.2- to 3.9-fold higher than the other two field campaigns; Meteorological Department Curaçao).

DOC concentrations were similar between seasons (Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.101), with seasonal averages of 93.1 μM in the 2021 wet season, 86.5 μM in the 2022 dry season, and 89.1 μM in the 2022 wet season (Table S5A, Figure S4A). During the 2022 wet season, DOC levels were on average 1.2-fold higher in the Westpunt zone compared to other zones, but not significant (Table ).

Abundances of prokaryotes and viruses varied overall between 1.1 × 105 and 11.1 × 105 mL–1, and viruses 1.0 × 106 and 30.0 × 106 mL‑1, respectively (Table ). The average abundances of prokaryotes and viruses (Table ) were, respectively, 1.9-fold and 2.5-fold higher during the wet season of 2022 (Table S5) compared to the wet season of 2021 (Kruskal–Wallis, viruses p = 0.014, prokaryotes p = 0.008; Table S6). The highest viral abundances were noted for the Westpunt zone, causing the relatively high Virus-to-Prokaryote Ratio (VPR) of 47 in the 2022 wet season (Table S5B).

Impact of Rainfall on Terrigenous Xenobiotics Distribution

The overall abundances of xenobiotics, represented by summed raw peak areas (Figure A), were significantly higher during the 2022 wet season, characterized by the highest rainfall of all three sampling periods (3.5-fold and 3.2-fold compared to the dry season of 2022 and the wet season of 2021, respectively; Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001; Dunn’s tests, p < 0.001 (Tables S6 and S7), while DOC concentrations remained stable (Figures S4A and S5). Season had a significant effect on the metabolomics composition (PERMANOVA, p = 0.001, r2 = 0.07), while zone and the season × zone interaction were not significant. The composition of the xenobiotics differed, particularly in the 2022 wet season (Figure B; PERMANOVA, p = 0.001, r2 = 0.20, followed by PERMDISP, p = 0.0028). The xenobiotics substance classes in the 2022 wet season displayed 1.4 to 7.1 times higher abundances than the dry season (Figure , Tables S6, S7). The most pronounced variations were observed for plastic additives, personal care, indoor environment, and food contact chemicals (5.6, 5.2, 5.1, and 4.9-fold higher than the wet season of 2021, Kruskal–Wallis, p < 0.001; Dunn’s test, p < 0.001), while other substance classes, like plant protection and industrial chemicals, showed only slight differences across the sampling periods (Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.002 and p = 0.001, respectively (Figure , Tables S6, S7).

Depth Variability of Terrigenous Xenobiotics

The total xenobiotic abundances were, on average, higher, 0.5 m above the reef than in surface waters (Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.013), averaging 1.3-fold across all sites (Figure S7, Table S8). This pattern was primarily driven by the 2021 wet season, when reef abundances were, on average, 1.5-fold higher than at the surface, while the 2022 dry season showed only a minor, nonsignificant increase (1.1-fold). Depth-related differences in xenobiotic composition were negligible, with PCoA and PERMANOVA analyses confirming no significant divergence between reef and surface samples across both seasons or within individual seasons (PERMANOVA, p > 0.23; Figure S6). Although no significant differences across zones were detected in paired reef-to-surface ratios (Kruskal–Wallis, 2022 dry season, p = 0.105; 2021 wet season p = 0.726), a trend toward higher ratios was observed in the Westpunt zone (2022 dry season 1.55-fold; 2021 wet season, 1.77-fold; Table S8), indicating a higher accumulation of xenobiotics at the reef depth.

Spatial Variability of Terrigenous Xenobiotics

Total xenobiotic abundances showed no consistent spatial patterns (Kruskal–Wallis by zone per season, p > 0.05; Figure A–C). During the 2022 high-precipitation season, xenobiotic abundances showed a greater fold increase relative to the dry season at the west side of the island (Westpunt) compared with the areas in front of Willemstad (Table S7). When deconvoluted into specific subgroups, different spatial distribution patterns emerged (Figure D–L), i.e., (a) the most homogeneous distribution, Group I (Figure S8A) is represented by the substance class human metabolites (Figure D–F), with the highest abundances during the 2022 wet season (Kruskal–Wallis by location, p = 0.852); (b) the higher wet season abundances but more patchily distributed Group II is represented by, e.g., drugs of abuse (Figure G–I; Kruskal–Wallis by location, p = 0.006; hotspot around the touristic area; Dunn’s Test, Marie Pampoen vs Directeurs Baai, p < 0.05); and (c) the patchy Group III is represented by the persistent, mobile, and toxic compounds (Figure J–L; Kruskal–Wallis by location across seasons, p < 0.05), with higher abundances during the dry season in comparison to other groups in the same season.

Drivers of Spatial Distribution

Effect size estimation based on omega squared (ω2) indicated substantial variability explained by rainfall and hydrodynamics. The linear model (Figure ) showed that rainfall had the largest effect (ω2 = 0.33), accounting for approximately one-third of the total variance in total terrigenous xenobiotic distribution across all seasons, consistent with our definition of these xenobiotics as having a terrestrial source. The second strongest predictor in the model was the prevailing current type (11%), which directly influences the water residence time along the coast of the island. Other drivers tested contributed to a lesser extent to the distribution of total xenobiotics, yet all were statistically significant (p < 0.05; model outputs provided in Table S9), i.e., sampling depth (8%), distance to fluvial runoff (4%), land cover (4%), spatial zone (3%), hydrodynamic connectivity across spatial zones (2%), geology from the coast (2%), and population density (1%). Specific substance groups responded differentially to the environmental drivers (Figure ). Personal care products reflected the response of the total xenobiotic pool but showed an additional influence from sampling depth (10%). The distribution of plant protection products was minimally influenced by the dominant current type (2%) but more strongly shaped by the catchment area bordering the sampling locations (7%). In contrast to the total xenobiotic pool, drugs of abuse were mainly driven by distance to fluvial runoff (13%) and population density (9%). The distribution of industrial chemicals was equally influenced by sampling depth and catchment area (5% respectively), whereas smoke-related compounds were associated with coastal geology (4%), spatial zone (3%), and distance to fluvial runoff (2%).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated spatial and seasonal variations in the abundance of dissolved organic and inorganic substances associated with anthropogenic activity across the land–sea continuum of the Caribbean Island Curaçao. Analysis of 115 nearshore samples identified about 30,000 distinct molecular features, underscoring the immense complexity of marine DOM. Our main objective was to assess the composition and spatial distribution of the substances resulting from human activity on land that enter the nearshore waters and can affect the coastal ecosystem. This system-specific approach yielded a curated list of 1,394 terrigenous xenobiotics in the nearshore waters of Curaçao. A pronounced land-sea gradient was evident across most fDOM fractions, and particularly in the terrestrial-indicator fraction of fDOMc. This gradient strongly correlated with the “curated” xenobiotics signal, underscoring the validity and importance of system-specific data curation to interpret molecular signals. This work provides the first island-wide, multiseason assessment of the spatial distribution of xenobiotic compound groups along the coastline of Curaçao.

Temporal Distribution of Xenobiotics

Seasonal precipitation was the primary driver of xenobiotic abundance in coastal waters. While rainfall-driven transport of terrestrial material is well recognized, the strong and consistent correlation to xenobiotic dynamics reported here is rarely documented so clearly. Increased precipitation enhances surface runoff and groundwater discharge and can trigger sewage overflows where stormwater and wastewater systems are not separated. ,− Precipitation can mobilize accumulated pollutants, particularly after dry periods through first-flush effects, leading to pronounced contamination peaks. − This pattern was most evident during the 2022 wet season (average 144 mm month–1), when xenobiotic abundances were more than 3.5-fold higher than during the less rainy 2021 wet season and the 2022 dry season. Macronutrient concentrations were also elevated under those rain conditions, as well exemplified during enhanced runoff in the heavily developed coastline area of (main urban center) Willemstad. Bulk dissolved organic carbon (DOC) concentrations, however, remained relatively stable across seasons. This suggests that rainfall disproportionately increased anthropogenic compounds and associated nutrients during wetter periods compared to natural soil-derived DOC.

These rainfall-driven inputs are likely amplified by the island’s wastewater infrastructure. Suspended particulate matter, including particulate organic carbon, may contribute to the transport and fate of pollutants across the land–ocean continuum by acting as both a source and a sink through adsorption and desorption processes. − Only 19% of Curacao’s residents are connected to centralized sewage infrastructure, while most households rely on cesspits (77%) or septic tanks (3%), allowing largely untreated wastewater to infiltrate groundwater. , In Willemstad alone, cesspit infiltration is estimated at 3.0–4.7 million m3 annually comparable to natural recharge rates. − Combined with treatment plants that frequently exceed capacity, this chronic leakage likely enhances nutrient and xenobiotic delivery to coastal waters. − Together, episodic rainfall and persistent wastewater inputs position precipitation as a key regulator of contaminant pulses in Curaçao’s nearshore ecosystems. Additional parameters, such as photodegradationgiven varying compound-specific photolabilityand biodegradation, represent important transformation pathways for xenobiotics , but could not be assessed in the present study. Future work should consider including measurements of light availability and biodegradation to better constrain these processes.

Beyond the general rainfall–xenobiotic relationship, compound group-specific and even compound-specific dynamics were evident. While precipitation can dilute some contaminants, it can also mobilize others, and these interacting processes ultimately structure xenobiotic distribution and ecological relevance. Molecular properties further modulate these patterns. More lipophilic compounds likely accumulate in soils and urban surfaces and are exported in discrete pulses during heavy rainfall, whereas more hydrophilic compounds are transported more continuously through the watershed and show a more consistent abundance with limited seasonal variability. Personal care products, biocides, and plastic additives showed disproportionately higher concentrations during the wet season (more than 5-fold). Although often embedded in product matrices and therefore less immediately mobile, , their seasonal amplification may reflect episodic release and shifts in usage patterns. While many of these compounds are biodegradable once released, they can bioaccumulate and exert chronic ecological stress. In contrast, xenobiotics such as drugs of abuse, plant protection products, and persistent, mobile, and toxic (PMT) substances exhibited little change with increasing precipitation. These relatively more water-soluble compounds possess long environmental half-lives and act through specific biochemical targets, contributing to their pronounced toxic potential. −

Concomitant with increases in the overall xenobiotic abundances was a surge in both prokaryotic and viral numbers, with increasing VPR (due to higher viral load). It is likely that microbial enrichment was at least partly due to soil and sediments, typically harboring significantly higher microbial densities than ocean waters. , Furthermore, the runoff-induced influx of organic and inorganic nutrients may have induced a boost in heterotrophic bacterial growth and, successively, viral production. With climate change intensifying the frequency and unpredictability of extreme weather events, subsequent strong rainfall and floods may further exacerbate terrigenous contaminants and microbial dynamics.

Spatial Distribution

Xenobiotic abundances can vary widely across spatial zones due to diverse land cover and pollutant sources. While in some locations in this study there is overlap in total xenobiotic abundances and coastal population densities, not all xenobiotic trends can be explained by land use or source points only. Notably, multiple compounds consistently accumulated along the less populated western coast of Curaçao, highlighting the importance of ocean circulation.

Around Curaçao, the northwestward Caribbean Current interacts with recurrent cyclonic eddies to alter nearshore transport and retention. During the 2022 wet season, persistent eddies reduced normal flushing (Figure S9) and enhanced nearshore accumulation. Particle tracking simulations indicate preferential buildup of passive tracersincluding nutrients and xenobioticsalong the western margin, a region characterized by limited water renewal and sustained terrigenous inputs , (Figure S9C). Flow patterns strongly govern substance distribution in marine environments, especially coral reefs. − At the reef scale, complex local hydrodynamics can even further increase the residence time of xenobiotics, as flow velocities within reef structures are lower than in overlying surface waters.

When grouped by the compound class, some of these patterns emerged more clearly. For instance, plant protection products (Group III) were detected island-wide, indicating broad hydrodynamic dispersal beyond agricultural areas (Figures S8 and S9). Island-scale hydrodynamics also contributed to the high abundance of Group I compounds. This group includes human metabolites, explaining their prevalence near Westpunt and Willemstad (Figure S8C) with relatively high urban input and wastewater discharge in the latter. Elevated concentrations near Piscadera and nearby locations were likely driven by effluent from the Klein Hofje wastewater treatment plant. Like Group I near Willemstad, Group II compounds, such as plastic additives, surfactants, and other domestic-use compounds, also clustered around urbanized regions. Group II, specifically drugs of abuse, was elevated at tourist hotspots such as Mambo Beach, linking their occurrence to tourism intensity and runoff.

This shows that xenobiotic distribution is determined by the combined imprint of human activities and physical transport processes. Hydrodynamic retention can amplify contamination far from primary sources, emphasizing that the risk of exposure is governed as much by circulation patterns as by local emission intensity. Especially during the 2022 wet season, the trifecta of excessive rainfall, reduced current velocities, and shifts in current direction likely drove the pronounced increase in xenobiotic abundances along the coast.

Finally, pharmaceuticals (group III, Figure S8), typically associated with septic leakage and incomplete wastewater treatment, , showed higher abundances in western locations than in central Willemstad. This suggests that there may be a more constant low-level release into the ocean that accumulates under conditions of reduced flushing. It is noteworthy to realize that even under dry-season conditions, such accumulation may have an ecological and potentially health risk (also depending on the type of pharmaceutical).

Implications

Our study provides a cross-season, island-wide assessment of present-day water quality in Curaçao, establishing a valuable baseline for future monitoring and management. Across the research, one consistent pattern emerged: xenobiotics were rarely confined to discrete local sources. Instead, inputs dispersed widely around the island, with concentrations in some cases exceeding those measured near their points of origin. This highlights that chemical pollution affects not only discharge sites and adjacent reefs but the entire island marine ecosystem. Although conducted within a confined island setting, the key processes identifiedhydrodynamic retention, widespread contaminant dispersal, and circulation-driven spatial variabilityare characteristic of many coastal systems, making these findings broadly transferable to nearshore environments facing similar pressures from runoff, wastewater discharge, and xenobiotic pollution. Overall, we show that anthropogenic contaminants are pervasive, seasonally intensified (driven by rain events), and redistributed far beyond their points of origin, exposing coral reef ecosystems to complex and dynamic chemical mixtures. The interaction between land-based inputs and circulation patterns amplifies both chronic and episodic stress, particularly under increasing rainfall extremes, underscoring the need to explicitly incorporate land–sea connectivity and physical transport processes into strategies aimed at enhancing coral reef resilience.
