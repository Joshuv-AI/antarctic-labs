Construction of an ideological and political education evaluation indicator system for Basic Nursing Courses from the perspective of the CIPP model
Authors: Xin Ye, Hang Xu, Man Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo construct an evaluation indicator system for ideological and political education (IPE) in Basic Nursing Courses, thereby providing a basis for improving the teaching quality of IPE in these courses.MethodsBased on the CIPP (Context-Input-Process-Product) model framework, a preliminary draft of the evaluation indicator system for IPE in Basic Nursing Courses was developed through methods, such as literature review and group discussion. Using the Delphi expert consultation method, 21 experts were invited to provide supportive feedback and suggestions for revising the evaluation system over two rounds. The scores, coefficient values and consistency ratios for the first-, second- and third-level indicators were calculated, and the weights of the indicators at each level were determined using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP).ResultsThe expert response rate in the two rounds of the Delphi survey was 95% and 100%, respectively. Expert authority coefficients were 0.856 and 0.865, and judgment basis coefficients were 0.865 and 0.880, respectively. Kendall’s W values were 0.218 and 0.276 (p < 0.001). The developed evaluation indicator system comprised 4 first-level indicators, 12 s-level indicators, and 55 third-level indicators.ConclusionGuided by the CIPP theoretical framework, this study tentatively established an evaluation indicator system for IPE in Basic Nursing Courses. The consensus among experts reached an acceptable level, and the system could serve as a theoretical framework and reference for subsequent assessments of teaching quality and empirical validation of IPE in Basic Nursing Courses. However, as the system is still in the preliminary stage, further validation of its reliability, validity and feasibility, as well as its application in real teaching scenarios, is required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04894-4.

Introduction

Ideological and Political Education (IPE) in the curriculum, an educational concept and reform initiative introduced in recent years within the field of higher education in China, aims to organically integrate the objectives of IPE into the entire teaching process of various specialist courses. It is a key pathway to fulfilling the fundamental mission of higher education, namely to foster virtue and cultivate talent [1–3]. This concept shares intrinsic commonalities with values education, civic education, character education, and professional ethics education found in the educational systems of many countries in terms of educational objectives, although it is not entirely identical [4–6]. The American Association of Colleges of Nursing (AACN) emphasises the need to cultivate students’ compassion, social justice and professional integrity; international nursing regulatory bodies such as the UK’s Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC) and Australia’s AHPRA have established values such as respect, empathy and compassion as core competencies in nursing education [7, 8].

As a foundational course for the core competencies and professional ethics of the nursing discipline, the role of IPE in Basic Nursing is of paramount importance [1]. The key principle is not to introduce a separate ideological and political course alongside the professional curriculum, but rather to organically integrate the objectives, contents, and methods of ideological and political education into the entire teaching process of the professional curriculum. In the Chinese higher education context, the integration of IPE into Basic Nursing is an educational approach with a strong policy background and cultural specificity. It emphasises guiding students to correctly cultivate patriotism, professional values, ethical standards, humanistic care, and social responsibility whilst imparting nursing knowledge and skills, thereby fostering nursing professionals who are ‘well-rounded in both moral character and professional competence’. This is an inevitable choice for serving the ‘Healthy China’ strategy and meeting society’s urgent demand for high-quality nursing professionals [9, 10].

The effectiveness of integrating ideological and political education into the curriculum cannot be determined solely on the basis of subjective perceptions; there is an urgent need for a scientific and objective system of evaluation indicators to measure and guide it effectively. Traditional teaching evaluations tend to focus on knowledge and skills objectives, making it difficult to adequately capture and assess the extent to which ideological and political objectives, such as students’ professional values and ethical decision-making abilities, have been achieved [11, 12]. To support the refinement of nursing teaching organisation, improve talent development, and advance teaching reform, it is crucial to establish a scientific and comprehensive evaluation indicator system for IPE in Basic Nursing Courses. The CIPP evaluation model developed by Daniel Stufflebeam has been widely applied in medical education to assess courses and promote iterative quality improvement. Through a comprehensive analysis across the four dimensions of context, input, process and product, it enables a holistic evaluation of the conditions, implementation and outcomes of IPE in courses [13, 14]. Guided by the CIPP model, this study aims to utilize the Delphi method to synthesise expert consensus and construct an evaluation indicator system for IPE in Basic Nursing Courses, covering the four core dimensions of context, input, process and product.

Materials and methods

Establishment of the research team

A research team comprising eight members was established in accordance with the objectives of this study, including one professor, two associate professors, one senior nurse practitioner, and four postgraduate students. The team divided tasks appropriately and worked collaboratively to complete the following tasks: drafting the preliminary version of the evaluation system for the foundational nursing curriculum; preparing the expert questionnaire; recruiting experts and distributing the questionnaire; summarising and analysing the questionnaire data; and determining the weightings of the indicators.

Inclusion criteria: (1) at least 10 years’ professional experience in nursing education, nursing management or clinical nursing; (2) holding a professional title at the intermediate level or above; (3) familiarity with the content of Basic Nursing education; (4) willingness to participate in this study and complete two rounds of consultation.

Theoretical framework and concept mapping

To systematically evaluate the educational outcomes of IPE in Basic Nursing Courses, this study adopted the CIPP evaluation model proposed by Daniel Stufflebeam as its theoretical framework. This model emphasised a decision-making orientation and enabled a systematic evaluation of a course through four dimensions: context, input, process and product, thereby supporting continuous quality improvement. The multifaceted objectives of IPE in Basic Nursing Courses, the complexity of its implementation process, and the subtle and long-term nature of its educational outcomes were highly compatible with the systematic, developmental and feedback-oriented characteristics of the CIPP model.

The logical mapping between the four evaluation dimensions of the CIPP model and the elements of IPE in Basic Nursing Courses is as follows: (1) Context evaluation: this dimension primarily pays attention to the necessity and goal orientation of IPE within the course, corresponding to the course’s positioning and objectives. It assesses, in particular, whether the course aligns with national health strategies, the institution’s educational mission, and the requirements for nursing talent development. (2) Input evaluation: This primarily highlights the prerequisites and resource guarantees required for course implementation, corresponding to the teaching staff, course resources, syllabus design and practical support. It assesses whether the fundamental conditions to support the implementation of IPE within the course are adequately established. (3) Process evaluation: This primarily concentrates the dynamic performance during the implementation of IPE within the course, corresponding to student participation, teacher guidance and teaching evaluation. It examines aspects such as teaching organization, classroom interaction, value guidance and formative assessment feedback. (4) Product evaluation: This focuses on phased outcomes following course implementation, covering student development, the growth of the teaching team and course development. In particular, it assesses the formation of students’ professional values, the enhancement of teachers’ ideological and political teaching capabilities, and the effectiveness of course development.

Literature review

The databases searched were PubMed, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and Wan Fang Data. For Chinese databases, the search was conducted using the keywords ideological and political education in the curriculum, nursing education, CIPP model, evaluation indicators and Delphi technique; for foreign-language databases, the search was conducted using the subject terms Ideological and Political Education, Nursing Education, CIPP Model, Evaluation Indicators and Delphi Technique [15–18]. The search period spanned from June 2000 to June 2024. During the construction of the indicator system, reference was made to the core concepts of China’s ‘Guidelines for the Development of Ideological and Political Education in Higher Education Curricula’ and the ‘National Standards for Teaching Quality in Nursing’. In addition, the system also drew on talent quality requirements from internationally authoritative standards. These included the ‘Standards for Accreditation in Nursing Education’ of the Commission on Accreditation in Nursing Education (CAN) in the United States and the ‘Statement of Standards for Nursing’ of the UK’s Quality Assurance Agency for Higher Education (QAA). Integrating the four dimensions of context, input, process and conduct within the CIPP evaluation model, a preliminary framework for the evaluation indicator system of IPE in Basic Nursing Courses was established, comprising 4 first-level indicators, 13 s-level indicators and 62 third-level indicators.

Development of the Delphi questionnaire

The expert consultation questionnaire was a semi-structured questionnaire completed online. It comprised three sections: The first section provided background information, including the research context, research objectives, the Delphi method implementation process, instructions for completing the questionnaire, and points to note, to assist experts in fully understanding the consultation task; The second section collected basic expert information, including gender, age, educational background, professional title, employer, field of expertise and years of experience in the relevant field. It also included self-assessment items regarding the expert’s familiarity with the research content and the basis for their judgement, which were used to calculate the expert authority coefficient. The basis for expert judgement primarily encompassed theoretical analysis, practical experience, peer knowledge and intuitive judgement. The third section was the expert evaluation form, in which experts were asked to evaluate the importance of items at all levels within the draft indicator system using a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated very unimportant, 2 unimportant, 3 neutral, 4 important and 5 very important. Additionally, a ‘Comments for Revision’ section was provided after each item, allowing experts to submit written suggestions regarding the wording, classification, deletion, supplementation and refinement of items through open-ended responses.

To enhance the clarity and practical usability of the questionnaire, the research team reviewed its item design, wording and logical structure before the formal survey. Based on internal discussions and preliminary feedback, specific expressions were further revised and refined.

Implementation of the Delphi process

This study was designed and reported in accordance with the ‘Conducting and Reporting Delphi Studies’ (CREDES) guidelines, and two rounds of expert consultation were conducted between September 2024 and March 2025. The research team selected experts in the fields of nursing education, nursing management, clinical nursing and IPE in the curriculum using purposive sampling based on the inclusion criteria. Before the formal consultation, research team members communicated with the experts by email to introduce the research background, objectives, consultation content and completion requirements. After obtaining the informed consent, the expert consultation questionnaires were distributed. Both rounds of expert consultation were conducted anonymously, and the experts were unaware of each other’s identities. Questionnaires were distributed by email, and experts were requested to complete and return them independently within two weeks. After collecting the questionnaires within the specified timeframe, the research team collated the data using a double-checking method and entered them into a database.

Following the first round of consultation, the research team analysed and revised the indicator system by taking into account the mean importance scores, coefficient of variation (CV), and written comments provided by the experts, and made decisions to retain, amend or delete the indicators. If the mean importance score was ≥ 3.50 and the CV was ≤ 0.25, and no expert had suggested amendment or deletion, the indicator was retained directly. If the mean importance score was ≥ 3.50 and the CV was ≤ 0.25, and at least 2 experts proposed the same modification, the item was modified following discussion by the research team. If the mean importance score was < 3.50 or the CV was > 0.25, and at least 2 experts recommended deletion; or if the quantitative criteria for deletion were not met but multiple experts strongly recommended deletion with sufficient justification, a decision was made to delete the item. For items that met the quantitative criteria but where expert opinions were largely consistent, the wording was insufficiently precise, or the content overlapped or was duplicated, the research team also organised discussions to make amendments. Items which experts generally recommended to add were included to the second round of consultation following discussion by the research team.

During the second round of consultation, the statistical results for each item from the first round, summaries of expert opinions, and revision notes were provided to the experts, who were asked to continue their assessment and scoring based on the results of the previous round. There was a one-month interval between the two rounds of consultation. Following the conclusion of the second round, the indicator system was finalised and optimised by integrating the experts’ scores and written recommendations, thereby establishing the final evaluation indicator system.

This study concluded the consultation process after the second round, primarily based on the following criteria: (1) Kendall’s W values for indicators at all levels increased in the second round compared with the first; (2) experts did not propose any new structural amendments; and (3) the hierarchical structure of the indicator system and the content of the items had stabilised, thereby achieving the objective of reaching a preliminary consensus. Statistical methods

A database was created using Microsoft Excel 2024, and statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages; continuous data with a normal distribution were presented as means and standard deviations; continuous data that did not follow a normal distribution were presented as medians and quartiles. Expert engagement was analysed based on the questionnaire response rate. The authority coefficient (Cr) was derived as the arithmetic mean of two dimensions: the judgment basis coefficient (Ca) and the familiarity degree coefficient (Cs). A value of Cr > 0.7 indicated a good level of reliability. Following the completion of two rounds of Delphi expert consultation and the establishment of the indicator system, the AHP was employed to determine the weights of indicators at each level. Based on the mean importance scores assigned by experts to each indicator in the second round, the relative importance of indicators at the same level was converted into a pairwise comparison judgement matrix using Saaty’s 1–9 scale. The eigenvectors and weights were then calculated, and consistency tests were performed on the judgement matrices at each level. The maximum eigenvalue (λmax), consistency index (CI) and consistency ratio (CR) were calculated, with CR < 0.10 indicating acceptable consistency of the judgement matrix (Appendix 1). The degree of consensus among experts was evaluated using the mean of indicator scores and the frequency of full marks. The degree of coordination among expert opinions was assessed using the CV and Kendall’s coefficient of harmony (Kendall’s W). The screening criteria was a mean importance score of ≥ 3.50 and a CV of ≤ 0.25; a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study strictly adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant research ethics guidelines, and has received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi University of Chinese Medicine (No. 2024-031-KY-09). Before the first round of questionnaires, members of the research team provided each expert with a detailed explanation by email regarding the study background, objectives, methods, principle of voluntary participation and scope of data use of the study. Experts participated in this study on a voluntary basis. Furthermore, all collected data were identified using codes. Data storage complied with confidentiality requirements, and the data was destroyed in accordance with regulations upon completion of the study.

Results

Basic information of the experts

Between September 2024 and March 2025, a total of 20 experts from 17 universities and Grade III Level A hospitals across nine provinces and municipalities completed two rounds of written consultation. The experts were aged between 36 and 55, with an average age of (42.58 ± 3.69) years; of these, seven were male (35.0%) and 13 were female (65.0%). In terms of educational background, 4 held a bachelor’s degree (20.0%), 12 held a master’s degree (60.0%) and 4 held a doctoral degree (20.0%); in terms of professional titles, 3 held intermediate-level titles (15.0%), 12 held associate senior-level titles (60.0%) and 5 held full senior-level titles (25.0%). Their professional fields included 8 lecturers in basic nursing (40.0%), 6 lecturers in specialized nursing subjects (30.0%), 4 lecturers in IPE (20.0%) and 2 clinical nursing managers (10.0%). The experts were drawn from multiple fields, including nursing education, IPE within the curriculum, and clinical nursing management, demonstrating good professional representativeness.

Expert participation positivity

In accordance with the Delphi method’s recommendations regarding the number of expert consultants, and taking into account the specialised multidisciplinary nature of the research topic, this study employed purposive sampling to recruit experts. Expert recruitment commenced in September 2024. Based on the inclusion criteria, the research team initially shortlisted 35 potential experts through channels, such as author searches in the literature, conference contact lists, and publicly available information on university websites. Following invitations via email, 22 experts agreed to participate, representing a response rate of 62.9%. Of these, one expert withdrew before the formal survey due to scheduling conflicts, and another failed to return the questionnaire within the specified timeframe after the first round of survey commenced. Ultimately, 20 experts completed both rounds of the survey, with no other dropouts or lost to follow-up.

In the first round, 21 questionnaires were distributed, with 20 valid responses received, yielding a valid response rate of 95.0%. 11 experts provided written comments, totally 13 suggestions. After the research team summarised and analysed the results of the first round and revised the items, the second round of consultation was conducted. 20 questionnaires were distributed again, and then 20 valid responses were received, yielding a valid response rate of 100.0%, of which 7 experts provided further suggestions for modification, totally 7 points. In both rounds of consultation, experts offered constructive suggestions regarding the addition or deletion of indicators, revision to wording, and the categorisation of content, indicating that the experts attached great importance to the research content and demonstrated a high level of engagement.

Expert authority

The authority coefficients in the first round were Cr = 0.856, Ca = 0.865, Cs = 0.850. In the second round, they were Cr = 0.865, Ca = 0.880, Cs = 0.850. The Cr values in both rounds exceeded the accepted threshold of 0.7, indicating high authority of expert opinions, as shown in Table 1.

Consensus among experts

In this study, the Kendall’s W values for the first-level indicators in the two rounds of expert consultation were 0.335 and 0.428, respectively; for the second-level indicators, they were 0.236 and 0.376, respectively; and for the third-level indicators, they were 0.206 and 0.252, respectively. The overall Kendall’s W values were 0.218 and 0.276, respectively, with all differences being statistically significant (p < 0.001). The Kendall’s W values for indicators at all levels in the second round were higher than those in the first round, indicating that the consistency of expert opinions tended to increase following the two rounds of consultation, as shown in Table 2.

Results of revisions based on expert consultation

Following the conclusion of the first round of written consultation, an analysis of the questionnaires from 20 experts revealed that 11 experts has proposed deletions, additions and amendments to the indicators at various levels, as detailed below: (1) The first-level indicators: no amendments were proposed; the experts unanimously agreed that the four first-level indicators corresponding to this theoretical model were consistent with the scope of this study; (2) the second-level indicators: one indicator, “4.4 Impact and Effectiveness of Social Service”, was deleted. 6 experts considered that the impact of social service is a long-term objective, whilst the short-term impact on ideological and political education is difficult to assess; (3) the third-level indicators: two indicators were added. Experts argued that the concept of integrating artificial intelligence into teaching should be reinforced during the teaching process to enrich classroom content; consequently, “2.3.4 Bidirectional integration of artificial intelligence and ideological and political education” was added to the course structure. Experts also emphasized the importance of evaluating the teaching staff, noting that IPE teachers should provide guidance during the instructional design process for foundational nursing courses; therefore, “2.1.4 The professional teacher team includes full-time IPE teachers as consultants” was added. When selecting indicators, the mean score must be ≥ 3.5 and the CV ≤ 0.25. Consequently, a total of nine indicators were deleted, including “4.4.1 Employer satisfaction”, “4.4.2 Graduate career development”, “4.4.3 Contribution to social health services”, 4.4.4 Inter-institutional exchange and demonstration effect, “4.4.5 Value of course development experience for promotion”, “4.4.6 Promotion of nursing service quality”, “4.4.7 Professional field influence”, “4.4.8 Guiding role in public health awareness”, and “4.1.2 Satisfaction and perceived gain of nursing students”, while all other indicators meeting the selection criteria were retained and two indicators were amended. Experts deemed that the wording of certain third-level indicators required revision. Specifically, “1.1.3 Respond to nursing students’ vague professional identity and weakened humanistic spirit” was amended to “Address nursing students’ professional identity confusion and lack of humanistic spirit”, and “3.1.1 Observe discipline, standardize operations, practice diligently” was amended to “Adhere to discipline standards and refine practical skills”.

After the second round of expert consultation and collating the feedback forms, the experts reached consensus that the first-, second-, and third-level indicators could be retained without any further amendment. Consequently, the ‘Evaluation Indicator System for Ideological and Political Education in Basic Nursing Courses from a CIPP Perspective’ was finalized, comprising 4 first-level indicators, 12 s-level indicators and 55 third-level indicators, as shown in Table 3.

Evaluation indicator system for IPE in basic nursing courses

Discussion

Scientific rigor of the evaluation indicator system for IPE in basic nursing courses

Following two rounds of Delphi surveys, this study developed an evaluation indicator system for IPE in Basic Nursing Courses based on the CIPP model, to be used for the systematic assessment of teaching quality in this area. The research team invited 21 experts to participate in this study. These experts hailed from 17 higher education institutions, medical institutions and research institutes across nine provinces and municipalities nationwide, with academic qualifications ranging from bachelor’s to master’s and doctoral degrees. This ensured the greatest possible balance in terms of the experts’ educational backgrounds and minimised cognitive biases arising from regional disparities. It is generally accepted that a response rate of over 50% is sufficient to meet research requirements, whilst a response rate exceeding 60% indicates a high level of enthusiasm. The response rates of the two rounds of Delphi surveys were 95% and 100% respectively, demonstrating a high level of enthusiasm and commitment among the experts. As the authority of the experts was a key factor in evaluating the questionnaire, a Cr value greater than 0.7 was generally considered to indicate that the participants possess sufficient theoretical knowledge and practical experience in nursing education, meaning that the experts’ judgements were relatively reliable. The Cr values for the two rounds were 0.856 and 0.865 respectively, suggesting that the invited experts possessed a high degree of authority and reliability.

In the first round of expert consultation, the coefficients of variation (CVs) for the importance scores of the indicators ranged from 0.075 to 0.558. Some indicators had CVs greater than 0.25, suggesting that expert opinions differed to some extent for a small number of indicators. In the second round of consultation, the CV range narrowed to 0.085–0.218, with all indicators having a CV ≤ 0.25, indicating a notable reduction in the dispersion of expert opinions and good consistency. The values of Kendall’s W were 0.218 and 0.276, respectively, showing a significant difference (p < 0.001), which indicated that, after two rounds of coordination, the experts’ opinions converged and a final consensus was reached for all indicators. Based on the experts’ recommendations, items were deleted, added and modified, resulting in a total of 4 first-level indicators, 12 s-level indicators and 55 third-level indicators. It was evident that the entire process of constructing this indicator system complied with Delphi research protocols, ensuring the standardisation and content rationality of the evaluation indicator system for IPE in Basic Nursing Courses.

Potential applicability of the evaluation indicator system for IPE in basic nursing courses

Within the theoretical framework of the CIPP model, the expert consultation results based on the Delphi method in this study provided preliminary content validity support for the indicator system, ensuring that the structure of each indicator systematically and comprehensively covered the entire process of IPE in curriculum— from the construction background and resource input through the teaching process to the final outcomes, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for subsequent validation of construct validity and practical effectiveness. This framework operationalised the educational philosophy of IPE within nursing teaching practice, translating abstract, macro-level requirements into objective indicators. It can support nursing teachers in conducting self-assessments, reflecting on their instruction, and evaluating teaching effectiveness. In the results of this expert consultation, the importance scores for the 2 first-level indicators, ‘Course Input’ (4.60) and ‘Course product’ (4.55), were both high, and their coefficients of variation were both below 0.25. Among the consulted experts, 85% held the title of associate professor or above, and most were involved in teaching administration and educational reform. They placed a high weighting on the “input-output” relationship, reflecting the importance and attention given by university administrators and the teaching community to course input and course effectiveness in the IPE of Basic Nursing Courses. This aligned with the classical understanding of the “input-output” relationship within the field of course evaluation. High-quality IPE within courses did not simply involve adding new knowledge points, but also required the organic integration of ideological and political objectives into the entire process of setting teaching objectives, organising teaching content, and implementing teaching. This process depended on sustaining investment by the teaching team in course design, case development and teaching reflection. The evaluation of ‘course product’ reflected, the experts’ substantive concern with the educational value of IPE within courses, which was consistent with the findings of previous studies on the construction of IPE systems [19, 20].

Among the second and third-level indicators, “2.1 Faculty Team”, “2.1.2 Political literacy and professional ethics of teachers”, “3.2.1 Using diverse teaching methods to stimulate value-based reflection”, “4.1.3 Establishment of professional identity and sense of mission”, and “4.3 Faculty Team Growth” were rated 4.70, 4.60, 4.65, 4.60 and 4.80 respectively in terms of importance. This emphasised that whilst maintaining the systematic, holistic and organic nature of IPE in the curriculum, attention must also was paid to the development of teaching staff, pedagogical innovation and the internalization of values by students. To this end, full-time nursing faculty should build upon a high-calibre teaching staff and promote value-based critical thinking through innovative teaching methods. When designing courses, ideological and political elements should be naturally embeded into the professional theoretical framework. They should increase interactive teaching activities such as group discussions and real-case analyses, whilst avoiding heavy-handed lecturing and simplistic indoctrination. By fostering an open and inclusive classroom atmosphere, they should encourage students to engage in in-depth critical thinking on topics such as nursing ethics and professional values, thereby promoting the internalization and transfer of values, and achieving a deep internalisation of students’ professional identity and sense of mission.

Furthermore, the course product received high scores in the sections ‘1.2 Course Objectives’ and ‘3.3.1 Comprehensive evaluation of ideological performance and professional competence’ within the course evaluation. This suggested that clearly defined course objectives may provide a guidance for IPE, whilst the comprehensive formative assessment offered a continuous feedback mechanism for shaping students’ professional identity and stimulating their ability for independent learning. It was worth noting that the realisation of these two aspects relied on the exemplary influence of a high-calibre teaching team. Educational institutions should prioritise the cultivation of teachers’ professional ethics and conduct, thereby achieving the sustainable development of nursing careers [21, 22]. The development and implementation of this system held substantial potential for promoting the overall improvement of IPE within nursing curricula. The 55 third-level indicators derived from this research were primarily used to construct the content framework and structural dimensions of course-based IPE evaluation. To enhance its applicability in practice, future research could further refine the operational definitions, types of evidence, scoring criteria and evaluators for each item. Appendix 2 presented a preliminary approach to operational definitions using representative indicators as examples, for reference in future development. The complete scoring guidelines, evaluator training manual and inter-rater reliability tests will need to be finalized during the empirical research phase; see Appendix 2.

It should be noted that the evaluation indicator system developed in this study has established a preliminary framework for evaluating IPE in Basic Nursing Courses and determined the respective weightings; however, its application in real-world educational contexts required further validation. During implementation, evaluators should undergo necessary training and scoring calibration to ensure that they accurately understanded the implications of each indicator and the scoring criteria, thereby enhancing the consistency of evaluation results. Furthermore, the applicability of this framework may vary across different settings, such as classroom teaching, practical training and clinical teaching. Particularly in clinical teaching environments, practical factors such as teaching resources, the time cost of evaluation and organisational implementation methods must be comprehensively considered. Future research should further examine the operability, stability and reliability of this evaluation system through pilot assessments, inter-rater reliability tests and application feedback. Furthermore, the evaluation framework proposed in this study can serve as a supplement to existing course evaluation systems, working in tandem with the institution’s established teaching quality evaluation and course assessment mechanisms to enhance the systematic and targeted nature of IPE evaluation in Basic Nursing Courses.

Conclusion and limitations

In accordance with the Delphi methodology, and supported by expert consultation and preliminary weighting analysis, this study established a preliminary framework for evaluating the integration of IPE in Basic Nursing Courses. The result of two rounds of expert consultation indicated that the positive, authority and consistency coefficients all reached acceptable levels. The items at all levels effectively reflected the core essence and key evaluation dimensions of IPE in Basic Nursing Courses, and can provide a theoretical framework for empirical research on the quality assessment of IPE in Basic Nursing Courses.

However, some items in this study received high scores and exhibited low dispersion, which may be because that the integration of IPE into the curriculum is a key component of national education policy and experts demonstrate a high degree of consensus regarding the relevant development directions. Whilst this result supported the indicators’ alignment with policy, it also suggested the potential for social desirability bias and a scoring ceiling effect, which may in turn affect the items’ discriminatory power. Furthermore, the sample size of experts remained relatively limited and they were primarily from universities and Grade A tertiary hospitals, failing to cover teaching contexts such as secondary vocational schools and vocational high schools. As no large-scale empirical testing has yet been conducted in real teaching settings, this tool could not be equated with evaluation instruments that have undergone comprehensive validation. Future research should involve diverse stakeholders—including students, peer faculty, teaching administrators, clinical preceptors, and employers—and utilize methods such as pretesting, reliability analysis, exploratory/confirmatory factor analysis, inter-rater agreement analysis, and longitudinal tracking to rigorously validate the structural stability, practical feasibility, and external validity of the system.
