The ODE/IM Correspondence between C ​ ( 2 ) ( 2 ) C(2)^{(2)} -type Linear Problems and 2d 𝒩 = 1 \mathcal{N}=1 SCFT Preprint: TIT/HEP-710 arXiv: 2604.14899
Naozumi Tanabe

Abstract
We study the ODE/IM correspondence between the linear problem associated with the supersymmetric affine Toda field equation for the twisted affine Lie superalgebra C ​ ( 2 ) ( 2 ) = 𝔬 ​ 𝔰 ​ 𝔭 ​ ( 2 | 2 ) ( 2 ) C(2)^{(2)}=\mathfrak{osp}(2|2)^{(2)} and two-dimensional 𝒩 = 1 \mathcal{N}=1 superconformal field theories (SCFTs). On the ODE side, we introduce a boundary condition more suitable for the conformal limit and the subsequent WKB analysis and diagonalize the resulting Lax operator. This leads to a WKB expansion from which we extract the WKB periods and non-local conserved quantities up to tenth order. On the IM side, we compute the eigenvalues of the local integrals of motion on the cylinder in both the Neveu–Schwarz and Ramond sectors of 2d 𝒩 = 1 \mathcal{N}=1 SCFTs. We then compare the two sides and verify, up to sixth order, that the WKB periods coincide with the eigenvalues of the local integrals of motion for highest-weight states in the Neveu–Schwarz sector.

2
Supersymmetric affine Toda field equations

In this section, we review the
\mathcal{N}=1
supersymmetric affine Toda field equations associated with affine Lie superalgebras admitting a purely odd simple root system, and derive the corresponding linear problems.
Our presentation follows the framework of
28
, but we formulate the linear problem without taking the bosonic limit, in order to prepare for the subsequent WKB analysis of the full
C(2)^{(2)}=\mathfrak{osp}(2|2)^{(2)}
system.
The specialization to the
C(2)^{(2)}
case will be carried out in the following sections.

Let
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
be an affine Lie superalgebra with simple roots
\{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}\}
, all of which are odd.
In this paper, we restrict ourselves to the case of twisted affine Lie superalgebras with
m=2
.

Now we introduce the
\mathcal{N}=1
supersymmetric affine Toda field equation associated with an affine Lie superalgebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}

39
.
To realize
\mathcal{N}=1
supersymmetry, we introduce the
\mathcal{N}=1
superspace with complex coordinates
\left(z,\theta,\bar{z},\bar{\theta}\,\right)
, where
\theta,\bar{\theta}
are Grassmann variables.
The supercovariant derivatives are defined by

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}+\theta\,\partial_{z},\quad\bar{D}\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\theta}}+\bar{\theta}\,\partial_{\bar{z}},

(2.1)

\displaystyle D^{2}

\displaystyle=\partial_{z},\quad\bar{D}^{2}=\partial_{\bar{z}},\quad\left\{D,\bar{D}\right\}=0.

(2.2)

We also introduce a
r

\varPhi\left(z,\bar{z},\theta,\bar{\theta}\,\right)=\phi\left(z,\bar{z}\right)+i\theta\,\eta\left(z,\bar{z}\right)+i\bar{\theta}\,\bar{\eta}\left(z,\bar{z}\right)+\theta\bar{\theta}\,F\left(z,\bar{z}\right),

(2.3)

where
\phi\left(z,\bar{z}\right)
and
F\left(z,\bar{z}\right)
are bosonic fields, while
\eta\left(z,\bar{z}\right)
and
\bar{\eta}\left(z,\bar{z}\right)
are fermionic fields.

The action of the
\mathcal{N}=1
supersymmetric Toda field theory for a Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
with purely odd simple roots is defined by

S[\varPhi]=\int d^{2}z\,d^{2}\theta\left[\frac{1}{2}D\varPhi\cdot\bar{D}\varPhi-\frac{m^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{r}e^{\beta\,\alpha_{i}\cdot\varPhi}\right],

(2.4)

where
\beta
is a coupling constant,
m
is a mass parameter, and
\alpha_{i}
are the simple roots of
\mathfrak{g}

22
;
23
;
31
;
13
.
The symbol “
\;\cdot\;
” denotes the inner product in the root space.
The action of the affine Toda field theory for an affine Lie superalgebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
is obtained by adding the affine root contribution
-\frac{m^{2}}{\beta^{2}}e^{\beta\alpha_{0}\cdot\varPhi}
.
Accordingly, the affine Toda field equation takes the form

D\bar{D}\varPhi+\frac{m^{2}}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\,e^{\beta\,\alpha_{i}\cdot\varPhi}+\frac{m^{2}}{\beta}\alpha_{0}\,e^{\beta\,\alpha_{0}\cdot\varPhi}=0.

(2.5)

2.1
Modified supersymmetric affine Toda field equations

The Toda field equation (
2.5
) without the affine root term is invariant under the superconformal transformation

\displaystyle z^{\prime}(z,\theta)

\displaystyle=f(z)+\theta\,\chi(z)\,g(z),\quad\theta^{\prime}=\chi(z)+\theta\,g(z),

(2.6)

\displaystyle\bar{z}^{\prime}\left(\bar{z},\bar{\theta}\,\right)

\displaystyle=\bar{f}\left(\bar{z}\right)+\bar{\theta}\,\bar{\chi}\left(\bar{z}\right)\,\bar{g}\left(\bar{z}\right),\quad\bar{\theta}^{\prime}=\bar{\chi}\left(\bar{z}\right)+\bar{\theta}\,\bar{g}\left(\bar{z}\right),

\displaystyle\varPhi^{\prime}\left(z,\theta,\bar{z},\bar{\theta}\,\right)

\displaystyle=\varPhi\left(z^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},\bar{z}^{\prime},\bar{\theta}^{\prime}\right)+\frac{\mu}{\beta}\log\left(D\theta^{\prime}\,\bar{D}\bar{\theta}^{\prime}\right),

where
f(z),\,g(z),\,\bar{f}\left(\bar{z}\right)
and
\bar{g}\left(\bar{z}\right)
are bosonic functions, while
\chi(z)
and
\bar{\chi}\left(\bar{z}\right)
are fermionic functions.
They satisfy

g(z)^{2}=\partial_{z}f(z)+\chi(z)\,\partial_{z}\chi(z),\quad\bar{g}\left(\bar{z}\right)^{2}=\partial_{\bar{z}}\bar{f}\left(\bar{z}\right)+\bar{\chi}\left(\bar{z}\right)\,\partial_{\bar{z}}\bar{\chi}\left(\bar{z}\right).

(2.7)

Here
\mu=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{i}
, where
\mu_{i}
are the fundamental weights satisfying
\mu_{i}\cdot\alpha_{j}=\delta_{ij}
.
Under this transformation, the affine Toda field equation (
2.5
) is mapped to

D\bar{D}\varPhi+\frac{m^{2}}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\exp(\beta\alpha_{i}\cdot\varPhi)+\frac{m^{2}}{\beta}\,P(z,\theta)\,\bar{P}\left(\bar{z},\bar{\theta}\,\right)\,\alpha_{0}\exp(\beta\alpha_{0}\cdot\varPhi)=0,

(2.8)

where

\displaystyle P(z,\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(D\theta^{\prime}\right)^{\!h}=g(z)^{h}+\theta\,h\,g(z)^{h-1}\,\partial_{z}\chi(z),

(2.9)

\displaystyle\bar{P}\left(\bar{z},\bar{\theta}\,\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\bar{D}\bar{\theta}^{\prime}\right)^{\!h}=\bar{g}\left(\bar{z}\right)^{h}+\bar{\theta}\,h\,\bar{g}\left(\bar{z}\right)^{h-1}\,\partial_{\bar{z}}\bar{\chi}\left(\bar{z}\right).

The exponent
h
is determined by
\mu\cdot\alpha_{0}=-\frac{h-1}{2}
.
If the extended root is expanded as
\alpha_{0}=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}\alpha_{i}
, then

h=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}+1,

(2.10)

namely the Coxeter number of
\mathfrak{g}
.
The equation (
2.8
) is no longer manifestly supersymmetric in the coordinates
(z,\bar{z})
, because the affine root term now carries the explicit factors
P(z,\theta)
and
\bar{P}\left(\bar{z},\bar{\theta}\,\right)
.
Nevertheless, it is related to the supersymmetric equation (
2.5
) through the superconformal transformation (
2.6
).

2.2
Super-Lax operator

We now rewrite the modified affine Toda field equation (
2.8
) as a flatness condition of a super-Lax pair.
Let
E_{\pm\alpha_{i}}\;(i=0,1,\dots,r)
denote the root generators of
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}
, and let
H
denote the Cartan generators.
They satisfy the following defining (anti-)commutation relations:

\{E_{\alpha_{i}},E_{-\alpha_{i}}\}=\varepsilon_{i}\,\alpha_{i}\cdot H,\quad[\alpha_{i}\cdot H,E_{\pm\alpha_{j}}]=\pm(\alpha_{i}\cdot\alpha_{j})E_{\pm\alpha_{j}},

(2.11)

where
\varepsilon_{i}
is a normalization constant chosen as
\pm 1
.

We introduce the super-Lax operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq D-A_{\theta}=D-\beta\,D\varPhi\cdot H-me^{\lambda}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+P(z,\theta)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}\!\right],

(2.12)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bar{D}-A_{\bar{\theta}}=\bar{D}+me^{-\lambda}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r}e^{\beta\,\alpha_{i}\cdot\varPhi}\varepsilon_{i}E_{-\alpha_{i}}+\bar{P}\left(\bar{z},\bar{\theta}\,\right)\,e^{\beta\alpha_{0}\cdot\varPhi}\varepsilon_{0}E_{-\alpha_{0}}\!\right],

where
\lambda
is the spectral parameter.
Then the modified affine Toda field equation (
2.8
) is equivalent to the flatness condition

F_{\theta\bar{\theta}}=DA_{\bar{\theta}}+\bar{D}A_{\theta}-\{A_{\theta},A_{\bar{\theta}}\}=0.

(2.13)

Equivalently, it appears as the compatibility condition for the supersymmetric linear problems

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{F}}\varPsi\left(z,\bar{z},\theta,\bar{\theta}\,\right)=0,\quad\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{F}}\varPsi\left(z,\bar{z},\theta,\bar{\theta}\,\right)=0.

(2.14)

We now consider the holomorphic part of the supersymmetric linear problem (
2.14
) in components.
Writing
\varPsi(z,\theta)=\varPsi_{0}(z)+i\theta\,\varPsi_{1}(z),\;D\varPhi(z,\theta)=i\eta(z)+\theta\,\partial_{z}\phi(z),\;P(z,\theta)=p_{0}(z)+\theta\,p_{1}(z)
and suppressing the anti-holomorphic part, the first equation in (
2.14
) becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\varPsi_{0}-\beta\,\partial_{z}\phi\cdot H\,\varPsi_{0}-me^{\lambda}\,p_{1}(z)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}\varPsi_{0}-i\left\{\!me^{\lambda}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+p_{0}(z)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}\!\right]-i\beta\,\eta\cdot H\!\right\}\varPsi_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.15)

\displaystyle i\varPsi_{1}-i\beta\,\eta\cdot H\,\varPsi_{0}-me^{\lambda}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+p_{0}(z)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}\!\right]\varPsi_{0}

\displaystyle=0.

Eliminating the fermionic component
\varPsi_{1}
, we arrive at the bosonic linear problem
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}\varPsi_{0}=0
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}=\partial_{z}

\displaystyle-\beta\,\partial_{z}\phi\cdot H-me^{\lambda}\,p_{1}(z)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}

\displaystyle-\left\{\!me^{\lambda}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+p_{0}(z)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}\!\right]-i\beta\,\eta\cdot H\!\right\}\!\left\{\!me^{\lambda}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+p_{0}(z)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}\!\right]+i\beta\,\eta\cdot H\!\right\}.

(2.16)

After taking the conformal limit, this becomes a linear problem in a single holomorphic variable, to which the ODE/IM correspondence will be applied.

2.3
Conformal limit of the linear problem

We now consider the conformal and light-cone limit of the supersymmetric linear problem (
2.14
), in which the anti-holomorphic dependence becomes negligible.
For this purpose, we choose the bosonic part of the potential to be of monomial type,

p_{0}(z)=z^{\frac{hM}{2}}-s^{\frac{hM}{2}},\quad\bar{p}_{0}\left(\bar{z}\right)=\bar{z}^{\frac{hM}{2}}-\bar{s}^{\frac{hM}{2}}.

(2.17)

Here
M
is a positive real parameter satisfying
M>\frac{1}{h-1}
, while
s
is an arbitrary parameter.

To specify the conformal limit, we fix the asymptotic behavior of the superfield near infinity and near the origin.
As
|z|\to\infty
, the potential term dominates, and the leading behavior of
\varPhi\left(z,\bar{z},\theta,\bar{\theta}\,\right)
is

\varPhi\left(z,\bar{z},\theta,\bar{\theta}\,\right)=\frac{M\mu}{\beta}\log\left(z\bar{z}\right)+\cdots.

(2.18)

Near the origin, we impose the boundary condition

\varPhi\left(z,\bar{z},\theta,\bar{\theta}\,\right)=\frac{l}{\beta}\log\left(z\bar{z}\right)+\theta\,\frac{\xi}{\beta z}+\bar{\theta}\,\frac{\bar{\xi}}{\beta\bar{z}}+\cdots.

(2.19)

Here the coefficient
l
characterizes the leading bosonic logarithmic behavior near the origin and plays a role analogous to an angular-momentum parameter in the associated ODE.
Similarly, the Grassmann-odd parameters
\xi
and
\bar{\xi}
arise from the leading singular terms of the fermionic components
\eta
and
\bar{\eta}
.

We now pass to the conformal and light-cone limit of the linear problem.
For this purpose, we first perform the gauge transformation

A_{\theta}\to A^{U}_{\theta}=UA_{\theta}U^{-1}-U\,DU^{-1},\quad\varPsi\to U\varPsi,

(2.20)

with
U=\exp\left[\frac{2}{h}\log P(z,\theta)\,\mu\cdot H\right]
.
This transforms the
P(z,\theta)
-dependence of the super-Lax operator into a form better suited to the scaling limit.
The resulting connection is

A^{U}_{\theta}=\beta\,D\varPhi\cdot H-\frac{2}{h}\,D\log P(z,\theta)\,\mu\cdot H+me^{\lambda}\,P(z,\theta)^{\!\frac{1}{h}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+E_{\alpha_{0}}\!\!\right).

(2.21)

The rescalings introduced below are chosen so that
x
,
E
, and
\tilde{\theta}
remain finite in the limit
\lambda\to\infty
,
\bar{z}\to 0
, and
z\to 0
.
After taking this limit, we apply the inverse gauge transformation and recover a simpler Toda-type Lax operator, which will serve as the starting point for the subsequent diagonalization and WKB analysis.
We rescale
z,\bar{z},\,s,\bar{s},\,\theta
and
\bar{\theta}
as

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\left(me^{\lambda}\right)^{\!\frac{2}{M+1}}z,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=s^{\frac{hM}{2}}\left(me^{\lambda}\right)^{\!\frac{hM}{M+1}},

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}

\displaystyle=\theta\left(me^{\lambda}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{M+1}},

(2.22)

\displaystyle\bar{x}

\displaystyle=\left(me^{-\lambda}\right)^{\!\frac{2}{M+1}}\bar{z},

\displaystyle\bar{E}

\displaystyle=\bar{s}^{\frac{hM}{2}}\left(me^{-\lambda}\right)^{\!\frac{hM}{M+1}},

\displaystyle\tilde{\bar{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\bar{\theta}\left(me^{-\lambda}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{M+1}},

and take the light-cone limit
\bar{z}\to 0
with
\lambda\to\infty
.
Then we take the conformal limit
z\to 0
with
x,\,E
kept finite.
In this limit, the differential equation for the super-Lax operator
U\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{F}}U^{-1}=D-A^{U}_{\theta}
reduces to the equation
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{F}}\varPsi=0
, where

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{F}}=\mathcal{D}-\frac{\varLambda(\tilde{\theta})\cdot H}{x}+\frac{2}{h}\,\mathcal{D}\log P(x,\tilde{\theta},E)\,\mu\cdot H-P(x,\tilde{\theta},E)^{\!\frac{1}{h}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+E_{\alpha_{0}}\!\!\right)

(2.23)

with
\mathcal{D}=\partial_{\tilde{\theta}}+\tilde{\theta}\,\partial_{x},\;\varLambda(\tilde{\theta})=\xi+\tilde{\theta}\,l,\;P(x,\tilde{\theta},E)=p_{0}(x,E)+\tilde{\theta}\,p_{1}(x,E)
.
After the rescaling (
2.22
), the light-cone limit, and the conformal limit, the bosonic part of the superspace potential takes the form
p_{0}(x,E)=x^{\frac{hM}{2}}-E
.
We further rescale the variables as

x\to\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{M+1}}x,\qquad E\to\epsilon^{-\frac{hM}{2(M+1)}}E,\qquad\tilde{\theta}\to\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2(M+1)}}\tilde{\theta},

(2.24)

and simultaneously

\xi\to\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2(M+1)}}\xi,\qquad p_{1}(x)\to\epsilon^{-\frac{hM-1}{2(M+1)}}p_{1}(x).

(2.25)

Under this rescaling, the bosonic part of the potential is brought to the form

p_{0}(x,E)=x^{\frac{hM}{2}}-E
up to an overall factor, and we may further fix
E=1
.
Accordingly, the super-Lax operator takes the form

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{F}}=\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2(M+1)}}\,\mathcal{D}-\frac{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2(M+1)}}\,\varLambda(\tilde{\theta})\cdot H}{x}+\frac{2}{h}\,\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2(M+1)}}\,\mathcal{D}\log P(x,\tilde{\theta})\,\mu\cdot H-\epsilon^{-\frac{M}{2(M+1)}}P(x,\tilde{\theta})^{\frac{1}{h}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+E_{\alpha_{0}}\right).

(2.26)

Multiplying the linear problem by an overall power of
\epsilon
, one can rewrite it in the equivalent form

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{F}}=\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathcal{D}-\frac{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\varLambda(\tilde{\theta})\cdot H}{x}+\frac{2}{h}\,\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathcal{D}\log P(x,\tilde{\theta})\,\mu\cdot H-P(x,\tilde{\theta})^{\!\frac{1}{h}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+E_{\alpha_{0}}\!\!\right),

(2.27)

where
\epsilon
plays the role of the Planck constant in the WKB expansion.

Applying inverse gauge transformation with the gauge parameter
\exp\left[-\frac{2}{h}\log P(x,\tilde{\theta})\,\mu\cdot H\right]
, one can obtain the Lax operator

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{F}}=\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathcal{D}-\frac{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\varLambda(\tilde{\theta})\cdot H}{x}-\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+P(x,\tilde{\theta})\,E_{\alpha_{0}}\!\right],

(2.28)

which leads to the bosonic Lax operator of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}=\epsilon\,\partial_{x}

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon\,l\cdot H}{x}-\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,p_{1}(x)\,E_{{\alpha_{0}}}

\displaystyle-\left\{\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+p_{0}(x)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}\!\right]-\frac{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\xi\cdot H}{x}\!\right\}\!\left\{\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r}E_{\alpha_{i}}+p_{0}(x)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}\!\right]+\frac{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\xi\cdot H}{x}\!\right\}.

(2.29)

In the case where the Cartan subalgebra is one-dimensional, the bosonic Lax operator reduces to

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}=\epsilon\,\partial_{x}-\frac{\epsilon\,l\cdot H}{x}-\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,p_{1}(x)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}-[E_{\alpha_{1}}+p_{0}(x)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}]^{2}+\left[\frac{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\xi\cdot H}{x},E_{\alpha_{1}}\right]+p_{0}(x)\left[\frac{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\xi\cdot H}{x},E_{\alpha_{0}}\right].

(2.30)

In this case,
\xi\cdot H
is controlled by a single Grassmann-odd parameter, and its nilpotency simplifies the quadratic expression in Eq. (
2.29
).
Accordingly, we consider the bosonic linear problem

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}\varPsi_{0}(x)=[\epsilon\,\partial_{x}+A(x)]\varPsi_{0}(x)=0,

(2.31)

with

A(x)\coloneqq-\frac{\epsilon\,l\cdot H}{x}-\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,p_{1}(x)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}-[E_{\alpha_{1}}+p_{0}(x)\,E_{\alpha_{0}}]^{2}+\left[\frac{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\xi\cdot H}{x},E_{\alpha_{1}}\right]+p_{0}(x)\left[\frac{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\xi\cdot H}{x},E_{\alpha_{0}}\right].

(2.32)

The reduced form (
2.30
) will be the starting point for the
C(2)^{(2)}
analysis in the next section.

\bm{C(2)^{(2)}}

3.1
The diagonalization approach to the linear problem

We now diagonalize the bosonic linear problem (
2.31
) by a sequence of gauge transformations.
The resulting diagonal connection determines the WKB solutions and the associated WKB periods.

To this end, we introduce a gauge transformation of the form

\mathbf{Gau}_{T}[A(x)]=T^{-1}(x)\,A(x)\,T(x)+\epsilon\,T^{-1}(x)\,\partial_{x}T(x),

(3.1)

where

T(x)\coloneqq T_{d}(x)\,T_{d-1}(x)\cdots T_{1}(x),

(3.2)

and each elementary matrix
T_{i}(x)
differs from the identity only in the
i
-th row:

T_{i}(x)_{ab}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&(a=b),\\
g_{i,\,b}(x)&(a=i,\;b\neq i,\;1\leq b\leq d),\\
0&(\text{otherwise}).\end{cases}

(3.3)

At the
i
-th step, the functions
g_{i,\,b}(x)
are fixed so that the transformed connection
A^{\prime}(x)=\mathbf{Gau}_{T_{i}}[A(x)]
satisfies

A^{\prime}_{ij}=0,\quad 1\leq j\leq d,\quad j\neq i.

(3.4)

In other words, we eliminate the off-diagonal entries in the
i
-th row order by order in
\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
These conditions give a coupled set of Riccati-type constraints for the gauge parameters.
Iterating this procedure row by row, we obtain a diagonal connection

A_{\mathrm{diag}}(x)=\mathbf{Gau}_{T_{1}}\circ\mathbf{Gau}_{T_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{Gau}_{T_{d}}[A(x)],

(3.5)

whose diagonal entries are written as

A_{\mathrm{diag}}(x)=\operatorname{diag}\left\{f_{1}(x),f_{2}(x),\dots,f_{d}(x)\right\}.

(3.6)

The transformed linear problem then reduces to a set of decoupled first-order equations,

[\epsilon\,\partial_{x}+f_{i}(x)]\varPsi_{i}(x)=0\quad(i=1,\dots,d),

(3.7)

whose solutions take the WKB form

\varPsi_{i}(x)\sim\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int^{x}f_{i}\left(x^{\prime}\right)\,dx^{\prime}\right].

(3.8)

The WKB periods associated with the

-\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f_{i}(z)\,dz=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}Q_{k}^{(i)}.

(3.9)

\mathcal{C}

3

We refer to
Q_{k}^{(i)}
as the
k

We now specialize the general construction to the case of

There are two simple fermionic roots

The resulting simple root is
\alpha_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})=\delta_{1}

Since the connection (

3.2.1
Branch structure and the vanishing of
\bm{p_{1}(x)}

Before proceeding to the higher-order diagonalization, let us first examine the leading-order structure of the third-row constraints.
This analysis reveals that the classical solution admits two branches, and that the behavior of the fermionic quantity
p_{1}(x)
depends on the branch.
These branch-dependent features will play an important role in the subsequent diagonalization.

To diagonalize the third row, we introduce the elementary gauge transformation
T_{3}(x)
, for which the transformed connection takes the form

\mathbf{Gau}_{T_{3}}[A(x)]=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\epsilon\,l}{x}+p_{0}-g_{3,\,1}&-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}-g_{3,\,2}&-1\\
-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}(g_{3,\,1}+p_{0})+\sqrt{\epsilon}\,p_{1}&-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}\,g_{3,\,2}+2p_{0}&-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}\\
r_{31}(x)&r_{32}(x)&f_{3}(x)\end{pmatrix},

(3.18)

where

\displaystyle r_{31}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\epsilon}\,p_{1}g_{3,\,2}-p_{0}^{2}-\frac{2\epsilon\,l}{x}\,g_{3,\,1}+g_{3,\,1}^{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}(p_{0}+g_{3,\,1})g_{3,\,2}+\epsilon\,g^{\prime}_{3,\,1},

(3.19)

\displaystyle r_{32}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}(g_{3,\,1}-p_{0})+\sqrt{\epsilon}\,p_{1}-\frac{\epsilon\,l}{x}\,g_{3,\,2}+(g_{3,\,1}-p_{0})g_{3,\,2}+\epsilon\,g^{\prime}_{3,\,2},

(3.20)

\displaystyle f_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\epsilon\,l}{x}-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}\,g_{3,\,2}+p_{0}+g_{3,\,1}.

(3.21)

We expand the gauge parameters in a WKB form compatible with the
\epsilon
-scaling and the Grassmann parity assignment:

g_{3,\,1}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}\,S_{3,\,1}^{(k)}(x),\quad g_{3,\,2}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\,S_{3,\,2}^{(k)}(x),

(3.22)

Here the half-integer shift in the expansion of
g_{3,\,2}(x)
reflects the fact that
g_{3,\,2}(x)
is Grassmann-odd and appears in the connection together with fermionic quantities of order
\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Substituting these expansions into the Riccati-type constraints
r_{31}(x)=0
and
r_{32}(x)=0
, we solve them order by order in
\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
At the leading order
\epsilon^{0}
, the
(3,1)
-constraint reduces to

S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)^{2}-p_{0}(x)^{2}=0,

(3.23)

so that there are two classical branches

S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)=\pm p_{0}(x).

(3.24)

Once a branch is chosen, the
(3,2)
-constraint at the leading order
\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
determines the leading coefficient
S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}
.
Indeed, keeping only the
\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
-terms in
r_{32}(x)=0
, we obtain

\left[S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)-p_{0}(x)\right]S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}(x)+\frac{\xi}{x}\left[S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)-p_{0}(x)\right]+p_{1}(x)=0.

(3.25)

In particular, in the minus branch
S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)=-p_{0}(x)
, this gives

S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}(x)=-\frac{\xi}{x}+\frac{p_{1}(x)}{2p_{0}(x)}.

(3.26)

In the plus branch
S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)=+p_{0}(x)
, one instead finds

p_{1}(x)=0.

(3.27)

At this order, however,
S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}(x)
remains undetermined.
Finally, the classical part of the diagonal component
f_{3}(x)
is read off from Eq. (
3.21
), so that at the leading order we have

f_{3}^{(0)}(x)=p_{0}(x)+S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{(minus branch)},\\
2p_{0}(x)&\text{(plus branch)}.\end{cases}

(3.28)

At this stage, the status of
p_{1}(x)
remains branch-dependent.
To determine whether the fermionic quantity
p_{1}(x)
survives in the minus branch, we must also examine the leading-order consistency of the second-row constraint.
We therefore next study the second-row diagonalization in the minus branch and compare the resulting condition with the plus-branch result.

After the second gauge transformation
T_{2}
, the connection becomes

\mathbf{Gau}_{T_{2}T_{3}}[A(x)]=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\epsilon\,l}{x}-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}\,g_{2,\,1}-g_{3,\,2}g_{2,\,1}+p_{0}-g_{3,\,1}&-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}-g_{3,\,2}&-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}\,g_{2,\,3}-g_{3,\,2}g_{2,\,3}-1\\
r_{21}(x)&f_{2}(x)&r_{23}(x)\\
0&0&f_{3}(x)\end{pmatrix},

(3.29)

where

\displaystyle r_{21}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}(g_{3,\,2}g_{2,\,1}+g_{3,\,1}+p_{0})+\sqrt{\epsilon}\,p_{1}-\frac{\epsilon\,l}{x}\,g_{2,\,1}+p_{0}g_{2,\,1}+g_{2,\,1}g_{3,\,1}+\epsilon\,g^{\prime}_{2,\,1},

(3.30)

\displaystyle f_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=2p_{0}-g_{3,\,2}g_{2,\,1}-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}(g_{2,\,1}-g_{3,\,2}),

(3.31)

\displaystyle r_{23}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi}{x}(g_{2,\,1}g_{2,\,3}+g_{3,\,2}g_{2,\,3}+1)-g_{3,\,2}g_{2,\,1}g_{2,\,3}+g_{2,\,1}+f_{3}\,g_{2,\,3}-2p_{0}g_{2,\,3}-\epsilon\,g^{\prime}_{2,\,3}.

(3.32)

Since the diagonal entry
f_{2}(x)
does not contain
g_{2,\,3}(x)
, the determination of
f_{2}(x)
at a given order only requires solving the constraint

r_{21}(x)=0
for
g_{2,\,1}(x)
.
The equation
r_{23}(x)=0
, which fixes
g_{2,\,3}(x)
, can be postponed because it is irrelevant for the computation of
f_{2}(x)
at this stage.

We now analyze
r_{21}(x)=0
in the minus branch of the third-row diagonalization.
Recall that the third-row gauge parameters were expanded as in Eq. (
3.22
), and that the minus branch is characterized by Eqs. (
3.24
) and (
3.26
).
For
g_{2,\,1}(x)
, we likewise adopt a fermionic WKB expansion compatible with the
\epsilon
-scaling,

g_{2,\,1}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\,S_{2,\,1}^{(k)}(x).

(3.33)

The half-integer shift reflects the fact that
g_{2,\,1}(x)
is Grassmann-odd and therefore starts at the same semiclassical order as the fermionic quantities
p_{1}(x)
and
\xi
.
At the leading order
\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
, the
(2,1)
-constraint reduces to

S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}(x)\left[S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)+p_{0}(x)\right]-\frac{\xi}{x}\left[S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)+p_{0}(x)\right]+p_{1}(x)=0,

(3.34)

In the minus branch we have
S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)=-p_{0}(x)
, and therefore

p_{1}(x)=0.

(3.35)

Hence, for
C(2)^{(2)}
we find
p_{1}(x)=0
in both the minus and the plus branches.

Moreover, the computation of the third row in the plus branch agrees with that of the first row in the minus branch.
Since the connection is supertraceless, the first diagonal component is determined once the second and third diagonal components are known.
Therefore, it is sufficient in what follows to work in the minus branch and analyze only the third and second rows.

3.2.2
Diagonalization of the third row

We now proceed to the higher-order diagonalization of the third row.
The corresponding WKB coefficients can be determined recursively from the super-Riccati system.
We expand the gauge parameters and the diagonal entry as

g_{3,\,1}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}\,S_{3,\,1}^{(k)}(x),\quad g_{3,\,2}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\,S_{3,\,2}^{(k)}(x),\quad f_{3}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}\,f_{3}^{(k)}(x).

(3.36)

Accordingly, we also expand

r_{31}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}\,r_{31}^{(k)}(x),\quad r_{32}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\,r_{32}^{(k)}(x).

(3.37)

Substituting these expansions into Eqs. (
3.19
)–(
3.21
) and collecting terms order by order in
\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
, we obtain a hierarchy of algebraic-differential equations.
At the leading orders, we obtain

\displaystyle r_{31}^{(0)}(x)

\displaystyle=-p_{0}(x)^{2}+{S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}}(x)^{2}=0,

(3.38)

\displaystyle r_{32}^{(0)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\xi}{x}\,p_{0}(x)+\frac{\xi}{x}\,S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)-p_{0}S_{32}^{(0)}+S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}(x)=0,

(3.39)

\displaystyle f_{3}^{(0)}(x)

\displaystyle=p_{0}(x)+S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x),

(3.40)

while the next orders give

\displaystyle r_{31}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\xi}{x}\,p_{0}S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}-\frac{2l}{x}\,S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}-\frac{\xi}{x}\,S_{31}^{(0)}S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}+2S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}S_{3,\,1}^{(1)}+S_{3,\,1}^{(0)\prime}=0,

(3.41)

\displaystyle r_{32}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\xi}{x}\,S_{3,\,1}^{(1)}-\frac{l}{x}\,S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}+S_{3,\,1}^{(1)}S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}-p_{0}S_{3,\,2}^{(1)}+S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}S_{3,\,2}^{(1)}+S_{3,\,2}^{(0)\prime}=0,

(3.42)

\displaystyle f_{3}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{l}{x}-\frac{\xi}{x}\,S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}+S_{3,\,1}^{(1)}.

(3.43)

To organize the higher-order equations, it is convenient to introduce the vectors

\bm{R}^{(k)}(x)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}r_{31}^{(k)}(x)\\[2.84526pt]
r_{32}^{(k)}(x)\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{S}^{(k)}(x)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}S_{3,\,1}^{(k)}(x)\\[2.84526pt]
S_{3,\,2}^{(k)}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(3.44)

At each order, the unknown coefficients enter linearly, while all nonlinear contributions are determined by lower-order data already fixed in the previous steps, as in the recursive diagonalization method proposed in
25
.
Therefore, the super-Riccati system at order
\epsilon^{k}
can be written in the form

\bm{R}^{(k)}(x)=-B(x)\,\bm{S}^{(k)}(x)+\bm{J}^{(k)}(x),

(3.45)

where
\bm{J}^{(k)}(x)
depends only on the lower-order coefficients
\bm{S}^{(j)}(x)
with
j<k
, which have already been determined recursively.
The matrix
B(x)
is the Jacobian of the leading-order constraints,

B_{ab}(x)\coloneqq-\frac{\partial r_{3a}^{(0)}(x)}{\partial S_{3,\,b}^{(0)}(x)}\quad(a,b=1,2),

(3.46)

where the derivative with respect to the Grassmann-odd variable
S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}(x)
is taken to be the left derivative.
In the present case, it is explicitly given by

B(x)=\begin{pmatrix}-2S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)&0\\
-\frac{\xi}{x}+S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}(x)&-2S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(3.47)

Imposing the constraints
\bm{R}^{(k)}(x)=0
, we obtain the recursive solution

\bm{S}^{(k)}(x)=B^{-1}(x)\,\bm{J}^{(k)}(x).

(3.48)

Thus all higher-order coefficients are determined recursively from the lower-order data.

With
B(x)
at hand, the higher-order coefficients are determined recursively, and the diagonal components
f_{3}^{(k)}(x)
can therefore be obtained order by order.
The explicit expressions for
f_{3}^{(k)}(x)
up to
k=6
are

\displaystyle f_{3}^{(0)}(x)

\displaystyle=0,

(3.49)

\displaystyle f_{3}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p^{\prime}_{0}(x)}{2p_{0}(x)}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dx}\log p_{0}(x)=d(*),

(3.50)

\displaystyle f_{3}^{(2)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{l(l+1)}{2x^{2}p_{0}(x)}-\frac{p^{\prime\prime}_{0}(x)}{16p_{0}(x)^{2}}+d(*),

(3.51)

\displaystyle f_{3}^{(3)}(x)

\displaystyle=d(*),

(3.52)

\displaystyle f_{3}^{(4)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{l\left(4(l+2)l^{2}+l-3\right)}{32x^{4}p_{0}(x)^{3}}+\frac{5l(l+1)p^{\prime\prime}_{0}(x)}{64x^{2}p_{0}(x)^{4}}-\frac{3p_{0}^{(4)}(x)}{1024p_{0}(x)^{4}}+\frac{5p^{\prime\prime}_{0}(x)^{2}}{256p_{0}(x)^{5}}+d(*),

(3.53)

\displaystyle f_{3}^{(5)}(x)

\displaystyle=d(*),

(3.54)

\displaystyle f_{3}^{(6)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{l(l+1)(16l(l+1)(6l(l+1)-13)+135)}{1536x^{6}p_{0}(x)^{5}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{35l(l+1)(8l(l+1)-9)p^{\prime\prime}_{0}(x)}{3072x^{4}p_{0}(x)^{6}}+\frac{5l(l+1)p_{0}^{(3)}(x)}{768x^{3}p_{0}(x)^{6}}+\frac{89l(l+1)p_{0}^{(4)}(x)}{12288x^{2}p_{0}(x)^{6}}-\frac{5p_{0}^{(6)}(x)}{32768p_{0}(x)^{6}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{135l(l+1)p^{\prime\prime}_{0}(x)^{2}}{2048x^{2}p_{0}(x)^{7}}-\frac{p_{0}^{(3)}(x)^{2}}{2048p_{0}(x)^{7}}+\frac{105p_{0}^{(4)}(x)\,p^{\prime\prime}_{0}(x)}{16384p_{0}(x)^{7}}-\frac{371p^{\prime\prime}_{0}(x)^{3}}{16384p_{0}(x)^{8}}+d(*),

(3.55)

Here
d(*)
denotes a total derivative.
The odd-order coefficients are total derivatives, while the even-order coefficients give non-trivial contributions to the local WKB periods.
These coefficients determine the local WKB periods discussed in Section
3.3
.

3.2.3
Diagonalization of the second row

We next consider the diagonalization of the second row.
Since the diagonal entry
f_{2}(x)
does not depend on
g_{2,\,3}(x)
, its determination at a given order only requires the constraint
r_{21}(x)=0
.
The equation
r_{23}(x)=0
, which fixes
g_{2,\,3}(x)
, can therefore be postponed.
Thus the computation of the diagonal coefficient
f_{2}(x)
decouples from the determination of
g_{2,\,3}(x)
at this stage.

We expand

g_{2,\,1}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\,S_{2,\,1}^{(k)}(x),\qquad f_{2}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}\,f_{2}^{(k)}(x).

(3.56)

Accordingly, we also expand

r_{21}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\,r_{21}^{(k)}(x).

(3.57)

At the lowest orders, the second-row equations take the form

\displaystyle r_{21}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\xi}{x}\,p_{0}+p_{1}+p_{0}S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}-\frac{\xi}{x}\,S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}+S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}=0,

(3.58)

\displaystyle f_{2}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=2p_{0},

(3.59)

\displaystyle r_{21}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi}{x}\,S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}-\frac{l}{x}\,S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}+p_{0}S_{2,\,1}^{(1)}+S_{2,\,1}^{(1)}S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}-\frac{\xi}{x}\,S_{3,\,1}^{(1)}+S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}S_{3,\,1}^{(1)}+S_{2,\,1}^{(0)\prime}=0,

(3.60)

\displaystyle f_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\xi}{x}\,S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}-\frac{\xi}{x}\,S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}+S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}.

(3.61)

Using the minus-branch data obtained in Subsection
3.2.2
,

S_{3,\,1}^{(0)}(x)=-p_{0}(x),\quad S_{3,\,2}^{(0)}(x)=-\frac{\xi}{x},

(3.62)

together with
p_{1}(x)=0
, we find

f_{2}^{(1)}(x)=0.

(3.63)

Thus, also in the second row, the first non-trivial contribution starts only at order
\epsilon^{2}
.
Substituting the third-row coefficients into
r_{21}^{(1)}=0
, we obtain

-\frac{S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}(x)\,p^{\prime}_{0}(x)}{2p_{0}(x)}+S_{2,\,1}^{(0)\prime}(x)-\frac{l\,\xi}{x^{2}}+\frac{\xi\,p^{\prime}_{0}(x)}{2x\,p_{0}(x)}=0.

(3.64)

Multiplying this equation from the left by
\xi
, and using the nilpotency
\xi^{2}=0
, we obtain an ordinary first-order equation for the bosonic combination
\xi\,S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}(x)
:

-\frac{\xi\,S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}(x)\,p^{\prime}_{0}(x)}{2p_{0}(x)}+\xi\,S_{2,\,1}^{(0)\prime}(x)=0.

(3.65)

This integrates to

\xi\,S_{2,\,1}^{(0)}(x)=C\sqrt{p_{0}(x)},

(3.66)

where
C
is a Grassmann-even nilpotent integration constant.
Accordingly,

f_{2}^{(2)}(x)=-\frac{C(l+1)}{2x^{2}\sqrt{p_{0}(x)}}.

(3.67)

This behavior is qualitatively different from that of the third row.
Unlike the third-row coefficients, which are obtained recursively from local algebraic-differential relations, the second-row coefficient
f_{2}(x)
already requires an integration at the first non-trivial step; see Eq. (
3.66
).
This essential difference suggests that the second row is governed by a non-local structure.
Higher-order coefficients are therefore expected to involve iterated integrals and to be related to non-local quantities rather than to the local WKB periods.

3.3
WKB periods

For the present
C(2)^{(2)}
problem, the WKB periods (
3.9
) associated with the third and second rows are defined by

Q_{i}^{(\mathrm{loc})}\coloneqq\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f_{3}^{(i)}(x)\,dx,\quad Q_{i}^{(\mathrm{nloc})}\coloneqq\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f_{2}^{(i)}(x)\,dx,

(3.68)

where
\mathcal{C}
is the Pochhammer contour shown in Fig.
3.1
.
Here,
Q_{i}^{(\mathrm{loc})}
are the local periods associated with the third row, while
Q_{i}^{(\mathrm{nloc})}
denote the non-local periods associated with the second row.

Since integrands of the form
p_{0}(x)^{a}\,x^{b}
appear repeatedly, it is convenient to introduce the contour integral

J(a,b)\coloneqq\oint_{\mathcal{C}}p_{0}(x)^{a}\,x^{b}\,dx=\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\left(x^{\frac{hM}{2}}-1\right)^{\!a}\,x^{b}\,dx=-\frac{e^{\pi ia}\,\pi i}{hM}\frac{\Gamma\left(-a-\frac{2(b+1)}{hM}\right)}{\Gamma(-a)\,\Gamma\left(1-\frac{2(b+1)}{hM}\right)}.

(3.69)

This definition allows us to express all period integrals in a common basis and to relate different integrals systematically through the recurrence relation below.
Using
\Gamma(x+1)=x\,\Gamma(x)
, one finds the recurrence relation

J\left(a+m,\,b+\frac{hMn}{2}\right)=e^{\pi im}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2(b+1)}{hM}+n\right)\,\Gamma(a+m+1)\,\Gamma\left(a+\frac{2(b+1)}{hM}+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(a+m+\frac{2(b+1)}{hM}+n+1\right)\,\Gamma\left(\frac{2(b+1)}{hM}\right)\,\Gamma(a+1)}\,J(a,b).

(3.70)

For convenience, we write
J(-n,-m)\eqqcolon J_{n,\,m}
.

Using the coefficients
f_{3}^{(k)}(x)
, the local periods up to sixth order are obtained as

\displaystyle Q_{2}^{(\mathrm{loc})}

\displaystyle=\frac{MJ_{2,\,2}}{2(M+1)}\left[l(l+1)-\frac{M-1}{8}\right],

(3.71)

\displaystyle Q_{3}^{(\mathrm{loc})}

\displaystyle=0,

(3.72)

\displaystyle Q_{4}^{(\mathrm{loc})}

\displaystyle=\frac{M^{2}\,J_{5,\,4}}{16(M+1)(4M+3)}\left[l(l+1)(8l(l+1)-5M-1)-\frac{(M-3)(M-1)(2M+1)}{16}\right],

(3.73)

\displaystyle Q_{5}^{(\mathrm{loc})}

\displaystyle=0,

(3.74)

\displaystyle Q_{6}^{(\mathrm{loc})}

\displaystyle=\frac{7M^{3}}{2048(M+1)(6M+5)(7M+5)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\bigg[l(l+1)\big(-1120l(l+1)M+32l(l+1)(24l(l+1)-17)

\displaystyle\qquad-46M^{3}+491M^{2}+514M+121\big)-\frac{(M-5)(M-1)(M(4M+5)(6M-13)-27)}{8}\bigg].

(3.75)

As expected from the fact that the odd-order coefficients
f_{3}^{(2k+1)}(x)
are total derivatives, the corresponding odd-order local periods vanish.
Thus the first non-trivial local periods arise only at even orders.
Higher-order local periods are summarized in Appendix
A
.

For later comparison with the SCFT side, it is convenient to introduce the symmetric polynomial

s_{1}\coloneqq\left(l+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\!\!2}=l(l+1)+\frac{1}{4}.

(3.76)

Then the local periods become polynomials in
s_{1}
.
For instance,

\displaystyle Q_{2}^{(\mathrm{loc})}

\displaystyle=M\,J_{2,\,2}\left(\frac{1}{M+1}\,s_{1}-\frac{1}{8}\right),

(3.77)

\displaystyle Q_{4}^{(\mathrm{loc})}

\displaystyle=\frac{M^{2}\,J_{5,\,4}}{2(4M+3)}\left(\frac{1}{M+1}\,s_{1}^{2}-\frac{5}{8}\,s_{1}-\frac{1}{64}M^{2}+\frac{9}{128}M+\frac{9}{128}\right),

(3.78)

\displaystyle Q_{6}^{(\mathrm{loc})}

\displaystyle=\frac{21M^{3}\,J_{8,\,6}}{8(6M+5)(7M+5)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigg[\frac{1}{M+1}\,s_{1}^{3}-\frac{35}{24}\,s_{1}^{2}-\frac{46M^{2}-537M-537}{768}\,s_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-\frac{M^{4}}{256}+\frac{95M^{3}}{3072}-\frac{95M^{2}}{2048}-\frac{475M}{3072}-\frac{475}{6144}\Bigg].

(3.79)

On the other hand, the second row gives the low-order non-local period

Q_{2}^{(\mathrm{nloc})}=-\frac{1}{2}C(l+1)J_{\frac{1}{2},\,2},

(3.80)

where
C
is a Grassmann-even nilpotent integration constant.
This already suggests a structure qualitatively different from the local periods arising from the third row.

4
Superconformal field theory

In this section, we use
\left(z,\bar{z}\right)
for the coordinates on the complex plane and focus on the holomorphic sector.
The
\mathcal{N}=1
superconformal symmetry is generated by the energy-momentum tensor
T(z)
and the supercurrent
G(z)
, whose OPEs are

\displaystyle T(z)\,T(w)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\frac{c}{2}}{(z-w)^{4}}+\frac{2T(w)}{(z-w)^{2}}+\frac{\partial_{w}T(w)}{z-w}+\mathrm{regular},

(4.1)

\displaystyle T(z)\,G(w)

\displaystyle\sim+\frac{\frac{3}{2}\,G(w)}{(z-w)^{2}}+\frac{\partial_{w}G(w)}{z-w}+\mathrm{regular},

(4.2)

\displaystyle G(z)\,G(w)

\displaystyle\sim+\frac{\frac{2c}{3}}{(z-w)^{3}}+\frac{2T(w)}{z-w}+\mathrm{regular},

(4.3)

where
c
is the central charge.
From these OPEs, one obtains the
\mathcal{N}=1
super-Virasoro algebra.
We expand the holomorphic fields as follows

T(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}L_{n}\,z^{-n-2},\quad G(z)=\sum_{r}G_{r}\,z^{-r-\frac{3}{2}},

(4.4)

where
r\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
in the Neveu–Schwarz (NS) sector and

r\in\mathbb{Z}
in the Ramond (R) sector.
The modes satisfy super-Virasoro (superconformal) algebra:

\displaystyle[L_{m},L_{n}]

\displaystyle=(m-n)L_{m+n}+\frac{c}{12}\left(m^{3}-m\right)\delta_{m+n,\,0},

(4.5)

\displaystyle[L_{m},G_{r}]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{m}{2}-r\right)G_{m+r},

(4.6)

\displaystyle\{G_{r},G_{s}\}

\displaystyle=2L_{r+s}+\frac{c}{3}\left(r^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\delta_{r+s,\,0}.

(4.7)

The primary field
\varPhi(z)
with conformal dimension
\varDelta
is defined by the OPE

T(z)\,\varPhi(w)=\frac{\varDelta\varPhi(w)}{(z-w)^{2}}+\frac{\partial_{w}\varPhi(w)}{z-w}+\cdots.

(4.8)

Let
A(z)
and
B(z)
be fields of conformal dimensions
\varDelta_{A}
and
\varDelta_{B}
, respectively.
We write their operator product expansion in the form

A(z)\,B(w)=\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\frac{\{AB\}_{k}(w)}{(z-w)^{k}}+:\!AB\!:\!(w)+\mathcal{O}(z-w).

(4.9)

Here
:\!AB\!:\!(w)
denotes the normal ordered product on the complex plane, defined in terms of the radial ordered product by

:\!AB\!:\!(w)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{w}dz\,\frac{\mathcal{R}(A(z)\,B(w))}{z-w}.

(4.10)

4.1
Free field realization of super-Virasoro algebras

In this subsection, we realize the
\mathcal{N}=1
super-Virasoro algebra in terms of a free boson and a free fermion.
We start from the quantum Miura transformation
11
:

\displaystyle T(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\!:\!\partial\phi\,\partial\phi\!:\!(z)+\frac{1}{2}\!:\!\psi\,\partial\psi\!:\!(z)-\alpha_{0}\rho\,\partial^{2}\phi(z),

(4.11)

\displaystyle G(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\;:\!\partial\phi\,\psi\!:\!(z)-2\alpha_{0}\rho\,\partial\psi(z),

(4.12)

where
\phi(z)
is the free boson and
\psi(z)
is the Majorana fermion.
Here,
\alpha_{0}
denotes the background-charge parameter.
For the
\mathcal{N}=1
super-Virasoro case, the quantum Miura transformation takes the above form with
\rho=\frac{1}{2}
.
We define their OPEs by

\displaystyle\phi(z)\,\phi(w)

\displaystyle\sim\log(z-w),

(4.13)

\displaystyle\phi(z)\,\psi(w)

\displaystyle\sim 0,

(4.14)

\displaystyle\psi(z)\,\psi(w)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{z-w}.

(4.15)

The central charge is given by

c=\frac{3}{2}-12\alpha_{0}^{2}\rho^{2}=\frac{3}{2}-3\alpha_{0}^{2}.

(4.16)

The free fields are expanded in modes as

\displaystyle\phi(z)

\displaystyle=q+a_{0}\log z-\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{a_{n}}{n}z^{-n},

(4.17)

\displaystyle\psi(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\psi_{r}\,z^{-r-\frac{1}{2}},

(4.18)

where
r\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
in the NS sector and
r\in\mathbb{Z}
in the R sector.
The modes satisfy the Neveu–Schwarz–Ramond (NSR) algebra:

[a_{m},a_{n}]=m\,\delta_{m+n,\,0},\quad[a_{0},q]=1,\qquad\{\psi_{r},\psi_{s}\}=\delta_{r+s,\,0}.

(4.19)

As primary fields corresponding to the NS and R vacua, we introduce the vertex operators

V_{\varLambda}(z)=\;:\!e^{\varLambda\phi}\!:\!(z),\quad W_{\varLambda}^{\pm}=\;:\!\sigma^{\pm}\,e^{\varLambda\phi}\!:\!(z),

(4.20)

where
\sigma^{\pm}
are the spin fields associated with the Majorana fermion
\psi
.
The conformal dimensions of these vertex operators are

\varDelta_{\mathrm{NS}}=\frac{1}{2}\varLambda(\varLambda+\alpha_{0}),\quad\varDelta_{\mathrm{R}}=\varDelta_{\mathrm{NS}}+\frac{1}{16}.

(4.21)

Here the shift by
\frac{1}{16}
in
\varDelta_{\mathrm{R}}
comes from the conformal weight of the spin field.
For later convenience, we also introduce the symmetric polynomial

\sigma_{1}\coloneqq\left(\varLambda+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\right)^{\!\!2}.

(4.22)

In terms of
\sigma_{1}
, the NS conformal dimension is written as

\varDelta_{\mathrm{NS}}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{1}-\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}}{8}.

(4.23)

In the NS sector, the highest-weight state
\ket{\varLambda}_{\mathrm{NS}}
is defined by

a_{n}\ket{\varLambda}_{\mathrm{NS}}=0\quad(n>0),\qquad\psi_{r}\ket{\varLambda}_{\mathrm{NS}}=0\quad\left(r>0,\;r\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(4.24)

together with

a_{0}\ket{\varLambda}_{\mathrm{NS}}=\varLambda\ket{\varLambda}_{\mathrm{NS}}.

(4.25)

In the R sector, the fermion is expanded in integer modes, and the presence of the zero mode
\psi_{0}
implies that the ground state is two-fold degenerate.
We denote the two Ramond highest-weight states by
\ket{\varLambda,\pm}_{\mathrm{R}}
, which satisfy

a_{n}\ket{\varLambda,\pm}_{\mathrm{R}}=\psi_{n}\ket{\varLambda,\pm}_{\mathrm{R}}=0\quad(n>0),

(4.26)

and

a_{0}\ket{\varLambda,\pm}_{\mathrm{R}}=\varLambda\ket{\varLambda,\pm}_{\mathrm{R}}.

(4.27)

The fermionic zero mode acts as

\psi_{0}\ket{\varLambda,\pm}_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{\varLambda,\mp}_{\mathrm{R}},

(4.28)

which realizes the Clifford algebra in the Ramond ground-state sector.

4.2
Local IoMs on the complex plane

On the complex plane, the local IoMs and the supercharge are defined by contour integrals around the origin

\bm{H}_{n}\coloneqq\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\,J_{n}(z)\quad(n\in\mathbb{Z}),\qquad\bm{Q}\coloneqq\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\,G(z).

(4.29)

Here,
J_{n}(z)
denotes the conserved current of spin
n
, which can be constructed from normal ordered products of the energy-momentum tensor
T
, supercurrent
G
and their derivatives.
The currents
J_{n}(z)
are determined up to total derivative terms by the requirement of mutual commutativity:

[\bm{H}_{m},\bm{H}_{n}]=0\quad(m,n\in\mathbb{Z}),\qquad[\bm{H}_{n},\bm{Q}]=0,\quad\{\bm{Q},\bm{Q}\}=2\bm{H}_{2}.

(4.30)

Since the charges are defined by contour integrals, currents differing by total derivatives give the same conserved charge.
In the present super-Virasoro case, the odd-spin bosonic currents
J_{2k+1}(z)
reduce to total derivatives and hence do not contribute to (
4.29
).
Moreover, imposing the commutativity conditions (
4.30
), one finds no additional independent local conserved charges of half-integer spin.
The local IoMs of the
\mathcal{N}=1
super-Virasoro theory have been studied in several equivalent formalisms.
In particular, the conserved quantities were written in superfield form in
37
, while higher-spin local IoMs were constructed explicitly in component form in
46
.
The first non-trivial currents are given by

\displaystyle J_{2}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq T(z),

(4.31)

\displaystyle J_{4}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\;:\!TT\!:\!(z)+\frac{1}{4}\!:\!\partial G\,G\!:\!(z),

(4.32)

\displaystyle J_{6}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\;:\!T\!:\!TT\!::\!(z)-\frac{c+2}{12}\!:\!\partial T\,\partial T\!:\!(z)+\frac{1}{2}\!:\!T\!:\!\partial G\,G\!::\!(z)-\frac{c-1}{48}\!:\!\partial^{2}G\,\partial G\!:\!(z).

(4.33)

4.3
Conformal transformation and zero mode formulae on the cylinder

To compare the ODE side with the CFT side, we need to map the conserved currents from the complex plane to the cylinder and evaluate their zero modes.
In Virasoro CFT, because the normal ordering prescriptions on the plane and on the cylinder are different, non-trivial correction formulae are required; such formulae were developed in
21
;
38
.
However, those results apply only to periodic operators.
They can therefore be used directly in the R sector, but not in the NS sector, where anti-periodic operators appear.
In this subsection, we present the corresponding formulae for the anti-periodic case.
Their derivation will be given in Appendix
B
.

We use the conformal map

z=e^{u},

(4.34)

from the cylinder coordinate
u
to the complex plane coordinate
z
.
For a primary field
A(z)=\sum\limits_{n}A_{n}z^{-n-\varDelta_{A}}
of conformal weight
\varDelta_{A}
, the transformed field on the cylinder is

\widetilde{A}(u)=\left(\frac{dz}{du}\right)^{\!\!\varDelta_{A}}A(z)=z^{\varDelta_{A}}\,A(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}A_{n}z^{-n}.

(4.35)

For a non-primary field, the transformation rule is written as

\widetilde{A}(u)=z^{\varDelta_{A}}\,A(z)+\delta A(z)\eqqcolon\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\widetilde{A}_{n}z^{-n},

(4.36)

where
\delta A(z)
denotes the extra contribution arising because
A(z)
is not a primary field.
In particular, for the energy-momentum tensor one has

\widetilde{T}(u)=z^{2}\,T(z)+\delta T(z)=z^{2}\,T(z)-\frac{c}{24},

(4.37)

where
\delta T(z)
is the contribution from the Schwarzian derivative.
For later convenience, we introduce

A_{R}(z)=A(z)+z^{-\varDelta_{A}}\,\delta A(z).

(4.38)

Under the conformal transformation (
4.34
), a conserved current
J(z)
is mapped to the corresponding field
\widetilde{J}(u)
on the cylinder.
To compute its zero mode, we need the normal ordered products on the cylinder.
For two fields
\widetilde{A}
and
\widetilde{B}
, the normal ordered product on the cylinder is defined by the time-ordered product with respect to the cylinder time
\operatorname{Re}u
:

(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})(v)\coloneqq\oint_{v}\frac{du}{2\pi i}\frac{\mathcal{T}\left(\!\widetilde{A}(u)\,\widetilde{B}(v)\!\right)}{u-v}.

(4.39)

This differs from the normal ordered product (
4.10
) on the complex plane, which is defined by radial ordering.
Using the conformal transformation (
4.34
) together with (
4.36
), one finds

(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})(v)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{v}\frac{dz}{z}\frac{z^{\varDelta_{A}}\,w^{\varDelta_{B}}\,\mathcal{R}(A_{R}(z)\,B_{R}(w))}{\log\frac{z}{w}},

(4.40)

which expresses the cylinder normal ordering in terms of operator products on the complex plane.
This rewriting is particularly useful for higher-spin operators and will allow us to derive explicit formulae for the zero modes on the cylinder.

For periodic operators, the normal ordered product on the cylinder can be written as

\displaystyle(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})(w)=\widetilde{A}_{-}(w)\,\widetilde{B}(w)

\displaystyle+(-1)^{|A||B|}\widetilde{B}(w)\,\widetilde{A}_{+}(w)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\left[b_{k}(\varDelta_{A}-1)-\frac{(\varDelta_{A}-1)_{k}}{k!}\right]\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}(w)\,w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}-k},

(4.41)

where

\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}(w)=\sum_{m}(\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k})_{m}\,w^{-\varDelta_{A}-\varDelta_{B}+k-m},

(4.42)

b_{k}(x)
denotes the Bernoulli polynomial of the second kind,

\frac{(t+1)^{x}}{\log(t+1)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}(x)\,t^{n-1},\qquad|t|<1,

(4.43)

and
(a)_{k}=a(a-1)\cdots(a-k+1)
.
Accordingly, the zero mode is given by

(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})_{0}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{A}_{-n}\,\widetilde{B}_{n}+(-1)^{|A||B|}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{B}_{-n}\,\widetilde{A}_{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\left[b_{k}(\varDelta_{A}-1)-\frac{(\varDelta_{A}-1)_{k}}{k!}\right](\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k})_{0}.

(4.44)

We summarize the derivation of these formulae in Appendix
B.1
.

In the NS sector, the relevant operators are anti-periodic, and their mode expansions are therefore half-integer moded rather than integer moded.
Correspondingly, the correction term in the cylinder normal ordering is modified from the periodic case, with
(\varDelta_{A}-1)_{k}
replaced by
\big(\!\varDelta_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\big)_{\!k}
.
As a result, the correction term in the cylinder normal ordering is modified accordingly, and one finds

\displaystyle(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})(v)=\widetilde{A}_{-}(w)\,\widetilde{B}(w)

\displaystyle+(-1)^{|A||B|}\,\widetilde{B}(w)\,\widetilde{A}_{+}(w)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\left[b_{k}(\varDelta_{A}-1)-\frac{\big(\!\varDelta_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\big)_{\!k}}{k!}\right]\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}(w)\,w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}-k}.

(4.45)

Accordingly, the zero mode is given by

(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})_{0}=\sum_{r>0}\widetilde{A}_{-r}\widetilde{B}_{r}+(-1)^{|A||B|}\sum_{r>0}\widetilde{B}_{-r}\widetilde{A}_{r}+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\left[b_{k}(\varDelta_{A}-1)-\frac{\big(\!\varDelta_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\big)_{\!k}}{k!}\right](\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k})_{0},

(4.46)

with
r\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
.
The derivation is summarized in Appendix
B.2
.

4.4
Local IoMs and their vacuum eigenvalues on the cylinder

We now turn to the local conserved currents on the cylinder.
They are obtained from the corresponding currents on the complex plane by the conformal transformation discussed in the previous subsection.
For the currents up to spin six, we write

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{2}(v)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{T}(v),

(4.47)

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{4}(v)

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{T}\widetilde{T})(v)+\frac{1}{4}(\partial\widetilde{G}\,\widetilde{G})(v),

(4.48)

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{6}(v)

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{T}(\widetilde{T}\widetilde{T}))(v)-\frac{c+2}{12}(\partial\widetilde{T}\,\partial\widetilde{T})(v)+\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{T}(\partial\widetilde{G}\,\widetilde{G}))(v)-\frac{c-1}{48}(\partial^{2}\widetilde{G}\,\partial\widetilde{G})(v).

(4.49)

The terms involving only
\widetilde{T}
and its derivatives were obtained in
5
;
21
;
38
.
The zero modes of
(\partial\widetilde{G}\,\widetilde{G})
,
(\widetilde{T}(\partial\widetilde{G}\,\widetilde{G}))
, and
(\partial^{2}\widetilde{G}\,\partial\widetilde{G})
are summarized in Appendix
B.3
.
Using these ingredients, one can evaluate the zero mode of each cylinder current
\widetilde{J}_{2k}(u)
by expressing it in terms of cylinder normal ordered products and then applying the formulae of the previous subsection to each term.
The corresponding conserved charges are obtained by integrating these currents along the spatial circle
u=i\sigma\;(0\leq\sigma<2\pi)
:

\bm{I}_{2k}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\sigma}{2\pi}\,\widetilde{J}_{2k}(i\sigma),\quad\bm{\mathcal{Q}}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\sigma}{2\pi}\,\widetilde{G}(i\sigma).

(4.50)

Substituting the mode expansions

\widetilde{T}(u)=\sum_{n}\widetilde{L}_{n}e^{-nu},\quad\widetilde{G}(u)=\sum_{r}\widetilde{G}_{r}\,e^{-ru},

(4.51)

one obtains each
\bm{I}_{2k}
as an operator written in terms of the cylinder modes.
Our main interest is in the action of these charges on highest-weight states, since their eigenvalues will later be compared with the WKB periods on the ODE side.
We therefore define

\bm{I}_{2k}\ket{\varDelta_{\mathrm{NS}}}=I_{2k}^{(\mathrm{NS})}\ket{\varDelta_{\mathrm{NS}}},\quad\bm{I}_{2k}\ket{\varDelta_{\mathrm{R}}}=I_{2k}^{(\mathrm{R})}\ket{\varDelta_{\mathrm{R}}}.

(4.52)

The explicit expressions for the local IoM eigenvalues in the NS sector up to sixth order are

\displaystyle I_{2}^{(\mathrm{NS})}

\displaystyle=\varDelta_{\mathrm{NS}}-\frac{c}{24},

(4.53)

\displaystyle I_{4}^{(\mathrm{NS})}

\displaystyle=\varDelta_{\mathrm{NS}}^{2}-\frac{4c+9}{48}\,\varDelta_{\mathrm{NS}}+\frac{c(4c+21)}{2304},

(4.54)

\displaystyle I_{6}^{(\mathrm{NS})}

\displaystyle=\varDelta_{\mathrm{NS}}^{3}-\frac{3c+13}{24}\,\varDelta_{\mathrm{NS}}^{2}+\frac{8c^{2}+77c+135}{1536}\,\varDelta_{\mathrm{NS}}-\frac{c(c+11)(8c+45)}{110592}.

(4.55)

For comparison with the ODE side, it is convenient to rewrite these eigenvalues in terms of the symmetric polynomial (
4.22
).
In this form, the dependence on the highest-weight is organized in a way that matches naturally with the WKB periods:

\displaystyle I_{2}^{(\mathrm{NS})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma_{1}-\frac{1}{16},

(4.56)

\displaystyle I_{4}^{(\mathrm{NS})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\,\sigma_{1}^{2}-\frac{5}{32}\,\sigma_{1}+\frac{9-2\alpha_{0}^{2}}{512},

(4.57)

\displaystyle I_{6}^{(\mathrm{NS})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\,\sigma_{1}^{3}-\frac{35}{192}\,\sigma_{1}^{2}-\frac{46\alpha_{0}^{2}-537}{6144}\,\sigma_{1}-\frac{24\alpha_{0}^{4}-190\alpha_{0}^{2}+475}{49152}.

(4.58)

These expressions agree with the corresponding NS-sector results in
3
.

We next turn to the R sector.
In the R sector, the corresponding eigenvalues are given by

\displaystyle I_{2}^{(\mathrm{R})}

\displaystyle=\varDelta_{\mathrm{R}}-\frac{c}{24}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\!\varLambda+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\right)^{\!2},

(4.59)

\displaystyle I_{4}^{(\mathrm{R})}

\displaystyle=\varDelta_{\mathrm{R}}^{2}-\frac{2c+3}{24}\,\varDelta_{\mathrm{R}}+\frac{c(c+3)}{576},

(4.60)

\displaystyle I_{6}^{(\mathrm{R})}

\displaystyle=\varDelta_{\mathrm{R}}^{3}-\frac{3c+10}{24}\,\varDelta_{\mathrm{R}}^{2}+\frac{c^{2}+7c+33}{192}\,\varDelta_{\mathrm{R}}-\frac{c\left(c^{2}+11c-1233\right)}{13824}.

(4.61)

Moreover, in the R sector, the supercurrent possesses a zero mode.
Accordingly, the supercharge
\bm{\mathcal{Q}}
acts non-trivially on the Ramond vacuum states:

\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\ket{\varLambda,\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\!\varLambda+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\right)\ket{\varLambda,\mp}.

(4.62)

Therefore, on the Ramond vacuum states one finds

\bm{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}\ket{\varLambda,\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\!\varLambda+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\right)^{\!2}\ket{\varLambda,\pm}=I_{2}^{(\mathrm{R})}\ket{\varLambda,\pm},

(4.63)

which is consistent with the super-Virasoro relation
\{\bm{\mathcal{Q}},\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\}=2\bm{I}_{2}
.
This provides a useful consistency check of the cylinder normalization in the R sector.

5
The ODE/IM correspondence for
\bm{C(2)^{(2)}}
-type ODEs

In this section, we study the ODE/IM correspondence for the
C(2)^{(2)}
-type system by comparing the WKB periods obtained in Section
3
with the eigenvalues of the local IoMs derived in Section
4
.
On the ODE side, we restrict attention to the local periods obtained from the third row.
On the SCFT side, we compare them with the NS sector eigenvalues of the local IoMs.
We therefore restrict ourselves to this sector and summarize the resulting ODE/IM dictionary.

WKB period
\bm{Q_{2}}

We begin with the lowest non-trivial local WKB period.
On the ODE side, the second local period is given by

Q_{2}^{(\mathrm{loc})}=M\,J_{2,\,2}\left[\frac{1}{2(M+1)}\,s_{1}-\frac{1}{16}\right].

(5.1)

On the SCFT side, the corresponding second local IoM in the NS sector is

I_{2}^{(\mathrm{NS})}=\frac{1}{2}\,\sigma_{1}-\frac{1}{16}.

(5.2)

The two expressions have exactly the same functional form, provided that we identify

\sigma_{1}=\frac{1}{M+1}\,s_{1},\quad\varLambda+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M+1}}\left(\!l+\frac{1}{2}\right).

(5.3)

With this identification, the ODE quantity becomes

Q_{2}^{(\mathrm{loc})}=M\,J_{2,\,2}\,I_{2}^{(\mathrm{NS})}.

(5.4)

Thus, the second WKB period is proportional to the eigenvalue of the second local IoM in the NS sector.
This provides the basic dictionary that will be used in the higher-order cases below.

WKB period
\bm{Q_{4}}

Since there is no third-order local IoM, we next consider the fourth-order one.
At this order, the comparison not only tests the identification (
5.3
), but also fixes the relation between the SCFT parameter
\alpha_{0}
and the ODE parameter
M
.
Using Eq. (
5.3
), the WKB period
Q_{4}
in Eq. (
3.78
) is rewritten as

Q_{4}^{(\mathrm{loc})}=\frac{2(M+1)M^{2}\,J_{5,\,4}}{4M+3}\left[\frac{1}{4}\,\sigma_{1}^{2}-\frac{5}{32}\,\sigma_{1}+\frac{-2M^{2}+9M+9}{512(M+1)}\right].

(5.5)

On the SCFT side, the corresponding fourth local IoM eigenvalue in the NS sector is

I_{4}^{(\mathrm{NS})}=\frac{1}{4}\,\sigma_{1}^{2}-\frac{5}{32}\,\sigma_{1}+\frac{9-2\alpha_{0}^{2}}{512}.

(5.6)

The quadratic and linear terms in
\sigma_{1}
already agree as a consequence of (
5.3
).
Requiring the constant terms to coincide further determines the parameter relation

\alpha_{0}=\frac{M}{\sqrt{M+1}}.

(5.7)

This is the same relation as the one needed for the higher order
Q_{k}
and
I_{k}
to agree in the
A_{r}^{(1)}
,
D_{r}^{(1)}
and
E_{6}^{(1)}
cases
30
;
25
.
With this identification, the fourth local WKB period becomes

Q_{4}^{(\mathrm{loc})}=\frac{2(M+1)M^{2}\,J_{5,\,4}}{4M+3}\,I_{4}^{(\mathrm{NS})}.

(5.8)

Thus, the comparison at fourth order fixes the remaining parameter dictionary and shows that the fourth WKB period is again proportional to the eigenvalue of the corresponding local IoM.

WKB period
\bm{Q_{6}}

Since there is no fifth-order local IoM, we next consider the sixth-order one.
At this stage, the identifications (
5.3
) and (
5.7
) are already fixed from the comparison of the second- and fourth-order quantities.
The sixth-order comparison therefore provides a non-trivial test of whether the same dictionary continues to hold for higher local charges.
Using the identifications (
5.3
) and (
5.7
), the WKB period
Q_{6}
in Eq. (
3.79
) is rewritten as

Q^{(\mathrm{loc})}_{6}=\frac{21(M+1)^{2}\,M^{3}\,J_{8,\,6}}{(6M+5)(7M+5)}\,I_{6}^{(\mathrm{NS})}.

(5.9)

Thus, the same dictionary consistently reproduces the sixth local IoM as well.
In particular, the agreement at this order is not used to determine any new parameter relation, but rather serves as a genuine higher-order check of the correspondence.
Therefore, the same identification consistently reproduces the first three non-trivial local IoMs in the NS sector.
This provides strong evidence for the ODE/IM correspondence between the third-row local WKB periods of the
C(2)^{(2)}
-type ODE and the NS sector local IoMs of the
\mathcal{N}=1
super-Virasoro CFT.

6
Conclusions and Discussion

In this paper, we studied the ODE/IM correspondence for the
C(2)^{(2)}
-type system and its relation to the
\mathcal{N}=1
superconformal field theory.
Our main purpose was to formulate the supersymmetric ODE side without taking the bosonic limit, and to compare the resulting WKB periods with the eigenvalues of local integrals of motion on the SCFT side.

On the ODE side, we generalized the modified supersymmetric affine Toda field equations and the associated linear problem of
28
, and proposed boundary conditions better adapted to the conformal limit.
By diagonalizing the full
C(2)^{(2)}
-type linear problem obtained from this setup, we recovered the local WKB periods associated with the third row, corresponding to the WKB quantities studied in
3
;
4
, and extended the computation from eighth to tenth order.
In addition, the analysis of the second row led to an extra structure that was not identified in
28
, and low-order computations suggest that it is related to non-local integrals of motion.
In this sense, our ODE analysis is based on the direct diagonalization of the full
C(2)^{(2)}
-type linear problem rather than on a reduced second-order ODE alone.

On the SCFT side, we derived the conformal-transformation formula from the plane to the cylinder for the anti-periodic case, extending the periodic formula of
38
.
Using this result, we computed the eigenvalues of the local integrals of motion on the cylinder in both the NS and R sectors.
While the integrable structure of the
\mathcal{N}=1
theory was formulated in
33
;
34
, and the eigenvalues of the IoMs were previously investigated numerically through the Suzuki equation in
3
, we derived explicit analytic expressions for the cylinder eigenvalues in the NS and R sectors and used them for a direct comparison with the ODE side.
We then confirmed that the NS sector eigenvalues of the cylinder IoMs agree with the local WKB periods of the
C(2)^{(2)}
-type linear problem.
In this way, we obtained an explicit ODE/IM dictionary for the NS sector based on the diagonalization of the full linear problem.

There remain several interesting directions for future work.
First, although the second-row analysis suggests the presence of non-local conserved quantities, their precise counterpart on the IM side has not yet been identified.
Second, while the R sector eigenvalues of the cylinder IoMs were obtained explicitly, the corresponding quantities on the ODE side are still unknown.
Finally, the present method is expected to apply more broadly to quantum integrable models associated with other affine Lie superalgebras, such as those studied in
42
;
44
;
43
;
45
.

Appendix B
Normal ordering on the cylinder

In this appendix, we derive the zero mode formulas for normal ordered products on the cylinder starting from Eq. (
4.40
).

Let

t\coloneqq\frac{z-w}{w},\quad z=w(1+t).

(B.1)

Then

\frac{1}{z}\frac{z^{\varDelta_{A}}\,w^{\varDelta_{B}}}{\log\frac{z}{w}}=w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}-1}\,\frac{(1+t)^{\varDelta_{A}-1}}{\log(1+t)}.

(B.2)

Using the Bernoulli polynomials of the second kind
b_{n}(x)
defined by Eq. (
4.43
),

\frac{(1+t)^{x}}{\log(1+t)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}(x)\,t^{n-1},

(B.3)

together with the OPE

A_{R}(z)\,B_{R}(w)=\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\frac{\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}(w)}{(z-w)^{k}}+\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{0}(w)+\cdots,

(B.4)

we expand the integrand of Eq. (
4.40
) around
z=w
.
The derivations below are obtained by substituting these expansions into Eq. (
4.40
) and taking the residue at
z=w
, with the singular part specified according to the boundary condition.

B.1
Periodic operators

We first consider the case of periodic operators, following the method of
38
.
In this case, the cylinder normal ordered product is governed by the same singular kernel
\frac{1}{z-w}
as in the periodic sector.
Substituting Eqs. (
B.3
) and (
B.4
) into Eq. (
4.40
) and taking the residue at
z=w
, only the terms for which the power of
(z-w)
is
-1
survive, namely those with
n=k
.
The singular part reproduces the mode decomposition

\widetilde{A}_{-}(w)\,\widetilde{B}(w)+(-1)^{|A||B|}\,\widetilde{B}(w)\,\widetilde{A}_{+}(w),

(B.5)

while the regular part gives the correction terms proportional to
\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}
.
The subtraction term in Eq. (
4.40
) contributes the coefficient

\frac{(\varDelta_{A}-1)_{k}}{k!}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})(v)=\widetilde{A}_{-}(w)\,\widetilde{B}(w)

\displaystyle+(-1)^{|A||B|}\widetilde{B}(w)\,\widetilde{A}_{+}(w)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\left[b_{k}(\varDelta_{A}-1)-\frac{(\varDelta_{A}-1)_{k}}{k!}\right]\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}(w)\,w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}-k}.

(B.6)

Accordingly, the zero mode is given by

\displaystyle(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})_{0}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{A}_{-n}\,\widetilde{B}_{n}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{|A||B|}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{B}_{-n}\,\widetilde{A}_{n}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\left[b_{k}(\varDelta_{A}-1)-\frac{(\varDelta_{A}-1)_{k}}{k!}\right](\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k})_{0}.

(B.7)

For later applications, it is convenient to rewrite the first two terms in Eq. (
B.6
) directly in terms of the normal ordered product on the complex plane.
For periodic operators, the mode expansions are integer-moded, and the contribution from the lower and upper modes can be expressed in a closed local form.
More precisely, one obtains

\displaystyle\quad\;\widetilde{A}_{-}(w)\,\widetilde{B}(w)+(-1)^{|A||B|}\widetilde{B}(w)\,\widetilde{A}_{+}(w)

\displaystyle=w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\,\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{0}(w)+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}-k}\,\frac{(\varDelta_{A}-1)_{k}}{k!}\,\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}(w).

(B.8)

This is obtained by substituting the OPE of
A_{R}(z)\,B_{R}(w)
into the residue expression for
\widetilde{A}_{-}(w)\,\widetilde{B}(w)+(-1)^{|A||B|}\,\widetilde{B}(w)\,\widetilde{A}_{+}(w)
and expanding
z^{\varDelta_{A}-1}
around
z=w
.
Substituting Eq. (
B.8
) into Eq. (
B.6
), we obtain the simplified expression

(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})(v)=w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\,\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{0}(w)+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}-k}\,b_{k}(\varDelta_{A}-1)\,\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}(w).

(B.9)

B.2
Anti-periodic operators

We next consider the case of anti-periodic operators.
In this case, the singular part of the kernel is modified from

\frac{1}{z-w}\qquad\text{to}\qquad\frac{\sqrt{zw}}{z-w}.

(B.10)

Equivalently, in terms of the variable
t=\frac{z-w}{w}
, the singular part is written as

\frac{1}{w}\frac{(1+t)^{\varDelta_{A}-\frac{1}{2}}}{t}.

(B.11)

Therefore, compared with the periodic case, the only modification is the subtraction of this singular kernel.
Using Eq. (
B.3
), we obtain

\frac{1}{z}\frac{(1+t)^{\varDelta_{A}}}{\log(1+t)}=\frac{1}{w}\frac{(1+t)^{\varDelta_{A}-\frac{1}{2}}}{t}+\frac{1}{w}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[b_{n}(\varDelta_{A}-1)-\frac{\big(\varDelta_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\big)_{\!n}}{n!}\right]t^{n-1}.

(B.12)

Substituting this expansion together with Eq. (
B.4
) into Eq. (
4.40
) and taking the residue at
z=w
, we again find that only the terms with
n=k
contribute.
The singular part now reproduces the half-integer mode decomposition

\widetilde{A}_{-}(w)\,\widetilde{B}(w)+(-1)^{|A||B|}\,\widetilde{B}(w)\,\widetilde{A}_{+}(w),

(B.13)

while the regular part gives the correction terms proportional to
\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})(v)=\widetilde{A}_{-}(w)\,\widetilde{B}(w)

\displaystyle+(-1)^{|A||B|}\,\widetilde{B}(w)\,\widetilde{A}_{+}(w)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\left[b_{k}(\varDelta_{A}-1)-\frac{\big(\varDelta_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\big)_{\!k}}{k!}\right]\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}(w)\,w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}-k}.

(B.14)

Accordingly, the zero mode is given by

\displaystyle(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})_{0}=\sum_{r>0}\widetilde{A}_{-r}\widetilde{B}_{r}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{|A||B|}\sum_{r\geq 0}\widetilde{B}_{-r}\widetilde{A}_{r}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\left[b_{k}(\varDelta_{A}-1)-\frac{\big(\varDelta_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\big)_{\!k}}{k!}\right](\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k})_{0},

(B.15)

with
r\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
.

In the anti-periodic case, one can similarly rewrite the first two terms in Eq. (
B.14
) directly in terms of the normal ordered product on the complex plane.
Because the mode expansions are now half-integer-moded, the resulting coefficient is shifted accordingly.
More precisely, one finds

\displaystyle\quad\;\widetilde{A}_{-}(w)\,\widetilde{B}(w)+(-1)^{|A||B|}\,\widetilde{B}(w)\,\widetilde{A}_{+}(w)

\displaystyle=w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\,\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{0}(w)+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}-k}\,\frac{\bigl(\varDelta_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\bigr)_{\!k}}{k!}\,\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}(w).

(B.16)

This is obtained by substituting the OPE of
A_{R}(z)\,B_{R}(w)
into the residue expression for
\widetilde{A}_{-}(w)\,\widetilde{B}(w)+(-1)^{|A||B|}\,\widetilde{B}(w)\,\widetilde{A}_{+}(w)
and expanding
z^{\varDelta_{A}-\frac{1}{2}}
around
z=w
.
Compared with the periodic case, the half-integer moding shifts the coefficient from
\frac{(\varDelta_{A}-1)_{k}}{k!}
to
\frac{\bigl(\varDelta_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\bigr)_{\!k}}{k!}
.
Substituting Eq. (
B.16
) into Eq. (
B.14
), we obtain the simplified expression

(\widetilde{A}\widetilde{B})(v)=w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}\,\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{0}(w)+\sum_{k=1}^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}}w^{\varDelta_{A}+\varDelta_{B}-k}\,b_{k}(\varDelta_{A}-1)\,\{A_{R}B_{R}\}_{k}(w).

(B.17)

In particular, the cylinder normal ordered product itself is sector-independent;
the difference between the periodic and anti-periodic cases appears only in the intermediate mode decomposition and in the corresponding zero mode formulae.
