A central limit theorem for the signatures of 2-bridge knots
Moshe Cohen, Cody Baker, Henry Dam, Rebecca Felber, Neal Madras, Ritvik Saha, Daisy Thackrah

Abstract
. Cohen, Lowrance, Madras, and Raanes [ CLMR24 ] computed the average (absolute value of) signature over all 2-bridge knots with crossing number c c by introducing the number s ⁡ ( c , σ ) s(c,\sigma) of 2-bridge knots of crossing number c c and signature σ \sigma . Here we provide a closed formula for this number. We use these calculations to show that the distribution of the signatures of 2-bridge knots with crossing number c c approaches a normal distribution as c c tends to infinity.

57K10; 60F05; 05A10

1.
Introduction

Many results from random topology show central limit theorems for genus distributions: Chmutov and Pittel
[
CP13
]
for random chord diagrams; Chmutov and Pittel
[
CP16
]
for randomly gluing of the sides of several polygonal discs; Even-Zohar and Farber
[
EZF21
]
for surfaces as above but with boundary (a 2D version); and Shrestha
[
Shr22
]
for random square-tiled surfaces. Unlike these random topological objects, knots have been carefully identified and tabulated
[
LM26
]
. A concern for generating (and proving results on) large random knots is whether these samples are representative. The goal of the work below is to understand the asymptotics of a well-understood, very large infinite family of knots so that one could eventually compare with random knot models.

Many random knot models appear in the literature: random walks in the braid group

; random petal diagrams
[
EZHLN16
,

]
; random grid diagrams
[

]
; random meander diagrams
[
OT22
]
; randomly embedded polygons
[
CS16
,
CCRS18
,
CNSS19
,
CSS24
]
; random diagrams
[
CCM16
]
; and others. Using work by Koseleff and Pecker
[
KPR10
,
KP11a
,
KP11b
,
KP15
]
, Cohen and Krishnan
[

]
and with Even-Zohar
[

]
developed and studied a model for random 2-bridge knots. These knots arise in models for polymers in work of Beaton, Eng, and Soteros with others
[

, where this study of randomness may be important. See also Blair, Pongtanapaisan, and Soteros
[

A knot diagram is a planar projection of a knot where the double-points are decorated by over- and under-crossing information. The crossing number
c(K)
of a knot
K
is the minimum number of such points over all possible diagrams of
K
. The bridge number of a knot is the minimum number of local maxima of a diagram of the knot over all possible diagrams. For more background see the textbook by Cromwell
[
Cro04
]
. The only 1-bridge knot is the unknot; a simple closed curve always has at least one local max. The family of 2-bridge knots have two local (non-nested) maxima from which four strands descend; the second and third strands are twisted
a_{1}
times (with sign indicating which direction they are twisted), the third and fourth strands are twisted
-a_{2}
times, the second and third strands are twisted
a_{3}
times, and so on, with some finite number
k
of twist regions, after which there are two local minima. See Figure
1
. One can write the finite continued fraction
[a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}]=p/q
for
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
to obtain a rational number, as in Kauffman and Lambropoulou
[
KL02
,
KL03
]
, or use the Farey graph as in Hoste, Shanahan, and Van Cott
[
HSVC21
]
.

Figure 1.
A 2-bridge knot with continued fraction [1,1,3,1] drawn horizontally instead of vertically for space so that the “maxima” are on the left

Instead of considering the set
K(c)
of 2-bridge knots with crossing number
c
, Cohen
[
Coh23
]
introduced a proxy set
T(c)
of words corresponding to 2-bridge knot diagrams with crossing number
c
. Since words and their reverses give two diagrams for the same knot, we abuse notation to refer to
T(c)
as a partially-double-counted set of knots, where the the palindromic knots are those counted only once. Cohen and Lowrance
[
CL24
]
used this combinatorial description to produce a convenient recursion between
T(c)
and
T(c-1)
together with two copies of
T(c-2)
, and they computed the average Seifert 3-genus
\overline{g}(c)
over
K(c)
. See also independent work on this topic by Suzuki and Tran
[
ST24a
]
and Ray and Diao
[
RD23
]
.
Cohen, Dinardo, Lowrance, Raanes, Rivera, Steindl, and Wanebo
[
CDL+24
]
computed the number
t(c,g)
of 2-bridge knots in
T(c)
with crossing number
c
and genus
g
. They then used this calculation to show a central limit theorem over
K(c)
: that the distribution of genera for 2-bridge knots with crossing number
c
asymptotically approaches a normal distribution as
c
approaches infinity. Perhaps this suggests some sort of “combinatorial nature” for the set of 2-bridge knots of crossing number
c
.

We now tell the analogous story for the signature of a knot. This is the signature, or difference between the numbers of positive and negative eigenvalues, of a matrix associated with the knot. This matrix is related to the Seifert surface of the knot which is used to obtain the genus of the knot. This invariant, introduced by Trotter
[
Tro62
]
and given a diagrammatic formulation for alternating knots by Traczyk
[
Tra04
]
and Dasbach and Lowrance
[
DL11
]
, plays an important role in comparing more difficult knot invariants. We note that signature is always even. For example, the knot in Figure
1
has signature 0. More background can be found in
[
CLMR24
]
. Cohen, Lowrance, Madras, and Raanes
[
CLMR24
]
computed the average (absolute value of) signature
\overline{|\sigma|}(c)
over all 2-bridge knots with crossing number
c
. This group also developed recursions for the number
s(c,\sigma)
of those knots in
T(c)
with crossing number
c
and signature
\sigma=2n
. Small values are given in the following Table
1
, which appears as Table 4 in
[
CLMR24
]
.

Below we will find closed formulas for these values and then use this result to show a central limit theorem for signatures of 2-bridge knots.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
c\geq 3
, let
K(c)
be the set of 2-bridge knots of a given crossing number
c
with each knot counted exactly once. Let
k(c,\leq x)
be the number of knots in
K(c)
with signature at most
x
.
For every real number
t
,

(1.1)

\lim_{c\rightarrow\infty}\frac{k\left(c,\,\leq t\sqrt{c}\right)}{|K(c)|}\;=\;\Phi(t),

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.
That is, over the set
K(c)
of 2-bridge knots with crossing number
c
, the distribution of signatures
is asymptotically normally distributed with mean 0 and variance
c
as
c
tends to infinity.

Using an important result on quasi-isomorphisms by Björklund and Hartnick
[
BH11
]
, Dunfield and Tiozzo
[
DT25
]
prove a central limit theorem for the signature of random positive 3-braids. Again, this seems to suggest some sort of “combinatorial nature” for the set of 2-bridge knots with
c
crossings.
Might our Theorem
1.1
together with this result shed light on the behavior of signatures of knots in general? More generally, perhaps the approach by Dunfield and Tiozzo may lead to a more unified set of central limit theorems for knot invariants.
The key may be to have an explicit mechanism that builds up classes of knots by a sequence of
simple steps, possibly with constraints.

The proof of our Main Theorem
1.1
will follow from these closed formulas for
s(c,\sigma)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
c\geq 3
, let
T(c)
be the partially-double-counted set of 2-bridge knots with crossing number
c
where the palindromic knots are those counted only once.

When
c=2m
is even, the number of 2-bridge knots with crossing number
c
and signature
2n
from among the set
T(c)
is

(1.2)

s(2m,2n)=\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m-|n|-2}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-2-i}.

When
c=2m+1
is odd, the number of 2-bridge knots with crossing number
c
and signature
2n
from among the set
T(c)
is

(1.3)

s(2m+1,2n)=\sum_{i=0}^{\min\{m+n-3,\;m-n\}}{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-3-i}

for all
n\neq 1
, and when
n=1
we have

(1.4)

s(2m+1,2)=1+\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}{2m-3-2i\choose m-2-i},

which is just one more than the formula given in Equation (
1.3
).

These are sums of binomial coefficients in columns of (the centered) Pascal’s triangle.

For more on this last sentence, see Example
3.1

3.1.
Assuming odd crossing number to prove even crossing number

Proof.

Assume Equation (
1.3
) for odd crossing number

s(2m+1,2n)=\sum_{i=0}^{\min\{m+n-3,\;m-n\}}{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-3-i}

for all
n\neq 1
, where the sum need only run until
m-n
for
n\geq 1
, and when
n=1
we have Equation (
1.4
)

s(2m+1,2)=1+\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}{2m-3-2i\choose m-2-i}.

We will show:

s(2m+2,2n)=\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m-|n|-1}{2m-2-2i\choose m+n-1-i}.

When
n\geq 1
, using Lemma
2.4

and that
\sigma=2n\neq-2
, followed by the inductive hypothesis, we have that

\displaystyle s(2m+2,2n)

\displaystyle=s(2m+1,2n+2)+s(2m+1,2n+4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-1}{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-2-i}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-2}{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-1-i}

\displaystyle=\left[{2m-3-2(m-n-1)\choose m+n-2-(m-n-1)}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-2}{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-2-i}\right]+\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-2}{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-1-i}

\displaystyle={2n-1\choose 2n-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-2}\left[{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-2-i}+{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-1-i}\right]

\displaystyle={2m-2-2(m-n-1)\choose m+n-1-(m-n-1)}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-2}\left[{2m-2-2i\choose m+n-1-i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-1}\left[{2m-2-2i\choose m+n-1-i}\right],

as desired.

When
n=0
, following the same lemma but following the alternative inductive hypothesis,

\displaystyle s(2m+2,0)

\displaystyle=s(2m+1,2)+s(2m+1,4)

\displaystyle=\left[1+\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}{2m-3-2i\choose m-2-i}\right]+\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}{2m-3-2i\choose m-1-i}

\displaystyle={0\choose 0}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}\left[{2m-3-2i\choose m-2-i}+{2m-3-2i\choose m-1-i}\right]

\displaystyle={2m-2-2(m-1)\choose m-1-(m-1)}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}\left[{2m-2-2i\choose m-1-i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}{2m-2-2i\choose m-1-i},

as desired.

Lastly, when
n<0
, we use Proposition
2.5
to get

s(2m+2,2n)=s(2m+2,-2n)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-|-n|-1}{2m-2-2i\choose m-n-1-i}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-|n|-1}{2m-2-2i\choose m-n-1-i}.

Since
{p\choose k}={p\choose p-k}
with
p=2(m-1-i)
and
k=(m-1-i)-n
, we have that

s(2m+2,2n)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-|n|-1}{2m-2-2i\choose m+n-1-i},

completing this part of the inductive argument.
∎

When

\displaystyle s(2m+1,2n)

\displaystyle=s(2m,2n-2)+s(2m,2n-4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-|n-1|-2}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-3-i}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-|n-2|-2}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-4-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-(n-1)-2}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-3-i}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-(n-2)-2}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-4-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-1}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-3-i}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-n}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-4-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-1}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-3-i}+\left[\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-1}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-4-i}+{2m-4-2(m-n)\choose m+n-4-(m-n)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-1}\left[{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-3-i}+{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-4-i}\right]+{2n-4\choose 2n-4}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-n-1}\left[{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-3-i}\right]+{2m-3-2(m-n)\choose m+n-3-(m-n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-n}{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-3-i},

as desired.

When
n=1
, Proposition
2.5
tells us that
s(2m+1,2)=s(2m+1,4)+1
.
We use again that
{p\choose k}={p\choose p-k}
with
p=2m-3-2i
and
k=m-1-i
to yield the desired result.

When
n\leq 0
, using Lemma
2.4

we have that

\displaystyle s(2m+1,2n)

\displaystyle=s(2m,2n-2)+s(2m,2n-4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m-|n-1|-2}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-3-i}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-|n-2|-2}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-4-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m+(n-1)-2}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-3-i}+\sum_{i=0}^{m+(n-2)-2}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-4-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m+n-3}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-3-i}+\sum_{i=0}^{m+n-4}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-4-i}

\displaystyle=\left[{2m-4-2(m+n-3)\choose m+n-3-(m+n-3)}+\sum_{i=0}^{m+n-4}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-3-i}\right]+\sum_{i=0}^{m+n-4}{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-4-i}

\displaystyle={2-2n\choose 0}+\sum_{i=0}^{m+n-4}\left[{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-3-i}+{2m-4-2i\choose m+n-4-i}\right]

\displaystyle={2m-3-2(m+n-3)\choose m+n-3-(m+n-3)}+\sum_{i=0}^{m+n-4}\left[{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-3-i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m+n-3}{2m-3-2i\choose m+n-3-i},

as desired.
∎

Together with the base cases discussed in Example
3.2
, these two inductive steps complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Before we get to the proof of the central limit theorem for
K(c)
we will prove a central limit theorem for
T(c)
. First we need to normalize
s(c,\sigma)
over all
\sigma
.
Recall from Proposition
2.2
that
the total number of knots in
T(c)
is
\frac{1}{3}\left(2^{c-2}-(-1)^{c}\right)
,
and hence

(4.1)

\lim_{c\rightarrow\infty}\frac{2^{c}}{|T(c)|}\;=\;12\,.

Definition 4.1
.

For real
t
, we define the cumulative sum
s(c,\,\leq t)
to be
\sum_{j:\,j\leq t}s(c,j)
.
We write
\Phi
for the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution

\Phi(t)\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-x^{2}/2}\,dx\,.

Theorem 4.2

(Central Limit Theorem for
T(c)
)
.

For every real number
t
,

(4.2)

\lim_{c\rightarrow\infty}\frac{s\left(c,\,\leq t\sqrt{c}\right)}{|T(c)|}\;=\;\Phi(t).

That is, for knots in
T(c)
, the distribution of signatures is asymptotically normally distributed with mean 0 and variance
c
as
c
tends to infinity.

The following property will be useful in the proof.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots
be a sequence of cumulative distribution functions such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}G_{n}(t)\,=\,\Phi(t)
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\{u_{n}\}
be a sequence of real numbers that converges to
u\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}G_{n}(u_{n})\,=\,\Phi(u)
.

Proof.

Assume
\{u_{n}\}
converges to
u
. Let
\epsilon>0
. Since
\Phi
is continuous, we can choose
real
a
and
b
such that
a<u<b
and

\Phi(u)-\frac{\epsilon}{2}<\Phi(a)<\Phi(b)<\Phi(u)+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,.

By our hypotheses, we can choose
N_{0}
such that
\Phi(u)-\epsilon<G_{n}(a)
,
G_{n}(b)<\Phi(u)+\epsilon
, and
u_{n}\in[a,b]
for all
n\geq N_{0}
.
Then for all
n\geq N_{0}
we have
G_{n}(a)\leq G_{n}(u_{n})\leq G_{n}(b)
and hence
\Phi(u)-\epsilon<G_{n}(u_{n})<\Phi(u)+\epsilon
.
Since
\epsilon
is an arbitrary positive number, the lemma follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

We begin with case that
c
is even, and write
c=2m
.
Since
s(2m,-2n)=s(2m,2n)
, it suffices to consider
n\leq 0
in the following and prove Equation (
4.2
)
for
t\leq 0
.

Definition 4.4
.

For real
x
and natural number
k
, we define the cumulative sum over the binomial coefficients
\mbox{Bin}(k,\leq x)
to be
\sum_{j:j\leq x}\binom{k}{j}
.

Recall that for
n\leq 0
, Theorem
1.2
says that the number
s(2m,2n)
of 2-bridge knots with crossing number
c
and signature
2n
from the set
T(c)
is
\sum_{i=0}^{m+n-2}\binom{2m-2i-4}{m+n-2-i}.

Example
3.1
and Table
2
interpret this term as the truncated sum of entries in a column of Pascal’s triangle.

From this, since
m+n-2-i\leq(2m-2i-4)/2
, we obtain

(4.3)

s(2m,\leq 2n)\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{m+n-2}\mbox{Bin}\left(2m-2i-4,\,\leq m+n-i-2\right).

Now we bring in probability.

Definition 4.5
.

For each positive integer
M
, let
X_{M}
be a binomially distributed random variable with
parameters
M
and
1/2
, i.e. for
k=0,1,\ldots,M
, we have

(4.4)

\Pr(X_{M}=k)\;=\;\binom{M}{k}\frac{1}{2^{M}}.

Then
X_{M}
has mean
M/2
and variance
M/4
. Accordingly, we let
X_{M}^{*}
be the “standardized” version of
X_{M}
,
i.e.

X_{M}^{*}\;:=\;\frac{X_{M}-M/2}{\sqrt{M/4}}\,,

and let
F_{M}
be the cumulative distribution function of
X_{M}^{*}
:

(4.5)

F_{M}(t)\;:=\;\Pr(X_{M}^{*}\leq t)\;=\;\Pr\left(X_{M}\leq(M/2)+t\sqrt{M/4}\right).

Now consider the terms in the sum of Equation (
4.3
).
Define

(4.6)

\rho_{m,i}(n)\;:=\;\frac{\mbox{Bin}(2m-2i-4,\,\leq m+n-i-2)}{2^{2m-2i-4}}.

We then observe that

\displaystyle\rho_{m,i}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\,k\,\leq\,m+n-i-2}\binom{2m-2i-4}{k}\,\frac{1}{2^{2m-2i-4}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X_{2m-2i-4}\,\leq(m-i-2)+n\right)\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{(by Equation \eqref{eq.XNdef})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\frac{X_{2m-2i-4}-(m-i-2)}{\sqrt{(m-i-2)/2}}\;\leq\;\frac{n}{\sqrt{(m-i-2)/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{2m-2i-4}\left(\frac{n\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{m-i-2}}\right)\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{(by Equation \eqref{eq.FNdef})}.

The classical De Moivre - Laplace Central Limit Theorem tells us that for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have

(4.7)

\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}F_{M}(t)\;=\;\Phi(t).

Now consider
n
as a function of
m
, specifically
n=\lfloor t\sqrt{m/2}\rfloor
for a constant
t\leq 0
. This choice gives the right scaling for the Central Limit Theorem. In particular,
since
n
grows with
m
, we have for every fixed
i
that

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{m-i-2}}\;=\;t\,.

Thus it follows from Equation (
4.7
) and Lemma
4.3
that for every
i
and every
t\leq 0

(4.8)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\rho_{m,i}\left(\lfloor t\sqrt{m/2}\rfloor\right)\;=\;\Phi(t).

Now for
n=\lfloor t\sqrt{m/2}\rfloor
with
t\leq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{s(2m,\leq 2n)}{|T(2m)|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2m-4}}{|T(2m)|}\sum_{i=0}^{m+n-2}\frac{\mbox{Bin}(2m-2i-4,\leq m+n-i-2)}{2^{2i}\times 2^{2m-2i-4}}

(by Equation (
4.3
))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2m-4}}{|T(2m)|}\sum_{i=0}^{m+n-2}\frac{1}{2^{2i}}\,\rho_{m,i}(n)\hskip 22.76219pt\hbox{(by Equation \eqref{eq.rhodef})}.

Using the above expression together with Equations (
4.1
) and (
4.8
) and the Dominated Convergence
Theorem, we obtain for
n=\left\lfloor t\sqrt{m/2}\right\rfloor\leq 0
that

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{s\left(2m,\leq t\sqrt{2m}\right)}{|T(2m)|}\;=\;\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{s(2m,\leq 2n)}{|T(2m)|}\;=\;\frac{3}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{i}}\,\Phi(t)\;=\;\frac{3}{4}\,\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{4}}\,\Phi(t)\;=\;\Phi(t)\,,

where for the first equality, note that
2n\leq t\sqrt{2m}<2n+2
.
This proves Equation (
4.2
) for the subsequence of even
c
.

Now we consider the case that
c
is odd, and we write
c=2m+1
. Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
By Theorem
2.6
, we have
for every
k
that

(4.9)

s(2m+1,2k-2m+2)\;=\;\binom{2m-1}{k}\;-\;s(2m+2,2k-2m+2)\,.

Summing Equation (
4.9
) over all

less than or equal to
(t/2)\sqrt{2m+1}+m-1
gives

(4.10)

\frac{s(2m+1,\,\leq t\sqrt{2m+1})}{|T(2m+1)|}\;=\;A_{m}(t)\;-\,B_{m}(t),

where

A_{m}(t)\;=\;\frac{2^{2m-1}}{|T(2m+1)|}\frac{\mbox{Bin}(2m-1,\,\leq(t/2)\sqrt{2m+1}-m+1)}{2^{2m-1}}

and

(4.11)

B_{m}(t)\;=\;\frac{|T(2m+2)|}{|T(2m+1)|}\frac{s(2m+2,\,\leq t\sqrt{2m+1})}{|T(2m+2)|}\,.

Similarly to the case of even

, we find that

\displaystyle A_{m}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2m-1}}{|T(2m+1)|}\,\Pr\left(X_{2m-1}-\frac{1}{2}(2m-1)\,\leq\,\frac{t}{2}\sqrt{2m+1}+\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2m-1}}{|T(2m+1)|}\,F_{2m-1}\left(\frac{(t/2)\sqrt{2m+1}+(1/2)}{\sqrt{(2m-1)/4}}\right),

and hence (using Equation (
4.1
) and Lemma

(4.12)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}A_{m}(t)\;=\;\frac{12}{4}\,\Phi(t)\,.

From Equations (
4.1
) and (

4.2
) holds for the subsequence of even

,
we can apply Lemma
4.3
to obtain

(4.13)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}B_{m}(t)\;=\;2\,\Phi(t)\,.

It now follows from Equations (
4.10
), (

) and (

) that

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{s(2m+1,\,\leq t\sqrt{2m+1})}{|T(2m+1)|}\;=\;3\Phi(t)\,-\,2\Phi(t)\;=\;\Phi(t).

Therefore Equation (
4.2
) holds for the subsequence of odd

, and the proof of Theorem
4.2
is complete.
∎

Now we turn to the Central Limit Theorem for the set
K(c)
of 2-bridge knots with crossing number
c
.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
k(c,\sigma)
be the number of knots in
K(c)
with signature
\sigma
, and let
k(c,\,\leq x)
be the number of knots in
K(c)

with signature at most
x
.

Let

be the set of palindromic knots with crossing number
c
, let
s_{p}(c,\sigma)
be the number of knots in
T_{p}(c)
with signature
\sigma
,
and let
s_{p}(c,\,\leq x)
be the number of knots in
T_{p}(c)
with signature at most
x
.
By construction, for every

and
\sigma
,

2\,|K(c)|\;=\;|T(c)|\,+\,|T_{p}(c)|\hskip 14.22636pt\hbox{and}\hskip 14.22636ptk(c,\sigma)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\left(s(c,\sigma)+s_{p}(c,\sigma)\right).

From Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.2
, we find

(4.14)

\lim_{c\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|T(c)|}{2|K(c)|}\;=\;1.

We can now prove the Main Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Following Equation (
4.6
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{k(c,\leq\sigma)}{|K(c)|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s(c,\leq\sigma)}{2\,|K(c)|}\,+\,\frac{s_{p}(c,\leq\sigma)}{2\,|K(c)|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s(c,\leq\sigma)}{|T(c)|}\,\frac{|T(c)|}{2\,|K(c)|}\,+\,\frac{s_{p}(c,\leq\sigma)}{2\,|K(c)|}\,.

It follows that

(4.15)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{k(c,\leq\sigma)}{|K(c)|}\,-\,\frac{s(c,\leq\sigma)}{|T(c)|}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{s(c,\leq\sigma)}{|T(c)|}\,\left|\frac{|T(c)|}{2\,|K(c)|}\,-\,1\right|\,+\,\frac{|T_{p}(c)|}{2\,|K(c)|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\frac{|T(c)|}{2\,|K(c)|}\,-\,1\right|\,+\,\left|\frac{2\,|K(c)|-|T(c)|}{2\,|K(c)|}\right|\,.

By Equation (
4.14
), the right-hand side of Equation (
4.15
) converges to 0 as

, uniformly in
\sigma
.
Therefore Equation (
1.1
) follows from
Equation (
4.2
) on the Central Limit Theorem for the set
T(c)
, and the theorem is proved.
∎
