Assessing patient awareness of bruxism before dental implant therapy
Authors: Özlem Saraç Atagün, Seval Ceylan Şen, Gülbahar Ustaoğlu, İrem Sezen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bruxism is a common condition that can lead to serious dental and implant-related complications. This cross-sectional study aimed to assess the presence and type of bruxism in patients seeking dental implant treatment and to evaluate their awareness of the condition. By increasing awareness and identifying bruxism before implant placement, the study seeks to reduce the risk of future implant and prosthetic complications. One hundred fifty systemically healthy patients aged 18–65 who presented for implant treatment were enrolled in the study. Bruxism was assessed through both clinical examination and self-reported questionnaires, in accordance with the 2025 international consensus on bruxism. Clinical evaluation included both intraoral and extraoral examinations to identify bruxism-related signs. Self-reported data were collected using the Bruxism Questionnaire to identify possible awake and sleep bruxism. Instrumentally-based methods were not applied in this study. The Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI) was applied to screen for temporomandibular disorder-related symptoms, and the Perceived Stress Scale-14 (PSS-14) was used to evaluate psychoemotional status. In addition, patients completed a bruxism awareness questionnaire before surgery. Among the 150 participants, 31.3% were clinically diagnosed with bruxism, while 26% reported experiencing sleep, awake, or both types of bruxism according to the self-reported questionnaire. Bruxism was significantly associated only with age (p = 0.042), but not with gender, education, or TMD severity. Awareness levels did not significantly differ by education, except for the association between bruxism and tooth loss (p = 0.014). Perceived stress scores and oral complaints showed no significant relationship with either bruxism diagnosis or type (p ≥ 0.05). Clinically based bruxism-related signs were frequently observed among patients seeking implant treatment, although awareness and self-recognition of bruxism appeared limited. Introduction

Bruxism is currently defined as a masticatory muscle activity characterized by clenching or grinding of the teeth and/or by bracing or thrusting of the mandible, occurring during sleep or wakefulness. According to the updated 2025 international consensus, bruxism is considered a behavior—not a disorder—and should be assessed as subjective-based, clinically based, or instrumentally based, depending on the evaluation method used1–3. Bruxism is a common behavior in the general population, with an estimated global prevalence of approximately 22%2,3.It is generally accepted today that bruxism is predominantly regulated by central nervous system mechanisms rather than peripheral factors4, and it can be categorized into awake bruxism and sleep bruxism. Although bruxism is considered a behavior, it may be associated with negative clinical consequences in some individuals, while in others it may also serve adaptive or protective functions, depending on the context1. Consequently, bruxism is linked to several potential impacts on the stomatognathic system and related structures, although the strength of the supporting evidence varies across outcomes. The etiology of bruxism is considered multifactorial, including factors as genetic, alcohol consumption, smoking, sleep disorders (ex., sleep apnea), systemic diseases (ex., gastroesophageal reflux, central nervous system disorders), various substances, medications, and psychosocial factors such as stress and anxiety4–6. Psychosocial factors, particularly stress, may influence bruxism through increased autonomic nervous system activity and heightened muscle tone. Stress-related activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and sympathetic pathways can elevate arousal responses during wakefulness and sleep, potentially facilitating parafunctional masticatory muscle activity7. Additionally, depression and chronic stress can contribute to the occurrence and severity of bruxism, as they influence the body’s ability to cope with tension and pain. From a multidisciplinary perspective, an individualized assessment and management approach is essential to minimize its potential implications and consequences6.

Epidemiological data on bruxism, especially awake bruxism, are predominantly based on self-report, and reported prevalence varies greatly between studies. The global sleep bruxism prevalence is 21%, and awake prevalence is 23%7. The Standardised Tool for the Assessment of Bruxism (STAB) has been proposed as one of the commonly used multidimensional frameworks combining self-reported information and clinical examination8,9. However, variations in the application of self-report components of STAB have been reported across studies, depending on study design and feasibility, and different levels of adaptation of these items have been used. A multidimensional approach combining self-reported information, clinical inspection, and, when indicated, instrumental assessments using electrophysiological tools has been proposed for the assessment of bruxism3.

Bruxism can result in various signs and symptoms such as dental wear, cervical dentin hypersensitivity, tooth sensitivity, restoration fractures, tooth mobility, muscle pain, and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) pain10–12. In addition, bruxism has been associated with dental implant failures, including crown mobility due to screw loosening, screw or implant fracture, prosthesis fracture (ceramic or metal) with ceramic chipping, severe marginal bone loss, and decementation of over-implant cemented prosthesis13–15.

The signs and symptoms associated with bruxism are complex and varied, requiring a multifaceted and multidisciplinary management protocol. Therapeutic interventions should priories non-invasive and reversible options, with priority to cognitive behavioral therapy (Ecological Momentary Assessment with electronical application), oral appliances (stabilizing splints or, when indicated and in patients with OSA, mandibular advancement devices (MADs), physical therapy when needed, and in particular cases may include pharmacological management (botulinum toxin type A (BTX-A), clonazepam, and clonidine), The control of the comorbid conditions related to bruxism should be always diagnosed and managed first, like Obstructive Sleep Apnea or Gastro Esophageal Reflux16–19.

The definition, prevalence, and effects of bruxism have varied across studies due to differences in diagnostic criteria and assessment methods.While earlier concerns were raised regarding the limitations of self-report-based approaches20–24, these issues have been discussed within the context of the development of multidimensional assessment frameworks for bruxism. In this study, awareness is operationally defined as the patients’ knowledge and recognition of bruxism’s etiological factors, clinical symptoms, and the potential need for treatment, particularly regarding the risk of implant and prosthetic complications. Considering this framework, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate this multidimensional awareness among patients scheduled for dental implant therapy. The secondary aims were to assess the associations between bruxism awareness, the presence of bruxism, demographic characteristics, perceived stress levels (PSS-14), and the severity of temporomandibular disorder (TMD) symptoms. The primary hypothesis was that patient awareness regarding bruxism and its potential impact on implant success would be limited. The secondary hypothesis was that bruxism awareness levels and the presence of bruxism would significantly correlate with specific clinical and demographic parameters.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study was approved by the Health Sciences University Gulhane Scientific Research Ethics Committee (2025/103), and retrospectively registered at Clinical Trials.gov (ID: NCT07088887). A total of 168 patients presenting to the Department of Periodontology at the Gulhane Faculty of Dentistry, University of Health Sciences, for implant treatment between March 1 and June 15, 2025, were approached for participation. Of these, 13 patients declined to participate, and 5 additional patients were excluded due to incomplete questionnaire responses, resulting in a final sample of 150 participants. Eligible participants were aged between 18 and 65 years (to minimize potential confounding factors related to systemic health conditions and medication use, which are more prevalent in older populations and could affect bruxism assessment and implant outcomes), systemically healthy, and able to comprehend and respond to the questionnaire items. Exclusion criteria included intellectual disabilities, dementia, psychosis, or suicidal tendencies; use of medications affecting muscle tone or sleep (e.g., benzodiazepines, muscle relaxants, antidepressants, antipsychotics); neurological conditions affecting movement; current orthodontic treatment; orofacial pain conditions or recent orofacial trauma; substance abuse disorders; and current use of occlusal appliances. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants following a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, methodology, and its potential contribution to scientific knowledge. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary. Individuals who agreed to participate and completed the informed consent forms were subsequently provided with a questionnaire. The decision to participate or not participate in the study did not affect the routine treatment protocol. Initially, an anamnesis form was completed for each patient, documenting their periodontal status, systemic medical conditions, and demographic information.

Bruxism assessment

Bruxism assessment was conducted before the completion of any questionnaires to prevent potential influence on participants’ self-reported awareness. A standardized clinical examination was performed in accordance with the 2025 international consensus on the assessment of bruxism1. Extraoral examination included palpation of the masseter muscle to detect pain and evaluation of masseter hypertrophy, while intraoral examination assessed dental wear, the presence of a linea alba on the buccal mucosa, and recurrent restoration failures. All clinical assessments were performed by G.U., and the diagnostic form used during the examination is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Immediately after the clinical examination, participants completed a Self-Reported Bruxism Questionnaire, derived from the Axis A of the Standardised Tool for the Assessment of Bruxism (STAB)8,9, to identify self-reported awake bruxism (AB) or sleep bruxism (SB). While the original STAB Axis A1.1 and A2 utilize frequency-based scales, these items were adapted into a binary (Yes/No) format for this study to enhance screening efficiency and ensure clear categorization for statistical comparison. A positive (‘Yes’) response was recorded if the behavior was reported at any frequency during the assessment period. Furthermore, a supplementary item regarding jaw pushing or supporting behaviors was included to specifically capture subtle awake parafunctional habits—such as mandibular thrusting or bracing—that patients might not readily identify through standard clenching or grinding questions alone. In accordance with the 2025 international consensus1, the previous grading system of ‘possible’, ‘probable’, and ‘definite’ was not utilized. Participants exhibiting clinically relevant bruxism-related signs during examination were categorized as having clinically based bruxism according to the multidimensional assessment framework. Since no instrumental assessments (e.g., EMG, polysomnography) were performed, ‘instrumentally based’ bruxism was not evaluated. Following these assessments, the Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI)25,26 was administered to screen for temporomandibular disorder-related symptoms. FAI is a widely used self-report instrument for screening temporomandibular disorder (TMD) symptoms. The original FAI consists of 10 items evaluating common TMD-related complaints, including difficulty in mouth opening and mandibular movements, pain during mastication, headache, neck pain, temporomandibular joint pain, joint sounds, parafunctional habits such as clenching or grinding, occlusal discomfort, and psychological tension. Each item is answered on a three-point response scale (“yes” = 10 points, “sometimes” = 5 points, “no” = 0 points). The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with validated cutoff values defining TMD severity as follows: 0–15 = no TMD, 20–40 = mild TMD, 45–65 = moderate TMD, and 70–100 = severe TMD. The scoring system and severity categories follow the original classification proposed by Fonseca et al.26. A Turkish validation study of the Fonseca Anamnestic Index reported excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.951) and high test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.906). These psychometric findings support the use of FAI as a reliable and valid screening tool for temporomandibular disorder symptoms27.

The Perceived Stress Scale-14 (PSS-14) was utilized to evaluate participants’ global stress perception over the previous month. The scale consists of 14 items assessing the degree to which individuals perceive their lives as unpredictable, uncontrollable, and overloaded during the last month28. The 14-item version was selected over the shorter PSS-10 to provide a more detailed assessment of various stress dimensions. The Turkish version of the scale, which has been formally validated for reliability and validity in the Turkish population (Cronbach’s α = 0.84, test-retest reliability = 0.87), was employed28. While the PSS is not a diagnostic tool for clinical stress, scores are generally categorized as low (0–18), moderate (19–37), or high (38–56)28–31. To ensure methodological rigor, the clinical examiner was fully blinded to the participants’ PSS-14 scores, as the clinical examination was completed prior to the administration of any self-report instruments.

The study protocol followed a strict chronological sequence to standardize data collection. First, the clinical examination was performed. Because this examination was conducted prior to the administration of any self-report instruments, the examiner was fully blinded to the participants’ questionnaire-based TMD and stress data. Immediately following the clinical examination, participants completed the self-report instruments, including a multidimensional bruxism awareness questionnaire (Supplementary File 2), which assessed knowledge of etiological factors, symptoms, treatment modalities, and the clinical significance of bruxism. As no standardized or validated instrument for measuring bruxism awareness exists in the literature, this questionnaire was developed specifically for the study. The item pool was created by reviewing previous research on oral parafunction and bruxism-related knowledge, adapting relevant questions from similar awareness surveys, and incorporating additional items based on current bruxism guidelines32–40. Content validity was confirmed through review by three periodontology experts, after which minor revisions were made for clarity. A pilot test with 10 individuals was conducted to ensure clarity and comprehensibility. Implant surgery was then performed on the same day, approximately 15–30 min after all questionnaires were completed. Finally, for patients positively diagnosed with bruxism, specific interventions—including comprehensive counseling regarding potential biomechanical risks to implant therapy and the recommendation of post-operative preventive measures, such as stabilization splints—were provided prior to their surgical discharge.

Statistical analysis

Using G*Power software, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 128 participants, based on a Type I error rate (α) of 0.05, a statistical power (1–β) of 0.95, and 2 degrees of freedom. The effect size (w = 0.35) was chosen as a conventionally accepted medium effect size for chi-square tests in behavioral and clinical research, according to Cohen’s guidelines. Cohen’s kappa was calculated to determine the level of agreement between clinical diagnosis and self-reported bruxism. The agreement was interpreted as follows: < 0, poor; 0.01–0.20, slight; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; and 0.81–1.00, almost perfect.

To manage missing data, all clinical assessment forms and self-reported questionnaires were manually reviewed for completeness immediately upon collection in the clinic. Minor omissions were addressed on-site with the participants, while individuals with largely incomplete responses that could not be resolved were excluded from the study during the initial screening phase. Consequently, the final analyzed dataset contained no missing variables, precluding the need for any statistical imputation methods. A total of 150 participants were successfully recruited and included in the final analysis, safely exceeding the minimum requirement. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and the Skewness-Kurtosis values. Since the data were found to be normally distributed, parametric tests were applied. Descriptive statistics for the variables were presented as mean, standard deviation, frequency (n), and percentage (%). Comparisons of continuous variables between groups were conducted using the Independent Samples t-test and One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Post hoc comparisons following ANOVA were performed using the Duncan test to identify differences between groups. The Chi-square test was used to analyze relationships between categorical variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate the relationships between continuous variables. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the multiple comparisons performed across questionnaire items without formal adjustment for multiplicity, these analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. All analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 26 for Windows).

Key demographic factors, including gender, age group, and education level, as well as the severity of TMD, as measured by the Fonseca Anamnestic Index, are displayed for descriptive comparison between the two groups. Statistical differences between groups were evaluated using the Pearson Chi-square test. In cases where expected cell counts were less than 5, such as in the analysis of TMD severity categories, Fisher’s Exact Test (Fisher-Freeman-Halton extension) was utilized to ensure statistical validity. Based on these analyses, there was no statistically significant difference in TMD severity between individuals with and without a bruxism diagnosis (p = 0.378). Although the proportion of bruxism appeared slightly higher in the mild TMD group, the total absence of bruxism cases in the severe TMD subgroup and the overall limited sample size in these categories limit the generalizability of this finding.

A statistically significant association was identified between age and the clinical diagnosis of bruxism (p = 0.042), with a higher prevalence observed in middle-aged and older individuals in this sample. No significant associations were found regarding gender or educational status. Additionally, although smoking was evaluated as a potential risk factor, Fisher’s Exact Test revealed no significant association between smoking status and the presence of either subjective-based or clinically based bruxism (p > 0.05). Table 3 summarizes the comparison of bruxism-related awareness domains according to educational level. Detailed item-level response distributions assessing knowledge, perceptions, and attitudes toward bruxism across the four educational groups (primary school, high school, university, and postgraduate) are presented in Supplementary Table 3. The aim was to determine whether levels of awareness regarding bruxism differ significantly by educational status. Chi-square tests were used to evaluate the statistical significance of response distribution differences for each item (p < 0.05 considered significant). The Chi-square analysis revealed that educational level had no significant impact on most aspects of bruxism-related awareness. The only statistically significant difference related to education level was observed in the awareness of the link between bruxism and tooth loss (p < 0.05). Participants with high school and university degrees demonstrated a higher level of correct recognition of this risk, whereas those in the primary education and postgraduate groups showed lower agreement, a finding that may reflect specific gaps in patient education or the influence of small subgroup sizes in the latter categories. .

This Chi-square analysis showed no statistically significant association between bruxism (either diagnosis or type) and specific oral complaints. Patients with and without bruxism reported similar symptom patterns, suggesting that these complaints are not bruxism-specific.Although certain numerical trends were observed—such as a higher frequency of tooth mobility in self-reported sleep bruxers and TMJ problems in awake bruxers—these did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05). The relatively small sample sizes in the bruxism subtype subgroups severely limit the power to detect these specific associations, suggesting that these trends should be interpreted with caution and warrant further investigation in larger cohorts .

No formal adjustment for multiple testing (e.g., Bonferroni correction) was performed due to the exploratory nature of the study; however, p-values were interpreted with caution, acknowledging the potential for Type I error inflation.

Discussion

The 2018 international consensus defined bruxism as repetitive masticatory muscle activity characterized by clenching or grinding of the teeth and/or bracing or thrusting of the mandible, distinguishing between sleep and awake bruxism. This consensus further clarifies these definitions, emphasizes that bruxism in healthy individuals should be viewed as a behavior rather than a disorder, and recommends assessing bruxism along a continuum using both self-report and instrumental methods without fixed cut-off points2. This study aimed to identify the presence and type of bruxism in patients undergoing implant therapy and to assess their awareness of the condition, to prevent potential implant and prosthesis-related complications.

Botelho et al. reported a negative association between self-reported bruxism and periodontal status in their cross-sectional study24. Saricam and Tayman’s case-control study found that bruxism is associated with increased tooth wear, muscle pain, sensitivity, radiographic bone changes, and higher trabecular complexity in interdental areas detected by fractal analysis32. Nakayama et al. conducted a preliminary study evaluating the relationship between bruxism-related signs and periodontal status, reporting that signs of awake bruxism were more prevalent than sleep bruxism among patients with periodontal disease, though no significant association was found between bruxism type and periodontal severity33. According to the results of our study, a clinical diagnosis of bruxism did not significantly affect the reporting rates of specific oral complaints such as halitosis, gingival bleeding, tooth grinding, or sensitivity. Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the distribution of these complaints between patients reporting only sleep or only awake bruxism. However, the notably small sample size in the second analysis (comparing sleep vs. awake bruxism) severely limits the generalizability of these findings. Some numerical trends, such as the apparent association of tooth mobility with sleep bruxism and TMD problems with awake bruxism, suggest that different results might emerge in a study with a larger sample. Based on the current data, no statistically significant relationship can be established between the presence or type of bruxism and the specific oral complaints examined, underscoring the need for further research with more robust sample sizes.

Although DC/TMD is widely accepted as the diagnostic gold standard for temporomandibular disorders, it requires a full clinical examination by trained personnel, which limits its feasibility in large-scale or questionnaire-based studies. Therefore, we selected the Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI), a quick, self-administered, and low-cost instrument that has demonstrated high screening accuracy and acceptable psychometric properties26. Nogueira Coutinho et al. reported a significant association between self-reported sleep bruxism and TMD among Brazilian undergraduate students, with sleep bruxism increasing the risk of TMD more than fivefold34. In contrast, our study did not find a statistically significant association between TMD severity and the presence of bruxism. Interestingly, none of the four individuals classified with ‘Severe’ TMD were diagnosed with bruxism. However, due to the limited number of cases in these categories—particularly among those with severe TMD—these observed differences may have lacked statistical power.

Several studies have evaluated the relationship between bruxism and dental implant complications; however, many of them relied on non-standardized or self-reported diagnostic approaches, which limit the strength of the evidence. More comprehensive and methodologically robust research is needed to draw definitive conclusions35–37. Zhou et al. conducted a meta-analysis demonstrating that bruxism significantly increases the risk of dental implant failure, with bruxers showing higher odds of prosthesis failure compared to non-bruxers, suggesting that bruxism is a significant contributing factor to both technical and biological complications in dental implant treatment36. Ionfrida et al. demonstrated a significant association between bruxism and dental implant failure, reporting an increased risk of implant loss in patients with bruxism compared to non-bruxers, with a pooled odds ratio of 4.6837. Häggman-Henrikson et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis examining implant failure and marginal bone loss (MBL) in patients with probable bruxism compared to non-bruxers, finding that implants in probable bruxers had a significantly higher risk of failure (OR 2.19), while follow-up time did not influence this risk38. In our study, the bruxism evaluation was performed on patients who had been scheduled for implant placement, and the implants were subsequently placed following this assessment. These individuals are planned to be followed longitudinally, allowing for future investigation of both short- and long-term implant outcomes. All patients with a bruxism diagnosis were advised regarding the potential risks for implant rehabilitation and provided with preventive measures, such as cognitive behavioral assessment, counseling and night guard recommendations. This follow-up will enable a better understanding of the potential relationship between implant outcomes and the presence and awareness of bruxism, supported by clinical data. Previous studies have highlighted the multifactorial etiology of bruxism and the importance of assessing both subjective symptoms and objective clinical signs. De Holanda et al.39 reported that self-reported sleep bruxism in puerperal women was associated with factors such as higher education levels, medication use during pregnancy, smoking history, severe anxiety, and morning headaches. Similarly, Şahin40 found that symptoms including clenching or grinding during sleep, tooth wear, muscle fatigue, temple or jaw pain, and joint discomfort were significantly more prevalent in bruxism patients. Bahammam et al.41 observed a weak association between jaw pain and bruxism, but a strong correlation between bruxism and jaw lock. In this study, 26% of participants reported bruxism symptoms, with awake bruxism being the most prevalent form (16.7%). Sleep bruxism, typically identified through external observation, was reported in 13.3% of participants. Additionally, 12% exhibited jaw pushing or supporting behaviors, suggesting masticatory muscle fatigue or discomfort. Objective clinical findings in our sample included tooth wear (32.0%), linea alba (27.3%), and muscle hypertrophy (26.0%). These findings, consistent with previous literature, demonstrate that combining subjective self-reports with objective clinical signs provides a more accurate identification and characterization of bruxism. This approach is particularly important for populations at risk, such as patients seeking dental implant therapy, where early recognition of bruxism may help prevent prosthetic complications and improve overall patient care.

An important finding of the present study was the discrepancy between clinically based bruxism prevalence (31.3%) and self-reported bruxism prevalence (26%). This difference suggests that a proportion of patients may exhibit objective clinical signs of bruxism without being fully aware of their parafunctional habits. Such underrecognition may be particularly relevant in implant candidates, as unrecognized bruxism-related biomechanical overload may contribute to technical and biological complications following implant therapy. These findings further support the importance of combining self-report instruments with clinical examination during pre-implant assessment.

In this study, the PSS-14 was selected because it is one of the most widely used instruments for evaluating perceived stress in clinical and behavioral research and has been frequently applied in studies investigating psychosocial contributors to bruxism30. Using PSS-14 allowed us to quantify general stress perception to explore its potential association with bruxism presence and subtype. Chanteux et al. reported that dental students with higher perceived stress and anxiety levels had a significantly increased risk of bruxism, emphasizing the importance of combining self-assessment questionnaires with clinical evaluation for accurate diagnosis42. Similarly, Saczuk et al. found that higher perceived stress levels measured by the PSS-10 were positively associated with an increased Bruxism Index in adults diagnosed with sleep bruxism, further supporting the potential link between stress and bruxism43. In contrast, our results indicate that patients’ perceived stress levels did not differ significantly based on clinical bruxism diagnosis or self-reported bruxism type. This non-significant finding may be attributed to a ‘ceiling effect’ caused by the general anticipatory anxiety related to the upcoming implant surgery, which likely elevated stress levels uniformly across all participants and masked the differences between the groups. Furthermore, while the PSS-14 evaluates stress over the past month, bruxism is often associated with more chronic, long-term stress patterns that this specific time-frame may fail to fully capture. These findings highlight the complex and multifactorial nature of bruxism, suggesting that perceived stress alone may not be a reliable predictor and should be considered alongside other psychological and physiological factors in diagnosis and management.

A review of the existing literature on bruxism awareness reveals a wide range of findings, highlighting the inconsistency and complexity surrounding patients’ recognition and understanding of the condition. Uma et al. reported that although bruxism awareness significantly increased with academic progression among dental students, the overall awareness level remained only moderate, indicating a persistent knowledge gap even within this group44. Similarly, in a study by Şahin et al.40, 33% of dental students were identified as having probable bruxism, with 68.8% of them self-reporting bruxism, while 28.1% of students without probable bruxism also believed they had the condition. In our study, patients’ educational status did not have a statistically significant impact on most aspects of bruxism-related awareness, knowledge, or perception. However, individuals with high school and undergraduate education levels demonstrated greater awareness that bruxism can lead to tooth loss. In contrast, those with only primary education showed more uncertainty regarding this issue. Interestingly, participants with postgraduate education exhibited the lowest level of agreement with this statement. This finding may reflect either a distinct knowledge base within this group or a random variation due to the small sample size (n = 11).

From a clinical perspective, the findings of this study highlight the importance of routine bruxism screening prior to implant therapy. Because many patients may not fully recognize or report their parafunctional habits, reliance solely on self-reported information may underestimate the true prevalence of bruxism-related risk. Therefore, combining patient questionnaires with clinical examination may improve risk identification before implant placement. Early identification of bruxism may allow implementation of preventive strategies such as patient education, behavioral counseling, occlusal splint therapy, modification of prosthetic design, and closer maintenance follow-up protocols to reduce potential biomechanical complications associated with implant treatment.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the diagnosis of bruxism relied on clinical examination and patient self-report rather than instrumental assessments such as electromyography (EMG) or polysomnography, which are considered the gold standard for a definitive diagnosis, particularly for sleep bruxism2. Second, as an observational study, it was not possible to determine causality or fully account for the timing of exposure and the onset of disease. Third, the study was conducted at a single center with participants exclusively seeking dental implant treatment, potentially limiting the external validity of the results. Fourth, the retrospective registration of the study may raise concerns regarding potential bias, although the protocol was consistently applied. Furthermore, while the PSS-14 and awareness questionnaire provided valuable insights, the study did not investigate other underlying risk factors, such as detailed sleep quality or specific medication use. The study-specific awareness questionnaire underwent expert review and pilot testing; however, internal consistency, construct validity, and factor structure were not formally evaluated. Therefore, findings related to awareness should be interpreted cautiously. Additionally, because multiple statistical comparisons were performed across several items in the awareness questionnaire without a formal alpha correction, the risk of Type I error inflation should be acknowledged; therefore, these findings and related associations should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Moreover, the awareness questionnaire was administered immediately after the clinical examination, which might have acted as a ‘priming’ factor, potentially increasing the participants’ immediate recognition of symptoms. Regarding statistical power, the small cell sizes in the ‘severe’ TMD category and the limited number of participants in the bruxism subtype groups (sleep vs. awake) restricted the interpretability of some associations and increased the risk of Type II errors. Finally, implant site characteristics were not evaluated, which may have provided further clinical insight. Therefore, future studies with larger, more heterogeneous samples and multi-center designs are warranted to strengthen the reliability of these findings.

Conclusion

Nearly one-third of patients scheduled for dental implant therapy exhibited bruxism. However, their overall awareness of the condition remains limited, particularly concerning the association between bruxism and tooth loss. This knowledge gap was significantly more pronounced among patients with lower educational backgrounds. Given these findings, systematic bruxism screening and targeted patient education are essential components of the pre-implant assessment to manage potential biological and technical risks and improve long-term treatment outcomes.
