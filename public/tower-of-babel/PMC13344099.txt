Do Textured Breast Implants Mitigate Malposition? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Peter M Vonu, Syeda Hoorulain Ahmed, Cameron Gerhold, Shajie Ur Rehman Usmani, Bruce A Mast
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe widespread utilization of silicone implants for cosmetic augmentation and breast reconstruction has encouraged many innovative design characteristics since inception in 1962. Despite a multitude of studies, there exists limited collective evidence supporting textured implants’ efficacy in reducing implant malposition. In this study, the authors present a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess all available data regarding textured devices associated with outcomes of implant malposition. A literature search was performed covering PubMed/Medline, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. Cohort studies assessing textured vs nontextured implants were included in the final review. Primary outcome data included rates of implant malposition, and secondary outcomes included capsular contracture, rippling, and infection. Nine studies met inclusion criteria, including 2011 patients undergoing breast reconstruction and 1229 undergoing cosmetic augmentation. Malposition occurred in 5.2% of breasts in the textured cohort and 8.9% in the smooth cohort; however, pooled analysis demonstrated no statistically significant difference between groups. No significant differences were observed for capsular contracture, seroma, or rippling. A higher rate of infection was observed in the textured cohort, although this finding was not consistent across all analyses. No cases of breast implant–associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma were reported. Review of existing literature failed to identify statistical superiority in textured implants for reducing malposition. Interpretation is limited by retrospective design and heterogeneity among included studies. The rise of silicone breast implant manufacturing in 1962 introduced widespread utilization in both aesthetic augmentation and breast reconstruction. Their profound impact encouraged technological exploration in response to documented shortcomings, which included capsular contracture, visible rippling, and implant malposition. This inspired the use of textured implant shells in the breast device arena, first described in 1972, featuring a polyurethane surface.1-3 The notion stemmed from data that a textured surface promoted optimum adherence to breast tissue, creating a more favorable capsule formation and organized collagen profile.1-3 Polyurethane was eventually abandoned because of concerns of shell delamination and carcinogenic metabolites. This welcomed the third-generation silicone implant using silicone polymer shells on both silicone- and saline-filled implants. This advance, however, was halted in the United States with the 1992 moratorium because of concern over silicone systemic absorption and its association with autoimmune disease.1-4 The moratorium was lifted in 2006 after long-term data demonstrated no causative correlation. However, the pervasive skepticism fueled fourth- and fifth-generation silicone implants with enhanced shell thickness and cohesivity profiles, which introduced textured, shaped, and round implants. These devices were widely celebrated in the 2010s with multiple studies yielding subjective aesthetic benefits and decreased rates of capsular contracture, reoperation, and malposition.1-5 Companies such as Mentor, Sientra, and Allergan manufactured these implants with varying degrees of texturing to encourage tissue ingrowth with a high coefficient of friction. Despite the perceived benefits, shortcomings of this technology included the development of late seroma, double bubble, and, most saliently, breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL).2,6 The latter, drawing much attention, prompted Allergan to voluntarily recall the salt-loss Biocell implants because of the correlation of salt-loss texturing with inflammation promoting lymphoma.2,6 Skepticism persisted in the United States regarding the use of any textured device; however, globally, their use continued to grow, with various microtextured devices touting low complication rates and high patient/surgeon satisfaction.7-9 The latest International Organization for Standardization (ISO) classification for mammary implants defines microtextured devices as having a surface texture profile of 10 to 50 μm, compared with macrotextured implants (>50 μm) and smooth implants (<10 μm).10 Although microtextured devices have been associated with a lower incidence of BIA-ALCL, cases have still been reported with their use.11

Despite the numerous studies that cite benefits of textured devices, no clear consensus exists regarding their superiority over smooth implants in regard to implant malposition specifically. This process refers to implant migration along the chest wall and any subsequent change postoperatively in scar orientation or nipple–areolar complex (NAC) position overlying an implant. Many studies have investigated this outcome head-to-head between implant types; however, results remain inconsistent. Moreover, there exists a paucity of data in both cosmetic augmentation and breast reconstruction. This study serves to analyze implant malposition across all available studies through meta-analysis to better characterize the proposed enhancement of implant pocket control in textured devices.

METHODS

Search Strategy

This systematic review was conducted following the guidelines provided by Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA), Cochrane Collaboration, and as per the AMSTAR 2 checklist.12,13 The study protocol has been registered in PROSPERO, designated CRD42024572809. Three independent reviewers (S.H.A., C.G., and S.U.R.U.) performed an extensive literature search covering articles from January 2000 to May 2024, covering PubMed/Medline, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. The search utilized Medical Subject Headings terms for PubMed and other keywords, including mammaplasty, breast reconstruction, breast enlargement, augmentation, textured implant, smooth implant, anatomical implant, and round implant.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

All articles identified were uploaded to Covidence (Melbourne, Australia), where duplicates were identified and removed. Titles and abstracts were initially screened, followed by a full-text review to select studies that met the inclusion criteria. Included studies were comparative ones reporting the incidence of malposition in female patients undergoing breast implant surgery, either for primary augmentation or for reconstruction postmastectomy for breast cancer, using textured and smooth implants. Excluded were studies not evaluating malposition after implantation, single-arm studies without a comparator cohort, studies with fewer than 5 patients, non-English articles, review articles (scoping, systematic, and narrative reviews), post hoc analysis of clinical trials, animal or cadaver studies, and studies without quantitative data like case reports, editorial letters, and technical notes.

Data Extraction

Three reviewers carried out data extraction (S.H.A., C.G., and S.U.R.U.). Baseline study characteristics were extracted and recorded. These consist of the publication date, country, study design, study center, duration, number of patients and breasts, age, BMI, comorbidities, type of surgery (reconstruction or augmentation), implant volume, and follow-up duration.

The incidence of malposition was regarded as the primary outcome of this systematic review. Secondary outcomes included incidence of capsular contracture, seroma, infection, and implant rippling. All included studies were assessed for risk of bias (ROB) using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale.14

Statistical Analysis and Data Synthesis

Dichotomous data were extracted as proportions, and continuous data were extracted as means with corresponding standard deviations (SDs). Median values and their interquartile ranges were converted to mean and SD using methods described by Luo et al.15 Quantitative data analysis was carried out using Review Manager software (RevMan) 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020). A random-effects model was applied to pool all the dichotomous outcomes to derive risk ratios (RRs) and their corresponding 95% CIs. Data heterogeneity was assessed with the Higgins I2 statistic: 25% to 50% indicated mild heterogeneity, 50% to 75% moderate, and over 75% severe heterogeneity. A P-value of ≤.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Study Characteristics

A comprehensive literature review on the specified databases yielded 1781 potentially relevant studies. After a thorough screening process based on the above exclusionary criteria, this systematic review included 9 studies with a total of 3240 female patients.9,16-23 Out of the total number of patients included, 2011/3240 (62.1%) underwent breast reconstruction, whereas 1229/3240 (37.9%) underwent a breast augmentation procedure. Because of surgeries performed unilaterally or bilaterally, a total of 5112 breasts were evaluated. Outcome statistics were typically reported per breast in reconstructive cohorts and per patient in augmentation cohorts, although 1 augmentation study reported outcomes using both per-breast and per-patient denominators.19 Figure 1 presents the PRISMA flow chart detailing the screening process.

The included studies identified 5112 total breast implants. Of these, 3139 implants (1745 patients) were smooth and 1973 implants (1120 patients) were textured. The textured cohort's weighted mean age was 42.0 ± 6.5 years and BMI of 23.1 ± 1.7 kg/m2. For the smooth implant cohort, the weighted mean age was 43.2 ± 6.7 years and BMI was 23.7 ± 2.4 kg/m2, respectively. The average volumes of textured and smooth implants were 363.6 ± 108.5 and 454.6 ± 121.8 cc, respectively. Patients were followed for a mean duration of 21.8 ± 6.4 months in the textured cohort and 16.3 ± 3.4 months in the smooth cohort.

Outcomes

Incidence of Malposition

Malposition was reported in 15/844 (1.8%) patients and 28/531 (5.2%) breasts in the textured cohort and 28/1504 (1.9%) patients and 63/706 (8.9%) breasts in the smooth cohort. A meta-analysis of 5 studies reporting the incidence of malposition in patients revealed no significant difference between the textured and smooth cohorts (RR: 0.97, 95% CI, 0.52-1.80; I2 = 0%, P = .92; Figure 2A). Similarly, no significant difference was seen in the comparison of breasts (RR: 0.46, 95% CI, 0.08-2.56; I2 = 86%, P = .38; Figure 2B). This statistic was also exclusive to breast reconstruction patients only. Dorfman et al reported malposition as a deviation of the nipple from the point of maximal projection.16 A significant difference favoring textured implants was observed with a deviation of 3.7 ± 6.2°, as opposed to 7.5 ± 10.5° for smooth implants (P < .001).16

Forest plot outlining incidence of malposition: (A) patients and (B) breasts.

Incidence of Capsular Contracture

A total of 16/868 (1.8%) patients and 68/778 (8.7%) breasts experienced capsular contracture after receiving a textured implant, whereas 26/1539 (1.7%) patients and 70/1116 (6.2%) breasts experienced capsular contracture after receiving a smooth implant. A meta-analysis of 6 studies reporting the number of patients with capsular contracture revealed no significant difference between textured and smooth groups (RR: 1.02, 95% CI, 0.48-2.1; I2 = 24%, P = .97; Figure 3A). In addition, there was no significant difference observed in terms of the number of breasts (RR: 1.02, 95% CI, 0.48-2.18; I2 = 76%, P = .96; Figure 3B).

Forest plot outlining incidence of capsular contracture: (A) patients and (B) breasts.

Incidence of Seroma

Out of 684 patients who received textured implants, 5 (0.7%) developed seroma, and out of 312 breasts, 8 (2.5%) experienced this complication. In comparison, among 1153 patients with smooth implants, 9 (0.7%) developed seroma, with 8 out of 265 breasts (3%) being affected. A meta-analysis of 4 studies examining the incidence of seroma found no significant difference between patients with textured and smooth implants, with a risk ratio (RR) of 0.92 (95% CI, 0.26-3.24; I2 = 0%, P = .90; Figure 4A). Similarly, there was no significant difference in the number of affected breasts, with an RR of 0.78 (95% CI, 0.29-2.08; I2 = 0%, P = .62; Figure 4B).

Forest plot outlining incidence of seroma: (A) patients and (B) breasts.

Incidence of Infection

The incidence of infection was documented in 33/783 (4.2%) patients and 11/510 (2.1%) breasts in the textured group and 21/1358 (1.5%) patients with 2/675 (0.3%) breasts in the smooth implant cohort. A meta-analysis of 5 studies on patients with infection revealed a significant difference favoring lower risk of infection in patients receiving smooth implants (RR: 2.90, 95% CI, 1.63-5.17; I2 = 0%, P < .001; Figure 5A). However, no significant difference was observed between the 2 cohorts in the number of infected breasts (RR: 4.16, 95% CI, 1.0-17; I2 = 0%, P = .05; Figure 5B).

Forest plot outlining incidence of infection: (A) patients and (B) breasts.

Incidence of Breast Rippling

Although there were no reported the incidence of rippling in patients with textured breast implants, studies that assessed outcomes in individual breasts reported 10/312 (7%) rippling. On the other hand, 2/386 (0.5%) patients and 41/265 (15.5%) breasts experienced rippling in smooth implants. A meta-analysis of 2 studies reporting the number of patients with implant rippling revealed no significant difference between textured and smooth groups (RR: 0.70, 95% CI, 0.07-6.65; I2 = 0%, P = .75; Figure 6A). There was also no significant difference observed in terms of number of breasts (RR: 0.18, 95% CI, 0.00-70.33; I2 = 94%, P = .57; Figure 6B).

DISCUSSION

The notion that textured implant shells mitigate implant malposition is based on the demonstrated integration of tissue along the implant surface.18 The larger surface area achieved with a textured implant promotes a higher coefficient of friction, thus creating a greater degree of contact within the capsule with the potential for securing implant position.24-26 Another posited benefit of textured devices is the ability to manufacture anatomically shaped implants to enhance the upper pole and breast projection. Although theoretically sound, studies have failed to identify any aesthetic superiority in blinded studies among cosmetic patients.24-26 Some data exist to suggest shaped, textured implant superiority, specifically in patients who underwent breast reconstruction following mastectomy; however, no clear consensus exists.8,17,22

Our review of the existing literature failed to identify substantial data supporting malposition reduction in textured implants across pooled studies in cosmetic and reconstructive investigations. These findings suggest that any potential benefit of surface texturing in preventing malposition may be limited or context dependent. Importantly, these results do not exclude a potential role for textured implants in specific clinical scenarios that were not adequately captured in the included studies. The development of implant malposition is likely multifactorial. Although factors such as implant selection, pocket dissection, anatomical plane, and the use of ADM or mesh are widely recognized contributors, the relative contribution of these variables could not be assessed in the present analysis. Observed differences in implant volume between cohorts further underscore the complexity of isolating the independent effect of implant surface characteristics.

This systematic review and meta-analysis also failed to identify any conferred benefit of textured devices in reducing capsular contracture, implant rippling, or seroma formation. Proposed mechanisms for reduced capsular contracture with textured devices—including disruption of fibroblast alignment and altered capsular remodeling—remain biologically plausible but were not supported by pooled clinical outcomes in this study.23,27-30 One explanation for why capsular contracture persists in textured devices is the unequivocal predisposition to bacterial adherence—a known risk factor for capsular contracture.31,32 Multiple studies have found significant biofilm burden adhesion to textured devices favoring textured implants.33,34 The analysis of infection demonstrated a statistically significant increase at the patient level; however, this finding was not reproduced in the breast-level analysis. Given the retrospective nature of the included studies, heterogeneity in outcome reporting, and differences in unit of analysis, this result should be interpreted with caution and may reflect underlying confounding rather than a direct causal relationship.

An important consideration not fully captured in this analysis is the role of implant surface in anatomically shaped devices. Texturing has historically been used to reduce implant rotation, which may be considered a form of malposition in shaped implants. In the absence of surface texturing, shaped implants are likely more susceptible to rotation, potentially limiting their clinical utility. Because implant shape was not consistently reported or stratified in the included studies, this analysis may not adequately reflect scenarios in which shaped–textured implants provide a functional advantage.

These findings should also be considered in the context of known safety concerns associated with textured implants, such as BIA-ALCL. Although microtextured implants have not demonstrated the same magnitude of epidemiologic risk observed with macrotextured surfaces, reported cases exist and the safety of their continued use remains debated.11 In contrast, smooth implants have not been shown to independently confer risk for BIA-ALCL in the absence of previous textured exposure and have demonstrated acceptable rates of capsular contracture in large prospective and registry-based studies.23,35,36

Several important confounding variables were not controlled in this analysis and may significantly influence the observed outcomes. These include heterogeneity in patient population, surgical indication, implant plane, implant shape (round vs anatomic), degree of surface texturing, manufacturer-specific characteristics, and variability in follow-up duration (5 of the 9 studies reported follow-up time). Additionally, outcome definitions—particularly for malposition—were not standardized, and reporting at the breast vs patient level further limits comparability. The retrospective design of the included studies introduces additional risk of review bias and unmeasured confounding. As such, the present analysis is unable to account for these variables, and their impact on the observed outcomes cannot be determined.

Furthermore, the included studies varied in methodology and outcome assessment. For example, quantitative definitions of malposition, such as those based on NAC deviation in Dorfman et al, may not be directly comparable to more subjective or clinically based assessments. We know that implant malposition occurs along a temporal spectrum; hence, it is plausible that all instances of malposition were not captured during the reviewed studies’ follow-up protocols. It is also assumed that subclinical malposition was not detected but conceivably addressed in future revision procedures such as secondary capsulotomy or fat grafting.16,17,21

Finally, this analysis does not fully address newer implant surface technologies, including devices marketed as microtextured or with low-range surface roughness (5-10 µm) that have been marketed commercially as “nano” or “micro-smooth,” despite falling within the ISO-defined smooth category (<10 µm). Limited representation of these devices (n = 2) in the included studies precluded meaningful subgroup analysis.9,18 Future investigations incorporating standardized outcome measures and prospective study design will be necessary to better define the role of implant surface characteristics in clinical outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

Based on currently available comparative studies, textured implants do not demonstrate a clear pooled advantage over smooth implants in reducing implant malposition. No consistent benefit was observed for capsular contracture, rippling, or seroma formation. Interpretation of these findings is limited by retrospective study design, heterogeneity in outcome definitions, mixing of augmentation and reconstruction cohorts, variation in implant characteristics and surgical technique, and inconsistent outcome denominators. Although a patient-level analysis suggested increased infection risk, this finding was not consistent across all analyses and should be interpreted with caution. Further prospective, standardized studies are needed to better define the role of implant surface characteristics in clinical outcomes.
