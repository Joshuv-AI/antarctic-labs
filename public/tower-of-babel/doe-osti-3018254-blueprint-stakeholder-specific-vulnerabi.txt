1 Introduction
Vulnerability management is a process of discovering, analyzing, and handling new or reported
security vulnerabilities in systems to prevent the systems from being exploited, to reduce risk,
and to protect assets. For vulnerability analysis, handling, and response, the prioritization of
organizational and analyst resources must precede. The Common Vulnerability Scoring System
(CVSS) is a standard prioritization method that was developed by the Forum of Incident
Response and Security Teams (n.d.) and is used to rate the severity of security vulnerabilities in
systems by assigning numerical severity scores, but it does not provide clear guidelines of how
the numerical severity scores might inform decisions.

The Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorization (SSVC) provides a method for
prioritizing vulnerabilities based on the needs of the stakeholders involved in the vulnerability
management process. Instead of the numerical scoring used in the CVSS, the SSVC focuses on
contextual decision-making to determine how quickly and effectively an organization should
respond to vulnerabilities. The SSVC was developed by Computer Emergency Response
Team/Coordination Center (CERT/CC) at the Software Engineering Institute (SEI), Carnegie
Mellon University (n.d.).

The main functionality of the SSVC accommodates the diversity of the stakeholders in the
vulnerability management process, including finders, vendors, coordinators, deployers, and
others. So, the SSVC should be designed to be used by any of these stakeholders, and it should
be customizable to enable specific stakeholder decision models and risk appetites.

The main concepts of the SSVC decision models are like decision trees. They include the
following:

 • Decision points are variables representing different aspects of the decision.
 • Decision values are a set of enumerated values for decision points that can be sorted, for
 example, by priority or urgency.
 • Outcomes are variables representing different possible results of the decisions.
 • Policies are mappings from each combination of decision point values to the set of
 outcome values.
 • Decision functions are functions that accept a set of decision point values and return
 outcome values based on policies.

2 Stakeholder Roles
The SSVC takes the form of decision trees, instead of numerical scores, to determine priority
levels for different vulnerability management communities.

The SSVC methodology categorizes stakeholders into three independent roles, with each role
using their own model or decision tree: the supplier, the deployer, and the coordinator. A
stakeholder’s role depends on whether they are supplying a software product, deploying a
software product, or coordinating vulnerability communications and disclosures.

Depending on the structure of an organization, one team or many teams could fill the role of a
supplier, a deployer, and/or a coordinator. The role of a team depends on which task or tasks they
are performing for each software product. A team using an external library in the software
product they are developing would be a deployer for the external library and a supplier for the
software they are developing. In a similar case, an information technology team could be
responsible for deploying software to their organization and act as a coordinator for a
vulnerability found in the software. The roles of the stakeholder are also determined by how the
products are built. If some parts are produced by other suppliers, the role of the stakeholder will
be as a coordinator.

For SSVC adoption, the electric vehicle supply equipment (EVSE) industry needs to customize
risk appetites as well as vulnerability decision guidance in accordance with aspects such as their
organization, the roles of their teams, and relevant policies. EVSE manufacturers have roles as a
supplier, a deployer, and a coordinator. The roles and responsibilities can vary across
organizations, and they are not only for the security team or technical staff.

The first step in adopting the SSVC is customized decision modeling for ingested vulnerabilities.
This tool provides a list of decisions that the EVSE industry can use as a first step in conducting
a coordinated vulnerability disclosure. Additionally, these models are flexible so that an industry
member can define and contribute their own stakeholder-specific decision model, if necessary.

The next step in vulnerability categorization is to define the outcomes for that decision defined in
the decision model. Once it is determined what decision needs to be made and what the possible
outcomes are, next is to define the information needed to make that decision. A decision usually
requires more than one piece of information, which is organized into decision points.

Each decision point requires an assigned value, which requires the definition of a data source.
This is called data mapping, wherein definitions indicate which data sources are relevant to each
decision point and how the data are interpreted from each data source to assign a value to the
decision point.

A policy is a function that takes a set of decision point values as the input and returns an
outcome as the output. The policy used to make a decision needs to be defined after conducting
the decision modeling, defining possible outcomes for that decision, and defining information
that is needed to make that decision. This tool provides a list of policies to be used by the EVSE
industry; however, a policy is specific to a stakeholder’s security posture and needs to be defined
per their own practices.

 2

The final step is to establish a governance process for the decision model output. A simple
governance process might include regular reviews of the decision model to check whether the
decision model is right or if a new additional decision model needs to be created. This process
should ensure that the decision model remains relevant to the organization’s needs and that the
data used to make decisions are accurate and current.

The next section describes three stakeholder-specific models to aid the EVSE industry in
adopting an SSVC to fit their needs.

2.1 Supplier Model
The supplier is typically a stakeholder that is building a product and supplying patches to remedy
or mitigate an identified vulnerability. In the supplier’s case, the decision is how urgently and
within what time frame does the stakeholder need to supply a patch.

The supplier model decision tree shown in Figure 1 has four supplier decision points—
exploitation, utility, technical impacts, and public safety impact—to make a decision to create a
patch. In the exploitation decision point, a stakeholder needs to check the present state of the
exploitation of the vulnerability. If there is no evidence of active exploitation, the outcome will
be “none.” If a public proof of concept (POC) exists in sources or the vulnerability has a well-
known method of exploitation, the outcome will be “POC.” If there is credible public reporting
of the exploitation, the outcome will be “active.”

After the exploitation phase, a stakeholder enters the “utility” decision point, where the
stakeholder needs to evaluate how useful the exploitation is to the attacker. The outcomes of this
decision point are determined by whether an attacker’s ability to automate the exploitation and
resources in the system is limited or not. Then the stakeholder enters the next decision points,
technical impact and public safety impact. These outcomes are determined by how the

exploitation impacts either technical or public safety aspects. More detailed descriptions of how
each outcome is determined at each decision point are presented in Section 3.

2.2 Deployer Model
The role of the deployer is to implement the supplied patch, remedy, or mitigation in their
product. When a patch is available, often the decision to be made is what time frame the patch
needs to be deployed. When a patch is not available, the decision might be to either shut down
services or accept the risk of not mitigating the vulnerability.

A deployer decision model has four decision points that are used to determine the priority of
patch deployment: exploitation, system exposure, utility, and human impact. The exploitation
and utility decision points are similar to those in the supplier model. At the system exposure
decision point, the outcome is determined by the accessible attack surface of the affected system
(or service). If the attack surface is a local service or a highly controlled network, the outcome of
the system exposure will be “small.” The outcome will be “open” if the attack surface is the
internet or another widely accessible network. The outcome of a “human impact” is determined
by a combination of safety and mission impacts.

The deployer model decision tree shown in Figure 2 has four supplier decision points—
exploitation, exposure, automatable, and human impact—to make the decision to deploy a patch.
In the exploitation decision point, a stakeholder needs to check the present state of exploitation
of the vulnerability. If there is no evidence of active exploitation, the outcome will be “none.” If
a public POC exists in sources or the vulnerability has a well-known method of exploitation, the
outcome will be “POC.” If there is credible public reporting of the exploitation, the outcome will
be “active.”

 Figure 2. Deployer model decision tree
After the exploitation phase, a stakeholder enters the “exposure” decision point. The stakeholder
evaluates the exposure of a program or service affected by the vulnerability. If the vulnerability
affects only a local program or service, the outcome will be “small.” If the vulnerability affects a
networked service with some access restrictions or mitigations in place, the outcome will be
“controlled.” If the vulnerability affects a service with a widely accessible network where access
cannot plausibly be restricted, the outcome will be “open.”

 6

The next decision point the stakeholder evaluates is the automation decision point. If all the steps
of the kill chain can be automated, the outcome will be “yes”; otherwise, the outcome is “no.”
The stakeholder then looks at the human impact of the vulnerability. This decision point is
compared to the situated safety impact and the mission impact. The situated safety impact
accesses the potential harm the vulnerability could cause—for example, this could be, but is not
limited to, physical harm, environmental harm, or financial harm. The mission impact examines
the impact the vulnerability could have on the mission’s functions, including the ability to
recover from the impact. More detailed descriptions of how each outcome is determined at each
decision point are presented in Section 3.

2.3 Coordinator Models
The role of coordinator is to manage the vulnerability disclosure process and to coordinate
communications among other stakeholders, such as the supplier or finder. Coordinators must
make two decisions about the vulnerability information: The first is whether to coordinate the
vulnerability, and the second is whether to publish information about a vulnerability.
Coordinators use a triage model for the first decision and a publish model for the second
decision.

2.3.1 Coordinator Triage Model
First, the coordinator uses the triage model. The decision outcome should be calculated when any
stakeholder (supplier, finder, or coordinator) first receives a report of a vulnerability and collects
the necessary information for each decision point. This is to decide whether the stakeholder
needs to act in coordinating the vulnerability patch or mitigation.

Then, after the triage model has been scored, the coordinator uses the publish model. This model
is used to decide whether a stakeholder (supplier, finder, or coordinator) should publish a report

3 Decision Points and Outcomes
This section describes which decision points are needed to use each model. Each model uses its
own collection of decision points and outcome values to determine the information that each role
should have access to and to determine the outcomes that each role has the option of taking.

Decision points can be categorized as either simple or complex. Simple decision points have
only one decision to make, whereas complex decision points comprise two or more sub-decisions
that are compounded to output an overall decision point value.

3.1 Supplier Model
Suppliers receive vulnerability reports affecting one or more versions of their product. As part of
the initial intake process, the supplier must determine which specific products and/or versions are
impacted by the reported vulnerability, and the supplier needs to address the products
individually because they could have multiple development processes. The decisions to provide
mitigations for the affected products are based on the following decision points: exploitation,
utility, technical impact, and public safety impact. In the exploitation decision point, a
vulnerability with a known exploitation is more likely to be given a higher priority. The value
has the present state of exploitation of the vulnerability. In the utility decision point, the priority
is dependent on how useful a vulnerability is to an attacker. The value represents the usefulness
of the exploit to the adversary. Exploitation is about how easy it would be to start such an attack,
and utility is about how much an adversary might benefit from an attack using the vulnerability
in question. In the technical impact (or public safety impact) decision point, the more severe the
technical impact (or public safety impact) of a vulnerability, the more likely it is to be given a
higher priority in the technical impact (or public safety impact) decision point.

3.2 Deployer Model
Deployers usually receive remediations or mitigations from their suppliers for products they have
deployed. After they receive remediations or mitigations, deployers must then decide whether to
deploy the remediation or mitigation to a particular instance. The decision should be made based
on the level of priority to deploy a given remediation or mitigation to their product. For example,
if an EVSE manufacturer develops and uses its own firmware, their development team might be
responsible for both the supplier and deployer roles. If an EVSE manufacturer uses some open-
source libraries for their firmware and the exploitation is on one of the open-source libraries,
their development team will have a role as the deployer only. Similar to the supplier case, there
are four categories of priority in decision outcomes: defer, scheduled, out of cycle, and
immediate. Although the priority names are the same as the supplier decision outcomes, the
meaning of the priority could have different implications for the deployer than for the supplier.

3.5 Adjusting for Risk Tolerance and Exporting Score
The values of decision outcomes and the structure of the decision tree model can be changed
depending on the risk appetite of the organization. These trees were developed using a moderate
risk tolerance. The existing decision points and values, however, are not recommended to be
changed because this will affect the ability to share knowledge of vulnerability decisions outside
the organization. Stakeholders such as the EVSE industry can decide to change the structure of
any of the models by removing the existing or adding new custom decision points that are
specific to their organization. These new decision points must be clearly defined; they should not
share the same name as existing decision points; and they should be ordinal, meaning they can be
ordered but not numeric. A stakeholder might also decide to internally change existing decision
point value definitions to something clearer to their team. When making changes to the decision
tree, is it recommend that the decision tree JSON is changed, allowing the stakeholder to import

4 Recommendations
This section describes the approach for the EVSE industry to adopt the SSVC with customized
risk appetites and customized decision models based on the existing models within the SSVC.

Data collection for each decision point will be stakeholder specific, which means that
stakeholders could use different avenues of collecting data for each decision point. When
information on a decision point is not available, the stakeholder might want to define default
values that are appropriate to their risk appetite; we suggested default values for some decision
points. Collecting information for all the decision points can be a bigger lift than some
stakeholders want to implement at one time. These stakeholders could implement a “ramping-
up” approach in their data collection and models. The model can be downsized to a bare-bones
version, keeping the earlier decision points and adding decision points later as the stakeholder
collects more data. This approach would involve the stakeholder creating their own bare-bones
model using the supplied models as a starting outline. They would need to decide their own
decision outcome values for each decision path, but the supplied models assist in this.

Many stakeholders will deal with vulnerabilities that have the same decision outputs at the same
time. In the SSVC, there is no guidance on which vulnerability should take priority; it is up to the
stakeholder to decide the order in which these vulnerabilities should be managed, but it is
recommended that the vulnerabilities are resolved in chronological order.

Before implementing the SSVC, it is recommended that each stakeholder review each decision
path to decide whether the given decision point value for the decision outcome value is
appropriate for the stakeholder’s risk appetite. If a stakeholder wants to decrease their risk, they
will want to increase the decision outcome value—for example, while using the supplier model,
a stakeholder can increase a decision path’s outcome value from “defer” to “scheduled.” Once
the SSVC is implemented, the SSVC should be regularly evaluated and maintained because the
available information and vulnerability management decisions could change over time.

Implementing the SSVC provides a method for stakeholders to prioritize vulnerabilities based on
real-world impacts and outcomes while connecting cybersecurity actions to organizational goals
by considering mission disruptions and public impacts. It improves consistency throughout the
industry by using structured models for all stakeholders while allowing any stakeholder to
customize the models based on their needs and risk appetite. The ability to share and store
decision outcomes boosts coordination among stakeholders by increasing the transparency of
other stakeholders’ decisions on patching vulnerabilities.
