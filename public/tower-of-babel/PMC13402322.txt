Photoinduced H-Bonding EDA complex-enabled skeletal editing of furans to pyridazines
Authors: Juan Tang, Pingyao Gan, Wenjing Li, Lin Peng, Guiyu Yang, Yuying He, Shikun Zhou, Ting Long, Chong Tian, Haiyan Fu, Shun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skeletal editing enables direct structural reorganization of molecular frameworks, providing a powerful strategy for rapid exploration of chemical space in lead optimization. Herein we report a mild, photoinduced skeletal editing strategy that converts furans into pharmacologically valuable pyridazines without external photocatalysts. Upon photoexcitation, the H-bonding electron donor–acceptor (EDA) complex formed between furan and hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP) enables activation of molecular oxygen, generating reactive oxygen species that promote oxidative ring opening of furan to a 1,4-dicarbonyl intermediate, which subsequently condenses with hydrazine hydrate to afford pyridazines. The method displays broad substrate scope, tolerates complex and drug-derived motifs, and can be implemented to continuous-flow processing with enhanced efficiency and scalability.

Introduction

In modern medicinal chemistry, lead optimization commonly relies on linear synthetic routes, where each structural modification requires a separate synthetic sequence, rendering the process time- and resource-intensive (Fig. 1A)1–3. Limited synthetic accessibility leaves many potentially valuable molecular frameworks underexplored, restricting chemical space exploration and hindering structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies. Skeletal editing has emerged as a powerful strategy to overcome this limitation by enabling direct atom transmutation or ring reconstruction within complex molecular frameworks4–39. Pyridazine is an attractive scaffold in pharmaceutical and agrochemical research owing to its distinctive polarity, H-bonding capacity, and broad bioactivity40–43. Despite these favorable properties, pyridazine motifs remain far less prevalent in approved drugs than pyridine- or pyrimidine-based frameworks (Fig. 1B), largely due to limited synthetic strategies and the absence of readily accessible precursors. In contrast to classical condensation approaches that rely on pre-functionalized building blocks44, skeletal editing provides a deconstructive–reconstructive pathway for direct heterocycle interconversion. This strategy enables the direct construction of pyridazines from readily available heterocyclic feedstocks. An illustrative example is the conversion of pyridines into pyridazines, as independently demonstrated by Levin45 and Hong46, using distinct pyridine derivatives as starting materials (Fig. 1C).

These advances underscore the power of skeletal editing to unlock underrepresented heterocycles from abundant feedstock heteroarenes. Furan, a readily available, electron-rich heterocycle prevalent in bioactive molecules and renewable resources46–49. The oxidative ring-opening of furans to generate 1,4-dicarbonyl intermediates has been widely reported under a variety of conditions, highlighting its intrinsic reactivity and potential as a versatile synthetic synthon50–55. Recent work has demonstrated the versatility of furan as a C₄ synthon across several skeletal editing modes, for example, Park and co-workers achieved a photocatalytic O-to-N atom exchange to convert furans into pyrroles56, while Sharma et al. demonstrated sulfenylcarbene-induced furan ring cleavage followed by nitrogen atom incorporation and expansion to pyridines (Fig. 1C)57. In addition, Ye and co-workers reported an enantioselective dearomative one-carbon ring expansion of benzofurans via chiral copper catalysis to install quaternary stereocenters (Fig. 1C)58. Despite these advances, direct skeletal editing of furans into pyridazines remains a significant challenge. Existing strategies typically rely on oxidative ring-opening of furans, followed by condensation to afford pyridazine derivatives59–63. However, these approaches generally require external photocatalysts or strong oxidants and involve multistep operations. Therefore, the development of a mild, efficient protocol based on a photocatalyst-free system with atmospheric O₂ as the green oxidant is highly desirable, which might greatly expand the accessible substrate range.

Photocatalytic skeletal editing has recently emerged as a powerful platform, enabling diverse molecular restructuring64–77, yet catalyst-free electron donor–acceptor (EDA)-complex-mediated examples remain scarce78. Herein, we achieve the direct conversion of furans to pyridazines through a photoinduced process driven by an in situ formed H-bonding EDA complex between furan and HFIP, without external photocatalyst (Fig. 1D). Upon photoexcitation, the EDA complex activates molecular oxygen to generate reactive oxygen species (¹O2 and O2•−), enabling furan ring opening to a 1,4-dicarbonyl intermediate that subsequently condenses with hydrazine hydrate to afford pyridazine derivatives. Notably, HFIP serves a dual role in this system, acting as both the H-bonding partner in EDA complex and the reaction solvent. This method establishes a mechanistically distinct route to pyridazines from furan-based starting materials and enables direct late-stage diversification of drug-like molecules. This photochemical protocol can be successfully adapted to flow chemistry systems for large-scale applications.

Results

Optimization studies

We initiated our investigation using 2-phenylfuran (1a) as a model substrate to evaluate the feasibility of the proposed furan to pyridazine skeletal editing transformation (Table 1). Systematic optimization revealed that the reaction proceeded most effectively under irradiation by a 390 nm LED at room temperature with five equivalents of hydrazine hydrate in HFIP as solvent, delivering 3-phenylpyridazine (2a) in 81% isolated yield after 32 h (entry 1). The reaction efficiency was highly dependent on the irradiation wavelength: optimal performance was observed at 390 nm (81% yield), while longer or shorter wavelengths led to diminished yields (entries 2–4). This trend highlights the critical role of wavelength in efficiently exciting the EDA complex and promoting the furan to pyridazine transformation. Solvent screening further highlighted the unique suitability of HFIP; replacing it with CH2Cl2 or CH3CN led to significantly diminished yields, while 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (TFE) gave only 24% yield (entries 5–7), likely due to less efficient formation of the EDA complex. Control experiments confirmed that both dioxygen and light are essential for the transformation, as no reaction occurred in their absence (entries 8 and 9). Notably, conducting the reaction under an oxygen atmosphere further improved the efficiency, affording the desired product in 85% yield within 24 h (entry 10). Substrates scope

With the optimized conditions established, we next investigated the substrate scope of this photoinduced furan to pyridazine transformation (Fig. 2). A wide variety of 2-arylfurans were compatible with the reaction, demonstrating excellent functional-group tolerance. Electron-donating (e.g., Me, OMe, tBu) and electron-withdrawing (e.g., F, CF₃, CN, NO₂, Acetyl) groups on the para-, meta- or ortho-position of the phenyl rings were well tolerated, affording the corresponding pyridazines in moderate to good yields (2b–2 l, 65%–87%). Furthermore, disubstituted and trisubstituted 2-arylfurans participated readily in the transformation, providing pyridazine derivatives with diverse substitution patterns in good yields (2m–2o, 71%–77%). Sterically encumbered substrates, such as 2-naphthylfuran, also gave 2p in 78% yields, indicating the reaction’s tolerance toward increased steric bulk. Heteroaryl-substituted furans were explored next. Electron-rich 2-thienylfurans, as well as electron-deficient pyridyl- and quinolyl-substituted furans, were successfully transformed into the corresponding pyridazine products in moderate yields (2q–2t, 51%–63%), demonstrating broad heterocycle compatibility. In addition to 2-substituted furans, we examined 3-arylfurans bearing substituents on the aryl ring. 3-Phenylfuran underwent the transformation smoothly to afford 4-phenyldiazine in 71% yield (2 u). Furthermore, electron-donating (e.g., OMe, tBu) and electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., Cl, CF₃, CN) at the para-, meta-, and ortho- positions of the phenyl ring were well tolerated, affording the corresponding pyridazine products in moderate to good yields (2v-2z, 2aa–2ad, 50%–71%). Notably, 3-pyridyl-substituted furan also participated in the transformation smoothly, delivering the desired pyridazine in 53% yield (2ae). Substrates bearing substitution at both the 3- and 4-positions of the furan ring, including 3,4-diarylfurans with varying electronic properties, underwent the transformation efficiently in good yields (2af–2ah, 60%–78%). Subsequently, alkyl-substituted furans were tested. Both 2-methylfuran and 2,5-dimethylfuran smoothly furnished the corresponding pyridazines in 43% and 45% yields, respectively (2ai, 2aj). The relatively lower efficiency observed for these substrates may arise from their diminished ability to form EDA complexes with HFIP. Notably, diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF), a highly electron-rich polycyclic furan, underwent smooth transformation under the standard conditions to afford the corresponding benzodiazine product in excellent yield (2ak, 95%). We further evaluated the applicability of this protocol to complex furan-containing molecules, including fragments derived from bioactive compounds and pharmaceutical agents. Substrates incorporating structural motifs from canagliflozin, JTK-853, prilafanamide, sesamol, estrone, and an empagliflozin analogue were all smoothly converted into the corresponding pyridazine derivatives (2am–2ar, 48%–71%). Furans bearing hydroxyl, aldehyde, carboxylic acid, boronic acid, or halogen substituents are not compatible under the standard conditions. Benzofuran substrates and furosemide afford only trace amounts of the desired products, and the starting material was recovered almost quantitatively after irradiation. In addition, we also examined a series of primary amines, including benzylamine, methylamine, cyclopropylamine, isopropylamine, and o-phenylenediamine, which were found to be compatible, affording the corresponding pyrrol-2-one products in moderate yields (4a-4e, 45%-65%). Notably, when 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran was employed as the substrate in combination with o-phenylenediamine, the corresponding diazocine product 4 f was obtained in 64% yield (see Supplementary Fig. 4). The successful incorporation of diverse nitrogen sources for N-heterocycle construction demonstrates that the developed EDA complex-mediated oxidative cleavage system provides a versatile platform for furan skeletal editing.

To further demonstrate the practicality and scalability of this protocol, scale-up experiments were conducted (Fig. 2). Under standard batch conditions, gram-scale reactions proceeded smoothly to afford the desired pyridazine in 68% isolated yield. Notably, the transformation could also be driven by natural sunlight, delivering pyridazine 2a in 70% conversion after 24 h, underscoring the mildness and operational simplicity of the photoinduced process. Moreover, the protocol was readily adapted to continuous-flow operation; at a flow rate of 0.2 mL·min⁻¹ (residence time = 4 h), the furan-to-pyridazine skeletal editing was scaled up by 50-fold to furnish the product in 87% isolated yield, highlighting the efficiency, robustness, and scalability of the process.

Mechanistic studies

We performed a series of mechanistic investigations to gain insights into the reaction mechanism. Radical trapping studies revealed that the addition of radical scavengers, including TEMPO, BHT and 1,1-diphenylethylene, significantly suppressed product formation, and key furan-derived radical adducts were detected by HRMS, indicating the involvement of radical species in the transformation (Fig. 3A). Notably, when 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF), a well-established 1O2 trap, was used as the substrate under irradiation but in the absence of hydrazine, rapid photooxidative ring opening occurred to afford the corresponding ring-opened product 3 in 98% yield. Subsequent addition of hydrazine smoothly produced the corresponding benzodiazine derivative 2ak, further corroborating the key role of photoinduced singlet oxygen (Fig. 3C). UV–vis absorption studies revealed the formation of a new absorption band when furan was dissolved in HFIP, consistent with the generation of an EDA complex, whereas no such interaction was observed for hydrazine, excluding its involvement in the photoactivation step (Fig. 3D). The intensity of this absorption band increased linearly with increasing furan concentration, supporting the assignment of a concentration-dependent EDA species (see the Supplementary Fig. 9). A kinetic profile revealed a rapid initial conversion followed by a gradual decrease in reaction rate, indicating that the reaction slows as the furan substrate is consumed (see Supplementary Fig. 10). Subsequent 1H NMR analysis showed progressive downfield shifts in the signals of 2-phenylfuran upon addition of HFIP, consistent with hydrogen-bonding interactions between the furan and HFIP (Fig. 3E). To further elucidate the reaction pathway, in situ 1H NMR spectroscopy was used to monitor the species formed during the transformation (Fig. 3F). Upon irradiation of 2-phenylfuran in HFIP (0.2 M), the reaction mixture gradually changed from orange to deep blue, accompanied by the appearance of signals attributable to a ring-opened 1,4-dicarbonyl intermediate 4. Subsequent addition of hydrazine hydrate led to rapid disappearance of the 1,4-dicarbonyl signals and formation of the pyridazine product, together with restoration of the original orange color, unambiguously identifying the 1,4-dicarbonyl species as a key on-pathway intermediate. Finally, the light-on/off experiments indicate that light is crucial for this transformation, ruling out the chain radical mechanism (Fig. 3G).

To obtain a comprehensive understanding of the interactions within the complex formed by furan and HFIP, DFT calculations were conducted (Fig. 4). Geometric optimization revealed a bond angle of 150.91° for the H-bonding EDA complex, which is well consistent with the geometric criteria of a weak-to-moderate H bond (>130°, <170°)79. Further analysis indicates that the H-bond interaction contributes an estimated interaction energy of −4.93 kcal·mol⁻¹ (ρBCP, bond critical point = 0.025462 au), while the overall binding energy of the H-bonded assembly reaches ΔEint ≈ −8.0 kcal·mol⁻¹, reflecting the cooperative contribution of H bonding and weak interaction. In contrast, the weakly associated complex exhibits a much smaller binding energy (ΔEint ≈ −1.8 kcal·mol⁻¹), highlighting the dominant role of H bonding in stabilizing the EDA complex (see the Supplementary Figs. 18, 19). Frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis reveals that, in the ground state, both the highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMO) of the EDA complex are primarily localized on the furan moiety, indicating that the interaction doesn’t strictly correspond to a classical donor–acceptor pair with fully separated orbitals. The narrower HOMO–LUMO energy gaps in excited state (6.09 eV vs. 7.12 eV) revealed that the electron transfer process occurred favorably under light irradiation (Fig. 4A). Noncovalent interaction (NCI) analysis further confirms that the EDA complex is cooperatively stabilized by a dominant H-bonding interaction, assisted by weaker interaction forces, as evidenced by the green Reduced Density Gradient (RDG) isosurfaces between furan and HFIP (Fig. 4B). Electrostatic potential (ESP) map surface analysis provides additional insight into charge redistribution within the complex. In the complex (mainly caused by weak interaction), electron density remains largely localized on the furan unit, whereas HFIP exhibits a relatively electron-deficient character. In contrast, formation of the H-bonding EDA complex induces pronounced polarization, with increased electron density on the HFIP fragment and a corresponding electron-deficient region on the furan moiety. This distinct ESP redistribution underscores the crucial role of H bonding in promoting charge polarization within the EDA complex, thereby facilitating its photoactivation (Fig. 4C). On the basis of the previous literatures71,74,81–85 and above mechanistic studies, we propose the reaction pathway (Fig. 4D). The electron-rich furan and HFIP first form an H-bonding EDA complex. Upon light irradiation, the EDA complex is excited and can undergo energy transfer to molecular oxygen, generating singlet oxygen (¹O2). The resulting ¹O2 engages the furan ring to form the peroxidic adduct 5, which subsequently fragments to deliver the 1,4-dicarbonyl compound 4 detected by HRMS. Condensation of 4 with hydrazine directly affords the pyridazine products. In parallel, a single-electron transfer (SET) pathway may also operate. Upon photoexcitation of the EDA complex, electron transfer to molecular oxygen becomes thermodynamically favorable (ΔG = –9.59 kcal·mol⁻¹, see Supplementary Fig. 24), generating a superoxide radical anion (O₂•−) together with a furan radical cation. Nucleophilic opening of the activated furan radical cation by O2•− leads to the same intermediate 4, which subsequently condenses with hydrazine to furnish the pyridazine framework.

In conclusion, we have developed a practical and efficient photoinduced skeletal editing strategy that converts readily available furans into valuable pyridazine scaffolds. Mechanistically, the photoexcited H-bonding EDA complex formed between furan and HFIP enables the activation of molecular oxygen, generating reactive oxygen species that promote oxidative ring opening of furan to a 1,4-dicarbonyl intermediate, which subsequently condenses with hydrazine to afford pyridazines. This method exhibits a broad substrate scope, tolerating complex furan motifs, and features operational simplicity and scalability. Notably, the seamless transfer of this protocol from small-scale batch photoreactions to continuous-flow processing highlights the robustness of the strategy and its potential for practical synthetic applications.

Methods

General procedure for the synthesis of furans

An oven-dried 100 mL Schlenk tube equipped with magnetic stirring bar was sequentially charged with aryl bromide (5.0 mmol), furan boronic acid (7.5 mmol,1.5 equiv.), Pd(PPh3)4 (5 mol %), Cs2CO3 (5.5 mmol, 1.1 equiv.), toluene/MeOH (40.0 mL /10.0 mL). The mixture was frozen with liquid nitrogen, and the atmosphere was replaced with N₂ by three freeze–pump–thaw cycles. Next, the mixture was stirred at 105 °C (oil bath heating) for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature, the reaction was diluted with DCM (Dichloromethane) and water, and then extracted with DCM (3×). The organic extract was dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated. The crude material was purified by silica gel column chromatography using ethyl acetate/n-hexane as eluent.

General procedure for the synthesis of pyridazines

An oven-dried 10 mL glass vial with a stir bar was charged sequentially with furan derivatives (0.2 mmol), hydrazine hydrate (1.0 mmol, 5.0 equiv.), and HFIP (1.0 mL, 0.2 M). Then, the reaction mixture was stirred and irradiated under air, with 390 nm LEDs (50 W) at room temperature for 24–32 h. After the reaction was finished, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by preparative thin layer chromatography (silica gel, n-hexane/EtOAc = 10:1 to 1:1) to afford the desired pyridazines.
