Deltoid Muscle Volume Correlates With Patient Outcome After Reverse Shoulder Arthroplasty
Authors: Elizabeth Knazek, Allison Cracchiolo, Chaoyang Chen, Antonio Bouffard, Stephen Lemos, Jason Holcomb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aims:Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) relies on a competent deltoid muscle to provide stability and function. This study aims to determine the importance of ultrasonically measured deltoid muscle volume on patient strength, function, and satisfaction after rTSA.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed 56 shoulders in 28 patients underwent rTSA with surveys of American Shoulder and Elbow Society questionnaire (ASES), simple shoulder test, and ultrasonography measurement of deltoid volume. Statistical analysis was done to determine the correlation between deltoid muscle volume and abduction strength, ASES score, Constant-Murley Score, Visual Analog Scale, range-of-motion testing, between surgical shoulder and nonsurgical repair groups, respectively.Results:A strong positive correlation was observed with deltoid muscle volume and abduction strength, and a moderate positive correlation between deltoid muscle volume and the Constant-Murley Score. No notable difference was observed between the mean deltoid volume for the surgical and nonsurgical sides. No correlation existed between deltoid volume and ASES. A decrease in deltoid muscle volume was observed with patients' age, but no trend regarding length of time since surgery. These findings indicated that deltoid muscle assessment in candidate selection and postoperative care are associated with improved postoperative outcomes.Conclusion:Deltoid volume is a robust predictor of postoperative abduction strength and has a moderate association with shoulder function including muscle strength and range of motion but does not correlate with pain or satisfaction. The results can inform clinicians to prioritize deltoid evaluation during patient selection and follow-up, guiding additional research on optimizing rehabilitation and implant design.Level of Evidence:IV.

The management of rotator cuff arthropathy was a challenge that led to the evolution of the reverse total shoulder prosthesis. Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) is a procedure initially designed for the rotator cuff-deficient arthritic shoulder; however, the indications for rTSA have expanded over the past 20 years. Initial designs aimed for reversal of the ball-and-socket configuration by medializing and distalizing of the center of rotation to optimize deltoid function by increasing the moment arm.1-3 More recent designs have lateralized the center of rotation with the aim of decreasing scapular notching. Overall, these designs have led to a notable improvement in American Shoulder and Elbow Society (ASES) and Visual Analog Scale (VAS).4,5

Although rTSA is effective for pain relief, it carries a markedly higher complication rate than anatomic TSA, often reported as high as 62% to 68%.6-10 A primary contributor to these complications is compromised deltoid function,11 including atrophy and fatty infiltration, which is common in chronic shoulder pathologies.12-14 Deltoid atrophy that is often accompanied with chronic shoulder pathologies can lead to compromised shoulder function.15 As deltoid integrity is crucial for successful rTSA,13 imaging biomarkers such as muscle cross-sectional area (CSA) and fat infiltration (FI) are increasingly used to assess prognosis and guide personalized treatment.16-18

MRI-based evaluations are considered important for risk mitigation and improving outcomes, underscoring that patient selection and deltoid optimization remain central to shoulder arthroplasty.19 MRI is rarely ordered by surgeons for deltoid volume, more often to evaluate the rotator cuff16,17,20 because MRI is an expensive tool. Ultrasonography is now considered as a cost-effective tool, yet efficiency is not fully understood. This study aims to determine whether there is a correlation between ultrasound-measured deltoid volume and patient function and satisfaction in postoperative rTSA patients.

A previous laboratory study was done by Audenaert et al21 to validate the ultrasonography efficiency in estimating deltoid volume using cadavers. This technique was also used to estimate volumes of triangular shaped muscles in contrast to the multiple regression equations that were developed for cylindrical muscles.22 Ultrasonography has the advantages of being a quick, inexpensive, well-tolerated test with visualization of muscles similar to that of CT or MRI without the cost.2,23-25 An ultrasonography technique was validated by comparison with cadavers which had deltoid volume estimated with a CT technique and confirmed by water displacement. Audenaert et al21 demonstrated that there was a notable relationship between deltoid volume and peak isokinetic abduction torque.

Our study used the ultrasonography technique described by Audenaert et al to calculate postoperative deltoid volumes in rTSA patients in an effort to compare function and pain outcomes with the nonsurgical side. This comparison included the collection of patient surveys: a Simple Shoulder Test, ASES questionnaire, a VAS for pain, and a Constant-Murley Score (CMS). The CMS included measuring range of motion (ROM) and strength to compare with measured deltoid volumes. Using objective ultrasonography data and correlation with patient satisfaction, outcome scores, ROM, and strength may predict patient outcomes and guide whether a patient is a good candidate for RTSA, help guide postoperative rehabilitation protocols, and have an effect on future implant design. We hypothesized that patients with greater deltoid volumes would show greater satisfaction, function, and CMSs.

Methods

Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval from the Henry Ford Health System (IRB #10189) was obtained before data collection or analysis. Retrospective data were collected from patients who underwent a reverse TSA (Reverse Shoulder Prosthesis, DJO Surgical) performed by a single surgeon at one institution. Patient inclusion criteria required a minimum follow-up of 1 year. Patients were called in for a single follow-up session at the time point of 12 months postoperatively, which included patient survey completion, a physical examination, and ultrasonography evaluation.

A total of 28 patients (56 shoulders) of preoperative diagnosis of glenohumeral osteoarthritis including 33 surgical shoulders and 23 healthy shoulders were evaluated. They were all primary rTSA without prior surgery. Five patients had a rTSA on both shoulders. Patients were guided through the informed consent process and were asked to complete the Simple Shoulder Test (SST) and ASES questionnaires. Patients who had bilateral procedures filled out two separate surveys.

Physical examinations were done with a focus on ROM and strength to calculate a CMS. ROM per CMS included forward flexion, elevation in the plane of the scapula, abduction, external rotation with the arm at the side, external rotation (hand on head), and internal rotation (thumb up the back) which were recorded with photographs. Three independent examiners then measured with a protractor the specific degrees of motion from these photographed examinations. Strength was evaluated using a dynamometer and JTECH tracker system (JTECH Medical), a computerized muscle strength evaluation program, per the technique for a standard CMS with the arm in abduction in the plane of the scapula.

The same ultrasonography evaluation technique was used based on the study of Audenaert et al21 which used cadavers and live specimens to confirm accurate measurement of deltoid volume. Each patient underwent height and weight measurements by the primary surgeon. Height, as per the previous paper, was measured with ultrasonography as the distance between deltoid origin and the muscular insertion of the deltoid on the humerus. Length was measured as the distance between deltoid origin at the scapular spine to its insertion on the clavicle. We estimated thickness by averaging thickness at the following seven points: 5, 10, and 15 cm distal to the origin at the acromion angle; the bicipital groove; 5 cm anterior to the groove; and 5 cm and 10 cm posterior to the groove. To avoid overcompressing the muscle and inducing bias, pressure was applied, released and then measured at its maximal thickness as per the described technique. The same measurement method was applied for the contralateral shoulder.

Once the measurements were complete, volume was calculated with the equation V = (length × height)/2 × T. T was defined as the mean thickness of the seven calculated points. This measurement was then evaluated with statistical analysis to determine whether there was correlation between volume and functional scores.

Statistical analysis was done using SPSS software (version 25, IBM). All variables were tested for outliers, normality, homogeneity of variances, and equality of variances. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used as statistical tests for normality. When the data distribution violated normality, Spearman correlation was done instead of the Pearson test. The Spearman test was done to determine the correlation between deltoid muscle volume and abduction strength, CMS, VAS, and ASES within surgical shoulder and nonsurgical repair groups, respectively. A paired t test was done to determine whether there was a notable difference between deltoid muscle volume and abduction strength, CMS, VAS, and ASES within surgical shoulder and nonsurgical repair groups, respectively. P values smaller than 0.05 were considered to be significant.

Discussion

This study aimed at measuring deltoid muscle size for prognosis of rTSA outcomes, using ultrasonography as a practical volumetric tool. The outcomes demonstrate a strong positive correlation between deltoid volume and abduction strength, and a moderate positive correlation between deltoid volume and CMS.

Deltoid function is widely recognized in the orthopaedic literature as a key determinant of successful rTSA outcomes,26 and poor deltoid function (or low deltoid muscle volume/sarcopenia) has been linked to worse functional outcomes and higher postoperative complication rates, especially related to strength and ROM.27 The complication rate for rTSA varies between 4.8% and 68%, although most commonly reported outcomes are not recent.28 The most common complication is dislocation (1.5% to 31% rate), followed by heterotopic ossification, limited external rotation, instability, infection, fracture, or peripheral nerve injury.29-33 Other inconstant factors may influenced the rTSA outcomes, including underlying pathology (such as preoperative medialization) and implant-specific variables. Variation in glenosphere size may also affect deltoid biomechanics, deltoid wrap potentially leading to deltoid fatigue over time.34-37

Use imaging biomarkers for deltoid function have been investigated and under development to understand prognosis and personalize treatment plans. Yoon et al17 used preoperative MRI to create 3-dimensional deltoid models and found that higher deltoid volume (adjusted for BMI) was the sole independent predictor of better Constant scores after rTSA in cuff-deficient patients. Like their work, our ultrasound-based volumes correlated moderately with CMSs and strongly with abduction strength, reinforcing volume's prognostic value. Bogner et al19 quantified postoperative deltoid CSA and FI using metal-artifact–reduced MRI 1 year after rTSA. They reported positive correlations between normalized CSA and both strength and Constant scores, and negative correlations for FI, particularly in the anterior deltoid segment. Our study did not assess FI but mirrors their CSA findings, showing that greater muscle mass favors functional recovery. The differences of our study from Yoon and Bogner studies are timing and metrics. For timing, Yoon focused on preoperative volume, while Bogner and our work measured postoperative parameters. For metrics, Yoon and we evaluated total volume, while Bogner segmented CSA and FI by region. Collectively, these studies underscore that deltoid muscle quantity and quality are critical determinants of rTSA success. Our ultrasonography approach offers a low-cost, bedside alternative to MRI, broadening opportunities for muscle assessment in candidate selection and rehabilitation planning.

Although still not an ideal option in some patients, rTSA does provide notable pain relief and return of function to many. Ten- and 15-year follow-up studies have demonstrated that patients continued to maintain their improved ROM and outcome scores.38,39 It seems intuitive that those with larger deltoid volumes would have greater strength and better function, but this has never been conclusively proven. Our results show a direct correlation with measured deltoid volume and abduction strength. As expected, aging and atrophy did occur because there was a correlation between muscle volume and age. Deltoid volume also correlated with the CMS. No particular trend was observed in size with length of time since surgery, and there was no statistical difference in volume of surgical vs. nonsurgical sides. Patient-reported pain level did not correlate with deltoid size (Pearson test, P = 0.383).

The CMS is a widely used clinical assessment tool for shoulder function evaluation, particularly in conditions including muscle injuries, shoulder instability, and arthritis.40 The score is divided into four categories including pain score (0 to 15 points), activities of daily living (0 to 20 points), ROM (0 to 40 points), and strength (0 to 25 points). The maximum total score is 100 points, and higher scores indicate better function. This study demonstrated that CMS provides a comprehensive assessment of both pain and functional ability of the shoulder considering multiple cofounding factors.

There are multiple variables that can affect clinical outcomes related to the prosthesis such as neck-shaft angle of the humerus, glenosphere diameter, eccentricity, lateral offset, glenoid baseplate tilt, and implant fixation.41 Understandably, there are multiple factors involved in postoperative function. Other studies have shown a difference in ASES with men achieving higher scores, despite both sexes achieving improvements in pain and ROM, with younger patients achieving greater motion.12,42 Some studies have shown differences in outcomes with varied indications for surgery.43 This study was looking at deltoid volume postoperatively, keeping other factors consistent by looking at patients who had the same surgeon and prosthesis.

The most common complications associated with rTSA include scapular notching, glenohumeral dislocation, implant loosening, acromion or scapular spine fracture, infection, nerve injury, and deltoid weakness.5 Some of the considerations regarding deltoid weakness may have to do with inappropriate tensioning of the deltoid. Over tensioning leads to a longer-range decline in deltoid function with risks of fracture to the acromion and decreased shoulder function.44,45 This is another potential aspect of using ultrasonography because ultrasonography could potentially be used to evaluate the deltoid muscle preoperatively and postoperatively.

Limitation of this study included the retrospective nature which limited patient number. The patient population came from a single surgeon and one medical center; multiple center collaborative investigation may be conducted in the future. Although ultrasonography is a readily available, inexpensive tool, there are some limits to the technique because it is operator dependent. Other studies have shown deltoid volume measured preoperatively with MRI does affect functional outcome.17 If ultrasonography could be used to provide similar data, accessibility and cost would be decreased in comparison with MRI use. The original paper by Holzbaur et al46 mentioned the possibility of overestimation of deltoid volume because they noted their volumes seemed larger than in similar studies. Audenaert et al21 hypothesized that this may be due to using points of measurement that were naturally in thicker, central parts of the muscle. rTSA continues to evolve, patient's postoperative outcomes can be influenced by complex biomechanical and anatomic factors.21

Another limitation of this study comes from not having a preoperative assessment of these patients. All patients had different levels of preoperative pain, function, and ROM. Multiple factors can affect patients' postoperative performance. Only one type of prosthesis was used in this study, although there are different prostheses available on the market. It would be of value in the future to evaluate another surgeon's patient population with a different prosthesis.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that increased deltoid volume correlated with increased shoulder abduction strength after rTSA. A moderate correlation was observed between CMS and deltoid volume measured by ultrasonography, which can indicate the postoperative outcomes 1 year after surgery. This study did not find an association between deltoid volume and VAS or ASES 1 year postoperatively.
