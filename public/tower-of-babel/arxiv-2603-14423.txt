Tighter Confidence Intervals under Without Replacement Sampling via Empirical Rate Functions
Shubhanshu Shekhar, Aaditya Ramdas

Abstract
We consider the problem of constructing confidence intervals (CIs) for the population mean of N N values { x 1 , … , x N } ⊂ Σ N \{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\subset\Sigma^{N} based on a random sample of size n n , denoted by X n ≡ ( X 1 , … , X n ) X^{n}\equiv(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}) , drawn uniformly without replacement ( WoR ). We begin by focusing on the finite alphabet ( | Σ | = k < ∞ |\Sigma|=k<\infty ) and moderate accuracy ( log ⁡ ( 1 / α N ) ≫ ( k + 1 ) ​ log ⁡ N \log(1/\alpha_{N})\gg(k+1)\log N ) regime, and derive a fundamental lower bound on the width of any level- ( 1 − α N ) (1-\alpha_{N}) CI in terms of the inverse of the WoR rate functions from the theory of large deviations. Guided by this lower bound, we propose a new level- ( 1 − α N ) (1-\alpha_{N}) CI using an empirical inverse rate function, and show that in certain asymptotic regimes the width of this CI matches the lower bound up to constants. We also derive a dual formulation of the inverse rate function that enables efficient computation of our proposed CI. We then move beyond the finite alphabet case and use a Bernoulli coupling idea to construct an almost sure CI for Σ = [ 0 , 1 ] \Sigma=[0,1] , and a conceptually simple nonasymptotic CI for the case of Σ \Sigma being a ( 2 , D ) (2,D) smooth Banach space. For both finite and general alphabets, our results employ classical large deviation techniques in novel ways, thus establishing new connections between estimation under WoR sampling and the theory of large deviations.

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{X}\equiv\mathcal{X}_{N}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}\}
denote a collection of
N
items lying in a support set (or alphabet)
\Sigma
, and define the population mean and variance associated with
\mathcal{X}_{N}
as

\displaystyle\mu\equiv\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i},\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma^{2}\equiv\sigma_{N}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-\mu_{N})^{2}.

(2)

For an integer
n<N
, let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
denote
n
observations drawn uniformly without replacement (
WoR
) from
\mathcal{X}_{N}
; that is,

X_{1}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(\mathcal{X}_{N}),\quad\text{and}\quad X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(\mathcal{X}_{N}\setminus\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1}\}),\;\text{for }i\geq 2.

Given these observations and a confidence parameter
\alpha\in(0,1]
, our goal is to construct a level-
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval (CI) for the unknown population mean. Formally, we want to construct a subset
C_{n}\equiv C_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\alpha)
, such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{N}\in C_{n}\right)\geq 1-\alpha
,
for each fixed
n
.
Note that the only source of randomness in the above expression is due to the sampling scheme; the elements
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
are themselves nonrandom quantities.

Our first goal in this paper is to understand the fundamental instance-dependent limits on the width achievable by any level-
(1-\alpha)
CI. Focusing on the finite alphabet case (i.e.,
\Sigma\subset[0,1],|\Sigma|<\infty
), we derive a new information-theoretic lower bound on the width of any level-
(1-\alpha)
CI in Theorem
3.4
, and then build upon this result to propose a new CI in
Section

3.2
. Both the lower and upper bounds depend on the
WoR
large deviation rate function terms, and in
Theorem

3.9
, we show that in certain regimes (that we refer to as
moderate accuracy
regimes; see Definition
3.3
) the width of our proposed CI nearly matches the lower bound modulo constants (see Remark
3.10
for a more precise discussion).
Next, in
Theorem

3.11
, we derive a dual representation of these inverse rate function terms that makes their evaluation computationally feasible. These results employ elementary type-counting arguments that rely crucially on the finiteness of
\Sigma
. To handle the case of continuous alphabets, such as
\Sigma=[0,1]
or when
\Sigma
is a smooth Banach space, we use a Bernoulli coupling identity to construct improved CIs with strong theoretical and empirical performance.

The construction of tight CIs for the population mean under
WoR
sampling is a fundamental problem that arises in many important applications. Some typical examples include risk-limiting audits
(
15
)
, survey sampling
(
4
)
, subsampled Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods for scalable Bayesian inference
(
1
)
, and large-scale kernel embedding
(
19
)
. In
Section

3.3.2

19
.

Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{X}_{N}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
denote a collection of
N
items
x_{i}\in\Sigma
for all
i\in[N]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,N\}
, with population mean and variance denoted by
\mu_{N}
and
\sigma_{N}^{2}
. Let
X^{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
denote a
WoR
sample from the set
\mathcal{X}_{N}
of size
n
. Denote the fraction
n/N
by
\beta_{N}
, and let
\bar{\beta}_{N}
denote
1-\beta_{N}
. The subscripts
N
are there to denote an implicit triangular array setup; whenever there is no confusion, we will drop the subscripts and simply use
\mathcal{X},\mu,\sigma^{2}
,
\beta
and
\bar{\beta}
, respectively.
Given the
WoR
sample
X^{n}
, we can construct the empirical distribution over the alphabet
\Sigma
as

\widehat{P}_{n}\equiv\widehat{P}(X^{n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
,
where
\delta_{X_{i}}
denotes the point mass at
X_{i}
.
Many results of this paper rely on the following fundamental coupling identity that relates
WoR
sampling distribution
\widehat{P}_{n}
to a simpler
i.i.d.
“Bernoulli Sampling” construction
(
12
, § 2)
.

Fact 2.1

(Key Coupling Identity)
.

Suppose
\Sigma
denotes an arbitrary alphabet,
and let
X^{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
denote a
WoR
sample from a finite population
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
with each
x_{i}\in\Sigma
. Now, let
J_{1},\ldots,J_{N}
denote
N

i.i.d.

\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\beta)
random variables, where
\beta=n/N
. Let
\widehat{P}_{n}\equiv\widehat{P}(X^{n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
and
\widehat{P}_{N,J}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
denote the empirical measures associated with
X^{n}
and
J^{N}
. Then, we have

\widehat{P}_{n}\;=_{d}\;\widehat{P}_{N,J}\mid\{S_{N,J}=n\}
,
where
=_{d}
indicates equality in distribution or law, and
S_{N,J}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{i}
.

This identity allows us to employ the standard concentration techniques for independent data to study the behavior of
WoR
samples for general alphabets, and we illustrate its utility in
Section

3.3
by developing new CIs when
\Sigma
is
[0,1]
or a smooth Banach space. For finite alphabets, this identity yields a more directly applicable finite-sample approximation result that we discuss next.

In the finite-alphabet setting (when
\Sigma=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\}\subset[0,1]
), the empirical distributions are also referred to as “types”, following
7
;
9
. Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}\equiv\mathcal{T}_{n}(\Sigma)
denote the set of all possible distinct types based on
n
observations from a finite
\Sigma
; that is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}(\Sigma)=\left\{t:t=\widehat{P}(x^{n})\;\text{for some }x^{n}\in\Sigma^{n}\right\},\quad\text{where}\quad\widehat{P}(x^{n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}.

(3)

It is easy to verify that
|\mathcal{T}_{n}|=\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}\leq(n+1)^{k}
; see
6
. For any type
t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, let
S_{t}=\{x^{n}\in\Sigma^{n}:\widehat{P}(x^{n})=t\}
denote the “type-class” of
t
; the collection of length-
n
sequences in
\Sigma
whose type is equal to
t
.
Using Stirling’s approximation, we can then obtain the following bound, which we will use often in
Section

3.1
and
Section

3.2
.

Fact 2.2

(Lemma 2.1.33 of
9
)
.

Let
t
denote any type in
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\Sigma)
, and let
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
denote a collection of
N
elements (with possible repetition) from
\Sigma
. Let
\widehat{P}_{N}\equiv\widehat{P}(\mathcal{X})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
denote the type or empirical distribution associated with
\mathcal{X}
. Suppose
X^{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
denotes a
WoR
sample from
\mathcal{X}
, and denote its type by
\widehat{P}_{n}\equiv\widehat{P}(X^{n})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{P}_{n}=t\right)\right)+I\left(t,\frac{n}{N},\widehat{P}_{N}\right)\right\rvert\leq 2\left(|\Sigma|+1\right)\left(\frac{\log(N+1)}{n}\right),

(4)

where

I(P,\beta,Q)\coloneqq d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q)+\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}d_{\text{KL}}\left(\frac{Q-\beta P}{1-\beta}\parallel Q\right),

with
d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q)=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}P(s)\log P(s)/Q(s)
for
P\ll Q
denoting the relative entropy (or KL divergence) between the two discrete distributions
P
and
Q
over
\Sigma
. This definition is valid under the condition that
Q(s)-\beta P(s)\geq 0
for all
s\in\Sigma
, which implies that
(Q-\beta P)/(1-\beta)
is a valid probability distribution.

This result establishes the probability of observing a type
t
constructed using
X^{n}
sampled
WoR
from a type
\widehat{P}_{N}\equiv\widehat{P}(\mathcal{X})
is approximately
\exp\left(-nI\left(t,n/N,\widehat{P}_{N}\right)\right)
. Thus, this rate function
I
introduced in Fact
2.2
plays the same role as relative entropy does for with-replacement sampling.

Prior Work.

In his seminal paper,
14
showed that the expectation of a continuous convex function
f
applied to a sum of i.i.d. random variables upper bounds the expectation of the same function applied to the sum of
WoR
samples. Formally, let
Y^{n}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
denote
i.i.d.

\mathrm{Uniform}(\mathcal{X}_{N})
random variables drawn with replacement, and let
X^{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
denote a
WoR
sample drawn from the same
\mathcal{X}_{N}
. Then, for any continuous and convex function
f
,

\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right)\right].

Combining this inequality with the exponential deviation bound for
[0,1]
-valued
i.i.d.
random variables
(
14
, Theorem 1)
yields that for any
\epsilon>0
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mu\right\rvert\geq\epsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-2n\epsilon^{2}\right).

Inverting this concentration inequality, we obtain the classic Hoeffding
1-\alpha
CI under
WoR
sampling:

\displaystyle C_{n}^{(H)}=\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\pm w_{n}^{(H)}\right],\quad\text{with}\quad w_{n}^{H}=\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{2n}}.

(5)

An important feature in the
WoR
case, not present in the
i.i.d.
setting, is that the uncertainty about the population mean reduces to
0
as
n
increases to
N
(i.e.,
\beta\uparrow 1
). Hoeffding CI stated in (
5
) does not capture this distinguishing feature of the
WoR
setting.

20
addressed this issue and sharpened Hoeffding CI in (
5
) by introducing a finite-sample correction and replacing
n
with
\frac{n}{1-(n-1)/N}
.

2
obtained a further improvement of Serfling’s result, replacing
(n-1)
with
n
, and then used it to obtain variants of Hoeffding-Serfling inequality, Bernstein-Serfling, and Empirical-Bernstein-Serfling inequalities.
We state the Bernstein-Serfling confidence interval (CI) obtained by
2
, which has the additional benefit of being variance adaptive, unlike the Hoeffding-Serling CI.
We have the following with probability at least
1-\alpha
for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
, for all
t\geq n
:

\displaystyle\mu\in\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\pm\frac{w_{n}^{(BM)}}{2}\right],

(6)

where

\displaystyle\frac{w_{n}^{(BM)}}{2}=\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2(1-n/N)(1+1/n)\log(2/\alpha)}{n}}+\left(\frac{4}{3}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{N}{n+1}-1\right)\left(1-\frac{n}{N}\right)}\right)\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{n}.

(7)

Since the above construction requires prior knowledge of
\sigma^{2}
,
2
also derived an empirical version of this CI in which
\sigma^{2}
is replaced by
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}
, the empirical variance, plus additional terms.

24
proposed a betting-based formalism for constructing CIs (and their time-uniform variants called confidence sequences) for the with- and without-replacement settings. In the
WoR
case, the CI after
n
observation is defined as

\displaystyle C_{n}^{(bet)}=\{m\in[0,1]:W_{n}(m)<1/\alpha\},

(8)

where the process
\{W_{t}(m):t\in[n]\}
for any
m\in[0,1]
is constructed to satisfy two properties: (i) at the true (unknown) population mean, the process
\{W_{t}(\mu):t\geq 1\}
is a non-negative martingale with initial value
1
, and (ii) for
m\neq\mu
, the process grows rapidly. One way of constructing such a collection of stochastic processes is to interpret
\{W_{t}(m):t\geq 1\}
as the evolution of the wealth of a gambler betting to disprove the claim that
\mu=m
. We refer the reader to
24
for more details.

Overview of Our Results.

In this paper, we begin with a natural question not addressed in prior work:

Is there a fundamental lower bound on the width of
reasonable
methods of constructing CIs for the population mean, and do there exist methods that achieve or approach this limit?

In Sections
3.1
and
3.2
we partially resolve this question for the case of finite alphabets (i.e.,
|\Sigma|=k<\infty
). In the process, we identify the inverse of the
WoR
rate-function (defined in Definition
3.1
) as the key complexity measure that characterizes the optimal CI width. In
Theorem

3.4
, we show that if there exists a method of constructing CIs that ensures the level-
(1-\alpha_{N})
coverage non-asymptotically in the
moderate accuracy
regime (Definition
3.3
) for all populations
\mathcal{X}_{N}
(meaning of
reasonable
), then its width must satisfy the following bound for some constant
\underline{A}>0
and small enough
\alpha_{N}
(see Remark
3.5
for precise conditions):

\displaystyle|C_{n}(X^{n},\alpha_{N})|\gtrsim\frac{1}{2}\left({g}_{+}^{-1}\left(\frac{\underline{A}\log(1/\alpha_{N})}{n}\right)-{g}_{-}^{-1}\left(\frac{\underline{A}\log(1/\alpha_{N})}{n}\right)\right),\quad\text{where}\quad g_{\pm}(\cdot)=J_{\pm}(\widehat{P}_{N},\beta,\cdot),

(9)

J_{\pm}
are the
WoR
rate functions in Definition
3.1
,
\widehat{P}_{N}
is the population distribution, and
\beta=n/N\in(0,1)
.

This lower bound suggests a natural CI construction: replace the population inverse rate-function (
g_{\pm}^{-1}
) with their empirical versions (
\hat{g}_{\pm}^{-1}
) and add some slack in the argument to account for this. In
Theorem

3.7
, we show that the resulting CI constructed using this idea contains the true mean with probability
1-\alpha_{N}
, and then in
Theorem

3.9
, we show that for large enough values of
N
(see Remark
D.4
), the width of this CI satisfies the following bound with probability at least
1-\alpha_{N}
:

\displaystyle|C_{n}(X^{n},\alpha_{N})|\leq\left({g}_{+}^{-1}\left(\frac{\bar{A}\log(1/\alpha_{N})}{n}\right)-{g}_{-}^{-1}\left(\frac{\bar{A}\log(1/\alpha_{N})}{n}\right)\right),

(10)

for some
\bar{A}>\underline{A}
, and assuming
\alpha_{N}
is small enough (see Remark
3.10
). The leading constant
(1/2)
in the lower bound statement is most likely an artifact of our proof, and could possibly be removed (at least in the asymptotic setting) by translating the stability assumption of
8
to the
WoR
case.

Thus, these results establish the inverse rate-function as the fundamental complexity term for the optimal CI width under certain
(n,\alpha_{N})
regimes.
From a computational perspective, the construction of our proposed CI requires solving a convex program whose complexity increases with the alphabet size. To address this, we derive a dual formulation of the rate-function terms (
J_{\pm}
) in
Theorem

3.11
. This reduces the computation to a two-dimensional convex program independent of the alphabet size, making the procedure computationally tractable. Some numerical results reported in Figure
3
verify the theoretic predictions.

The derivations of the above results are based fundamentally on the finite alphabet version of Fact
2.1
, which we stated in Fact
2.2
. Although the approximations of Fact
2.2
are asymptotically tight, they may end up being quite loose in the finite-sample regime. Furthermore, our proposed CI is only valid for observations lying in a finite alphabet
\Sigma
, and does not admit a natural extension to continuous alphabets. For these reasons, while Definition
3.6
yields a near-optimal CI in theory, its direct practical utility is limited. In the remainder of the paper, we develop two new practically useful CIs starting with the fundamental identity in Fact
2.1
. In
Section

3.3.1
, we propose an
almost sure
CI that lies midway between nonasymptotic CIs (such as Bernstein-Serfling) and CLT-based asymptotic CIs in terms of validity and performance, while in
Section

3.3.2
, we use Fact
2.1
to construct a simple nonasymptotic CI for observations lying in a
(2,D)
-smooth Banach space. We validate the practical usefulness of these CIs via some numerical experiments.

3
Main Results

In the with-replacement setting,
21
showed that the width of the CIs for bounded means is characterized by certain inverse relative entropy (or with-replacement large deviation rate function) terms.
In this section, we will derive similar results for the without-replacement setting, in terms of
WoR
rate function terms based on
I
, as defined below.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
P
denote any probability distribution supported on
\Sigma\subset[0,1]
with
|\Sigma|=k
, and fix a constant
\beta\in(0,1)
. Then, for any
m\in[0,1]
, we define the following two rate function terms:

\displaystyle J_{+}(P,\beta,m)\coloneqq\inf\left\{I(P,\beta,Q):\min_{s\in\Sigma}Q(s)/P(s)\geq\beta,\;\text{and}\;\mu_{Q}\geq m\right\},

(11)

\displaystyle J_{-}(P,\beta,m)\coloneqq\inf\left\{I(P,\beta,Q):\min_{s\in\Sigma}Q(s)/P(s)\geq\beta,\;\text{and}\;\mu_{Q}\leq m\right\}.

(12)

In both these definitions,
\mu_{Q}
denotes the mean value associated with the distribution
Q
; and the infimum is over all the distributions supported on
[0,1]
such that
Q\gg P
with
\min_{s\in\Sigma}Q(s)/P(s)\geq\beta
with the corresponding mean constraints.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that for a fixed distribution
P
with mean
\mu_{P}
, the term
J_{+}(P,\beta,m)
is equal to zero for all
m\leq\mu_{P}
, and
J_{-}(P,\beta,m)
is equal to zero for all
m\geq\mu_{P}
. Thus, the maximum of two is
J(P,\beta,m):=\max\{J_{+}(P,\beta,m),J_{-}(P,\beta,m)\}=\boldsymbol{1}_{m\geq\mu_{P}}J_{+}(P,\beta,m)+\boldsymbol{1}_{m<\mu_{P}}J_{-}(P,\beta,m)
.

The next two subsections (
Section

3.1
and
Section

3.2
) present the results for the finite alphabet case, and the final subsection is concerned with CIs for more general alphabets.

3.1
Lower Bound for Finite Alphabet

Consider a shrinking sequence of confidence levels, denoted by
\{\alpha_{N}\in(0,1]:N\geq 1\}
, with
\alpha_{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
, and introduce the following terms:

\displaystyle r_{N}=(|\Sigma|+1)\log(N+1),\quad\text{and}\quad a_{N}=\log(1/2\alpha_{N})-r_{N}.

(13)

Here,
|\Sigma|
denotes the alphabet size, which we assume to be finite, and
N
denotes the population size. In this section and the next, we will assume that the confidence parameter
\alpha_{N}
decays at a polynomial rate with
N
, and we refer to such CIs as
moderately accurate CIs
.

Definition 3.3

(Moderate Accuracy Regime)
.

Consider a sequence of populations
\{\mathcal{X}_{N}:N\geq 1\}
supported on finite alphabets
\{\Sigma_{N}:N\geq 1\}
with means
\{\mu_{N}:N\geq 1\}
. For each
N
, let
(X_{N,1},\ldots,X_{N,n})
with
\beta_{N}=n/N
denote a
WoR
sample, and let
C_{n}
denote a CI for the mean
\mu_{N}
constructed based on
(X_{N,1},\ldots,X_{N,n})
. We say that
\{C_{n}:n=\beta_{N}N,\;N\geq 1\}
is a sequence of
moderate accuracy
CIs if

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{N}\in C_{n}\right)\geq 1-\alpha_{N},\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{(|\Sigma_{N}|+1)\log(N+1)}{\log(1/2\alpha_{N})}=0.

(14)

If the alphabet does not change with
N
(
\Sigma_{N}=\Sigma
for all
N\geq 1
), then this is equivalent to the condition that
\alpha_{N}
decays super-polynomially in
N
; that is,
\alpha_{N}=o\left(N^{-c}\right)
for all
c>0
.

Our first main result establishes a fundamental lower bound on the width of moderately accurate CIs.

Theorem 3.4

(Width lower bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
denote a method of constructing confidence intervals for the population mean
\mu_{N}
of
\mathcal{X}_{N}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\subset\Sigma^{N}
. With
\widehat{P}_{N}\equiv\widehat{P}(\mathcal{X}_{N})\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{N}}\delta_{x}
, define

\displaystyle W_{n}(X^{n},\widehat{P}_{N},\alpha_{N})\coloneqq|\mathcal{C}(X^{n},\widehat{P}_{N},\alpha_{N})|.

(15)

Let
\widetilde{P}_{n}=\argmin_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}I(t,\beta,\widehat{P}_{N})
denote the element of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
closest to
\widehat{P}_{N}
in
I
, and define
b^{*}_{+}
and
b^{*}_{-}
as

\displaystyle b_{+}^{*}:=\inf\left\{m\geq 0:J_{+}(\widetilde{P}_{n},\beta,m)\geq\frac{a_{N}}{n}\right\},\quad\text{and}\quad b_{-}^{*}:=\sup\left\{m\leq 1:J_{-}(\widetilde{P}_{n},\beta,m)\geq\frac{a_{N}}{n}\right\}.

(16)

Then, for large enough
N
with
\alpha_{N}
in the moderate accuracy regime (see Remark
B.1
for precise conditions), we have with probability at least
1-\alpha_{N}
,

\displaystyle W_{n}(X^{n},\widehat{P}_{N},\alpha_{N})\geq\max\{b^{*}_{+}-\mu_{N},\;\mu_{N}-b^{*}_{-}\}\geq\frac{b^{*}_{+}-b^{*}_{-}}{2}.

(17)

Note that the same bound holds for the worst-case width
\max_{x^{n}\subset\mathcal{X}_{N}}|\mathcal{C}(x^{n},\widehat{P}_{N},\alpha_{N})|
with probability
1
.

Proof outline of
Theorem

3.4
.

The starting point of the argument is the standard duality between confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. In particular, we consider null and alternative population distributions
P
and
Q
with means
\mu_{P}
and
\mu_{Q}>\mu_{P}+w
, where
w
is such that
\mathbb{P}_{P}(|C_{n}|>w)\leq\alpha_{N}
. Then, given a level-
(1-\alpha_{N})
CI constructed using the
WoR
sample
X^{n}
, denoted by
C_{n}
, we define a hypothesis test
\Psi
that rejects the null (i.e.,
P
) if
\mu_{Q}\in C_{n}
. The level-
(1-\alpha_{N})
property of the CI implies that its type-I and type-II errors are at most
2\alpha_{N}
and
\alpha_{N}
respectively. Next, we show that for every test
\Psi
with errors at most
(2\alpha_{N},\alpha_{N})
, there exists a test
\Psi^{\prime}
based only on the type (and not the order of observations) with errors bounded above by
(4\alpha_{N},2\alpha_{N})
. Finally, using Fact
2.2
, we establish that for large enough values of
N
(precise conditions in Remark
B.1
), the type
\tilde{P}_{n}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
closest to
P
in terms of
I
must lie in the acceptance region of any test whose type-I error is smaller than
4\alpha_{N}
. On some simplification, this leads to the stated lower bounds on the values of
w
. The details are in
Appendix

B
.

Remark 3.5
.

The moderate accuracy assumption implies that
\log(1/2\alpha_{N})-r_{N}\approx\log(1/2\alpha_{N})
for large enough
N
. This in turn means that
b^{*}_{+}\approx J_{+}(\widehat{P}_{N},\beta,\cdot)^{-1}\left(\log(1/2\alpha_{N})/n\right)
and
b^{*}_{-}\approx J_{-}(\widehat{P}_{N},\beta,\cdot)^{-1}\left(\log(1/2\alpha_{N})/n\right)
in this regime.
Motivated by the above result, we now propose a new CI whose end points are empirical versions of
b^{*}_{-}
and
b^{*}_{+}
introduced in
Theorem

3.4
.

3.2
Proposed CI for Finite Alphabet

Having established the lower bound on the width of any CI in
Theorem

3.4
, we now use these insights to construct new a level-
(1-\alpha_{N})
CI for
\mu_{N}
. As before, assume that
\mathcal{X}_{N}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
take values in the finite alphabet
\Sigma
, and define a new CI based on empirical inverse rate functions as follows.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
X^{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
denote a random sample of size
n=\beta N
from
\mathcal{X}_{N}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
drawn uniformly without replacement. Denote the types (or empirical distributions) of
\mathcal{X}_{N}
and
X^{n}
with
\widehat{P}_{N}\equiv\widehat{P}(\mathcal{X}_{N})
and
\widehat{P}_{n}\equiv\widehat{P}(X^{n})
respectively. For any
\alpha_{N}>0
, define the confidence interval

\displaystyle C_{n}\equiv C_{n}(X^{n},\alpha_{N},\beta)=\left[b_{-},b_{+}\right],

(18)

where
b_{-}\equiv b_{-}(\widehat{P}_{n},c_{N})
and
b_{+}\equiv b_{+}(\widehat{P}_{n},c_{N})
are defined as follows, with
c_{N}=\log(2/\alpha_{N})+2r_{N}
:

\displaystyle b_{-}(\widehat{P}_{n},c_{N})=\sup\left\{m\leq 1:J_{-}(\widehat{P}_{n},\beta,m)\geq\frac{c_{N}}{n}\right\},~b_{+}(\widehat{P}_{n},c_{N})=\inf\left\{m\geq 0:J_{+}(\widehat{P}_{n},\beta,m)\geq\frac{c_{N}}{n}\right\}.

(19)

We use the convention that
\inf\emptyset=1
and
\sup\emptyset=0
, so that
C_{n}
is always a subset of
[0,1]
.

Recall that the two rate-function terms,
J_{-}
and
J_{+}
, were introduced in Definition
3.1
, and
r_{N}
was defined in (
13
). Intuitively, the first term in
c_{N}
(i.e.,
\log(2/\alpha_{N})
) comes from the usual two-sided tail bound, and the remaining
2r_{N}
term accounts for the finite alphabet probability approximation result in Fact
2.2
, along with a union bound over all types.
Our next result shows that the CI introduced above in Definition
3.6
is a valid level-
(1-\alpha_{N})
CI for the population mean
\mu\equiv\mu_{N}
associated with the finite population
\mathcal{X}_{N}
.

Theorem 3.7
.

For all
N\geq 1
, the set
C_{n}
from Definition
3.6
is a
(1-\alpha_{N})
CI for
\mu_{N}
.

The proof of this result is given in
Appendix

C
.
We emphasize that
Theorem

3.7
holds for every
N,n,\alpha_{N}
, avoiding the moderate accuracy condition.

Remark 3.8
.

If confidence parameter
\alpha_{N}
satisfies the
moderate accuracy
condition of Definition
3.3
, such that
\log(1/2\alpha_{N})+2r_{N}\asymp\log(1/2\alpha_{N})
, then the width of the CI above matches the lower bound obtained in
Theorem

3.4
, with two differences: (i) there is an additional factor of
2
compared to (
17
), and (ii) the terms
b_{-}
and
b_{+}
are defined using
\widehat{P}_{n}
instead of
\widehat{P}_{N}
that was used in the definitions of
b^{*}_{-}
and
b^{*}_{+}
. The factor
2
is likely an artifact of our lower-bound proof and it should be possible to remove it for large
N
values, for example, using the arguments developed by
8
.

In our next result, we analyze the relation between the population and the empirical inverse rate function terms.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
g_{\pm}
and
\widehat{g}_{\pm}
denote the population and empirical inverse rate functions:

\displaystyle{g}_{+}(x)=\inf\{m\geq 0:J_{+}(P,\beta,m)\geq x\},\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{g}_{+}(x)=\inf\{m\geq 0:J_{+}(\widehat{P}_{n},\beta,m)\geq x\},

(20)

\displaystyle{g}_{-}(x)=\sup\{m\leq 1:J_{-}(P,\beta,m)\geq x\},\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{g}_{-}(x)=\sup\{m\leq 1:J_{-}(\widehat{P}_{n},\beta,m)\geq x\}.

(21)

Then, with probability at least
1-\alpha_{N}
, we have the following with
A=1+2/\sigma^{2}
, and large enough values of
n
(see Remark
D.4
for the precise meaning of “
n
large enough”):

\displaystyle b_{+}(\widehat{P}_{n})=\widehat{g}_{+}\left(\frac{c_{N}}{n}\right)\leq{g}_{+}\left(A\times\frac{c_{N}}{n}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad b_{-}(\widehat{P}_{n})=\widehat{g}_{-}\left(\frac{c_{N}}{n}\right)\geq{g}_{-}\left(A\times\frac{c_{N}}{n}\right).

(22)

Proof outline of
Theorem

3.9
.

To prove this result, we first show in Lemma
D.1
that
C_{n}=[b_{-},b_{+}]\subset\widetilde{C}_{n}\coloneqq[\widehat{\mu}_{n}\pm\sqrt{\bar{\beta}c_{N}/2n}]
; that is, the CI constructed in Definition
3.6
is contained in a larger CI of half-width
\sqrt{\bar{\beta}c_{N}/2n}
. Although this new CI is wider than the lower bound, it is important for certain approximation arguments used in rest of the proof.
Lemma
D.2
and
D.3
show that for small enough
\delta>0
, we have:

\displaystyle J_{+}(P,\beta,\mu_{P}+\delta)\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\bar{\beta}\sigma^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{3}\right).

(23)

This cubic approximation allows us to conclude that with probability
1-\alpha_{N}
, we have

\displaystyle J_{+}(\widehat{P}_{n},\beta,m)\geq J_{+}(P,\beta,m)-(A-1)\frac{c_{N}}{n},\quad\text{for all}\quad m\in\widetilde{C}_{n},

(24)

yielding the result. Repeating these steps for
J_{-}
gives the lower bound on
b_{-}
. The details are in
Appendix

D
.

Remark 3.10
.

Assuming that
\alpha_{N}
is small enough such that
\log(2/\alpha_{N})+2r_{N}\asymp\log(2/\alpha_{N})
, we have the following with some constants
\bar{A}>\underline{A}>0
, and
u_{N}=\log(2/\alpha_{N})/n
:

\displaystyle\frac{{g}_{+}(\underline{A}u_{N})-{g}_{-}(\underline{A}u_{N})}{2}\;\leq\;|C_{n}(X^{n},\alpha_{N})|\;\leq\;{g}_{+}(\bar{A}u_{N})-{g}_{-}(\bar{A}u_{N}).

(25)

Thus, our proposed CI width matches the information-theoretic lower bound of
Theorem

3.4
, up to the leading factor
(1/2)
and the constant
\underline{A}<\bar{A}
inside the argument of the inverse rate function terms
g_{\pm}
.

A Duality Result.

Since the definitions of
J_{+}
and
J_{-}
in Definition
3.6
involve optimizing over the entire probability simplex, direct evaluation of these terms can become computationally infeasible with increasing
|\Sigma|
.
To address this, we present a dual representation of these rate functions that restates them as two-dimensional convex programs. For simplicity, we assume that the end points
0,1
do not belong to
\Sigma
below.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
P
denote a probability distribution supported on a finite set
\Sigma\subset(0,1)
, and consider any
m\in[0,1]
and
\beta\in(0,1)
such that
\beta\mu_{P}+\bar{\beta}>m
, where
\mu_{P}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[X]=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}s\times p(s)
and
\bar{\beta}=1-\beta
.
Then, we have the following duality result:

\displaystyle J_{+}(P,\beta,m)

\displaystyle=\inf\;\left\{I(P,\beta,Q)\;\boldsymbol{:}\;{Q\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]),\,\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X]\geq m}\right\}

(26)

\displaystyle=\sup_{(\lambda,\rho)\in\mathcal{D}_{D}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\log\left(\frac{1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta\rho}e^{\beta\lambda X}}{\beta}\right)\right]+\lambda\left(m-\beta\mu_{P}\right)+\rho\bar{\beta}\right\},

(27)

where
\mathcal{D}_{D}
denotes the following dual feasible set for the dual optimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{D}=\left\{(\lambda,\rho)\;\boldsymbol{:}\;\lambda\geq 0,\,\rho\in\mathbb{R},\rho+\lambda\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\log\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\right\}.

(28)

A similar result holds for
J_{-}
since
J_{-}(P,\beta,m)=J_{+}(P^{\prime},\beta,1-m)
, with
P^{\prime}=P\circ f^{-1}
for
f(x)=1-x
.

The proof of this result is in
Appendix

E
, and it begins by observing that we can restrict our attention to a finite dimensional subset of the domain of the primal problem stated in (
26
) without loss of optimality. The rest of the proof then follows by some standard finite dimensional convex duality arguments.

Remark 3.12
.

In the statement of
Theorem

3.11
, we have assumed that the alphabet
\Sigma
does not contain the end points
\{0,1\}
. If we allow the alphabet
\Sigma
to contain the end points, the expression of the dual optimization problem stated in
Theorem

3.11
does not change: it is still the same two-parameter concave maximization over the domain
\mathcal{D}_{D}
. However, some steps of the proof as described in
Appendix

E
, mainly Lemma
E.1
and Lemma
E.2
, will need to be modified accordingly.

Remark 3.13
.

As mentioned earlier, this duality result is practically important: it reduces the task of evaluating the rate function to that of solving a convex optimization problem over a two dimensional domain
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
. Importantly, the dimension of the optimization domain does not increase as the support size
|\Sigma|
increases. This means that our confidence interval proposed in
Section

3.2
can be constructed in a computationally feasible manner, by using off-the-shelf optimization programs to estimate rate functions.

Remark 3.14
.

Our approach for constructing the CI in Definition
3.6
has some parallels with the moderate deviation principle (MDP)-based approach developed by
11
, who developed a general framework for CIs by inverting the MDP rate functions for certain estimators. The intervals so constructed are then shown to satisfy an asymptotic notion of minimality as described in
11
. In contrast to their work, we invert the exact rate function of the empirical distribution
\widehat{P}_{n}
in the finite-alphabet setting to obtain a nonasymptotic level-
(1-\alpha_{N})
guarantee, owing to standard type-based approximation results for finite alphabets.

3.3
CIs for General Alphabets

The CI constructed in
Section

3.2
has three main drawbacks. First, it uses the combinatorial bound of Fact
2.2
that makes it too conservative in practice. Second, it applies on the case of finite
\Sigma
, which strongly limits its scope. Finally, its theoretical guarantees are valid only for small values of
\alpha_{N}
. In this section, we illustrate how the general Bernoulli coupling idea of Fact
2.1
can still be useful in constructing more practical CIs that can relax one or more of these conditions.

To illustrate the basic idea, consider a population
\mathcal{X}_{N}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
with elements in an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma
, and let
\{J_{i}:i\in[N]\}
denote
i.i.d.

\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\beta)
random variables. Fact
2.1
tells us that conditioned on
S_{N,J}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{i}
being equal to
n=\beta N
, the law of
\widehat{P}_{N,J}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
is equal to that of the
WoR
distribution
\widehat{P}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
.
Hence, for any subset
E\subset\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
, Fact
2.1
implies the following (with
\widehat{P}_{n}=\widehat{P}(X^{n})
,
\widehat{P}_{N,J}=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
, and
S_{N,J}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{i}
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{P}_{n}\in E\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{P}_{N,J}\in E\middle|S_{N,J}=n\right)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{P}_{N,J}\in E,\,S_{N,J}=n\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{N,J}=n\right)}\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{P}_{N,J}\in E\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(S_{N,J}=n\right)}

(29)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)N}\,\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{P}_{N,J}\in E\right)\leq 1.18\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)N}\,\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{P}_{N,J}\in E\right).

(30)

The bound on
1/\mathbb{P}(S_{N,J}=n)
follows from the fact that
S_{N,J}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(N,\beta)
, and by employing Stirling’s inequalities for the factorial function. The details are in
Section

I.1
.

Since the
i.i.d.
empirical distribution
\widehat{P}_{N,J}
can be analytically more tractable than the
WoR
distribution
\widehat{P}_{n}
, it can help us design new CIs that complement or improve upon existing CIs for more general
\Sigma
, even when taking the
1.18\sqrt{(1-\beta)n}
-factor into account. In particular, we discuss the following two cases:

•

In
Section

3.3.1
, we construct an “almost sure” CI, as defined by
16
, for the case of
\Sigma=[0,1]
. In terms of assumptions and validity, this new CI lies between non-asymptotic CIs, such as those proposed by
14
;
20
;
2
;
23
, and the asymptotic CI constructed using the central limit theorem (CLT) for without replacement case
(
13
)
. We discuss this further in Remark
3.17
.

•

In
Section

3.3.2
, we consider the case of
\Sigma
being a
(2,D)
-smooth Banach space, and illustrate how the approach based on (
30
) can be used to construct a CI whose width is tighter than the existing CI of
19
in normal parameter ranges (see Figure
4
).

These examples illustrate how the same Bernoulli coupling identity of Fact
2.1
, underlying the construction of the near-optimal CI for finite alphabets, can also be used in designing CIs for more general alphabets.

Figure 1
:
Comparison of the distribution of widths (over
200
trials) of the almost sure CI of (
39
) (denoted by AS-CI), the CLT-based asymptotically valid CI, and the empirical Bernstein CI derived by
2
using populations of size
N=1000
generated using
\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)
distributions, with
WoR
samples of size
500
. In both examples the size of our almost sure CI lies midway between the asymptotically valid CLT-CI, and the nonasymptotic CI of
2
, denoted by BM2015.

3.3.1
An “Almost Sure” CI for
\mathbf{\Sigma=[0,1]}

We now turn to the simplest continuous alphabet
\Sigma=[0,1]
, although the results of this section are equally valid for any bounded interval
[a,b]
. We will work under the following assumption.

Assumption 3.15
.

Let
(\mathcal{X}_{N})_{N\geq 1}
denote a collection of finite subsets of
\Sigma=[0,1]
, such that
\widehat{P}_{N}\equiv\widehat{P}(\mathcal{X}_{N})\to P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
weakly as
N\to\infty
, with
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}[X^{2}]=m_{2}>0
.
If
\mu_{N}
denotes the mean
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}_{N}}[1]
, then this also implies that there exists a
\mu_{X}\in[0,1]
such that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu_{N}=\mu_{X}
.
We assume that
X^{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
denotes a
WoR
sample of size
n=\beta_{N}N
, with
\lim_{N\to\infty}\beta_{N}=\beta
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
.

As mentioned earlier, our starting point is the inequality (
30
) based on Fact
2.1
. In particular, for some
\epsilon>0
to be specified later and for any
\lambda\geq 0
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\geq\mu_{N}+\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1.18\sqrt{\bar{\beta}n}\,\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{i}x_{i}-n\mu_{N}\geq n\epsilon\right)=1.18\sqrt{\bar{\beta}n}\,\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(J_{i}-\beta_{N})x_{i}\geq n\epsilon\right)

(31)

\displaystyle\leq 1.18\sqrt{\bar{\beta}n}\exp\left(-n\lambda\epsilon+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(J_{i}-\beta_{N})x_{i}}\right]\right)\right).

(32)

Since each
J_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\beta_{N})
, we can calculate the expectations in (
32
) exactly as
\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(J_{i}-\beta_{N})x_{i}}]=\beta_{N}e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}_{N}x_{i}}+\bar{\beta}_{N}e^{-\lambda\beta_{N}x_{i}}
. Introduce the term

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N}(\lambda)=\int f_{N,\lambda}(x)d\widehat{P}_{N}(x),\quad\text{for}\quad f_{N,\lambda}(x)\coloneqq\log\left(\beta_{N}e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}_{N}x}+\bar{\beta}_{N}e^{-\lambda\beta_{N}x}\right),

(33)

and observe that we can plug this into (
32
) to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{X}_{n}\geq\mu_{N}+\epsilon\right)\leq 1.18\sqrt{\bar{\beta}_{N}n}\exp\left(-n\left(\lambda\epsilon-\frac{1}{\beta_{N}}\Lambda_{N}(\lambda)\right)\right),

(34)

where we use
\bar{X}_{n}
to denote the
WoR
sample mean
(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
Optimizing the expression above with respect to
\lambda
, and denoting by
\Lambda^{*}_{N}
the convex conjugate of
\Lambda_{N}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bar{X}_{n}\geq\mu_{N}+\epsilon)\leq 1.18\sqrt{\bar{\beta}n}\exp\left(-N\Lambda^{*}_{N}(\beta_{N}\epsilon)\right).

(35)

Thus, for any
\alpha_{N}\in(0,1)
, we can invert the above bound to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\bar{X}_{n}-\mu_{N}|>\epsilon_{N}\coloneqq\frac{(\Lambda^{*}_{N})^{-1}\left(t_{N,\alpha_{N}}\right)}{\beta_{N}}\right)\leq\alpha_{N},\;\text{where}\;t_{N,\alpha_{N}}=\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\frac{2.36\sqrt{(1-\beta_{N})n}}{\alpha_{N}}\right).

(36)

This implies the following non-asymptotic level-
(1-\alpha_{N})
CI for the population mean
\mu_{N}
:

\displaystyle C^{\mathrm{orc}}_{N}\equiv C^{\mathrm{orc}}_{N}(\alpha_{N})=\left[\bar{X}_{n}-\epsilon_{N},\bar{X}_{n}+\epsilon_{N}\right].

(37)

This “oracle” CI depends on
\Lambda^{*}_{N}
which is defined using the unknown distribution
\widehat{P}_{N}
. Since we only have access to the
WoR
sample
X^{n}
, a natural idea is to replace the usage of
\widehat{P}_{N}
with
\widehat{P}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
, to get

\displaystyle C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}(\alpha_{N})=\left[\bar{X}_{n}-\widehat{\epsilon}_{N},\bar{X}_{n}+\widehat{\epsilon}_{N}\right],\quad

(38)

where

\displaystyle\quad\widehat{\epsilon}_{N}=\frac{1}{\beta_{N}}\left(\hat{\Lambda}_{N}^{*}\right)^{-1}\left(t_{N,\alpha_{N}}+\frac{1}{N^{1.1}}\right),\quad\hat{\Lambda}_{N}(\lambda)\coloneqq\int f_{N,\lambda}(x)d\widehat{P}_{n}(x),

(39)

recalling
f_{N,\lambda}
from (
33
), and
t_{N,\alpha_{N}}
from (
36
).

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
E_{N}
denote the event that
\{C^{\mathrm{orc}}_{N}(\alpha_{N})\not\subset C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}(\alpha_{N})\}=\{\hat{\epsilon}_{N}<\epsilon_{N}\}
that the purely empirical CI constructed above does not contain the oracle CI with finite-sample validity. Then, under Assumption
3.15
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(E_{N}\text{ infinitely often}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{N\geq 1}\cup_{N^{\prime}\geq N}E_{N^{\prime}}\right)=0
. Consequently, with
\{\alpha_{N}\in(0,1]:N\geq 1\}
such that
\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{N}<\infty
, we can conclude that the empirical CIs,
\{C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}:n\geq 1\}
, do not contain the population mean only finitely often almost surely:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{N\geq 1}\cup_{N^{\prime}\geq N}\{\mu_{N}\not\in C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}(\alpha_{N})\}\right)=0,\quad\iff\quad\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{1}_{\mu_{N}\not\in C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}(\alpha_{N})}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{<}}\;\infty.

(40)

The idea behind the proof is that both
\Lambda_{N}
and
\widehat{\Lambda}_{n}
can be well-approximated by quadratic functions near the origin, which then allows us to then get the required control over their inverses needed to obtain the result stated in
Theorem

3.16
. The details of the proof of are in
Appendix

F
and a detailed formal discussion of the meaning of almost-sure coverage is presented in
Section

F.1
.

Remark 3.17
.

The event
\{E_{N}\text{ infinitely often}\}
having probability zero implies that there is a random finite cutoff
N^{*}
, such that with probability one, the CI defined in (
39
) eventually contains the oracle interval
C^{\mathrm{orc}}_{N}
for all
N\geq N^{*}
. This implies
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mu_{N}\in C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n})\geq 1-\limsup_{N\to\infty}\alpha_{N}
. If, in addition, we choose a vanishing sequence of
\alpha_{N}\downarrow 0
with
\sum_{N}\alpha_{N}<\infty
, then by another application of Borel-Cantelli Lemma, we obtain (
40
), which implies that only finitely many miscoverages happen almost surely. This is exactly the defining property of “almost sure CIs”
(
16
)
, and also parallels the construction of hypothesis tests with only finitely many errors by
5
. Similar ideas have also been employed in related problems of prediction
25
and classification
10
.

Finite-sample valid
WoR
confidence intervals (e.g., Serfling-Hoeffding or Serfling-Bernstein CI) guarantee
\mathbb{P}(\mu_{N}\in C_{n})\geq 1-\alpha_{N}
for each fixed
N
, but they are generally wider and require prior knowledge of the range (for example,
[0,1]
) in which the observations lie. Classical CLT-based asymptotic confidence intervals, on the other hand, only guarantee
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mu_{N}\in C_{n})=1-\alpha
for a prespecified level
\alpha
, and in particular, may miscover infinitely often along the path. Thus, in practice our empirical CI defined in (
39
) sits between these two extremes. It does not require a bound on the range (like CLT, and unlike Serfling-Hoeffding or Serfling-Bernstein), but its coverage guarantee is strictly stronger than the CLT-based confidence intervals.

3.3.2
WoR
CI in Smooth Banach Spaces

To further illustrate the power of the coupling stated in Fact
2.1
, we now consider the case in which
\Sigma
is a
(2,D)
smooth Banach space with norm
\|\cdot\|
. Recall that a
(2,D)
-smooth Banach space is a complete normed linear space with the property that for any
u,v\in\Sigma
, we have

\displaystyle\|u+v\|^{2}+\|u-v\|^{2}\leq 2\|u\|^{2}+2D^{2}\|v\|^{2}.

(41)

For
D=1
, this reduces to the inequality implied by the parallelogram identity (that holds with equality for Hilbert spaces), and larger values of
D
in (
41
) indicate further deviation from the Hilbert space case.

Given
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\subset\Sigma
, with
\|x_{i}\|\leq d
for all
i\in[N]
, a
WoR
sample
X^{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
with
n/N=\beta\in(0,1)
, and a parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we construct a new level-
(1-\alpha)
CI for the population mean
\mu_{N}=(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}
using the
WoR
sample.

Theorem 3.18
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}_{N}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
denotes a finite subset in a
(2,D)
-smooth Banach space
(\Sigma,\|\cdot\|)
for a known
D\geq 1
,
\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\|x_{i}\|\leq d
for a known constant
d>0
. For any
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
and
\lambda\geq 0
, introduce the following terms:

\displaystyle\ell_{n}\equiv\ell_{n}(\alpha,\beta)=\log\left(\frac{2.36\sqrt{(1-\beta)n}}{\alpha}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad g(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(1+D^{2}\left(\beta e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}d}+\bar{\beta}e^{\lambda\beta d}-1-2\lambda\beta\bar{\beta}d\right)\right).

(42)

Then, given
X^{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
drawn
WoR
from
\mathcal{X}_{N}
and an
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the following is a level-
(1-\alpha)
CI for the population mean
\mu_{N}=(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}
:

\displaystyle C^{*}_{n}(X^{n},\alpha,d)=\{x\in\Sigma:\|\bar{X}_{n}-x\|\leq\epsilon^{*}_{n}\},\quad\text{with}\quad\epsilon^{*}_{n}=\inf_{\lambda>0}\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\frac{\ell_{n}}{n}+g(\lambda)\right).

(43)

For small enough values of
\lambda
, we can show that
g(\lambda)\lesssim\lambda^{2}
, which leads to the following closed-form CI

\displaystyle C_{n}(X^{n},\alpha,d)=\{x\in\Sigma:\|\bar{X}_{n}-x\|\leq\epsilon_{n}\},\quad\epsilon_{n}=dD\sqrt{\frac{3\bar{\beta}\ell_{n}}{n}}\quad\text{for}\;n\geq\frac{4(\max\{\beta,\bar{\beta}\})^{2}\ell_{n}}{3D^{2}\bar{\beta}}.

(44)

Remark 3.19
.

The constant
3
in the expression for
\epsilon_{n}
in (
44
) can be reduced to
2.4
for

n\geq\frac{5\ell_{n}}{0.81\bar{\beta}D^{2}d^{2}}.

For some typical values of the parameters, such as
\beta=0.3
,
d=1
,
D=1.5
and
\alpha=0.05
, we see that the condition is satisfied for
N\geq 67
. More generally, for sufficiently large values of
N
(with other parameters fixed), we can bring the constant arbitrarily close to
2
(see Lemma
G.1
in
Appendix

G
).

The starting point of the proof of
Theorem

3.18
is again the coupling inequality (
30
), which allows us to bound the probability of the event
\{\|\bar{X}_{n}-\mu_{N}\|\geq\epsilon\}
with
1.18\sqrt{n\bar{\beta}}
times the probability of the event
\{\|(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{N}(J_{i}-\beta)x_{i}\|\geq\epsilon\}
. Since the latter event involves studying the deviation of sum of independent zero-mean
\Sigma
-valued random variables, we can employ classical results such as
17
for obtaining a bound. The details are in
Appendix

G
.

Comparison with
19
.

This problem of deriving CIs for the mean of Banach space valued objects from a
WoR
sample was first considered by
19
. In particular,
19
combined Serfling’s martingale-based arguments with the concentration inequality of
17
to derive the following CI:

C_{n}^{(\text{Sch})}=[\bar{X}_{n}-\epsilon_{n,\text{Sch}},\bar{X}_{n}+\epsilon_{n,\text{Sch}}],\quad\text{with}\quad\epsilon_{n,\text{Sch}}=dD\sqrt{{\big(8(\bar{\beta}+1/N)\log(2/\alpha)\big)}/{n}}
. Comparing the width of the CI in (
44
) with that of
19
stated above, we see that

\left({\epsilon_{n,\text{Sch}}}/{\epsilon_{n}}\right)^{2}\;\geq\;{(8\log(2/\alpha))}{(3\ell_{n})}
,
where we used the fact that
(\bar{\beta}+1/N)/\bar{\beta})\geq 1
. Thus, we see that a sufficient condition for
\epsilon_{n}
to be smaller than
\epsilon_{n,\text{Sch}}
is
\log(1.18\sqrt{\bar{\beta}n})/\log(2/\alpha)\leq 5/3
. For natural values such as
N=20000
and
\alpha=0.05
, we can see in Figure
4
that this ratio is larger than
1
for all values of
\beta\in[0,1]
(equivalently
n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
). More generally, if we work in the “moderately accurate” regime with
\alpha_{N}\asymp n^{-\gamma}
for some
\gamma>0
, then, an asymptotic lower bound is

\left({\epsilon_{n,\text{Sch}}}/{\epsilon_{n}}\right)^{2}\gtrsim{(8\gamma/3)}/{\left(1/2+\gamma\right)}
.
Thus, a sufficient condition for asymptotically tighter CI is if
\alpha_{N}\asymp n^{-\gamma}
with
\gamma>0.3
. We numerically compare these CIs on an MNIST example in
Figure

4
in Appendix
H
.

4
Conclusion

This paper constructed confidence intervals (CIs) for means under without-replacement (
WoR
) sampling. For the case of finite alphabets, in
Theorem

3.4
we established a fundamental lower bound on the width of all
moderately accurate
(Definition
3.3
) confidence intervals in terms of inverse
WoR
rate functions. Then, by replacing the population rate quantities with their empirical analogs, in
Section

3.2
, we constructed a non-asymptotically-valid CI and proved that its width nearly matches the lower bound (
Theorem

3.9
). We also developed a computationally efficient dual characterization in Theorem
3.11
, that reduces the evaluation of the rate-function terms to a computationally efficient two-dimensional convex program, independent of the alphabet size. Both our upper and lower bounds for finite alphabets rely crucially on a certain probability approximations that are a consequence of a fundamental Bernoulli coupling identity (Fact
2.1
).

In
Section

3.3
, we move beyond finite alphabets and construct CIs in two practically relevant settings. In
Section

3.3.1
, we develop an
almost sure
CI for the case of
\Sigma=[0,1]
, which does not need knowledge of the range of the alphabet, and delivers a stronger coverage guarantee than CLT-based CIs. Finally, in
Section

3.3.2
we develop a simple and non-asymptotic CI for smooth Banach spaces.
A unifying device used throughout the paper is the Bernoulli coupling identity (Fact
2.1
), which transfers
WoR
sampling problems to settings governed by independent empirical measures and their large deviation rate functions.

Our work opens up several promising directions for future work, such as extending our finite alphabet guarantees of
Section

3.1
and
Section

3.2
to general alphabets, sharpening the lower bound of
Theorem

3.4
by removing the factor
1/2
, and extending our results to weighted
WoR
sampling and time-uniform settings.

Appendix A
Additional Background

We begin by recording some alternative representations of the term
I(P,\beta,Q)
used in Fact
2.2
.

Remark A.1
.

We can rewrite the information term
I(P,\beta,Q)
in a more interpretable form by introducing the distribution
R=(Q-\beta P)/\bar{\beta}
. A simple rearrangement then implies that
Q=\beta P+\bar{\beta}R
. Now, plugging this expression of
Q
into the definition of
I(P,\beta,Q)
gives us

\displaystyle I(P,\beta,Q)

\displaystyle=d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q)+\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}d_{\text{KL}}(R\parallel Q)=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\beta d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel\beta P+\bar{\beta}R)+\bar{\beta}d_{\text{KL}}(R\parallel\beta P+\bar{\beta}R)\right).

(45)

For
\beta=1/2
,
I(P,\beta,Q)
simply reduces to twice the Jensen-Shannon divergence between
P
and
R
. In fact, we can verify that
I(P,\beta,Q)
is an
f
-divergence, with
f(x)=x\log x+(\bar{\beta}/\beta)\frac{1-\beta x}{\bar{\beta}}\log\left(\frac{1-\beta x}{\bar{\beta}}\right)
. Finally, we note that
I(P,\beta,Q)
also admits the following interesting formulation

\displaystyle I(P,\beta,Q)=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(H(Q)-\beta H(P)-\bar{\beta}H(R)\right)=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(H(Q)-\beta H(P)-\bar{\beta}H\left(\frac{Q-\beta P}{\bar{\beta}}\right)\right),

(46)

where
H(P)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[-\log P(X)]
denotes the Shannon entropy of the discrete distribution
P
.

The remark above notes that the rate function
I
is an instance of an
f
-divergence. Recall that
f
-divergences are a generalization of relative entropy using a convex, lower semicontinuous function
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
with
f(1)=0
, such that for any two distributions
P\ll Q
, we have

\displaystyle D_{f}(P\parallel Q)=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[f\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}(X)\right)\right].

(47)

For the general case when
P\not\ll Q
, see
[
18
, Definition 7.1]
. As with relative entropy, the family of
f
-divergences also satisfy a data processing inequality (DPI), that we recall next.

Fact A.2

(Theorem 7.4 of
18
)
.

Let
K:\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
denote a stochastic kernel associated with two measurable spaces
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}})
and
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}})
, and for any
P_{X},Q_{X}
on
\mathcal{X}
, let
P_{Y}=P_{X}K
and
Q_{Y}=Q_{X}K
. That is, for any
E\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, we have
P_{Y}(E)=\int K(E\mid x)dP_{X}(x)
(define
Q_{Y}(E)
similarly). Then, for any
f
-divergence
D_{f}
, we have

\displaystyle D_{f}(P_{Y}\parallel Q_{Y})\leq D_{f}(P_{X}\parallel Q_{X}).

(48)

In other words, passing distributions through a common stochastic kernel can only bring them closer in
D_{f}
.

We use this fact in the proof of Lemma
D.1
in
Appendix

D
and in Lemma
E.1
in
Appendix

E
.

Finally, we recall a one-dimensional version of the inverse function theorem
[
22
, Theorem 2-11]
that will be used in the proof of
Theorem

3.16
in
Appendix

F
.

Fact A.3

(Inverse Function Theorem)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open set,
f\in C^{1}(U)
, and consider an
a\in U
with
f^{\prime}(a)\neq 0
, and
f(a)=b
. Then, there exist open intervals
V\subset U
(with
a\in V
) and
W\subset\mathbb{R}
(with
b\in W
), such that
f:V\to W
is a bijection with a
C^{1}
inverse
f^{-1}:W\to V
. Moreover, for all
y\in W
, we have

\displaystyle(f^{-1})^{\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(f^{-1}(y))}.

(49)

In particular, this implies that
(f^{-1})^{\prime}(b)=1/f^{\prime}(a)
.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
3.4
(Lower Bound on any CI Width)

We now present the details of the proof using the outline presented after the statement of
Theorem

3.4
.

Step 1:

Consider the following setting: Let
\mathcal{X}^{*}_{N}\subset\Sigma^{N}
denote a finite collection of items lying in the finite alphabet
\Sigma
with empirical distribution
P^{*}
, and suppose that
X^{n}\equiv(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
denotes the
n
without replacement samples from
(\mathcal{X}^{*}_{N},P^{*})
. For two other pairs
(\mathcal{X}_{N},P)
and
(\mathcal{Y}_{N},Q)
, consider the hypothesis testing problem:

\displaystyle H_{0}:P^{*}=P,\quad\text{versus}\quad H_{1}:P^{*}=Q.

(50)

Fix a
w>0
(to be specified below), and assume that
\mu_{Q}>\mu_{P}+w
, where
\mu_{Q}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{N}}y
and
\mu_{P}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{N}}x
.
Since we have a method
\mathcal{C}
of constructing CIs for the population mean, we can use it to define a hypothesis test for the above problem. In particular, we define the test that rejects the null if the CI contains
\mu_{Q}
:

\displaystyle\Psi(X^{n})=\boldsymbol{1}_{\mu_{Q}\in C_{n}},\quad\text{where}\quad C_{n}=\mathcal{C}(X^{n},\alpha_{N}).

(51)

Throughout the proof, we will assume that the parameter
w>0
satisfies
\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(|C_{n}|\geq w)<\alpha_{N}
.

Type-II error of
\Psi
.
Observe that the type-II error of this test is at most
\alpha_{N}
; that is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}(\Psi=0)=\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}\left(\mu_{Q}\not\in C_{n}\right)\leq\alpha_{N},

(52)

where the inequality follows simply from the defining property of a level-
(1-\alpha_{N})
CI for the mean. Interestingly, the test
\Psi
is so constructed, that the above property has no dependence on the width of the CI under
Q
.

Type-I error of
\Psi
.
A similar argument implies that type-I error of this test is at most
2\alpha_{N}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}\left(\Psi=1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}\left(\mu_{Q}\in C_{n}\right)

(53)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}\left(\left(\{\mu_{Q}\in C_{n}\}\cap\{\mu_{P}\in C_{n}\}\right)\,\bigcup\,\left(\{\mu_{Q}\in C_{n}\}\cap\{\mu_{P}\not\in C_{n}\}\right)\right)

(54)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}\left(|C_{n}|>w\right)+\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}\left(\{\mu_{P}\not\in C_{n}\}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{N}+\alpha_{N}=2\alpha_{N}.

(56)

Here, the first inequality uses the fact that by construction
|\mu_{Q}-\mu_{P}|>w
, and thus the event
\{\mu_{Q}\in C_{n}\}\cap\{\mu_{P}\in C_{n}\}
is contained in
\{|C_{n}|>w\}
, which occurs with probability at most
\alpha_{N}
.

Step 2:

We now show, by employing the construction used in the proof of
[
26
, Lemma 1]
, that there exists a hypothesis test,
\Psi^{\prime}
, that only depends on the type,
\widehat{P}(X^{n})
, and not on the order of observations, with error comparable to
\Psi
. In particular, let
A=\{x^{n}\in\Sigma^{n}:\Psi(x^{n})=1\}
denote the set of all sequences in
\Sigma^{n}
on which
\Psi
rejects the null
H_{0}
. For any type (or empirical distribution with
n
observations)
t
, let
S_{t}=\{x^{n}\in\Sigma^{n}:\widehat{P}(x^{n})=t\}
denote its associated
type-class
. Recall that the type class associated with
t
is simply the collection of all observations
x^{n}\in\Sigma^{n}
whose type is
t
. Then, let us define the following set:

\displaystyle A^{\prime}=\{t:t=T(x^{n})\text{ for }x^{n}\in A,\text{ and }|S_{t}\cap A|\geq|S_{t}|/2\}.

(57)

In words, the set
A^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{T}_{n}(\Sigma)\subset\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
, consists of those types with
n
observations, whose type-classes have a majority of their points in
A
(see Figure
2
for an illustration). Now, we can define the test

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}(X^{n})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\widehat{P}(X^{n})\in A^{\prime}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(58)

By construction, the performance of this type-based test is comparable to that of
\Psi
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}(\Psi^{\prime}=0)=\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}(\widehat{P}(X^{n})\in(A^{\prime})^{c})=\sum_{t\in(A^{\prime})^{c}}\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}(\widehat{P}(X^{n})=t)\leq 2\sum_{x^{n}\in A^{c}}\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}(x^{n})\leq 2\alpha_{N}.

(59)

Thus, we see that the type-II error of this new type-based test
\Psi^{\prime}
is at most two times that of
\Psi
. A similar argument also implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(\Psi^{\prime}=1)=\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(\widehat{P}(X^{n})\in A^{\prime})=\sum_{t\in A^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(\widehat{P}(X^{n})=t)\leq 2\sum_{x^{n}\in A}\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(X^{n}=x^{n})\leq 4\alpha_{N}.

(60)

Thus, the type-based test
\Psi^{\prime}
controls both the type-I and type-II errors at level
4\alpha_{N}
and
2\alpha_{N}
respectively.

Figure 2
:
Illustration of Step 2 in the proof of
Theorem

3.4
. In all the four plots, the large square represents the observation space
\Sigma^{n}
, and the smaller squares represent the different type-classes. The shaded gray region represents the rejection region associated with a given binary hypothesis test,
A=\{x^{n}\in\Sigma^{n}:\Psi(x^{n})=1\}
. Given any such
A
associated with a test
\Psi
, we can partition the type classes into red (less than half intersection with
A
), and blue (more than half intersection with
A
). This leads to a type-based test shown in the blue shaded region in the third plot, whose type-I and type-II errors are no more than twice that of the original test
\Psi
.

Step 3:

We now show that types that are too close to
P
(the null distribution) cannot lie in
A^{\prime}
, the rejection region of
\Psi^{\prime}
. We begin by assuming that
N
is large enough to ensure that
\log(1/4\alpha_{N})>3r_{N}
. Now, suppose that there exists a type
t^{*}
in
A^{\prime}
such that
I(t^{*},\beta,P)\leq g_{N}
, for some
0<ng_{N}<\log(1/4\alpha_{N})-r_{N}
. Then, observe the following:

\displaystyle 4\alpha_{N}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t\in A^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(\widehat{P}(X^{n})=t)\geq\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(\widehat{P}(X^{n})=t^{*})\geq\exp\left(-nI(t^{*},\beta,P)-r_{N}\right)

(61)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-ng_{N}-r_{N}\right).

(62)

On rearranging the above chain, we get that

\displaystyle\log(1/4\alpha_{N})\leq ng_{N}+r_{N}\quad\text{or}\quad ng_{N}\geq\log(1/4\alpha_{N})-r_{N},

(63)

which contradicts the assumption that
ng_{N}<\log(1/4\alpha_{N})-r_{N}
.

Now, with
\mathcal{T}_{n}
denoting the set of all possible types constructed using
x^{n}\in\Sigma^{n}
, we define
\widetilde{P}_{n}
as the projection (in terms of the rate function
I
) of
P
onto
\mathcal{T}_{n}
closest to
P
; that is,

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{n}=\argmin_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\;I(t,\beta,P)=\argmin_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\;d_{\text{KL}}(t\parallel P)+\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}d_{\text{KL}}\left(\frac{P-\beta t}{1-\beta}\parallel P\right).

(64)

Furthermore, there exists a
t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, such that
t_{s}=P(s)+\delta_{s}
, where
|\delta_{s}|\leq 1/n
and
\sum_{s\in\Sigma}\delta_{s}=0
. Using this fact, along with the inequality
\log(1+x)\leq x
for all
x\geq 0
, we get

\displaystyle d_{\text{KL}}(t\parallel P)=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}P(s)+\delta_{s})\log\left(1+\frac{\delta_{s}}{P(s)}\right)\leq\sum_{s\in\Sigma}\left(\delta_{s}+\frac{\delta_{s}^{2}}{P(s)}\right)\leq\frac{|\Sigma|N}{n^{2}}.

(65)

The last inequality above uses the fact that
P(s)\geq 1/N
for all
s\in\mathrm{supp}(P)\subset\Sigma
. An exactly analogous argument gives us the following bound

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}d_{\text{KL}}\left(\frac{P-\beta t}{P}\parallel P\right)\leq\frac{\beta}{\bar{\beta}}\frac{|\Sigma|N}{n^{2}}.

(66)

Combining the two previous displays, we get

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}I(t,\beta,P)\leq\frac{|\Sigma|}{\beta n}\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\bar{\beta}}\right)=\frac{|\Sigma|}{\beta\bar{\beta}n}.

(67)

which is less than
r_{N}/n
for
N
larger than
\exp(1/\beta\bar{\beta})
. Hence, for such values of
N
(and hence
n=\beta N
), the type
\widetilde{P}_{n}
must lie in the set
(A^{\prime})^{c}
for any level-
4\alpha_{N}
type-based hypothesis test
\Psi^{\prime}
.

Step 4:

The previous step immediately implies that

\displaystyle p_{1}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}\left(\widehat{P}(X^{n})=\widetilde{P}_{n}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}(\Psi^{\prime}=0)\leq 2\alpha_{N}.

(68)

Now, by another application of Fact
2.2
, we know that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\log p_{1}+I(\widetilde{P}_{n},\beta,Q)\right\rvert\leq 2(|\Sigma|+1)\frac{\log(N+1)}{n}=\frac{2r_{N}}{n}.

(69)

Using this inequality in (
68
), we get

\displaystyle-I(\widetilde{P}_{n},\beta,Q)-r_{N}\leq\frac{\log(p_{1})}{n}\leq\frac{\log(2\alpha_{N})}{n},

(70)

On rearranging, we get the required inequality

\displaystyle I(\widetilde{P}_{n},\beta,Q)+r_{N}\geq\frac{\log(1/2\alpha_{N})}{n}.

(71)

This can be interpreted as follows: for any
w>0
such that
\mathbb{P}_{P}(|C_{n}|>w)<\alpha_{N}
, the distribution
Q
with mean
\mu_{Q}\geq\mu_{P}+w
must satisfy (
71
). This immediately implies that

\displaystyle J(\widetilde{P}_{n},\beta,\mu_{P}+w)\coloneqq\inf\{I(\widetilde{P}_{n},\beta,Q):\mu_{Q}\geq w\}\geq\frac{\log(1/2\alpha_{N})-r_{N}}{n}.

(72)

This leads to the conclusion that

\displaystyle w\geq J(\widetilde{P}_{n},\beta,\cdot)^{-1}\left(\frac{\log(1/2\alpha_{N})-r_{N}}{n}\right),\quad\text{with}\quad\quad r_{N}=(|\Sigma|+1)\log(N+1).

(73)

The right side of the above inequality is nonzero only if
\alpha_{N}
is sufficiently small to make the argument of
J^{-1}
positive. A sufficient condition for this is if
\alpha_{N}
is smaller than
(1/2)(N+1)^{-|\Sigma|-1}
, which falls within our definition of
moderate accuracy
(Definition
3.3
).

Completing the proof.

Using the notation of the statement of Theorem
3.4
, we can interpret (
72
) as
w\geq b^{*}_{+}-\mu_{P}
. This implies that
\mathbb{P}_{P}(|C_{n}|\geq b^{*}_{+}-\mu_{P})\geq\mathbb{P}_{P}(|C_{n}|\geq w)\geq\alpha_{N}
. Repeating the same argument with
\mu_{Q}\leq\mu_{P}-w
yields
\mathbb{P}_{P}(|C_{n}|\geq\mu_{P}-b^{*}_{-})\geq\alpha_{N}
, and together they imply

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{P}\left(|C_{n}|\geq\max\{b^{*}_{+}-\mu_{P},\,\mu_{P}-b^{*}_{-}\}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{P}\left(|C_{n}|\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(b^{*}_{+}-b^{*}_{-}\right)\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{P}\left(|C_{n}|\geq w\right)\leq\alpha_{N}.

(74)

Setting
P\leftarrow\widehat{P}_{N}
and
\mu_{P}\leftarrow\widehat{\mu}_{N}
completes the proof.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
3.7
(Validity of Proposed CI)

Given any
x^{n}\in\Sigma^{n}
, we denote its type or empirical distribution by
\widehat{P}(x^{n})
. Furthermore, for any
n\geq 1
, we use
\mathcal{T}_{n}
to denote the set of all distinct types possible with observations drawn from
\Sigma^{n}
. Consider the probability of the event that the population mean
\mu_{N}
does not lie in
[b_{-},b_{+}]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{N}\not\in[b_{-},b_{+}]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(b_{+}<\mu_{N})+\mathbb{P}(b_{-}>\mu_{N}).

(75)

We will show that both the terms on the right side of the inequality above are no larger than
\alpha_{N}/2
to complete the proof. Note that
b_{+}
depends on the observed data only through its type
\widehat{P}(X^{n})
, which implies the following chain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(b_{+}(\widehat{P}(X^{n}))<\mu_{N}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:b_{+}(t)<\mu_{N}}\mathbb{P}(\widehat{P}(X^{n})=t)

(76)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:b_{+}(t)<\mu_{N}}\exp\left(-nI(t,\beta,\widehat{P}_{N})+r_{N}\right)

(77)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:b_{+}(t)<\mu_{N}}\exp\left(-nJ_{+}(t,\beta,\mu_{N})+r_{N}\right)

(78)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:b_{+}(t)<\mu_{N}}\exp\left(-nJ_{+}(t,\beta,b_{+}(t))+r_{N}\right).

(79)

In the above display:

•

The probability upper bound (
77
) follows directly from Fact
2.2
.

•

The inequality (
78
) uses the fact that
I(t,\beta,\widehat{P}_{N})\geq J_{+}(t,\beta,\mu_{N})=\inf_{Q:t\ll Q,\,\mu_{Q}\geq\mu_{N}}I(t,\beta,Q)
.

•

The inequality (
79
) uses the fact that
\mu_{N}>b_{+}(t)
and that
J_{+}(t,\beta,\cdot)
is an increasing function of it’s third argument.

Continuing from (
79
), we now observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mu_{N}>b_{+})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:b_{+}(t)<\mu_{N}}\exp\left(-c_{N}+r_{N}\right)

(80)

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\{t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:b_{+}(t)<\mu_{N}\}\right|\exp\left(-c_{N}+r_{N}\right)

(81)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\mathcal{T}_{n}\right|\exp\left(-c_{N}+r_{N}\right)=\exp\left(-c_{N}+r_{N}+\log|\mathcal{T}_{n}|\right)

(82)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-c_{N}+2r_{N}\right)

(83)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\log(2/\alpha_{N})-2r_{N}+2r_{N}\right)=\alpha_{N}/2.

(84)

Above, we have used the following arguments:

•

The inequality (
80
) follows from the definition of
b_{+}(t)=\inf\{m\geq 0:J_{+}(t,\beta,m)\geq c_{N}/n\}
; which implies that the value of
J_{+}(b_{+}(t))
is at least
c_{N}/n
.

•

The inequality (
83
) relies on the fact that the number of distinct types based on points in
\Sigma^{n}
can be upper bounded by
(n+1)^{|\Sigma|}
. This, in turn, implies that
\log(|\mathcal{T}_{n}|)\leq r_{N}=(|\Sigma|+1)\log(N+1)
.

Following the exact same steps, we can show that the other term in (
75
),
\mathbb{P}(b_{-}>\mu_{N})
, can also be bounded by
\alpha_{N}/2
. Together, these two statements lead to the required conclusion of
Theorem

3.7
.

Proof of Lemma
D.1

Consider any
Q\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])
with mean
\mu_{Q}\geq m
with
I(P,\beta,Q)<\infty
, and let
K:2^{\{0,1\}}\times[0,1]
denote a channel (i.e., stochastic kernel), such that for any
x\in[0,1]
,
K(\cdot,x)
is the
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(x)
distribution. In other words, suppose we input a
[0,1]
-valued random variable
X
to this channel, then the output
Y
has an unconditional distribution
P_{Y}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\mathbb{E}[X])
. Now, let
Y\sim P_{Y}
and
Y^{\prime}\sim Q_{Y}
denote the outputs after passing
X\sim P
and
X^{\prime}\sim Q
respectively through the channel
K
. Hence,
P_{Y}=\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\mu_{P})
and
Q_{Y}=\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\mu_{Q})
.

Recall that in Remark
A.1
we established that
I
is an
f
-divergence with
f(x)=x\log x+(\bar{\beta}/\beta)\frac{1-\beta x}{\bar{\beta}}\log\left(\frac{1-\beta x}{\bar{\beta}}\right)
. Hence, by the data processing inequality for
f
-divergences
[
18
, Theorem 7.4]
, we get that

\displaystyle I(P,\beta,Q)\geq I(P_{Y},\beta,Q_{Y})=d_{KL}(\mu_{P}\parallel\mu_{Q})+\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}d_{KL}(\mu_{R}\parallel\mu_{Q}),\quad\text{where}\quad\mu_{R}=\frac{\mu_{Q}-\beta\mu_{P}}{\bar{\beta}}.

(94)

The next step is to apply Pinsker’s inequality, which says that
d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q)\geq 2\|P-Q\|_{TV}^{2}
, to get

\displaystyle I(P,\beta,Q)\geq 2|\mu_{P}-\mu_{Q}|^{2}+\frac{2\bar{\beta}}{\beta}\left\lvert\frac{\mu_{Q}-\beta\mu_{P}-\bar{\beta}\mu_{Q}}{\bar{\beta}}\right\rvert^{2}=\left(2+\frac{2\beta}{\bar{\beta}}\right)|\mu_{Q}-\mu_{P}|^{2}=\frac{2}{\bar{\beta}}|\mu_{Q}-\mu_{P}|^{2}.

(95)

This implies that for any
m\geq\mu_{P}
, we have

\displaystyle J_{+}(P,\beta,m)=\inf_{Q:\mu_{Q}\geq m}I(P,\beta,Q)\geq\inf_{\mu_{Q}\geq m\geq\mu_{P}}\frac{2}{\bar{\beta}}|\mu_{Q}-\mu_{P}|^{2}=\frac{2}{\bar{\beta}}|m-\mu_{P}|^{2}.

(96)

Combining this inequality with the definition of
b_{+}=\inf\{m\geq 0:J_{+}(\widehat{P}_{n},\beta,m)\geq c_{N}/n\}
, we get

\displaystyle b_{+}\leq\widehat{\mu}_{n}+\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\beta}c_{N}}{2n}},\quad\text{where}\quad\widehat{\mu}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{P}_{n}}[X].

(97)

An exactly analogous argument gives us
b_{-}\geq\widehat{\mu}_{n}-\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\beta}c_{N}}{2n}}
, which concludes the proof.

Proof of Lemma

We obtain this result by using Taylor approximation of the functions
\phi_{i}(q)=p_{i}\log(p_{i}/q)+(\bar{\beta}/\beta)r\log(r/q)
, around
q=p_{i}
and with
r=(q-\beta p_{i})/\bar{\beta}
. In particular, observe that for
i\in[k]
, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(q)=\phi_{i}(p_{i})+\phi_{i}^{\prime}(p_{i})(q-p_{i})+\frac{\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(p_{i})}{2}(q-p_{i})^{2}+\frac{\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime\prime}(z_{i})}{6}(q-p_{i})^{3},\quad\text{for some}\quad z_{i}\in[p_{i},q]\cup[q,p_{i}].

(98)

Let us now look at these terms individually.

•

At
q=p_{i}
, we have
\phi_{i}(p_{i})=0
. This is because

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(p_{i})=p_{i}\log\left(\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}}\right)+\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}\left(\frac{p_{i}-\beta p_{i}}{\bar{\beta}}\right)\log\left(\frac{p_{i}-\beta p_{i}}{\bar{\beta}p_{i}}\right)=0.

(99)

•

Next, we look at the first derivative
\phi_{i}^{\prime}(q)
:

\displaystyle\phi_{i}^{\prime}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dq}\left(p_{i}\log\left(\frac{p_{i}}{q}\right)+\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}r\log\left(\frac{r}{q}\right)\right)

(100)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p_{i}}{q}+\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}\left(r^{\prime}\log\left(\frac{r}{q}\right)+r\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right)=-\frac{p_{i}}{q}+\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{r}{q}\right)+\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}\left(r^{\prime}-\frac{r}{q}\right)

(101)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p_{i}}{q}+\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{r}{q}\right)+\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}-\frac{q-\beta p_{i}}{\bar{\beta}q}\right)=-\frac{p_{i}}{q}+\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{r}{q}\right)+\frac{p_{i}}{q}

(102)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{r}{q}\right).

(103)

Plugging in the value of
q=p_{i}
(which implies
r=p_{i}
as well), we get that
\phi^{\prime}_{i}(p_{i})=0
.

•

The second derivative of
\phi_{i}
has the following expression:

\displaystyle\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dq}\phi_{i}^{\prime}(q)=\frac{d}{dq}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{r}{q}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}-\frac{1}{q}\right)=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{q-\beta p_{i}}-\frac{1}{q}\right)

(104)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{i}}{(q-\beta p_{i})q}.

(105)

This implies that
\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(p_{i})=1/\bar{\beta}p_{i}
.

•

Finally, we need to evaluate the third derivative of
\phi_{i}

\displaystyle\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime\prime}(q)=\frac{d}{dq}\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(q)=\frac{d}{dq}\left(\frac{p_{i}}{q(q-\beta p_{i})}\right)=\frac{q(q-\beta p_{i})-p_{i}(2q-\beta p_{i})}{q^{2}(q-\beta p_{i})^{2}}.

(106)

Now, under the assumption that
q=p_{i}+h_{i}
, with

, we get

\displaystyle\left\lvert\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime\prime}(p_{i}+h_{i})\right\rvert\leq\frac{|q(q-\beta p_{i})|+p_{i}|2q-\beta p_{i}|}{(p_{i}-\kappa p_{i})^{2}(p_{i}-\kappa p_{i}-\beta p_{i})^{2}}\leq\frac{(1+\kappa)(\bar{\beta}+\kappa)+(2-\beta+2\kappa)}{(1-\kappa)^{2}(\bar{\beta}-\kappa)^{2}}\frac{1}{p_{i}^{2}}.

(107)

Plugging all these values, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{i}(p_{i}+h_{i})=0+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{h_{i}^{2}}{2\bar{\beta}p_{i}}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}_{i}(z_{i})}{6}h_{i}^{3},

(108)

where each
z_{i}\in[p_{i}+h_{i},p_{i}]\cup[p_{i},p_{i}+h_{i}]
. This completes the proof.

Proof of Lemma
D.3

Recall that by definition

\displaystyle J_{+}(P,\beta,\mu+\delta)

\displaystyle=\inf\{I(P,\beta,Q):Q\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]),\;\mu_{Q}\geq\mu+\delta\}

(109)

\displaystyle\leq\inf\{I(P,\beta,Q):Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Sigma),\;\mu_{Q}\geq\mu+\delta\}.

(110)

Let
Q=P+h
, where
h=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})
is such that
\sum_{i}h_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{i}s_{i}\geq\delta
. Let us fix a
\kappa\in(0,\bar{\beta})
, and define
\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}=\{h\in[-1,1]^{\Sigma}:\sum_{i}h_{i}=0,\;\sum_{i}h_{i}s_{i}\geq\delta,\;|h_{i}|/p_{i}\leq\kappa\}
. Observe that this leads to

\displaystyle J_{+}(P,\beta,\mu+\delta)\leq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}}\frac{1}{2\bar{\beta}}\sum_{i}\frac{h_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}+R_{3}(h).

(111)

Let us define
h^{*}
as the solution of

\displaystyle h^{*}\in\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\kappa}}\;\frac{1}{2\bar{\beta}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{h_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}.

(112)

We will derive a closed-form expression for
h^{*}
in the following steps:

•

First, we will consider the optimization problem without the “box constraints”; that is, without the constraints that
|h_{i}|\leq\kappa p_{i}
.

•

For this relaxed problem, we will show that we can replace the mean inequality constraint with an equality.

•

Then, we will obtain a closed form solution to this problem using Lagrange multipliers.

•

Finally, we will verify that the solution of this problem also satisfies the box constraints of the original problem.

Let
h
be any strictly feasible vector satisfying
\sum_{i}h_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i}h_{i}s_{i}=\delta+t
for some
t>0
. Let let
\theta=\delta/(\delta+t)
, and define
h^{\prime}=\theta h
. Observe that
\sum_{i}h^{\prime}_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i}h^{\prime}_{i}s_{i}=\theta(\delta+t)=\delta
, and the objective at
h^{\prime}
strictly decreases:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{h_{i}^{\prime 2}}{2p_{i}}=\theta^{2}\sum_{i}\frac{h_{i}^{2}}{2p_{i}}<\sum_{i}\frac{h_{i}^{2}}{2p_{i}}.

(113)

Thus, without loss of optimality, we can work with equality mean constraint, and write the following Lagrangian with
\lambda,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle L(h,\lambda,\gamma)=\sum_{i}\frac{h_{i}^{2}}{2p_{i}}-\lambda\sum_{i}h_{i}-\gamma\left(\sum_{i}s_{i}h_{i}-\delta\right).

(114)

The stationarity condition then implies

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_{i}}=\frac{h_{i}}{p_{i}}-\lambda-\gamma s_{i}=0\quad\implies\quad h_{i}=p_{i}(\lambda+\gamma s_{i}).

(115)

On enforcing the two equality constraints, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i}h_{i}=\lambda\sum_{i}p_{i}+\gamma\sum_{i}p_{i}s_{i}=\lambda+\gamma\mu=0

(116)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}s_{i}h_{i}=\lambda\sum_{i}p_{i}s_{i}+\gamma\sum_{i}p_{i}s_{i}^{2}=\lambda\mu+\gamma(\sigma^{2}+\mu^{2})=\delta.

(117)

Solving for
(\lambda,\gamma)
, we get

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{\delta}{\sigma^{2}},\lambda=-\frac{\delta}{\sigma^{2}}\mu,\quad\text{which implies}\quad h^{*}_{i}=p_{i}\left(-\frac{\delta}{\sigma^{2}}\mu+\frac{\delta}{\sigma^{2}}s_{i}\right)=\frac{\delta}{\sigma^{2}}p_{i}(s_{i}-\mu).

(118)

Finally, we observe that this solution also satisfies the “box constraint” necessary for the Taylor expansion in Lemma
D.2
to hold; that is,

\displaystyle\frac{|h^{*}_{i}|}{p_{i}}=\frac{\delta}{\sigma^{2}}|s_{i}-\mu|\leq\frac{\delta}{\sigma^{2}}\leq\kappa,

(119)

where the last inequality follows from the assumption made on
\delta
. Plugging this value of
h^{*}
in (
111
), we get

\displaystyle J_{+}(P,\beta,\mu+\delta)\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\bar{\beta}\sigma^{2}}+\frac{C_{\beta,\kappa}}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\delta^{3}p_{i}|s_{i}-\mu|^{3}}{\sigma^{6}}\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\bar{\beta}\sigma^{2}}+\frac{C_{\beta,\kappa}}{6\sigma^{4}}\delta^{3}.

(120)

This completes the proof.

Appendix E
Proof of Theorem

We begin by stating the primal form of the definition of the (mean-constrained) rate function:

\displaystyle I(P,\beta,Q)\coloneqq d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q)+\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}d_{\text{KL}}\left(\frac{Q-\beta P}{1-\beta}\parallel Q\right)

\displaystyle Q\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])

(123)

(124)

\displaystyle\beta P(s)-Q(s)\leq 0,\;\text{for }s\in\Sigma.

(125)

We will show that the optimal value of this primal minimization problem is the same as the optimum value of the dual problem described in

Outline of the proof.

Recall that we have

, with

, we will use

and

and

to denote the value

. For any

, we will use
\bar{\beta}
to denote

. We now describe the steps involved in our proof.

•

Reduction to a finite-dimensional problem:
The problem as stated in (
122
) has an infinite-dimensional domain: The space of all probability distributions on the bounded domain with two additional restrictions. As the first step, we will observe in Lemma
E.1
that an optimal solution to (

) must lie in a finite-dimensional domain, that we denote henceforth by
\mathcal{D}_{P}
.

•

Verification of strong duality:
We begin by showing in Lemma

that there exists a point

in

that is strictly feasible, and thus Slater’s condition for strong duality is satisfied. This implies that the duality gap is zero and that the primal and dual problems have the same optimal values.

•

Characterization of the primal optimal:
Next, we will use first-order conditions to characterize the primal optimal solution. In particular, in this step, we will show that the primal optimal solution places a mass
q^{*}_{i}=\frac{\beta p_{i}}{1-\bar{\beta}e_{i}}
with
e_{i}=\exp(\beta(a+bs_{i}+c_{i}))
on the point
s_{i}\in\Sigma
.

•

Simplification of dual objective:
Finally, by using the KKT conditions, we will simplify the dual objective problem and reduce it to a two-dimensional optimization problem (as compared to the possibly infinite-dimensional primal problem).

Reduction to a finite-dimensional problem.

The first step of the proof is that we can reduce (
122
) to a finite-dimensional problem.

Lemma E.1
.

For any feasible point,
\widetilde{Q}
, for the optimization problem stated in (
122
), there exists another point
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Sigma_{1})\subset\mathcal{P}([0,1])
with
\Sigma_{1}\coloneqq\Sigma\cup\{1\}
, such that
I(P,\beta,Q)\leq I(P,\beta,\widetilde{Q})
, and
\mu_{Q}\geq\mu_{\widetilde{Q}}
.

As a consequence,
J_{+}(P,\beta,m)
can be written as the optimum value associated with the following minimization problem:

minimize

\displaystyle I(P,\beta,Q)\quad\text{subject to}\;\;Q\in\mathcal{D}_{P},

(126)

where the primal feasible set
\mathcal{D}_{P}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{P}\coloneqq\{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Sigma_{1}):\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X]\geq m,\;\text{and}\;Q(s)\geq\beta P(s),\,\forall s\in\Sigma\},\quad\text{with}\quad\Sigma_{1}\coloneqq\Sigma\cup\{1\}.

(127)

Proof.

Any feasible
\widetilde{Q}\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])
for which
I(P,\beta,\widetilde{Q})<\infty
must place non-zero mass at each
s\in\Sigma
. Now, let us define
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Sigma_{1})
as follows:

\displaystyle Q(s)=\begin{cases}\widetilde{Q}(s),&\text{ if }s\in\Sigma\\
\widetilde{Q}([0,1]\setminus\Sigma),&\text{ if }s=1.\end{cases}

(128)

Since
\widetilde{Q}
is feasible, we have the following:

\displaystyle m\leq\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{Q}}[X]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}s\times\widetilde{Q}(s)+\int_{[0,1]\setminus\Sigma}sd\widetilde{Q}(s)

(129)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}s\times Q(s)+\int_{[0,1]\setminus\Sigma}sd\widetilde{Q}(s)

\displaystyle(\text{since }Q(s)=\widetilde{Q}(s),\;\forall s\in\Sigma)

(130)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in\Sigma}s\times Q(s)+\int_{[0,1]\setminus\Sigma}1d\widetilde{Q}(s)

\displaystyle(\text{since }s\leq 1)

(131)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\Sigma_{1}}s\times Q(s)=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X]

(132)

Thus
Q
is also feasible for (
122
).
Furthermore, we can interpret
Q
as a distribution obtained by passing
\widetilde{Q}
through a stochastic kernel (or a channel in information-theoretic terminology)
\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{B}_{[0,1]}\times[0,1]\to[0,1]
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\cdot\mid s)=\begin{cases}\delta_{s},&\text{ if }s\in\Sigma,\\
\delta_{1},&\text{ if }s\in[0,1]\setminus\Sigma,\end{cases}

(133)

where
\delta_{s}
denotes the unit point-mass at
s\in[0,1]
. Note that this channel has no effect on a
P
supported on
\Sigma
. Thus, an application of data processing inequality (DPI) for
f
-divergences
[
18
, Theorem 7.4]
implies that

\displaystyle I(P,\beta,\widetilde{Q})\geq I(\mathcal{K}P,\beta,\mathcal{K}\widetilde{Q})=I(P,\beta,Q).

(134)

Thus, for every feasible
\widetilde{Q}\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])
, there exists a feasible
Q\in\mathcal{D}_{P}
with equal or lower objective function value. This completes the proof.
∎

Verification of strong duality.

Due to the convexity of relative entropy, it follows that
I(P,\beta,Q)
is a convex function over the (convex) domain
\mathcal{D}_{P}
. We now show that for this convex optimization problem, sufficient conditions for strong duality to hold are satisfied.

Lemma E.2
.

Introduce the dual variables
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k})\in[0,\infty)^{k}
lying in the dual feasible set
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)^{k+1}
. Recall that
k
denotes the cardinality of the finite alphabet
\Sigma\subset(0,1)
. Then, we have the following:

\displaystyle J_{+}(P,\beta,m)=\sup_{(\rho,\lambda,\boldsymbol{\gamma})\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)^{k+1}}\;\inf_{Q\in[0,\infty)^{k+1}}L(Q,\rho,\lambda,\boldsymbol{\gamma}),\quad\text{where}

(135)

\displaystyle L(Q,\rho,\lambda,\boldsymbol{\gamma})\coloneqq I(P,\beta,Q)+\rho\left(1-\sum_{s\in\Sigma_{1}}Q(s)\right)+\lambda\left(m-\sum_{s\in\Sigma_{1}}s\times Q(s)\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma_{i}(\beta P(s_{i})-Q(s_{i})).

(136)

Proof.

Following
3
, a sufficient condition (Slater’s constraint qualification) for strong duality is if there exists a strictly feasible point in the domain of the primal problem.

To verify this, let us define
Q_{\epsilon}=\epsilon P+(1-\epsilon)\delta_{1}
, where
\delta_{1}
denotes the point-mass at
x=1
(i.e., the probability distribution that places its entire mass at
x=1
). By construction
Q_{\epsilon}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}(\Sigma_{1})
for all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Let
\mu_{P}
denote
\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]
, and observe that for
0<\epsilon<(1-m)/(1-\mu)
, the distribution
Q_{\epsilon}
satisfies
m-\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\epsilon}}[X]<0
, where
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma\cup\{1\}
. Furthermore, for any
s\in\Sigma\setminus\{1\}
, we have
\beta P(s)-Q_{\epsilon}(s)=(\beta-\epsilon)P(s)<0
for
\epsilon>\beta
.

Thus, with
\beta<\epsilon<(1-m)/(1-\mu_{P})
, the point
Q_{\epsilon}
is strictly primal feasible, which in turn implies that strong duality holds. As a result, the rate function term
J_{+}(P,\beta,m)
as defined in (
122
) is also equal to the dual formulation, defined in (
135
).
∎

Primal and dual optimal solutions.

The objective function in the dual optimization problem is

\displaystyle M(\rho,\lambda,\boldsymbol{\gamma})\coloneqq\inf_{Q\in[0,\infty)^{k+1}}L(Q,\rho,\lambda,\boldsymbol{\gamma}),\quad\text{and}\quad J_{+}(P,\beta,m)=\sup_{(\rho,\lambda,\boldsymbol{\gamma})\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)^{k+1}}M(\rho,\lambda,\boldsymbol{\gamma}).

(137)

Our next result presents a nearly complete characterization of the primal optimal variable.

Lemma E.3
.

Suppose the finite alphabet
\Sigma
does not contain the end-point
1
, and let
(\rho^{*},\lambda^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)^{k+1}
and
Q^{*}\in\mathcal{D}_{P}
denote the optimal dual and primal variables. Then, for every
s\in\Sigma
, the following is true:

\displaystyle\rho^{*}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\right)-\lambda^{*},\quad\text{and}\quad Q^{*}(s)=\frac{\beta P(s)}{1-\exp\left(-\beta\left(\lambda^{*}(1-s)-\gamma^{*}_{s}\right)\right)}.

(138)

The proof of this result is given in

.

Simplifying the dual objective.

For the final step, we use the complementary slackness conditions to obtain the final simplified form of the dual optimization problem.

Lemma E.4
.

The dual optimal
(\rho^{*},\lambda^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})
must satisfy
\gamma_{s}^{*}=0
for all
s\in\Sigma
. This fact implies that the rate function
J_{+}(P,\beta,m)
is equal to the optimum value of the following problem:

\displaystyle J_{+}(P,\beta,m)=\sup_{(\lambda,\rho)\in\mathcal{D}_{D}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\log\left(\frac{1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda X+\rho)}}{\beta}\right)\right]+\lambda(m-\beta\mu_{P})+\rho\bar{\beta}\right\},

(139)

where
\mathcal{D}_{D}=\{(\lambda,\rho)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}:\rho+\lambda\leq(1/\beta)\log(1\bar{\beta})\}
and
\mu_{P}=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}s\times P(s)
.

The proof of this result is in

E.1
Proof of Lemma
E.3

Let
\rho^{*},\lambda^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}
and
Q^{*}
denote a pair of dual and primal optimal variables. Then, we must have the following:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial Q(s)}L(Q,\rho^{*},\lambda^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\right\rvert_{Q=Q^{*}}=0,\quad\text{for }s\in\Sigma\subset(0,1).

(140)

At the end point
s=1
, we have the inequality constraint

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial dQ(1)}L(Q,\rho^{*},\lambda^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\right)-\rho^{*}-\lambda^{*}\geq 0

(141)

For other values of
s\in\Sigma\setminus\{1\}
, the condition reduces to (dropping the arguments of
L
)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial Q(s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial Q(s)}\left(P(s)\log\left(\frac{P(s)}{Q(s)}\right)\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial Q(s)}\left(\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}\left(\frac{Q(s)-\beta P(s)}{\bar{\beta}}\right)\log\left(\frac{Q(s)-\beta P(s)}{\bar{\beta}Q(s)}\right)\right)

(142)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\partial}{\partial Q(s)}\left(Q(s)\left(\rho^{*}+\lambda^{*}+\gamma_{s}^{*}\right)\right)

(143)

\displaystyle=:T_{1}+T_{2}-\left(\rho^{*}+\lambda^{*}+\gamma^{*}_{s}\right)

(144)

We now derive the expressions of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial Q(s)}\left(P(s)\log\left(\frac{P(s)}{Q(s)}\right)\right)=-\frac{P(s)}{Q(s)}.

(145)

Similarly, to evaluate
T_{2}
, we introduce the notation
R(s)=(Q(s)-\beta P(s))/\bar{\beta}
, and proceed as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{\bar{\beta}}T_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial Q(s)}\left(R(s)\log\left(\frac{R(s)}{Q(s)}\right)\right)

(146)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial R(s)}{\partial Q(s)}\log\left(\frac{R(s)}{Q(s)}\right)+R(s)\frac{\partial}{\partial Q(s)}\log\left(\frac{R(s)}{Q(s)}\right)

(147)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\log\left(\frac{R(s)}{Q(s)}\right)+R(s)\frac{\partial}{\partial Q(s)}\log\left(\frac{R(s)}{Q(s)}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{since }\partial R(s)/\partial Q(s)=1/\bar{\beta})

(148)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\log\left(\frac{R(s)}{Q(s)}\right)+R(s)\frac{\partial}{\partial Q(s)}\left(\log R(s)-\log Q(s)\right)

(149)

Now, observing that
\partial\log R(s)/\partial Q(s)=(1/R(s))\times\partial R(s)/\partial Q(s)=(1/R(s))\times(1/\bar{\beta})
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{\bar{\beta}}T_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\log\left(\frac{R(s)}{Q(s)}\right)+R(s)\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}R(s)}-\frac{1}{Q(s)}\right)

(150)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\log\left(\frac{R(s)}{Q(s)}\right)+\frac{Q(s)-\beta P(s)}{\bar{\beta}}\left(\frac{\beta P(s)}{Q(s)\,\left(Q(s)-\beta P(s)\right)}\right)

(151)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\log\left(\frac{R(s)}{Q(s)}\right)+\frac{\beta}{\bar{\beta}}\frac{P(s)}{Q(s)}

(152)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\log\left(\frac{R(s)}{Q(s)}\right)-\frac{\beta}{\bar{\beta}}T_{1},

(153)

where the last equality uses the fact that
T_{1}=P(s)/Q(s)
as derived in (
145
). Plugging the above equality back into (
144
), we get for any
s\in\Sigma\setminus\{1\}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial Q(s)}=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{Q(s)-\beta P(s)}{\bar{\beta}Q(s)}\right)-\lambda^{*}s-\rho^{*}-\gamma_{s}^{*}.

(154)

Setting this equal to zero gives us the required
Q^{*}(s)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{Q^{*}(s)-\beta P(s)}{\bar{\beta}Q^{*}(s)}\right)-\lambda^{*}s-\rho^{*}-\gamma_{s}^{*}=0

(155)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{-}\beta\frac{P(s)}{Q^{*}(s)}={\bar{\beta}}\exp\left(\beta\left(\lambda^{*}s+\rho^{*}+\gamma^{*}_{s}\right)\right)

(156)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\beta\frac{P(s)}{Q^{*}(s)}=1-{\bar{\beta}}\exp\left(\beta\left(\lambda^{*}s+\rho^{*}+\gamma^{*}_{s}\right)\right)

(157)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle Q^{*}(s)=\frac{\beta P(s)}{1-{\bar{\beta}}\exp\left(\beta\left(\lambda^{*}s+\rho^{*}+\gamma^{*}_{s}\right)\right)}.

(158)

This completes the proof of Lemma
E.3
.

Proof of Lemma
E.4

This result follows by an application of the following complementary slackness conditions:

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}\left(m-\sum_{s\in\Sigma_{1}}s\times Q^{*}(s)\right)=0

(159)

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}_{s}\left(Q^{*}(s)-\beta P(s)\right)=0,\quad\text{for all}\quad s\in\Sigma.

(160)

•

By construction, the optimum
Q^{*}(s)
must be nonnegative, which means that
1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda^{*}s+\rho^{*}+\gamma^{*}_{s})}\in(0,1)
, which leads to the strict inequality
Q^{*}(s)>\beta P(s)
. Hence, to ensure the complementary slackness condition (
160
), we must have
\gamma^{*}_{s}=0
for all
s\in\Sigma
.

•

Next, the condition that
Q^{*}(s)
must be in
[0,\infty)
means that
\lambda^{*}
cannot be zero. This combined with (
159
) means that we must have
\sum_{s\in\Sigma_{1}}s\times Q^{*}(s)=m
.

These points allow us to reduce the dual to a two-variable optimization problem, with the primal optimal variables completely characterized by
\lambda^{*}>0
, and
\rho^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\rho^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\rho^{*}\leq(1/\beta)\log(1/\bar{\beta})-\lambda^{*}
:

\displaystyle Q^{*}(s)\equiv Q_{\lambda^{*},\rho^{*}}(s)=\frac{\beta P(s)}{1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda^{*}s+\rho^{*})}},\;\text{for }s\in\Sigma\subset(0,1),\quad\text{and}\quad Q^{*}_{\lambda^{*},\rho^{*}}(1)=1-Q^{*}_{\lambda^{*},\rho*}(\Sigma).

(161)

In other words, we can find
J_{+}(P,\beta,m)
by optimizing
I(P,\beta,Q_{\lambda,\rho})
, over all
Q_{\lambda,\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(\Sigma_{1})
of the form stated in (
161
), over all feasible
(\lambda,\rho)
:

\displaystyle J_{+}(P,\beta,m)=\sup_{(\lambda,\rho)\in\mathcal{D}_{D}}\left\{d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q_{\lambda,\rho})+\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}d_{\text{KL}}\left(R_{\lambda,\rho}\parallel Q_{\lambda,\rho}\right)\right\},

(162)

where

\displaystyle R_{\lambda,\rho}=(Q_{\lambda,\rho}-\beta P)/\bar{\beta},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}_{D}=\{(\lambda,\rho)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}:\lambda+\rho\leq 1/\beta\log(1/\bar{\beta})\}

(163)

The first term of the dual objective is equal to

\displaystyle d_{\text{KL}}(P\parallel Q_{\lambda,\rho})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}P(s)\log\left(\frac{P(s)}{Q_{\lambda,\rho}(s)}\right)=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}P(s)\log\left(\frac{P(s)\left(1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda s+\rho)}\right)}{\beta P(s)}\right)

(164)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}P(s)\log\left(\frac{1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda s+\rho)}}{\beta}\right).

(165)

The second term of the dual objective is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}d_{\text{KL}}(R_{\lambda,\rho}\parallel Q_{\lambda,\rho})

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}\sum_{s\in\Sigma_{1}}R_{\lambda}(s)\log\left(\frac{R_{\lambda}(s)}{Q_{\lambda,\rho}(s)}\right)

(166)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}\left(Q_{\lambda,\rho}(s)-\beta P(s)\right)\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{Q_{\lambda,\rho}(s)-\beta P(s)}{\bar{\beta}Q_{\lambda,\rho}(s)}\right)+\frac{Q_{\lambda,\rho}(1)}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\right).

(167)

Now, observe that for
s\in\Sigma
, we have the following, with
e_{s}=\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda s+\rho)}
:

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{\lambda,\rho}(s)-\beta P(s)}{\bar{\beta}Q_{\lambda,\rho}(s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta P(s)/(1-e_{s})-\beta P(s)}{\bar{\beta}\beta P(s)/(1-e_{s})}=\frac{e_{s}}{\bar{\beta}}=\exp(\beta(\lambda s+\rho)),

(168)

which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{Q_{\lambda}(s)-\beta P(s)}{\bar{\beta}Q_{\lambda}(s)}\right)=\lambda s+\rho.

(169)

Hence, combining this with (
167
), we get the following:

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}d_{\text{KL}}\left(R_{\lambda,\rho}\parallel Q_{\lambda,\rho}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}\left(Q_{\lambda,\rho}(s)-\beta P(s)\right)(\rho+\lambda s)+\frac{Q_{\lambda,\rho}(1)}{\beta}\log\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}.

(170)

Next, we use the fact that
\sum_{s\in\Sigma}(Q_{\lambda,\rho}(s)-\beta P(s))=\bar{\beta}-Q_{\lambda,\rho}(1)
, and that
\sum_{s\in\Sigma}s\left(Q_{\lambda,\rho}(s)-\beta P(s)\right)=m-\beta\mu_{P}-Q_{\lambda,\rho}(1)
, and plug it into (
170
) to get

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\beta}}{\beta}d_{\text{KL}}(R_{\lambda,\rho}\parallel Q_{\lambda,\rho})

\displaystyle=\rho\left(\bar{\beta}-Q_{\lambda,\rho}(1)\right)+\lambda\left(m-\beta\mu_{P}-Q_{\lambda,\rho}(1)\right)+\frac{Q_{\lambda,\rho}(1)}{\beta}\log\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}

(171)

\displaystyle=\rho\bar{\beta}+\lambda(m-\beta\mu_{P})+Q_{\lambda,\rho}(1)\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\log\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}-\rho-\lambda\right)}_{\coloneqq c(\lambda,\rho)}.

(172)

Now, we argue that at any
(\lambda,\rho)
such that
c(\lambda,\rho)>0
the primal optimal
Q^{*}_{\lambda,\rho}
will set
Q^{*}_{\lambda,\rho}(1)=0
to drive this term to
0
. This leads to the final expression

\displaystyle J_{+}(P,\beta,m)=\sup_{(\lambda,\rho)\in\mathcal{D}_{D}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\log\left(\frac{1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda X+\rho)}}{\beta}\right)\right]+\lambda(m-\beta\mu_{P})+\rho\bar{\beta}\right\},

(173)

where
\mathcal{D}_{D}=\{(\lambda,\rho)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}:\rho+\lambda\leq(1/\beta)\log(1\bar{\beta})\}
. This completes the proof.

Remark E.5
.

As a sanity check, observe that the dual problem (
27
) is a convex program; that is, it is a maximization problem with a concave objective function and a convex domain of optimization. The convexity of the domain
\mathcal{D}_{D}
, follows from the fact that it is the intersection of two convex sets:
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
and
\{\rho+\lambda\leq(1/\beta)\log(1/\bar{\beta})\}
. To establish the concavity of the objective function, let us rewrite it as
\left(\sum_{s\in\Sigma}f_{s}(\lambda,\rho)\right)+\lambda(m-\beta\mu_{P})+\rho\bar{\beta}
, with
f_{s}(\lambda,\rho)\coloneqq P(s)\log\left(\left(1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\rho+\lambda s)}\right)/\beta\right)
. As we show next, each
f_{s}
is jointly concave in its arguments, which means that the overall objective function in (
27
) is also concave. In particular, for any
a\in(0,1)
and two points
(\lambda_{1},\rho_{1}),(\lambda_{2},\rho_{2})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
, let
\lambda_{a}=a\lambda_{1}+\bar{a}\lambda_{2}
and
\rho_{a}=a\rho_{1}+\bar{a}\rho_{2}
denote their convex combinations. Then, we have the following chain:

\displaystyle\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda_{a}s+\rho_{a})}\leq a\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda_{1}s+\rho_{1})}+\bar{a}\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda_{2}s+\rho_{2})}

(174)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle 1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda_{a}s+\rho_{a})}\geq a\left(1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda_{1}s+\rho_{1})}\right)+\bar{a}\left(1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda_{2}s+\rho_{2})}\right)

(175)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\log(1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda_{a}s+\rho_{a})})\geq a\log\left(1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda_{1}s+\rho_{1})}\right)+\bar{a}\log\left(1-\bar{\beta}e^{\beta(\lambda_{2}s+\rho_{2})}\right).

(176)

The first inequality follows from the convexity of
\exp(\cdot)
, and the last inequality uses the concavity of
\log(\cdot)
.
Multiplying both sides of the last inequality by
P(s)
and subtracting
P(s)\log(\beta)
gives us the required
f_{s}(\lambda_{a},\rho_{a})\geq af_{s}(\lambda_{1},\rho_{1})+\bar{a}f_{s}(\lambda_{2},\rho_{2})
.

2.

We begin with the Taylor series approximation of

Lemma F.2

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(G_{N}^{c}\;\text{i.o.}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{N\geq 1}\cup_{M\geq N}G_{M}^{c}\right)=0.

(190)

This means that the event

. To prove the second part, note that the argument also works with an arbitrary sequence

\{\alpha_{N}:N\geq 1\}

F.1
Formal Description of Almost Sure Coverage

The statement of
Theorem

3.16
contains two “almost sure” events; one on the containment of the oracle CI within the empirical or computable CI, and the other about the empirical CIs covering the population mean. In this section, we construct the relevant probability spaces to provide a rigorous definition of these events.

We begin by considering a triangular array
\{\mathcal{X}_{N}:N\geq 1\}
where
\mathcal{X}_{N}=\{x^{(N)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(N)}_{N}\}
denotes the
N^{th}
population in the infinite sequence. We assume that these arrays satisfy Assumption
3.15
:

\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x^{(N)}_{i}}\Rightarrow P_{X},\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x^{(N)}_{i}\to\mu.

(210)

For every
N\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{S}_{N}
denote the set of all permutations of
[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}
with the associated uniform law
\mathbb{P}_{N}
, and define the product space

\displaystyle(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{\infty},\mathbb{P})\quad=\quad\left(\prod_{N\geq 1}\mathcal{S}_{N},\,\underset{N\geq 1}{{\otimes}}2^{\mathcal{S}_{N}},\,\underset{N\geq 1}{{\otimes}}\mathbb{P}_{N}\right).

(211)

Here we use
\prod
to denote the cartesian product of the sample spaces, and
\otimes
to denote the construction of product sigma-algebra and product measure.
Let
\Pi_{N}:\Omega\to\mathcal{S}_{N}
denote the
N^{th}
coordinate projection, and for
N\geq 1
, the
WoR
sample can be written as

\displaystyle X^{(N)}_{i}(\omega)=x^{(N)}_{\Pi_{N}(\omega)(i)},\quad\text{for}\quad\omega\in\Omega,\;i\in[\beta_{N}N].

(212)

Fix a sequence
\{\alpha_{N}:N\geq 1\}
with
\alpha_{N}\to 0
and
\sum_{N\geq 1}\alpha_{N}<\infty
, and define the following non-containment and miscoverage events, respectively:

\displaystyle M_{N}^{\mathrm{orc}}=\{\mu_{N}\not\in C^{\mathrm{orc}}_{N}\},\quad E_{N}=\{C^{\mathrm{orc}}_{N}\not\subset C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}\},\quad\text{for}\quad N\geq 1.

(213)

Both of these events are
2^{\mathcal{S}_{N}}
, and therefore
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
, measurable. Using these, we can define the two almost sure events used in
Theorem

3.16
. The first is about the eventual containment of
C^{\mathrm{orc}}_{N}
in
C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}
:

\displaystyle E_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\exists N_{0}(\omega)<\infty,\;\text{s.t.}\;\forall N\geq N_{0}(\omega),\;C^{\mathrm{orc}}_{N}(\omega)\subset C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}(\omega)\right\}

(214)

\displaystyle=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\left\lvert\left\{N\geq 1:C^{\mathrm{orc}}_{N}(\omega)\not\subset C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}(\omega)\right\}\right\rvert<\infty\right\}

(215)

\displaystyle=\cup_{N\geq 1}\cap_{N^{\prime}\geq N}E_{N}^{c}

(216)

\displaystyle=\left(\cap_{N\geq 1}\cup_{N^{\prime}\geq N}E_{N}\right)^{c}.

(217)

It follows that
E_{\infty}
is
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=\otimes_{N\geq 1}2^{\mathcal{S}_{N}}
-measurable, and the first statement of
Theorem

3.16
says that
\mathbb{P}(E_{\infty})=1
, and the empirical CI is wide enough to contain the population CI after a random finite number of rounds.

The second almost sure event in
Theorem

3.16
is a consequence of the choice of the sequence of
\alpha_{N}\downarrow 0
, and considers the event

\displaystyle M_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\exists N_{1}(\omega)<\infty,\;\text{s.t.}\;\forall N\geq N_{1}(\omega),\mu_{N}\in C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}(\omega)\right\}

(218)

\displaystyle=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\left\lvert\left\{N\geq 1:\mu_{N}\not\in C^{\mathrm{emp}}_{n}(\omega)\right\}\right\rvert<\infty\right\}

(219)

\displaystyle=\cup_{N\geq 1}\cap_{N^{\prime}\geq N}M_{N}^{c}

(220)

\displaystyle=\left(\cap_{N\geq 1}\cup_{N^{\prime}\geq N}M_{N}\right)^{c}.

(221)

Again,
M_{\infty}
is
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
-measurable, and the second part of
Theorem

3.16
says that
\mathbb{P}(M_{\infty})=1
. Together, these two events give us an “almost sure” coverage guarantee as used by
16
.

Appendix G
Proof of Theorem

(Banach Space CI)

For any
\epsilon>0
, we have the following by Fact
2.1
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bar{X}_{n}-\mu_{N}\|\geq\epsilon\right)

(223)

where
(J_{1},\ldots,J_{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\beta)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(W\geq n\epsilon\right)\leq e^{-\lambda n\epsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda W}\right].

(224)

We can extract a bound on the moment generating function (MGF)
\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda W}]
from the proof of

to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda W}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\cosh(\lambda W)\right]\leq 2\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1+e_{i}),\quad\text{where}\quad e_{i}=D^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(e^{\lambda\|Z_{i}\|}-1-\lambda\|Z_{i}\|\right)\right].

(225)

To complete the construction of the CI, we need to obtain an upper bound on each
e_{i}
, and optimize over
\lambda\geq 0
. We present two methods of doing this: the first method gives a tighter CI whose width is the solution of a one-dimensional optimization problem, while the second method gives a looser CI with a closed-form expression of its width.

We start with expression of
e_{i}
in the MGF bound (
225

\displaystyle\frac{e_{i}}{D^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\|Z_{i}\|}-1-\lambda\|Z_{i}\|\right]

(226)

\displaystyle=\beta\left(e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}\|x_{i}\|}-1-\lambda\bar{\beta}\|x_{i}\|\right)+\bar{\beta}\left(e^{\lambda\beta\|x_{i}\|}-1-\lambda\beta\|x_{i}\|\right)

\displaystyle(\text{since }Z_{i}=(J_{i}-\beta)x_{i})

(227)

\displaystyle\leq\beta\left(e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}d}-1-\lambda\bar{\beta}d\right)+\bar{\beta}\left(e^{\lambda\beta d}-1-\lambda\beta d\right),

\displaystyle(\text{since }\|x_{i}\|\leq d)

(228)

(229)

where the inequality uses the fact that

is an increasing function.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W>n\epsilon)\leq e^{-n\lambda\epsilon+ng(\lambda)},\quad\text{where}\quad g(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(1+D^{2}\left(\beta e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}d}+\bar{\beta}e^{\lambda\beta d}-(1+\lambda d)\right)\right).

(230)

This implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bar{X}_{n}-\mu_{N}\|>\epsilon\right)\leq 2.36\sqrt{\bar{\beta}n}\,e^{-n(\lambda\epsilon-g(\lambda))},\quad\text{for all}\quad\lambda\geq 0.

(231)

Inverting this tail inequality, we get the following result.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\bar{X}_{n}-\mu_{N}\|>\epsilon_{n}(\lambda)\right)\leq\alpha,

(232)

where

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\frac{2.36\log(\sqrt{(1-\beta)n}/\alpha)}{n}+g(\lambda)\right).

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}^{*}=\inf_{\lambda>0}\;\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\frac{\ell_{n}}{n}+g(\lambda)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\ell_{n}\equiv\ell_{n}(\alpha,\beta)=\log\left(\frac{2.36\sqrt{(1-\beta)n}}{\alpha}\right).

(234)

\epsilon

I.1
Justification of Equation (
30
)

We begin by recalling the following bounds for the factorial function for
k\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi k}\left(\frac{k}{e}\right)^{k}e^{1/12k}\leq\;k!\;\leq\;\sqrt{2\pi k}\left(\frac{k}{e}\right)^{k}e^{1/(12k+1)},

(243)

which can be simplified to the following for
k\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi k}\left(\frac{k}{e}\right)^{k}\leq\;k!\;\leq\;e\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{k}{e}\right)^{k}.

(244)

We can then use these inequalities to bound
p_{n}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(S_{N,J}=n)
as follows:

\displaystyle p_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{N!\beta^{n}\bar{\beta}^{N-n}}{(N-n)!n!}=\frac{N!\beta^{n}\bar{\beta}^{N-n}}{(\bar{\beta}N)!(\beta N)!}\geq\frac{\left(\sqrt{2\pi N}(N/e)^{N}\right)\beta^{n}\bar{\beta}^{N-n}}{\left(e\sqrt{N\bar{\beta}}(\bar{\beta}N/e)^{\bar{\beta}N}\right)\left(e\sqrt{\beta N}(\beta N/e)^{\beta N}\right)}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{e^{2}\sqrt{\beta\bar{\beta}N}},

(245)

In particular, this implies
(1/p_{n})\leq e^{2}\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)N/2\pi}\leq 1.18\sqrt{N\beta(1-\beta)}=1.18\sqrt{n(1-\beta)}
.

I.2
Properties of
f_{N,\lambda}

We use the Taylor expansion of the function
f_{N,\lambda}(x)=\log\left(\beta_{N}e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}_{N}x}+\bar{\beta}_{N}e^{-\lambda\beta_{N}x}\right)
in terms of the variable
\lambda
around
0
in the proof of
Theorem

3.16
. First, we observe that it’s value at
0
:

\displaystyle f_{N,0}(x)=\log\left(\beta_{N}+\bar{\beta}_{N}\right)=\log 1=0.

(246)

First derivative.

We now evaluate the first derivative of
f_{N,\lambda}
.

\displaystyle\frac{df_{N,\lambda}(x)}{d\lambda}=\frac{\beta_{N}\bar{\beta}_{N}x\left(e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}_{N}x}-e^{-\lambda\beta_{N}x}\right)}{\beta_{N}e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}_{N}x}+\bar{\beta}_{N}e^{-\lambda\beta_{N}x}}:=\frac{A(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}.

(247)

Observe that
A(0)=0
, which implies that
df_{N,\lambda}(x)/d\lambda|_{\lambda=0}=0
, and that
B(\lambda)\geq\min\{\beta_{N},\bar{\beta}_{N}\}
. Thus, for
|\lambda|\leq L
, we can upper bound the magnitude of the first derivative of
f_{N,\lambda}
as

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\sup_{|\lambda|\leq L}\left\lvert\frac{df_{N,\lambda}(x)}{d\lambda}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\beta_{N}\bar{\beta}_{N}e^{L}}{\min\{\beta_{N},\bar{\beta}_{N}\}}\leq e^{L}.

(248)

Second derivative.

The second derivative of
f_{N,\lambda}
has the following expression:

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f_{N,\lambda}(x)}{d\lambda^{2}}=\frac{d}{d\lambda}\frac{A(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}=\frac{B(\lambda)A^{\prime}(\lambda)-A(\lambda)B^{\prime}(\lambda)}{B^{2}(\lambda)},

(249)

where

\displaystyle A^{\prime}(\lambda)=\beta_{N}\bar{\beta}_{N}x^{2}\left(\bar{\beta}_{N}e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}_{N}x}+\beta_{N}e^{-\lambda\beta_{N}x}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad B^{\prime}(\lambda)=\beta_{N}\bar{\beta}_{N}x\left(e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}_{N}x}-e^{-\lambda\beta_{N}x}\right)=A(\lambda).

(250)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f_{N,\lambda}(x)}{d\lambda^{2}}=\frac{A^{\prime}(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}-\left(\frac{A(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}\right)^{2}=\frac{A^{\prime}(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}-\left(\frac{df_{N,\lambda}(x)}{d\lambda}\right)^{2}.

(251)

At
\lambda=0
, this value is equal to
\beta_{N}\bar{\beta}_{N}x^{2}
, and furthermore for
|\lambda|\leq L
, we can get the following upper bound:

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\sup_{|\lambda|\leq L}\left\lvert\frac{d^{2}f_{N,\lambda}(x)}{d\lambda^{2}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq e^{L}+e^{2L}\leq 2e^{2L}.

(252)

Third derivative.

Finally, we evaluate the third derivative of
f_{N,\lambda}(x)
as

\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}f_{N,\lambda}(x)}{d\lambda^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(\frac{A^{\prime}(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}-\left(\frac{df_{N,\lambda}(x)}{d\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)=\frac{A^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}-\frac{A^{\prime}(\lambda)B^{\prime}(\lambda)}{B^{2}(\lambda)}-2\frac{df_{N,\lambda}(x)}{d\lambda}\frac{d^{2}f_{N,\lambda}(x)}{d\lambda^{2}}

(253)

\displaystyle=\frac{A^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}-\frac{A^{\prime}(\lambda)B^{\prime}(\lambda)}{B^{2}(\lambda)}-2\frac{A(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}\left(\frac{A^{\prime}(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}-\left(\frac{A(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}\right)^{2}\right)

(254)

\displaystyle=\frac{A^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}-\frac{A^{\prime}(\lambda)B^{\prime}(\lambda)}{B^{2}(\lambda)}-2\frac{A(\lambda)A^{\prime}(\lambda)}{B^{2}(\lambda)}+2\frac{A^{3}(\lambda)}{B^{3}(\lambda)}.

(255)

The only new term we need to evaluate is
A^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)
which is equal to
\beta_{N}\bar{\beta}_{N}x^{3}\left(\bar{\beta}_{N}^{2}e^{\lambda\bar{\beta}_{N}x}-\beta_{N}^{2}e^{-\lambda\beta_{N}x}\right)
. Next, we can loosely upper bound the magnitude of the third derivative as

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\sup_{|\lambda|\leq L}\left\lvert\frac{d^{3}f_{N,\lambda}(x)}{d\lambda^{3}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\sup_{|\lambda|\leq L}\left(\left\lvert\frac{A^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{B(\lambda)}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\frac{A^{\prime}(\lambda)B^{\prime}(\lambda)}{B^{2}(\lambda)}\right\rvert+2\left\lvert\frac{A(\lambda)A^{\prime}(\lambda)}{B^{2}(\lambda)}\right\rvert+2\left\lvert\frac{A^{3}(\lambda)}{B^{3}(\lambda)}\right\rvert\right)

(256)

\displaystyle\leq e^{L}+e^{2L}+2e^{2L}+2e^{3L}\leq 6e^{3L}.

(257)

I.3
Proof of Lemma
G.1

It suffices to show that the derivative of

[0,1]
. Observe that by the quotient rule, we have the following for

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{e^{x}-1-x}{x^{2}}\right)=\frac{(e^{x}-1)x^{2}-(e^{x}-1-x)2x}{x^{4}}=\frac{e^{x}(x^{2}-2x)+x^{2}+2x}{x^{4}}

(258)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{x}(x-2)+x+2}{x^{3}}.

(259)

We can see that
\lim_{x\downarrow 0}h^{\prime}(x)=0
, and that for

is an increasing function of

, while the denominator is positive. Thus, we can conclude that
h^{\prime}(x)>0
for all

, and in particular, this means that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[0,a]}h(x)=h(a)\quad\text{$\implies$}\quad e^{x}-1-x\leq h(a)\,x^{2}\quad\text{for all }x\in[0,a].

(260)
