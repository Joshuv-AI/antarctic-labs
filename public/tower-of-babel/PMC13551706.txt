Association of the triglyceride-glucose index with one-year all-cause mortality in patients with spontaneous basal ganglia hemorrhage: a retrospective study
Authors: Liyuan Zhang, Zheng Wang, Jun Wang, Dekang Nie, Ping Xu, Yao Ding, Guan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a marker of insulin resistance, and its relationship with the prognosis of patients with spontaneous basal ganglia hemorrhage (SBGH) has not been clearly established. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between the TyG index and one-year all-cause mortality.MethodThis single-center, hospital-based retrospective study included 282 patients with SBGH. Missing values were imputed using a random forest algorithm. The primary outcome was one-year all-cause mortality, which was confirmed by standardized telephone follow-ups. Variables considered for multivariable modeling were prespecified based on established clinical relevance and prior literature, after which bidirectional stepwise selection was used to select the final model covariates. Association between the TyG index and mortality was assessed using Cox proportional hazards models. Additional analyses included Kaplan-Meier survival curves and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses. Incremental predictive performance of adding TyG to the baseline model was assessed using the AUC, IDI, and NRI. Robustness was examined using E-values and sensitivity analyses, including complete-case analyses, exclusion of extreme values, further adjustment for AST and INR, and subgroup analyses.ResultsAmong the 282 participants, 49 (17.4%) died during the one-year follow-up. The results revealed significant associations between the TyG index and mortality. Specifically, for each 1-SD increase in the TyG index, the risk of all-cause mortality increased by 61.1% (HR = 1.611, 95% CI = 1.184–2.191). RCS analyses suggested an approximately linear relationship (P for nonlinearity > 0.05) and identified a cutoff value of 8.499. Patients with TyG ≥ 8.499 had worse one-year survival (log-rank P = 0.002). Adding the TyG index to the basic model increased the AUC from 0.691 to 0.723 (optimism-corrected 0.661 vs. 0.703; ΔAUC was not significant), improved IDI (0.044, P = 0.047), but not continuous NRI (0.090, P = 0.299). Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of these associations. Subgroup analyses suggested that the association was generally consistent, with limited evidence of interaction.ConclusionThe TyG index may aid early risk stratification in SBGH. However, further research is required to determine if improved TyG index control would lead to better clinical results in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05068-0.Keywords: Triglyceride-glucose, Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage, Basal ganglia, One-year all-cause mortality

Introduction

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH) is a common subtype of hemorrhagic stroke, specifically intracranial hemorrhage caused by nontraumatic cerebral vascular rupture, which is characterized by high rates of morbidity, disability and mortality [1]. The basal ganglia are located deep within the white matter of the brain and represent an important nerve functional area that is closely related to sensory, motor, visual, behavioral, and other functions [2, 3]. They are the most common site for sICH, accounting for 50–70% of all cases, and the 30-day mortality is 33.3% to 50.6% [4, 5], while 41% of survivors have some degree of disability [6]. Therefore, early identification of effective prognostic biomarkers is of great significance for risk stratification and clinical management of sICH patients.

The complex pathological process of sICH extends beyond the mechanical compression and hemorrhagic injury caused by acute hematoma, often involving a cascade of metabolic dysregulation that exacerbates secondary brain injury [7]. Many studies have shown that hyperglycemia due to insulin resistance (IR) after sICH has a major negative impact on patient prognosis [8]. IR is the core pathological basis and main driving factor in the development of metabolic syndrome, and metabolic disorders can increase blood viscosity and brain water content, resulting in reduction in cerebral blood flow and metabolic acidosis. Furthermore, the accumulation of nitrogenous waste due to hyperglycemia and local cellular metabolic dysfunction further promotes neuroinflammation, disrupts blood-brain barrier integrity, and exacerbates cerebral edema, thus worsening neurological outcomes [9].

Although the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HEC) technique is the reference standard for assessing IR, its complexity and high cost limit its clinical application [10]. Homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), derived from fasting glucose and insulin, is more feasible but depends on insulin assays that are not fully standardized [11]. Lipid- and glucose-based surrogates have therefore been proposed, including the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio and the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index [12, 13]. The TyG index, calculated from fasting glucose and triglyceride levels, captures key aspects of dysregulated glucose-lipid metabolism and has shown good performance for identifying IR and for risk stratification in clinical settings, often outperforming TG/HDL-C in predicting adverse outcomes [14, 15].

To date, studies using the TyG index to predict intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) prognosis have mainly focused on critically ill patients [16–19], and prior studies have generally included patients with ICH without distinguishing hemorrhage location. It is well known that the location and volume of bleeding are important factors affecting the prognosis of patients. Therefore, this study aims to focus on patients with spontaneous basal ganglia hemorrhage (SBGH), explore the association between the TyG index and one-year all-cause mortality, and evaluate its predictive value as an early blood biomarker for mortality risk, in order to provide a new metabolic perspective and scientific basis for early risk identification in this specific population.

Study participants and detailed assessment

We carried out a retrospective study using the medical records of 346 patients diagnosed with SBGH admitted to the Department of Neurosurgery at the First People’s Hospital of Yancheng from January to December 2023 (Fig. 1). The inclusion criteria were as follows: ① age ≥ 18 years and a diagnosis of intracerebral hemorrhage confirmed by computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [20]; ② the hematoma was located in the basal ganglia; ③ first cerebral hemorrhage event. The exclusion criteria were as follows: ① secondary hemorrhage (including trauma, tumors, coagulation disorders, and cerebrovascular malformations); ② a documented history of active systemic malignancy or severe psychiatric disorders (including schizophrenia, major depressive disorder) requiring ongoing pharmacotherapy; ③ severe complications (including large cerebral infarction, severe intracranial infection after surgery, multiple cerebral hemorrhages, combined arteriovenous malformations, and cerebral aneurysms); ④ pregnancy or lactation; and ⑤ missing key indicators (including baseline triglycerides and fasting blood glucose). Blinded assessments of cranial CT scans were performed by two neuroradiologists. Selected cases underwent MRI to rule out secondary causes and to confirm hematoma location when CT findings were unclear. Any disagreements were discussed and resolved by consensus. This study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective study design, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

Data collection and imputation

A comprehensive data collection process was implemented using the electronic medical record (EMR) system, encompassing patient demographic characteristics, comorbidities, physical examination, and laboratory and imaging data. Demographic data included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), smoking and drinking history; comorbidity data included hypertension, diabetes, liver disease, and kidney disease. Physical examination included systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and the status of consciousness at admission. Blood samples were collected after the patients had fasted for at least 8 h. Laboratory analysis included triglycerides (TG), fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase isoenzymes (CKMB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), creatinine (Cr), prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT). The samples were analyzed using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Beckman AU5800) based on the enzymatic method at the hospital’s central laboratory. To minimize potential batch or time-related effects, we confirmed that no changes occurred in the testing platform or calibration procedures during the study year. Imaging characteristics included hematoma volume, which, as described in previous studies on cerebral hemorrhage, was calculated using the “0.5×a×b×c method”, where “a” and “b” are the largest diameters measured on the CT scans and “c” is the slice thickness (cm) [21]. BMI was divided into two groups based on the standardized thresholds in Asian populations as follows: Normal, BMI < 24 kg/m2; Overweight, BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2. Information on hypertension, diabetes, cancer, liver disease, kidney disease, and psychiatric disorders was obtained through a comprehensive review of the hospital EMR system and self-reported history provided by the participants. The TyG index was calculated as follows: ln[TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2]. The participants were followed up for one year through telephone interviews conducted by experienced neurologists. Patients who could not be contacted after two attempts were considered lost to follow-up. In our study, 13 patients (4.61%) were lost to follow-up. Their survival times were censored at the date of last contact. The cause of death was ascertained according to International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision; all-cause mortality refers to deaths attributable to any cause. To reduce the data bias introduced by variable deletion, we used the Random Forest method to impute covariates with missing data proportions of less than 20% [22].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) [25th to 75th percentiles]. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages. The Mann-Whitney U test and Pearson’s chi-square test were used to compare the continuous variables and categorical variables, respectively. We used restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th centiles to model and visualize the dose-response relationship of the TyG index with the risk of mortality. Based on the RCS curve, an optimal TyG cutoff was identified at the point where the estimated risk began to increase materially (the inflection point of the spline function). Participants were subsequently categorized into “low” and “high” TyG groups according to this cutoff. The survival curves of the two groups were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared via the log-rank test. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, the proportional hazards assumption was tested for the TyG index and the global model using the cox.zph function. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the associations between the TyG index and mortality among patients with SBGH. Multivariable-adjusted HRs were estimated per 1-standard deviation (SD) increment in the TyG index and by comparing patients with high versus low TyG index levels. Before constructing the multivariable model, we assessed multicollinearity between the TyG index and other variables by calculating the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF). If GVIF^(1/2Df) was ≥ 2 (where Df denotes degrees of freedom), the variable was considered to exhibit significant multicollinearity. AST, ALT, INR, and PT showed significant multicollinearity (Table S2). Variables considered for multivariable modeling were prespecified based on established clinical relevance and prior literature, after which bidirectional stepwise selection was used to select the final model covariates, and the Akaike information criterion (AIC) was used as the evaluation criterion for model quality. The following covariates were adjusted for in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards models: gender, age, BMI, hematoma volume, diabetes, and LDH. The potential impact of unmeasured confounding factors was assessed using E-value [23], which quantifies the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the TyG index and mortality to fully explain away the observed association. To evaluate the incremental performance of the TyG index over basic risk factors for mortality in SBGH patients, we estimated the area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval, and the AUCs of the two models were compared using a statistical test implemented for time-dependent AUC comparison. The baseline model (Model A) included gender, age, BMI, diabetes, hematoma volume, and LDH; the extended model (Model B) additionally incorporated TyG. To account for potential overoptimism, optimism-corrected AUCs were derived using bootstrap internal validation. In addition, the corresponding Youden index, sensitivity, and specificity were reported for each model. Net reclassification index (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement index (IDI) assess whether the new model provides improved risk stratification compared to the basic model.

Several additional analyses were performed to confirm the robustness of our results. Firstly, we repeated the main analyses in participants without missing values in all covariates. Secondly, we refitted Model 2 with additional adjustment for the INR as a representative marker of coagulation status and AST as a representative marker of hepatic injury. Thirdly, TyG outliers defined by the IQR rule (Q1-1.5×IQR, Q3 + 1.5×IQR) were excluded and the Cox models were refitted. Finally, subgroup analyses were conducted according to baseline age, gender, BMI, and hematoma volume to identify susceptible subgroups. To control for multiple comparisons, we applied the Bonferroni correction to reduce the risk of false-positive errors. All the statistical analyses and plotting were performed with R software (version 4.3.3).

Clinical outcomes

Multivariate-adjusted RCS models showed a linear dose-response association between the TyG index and the risk of one-year all-cause mortality (P for nonlinearity > 0.05) (Fig. 2). Based on the spline curve, the optimal TyG cutoff value was 8.499. Kaplan-Meier curves showed that patients in the high TyG group had a higher risk of death within one year than those in the low TyG group (log-rank test, P = 0.002) (Fig. 3). Based on Schoenfeld residual tests, no significant violations of the proportional hazards assumption were observed (P > 0.05), indicating that the model assumptions were met (Table S3 and Figure S1). After adjustment for covariates, Cox regression analyses showed that each 1-SD increase in the TyG index was associated with a 61.1% higher risk of death (HR = 1.611, 95% CI = 1.184–2.191). When TyG was analyzed categorically, participants in the high TyG group had a higher risk of one-year all-cause mortality than those in the low group (HR = 2.605, 95% CI = 1.188–5.709) (Table 2). Additionally, the E-values were 2.603 (continuous TyG) and 4.650 (high vs. low), with E-values for the lower confidence limits of 1.651 and 1.661, respectively.

Incremental predictive values of the TyG index in participants with SBGH

Discriminative performance of the prediction models was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (Fig. 4). The baseline model (Model A) demonstrated an AUC of 0.691 (95% CI, 0.593–0.829), whereas the extended model incorporating TyG (Model B) showed an AUC of 0.723 (95% CI, 0.619–0.846). After optimism correction, the AUCs were 0.661 for Model A and 0.703 for Model B. The difference in AUC between the two models did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05). At the optimal cutoff determined by the Youden index, Model B exhibited a higher sensitivity (0.863 vs. 0.677) compared with Model A. Reclassification analysis demonstrated a significant improvement in integrated discrimination after inclusion of TyG, with an IDI of 0.044 (95% CI: 0.000-0.138; P = 0.047). In contrast, the continuous NRI was not statistically significant (0.090, 95% CI: -0.114-0.413; P = 0.299) (Table 3).

Results of sensitivity analysis

Additional sensitivity and subgroup analyses were conducted to confirm the robustness of our results. First, after excluding participants with missing covariate data (Tables S1 and S4), the association between the TyG index and mortality remained materially unchanged. Second, to mitigate potential residual confounding related to hepatic injury and coagulation status under collinearity constraints, we refitted Model 2 with additional adjustment for AST and INR, and the results were consistent with the main analyses (Table S5). Third, after excluding TyG outliers identified using the IQR method, the TyG-mortality association remained similar to that observed with primary analyses (Table S6). Additionally, subgroup analyses stratified by age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), sex (female vs. male), BMI (< 24 vs. ≥ 24 kg/m²), and hematoma volume (< 30 vs. ≥ 30 mL) showed consistent results with the primary analyses (Fig. 5, Table S7 and Table S8).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we found that a higher baseline TyG index was significantly associated with increased one-year all-cause mortality among patients with SBGH, and this association remained robust after multivariable adjustment. When TyG was modeled continuously, each 1-SD increase in TyG was associated with a 61.1% higher hazard of death. Restricted cubic spline analyses further supported an approximately linear dose-response relationship between TyG and mortality. Consistent findings were observed when TyG was analyzed categorically, with higher TyG levels corresponding to a greater mortality risk. In terms of predictive performance, the model incorporating TyG showed some incremental improvement over the baseline clinical model, as reflected by a significant increase in IDI. Sensitivity analyses reinforced the robustness of the observed associations. Subgroup analyses generally revealed consistent patterns across different population strata.

Current evidence linking the TyG index to outcomes in hemorrhagic stroke remains limited. Yang et al. conducted a case–control study using 791 patients from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV) and 1113 patients from the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD), reporting that the TyG index may serve as a potential predictor of in-hospital mortality among critically ill patients with ICH [24]. Xie and colleagues reported that higher TyG levels were associated with greater neurological impairment in patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) [25]. Another study further suggested that elevated TyG was linked to a higher likelihood of unfavorable functional outcomes among patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) [26]. Against this background, our study provides new evidence that a higher TyG index is positively associated with one-year all-cause mortality in patients with SBGH. These findings address an important knowledge gap and extend prior observations to a more anatomically homogeneous subtype of ICH. Notably, adding TyG to the baseline clinical model yielded a numerically higher AUC, although the improvement in overall discrimination did not reach statistical significance. This is not unexpected, because the AUC is relatively insensitive to incremental gains when the baseline model already achieves moderate discrimination [27]. Importantly, despite the lack of a significant increase in AUC, reclassification analyses showed a statistically significant improvement in the IDI, indicating that inclusion of TyG enhanced the model’s ability to assign higher predicted risks to individuals who subsequently experienced the event [28]. This suggests that TyG may contribute to more refined individual risk estimation, rather than substantially altering global discrimination metrics. The continuous NRI did not reach statistical significance, implying that the overall reordering of predicted risks across individuals was modest [29]. Nevertheless, the observed improvement in IDI, together with the higher sensitivity of the TyG-extended model at the optimal Youden cutoff, supports the notion that TyG may improve clinical risk stratification, particularly by enhancing event detection. Taken together, these findings suggest that TyG provides incremental prognostic information beyond traditional clinical risk factors, with modest but potentially clinically relevant improvements in risk prediction, even in the absence of a statistically significant change in AUC. In sensitivity analyses, the approximately linear association between TyG and mortality was generally consistent across subgroups. However, no statistically significant interactions were observed, which may reflect the limited sample size, reduced statistical power, and the burden of multiple comparisons. Accordingly, these subgroup-specific trends should be considered exploratory findings, and their clinical significance requires further validation in larger prospective studies.

The exact biological mechanism underlying the relationship between the TyG index and the mortality after cerebrovascular disease remains uncertain. A plausible pathway involves IR, given that glucose may reflect hepatic IR while triglycerides may reflect adipose tissue IR, making TyG a composite surrogate of systemic IR [30]. IR can impair the activity of glucose transporters and exacerbate hyperglycemia, which in the acute phase of intracerebral hemorrhage is linked to heightened oxidative stress and secondary neuronal injury [31, 32]. Hyperglycemia can also downregulate aquaporin-4 (AQP-4) expression in the brain, which may further increase vasogenic brain edema and result in severe blood-brain barrier (BBB) destruction [33]. BBB dysfunction can lead to brain edema, ionic homeostasis disruption, altered signaling, and immune infiltration, consequently causing neuronal cell death [34, 35]. In addition, recent studies have shown that hyperglycemia can augment cerebral hematoma expansion through plasma kallikrein (PK)-mediated osmotically sensitive inhibition of hemostasis, potentially worsening cerebral perfusion and clinical outcomes [36]. Moreover, hyperglycemia induces superoxide production and inflammatory responses, which expand the hemorrhagic lesion, impair neuronal autophagy, and increase neurotoxicity [7]. Existing research indicates that the bidirectional interaction between insulin resistance and chronic inflammation may be a key contributor to secondary brain injury in hemorrhagic stroke [16, 37]. While acute metabolic stress may transiently elevate the TyG index, sensitivity analyses excluding outliers confirmed the association’s stability, supporting the TyG index as a stable marker despite acute perturbations.

Our study must be interpreted in the context of its limitations. First, the hospital-based, retrospective, single-center design may affect generalizability and introduce selection bias. Second, the number of outcome events relative to the number of covariates raises the possibility of overfitting, particularly in subgroup and interaction analyses. Thus, we may need to validate our findings in an independent validation cohort. Third, although our study adjusted for multiple confounders, several sources of residual confounding may remain; for instance, medication data (e.g., insulin therapy, statins) and certain demographic or clinical parameters not included in the analysis may affect the results. For example, a patient receiving insulin therapy might have artificially normalized glycemic levels, while statin use could attenuate the association between the TyG index and mortality by lowering triglyceride concentrations. Additionally, our models lacked adjustment for severity of illness or acute treatment patterns at admission, as these data were unavailable. While we adjusted for physiological surrogates (laboratory markers), these may inadequately capture the impact of initial neurological injury on mortality. Reassuringly, we quantified robustness to unmeasured confounding using E-values; however, this approach does not eliminate residual confounding and should be interpreted as complementary evidence. Additionally, the retrospective design precludes causal interpretation of the relationship between TyG and mortality. Future longitudinal studies should aim to track changes in the TyG index over time and assess their long-term effects on adverse outcomes. Taken together, our findings should be interpreted as identifying statistical rather than underlying biological causation. Prospective investigations incorporating detailed metabolic, clinical, and outcome assessments are needed to confirm these results.

Conclusion

Patients with SBGH may benefit from using the TyG index as a predictive biomarker. In clinical practice, incorporating TyG into early risk assessment could facilitate timely identification of high-risk individuals who may benefit from intensified metabolic monitoring and tailored therapeutic interventions. More research is required to determine whether improving TyG-related metabolic abnormalities would lead to better clinical outcomes in the future.
