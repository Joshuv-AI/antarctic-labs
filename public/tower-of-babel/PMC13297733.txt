Antibiotic prescribing practices and appropriateness of prescriptions in outpatient paediatric acute respiratory tract infections in Viet Nam
Authors: Hai Nguyen-Tran, Nhat-Long Ngo, Nga Thi Thuy Do, Tien Viet Dung Vu, Thach Ngoc Pham, Ta Thi Dieu Ngan, Cao Hung Thai, Thanh Vinh Khuong, Thuy Thi Bich Le, Thanh Huyen Duong, Daniel Olson, Nam Nguyen, H Rogier Van Doorn, Sonia Lewycka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a growing challenge caused by many factors including antibiotic overprescribing. We sought to understand prescribing practices and appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions for paediatric acute respiratory tract infections (ARIs) in commune health centres in one province in northern Viet Nam to help identify potential factors associated with overprescribing and areas to target for intervention to reduce AMR.MethodsWe performed a retrospective review of electronic health records for paediatric outpatient encounters with an ARI diagnosis in 112 commune health centres between 1 January and 31 December 2019. We analysed patterns of antibiotic prescriptions, including classification using the WHO Access, Watch and Reserve (AWaRe) classification. We evaluated antibiotic appropriateness for acute pharyngitis using decision trees based on local guidelines. Mixed-effects logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with antibiotic prescribing decisions.ResultsA total of 35 675 encounters were reviewed. Of all encounters, 34 673 (97.2%) received antibiotics and 4268 (12.3%) encounters had a Watch group prescription. The most frequent diagnosis was acute pharyngitis (21 269, 59.6%), of which 20 826 (97.9%) encounters were prescribed antibiotics. Appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions for acute pharyngitis diagnoses varied by guideline used.ConclusionsAntibiotics are frequently prescribed for paediatric ARIs in Viet Nam. Acute pharyngitis was the most common diagnosis, with many antibiotics prescribed inappropriately. Provider awareness about available guidelines alongside updated changes to guidelines may help improve prescribing practices and help decrease the burden of AMR in Viet Nam.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a growing challenge worldwide. AMR is considered one of the top 10 global public health threats by the WHO, and if not controlled, one report estimates worldwide mortality secondary to AMR may be as high as 10 million by 2050.1–3 Several factors contribute to AMR, including misuse of antibiotics such as overprescribing, prescriptions by untrained staff, lack of access to diagnostic tests, and knowledge gaps in appropriate antibiotic usage.4 These challenges are further exacerbated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where resources, medical personnel, diagnostic access and funding to combat AMR are often limited.5

One LMIC in which AMR has become increasingly challenging is Viet Nam, where high proportions of resistance have been reported.6–9 This is in part due to antibiotic overuse whereby many antibiotics are available and sold through private pharmacies without prescriptions.10,11 Furthermore, resources such as diagnostics to aid in management decisions are frequently unavailable in primary care settings, which can lead to overtreating without the ability for diagnostic confirmation. With the growing concern for AMR, the Viet Nam Ministry of Health (MoH) developed a National Action Plan on AMR in 2013, outlining recommendations to mitigate AMR.12,13 This was followed with a second national strategy for the period of 2023–2030 with a stronger focus on action outside tertiary hospital settings.14 Additional evidence-based approaches to reduce AMR include the establishment of antimicrobial stewardship programmes and their implementation, given that inappropriate antibiotic use, prescriptions and sales are major factors in the rising burden of AMR in Viet Nam.11,15,16

Among children, AMR and inappropriate antibiotic use is a particularly significant challenge, especially with acute respiratory tract infections (ARIs) diagnoses. Antibiotics are frequently not recommended for ARIs as they are commonly caused by viruses and are self-limiting.17 However, in one of our studies in Viet Nam assessing outpatient antibiotic prescribing for ARIs by the WHO Access, Watch and Reserve (AWaRe) classification, we found that children aged <5 years with ARIs were five times more likely to be prescribed a Watch group antibiotic compared with adults aged 18–49 years (adjusted relative risk: 5.61; 95% CI: 5.13–6.13].18 These overtly broad antibiotics are often unnecessary and contribute not only to AMR but also can be associated with increased risk of side effects.

Pharyngitis in particular is a common ARI diagnosis for which antibiotics are prescribed. Acute pharyngitis can be caused by a variety of pathogens including viruses and bacteria, commonly group A streptococci (GAS) for which antibiotics are indicated. Antibiotic treatment for GAS pharyngitis provides symptom relief, shortens the duration of symptoms and prevents transmission. Most importantly, treatment is recommended to prevent complications such as acute rheumatic fever (ARF) in settings with moderate-to-high risk, such as Viet Nam.19,20 Decision around treatment for pharyngitis may also be influenced by age—ARF is rare in children aged <3 years and thus treatment is not recommended for children in this age group—and GAS prevalence.17,21,22 The prevalence of GAS pharyngitis worldwide is variable, with one study finding the prevalence of GAS in LMICs to be 17.6%.23 Specifically in Viet Nam, a 1990s study evaluating the utility of rapid streptococcal testing in children 3–16 years of age presenting to outpatient settings with pharyngitis symptoms found the prevalence of GAS to be 31%; however, recent prevalence data specifically in Viet Nam have not been reported.24

Diagnosis of bacterial (i.e. GAS) pharyngitis by providers is made through use of guidelines, clinical scoring systems, and laboratory testing such as culture, rapid antigen detection tests and nucleic acid amplification tests.19 For Viet Nam and other LMICs, outpatient settings usually do not have access to these diagnostics, and guidelines are often used instead. Such guidelines include the 2014 Viet Nam MoH Guidelines for Medical Examination and Treatment at Commune and Ward Health Stations and the 2016 Viet Nam MoH Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Diseases of the Ear, Nose, and Throat (ENT).25,26 International guidelines are also available and include the Centor criteria, which are used in the WHO AWaRe Antibiotic Book.17,21,22 Provider use of guidelines to aid in decision making is variable leading to differences in practice among providers despite having the same set of guidelines available for reference. This in turn can lead to inconsistency in prescribing practices that may be considered inappropriate per guidelines. Therefore, it is important to evaluate prescribing practices and the appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions, particularly for common paediatric ARI diagnoses, such as acute pharyngitis, to identify ways to improve prescribing practices and decrease the burden of AMR.

The primary objective of this study was to determine the appropriateness of antibiotic prescribing practices for ARIs, specifically acute pharyngitis, in paediatric patients in northern Viet Nam based on available guidelines. Secondarily, the study aimed to describe the antibiotics prescribed for paediatric ARIs and determine patient factors that may influence antibiotic prescribing practices.

Methods

Ethics

The ‘Implementation of C-Reactive Protein Point of Care Testing to Improve Antibiotic Targeting in Respiratory Illness in Viet Nam Primary Care’ (ICAT) trial protocol was approved by the Oxford University Tropical Research Ethics Committee (OxTREC; reference number: 53–18) and the ethical committee of the National Hospital for Tropical Diseases in Viet Nam (reference: 07/HDDD-NDTW/2019).27,28 The ‘Assessing the Impacts of Educational and Collective Action Interventions Delivered in Primary Healthcare on Reducing Prescription of Antibiotics for Patients with ARIs in Routine Care’ (Co-Act) trial protocol was approved by the OxTREC (reference number: 528–19) and the ethical committee of the National Institute of Hygiene and Epidemiology (reference: HDDD-30/2019). Permission to carry out this study was obtained from each facility and local authorities.

Study design

This was a retrospective study evaluating antibiotic prescribing practices among all healthcare encounters of patients ≤15 years of age who presented for an ARI in 2019 at 112 commune health centres in six rural districts (Hai Hau, Nam Truc, Nghia Hung, Truc Ninh, Xuan Truong, Y Yen) of Nam Dinh, a northern province in Viet Nam. Encounters with ARIs were defined as those with corresponding electronic health records (EHRs) containing an ICD-10 diagnosis code among the following: H65, J00, J01, J02, J03, J04, J06, J09+J11, J12+J18, J20+J21, J22.29

Patient data

De-identified baseline data were used from two studies conducted by the Oxford University Clinical Research Unit (OUCRU) in Viet Nam: Co-Act and ICAT.28 The first study was a cluster-randomized controlled trial seeking to evaluate the impact of educational and participatory action research interventions in the community, primary healthcare centres and hospitals to reduce inappropriate antibiotic use. The second study was a cluster randomized controlled trial aiming to assess the use and impact of C-reactive protein point-of-care testing on antibiotic prescribing for ARIs in primary care centres. Under these trials, all EHRs related to ARIs were collected and included patient-level data on demographic characteristics (e.g. age, sex), presenting signs and symptoms, clinical diagnoses and the corresponding ICD-10 codes, and treatments (e.g. antibiotics prescribed, duration of prescriptions, other medications prescribed—including symptomatic relief agents). Geographical location data at the district and commune levels were also recorded. To ensure that routine care was not influenced by trial interventions, our study used EHRs from the pre-intervention phase in 2019 only.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis was performed on encounters. For summary statistics of antibiotic prescriptions, encounters with duplicate prescriptions or packs/pills listed without durations were excluded from the primary analysis. For encounters with more than one antibiotic prescribed, duration (days) used in the analysis was the duration from the prescription with the longest duration prescribed. Additionally, for encounters with more than one antibiotic prescribed, WHO AWaRe classification was based on the antibiotic with the highest restriction level. No Reserve antibiotics were identified, and all Watch antibiotics in our data were oral; therefore, the hierarchy used was Not Recommended > Watch > Access. As a sensitivity analysis, we also analysed all encounters, and explored two additional approaches to antibiotic prescription duration: (i) for those with duplicate prescriptions, days were added to determine total duration; and (ii) for those with only packs/pills listed, we used the median duration for each pack/pill combination in similar prescriptions from other encounters with known durations.

To determine the appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions specifically for acute pharyngitis, decision trees were created using the 2014 Viet Nam MoH Guidelines for Medical Examination and Treatment at Commune and Ward Health Stations (Figure 1) and the 2016 Viet Nam MoH Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Diseases of the ENT (Figure 2), which are two guidelines that were available for use by providers.25,26 We determined appropriateness by assessing if patients met various criteria in the decision trees based on signs and symptoms reported. When duration of symptoms was unknown based on available records, and symptom duration was required to make a recommendation for when antibiotics should or should not be prescribed, patients were classified as ‘unclassifiable’. For the 2014 guideline, we assessed appropriateness using more ‘strict’ criteria (i.e. all symptoms listed needed to be present and only antibiotics prescribed that are listed were considered appropriate) and with more ‘lenient’ criteria (i.e. any of the symptoms listed and any antibiotic prescribed within the same antibiotic class listed, even for encounters with more than one antibiotic prescribed, were considered appropriate). As an additional analysis, we evaluated the appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions for acute pharyngitis using a decision tree with ‘strict’ (i.e. only antibiotics prescribed that are listed were considered appropriate) and ‘lenient’ (i.e. any antibiotic prescribed within the same antibiotic class listed, even for encounters with more than one antibiotic prescribed, were considered appropriate) criteria from Centor criteria, a validated and commonly used scoring system that predicts the likelihood of GAS pharyngitis, including in the WHO AWaRe Antibiotic Book (Figure S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).17,21,22

To examine factors that may influence antibiotic prescribing practices, we performed a mixed-effect logistic regression model to estimate the OR and 95% CI for the outcome of being prescribed at least one antibiotic for an ARI, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Fixed effects included age (stratified into <3 years and 3–15 years), sex, district and clinical ARI ICD-10 diagnosis (stratified into four groups based on general anatomical region of diagnosis and viral diagnoses). Random effect was assigned to the commune with the purpose of accounting for the prescription variability among communes. As an exploratory analysis, we also examined factors that may influence antibiotic prescribing practices for receiving a Watch antibiotic over an Access antibiotic using similar fixed and random effects. All statistical analysis was performed using R, version 4.4.1.

There were 35 675 paediatric encounters for ARIs in the 112 commune health centres in 2019 (Table 1), of whom of 18 765 (52.6%) were males; median age was 8 years (IQR: 4–11). The most common ICD-10 code associated with an encounter was ‘J02: Acute pharyngitis’ (59.6%, Table 1). Of the 35 675 encounters, 34 673 (97.2%) received an antibiotic.

Characteristics of antibiotics prescribed

Of the antibiotics prescribed, the most common antibiotic classes prescribed in encounters were penicillins with or without β-lactamase inhibitors (55.2%) followed by cephalosporins (37.2%), macrolides (2.7%) and others (including trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, metronidazole, fluoroquinolones, spiramycin/metronidazole; 0.7%). There were 1342 (4.2%) encounters that were prescribed more than one antibiotic. Differences were seen in categories of antibiotics prescribed for the 0 to <3 years age group versus the 3–15 years age group (Table 2). A majority of antibiotics (86.4%) were prescribed from the WHO AWaRe Access group, but Watch and Not Recommended antibiotics were also prescribed (Table 2). As a sensitivity analysis, keeping all data including encounters with duplicate prescriptions and with only pack/pill combination listed (additional 2501 encounters), we found similar patterns in antibiotics prescribed (Table S1).

Appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions for acute pharyngitis

Using the 2014 Viet Nam MoH Guidelines for Medical Examination and Treatment at Commune and Ward Health Stations, 322 (1.5%) of all 21 269 prescriptions for acute pharyngitis were appropriate based on more strict criteria, versus 5153 (24.2%) of 21 269 using more lenient criteria (though 40.7% and 14.9% of encounters were unclassifiable, respectively, given symptom duration was unknown). Of classifiable encounters, 2.6% (322/12 610) and 28.5% (5153/18 108) were appropriate based on more strict and lenient criteria, respectively. Using the 2016 Viet Nam MoH Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Diseases of the ENT, 14 405 (67.7%) of all 21 269 prescriptions were appropriate. Using the Centor criteria, 589 (2.8%) of all 21 269 prescriptions for acute pharyngitis were appropriate based on the stricter criteria versus 654 (3.1%) of 21 269 using more lenient criteria. As the 2016 Viet Nam MoH Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Diseases of the ENT and Centor criteria do not use duration of symptoms to guide prescribing decision, all encounters were classifiable.

Factors associated with antibiotic prescriptions

Factors associated with antibiotic prescriptions are presented in Figure 3. Younger children (<3 years) were actually less likely to receive an antibiotic for an ARI compared with older children 3–15 years of age (OR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.58–0.91; P < 0.005). Compared with Hai Hau, the Nam Truc district was significantly less likely to prescribe antibiotics (OR: 0.08; 95% CI: 0.02–0.28; P < 0.001). Encounters with ICD-10 codes grouped into other upper respiratory tract and viral diagnoses were less likely to be prescribed an antibiotic compared with encounters with a diagnosis of acute pharyngitis (OR: 0.39; 95% CI: 0.32–0.48 and OR: 0.17; 95% CI: 0.11–0.26, respectively). In evaluating factors associated with being prescribed a Watch versus Access antibiotic prescription (Figure S2), younger children (<3 years) were more likely to receive a Watch antibiotic for an ARI compared with older children (OR: 1.76; 95% CI: 1.58–1.96; P < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were seen between districts, but those with ICD-10 codes associated with the upper respiratory tract (Group 1), viral infections (Group 2), and the lower respiratory tract (Group 3) diagnoses were all more likely to receive a Watch antibiotic for an ARI compared with acute pharyngitis.

Discussion

We found that a diagnosis of ARI in children almost always led to an antibiotic prescription in primary care clinics in northern Viet Nam, where 97.2% of encounters had an antibiotic prescribed. Acute pharyngitis was the most common diagnosis (59.6%) and while older children (3–15 years old) were more likely to be prescribed an antibiotic for ARIs compared with younger children (<3 years), younger children were more likely to be prescribed a Watch antibiotic, highlighting potential diagnoses and populations to target for interventions to improve antibiotic prescribing practices and help reduce AMR.

ARIs are common diagnoses in the paediatric population and are commonly caused by viruses and are self-limiting, yet antibiotics are frequently prescribed, which can subsequently contribute to AMR.30 Our results are similar to another study evaluating antibiotic use in Bavi, Viet Nam, which found that children were also frequently treated with antibiotics (91%). Only 22% of these antibiotics had a doctor’s prescription, while 67% consulted a seller in a pharmacy and 11% of caregivers decided themselves.30 Inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions are a preventable driver of AMR, in addition to potential unnecessary costs for families and increased risk of adverse drug events. Leveraging our results, children are a particularly important population to target for antibiotic overprescribing. Furthermore, in our study, Nam Truc district was less likely to prescribe antibiotics compared with Hai Hau district. Nam Truc district is closest to Nam Dinh Provincial Hospital and patients may preferentially seek care at the hospital. Lower patient volumes in Nam Truc may have led to less medication availability and subsequently fewer prescriptions at these centres. However, further investigation evaluating differences between districts is needed to determine if specific interventions for improving antibiotic prescribing practices are also needed for certain districts.

Although local and international guidelines generally suggest that most paediatric ARIs are due to viral infections and do not necessitate the use of antibiotics, antibiotics are still frequently prescribed.25,26 Using the 2014 Viet Nam MoH Guidelines for Medical Examination and Treatment at Commune and Ward Health Stations, we found that for acute pharyngitis encounters, only 24.2% of antibiotic prescriptions were considered appropriate using lenient criteria, decreasing to 1.5% when stricter criteria were applied. Of note, up to 40.7% of encounters were unclassifiable due to missing information on duration of symptoms. However, if only classifiable encounters were included, a similarly small proportion of encounters still would have been considered appropriate. When applying guidelines used more internationally (i.e. Centor criteria), less than 5% of antibiotic prescriptions were considered appropriate using both lenient (3.1%) and strict (2.8%) criteria.31 Therefore, increasing awareness and improved adherence to guidelines are key areas for antibiotic stewardship interventions.

Although awareness and improved adherence to guidelines are important, we recognize that various guidelines for diagnosing and managing paediatric ARIs exist and within each guideline there are different nuances, whereby opting to use one guideline over another may influence prescribing practices. Additionally, we recognize that in populations with higher prevalences of GAS and in moderate-to-high-risk populations for ARF, such as Viet Nam, providers may have a different risk tolerance for the development of complications if untreated. Our evaluation using the 2016 Viet Nam MoH Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Diseases of the ENT classified 67.7% of antibiotic prescriptions for acute pharyngitis as appropriate, compared with less than 30% using the 2014 guidelines with lenient criteria. This proportion appears high, though could reflect a higher prescribing rate in a population with a high GAS prevalence. The higher proportion of encounters that were appropriate using the 2016 guideline also likely reflects its broader criteria, which allow any listed symptoms to justify an antibiotic prescription and permit greater flexibility in the antibiotic prescribed. However, these criteria may not accurately reflect the true need for antibiotics where additional updated studies looking at prevalence of GAS in Viet Nam would help inform guidelines. Further, continued improvement and harmonization of management guidelines for infections, similar to the WHO AWaRe Antibiotic Book, is also critical to ensure consistency and reduce the risk of overprescriptions justified by guidelines whose criteria are too lenient.

Finally, though the majority (86.4%) of antibiotics prescribed for paediatric ARI encounters were considered Access antibiotics per the WHO AWaRe classification, over 10% of prescriptions were Watch antibiotics. While younger children aged <3 years were less likely to be prescribed an antibiotic compared with older children—potentially driven by the predominance in acute pharyngitis diagnoses in the study and most guidelines not recommending antibiotics for those <3 years given that ARF is extremely rare in this population—we found that younger children <3 years were more likely to be prescribed a Watch antibiotic compared with older children. Furthermore, upper respiratory tract, lower respiratory tract and viral infection groupings of ICD-10 ARI diagnoses were all more likely to receive a Watch antibiotic compared with acute pharyngitis. As first-line antibiotics for most ARI infections should be Access antibiotics, we would not expect to see a significant difference between these diagnoses and would have expected less for viral infections.17 Therefore, as Watch antibiotics are overtly broad and have higher potential to contribute to AMR, further investigation into why Watch antibiotics are being prescribed more for these other diagnoses is needed.

This study has limitations. Data from the EHRs may lack details about the illness course (e.g. previous treatment, presence, severity and length of symptoms) and provider/system factors (e.g. prescriber’s years of experience, prescribing preference, prescriber’s risk tolerance level of undertreating, antibiotic availability) that may influence antibiotic prescribing practices. Our assessment of the appropriateness of antibiotic prescriptions was limited as a large number of encounters were unclassifiable due to unknown symptom duration; however, encounters with inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions still accounted for more than half of the total prescriptions, highlighting the frequency of overprescribing practices. Appropriateness was determined solely on the choice of antibiotic class; therefore, there may be encounters that received an inappropriate dose/duration despite appropriate choice of antibiotic class. For the small (4.2%) proportion of encounters with more than one antibiotic prescribed, WHO AWaRe classification was based on the highest restriction level based on our assignment used of Not Recommended > Watch > Access. While this approach may differ from others that may rank antibiotics differently, as the proportion of encounters with more than one antibiotic prescribed was small, the impact on comparability is likely minimal. This study focused on public sectors in rural areas, where access to and use of antibiotics may be limited and managed by public health regulators and reimbursement agencies. As such, the findings do not reflect practices in the private sector and urban areas, where differences in factors influencing prescribing practices may exist.32 Furthermore, although encounters were from multiple commune health centres in northern Viet Nam, as the centres were restricted to one province, this may affect the generalizability of the findings, though AMR and overprescribing have been reported throughout the country.6,33

AMR is a growing problem worldwide and is associated with high morbidity and mortality.2 Though recognized as a major issue in Viet Nam, more emphasis on antibiotic stewardship within the paediatric population is important. Antibiotics were almost always prescribed for paediatric ARI outpatient encounters in northern Viet Nam. Despite available guidelines that would suggest that most ARIs are viral and self-limiting, antibiotics are frequently prescribed, with the majority assessed as inappropriate. Improved antibiotic prescribing practices in paediatric ARIs would not only decrease the potential development of AMR but also avoid unnecessary costs and side effects for patients. Targeted interventions in younger age groups who are more likely to receive Watch antibiotics, increased provider awareness about various guidelines, and harmonized guidelines, such as the WHO AWaRe Antibiotic Book, are key areas to focus efforts to help improve antibiotic prescribing practices in Viet Nam and help reduce the burden of AMR.
