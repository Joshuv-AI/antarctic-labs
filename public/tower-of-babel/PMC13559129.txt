The dose-effect relationship between red blood cell transfusion volume and adverse events in pediatric cardiac surgery
Authors: Wenting Wang, Qindong Liu, Tianyu Gao, Yu Jin, He Wang, Jia Liu, Bingyang Ji, Jinping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric cardiac surgery requiring cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is associated with high transfusion rates. While transfusions increase risks of adverse events, existing evidence cannot differentiate whether risks arise from transfusion itself or cumulative volumes. This study aims to explore the relationship between intra-operative transfusion volume and adverse outcomes, exploring whether transfusion volume has a linear or threshold effect on adverse outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included pediatric patients (≤14 years old) undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB, categorized by intra-operative red blood cell (RBC) volume transfused: 0, 0.1–22.2 mL/kg, 22.3–27.0 mL/kg, 27.1–34.5 mL/kg and >34.5 mL/kg. Outcomes included prolonged mechanical ventilation (MV), acute kidney injury (AKI), severe AKI and composite complications (chest drainage >10 mL/kg/d, culture-confirmed pulmonary infection, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) implantation, and in-hospital mortality). Multivariable logistic regression was used to analyze the relationship between intra-operative RBC transfusion volume and outcomes.ResultsA total of 19,081 children were included, of whom 44.5% received RBC transfusion during surgery. Among these, 10.7% received 0.1–22.2 mL/kg, 11.3% received 22.3–27.0 mL/kg, 11.3% received 27.1–34.5 mL/kg and 11.0% received >34.5 mL/kg RBC transfusion. Higher RBC transfusion volumes correlated with certain adverse events. However, after adjustment for potential confounders, no consistent linear dose-effect relationship was observed. Instead, a threshold-effect association was identified. Compared with no transfusion, RBC transfusion >34.5 mL/kg was associated with increased risks of prolonged MV [odds ratio (OR): 3.45, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.78–4.28; P<0.001], AKI (OR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.01–1.44; P=0.04), and composite complications (OR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.54–3.43; P<0.001), whereas low-volume transfusion was inversely associated with composite complications (0.1–22.2 mL/kg: OR, 0.36; 95% CI: 0.17–0.76; P=0.007), prolonged MV (0.1–22.2 mL/kg: OR, 0.55; 95% CI: 0.40–0.77; P=0.001; 22.3–27.0 mL/kg: OR, 0.64; 95% CI: 0.48–0.85; P=0.002), and AKI (0.1–22.2 mL/kg: OR, 0.81; 95% CI: 0.68–0.95; P=0.01).ConclusionsIn this large cohort of pediatric patients undergoing surgery for congenital heart disease (CHD), there was a threshold effect between intraoperative RBC transfusion volume and adverse outcomes. Besides, low-volume transfusion was inversely associated with these outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating RBC transfusion using weight-adjusted volume and suggest that an optimal transfusion range may exist in pediatric cardiac surgery.Keywords: Congenital heart disease (CHD), cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), red blood cell transfusion (RBC transfusion), adverse event

Highlight box

Key findings

• No linear dose-response relationship was observed between perioperative red blood cell (RBC) transfusion volume and major postoperative complications in pediatric congenital heart disease surgery.

• A clear threshold effect was identified: low-volume transfusion was inversely associated with composite complications, while transfusion >34.5 mL/kg was associated with increased risks of prolonged mechanical ventilation, acute kidney injury and composite complications.

What is known and what is new?

• It is well established that RBC transfusion is associated with adverse outcomes in pediatric cardiac surgery, and prior studies have suggested a potential dose-effect relationship. However, whether increasing transfusion volume leads to a linear increase in risk remains controversial.

• This study, based on a large cohort, demonstrates that the relationship is non-linear, with risks increasing only beyond a certain transfusion threshold.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings suggest that small-to-moderate RBC transfusion may be clinically necessary and relatively safe, particularly in the context of cardiopulmonary bypass-related hemodilution.

• Clinical focus should shift from simply reducing transfusion rates to avoiding high-volume transfusion.

• Future strategies should emphasize individualized, goal-directed transfusion protocols and comprehensive patient blood management to minimize excessive transfusion and improve outcomes.

Introduction

As one of the most important cornerstones of modern medicine, blood transfusion has profoundly changed the pattern of critical care and perioperative management, saving countless patients’ lives. According to statistics, approximately 118 million units of blood are collected globally each year to meet clinical needs, of which over 20% are used for pediatric patients (1,2). Among them, pediatric congenital heart disease (CHD) surgery is one of the major indications for pediatric blood transfusion, mainly due to blood loss and hemodilution caused by cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Therefore, it is almost inevitable to avoid transfusion in pediatric CHD surgery (3-6). However, there is still too much concern about the indications, dosages, and incidence of adverse events of blood transfusion in pediatric CHD patients due to their unique pathological and physiological feature and the relatively high incidence of transfusion reactions (6-9).

Packed red blood cell (pRBC) is the most commonly used blood product and plays an important role in pediatric CHD surgery. In critical situations such as massive bleeding, red blood cell (RBC) transfusion can significantly improve the success rate of treatment (10). In addition, RBC transfusion can not only enhance the body’s oxygen delivery capacity, but also improve tissue oxygenation and strengthen hemostasis and coagulation functions (7,11). However, there are also plenty of risks accompanied by RBC transfusion. For instance, studies have suggested that pediatric patients suffer from prominent issues such as cytomegalovirus infection, transfusion related immune tolerance, and necrotizing enterocolitis caused by RBC transfusion (12); moreover, as the storage time of RBC prolongs, the risk of liver, lung and renal complications, as well as infection also increases (13-15).

At present, the rate and volume of RBC transfusion in pediatric CHD surgery are high, which brings them a four times higher risk of complications than adults, but for now there is no sufficient evidence showing that pediatric CHD patients need more RBC transfusion (16). Some studies have reported that, compared with non-transfused children, RBC transfusion is associated with an increased risk of transfusion-related complications. Besides, research on restrictive transfusion strategies have shown that the less the RBC volume used, the shorter the hospital stay. Moreover, some studies have suggested that with increasing RBC transfusion volume, the risk of adverse events rises in pediatric cardiac surgery patients. Therefore, it remains unclear whether RBC transfusion in pediatric CHD surgery is merely associated with adverse outcomes or whether there exists a dose-effect relationship between transfusion volume and postoperative complications (9,17-22).

Therefore, this study aims to analyze the dose-effect relationship between intra-operative RBC transfusion and postoperative adverse outcomes in pediatric CHD patients, so as to clarify the role of RBC transfusion in the occurrence of adverse outcomes and provide a basis for optimizing RBC transfusion strategies. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0369/rc).

Methods

Study design and population

This is a retrospective, single-center cohort study. Pediatric patients aged ≤14 years who underwent cardiac surgery with CPB between 2014 and 2021 were included from Fuwai hospital. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) off-pump surgery; (II) heart transplantation; and (III) incomplete clinical data. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital (approval No. 2023-2265). Given the retrospective study design, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Data collection

Based on the advice from clinical experts concerning transfusion related risk factors, clinical practice and published literature, the following clinical variables were collected: demographics (such as age, sex, weight, gestational age), type of surgery (elective or emergency), Society of Thoracic Surgeons and European Association for Cardiothoracic Surgery (STAT) Mortality Categories, transfusion history, cyanotic congenital heart disease (CCHD), preoperative laboratory tests [such as hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (HCT), serum creatinine (SCR), blood urea nitrogen (BUN)], type of CHD surgery, intraoperative variables [such as ultrafiltration volume, priming volume, CPB duration, aortic cross clamping (ACC) duration] and postoperative variables [such as mechanical ventilation (MV) duration, length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay, chest drainage volume, length of hospital stay, incidence of liver injury and in-hospital mortality]. Considering the temporal relationship between RBC transfusion and the occurrence of postoperative adverse outcomes, particularly acute kidney injury (AKI), only intraoperative RBC transfusion volume was included as the exposure variable in the present study in order to minimize the potential influence of reverse causation.

Study outcomes

The outcomes of this study included the incidence of postoperative prolonged MV, AKI, severe AKI and composite complications, which included pleural fluid volume exceeding 10 mL/kg/d, culture-confirmed pulmonary infection, the need for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) implantation, and in-hospital mortality.

Disease definition

The complexity of CHD surgery was scored using the STAT mortality category (23). STAT 1 referred to the lowest mortality risk and STAT 5 referred to the highest mortality risk. The definition of CCHD was based on the anatomy of CHD, including tetralogy of Fallot (TOF), double outlet right ventricle (DORV), severe pulmonary stenosis (PS), pulmonary atresia (PA), transposition of the great arteries (TGA), tricuspid atresia (TA), single ventricle (SV), total anomalous pulmonary venous connection (TAPVC) and corrected transposition of the great arteries (CTGA). Prolonged MV was operationally defined as a duration of MV exceeding 3 days. Liver injury was characterized by alanine aminotransferase (ALT) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels exceeding twice the upper limit of normal, as determined by laboratory results obtained within 7 days postoperatively. AKI was diagnosed according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria. Given that postoperative urine output in pediatric patients was significantly influenced by diuretic administration and fluid management, the diagnosis of AKI in this study was based solely on SCR elevation. Severe AKI was diagnosed when an SCR increased ≥3 times the baseline or an absolute SCR value of ≥353.6 µmol/L (4 mg/dL) within 7 days postoperatively compared to the most recent preoperative measurements. Culture-confirmed pulmonary infection was defined by the presence of radiographic evidence of pulmonary involvement and positive blood cultures.

Perioperative RBC transfusion regimen

After a long-term practice, our center has developed a comprehensive set of patient blood management measures that have been implemented for decades. These measures take into consideration the patient’s condition, combined with clinical guidelines, and RBC transfusion is applied according to strict criteria (24). The criteria included: (I) Hb below 80 g/L during neonatal CPB, or below 120 g/L after reinfusion of residual pump blood and cell saver RBC or ultrafiltration post-surgery; (II) for children under 1 year old, Hb below 75 g/L during CPB, or below 100 g/L after reinfusion of residual pump blood and cell saver RBC or ultrafiltration post-surgery; (III) for children aged 1–7 years, Hb below 75 g/L during CPB, or below 90 g/L after reinfusion of residual pump blood and cell saver RBC or ultrafiltration post-surgery; (IV) for children aged 7–14 years, Hb below 70 g/L during CPB, or below 80 g/L after reinfusion of residual pump blood and cell saver RBC or ultrafiltration post-surgery; (V) Hb below 90 g/L during the postoperative period in ICU. Although no uniform fixed target Hb was applied to all patients, transfusion was performed to maintain Hb above institutional threshold levels and to support adequate oxygen-carrying capacity according to the patient’s clinical condition.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, patients were first categorized into five groups according to intraoperative pRBC transfusion status and the quartiles of weight-adjusted pRBC transfusion volume among transfused patients. Descriptive statistics were first developed to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared between groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using either the chi-square test (χ2 test) or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the specific circumstances. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the associations between different pRBC transfusion-volume categories and postoperative adverse outcomes, using the no-transfusion group as the reference. Covariates were selected primarily based on clinically relevant factors previously reported to be associated with perioperative pRBC transfusion and adverse postoperative outcomes, supplemented by the results of univariate logistic regression analysis. Variables with a P value less than 0.1 were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. The final adjusted models included age, sex, body weight, preterm, preoperative oxygenation status, history of previous cardiac surgery, emergency surgery, preoperative SCR, Hb concentration, BUN, preoperative pRBC transfusion, STAT category, CPB duration, lowest intraoperative temperature, intraoperative bleeding volume and priming volume. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). All VIF values were <5, indicating no substantial multicollinearity among the included variables (Tables S1-S4). To visually illustrate the relationship between RBC transfusion volume and postoperative outcomes, bar charts were generated to show the incidence of adverse outcomes across transfusion-volume groups. In addition, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to assess the adjusted dose-effect relationship between RBC transfusion volume and postoperative adverse outcomes. Subgroup analyses were further performed to evaluate the consistency of these associations across different clinical subgroups. Stratification variables included sex and preoperative oxygenation status, defined as cyanotic or non-cyanotic CHD. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, and an adjusted P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.3.0. All tests in this study were two-tailed, with the significance threshold set at P<0.05. The main R packages used included car, dplyr, flextable, ggplot2, nortest, officer, purrr, readr, rms, stringr, tableone, tidyr and tidyverse.

Results

Characteristics and blood product transfusion

A total of 19,081 children were included in this study (Figure 1). As shown in Table 1, a total dosage of 9,367 units RBC was transfused for 8,483 (44.5%) patients, while 10,598 (55.5%) patients did not receive any RBC transfusion. Among patients who received intraoperative RBC transfusion, 2,050 (10.7%) patients received 0.1–22.2 mL/kg RBC transfusion with a total RBC transfusion of 2,142 units, 2,162 (11.3%) patients received 22.3–27.0 mL/kg RBC transfusion with a total transfusion of 2,215.5 units, 2,165 patients (11.3%) received 27.1–34.5 mL/kg RBC, with a total transfusion volume of 2,210 units; and 2,106 patients (11.0%) received >34.5 mL/kg RBC, with a total transfusion volume of 2,799.5 units.

The relationship between RBC volume and adverse outcomes

Regarding the incidence of adverse outcomes, with the increase of RBC transfusion volume, the duration of MV and length of PICU stay gradually increased. At the same time, the incidence of AKI and severe AKI also increased significantly across RBC transfusion groups. However, no clear dose-effect relationship was observed for liver injury, postoperative pleural fluid drainage >10 mL/kg/d, culture-confirmed pulmonary infection, ECMO support, or in-hospital mortality. In addition, in children with who received RBC transfusion, the rates of ECMO support, pleural drainage volume >10 mL/kg/d, prolonged MV, and in-hospital mortality tended to increase with increasing RBC transfusion volume (Table 3). RCS analysis further suggested a significant nonlinear association between RBC transfusion volume and postoperative adverse outcomes (Figures 2-4).

After adjusting for potential confounding factors through multivariable logistic regression, no dose-effect relationship was observed between the volume of RBC transfusion and composite complication rate, AKI, severe AKI, or prolonged MV. For composite complications, compared with the no-transfusion group, patients receiving 0.1–22.2 mL/kg RBC had a lower risk of composite complications [odds ratio (OR): 0.36, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.17–0.76; P=0.007]. The 22.3–27.0 mL/kg and 27.1–34.5 mL/kg groups were not significantly associated with composite complications, whereas patients receiving >34.5 mL/kg RBC had a significantly increased risk of composite complications (OR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.54–3.43; P<0.001). For prolonged MV, patients receiving 0.1–22.2 mL/kg and 22.3–27.0 mL/kg RBC had lower risks of prolonged MV, with ORs of 0.55 (95% CI: 0.40–0.77; P=0.001) and 0.64 (95% CI: 0.48–0.85; P=0.002), respectively. The 27.1–34.5 mL/kg group was not significantly associated with prolonged MV, whereas the >34.5 mL/kg group had a markedly increased risk of prolonged MV (OR: 3.45, 95% CI: 2.78–4.28; P<0.001). For severe AKI, none of the pRBC transfusion groups showed a statistically significant association with increased risk compared with the no-transfusion group. For AKI, patients receiving 0.1–22.2 mL/kg RBC had a lower risk compared with the no-transfusion group (OR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.68–0.95; P=0.01). The 22.3–27.0 mL/kg and 27.1–34.5 mL/kg groups were not significantly associated with AKI, whereas patients receiving >34.5 mL/kg RBC had a higher risk of AKI (OR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.01–1.44; P=0.04) (Table 4).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses showed no significant effect modification by sex for the associations between pRBC transfusion volume and composite complications, AKI, or prolonged MV (Tables 5-7). In contrast, preoperative oxygenation status significantly modified the associations of pRBC transfusion volume with composite complications and prolonged MV. Specifically, low-volume pRBC transfusion was associated with lower risks of these outcomes mainly among non-cyanotic patients, whereas high-volume transfusion (>34.5 mL/kg) was associated with increased risks in both cyanotic and non-cyanotic patients. No significant interaction was observed between pRBC transfusion volume and either sex or preoperative oxygenation status for AKI.

Discussion

This study was the first large cohort to systematically explore the dose-effect relationship between intra-operative RBC transfusion and adverse outcomes in pediatric CHD surgery. Initial findings suggested that there might be a dose-effect relationship between certain adverse events and RBC transfusion volume, but after adjusting for confounding factors, this relationship no longer existed. Instead, our findings suggested a possible threshold-effect association. Specifically, RBC transfusion volume >34.5 mL/kg was significantly associated with increased risks of prolonged MV, AKI, and composite complications. These findings indicated that, in pediatric CHD surgery, the association between RBC transfusion and adverse outcomes may become clinically evident only when transfusion volume exceeds a certain threshold. Notably, low-volume pRBC transfusion was inversely associated with prolonged MV, AKI, and composite complications, suggesting that limited transfusion may be necessary in this population.

Since its birth, the benefits and drawbacks of RBC transfusion have been the focus of discussion. The extreme shortage of blood for clinical needs has driven researchers to explore more rigorous management strategies for RBC transfusion, of which some have been proved quite effective and written into clinical guidelines, but there still exists doubts about the relationship between RBC transfusion volume and adverse events. Numerous studies have reported a dose-effect relationship between RBC transfusion and negative outcomes in both non-cardiac and adult cardiac surgery. For example, study involving patients with isolated severe traumatic brain injury revealed that the greater the RBC transfusion volume, the higher the mortality (25). Similarly, analysis found in patients undergoing spinal surgery that increased volume of RBC transfusion correlated with higher risk of thrombosis (26). Furthermore, literature had reported that the RBC transfusion volume for neonate was associated with dose-dependent increases in renal, central nervous system, and cardiovascular complications, as well as 30-day mortality (27). In the field of cardiac surgery, there also existed an observed relationship between elevated intraoperative and postoperative RBC transfusion and AKI, respiratory failure and all-cause mortality (21,28-30). Some studies even pointed out that only a single unit of RBC transfusion would increase the risk of complications (31,32). Collectively, these studies support the view that the more RBC transfusion, the worse the outcomes.

However, studies in recent years have begun to challenge this traditional dose-effect relationship. For example, studies have pointed out that a single unit of RBC transfusion had no impact on the survival rates of patients undergoing non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) resection, despite poorer prognoses associated with transfusion of multiple RBC units (33). Coincidentally, studies in trauma medicine research demonstrated that a RBC transfusion volume exceeding 20 mL/kg was a more accurate predictor of adverse events, proving that small to moderate RBC transfusion volume would not increase adverse events (34). In children undergone non-cardiac surgery, researchers also figured out that only a larger dose of RBC transfusion would elevate mortality risk (35). In addition, Wang et al. stratified patients based on transfusion risk scores (36). It was found that in medium and high transfusion risk groups, low to moderate volume of RBC transfusion did not significantly increase the risk of major adverse events, and the risk increased significantly only when the volume exceeded a specific threshold. This suggested that a certain amount of RBC transfusion was safe but necessary in specific population, and the risk mainly stems from unnecessary excessive transfusion.

The findings of this study align with this prevailing view, which means that small to moderate volume of RBC transfusion in pediatric CHD surgery is generally safe, whereas massive transfusion significantly increases the risk of adverse events. This may be closely related to the objectives of RBC transfusion and the characteristics of patients. First of all, the majority of the children in this study featured in low weight, necessitating a relatively large priming volume, which resulted in severe hemodilution. In such cases, a considerable portion of RBC transfusion was administered to correct the excessive hemodilution and ensure adequate tissue perfusion and organ oxygenation. In fact, approximately 36.4% of RBC in our study were utilized for CPB priming, which appeared to exert a protective effect rather than pathological one. This hypothesis was confirmed by studies indicating that preventive RBC transfusion to maintain physiological homeostasis offered more advantages than disadvantages, and wouldn’t increase the risk of death (37-40). The present study also found that, compared with no transfusion, low-volume pRBC transfusion was associated with lower risks of prolonged MV, AKI, and composite complications. Although this finding appears to contrast with the prevailing view that pRBC transfusion is associated with increased postoperative morbidity, it may be biologically plausible in the context of pediatric cardiac surgery. During CPB, marked hemodilution can reduce Hb concentration and thereby impair oxygen delivery. Given that inadequate oxygen delivery during CPB has been associated with postoperative organ injury (41), low-volume pRBC transfusion may help restore oxygen-carrying capacity in children with clinically relevant anemia or hemodilution. Secondly, previous studies concentrated excessively on outcomes of limited clinical significance, such as venous thromboembolism, superficial infection, length of hospital stay or inotropic drug score et.al. (21,42-44). In contrast, our study devoted to explore the relation between RBC volume and more critical complications including prolonged MV, severe AKI, ECMO and other serious complications that substantially influence the prognosis of pediatric patients (45,46). In addition, the RBC transfusion volume is often positively correlated with the complexity of surgery and the severity of underlying disease. In this study, children with massive RBC transfusion were typically younger, of lower weight, diagnosed with CCHD and experienced longer duration of surgery and CPB. Although these factors may act as confounding variables, even after adjustments through multivariate regression analysis, RBC transfusion volume remained an independent risk factor for certain outcomes, suggesting that transfusion itself did have independent adverse effects.

In addition, the present study found that preoperative oxygenation status modified the association between pRBC transfusion volume and postoperative outcomes, which may be related to differences in oxygen delivery reserve between patients with CCHD and non-CCHD. Children with CCHD are chronically exposed to hypoxemia and often develop compensatory erythrocytosis, resulting in relatively higher preoperative Hb levels (47). Therefore, these patients may have a certain degree of tolerance to CPB-related hemodilution, and low-volume pRBC transfusion may have a limited effect on improving oxygen delivery. In contrast, patients with non-CCHD usually have lower preoperative Hb levels and may be more susceptible to reduced oxygen-carrying capacity during surgery. Thus, the association between low-to-moderate pRBC transfusion volume and lower risk of adverse outcomes among non-cyanotic patients may reflect the potential clinical benefit of appropriately correcting perioperative anemia or hemodilution.

Based on the above results, the decision to administer RBC transfusion must be carefully weighed to avoid unnecessary and excessive blood transfusion, which naturally leads to the discussion of restrictive blood transfusion strategies. Studies have shown that restrictive transfusion strategy can reduce the amount of RBC transfusion without increasing the risk of adverse events (20). However, the existing studies are predominantly focus on postoperative RBC transfusion, while there is a lack of high-level evidence to guide intraoperative RBC transfusion, which account for more than half of the RBC transfusion (48). Furthermore, establishing a precise RBC transfusion target—whether it is to achieve specific target of physiological parameters such as lactate levels, mixed venous oxygen saturation, regional oxygen saturation monitoring, or to achieve a particular Hb value, remains a key point for future research. Although the safety and efficacy of restrictive blood transfusion strategy in pediatric CHD surgery patients have not been thoroughly evaluated, significant variability persists in RBC practices across different centers. The substantial heterogeneity among pediatric and CHD patients, in terms of disease, surgical procedures, and individual characteristics, poses challenges to a nationwide critical assessment of RBC transfusion practices. Consequently, RBC transfusion decisions should be informed by a comprehensive evaluation of each patient’s symptoms, clinical and physiological signs, and laboratory results (6). Therefore, a uniform RBC transfusion strategy is not recommended for this population. Future efforts should focus on the development of individualized and goal-directed transfusion protocols, incorporating a range of patient blood management strategies. These strategies should include preoperative optimization of iron supplementation, miniaturization of the CPB circuit, blood recovery techniques, and precise monitoring. Together, these measures form a comprehensive approach aimed at reducing the overall transfusion volume and enhancing the prognosis for pediatric patients.

As the first large-scale study in this field, it clarified the threshold effect between RBC transfusion and severe complications, thereby providing a novel basis for clinical decision-making. Despite the significant insights offered by this study, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design and reliance on data from a single center may affect the generalizability of the findings. Besides, incomplete data resulted in the exclusion of 733 children, which may introduce selection bias; Nevertheless, adjustments were made for known confounders such as age, weight, CPB duration, and surgery type through multivariable logistic regression model, which confirmed the independent association of RBC transfusion with adverse outcomes. In addition, the use of culture-confirmed criteria may have underestimated the true incidence of postoperative pulmonary infection, and that this outcome should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, long-term prognosis of RBC transfusion volume was not evaluated, thus failing to make a comprehensive understanding of the impact of transfusion.

Conclusions

This study confirms the presence of a significant threshold-effect concerning the risk of adverse events following pediatric CHD surgery in relation to the volume of RBC transfusion, as opposed to a straightforward linear dose-effect relationship. These findings robustly advocate for the clinical implementation of more judicious RBC transfusion strategies and the proactive advancement of individualized patient blood management to enhance the prognostic outcomes in pediatric patients.
