Comparable myopia control efficacy of highly aspherical lenslets and diffusion optics technology spectacle lenses: a one-year real-world cohort study
Authors: Shanshan Pan, Panpan Zhang, Desheng Song, Jiaqi Yao, Bin Lou, Jiaona Jing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare the real-world efficacy of Highly Aspherical Lenslets (HAL) spectacles, Diffusion Optics Technology (DOT) spectacles, and single vision lenses (SVL) for controlling myopia progression in children and adolescents.MethodsA total of 159 participants aged 6 to 13 years were enrolled from the Ophthalmology Clinic of Nanjing Children’s Hospital between April 2024 and September 2025, comprising 53 subjects each in the HAL, DOT, and SVL groups. Participants were further stratified into a younger subgroup (6 to < 10 years) and an older subgroup (10 to13 years). One-way analysis of variance was used to compare changes in spherical equivalent (SE) and axial length(AL) after one year.ResultsBoth HAL and DOT groups were associated with significantly slower progression in SE and AL than the SVL group (all P < 0.001), with no statistically significant differences between the two treatment groups (P = 0.35 for SE; P = 0.22 for AL). In both age subgroups, axial elongation was similar between HAL and DOT (P = 0.52 and P = 0.15), and both were significantly slower than SVL in the older subgroup (both P < 0.001). In the younger subgroup, HAL also slowed axial elongation significantly compared with SVL (P = 0.003), whereas the difference between DOT and SVL did not reach statistical significance after Bonferroni correction (P = 0.02), although the observed absolute difference of 0.11 mm per year may still be clinically meaningful. Pearson correlation analysis showed that only age was significantly correlated with AL elongation in both the HAL (r= − 0.35, P = 0.024) and DOT groups (r= − 0.48, P = 0.001).ConclusionIn this real-world cohort, both HAL and DOT lenses were linked to slower myopia progression, with similar progression rates between HAL and DOT. However, the efficacy of DOT in younger children (aged 6 to < 10 years) requires further investigation with larger sample sizes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12886-026-05085-0.Keywords: Myopia, Spectacle lenses with highly aspherical lenslets, Diffusion optics technology lenses

Introduction

The global prevalence of myopia continues to rise and is estimated to reach 50% by mid-century [1]. This trend is particularly pronounced among children in East Asia, accompanied by a marked increase in cases of high myopia, presenting a significant public health challenge. High myopia is associated with severe ocular complications, including retinal detachment [2], glaucoma [3], and choroidal neovascularization [4], underscoring the urgent need for effective preventive interventions.

Currently, various clinical strategies are employed to slow myopia progression, including environmental measures such as increasing outdoor activities, pharmaceutical treatments like low-dose atropine, and optical interventions with specially designed myopia control spectacles (MCS) [5, 6]. Due to their convenience, non-invasive nature, and cost-effectiveness, MCS remains the predominant approach for myopia control. In recent years, numerous advanced MCS designs have emerged, aiming to further retard myopia progression. Previous clinical studies have demonstrated that spectacle lenses incorporating highly aspherical lenslets (HAL) [6, 7] and diffusion optics technology (DOT) [8] exhibit superior efficacy in slowing myopia progression compared to single vision lenses (SVL). However, direct comparisons of clinical effectiveness between these two specialized lens types, particularly within real-world cohort studies, remain scarce. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the effectiveness of HAL, DOT, and SVL in controlling myopia progression in a real-world clinical setting, providing valuable evidence to support precision myopia management strategies.

Method

Subjects

This retrospective cohort study collected clinical data from children with mild to moderate myopia who underwent myopia correction using only HAL, DOT, or SVL at Nanjing Children’s Hospital between April 2024 and September 2025. A total of 159 participants who completed the 12-month follow-up without changing the assigned intervention were included in the final analysis: 53 patients in the HAL group, 53 patients in the DOT group, and 53 patients in the SVL group. The case screening flowchart is shown in Supplementary Figure 1. This study strictly adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Children’s Hospital (approval number: 202403011-1). As a retrospective cohort analysis, all clinical observation indicators were recorded in the electronic medical record system, and de-identified data were exported for analysis. Therefore, the Ethics Committee waived the requirement for informed consent.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) aged between 6 and 13 years; (2) spherical equivalent (SE) ranging from − 0.75 to − 6.00 D; (3) astigmatism between − 1.50 D and 0.00 D; (4) best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 0.00 LogMAR or better. Exclusion criteria: (1) Any ocular or systemic conditions potentially affecting visual function or refractive development, including strabismus, cataract, glaucoma, or retinal disease; (2) previous use of myopia control interventions other than SVL; (3) initiation of additional interventions within one year after spectacle wear, such as atropine eye drops, red-light therapy, or vision training.

Data collection

Axial length (AL) was measured using an IOLMaster 500 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Germany) before cycloplegia. A minimum of five repeated measurements was performed for each eye, and the average value was recorded. Objective refraction was conducted using an autorefractor (ARK-1, NIDEK, Shanghai, China) at least 30 min after instillation of three drops of compound tropicamide (0.5% tropicamide and 0.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride; Xingqi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China), administered at 5-minute intervals. Subsequently, subjective refraction was performed by experienced optometrists using a phoropter (Nidek RT-5100, Nidek Co., Ltd., Japan) to determine the final refractive error, applying the principle of maximum plus to maximum visual acuity. All enrolled subjects were followed for one year after spectacle dispensing, with AL and SE measured every six months thereafter.

Sample size calculation

Sample size calculation was based on mean annual axial elongation and standard deviation(SD) for HAL, DOT, and SVL reported in previous randomized controlled trial (RCT) studies [7, 8]. Using a one-sided test with a significance level of 0.05, each group required 49 subjects to achieve 0.80 statistical power. In this study, 53 patients were enrolled in each group, meeting the sample size requirement. Sample size was determined using PASS software.

Stratified analysis

To evaluate potential differences in the efficacy of various optical interventions across age groups, participants were stratified into a younger subgroup (aged 6 to < 10 years) and an older subgroup (aged 10 to 13 years) for separate statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Due to the high correlation between biometric values of the right and left eyes (Spearman’s correlation coefficient = 0.96), data from the right eye were used for primary analysis. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

Differences among the HAL, DOT, and SVL groups were examined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests for multiple comparisons. For these pairwise comparisons, the Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold was set at P < 0.017 (0.05/3). For other analyses, statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. To control for potential confounding, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to compare 12-month changes in AL and SE, adjusting for baseline AL, baseline SE, age, and sex. Residual confounding due to unmeasured variables may remain because of the non-randomized design. The assumption of homogeneity of regression slopes for ANCOVA was evaluated by examining covariate × group interaction terms. No significant interactions were observed (all P > 0.05), confirming that this assumption was satisfied.

To assess the impact of missing data, a conservative sensitivity analysis was performed assuming that excluded HAL and DOT participants had the same axial elongation and SE progression as the SVL group (0.38 mm and − 0.82 D, respectively). Group comparisons were then recalculated using ANOVA.

Effect sizes were calculated to quantify treatment effects. For ANCOVA models, partial eta-squared (η²p) was reported, with values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively. Cohen’s d was calculated for pairwise comparisons, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 considered small, medium, and large effects, respectively.

Axial elongation

After one year of follow-up, mean AL increases were 0.22 ± 0.15 mm in the HAL group, 0.25 ± 0.20 mm in the DOT group, and 0.38 ± 0.14 mm in the SVL group. Compared to the SVL group, both the HAL and DOT groups were associated with significantly slower axial elongation, whereas no statistically significant difference was observed between the HAL and DOT groups (P = 0.35) (Table 2; Fig. 1). The mean differences in AL elongation were − 0.16 mm (95%CI: -0.22 to -0.10) for HAL vs. SVL, -0.13 mm (95%CI: -0.20 to -0.06) for DOT vs. SVL, and − 0.03 mm (95%CI: -0.10 to 0.04) for HAL vs. DOT. The corresponding effect sizes were large for HAL vs. SVL (Cohen’s d = 1.07; 95%CI: 0.66 to 1.48), moderate-to-large for DOT vs. SVL (Cohen’s d = 0.73; 95%CI: 0.34 to 1.12), and small for HAL vs. DOT (Cohen’s d = 0.17; 95%CI: -0.21 to 0.55).

SE increase

After one year of follow-up, mean changes in SE were − 0.41 ± 0.35 D in the HAL group, − 0.50 ± 0.38 D in the DOT group, and − 0.82 ± 0.40 D in the SVL group. Both HAL and DOT groups were associated with significantly smaller SE progression compared to the SVL group, while no statistically significant difference was observed between HAL and DOT groups (P = 0.22) (Table 2; Fig. 1). The mean differences in SE progression were 0.41 D (95%CI: 0.26–0.56) for HAL vs. SVL, 0.32 D (95%CI: 0.16–0.48) for DOT vs. SVL, and 0.09 D (95%CI: − 0.07 to 0.25) for HAL vs. DOT. Effect sizes were large for both HAL vs. SVL (Cohen’s d = 1.05; 95%CI: 0.64 to 1.46) and DOT vs. SVL (Cohen’s d = 0.82; 95%CI: 0.42 to 1.22), while the comparison between HAL and DOT showed a small effect (Cohen’s d = 0.23; 95%CI: -0.15 to 0.61).

After adjusting for baseline SE, AL, age, and sex in an ANCOVA model, adjusted 12-month ΔSE values were − 0.40 D (95%CI, − 0.48 to − 0.33) for the HAL group, − 0.50 D (95%CI, − 0.56 to − 0.44) for the DOT group, and − 0.83 D (95%CI, − 0.91 to − 0.75) for the SVL group, demonstrating a significant overall between-group effect (F = 12.52, P < 0.001). Post-hoc analysis confirmed that SE progression remained significantly smaller in both HAL (P < 0.001) and DOT (P = 0.001) groups relative to the SVL group, with no statistically significant difference between HAL and DOT groups (P = 0.15) (Table 3). The ANCOVA model for 12-month SE change also demonstrated a large effect size (η²p = 0.24). The mean differences were 0.43 D (95%CI: 0.28–0.58) for HAL vs. SVL, 0.33 D (95%CI: 0.17–0.49) for DOT vs. SVL, and 0.10 D (95%CI: − 0.06 to 0.26) for HAL vs. DOT. Effect sizes were large for both HAL vs. SVL (Cohen’s d = 1.13; 95%CI: 0.72 to 1.54) and DOT vs. SVL (Cohen’s d = 0.85; 95%CI: 0.45 to 1.25), while the comparison between HAL and DOT showed a small effect (Cohen’s d = 0.25; 95%CI: − 0.13 to 0.63).

Sensitivity analysis for missing data

Under the conservative assumption that all excluded participants in the HAL and DOT groups had the same AL elongation (0.38 mm) and SE progression (–0.82 D) as the SVL group mean, HAL and DOT groups remained significantly better in controlling AL elongation (HAL: P < 0.001; DOT: P = 0.002) and SE progression (HAL: P < 0.001; DOT: P = 0.006) compared to SVL. The differences between HAL and DOT groups remained statistically non-significant for both outcomes (AL: P = 0.18; SE: P = 0.21). These sensitivity analyses support the robustness of our primary findings.

Exploratory analysis of influencing factors

Correlation analysis revealed that older age was significantly associated with slower AL elongation in both the HAL group (P = 0.001) and the DOT group (P = 0.01), whereas no such association was observed in the SVL group. Baseline AL, SE, and sex were not significantly correlated with AL elongation in any of the three groups(Table 4). This exploratory analysis using Pearson correlation should be interpreted with caution, particularly for categorical variables such as sex.

Discussion

Given the availability of multiple optical interventions to slow myopia progression, evaluating their real-world effectiveness is crucial for clinicians. This study aimed to provide insights from clinical practice, although its non-randomized design limits causal inference. HAL spectacles have a 9 mm central optical zone for full distance refractive correction, surrounded by 11 concentric rings of 1,021 aspherical lenslets (each 1.12 mm in diameter), with defocus power progressively increasing from + 3.50 D centrally to + 5.50 D peripherally [7] (see Supplementary Figure 2). The geometry of these lenslets generates a volume of myopic defocus in front of the retina at any eccentricity, serving as a myopia control signal [9]. DOT lens has a 5 mm central optical zone that provides full distance refractive correction, while thousands of surrounding microdots scatter light to reduce inter-cone signal differences—preserving excellent visual acuity and functional peripheral vision—thereby modifying retinal contrast signals to slow myopia progression [8] (see Supplementary Figure 2). These distinct optical principles lead to different light–retina interactions between HAL and DOT lenses, which may underlie differences in observed associations with myopia progression.

In this study, AL elongation observed in the HAL group was consistent with findings reported by Tang et al. [10] but was greater than results from studies conducted by Bao et al. and Lembo et al. [7, 11]. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Bao et al. reported 1-year AL and SE changes of 0.13 mm and − 0.27 D, respectively, in the HAL group [7]. A retrospective real-world study by Lembo et al., including 146 European patients, reported corresponding changes of 0.15 mm and − 0.30 D [11]. Several factors may explain these discrepancies. First, spectacle wear compliance, a critical determinant of myopia control efficacy, was not systematically recorded in this real-world retrospective study. In contrast, participants in RCTs typically demonstrate higher compliance due to regular monitoring, reminder systems, and stringent inclusion criteria that select motivated subjects. The absence of compliance data in this study limits the attribution of observed associations solely to lens design and may partially explain why AL elongation in the HAL and DOT groups exceeded that reported in previous RCTs. Secondly, myopia control efficacy is influenced by factors such as age, sex, and baseline SE [12, 13]. This study enrolled younger children on average, who are at higher risk of rapid myopia progression and may exhibit poorer compliance, potentially contributing to the observed differences. Lastly, slower myopia progression rates in European populations compared to Asian populations may contribute to relatively better treatment outcomes with MCS among European patients [14, 15].

In this study, AL elongation in the DOT group exceeded that previously reported in an RCT (0.15 mm) [8]. This discrepancy could similarly be attributed to variations in study design, spectacle wear compliance, and characteristics of the study population. Although RCTs are considered the gold standard for evaluating treatment efficacy, they are typically conducted in highly selective populations under controlled conditions, with randomization substantially reducing known or suspected confounding factors [16]. However, several limitations restrict the generalizability of RCTs to real-world clinical practice. These include stringent participant selection criteria, a focus on compliant patients who may respond better to treatment, potentially leading to an overestimation of treatment effects, more frequent assessments and follow-up visits compared to routine clinical care, and specialized clinical settings that differ markedly from typical practice environments [16]. Consequently, findings from RCTs may not fully represent outcomes in broader patient populations and varied clinical contexts. Conversely, real-world studies offer greater generalizability, albeit typically at the cost of reduced internal validity [16]. Thus, results from RCTs and real-world studies can complement each other by addressing their respective limitations, providing clinicians with more comprehensive guidance for evaluating treatment effectiveness.

This study identified age as the only factor significantly associated with AL elongation in both HAL and DOT groups, consistent with previous studies [8, 10, 13] demonstrating a negative correlation between baseline age and annual AL changes. Although some studies suggest higher baseline myopia is associated with slower AL elongation during MCS use [13], others have reported no statistically significant correlation between baseline myopia and AL change in children wearing MCS [10, 12], a finding echoed by the present study. These conflicting results indicate that the relationship between baseline myopia and AL elongation following MCS treatment warrants further investigation.

Results from this study indicate that, compared with the SVL group, DOT group patients exhibited significantly slower progression in both SE and AL over one year. However, among younger children aged 6 to < 10 years, no statistically significant difference in AL elongation was observed between DOT and SVL groups, although the observed absolute difference of 0.11 mm per year may still be clinically meaningful. This may be attributed to faster myopia progression in younger children. These findings suggest that caution should be exercised when considering DOT spectacles for myopia control in this younger age group. It should be noted that the subgroup analysis by age was not prespecified in the sample size calculation, and the smaller sample size in the younger subgroup may have limited statistical power to detect a true difference between DOT and SVL. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance after correction does not necessarily imply absence of clinical benefit. Future studies with larger sample sizes and adequate power for subgroup analyses are needed to confirm these findings.

In current clinical practice, HAL and DOT lenses are commonly used optical interventions for myopia control. MCS have no age restrictions and can correct relatively high degrees of myopia. For example, in mainland China, both HAL and DOT lenses have a maximum correction limit of − 10.00 D when combining myopia and astigmatism. Additionally, both brands of MCS are moderately priced. Due to their non-invasive nature, MCS are convenient, safe, and generally acceptable to adolescents with myopia and their parents [17]. Results from this study demonstrate that both types of MCS were associated with slower myopia progression. Although HAL lenses showed slightly greater reductions in AL elongation and SE progression compared to DOT lenses, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, both types of spectacles are suitable for clinical use in myopia management. It is noteworthy that clinical studies have reported that wearing HAL lenses may directly or indirectly increase astigmatism in myopic children, primarily due to changes in corneal astigmatism. When using HAL lenses for myopia control, regular monitoring of astigmatism is recommended to promptly address any changes [18], thereby balancing myopia control efficacy with potential astigmatic effects to preserve visual health. Another optical intervention for myopia is orthokeratology (Ortho-K), involving lenses worn overnight to provide spectacle-free daytime vision. Ortho-K lenses offer greater convenience for children with active lifestyles. Moreover, orthokeratology may improve appearance satisfaction and peer perception [19] and has been associated with better vision-related quality of life compared to spectacle wear [19, 20]. Previous studies have reported favorable myopia control outcomes with orthokeratology. A recent systematic review indicated that orthokeratology lenses with smaller optical zones slow AL elongation by 0.04–0.19 mm per year, outperforming conventional designs (0.14–0.31 mm per year) [21], with overall outcomes comparable to those observed with HAL and DOT spectacles in this study. In summary, orthokeratology and MCS have respective advantages and limitations. Clinicians should select the most appropriate myopia control method based on individual patient characteristics and needs.

This study has several limitations. First, only one-year data were analyzed. Spectacle efficacy in slowing myopia progression typically decreases over time, with long-term outcomes often less pronounced than short-term results [7, 22]. Second, due to the non-randomized and retrospective design, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Factors such as baseline progression rate, parental myopia, parental preferences, outdoor activity levels, near-work activities, compliance with lens wear, and socioeconomic status, which were not captured in this dataset, may have influenced both the choice of optical interventions and the observed outcomes. Therefore, the observed associations between lens type and myopia progression should not be interpreted as causal. Future prospective studies with standardized data collection on these variables are warranted to confirm our findings. Third, although a standardized cycloplegic protocol with compound tropicamide was used, this agent may not fully suppress accommodation in all children, particularly younger or highly accommodative patients. This limitation could introduce minor variability in SE measurements. Future studies employing stronger cycloplegic agents, such as cyclopentolate, may provide more precise refractive data. Fourth, although a conservative sensitivity analysis was performed assuming worst-case outcomes for excluded participants, residual selection bias cannot be completely ruled out. Complete-case analysis may overestimate treatment effects if patients with poorer progression or lower compliance were more likely to drop out. Nevertheless, the sensitivity analysis confirmed that the main conclusions remained statistically significant even under conservative assumptions.

In summary, findings from this study indicate that, under real-world conditions in China, the use of HAL and DOT spectacles was associated with more favorable myopia control outcomes compared to SVL in children with low to moderate myopia, with no statistically significant difference detected between the two treatment groups. In younger children, the difference in AL elongation between DOT and SVL did not reach statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons. Nevertheless, the observed absolute difference of 0.11 mm per year (28% reduction) may still be clinically meaningful. Further research is warranted to determine the efficacy of DOT in this age group.
