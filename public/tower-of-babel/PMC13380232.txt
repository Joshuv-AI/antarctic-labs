Engineering single-dose plasmid DNA for sustained in vivo delivery of designer incretins
Authors: Ebony N. Gary, Yangcheng Gao, Casey E. Hojecki, Nicholas J. Tursi, Wujuan Zhang, Aaron R. Goldman, Niklas Laenger, Martina Tomirotti, Mohammad Suhail Khan, Riya Sahai, Jacob Oblander, Jinwei Huang, Zi Jie Lin, Jiayan Cui, Xiaoyang Liu, Laurent Humeau, Jesper Pallesen, Elizabeth M. Parzych, David B. Weiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Incretin mimetic drugs have transformed the treatment of obesity and type 2 diabetes; however, production and patient compliance challenges remain. Plasmid DNA has recently demonstrated the ability to deliver functional monoclonal antibodies for over a year in human patients. We generated plasmid-encoded long-acting incretins (pLincretins) by fusing cleavage-resistant glucagon-like peptide-1 and gastric inhibitory polypeptide analogues with IgG heavy chain (Fc) components. Plasmids were delivered to diet-induced obese mice using a clinically validated electroporation device. A single administration drove sustained expression, supporting durable reductions in body weight, food intake, and blood glucose. Building on this framework, we used AI-guided protein modeling and a synthetic consensus approach to design a dual glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R)/glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor agonist [plasmid-encoded synthetic consensus incretin (pSynCretin)]. pSynCretin demonstrated enhanced GLP-1R avidity and induced potent weight loss in vivo. These findings establish DNA-launched incretin mimetics as a potential therapeutic tool that combines the potency of next-generation metabolic hormones with the durability, safety, and translational feasibility of plasmid delivery.

Introduction

Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) (see Glossary) and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) are incretin hormones that are used for the treatment of both type II diabetes (T2D) and obesity. Both peptides promote pancreatic β-cell proliferation and stimulate insulin secretion [1,2]. GLP-1, derived from the proglucagon molecule, is 31 amino acids long and is secreted by L cells located in the lower intestine and colon [3,4]. GLP-1 delays gastric emptying and reduces appetite by stimulating the central nervous system [5]. GIP is secreted by K cells in the upper part of the small intestine [6] and inhibits gastric acid secretion [7]. The half-life of native GLP-1 is approximately 2 min, while that of native GIP is slightly longer, ranging from 4 to 5 min [8, 9]. Both GLP-1 and GIP are inactivated via N-terminal degradation by the enzyme dipeptidyl peptidase IV (DPP IV) [10]. Due to their small size, short half-life, and the ubiquitous presence of DPP IV, both GLP-1 and GIP exhibit rapid in vivo decay [11].

The advent of incretin mimetic drugs has revolutionized the treatment of both diabetes and obesity. Semaglutide is a synthetic GLP-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA) that has demonstrated remarkable efficacy with respect to weight reduction [12]. Chemical modifications to native GLP-1 resulted in a drug with a half-life of 5–7 days. While this is much improved over the native molecule, it still necessitates weekly subcutaneous administration [13]. Furthermore, the production of semaglutide and other incretin mimetics is costly [14]. This high treatment cost imposes a significant economic burden on patients [15]. Due to these combined factors, developing long-acting incretin mimetics is of clinical importance.

Synthetic DNA (synDNA) delivery offers significant advantages over traditional protein formulations, particularly in terms of production efficiency, stability, and cost. The synDNA platform represents a technically facile, scalable platform for in vivo protein production. synDNA plasmids have favorable thermostability profiles and support long-term functional protein expression in vivo [16] without the risk of chromosomal integration [17,18]. Importantly, we have advanced electroporation (EP) delivery of DNA-encoded monoclonal antibodies (DMAbs) to the clinic, demonstrating sustained expression of two anti-SARS-CoV-2 monoclonal antibodies for over 72 weeks in humans, with no anti-drug responses or severe adverse events reported [19], positioning the platform for further continued expansion in the delivery of biologics. Several studies have demonstrated the ability to deliver incretins using nucleic acid-based delivery approaches. These have included plasmid DNA in polymer-based carrier formulations [20–22], genetically modified bacteria [23], human stem cells [24], and targeted in vivo gene integration [25]. Our laboratory has advanced in vivo EP delivery of plasmid DNA to the clinic for several vaccine candidates in the infectious disease [26–29] and cancer spaces [30–34]. In this research article, we present optimized synthetic plasmids designed for the in vivo expression of incretin mimetics, including GLP-1, GIP, and a synthetic consensus (SynCon) incretin analogue of our own design, utilizing synDNA-EP delivery.

We designed incretin-mimetic expression plasmids encoding GLP-1 or GIP with a mutation at the second position to resist DPP IV cleavage and fused these sequences via glycine–serine (GS) linkers to mouse and human IgG heavy chain fragments to increase molecular size, promote dimerization, and support antibody fragment-crystallizable (Fc) -dependent recycling in vivo. These plasmid-encoded log-acting incretins (pLincretins) were expressed both in vitro and in vivo. We compared the standard mouse GLP-1RA (semaglutide) treatment regimen of daily subcutaneous dosing with single-dose pLincretin treatment. While both pLincretins, pGLP1-mFc and pGIP-mFc, supported weight loss in diet-induced obese (DIO) mice, pGLP1-mFc displayed the best control of overall blood glucose levels. In stark contrast to the semaglutide regimen, pLincretin treatment-mediated effects persisted for up to 4 weeks following a single administration, whereas semaglutide treatment-mediated effects were reversed immediately upon treatment cessation. We further improved this design strategy by coupling it with our validated SynCon approach [35–39] and AI-assisted molecular modeling to generate a unique plasmid-encoded synthetic consensus incretin (pSynCretin) molecule, which retained the ability to bind and signal through both native incretin receptors. Purified pSynCretin-mFc exhibited higher avidity for the GLP-1R in vitro. Single-dose delivery of pSynCretin supported weight loss in DIO mice. This control was independent of diet, as pSynCretin-mFc-treated animals maintained on a high-fat diet (HFD) displayed lower body weights and food intake compared with nontreated, HFD-fed mice. These findings support previous nucleic acid-based incretin approaches and provide further compelling evidence for the use of EP DNA delivery to support long-term expression of functional protein biologics in vivo.

Results

Synthetic GLP-1 and GIP analogues express both in vitro and in vivo and support sustained weight loss and blood glucose control.

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 cleavage-resistant sequences representing the human incretins GLP-1 and GIP, fused to mouse IgG heavy chains, were designed, codon-optimized for mammalian expression, and cloned into the pVax1 vector to create plasmid-encoded long-acting incretins (pLincretins), pGLP1-mFc and pGIP-mFc (Figure 1A,B). We used AlphaFold 3 and AlphaFold-Multimer to predict the folding of these proteins (Figure 1C,D). We compared the predicted binding mode of pGLP1-mFc and pGIP-mFc with their respective receptors to the resolved crystal structures of GLP-1R in complex with GLP-1 (PDB: 3IOL) and GIP complexed with GIPR (PDB: 7DTY), respectively. The alignments show close binding homology to reference binding patterns (Figure S1A,B in the Supplemental information online). We also calculated the Predicted Aligned Error (PAE) for the interaction, obtaining an average score of 14.435 Å for the GLP-1 portion of the pGLP1-mFc and the GLP-1 receptor (GLP-1R), and an average score of 12.32 Å for the incretin helix of the pGIP-mFc with the GIP receptor (Figure S2A,B in the Supplemental information online). This further supports functionality. We expressed the constructs in vitro using Expi293F cells. The fusion proteins exhibited the expected molecular weights of ~30 kDa (Figure 1E). Protein expression levels in transfection supernatants were quantified by ELISA, yielding concentrations greater than 100 ng/ml per construct in culture supernatants (Figure 1F,G). We characterized the biological activity of the constructs in transfection supernatants using reporter cell lines, which couple incretin receptor [GLP-1R or glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor (GIPR)] binding-induced cyclic AMP (cAMP) activity to firefly luciferase. pGLP1-mFc, and not pGIP-mFc, induced significant luciferase signal from GLP-1R reporter cells (Figure 1H), while only pGIP-mFc induced luciferase signal in GIPR reporter cells (Figure 1I). These data validate our design approach and demonstrate that pLincretins retain predicted biological activity when expressed from synDNA plasmids in vitro.

To define the in vivo expression kinetics of pLincretins, we depleted BALB/c mice of CD4 and CD8 T cells via administration of anti-CD4 and anti-CD8 antibodies, as previously described [16] and injected either 50 μg or 100 μg of each pLincretin via intramuscular injection followed by in vivo EP (Figure 1J). We collected serum from these mice longitudinally and quantified human GLP-1 and GIP levels over time. Among pGLP1-mFc-treated animals, detectable GLP-1 levels peaked on day 28, with the 50 μg group achieving an average Cmax of ~40 ng/ml and the 100 μg group achieving an average Cmax of ~70 n g/ml (Figure 1K). By day 42 post-administration, all pGLP1-mFc-treated animals had 25–40 ng/ml of detectable GLP-1 in their sera, and importantly, these levels remained consistent for 70 days post-administration (Figure 1K). Among pGIP-mFc-treated mice, detectable GIP levels peaked between days 14 and 28, with an average Cmax of 1045 ng/ml GIP detected on day 14 in the 50 μg dose group and an average Cmax of ~2000 ng/ml GIP in the 100 μg dose group (Figure 1L). At day 42 post-administration, the serum GIP concentrations in pGIP-mFc-treated animals ranged from 450 to 1250 ng/ml, and these levels remained stable through day 70 post-administration (Figure 1L). These data suggest that plasmid delivery of incretin analogues can support stable in vivo expression with a single treatment.

We evaluated the efficacy of a single dose of pGLP1-mFc or pGIP-mFc in male DIO mice. Sixteen- to twenty-week-old male DIO mice were switched to a normal-fat diet, depleted of T cells, and given a single intramuscular administration of 50 μg of pGLP1-mFc or pGIP-mFc, followed by in vivo EP on study day 0. Body weight was recorded daily for 4 weeks, and intraperitoneal glucose tolerance testing (IPGTT) was performed at the terminal timepoint (Figure 1M). The switch to normal-fat mouse chow resulted in rapid weight loss during the first week of the study, which then tapered off. Both pGLP1-mFc and pGIP-mFc supported sustained weight loss, resulting in a statistically significant decrease in body weight percentage compared with naïve controls at the end of the study (Figure 1N). At the terminal timepoint, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 2 mg/kg of 20% glucose, and blood glucose levels were measured every 30 min for 2 h. Interestingly, pGLP1-mFc, but not pGIP-mFc, supported better blood glucose control (Figure 1O).

Depletion of T cells was performed to prevent potential xenogeneic responses against the non-native linkers contained in these constructs. We and others have previously demonstrated that depletion is necessary for the delivery of human IgG-based molecules (such as monoclonal antibodies) [16]. Importantly, these same constructs were expressed in nonhuman primates [40] and for over a year in immunocompetent human subjects without generating anti-drug antibodies [19], demonstrating that this preclinical technical approach can predict in vivo expression in immunocompetent hosts when the Fc-containing portion of the biologic is host-matched. Further, the switch to low-fat or standard diet feeding upon initiation of incretin therapy is standard for mouse studies. Thus, we evaluated the effects of T cell depletion and diet switch on weight control in DIO mice. Sixteen- to twenty-week-old male DIO mice were maintained on HFD and were either left immunocompetent (PBS-injected) or depleted of T cells (via administration of anti-CD4 and CD8 antibodies as previously described [41,42]) and given a single intramuscular administration of 50 μg of pGLP1-mFc. A fourth group was depleted and injected with 50 μg of a human Fc-containing GLP-1 construct developed in support of further translation of this approach (pGLP1-huFc). Body weights were recorded daily for 2 weeks post-treatment (Figure S3A in the Supplemental information online). Independent of depletion, all animals receiving pGLP1-mFc displayed decreased body weight throughout the 2-week study (Figure S3B). When we calculated the area under the curve (AUC) of body weight percentages over the course of the study, all treated animals had significantly decreased AUCs as compared with empty plasmid-treated animals, and T cell-depleted and nondepleted weight loss were not statistically different (Figure S3C). We further analyzed in vivo expression levels in another cohort of DIO animals. On day 0, male 16- to 18-week-old DIO mice were treated with 50 μg pGLP1-mFc with or without administration of anti-CD4 and anti-CD8 (depletion) antibodies (Figure S3D). Nondepleted mice had significantly lower GLP-1 in their sera at both time points, suggesting that xenogeneic responses against this construct (Figure S3E) may result in decreased in vivo expression, even though weight loss effects remain unchanged.

DNA is nonimmunogenic and therefore amenable to multi-dose regimens, as there are no anti-vector responses. Further, we have demonstrated that DMAbs delivered via EP can express for extended periods in mice and human patients. We therefore switched DIO mice to normal diet (ND), depleted them of T cells, and administered either a single 50 μg dose of pGLP1-mFc on day 0, or two doses separated by 3 weeks, and evaluated weight control for 6 weeks (Figure S4A in the Supplemental information online). Animals receiving a single dose of pGLP1-mFc lost ~20% of their starting body weight by 3 weeks post-treatment, after which they began to slowly gain weight, such that by day 42 they had lost an average of 15% of their starting body weight (Figure S4B). A second administration of pGLP1-mFc at day 21 supported continued weight control, with animals receiving two doses losing ~20% of their starting body weight by day 42 (Figure S4B). Together, these data suggest that pLincretins can support sustained weight loss over extended periods following a single treatment and demonstrate the feasibility of the plasmid DNA approach to long-term incretin therapy in vivo.

Continuous semaglutide dosing is required to maintain weight control, while plasmid DNA supports sustained metabolic benefits

To compare the durability of plasmid DNA-delivered GLP-1 analogues with conventional long-acting peptide therapy, we evaluated metabolic outcomes following a single pGLP1-mFc administration versus short-course semaglutide treatment in DIO mice. Female C57BL/6 mice were fed an HFD, consisting of 60% kcal from fat, for 10 weeks to establish obesity. On day 0, mice were switched to an ND and treated with pGLP1-mFc, left untreated, or treated daily with 5 μg of semaglutide biosimilar for 1 week. Body weight and food intake were monitored daily, and blood glucose levels were monitored weekly (Figure 2A). As in male mice, the switch to a normal-fat diet caused a slight longitudinal decrease in body weight in naïve control mice. As expected, semaglutide treatment produced rapid weight loss during dosing, but animals regained weight immediately upon cessation of therapy (Figure 2B,C). By contrast, a single administration of pGLP1-mFc induced gradual and sustained reductions in body weight persisting throughout the observation period (Figure 2B,C). Low-dose (25 μg) pGLP1-mFc treatment did not induce substantial weight loss (Figure 2B,C). When we calculated the AUCs of body weight, we observed a dose-dependent but nonsignificant trend in body weight decrease in AUC among pGLP1-mFc-treated animals (Figure 2D). We measured chow weight in mouse cages and calculated the cumulative food intake in each cage. While we observed a trend toward decreased food intake in 50 μg pGLP1-mFc- and semaglutide-treated animals, it was not statistically significant (Figure 2E). Weekly blood glucose measurements were variable among treatment groups. However, 50 μg pGLP1-mFc significantly lowered blood glucose levels compared with naïve mice (Figure 2F). When blood glucose AUCs were calculated for the entire treatment period, only pGLP1-mFc-treated animals had statistically significantly lowered AUCs compared with control animals (Figure 2G).

The effects of weight loss in general, and incretin therapy-mediated weight loss specifically, on lipid profiles remain unclear. Several studies have reported that incretin analogue therapy reduces postprandial triglycerides and alters lipid profiles in human patients. Mouse studies have observed either no effect on dyslipidemia in obese mouse models [43] or described decreases in specific lipid species in mouse tissues [44]. We subjected mouse serum to ultraperformance liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry to identify lipid species. Among the top 20 increased and decreased lipid species (Figure 2H), we observed increases in ceramides and phosphatidylcholines in the serum of naïve animals compared with pLincretin- or semaglutide-treated animals. Conversely, we observed a trend of decreased serum triglycerides (TG) in naïve and pLincretin-treated animals compared with semaglutide-treated animals at this terminal timepoint. A decrease in TGs has been associated with weight loss in human patients [45]. These data demonstrate that plasmid-delivery of GLP-1 analogues can induce sustained weight and blood glucose control in mice with a single administration and potentially improve lipid chemistry in vivo, supporting the continued evaluation of this platform for metabolic therapy delivery.

Structure-based AI modeling and SynCon design yielded a dual GLP-1/GIP receptor agonist with enhanced binding properties

We hypothesized that a peptide retaining binding to both GLP-1R and GIPR could exhibit dual incretin receptor agonist activity. To test this, we designed a SynCon peptide by aligning native GLP-1, GIP, and second-generation incretin mimetics. This synthetic consensus incretin (SynCretin) sequence was generated by selecting the most frequent amino acids at each position in the alignment or defaulting to the amino acids represented in native human GLP-1. To increase the helicity and structural stability of the peptide molecule, we incorporated an 11 amino acid helical peptide sequence. This consensus incretin was then fused to mouse IgG heavy chain fragments to promote dimerization and extend in vivo half-life. Finally, we codon-optimized this sequence for mammalian expression and cloned it into a modified pVax1 vector to create plasmid-encoded SynCretin (pSynCretin-mFc) (Figure 3A). As a first step to evaluate the SynCretin molecule, we predicted its folding via AlphaFold 3 and AlphaFold-Multimer and analyzed whether it retained structural features critical for GLP-1R (Figure 3B) and GIPR (Figure 3C) recognition. The predictions showed that pSynCretin-mFc retained the incretin alpha-helical features, and the predicted binding interfaces demonstrated homology with native GLP-1 and GIP. We also calculated the PAE for each interaction, obtaining an average score of 12.50 Å for the SynCretin portion of the pSynCretin-mFc and the GLP-1R, and an average score of 12.36 Å for the SynCretin portion of the pSynCretin-mFc with the GIP receptor (Figure S2C,D). This further indicates that pSynCretin-mFc has better binding abilities for both receptors compared with the native GLP-1 and GIP counterparts. We expressed the pSynCretin-mFc and a non-Fc-containing construct (pSynCretin) alongside pGLP1-mFc and pGIP-mFc constructs in vitro and observed an ~35 kDa protein band in the supernatant when we probed for mouse IgG Fc via western blot (Figure 3D). Interestingly, we could also detect pSynCretin-mFc when we probed for human GLP-1 via western blot but not when we probed for human GIP (Figure 3D). SynCretin-mFc signaled through both GLP-1R (Figure 3E) and GIPR (Figure 3F), resulting in robust luciferase reporter gene expression. We used sodium thiocyanate avidity ELISA to calculate avidity indices of this molecule compared with the first-generation pLincretins pGLP1-mFc and pGIP-mFc. pSynCretin-mFc exhibited significantly increased avidity for GLP-1R compared with pGLP1-mFc (Figure 3G) and maintained similar avidity for GIPR as both pSynCretin-mFc and pGIP-mFc in this assay (Figure 3G). We evaluated the ability of GLP1-mFc and SynCretin-mFc in transfection supernatants to bind GLP-1R and GIPR on reporter cells compared with the commercial dual agonist tirzepatide and GLP-1R agonist semaglutide. Both SynCretin-mFc and tirzepatide signaled through both receptors (Figure 3H,I), while GLP1-mFc and semaglutide only induced luciferase signals from GLP-1R reporter cells (Figure 3H) and not GIPR reporter cells (Figure 3I). Together, these data suggest that SynCretin-mFc indeed binds and signals through both GLP-1R and GIPR, supporting in vivo characterization of its effects on metabolism.

pSynCretin-mFc supports weight loss in DIO mice

We compared a single dose of pSynCretin-mFc or pGLP1-mFc with a single therapeutically relevant dose of the long-acting incretin analogue semaglutide in male DIO mice. Sixteen- to twenty-week-old male DIO mice were switched to a normal-fat diet and received one administration of 50 μg of pGLP1-mFc, pSynCretin-mFc, or 5 μg of semaglutide biosimilar on study day 0. Body weight was recorded daily for 3 weeks (Figure 3I). The switch to normal-fat mouse chow resulted in rapid weight loss during the first week of the study, which then tapered off as previously observed. A single administration of 5 μg of semaglutide biosimilar resulted in rapid weight loss during the first 4 days of the study; however, semaglutide-treated mice began gaining weight on study day 5, and there was no significant difference between naïve and semaglutide-treated animals at the end of the study (Figure 3J). Conversely, pSynCretin-mFc and pGLP1-mFc supported sustained weight loss, resulting in statistically significant decreases in body weight percentage compared with naïve controls at the end of the study (Figure 3J). When we performed an IPGTT at the end of this study, pSynCretin-mFc treatment was associated with significantly improved glucose tolerance compared with naïve animals. These data suggest that pLincretins can support sustained weight loss over an extended period while maintaining the glucose-controlling activity observed in peptide-based formulations.

We also evaluated the ability of pSynCretin-mFc to control body weight when animals were maintained on an HFD. Male DIO mice were maintained on HFD and treated with 50 μg pSynCretin-mFc as before, and body weights were recorded daily for 3 weeks (Fig. S5A). Even under HFD-fed conditions, pSynCretin-mFc treatment was associated with decreased body weight (Fig. S5B). These data indicate that the SynCon approach to incretin mimetic design can be used to generate new molecules that mimic the mechanism of action of traditional GLP-1 protein analogues. This data further underscores that plasmid delivery of incretin mimetics can support single-administration regimens with long-lasting therapeutic effects.

Discussion

Metabolic disease represents one of the most pressing global health burdens, yet the bene-fits of incretin mimetic therapies remain unevenly accessible due to the cost, complexity, and tolerability challenges of repeated peptide administration. The synDNA platform offers a transformative solution: long-acting, cost-effective, in vivo production of therapeutic proteins with a single administration. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated the ability to deliver nucleic acid-encoded incretin molecules [20–22,46] in vivo using various other delivery and expression approaches. We have previously demonstrated that DNA plasmids can drive durable expression of monoclonal antibodies [16,40,42,47,48], vaccine antigens, cytokines [41,49], and metabolic enzymes [50–52] in animal models and in humans [26,53,54] when delivered by simple intramuscular injection of naked DNA followed by in vivo electroporation [intramuscular electroporation (IM-EP)]. Most notably, our platform for DMAbs delivered via IM-EP has demonstrated expression of two monoclonal antibodies for more than 72 weeks in human patients [19]. Furthermore, EP has been administered safely to thousands of human vaccine recipients without serious adverse events. These precedents establish a strong clinical foundation for expanding IM-EP DNA-launched therapeutics into the field of metabolic disease and support previous work demonstrating the utility of DNA delivery for incretin therapy generally.

Here, we demonstrate that plasmid-encoded long-acting incretin analogues (pLincretins) can achieve durable efficacy in DIO mice. A single administration supported sustained expression, gradual onset of activity, and durable reductions in body weight, food intake, blood glucose, and hepatic lipid accumulation—effects that outlasted daily semaglutide treatment. The pharmacokinetics of synDNA delivery may indeed provide a tolerability advantage over peptide injections; plasmid-mediated transcription and translation lead to a slow rise in serum concentrations (Tmax ~14–28 days), followed by stable, low-level production. This gradual expression profile, typically a challenge for vaccine immunogenicity, could mitigate some of the gastrointestinal side effects that necessitate the slow dose escalation of current peptide-based therapies.

Beyond reproducing the known benefits of GLP-1 analogues, the DNA platform uniquely enables the rapid design and testing of designer molecules. Using AI-guided structural modeling and a validated SynCon approach, we generated a dual GLP-1R/GIPR agonist (pSynCretin) that displayed enhanced GLP-1R avidity, signaled through both GLP-1R and GIPR as well as the commercial dual agonist tirzepatide in vitro, and sustained metabolic efficacy in mice after a single dose. The speed and modularity of this approach suggest that dual or even triple agonists could be rapidly prototyped and optimized, offering a route to therapies that engage multiple metabolic pathways with unprecedented efficiency.

Despite these promising results, several limitations of these studies should be noted. These studies were designed to demonstrate the feasibility of simple IM-EP DNA delivery as a platform for long-term incretin therapy in vivo; as such, detailed metabolic analyses of incretin signaling were not performed. Experiments were conducted in T cell-depleted mice to mitigate potential xenogeneic immune responses against incretin–linker–Fc fusion proteins, particularly for constructs incorporating human Fc domains. This approach reflects a species-matching constraint inherent to preclinical mouse models rather than a limitation of the DNA delivery platform itself. T cell depletion is a well-established and necessary strategy for evaluating DNA-encoded human biologics in mice and has been routinely employed in the preclinical development of DMAbs [16]. Importantly, this experimental design has demonstrated strong translational validity [19]. DNA-encoded biologics evaluated under these conditions in mice have subsequently shown robust, durable expression in immunocompetent human subjects. This includes our recent clinical trial data demonstrating sustained expression of two DNA-encoded human monoclonal antibodies (encoding the Evusheld [55] dual antibody cocktail) for up to 72 weeks in human patients, without anti-drug responses or serious adverse events [19]. Thus, while T cell depletion is required to enable accurate assessment of Fc-bearing constructs in immunocompetent murine hosts, it does not reflect a biological dependency of the platform and does not diminish the clinical relevance of these findings.

Indeed, our data support the conclusion that plasmid-encoded incretin mimetics are expected to function effectively in their species-matched clinical context, consistent with prior translational experience. When we compared expression levels in depleted and nondepleted mice, we detected decreased expression of pGLP1-mFc in nondepleted animals. However, when we performed preliminary weight-loss studies in depleted and nondepleted animals, we observed similar weight control independent of depletion status, suggesting that any xenogeneic reductions in construct expression did not affect weight control.

We developed a unique synthetic incretin molecule that bound to and signaled through both GLP-1R and GIPR, supporting weight loss in DIO mice. We were unable to compare the in vivo efficacy of pSynCretin-mFc to a commercially available dual agonist, which will be important for the translation of nucleic acid-delivered dual agonists in the future and is the subject of ongoing studies. Finally, we did not evaluate the effects of long-term incretin signaling on target cells in vivo. However, the physiological and pharmacological effects of chronic incretin receptor activation have been extensively characterized in both preclinical and clinical settings. Long-acting GLP-1R agonists and dual GLP-1R/GIPR agonists are administered chronically in humans for extended periods, often multiple years, with sustained glycemic and weight-loss efficacy and no evidence of functional decline [56–58], suggesting that sustained signaling has minimal adverse effects on metabolism in humans. Mechanistically, GLP-1R signaling has been shown to enhance β-cell proliferation [59], reduce ER stress [60], and support insulin secretion [61]. These data, together with extensive clinical experience demonstrating the sustained efficacy of chronic GLP-1R agonist therapy, mitigate concerns that prolonged incretin receptor activation, such as that generated by our continuous in vivo expression from plasmid DNA or the integration of GLP-1-expressing DNA as described in other nucleic acid approaches [25], could induce target cell exhaustion. Ongoing studies evaluating the ability to use regulatable plasmid expression to induce targeted therapeutic interruption represent a future avenue to mitigate the effects of prolonged receptor activation. Future work will assess durability and potential immunogenicity in immunocompetent settings and explore clinically translatable strategies, including our human Fc fusion constructs and Fc modifications to extend in vivo half-life. The in vivo glucose tolerance, weight loss, and metabolic phenotypes, coupled with in vitro reporter cell signaling reported in this article, establish functional incretin receptor engagement. However, deeper metabolic profiling, including insulin sensitivity, ex vivo islet analysis, expanded tissue lipidomics, and structural and biochemical analyses of these products, will further refine benchmarking of pLincretins against approved therapies. Future studies will also include head-to-head comparisons against dual receptor agonists.

In summary, our findings establish IM-EP of plasmid DNA as a transformative platform for incretin-based therapy. A single injection can replicate and extend the benefits of repeated injections with peptide analogues while lowering manufacturing costs and eliminating distribution barriers. By uniting durability, translational precedent, and rapid analogue design, DNA-launched incretin mimetics represent a promising strategy to expand access to effective obesity and diabetes therapies.

Concluding remarks

DNA-encoded biologics represent a potentially transformative approach for long-term therapeutic protein delivery. In this research article, we demonstrate that a single intramuscular administration of plasmid DNA can support sustained in vivo production of functional incretin mimetics, driving durable weight loss and glucose control in obese animals. In addition to native incretin constructs, we utilized AI-guided engineering and a validated SynCon protein design approach to generate a custom dual incretin receptor agonist with improved avidity for the GLP-1R.

These studies expand the potential application space of DNA delivery beyond infectious disease vaccination and monoclonal antibody delivery into chronic metabolic disease therapy. Importantly, the recent clinical demonstration of long-term (>72 weeks) expression of DNA-delivered biologics in humans following Cellectra INOVIO Pharmaceuticals electroporation (IM-EP) [19] supports the translational feasibility of this broader platform approach.

While additional studies will be required to optimize dose control, evaluate long-term safety, and further characterize tissue-specific metabolic effects, the current work establishes DNA electroporation as a flexible platform for programmable in vivo biologic production. Beyond metabolic regulation, incretin signaling has increasingly been implicated in the modulation of systemic inflammation and immune function, particularly in chronic inflammatory diseases [62,63]. As such, programmable DNA-delivered incretin biologics may allow for additional applications in immunometabolic diseases beyond glycemic control and weight management. More broadly, these findings support the concept that nucleic acid delivery technologies may enable a new class of durable, engineerable therapeutics for chronic disease management. Addressing these challenges will be important for the continued clinical development of programmable DNA biologics (see Outstanding questions).

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODELS AND SUBJECT DETAILS

Animals, treatment, and experimental design

All animal experiments were conducted at The Wistar Institute under approved IACUC protocol 201653. Mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories. The mice were group-housed with 5 animals per cage under standard laboratory conditions in a temperature- and humidity-controlled facility with a 12-h light–dark cycle. Diet-induced obese (DIO) C57BL/6 mice were purchased having been fed high-fat diet (HFD) (60 kCal from fat) or were placed on HFD at 6–8 weeks old and maintained on diet for a minimum of 12 weeks. Mice were treated with recombinant semaglutide biosimilar by subcutaneous injection either once or daily for one week. To prevent potential xenogenic responses against the non-native linkers contained in these constructs, T cell depletion (Anti-CD4+/CD8+ mAbs, 200 μg per mouse, given intraperitoneally) was performed at the time of plasmid injection. Semaglutide control animals were similarly depleted for consistency. For DNA plasmid therapy, 50–100 μg of total plasmid DNA was formulated in water supplemented with hyaluronidase (12U/injection; Sigma) and injected into the tibialis anterior followed by in vivo electroporation using the Cellectra™ 3P device (Inovio pharmaceuticals). Upon treatment initiation all animals were switched to standard chow (10% kCal from fat) unless otherwise indicated. Chow weight, body mass, and blood glucose measurements were conducted at a standardized time each week to minimize variations. At necropsy livers were collected after perfusion with ice-cold PBS.

METHOD DETAILS

DNA constructs

Codon-optimized DNA plasmids encoding modified human GLP-1 and GIP fused to mouse IgG heavy chain fragments were produced commercially and subcloned into the pVax expression vector with an IgE leader sequence to facilitate secretion. Similarly, the pSynCretin construct was created by aligning the sequences of native human GLP-1, GIP, and other incretin analogues. The resultant peptide sequence was codon-optimized for mammalian expression, fused to mouse Fc, and subcloned into the pVax expression vector with an IgE leader.

Transfection

Expi293F cells (ThermoFisher) were cultured in Expi293 Expression Medium (ThermoFisher). Cells were transfected according to the manufacturer’s instruction using Expifectamine (ThermoFisher) Supernatants were harvested and collected 6 days after transfection.

Western blot

Protein samples in the Transfection supernatant were separated by SDS-PAGE on NuPAGE™ Bis-Tris Mini Protein Gels, 10% (ThermoFisher) and transferred to PVDF membranes (ThermoFisher) using the iBlot™ 2 Dry Blotting System (ThermoFisher). Membranes were blocked by Intercept (PBS) Blocking Buffer (LICORBIO) 1 hour at room temperature, then incubated with Goat anti-Mouse IgG Secondary Antibody overnight with gentle shaking at 4°C. After washing four times with TPBS (PBS + 0.1% Tween 20), membranes were scanned using a LI-COR Odyssey CLx 9140 Imaging System (LICORBIO). For GLP-specific detection, a polyclonal anti-human GLP-1 antibody was used as the primary (bs-0038r, Bioss) followed by VRDye™ 490 Goat anti-Rabbit IgG secondary Antibody (LICORBIO). For GIP-specific detection, a polyclonal rabbit anti-human GIP antibody (bs-0098r, Bioss) was used as the primary antibody and VRDye™ 490 goat anti-rabbit IgG (LICORBIO) as was used as the secondary antibody.

Luciferase assays

The functional activity of pGLP1-mFc and pGIP-mFc was evaluated using cell-based reporter assays. Human GLP-1R Reporter Assay Kit (IB33001) and Human GIP-R Reporter Assay Kit (IB38001) were purchased from Indigo Biosciences and used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, reporter cells were thawed and reconstituted prior to incubation with serial dilutions of transfection supernatants containing pGLP1-mFc and pGIP-mFc. After 18 hr incubation, luciferase activity was quantified using a Synergy H1 Plate Reader (BioTek). In some studies, stable GLP-1R and GIP-R CRE/Luciferase reporter cells were purchased from Signosis Bio and maintained and tested according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

ELISA

Sandwich ELISAs for GLP-1 were performed using a pair of monoclonal antibodies (ABS-046–03-02 and ABS-0330–0B-05) (Thermofisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, after coating, plates were blocked with superblock (Thermofisher), and serum samples were incubated for 2 hrs at room temperature, followed by washing, and then detected with biotinylated (ABS-033–10B-05). Following incubation with secondary, streptavidin-HRP was added for 15 min at room temperature. After a final wash, TMB ultra (Thermofisher) was used for detection and ODs were read at 450 nm on a BioTek plate reader. GLP-1 quantification was performed using a standard curve with GLP-1 concentrations ranging from 1000 ng/ml to 0.064 ng/ml. GIP-mFc was detected in samples by coating plates with polyclonal rabbit anti-human GIP (BS-0098R, Bioss) and detecting with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Bethyl). GIP quantification was performed using a standard curve of purified mouse IgG. For GLP-1R avidity ELISAs, plates were coated with 1 ug/ml of recombinant human GLP-1R (Sino Biologicals) overnight, blocked with superblock, and a 1:10 dilution of transfection supernatant was added to all wells and incubated for 1 hour at 37°C. After washing, plates were incubated with decreasing concentrations of sodium thiocyanate (NaSCN) ranging from 4M to 0M and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Remaining mouse Fc-bearing constructs were detected with a 1:10,000 dilution of anti-mouse IgG H+L HRP (Bethyl) for 30mins at room temperature. Finally, plates were washed and HRP was detected with TMB and the reaction was stopped with 2N H2SO4 before ODs were read at 450 nm. Avidity indices were calculated as the average OD of duplicate assays in the presence of 0M NaSCN, divided by the average OD of duplicate assays in the presence of 4M NaSCN, multiplied by 100.

Lipidomic profiling

Serum was isolated from terminal blood and stored at −80°C until the time of assay. After thawing on ice, lipids were extracted from 50 μl of serum with 2:1:1 chloroform:methanol:0.88% sodium chloride spiked with EquiSPLASH LIPIDOMIX standard (Avanti Polar Lipids 330731), and the aqueous layer was re-extracted with synthetic organic phase, as described previously [64] extracts were dried and resuspended in 100 μl of 9:1 methanol/chloroform, and 10 μl of each sample was injected for lipidomic analysis. Further, technical injections were performed for a quality control (QC) sample that was generated by pooling equal aliquots of all sample extracts. Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC)-electrospray-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was performed for positive and negative ion modes separately on a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive HF-X Mass Spectrometer interfaced with Vanquish Horizon UHPLC System. Gradient LC separation used an Accucore C30 Column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 2.6 μm particle size, Thermo Scientific 27826–152130) with 50:50 acetonitrile/water and 88:10:2 isopropanol/acetonitrile/water mobile phase solvents, both containing 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid. MS1 scans were acquired at 120,000 resolution with a scan range of 300–2,000 m/z for position ion mode and 250–2,000 m/z for negative ion mode, and data-dependent MS2 scans were acquired for the top 20 ions at 15,000 resolution with 0.4 m/z isolation width and stepped NCE of 20/30 for positive ion mode and 20/30/40 for negative ion mode. Lipid species were identified and quantified using Thermo Fisher Scientific LipidSearch 4.2. Identified lipid species were filtered by expected adduct and annotation grade based on lipid class as well as average peak quality and variation of technical injections of the QC sample pool. Relative quantification was based on MS peak areas. Lipid data were normalized by the internal standard of each lipid class in EquiSPLASH LIPIDOMIX and the total lipid signal in each sample.

Serum was isolated from terminal blood and stored at −80°C until the time of assay.

Glucose tolerance tests

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance testing (IPGTT) was performed after 6–12 hrs of fasting, baseline blood samples were obtained from the submandibular vein. 2 g/kg of glucose solution was administered by intraperitoneal injection. Subsequent blood collection was performed via submandibular bleed or tail vein nick at 30-minute intervals for a 2 hr period. Blood glucose was measured using a True Metrix Pro blood glucose meter and strips (McKesson).

Protein structure prediction and modeling

The quaternary assembly involving two dimerized GLP1-mFc and two GLP1 receptors was modeled using AlphaFold-Multimer and AlphaFold 3 [65,66] locally installed on the Wistar Institute High-Performance Cluster. The Wistar Institute has an AlphaFold 3 and multimer non-commercial ShareAlike license and has signed the AlphaFold 3 Model Parameters Access Terms of Use policy. For each simulation, five structures were predicted without a template, and the top ranked structure based on the ipTM+pTM score was selected as the most representative of the interactions. The selected structures were then inspected using PyMOL (Schrodinger, LLC. 2010. The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.10). Color adjustment and figure preparation were also performed in the same version of PyMOL. Predicted Aligned Error (PAE) for each segment involved in the interaction with a receptor was extracted from the pkl file of the selected best prediction. A global average PAE between both components of the dimerized incretin-mFc constructs was calculated.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical analysis

All statistics were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 or 11. Error bars represent means ± SEM or the mean ± SD where denoted. Normality was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Outliers were determined and removed using the ROUT algorithm. For data deemed normal, ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to determine statistical significance between groups of three or more (Tukey’s multiple comparison test). For data deemed non-normal, a non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was performed in order to determine statistical differences between groups of three or more. Statistical differences between groups of two were determined via unpaired t test for data deemed normal, or Mann-Whitney U test for data deemed non-normal. In all data *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.
