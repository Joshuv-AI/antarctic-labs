Nucleotide variations in 3’ untranslated region contribute to the enhanced virulence and replication efficiency of reemerging Japanese encephalitis virus genotype V
Authors: Qiqi Xia, Yan Zhang, Jiayang Zheng, Ke Liu, Beibei Li, Zongjie Li, Donghua Shao, Yafeng Qiu, Zhiyong Ma, Jianchao Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTJapanese encephalitis virus, a major causative agent of viral encephalitis in Asia, is classified into five genotypes. Genotype V (GV) has reemerged after more than 50 years of apparent absence, following identification of the sole GV isolate – the Muar strain – in 1952. Newly reemerged GV strains are genetically distinct from the ancestral Muar strain. In this study, we compared virulence and replication efficiency between the newly reemerged XZ0934 strain and the ancestral Muar strain. Viral titers in cultured cells, as well as viremia and mortality in mice infected with the XZ0934 strain, were significantly higher than those observed with the Muar strain, indicating enhanced virulence and replication efficiency of the XZ0934 strain. Multiple nucleotide variations in the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) were identified between the XZ0934 and Muar strains; such variations contributed to the increased virulence and replication efficiency of the XZ0934 strain. These nucleotide variations resulted in substantial differences in the stem – loop (SL) I, SLII, and 3’SL structures of the 3’UTR and led to differential production of subgenomic flaviviral RNA (sfRNA). The variations also were responsible for differential inhibition of type I interferon production in vitro and in vivo between the two strains. These effects collectively contributed to the enhanced virulence and replication efficiency of the XZ0934 strain relative to the Muar strain. Our findings provide insight into the role of genetic variation in determining virulence and replication efficiency between newly reemerged and ancestral GV strains, as well as the molecular basis underlying GV virus reemergence.

Introduction

Japanese encephalitis (JE) is a significant zoonotic disease caused by Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), which is transmitted among vertebrate hosts through arthropod vectors in a zoonotic cycle. Several vertebrate species, including swine, birds, and sheep, act as amplifying hosts, developing viremia at levels sufficient to infect arthropod vectors. The primary vectors are Culex mosquitoes, which transmit JEV from a viremic vertebrate to a susceptible host via biting [1,2]. Currently, JE is endemic in East and Southeast Asia, the Indian subcontinent, and Australia [3], causing approximately 20,000 human infections annually and placing more than 3 billion people at risk of infection [4]. No specific antiviral drugs are available against JEV; therefore, vaccination remains the most effective strategy for controlling JEV infection [5].

JEV belongs to the genus Orthoflavivirus within the family Flaviviridae, which includes more than 70 species, such as West Nile virus (WNV), Zika virus, dengue virus (DENV), and yellow fever virus. Its genome is a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA comprising a 5’ untranslated region (UTR), a long open reading frame, and a 3’UTR. The open reading frame encodes a large polyprotein that is proteolytically processed by viral and host proteases to generate three structural proteins – capsid (C), premembrane/membrane (prM), and envelope (E) – and seven non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2A, NS2B, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, and NS5) [6,7]. The structural proteins are essential for receptor-mediated endocytosis, determination of virulence and antigenicity, and induction of host immune responses [8–11]. The non-structural proteins primarily participate in viral RNA replication, virion assembly, and evasion of host immune responses [12,13]. The 5’UTR and 3’UTR also contribute to viral replication [14,15], host adaptation [16–18], and pathogenicity [19,20]. For example, mutations within the 3’UTR can alter JEV virulence in mice [21].

The 3’UTR of JEV (approximately 500–600 nucleotides in length) is divided into three domains – stem – loop (SL), dumbbell (DB), and 3’ stem – loop (sHP – SL) – according to sequence variability and predicted secondary structure [22]. The SL domain is the most variable region within the 3’UTR and forms four SL structures (SLI, SLII, SLIII, and SLIV). The DB domain is moderately conserved and forms repeated DB structures (DB1 and DB2). The sHP – SL domain is considered the most conserved region; it contains a cyclization sequence and the distal 3’ stem – loop (3’SL) structure [23–25]. In addition to roles in viral replication and pathogenicity, the 3’UTR, through its complex secondary structures (e.g. SLII, SLIV, DB1, and DB2), impedes degradation of the viral genome by the host cytoplasmic 5’–3’ exoribonuclease XRN1 and terminates XRN1-mediated decay, generating viral RNA fragments of varying lengths in infected cells, termed subgenomic flaviviral RNA (sfRNA) [20,26]. These sfRNAs are involved in regulation of type I interferon (IFN-I) production and immune responses [27–29]; they contribute to the viral replication and pathogenicity of orthoflaviviruses [30–32], including DENV, WNV, and Zika virus [18,20,33].

JEV is classified into five genotypes – genotype I (GI), GII, GIII, GIV, and GV – based on envelope (E) gene and genomic sequences. Historically, GIII was the dominant genotype circulating in Asia until the end of the 20th century [34]. During the past two decades, it has been replaced by GI as the most frequently isolated genotype in multiple Asian countries, indicating a shift in genotype prevalence [35,36]. Notably, the GV virus (Muar strain) was first isolated in Singapore in 1952 from a patient who originated from Muar, Malaysia; it remained the sole representative of this genotype for more than 50 years [37,38]. In 2009, a new GV virus (XZ0934 strain) was isolated from Culex mosquitoes in China [39]. Since 2010, multiple GV strains have been identified in the Republic of Korea (ROK), where they have become the dominant genotype [3,40]. Pathological analyses in mice indicate that the virulence of GV viruses, including the Muar, XZ0934, and 43,279 strains, is greater than that of the currently co-circulating GI and GIII viruses [10,40,41]. To date, nine human cases caused by GV viruses have been reported in China and the ROK [3], suggesting reemergence and geographic expansion of this genotype, with important implications for both public health and the livestock industry.

Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that the GV virus is the oldest genotype among the five genotypes and has undergone evolutionary divergence within its lineage. Currently, GV viruses are classified into three distinct clades, comprising one clade represented by the ancestral Muar strain and two clades formed by the newly reemerged XZ0934 strain and ROK strains, respectively [3]. Moreover, the clade of ROK strains has further diverged into two sub-lineages, highlighting ongoing evolution among local GV viruses [40]. Nucleotide and amino acid variations are present across the three GV clades; however, the contributions of these genetic differences to virulence and replication remain poorly understood. In this study, we compared virulence and replication between the newly reemerged XZ0934 strain and the ancestral Muar strain to clarify the role of genetic differences in determining virulence and replication efficiency and to gain insight into the molecular basis of GV virus reemergence.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Shanghai Veterinary Research Institute, China (IACUC No. SV-20221216-G01) and carried out in line with the Guidelines on the Humane Treatment of Laboratory Animals (Ministry of Science and Technology of the People’s Republic of China, Policy No. 2006 398).

Generation of chimeric recombinant viruses

To generate chimeric recombinant viruses, full-length infectious cDNA clones of the GV XZ0934 strain (GenBank No. JF915894.1) and the Muar strain (GenBank No. HM596272.1) were assembled using a four-plasmid system, which contained overlapping cDNA fragments covering the entire viral genome, as reported previously [42]. Plasmids designed for the exchange of viral gene segments between the XZ0934 and Muar strains were generated by homologous recombination, then used to construct full-length infectious cDNA clones of the corresponding chimeric recombinant viruses. Full-length viral RNA was transcribed in vitro from infectious cDNA templates using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE T7 kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The resulting RNA transcripts were treated with DNase I to remove residual cDNA templates and subsequently transfected into BHK cells using DMRIE-C (Invitrogen) to rescue viruses. The primer sequences used for constructing these recombinant viruses are listed in Table S1. All rescued viruses were verified by Sanger sequencing to confirm the absence of unintended mutations.

Immunofluorescence and quantitative reverse transcription – PCR (qPCR) assays

BHK-21 cells were exposed with JEV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 and harvested 24 hours later for detecting viral NS3 expression via immunofluorescence staining with anti-NS3 polyclonal antibodies, as reported earlier [43]. For qPCR analysis, total RNA was extracted from JEV-infected samples using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and reverse-transcribed with SuperScript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. qPCR was carried out using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan) and gene-specific primers (Table S2) as previously described [44]. Target gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH).

Growth kinetics and viral plaque assays

Pre-seeded cells were inoculated with JEV at an MOI of 0.01, and culture supernatants were collected at specified time points for viral titration using the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay on BHK-21 cells. For plaque assays, BHK-21 cells were infected with 100 plaque-forming units (PFU) of JEV and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After three washes with phosphate-buffered saline, cells were overlaid with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium containing 2% fetal bovine serum and 1% agarose, then incubated for 5 days. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 2 h, and viral plaques were visualized by staining with 0.5% crystal violet.

Virulence testing in mice

Female C57BL/6 mice, three weeks old and weanling (8 mice per group, obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd.), were used. To evaluate neuroinvasiveness, mice received intraperitoneal injections (100 μL per mouse) of JEV at doses ranging from 10° to 103 PFU; for neurovirulence assessment, intracerebral inoculations (30 μL per mouse) were performed. All inoculated animals were observed daily for 21 days. Mice that developed neurological symptoms, such as paresis and tremors, were humanely euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation, in compliance with the Guidelines on the Humane Treatment of Laboratory Animals (Policy No. 2006 398).

Detection of viremia and viral loads by qPCR

Three-week-old C57BL/6 mice (5 mice per group) were inoculated intraperitoneally with JEV at a dose of 103 PFU (100 μL per mouse). Blood samples were collected daily from 1 to 4 days post-inoculation (dpi) for viremia detection, and brain tissues were collected at 7 dpi for viral load quantification, using qPCR with NS1 gene – specific primers (Table S2). For viremia in blood (Figure 4G), absolute quantification was performed: a standard curve was generated from ten-fold serial dilutions of a plasmid containing the JEV NS1 fragment with known copy numbers, and viral copy numbers in samples were interpolated and expressed as copies per microliter of cDNA. For viral loads in brain tissues (Figure 4H), relative quantification was used: NS1 expression was normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH (Table S2) as an internal control, and the relative expression was calculated using the 2− ΔΔCt method.

Detection of IFN-I expression

Cells were infected with JEV at an MOI of 0.1 in the absence of any exogenous inducer (e.g. poly(I:C), LPS, or recombinant cytokines) and harvested at the indicated time points for assessment of IFN-α and IFN-β expression by qPCR and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) methods. Three-week-old C57BL/6 mice were inoculated intraperitoneally with JEV alone at a dose of 103 PFU (100 μL per mouse), and no additional stimuli were administered. Blood and brain samples were collected from infected mice at 3 and 7 dpi, respectively, for evaluation of IFN-α and IFN-β expression by qPCR and ELISA. qPCR was performed using primers specific for IFN-α and IFN-β (Table S2). Concentrations of IFN-α and IFN-β were measured using ELISA kits purchased from Cusabio (Wuhan, China), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. For detection of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), total RNA was extracted from infected A549 cells or mouse blood/brain tissues, and qPCR was performed using primers specific for human MX1, human ISG15, mouse MX1, and mouse ISG15 (primer sequences provided in Table S2). Expression levels were normalized to GAPDH.

Northern blot analysis of subgenomic flaviviral RNA (sfRNA)

BHK-21 cells were infected with the indicated viruses at an MOI of 1. At 48 h post-infection, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. RNA samples (10 μg per lane) were separated on a 1.5% denaturing agarose gel containing formaldehyde, transferred to a positively charged nylon membrane (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), and cross-linked by UV irradiation. The membrane was hybridized with a digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled RNA probe complementary to the 3’UTR of JEV (nucleotides 1–200 of the 3’UTR). Hybridization and detection were performed using the DIG Northern Starter Kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA structure modelling

To experimentally validate the secondary structures of the 3’UTR, selective 2’-hydroxyl acylation analyzed by primer extension (SHAPE) was performed. RNA fragments corresponding to the regions of XZ0934 and Muar strains were transcribed in vitro using T7 RNA polymerase. RNA was folded under native conditions and modified with 1M7 (1-methyl-7-nitroisatoic anhydride) as described previously [45,46]. Modified RNA was reverse transcribed using 5’ 6-FAM-labeled primers, and the resulting cDNA fragments were separated by capillary electrophoresis. SHAPE reactivities were normalized and used as constraints for secondary structure modeling using RNAstructure software (https://rna.urmc.rochester.edu/RNAstructure.html). The final structures were visualized using VARNA software (http://varna.lisn.upsaclay.fr/).

Sequence alignments of GV strains

Full-length genomic sequences of GV viruses were retrieved from the GenBank database (Table S3). Multiple sequence alignments at both nucleotide and amino acid levels were performed using MEGA software (https://www.megasoftware.net), with the XZ0934 strain serving as the reference.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. Comparisons between groups were made using Student’s t-test or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Kaplan – Meier survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sequence alignment of GV viruses

GV viruses with full-length genome sequences available in the GenBank database (Table S3), including the ancestral Muar strain isolated in 1952, the newly reemerged XZ0934 strain isolated in China in 2009, and five newly reemerged GV strains isolated in the ROK between 2010 and 2023, were used for multiple nucleotide and amino acid sequence alignments; the XZ0934 strain served as the reference. The XZ0934 strain exhibited nucleotide and amino acid identities of 90.6% and 98.3%, respectively, with the Muar strain, and identities of 97.5%–97.6% and 99.4%–99.6%, respectively, with the newly reemerged ROK strains (Table 1). Additionally, sequence alignments of individual viral genes (C, prM, E, NS1, NS2A, NS2B, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, and NS5) and UTRs from the reference XZ0934 strain against other GV strains revealed lower nucleotide and amino acid identities with the Muar strain than with the newly reemerged ROK strains (Table 1). These findings indicate that the XZ0934 strain is genetically distinct from the Muar strain and more closely related to the newly reemerged ROK strains. XZ0934 strain is more virulent than Muar strain in mice

Replication kinetics of the XZ0934 and Muar strains in BHK-21 cells were compared via the median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay. Viral titers of the XZ0934 strain in culture supernatants peaked at 36 h post-infection, reaching a maximum of 1.81 × 108 PFU/mL, whereas the Muar strain reached a maximum titer of 3.31 × 106 PFU/mL at 48 h post-infection (Figure 1(A)). A significant difference in viral titer was observed between the two strains (p = 0.007). Analysis of plaque morphology in BHK-21 cells showed that XZ0934 strain plaques had a mean diameter of 2.45 mm, which was significantly larger than the mean diameter of Muar strain plaques (1.87 mm) (Figure 1(B)). These results demonstrate that the XZ0934 strain replicates more efficiently than the Muar strain in BHK-21 cells. Mice are a well-established small-animal model for evaluation of JEV virulence, including neurovirulence and neuroinvasiveness [47]. To assess neurovirulence, mice were inoculated intracerebrally with the XZ0934 or Muar strains at doses of 102, 101, and 10° PFU, then monitored daily for 21 days. Survival analysis showed that no mice survived in either group at a dose of 102 PFU (Figure 1(C)). Mice inoculated with the XZ0934 strain exhibited mortality rates of 100% and 87.5% at doses of 101 and 10° PFU, respectively, whereas mortality rates of 75% and 12.5% were observed for the Muar strain (Figure 1(D) and 1(E)). These results indicate that the neurovirulence of the XZ0934 strain was significantly greater than that of the Muar strain.

To assess neuroinvasiveness, mice were inoculated intraperitoneally with the XZ0934 or Muar strains at doses of 103, 102, and 101 PFU, then monitored daily for 21 days. Survival analysis demonstrated that mice infected with the XZ0934 strain exhibited mortality rates of 100%, 87.5%, and 25% at doses of 103, 102 and 101 PFU, respectively, whereas mortality rates of 87.5%, 12.5%, and 0% were observed for the Muar strain (Figure 1(F)–1(H)). Significant differences in survival between the two strains were observed at doses of 103 and 102 PFU, indicating greater neuroinvasiveness of the XZ0934 strain compared with the Muar strain. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the XZ0934 strain is more virulent than the Muar strain in mice.

Amino acid variations in structural proteins partially contribute to the enhanced virulence of the XZ0934 strain compared with the Muar strain

Amino acid variations alter JEV virulence [48]. Alignment of amino acid sequences between the XZ0934 and Muar strains revealed 59 amino acid differences distributed across three structural proteins and seven non-structural proteins (Table 2). Previous studies have demonstrated that amino acid variations in structural proteins help to determine JEV virulence [41]. Therefore, chimeric recombinant viruses containing exchanged structural protein regions between the XZ0934 and Muar strains were generated to assess the effects of these variations on virulence (Figure 2(A). Only one nucleotide difference at position 36 within the 5’UTR was identified between the XZ0934 and Muar strains. This variation was considered unlikely to exert a strong effect on virulence and was retained with the structural protein region to simplify construction of chimeric recombinant viruses. Rescued recombinant viruses were confirmed by immunofluorescence assays using an anti-NS3 antibody in BHK-21 cells (Figure 2(B)). Analysis of replication kinetics showed that viral titers of the XZ0934/Muar-str strain were comparable to those of its parental XZ0934 strain, whereas titers of the Muar/XZ0934-str strain were significantly higher than those of its parental Muar strain in BHK-21 cells (Figure 2(C)). Plaque morphology analysis in BHK-21 cells demonstrated that plaques produced by the XZ0934/Muar-str strain were significantly smaller than those produced by the parental XZ0934 strain; plaques formed by the Muar/XZ0934-str strain were significantly larger than those formed by the parental Muar strain (Figure 2(D)). Mice were inoculated intraperitoneally with recombinant viruses to determine the median lethal dose (LD50) of neuroinvasiveness (Figure 2(E–H)). The LD50 value for the recombinant XZ0934/Muar-str strain (XZ0934 strain harboring structural proteins of the Muar strain) was 101.57 PFU (Figure 2(F)), which was higher than that of its parental XZ0934 strain (100.7 PFU; Figure 2(E)) but lower than that of the Muar strain (102.57 PFU; Figure 2(G)). These results suggest that replacing structural proteins of the XZ0934 strain with those of the Muar strain reduced neuroinvasiveness, although not to the level observed for the Muar strain. Conversely, the LD50 value for the recombinant Muar/XZ0934-str strain (Muar strain containing structural proteins of the XZ0934 strain) was 101.25 PFU (Figure 2(H)), which was lower than that of its parental Muar strain (102.57 PFU; Figure 2(G)) but higher than that of the XZ0934 strain (100.7 PFU; Figure 2(E)). This finding indicates that substitution of Muar structural proteins with those of the XZ0934 strain increased neuroinvasiveness, although not to the level of the XZ0934 strain. Collectively, these observations demonstrate that amino acid variations in structural proteins partially influence the difference in virulence between the XZ0934 and Muar strains; additional genomic variations may further contribute to the enhanced virulence of the XZ0934 strain.

Nucleotide variations in the 3’UTR are involved in differences in replication efficiency between the XZ0934 and Muar strains in vitro

In total, 31 nucleotide differences in the 3’UTR were identified between the XZ0934 and Muar strains. These variations – distributed across distinct structural domains of the 3’UTR (Table 3) – were hypothesized to contribute to differences in virulence and replication efficiency between the two strains. To explore this possibility, we generated chimeric recombinant viruses with 3’UTRs exchanged between the XZ0934 and Muar strains (Figure 3(A)). The resulting recombinant XZ0934/Muar-3’UTR strain (XZ0934 strain harboring the 3’UTR of the Muar strain) and Muar/XZ0934-3’UTR strain (Muar strain containing the 3’UTR of the XZ0934 strain) were confirmed by immunofluorescence assays using anti-NS3 antibodies in BHK-21 cells (Figure 3(B)). Analysis of plaque morphology in BHK-21 cells showed that plaques produced by the XZ0934/Muar-3’UTR strain were comparable in size to those produced by its parental XZ0934 strain. In contrast, plaques formed by the Muar/XZ0934-3’UTR strain were significantly larger than those produced by its parental Muar strain (Figure 3(C and D)). Nucleotide variations in the 3’UTR contribute to differences in virulence and replication efficiency between the XZ0934 and Muar strains in mice

The virulences of XZ0934/Muar-3’UTR and Muar/XZ0934-3’UTR strains were compared with those of their respective parental XZ0934 and Muar strains in mice after intracerebral and intraperitoneal inoculation (Figure 4). The survival of mice inoculated intracerebrally with the XZ0934/Muar-3’UTR strain at doses of 102 (Figure 4(A)), 101 (Figure 4(B)), and 10° PFU (Figure 4(C)), or inoculated intraperitoneally at doses of 103 (Figure 4(D)) and 102 PFU (Figure 4(E)), was significantly increased compared with the survival of mice inoculated with the parental XZ0934 strain. These results demonstrate that substitution of the 3’UTR of the Muar strain into the XZ0934 genetic background significantly reduced the virulence, including neurovirulence and neuroinvasiveness, of the XZ0934 strain in mice. In contrast, the survival of mice inoculated intracerebrally with the Muar/XZ0934-3’UTR strain at doses of 102 (Figure 4(A)), 101 (Figure 4(B)), and 10° PFU (Figure 4(C)), or inoculated intraperitoneally at doses of 103 (Figure 4(D)) and 102 PFU (Figure 4(E)), was significantly reduced compared with the survival of mice infected with the parental Muar strain. These findings indicate that replacement of the Muar 3’UTR with that of the XZ0934 strain considerably increased virulence, including neurovirulence and neuroinvasiveness, of the Muar strain in mice. To provide a complete virulence profile, the LD50 values for both neurovirulence (i.c.) and neuroinvasiveness (i.p.) were calculated for the parental and 3’UTR-exchanged viruses (Table S4). The XZ0934 strain exhibited the lowest LD50 by both routes indicating the highest virulence, whereas the Muar strain showed markedly higher LD50 values, indicating lower virulence. Exchange of the entire 3’UTR reversed the virulence phenotypes: the XZ0934/Muar-3’UTR chimera exhibited increased LD50 values, reduced virulence compared with the parental XZ0934 strain, while the Muar/XZ0934-3’UTR chimera showed decreased LD50 values enhanced virulence relative to the Muar parent. These quantitative LD50 results are consistent with the survival data presented in Figure 4A–F. Viral loads in blood and brain are critical determinants of successful central nervous system invasion by JEV and other orthoflaviviruses [49,50]. Accordingly, blood samples were collected from mice inoculated intraperitoneally with the XZ0934, Muar, XZ0934/Muar-3’UTR, and Muar/XZ0934-3’UTR strains from 1 to 4 dpi and analyzed for viral viremia by qPCR. Levels of viremia in mice infected with the XZ0934 strain were significantly higher than those observed in mice infected with the Muar strain (Figure 4(G)), further confirming more efficient replication of the XZ0934 strain in mice. Viremia levels in mice infected with the XZ0934/Muar-3’UTR strain were significantly lower than those detected in mice infected with its parental XZ0934 strain (Figure 4(G)). In contrast, viremia levels in mice infected with the Muar/XZ0934-3’UTR strain were generally higher than those in mice infected with the parental Muar strain; they were significantly increased at 2 dpi (Figure 4(G)).

Brain tissues were collected from mice inoculated intraperitoneally with the XZ0934, Muar, XZ0934/Muar-3’UTR, and Muar/XZ0934-3’UTR strains at 7 dpi, then analyzed by qPCR to determine viral loads. NS1 gene expression levels in mice infected with the XZ0934 strain were significantly higher than levels detected in mice infected with the Muar strain (Figure 4(H)). This elevated viral load was substantially reduced after the XZ0934 3’UTR had been substituted with that of the Muar strain (XZ0934/Muar-3’UTR). Additionally, NS1 gene expression in mice infected with the Muar/XZ0934-3’UTR strain was higher than the expression observed in mice infected with the parental Muar strain (Figure 4(H)). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that nucleotide variations within the 3’UTR contribute to differences in virulence and replication efficiency between the XZ0934 and Muar strains in mice, promoting enhanced virulence and replication efficiency of the XZ0934 strain.

After intracerebral inoculation, mice infected with the Muar/XZ0934-SLI and Muar/XZ0934-3’SL strains at a dose of 10° PFU (Figure 6(E)), or with the Muar/XZ0934-SLII and Muar/XZ0934-3’SL strains at a dose of 101 PFU (Figure 6(F)), exhibited significantly reduced survival relative to mice infected with the parental Muar strain. Collectively, these findings indicate that nucleotide variations within the SLI, SLII, and 3’SL structures of the 3’UTR jointly contribute to differences in virulence and replication efficiency between the XZ0934 and Muar strains in mice, underscoring the roles of these RNA structures in determining virulence and replication efficiency in vivo.

Discussion

The GV viruses have reemerged in China and the ROK [3] after more than 50 years of apparent absence and exhibit clear genetic divergence from the ancestral Muar strain isolated in 1952 [37,38]. In the present study, the newly reemerged XZ0934 strain was compared with the Muar strain to clarify the contributions of genetic differences to virulence and replication efficiency. We also aimed to gain insight into the molecular basis underlying GV virus reemergence.

As the first newly reemerged GV isolate identified in China in 2009 [52], the XZ0934 strain exhibited genetic distinction from the Muar strain and a closer relationship to recently identified GV strains from the ROK. Accordingly, the XZ0934 strain was selected as a representative newly reemerged GV virus for comparison with the ancestral Muar strain. Mice inoculated with the XZ0934 strain exhibited significantly higher mortality than those inoculated with the Muar strain. The LD50 for neuroinvasiveness of the XZ0934 strain (100.7 PFU) was lower than that of the Muar strain (102.57 PFU). Additionally, viremia levels in mice infected with the XZ0934 strain were significantly higher than those observed after infection with the Muar strain. Viral titers of the XZ0934 strain in cultured cells were also substantially higher than those of the Muar strain. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the XZ0934 strain exhibits enhanced virulence and replication efficiency compared with the ancestral Muar strain. Notably, a previous study revealed strain-specific differences among newly reemerged GV viruses. A GV strain isolated in the ROK in 2015 (strain 43,279) exhibited virulence and plaque size comparable to those of the Muar strain, whereas another GV strain isolated in 2020 (strain 43,413) showed reduced virulence and smaller plaque size relative to the Muar strain [40], contrasting with the phenotype observed for the XZ0934 strain. These observations indicate heterogeneity in virulence and replication characteristics among newly reemerged GV viruses, suggesting ongoing evolutionary diversification within this lineage. Consistent with this interpretation, newly reemerged GV viruses have been phylogenetically classified into two major clades represented by the XZ0934 strain and ROK strains; the ROK viruses have diverged into two sub-lineages [40]. Similar strain-specific differences in virulence and replication efficiency have been reported among GI and GIII JEV lineages [53,54].

Factors underlying the reemergence of GV viruses remain unclear. Enhanced virulence and replication efficiency of the XZ0934 strain relative to the Muar strain may provide insight into this phenomenon. The zoonotic transmission cycle of JEV is maintained through mosquito vectors and vertebrate amplifying hosts [55]. High levels of viremia in amplifying hosts are essential for efficient mosquito infection and sustained viral transmission. Increased replication efficiency and viremia of GI viruses have been implicated in the replacement of GIII viruses by GI viruses in several Asian countries over the past 20 years [56]. In the present study, the XZ0934 strain exhibited higher replication efficiency and induced greater levels of viremia in mice compared with the Muar strain. These features may facilitate mosquito infection and accelerate the transmission cycle of the XZ0934 strain, at least partly contributing to GV virus reemergence. However, this hypothesis requires further experimental validation.

Structural proteins play important roles in determination of JEV virulence [57]. Multiple amino acid differences were identified between the XZ0934 and Muar strains, including 10 variations in the capsid protein, one in prM, and six in the envelope protein. To assess the contribution of these variations to enhanced virulence of the XZ0934 strain, we generated chimeric recombinant viruses containing exchanged structural protein regions. The recombinant XZ0934/Muar-str virus, which harbored structural proteins of the Muar strain, exhibited reduced neuroinvasiveness compared with the parental XZ0934 strain, although virulence did not decline to the level observed for the Muar strain. Of note, this chimeric virus produced plaques similar in size to the Muar strain, indicating that plaque size, which primarily measures viral replication and spread in BHK-21 cells in vitro, does not fully correlate with neuroinvasiveness LD50. Conversely, the recombinant Muar/XZ0934-str virus, which contained structural proteins of the XZ0934 strain, showed increased neuroinvasiveness relative to the parental Muar strain; its virulence did not reach the level of the XZ0934 strain. Although direct comparison of neurovirulence among these chimeric viruses was not performed because the intracerebral route bypasses peripheral barriers and would not provide additional insight into the combined contributions of structural and non-structural elements, the neuroinvasiveness data clearly indicate that amino acid variations within structural proteins partially contribute to the enhanced virulence of the XZ0934 strain.

Multiple nucleotide variations within the 3’UTR were identified between the XZ0934 and Muar strains. To investigate the contributions of the 3’UTR to enhanced virulence and replication efficiency of the XZ0934 strain, we generated chimeric recombinant viruses containing exchanged 3’UTRs, given that the 3’UTR influences the virulence and viral replication of orthoflaviviruses, including JEV, DENV, and WNV [16,21,58]. The recombinant XZ0934/Muar-3’UTR virus, which harbored the 3’UTR of the Muar strain, exhibited reduced viral titers, viremia, and virulence compared with the parental XZ0934 strain. In contrast, the recombinant Muar/XZ0934-3’UTR virus, which contained the 3’UTR of the XZ0934 strain, displayed increased viral titers, viremia and virulence relative to the parental Muar strain. These findings suggest that nucleotide variations within the 3’UTR contribute to differences in virulence and replication efficiency between the two strains; such variations may underlie the enhanced pathogenicity of the XZ0934 strain.

Nucleotide variations within the 3’UTR resulted in pronounced differences in SLI, SLII, and 3’SL structures between the XZ0934 and Muar strains. The roles of these structures in virulence and replication efficiency were assessed using chimeric recombinant viruses in which each structure was individually exchanged between the two strains. Substitution of SLI, SLII, or 3’SL structure altered viral titers, viremia, and virulence relative to the corresponding parental strains, implying that all three RNA elements jointly contribute to differences in virulence and replication efficiency between the XZ0934 and Muar strains. We acknowledge that other 3’UTR elements, including SLIII, SLIV, DB1, and DB2, were not examined in this study because they showed only minor sequence variations and predicted structural conservation between the two strains. However, we cannot formally exclude the possibility that these regions also contribute to the observed phenotypic differences to a lesser extent, an issue that warrants future investigation.

Multiple amino acid differences were also present within the non-structural proteins of the XZ0934 and Muar strains. However, our data indicate that the non-structural proteins do not play a major role in the differential virulence between the two strains. Specifically, the chimeric viruses that exchanged only the structural proteins (leaving all non-structural proteins and UTRs otherwise intact) did not fully recapitulate the virulence of the reciprocal parental strain (Figure 2). The remaining difference in virulence between the XZ0934/Muar-str virus and the Muar strain, as well as between the Muar/XZ0934-str virus and the XZ0934 strain, can be attributed to the 3’UTR variations demonstrated in Figures 3–6. Therefore, although multiple amino acid differences exist within the non-structural proteins of the XZ0934 and Muar strains, they do not appear to substantially contribute to the virulence phenotype under the conditions tested.

IFN-I is a critical mediator of host defense against flavivirus infection [59,60]. The capacity to antagonize IFN-I – mediated antiviral responses is a major determinant of JEV virulence and replication efficiency [34,35]. For example, amino acid substitutions in NS5 (NS5-V372A and NS5-H386Y) enable GI viruses to inhibit IFN-I production more effectively than GIII viruses, conferring replication and host adaptation advantages in birds [42]. Accordingly, we compared IFN-I antagonism between the XZ0934 and Muar strains to elucidate mechanisms underlying the enhanced virulence and replication efficiency of the XZ0934 strain. A549 cells are known to have a relatively blunted IFN induction response to JEV infection, but the consistent and significant differences between strains validate the utility of this assay for comparative purposes. The XZ0934 strain induced significantly lower levels of IFN-α and IFN-β than the Muar strain, indicating a greater capacity to suppress IFN-I production. Moreover, the recombinant XZ0934/Muar-3’UTR virus, which harbored the 3’UTR of the Muar strain, induced higher levels of IFN-α and IFN-β than the parental XZ0934 strain. Conversely, the recombinant Muar/XZ0934-3’UTR virus, containing the 3’UTR of the XZ0934 strain, induced lower levels of IFN-α and IFN-β than the parental Muar strain. These findings suggest that nucleotide variations within the 3’UTR are responsible for differences in IFN-I induction between the two strains. Furthermore, the exchange of individual SLI, SLII, and 3’SL structures between XZ0934 and Muar strains altered IFN-α and IFN-β induction relative to the corresponding parental viruses, indicating that all three RNA structures collectively contribute to differential IFN-I induction. Consistently, the mRNA expression levels of downstream interferon-stimulated genes MX1/ISG15 (in human A549 cells) and MX1/ISG15 (in mouse blood and brain) were significantly lower in XZ0934-infected samples than in Muar-infected samples, and these patterns were similarly reversed by exchanging the 3’UTR or its individual stem-loop structures. Overall, nucleotide variations within the 3’UTR modulate the ability of JEV to antagonize IFN-I production, promoting the enhanced virulence and replication efficiency of the XZ0934 strain compared with the Muar strain.

Although we showed that nucleotide variations within the 3’UTR underlie differences in the ability of the XZ0934 and Muar strains to inhibit IFN-I production, the mechanisms responsible for this effect remain unclear. The 3’UTR of orthoflaviviruses is known to direct generation of sfRNAs, which regulate IFN-I production and host immune responses [27–29], and contribute to viral replication and pathogenicity in orthoflaviviruses (e.g. DENV, WNV, and Zika virus [18,20,33]). For example, sfRNAs of WNV facilitate viral evasion of IFN-I – mediated antiviral responses [28]; mutant yellow fever virus and WNV strains with impaired sfRNA production exhibit attenuated virulence in mice [19,20]. Secondary structures within the 3’UTR, including SLII, SLIV, DB1, and DB2, are critical for sfRNA generation in orthoflaviviruses [61]. Our Northern blot analysis directly demonstrates that the 3’UTR variations significantly alter sfRNA production, with the more virulent XZ0934 strain generating higher levels of sfRNA than the Muar strain. This finding supports the model that sfRNA acts as a key effector in suppressing IFN-I responses. Based on our findings and the established role for 3’UTR secondary structures in sfRNA biogenesis, it is plausible that differences in IFN-I antagonism between the two strains result from altered sfRNA production driven by nucleotide variations within the 3’UTR, this hypothesis requires further experimental validation.

In conclusion, we compared virulence and replication efficiency between the newly reemerged XZ0934 strain and the ancestral Muar strain. The XZ0934 strain exhibited substantially greater virulence and replication efficiency than the Muar strain. Multiple nucleotide variations within the 3’UTR were identified between the two strains; these variations contributed to clear phenotypic differences. The variations induced pronounced alterations in the SLI, SLII, and 3’SL structures of the 3’UTR and explained differential inhibition of IFN-I production both in vitro and in vivo. Collectively, our findings support a critical role for 3’UTR nucleotide variation in modulation of host antiviral responses and provide mechanistic insight into the enhanced virulence and replication efficiency of reemerging JEV GV strains.
