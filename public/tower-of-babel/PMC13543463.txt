Recovery kinetics following matches in congested vs. non-congested schedules: effects on pressure pain sensitivity, delayed onset muscle soreness, and muscular performance in soccer players
Authors: Xiaoshuang Wang, Yongxing Zhao, Yuqing Duan, Qiling Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated recovery responses in male Under-19 soccer players over a 48-hour period following a match, comparing congested (CW) versus non-congested (RW) schedules. Nineteen male provincial-level players (Tier 2: trained/developmental) who started and played at least 60 min in each match of both study weeks participated. Assessments of Countermovement Jump (CMJ) height (cm), Pressure Pain Sensitivity (PPS, kPa), and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS, au) were conducted at pre-match, 15 min post-match, 24 h, and 48 h post-match. No training or specific recovery strategies were implemented between assessments. Linear mixed-model analyses showed significant Week × Time interactions for PPS (p < .001) and VAS (p < .001), but not for CMJ (p = .071). Post-hoc contrasts indicated that CMJ height was significantly lower in the congested week at 24 h (− 1.31 cm, p = .027) and 48 h (− 1.59 cm, p < .001). PPS was lower during the congested week at 24 h (− 26.96 kPa, p < .001) and 48 h (− 23.09 kPa, p < .001), while VAS values were higher at all post-match time points (Post: +16.63 au; 24 h: +12.53 au; 48 h: +21.84 au; all p < .001). These findings suggest that fixture congestion delays neuromuscular and sensory recovery, reflected by prolonged impairments in jump performance and elevated pain sensitivity.Keywords: Football, Recovery, Muscle power, Fatigue, Players

Introduction

Congested weeks (CW) in soccer refer to periods when teams play multiple matches within a short time frame—typically three games in a single week—due to tight scheduling in leagues, or cup competitions [1]. This can lead to increased physical strain and repeated demands on starting players, potentially affecting their recovery processes [2]. Research on soccer fixture congestion has grown over the last decade, revealing mixed effects on player performance and injury rates [3, 4]. While physical performance metrics like total distance covered and sprint distance are generally maintained during congested schedules [5, 6], some studies show that injury rates significantly rise when players participate in two matches per week compared to one (25.6 vs. 4.1 injuries per 1,000 h) [5]. Alongside these performance and injury findings, evidence suggests that players with high match exposure during congested periods may experience increased muscle soreness [7]. Elevated plasma urea levels have also been reported, indicating heightened physiological stress and muscle damage [8].

For example, a previous study [9] reported that players who accumulated greater match exposure (playing more than 60 min in the preceding match) exhibited significantly higher pre-game creatine kinase concentrations and greater perceived fatigue compared with less-exposed teammates during congested weeks. Additionally, Nédélec et al. [10] reported that the frequency of playing actions, such as sprints and directional changes, significantly correlated with increased muscle soreness and decreased performance up to 72 h post-match. Delaval et al. [11] showed that perceived fatigue and muscle soreness were linked to noncontact injuries, suggesting that effective recovery monitoring could help prevent injuries during congested schedules.

The increased exposure to high-intensity demands, such as sprinting and high-speed running, combined with the cumulative workload in competitive scenarios during congested periods, can lead to muscular impairments in soccer players [12], particularly through delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) [13]. DOMS typically manifests 24 to 48 h after intense exercise and is characterized by muscle stiffness, soreness, and reduced range of motion [14]. Pain-related sensory input arising with DOMS can modulate motor output via inhibitory mechanisms, reducing voluntary activation and rate of force development, thus in practical terms, greater perceived soreness (e.g., higher visual analogue scale [VAS] scores) and heightened pressure pain responses are expected to coincide with transient decrements in neuromuscular performance (e.g., countermovement jump power/height) [15, 16]. Thus, integrating perceptual (VAS) and mechanical nociception measures (pressure-based tests) with jump performance can provide a theoretically grounded view of “neuromuscular recovery,” wherein pain-related feedback can temporally constrain the restoration of explosive force production following matches [17].

As the severity of DOMS increases, it can lower the pressure pain sensitivity (PPS) [18], a measure of how much force can be applied to a muscle before it causes pain [19]. Higher levels of muscle damage and soreness can reduce PPS indicating an increased sensitivity to pressure and discomfort in the affected muscles [20]. This heightened sensitivity can, in turn, compromise muscular power, as discomfort from DOMS and reduced pain tolerance may inhibit the ability to generate maximal force effectively during powerful movements such as jumping [21]. Over time, if the player continues to play through muscle fatigue and soreness without adequate recovery, the decline in muscular power and the persistent pain sensitivity can lead to a drop in performance [22].

Existing research on congested fixture schedules has largely concentrated on match running performance [4] whereas investigations into recovery kinetics across consecutive matches are scarce. Furthermore, only one study [8] has directly compared congested and regular schedules. Critically, few studies have linked pain-related markers (PPS/VAS) to concurrent neuromuscular outputs across the immediate (post-match) and short-term (24–48 h) recovery windows, which is essential to understand whether pain and perceived soreness track the time course of functional recovery. The absence of analysis on recovery kinetics across multiple days (post-match; 24 h [match day + 1], 48 h [match day + 2]) is a critical limitation for understanding the complete recovery process. Moreover, comparing these findings with data from regular weeks is essential to determine whether the observed recovery patterns result from the competitive schedule itself or simply reflect the players’ normal recovery cycle. Given these gaps in the literature, the aim of this study is to compare recovery responses in male soccer players over a 48-hour period following a match, comparing congested (≤ 3 days between matches) versus non-congested (≥ 6 days between matches) schedules.

Methods

Study design and context

This study followed an observational repeated-measures crossover design conducted over two consecutive weeks. Players were recruited from two provincial-level (Tier 2; trained/developmental [23]) under-19 male soccer teams competing in the same league. Each player was measured under both weekly schedules: (i) a congested week (two matches within seven days, ≤ 3 days apart) and (ii) a regular week (one match within seven days, ≥ 6 days between matches).

Week order (Fig. 1) was counterbalanced at the team level to mitigate order effects: Team A followed a CW→RW sequence across Weeks 1–2, whereas Team B followed an RW→CW sequence. Outcomes were collected at identical relative time points in each condition (pre, post, 24 h, 48 h). Thus, week type and time were within-player factors, while week order was implemented between teams; players were therefore nested within teams, which was accounted for in interpretation.

Illustration of the regular week (one match) and the congested week (two matches within the same seven-day period), along with the assessment time points (Pre-, Post-, 24 h, and 48 h post-match)

For each match, players were assessed at pre- and post-match intervals, and again 24 and 48 h afterward. To provide context, no training sessions occurred 24 h post-match, and players were evaluated prior to the training session that took place at 48 h. Thus, there were no training sessions between the matches and recovery assessments. Figure 1 illustrates both the regular and congested weeks observed, along with the assessment time points. Additionally, no structured recovery strategies were implemented. Due to the players’ competitive level, they were not familiar with specific or systematic recovery methods, nor did they report to the coaching staff the use of any standardized recovery interventions. This does not exclude the possibility that some individual routines (e.g., sleep, nutrition, or hydration) may have been more effective than others; however, these were part of their normal, unsupervised daily habits rather than organized recovery protocols. Eligibility criteria included only players who were starters in all matches, ensuring that any effects on recovery kinetics were not influenced by variations in starting roles. The researchers had no involvement in selecting the starters or non-starters. The study took place in the middle of the season, during a period when the league competition included an additional match (a congested week), allowing for a comparison between these types of weeks.

Participants

After the observation period concluded, it was confirmed that 19 players met the inclusion criteria for analysis, as they were starters and played at least 60 min per match in all observed matches across the two weeks. The players had an average age of 18.1 ± 0.6 years and 6.5 ± 1.7 years of playing experience. Their average playing time per match was 78.3 ± 11.9 min, with a mean body mass of 70.4 ± 5.7 kg and a height of 173.9 ± 6.2 cm.

After receiving approval from both the teams and participants, the observation began, following necessary inclusion criteria for fair comparison across different conditions (i.e., types of weeks). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) being a starter in all matches during both regular and congested weeks; (ii) playing at least 60 min in each match; (iii) not being injured or ill during the assessment period; and (iv) being available for assessment at all time points. The exclusion criteria were defined as: (i) being a goalkeeper, due to the unique physical demands compared to outfield players; (ii) having a specific or dedicated recovery strategy implemented by the player or coach that could influence the regular recovery kinetics; and (iii) using any medication that could affect recovery kinetics.

This study followed the ethical guidelines for research involving human participants. The research was conducted in full compliance with the ethical standards set by Hefei Normal University ethical committe boards (register 2024LLSP007) and the principles described in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to participation, all players, as well as their parents or legal guardians (for those under the age of 18), were fully informed about the purpose of the study, the procedures involved, and any potential risks. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant and their guardian, ensuring that participation was voluntary and based on an understanding of the study’s nature. All data collected during the study was treated with the utmost confidentiality, with player identities anonymized to ensure privacy. No personal identifying information was used in any part of the analysis or publication, and all data was securely stored in compliance with data protection regulations.

Assessments

The assessments were conducted based on the available resources and context. To facilitate this, a room near the team’s locker room was used, where participants were evaluated at four time points: one hour pre-match, 15 min post-match, 24 h post-match, and 48 h post-match. The assessments were carried out by the same group of researchers to ensure consistency in the evaluation conditions. To further improve the accuracy of the assessments, a pilot test was conducted two weeks prior to the start of the observational period for both teams. This allowed the players to familiarize themselves with the tests and procedures, helping to minimize any variability. During the observational period, the sequence in which players were assessed, as well as the order of tests, was kept consistent to ensure the best replicability of conditions. The tests began with the DOMS questionnaire, followed by PPS evaluation, and concluded with a jumping test.

Delayed onset muscle soreness

Delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) was assessed using a single-item 100-mm horizontal Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for lower-limb muscle soreness. The measure was not newly developed for the present study. Rather, it was based on the standard VAS approach originally described for pain-intensity assessment and subsequently used as a graphic pain-rating method [24, 25]. In Chinese populations, the use of a linear analogue scale for pain assessment has been evaluated previously, with evidence indicating that it can be used with relative accuracy when the procedure is clearly explained to participants [26]. The use of VAS-based pain scoring is also consistent with previous DOMS assessment protocols in which muscle pain after eccentric exercise was quantified using a VAS [27].

The VAS consisted of a 100-mm horizontal line, with the left anchor labelled “no pain” and the right anchor labelled “extreme pain”. Participants were instructed to place a vertical mark on the line corresponding to the intensity of their lower-limb muscle soreness at the specific time of assessment. The soreness score was calculated by measuring, in millimetres, the distance from the left-hand anchor (“no pain”) to the participant’s mark. This procedure yielded a continuous score from 0 to 100 arbitrary units, with higher values indicating greater perceived lower-limb muscle soreness.

Pressure pain sensitivity

Pressure pain sensitivity in the hamstrings was assessed using an analogue pressure algometer (Force Dial FDK 20, Wagner Instruments, Greenwich. Connecticut) with a 1cm2 probe area. This device was confirmed for its reliability and accuracy previously [28]. Prior to testing, participants were positioned prone with their knees extended, ensuring the hamstring muscles were relaxed. Specific measurement site on the hamstring muscle group (the muscle belly of the biceps femoris and semitendinosus, approximately halfway between the ischial tuberosity and the popliteal crease) was identified. The algometer probe was then applied perpendicularly to the skin over the marked sites. Pressure was gradually increased at a constant rate of approximately 1 kPa until the participant verbally indicated that the sensation of pressure first changed to pain. At this point, the pressure reading was immediately recorded as the PPS. Two measurements were taken at each site, with a 30 s rest period between repetitions to avoid sensitization or habituation, and the mean value for each site was used for analysis.

Vertical jump

CMJ height was assessed to evaluate lower limb power. For this measurement, participants performed jumps on a validated [29, 30], low-cost, validated jump mat system (ChronoJump). Participants were instructed to stand upright with their hands placed firmly on their hips throughout the entire movement to eliminate the contribution of arm swing. From this standing position, they were instructed to perform a rapid downward countermovement followed immediately by a maximal concentric upward thrust, aiming to jump as high as possible. Participants were given clear instructions to land softly on the mat with extended knees. Each participant performed two maximal CMJ attempts, with a rest period of approximately 30 s between attempts. The highest jump height recorded from the two attempts, measured in centimeters (cm), was used for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

An a priori sample size estimation was conducted to determine the number of participants required for the study, aiming for adequate statistical power while considering practical limitations in participant recruitment. The study design involves a 2 (Week Type: regular vs. congested) × 4 (Assessment Time: pre, post, 24 h, 48 h) repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) to investigate recovery kinetics. The sample size calculation was performed targeting the detection of a medium effect size for the main effects and interaction (ηp2, of 0.06 or a Cohen’s f of 0.25). A desired statistical power of 0.80 (80%) was set, with an alpha level (α) of 0.05. Given the repeated-measures nature of the design, a moderate-to-high correlation among repeated measures was assumed (e.g., ρ = 0.6), since they were the same players. Using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software, specifically for a ‘F tests - ANOVA: Repeated measures, within-between interaction’ analysis, a sample size of 18 participants was determined to provide sufficient power to detect a medium effect size for the within-subjects (Assessment Time), between-subjects (Week Type), and interaction effects.

All analyses were performed using linear mixed-effects models to account for the repeated-measures structure of the data. Separate models were fitted for each dependent variable (CMJ height, PPS, and VAS). The fixed factors were Week (Regular vs. Congested), Time (Pre, Post, Post-24 h, Post-48 h), and their interaction (Week × Time). PlayerID was included as a random intercept to account for within-player repeated observations across weeks and times. Because the estimated random-effect variance approached zero, model inferences were based on cluster-robust standard errors at the player level, which provide valid estimates under within-subject dependence.

Estimated marginal means (EMMs) for each Week × Time condition were obtained from the fitted models together with 95% confidence intervals. Pairwise contrasts (Congested − Regular) were computed at each time point, and the corresponding p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons when required. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated for each pairwise comparison using the model-based estimated means and pooled residual standard deviation, allowing direct comparison with the magnitudes reported in previous studies.

All analyses were conducted in Python 3.11 using the statsmodels package (version 0.14). Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Complete EMMs, pairwise contrasts with p-values, and corresponding effect sizes are reported in Table 1.

Results

Table 1 displays the descriptive statistics for various outcomes across the assessment time points and week types. Additionally, post-hoc comparisons within each week type can also be observed in Table 1.

The linear mixed-model revealed a significant main effect of Time (p < .001) and a significant Week × Time interaction (p = .003), whereas the main effect of Week was not significant (p = .467). Mean differences between weeks (CW − RW) were small at the earlier time points: at Pre, the difference was − 0.16 cm (95% CI = − 0.61 to + 0.29, p = .48); at Post, − 0.53 cm (− 1.88 to + 0.83, p = .44). At Post 24 h, the congested week showed a greater decline in jump height (− 1.31 cm, − 2.47 to − 0.16, p = .027), and at Post 48 h the difference further widened (− 1.59 cm, − 2.40 to − 0.78, p < .001). Thus, while pre- and immediate post-match values did not differ between weeks, CMJ performance was significantly lower in the congested week from 24 h onward, indicating a delayed recovery of neuromuscular function. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the recovery kinetics of players across a congested week (CW) and a regular week (RW), examining changes in selected variables at four assessment points: baseline (Pre-match), immediately after the match (Post-match), and during recovery at 24 h and 48 h post-match. The primary findings suggested that both the type of week and the assessment time point appeared to influence player responses, with significant interactions observed for PPS and VAS. Specifically, the congested week tended to produce a greater decrement in CMJ height and increased perceived fatigue (higher VAS scores) and pain sensitivity (lower PPS values) compared to the regular week, particularly evident in the recovery phases. While both week types showed acute post-match decrements followed by recovery, the magnitude of these changes differed between the congested and regular schedules, indicating a likely but not definitive prolonged impact of fixture congestion on player impairment. Specifically, the CW was associated with notably lower PPS, higher VAS, and a greater reduction in CMJ at 24 h and 48 h post-match compared with the RW.

Regarding CMJ height, our findings indicated a significant overall reduction during the CW compared to the RW, alongside a pronounced decrease immediately post-match in both conditions, followed by a gradual, albeit incomplete, recovery over 48 h. This acute post-match decrement in CMJ height is consistent with some studies in soccer [31, 32], which frequently report reduced explosive power following competitive matches, attributed to accumulated fatigue. The sustained lower CMJ values during the CW, particularly at 24 h and 48 h post-match, suggest an impaired recovery capacity under increased fixture demands. This aligns with research highlighting that insufficient recovery time between matches in congested schedules can lead to fatigue accumulation, impacting neuromuscular function and jump performance more severely than in regular periods [9, 33]. Peripheral fatigue may stem from muscle damage, excitation-contraction coupling failure, and metabolic perturbations (e.g., glycogen depletion, accumulation of metabolites) induced by high-intensity intermittent exercise during matches and less time for recovery [34, 35]. The incomplete recovery observed in the CW reinforces the potential importance of implementing adequate recovery strategies, such as optimizing sleep and nutrition, maintaining proper hydration, and incorporating evidence-based modalities like cold-water immersion, active recovery, and compression garments to mitigate the cumulative impact of match load on lower-limb power [36].

Concerning PPS, our results revealed a significant interaction between the type of week and assessment time point. Specifically, PPS values were significantly lower during the congested week compared to the regular week, particularly at Post-match, Post 24 h, and Post 48 h. This acute increase in pain sensitivity immediately following a match is a common finding in the literature, often linked to match subsequent inflammatory response [37, 38]. The more pronounced and sustained increase in pain sensitivity during the congested week, especially at 24 h and 48 h post-match, likely reflects peripheral sensitization mechanisms, such as the activation and sensitization of muscle nociceptors due to inflammatory mediator release and tissue damage. However, given the repeated exposure to high workloads and reduced recovery time, central mechanisms of pain modulation cannot be ruled out and may contribute to the prolonged sensitivity observed in congested weeks. Eventually, the absence of adequate recovery can lead to persistent inflammation [39]. Usually, contributors to increased pain sensitivity post-exercise include the release of inflammatory mediators from damaged muscle tissue, which sensitize peripheral nociceptors [40, 41]. The inability of PPS to fully return to baseline in the congested week should therefore be interpreted cautiously, as it may involve both peripheral and central adaptations rather than solely reflecting local muscle recovery.

Regarding VAS scores, our study found a significant main effect of the type of week, with generally higher VAS scores during the congested week compared to the regular week. Both week types showed an increase in VAS scores immediately post-match (Congested: 65.8 ± 8.7 au; Regular: 58.4 ± 7.9 au), consistent with the acute perception of fatigue and soreness following a soccer match. However, VAS scores remained considerably higher and exhibited a slower return toward baseline during the congested week—particularly at Post-24 h (Congested: 62.3 ± 7.4 au; Regular: 44.9 ± 8.1 au) and Post-48 h (Congested: 51.5 ± 7.2 au; Regular: 36.7 ± 6.8 au)—compared to the regular week. This prolonged elevation in perceived fatigue aligns with existing literature indicating that congested match schedules lead to accumulated psychological and physiological stress, manifesting as heightened subjective fatigue [42] and reduced well-being [43]. The higher VAS scores in the congested week can be related with the cumulative physiological demands, inadequate recovery, and potential psychological stressors associated with repeated high-intensity performance [44]. Nonetheless, given the small sample size and uncontrolled workload between matches, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than strictly causal. The slower recovery of subjective well-being in the congested week emphasizes the impact of fixture density on athletes beyond purely physical parameters.

Despite the findings, the present study has certain limitations that warrant consideration for future research. Firstly, the relatively small sample size, while consistent across conditions, may limit the generalizability of the findings to other competitive contexts. Secondly, the study did not include other objective measures such as blood markers (e.g., creatine kinase, cortisol, inflammatory cytokines) or heart rate variability, which could offer a better understanding of the physiological stress response and recovery status. Additionally, the study focused solely on male soccer players; future investigations should explore the impact of congested schedules on female athletes, given potential physiological differences in fatigue and recovery. Thirdly, the external and internal workloads between matches (e.g., GPS-derived running measures, session rating of perceived exertion, match minutes, and intensity) were not controlled or reported. This represents a major limitation, as variations in match demand could have influenced CMJ, PPS, and VAS responses independently of the week type, potentially confounding the observed recovery patterns. Finally, while the study compared two distinct week types, the specific training and recovery interventions applied during these periods were not explicitly controlled. Moreover, jump performance was assessed using a contact mat, which provides only global outcome measures (e.g., jump height) and does not capture detailed kinetic or temporal parameters of force application; the use of force platforms in future studies would allow deeper insight into neuromuscular strategy and mechanical efficiency during recovery.

Despite the limitations, the findings provide preliminary evidence that may support practical implications for coaches, sports scientists, and medical staff involved in managing soccer players, particularly those competing in congested fixture periods. The observed reductions in CMJ height and increased pain sensitivity and perceived fatigue during congested weeks should be interpreted as trends indicating the need for individualized recovery strategies to mitigate cumulative fatigue. Coaches should consider adjusting training load and intensity, especially in the days following a match in a congested schedule, to prioritize recovery. Practical individualized approaches may include lighter, low-impact sessions (e.g., cycling or mobility drills), hydrotherapy (such as cold-water immersion or contrast baths), individualized nutritional recovery plans emphasizing protein and carbohydrate timing, and recovery monitoring using wellness questionnaires or neuromuscular testing. Furthermore, regular monitoring of objective (e.g., CMJ) and subjective (e.g., VAS) markers of fatigue is crucial to individualize player management and identify those at higher risk of under-recovery. The sustained impact on CMJ and pain sensitivity suggests, though does not confirm, that recovery efforts should extend beyond 24 h post-match in congested schedules, potentially requiring more proactive and prolonged recovery interventions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this exploratory study suggests that congested match schedules may negatively influence both functional and perceptual markers of recovery in soccer players, as reflected by greater and more prolonged decrements in CMJ height, increased PPS, and elevated VAS scores compared to regular weeks. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as incomplete recovery at 48 h does not necessarily indicate impaired performance in subsequent matches, particularly in the absence of contextual workload or match-performance data. Nonetheless, the results highlight the potential cumulative demands of fixture congestion and underscore the importance of individualized and extended recovery strategies to support player well-being.
