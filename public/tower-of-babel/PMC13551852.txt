Sexual differences of temporal marking and communication behaviours of common leopards (Panthera pardus) in Luolong County, Tibet
Authors: Keji Guo, Yonglei Lv, Zijun Tang, Ming Su, Tong Zhang, Fu Shu, Qi Li, Changjian Wang, Mengfei Zhang, Haochun Chen, Yang Yu, Yi Chen, Yang Xiao, Jinzhen Liu, Muhammad Zaman, Zuofu Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarnivores frequently rely on scent and visual markings for intraspecific communication, delineating territorial boundaries, attracting mates and assessing competitors. However, studying such behaviours in rare and elusive species poses significant challenges. To enhance our understanding of the diel marking behaviours of common leopard (Panthera pardus), from November, 2019 to June, 2023, we conducted a comprehensive camera-trap survey in Luolong county of Tibet, China.MethodsOur monitoring encompassed 159 locations yielded 1,208 (excluded 700 unknown sex) individual leopard detections over 60,000 trap days, allowing us to quantify the frequency and temporal patterns of marking behaviours. Of these, 153 detections involved territorial marking in snowy conditions, while 355 occurred in snow-free environments.ResultsFemale leopards in mixed forests and shrublands exhibited higher diurnal activity in snow-presence days. Male leopards engaged in a range of behaviours - including sniffing and rolling - primarily within mixed, coniferous and shrubland habitats. Male leopards engaged more frequently in behaviours such as sniffing, scraping, urine spraying, body rubbing, vocalizations, rolling and scat deposition with notable differences in scraping frequency compared to females. Female leopards displayed similar behaviours but at reduced levels particularly in scraping activities. The number of scrapes observed at camera-trap sites appears to be an indicator of leopard visitation rates suggesting its usefulness for optimizing monitoring efforts.ConclusionOur findings contribute valuable insights into how environmental factors influence social communication strategies in leopards, enhance our understanding of male and female leopard territorial and making behaviours in various environments, and benefit for the conservation and monitoring of carnivore populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12862-026-02539-3.Keywords: Camera traps, Common leopard, Genders, Habitat factors, Temporal marking behaviour

Introduction

Solitary apex carnivores rarely engage in direct interactions or overt visual displays, owing to their typically expansive territories and low population densities. Consequently, many such species depend heavily on marking behaviours—including scent marking, urine spraying and visual signals such as scratching, clawing, or ground scraping—to facilitate indirect communication with potential mates and competitors [1, 2]. These markings are often concentrated in prominent locations that serve as communication hubs for multiple individuals enabling effective interaction within the local population [3, 4]. Typically, scent marks are dispersed throughout an animal’s territory but tend to be densely clustered along frequently travelled routes and key areas [5, 6]. When selecting sites for scent marking, carnivores often opt for microhabitats that better preserve the scent and protect it from environmental elements such as precipitation thereby enhancing the effectiveness of their marking [7].

Marking behavior serves as an effective means of signaling territorial ownership reducing the likelihood of conflicts with potential rivals and enabling potential mates to evaluate an individual’s status and quality [2, 8]. Consequently, this form of communication has a direct impact on an individual’s fitness. However, the specific methods of marking can vary even among members of the same family [9], highlighting the need for species-specific investigations. Furthermore, understanding marking behavior is crucial for the conservation and monitoring of carnivore populations, as marking sites are frequently targeted in camera-trapping surveys [5, 10, 11].

Worldwide, leopards (Panthera pardus) possess the most extensive geographical distribution among wild felid species demonstrating exceptional flexibility in both dietary preferences and habitat selection [12, 13]. They typically exhibit a polygynous mating system, wherein male territories overlap with multiple females [13]. They also show intrasexual territoriality with the extent of home range overlap varying across different populations [14, 15]. In environments with high population densities, territorial behaviour tends to diminish likely as a strategy to reduce the risks associated with conflicts and potential injuries [16].

Though generally solitary, leopards display subtle behavioural adjustments such as reduced secrecy in areas lacking dominant predators [17]. They form only temporary associations during mating or when females are caring for their young which remain with them until they reach approximately 13 to 22 months of age [18]. Male leopards defend their territories primarily through scent marking along boundary lines, whereas the scent marking behaviours of females are less thoroughly understood [2]. Male leopards tend to respond aggressively toward unfamiliar individuals but are more tolerant toward neighbouring residents, possibly due to the “dear enemy” phenomenon [19]. Females often form matrilineal kin groups in that related females show greater overlap in their home ranges compared to unrelated females [18]. Female leopards may accept the costs associated with increased resource competition because they benefit from inclusive fitness advantages [8, 10, 18].

Understanding scent marking behaviors and the selection of marking sites might benefit the conservation, however data on individual marking frequencies remain scarce for common leopard (Panthera pardus) from Tibet, China and study on their behavioral ecology is important implications for population monitoring and conservation [2, 20]. Besides this, investigating the marking behavior of common leopards (hereafter, leopards) is particularly challenging due to their elusive nature, and it often requires employing various research methods such as camera trapping, snow tracking and transect counts [2, 21]. Each technique offers different insights into the behavior’s characteristics. Camera trapping, which is now the primary method for estimating felid populations often involves placing cameras at marking sites [22]. However, it remains unclear to what extent the number of scent marks correlates with leopard’s visitation rates captured through camera traps.

Scent marking is primarily understood as a behavior used by animals to delineate and assert control over their territory [9, 23]. Nevertheless, non-territorial species also engage in scent marking as a means of communication with conspecifics. Consequently, not all scent marks are related to territorial claims and the significance of these marks can differ depending on the species, their social structures and ecological contexts [24]. Since scent signals can persist in the environment for extended periods, they serve as a form of olfactory communication that allows an individual to be identified even in its absence. The scent may also deliver a warning or indicate an individual’s fitness level to others, who must then assess the potential risks of detection and invasion—considering both their own competitive strength and the importance of the resources being marked [7, 25]. This reduces the need for direct confrontations, thereby lowering the costs associated with physical conflicts [24].

To address existing knowledge gaps and gain a more comprehensive understanding of leopard making activities and enhance population monitoring efforts on conservation, we conducted the first-ever integrated study using camera trapping to assess the frequency of leopards engagement in various making activities across different spatial and temporal scales [26]. Our research also examined the influence of intrinsic factors such as sex along with environmental variables like habitat type and weather conditions on the frequency of marking behaviour [6]. The focus of this study was in Luolong County, Tibet, a part of leopards range where making behaviour remain largely unstudied. Moreover, we compared our findings with snow presence and absence offering a more complete overview of leopards making patterns throughout their distribution. Seasonal snow cover on ground is also influenced the detectability and activity variations of felids and shown clear interspecific changes in snow presence and absence periods [6]. To further strengthen our insights, we analysed how making behaviours correlated with detection rates obtained from recent camera-trapping surveys conducted simultaneously in the same areas [27]. This comparative approach aimed to determine whether sex‑based territorial marking could serve as a reliable predictor of sex‑biased diel marking activity in the presence or absence of snow using camera trap data. We hypothesized that male and female leopards use the same or different substrates for scent marking and exhibit distinct patterns of diel activity patterns with males likely showing different temporal marking behaviors due to their larger home ranges compared to females [25]. Additionally, we propose that their activity patterns vary between day and night influenced by the presence or absence of snow as many felid species tend to be crepuscular or nocturnal [28, 29]. Finally, we predict that the locations of temporal marking sites will differ based on habitat characteristics and that understanding these patterns can aid in population monitoring and future conservation efforts.

Materials and methods

Study area

In the southeastern part of Tibet and the southwestern area of Qamdo, Luolong County spans longitudes from 95°10′ to 95°50′ E and latitudes from 30°10′ to 31°50′ N. The region lies along the upper reaches of the Nujiang River and borders the northern segment of the Hengduan Mountains, with its western boundary adjoining the southeastern tip of the Nyainqêntanglha Mountains. The terrain’s average elevation is approximately 3,700 m above sea level, covering an area close to 8,108 km2. The local economy primarily hinges on agriculture and livestock farming, cultivating crops such as wheat, highland barley and rapeseed, while rearing animals like yaks, goats and pigs [30]. Human activities are mostly concentrated in residential zones and along roads, where grazing and harvesting of medicinal fungi like caterpillar fungus (Ophiocordyceps sinensis) are common [31, 32].

The climate in this area is classified as a temperate semi-arid monsoon climate, typical of the southeastern Tibetan Plateau. It features abundant sunlight and a significant temperature variation, with dry winters and mild summers. The average annual temperature hovers around 5.5 °C with recorded extremes reaching a maximum of 30.6 °C and a minimum of -22.1 °C. Precipitation averages 424.6 mm annually, predominantly falling between May and September. The region receives over 2,500 h of sunshine each year, with a frost-free period of approximately 120 days. The relative humidity averages 55%, while annual evaporation totals approximately 2,026 mm.

Biodiversity in Luolong County is notably rich, including 12 large mammalian species [33]. The prey species include white lipped deer (Cervus albirostris) pika (Ochotona curzoniae), woolly hare (Lepus oiostolus), alpine musk deer (Moschus chrysogaster),), himalayan marmot (Marmota himalayana), blue sheep (Pseudois nayaur), wild boar (Sus scrofa), Chinese serow (Capricornis milneedwardsii).

Vegetation types encompass coniferous forests, shrublands and meadows. Coniferous stands are predominantly composed of Picea likiangensis and Abies georgei. The shrublands primarily consist of species like Caragana spp., Berberis spp., Rhododendron spp., and Spiraea spp., while the meadows are primarily dominated by Carex species see Fig. 1.

Camera trapping methods

We employed a camera-trapping approach to gather data on the occurrence and temporal marking behaviours of leopards. Our measurements included both the rates of temporal marking as well as visitation frequencies at the identified marking sites at different camera stations [8, 34, 35]. We established a 5 × 5 km grid system within leopards distribution ranges and average home ranges [36]. During field surveys, camera traps were strategically placed within each study sites grid cell—primarily at identified or anticipated leopard marking sites along natural trails and in areas where pugmarks or marking behaviours were most frequently detected. This placement strategy helps mitigate biases between marked and unmarked locations. Moreover, camera placement was also adjusted to account for practical field conditions and grids with heavy grazing activity had fewer cameras, while extra cameras were deployed in grids with higher leopard movement rates resulting in a semi-systematic distribution tailored to on-the-ground ecological patterns [22]. Each camera operated for roughly six months (180 days), maintaining stable performance for 4 to 5 months. All cameras were permanently fixed at their individual locations and did not change positions. These cameras operated continuously without any delay between consecutive captures.

A total of 159 camera trap locations were carefully chosen for leopard monitoring or photograph. Cameras that were stolen or damaged were excluded from the dataset. At each sampling site, two to four cameras were installed at intervals of 3–4 km. Cameras were usually mounted on rocks or tree trunks at a height of around 50 cm above ground. No bait or lures were used to attract leopards. All cameras were installed within the same grid system or either at different sites, cameras were placed facing each other to capture leopard images from both sides. From November 19, 2019 to June 18, 2023, we monitored a total of 159 sites were covering approximately 1,000 km² at elevations ranging from 1,878 to 3,138 m of these monitored locations.We employed (Boly BG962-X36W and SG2060-K) camera traps (Bolymedia Communications; Shenzhen- Co., Ltd. China), capable of capturing color images and video during daylight hours and monochrome images and video at night, with a trigger speed of 0.20 s. Each photograph was tagged with metadata including date, time, moon phase and temperature with habitat types [37].

During camera trap deployment and the subsequent analysis of recorded marking behaviours observed during leopards visits sites where no marking activity was detected were classified as non-marking locations (Fig. 1). Following data collection, we isolated leopard images from the complete dataset. Three independent observers reviewed these images to identify individual leopards based on their unique coat patterns and other distinctive features and to determine their sex by examining the presence or absence of the scrotum (Fig. 2) and unknown sex images were removed from the final analysis due to unclear morphological features in images/videos. A total of 36 distinct camera stations captured image of male leopards, 70 camera locations recorded females and 6 camera locations recorded unidentified individuals. For every leopards visitation within 30 min delay at each camera stations, we recorded the visit duration (time and date), whether the leopard exhibited any of the making behaviours detailed in Table 1, and where marking occurred, we also recorded the duration of each observed behaviour. Further environmental variables noted at each detection event included weather conditions (precipitation or dry), ambient temperature and presence or absence of snow cover on ground. Several visits by individuals of the identical sex (male or female) to a single marking site within 30 min were defined as a single marking event. For frequent marking activity detections of the same sex at the same site within 30 min, only the first detected marking behaviour was taken as a variable to avoid marking activity overestimation.

Videos based marking sites

Initially, we classified the winter months into two distinct phases by examining the duration of recordings from the camera trap stations differentiating periods based on snow cover and temperature conditions. The first phase spanned from December 25th to February 25th (year 2019 to 2023), characterized by extremely cold temperatures ranging from − 5 °C to − 27 °C and prevalent snow cover (winter or snow presence days based on video records). The second phase occurred in November, March, and April, featuring milder temperatures between 0 °C and 20 °C with no snow present (summer or snow absence days based on video records) as described by Zaman et al. [8] and snow presence or absence can be altered based on slope or aspect. To reduce bias from elevation or topographic influences on snow status, we ensure snow is either entirely absent at all camera stations or entirely present at all camera stations.

Next, we closely examined 30-second video clips from various camera traps to identify instances of leopards marking and communication behaviours. These behaviours were categorized based on whether they occurred during periods when leopards were active on snow-covered ground or on snow-free terrain [31]. The habitat classification approach followed the methodology employed by Zaman et al. [8]. Each known marking site was categorised according to a hierarchical landform system using descriptors such as bare lands, mixed forests, herbaceous or shrub-dominated areas and coniferous forests based on the location of the camera traps and marking substrates such as hanging stones, soils, vegetations and tree trucks were also noted within each habitat [2].

Lastly, for each camera location, we categorized the diel activity patterns of leopard markings into three groups based on the timing of recorded videos, which were timestamped with both date and camera ID: (1) nocturnal, characterized by activities primarily occurring from one hour after sunset until one hour before sunrise; (2) diurnal, occurring mainly between one hour after sunrise and one hour before sunset; and (3) crepuscular, encompassing periods from one hour before sunrise to one hour after sunrise and from one hour before sunset to one hour after sunset [42].

During the monitoring period, we recorded both the number of videos captured and the total number of days each camera was operational at each site. Data analysis

We explored how the frequencies of leopards marking events varies between males and females considering the presence or absence of snow cover. To achieve this, we modeled the distribution of peak marking times using non-parametric kernel density estimates implemented through the “Overlap” package, with default bandwidth settings as recommended by Johansson et al. [11]. Circular density plots were employed to visualize these patterns and the coefficient of overlap (∆1 and ∆5) was calculated ranging from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (complete overlap). Video data were analyzed at intervals of at least 30 min to prevent pseudoreplication [43]. Each camera trap was treated as a separate spatial point to record the timing of temporal territorial marking and communication behaviors occurring between sunset and sunrise. Since these times vary throughout the year depending on latitude, seasonal changes and time zones—Tibet time being offset by two hours from Beijing time—all timestamps were converted to Tibet local time for consistency [45]. To account for seasonal variations in daylight hours [46], we utilized the “sunTime” function within the “Overlap” package (version 0.3.2) in R (version 3.5.1; www.r-project.org), converting all times to radians for analysis, following procedures described by Johansson et al. [11]. We also examined individual site usage based on the number of visits and inter-visit intervals to investigate variability in scent marking behaviors across different habitats and communication contexts. Finally, to identify significant differences in the timing of marking events between males and females, we applied Watson’s two-sample test using the “circular” package (version 0.4–94) in R 4.0.5.

Comparison of temporal marking activities behaviours between day and night

We examined the interaction between snow presence and absence focusing on how these conditions influence the detection rate of various making behaviors exhibited by male and female leopards [47]. Our analysis involved pairwise comparisons between daytime and nighttime temporal marking time considering whether snow cover was present or absent. We employed the Mann–Whitney U test across different habitat types to evaluate differences in these behaviors [8]. These comparisons aimed to identify variations in the independent detection frequencies of each communication behavior using the time of day (day or night) and sex (male or female) as grouping factors and each independent detection rates as an explanatory factor. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05. Additionally, we compared making behaviors between males and females under conditions of snow presence or absence using the non-parametric Kolmogorov-Smirnov Z test and we used male or either set as response variables and making behaviours as explanatory variables and differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Leopard abundance

Throughout the study period, camera traps recorded a total of 1,208 distinct sightings of leopards over 60,000 trap days with 700 unknown sex detections. The dataset included 153 (AI, 12.665) detection events where leopards were seen marking their territory in snowy conditions, while the remaining 355 (AI, 29.387) sightings occurred in environments free of snow (Table 2). The AI for territorial marking across all habitat types was notably higher in males (AI,13.493) compared to female (AI, 7.533) leopards. Specifically, the highest AI values for males were detected during twilight (AI, 6.788) or daytime in the snow absence periods. Whereas in snow presence periods the highest AI values for females were observed during the daytime (AI,1.572). Applying kernel density analysis demonstrated how snow cover influences leopards activity patterns indicating behavioural adjustments to either favor or avoid snowy conditions. Marking behaviours were observed both in the presence and absence of snow during daytime with increased activity levels during daylight, particularly around 06:00 and 12:00 h when snow was present.

Leopard marking activities linked to habitats

We observed that during periods without snowfall leopards marked more frequently between daytime compared and nighttime (Fig. 4a). Female leopards exhibited significant differences in their marking behavior between daytime and nighttime in coniferous forests (U = 16131.5, Z=-2.551; p < 0.05). Specifically, their marking activity was notably higher or lower during these periods (referring to diurnal vs. nocturnal activity). In contrast, in other forest types, there were no significant differences in marking behavior between day and night. Additionally, male leopards showed significant variation in their marking activity within bare (U = 20194.0; Z =-1.944; p < 0.05) and mixed grassland habitats (U = 21181.0; Z= -1.211; p < 0.05).

Communication behaviors recorded with camera trapping

The presence of snow cover was recorded alongside behaviors such as olfactory inspection and marking. Male leopards were observed engaging in sniffing (42 visits), scraping (17 visits), claw marking (two visits), urine spraying (one visit), body rubbing (6 visits), vocalizations (1 visits), rolling (3 visits) and scat deposition (16 visits). These behaviors, particularly fecal deposition and olfaction were predominantly associated with mixed, coniferous and shrub vegetation types and substrates like soils, tree barks, trial intersection points (Fig. 5a).

Female leopards exhibited similar behaviors with observations including sniffing (8 visits), scraping (1 visit), claw marking (1 visit), urine spraying (1 visit) and scat deposition (1 visit), as detailed in Fig. 5a. During the days without snow, male leopards scent marking behaviors included sniffing (84 visits), scraping (23 visits), urine spraying (2 visits), body rubbing (8 visits), vocalization (1 visit), rolling (37 visits), and scat deposition (14 visits), as detailed in Fig. 5b. Notably, rolling and olfactory behaviors were predominantly observed in mixed, coniferous and shrubland habitats.

During the days without snow, female leopards exhibited similar scent marking behaviors but with differing frequencies: sniffing (50 visits), scraping (4 visits), claw marking (1 visit), body rubbing (3 visits), urine spraying (6 visits), vocalizations (1 visit), rolling (6 visits), and scat deposition (7 visits), as detailed in Fig. 5b. Behavioral observations indicated that sniffing (46 visits) occurred across all habitat types and scat deposition was recorded in 16 visits, scraping in 9 visits and singular visits each for claw marking, vocalization and rolling behaviors.

There is a significant difference of frequencies of communication behaviors, particularly in scraping activity between male and female. Male leopards showed higher frequencies of scraping compared to females (Z = 1.607; p = 0.011). These findings suggest sex-based variation in scent marking behaviors, with males exhibiting more pronounced scraping activities.

Discussion

To date, there is a lack of detailed research in existing literature on leopard territorial marking behaviors from China. Our research presents novel findings that address a significant gap in understanding the temporal activity, communication behaviors and observations of free- roaming common leopards in Tibet, China. This study highlights the importance of comprehending the spatial and temporal distribution patterns of marking and communication behaviours of leopards in different territorial locations. These temporal occurrence areas can serve as vital tools for conservation efforts, helping to prioritize actions aimed at protecting their natural habitats [48].

In our research, we concentrated solely on the presence or absence of snow at camera trap locations during the winter months when were observed. This study did not address variations in territorial marking between mating and non- mating periods, nor did it investigate behaviors across different ages due to insufficient data resulting from limited age class [49]. Although we only identified male and female, these preliminary assessments can provide vital baseline information for future conservation and management efforts.

Gender detection rates and temporal marking events

In general, solitary large felids rely mainly on chemical scent-marking behaviors to adjust intraspecific territorial competition, mate advertisement and social information interchange through diverse terrestrial ecosystems [50, 51]. Diel activity allocation shapes of scent marking denote plastic adaptive traits formed by abiotic environmental stressors (e.g., snow occurrence, human disturbance) or biotic selective pressures and marked sexual dimorphism in these behavioral syndromes has long been documented through the Felidae family [52, 53]. The current study explained sex-specific difference in diel marking rhythms, generally marking frequency and snow-mediated behavioral plasticity amongst common leopards [54], and further explained the multi-faceted physiological, reproductive and ecological drivers leading intrasexual behavioral variation providing targeted understandings into the behavioral evolution of cold-climate pantherine species [55].

Furthermore, consistent with the behavioral merging of most temperate solitary felids, our data confirmed that scent-marking and body-rubbing behaviors of both male and female leopards were strongly skewed toward crepuscular and nocturnal time windows and this integrated temporal liking persisted firm through coniferous forests, mixed shrublands and grassland habitats irrespective of seasonal snow gathering [56, 57]. This pattern was consistent across various habitat types, contrasting with previous research that depicted common leopards in India [19], such as leopard marking increased the roadsides. The crepuscular-nocturnal specialization detected in our focal leopard population have also been observed in other felid taxa and sub-species such as the leopard (Panthera pardus, including North China leopard P. p. japonensis [22], Amur leopard P. p. orientalis) and tiger (Panthera tigris, including the Amur tiger P. t. altaica) in China [48] and Asiatic lion (Panthera leo persica) in India [58].

From an ecological adaptive viewpoint, this rhythm assists dual core functions and it reduces detrimental encounters with diurnal anthropogenic disturbances such as logging, livestock grazing and tourist activities [59], while coordinating activity peaks with prevailing crepuscular ungulate prey [54], thereby optimizing foraging efficacy and dropping energy intake (e.g., jaguars Panthera onca) [60]. Moreover, our (AI) data showed male leopards kept regularly higher crepuscular and nocturnal marking activity than females under same snow cover situations, preliminarily revealing snow cover utilizes sex-biased modulation effects on felids behavioral flexibility [6].

We also observed that significant sexual dimorphism in territorial marking occurrence across all measured habitat types: reproductively mature male leopards achieved far more frequent rubbing, scraping and olfactory signaling behaviors than conspecific females, a shape stable with the behavioral features of almost all solitary polygynous felids [61]. Beyond quantifiable occurrence gaps, snow cover represented as a pivotal abiotic moderator that reformed the temporal peak of scent marking and amplified sex-based niche divergence in diel behavioral allocation [62]. Under snow free periods, male and female leopards showed partially overlapping diel marking time budgets; females determined their marking activities entirely during twilight periods, while males preferred marking behaviors through twilight and early nighttime phases.

In contrast, determined snow cover prompted dramatic temporal variation between sexesand the population-wide marking peak lifted from twilight to evening and midnight hours and sex-based differences in marking timing became statistically significant [63]. Specifically, female leopards inhibited their scent-marking behaviors to narrow dawn-dusk time windows, whereas males extended nocturnal patrol cycles and extended midnight marking period to combine territorial boundaries [64].

Three consistent mechanisms widely clarify the inherent sex-based differences in marking frequency and temporal rhythms among temperate leopards, cover endocrine physiology, divergent reproductive strategies and snow-induced resource competition [65]. First, hormonal controlling alterations constitute the intrinsic physiological basis of sexually dimorphic scent marking across Felidae (e.g., clouded leopard Neofelis nebulosa) [66]. Circulating testosterone in mature male leopards upregulates the combination and secretion of felinine and volatile pheromones from apocrine glands in paw pads, anal sacs and facial skin, instantaneously enriching territorial patrol motivation and marking rate to compete for mating resources [67, 68]. This endocrine-driven shape has been tested in tigers (Panthera tigris), where males maintain 2–3 times greater marking rates than females and marking strength is completely associated with serum testosterone levels [69]. Alike shapes are also recognized in fishing cats (Prionailurus viverrinus) [70] and Asiatic lions (Panthera leo persica), where male individuals depend on high-frequency scent marking to signal social dominance and eliminate intrasexual competitors [71]. For female leopards, marking behaviors are controlled mainly by estrogen and progesterone [72], their scent signs are firmly tied to estrous cycles and mainly function to discharge mating cues for males, rather than territorial defence, causing in lower overall marking rate and fixed twilight-biased temporal liking [73].

Second, sex-asymmetric reproductive fitness approaches initiative divergent diel time allocation of marking behaviors beneath inconstant snow conditions [25]. As polygynous solitary carnivores, male inhabit bigger home ranges (e.g., jaguars Panthera onca and pumas Puma concolor) [74], and rank territorial expansion and mate control [13]. Under snowy conditions, inadequate prey resources and bigger movement costs intensify intraspecific competition; extended midnight marking enables males to lengthen the maintenance time of chemical signals in low-temperature [75], low-wind snowy environments, exploiting territorial coverage and mating chances [59]. Otherwise, female fitness is primarily firm by cub existence rather than territorial size [76]. Focused twilight marking denotes an optimal risk-averse tactic for females. Furthermore, female leopards twilight phases balance proficient mating signal communication and real-time anti-predator vigilance evading lengthy nocturnal activities that may depiction vulnerable cubs [77]. This reproductive trade-off is global through large felids, comprising Persian leopards (Panthera pardus saxicolor) [78], and pumas, whose females energetically decrease avoidable nocturnal social behaviors to confirm offspring protection [79].

Third, snow-induced resource redeployment reforms intraspecific competitive significances and more intensifies sex-based temporal difference [80]. When snow cover persists, available prey resources are focused in low-altitude shrub land corridors and forest ridges, turning these microhabitats into high-value ecological hotspots [81]. Male leopards select watching and marking these resource hotspots throughout midnight hours to control prey and mating resources [22]. By comparison females select stable microhabitats appropriate for cub raising [10], and their marking technique is only attuned faintly to adjust to microclimate variations instead of increasing nighttime activity [10]. Especially, multiple prior studies have proven that felids can energetically shift the functional orientation of scent marking built on resource accessibility, for sympatric Eurasian lynx (Lynx lynx) and Amur tigers (Panthera tigris altaica), mate advertisement were proceeds preference over territorial defence during mating seasons and this practical shift is more complex to snow-induced resource shortage in females than males [82, 83]. Males generally exhibited higher marking rates, which indicated earlier observations from captivity studies where male Sunda clouded leopards (Neofelis diardi) showed increased marking activity compared to females [9]. In some species, like Eurasian lynx (Lynx lynx) and tigers (Panthera tigris), the advertisement of mates can take precedence over territorial defence [84, 85].

Marking sites in different habitats: day versus night

Diel divergence in leopards scent marking through different habitats is mainly controlled by habitat microclimate shifting the chemical perseverance of scent secretions, a mechanism empirically confirmed across numerous pantherine and small felid examination globally [86]. Furthermore, prior studies have shown that high diurnal temperature in open bare and mixed grassland increased the pheromone evaporation, driving male leopards to rise daytime marking to endure functional territorial signals, an identical behavioral alteration described in male cheetah (Acinonyx jubatus) [87], that increase diurnal urine-spraying within open grassland habitats under hot ambient conditions [88]. In another aspect, cooler humid microclimate inside closed-canopy coniferous forest and snow-covered shrub land reduces scent deprivation (e.g., ocelot Leopardus pardalis) [89], permitting coniferous forest-dwelling female leopards and snow-range male shrub land leopards to focus marking into a single optimal daily window, alike to seasonal compressed marking activity recognized for snow leopard (Panthera uncia) during cold snowy months [6]. Homogenous closed-canopy forests stabilize daily temperature variations evenly during 24 h, eliminating abiotic pressure for diel marking alterations, a regular shape also noted in uninterrupted rainforest populations of Sunda clouded leopards (Neofelis diardi) occupying uniform dense evergreen forest [9].

In addition, sexual alterations in marking pattern timing and habitat selection stem from divergent core life-history strategies [21]. Males priorities open-habitat diurnal marking for intrasexual territorial defence beside rival conspecifics, while females limit most marking to sheltered mixed forests for safe oestrus signalling, similar to resource-based sex-biased marking detected in north American bobcats (Lynx rufus), where males watch open scrub diurnally and females mark entirely within dense wooded core ranges to defend kittens [3].

Female leopards demonstrated substantial differences in marking frequencies across mixed forests and shrublands with considerably more marking events occurring during daylight hours. These behaviors predominantly took place in vegetation including forest ridges and tree trunks, small paths, highlighting the importance of such features as key locations for scent-marking [90]. Our findings reveal that male and female leopards display distinct habitat preferences in their marking behavior with marking events collected in different habitat types. Notably, both sexes in leopards display consistent liking for trail interactions or small corridors were found in India [91].

Our results showed that both sex leopards regularly choice natural trails, or small animal trials and rocky outcrops for round-the-clock marking and movement to cut locomotive energy budgets and maximize conspecific mark encounter rates, a convergent corridor-marking practice also extensively recognized for Iberian lynx (Lynx pardinus) [92], and numerous sympatric felid groupings through central Indian protected forests [93].

Moreover, dense deciduous foliage and raised rocky outcrops offer dual aids of predation camouflage and higher hunting vantage, clarifying universal felid liking for these marking terrains as alike showed in tiger (Panthera tigris) populations inhabiting Indian riverine mixed forests that focus scent marks on higher rocky trail edges [94].

Habitat-dependent day–night and sex-specific marking plasticity of leapards denotes a phylogenetically preserved adaptive trait across Felidae, with analogous spatiotemporal marking alterations confirmed through geographically divided taxa including hearctic bobcats (Lynx rufus) [3], tigers (Panthera tigris) [24].

This study also explored that targeted camera trapping at leopard marking hotspots meaningfully increases species detection success [95], and sampling robustness, an effective monitoring practice previously usually applied for population surveys of tigers (Panthera tigris) [96], and Sunda clouded leopards (Neofelis diardi) across southeast Asian ecosystems [53, 93]. Prior global carnivore monitoring trials confirm deploying cameras at scent-marking locations improves survey cost-efficiency and data reliability for large felid conservation assessments across tropical, temperate and montane biomes [10, 73].

Results on marking behaviors from our study provide important information which primarily rely on camera-trapping and often target marking sites [97, 98], for optimizing leopard monitoring programs. An advantage of using marking sites for camera-trapping is the higher detection rate, which generally results in larger sample sizes and consequently obtain more robust information benefit for conservational action [5, 20]. These elevated areas potentially offer both strategic vantage points for hunting and crucial refuge zones. This phenomenon has been documented in various felid species, such as tigers (Panthera tigris) [99], servals (Leptailurus serval) [100], bobcat cats (Lynx rufus) [3, 4], Sunda clouded leopards (Neofelis diardi) [101] and Persian leopards (Panthera pardus saxicolor) [25].

Communication behavior

This study documented that male-biased elevation in olfactory investigation, fecal deposition and synchronized vocal signalling on snow-covered days reveals strengthened intraspecific territorial and reproductive chemical communication in leopards, a snow-triggered behavioural flow also described in montane Persian leopards (Panthera pardus saxicolor). These multi-modal communication occasions cluster along animal trails, rocky outcrops and tree bases across mixed, coniferous and shrub land vegetation since such linear topographic structures maximize conspecific signal meet chance, constant with intense communication-site selection recognized in European Iberian lynx (Lynx pardinus) [102], and Indian tiger (Panthera tigris) [103].

Females of leopard attention thorough sniffing activity inside mixed and shrubland habitats surrounding trails, dense undergrowth and stone substrates to detect male reproductive pheromones without avoidable open-habitat exposure, similar concealed olfactory examining behaviour detected in Neotropical jaguar (Panthera onca) females during pre-oestrus stages [104]. In adding, equal frequencies of investigative sniffing and body rolling between male and female leopards diverge from the well-documented sex-biased scraping trend in pumas (Puma concolor) [105], where males spend far more energy into scrape marking for territory demarcations (e.g., jaguars Panthera onca) [106].

Males of leopard make full suites of scent-marking actions (sniffing, scraping, urine spraying, body rolling) through mixed forest, conifer and shrub land, whereas females limit urine marking and scat deposition away from open grassland to decrease predation and conspecific agonistic risk, a habitat-filtered marking divergence also identified in African servals (Leptailurus serval) [107, 108]. Higher rolling and vocalization rates in male leopards function to advertise territory ownership and reproductive accessibility; analogous sex alterations in vocal and rolling communication have been validated across New World felids including jaguar (Panthera onca) and puma (Puma concolor) at shared communal communication hubs [21, 109].

Communal communication hotspots frequently visited by multiple unrelated conspecifics denote core olfactory signalling nodes through Pantherinae, as definite via long-term camera-trapping research on tigers (Panthera tigris), jaguars (Panthera onca) [22, 98]. Scrape abundance at camera locations acts as a quantitative proxy for recurrent leopard visitation and future site use, so ranking scrape-rich terrain for camera deployment improves trapping efficiency; this optimization tactic has effectively raised population detection accuracy for pumas (Puma concolor) and bobcats (Lynx rufus) [69, 70].

The study is clearly demonstrated that the number of leopard scrapes recorded at potential camera-trapping locations is not only an indicator of frequent marking activity but also serves as a reliable predictor of future visits to those sites. Consequently, we recommend field teams to carefully monitor and quantify scrape abundance in the landscape when selecting camera-trapping sites. This approach can significantly boost detection rates, ultimately making surveys more efficient and yielding more robust estimates of population density or abundance [39, 110].

Implication for conservation

The intersexual overlapping ranges suggest that leopards frequently share marking sites, while interactions between individuals of the same sex may occur near territorial boundaries or between resident and transient animals. Temporal segregation between males and females appears to facilitate space use and may serve to protect females with dependent cubs from infanticidal males [29], this is unlikely in our study system where no other dominant predators are present. As a solitary species, leopards employ flexible and adaptive strategies to structure and sustain their socio-spatial arrangements. Additionally, further research into fine-scale behavioral responses, especially to olfactory cues and behavioral flexibility among individuals can inform strategies to maintain healthy populations. Implementing experimental studies that explore these aspects could provide deeper insights into social dynamics ultimately aiding in the development of more targeted conservation efforts for solitary species like the leopard.
