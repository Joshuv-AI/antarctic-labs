LtrB inhibits Vibrio parahaemolyticus motility while promoting biofilm formation and seafood adhesion
Authors: Wenli Cai, Wu Xu, Yanyan Ding, Huimin Wang, Xi Luo, Xue Li, Miaomiao Zhang, Yiquan Zhang, Renfei Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vibrio parahaemolyticus is a major seafood-borne pathogen whose ability to form biofilms enhances its persistence in food-processing environments and contributes to its resistance to antimicrobial agents. In this study, we investigated the role of the LysR-type transcriptional regulator LtrB (VPA0388) in coordinating the switch between motility and biofilm formation in V. parahaemolyticus. We demonstrated that LtrB is required for proper biofilm development, extracellular matrix production, and wrinkled colony morphotype formation, while simultaneously repressing both swimming and swarming motility. Mechanistically, we showed that LtrB acts as a direct transcriptional regulator that binds to the promoter regions of multiple genes, including biofilm-matrix genes (cpsA, scvE, cpsQ, mfpA) and flagellar genes (flgM, flgA, flgB, flgK, lafA, fliD), activating the former and repressing the latter. In contrast, the regulation of motY and fliM appears to be indirect, as no binding of LtrB to their promoter regions was detected. Furthermore, LtrB exerts these regulatory effects largely independently of the c-di-GMP signaling pathway, as intracellular c-di-GMP levels remain unaltered upon ltrB deletion. Consistent with its role in promoting a sessile lifestyle, LtrB was required for bacterial adhesion to biotic (mussel and shrimp) and abiotic (glass and stainless steel) surfaces relevant to food production. These findings establish LtrB as a regulator that promotes a sessile, biofilm-forming lifestyle by downregulating motility and upregulating matrix production, thereby facilitating persistent colonization in food-related environments.

Introduction

Vibrio parahaemolyticus is a Gram-negative, halophilic bacterium with a worldwide distribution in marine and estuarine environments (Letchumanan et al., 2014). It represents a major cause of foodborne gastroenteritis, accounting for millions of infections annually (Letchumanan et al., 2014). Transmission occurs predominantly through the consumption of raw or undercooked seafood, and the risk of infection is significantly elevated in warm seasons and coastal areas (Letchumanan et al., 2014; Scanes et al., 2025). Driven by global climate change and rising sea temperatures, the geographic range of V. parahaemolyticus is expanding, posing a growing threat to food safety and public health (Scanes et al., 2025). In seafood supply chains, the prevalence of V. parahaemolyticus contamination in raw products has been reported to range from ∼20% to over 40% across regions, with isolation rates roughly doubling in warmer months, underscoring the food safety and economic burdens of this pathogen (Chen et al., 2010a, Chen et al., 2010b; Deepanjali et al., 2005; Jiang et al., 2024; Lopatek et al., 2015). In both natural habitats and food-processing settings, the bacterium forms complex biofilms that enhance its resistance to antibiotics, disinfectants, and harsh environmental conditions (Ashrafudoulla et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2022). These biofilms substantially increase its persistence on food and equipment surfaces, making biofilm control a critical challenge in contamination prevention (Ashrafudoulla et al., 2021; D. Wang et al., 2022).

Biofilms are highly organized multicellular communities formed following bacterial attachment to biotic or abiotic surfaces and are accompanied by the secretion of extracellular polymeric substances (Yildiz and Visick, 2009). In V. parahaemolyticus, biofilm formation involves diverse molecular components, such as exopolysaccharides (EPS), proteins, extracellular DNA (eDNA), and specific membrane-fusion proteins (Mfps) (Enos-Berlage et al., 2005; Li et al., 2020; Yildiz and Visick, 2009). The coordinated production of these components is regulated by complex networks in which transcriptional regulators play a central role by sensing environmental signals and modulating downstream target genes. In addition to the well-characterized quorum-sensing (QS) circuitry (Gode-Potratz and McCarter, 2011; Hawver et al., 2016; Rutherford and Bassler, 2012), several alternative sigma factors and two-component systems have been shown to govern biofilm formation in vibrios (Gu et al., 2019; Kitts et al., 2023; Teschler et al., 2017; Q. Wang et al., 2022). In recent years, several key transcription factors—including QS regulators (Enos-Berlage et al., 2005; Guo et al., 2018; L. Wang et al., 2013), LysR-type regulators such as LtrA (Xiong et al., 2025), CalR (Chang et al., 2025), BfrR (Sun et al., 2025), OxyR (Chung et al., 2016), and AcsS (Ni et al., 2025), as well as SbfR (Y. Zhou et al., 2025) and RobA (Zhong et al., 2021)—have been identified as important controllers of V. parahaemolyticus biofilm development. However, the continued identification of novel regulators and their mechanistic roles remain essential to fully understand this pathogen's environmental adaptation strategies.

A dynamic balance governs the transition between bacterial motility and biofilm formation. V. parahaemolyticus employs two distinct flagellar systems to navigate different environments: a single polar flagellum for swimming in liquids and multiple lateral flagella for swarming on solid or viscous surfaces (McCarter, 2004). Flagellum-mediated motility serves as a critical initial step in biofilm development, but motility-related genes are typically downregulated as the biofilm matures (Guttenplan and Kearns, 2013; Jung et al., 2019; Yildiz and Visick, 2009). The reciprocal regulation of motility and biofilm formation is a conserved theme in bacterial physiology, governed by intricate signaling networks that integrate environmental cues such as nutrient availability, surface contact, and population density (Belas, 2014; Guttenplan and Kearns, 2013; Mattingly et al., 2018). In V. cholerae, the transition from a motile to a biofilm-forming state is controlled by the second messenger c-di-GMP, which modulates flagellar gene expression and EPS production through multiple downstream effectors (Conner et al., 2017; Tischler and Camilli, 2004). This phase transition is precisely regulated in a temporally coordinated manner: initial surface contact triggers a reduction in motility, while the subsequent upregulation of matrix synthesis consolidates the biofilm architecture and ensures long-term surface persistence (Guttenplan and Kearns, 2013; Yildiz and Visick, 2009). The timing of these events is critical, as premature repression of motility may impair surface colonization, whereas delayed matrix production could result in dispersal or inefficient biofilm maturation (Belas, 2014; McDougald et al., 2011). Nevertheless, the coordinated regulatory mechanisms that orchestrate this switch are still not fully elucidated, and understanding how transcriptional networks balance these opposing lifestyles remains an active area of investigation.

The gene VPA0388 (VP_RS17100), located on the small chromosome of V. parahaemolyticus RIMD2210633, encodes a putative LysR-type transcriptional regulator (Makino et al., 2003). LysR-family proteins, which are ubiquitous in Gram-negative bacteria, are structurally and functionally conserved and typically act as global regulators modulating diverse cellular pathways, including biofilm formation, virulence, and motility (Demeester et al., 2024). Sequence analysis revealed that VPA0388 contains a conserved N-terminal helix-turn-helix (HTH) DNA-binding domain and a C-terminal substrate-binding domain characteristic of the LysR family, consistent with its predicted role as a transcriptional regulator (Fig. S1). Despite this structural characterization, the functional role of VPA0388 remains uncharacterized. Notably, previous work has shown that VPA0388 is markedly upregulated in smooth-colony variants, which produce significantly less biofilm than rugose strains (Q. Wu et al., 2022). This differential expression strongly suggests that VPA0388 may be a novel and important player in the regulatory network controlling the biofilm-motility switch, yet its specific function and mechanistic basis are entirely unknown.

In this study, we designate VPA0388 as LtrB (LysR-type transcriptional regulator B) and employ a combination of molecular genetics and phenotypic analyses to systematically define its role in V. parahaemolyticus in orchestrating the transition between biofilm formation, motility, and surface colonization. We demonstrate that LtrB is important for extracellular matrix production, colony morphotype development, and adhesion to seafood and food-processing surfaces. Mechanistically, LtrB activates transcription of EPS biosynthesis genes (cpsA, scvE) and Mfp-related genes (cpsQ, mfpA), while simultaneously repressing genes involved in the assembly and function of both polar and lateral flagella. These findings reveal how LtrB orchestrates the biofilm-motility switch to promote persistent V. parahaemolyticus colonization in food-related environments.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and genetic constructs

V. parahaemolyticus RIMD2210633 was used as the parental strain (WT) in all experiments. To construct a ltrB-deficient mutant (ΔltrB), a double-crossover homologous recombination strategy was employed (Sun et al., 2025). Briefly, an internal 543-bp fragment of ltrB (nucleotide positions 128-670) was amplified and cloned into the suicide vector pDS132, which carries a sacB counter-selection marker and a chloramphenicol resistance gene (Philippe et al., 2004). After verification by sequencing, the resulting plasmid (pDS132-ΔltrB) was transferred into the WT strain via conjugative mating using E. coli S17-1 λpir as the donor. Primary recombinants were selected on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar containing chloramphenicol (20 μg/mL). To resolve the integrated plasmid, these integrants were grown under non-selective conditions, followed by counter-selection on sucrose (10%) to eliminate clones retaining the sacB-containing vector backbone. Successful gene deletion was confirmed by PCR amplification across the recombination junctions, with the expected size shift verified by electrophoresis. Complete absence of the target sequence was further confirmed by nucleotide sequencing of the amplified region.

For genetic complementation (Sun et al., 2025), the full-length ltrB open reading frame preceded by an optimized ribosome-binding site (AGGAGG) was cloned into the arabinose-inducible vector pBAD33 using BamHI/HindIII restriction sites. After sequence confirmation, the resulting plasmid (pBAD33-ltrB) was introduced into the mutant strain to generate the complementation strain (C-ΔltrB). Control strains containing the empty vector (WT/pBAD33 and ΔltrB/pBAD33) were similarly constructed in both the WT and mutant backgrounds. Growth conditions

V. parahaemolyticus was cultivated using a standardized protocol (Sun et al., 2025). Briefly, a stationary-phase culture grown in Heart Infusion (HI) broth (BD Biosciences, USA) was diluted 50-fold into 5 mL of fresh medium and incubated at 37°C with shaking until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 1.4. This starter culture, referred to as the bacterial seed, was then diluted 103-fold into another 5 mL of HI broth and incubated under the same conditions until the OD600 again reached 1.4, after which the cells were harvested. For strains carrying pBAD33-based plasmids, chloramphenicol (5 μg/mL) was added to the medium for selection, and L-arabinose (0.1% w/v) was included to induce gene expression. Importantly, WT/pBAD33, ΔltrB/pBAD33, and C-ΔltrB were cultured under identical conditions with respect to antibiotic selection and arabinose induction; thus, any potential effects of plasmid burden or inducer are normalized across all strains, allowing direct attribution of phenotypic differences to LtrB function.

Growth curve analysis

Growth curves were monitored using an MGC-200 automated spectrophotometer (Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology, China) (Y. Zhou et al., 2025). The bacterial seed was diluted 2000-fold into 5 mL of HI broth and mixed thoroughly. From this suspension, 200 μL aliquots were dispensed into eight separate wells of a sterile 96-well plate for each bacterial variant. The instrument maintained the cultures at 37°C under continuous orbital shaking (800 rpm) and automatically recorded the OD600 every 60 min throughout the experiment. All strains were cultured in HI broth supplemented with chloramphenicol (5 μg/mL) and L-arabinose (0.1% w/v) to maintain consistent selective and inductive conditions across genotypes. Three independent biological replicates (each performed on different days with freshly prepared cultures) were conducted, and within each biological replicate, eight technical replicates (wells) were measured for each strain.

Crystal violet (CV) staining

CV-based biofilm quantification was performed using the methodology from previous studies (Sun et al., 2025; Y. Zhou et al., 2025). For biofilm induction, the bacterial seed culture was diluted 50-fold into 2 mL of HI medium supplemented with 5 μg/mL chloramphenicol and 0.1% L-arabinose in 24-well polystyrene plates. The plates were incubated at 37°C with continuous orbital shaking (150 rpm) for 24 h. After incubation, culture supernatants were collected to measure the OD600, which reflected planktonic cell density. Adherent biofilms were gently rinsed three times with sterile distilled water, stained with 0.1% CV solution, and then decolorized with 2.5 mL of 20% acetic acid. The absorbance of the solubilized dye at 570 nm (OD570) was measured as an indicator of biofilm biomass. Normalized biofilm formation was calculated as the ratio of CV staining (OD570) to planktonic growth (OD600). Three independent biological replicates (each performed on different days with freshly prepared cultures) were conducted, and within each biological replicate, four technical replicates (wells) were measured for each strain.

Colony morphology

To assess bacterial colony morphology (Sun et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2025), HI agar, supplemented with chloramphenicol (5 μg/mL) and L-arabinose (0.1%), was spot-inoculated with 2 μL aliquots of the bacterial seed culture. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 48 h before morphological examination. Three independent biological replicates (each performed on different days with freshly prepared cultures) were conducted, and within each biological replicate, three technical replicates were detected for each strain.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) assay

Visualization of surface-adherent microbial communities was performed using SEM according to established protocols (Sun et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2025). Biofilms were formed on glass surfaces during a 24-h incubation in HI broth at 37°C under constant orbital shaking (150 rpm). These biofilms were then fixed overnight (approximately 12 h) at 4°C in 4% glutaraldehyde. After fixation, samples were washed three times with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and gradually dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series (30%, 50%, 60%, 70%, 90%, 95%, and two changes of 100% ethanol), with each step lasting 10 min. Following complete dehydration, samples were coated with a conductive gold-palladium layer using an automatic sputter coater (Hitachi Ion Sputter MC1000, Japan). High-resolution imaging was carried out using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; HITACHI SU8010, Japan), with representative images captured from triplicate samples to ensure reproducibility of the observed structural trends.

Quantification of key biofilm matrix components

The quantification of key biofilm components was performed using established protocols (Sun et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2024). Biofilms were grown on surfaces for 24 h in HI broth supplemented with 5 μg/mL chloramphenicol and 0.1% L-arabinose at 37°C with continuous agitation (150 rpm). After incubation, non-adherent cells were removed by gentle pouring. The firmly attached biofilms were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and then transferred to chilled 0.01 M potassium chloride solution. Biofilm integrity was disrupted by sonication, followed by centrifugation to remove debris. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter (MF-Millipore™, USA) prior to analysis. EPS content was measured using the phenol-sulfuric acid method, with absorbance read at 490 nm. Total protein was quantified using a commercial bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (TIANGEN, China). Fluorometric quantification of eDNA was performed using the M5 Pic5 Green dsDNA Assay Kit (Mei5 Biotechnology, China) on a Tecan Infinite M200 microplate reader (Switzerland) with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 535 nm. All measurements were normalized to control samples. The WT/pBAD33 strain was used as the reference standard (set at 100%), with the ΔltrB/pBAD33 and C-ΔltrB strains expressed as relative percentages of this baseline. Each assay was performed with three independent biological replicates, and within each biological replicate, at least four technical triplicates were measured for each strain.

Motility assays

Bacterial motility was assessed using established protocols (Sun et al., 2025). Swimming motility was evaluated by stab-inoculating 2 μL of bacterial seed culture into the HI plates containing 0.5% Noble agar (BD Biosciences, USA) and supplemented with chloramphenicol (5 μg/mL) and L-arabinose (0.1% w/v) to ensure uniform selective and inductive conditions across all strains. To capture both early migratory capacity and sustained motility behavior, migration distances were measured hourly from 2 to 6 h post-inoculation. The 2-h time point was chosen to assess initial swimming before nutrient depletion or metabolic by-product accumulation in the soft agar could confound the measurements, thereby providing a reliable baseline for comparative analysis between strains; the extended time course (3-6 h) was included to evaluate sustained motility performance. Swarming motility was assessed by spotting 2 μL of bacterial seed onto the surface of HI plates solidified with 2.0% Noble agar and supplemented with chloramphenicol (5 μg/mL) and L-arabinose (0.1% w/v). Swarming expansion was recorded every 12 h from 24 h of incubation at 37°C. All assays were performed with at least three independent biological replicates (each conducted on separate days with independently prepared cultures), and within each biological replicate, four technical triplicate samples were measured for each strain.

Measurement of intracellular c-di-GMP levels

Intracellular c-di-GMP levels were quantified following a previously validated protocol (Sun et al., 2025). Briefly, bacterial cells from 1 mL of culture (OD600 ∼1.4) were pelleted, washed with PBS, heat-denatured at 100°C for 5 min, and sonicated for 15 min. The lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 10,000 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was used for total protein quantification by BCA assay and for c-di-GMP detection using a commercial competitive ELISA kit (Mskbio, China). Intracellular c-di-GMP concentrations were normalized to total protein and expressed as pmol c-di-GMP per gram of total protein (pmol/g protein). All measurements were performed with at least three independent biological replicates, each with four technical replicates.

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Bacterial cultures were harvested at OD600 value of approximately 1.4. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Vazyme Biotech, China) based on phenol-guanidine isothiocyanate. Reverse transcription was performed with 1 μg of purified RNA using a commercial cDNA synthesis kit (FastKing, Tiangen Biotech, China). RT-qPCR was carried out using SYBR Green chemistry (Tiangen Biotech, China) on a Roche LightCycler 480 system (Switzerland). Transcript levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Rao et al., 2013), with 16S ribosomal RNA serving as the internal reference gene for normalization. The Ct values for 16S rRNA were highly consistent across all strains tested (data not shown), confirming its stability under the experimental conditions used.

Luminescence assay

The promoter activity of each target gene was assessed using a bioluminescent reporter system (Y. Y. Zhou et al., 2013). Briefly, each target promoter region was amplified by PCR and fused upstream of the promoterless luxCDABE operon in the vector pBBRlux, which carries a chloramphenicol resistance gene. The resulting reporter plasmid was transformed into the WT and ΔltrB strains. Transformants were grown in HI broth at 37°C to an OD600 value of approximately 1.4, and luminescence was measured using a Tecan Infinite® 200 Pro microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). Reporter activity was expressed as relative light units (RLU) normalized to the OD600 value. The final result was expressed as the fold change in RLU, which was calculated by dividing the RLU in ΔltrB by the RLU in WT.

Purification of 6× His-tagged LtrB (His-LtrB)

The full coding sequence of ltrB was cloned into the pET28a expression vector (Novagen, USA). The resulting recombinant plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli BL21λDE3 for the production of His-LtrB protein. Expression and purification were performed according to previously described protocols (Zhang et al., 2023). After dialysis, the protein solution was concentrated to approximately 0.8 mg/mL, and the high purity of the sample was verified by 12% SDS-PAGE.

Electrophoretic mobility-shift assay (EMSA)

EMSA was performed similarly according to published protocols (Zhang et al., 2023). Briefly, upstream regulatory DNA fragments of each target gene were amplified by PCR. Binding reactions (10 μL) were assembled in binding buffer (0.5 mM EDTA, 1 mM MgCl2, 50 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM DTT, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, and 0.125 mg/mL salmon sperm DNA) containing 100 ng of amplified target DNA and increasing amounts of purified His-LtrB protein. After incubation at room temperature for 20 min, the DNA-protein complexes were resolved on a 6% (w/v) native polyacrylamide gel. The gel was stained with ethidium bromide (EB) and visualized under a UV transilluminator.

Evaluation of bacterial colonization on food processing substrates

Systematic evaluation of microbial adhesion was conducted using standardized surfaces relevant to food processing. Rectangular specimens (1 × 1.8 cm) were prepared from four substrates: mussel (Mytilus galloprovincialis) tissue, shrimp (Parapenaeopsis hardwickii) exoskeleton, glass, and 316L stainless steel. Following established protocols (Sun et al., 2025), each specimen was immersed in HI broth supplemented with chloramphenicol (5 μg/mL) and L-arabinose (0.1% w/v) inoculated with the bacterial seed culture. After 24-h of incubation, specimens were aseptically transferred to sterile homogenization bags containing sterile PBS. Adherent cells were detached by mechanical homogenization under standardized conditions to ensure consistent recovery across all samples. Viable counts were determined by spread plating on HI agar plates. In accordance with common practice for reporting surface colonization, results are expressed as the logarithm of viable cells per unit area (log10(CFU/cm2)).

Statistical methods

Experimental validation was performed with at least three independent biological replicates (each conducted on different days using freshly prepared bacterial cultures). For each biological replicate, at least three technical replicates (parallel measurements within the same experiment) were measured. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between groups was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with p < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

LtrB contains conserved LysR-type domain architecture

To validate the classification of LtrB as a LysR-type transcriptional regulator, we performed conserved domain searches and amino acid sequence alignments. As shown in Fig. S1a, LtrB possesses an N-terminal helix-turn-helix (HTH) DNA-binding domain (residues 3-61) within the LysR superfamily architecture (residues 1-181) and a C-terminal substrate-binding domain (residues 108-282), which are characteristic features of the LysR family. Alignment with representative LysR-type regulators from Vibrio species, including LtrA, CalR, OxyR, and BfrR, revealed that LtrB shares high sequence similarity within the HTH domain, particularly at residues known to be critical for DNA recognition and binding (Fig. S1b). These analyses provide a structural basis for the functional classification of LtrB as a LysR-type transcriptional regulator.

Genetic ablation of ltrB had no effect on growth kinetics

To determine whether deleting ltrB affects growth, we measured the proliferation of V. parahaemolyticus in three strains: WT/pBAD33, ΔltrB/pBAD33, and C-ΔltrB. Deletion of ltrB impairs biofilm formation and colony architecture in V. parahaemolyticus

CV staining revealed a substantially reduced surface-attached biofilm formation in the ΔltrB/pBAD33 strain compared to both the WT/pBAD33 and C-ΔltrB strains (p < 0.05; Fig. 2a). Notably, the C-ΔltrB strain showed enhanced biofilm production relative to the WT/pBAD33 strain (p < 0.01; Fig. 2a). Macroscopic colony morphology analysis indicated that the WT strain formed characteristically wrinkled colonies, whereas the ΔltrB mutant produced smooth colonies (Fig. 2b). Both the WT/pBAD33 and C-ΔltrB strains exhibited intermediate phenotypes—less wrinkled than the WT strain but significantly more structured than the ΔltrB/pBAD33 strain, with no visible differences between them (Fig. 2b). LtrB functions as negative regulator of bacterial motility phenotypes

Flagella-based motility is critical for biofilm development (Yildiz and Visick, 2009). In V. parahaemolyticus, loss of polar flagellum prevents the development of mature biofilms (Jung et al., 2019). To determine whether LtrB influences flagella-based motility, we performed swimming and swarming assays. In the swimming assay (Fig. 3a), at 2 and 3 h, the diameter of ΔltrB/pBAD33 was significantly larger than those of WT/pBAD33 and C-ΔltrB, whereas no significant difference was observed between WT/pBAD33 and C-ΔltrB. The 2-h data, which primarily reflect early diffusion and swimming initiation, clearly showed that loss of LtrB enhanced initial migratory capacity. At 4 h, all pairwise comparisons among the three strains differed significantly: C-ΔltrB showed the largest diameter, ΔltrB/pBAD33 was intermediate, and WT/pBAD33 was the smallest. At 5 and 6 h, the diameters of ΔltrB/pBAD33 and C-ΔltrB were both significantly larger than that of WT/pBAD33, with no significant difference between the two. This temporal pattern, from early (2 h) to later (3-6 h) time points, indicates that the enhanced swimming phenotype of the ΔltrB mutant is not merely a transient diffusion effect but is sustained over the course of the assay. The convergence of C-ΔltrB with ΔltrB/pBAD33 at later time points (5-6 h) suggests that the complementation plasmid may have been lost or the inducer may have become ineffective during prolonged incubation, causing C-ΔltrB to effectively revert to the ΔltrB phenotype. In the swarming assay (Fig. 3b), at 24, 36, and 48 h, the diameter of ΔltrB/pBAD33 was significantly larger than those of WT/pBAD33 and C-ΔltrB, while no significant difference was detected between WT/pBAD33 and C-ΔltrB. At 60 and 72 h, no significant differences were observed among the three strains. The swarming data thus show that LtrB deletion significantly enhances early-stage (24-48 h) swarming motility, while the lack of differences at later time points (60-72 h) likely reflects the plateau phase of swarming expansion on the agar surface. LtrB does not affect the intracellular c-di-GMP levels

Given the well-established role of c-di-GMP in controlling biofilm formation and motility in Vibrio species (Conner et al., 2017; Homma and Kojima, 2022; Yao et al., 2025), we investigated whether LtrB regulates this signaling pathway. As shown in Fig. S2, no significant differences in c-di-GMP levels were observed among WT/pBAD33, ΔltrB/pBAD33, and C-ΔltrB (p > 0.05), indicating that LtrB does not affect c-di-GMP metabolism under the conditions tested. This result suggests that LtrB regulates the biofilm-motility switch through a c-di-GMP-independent mechanism, likely via direct transcriptional control of its target genes.

LtrB-mediated regulation of genetic determinants governing biofilm architecture and flagellar function

To investigate the transcriptional regulatory role of LtrB, we analyzed the expression of key genes involved in EPS synthesis (cpsA and scvE), membrane fusion proteins (Mfp; cpsQ and mfpA), polar flagellar assembly (flgM, flgA, and flgB), and lateral flagellar formation (lafA, motY, fliM, and fliD). RT-qPCR revealed that in the ltrB mutant, EPS and Mfp-related genes were significantly downregulated: cpsA (0.31-fold), scvE (0.32-fold), cpsQ (0.54-fold), and mfpA (0.17-fold) relative to the WT strain (Fig. 4a). In contrast, flagellar genes were markedly upregulated: flgM (4.56-fold), flgA (2.36-fold), flgB (9.51-fold), flgK (1.46-fold), lafA (3.96-fold), motY (21.97-fold), fliM (7.23-fold), and fliD (2.78-fold). These expression patterns were corroborated by luminescence-based promoter activity assays, which yielded consistent results (Fig. 4b). To determine whether LtrB directly binds to the promoter regions of these target genes, we performed EMSA using purified His-LtrB protein. As shown in Fig. 4c, His-LtrB bound in a dose-dependent manner to the upstream DNA fragments of cpsA, scvE, cpsQ, mfpA, flgM, flgA, flgB, flgK, lafA, and fliD, but did not bind to the intergenic region between motY and fliM or to the 16S rDNA fragment (negative control). The transcriptional changes observed for motY and fliM are likely indirect effects, as no direct binding of LtrB to their regulatory regions was detected. Discussion

This study elucidates the regulatory role of the LysR-type transcriptional regulator LtrB in the switch between biofilm formation and motility in V. parahaemolyticus. Our findings demonstrate that LtrB acts as a positive regulator of biofilm development while serving as a repressor of flagellar motility (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). By coordinating these mutually exclusive lifestyles, LtrB facilitates persistent bacterial colonization in food-related environments (Fig. 5). This discovery provides crucial molecular insights into the environmental adaptation strategies of V. parahaemolyticus.

It is noteworthy that while another LysR-type regulator, LtrA (VPA0519), was recently reported to similarly promote biofilm formation and repress motility (Xiong et al., 2025), LtrA and LtrB share only 21.39% amino acid identity and show no significant nucleotide sequence similarity (data not shown). Moreover, LtrB exerts its regulatory effects through direct binding to target gene promoters (as demonstrated by EMSA in this study) and operates independently of c-di-GMP signaling (Fig. S2), whereas LtrA can regulate biofilm formation through modulation of intracellular c-di-GMP levels (Xiong et al., 2025). The target gene repertoires of these two regulators also differ substantially. Thus, despite their shared LysR-family classification, LtrA and LtrB represent evolutionarily distinct regulators that control the biofilm-motility switch through fundamentally different molecular mechanisms. Additionally, while our EMSA data confirm direct binding of LtrB to the promoters of cpsA, scvE, cpsQ, mfpA, flgM, flgA, flgB, flgK, lafA, and fliD, the regulation of motY and fliM appears to be indirect. This suggests that LtrB may also modulate motility through downstream regulatory cascades, potentially involving other transcription factors or post-transcriptional mechanisms, a feature commonly observed in LysR-type regulator networks (Demeester et al., 2024).

Our study reveals that LtrB maintains the biofilm-motility balance through a dual regulatory mechanism. Through direct binding to target gene promoters (as demonstrated by EMSA), LtrB activates transcription of EPS biosynthesis genes—notably cpsA from the cpsA-K locus, which is required for forming wrinkled colonies (Chen et al., 2010; M. Liu et al., 2022), and scvE from the scvA-O locus (M. Liu et al., 2022)—along with genes in the cpsQ-mfpABC operon, which encodes the biofilm regulator CpsQ and the MfpABC proteins (Enos-Berlage et al., 2005; Gao et al., 2017). This coordinated activation promotes matrix production and stabilizes biofilm architecture. Conversely, LtrB directly represses the expression of both polar and lateral flagellar genes, including motY, which showed a 21.97-fold increase in steady-state mRNA levels by RT-qPCR (Fig. 4a) and a ∼3.38-fold increase in promoter activity by luminescence reporter assay (Fig. 4b). Both assays consistently indicate LtrB-mediated repression; the quantitative difference likely reflects the distinct nature of the measurements (steady-state mRNA vs. promoter activity) and does not undermine the regulatory conclusion, thereby attenuating bacterial motility and driving the transition from a planktonic to a sessile lifestyle—a prerequisite for biofilm initiation and maturation.

The pronounced upregulation of motY is physiologically significant because MotY functions as a motor component (together with MotA and MotB) required for lateral flagellar rotation (McCarter, 2004); its substantial transcriptional derepression in the ΔltrB mutant provides a mechanistic basis for the enhanced swarming phenotype observed in our study, particularly during early-to-mid stages of swarming (24-48 h, Fig. 3b). Similarly, the upregulation of fliM (7.23-fold) and fliD (2.78-fold), which encode the flagellar motor switch protein and filament cap protein (McCarter, 2004), respectively, likely contributes to increased flagellar assembly and function, thereby promoting both swimming and swarming motility. Interestingly, our measurement of intracellular c-di-GMP levels (Fig. S2) revealed that LtrB deletion did not significantly alter the cellular concentration of this second messenger, suggesting that LtrB operates independently of the c-di-GMP signaling cascade, at least under the conditions tested. This distinguishes LtrB from several other LysR-type regulators in V. parahaemolyticus that have been shown to modulate c-di-GMP metabolism (e.g., CalR and LtrA) (Chang et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2025), and it reinforces the notion that LtrB exerts its regulatory effects primarily through direct transcriptional control of biofilm-matrix and flagellar genes rather than through global changes in c-di-GMP pools. The temporal resolution of our motility assays further supports this regulatory model: the sustained enhancement of swimming motility in the ΔltrB mutant from 2 to 6 h (Fig. 3a) and the increased swarming expansion at 24-48 h (Fig. 3b) are consistent with the persistent transcriptional upregulation of flagellar genes observed at the RNA level. This regulatory strategy ensures efficient switching from a free-swimming to a surface-attached state. Notably, while this regulatory mode resembles those previously reported for CalR (Chang et al., 2025) and LtrA (Xiong et al., 2025), the specific target genes and regulatory outputs differ substantially. CalR directly activates cpsA and the c-di-GMP phosphodiesterase gene VPA1429 while repressing the c-di-GMP diguanylate cyclase genes VPA0184 and VPA0198, as well as the c-di-GMP-binding regulator ScrP; it also indirectly activates scvE (Chang et al., 2025). LtrA, in contrast, governs a broader regulon that includes not only EPS genes (cpsC-K and scvD) but also genes associated with type III and type VI secretion systems, and it positively regulates c-di-GMP levels through repression of the phosphodiesterase VopY (Xiong et al., 2025). LtrB occupies a distinct position within this network: it directly activates both EPS genes (cpsA, scvE) and the cpsQ-mfpABC operon, which encodes a biofilm regulator and membrane-fusion proteins, while repressing polar and lateral flagellar genes (Enos-Berlage et al., 2005; M. Liu et al., 2022). Unlike CalR, LtrB does not appear to directly modulate c-di-GMP metabolic genes under the tested conditions, and unlike LtrA, it does not broadly impact secretion system genes. These differences indicate that LysR family members constitute a finely partitioned regulatory network in V. parahaemolyticus, with LtrB specializing in coordinating matrix production and motility repression during the sessile transition.

Notably, the complemented strain C-ΔltrB/pBAD33 exhibited higher biofilm biomass than the WT/pBAD33 strain (Fig. 2a). This observation likely reflects LtrB overexpression from the arabinose-inducible pBAD33 plasmid, which contains the strong PBAD promoter (Guzman et al., 1995). It is important to note that while all strains were cultured under identical conditions (same concentrations of chloramphenicol and L-arabinose), the ltrB gene in the C-ΔltrB strain is transcribed from the strong PBAD promoter on the plasmid, whereas in the WT/pBAD33 strain, it is expressed from its native chromosomal promoter. Consequently, LtrB expression in the complemented strain is expected to be higher than physiological levels. Given that LtrB functions as a positive activator of biofilm-matrix genes, elevated LtrB abundance would be expected to drive increased transcription of downstream targets (cpsA, scvE, cpsQ, mfpA), thereby enhancing matrix production and biofilm formation. This overexpression effect does not diminish the key conclusion that LtrB promotes biofilm formation; rather, it reinforces the positive regulatory role of LtrB in a dose-dependent manner. Importantly, the critical observation remains that deletion of ltrB significantly reduces biofilm formation relative to both the WT and complemented strains, firmly establishing LtrB as an essential positive regulator of biofilm development.

Given the central role of QS in coordinating motility and biofilm formation in V. parahaemolyticus (Gode-Potratz and McCarter, 2011; F. Liu et al., 2023; Yildiz and Visick, 2009; Zamorano-Sánchez et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023), it is important to consider where LtrB fits within the established QS hierarchy. Previous transcriptomic analysis revealed that ltrB is significantly upregulated in smooth colony variants compared to wrinkly spreaders (Q. Wu et al., 2022). Since smooth variants are typically associated with different QS states (Gode-Potratz and McCarter, 2011; L. Wang et al., 2013; K. Wu et al., 2019), this expression pattern raises the possibility that LtrB functions downstream of OpaR. Notably, OpaR represses biofilm formation while promoting motility-related genes in V. parahaemolyticus (Enos-Berlage et al., 2005; Gode-Potratz and McCarter, 2011; F. Liu et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2021; Zamorano-Sánchez et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023) —outcomes opposite to those of LtrB. Based on this opposing relationship, we hypothesize that LtrB may act as a downstream effector of OpaR that fine-tunes the motile-to-sessile transition by integrating QS signals with other environmental cues to balance matrix production and flagellar gene expression. However, this model remains speculative; whether OpaR directly regulates ltrB transcription or whether LtrB functions independently of the QS cascade requires future experimental validation through promoter binding assays and epistasis analyses. Importantly, the observation that ltrB is upregulated in smooth variants—which are biofilm-deficient—while ltrB deletion also yields a smooth, low-biofilm phenotype may seem contradictory at first glance (Q. Wu et al., 2022). We propose that this apparent paradox can be reconciled by considering that LtrB's regulatory output is likely context-dependent and modulated by the concurrent action of other transcriptional regulators (e.g., OpaR) that collectively determine the final colony morphotype and matrix production. In smooth variants, the elevated ltrB transcript level may not translate into proportional LtrB protein activity due to post-transcriptional or post-translational regulation, or because the smooth-variant genetic background carries additional mutations that override LtrB's positive effect on biofilm formation (Q. Wu et al., 2022). Alternatively, LtrB may operate in a dose-dependent manner, where moderate expression promotes biofilm, but excessive expression (as seen in smooth variants) leads to a different regulatory outcome, possibly through titration of co-factors or altered oligomerisation states (Demeester et al., 2024). It is also formally possible that the smooth-variant upregulation of ltrB represents a compensatory response to other regulatory perturbations rather than a direct cause of the smooth phenotype. Thus, rather than a simple linear correlation, LtrB functions within a complex regulatory network where its effect on colony morphology and biofilm formation is influenced by the genetic and environmental context. Nevertheless, the current study provides a solid foundation for positioning LtrB as a key node in the regulatory network that controls the biofilm-motility switch, and our findings open new avenues for investigating its interplay with QS regulators. Collectively, our findings support a model in which LtrB acts as a pivotal downstream effector that, upon the establishment of surface contact, reinforces the motile-to-sessile transition by sustaining matrix production and restraining flagellar gene expression during biofilm maturation.

LtrB exerts global control over biofilm-matrix constituents (Fig. 2d and e). Deletion of ltrB resulted in a marked reduction of key extracellular-matrix components (EPS, proteins and eDNA), positioning LtrB as a regulator within the biofilm-formation network. This finding aligns with the previously observed differential expression of LtrB during smooth-to-wrinkled morphotype switching (Q. Wu et al., 2022). Importantly, loss of LtrB converts colony morphology from wrinkled to smooth, and SEM reveals that the mutant fails to form tightly packed multicellular aggregates (Fig. 2b–c). This phenotypic shift not only compromises biofilm structural integrity but also severely impairs bacterial colonization on seafood and food-processing surfaces, underscoring the important role of LtrB in the food-contamination chain.

From an ecological perspective, the LtrB-mediated regulatory mechanism provides V. parahaemolyticus with a competitive advantage in marine environments and food-processing facilities. As rising global temperatures expand the geographic range of this pathogen, its ability to form biofilms becomes increasingly critical for environmental persistence (Ashrafudoulla et al., 2021; Scanes et al., 2025). Our data demonstrate that LtrB is important for biofilm formation on food-contact surfaces such as stainless steel and glass, highlighting its direct role in persistence throughout the food chain. Notably, LtrB-enhanced biofilm development is accompanied by a sharp decrease in motility, appearing to contradict the classical view that flagellum-driven initial attachment is prerequisite for biofilm formation (Jung et al., 2019; Yildiz and Visick, 2009). We therefore propose that LtrB operates primarily during the mid-to-late stages of biofilm development, after initial, likely flagellum-mediated, surface attachment has occurred, orchestrating the transition from initial adhesion to mature biofilm architecture and stabilizing the sessile community by downregulating motility. However, it is important to acknowledge that the colonization assays in this study were performed over a 24-h incubation period, which primarily captures early-to-mid stage biofilm establishment. In food-processing environments, V. parahaemolyticus biofilms often persist for extended periods and undergo significant physiological maturation, during which regulatory networks may be dynamically remodeled (Gode-Potratz and McCarter, 2011; Q. Wang et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2026). The relative contribution of LtrB to biofilm maintenance and resilience at later time points (e.g., beyond 48-72 h) therefore remains to be determined. Given that the motile-to-sessile transition is a highly dynamic process, future time-course studies using older biofilms will be essential to fully elucidate whether LtrB's activating effect on matrix production and repressive effect on motility are sustained, attenuated, or superseded by other regulators as biofilms age. Such investigations will provide a more comprehensive understanding of LtrB's role in long-term surface colonization and persistence in food-related environments.

Given the high tolerance of V. parahaemolyticus biofilms to conventional disinfectants and antibiotics (Ashrafudoulla et al., 2021; D. Wang et al., 2022), targeting the LtrB regulatory pathway may offer a new strategy for controlling food contamination. In principle, interfering with LtrB binding to its DNA targets or blocking its ability to activate biofilm-matrix genes could weaken bacterial colonization on seafood (e.g., mussels and shrimp shells) and processing surfaces (e.g., glass and stainless steel), thereby reducing the risk of foodborne illness. However, we acknowledge that our current experimental conditions, which employed rich laboratory media and static incubation, do not fully recapitulate the complex and dynamic environments of industrial food production, where bacteria encounter nutrient limitation, low temperatures, desiccation, and sub-lethal concentrations of disinfectants. Therefore, while our findings establish a strong foundation, additional studies under such environmentally relevant stress conditions are necessary to assess the translational applicability of targeting LtrB for biofilm control in real-world seafood processing systems. Furthermore, it should be noted that while LtrB deletion reduces biofilm formation, it simultaneously enhances bacterial motility; the net effect of this phenotypic shift in complex environments requires further evaluation.

Several limitations of this study remain. First, how environmental signals modulate LtrB activity has not been fully elucidated—an important gap for understanding its function across different habitats. The co-inducer molecules that modulate LysR activity have been identified for only a subset of family members, and in many cases, the specific signals that activate or repress these regulators remain unknown (Demeester et al., 2024; Maddocks and Oyston, 2008). Identifying the physiological signals that control LtrB activity will be critical for understanding its role in environmental adaptation. Second, the interaction network between LtrB and other known biofilm regulators (e.g., OpaR, CalR) warrants further clarification. Third, our colonization and biofilm assays were performed at a single 24-h time point, which, while informative for early biofilm establishment, does not capture the full temporal dynamics of LtrB-mediated regulation in mature biofilms that are more representative of persistent contamination in food-processing settings. Extended time-course experiments are needed to determine whether LtrB's regulatory influence changes as biofilms age beyond 24 h. Fourth, the present study did not investigate the contribution of LtrB to virulence, antimicrobial tolerance, or host-pathogen interactions. While our findings demonstrate that LtrB promotes biofilm formation and surface colonization, the biological significance of this regulator remains incompletely defined without in vivo or infection-related studies. Future work using animal infection models and antimicrobial susceptibility testing is needed to determine whether LtrB contributes to pathogenicity and to assess the translational potential of targeting LtrB. Nevertheless, our current results provide a robust foundation for these future investigations. Fifth, whether LtrB controls other cellular processes beyond biofilm formation and motility, such as stress responses or metabolic pathways, remains to be explored. Genome-wide transcriptomic or ChIP-seq analyses would be valuable to define the full LtrB regulon and to identify additional targets that may contribute to its phenotypic effects.

In summary, this study establishes LtrB as an important regulator of the biofilm-motility switch in V. parahaemolyticus and elucidates the molecular mechanism by which it balances these two lifestyles through direct control of biofilm-matrix and flagellar gene expression. While our findings demonstrate LtrB's role during early-to-mid stage biofilm development, further time-course studies are warranted to determine whether its regulatory functions are sustained or modified in more mature biofilms that better reflect long-term persistence in food-processing environments. Importantly, the LysR-type classification of LtrB is not only supported by its conserved domain architecture (Fig. S1) but is also functionally substantiated by EMSA, which confirm that LtrB directly binds to the promoter regions of its target genes. These biochemical data provide compelling evidence for its role as a direct transcriptional regulator. This discovery not only advances our understanding of bacterial environmental adaptation but also identifies a potential target for controlling V. parahaemolyticus contamination along the food chain, with important implications for food safety and public health. Targeting such a regulatory node, rather than individual downstream pathways, may offer a broader and more effective strategy for interference.
