Development and application of an intelligent assessment system for medical clinical skill training
Authors: Qi Dai, Kaiyu Chen, Cheng Han, Boli Zheng, Enguang Zou, Yisu Ge, Changfeng Cheng, Yuqian Gan, Jiatao Song, Huiling Chen, Zhaomin Chen, Xuebo Zhu, Gang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe digital transformation of medical education represents a significant global development trend. However, clinical skill assessment, a vital method to inspect education outcome, has historically depended on conventional manual evaluation methods. To further explore intelligent training paradigms and realize autonomous instruction and automated assessment, this study introduces contrastive learning methods to construct a novel clinical skill assessment system characterized by unified standard, visible process, and objective evaluation. Practical validation indicates that the model effectively differentiates varying levels of proficiency in the specific surgical procedures. Under the optimal parameter combination, the prediction of one operational procedure achieved a peak accuracy of 94.01%. With a one-point tolerance, accuracy further improved to 96.41%. Overall, depending on its objectivity and standardization, the model significantly enhances the scientific rigor and efficiency of the clinical skill training process, while also providing immediate feedback on performance.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The digital transformation of medical education is a significant global development trend. Numerous nations are formulating related policies and strategic plans to promote the deep integration of medical education with modern information technology, thereby enhancing the quality of medical talent cultivation. The Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) in the United States has consistently emphasized the use of information technology to optimize assessment and feedback mechanisms for residents (e.g., 360-degree assessment, the Milestone system), aiming to improve educational quality1. The International Association for Medical Education (AMEE) has a dedicated technology-enhanced learning committee to guide its decisions regarding various aspects of artificial intelligence (AI) in teaching. The focus of medical education is progressively shifting from traditional pedagogical approaches to digital learning methods2.

The assessment of clinical skills has long depended on traditional manual evaluation methods, such as standardized patients (SP), direct observation of procedural skills, and the objective structured clinical examination. While these methods possess high face validity, their scoring process relies on the conventional model of “on-site expert observation + checklists,” which is time-consuming, susceptible to human bias, and often fails to accurately reflect medical students’ clinical skill proficiency and training outcomes. Existing research has introduced methods like video recording to enhance assessment accuracy and efficiency, yet the evaluation process remains costly and time-intensive. Furthermore, studies indicate that even with standardized scoring rubrics and examiner training, significant discrepancies persist in ratings for the same clinical skill among different examiners. The average inter-rater group variance exceeds 5%, representing a substantial gap from the high-consistency standards required for objective assessment3. The upfront investment is often disproportionate to the effectiveness, and the feedback provided is typically delayed and lacks specific detail4,5. Consequently, constructing a novel clinical skill assessment system characterized by “unified standards, visible process, and objective evaluation” has become a critical breakthrough point for medical education reform.

Presently, the rapid advancement of computer science, particularly AI, is progressively turning autonomous instruction and automated assessment into realities. Increasing emphasis is being placed on the application and integration of innovative technologies, such as augmented reality (AR), in medical education, with their use cases expanding beyond skills training and assessment6. In the practice of clinical skills teaching, AI can be applied to process recognition, motion analysis, error detection, and score determination, thereby enabling real-time recognition and detailed modeling of operational behaviors7. AI-assisted instructional systems, including virtual patients, VR simulation training platforms, and intelligent tutoring systems, are being incrementally introduced into skill training. These tools help students develop operational thinking, reinforce standard procedures, and achieve personalized learning in a risk-free environment8,9. However, most of the existing research focuses on identifying single skill points or providing preliminary grading. These systems are not yet capable of comprehensively scoring the complete operational workflow and struggle to cover comprehensive dimensions such as procedural sequence, motion precision, operational logic, and humanistic care10,11. Basic surgical skills, like dressing changes and catheterization, are multi-step and highly standardized, making it difficult for current methods to meet the high-precision scoring requirements. Therefore, there is an urgent need to construct a novel assessment system that integrates AI with standardized principles. Such a system must overcome the bottlenecks of traditional assessment—namely, subjectivity, lack of feedback, and data gaps—to realize a full-process, multi-dimensional, and quantifiable intelligent evaluation of basic operational skills.

This study targets the wound dressing change procedure—a core skill in basic clinical practice, licensing examinations, and standardized residency training—as its entry point. It proposes and validates an interpretable and scalable unified framework combining contrastive learning and support set retrieval for clinical skill assessment, which is also adaptive to new scenarios by simply updating the support set.

As one of China’s innovative intelligent assessment systems for basic clinical skills, this project holds threefold groundbreaking value:

Results

The data used in this study were sourced from video recordings of residents performing a complete wound dressing procedure in a simulated environment at The First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. The experimental videos were captured from three distinct perspectives: a frontal view, a trolley-side view, and a non-trolley-side view. All footage was recorded using Logitech cameras with a minimum resolution of 720p HD. The frontal camera was positioned 2 m away from the operator at a height of 1.6 m, while the trolley-side and non-trolley-side cameras were each placed 1 m away at an approximate height of 2 m. In cases where a specific camera angle was missing, such as when one of the three views was not recorded, the corresponding data segment was discarded entirely. Moreover, any recordings exhibiting significant camera shake or obstructions during the operation were also excluded. All raw videos underwent a preliminary screening process to ensure completeness and visual clarity. To enhance data diversity, multiple valid operation videos from the same operator that met the established scoring criteria were included in the final dataset. Each video was meticulously scored by trained senior surgical experts according to the standardized objective structured assessment of technical skills (OSATS) scale. The scoring criteria for this OSATS scale are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. The scale disaggregates the dressing change procedure into key stages and core skill points, with each scoring element possessing explicit criteria and corresponding point values. Specific scoring categories included operational judgment, pre-operative preparation, operational process, post-operative management, overall evaluation. The experts assigned scores based on the operator’s performance in each segment, culminating in an overall skill score for the procedure. These expert ratings constitute the ground truth labels for model training and evaluation in this study. In addition, on this basis, this study established a surgical video resource website (http://tool.valiant-hcc.com/index.html) to demonstrate the results and realize resource sharing and information exchange.

The video data utilized for the experiments focused on the content within the operational process category. Given that critical skill information is predominantly reflected in the surgeon’s fine hand movements and the specific conditions of the surgical area captured from the frontal perspective, subsequent video analysis experiments focused primarily on the frontal view footage.

This research primarily focuses on four types of surgical operational procedure videos, designated as operational procedure 3.1, operational procedure 3.2, operational procedure 5.2, and operational procedure 6. In total, the dataset comprises 2686 training videos, 107 support set videos, and 687 validation videos. The data composition for each procedure is as follows: operational procedure 3.1 includes 870 total videos, consisting of 670 training, 28 support, and 172 validation videos; operational procedure 3.2 includes 870 total videos, with 674 training, 24 support, and 172 validation videos; operational procedure 5.2 includes 870 total videos, comprising 676 training, 22 support, and 172 validation videos; and operational procedure 6 includes 867 total videos, made up of 666 training, 33 support, and 171 validation videos. In addition, on this basis, this study established a surgical video resource website (http://tool.valiant-hcc.com/index.html) to realize resource sharing and information exchange.

Hyperparameter comparison experiments

The selection of critical hyperparameters significantly influences the performance of the contrastive learning model. These experiments utilized the six-action videos corresponding to the operational process as the benchmark test. Adjustments were made to two key hyperparameters: the backbone network freezing strategy and the total loss function weight λ. Adjusting the freezing strategy balances the utilization of pre-trained knowledge with the learning of task-specific features from the dressing change videos. The weighting coefficient λ controls the contribution of the cross-set contrastive loss Lloss relative to the combined intra-class contrastive loss.

Supplementary Table 2 lists the model parameter names corresponding to the hyperparameter strategies. The freeze strategy F1 denotes fine-tuning only the final bottleneck block of layer 4 in the backbone. The freeze strategy F2 denotes fine-tuning all layer 4; and F3 denotes fine-tuning both layer 3 and layer 4. Supplementary Table 3 presents the accuracy (AUC) and macro accuracy (MacroAUC) for the corresponding models. As shown in the table, θ2,2 achieved a strict accuracy of 79.39%, the highest among all combinations. In assessments with a higher tolerance margin, its accuracy (≤1 tolerance margin) was 87.27%, indicating a very high proportion of predictions falling within 1 point of the expert scores, second only to the 88.48% achieved by θ1,2. For the ≤2 tolerance margin, θ1,3 reached 95.15%, while other models also exceeded 91% accuracy, demonstrating that the prediction performance within a 2-point tolerance is substantial.

Further analysis of performance under specific conditions reveals that when fixing the loss weight λ at 0.5 and comparing θ2,1, θ2,2, and θ2,3, θ2,2 achieved higher accuracy than the other two within the 1-point tolerance margin. This indicates that the optimal number of fine-tuned parameters is neither maximized nor minimized. The F2 strategy provided a more balanced and holistically superior performance. Likewise, fixing the freeze strategy at F2 and comparing different λ values (θ1,2, θ2,2, and θ3,2), θ2,2 was significantly superior to the other two models at strict accuracy.

Although θ2,2 did not achieve the absolute optimum on every single metric, it demonstrated robust and well-balanced performance across all evaluation dimensions. Notably, given the inherent subjectivity in the expert scoring of these procedural videos, the θ2,2 model, which excelled within the ≤1 tolerance margin, was selected as a robust and efficient model for the subsequent skill assessment tasks.

Model performance on different actions

The support set is a standard term in the few-shot learning and meta-learning literature, denoting a small set of labeled reference examples used during inference. Unlike a validation set, which serves hyperparameter tuning, the support set directly participates in the inference pipeline. The query videos are scored by computing feature similarity against the support reference samples. This paradigm was first formalized by Vinyals et al.12 in matching networks and has since been widely adopted in prototypical networks13, meta- learning14, relation network15, and subsequent works. In our framework, the support set comprises expert-reviewed representative procedure videos at each score level, serving as the reference library for the retrieval-based scoring system at inference time. In our specific implementation, we selected samples from operational procedure 3.1, operational procedure 3.2, operational procedure 5.2, and operational procedure 6.

Due to the sparsity of videos for certain rating levels, mandating a fixed-size support set could lead to an insufficient number of videos or the inclusion of low-quality, non-representative samples. To address this data sparsity, this study dynamically adjusts the support set size based on the following criteria:

If the video count for a label is ≤5, 1 video is selected for the support set.

If the video count for a label is ≤5, 1 video is selected for the support set.

If the count is > 5 and ≤10, 2 videos are selected.

If the count is > 5 and ≤10, 2 videos are selected.

If the count is > 10 and ≤20, 3 videos are selected.

If the count is > 10 and ≤20, 3 videos are selected.

If the count is > 20, 5 videos are selected.

If the count is > 20, 5 videos are selected.

Comparison experiments of different backbones

To evaluate the impact of different backbones on overall model performance, this experiment conducted a comparison with three other representative video classification models: multiscale vision transformers (MVIT)16, temporal relation network (TRN)17, and temporal segment network (TSN)18. Additionally, a temporal shift module (TSM) model pre-trained on Kinetics-400 was included. Specific model information is visible in Supplementary Table 4. This experiment used the operational procedure 6 dressing change videos as the benchmark test for prediction.

It should be noted that the backbones for TRN, TSN, and TSM are all based on ResNet-50. Their output feature dimension is (2048, 7, 7). In this study, we applied average pooling to compress the last two dimensions, resulting in a 1D feature vector of 2048. For MVIT, the feature output is the embedding of the class token, resulting in a 1D vector of length 768.

As shown in Supplementary Table 5, TSM (sthv2) and TSM (kinetics) demonstrated the best or second-best performance. TSM (sthv2) achieved the highest strict accuracy at 79.39% and, along with TSM (kinetics), reached the peak accuracy of 93.33% within a 2-point tolerance. In terms of MacroAUC, which measures overall classification performance, TSM (kinetics) achieved 0.9289, indicating the strongest ability to discriminate between classes. These results suggest that TSM’s efficient temporal shift module effectively captures critical temporal information.

To assess statistical reliability, we compute 95% confidence intervals (CI) for all performance metrics using bootstrap resampling (N=1000). For each iteration, we draw a sample of the same size as the original test set from the predictions, compute all metrics, and report the mean and 95% CI (2.5th and 97.5th percentiles) across all iterations. Detailed information can be found in Supplementary Tables 6, 7, and 8.

To further illustrate the superiority of our contrastive learning approach, Supplementary Table 9 also presents the prediction results derived directly from the classification and regression models. The hyperparameters for TSM(sthv2) models strictly adhere to the configurations specified in their respective original papers, utilizing the support set as validation data to identify the optimal weights. For all four actions, we trained both a direct classification baseline and a regression model built upon the TSM (SthV2) architecture, subsequently averaging the video accuracy across the four action categories. Specifically, the models were trained on the training set, utilizing the support set as validation data to determine the optimal model weights. Final evaluations were conducted on the test set, with detailed results provided in Supplementary Table 9. For the regression model, the predicted scores were rounded to the nearest integer before being compared against the ground truth video labels to determine prediction correctness. Compared to the identical backbone architecture, our proposed contrastive learning approach outperforms both the classification and regression baselines in terms of precision, yielding an average accuracy improvement of 10.7% (87.09% vs 76.39% vs 0.76%).

In contrast, the TRN model lagged significantly across all metrics, with a MacroAUC of only 0.6822, making it unable to accurately classify the data used in this study. Furthermore, the results for MVIT show that while its strict accuracy was relatively low at 69.70%, its MacroAUC (0.9242) was superior even to the TSM (sthv2). This may imply that while MVIT has biases in determining the single most likely class, its overall grasp of inter-class differentiation remains strong. This could be related to the characteristics of vision transformer architectures requiring massive-scale pre-training.

However, Class 13 and Class 14 were problematic for all models, consistently showing low AUC scores. The correctly matched results demonstrate the robustness of the proposed model. For instance, in the matching cases with ground truth scores of 11 and 12 in Fig. 7, the operators in the test and support videos wear different colored attire. There are also noticeable differences in patient mankind, the operating room background, and the video’s lighting and camera angles. The incorrect matching results in Fig. 8 show that the model demonstrates a pronounced overestimation tendency. Specifically, samples with ground truth scores of 10, 11, and 12 were misclassified as 14 or 15, resulting in a score discrepancy of 3 to 4 points.

While this gap appears numerically large, an analysis of the similarity distribution reveals that the similarity margin between the true class and the predicted class is often minimal. For example, in the sample with a true score of 12, the similarity to the true class was 0.842, while the similarity to the predicted class was 0.856. This negligible difference of 0.014 resulted in a 3-point score deviation. Similarly, for the sample with a true score of 10, the similarity margin was only 0.077, yet it was ultimately assigned to a class 4 points higher. This phenomenon suggests that the model effectively recognizes the proximity between different skill levels in the similarity space. However, because the final output is a discretized single score, these minimal similarity differences are amplified into large score errors.

Conversely, there were also minor misclassifications, such as underestimation (a true score of 15 predicted as 14) or slight overestimation (a true score of 14 predicted as 15). These 1-point errors can be reasonably attributed to the subjectivity and ambiguity inherent in the manual scoring process.

However, more concerning than these minor deviations is the model’s systematic bias towards higher scores across multiple examples. This phenomenon suggests that during feature learning, the model forms tighter clusters for high-score samples but fails to adequately capture the non-standard (or highly variable) features of low-score samples. This results in a tendency to align with high-score classes during similarity ranking. Furthermore, in the sample with a true score of 11, a tie in the highest similarity occurred (0.795 for both Class 13 and Class 14). It was ultimately classified as 14 due to the decision rule, further exacerbating the overestimation trend.

In summary, while the model can generally distinguish between different skill levels, it suffers from a systematic overestimation problem at the fine-grained level. The root cause appears to be the high inter-class similarity among high-score categories, combined with a discretization-based decision mechanism that amplifies minimal similarity differences, especially for boundary samples. Therefore, future research direction should consider introducing a regression-based prediction model. This would better align with the continuous nature of surgical skill assessment and avoid the numerical deviations caused by discrete classification.

Discussion

This study establishes an intelligent automated scoring system for fundamental clinical skills, leveraging the advantages of AI to achieve objective, accurate, and efficient assessment of medical students’ clinical abilities with timely feedback, thereby creating a closed-loop system. First, the system systematically validates the utility of an intelligent scoring system based on support set retrieval and contrastive learning in the context of dressing change procedures, which is significant for ensuring the quality of clinical skills training. Second, the system’s computations rely on extensive clinical skill operation data and standardized assessment metrics. Through quantitative analysis, it effectively controls inter-rater variability, thereby enhancing inter-rater reliability and consequently improving the scientific validity and reliability of the assessment results19. Furthermore, the system can record and analyze students’ operational processes in real-time, promptly identifying problems and deficiencies in their clinical skill execution. It provides specific feedback and improvement suggestions, helping students engage in targeted learning and training and promoting the continuous enhancement of their clinical skills20. Moreover, the automated scoring system enables large-scale, high-frequency skill examinations, meeting the needs of medical institutions at various levels. It also provides medical educators with rich teaching data, helping them understand student learning situations and skill mastery levels, thereby optimizing teaching content and methods and improving the quality and effectiveness of clinical skills training.

Basic operational skills are a fundamental requirement across all clinical disciplines. Standardized execution is directly linked to nosocomial infection control, patient safety, and prognosis. For example, inadequate attention to aseptic technique details during a dressing change can lead to severe infections, potentially jeopardizing patient recovery21. However, current residency training assessments still widely rely on examiner experience, suffering from significant subjectivity and distinct inter-group variability22,23. The intelligent scoring system proposed in this study, through data-driven quantitative scoring, allows skill training to overcome the limitation of “reliance on senior faculty” and achieve homogenization across institutions. Instantly, fine-grained feedback helps young doctors quickly identify operational weaknesses, thereby shortening their learning curve and reducing error rates. In the long term, the adoption of such a tool is expected to improve training equity and ultimately translate into improved patient outcomes.

Compared to related research in recent years, existing AI-based skill assessments predominantly focus on binary classification or coarse-grained action recognition, often lacking in multi-level scoring, detailed granularity, and cross-center consistency. Hira et al. used attention networks for binary grading of surgical skill videos but failed to cover skill-level differentiation24. Wan et al. also used spatio-temporal attention mechanisms to distinguish experts from novices, remaining within a binary classification framework25. In JAMA Network Open, Kitaguchi et al. developed a 3D convolutional network for skill assessment in laparoscopic operations, but the results primarily served to differentiate operational levels rather than providing detailed feedback26. Igaki et al. also reported a scoring system based on intraoperative videos; although it verified the objectivity of AI scoring, it lacked pedagogical explainability27. Furthermore, systematic reviews and multi-institutional studies indicate that most current video AI systems suffer from insufficient external validity, a lack of standardization, and limited interpretability28–30. In contrast, this study explicitly embeds expert scoring via support set retrieval, enabling the AI output to be not just a “score” but also traceable back to reference samples. This enhances generalization capabilities in few-shot, multi-level scenarios and provides explainable feedback highly consistent with expert evaluations.

The support set retrieval and contrastive learning loss function design adopted in this study enables the model to maintain stable performance on multi-center, heterogeneous video datasets. Specifically, the intra-loss constrains feature similarity among samples within the same class, while the cross-loss establishes consistency between the training set and the support set, thereby enhancing the discriminative power of the feature representations for the multi-level scoring task. Unlike traditional direct classification methods, the scoring results of this study can be traced back by referencing support set samples, ensuring the transparency and interpretability of the prediction process. The experimental results demonstrate that the framework achieved a peak accuracy of 94.01% in scoring specific actions. When the tolerance was relaxed to ≤1 (a one-point margin), the accuracy improved to 96.41%. This indicates that the framework can achieve highly consistent effects with expert scoring at both the action level and the overall assessment level, providing a reliable basis for establishing an objective and generalizable medical skill assessment system.

Despite the rapid development of automated scoring systems, their practical application in clinical training still faces several challenges: First, data dependency: A lack of high-quality labeled data impacts model training, and annotation costs are high31,32. Second, clinical applicability: Most systems are still confined to simulated environments, with limited application in real clinical scenarios. Third, lack of standardization: Scoring criteria have not been unified across different systems, making horizontal comparisons difficult. Fourth, scoring comprehensiveness needs improvement: Most research focuses on identifying single skill points or preliminary grading. These systems are not yet capable of comprehensively scoring the entire operational process, failing to cover integrated dimensions such as procedural sequence, motion finesse, operational logic, and humanistic care33,34.

Overall, this framework enhances the scientific rigor and generalizability of clinical skills training through its objective and standardized scoring mechanism, offering an important reference for constructing a unified medical education assessment system. In the future, this research will focus on the following aspects: First, expanding the dataset by incorporating more clinical operation items and real-world clinical videos from multiple centers to enhance the model’s generalization capabilities. Additionally, more rigorous evaluation strategies, such as leave-one-center-out testing or independent validation on unseen centers, will be adopted to determine whether the model captures truly generalizable skill-related features. Furthermore, the research will explore domain generalization or adaptation strategies to improve robustness under distribution shift. Second, exploring multi-view information fusion techniques by integrating video streams from different camera angles to build a more comprehensive understanding of the operational scene and overcome single-view occlusion issues. Third, investigating the transition from the current discrete retrieval framework to a continuous regression prediction model to directly output a continuous skill score, making the evaluation more aligned with the nature of clinical skill assessment and potentially resolving scoring biases introduced by discrete decision-making. Fourth, deploying this model in clinical training by setting up appropriate training venues, collecting and uploading data from multiple cameras, providing real-time analysis and feedback, and truly achieving autonomous instruction and autonomous intelligent examination.

Methods

Dataset construction and scoring system establishment

In this study, the entire process of trainees performing procedures was recorded in a standardized clinical skills training room using multi-angle, fixed-position cameras (front, left, and right-side multi-perspectives) and an ambient sound acquisition system. This setup ensured complete coverage of all operational steps and potential common errors. The subjects included interns and resident physicians from different grades. Before filming, informed consent forms were distributed, and procedural and operational instructions were provided to ensure the performance was as close as possible to an actual training scenario.

After video acquisition, professional video processing tools were used for frame splitting and compression. Subsequently, two or more experts with clinical teaching experience annotated each video segment. The annotations included: sequential marking of operational steps, localization of key time nodes, identification of common erroneous behaviors, assignment of scoring labels, and subjective evaluation of the operation’s overall completeness and standardization. Upon completion, another expert reviewed all annotations, performing corrections and score merging to generate a video dataset with multi-dimensional scoring labels.

On this basis, referring to the Supplementary Table 1 and integrating expert consensus, a scientific, clear, and quantifiable scoring metric system was developed. This system defined the scoring dimensions and key points for each operational item. Subjective scoring dimensions, such as demonstration of humanistic care, were supplemented through expert discussions. All scoring items were ultimately converted into standardized scoring labels for model training input.

To ensure the universality and computability of the scoring system, all scoring items were index-coded in a pre-operative definition checklist and managed in a structured tabular format, facilitating subsequent automatic matching and result generation within the platform system.

The analysis in this study primarily focused on single, frontal-view videos. During the data preprocessing stage, the originally captured compressed-format videos were first decoded into processable image frames. Subsequently, bilinear interpolation was used to uniformly resize each frame to 1920×1080 pixels. Following this, 16 frames were randomly sampled from each complete video to serve as the input sequence for the model. To enhance the model’s generalization capabilities and reduce the risk of overfitting, a data augmentation strategy was adopted. Specifically, image frames in the training set were subjected to multi-crop and random horizontal flipping with a probability of 0.5. In contrast, frames in the support and test sets underwent standard center cropping, resulting in final image dimensions of 224×224 pixels.

Model architecture

This study reframes the scoring problem as a video retrieval task. The core idea is to utilize contrastive learning to retrieve the most similar reference video from a pre-constructed support set corresponding to the video being evaluated. The expert score of this reference video is then assigned to the video under evaluation, thereby achieving an automated score. For instance, assume the support set contains Video A, expertly rated at 9 points, and Video B, rated at 5 points. When a new surgical video, X, is input to the model, the model, through contrastive learning, determines that Video X is most similar to Video A in terms of key operational features. Consequently, the automated score for Video X will tend toward 9 points.

This research introduces a supervised contrastive learning framework to optimize the backbone model’s comprehension capabilities for video. First, representative videos from each scoring level are selected to form the support set. Subsequently, the backbone network is used to extract video features. The model is continuously trained by pulling the same-class videos closer together and pushing different-class videos further apart in the feature space. Finally, based on the learned model and the support set samples, skill score predictions are made for new videos under evaluation.

Specifically, the primary workflow of the contrastive learning-based clinical surgical video scoring method proposed in this study is illustrated in Fig. 8. The entire process begins with the acquisition and preparation of surgical video data, which includes collecting video materials of surgical procedures and their corresponding skill scoring labels provided by experts. After data preparation, the dataset is meticulously partitioned into three independent subsets: a training set, a support set, and a test set. The support set contains representative video samples for each skill score level, serving as a benchmark for subsequent contrastive learning and score prediction.

Next, the video data in the training, support, and test sets all undergo a unified preprocessing procedure. This aims to augment the raw video data and convert it into a format suitable for model input. Following preprocessing, the same feature extraction network is employed to extract high-dimensional feature representations from the videos in the training and support sets, respectively.

During the model training phase, the spatiotemporal features from the training and support sets are primarily used to optimize the backbone model parameters. Based on the contrastive learning framework, features from the training set are compared against those from the support set. A defined loss function is used to minimize the distance between same-class features while maximizing the distance between different-class features. This training process is conducted iteratively until a preset number of epochs is reached.

Upon completion of training, the trained model is used to re-extract and store the features for all video samples within the support set, thereby constructing a support set feature library. This library will be used for subsequent skill score predictions.

For any video in the test set, or any new video to be evaluated, it undergoes the same preprocessing and feature extraction pipeline. The resulting spatiotemporal features are then compared against all sample features in the support set feature library to calculate similarity. Finally, the skill score level for the query video is predicted based on the highest similarity found between its spatiotemporal features and the spatiotemporal features of the support set samples. After prediction, the model’s results are compared with the ground-truth expert scoring labels, and the model’s performance is comprehensively assessed using a series of evaluation metrics.

Temporal shift module

To efficiently capture temporal dependencies between video frames without significantly increasing computational cost, this study integrates a TSM into the feature extraction network, with its specific structure illustrated in Fig. 9b. Traditional 2D convolutional neural network (CNN)-based video analysis methods typically process each frame independently, overlooking temporal information interaction. Conversely, while 3D CNNs can extract spatio-temporal features simultaneously, they are computationally intensive and demand significant hardware resources.

The TSM addresses this issue through a novel and efficient approach. The core idea of this module is to shift a portion of the feature map channels along the temporal dimension before performing the 2D convolution operation. Specifically, for a given feature map tensor, some channels are shifted one temporal step backward, another portion is shifted one temporal step forward, while the remaining channels are left unchanged.

In this way, when the subsequent 2D convolutional kernel operates on the feature map of the current frame, its receptive field effectively incorporates information from adjacent frames. When processing the features of the tth frame, the convolution operation can simultaneously see partial features from the (t-1)th and (t+1)th frames due to the channel shift. This parameter-free temporal information fusion operation enables standard 2D CNNs to effectively model temporal information with zero additional computational overhead. This module enhances the model’s ability to understand dynamic changes in video at a very low cost, which is crucial for identifying and evaluating clinical operational steps that possess continuity and a specific sequence.

Model inference

After the model training is complete, for each sample in the test set requiring prediction, its cosine similarity with every sample in the support set is calculated. The sample is then assigned to the label of the support set sample with the highest cosine similarity, as illustrated in Fig. 9d. Specifically, each video in the support set saves its final, post-processed feature vector {fsupp,j}j=1Nsupp. These feature vectors, along with their corresponding known skill rating labels, are stored to form a reference feature library.

For a new video requiring skill level assessment, feature extraction is performed to obtain its feature vector ftest. The cosine similarity between the feature vector of the video to be assessed and all feature vectors in the support set feature library is computed, yielding a similarity vector Stest=s1,s2,…,sNsupp. The index j*=argmaxjsj corresponding to the maximum value in the similarity vector is identified. The label ysupp,j* of the j* sample in the support set is assigned as the predicted score ytestˆ for the video under assessment.

Implementation details

All experiments were conducted on the same hardware setup. The CPU is an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6430 operating at 1900MHz frequency. The system is equipped with 512GB of RAM. The GPU device is an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 D. The PyTorch 2.4.1 deep learning framework and MMAction2 were used as the foundational framework for the video understanding task.

The feature extraction network in the experiment employs the TSM as the backbone, which primarily includes layers such as conv1, maxpool, layer1, layer2, layer3, and layer436. Among these, conv1 and maxpool contain only a small number of parameters and have minimal impact on the overall backbone. The model was pre-trained on the Something-Something V2 dataset35. When fine-tuning for this specific task, only the parameters of layer 4 in the backbone were trained.

During training, the Adam optimizer was used with a learning rate of 0.001 and a weight decay of 0.0001. The model was trained for a total of 200 epochs. The parameters yielding the highest matching accuracy with the support set during the training process were recorded for subsequent validation on the test set. The batch size for the training, test, and support sets was set to 16. The temperature hyperparameter τ for the loss function was set to 0.07. The coefficients λ, α, and β in the total loss function were all set to 0.5. For data augmentation, the scales for multi-crop were (1, 0.875, 0.75, 0.66), and the random horizontal flip probability was 0.5. After extracting the corresponding support set, the remaining data was partitioned such that the test set comprised 20%, and the rest constituted the training set.

Statistical analysis

When evaluating model accuracy, a predicted skill level for the test set is considered correct only if it exactly matches the ground-truth label. However, considering that skill level assessment by experts can involve inherent subjectivity, the boundaries between adjacent scores may be ambiguous, particularly for operations with similar performance levels.

Based on this, to more comprehensively reflect the model’s practical utility and its tolerance for slight rating discrepancies, this study introduces two additional tolerance accuracy metrics. A prediction is deemed correct if the absolute difference between the predicted skill level and the ground-truth label is less than or equal to 1. Similarly, a second metric considers a prediction correct if the absolute difference is less than or equal to 2.

Furthermore, the calculation of the MacroAUC requires class scores for each test sample, indicating the likelihood of it belonging to each respective class. In conventional classification models, these scores are typically obtained directly from the model’s final output layer.

However, the prediction method adopted in this study, which is based on contrastive learning and nearest-neighbor principles, derives those class confidence scores differently. Specifically, for a video from the test set awaiting evaluation, its normalized spatio-temporal features are first extracted.
