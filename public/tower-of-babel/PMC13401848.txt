A deep learning representation and spatial Bayesian cell-type deconvolution for spatial transcriptomics
Authors: Xiao Yang, Yanbin Feng, Yanfang Zhao, Huamei Li, Xiaozhou Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpatial transcriptomics provides unprecedented insights into gene expression in the spatial context of tissues; however, some mainstream techniques lack quantitative analysis of uncertainty.MethodsIn this study, we propose a computational framework—Spatial Deconvolution via Deep Gaussian Processes (SDDGP)—that integrates deep Gaussian processes with Bayesian inference for spatially aware deconvolution of spatial transcriptomics data. The framework consists of three tightly coupled components. A multi-layer deep Gaussian process (DGP) captures nonlinear spatial dependencies in cell-type composition by propagating spatial coordinates through successive Gaussian process (GP) layers, each equipped with sparse variational inducing points and a radial basis function (RBF) kernel that can be combined with an external Matern spatial kernel. The DGP output serves as an adaptive, spatially informed prior for per-spot cell-type proportions, which are then estimated via a Negative-Binomial Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler with automatic proposal tuning, thinning, and split-chain convergence diagnostics.ResultsWe compared our method with existing approaches on two datasets: a pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) dataset with matched single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, and a human thymus dataset. Across both datasets, our method estimated cell type proportions more accurately than existing methods, particularly for rare cell types, and recovered biologically meaningful spatial patterns such as the distribution of T cells and plasma cells in PDAC. The Bayesian formulation also provides a quantification of uncertainty for each estimate, which aids the interpretation of spatial heterogeneity in complex tissues.

Introduction

Recent advances in spatial transcriptomics technology have revolutionized our ability to study gene expression in the context of native tissues, enabling researchers to map molecular activity to specific spatial locations (Rodriques et al., 2019; Ståhl et al., 2016), use spatial localization information to perform gene expression profiling across many tissue locations (Burgess, 2019), and characterize the transcriptomic landscape of tissues (Biancalani et al., 2021; Dries et al., 2021; Fischl et al., 2014; Moses & Pachter, 2022; Pham et al., 2020; Prinz et al., 2011; Soldatov et al., 2019; Svensson, Teichmann & Stegle, 2018; Xu et al., 2024a). However, each spatial location (spot) typically contains multiple different cell types. This requires the use of deconvolution methods to estimate the cell type composition at each location (Chen, Ruan & Wang, 2024).

Deconvolution methods for spatial transcriptomics can be broadly categorized into reference-based and reference-free methods. Reference-based methods use single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from similar tissues to create cell-type, specific gene expression features, that are then used to estimate cell-type proportions in spatial data (Cable et al., 2022). In contrast, reference-free methods attempt to simultaneously learn cell type features and their proportions directly from spatial data (Andersson et al., 2020; Miller et al., 2022).

While reference-free methods offer flexibility in the absence of matching scRNA-seq data, challenges exist in accurately assigning cell type identities to the components found. Reference-based methods provide more interpretable results, but are sensitive to the quality and relevance of the reference data, and difficulties often arise when dealing with rare cell types or similar transcriptional profiles (Xu et al., 2024b).

Existing reference-based deconvolution methods span a range of inference strategies. Many rely on deterministic optimization. For example, nonnegative least squares (Elosua-Bayes et al., 2021) which yield point estimates but do not quantify uncertainty in the inferred proportions. Probabilistic approaches such as Cell2location (Kleshchevnikov et al., 2022) address this limitation by adopting Bayesian generative models with negative binomial likelihoods, yet they typically approximate the posterior through variational inference rather than exact sampling, and their spatial regularization operates at a relatively coarse level. A framework that combines full posterior sampling with expressive, hierarchical spatial priors remains largely unexplored. Stereoscope (Andersson et al., 2020) takes a two-stage maximum-likelihood approach: it first fits a negative binomial model to the scRNA-seq reference to learn cell-type-specific expression parameters, then holds these parameters fixed while estimating per-spot cell-type proportions from the spatial data via MAP inference. Its formulation is elegant and requires minimal hyperparameter tuning, though the fixed-parameter transfer means it does not explicitly model spot-level technical variation or leverage spatial neighborhood information. To address these limitations, we propose Spatial Deconvolution via Deep Gaussian Processes (SDDGP), a Bayesian deconvolution framework that integrates three modeling strategies into spatial transcriptomics: (1) deep Gaussian processes to capture nonlinear spatial dependencies across different tissue regions; (2) Inducer-based sparse variational inference to ensure the computability of the Gaussian process at the scale of typical spatial transcriptomics datasets; and (3) adaptive Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling, which not only provides point estimates but also delivers fully calibrated posterior uncertainty—including confidence intervals for each measurement point, convergence diagnostic metrics (per-chain R-hat), and effective sample size.

SDDGP approach improves the accuracy of cell type proportion estimates and provides an uncertainty measure for each estimate, enabling for a more nuanced interpretation of spatial heterogeneity. To evaluate performance, we performed spatial deconvolution of spatial transcriptomics data using SDDGP, CARD (Ma & Zhou, 2022), Cell2location (Kleshchevnikov et al., 2022), SPOTlight (Elosua-Bayes et al., 2021), Seurat (Stuart et al., 2019), and Redeconve (Zhou et al., 2023), respectively, and obtained their respective cell inference results. Furthermore, we used the datasets containing matched scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics data from pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and human thymocyte (Thymus) datasets to demonstrate the validity of our method.

Materials & Methods

Compare methods

In our study, we compared with five deconvolution methods. Portions of this text were previously published as part of a paper (Li et al., 2022).

CARD. CARD models spatial transcriptomics data as a weighted combination of cell type-specific expression profiles derived from a scRNA-seq reference, and incorporates spatial location information through a conditional autoregressive prior to encourage smooth proportion estimates across neighboring spots. Proportions are solved via an optimization-based framework rather than full Bayesian sampling. We followed the guidelines and used the recommended default parameters from the CARD GitHub repository (https://github.com/YingMa0107/CARD).

Cell2location. Cell2location employs a hierarchical Bayesian framework built on a negative binomial observation model. It estimates cell abundance, rather than proportions, at each spatial location by decomposing gene expression through variational inference, with a structured prior encoding both the reference expression signatures and a regularization on total cell counts per spot. We followed the guidelines provided on the Cell2location website (https://cell2location.readthedocs.io/en/latest/notebooks/cell2location_tutorial.html), training the single-cell regression model with max_epochs = 250 and lr = 0.002, and the cell2location model with max_epochs = 30,000.

Seurat. Seurat (specifically the anchor-based integration and label transfer module) projects spatial spots and single-cell references into a shared low-dimensional space, identifies mutual nearest neighbors as anchors, and transfers cell type labels via a weighted voting scheme. It does not explicitly model proportions as a mixture but outputs prediction scores that can be interpreted as relative composition estimates. We followed the instructions on the Seurat 3.2 website (https://satijalab.org/seurat/archive/v3.2/integration.html), with parameters dim = 1:30 and normalization.method = “SCT”.

SPOTlight. SPOTlight combines non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) with non-negative least squares (NNLS). It first uses NMF to extract cell type-specific expression topics from the scRNA-seq reference, then applies NNLS to decompose each spot’s expression profile as a linear combination of these topics, yielding estimated cell type proportions. We followed the guidelines and used the recommended default parameters from the SPOTlight GitHub repository (https://github.com/MarcElosua/SPOTlight).

Redeconve. Redeconve takes a regression-based approach, treating each spot’s expression profile as a linear mixture of single-cell references and solving for proportions through a regularized regression framework. A distinctive feature is its use of cell-to-cell similarity measures to improve the resolution of closely related cell types. We followed the guidelines and used the recommended default parameters from the Redeconve GitHub repository (https://github.com/ZxZhou4150/Redeconve).

Our selection was based on two considerations. First, we aimed to cover a variety of methodological paradigms: CARD and Cell2location represent probabilistic spatially aware frameworks; Redeconve employs a set regression strategy; Seurat’s label transfer provides an anchor-based reference mapping method; and SPOTlight uses non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) with initialization combined with non-negative least squares. This diversity ensures that the evaluation of SDDGP is not limited to a single class of algorithms. Second, practical reproducibility also played a role—we limited our comparison to methods that are publicly available, well-documented, and run stably on our dataset using default or recommended parameter settings.

SDDGP differs from existing reference-based deconvolution methods such as robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) in two practically important ways: how inference scales with dataset size, and what information is returned beyond point estimates.

RCTD solves a maximum-likelihood problem per spot through iterative optimization, an approach that performs well at moderate scale but becomes expensive as the number of spots grows. SDDGP instead runs per-spot MCMC chains independently within a parallel FORK cluster. The number of workers is automatically set to min(detectCores() − 1, ⌊n/30⌋, 12) to balance throughput against memory. Overview of our SDDGP deconvolution framework

Our deconvolution framework consists of several key components: (1) construction of a reference matrix from scRNA-seq data, (2) adaptive prior computation, (3) deep Gaussian process (DGP) forward process, and (4) adaptive MCMC sampling. The quality of the reference matrix is critical for accurate deconvolution. The reference matrix is constructed through the following steps. (a) Common genes shared by the single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics datasets are identified, excluding mitochondrial genes that often exhibit technical variability. (b) Expression values are standardized using a log2(CPM + 1) transformation, and average expression profiles are then calculated for each cell type. (c) Informative genes are screened by computing the log2-fold change (log2FC) of each gene in a target cell type relative to all other cell types. Genes with log2 FC > 1.5 and expression >1 in the target cell type are retained. (d) The final gene set in the reference matrix comprises those genes that pass the above filters and show stable expression. (e) Each column (cell type) of the reference matrix is normalized to sum to one, yielding relative expression profiles.

This approach ensures that the reference matrix captures cell type-specific expression patterns while minimizing the effects of technical variation and biological noise.

Bayesian modeling with negative binomial likelihood

We modeled the gene expression observed at each spatial location as a mixture of cell type-specific expression profiles, with the mixing ratio representing the abundance of cell types. Unlike most existing approaches that use linear regression or log-normal models, we used a negative binomial distribution model to account for the overdispersion of RNA-seq data.

In our sparse GP framework, only M induced points (M ≪ N) are retained; therefore, we need only compute the M × M induced covariance matrix Kuu and the M × N cross-covariance matrix Kuf. The time complexity of the core operations (the Cholesky decomposition of Kuu and the subsequent linear solver) is O(M2N), and the space complexity is O(MN). We set M adaptively to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\min (50,\max (20, \left\lfloor 0.1N \right\rfloor ))$\end{document}min50,max20,0.1N, meaning that for typical datasets with hundreds to thousands of samples, M is at most 50, making the computational cost of the GP component nearly negligible compared to the MCMC sampling for each sample point; however, when N exceeds several hundred, the entire GP becomes a significant bottleneck.

To ensure the quality of sparse approximations, we adopted the following design: (1) K-means-induced point placement. Instead of random selection, we perform multiple rounds of restarted K-means clustering on spatial coordinates to determine the positions of induced points, ensuring they fully cover the tissue geometry, which is particularly beneficial for spatially heterogeneous and sparsely sampled regions. (2) Spatial kernel enhancement. In the first-layer DGP, we fuse a standard RBF kernel with a spatial prior based on physical coordinates: the RBF component operates within the latent representation of the DGP (with learnable length and variance) and serves as the standard kernel function for the GP layer; while the Matérn component performs a single computation based on the raw Euclidean distance between spatial coordinates and is injected as a fixed structural prior into the first-layer kernel function. This forms a hybrid kernel capable of encoding spatial structure within the domain, thereby compensating for local spatial trends that a purely data-driven RBF kernel might overlook. (3) Uncertainty-weighted downstream propagation. The framework does not blindly trust DGP outputs: it propagates the prediction variance of the sparse GP forward to compute a normalized uncertainty score for each sample point, and uses this to linearly adjust the DGP prior weights downward. When the approximation is likely unreliable (e.g., when a sample point is far from any induced point), the MCMC sampler falls back to a data-driven Dirichlet prior. (4) Multi-layer architecture. By stacking GPs, deep GPs can capture non-stationary spatial patterns that are difficult for single-layer sparse GPs to characterize, further narrowing the gap between the sparse form and the full form.

Metropolis–Hastings algorithm

Sampler and parameterisation. Per-spot inference is carried out with a Metropolis–Hastings sampler implemented C++ for efficiency. To respect the simplex constraint on the cell-type proportion vector θ, we sample in the additive log-ratio (ALR) space rather than on the simplex directly, which removes the need for boundary corrections and yields better-behaved Gaussian proposals. The over-dispersion parameter φ of the Negative-Binomial likelihood is updated on the log scale under a weakly informative Gamma (0.5, 50) prior, while θ is assigned a Dirichlet prior whose concentration is informed by the DGP-derived spatial priors.

The final estimate of cell type proportions was calculated as the mean of the a posteriori samples to provide a point estimate for downstream analyses. The standard deviation of the a posteriori samples provides a measure of uncertainty for each estimate.

Principles of probability distribution selection in spatial deconvolution

The Poisson distribution assumes that the variance is equal to the mean, which is not suitable for over-dispersed scRNA-seq data, while sc data exhibit variance much larger than the mean, thus the over-dispersed nature of RNA-seq data determines the necessity of the negative binomial distribution.

For each spot, the proportion of cells inferred therein sums to 1, so its normalization constraints naturally fit the Dirichlet distribution, and because of its conjugate prior advantage, it can form a conjugate pair with the multinomial/negative binomial distributions, and the posterior distribution is still a Dirichlet distribution, which facilitates Bayesian inference. The Gamma distribution is chosen because φ must be positive (φ > 0), the support set of the Gamma distribution is (0, ∞) and because of its covariance property, it forms a covariance pair with the φ parameter in the negative binomial distribution, which avoids complex calculations.

Determination of the hyperparameter

Adaptation of the proposal distribution. We use a Gaussian random-walk proposal with separate standard deviations for θ (in ALR space) and for log φ, initialised at 0.5 and 0.3 respectively. During an adaptation phase covering the first 40% of iterations (the burn-in window), the proposal scales are updated multiplicatively according to the running acceptance rate: if the rate falls below the lower bound of the target interval [0.23, 0.35], the scale is shrunk by a factor of (1 − η); if it exceeds the upper bound, the scale is enlarged by (1 + η), with η = 0.05. The scales are further clipped to the range [0.01, 3.0] to guard against degeneracy. The target interval brackets the theoretically optimal rate of 0.234 for random-walk Metropolis in moderate dimensions (Roberts & Rosenthal, 2001). Adaptation is disabled after burn-in so that the retained samples come from a time-homogeneous chain and the usual MCMC guarantees apply.

PDAC: The PDAC-A dataset was generated by Moncada et al. (2020) using the original microarray-based Spatial Transcriptomics platform, from a single human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma tumor. Results

SDDGP workflow

We developed SDDGP (Fig. 1) to perform spatial deconvolution of cell types using spatially resolved transcriptome (SRT) data and matched scRNA-seq data annotated with cell types. Briefly, SDDGP was designed to infer the cell type of each spot in the SRT data. Using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) counts data as a reference, specific gene screening (log2FC) was performed to obtain gene expression profiles of different cell types, and gene expression at spatial points was regarded as a linear combination of different cell types. Subsequently, the posterior distributions of θ and φ estimated through sampling are sampled using the representation learning capabilities of the Bayesian statistical uncertainty quantification of MCMC (Hastings, 1970) to sample the posterior distributions of θ and φ estimated through sampling, thereby avoiding the direct solution of high-dimensional integrals and instead approximating the posterior distributions via sampling. The inferred “cell types” represent the predominant cell type composition in the given SRT data, thus implying functional units. Finally, we reconstructed the cell type landscape using the ratio matrix obtained through deconvolution. We demonstrated our deconvolution method’s optimality using four metrics (see Appendix): Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), root mean square error (RMSE), and Jensen–Shannon divergence (JS). It also uncovered subtle biological mechanisms in Spatial transcriptomics (ST) data, validated via simulations and the PDAC and thymus datasets.

Overview of the SDDGP.

Using scRNA-seq data as a reference to obtain the gene expression characteristics of different cell types to treat the gene expression of spatial points as a linear combination of different cell types, Bayesian inference was used to estimate the proportion of cell types in each spatial point (θ) and the discrete parameter of the negative binomial distribution (ϕ) at each spatial point, and then the posterior distributions of θ and ϕ are estimated by sampling using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), which avoids solving the high-dimensional integrals directly, and approximates the posterior distributions by sampling. The inferred “cell type” represents the predominant cell type composition in the given SRT data, thus implying the functional unit.

Comparing with state-of-the-art deconvolution methods, algorithms including CARD (Ma & Zhou, 2022), Cell2location (Kleshchevnikov et al., 2022), SPOTlight (Elosua-Bayes et al., 2021), Seurat (Stuart et al., 2019), and Redeconve (Zhou et al., 2023) demonstrate superiority in terms of reconstruction accuracy, cell abundance estimation per point, sparsity of the reconstructed cellular composition, cell state resolution, and memory usage. Application to human pancreatic cancer data reveals new insights into tumor-infiltrating T cells, and while application to human thymus data provides novel clues regarding plasma cells. SDDGP identifies clonally specific T cell infiltration in pancreatic cancer

To demonstrate the capacity of SDDGP to address biological questions, we further analyzed its deconvolution results from a human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) spatial transcriptomics dataset.

T cell subtype analysis. We first examined tumor-infiltrating T cells, a key determinant of the immune landscape in PDAC. In the original reference single-cell dataset, T and NK cells were annotated as a single mixed population. We therefore performed a stepwise sub-clustering procedure: (1) extracting T/NK cells and removing low-quality cells; (2) coarse sub-clustering followed by a second round of quality control; and (3) final annotation into four subtypes—CD4+ T, CD8+ T, Treg, and NK cells. We then reran all deconvolution algorithms using these refined subtypes together with the remaining cell types as the reference.

The identity of the resolved T cell subtypes was confirmed by canonical marker gene expression (Fig. 4E): CD8+ T cells expressed CD8A, CD8B, GZMB, GZMK, PRF1, and NKG7; CD4+ T cells showed high CD4, IL7R, and TCF7; and Treg cells were marked by FOXP3, CTLA4, IL2RA, and TIGIT. These distinct molecular profiles indicate that the reference sub-clustering successfully separated closely related T cell populations, providing a reliable basis for downstream deconvolution. Including Cell2location, and SPOTlight, detected T cell signals across nearly all spots, a result difficult to reconcile with the characterization of PDAC as an immunologically “cold” tumor (Halbrook et al., 2023). Both CARD and SDDGP, by contrast, recovered T cell signals at a more limited number of spots. Importantly, this does not imply that T cells are globally rare in the tissue: as quantified in Fig. 4D, all three T cell subtypes show substantial enrichment in the Non-cancer region (fold enrichment > 1.0). Rather, T cells are selectively excluded from the Cancer core, with near-zero enrichment across all subtypes, consistent with the immune exclusion phenotype widely reported in PDAC. SDDGP further complements this point estimate with posterior credible intervals and convergence diagnostics (R-hat and ESS) for each spot, allowing users to distinguish genuine low-abundance signals from estimation uncertainty—a distinction that is particularly relevant for rare infiltrating populations. We further resolved the specific cells underlying each spot-level estimate (Fig. 4A, lower row), confirming that the detected T cell signals originate from a small number of individual cells rather than from diffuse low-level noise.

Discussion

In this study, we have presented a novel Bayesian reference-based deconvolution method for spatial transcriptomics data. Our approach addresses several limitations of existing methods by combining a negative binomial model, an adaptive Dirichlet prior, DGP, and MCMC sampling for robust parameter estimation and uncertainty quantification. Analysis of the deconvolution results (see the “Results” section) indicates that our method more accurately captures the statistical properties of spatial transcriptomics data.

The ALR transformation provides a principled method for enforcing simplex constraints on cell type ratios during MCMC sampling. This method avoids the constraints and biases, such as projection or truncation, that complicate other methods.

We employ Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling to quantify uncertainty. This not only provides point estimates of cell type proportions but also quantifies the uncertainty associated with each estimate. This uncertainty information is of great value for subsequent analysis, enabling researchers to identify regions of high confidence or flag potential problem areas for further investigation.

To demonstrate the applicability of SDDGP across different resolution platforms, we applied SDDGP to single-cell resolution MERFISH data from the mouse medial prefrontal cortex. We evaluated the accuracy of the deconvolved results using the adjusted Rand index (ARI) and purity metrics. SDDGP demonstrated outstanding performance on both datasets (Fig. S4).

At the same time, since Cell2location also employs a Bayesian approach, we included it in our benchmark tests; however, its performance was less than satisfactory. Although both SDDGP and Cell2location utilize a Bayesian framework based on the negative binomial likelihood function, they differ significantly in their inference and spatial modeling strategies, which may account for the performance gap. Specifically, Cell2location relies on variational inference, which works by optimizing a parametric approximation of the posterior distribution and is susceptible to initialization conditions and local optima. In contrast, SDDGP performs a full MCMC sampling at each location, employs adaptive prior distribution adjustment, and monitors convergence through branch-and-bound R-hat and effective sample size diagnostics. This provides a stronger guarantee that the reported posterior summaries (including 95% credibility intervals) accurately reflect the target distribution.

Applying our approach to PDAC spatial transcriptomics data revealed spatial patterns of cell distribution consistent with known histologic features of this cancer type. The ability to accurately resolve the cell type composition at each spatial location enables a more detailed analysis of tumor microenvironment heterogeneity, cell–cell interactions, and their potential impact on disease progression and treatment response.

For example, our analysis identified regions with high immune cell infiltration at the tumor-stroma interface, which may represent immunologically active regions where antitumor immune responses are mounted or suppressed. These insights could inform the development of immunotherapeutic strategies targeting specific microenvironmental ecological niches.

Similarly, identifying of distinct fibroblast populations with different spatial distributions may help characterize the heterogeneity of cancer-associated fibroblasts, which are known to play a complex role in tumor progression and drug resistance.

Furthermore, SDDGP demonstrates excellent scalability. We used ‘/usr/bin/time -v’ to measure memory usage and runtime for SDDGP and other deconvolution methods. When tested with the HD Mouse Brain Cortex dataset, SDDGP did not encounter any memory or overflow issues and ran stably (Table S5).

Despite its merits, our approach has several limitations that can be addressed in future work. Differences between the reference and spatial datasets (e.g., batch effects or missing cell types) may lead to inaccurate inverse convolution results. Integrating batch correction methods or allowing the discovery of new cell states could alleviate this limitation. Additionally, for scenarios without a reference, we have developed a tool called AGED (https://github.com/njjyxl/AGED) to address this issue.

Future research could also include extending this method to handle multimodal spatial data, such as joint analysis of transcriptomics and proteomics, or integrating histological image information to guide the deconvolution process. From an engineering perspective, given the algorithm’s parallel nature, numerous components could be ported to CUDA to achieve improved computational efficiency.

Conclusions

We have developed a deep learning-based deconvolution method with Bayesian reference for spatial transcriptomics data analysis, which combines negative binomial modeling, adaptive prior, deep Gaussian processes, and adaptive MCMC sampling. Our method demonstrates higher accuracy in estimating cell type proportions, particularly for rare cell types, and provides uncertainty quantification to account for spatial heterogeneity in greater detail.

This method can resolve the cell type composition at each spatial location, enabling detailed analysis of tissue structure and cell–cell interactions, with potential applications in cancer research, developmental biology, and neuroscience. By enhancing our understanding of the spatial organization of complex tissues, this approach contributes to the broader goal of elucidating the relationship between tissue structure and function in tissues and diseases.

Four panels summarize the sampling quality across all spots. (Top left) Distribution of split-chain R-hat values; the dashed red line marks the 1.05 threshold. Spot-level concordance between estimated and ground-truth cell-type proportions on the PDAC dataset

Each row corresponds to a representative cell type Acinar cells (top), Cancer clone A (middle), and Ductal centroacinar cells (bottom) and each column to a deconvolution method: SDDGP, Seurat, SPOTlight, Redeconve, CARD, and Cell2location. Scatter plots show the log-scale estimated proportion (y-axis) versus the ground-truth proportion (x-axis) for every spot; the red diagonal line indicates perfect agreement, and the Pearson correlation coefficient is annotated in each panel. The leftmost column displays the spatial distribution of the Getis-Ord Gi* hotspot statistic for each cell type, with darker shading indicating stronger local enrichment. SDDGP achieves the highest or near-highest correlation across all three cell types (r = 0.95, 0.963, and 0.951, respectively), with data points distributed tightly along the diagonal. In contrast, SPOTlight and Cell2location show wider scatter and lower correlations, particularly for Cancer clone A (r = 0.75 and 0.91) and Ductal centroacinar cells (r = 0.873 and 0.46), where off-diagonal dispersion is more pronounced.

Applicability of SDDGP on the MERFISH platform

To validate the applicability of SDDGP on the MERFISH platform, we applied this method, along with other deconvolution methods, to single-cell resolution MERFISH data from the medial prefrontal cortex of mice. We evaluated the results using real-world data by adjusting the Rand index (ARI) and purity metrics.
