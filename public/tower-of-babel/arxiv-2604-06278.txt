A Comparative Study of Penalised, Bayesian, Spatial, and Tree-Based Models for Provincial Poverty in Indonesia: Small Samples and High Collinearity
Ahmad Hakiim Jamaluddin, Andrea Tri Rian Dani, Nor Idayu Mahat, Vita Ratnasari, Shukor Sanim Mohd Fauzi

Abstract
Identifying the structural drivers of poverty in regional datasets is frequently hindered by small sample sizes and high multidimensional collinearity, which can result in unstable and misleading policy advice. This paper evaluates the provincial causes of poverty in Indonesia by addressing these specific statistical hazards. We employ a rigorous model-comparison framework designed for small samples ( n = 34 n=34 ) with high collinearity, comparing standard linear models with frequentist penalisation, Bayesian shrinkage priors, an adjusted spatial intrinsic conditionally autoregressive (ICAR) model, and complex machine learning ensembles. To ensure a robust evaluation, we measure predictive performance using strict Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV). The results demonstrate that algorithmic complexity is inherently risky in regional datasets: simple linear shrinkage models (Ridge, Elastic Net, LASSO) achieve the superior out-of-sample prediction, whereas complex ensembles like BART suffer from severe overfitting. Across all successful regularised models, ICT skills consistently emerge as the most stable proxy for lower provincial poverty. The primary contribution of this paper is demonstrating that, in data-constrained regional analysis, parametrically regularised linear shrinkage provides a more reliable mathematical foundation for isolating structural development priorities, such as ICT, than either naive OLS or unconstrained machine learning. Keywords: Statistical modelling; Regularisation; ICT skills; Indonesia; Poverty; Spatial diagnostics. JEL Classification: C11; C14; C21; I32; O15; R12



1
Introduction

Indonesia is the world’s fourth most populous country and remains one of South-East Asia’s most dynamic emerging economies. Nonetheless, its provincial development profile is notably uneven. Recent data from 2024 indicates that poverty rates range from 4.53% to 26.80%, whilst ICT skills vary from 29.82% to 92.36%. Whilst identifying the drivers of these disparities is essential for regional equity, the pursuit of “place-based policy” is often built on fragile empirical foundations. When sample sizes are small and predictors are deeply intertwined, standard statistical tools can produce “false positives” that misdirect public investment. These gaps matter not only for equity but for the practical design of policy, reflecting long-standing structural challenges in the Indonesian developmental trajectory
(
World Bank 2023
;
United Nations 2024
;
Suryahadi et al. 2012
)
.

Despite the importance of these trends, much of the existing literature on Indonesian development continues to rely on unpenalised regressions, often overlooking how collinearity and limited data points can distort results. Analysing these drivers presents three fundamental methodological difficulties that motivate this study. First, the sample is constrained: with only 34 provinces, parameter uncertainty is substantial, and complex models risk severe out-of-sample overfitting
(
Hastie et al. 2009
;
Varma and Simon 2006
)
. Second, the predictors are highly correlated; provinces with stronger ICT skills also tend to possess better sanitation and higher life expectancy. This multicollinearity threatens the stability of coefficient estimates and can lead to erroneous sign inversions, making it difficult for policymakers to discern which interventions actually reduce poverty
(
Belsley et al. 2005
;
Greene 2018
)
. Third, raw poverty rates are spatially clustered, meaning geographic proximity often serves as a proxy for unobserved regional dependencies
(
Anselin 1988
;
Cliff and Ord 1981
)
. These three issues, i.e., small samples, collinearity, and spatial bias, create a “perfect storm” for statistical unreliability.

We therefore adopt a structured model-comparison strategy to determine which framework offers the most reliable guidance for regional analysis. Rather than the indiscriminate application of complex algorithms to see what fits, a process that often leads to biased inferences in micro-samples
(
Wolpert 1996
)
, we systematically evaluate a portfolio of conceptual approaches: linear baselines, frequentist and Bayesian regularisation (shrinkage), spatial models, and tree-based ensembles (BART, Random Forest, XGBoost). Our objective is to establish a rigorous predictive framework for analysing small-sample regional data where the costs of model-misidentification are high.

Three methodological findings emerge. First, simple linear shrinkage proves to be the optimal predictive framework. Under strict Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV), Ridge regression, Elastic Net, and LASSO achieve the highest out-of-sample accuracy. Whilst models such as Random Forest and XGBoost offer moderate improvements over unpenalised OLS, highly unconstrained algorithms such as BART suffer from severe overfitting. Second, within these optimal shrinkage frameworks, ICT skills serve as the most stable negative structural proxy for provincial poverty, remaining robust even under Horseshoe shrinkage and spike-and-slab selection. Third, although raw poverty rates show spatial clustering, robust Monte Carlo residual diagnostics do not indicate remaining spatial dependence; geographic proximity acts largely as a proxy for shared socioeconomic covariates rather than a distinct spillover effect.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the conceptual background. Section 3 describes the data. Section 4 summarises the evaluation strategy. Section 5 presents the empirical findings. Section 6 discusses the predictive volatility of machine learning in micro-samples, and Section 7 concludes.

2
Theoretical Framework and Literature Review

2.1
Poverty as a multidimensional and spatially embedded phenomenon

Modern poverty analysis has moved beyond a purely income-based notion of deprivation. In the capability framework of
Sen 1999
, poverty reflects shortfalls in substantive freedoms, including education, health, and social participation.
Alkire and Foster 2011
formalise this multidimensional view by aggregating deprivations across domains. In Indonesia, these domains are unequally distributed across space, with marked provincial disparities in schooling, health access, infrastructure, and digital capability.

The spatial dimension remains conceptually important. Provinces do not develop in isolation. Geographic distance, connectivity, and regional spillovers may matter for market access, service delivery, and the diffusion of technology
(
Calderón and Servén 2010
;
Banerjee et al. 2014
)
. Yet a statistical distinction is needed between raw spatial clustering and residual spatial dependence after observed provincial characteristics are controlled for. The former may be strong while the latter is statistically invisible.

2.2
Human capital, health, inequality, infrastructure, and digital capability

Human capital theory predicts that schooling raises productivity and earnings capacity
(
Becker 1964
;
Psacharopoulos and Patrinos 2018
)
. Health matters both directly, through productivity, and indirectly, through vulnerability to shocks and treatment costs
(
Wagstaff 2002
;
Sparrow et al. 2013
)
. Inequality may weaken the poverty-reducing effect of growth when gains are unevenly shared
(
Bourguignon 2004
;
Yusuf et al. 2014
)
. Infrastructure, including sanitation, water, and electricity, affects health, time allocation, and labour-market participation
(
Calderón and Servén 2010
)
.

Digital capability has become increasingly central. ICT skills can improve job search, access to information, take-up of public services, and integration into formal markets
(
Aker and Mbiti 2010
;
Monde 2021
)
. But it is vital to recognise that the relationship between digital infrastructure and poverty is highly endogenous. Wealthier, more developed provinces naturally attract and sustain better digital infrastructure and foster more digitally literate populations. In a country as geographically fragmented as Indonesia, ICT acts primarily as an accurate composite proxy for a wider, unobserved bundle of provincial advantages, including institutional quality and economic integration, rather than functioning as an isolated causal lever.

2.3
Why a model portfolio is needed

The provincial poverty problem is not well suited to a single-model strategy. OLS is fragile under collinearity and small samples. Penalised estimators such as ridge, LASSO, and elastic net help to stabilise coefficient estimates
(
Hoerl and Kennard 1970
;
Tibshirani 1996
;
Zou and Hastie 2005
)
. Bayesian shrinkage priors allow regularisation while retaining full posterior uncertainty
(
Gelman et al. 2013
;
Carvalho et al. 2010
)
. Spike-and-slab selection provides posterior inclusion probabilities that are easy to interpret
(
Mitchell and Beauchamp 1988
;
George and McCulloch 1993
)
. Finally, nonparametric methods such as BART and tree ensembles are often deployed to detect threshold effects
(
Chipman et al. 2010
;
Breiman 2001
;
Chen and Guestrin 2016
)
, though their utility in micro-samples requires strict out-of-sample validation.

The advantage of a model portfolio is not that every specification must perfectly align, but rather that it systematically filters out specification-sensitive noise while explicitly controlling for overfitting.

2.4
Statistical regularisation and the bias-variance tradeoff

The primary challenge in provincial-level analysis is the unfavourable ratio of predictors to observations (
p/n
). In such settings, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimators are often unbiased but suffer from high variance, leading to poor out-of-sample generalisation. This instability is exacerbated by multicollinearity, where the inclusion of highly correlated predictors, such as ICT skills and sanitation, inflates the variance of the coefficient estimates
(
Belsley et al. 2005
)
.

To mitigate these risks, the statistical literature has increasingly moved towards
regularisation
. Frequentist penalisation techniques, such as Ridge regression
(
Hoerl and Kennard 1970
)
and LASSO
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
, introduce a small amount of bias into the model in exchange for a significant reduction in variance. Whilst Ridge regression shrinks coefficients towards zero to handle collinearity, LASSO performs simultaneous variable selection by forcing some coefficients to exactly zero. The Elastic Net
(
Zou and Hastie 2005
)
provides a compromise between these two, which is particularly useful when predictors are grouped in high-correlation blocks, as observed in our Indonesian dataset.

2.5
Bayesian shrinkage and the search for stable proxies

An alternative to frequentist penalisation is the use of Bayesian shrinkage priors. Unlike hard-thresholding methods, Bayesian frameworks allow for the incorporation of prior information regarding the sparsity of the data
(
Gelman et al. 2013
)
. The Horseshoe prior
(
Carvalho et al. 2010
)
and Spike-and-Slab selection
(
Mitchell and Beauchamp 1988
;
George and McCulloch 1993
)
have become the gold standard for high-dimensional inference. These methods are designed to identify “signals” (stable structural drivers) amongst “noise” (spurious correlations), providing posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) that offer a more nuanced measure of variable importance than traditional
p
-values.

2.6
The limitations of algorithmic complexity in micro-samples

In recent years, tree-based machine learning ensembles such as Random Forest
(
Breiman 2001
)
, XGBoost
(
Chen and Guestrin 2016
)
, and Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART)
(
Chipman et al. 2010
)
, have gained popularity due to their ability to capture non-linearities and complex interactions without explicit functional specifications. However, their performance in “micro-samples” (
n<50
) remains a subject of debate.

Whilst these algorithms are powerful for large-scale informatics, they are prone to “learning the noise” in small cross-sectional data. Consequently, their inclusion in our model portfolio is not to seek a superior fit, but to test the limits of algorithmic complexity. By comparing these “black-box” models against regularised linear frameworks using strict Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV), we can determine whether the underlying structure of provincial poverty is truly non-linear, or if complexity simply introduces predictive volatility
(
Wolpert 1996
;
Vehtari et al. 2017
)
.

2.7
Spatial dependency: process vs. proxy

Finally, the inclusion of spatial modelling, specifically the BYM2 ICAR specification
(
Riebler et al. 2016
)
, addresses the potential for geographic spillover. A common pitfall in regional science is assuming that spatial clustering in the outcome variable necessitates a spatial model. However, if the spatial pattern is entirely explained by the distribution of observed covariates, the overhead of a spatial random effect becomes redundant
(
Anselin 1995
)
. Our blueprint explicitly tests this by comparing the predictive accuracy of spatial versus non-spatial models to ensure the most parsimonious interpretation of the Indonesian poverty landscape.

3.5
Spatial autocorrelation and the case for parsimony

Raw poverty rates exhibit significant positive spatial autocorrelation across the Indonesian archipelago. As illustrated in the choropleth visualisation (Figure
2
), there is a pronounced longitudinal poverty gradient, with higher intensities concentrated in the eastern provinces, specifically within the Nusa Tenggara and Papua clusters.

Given the small sample size (
n=34
), we eschew standard asymptotic assumptions and calculate empirical
p
-values for the statistic originally proposed by
Moran 1950
via 999 Monte Carlo permutations. The resulting empirical Moran’s
I
is
0.358
(
p=0.009
), providing formal evidence of the geographic clustering observed in the map.

Nonetheless, the conditional picture is markedly different. Once the nine socioeconomic covariates are included in an OLS framework, the residual Moran’s
I
falls to
-0.058
(
p=0.566
). This sharp distinction between raw clustering and residual independence strongly implies that geographic proximity acts primarily as a surrogate for underlying socioeconomic fundamentals, such as shared infrastructure or educational levels, rather than representing a distinct spatial spillover process
(
Anselin 1995
)
. Consequently, whilst we include the BYM2 spatial model (M11) in our portfolio for completeness, this exploratory evidence suggests that the spatial random effect may be statistically redundant.

4
Statistical Models and Methods

A unified notation is adopted throughout. Let
Y_{i}
denote the poverty rate for province
i
(
i=1,\ldots,34
), and let
\bm{x}_{i}=(1,X_{i1},\ldots,X_{i9})^{\top}
denote the covariate vector including an intercept.

To ensure stable computation, standard frequentist penalised models (ridge, LASSO, elastic net) are estimated using
standardised
predictors. Consequently, their raw coefficients represent standard-deviation changes. Conversely, the Bayesian models are fitted using the
original, unstandardised
scales to preserve direct interpretability. In Section 5, all frequentist coefficients are mathematically re-scaled back to their original units prior to reporting to guarantee a perfectly coherent “apples-to-apples” comparison with the Bayesian posteriors.

4.1
Frequentist benchmark models

M1: Ordinary least squares

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}=\argmin_{\bm{\beta}}\left\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\bm{\beta}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(1)

M2: Ridge regression

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{Ridge}}=\argmin_{\bm{\beta}}\left\{\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}_{-0}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(2)

M3: LASSO

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{LASSO}}=\argmin_{\bm{\beta}}\left\{\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}_{-0}\|_{1}\right\}.

(3)

M4: Elastic net

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathrm{EN}}=\argmin_{\bm{\beta}}\left\{\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left[\alpha\|\bm{\beta}_{-0}\|_{1}+(1-\alpha)\|\bm{\beta}_{-0}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right\},

(4)

with
\alpha=0.5
.

All three penalised models are estimated using the
glmnet
package
(
Friedman et al. 2010
)
in
R

(
R Core Team 2023
)
, using
n_{\mathrm{folds}}=34
in
cv.glmnet
, representing a strict Leave-One-Out validation.

4.2
Bayesian linear regression with weakly informative Gaussian priors (M5)

The sampling model is

Y_{i}\,|\,\bm{\beta},\sigma^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta},\sigma^{2}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,34.

(5)

The prior specification in the custom Gibbs sampler is

\bm{\beta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0}),\qquad\bm{\Sigma}_{0}=\mathrm{diag}(100,25,\ldots,25),

(6)

and

\sigma^{2}\sim\mathrm{Inverse\text{-}Gamma}(a_{0},b_{0}),\qquad a_{0}=3,\quad b_{0}=2.

(7)

Posterior computation uses a custom Gibbs sampler with 20,000 iterations, 10,000 burn-in, and thinning by 5. A separate
brms
implementation of the same broad model class is used for PSIS-LOO prediction and information criteria.

4.3
Bayesian ridge (M6), Bayesian LASSO (M7), and horseshoe shrinkage (M8)

M6, M7, and M8 are estimated in
brms
. M6 uses a Student-
t(1,0,1)
prior on the slopes, M7 uses a double-exponential prior following the Bayesian LASSO framework
(
Park and Casella 2008
)
, and M8 uses the horseshoe prior with global–local shrinkage. The horseshoe model is fitted with four chains, 4,000 iterations per chain, 2,000 warm-up,
adapt_delta
=0.99, and
max_treedepth
=15. Convergence diagnostics for the horseshoe model are reported in Appendix C.

4.4
Spike-and-slab stochastic search variable selection (M9)

The spike-and-slab model uses binary inclusion indicators and is estimated with a custom Gibbs sampler. The primary inferential summaries are posterior inclusion probabilities (PIP).

4.5
Bayesian Beta Regression (M10)

Because poverty is a bounded proportion, the outcome is rescaled to
\tilde{Y}_{i}=Y_{i}/100
and modelled as a Beta distribution
(
Ferrari and Cribari-Neto 2004
)
:

\tilde{Y}_{i}\,|\,\mu_{i},\phi\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\mu_{i}\phi,(1-\mu_{i})\phi).

(8)

While its log-likelihood-based information criteria are on a different mathematical scale, its back-transformed predictive errors are preserved for direct comparison.

4.6
Bayesian spatial ICAR model (M11)

The spatial model follows the BYM2 specification
(
Besag et al. 1991
;
Riebler et al. 2016
)
, which decomposes the spatial random effect into an intrinsic conditionally autoregressive (ICAR) component and an unstructured exchangeable component. The model is formulated as:

Y_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}+u_{i}+\varepsilon_{i},\qquad\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}),

(9)

where
u_{i}
represents the structured spatial effect. This model was implemented using the
brms
package, with the underlying spatial weight matrix processed via standard spatial utility functions in
R

(
Bivand et al. 2013
)
.

Given the archipelagic geography of Indonesia, standard Euclidean
k
-nearest neighbours are mathematically unsound as they fail to account for maritime boundaries. Consequently, the spatial weight matrix was explicitly constructed using adjusted topological contiguity rules (
k=2
minimal routing) to reflect real ferry and transit borders between adjacent island chains. This customisation, performed via
spdep
utilities, ensures the adjacency graph remains fully connected to satisfy the mathematical assumptions of the BYM2 framework.

4.7
Tree-Based Non-Linear Architectures (M12, M14, M15)

We evaluate three tree-based algorithms: Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART, M12), implemented via the
BART
package
(
Sparapani et al. 2021
)
; Random Forest (M14), using the
randomForest
package
(
Liaw and Wiener 2002
)
; and XGBoost (M15). These models are heavily utilised in literature to capture vital non-linear structural thresholds, but their utility in micro-sample cross-sections requires strict out-of-sample validation to detect overfitting.

4.8
Gaussian Process Regression (M13)

Gaussian Process regression
(
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
)
is included as a flexible nonparametric alternative. We use a squared exponential kernel with automatic relevance determination (ARD) to allow different length scales per predictor. The model is fitted with a zero mean function and a constant noise term. Hyperparameters are estimated by maximising the marginal likelihood. Strict 34-fold LOOCV is applied for comparability.

4.9
Model comparison criteria

The primary model comparison criterion is the leave-one-out root mean squared error,

\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{LOO}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}^{(-i)}\right)^{2}},

where
\hat{y}_{i}^{(-i)}
denotes the prediction for observation
i
obtained from a model fitted without using that observation. We adopt LOOCV-RMSE as the principal criterion because the response is continuous, all competing methods ultimately produce point predictions for the same provincial poverty rate, and RMSE summarises prediction error on the original percentage-point scale. This makes the comparison directly interpretable and fully aligned across heterogeneous model classes, including Gaussian, beta, spatial, Bayesian, and machine-learning specifications, without relying on likelihood-based scores that are less directly comparable across different model families or link functions.

LOOCV is particularly appropriate in the present setting because the sample is very small (
n=34
). Holding out more than one observation per split would further reduce an already limited training sample, whereas LOOCV preserves
n-1=33
observations for model fitting in every iteration while still delivering a strictly out-of-sample prediction for each province. We recognise that cross-validation estimates in small samples can themselves be noisy; however, for a dataset of this size, LOOCV offers the most data-efficient and split-invariant assessment of predictive performance, provided that all preprocessing, tuning, and estimation steps are repeated within each training fold rather than performed once on the full dataset
(
Varma and Simon 2006
;
Neunhoeffer and Sternberg 2019
;
Vabalas et al. 2019
;
Varoquaux 2018
)
.

A further reason for privileging LOOCV-RMSE over WAIC as the main comparison metric is that our objective is predictive accuracy under a common loss function, not merely relative fit within a particular likelihood formulation. For Bayesian models fitted in
brms
, we therefore use PSIS-LOO
(
Vehtari et al. 2017
)
rather than WAIC as the default approximation to exact leave-one-out assessment. Although WAIC and Bayesian cross-validation are asymptotically equivalent in singular learning settings
(
Watanabe 2010
)
, PSIS-LOO has better finite-sample robustness and, crucially, provides Pareto-
k
diagnostics that identify influential observations for which the approximation may fail
(
Vehtari et al. 2017
)
. Whenever Pareto
k>0.7
is detected, we replace the approximation with exact leave-one-out re-fitting for the affected observations.

This diagnostic-refit strategy ensures that the Bayesian models are evaluated under the same out-of-sample logic as the frequentist and machine-learning models. Ridge, LASSO, elastic net, random forest, XGBoost, Gaussian process regression, and BART are all assessed through explicit 34-fold iterative refitting, with tuning carried out inside the training portion of each fold. Hence, every model is compared using the same target quantity: the average squared prediction error for unseen provinces.

One qualification is required for the spatial ICAR model (M11). In that model, removing one province under exact LOOCV changes the neighbourhood graph itself, so exact leave-one-out no longer preserves the original spatial structure. Accordingly, the reported
\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{LOO}}
for M11 relies on the PSIS approximation alone and should be interpreted more cautiously than the corresponding values for the non-spatial models. Even with this caveat, using LOOCV-RMSE as the common benchmark remains preferable to mixing information criteria, in-sample fit indices, or model-specific diagnostics across competing classes.

6
Discussion

Across the full model portfolio, one substantive conclusion is the most stable: ICT skills emerge as the strongest negative structural correlate of provincial poverty in Indonesia. This result is broadly consistent with the wider development literature, which increasingly argues that digital capability can support poverty reduction by improving access to information, labour-market matching, market participation, education, and public services
(
Aker and Mbiti 2010
;
Galperin and Viecens 2017
;
Lechman and Popowska 2022
)
. It also complements the Indonesian evidence in
Monde 2021
, who shows that digital disadvantage and poverty are spatially intertwined. However, our results add an important qualification to that literature. In large cross-country or panel settings, ICT is often interpreted as one channel through which growth and inclusion improve. In our much harsher provincial small-
n
setting, ICT survives not merely as one correlate among many, but as the only predictor that remains consistently stable after aggressive regularisation. This suggests that ICT skills should be interpreted less as a narrowly isolated treatment variable and more as a summary proxy for a wider bundle of provincial advantages, including institutional quality, educational readiness, service accessibility, and economic integration.

This distinction matters because several development indicators that are theoretically important in the literature, such as schooling, sanitation, electricity, and life expectancy, do not display the same degree of stand-alone statistical stability once entered jointly. That does not imply that these variables are unimportant in substantive reality. Rather, it indicates that in provincial data they move together so strongly that naive regression coefficients cannot be trusted to cleanly separate their marginal contributions. The sign reversals between OLS and shrinkage models illustrate exactly the instability warned about in the collinearity literature
(
Belsley et al. 2005
;
Zou and Hastie 2005
;
Carvalho et al. 2010
)
. In this sense, our findings refine rather than contradict the broader poverty literature: the standard developmental fundamentals remain relevant, but their individual coefficients become fragile in small, highly collinear regional samples. The methodological implication is that empirical poverty studies at the provincial level should be far more cautious about ranking determinants from unpenalised regression output alone.

A second contribution concerns the interpretation of spatial structure. Many studies of regional poverty, including Indonesian applications, begin from the observation that poverty is geographically clustered and then proceed directly to spatial specifications. Our results support only the first half of that logic. Raw provincial poverty is indeed spatially autocorrelated, but once the socioeconomic covariates are included, the residual dependence vanishes under exact Monte Carlo testing. This pattern is consistent with the distinction in spatial econometrics between spatial process and spatial proxy: geography may matter because neighbouring places share observable fundamentals, without necessarily implying an additional residual spillover process after those fundamentals are controlled for
(
Anselin 1995
)
. Relative to empirical studies that interpret clustering itself as evidence of a remaining spatial mechanism, our findings suggest a more parsimonious conclusion for the Indonesian provincial case: at this level of aggregation, much of the apparent geography of poverty is compositional. This does not rule out genuine spillovers at finer administrative levels, but it does show that province-level spatial random effects are not automatically warranted.

The third and most important methodological lesson concerns algorithmic complexity in micro-samples. The machine-learning validation literature has repeatedly warned that when samples are limited, predictive performance estimates become volatile and easily overstated, especially when model tuning and evaluation are not cleanly separated
(
Vabalas et al. 2019
;
Varoquaux 2018
;
Neunhoeffer and Sternberg 2019
)
. Our empirical results are highly consistent with that warning. Under strict LOOCV, BART performs extremely poorly out-of-sample, and Gaussian Process regression also fails to generalise well. Random Forest and XGBoost improve modestly on OLS, but neither surpasses the best linear shrinkage models. Hence, the present application does not support the common presumption that more flexible algorithms necessarily uncover deeper structure in social data. On the contrary, the evidence indicates that in an
n=34
regional cross-section, additional flexibility primarily increases variance.

This point also affects how the tree-based variable-importance profiles should be interpreted. The comparatively strong Random Forest importance assigned to ICT skills is substantively plausible, but it must be treated cautiously because the random-forest literature shows that standard importance measures are biased in the presence of correlated predictors
(
Strobl et al. 2007
;
Strobl et al. 2008
;
Gregorutti et al. 2017
)
. In other words, the machine-learning outputs do not overturn the shrinkage-based conclusion; if anything, they reinforce the need for caution when covariates come in tightly correlated blocks. The relative flatness of the BART importance profile is therefore also informative: it suggests that once regularisation is imposed through many weak learners, the model diffuses importance across a bundle of related provincial characteristics rather than identifying many sharply distinct non-linear thresholds.

Taken together, these comparisons place our findings in a more precise position within the literature. Substantively, the results align with prior work that identifies digital capability as a relevant poverty correlate, but they sharpen the claim by showing that ICT is the most stable predictor once small-sample uncertainty and multicollinearity are properly confronted. Methodologically, the results support the growing argument that careful regularisation and strict out-of-sample validation are more informative than indiscriminate algorithmic complexity in small regional datasets. For applied regional science, this is a consequential point: when the number of observational units is fixed and low, better inference may come from disciplined shrinkage and conservative validation rather than from increasingly flexible black-box architectures.

Because the analysis remains cross-sectional, these findings should not be interpreted causally. Reverse causality and omitted-variable bias remain serious concerns: richer provinces are better positioned to invest in digital capability, infrastructure, and service provision, whilst those same underlying conditions also reduce poverty. We therefore do not claim that increasing ICT skills alone would mechanically produce the full poverty reductions implied by the fitted coefficients. Rather, the policy interpretation is that provinces with stronger ICT capability also tend to possess a broader development package associated with lower poverty. A realistic policy implication is thus not “ICT alone”, but integrated regional upgrading in which digital inclusion is pursued alongside education, health access, and basic service quality.

Several limitations remain. First, with only 34 provinces and nine predictors, uncertainty is inevitably large, even after shrinkage. Second, province-level analysis may smooth over important within-province heterogeneity and more localised spatial spillovers. Third, whilst LOOCV is appropriate here, the small-sample validation literature reminds us that all error estimates in limited datasets retain non-trivial uncertainty
(
Vabalas et al. 2019
;
Varoquaux 2018
)
. These limitations do not overturn the main conclusion, but they do reinforce the need for modest inferential claims and for future work using longer panels or finer geographic units.

7
Conclusion

This paper has presented a comparative analysis of provincial poverty in Indonesia using a model portfolio explicitly designed for a difficult empirical setting: small sample size, strong predictor collinearity, and potential spatial dependence. Evaluated under strict Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation, three conclusions emerge.

First, ICT skills are the most robust provincial correlate of lower poverty. This result is consistent with a growing literature that links digital capability to poverty reduction through information access, labour-market inclusion, education, and service connectivity
(
Aker and Mbiti 2010
;
Galperin and Viecens 2017
;
Lechman and Popowska 2022
)
. However, our contribution is more specific. In contrast to broader macro-level studies, we show that in a high-collinearity provincial setting ICT is the only predictor that remains clearly stable across frequentist shrinkage, Bayesian shrinkage, and spike-and-slab selection. The most credible interpretation is therefore that ICT skills capture a wider package of structural development advantages rather than a clean, isolated causal mechanism.

Second, spatial modelling adds little once provincial socioeconomic composition is controlled for. Although raw poverty rates are geographically clustered, residual spatial dependence disappears after covariate adjustment, and the BYM2 ICAR specification does not improve predictive performance. For this Indonesian provincial application, the evidence therefore favours a parsimonious reading in which geography primarily proxies observed development disparities rather than an additional residual spillover process.

Third, and most importantly for applied statistical practice, highly flexible machine-learning models are not automatically superior in small regional datasets. Our results closely match the recent validation literature showing that limited samples make predictive assessment unstable and leave complex models especially vulnerable to overfitting
(
Vabalas et al. 2019
;
Varoquaux 2018
;
Neunhoeffer and Sternberg 2019
)
. In this application, predictive dominance belongs to simple linear shrinkage estimators—Ridge, Elastic Net, and LASSO—not to BART or other unconstrained non-linear architectures. Thus, the main methodological contribution of the paper is to demonstrate that, in small-
n
regional poverty analysis, disciplined regularisation and strict cross-validation provide a more reliable inferential foundation than either naive OLS or complexity-heavy machine learning.

More broadly, the paper offers a practical blueprint for empirical work in regional science when the data environment is constrained: regularise aggressively when predictors are structurally bundled, validate out-of-sample with procedures that penalise overfitting, distinguish raw spatial clustering from residual spatial dependence, and frame substantive claims conservatively when only cross-sectional evidence is available. For Indonesian poverty policy, the message is correspondingly cautious but actionable: digital capability appears central, yet it should be advanced as part of a broader place-based development strategy rather than as a stand-alone intervention.
