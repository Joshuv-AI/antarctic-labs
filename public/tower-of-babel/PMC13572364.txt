Bidirectional cross-modal fusion with tensor interaction for drug-target binding prediction
Authors: Xiaoxuan Liu, Deshinta Arrova Dewi, Shuangwen Zhao, Jingwen Fei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of drug–target binding affinity is central to computational drug discovery, yet it remains difficult because binding is governed by complex, non-linear interactions between chemical substructures and protein residue environments. Many existing deep learning approaches learn drug and protein representations largely in isolation and combine them only at the final stage, which can weaken their ability to capture informative cross-modal dependencies. To address this limitation, we propose Bidirectional Cross-Modal Fusion with Tensor Interaction (BiT-Fusion), a framework that strengthens interaction modeling through bidirectional fusion and multiplicative coupling between drug and protein features. BiT-Fusion enables more effective information exchange across modalities while preserving complementary signals from molecular graphs and protein sequences. Experiments on the widely used Davis and KIBA benchmarks show that BiT-Fusion delivers competitive and consistent improvements across multiple evaluation metrics. Ablation analyses further verify that bidirectional fusion and tensor-based interaction are the main contributors to the performance gains. Overall, these results suggest that enhancing cross-modal interaction learning is a practical and interpretable direction for improving drug–target binding prediction, with potential relevance to AI-enabled drug discovery, precision medicine, and the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 3 on good health and well-being.Keywords: Drug–target binding affinity prediction, Deep learning, Cross-modal fusion, Tensor interaction, Computational drug discovery, Precision medicine, Sustainable drug discovery, SDG 3, Good health and well-beingSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Reliable estimation of drug–target binding is a cornerstone of computational drug discovery, because it supports target identification, lead optimization, and drug repurposing by prioritizing candidate interactions before expensive wet-lab assays. Although experimental technologies have advanced, exhaustive measurement of affinities across the space of small molecules and proteins remains infeasible in routine pipelines. This practical gap has motivated continuous progress in data-driven drug–target affinity (DTA) prediction, where models learn to infer binding strength from historical measurements and widely available molecular representations.

The development of DTA methods has been tightly coupled with the availability of curated benchmark datasets. The Davis benchmark reports dissociation constants for kinase inhibitors and kinases, enabling controlled evaluation for regression and ranking metrics1,2. The KIBA benchmark integrates heterogeneous kinase bioactivity measurements into a unified score to support large-scale learning3. Beyond these canonical benchmarks, BindingDB provides a broad collection of experimentally measured binding affinities across targets and ligands4,5, while PDBbind offers affinity labels aligned with experimentally solved protein–ligand complex structures6,7. These resources have encouraged systematic comparison across modeling paradigms and have highlighted persistent challenges such as generalization to novel drugs, novel targets, and sparse interaction regimes.

Earlier DTA models typically relied on handcrafted descriptors and similarity-driven learning. KronRLS combines drug and target kernels using Kronecker-structured regularized least squares, which can be strong when similarity information is informative and well-calibrated8,9. SimBoost constructs feature representations from similarity networks and affinity patterns, and then applies gradient boosting for regression10. These methods remain useful baselines, but they require careful feature design and often have limited ability to represent nonlinear interaction mechanisms between chemical substructures and residue contexts.

Deep learning introduced end-to-end representation learning for DTA, reducing dependence on engineered features. DeepDTA encodes drug SMILES strings and protein sequences using one-dimensional convolutional networks and predicts affinity via multilayer perceptrons11–13. WideDTA extends the input space with additional sequence-derived patterns to enrich the learned representations14. DeepAffinity further improves interpretability by connecting learned representations to binding-site related signals and by leveraging sequence modeling components that can capture contextual dependencies15. Despite these successes, SMILES-based compound encoders may not fully exploit the structural inductive bias of molecular graphs, and many architectures still treat drug and target as largely independent feature extractors whose outputs are merged at the end.

Motivated by the graph nature of small molecules, graph neural networks have become standard for drug representation learning. GraphDTA demonstrated that replacing SMILES encoders with graph-based encoders yields consistent improvements on Davis and KIBA, using backbones such as GCN, GAT, and GIN16. This line of work builds on general message passing formulations17 and widely used GNN variants including graph convolution18, graph attention19, and expressive graph isomorphism networks20. In parallel, transformer-style architectures have shown strong capability for modeling long-range dependencies and contextual interactions21. MolTrans applies transformer-based interaction modeling for drug–target prediction and reports competitive performance across benchmark datasets22. Recent models have also emphasized interpretability and local interaction learning. DrugBAN integrates bilinear attention with domain adaptation and provides interaction maps that facilitate residue-level analysis23. GEFA explores early fusion in which drug information is injected into target-side representation learning to better reflect binding-conditioned feature changes24.

Although current approaches have improved predictive accuracy, two gaps are still prominent in practical DTA settings. The first gap concerns interaction modeling symmetry. Many pipelines implement late fusion through concatenation or adopt interaction modules that are effectively unidirectional, which may bias the learned binding mechanism toward one modality and compress the cross-modal dependency into a shallow operator. In realistic discovery scenarios, both modalities can be limiting factors, including settings with novel compounds against known proteins, known compounds against novel proteins, and fully novel drug–target pairs. A representation that allows reciprocal conditioning is therefore desirable. The second gap concerns interaction expressiveness. A large portion of fusion mechanisms rely on additive attention or low-order bilinear matching, which may be insufficient to capture higher-order compositional effects that arise from cooperative residue groups and multiple chemical motifs. A direct tensor outer-product is expressive but computationally prohibitive, which motivates structured tensor fusion or compact bilinear approximations that preserve higher-order signals with tractable parameterization25,26.

To address these challenges, we propose Bidirectional Cross-Modal Fusion with Tensor Interaction for Drug–Target Binding Prediction (BiT-Fusion). BiT-Fusion is designed to improve both cross-modal conditioning and interaction capacity while remaining compatible with standard DTA inputs. The overall framework is illustrated in Fig. 1. The model encodes compounds as molecular graphs using a message passing graph encoder and encodes protein targets from primary sequences using a sequence encoder. A bidirectional cross-attention module is then used to update drug and protein features through reciprocal conditioning, so that each modality is refined using information from the other rather than being fused only at the end. On top of these context-enhanced features, a tensor interaction module captures higher-order cross-modal correlations in a structured form, aiming to represent complex binding patterns without the cost of naive tensor expansion. In addition, the prediction stage adopts a multi-sample dropout head to stabilize learning and to approximate an ensemble-like effect27, and we incorporate training objectives that emphasize both accurate regression and robust ranking when appropriate28,29.

Overall architecture of the proposed BiT-Fusion framework for drug–target binding prediction.

We evaluate BiT-Fusion on representative benchmarks including Davis1 and KIBA3, and compare against baselines spanning kernel methods8, boosting-based models10, CNN-based frameworks11,14, graph-based predictors16, and attention-driven interaction models22–24. The results indicate that explicit bidirectional cross-modal fusion combined with structured tensor interaction yields consistent gains in affinity prediction accuracy and ranking quality, while maintaining a clear interaction pathway that can be inspected through learned attention patterns.

Related work

This section reviews prior studies that are most relevant to BiT-Fusion. We focus on drug–target affinity prediction, modality-specific representation learning, and interaction-centric fusion mechanisms.

Drug–target affinity prediction

Computational prediction of drug–target binding affinity has long been studied with both classical machine learning and deep learning. Early work often relied on similarity information derived from drugs and targets and used kernel or boosting-based regressors. KronRLS formulates drug–target prediction as a regularized least squares problem over Kronecker-structured kernels, and it provides a strong baseline when similarity kernels are well-defined8. SimBoost constructs pairwise features based on similarity networks and known affinities, and then applies gradient boosting for affinity regression10. These approaches can be effective in settings where carefully engineered descriptors and similarity measures are available, but they typically do not learn fine-grained interaction patterns directly from raw molecular and sequence inputs.

Deep learning has shifted the field toward end-to-end representation learning from standard DTA modalities. DeepDTA uses one-dimensional convolutional networks over SMILES strings and protein sequences, followed by a multilayer perceptron for regression11. WideDTA extends this pipeline with additional sequence-derived patterns to enrich input signals14. DeepAffinity introduces a unified neural representation for compounds and proteins and emphasizes interpretability by connecting learned representations to binding-related evidence in the data15. While these methods reduce dependence on handcrafted features, many of them learn drug and target representations independently and fuse them only at later stages, which may limit the capacity to capture structured cross-modal dependencies.

Representation learning for drugs and proteins

A key design decision in DTA modeling is the choice of representation for each modality. For drug molecules, graph-based encoders have become a standard choice because molecular graphs provide an explicit inductive bias that reflects chemical connectivity. GraphDTA demonstrates that replacing SMILES encoders with graph neural networks improves prediction on Davis and KIBA, and it evaluates several GNN backbones such as graph convolution, graph attention, and graph isomorphism networks16,30. These architectures are rooted in the broader message passing paradigm for molecular property prediction17,31 and commonly used variants such as GCN18, GAT19, and GIN20.

For protein targets, sequence encoders remain widely used because primary sequences are readily available even when structural data is limited. Convolutional encoders, recurrent models, and Transformer-style encoders have all been applied to capture local motifs and long-range dependencies. Transformers have become a general mechanism for contextual modeling in multiple domains21, and DTA-oriented studies have adopted Transformer components to strengthen interaction modeling under the sequence-only regime. MolTrans employs a Transformer-based interaction mechanism for drug–target prediction and reports competitive performance on standard benchmarks22. In practice, the choice of protein encoder reflects a trade-off between capturing long-range context and maintaining computational efficiency when training on large benchmark datasets.

Interaction modeling and fusion mechanisms

Beyond modality-specific encoders, the central challenge in DTA lies in modeling how drug substructures interact with residue-level target contexts. A common design in earlier deep learning models is late fusion, where drug and protein embeddings are concatenated and passed to an MLP11,16,32. Late fusion is simple and scalable, but it can compress the interaction space into a shallow operator that does not explicitly align substructures to residues.

To address this limitation, recent work has explored interaction-centric fusion using attention or early fusion. GEFA proposes an early fusion approach that injects drug information into target-side representation learning, aiming to model binding-conditioned feature changes and improve affinity prediction24,33–35. DrugBAN uses bilinear attention to emphasize local interaction learning and adds domain adaptation to improve robustness under distribution shift, while also providing interpretable interaction maps23,36. MolTrans similarly uses attention-based interaction modeling within a Transformer framework22,37,38. These efforts highlight that learning explicit cross-modal interactions can improve performance and interpretability. However, many interaction modules remain effectively asymmetric, or they rely on low-order fusion operators that may not fully capture higher-order compositional effects.

Higher-order fusion has been studied extensively in multimodal learning, where tensor-based formulations preserve cross-modal feature products while compact approximations control complexity. Tensor Fusion Network shows that structured tensor fusion can capture rich cross-modal correlations25. Multimodal Compact Bilinear Pooling provides an efficient approximation to bilinear pooling that retains second-order statistics with manageable computation26. These ideas motivate the tensor interaction component of BiT-Fusion, which aims to enrich cross-modal coupling beyond additive attention or simple bilinear scoring, while keeping the model tractable for DTA benchmarks.

Multi-scale, multi-view, and transformer-based DTI modeling

Recent DTI and DTA studies have increasingly emphasized multi-scale feature extraction, multi-view representation learning, and Transformer-based interaction modeling. DeepFusion introduced a multi-scale feature fusion strategy to combine drug and protein similarity information with local substructure features for DTI prediction39,40. AttentionDTA incorporated attention mechanisms into binding affinity prediction to improve the modeling of drug–target relevance41, while TransformerCPI adopted self-attention to improve sequence-based compound–protein interaction prediction and further examined label reversal settings42. MGraphDTA explored multiscale graph neural networks to capture both local and global molecular structures for explainable affinity prediction43. BACPI developed a bi-directional attention neural network for compound–protein interaction and binding affinity prediction44, and mutual interaction neural networks further highlighted the importance of explicitly modeling drug–target interaction information45. Multi-TransDTI used simple universal dictionaries and a multi-view Transformer strategy to represent drugs and targets46. DeepPurpose provided a flexible deep learning library for DTI prediction and facilitated systematic comparison across encoders and model architectures47. More recently, DTIAM unified the prediction of drug–target interactions, binding affinities, and drug mechanisms within a single framework48, and MDF-DTA proposed a multi-dimensional fusion approach for drug–target binding affinity prediction49. Application-oriented studies have also explored deep learning for target/pathway-specific drug repositioning, such as repositioning traditional Chinese medicine molecules toward PI3K pathway proteins50.

Compared with these studies, BiT-Fusion focuses specifically on bidirectional cross-modal conditioning between molecular graph features and protein sequence features, followed by a compact tensor-inspired multiplicative interaction block. Thus, the proposed framework is most closely related to interaction-aware and fusion-based DTA methods, but differs by jointly emphasizing reciprocal feature refinement and compact higher-order coupling.

Interaction-aware structure-based drug design

In parallel with sequence- and graph-based DTI prediction, interaction-aware structure-based drug design has become an active direction for molecular generation and optimization. Recent reinforcement-learning-guided molecular diffusion models have attempted to steer semi-flexible molecular generation toward protein pockets by optimizing docking-related and interaction-oriented objectives51,52. Similarly, deep reinforcement learning has been used as an interaction agent to guide fragment-based three-dimensional molecular generation for protein pockets53,54. These structure-based studies explicitly exploit pocket–ligand spatial interactions and are complementary to DTA prediction models such as BiT-Fusion. While BiT-Fusion does not require experimentally resolved complex structures, future extensions could incorporate structure-aware priors or pocket-level annotations when reliable structural information is available.

Robust training and uncertainty-oriented design

Robustness is another practical concern in DTA prediction because benchmark datasets are noisy and exhibit distribution shifts across protein families and chemical scaffolds. Regularization and ensembling strategies are commonly used to improve stability. Dropout has been interpreted as an approximate Bayesian inference mechanism that can provide uncertainty-aware behavior and act as a regularizer27. R-Drop proposes a regularization approach that enforces consistency between stochastic forward passes under dropout, improving generalization in neural models29. Ranking-oriented objectives have also been used in scenarios where relative ordering is important, with RankNet providing a classical formulation of learning to rank with gradient-based optimization28. These directions motivate the robust prediction head and training objective design in BiT-Fusion, where we integrate multi-sample dropout behavior with loss terms that promote stable predictions and improved ranking quality.

In summary, existing DTA methods have progressed from similarity-based learning to deep representation learning and attention-driven interaction modeling. BiT-Fusion builds on this trajectory by combining bidirectional cross-modal conditioning with a tensor interaction module to capture higher-order dependencies, while incorporating robust training components to improve stability across benchmark evaluations.

Experiments

This section evaluates the proposed Bidirectional Cross-Modal Fusion with Tensor Interaction (BiT-Fusion) framework on two widely used drug–target affinity (DTA) benchmarks, namely Davis and KIBA. The experiments are designed to answer three questions: whether BiT-Fusion improves affinity prediction over representative classical and deep learning baselines, whether the improvement is consistent across different evaluation metrics and datasets, and which model components contribute most to the final performance. To provide a comprehensive evaluation, we report regression accuracy, ranking consistency, goodness-of-fit behavior, multi-perspective method comparison, and component-level ablation results.

Experimental setup

Datasets

We conduct experiments on the Davis and KIBA benchmarks, which are commonly used for evaluating drug–target binding affinity prediction models. The Davis dataset contains experimentally measured binding affinities between kinase inhibitors and kinase targets, and has been widely adopted for controlled evaluation of DTA regression models1. The KIBA dataset integrates heterogeneous bioactivity measurements into a unified KIBA score, providing a larger and more diverse benchmark for assessing the generalization ability of affinity prediction models3. Following common practice in prior studies, we use the standard benchmark splits and report the results under a five-fold evaluation protocol. The reported metrics are summarized using the mean and standard deviation over repeated folds when applicable.

Input construction and preprocessing

For each drug molecule, the SMILES string is converted into a molecular graph using RDKit. Atoms are treated as graph nodes, and chemical bonds are treated as graph edges. To preserve bond-level chemical information, we construct a weighted adjacency matrix with bond-type-aware weights, where single, double, triple, and aromatic bonds are assigned different weights. Self-loops are added to retain each atom’s own representation during graph message passing. Atom features include element type, atom degree, hybridization state, aromaticity, hydrogen count, ring membership, and charge-related descriptors. These features are padded within each mini-batch to support efficient graph computation.

In addition to the molecular graph view, we construct an auxiliary substructure-level drug representation using Morgan fingerprints. Specifically, ECFP indices are computed with radius 2 and 8192 bits. Instead of using dense binary fingerprints directly, we store the activated bit indices and reserve index 0 for padding. These sparse substructure indices are then mapped into learnable embeddings during training. This design provides complementary local substructure information and helps the model capture chemical motifs that may not be fully summarized by graph-level pooling alone.

For protein targets, amino-acid sequences are used as the primary input. Residue-level embeddings with dimension 768 are obtained from a ProteinBertModel in the TAPE toolkit when available, and the computed embeddings are cached to reduce repeated preprocessing cost. During batching, protein sequences are padded or truncated to a maximum length of 512 residues to balance sequence coverage and computational efficiency. The training set is further divided into training and development subsets with an 80/20 ratio for model selection. Label normalization statistics are computed only from the training subset and are then consistently applied to the development and test sets. All final metrics are calculated after transforming predictions back to the original label scale.

Baselines

We compare BiT-Fusion with representative methods from several DTA modeling families. Classical machine learning baselines include kernel-based learning for drug–target interaction prediction8 and SimBoost10. Deep sequence-based models include DeepDTA11 and WideDTA14. Graph-based and interaction-centered models include GraphDTA16, MolTrans22, and DrugBAN23. In the result figures, we also include RF and XGBoost as simple machine learning references. These baselines cover handcrafted similarity learning, sequence convolution, molecular graph modeling, transformer-based interaction learning, and bilinear attention, enabling a broad comparison with the proposed bidirectional tensor-fusion design.

Baseline result sources

For BiT-Fusion and all ablation variants, we used the same preprocessing pipeline, model-selection strategy, and five-fold evaluation protocol described above. For external baselines, we used reproduced results when public implementations and compatible benchmark settings were available. When reproduction under identical preprocessing was not feasible, we reported the results from the corresponding original papers or commonly reported benchmark comparisons. Therefore, the main comparative figures should be interpreted as a transparent benchmark-level comparison rather than a claim that every baseline was retrained under a completely identical implementation environment. To improve statistical transparency, we report mean and standard deviation over repeated folds for BiT-Fusion and its ablation variants.

Implementation details

All experiments are implemented in PyTorch. BiT-Fusion is optimized using AdamW55 with a batch size of 512, a learning rate of , and up to 200 training epochs. Mixed-precision training is enabled to improve training efficiency. During evaluation, we maintain an exponential moving average (EMA) of model parameters with a decay rate of 0.999, which helps stabilize the final prediction behavior. To improve generalization, we incorporate multi-sample dropout, R-Drop regularization29, and a pairwise ranking loss inspired by learning-to-rank formulations28. Multi-sample dropout provides an ensemble-like effect under stochastic forward passes and has been connected to uncertainty-aware regularization27. Unless otherwise specified, the same training configuration is used for both Davis and KIBA.

Dimensionality settings

In our notation, N denotes the number of atoms in a molecule rather than the hidden representation dimension. Since different molecules contain different numbers of atoms, N is molecule-dependent and is padded only for mini-batch computation. The hidden representation dimension is denoted by d. In the default BiT-Fusion configuration, atom-level drug representations and residue-level protein representations are projected into the same hidden space with d=128. The compact multiplicative interaction feature has dimension . Thus, the drug encoder output should be interpreted as N atom embeddings with d hidden features per atom, while the final molecule-level vector has dimension d. Similarly, the residue-level protein representation contains L residue embeddings with d hidden features per residue. In mini-batch computation, molecules and protein sequences are padded to the batch-level maximum length or to the predefined maximum sequence length, and padding positions are excluded by masks.

The comparative results also provide insight into why BiT-Fusion improves over existing architectures. DeepDTA and WideDTA mainly rely on sequence-style encoders and late-stage fusion, which limits explicit modeling of atom–residue interactions. GraphDTA strengthens drug representation by using molecular graphs, but the interaction between drug and protein features is still relatively shallow after independent encoding. MolTrans and DrugBAN introduce stronger interaction modeling, but their interaction modules are not designed to simultaneously emphasize reciprocal conditioning and higher-order multiplicative coupling. In contrast, BiT-Fusion updates drug and protein representations in both directions and then applies tensor-style interaction to the context-enhanced features. This design allows the model to represent both aligned cross-modal evidence and nonlinear feature products, which likely explains its consistent gains across metrics.

Ablation and component analysis

To verify the contribution of each component, we conduct component-wise ablation experiments by removing or replacing one module at a time. Figure 5 ranks different components according to the average CI drop across datasets. The largest performance degradation occurs when replacing the drug GCN branch with an MLP, resulting in an average CI drop of 3.90%. This confirms that molecular graph topology is essential for representing chemical structures in DTA prediction. Removing the cross-attention module leads to a 3.18% average CI drop, demonstrating that explicit cross-modal interaction is a major source of the final improvement. The tensor network and TextCNN protein branch also show notable influence, each producing a 2.34% average CI drop when removed or replaced. These results support the core hypothesis of this work: accurate affinity prediction requires not only strong unimodal encoders but also expressive interaction modeling between chemical and protein features.

Component importance measured by average CI drop.

Regularization and training-stability components provide smaller but still meaningful gains. Removing multi-sample dropout causes a 1.62% average CI drop, while removing EMA causes a 1.39% drop. This indicates that prediction stability is important for DTA tasks, especially because affinity labels may contain experimental noise and the training distribution may be sparse for certain drug or protein regions. R-Drop and rank loss show relatively moderate effects, with average CI drops of 0.67% and 0.45%, respectively. Although these components are not the dominant contributors, they improve robustness and ranking consistency, which is useful in practical virtual screening scenarios where relative candidate ordering is often as important as absolute affinity prediction.

Figure 6 provides a more fine-grained view of ablation effects across datasets and metrics. Removing the cross-attention module causes substantial degradation in MSE on both Davis and KIBA, indicating that reciprocal drug–protein conditioning directly affects regression accuracy. Removing the tensor network also leads to consistent deterioration across CI, MSE, and , confirming that multiplicative interaction features complement the attention module. In particular, the heatmap shows that structural and interaction components have stronger effects than regularization-only components, suggesting that the improvement of BiT-Fusion mainly comes from architectural design rather than only from training tricks.

Ablation degradation heatmap across datasets and metrics.

Overall, the ablation results are consistent with the main comparative findings. The drug graph encoder provides chemically meaningful structural representations, the protein sequence encoder captures residue-level sequence patterns, the bidirectional cross-attention module enables reciprocal information exchange, and the tensor interaction module strengthens higher-order cross-modal coupling. The robust prediction head further stabilizes the learned mapping. These components are complementary rather than redundant, and their combination forms the main reason why BiT-Fusion achieves consistent improvements on both Davis and KIBA.

Conclusion and discussion

In this work, we proposed Bidirectional Cross-Modal Fusion with Tensor Interaction (BiT-Fusion) for drug–target binding prediction. The central idea is to move beyond late fusion by explicitly modeling reciprocal conditioning between drug substructures and protein residue contexts, while strengthening interaction expressiveness through a tensor-style coupling module. BiT-Fusion integrates a drug graph encoder and a protein sequence encoder with bidirectional cross-attention, a tensor interaction module, and a robust prediction head, forming an end-to-end framework for affinity estimation.

Conclusion

Experiments on the Davis and KIBA benchmarks demonstrate that BiT-Fusion achieves consistently strong performance across multiple metrics. The results indicate improvements in both ranking consistency and regression accuracy, reflected by CI and MSE, and favorable goodness-of-fit behavior under and as reported in our figures. Ablation studies further support the design choices. Removing bidirectional cross-attention or the tensor interaction module leads to the most notable performance drops, showing that explicit bidirectional interaction learning and higher-order fusion contribute materially to the final gains. In addition, robust training components, including multi-sample dropout, EMA evaluation, and consistency-style regularization, provide additional stability and measurable improvements.

Discussion and future work

The empirical evidence suggests that interaction modeling benefits from being both symmetric and expressive. Bidirectional cross-attention encourages reciprocal information flow, which is well aligned with the intuition that binding depends on localized correspondence between residues and chemical motifs. The tensor interaction module complements attention by adding multiplicative coupling, helping capture compositional effects that are difficult to represent with additive fusion alone. At the same time, several limitations remain. Our evaluation follows standard benchmark splits, which do not fully represent realistic discovery settings such as scaffold splits, unseen protein-family generalization, or time-split evaluation. Moreover, attention maps provide a useful interpretability signal, but they do not constitute causal attribution, and further validation with known binding site annotations would strengthen biological credibility. Future work will therefore focus on evaluating BiT-Fusion under more challenging generalization protocols, integrating additional structure-aware priors when available, and improving explanation reliability through complementary attribution analyses and targeted biological validation. We also plan to explore factorized or low-rank variants of the tensor interaction module to reduce computation while preserving interaction capacity.

Data availability

The datasets analysed in this study are publicly available benchmark datasets for drug–target binding affinity prediction. The Davis dataset is based on the kinase inhibitor selectivity data originally reported by Davis et al. and can be accessed through the original publication and its associated supplementary materials at https://doi.org/10.1038/nbt.1990. The KIBA dataset is based on the integrated kinase inhibitor bioactivity matrix introduced by Tang et al. and is available through the original supporting dataset at https://doi.org/10.1021/ci400709d.s002; an archived public dataset record is also available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.5105698. These datasets provide drug–target affinity information for kinase-focused benchmark evaluation. In this study, the Davis and KIBA data were used for model training, validation, and testing. During preprocessing, drug SMILES strings were converted into molecular graphs, auxiliary Morgan fingerprint features were generated, and protein sequences were converted into residue-level representations as described in the Methods section. No new wet-lab, clinical, or patient-related data were generated in this study. The processed data splits, preprocessing settings, implementation details, and experimental configuration files generated during the current study are available from the corresponding author, Deshinta Arrova Dewi, upon reasonable request at deshinta.ad@newinti.edu.my.
