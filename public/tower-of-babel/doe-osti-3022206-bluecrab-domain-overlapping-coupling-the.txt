 ABSTRACT

 Coupling low- and high-fidelity codes is a useful way to model complex engineering systems. Thanks
to the high-fidelity code, complex phenomena can be resolved in areas of the system where this is required,
yet the efficiency of the lower-fidelity code is still retained in modeling the rest of the system. This document
details the theory and implementation of coupling two different thermal-hydraulic codes: the system
thermal-hydraulics (STH) code System Analysis Module (SAM) and the coarse-mesh computational
fluid dynamics (CFD) code Pronghorn. Both applications are included in the Comprehensive Reactor
Analysis Bundle (BlueCRAB) code suite and are based on the Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation
Environment (MOOSE) framework. The domain overlapping (DO) coupling approach was adopted, as it
offers proven advantages over more conventional domain decomposition methods. In the DO coupling,
SAM provides Pronghorn with boundary conditions that depend on the system-level simulation of the entire
plant. In return, the overlapping coupled SAM components—termed “surrogate components”—receive
friction factors and source terms computed online based on the Pronghorn simulation. The framework is
designed to be generic and enable coupling regardless of geometry and the number of inlet/outlet boundaries
in the DO coupled domain. The developed method leads to consistent pressure drops, enthalpies, and scalar
concentrations when comparing the coupled SAM and Pronghorn simulations. This document presents the
DO coupling approach, along with two verification and two demonstration cases. The proposed problems
explore different physical aspects relevant to nuclear reactor analysis, including buoyancy-driven flows,
complex flow patterns, and multiple inlets and outlets. Periodically, new versions of this “BlueCRAB
Domain Overlapping Coupling: Theory & Verification” report will be issued to reflect future developments
and verification tests.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 system thermal-hydraulic (STH) legacy codes are used by the nuclear industry to evaluate safety-relevant
transients in nuclear systems. These codes enable fast, system-wide analysis by utilizing a simplified descrip-
tion of the fluid flow, based on a zero or one-dimensional physics representation. Typically, this approach
includes momentum and heat transfer closure models that have been empirically derived. Unfortunately,
multi-scale phenomena such as complex 3D flow patterns, turbulent structures, thermal stratification, and
mixing cannot be captured by these simplified models. These phenomena become even more crucial and
challenging in applications involving non-conventional coolants, as is the case for most non-light-water
reactors (non-LWRs). Therefore, enhancing the applicability of STH codes for non-LWRs is a pressing
demand in the nuclear industry.
 The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC)’s vision and strategy for non-LWRs underscores the im-
portance of developing and adapting codes suitable for advanced reactor designs [1]. In alignment with
this vision, the NRC has proposed the Comprehensive Reactor Analysis Bundle (BlueCRAB) code suite to
perform safety analyses of these reactors. This suite, developed under the Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling
and Simulation (NEAMS) program, includes the STH code SAM [2] and the coarse-mesh computational
fluid dynamics (CFD) code Pronghorn [3], both of which are intended for thermal-hydraulic analysis. SAM
addresses the need for a fast-running, system-level transient analysis code. Pronghorn, on the other hand,
enables resolution of the multi-scale phenomena occurring in the reactor core—phenomena traditionally
beyond the scope of STH codes.
 This report details the theory and verification of coupling the STH code SAM and the coarse-mesh CFD
code Pronghorn, both of which are included in the BlueCRAB code suite and are based on the Multiphysics
Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) framework [4]. BlueCRAB implements a coupling
approach based on the method originally presented in [5]. The DO coupling approach was adopted, as it
offers proven advantages over more conventional domain decomposition methods [6, 7]. In the DO coupling,
SAM provides Pronghorn with boundary conditions (BCs) that depend on the system-level simulation of
the entire plant. In return, the overlapping coupled SAM components—termed “surrogate components”—
receive friction factors and source terms computed online based on the Pronghorn simulation. This method
leads to consistent pressure drops, enthalpies, and scalar concentrations when comparing the coupled SAM
and Pronghorn simulations.
 This present work introduces the latest developments implemented for the DO coupling in BlueCRAB.
These developments afford improved robustness and convergence as compared to the preliminary version
[5], and are of increasing importance, as they enable support for a wider range of fluid flow and heat
transfer conditions, including natural circulation and thermal expansion effects. This work demonstrates the
effectiveness of the improved formulation by considering a series of verification and demonstration exercises
suggested by the NRC. The following test cases were chosen to reflect fluid flow and heat transfer conditions
relevant to safety analyses of non-LWRs:

 1. Canonical heated pipe
 2. Natural circulation loop
 3. Non-symmetric flow in a tank
 4. Multiple junctions with asymmetric flow.

 The standalone SAM and Pronghorn simulations, as well as the coupled SAM-Pronghorn ones, closely
match the analytical solutions pertaining to the pressure drops and energy balance for cases 1 and 2. Cases

 1

3 and 4 involve more complex, non-symmetric flow behavior that precludes the derivation of analytical
solutions. Therefore, for these cases, we rely solely on comparisons between SAM and Pronghorn during
coupled simulations. As such, these are treated as demonstration problems.
 The DO coupling implementation in BlueCRAB applies to problems regardless of geometry and flow
configuration, including variations in the number of inlets and outlets as well as different geometric character-
istics. This report introduces and discusses the DO coupling and how it was applied to the proposed test cases.
Continuous efforts are aimed at improving the DO custom action system that currently exists in BlueCRAB,
as well as the user interface. Future publications pertaining to DO will focus on input definitions and user
guidelines.

 2. METHOD

 This section presents the DO coupling formulation and the SAM-Pronghorn interface. The coupling
interface was designed to be generic and address problems regardless of geometry and the number of
inlets/outlets. Additionally, its formulation ensures consistent results in both codes. These aspects are detailed
here in the following order. Section 2.1. presents an overview of the DO coupling framework in BlueCRAB,
including data transfer between the two codes and the adopted notations for describing the formulations and
numerical scheme. Section 2.2. describes the DO formulation for preserving momentum balance and mass
flow, including in the general case involving multiple outlets. Lastly, Sections 2.3. and 2.4. detail the DO
formulation for coupling energy and scalar quantities, respectively.

 2.1. Coupling Framework

2.1.1. Coupling Interface

 Fig. 1 schematically presents the data transferred between the SAM-Pronghorn coupled simulations in
accordance with the DO approach. As a system-level code, SAM can naturally provide the required BCs to
a coupled Pronghorn model. The set of Pronghorn BCs includes mass flow rates, temperature, and scalar
quantities at the inlets, along with pressure values at the outlets. In return, the SAM simulation receives
friction factors and source terms for both energy and passive scalars. These terms are determined based on
the Pronghorn simulation results. They ensure that the SAM simulation results are consistent with those
of Pronghorn in terms of pressure drops, temperature rise, and scalar quantities for the overlapped domain.
Sections 2.2.–2.4. present how these terms are computed in DO coupled simulations.

 The DO framework proposed in Fig. 2 is applicable to problems with different configurations. For the

 It should be noted that both the interface boundaries and the reference plane require careful attention as
regards their definition. The following directives should be adhered to:

 • Interface planes in the Pronghorn model should be placed in locations where complex 3D flow effects
 are absent or minimal.
 Explanation: the SAM surrogate model assumes a 1D representation of the flow field. This directive is
 intended to minimize the discrepancy between the two different physical interpretations (2D/3D vs. 1D).
 • The reference plane and the branch should be as close as possible to each other in natural circulation
 problems. Ideally, the branch point should be located on the reference plane’s center of mass.
 Explanation: this directive ensures that the SAM surrogate components accurately model the buoyancy
 head in systems that operate via natural circulation. Section 2.3. “Energy Formulation” further
 discusses this constraint, whereas Section 3.2.2. provides a verification example in the form of a natural
 circulation loop problem.

2.1.2. Coupling Notation

 Before presenting the DO formulation and associated numerical scheme, we first present a convenient
nomenclature for assessing the framework. Hereafter in this report, thermodynamic intensive properties, such
as enthalpy, pressure, and scalar concentrations, are denoted using lowercase letters for SAM and uppercase
letters for Pronghorn. Extensive flow properties, including mass and volumetric flow rates as well as flow
split ratios, follow the same convention.
 The subscript E indicates that a bulk quantity (e.g., enthalpy or mass flow rate) is computed over an
interface in either Pronghorn or SAM. The subscripts ‘re f 0 and B indicate that bulk quantities are computed
at the Pronghorn reference plane and at the SAM branch component, respectively.
 The aforementioned subscripts are generally accompanied by the index i, denoting the identity of the 1D
SAM surrogate component and the interface with which it is associated. Its valid range is 1 ≤ i ≤ N, where
N ≥ 2 represents the number of interfaces (with N = 2 corresponding to the basic case of a single inlet and
a single outlet).

 • SAM 1D standard components are replaced by surrogate components.

 • Surrogate components employ friction factors and source terms that reproduce the flow and
 heat/scalar transport captured by the coupled Pronghorn model.

 • All 1D surrogate components are connected into a single branch to support multiple inlets and

 2.2. Momentum and Continuity Formulation
 This section presents the formulation developed to achieve continuity in mass and momentum coupling
between SAM and Pronghorn. This formulation assumes the notation presented in Section 2.1.1., so readers
are encouraged to review that section before proceeding. SAM’s surrogate components are governed by the
same momentum equation as standard 1D components [2]:

where f is the friction factor, ρ is the density, Dh is the hydraulic diameter, and g is the gravity. Unlike standard
1D components, DO surrogates do not rely on typical empirical models for the friction factor calculation (e.g.,
Blasius [8] for turbulent pipes, or Cheng-Todreas [9] for hexagonal rod bundles). Instead, the DO approach
prescribes friction factors that reproduce pressure drops equal to those calculated by a concurrently solved
Pronghorn simulation.
 When coupling fluid flow, SAM provides Pronghorn with inlet mass flow rates ṁ E,i and outlet pressures
p E,i . Pronghorn then resolves the flow field in detail and returns an equivalent friction factor that accurately
accounts for local effects. Since the SAM calculation of pressure drops and flow rates changes depending
on the friction factor, a fixed-point iteration scheme was employed to ensure consistency between the two
codes. Fig. 4 schematically illustrates the coupling scheme and iterative process. In each time step, the SAM
simulation generates new BCs by using Pronghorn-informed friction factors. Pronghorn then runs again
with these new BCs, and new friction factors are computed based on the new solution. This iterative process
continues until a convergence criterion is met: namely, that the difference of the calculated pressure drops of
each of the two codes falls below a specified tolerance.

2.2.1. Hydraulic resistance correction

 The hydraulic resistance correction Fh is intended to account for differences in the pressure drop calcula-
tions in the Pronghorn domain and the SAM surrogate components. It encompasses both friction and form
losses. Eq. 6 presents the correction definition used in this work:

 This equation employs the Darcy-Weisbach [10] formulation to ensure consistency with the SAM governing
equation for the conservation of linear momentum in the flow direction (i.e., the second term on the right-hand
side of Eq. 1). The denominator of Eq. 6 is based on SAM’s mass ṁ E,i and volumetric flux v̇ E,i , since these
values are readily available during the simulation. Because of this choice, the equation also depends on the
flow area Ai as well as the hydraulic diameter Dh,i of the surrogate component i. Thus, careful consideration
must be given to the surrogates’ areas Ai . The area of a component i should be consistent with its BC
counterpart modeled in Pronghorn; otherwise, the volumetric flux v̇ E,i will differ between the two codes,
leading to unrealistic values for the dynamic pressures. This can then cause numerical instability in the form
of increasing the number of fixed point iterations required to achieve convergence, or may lead to divergence.

2.2.2. Flow split controller

 The flow split controller Fγi is the second of the two components that form the correction term Fi
used in Eq. 2. This term is only utilized in cases involving multiple outlets. In cases with multiple outlet
surrogate components (i.e., multiple components with flow moving away from the coupled branch), coupling
the hydraulic resistance is not enough to make the two codes fully consistent with each other. This is because
the flow split between the outlet surrogate components is controlled by the ratio between the two components’
friction factors. If the friction factor in each outlet component is only controlled by its own pressure drop, this
can lead to solutions for which Pronghorn and SAM are consistent in terms of pressure drop but not in terms
of the mass flow split between components (i.e., there is nothing to constrain what percentage of the total
outlet mass flow rate each outlet surrogate component should receive). The flow split controller is therefore
intended to add this extra constraint so as to reproduce the same flow split among the multiple outlets in
SAM as is calculated in Pronghorn. The key idea is to either increase or decrease the flow resistance in the
SAM components in order to alter the ratio between the outlet components’ resistance. The controller utilizes
mass flow rate ratios in SAM’s DO component, denoted as γi , and the Pronghorn model, denoted as Γi . This
approach provides a more general and dimensionless formulation that serves as a better option for calculating
friction factors. Fig. 5 in combination with Eqs. 7 and 8 demonstrate computation of these ratios.

 The thermal relaxation factor, r T ∈ (0, 1], controls the convergence of the energy coupling. The proposed

in Pronghorn, but is often slightly different as a result of the presence of 3D effects in Pronghorn, minor total
fluid volume or fluid property differences between codes, and energy equation differences between codes,
particularly when a porous media formulation is applied in Pronghorn. The DO energy iteration formula
in Eq. 15 is therefore aimed at determining a surrogate Q̇ that adequately reproduces the same enthalpy
change across the coupled region. This equation is derived from SAM’s energy governing equation in Eq. 11.

represents the net balance of energy entering/leaving the coupled domain.
 Finally, the denominator of the convective correction term represents the volume of the SAM surrogate
component, which is the product of the component length Li and its cross-sectional area Ai . This fraction
arises because the energy is expressed as a volumetric heat sink/source. There is no specific requirement for
the overall volume of the surrogate components, since the DO coupling ensures consistent mass flow rates and
energy between SAM and Pronghorn. However, as explained in Section 2.2., the cross-sectional area of the
given surrogate component i must be somewhat consistent with its connected Pronghorn BC interface. This is
not a strict requirement, but large cross-sectional area and volume differences between Pronghorn and SAM
can lead to increased fixed point iterations or numerical divergence. Additionally, the vertical dimension of
surrogate components must match that of the Pronghorn domain to accurately account for the buoyancy head
in natural circulation cases. In fact, this requirement is equivalent to the Section 2.1.1. directive for defining
both the DO branch and the reference plane at the same location.

 3.2. Natural Circulation Loop

3.2.1. Problem description

 This section provides verification of the DO coupling method for problems involving natural circulation.
The reference problem consists of a rectangular loop with two horizontal and two vertical pipe sections with a
constant circular cross-section. The loop includes a heat source in the lower-right portion and a heat sink in
the upper-left portion. The working fluid in the loop is helium, such that thermal-expansion between the heat
source and heat sink generates buoyancy, thus establishing natural circulation.
 The heat sink/source in the loop has a power of Q. The vertical distance H between the centers of the
heated and the cooled portions determines the buoyancy head. Two configurations are considered: a short
loop with reduced buoyancy head and lower power input, and a tall loop with increased vertical separation
and higher power. The short loop operates with a lower mass flow rate due to the limited buoyancy driving
force, whereas the tall loop achieves stronger natural circulation and supports a higher thermal load. Figure 8
schematically presents the problem geometry and the temperature distribution in the loop once natural
circulation is established. Table 4 presents the problem parameters.
 The natural circulation loop problem adds complexity in comparison to the forced convection from
the previous section. Here, the problem involves a thermally expandable working fluid and tight iteration
between friction losses and buoyancy. This problem is meant to reproduce conditions similar to loss of forced
circulation (LOFC) in HTGRs, with natural circulation contributing to reactor core heat removal.

 3.3. Non-symmetric Flow in a Tank

3.3.1. Problem description

 Figure 14 schematically presents Problem 3 (non-symmetric flow in a tank). This problem consists of
a 2D rectangular cavity with water entering through an inlet located at the upper-left side of the tank and
exiting through an outlet positioned at the bottom-right side. Water enters the tank through a relatively smaller
opening, expands into the larger tank cross-section, and subsequently contracts toward the outlet.
 The problem is intended to demonstrate that the DO coupling is sufficiently robust to handle non-trivial
internal flow in confined geometries with asymmetric boundary conditions. Specifically, the pressure field is
defined by the superposition of the hydrostatic column and dynamic effects associated with the through-flow.
The tank geometry induces a non-uniform internal flow field characterized by expansion-contraction losses,
asymmetric shear layers, and localized recirculation zones.

 Summary

 • This section presented verification and demonstration of the DO coupling for various problems
 in order to assess its robustness.

 • The problems included:

 – Problem 1 (canonical heated pipe): Heated pipe flow with incompressible fluid and
 heating, verified based on the analytical solution.
 – Problem 2 (natural circulation loop): Simple helium loop system transitioning from
 forced to natural circulation, verified based on the analytical solution.
 – Problem 3 (non-symmetric flow in a tank): Water tank with inlet at the top and outlet at
 the bottom, demonstrating consistent Pronghorn-SAM results.
 – Problem 4 (multiple junctions with asymmetric flow): Two independent loops connected
 to a single tank, demonstrating consistent Pronghorn-SAM results.

 • All DO coupled simulations used an absolute error tolerance of η = 10−5 and a maximum of
 30 fixed-point iterations in MOOSE’s TransientMultiApp setup.

 • The iteration count is generally satisfactory. The following considerations are noteworthy:

 – The iteration count ranges from roughly 5 to 10 during dynamic phases.
 – In steady state, the iteration count typically decreases to between 1 and 3. Counts above 1
 occur in cases involving natural circulation and flow split in multiple outlets.
 – A peak in iterations is expected at the start of the coupling process, due to the independent
 initialization of SAM and Pronghorn.
