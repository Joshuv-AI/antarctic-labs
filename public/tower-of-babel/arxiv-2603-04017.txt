An Unconventional View on Beta-Reduction in Namefree Lambda-Calculus
Rob Nederpelt, Ferruccio Guidi

Abstract
Terms in the lambda-calculus can be represented as planar trees decorated with symbols for ‘abstraction’ and ‘application’, and having variables as leaves. In this paper, we concentrate on the branches of such trees, rather than on the trees themselves. We reformulate several well-known notions of beta-reduction in this view. In a natural manner, this reconsideration eventually leads to a new form of beta-reduction, being expanding – in the sense that the reduction of term t 1 t_{1} to term t 2 t_{2} entails that the tree of t 1 t_{1} is a subtree of the tree of t 2 t_{2} .

1.1
Introduction

It is well known that practical implementations of the
\lambda
-calculus turn
\alpha

[
9

Generally speaking,
\beta

Experience shows that this update, known as lift according to a well-established terminology, is a time consuming operation
[

that, precisely, computing machines strive to avoid (
[

(
[

\beta

One of the namefree systems we present in this article
is based just on
\beta
-reduction at a distance,
an extension of
\beta
-reduction that has been introduced in (
[

Such a reduction relation allows, for example, not only

In deviation of the usual notation, lambda terms are presented as the set of complete branches belonging to rooted trees that are composed of vertices and labeled edges. With this representation, one obtains a transparent view on matching pairs of abstraction and application, each of which pairs may generate a beta-reduction at a distance. This transparency considerably facilitates our discussion.

{\bf t}\not\equiv{\bf t}^{\prime}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
{\bf t},{\bf t^{\prime}}\in{\cal T}^{\it exp}
. Then
{\bf t}\rightarrow_{\it ef}{\bf t^{\prime}}
implies
{\bf t}\subset{\bf t^{\prime}}
.

Proof
Obvious.
\Box

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
{\bf t}\in{\cal T}^{\it exp}
and
p\,n\in^{\wedge}{\bf t}
. Assume that
n
is an inner num-variable. Then there is an
{\rm L}\in p
that binds the
n
and a matching
{\rm A}\in p
.

Proof
An inner num-label
n
can only appear in a
\rightarrow_{\it ef}
-reduction, when the
{\rm L}
binding
n
is pivotal in the reduction and this
{\rm L}
is matched to an
{\rm A}
(see definition
4.3
). This situation is maintained by the expanding nature of
\rightarrow_{\it ef}
-reduction (Theorem
4.5
).
\Box

4.2
Tracing the binder in expanding beta-reduction

Let
{\bf t}_{0}\in{\cal T}^{\it exp}
and
{\bf t}_{0}\twoheadrightarrow_{\it ef}{\bf t}
, so
{\bf t}\in{\cal T}^{\it exp}
is the result of a series of expanding, focused reductions. These reductions may introduce inner variables, so it is not immediately clear what the binders are for (inner or outer) variables. In this section, we investigate how to determine the binder of a num-variable in
{\bf t}
.

Let
p\,n\in^{\wedge}{\bf t}
. Here,
n
can be an inner or an outer num-label. We describe a pushdown automaton
{\cal P}
that finds the
{\rm L}
-binder
of
n
, i.e., the label
{\rm L}\in p
that binds
n
(this label always exists, since
{\cal T}^{\it exp}
only contains closed terms).

We now present and explain the action of the pushdown automaton
{\cal P}
. Let
{\bf t}\in{\cal T}^{\it exp}
and
p\,n\in^{\wedge}{\bf t}
. Assume that we desire to apply algorithm
{\cal P}
to find the
{\rm L}
-binder of
n
.

Remark 4.7
.

Preliminary remarks.

In algorithm
{\cal P}
, we employ states that are pairs of natural numbers:
(k,l)
. We start with the insertion of a pair
(n,i)
in the string
p\,n
, between
p
and
n
. Here,
i
originally is
0
or
1
. The automaton moves the pair to the left through
p
, one step at a time, successively passing the labels in
p
and meanwhile adapting the numbers in the pair.

The automaton has an outside stack that will contain certain states that are pushed at the top of the stack; a state on top of the stack can also be popped back, i.e., inserted into the path
p
, again.

The transitions are described in Definition
4.8
. A possible one-step transition is denoted by the symbol
\rightarrow
.

The procedure may be complicated by several recursive calls. In every recursive call, the algorithm starts with a ‘new’ num-variable
j
on the path
p
. Subsequently, it finds not only the
{\rm L}
binding this
j
, but also the
{\rm A}
matching the
j
.

The formal description of
{\cal P}
is the following.

Procedure 4.8
.

Preparation:
Transform
p\,n
into
p\,(n,i)\,n
, where
i=0
if the goal is to find the
{\rm L}
-binder, and
i=1
in the recursion, when both the
{\rm L}
-binder of
n
and the matching
{\rm A}
are detected.

Now
start

{\cal P}
employing the following transition rules.

(1)

first step:

{\it stack}=\emptyset

(2)

p\penalty\ {\rm L}\penalty\ (m,k)\penalty\ q\penalty\ \rightarrow\penalty\ p\penalty\ (m\makebox{--}1,k)\penalty\ {\rm L}\penalty\ q
, if
m>0

(3)

p\penalty\ {\rm A}\penalty\ (m,k)\penalty\ q\penalty\ \rightarrow\penalty\ p\penalty\ (m,k)\penalty\ {\rm A}\penalty\ q
, if
m>0

(4)

p\penalty\ {\rm S}\penalty\ (m,k)\penalty\ q\penalty\ \rightarrow\penalty\ p\penalty\ (m,k)\penalty\ {\rm S}\penalty\ q
, if
m>0

(5)

p\penalty\ j\penalty\ (m,k)\penalty\ q\penalty\ \rightarrow\penalty\ p\penalty\ (j,1)\penalty\ j\penalty\ q
, if
m>0
;
push

(m,k)

(6)

p\penalty\ {\rm L}\penalty\ (0,l)\penalty\ q\penalty\ \rightarrow\penalty\ p\penalty\ (0,l\makebox{+}1)\penalty\ {\rm L}\penalty\ q
, if
l>0

(7)

p\penalty\ {\rm A}\penalty\ (0,l)\penalty\ q\penalty\ \rightarrow\penalty\ p\penalty\ (0,l\makebox{--}1)\penalty\ {\rm A}\penalty\ q
, if
l>0

(8)

p\penalty\ j\penalty\ (0,l)\penalty\ q\penalty\ \rightarrow\penalty\ p\penalty\ (0,l)\penalty\ j\penalty\ q
, if
l>0

(9a)

p\penalty\ (0,0)\penalty\ q\penalty\ \rightarrow\penalty\ p\penalty\ {\it pop}\penalty\ q
, if
{\it stack}\not=\emptyset

(9b)

p\penalty\ (0,0)\penalty\ q\penalty\ \rightarrow{\it stop}
, if
{\it stack}=\emptyset
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
{\bf t}\in{\cal T}^{\it exp}
and
p\,n\in^{\wedge}{\bf t}
. Apply
{\cal P}
to
p\,(n,i)\,n
, where
i=0
or
1
.

(i)
If
{\cal P}
stops in
p^{\prime}\,(0,0)\,q^{\prime}\,n
with empty stack (see rule 9b), then each possible recursion has ended. Moreover,
q^{\prime}\,n\equiv{\rm L}\,q^{\prime\prime}\,n
, and the mentioned
{\rm L}
binds the
n
.

(ii)
If
{\cal P}
stops in
p^{\prime}\,(0,0)\,q^{\prime}\,n
with non-empty stack (see rule 9a), the
{\rm A}
matching the binding
{\rm L}
of
n
has been found, the top-element of the stack is popped and
{\cal P}
continues where it had stopped before the recursion step.

Proof
See Section

.
