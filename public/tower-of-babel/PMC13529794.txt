Spatiotemporal dynamics and antimicrobial resistance of bacterial pathogens recovered from various fomites at a paediatric care facility in Ghana
Authors: Fleischer C N Kotey, Eric Yaw Wirekoh, Mame Yaa Nyarko, Eric S Donkor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHospital fomites are major reservoirs of nosocomial pathogens, facilitating healthcare-associated infections, particularly in critical units. However, longitudinal studies investigating the persistence of pathogens on hospital surfaces and their antimicrobial resistance (AMR) patterns remain limited. This study examined the spatiotemporal distribution and antimicrobial resistance of bacterial pathogens isolated from fomite surfaces at Princess Marie Louise Children’s Hospital (PML), Accra, Ghana. A 12-week longitudinal study (September 4–November 26, 2023) was conducted across four departments: Emergency, Neonatal Intensive Care, Malnutrition, and Outpatient. High-touch surfaces (including bed rails, incubators, infusion stands, blood pressure cuffs, thermometers, stethoscopes, etc.) were sampled weekly using sterile saline-moistened swabs. Samples were promptly transported in STGG medium on ice to the bacteriology laboratory of the University of Ghana Medical School’s Department of Medical Microbiology, for standard culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing using the Kirby-Bauer method. A total of 1,120 bacterial isolates belonging to 33 genera were recovered from 600 swab samples. Among Gram-positive bacteria, Staphylococcus epidermidis (97 isolates, 34.3%), Staphylococcus haemolyticus (42 isolates, 14.8%), and Staphylococcus aureus (34 isolates, 12.0%) predominated. The most frequently isolated Gram-negative bacteria were Acinetobacter baumannii (91 isolates, 18.2%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (54 isolates, 10.8%), and Escherichia coli (38 isolates, 7.6%). Contamination levels varied by surface type. Thermometers (N = 25 isolates), thermometer trays (N = 24), weighing tables (N = 24), and IV setting trays (N = 22) exhibited the highest contamination frequencies. Among individual items, Babe’s Cot A recorded the highest contamination (N = 30), while Omicron thermometer B had the lowest (N = 18) within the top-ranked fomites.AMR rates were moderate to high across several key pathogens, with resistance ranging from 0–93% in Staphylococcus aureus, 66.7–91.7% in Enterococcus faecium, 18.9–90% in Enterococcus faecium, 29.6–88.9% in Escherichia coli, and 40–82% in Klebsiella pneumoniae. Overall, the evaluated hospital fomites were heavily contaminated with a diverse array of bacterial pathogens exhibiting substantial antimicrobial resistance, underscoring the need to strengthen infection prevention and control measures in healthcare settings.Keywords: Hospital fomites, Antimicrobial resistance, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniaeSubject terms: Microbiology, Environmental sciences, Health care

Introduction

The patient care environment hosts many fomites, including medical equipment, that can facilitate the transmission of microorganisms, some of which are pathogenic, in healthcare settings1–5. Indeed, up to 60% of surfaces within the patient care environment may be contaminated with organisms associated with healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), which are leading causes of morbidity mortality, and economic challenges6. A wide range of pathogenic microorganisms have been linked to fomites, including bacteria (such as Staphylococcus spp., Enterococcus spp., Acinetobacter spp., Pseudomonas spp., and Streptococcus spp.), viruses (including Hepatitis A virus, influenza viruses, and coronaviruses), and fungi (such as Aspergillus spp. and Candida spp.) that frequently cause healthcare-associated infections6–10. The types of pathogens present in patient care environments are influenced by several factors, including the number of patients in a room, the intensity of clinical activity, and the prevalence of infectious diseases in the surrounding community8 Also, the survival duration of pathogens on fomites varies considerably depending on factors such as the initial inoculum size, ambient environmental conditions, biofilm-forming capacity, and the nature of the surface material4,11.

Following the transmission of pathogens from fomites to patients, progression from colonisation to healthcare-associated infection, as well as infection severity, is often more pronounced in high-risk patients, particularly severely ill and immunocompromised individuals. Consequently, fomite-mediated transmission and healthcare-associated infections are of particular concern in intensive care units (ICUs), oncology units, neonatal wards, and other critical care settings12. It is thus important that investigations into pathogens harboured on hospital fomites — especially in critical care environments — are regularly conducted to characterise contemporary profiles of fomite-associated pathogens. However, most existing studies on fomite-associated pathogens have been cross-sectional, thereby limiting insights into the spatiotemporal variations of pathogens on fomites. To address this knowledge gap and contribute to strategies aimed at reducing the burden of healthcare-associated infections, this study investigated the spatiotemporal dynamics and antimicrobial resistance patterns of bacterial pathogens isolated from fomite surfaces via a 12-week longitudinal study conducted at a paediatric hospital in Accra, Ghana.

Methodology

Study setting and design

This study was conducted at Princes Marie Louise (PML) Children’s Hospital in Accra, Ghana, a paediatric facility under the Ghana Health Service. The hospital provides both general and specialised healthcare services for children, particularly within the Greater Accra Region, and has an emergency department, outpatient department, operating theatre, and more than 100 inpatient beds. The hospital is also notable for hosting the country’s only dedicated malnutrition ward and for the historical identification of the first documented case of kwashiorkor in 1933 by Dr. Cecily Williams.

This longitudinal study was conducted over a 12-week period, from September to November 2023, and focused on four selected hospital departments: the Emergency Unit (ER), Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU), Malnutrition Unit (MU), and Outpatient Department (OPD). These departments were selected because of the high vulnerability of patients to healthcare-associated infections, frequent use of invasive medical devices, and prolonged patient contact with environmental surfaces.

High-touch surfaces were defined as surfaces frequently contacted by healthcare personnel, patients, or their caregivers. Low-touch surfaces were defined as surfaces located more than 5 m from patient care areas and with minimal direct contact by healthcare personnel or patients. The identification of these surfaces was based on investigator assessment, existing literature, and observational input from clinicians at the study site.

Sample collection and procedures

In line with national infection prevention and control guidelines and following a comprehensive literature review, fomite sampling sites were selected based on their high contact frequency with patients and healthcare personnel, as well as their documented potential for microbiological contamination9,13,14. The surfaces and fomites sampled included bed rails, incubator surfaces (in NICU), infusion stands, blood pressure cuffs, thermometers, stethoscopes, medication trolleys, and doorknobs. Each fomite was labelled with its location of origin and the date of sampling. For portable equipment that could be shared between units (such as thermometers and stethoscopes), the assigned ‘home unit’ was documented, and sampling was conducted when the equipment was located in that designated unit. When the same type of equipment was present in multiple units, each item was treated as a distinct sampling point. The identified fomites and swab sites were sampled weekly using a sterile cotton swabs premoistened with 0.9% sterile saline. Each swab was placed in skim milk, tryptone, glucose, and glycerine (STGG) transport medium and promptly transported on ice to the bacteriology laboratory of the Department of Medical Microbiology, University of Ghana Medical School, for microbiological analysis.

Isolation and identification of bacteria

Samples were streaked onto 5%–10% sheep blood agar and chocolate agar plates (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, Hants, UK) and incubated microaerophilically at 37 °C. Mannitol salt agar and MacConkey agar plates (Oxoid Ltd.) were also inoculated and incubated aerobically at the same temperature. After 18–24 h, growth was assessed, and subculturing was performed until pure isolates were obtained based on colonial morphology and Gram staining. Pure bacterial isolates were then subjected to species identification using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-ToF MS). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was subsequently performed on pathogenic isolates.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST)

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar plates (90 mm diameter). The susceptibility profiles of the isolates were assessed against a panel of antibiotics according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (2023, 33rd edition). The antibiotics tested against Gram-positive bacteria were: vancomycin (30 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), teicoplanin (30 µg), penicillin (10), linezolid (30 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), rifampicin (5 µg), clindamycin (2 µg), gentamicin (5 µg), cotrimoxazole (1.25/23.75 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), cefoxitin (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg). For Gram-negative bacteria, the antibiotics tested were: tetracycline (30 µg), gentamicin (5 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), amikacin (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), meropenem (10 µg), cefixime (5 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), cefuroxime (30 µg), co-amoxiclav (30 µg), cefoxitin (30 µg) piperacillin‒tazobactam (30/6 µg) and levofloxacin (5 µg). Due to the large antibiotic panel, testing was performed in batches, with a maximum of 6–8 antibiotic discs per plate to prevent overlapping zones of inhibition. Gram-positive and Gram-negative isolates were tested with their respective antibiotic panels on separate plates. Mueller-Hinton agar plates were incubated at 35 ± 2 °C for 16–18 h. For fastidious organisms, such as Enterococcus and Streptococcus species, isolates were inoculated onto 5% sheep blood agar plates and incubated under microaerophilic conditions for 18–24 h, in accordance with CLSI guidelines. The zones of inhibition were measured in millimetres (mm), and susceptibility interpretations (susceptible or resistant) were determined using CLSI breakpoint criteria. Intermediate results were interpreted as resistant for surveillance purposes and to support conservative antimicrobial stewardship and minimise the risk of treatment failure at standard antibiotic doses.

Quality control

Quality control was performed using reference strains of the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The reference strains used were Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 for testing of antibiotics, including linezolid and vancomycin, against Gram-positive bacteria; Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used during the testing of most Gram-negative bacteria, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853 for testing Pseudomonas species. These strains served as positive controls for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, while known resistant strains served as negative controls, where applicable. Quality assessment of MALDI-ToF in routine diagnostics was conducted as described by Cuénod et al.15.

Ethical consideration

The Department of Clinical Research at Princess Marie Louise Children’s Hospital (PML) reviewed the study proposal and granted verbal ethical approval in accordance with Ghana Health Service regulations and the hospital’s research guidelines for investigating hospital fomites through environmental sampling.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected in both field and laboratory settings. Initial recordings were made on physical data sheets and laboratory notebooks and were subsequently digitised, stored, and managed using Microsoft Excel 2020. Results obtained from MALDI-ToF analysis were organised into an Excel spreadsheet for further analysis, enabling the generation of graphs, tables, and charts. Mind map diagrams were generated with MindMeister Version 7.2.0.

Spatiotemporal distribution of the most isolated bacterial pathogens

A total of 33 bacterial genera were isolated from various fomite surfaces (Fig. 2). Of the 1,120 bacterial isolates identified, 620 (55.4%) were Gram-positive. This group included 337 non-pathogenic isolates, specifically Bacillus species, which were excluded from detailed analysis as they were presumed to be environmental contaminants. The study further identified 500 Gram-negative isolates (44.6%). The most recovered Gram-positive bacteria were Staphylococcus epidermidis (97 isolates, 34.3%), Staphylococcus haemolyticus (42 isolates, 14.8%), and Staphylococcus aureus (34 isolates, 12.0%). Among the Gram-negative bacteria, Acinetobacter baumannii was the most prevalent (91 isolates, 18.2%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (54 isolates, 10.8%), and Escherichia coli (38 isolates, 7.6%). Notably, Enterobacter roggenkampii was the most recovered bacterium in Week 11, with 30 isolates from Babe’s Cot A in the Emergency Unit. Figures 1a and b show the frequencies of the bacterial species isolated from each department.

Spatiotemporal distribution of predominant bacterial species recovered from fomite surfaces across the 12 weeks.

The pathogens Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae remained present throughout the 12-week sampling period. During the first three weeks, the predominant isolates varied: Staphylococcus aureus was most common in Week 1, followed by Enterobacter hormaechei in Week 2, and both Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii in Week 3. From Weeks 4 to 10, Acinetobacter baumannii emerged as the predominant pathogen isolated. Among the key bacterial species identified over the 12 weeks, two Gram-positive bacteria were particularly notable: Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus faecium.

Escherichia coli was absent in Weeks 3 and 4, reappeared intermittently thereafter, but disappeared again by Week 10. It showed notable persistence in other weeks, averaging four isolates per week (N = 4), with a peak of eight isolates (N = 8) in Week 9. The two Enterobacter species exhibited different patterns: Enterobacter hormaechei first appeared in Week 2 and recurred intermittently until Week 12, whereas Enterobacter roggenkampii was detected only between Weeks 9 and 12, peaking at 30 isolates in Week 11 as the predominant isolate.

Overall, Week 11 recorded the highest number of bacterial isolates (N = 53), while Week 3 had the lowest (N = 7).

A similar sinusoidal pattern of contamination was observed from Weeks 2 to 12 in the NICU, MU, and OPD.

Discussion

This study conducted at Princess Marie Louise (PML) Children’s Hospital revealed significant bacterial contamination on various fomite surfaces. The predominant Bacillus species identified were B. pumilus, B. subtilis, and B. cereus, which are commonly found in soil and other environmental habitats16. Notably, 30.0% of the isolated Bacillus species (N = 337) were considered environmental contaminants rather than clinically-significant pathogens. After excluding these organisms, the total bacterial contamination observed (N = 783) in this study exceeded those reported by Moges et al.17 and Mulu et al.18, who documented 113 and 243 isolates, respectively, from hospital environments in Northwest Ethiopia. Moges et al.17 further reported that 57.5% of their isolates originated from hospital environmental surfaces, highlighting the importance of fomites as potential reservoirs of microbial contamination. Similarly, Addae-Nuku et al.19 identified 294 bacterial isolates in hospital wastewater in Ghana. The predominance of Gram-positive bacteria in this study is consistent with findings reported by Segujja et al.20 (25.75%), Dada et al.10 (71.9%), and Biru et al.6 (80.7%). This observation reflects the ecological characteristics of these organisms, which commonly colonise human skin and the anterior nares and are readily transmitted through direct contact, respiratory droplets generated during talking or sneezing, and contact with contaminated surfaces21. Among the isolates identified, Staphylococcus epidermidis was the most frequently recovered organism, accounting for 40.7% of isolates, followed by Staphylococcus haemolyticus at 14.8%. Although these organisms are generally considered opportunistic pathogens with relatively low virulence, they are increasingly associated with subclinical infections, particularly in patients undergoing invasive procedures or those with indwelling medical devices22,23. In addition, they form part of the normal human skin microbiota and can be readily transferred to environmental surfaces within healthcare settings.

This study reinforces prior research indicating that S. aureus is the most frequently isolated clinically-significant Gram-positive bacterium on fomite surfaces10,24,25. Over the 12-week period, the total number of Gram-positive bacterial isolates was lower than those reported in studies by Tagoe et al.26, Thompson et al.27, Mulu et al.18, and Dada et al.9. However, Gram-positive bacteria were still predominant compared with Gram-negative bacteria in this study, likely reflecting multiple factors. First, Gram-positive organisms, particularly staphylococci, are common commensals of human skin and are continuously shed by healthcare workers, patients, and visitors, leading to environmental contamination. Second, although effective disinfection practices may reduce Gram-negative bacterial survival on surfaces, the observed pattern may primarily reflect the natural ecology of healthcare environments where Gram-positive skin flora predominates on frequently touched surfaces. The effectiveness of disinfection protocols and environmental cleaning practices may also contribute to the relative reduction in Gram-negative bacteria, which are often more susceptible to common disinfectants.

The study also found a low prevalence of Enterococcus casseliflavus (2.0%) and Enterococcus gallinarum (2.0%), whereas Al-Harbi et al.28 reported a higher rate of 26%. This lower prevalence may result from the use of non-selective media targeting non-fastidious organisms, leading to fewer identifications of Enterococci. Additionally, this study successfully isolated various streptococcal species (S. parasanquinis, S. mitis oralis, S gallolyticus, S infantarius), none of which were isolated in the investigations conducted by Moges et al.17, Addae-Nuku et al.19, and Dada et al.10.

This study further identified the presence of Gram-negative bacteria on various inanimate surfaces and noted fluctuations in their prevalence over time. The predominant one was Acinetobacter baumannii (19.4%, N = 97), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (10.8%, N = 54), Escherichia coli (7.6%, N = 38), Enterobacter roggenkampii (6.8%, N = 34), and Chromobacterium violaceum (4.8%, N = 24). The rates of prevalent nosocomial pathogens were comparable in this study. This observation indicates that bacterial contamination by these pathogens in the hospital has persisted for a long time, despite numerous studies documenting their presence and providing disinfection recommendations.

Chromobacterium violaceum is a rare human pathogen that thrives in untreated underground water sources and is a common saprophyte in tropical and subtropical soil and water29. Its presence on hospital surfaces often stems from its ubiquity in soil and stagnant water, as well as contamination from cleaning tools, and from the use of untreated water for cleaning and disinfection29. This is a common source of water for such activities in most developing countries because of water scarcity. Both C. violaceum and Pseudomonas aeruginosa form biofilms that help them resist disinfectants30. Additionally, opportunistic and difficult-to-treat pathogens such as Stenotrophomonas maltophilia and Pantoea dispersa are emerging public health threats due to their broad-spectrum drug resistance and links to complications in formula feeds31,32. The identification of lesser-known nosocomial pathogens in the paediatric healthcare environment of Ghana, as reported first in this study, especially on fomite surfaces, highlights the necessity for vigilant monitoring and effective infection control practises to enhance patient safety and care.

Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae consistently persisted on various fomites across all four departments throughout the 12-week study. Kramer et al.11 noted that Gram-negative bacteria, such as Klebsiella spp. and Acinetobacter spp., can survive on inanimate surfaces for months. Indeed, higher temperatures and increased ventilation in open environments, such as outpatient departments, are generally less conducive to bacterial persistence than the cooler and more humid conditions commonly found in enclosed areas such as emergency units and intensive care units11.

The study found that the Emergency Unit had the highest contamination rate at 41.3% (N = 323), followed by the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit at 26.7% (N = 209). Both units benefit from extensive air conditioning. Interestingly, the outpatient department (OPD) recorded a contamination rate of 17.8% (N = 140), slightly higher than the malnutrition unit’s 14.2% (N = 111), despite the latter being cooler. This may be due to the OPD’s higher foot traffic, which involves numerous patients and relatives, whereas the Malnutrition Unit only admits children with protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) and their mothers for care. Understanding opportunistic and environmental bacteria highlights the risk of pathogen transmission from contaminated surfaces to vulnerable patients, especially infants, in healthcare settings. Although immunocompetent individuals may carry these bacteria without harm, they can later develop community-acquired infections and share the pathogen in the community. Research on less commonly isolated Enterobacter species is limited because their historical classification as general coliforms is based on imprecise identification methods. This study utilised the MALDI-TOF analyser, which is a more accurate technique for species-level differentiation within the Enterobacter genus.

Previous studies have shown that fomites contamination rates are typically higher in neonatal intensive care units than in other hospital departments8,33. However, this study found that Baby’s Cot A in the Emergency Unit had the highest levels of contamination. The increased foot traffic of patients and clinicians in the Emergency Unit of PML, which serves as the country’s only pediatric hospital and manages numerous referral cases before admission to other wards, may contribute to higher risks of bacterial contamination and pathogen transmission. Infection prevention and control measures are more rigorously enforced in the neonatal intensive care unit; clinicians must wash their hands and wear head and foot coverings, and visitor access is strictly limited. In contrast, the emergency unit permits more liberal visitor access, likely contributing to the higher levels of surface contamination observed there.

Thermometers, which are frequently used in hospitals, presented various issues in this study, including malfunctioning and sharing between departments. This practise may lead to increased contamination, particularly in the emergency, outpatient, and neonatal intensive care units. Notably, the fomite with the lowest contamination rate was medication tray A in the Emergency Unit, with only 0.9% contamination (N = 7). Remarkably, no bacterial growth was detected on this surface during 7 of the 12 sampling weeks (58.3%), contrasting with higher environmental contamination levels reported in hospital-based studies of inanimate surfaces1,5,11. The relatively low contamination observed may be associated with the storage of paediatric medications under refrigerated conditions, which can reduce the growth rate and metabolic activity of many clinically important mesophilic bacteria34. In addition, environmental conditions such as reduced temperature and controlled storage environments have been shown to limit microbial survival and persistence in healthcare settings35.

The overall antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among the isolates was high, with 96.1% of Gram-positive and 85.6% of Gram-negative pathogens exhibiting resistance, corresponding to an overall resistance rate of 87.3%. . Although this study focused on fomites, these findings are comparable to those reported by Agyepong et al.7 who reported a similar resistance pattern, with 89.5% of clinical Gram-negative bacteria resistant at the Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital in Kumasi, Ghana. Similarly, Tagoe and Desbordes26 reported a 75% resistance rate among 80% of pathogenic clinical isolates at the Central Regional Hospital in Cape Coast, Ghana. Notably, S. aureus displayed considerable resistance to penicillin (96.3%) and gentamicin (44.4%), which is consistent with Agyepong et al.’s7 report of 57.5% resistance to cefoxitin, a marker characteristic of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA)36. The significant rate of cross-resistance of S. aureus to aminoglycosides, such as gentamicin, and tetracyclines, further corroborates evidence that antimicrobial resistance in S. aureus remains a persistent global public health challenge37. MRSA remains clinically significant, with resistance largely mediated by the mecA gene encoding altered penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a), leading to reduced susceptibility to nearly all β-lactam antibiotics38,39. This reinforces the organism’s adaptability and continued burden in healthcare-associated infections worldwide. Current literature further confirms that S. aureus employs multiple resistance mechanisms, including enzymatic drug inactivation, target site modification, and efflux pump activity, contributing to multidrug resistance across several antibiotic classes40. These mechanisms significantly limit therapeutic options and necessitate ongoing antimicrobial stewardship and surveillance. Despite increasing resistance trends, combination therapies such as ampicillin and gentamicin remain included in some national and international treatment guidelines for specific clinical indications, particularly in empirical neonatal and severe infection management, though their use is increasingly guided by local susceptibility patterns41,42. It is noted that S. aureus isolates in this study showed no resistance to linezolid (0%). In contrast, low resistance rates were observed for rifampicin (3.1%), clindamycin (7.5%), and erythromycin (2.9%).

Among the Gram-negative isolates, Acinetobacter baumannii exhibited high resistance to ceftazidime (68.9%) and ceftriaxone (86.7%), whereas Escherichia coli exhibited 66.7% resistance to tetracycline and 88.9% resistance to ciprofloxacin. The resistance rate of Klebsiella pneumoniae exceeded 50% for multiple antibiotics, with a notable rate of 70.0% for amoxicillin/clavulanic acid. These observed resistance patterns likely reflect selective pressure from frequent antibiotic use, consistent with the prescriptions recommended in the Ghana Standard Treatment Guidelines41. The widespread, often unregulated availability of these medications can also promote self-medication, further contributing to resistance. Less commonly isolated pathogens also recorded high resistance rates, although this may partly reflect the smaller number of isolates subjected to sensitivity testing. However, these pathogens are commonly associated with elevated resistance levels.

Of particular concern is the detection of multidrug-resistant organisms including MRSA and carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria among the contaminants of the fomites. These pathogens pose serious threats to patient safety, as they are associated with limited treatment options, prolonged hospitalizations, increased healthcare costs, and elevated mortality rates. Their presence on environmental fomites creates reservoirs for cross-transmission among vulnerable paediatric and neonatal patients. These findings underscore the urgent need for enhanced infection prevention and control interventions, including rigorous environmental cleaning protocols with appropriate disinfectants, strict hand hygiene compliance monitoring, contact precautions for patients colonized or infected with these organisms, antimicrobial stewardship programmes to prevent further resistance development, and regular surveillance to monitor resistance trends and intervention effectiveness.

Limitations

We acknowledge that patient-shared equipment may introduce cross-contamination between units, which represents a limitation in precisely attributing certain isolates to specific wards. However, this also reflects the realistic movement of pathogens within the hospital environment. Moreover, although Bacillus species were excluded from theprimary analysis as presumed environmental contaminants, certain species, particularly B. cereus, have recognised clinical significance.

Conclusion

The evaluated fomites were heavily contaminated by a diverse range of bacteria with the highest contamination rates observed on thermometers, IV setting trays, thermometer trays, and weighing tables. Antimicrobial rates among the isolated pathogens were moderate to high, underscoring the need to strengthen infection prevention and control measures in hospital settings34–36.
