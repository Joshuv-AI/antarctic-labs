Shape Derivative-Informed Neural Operators with Application to Risk-Averse Shape Optimization
Xindi Gong, Dingcheng Luo, Thomas O’Leary-Roseberry, Ruanui Nicholson, Omar Ghattas

Abstract
Shape optimization under uncertainty (OUU) is computationally intensive for classical PDE-based methods due to the high cost of repeated sampling-based risk evaluation across many uncertainty realizations and varying geometries, while standard neural surrogates often fail to provide accurate and efficient sensitivities for optimization. We introduce Shape-DINO, a derivative-informed neural operator framework for learning PDE solution operators on families of varying geometries, with a particular focus on accelerating PDE-constrained shape OUU. Shape-DINOs encode geometric variability through diffeomorphic mappings to a fixed reference domain and employ a derivative-informed operator learning objective that jointly learns the PDE solution and its Fréchet derivatives with respect to design variables and uncertain parameters, enabling accurate state predictions and reliable gradients for large-scale OUU. We establish a priori error bounds linking surrogate accuracy to optimization error and prove universal approximation results for multi-input reduced basis neural operators in suitable C 1 C^{1} norms. We demonstrate efficiency and scalability on three representative shape OUU problems, including boundary design for a Poisson equation and shape design governed by steady-state Navier–Stokes exterior flows in two and three dimensions. Across these examples, Shape-DINOs produce more reliable optimization results than operator surrogates trained without derivative information. In our examples, Shape-DINOs achieve 3–8 orders-of-magnitude speedups in state and gradient evaluations. Counting training data generation, Shape-DINOs reduce necessary PDE solves by 1–2 orders-of-magnitude compared to a strictly PDE-based approach for a single OUU problem. Moreover, Shape-DINO construction costs can be amortized across many objectives and risk measures, enabling large-scale shape OUU for complex systems.

1
Introduction

Computational models of physical systems often must account for geometry that is either variable or uncertain. An example of the former is shape optimization, where many iterations over a changing geometry are required during optimization to maximize given performance objectives, while an example of the latter is uncertainty quantification, where geometric features such as bathymetry or as-built geometry are uncertain and many geometries must be sampled to propagate that uncertainty into quantities of interest. In many settings, the geometric variability is encoded using problem-specific parameterizations that should be accounted for simultaneously with additional sources of uncertainty, such as spatially varying material properties or forcing fields, further complicating the modeling and analysis of these systems.
We are particularly interested in systems governed by partial differential equations (PDEs). Throughout, we will denote the geometric (shape) parameters by
z
, uncertain parameters by
m
, and the PDE state by
u
, and write the implicitly defined solution map as

(m,z)\mapsto u(m,z)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathcal{R}(u,m,z)=0,

where
\mathcal{R}
denotes an abstract residual form of the governing PDE.

In this work, we develop efficient neural operator learning formulations for the parametric solution map

(m,z)\mapsto u(m,z),

with the goal of accelerating outer-loop tasks involving simultaneous variations in the model parameters and domains.
While the proposed operator learning framework is broadly applicable to a range such tasks, we restrict our attention here to problems of shape optimization under uncertainty (OUU),
which arise naturally when geometric design decisions must be made in the presence of uncertain model parameters.

We treat the uncertainty in the parameters by modeling
m
as a random variable distributed according to a reference probability measure
\mu_{m}
, which may be informed, for example, by prior beliefs or the solution of a Bayesian inverse problem.
As a consequence, any quantities of interest that depend on the PDE solution, i.e.,
Q=Q(u(m,z),m,z)
,
are random variables whose distribution reflects the combined effects of parameter uncertainty and geometric variability.

Rather than optimizing a single realization of
Q
, one instead seeks to optimize a scalar
risk measure

\rho
that encodes the designer’s tolerance to uncertainty
[
1
]
. This leads to the shape OUU problem

\min_{z}\mathcal{J}(z)=\rho_{m\sim\mu_{m}}\!\left(Q(u(m,z),m,z)\right)+\mathcal{P}(z),

(1)

where
\mathcal{P}(z)
is a regularization or cost term on the geometry, enforcing design constraints or prior geometric preferences. Common choices of
\rho
include the expectation, mean–variance functionals, conditional value-at-risk (CVaR), worst-case risk, and entropic risk measures
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. These allow practitioners to interpolate between risk-neutral designs and highly conservative designs that emphasize tail events or worst-case performance
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

Computational challenges

Shape OUU presents several interrelated computational challenges:

1.

Geometric variability.
Modeling changes in geometry typically requires re-meshing or mesh deformation, leading to substantial computational overhead for each variation of the design variable
z
.

2.

Sampling complexity.
The estimation of risk measures requires repeated evaluations of the forward model across many realizations of the uncertain parameters
m
, resulting in prohibitively large sampling costs.

3.

Fast online evaluation.
Efficient (online) query capabilities are essential to mitigate the high sample complexity inherent in optimization under uncertainty.

4.

Derivative accuracy.
Nonlinear optimization algorithms require accurate gradients and, for fast convergence, Hessian information.
In the context of risk-averse shape optimization, this necessitates the ability to differentiate the PDE solution with respect to the design variables
z
(i.e., the shape sensitivities).

Because these difficulties are tightly coupled, shape OUU quickly becomes computationally intractable when the cost of a single high-fidelity PDE solve is large, as is typical in complex three-dimensional simulations. In such settings, the combined demands of sampling, geometric variation, and derivative evaluation overwhelm traditional optimization workflows.

This computational bottleneck has driven the development of advanced surrogate modeling techniques, such as neural operators, that explicitly account for geometric variability and thereby enable tasks such as shape optimization. In this context, neural operators
[
8
]
approximate the parametric solution map
(m,z)\mapsto u(m,z)
by a nonlinear surrogate
u_{\theta}(m,z)
, parameterized by weights
\theta
, which encodes discretization-invariant structure in the inputs and outputs to provide efficient approximations.

However, existing neural operator approaches typically have not addressed the simultaneous parametrization of auxiliary spatial fields
m
along with the shape parameters
z
, nor do they explicitly control errors in the surrogate approximations of the
Fréchet derivatives of PDE states
u
with respect to the design and uncertain parameters,
D_{z}u
and
D_{m}u
. As a consequence, their deployment in optimization settings lacks guarantees on the accuracy of the resulting gradients and, ultimately, on the quality of the computed optimal designs. This gap motivates the approach developed in this work.

1.1
Contributions

In this work, we develop
shape derivative-informed
neural operators to address the aforementioned challenges in a unified manner. Our approach accommodates geometric variability by representing families of PDEs through diffeomorphic mappings to a common reference domain. This formulation enables the entire learning problem to be posed on a fixed reference mesh, while the trained operator remains capable of making predictions across a broad range of geometric transformations.

The central contribution of this work is the extension of derivative-informed operator learning to this geometrically complex setting. We construct a unified surrogate that is jointly parameterized by the shape variables
z
and auxiliary spatial fields
m
, and is explicitly trained to accurately approximate both the PDE solution
u
and its Fréchet derivatives with respect to each set of parameters.

Our main contributions are as follows.

(C1)

A priori error bounds for surrogate-driven optimization and inversion.

We analyze optimization problems of the form (
1
) where the objective
\mathcal{J}(z)
is approximated by a surrogate objective
\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(z):=\rho_{m\sim\mu}(Q(u_{\theta}(m,z),m,z))+\mathcal{P}(z)
,
using an approximate solution operator
u_{\theta}(m,z)
.
In particular, standard techniques from optimization theory are used to show that when
\mathcal{J}
is strongly convex,
the optimization errors of first-order stationary points of the surrogate objective
\mathcal{J}_{\theta}

can be bounded in terms of errors in the objective’s derivatives,
D_{z}\mathcal{J}-D_{z}\mathcal{J}_{\theta}
(
Theorem

2.1
).
Moreover, in the risk neutral setting where
\rho(\cdot)=\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
,
we show that this derivative error of the objective is bounded by a combination of the
L^{2}_{\mu}
errors of the output
\|u(m,z)-u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}
, and the
z
-derivative
\|D_{z}u(m,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(m,z))\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}
. These results provide theoretical justification for derivative-informed operator learning in the context of risk-neutral optimization, and can be easily extended to other settings such as risk averse optimization and inverse problems.

(C2)

Approximation theory for multi-input neural operators.

We establish universal approximation results for neural operators demonstrating that multi-input neural operators
u_{\theta}(m,z)

can approximate both the solution operator
u(m,z)
and its
z
-derivatives
D_{z}u(m,z)
arbitrarily well over compact domains for
z
but possibly unbounded domains in
m
(
Theorem

3.1
). Moreover, in the risk neutral setting, this implies that the surrogate objective yields stationary points with arbitrarily small optimization error (
Theorem

3.2
). These results justify the use of neural operators for shape optimization under uncertainty as characterized by first-order stationary points of the corresponding optimization problems.

(C3)

Efficient algorithms for shape derivative-informed operator learning.

We develop practical algorithms for learning operators over families of geometries by representing arbitrary diffeomorphic transformations
\chi_{z}
of a reference mesh via extensions.
We present efficient formulations of the governing PDEs posed either on the deformed domain or, equivalently, on the reference domain using the inverse diffeomorphism
\chi_{z}^{-1}
.
We further delineate conditions for existence and efficient computation of derivatives of the solution with respect to both
m
and
z
, leveraging the implicit function theorem together with adjoint and forward sensitivity methods. Building on these components, we extend multi-input reduced-basis derivative-informed neural operators
[
9
]
to the shape-parametric setting.

We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework through a collection of challenging operator learning problems involving geometric variability, culminating in risk-averse PDE-constrained shape optimization problems. Empirically, the proposed approach yields two key benefits:
(i) improved optimization accuracy, consistent with the bounds on the optimization error developed for the risk neutral setting,
and (ii) improved generalization accuracy per unit of computational effort.

2.1
Notation for Operator Learning on Hilbert Spaces

We begin by introducing some common notation used for operator learning on Hilbert spaces.
Additional notation will be introduced where they are used.
In this work, we will consider state variables, model parameters, and design variables that belong to (finite- or infinite-dimensional) separable Hilbert spaces over
\mathbb{R}
.
Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is such a Hilbert space, we will use
\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}
to denote the inner product on
\mathcal{X}
, which generates the norm
\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}}=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}}
.
We will use
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
to denote its topological dual and
\langle l,v\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{X}}
to denote the duality pairing between
l\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
and
v\in\mathcal{X}
.
For Hilbert spaces,
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
can be identified with
\mathcal{X}
by the Riesz map,
\mathsf{R}_{\mathcal{X}}:\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, i.e., for
l\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
,

\langle\mathsf{R}_{\mathcal{X}}l,v\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}=\langle l,v\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{X}}.

We use
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
to denote the space of bounded linear operators between the two Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
.
This is a Banach space with the operator norm
\|A\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}=\sup_{\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}}=1}\|Av\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
For any
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, we let
A^{*}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{X})
denote the adjoint of
A

and
A^{T}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime},\mathcal{X}^{\prime})
denote the transpose of
A
,
such that
\langle w,Av\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}=\langle A^{*}w,v\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}
and

\langle l,Av\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{Y}}=\langle A^{T}l,v\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{X}}

for any
v\in\mathcal{X},w\in\mathcal{Y}
, and
l\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
.
In particular, when
v\in\mathcal{X}
is a vector, we have
v^{T}=\mathsf{R}_{\mathcal{X}}^{-1}v
.
We use this notation to define the outer product of two vectors
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
,
denoted
v_{1}v_{2}^{T}
,
which is the linear operator given by
(v_{1}v_{2}^{T})v_{3}=v_{1}\langle v_{2},v_{3}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}
.

We will also consider the space of Hilbert–Schmidt operators
\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, which include continuous linear operators that have finite Hilbert–Schmidt norm, defined as

\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|Av_{i}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2},

where
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is any orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{X}
.
We note that when either
\mathcal{X}
or
\mathcal{Y}
is finite dimensional, the Hilbert–Schmidt norm is equivalent to the operator norm.

We will represent uncertainty in the model parameters through Borel probability measures
\mu
on Hilbert spaces.
For Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
,
we let
L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})

denote the Bochner space of operators
F:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
that are Bochner integrable with finite norm, i.e.,

\|F\|_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}^{p}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[\|F(x)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{p}]=\int\|F(x)\|^{p}_{\mathcal{Y}}d\mu(x)<\infty

when
p\in[1,\infty)
, and

\|F\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})}=\esssup_{x}\|F(x)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}.

when
p=\infty
. Note that we will occasionally use the shorthand
L^{p}_{\mu}
instead of
L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})
when the input and output spaces are clear.

Additionally, we will consider mappings that are Fréchet differentiable.
For such a mapping,
F:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, we denote its derivative at
x\in\mathcal{X}
as
D_{x}F(x)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, such that

\lim_{\|h\|_{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\|F(x+h)-F(x)-D_{x}F(x)h\|_{\mathcal{Y}}}{\|h\|_{\mathcal{X}}}=0.

Moreover, we will write the space of continuously Fréchet differentiable mappings as

C^{1}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})
or
C^{1}(\mathcal{B};\mathcal{Y})

if we are considering a subset of the input space
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{X}
.

2.2
Partial Differential Equations on Parametric Domains

For shape optimization, we consider a family of spatial domains
\Omega_{z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d=2,3
, parametrized by finite-dimensional shape variables
z\in\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}

Specifically, we assume each
\Omega_{z}
is coupled to a reference domain
\Omega_{0}
through a

\chi_{z}:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a hold-all domain containing the closure of
\Omega_{0}
.
Moreover, we assume that the reference domain
\Omega_{0}
is bounded and Lipschitz, such that for each
z
within some closed admissible set
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}\subset\mathcal{Z}
, the resulting spatial domain
\Omega_{z}=\chi_{z}(\Omega_{0})
is also bounded and Lipschitz.

We are concerned with PDE problems defined on the family of domains
\Omega_{z}
.
Such PDE problems relate the shape variable
z

\widetilde{m}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{z})
such as spatially varying coefficient fields or forcing terms,

\widetilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z})

Here,
\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{z})
and
\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z})
are separable Hilbert spaces for the model parameters and the state, respectively, and are defined over the spatial domain
\Omega_{z}
.
These are typically given as Lebesgue spaces
L^{2}(\Omega_{z})
or Sobolev spaces
H^{k}(\Omega_{z})
. Then, for each instance of
\Omega_{z}

the PDE problem can be stated abstractly in residual form as

\text{given }\Omega_{z}\text{ and }\widetilde{m}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{z}),\quad\text{find }\widetilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z})\quad\text{such that}\quad\mathcal{R}_{z}(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{m})=0,

(2)

where
\mathcal{R}_{z}:\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z})\times\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{z})\rightarrow\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{z})^{\prime}

\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{z})^{\prime}
.

Alternatively, we can state the PDE on the reference domain
\Omega_{0}
using the pullback through
\chi_{z}
.
To this end, let
\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{0})
and
\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})
be the corresponding function spaces defined on the reference domain
\Omega_{0}
.
Then, (

) can be equivalently formulated on

\text{given }z\in\mathcal{Z}\text{ and }m\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{0}),\quad\text{find }u\in\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})\quad\text{such that}\quad\mathcal{R}(u,m,z)=0,

(3)

where (
3
) is derived from (
2
)
through the fact that
u=\widetilde{u}\circ\chi_{z}
,

,
and
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})\times\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{0})\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{0})^{\prime}

is given by the appropriate change of variable of the associated operators.
We list the coordinate transformations for some commonly used differential and integral operations in
Section

A.1
. Note that in (
3

Under the assumption that the PDE admits a unique solution for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
and
m\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{0})

such that we can abstractly write

(5a)

\displaystyle\|T_{\chi}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H^{1}(\Omega_{1}),H^{1}(\Omega_{0}))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\|\chi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{0})}^{2}\right)\|\chi^{-1}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{1})}^{d}.

(5b)

Proof.

The fact that problems of the form (
2
) can be equivalently formulated as (
3
) is of essential importance to this work.
Due to this fact, we can formulate both the shape optimization problem and the operator learning task for all
\Omega_{z}
on
\Omega_{0}
via the parametrization with shape parameter
z
, which now explicitly appears in the residual operator
\mathcal{R}
in (
3
).

2.3
Derivatives of the Solution Operator

In this work, we assume that that solution operator is continuously differentiable with respect to
m
and
z
.
Generally, this allows us to solve shape optimization problems involving the PDE by derivative-based optimization algorithms.
More specific assumptions will be made in subsequent sections when deriving formal theoretical results.

Since we are working directly in the reference domain, the Fréchet derivative operators
D_{m}u(m,z)
and
D_{z}u(m,z)
can be computed through implicit differentiation of the PDE
(
3
)
as in standard forward and adjoint sensitivity methods, and does not need to explicitly involve shape calculus.
That is, at any
m,z
and
u(m,z)
and for any direction of the input space
(h_{m},h_{z})\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{Z}
,
the derivative action, or the Jacobian-vector product (JVP),
h_{u}:=D_{m}u(m,z)h_{m}+D_{z}u(m,z)h_{z}
is given by
the solution of the linearized state equation

D_{u}\mathcal{R}(u,m,z)h_{u}=-\left(D_{m}\mathcal{R}(u,m,z)h_{m}+D_{z}\mathcal{R}(u,m,z)h_{z}\right).

(6)

On the other hand, to compute the action of the derivative’s adjoint, or the vector-Jacobian product (VJP), in some direction
v\in\mathcal{U}
,
we solve the adjoint equation

D_{u}\mathcal{R}(u,m,z)^{T}p=-v^{T},

(7)

for the adjoint variable
p\in\mathcal{U}
, such that

v^{T}D_{m}u(m,z)=D_{m}\mathcal{R}(u,m,z)^{T}p,\qquad v^{T}D_{z}u(m,z)=D_{z}\mathcal{R}(u,m,z)^{T}p.

(8)

The forms above imply that having already computed the solution
u=u(m,z)
, the cost of subsequently computing a single VJP or JVP amounts to solving a single linear PDE with the operator
D_{u}\mathcal{R}
or its adjoint (transpose).
Moreover, at a single point
(m,z,u(m,z))
, the computation of multiple derivative actions always shares the same linear operator
D_{u}\mathcal{R}
, with the only change being the right hand side of (
6
) or (
7
); this often leads to significant computational savings when linear algebraic operations are amortizable.

We note that although we will frequently refer to derivatives with respect to
z
as
shape derivatives
,
this type of differentiation aligns more closely with the notion of
material derivatives
in shape optimization,
since the derivatives are not only affected by the motion of the domain boundary,
\partial\Omega_{z}
,
but also by the motion of the interior through the definition of
\chi_{z}
(for example, see
[
61
, Chapter 11]
).

2.4
Shape Optimization under Uncertainty

Let
Q_{z}:\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z})\times\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{z})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

define a scalar quantity of interest (QoI) that depends on the model parameters, state variables, and indexed by the shape variables
z
that define the domain
\Omega_{z}
.
Commonly, these are formulated as integral quantities over the domain
\Omega_{z}
or its subsets, such as in tracking objectives, forces, energies, etc.
In shape optimization problems, we are interested in minimizing
Q_{z}(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{m})
with respect to
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
subject to the constraint that
(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{m})
solves the PDE (
2
).
As in the case of the PDE residual,
we can reformulate the QoI by writing it in terms of reference-domain quantities, such that

Q(u,m,z)=Q_{z}(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{m}),

(9)

with
u=T_{\chi}\widetilde{u}
and
m=T_{\chi}\widetilde{m}
.
One then aims to minimize
Q(u,m,z)
where
u=u(m,z)
solves (
3
).

Often, the model parameters
m
are uncertain,
which implies that for each fixed
z
, the resulting value of the QoI is also uncertain.
In this work, we model the uncertainty in
m
through its probability distribution
\mu_{m}
, defined as a Borel probability measure over
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{0})
.
Subsequently, for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
, the quantity
Q(u(\cdot,z),\cdot,z)
is a random variable whose value depends on the realization of
m
.
Thus, under appropriate moment assumptions on
Q(u(\cdot,z),\cdot)

(e.g., it belongs to
L^{p}_{\mu_{m}}(\mathcal{M};\mathbb{R})
)
the shape optimization problem can be formulated using a risk measure
\rho:L^{p}_{\mu_{m}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
; a functional that provides a statistical summary of the distribution and chosen to express a particular risk preference.
The shape OUU problem is then given by

\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\mathcal{J}(z)=\rho_{m\sim\mu_{m}}(Q(u(m,z),m,z))+\mathcal{P}(z)

(10)

where
\mathcal{P}:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a penalization/regularization term that accounts for any additional cost of the shape associated with
z
.

In this work, we consider the following risk measures in our numerical examples, which we introduce below in terms of a generic random variable
\xi\sim\mu_{\xi}
.

Mean

The canonical example of a risk measure is the mean (or expectation) of the random variable,

\rho_{\xi\sim\mu_{\xi}}^{\mathrm{mean}}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mu_{\xi}}[\xi].

(11)

The mean is risk neutral and does not explicitly account for events in the upper tails of the distributions, which often represent catastrophic failures in engineering systems.
For this reason, we also consider two risk-averse functionals as examples.

Conditional value-at-risk

The conditional value-at-risk (CVaR), which is defined for a quantile
\beta\in[0,1]

\rho_{\xi\sim\mu_{\xi}}^{\mathrm{CVaR},\beta}(\xi)=\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}{t+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\mathbb{E}[(\xi-t)^{+}]},

(12)

where
(x)^{+}:=\max(x,0)
.
When the underlying random variable
\xi
is continuously distributed, the CVaR can be interpreted as the conditional expectation of
\xi
given that it exceeds its
\beta
quantile.
As a result, the CVaR can be used to explicitly control the upper tail of the distributions and has been explored for risk-averse design in engineering
[
3
,
62
]

Entropic risk measure

The entropic risk measure is given by

\rho_{\xi\sim\mu_{\xi}}^{\mathrm{entropic},\beta}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\mathbb{E}[\exp(\beta\xi)]\right),

(13)

where the parameter
\beta>0
specifies the degree of risk aversion.
The entropic risk measure has also been considered for risk-averse optimization of physical systems, such as in
[
63
]
.

Remark 2.2

(On the distribution of the model parameters)
.

In this work, we will focus on the case where the distribution of the model parameter
\mu_{m}
is defined directly on the reference domain.
In this case, the uncertain parameter field in the spatial domain
\Omega_{z}
for any
z
is the pushforward (in terms of the spatial variables) by
\chi_{z}

from a random field defined on the reference domain
\Omega_{0}
, i.e.,
\widetilde{m}=T_{\chi_{z}}^{-1}m
and
m\sim\mu_{m}
.
This choice is made to simplify the notation and the required variable transformations.
Nevertheless, our formulations and procedures can be extended to cases where the uncertain parameter field is prescribed on the spatial domain via more complicated transformations of some underlying random element.

2.5
Optimization via Sample Average Approximation

One conventional approach to solving the OUU problem (
10
)
is by replacing expectations with sample average approximations (SAA) taken from i.i.d. samples
m^{(i)}\sim\mu_{m},i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{SAA}}
.
This defines a new objective function, in which the risk measure component of (
10
) is replaced by its SAA.

For example, when using
\rho=\rho^{\mathrm{mean}}
,
the SAA of the objective function becomes

\widehat{\mathcal{J}}^{\mathrm{mean}}(z):=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{SAA}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{SAA}}}Q(u(m^{(i)},z),m^{(i)},z).

(14)

Similarly, for the entropic risk measure, we take

\widehat{\mathcal{J}}^{\mathrm{entropic}}(z):=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{SAA}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{SAA}}}\exp(\beta Q(u(m^{(i)},z),m^{(i)},z))\right).

(15)

In the case of the CVaR, in addition to the SAA, we will also take a smooth approximation to the maximum function,
(x)_{\varepsilon}^{+}\approx(x)^{+}
,

(x)^{+}_{\epsilon}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $x<0$}\\
\frac{x^{3}}{\epsilon^{2}}-\frac{x^{4}}{2\epsilon^{3}},&\text{if $0\leq x\leq\epsilon$}\\
x-\frac{\epsilon}{2},&\text{$x\geq\epsilon$},\end{cases}

(16)

with a small smoothing parameter
\epsilon\ll 1

[
3
]
.
This smoothing preserves convexity and ensures uniform convergence
(x)^{+}_{\epsilon}\rightarrow(x)^{+}
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.
The resulting approximation to the CVaR objective function is then given by

\widehat{\mathcal{J}}^{\mathrm{CVaR},\beta,\varepsilon}(z,t):=t+\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{SAA}}(1-\beta)}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{SAA}}}(Q(u(m^{(i)},z),m^{(i)},z)-t)_{\varepsilon}^{+},

(17)

which preserves differentiability with respect to
(z,t)
.
Finally, the SAA for the objective function is a combination of the risk measure
and the penalization, which we minimize with respect to
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}

\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\widehat{\mathcal{J}}(z):=\widehat{\mathcal{J}}^{\mathrm{risk}}(z)+\mathcal{P}(z)

(18)

or in the case of CVaR, additionally over
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

The resulting SAA objective function can then be minimized by conventional derivative-based optimization algorithms, e.g., quasi Newton methods (or Newton if the problem has sufficient smoothness),
where the derivatives can be efficiently computed by the adjoint method.
That is, in order to compute the derivative of
the mapping

z\mapsto Q(u(m^{(i)},z),m^{(i)},z)

with respect to
z
, we can proceed by the chain rule,

D_{z}(Q(u(m^{(i)},z),m^{(i)},z))=D_{u}Q(u(m^{(i)},z),m^{(i)},z))D_{z}u(m^{(i)},z)+D_{z}Q(u(m^{(i)},z),m^{(i)},z).

The first term is simply a VJP of the solution operator
(v^{(i)})^{T}D_{z}u(m^{(i)},z)
in the direction

v^{(i)}=\mathsf{R}_{\mathcal{V}}D_{u}Q(u(m^{(i)},z),m^{(i)},z)

and can be evaluated by solving the adjoint PDE (
7
) once for each
m^{(i)}
,
while the latter term typically has an analytically available form and does not require solving additional PDEs.

Evidently, the per-iteration computational cost of shape optimization under uncertainty
tends to be dominated by (1) evaluating the objective function via
n_{\mathrm{SAA}}
solutions of the state PDE (i.e., evaluating
u(m^{(i)},z)
)
and (2) subsequently evaluating its gradient through
n_{\mathrm{SAA}}
solutions of the adjoint equation.
For this reason, accurately solving PDE-constrained OUU problems can often lead to prohibitive computational costs.
This is especially true when the risk measure requires a large number of samples to accurately estimate and when the underlying PDE is expensive to solve.

2.6
Optimization using Operator Surrogates

In order to alleviate the computational cost associated with PDE-constrained OUU,
one can replace the high-fidelity PDE model with a surrogate model that is of lower fidelity but is fast to evaluate.
In this work, we are interested in operator surrogates, i.e.,
approximations to the solution operator
u_{\theta}(m,z)\approx u(m,z)
.
This defines an approximation to the optimization problem (
10
), i.e.,

\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(z):=\rho_{m\sim\mu_{m}}(Q(u_{\theta}(m,z),m,z))+\mathcal{P}(z)

(19)

which uses the surrogate objective function
\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(z)
as an approximation to the true objective function
\mathcal{J}(z)
.

Since the surrogate model is fast to evaluate, (
19
) can be rapidly solved using an SAA with large number of samples, essentially eliminating the sampling error.
Instead, the approximation error of the surrogate model becomes the dominant source of error in solving the OUU problem.
Thus, the tradeoff in surrogate-based optimization becomes one of balancing the approximation accuracy of the surrogate with its construction cost
measured in the size of the training dataset. Conventionally, the training dataset consists of input-output pairs,
(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})\mapsto u^{(i)}=u(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})
over the input space,
where each data tuple is generated by solving the underlying PDE.

Moreover, as noted in
[
9
]
, when
\mathcal{Z}
is high dimensional,
derivative-based algorithms are still required to effectively solve (
19
) (or its SAA).
Thus, the errors introduced by the surrogate in approximating
\mathcal{J}(z)

and its derivatives
D_{z}\mathcal{J}(z)
(or equivalently, its gradients
\mathsf{R}_{\mathcal{Z}}D_{z}\mathcal{J}(z)
)
are both important.
In particular, mismatch of the derivatives (
D_{z}\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(z)
compared to
D_{z}\mathcal{J}(z)
) can introduce spurious local minima in
\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(z)

even when the original PDE-constrained OUU problem is well-posed.

For example, when the underlying objective function is strongly convex and differentiable, the error in the approximate stationary points are controlled explicitly by their gradient errors.
We summarize this in the following theorem, the proof of which is based on standard variational analysis and is presented in
Appendix

B
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{Z}
is an open set and
\mathcal{J}\in C^{1}(\mathcal{B};\mathbb{R})
is a
\lambda
-strongly convex objective function, i.e., given
\lambda>0
,

\mathcal{J}(z_{2})\geq\mathcal{J}(z_{1})+\langle D_{z}\mathcal{J}(z_{1}),z_{2}-z_{1}\rangle_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{Z}}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|z_{2}-z_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}

(20)

for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
.
Additionally, suppose
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}\subset\mathcal{B}
is a closed, convex, and nonempty set, and let
z^{\star}
be the unique minimizer of
\mathcal{J}
over
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
.
Furthermore, let
\mathcal{J}_{\theta}\in C^{1}(\mathcal{B};\mathbb{R})
be an approximation of
\mathcal{J}
and let
z^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
be any point satisfying the first order optimality conditions, i.e.,

\langle D_{z}\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(z^{\dagger}),z-z^{\dagger}\rangle_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{Z}}\geq 0\qquad\forall z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}.

(21)

Then, the optimization error is bounded by

\|z^{\star}-z^{\dagger}\|_{\mathcal{Z}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\|D_{z}\mathcal{J}(z^{\dagger})-D_{z}\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(z^{\dagger})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}

(22)

and the optimality gap is bounded by

\mathcal{J}(z^{\dagger})-\mathcal{J}(z^{\star})\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|D_{z}\mathcal{J}(z^{\dagger})-D_{z}{\mathcal{J}}_{\theta}(z^{\dagger})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}^{2}.

(23)

3
Shape Derivative-Informed Operator Learning

Motivated by the previous discussion, we seek formulations of operator learning for shape varying systems that provide theoretical and practical guarantees for controlling the derivative errors discussed in the previous section.
In this section we proceed by deriving various universal approximation theorems for the simultaneous control of the operator error and the derivatives with respect to the shape parameters when using reduced basis architectures.
This universal approximation theory is specifically targeted to the OUU problem, which does not specifically require control of the derivatives with respect to
m
.
After this we discuss a practical formulation for training shape derivative-informed neural operators.
We also describe an effective strategy for computing reduced bases for the parameter and state spaces, with a discussion on the required adaptations in the context of shape optimization under uncertainty.

65
,
64
,
56
]
, which is also known as the principal component analysis (PCA).
For the input basis
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, common choices used in operator learning include the POD (PCA), as well as active subspace (AS) bases that are constructed using the derivatives of the underlying mapping
[
66
,
67
,
60
]
.
We elaborate on these approaches in
Section

3.4
.
For the shape, we assume that
z
is a finite parametrization of the diffeomorphism
\chi_{z}
, and we do not perform further dimension reduction.
Nevertheless, one can also consider analogous dimension reduction strategies when the hypothesized space of admissible designs is infinite or high dimensional.

In a reduced basis neural operator (RBNO), the mapping from the input coefficient space to the output coefficient space is typically approximated by a dense neural network,
g_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{r_{m}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}}
.
The network takes inputs
a_{0}=(m_{r},z)\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{m}+d_{z}}
and maps to the output
a_{d_{L}+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}}
, i.e.,
a_{d_{L}+1}=g_{\theta}(a_{0})
,
through
d_{L}
hidden layers with widths
d_{W,1},d_{W,2},\dots,d_{W,d_{L}}
, where each
a_{i}
is

\displaystyle a_{i}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{i-1}a_{i-1}+b_{i-1}),\quad i=1,2,\dots,d_{L},

\displaystyle a_{d_{L}+1}

\displaystyle=W_{d_{L}}a_{d_{L}}+b_{d_{L}}.

The collection of network parameters is
\theta=\{(W_{i},b_{i})\}^{d_{L}}_{i=0},
where the weights are of dimensions

W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{W,1}\times(r_{m}+d_{z})},\quad W_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{W,i+1}\times d_{W,i}},i=1,2,\dots,d_{L-1},\quad W_{d_{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}\times d_{W,d_{L}}},

and the biases are of dimensions

b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{W,i+1}},\qquad i=0,\dots,d_{L-1},\quad b_{d_{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}}.

Here, we focus on the case where
g_{\theta}
uses the Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) as the activation function,

\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}(x)=x\Phi_{\text{Gauss}}(x),

(29)

where
\Phi_{\text{Gauss}}(x)
is the cumulative distribution function for the standard Gaussian distribution.
The full neural operator architecture then takes the form

u_{\theta}(m,z)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}(m-b_{m}),z)+b_{u},

(30)

where
b_{u}\in\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})
and
b_{m}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{0})
are bias terms that can be selected based on the particular choices of reduced bases.

3.2
Universal Approximation Results for Reduced Basis Neural Operators

Under
2.1
, we can show that the reduced basis architectures (
30
) can approximate the solution operator
u(m,z)
and its
z
-derivative to arbitrary accuracy in the following sense:

Theorem 3.1

(Universal approximation via multi-input neural operators)
.

Suppose
u
satisfies
2.1

with the Borel probability measure
\mu_{m}
, compact admissible set
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
, and open set
\mathcal{B}\supset\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
for some
p\geq 2
.
Let
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}

be orthonormal bases of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{M}
, respectively.
Additionally, let
b_{u}\in\mathcal{U}
and
b_{m}\in\mathcal{M}
be any two vectors.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0

there exist ranks
r_{m}
and
r_{u}
along with a GELU neural network

g_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{r_{m}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}}

such that the RBNO
u_{\theta}(m,z)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}(m-b_{m}),z)+b_{u}
satisfies

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|u(m,z)-u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{p}]+\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|D_{z}u(m,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}^{p}]\leq\epsilon^{p}.

(31)

The proof of
Theorem

3.1
is presented in
Appendix

C
. Supporting lemmas are first developed in
Sections

C.1
and
C.2
. These are then combined to complete the proof in
Section

C.3
.

Note that the approximation error of the neural operator is measured in a mixed norm, being taken as an
L^{p}_{\mu_{m}}
norm in the
m
direction and as a supremum in the
z
direction over the compact admissible set.
This matches the form of the approximation error required to accurately represent the first-order optimality conditions of the OUU problem (e.g.,
Proposition

2.2
).
In particular,
Theorem

3.1
implies that the RBNO architecture is capable of approximating the solution operator such that when used as a surrogate for expectation minimization (assuming the true cost functional is strongly convex), its stationary points can be arbitrarily close to the true minimizer.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
u
satisfies
2.1
and
Q
satisfies
2.2

with the Borel probability measure
\mu_{m}
, a convex and compact admissible set
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
, and an open set
\mathcal{B}\supset\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
for some
p\geq 2
.
Consider now the optimization problem with the mean objective,

\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\mathcal{J}(z):=\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[Q(u(m,z),m,z)]+\mathcal{P}(z),

where
Q
,
u
, and
\mathcal{P}
are such that
\mathcal{J}\in C^{1}(\mathcal{B};\mathbb{R})
is strongly convex. Let
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be orthonormal bases of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{M}
, respectively, and let

b_{u}\in\mathcal{U}
and
b_{m}\in\mathcal{M}
be any two vectors.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0

there exist ranks
r_{m}
and
r_{u}
along with a GELU neural network

g_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{r_{m}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}}

such that
any point
z^{\dagger}
satisfying the first-order optimality conditions of the surrogate minimization problem

\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(z):=\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[Q(u_{\theta}(m,z),m,z)]+\mathcal{P}(z)

with the RBNO
u_{\theta}(m,z)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}(m-b_{m}),z)+b_{u}

has its optimization error bounded by

\|z^{\star}-z^{\dagger}\|_{\mathcal{Z}}\leq\epsilon

(32)

and its optimality gap bounded by

\mathcal{J}(z^{\dagger})-\mathcal{J}(z^{\star})\leq\epsilon^{2}.

(33)

Theorem

3.2
combines the optimization error bounds in
Theorem

2.1
, the error expressions in
Proposition

2.2
, with the universal approximation result
Theorem

3.1
. We present its proof in
Section

C.4
.
While the strong convexity assumption is strict, we note that it can often be achieved in practice when using strongly convex penalization functionals
\mathcal{P}
.
Alternatively, one can reinterpret this result in terms of a bounded neighborhood around the true minimizer
z^{\star}
over which the objective is strongly convex.
Then, derivative accuracy of the surrogate within such a neighborhood yields bounded optimization errors for any surrogate stationary points that fall within the neighborhood (see, for example,
[
55
]
).

3.3
Shape Derivative-Informed Operator Learning

Although
Theorem

3.2
suggests that the approximation error can be controlled uniformly over
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
when
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
is compact,
in practice, it is typically not possible to ascertain supremum errors bounds via supervised learning.
Instead, as a practically actionable operator learning formulation, we adopt mean-squared-error (MSE) losses for the output values and the Fréchet derivatives of the operator in order to improve accuracy in the derivatives, following
[
51
,
9
]
. To this end, we consider a joint distribution
\mu:=\mu_{m}\otimes\mu_{z}
, where
\mu_{z}
is a postulated sampling distribution over
\mathcal{Z}
whose support contains
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
.
We then solve the following problem in constructing our operator surrogate:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}\bigg[\|u(m,z)-u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|D_{m}u(m,z)-D_{m}u_{\theta}(m,z)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{U})}

\displaystyle+\|D_{z}u(m,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(m,z)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}\bigg],

(34)

We refer to this Sobolev training formulation as the
H^{1}_{\mu}
formulation, since the loss function resembles
H^{1}
Sobolev norm in the finite dimensional context.
This is in contrast to the conventional
L^{2}_{\mu}
training formulation,

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}[\|u(m,z)-u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}],

(35)

which does not involve the derivative error.

Note that in (
3.3
) we have additionally included the derivative with respect to the uncertain parameter,
D_{m}u
, in the training loss.
This differs from the approach presented in
[
9
]
.
Although this is not required for approximation theory presented in
Theorems

2.1
and
3.2
,
it does serve as additional training data that can be used to improve the generalization accuracy.
Moreover, as we will discuss in
Section

4.2.1
, the computational cost of generating this additional Jacobian data is negligible.

The formulation of (
3.3
) is general and applies to any neural operator architecture. However, training on the full
\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{U})
and
\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})
norms for the derivatives can lead to prohibitive computational costs, both in generating the derivative (Jacobian) data and in evaluating the loss during training.
Instead, to make
H^{1}_{\mu}
learning tractable, we leverage the nature of reduced basis architectures (
30
)
and project both the state and Jacobian directly in the span reduced bases.
This leads to the latent space form of the training problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}\bigg[

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}(u(m,z)-b_{u})-g_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}(m-b_{m}),z)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}D_{m}u(m,z)\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}-D_{1}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}(m-b_{m}),z)\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle+\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}D_{z}u(m,z)-D_{2}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}(m-b_{m}),z)\|^{2}_{F}\bigg],

(36)

where
D_{i}
denotes the derivative with respect to the
i^{th}
argument. Since the output and derivatives of the RBNO lie only in the reduced subspaces, (
3.3
) is equivalent to (
3.3
).
Moreover, as the losses are computed only in the latent spaces of dimensions
r_{m}
,
d_{z}
, and
r_{u}
, training can be performed efficiently.
This approach was developed in
[
51
]
and has been utilized in various other works
[
52
,
53
,
9
]
.
We note that the
L^{2}_{\mu}
loss (
35
) can analogously be projected into the latent space, amounting to just the first term in (
3.3
).

3.4
Practical Dimension Reduction

Although any choice of orthonormal bases suffices for the purpose of the universal approximation results of
Theorems

3.1
and
3.2
,
an effective dimension reduction strategy allows smaller input and output ranks to be chosen thereby requiring a smaller latent space neural network.
To this end, we adopt the strategy considered for the DIPNet architecture
[
67
]
.
Namely, we use POD for the output
u
and AS for the uncertain parameter
m
,
which balances computational practicality with effectiveness of dimension reduction.
Both are constructed using the data-generating distribution
\mu:=\mu_{m}\otimes\mu_{z}
.

Here, we present the dimension reduction procedures and highlight small modifications to the standard theoretical results that arise due to (1) the need to account for the changing domains in the state variable and (2) only performing dimension reduction on one of the two input variables (namely, the uncertain parameter).

3.4.1
Proper Orthogonal Decomposition for the State Variable

We compute the POD directly on the reference domain.
That is, we use the covariance operator for the state variable,

\mathcal{C}_{u}=\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}[(u(m,z)-\bar{u})(u(m,z)-\bar{u})^{T}],

(37)

where
\bar{u}=\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}[u(m,z)]
is the mean over the training distribution,
and the integrand consists of the outer product

(u(m,z)-\bar{u})(u(m,z)-\bar{u})^{T}v=(u(m,z)-\bar{u})\langle u(m,z)-\bar{u},v\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}
.
The POD basis is then given by the first
r_{u}
eigenvectors of
\mathcal{C}_{u}
, i.e.,
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r_{u}}=\{\phi_{i}^{\mathrm{POD}}\}_{i=1}^{r_{u}}
satisfying

\mathcal{C}_{u}\phi_{i}^{\mathrm{POD}}=\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{POD}}\phi_{i}^{\mathrm{POD}},\qquad i\in\mathbb{N},

(38)

where
\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{POD}}
are given in descending order.
When using the mean-shifted POD, we adopt
b_{u}=\bar{u}
as the bias term. This essentially corresponds to the affine decoder
u_{r}\mapsto\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}u_{r}+\bar{u}
.
While not required, mean-shifting of the covariance ensures that the affine subspace
\bar{u}+\mathrm{span}(\phi_{1}^{\mathrm{POD}},\dots,\phi_{r_{u}}^{\mathrm{POD}})
corresponding to the above decoding operation satisfies any linear, nonparametric constraints (e.g., homogeneous boundary conditions, divergence free conditions)
[
60
]
.

Standard results for POD suggest that the truncation error associated with the POD basis is bounded by the sum of the trailing eigenvalues in the truncated eigendirections. This is summarized below (see
[
57
,
60
]
for a proof).

Proposition 3.1

(POD reconstruction error bound)
.

Suppose
\mu
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{Z}
and
u\in L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{Z};\mathcal{U})
.
Let
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r_{u}}=\{\phi_{i}^{\mathrm{POD}}\}_{i=1}^{r_{u}}
be the first
r_{u}
POD basis vectors.
Then, the reconstruction error for the projection
P_{\mathcal{U},r_{u}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{u}}\phi_{i}\phi_{i}^{T}

is bounded by

\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}[\|(I-P_{\mathcal{U},r_{u}})(u(m,z)-\bar{u})\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}]\leq\sum_{i>r_{u}}\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{POD}}.

(39)

Since we compute the POD in the reference domain,
this truncation error bound uses the reference domain function space
\mathcal{U}
.
For
\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})=L^{2}(\Omega_{0})
or
H^{1}(\Omega_{0})
, we can additionally consider the error bound for the truncated state variable pushed forward to the spatial domain
\Omega_{z}
.

Proposition 3.2

(POD reconstruction error bound in spatial domain norms)
.

Consider the setting of
Proposition

3.1
.
Additionally, assume that there exist constants
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\|\chi_{z}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{0})}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{0}\qquad\forall z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad},

(40a)

\displaystyle\|\chi_{z}^{-1}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{z})}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}\qquad\forall z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}.

(40b)

We have the following:

1.

If
\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})=L^{2}(\Omega_{0})
and
\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z})=L^{2}(\Omega_{z})
, then

\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}[\|T_{\chi_{z}}^{-1}((I-P_{\mathcal{U},r_{u}})(u(m,z)-\bar{u}))\|_{\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z})}^{2}]\leq\gamma_{0}^{d}\sum_{i>r_{u}}\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{POD}}.

(41)

2.

If
\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})=H^{1}(\Omega_{0})
and
\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z})=H^{1}(\Omega_{z})
, then

\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}[\|T_{\chi_{z}}^{-1}((I-P_{\mathcal{U},r_{u}})(u(m,z)-\bar{u}))\|_{\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z})}^{2}]\leq(1+\gamma_{1}^{2})\gamma_{0}^{d}\sum_{i>r_{u}}\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{POD}}.

(42)

Proof.

Consider first the case of
\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})=H^{1}(\Omega_{0})
and
\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z})=H^{1}(\Omega_{z})
.
Then, by
Proposition

2.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\|T_{\chi_{z}}^{-1}((I-P_{\mathcal{U},r_{u}})(u(m,z)-\bar{u}))\|_{\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\|\chi_{z}^{-1}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{z})}^{2}\right)\|\chi_{z}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega_{0})}^{d}\|(I-P_{\mathcal{U},r_{u}})(u(m,z)-\bar{u})\|_{\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\gamma_{1}^{2})\gamma_{0}^{d}\|(I-P_{\mathcal{U},r_{u}})(u(m,z)-\bar{u})\|_{\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})}^{2}

for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
.
Thus, taking expectations with respect to
(m,z)\sim\mu
and applying the standard POD bound yields the desired result.
The proof is analogous for
\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})=L^{2}(\Omega_{0})
.
∎

3.4.2
Active Subspaces for the Uncertain Parameter

We then construct our dimension reduction for
m
based on the truncated representation of the solution operator in the reduced POD coordinates.
That is, we compute the AS for the mapping
P_{\mathcal{U},r_{u}}u(m,z)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}u(m,z)
,
for which the derivative covariance is given by

\mathcal{H}_{m}=\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}[D_{m}u(m,z)^{*}\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}D_{m}u(m,z)].

(43)

The subspace basis is then given by the first
r_{m}
eigenvectors of
\mathcal{H}_{m}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{H}_{m}\psi_{i}^{\mathrm{AS}}=\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{AS}}\psi_{i}^{\mathrm{AS}},\qquad i\in\mathbb{N},

(44)

where
\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{AS}}
are again given in descending order.
When then take
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r_{m}}=\{\psi_{i}^{\mathrm{AS}}\}_{i=1}^{r_{m}}
and
b_{m}=\bar{m}
.
AS captures dominant directions in the parameter space that have the greatest impact on the output as measured by the size of the derivatives
[
66
]
.
Since the dimension reduction explicitly takes into account sensitivity information,
it is typically more effective than POD for input dimension reduction,
which considers only the input variance and is agnostic to the mapping itself.
Here we note that our AS based on (
43
) differs from the standard AS in that only the
m
-derivatives are considered.

Theoretical properties of AS can be analyzed when the underlying distribution
\mu_{m}
satisfies the subspace Poincaré inequality. That is, let
\mathcal{G}(P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})
be the sub sigma algebra generated by an orthogonal projection
P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}
of rank
r
.
Then, for any
f\in L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}
, its conditional expectation

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m}}[f|\mathcal{G}(P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})]

is the best
L^{2}_{\mu}
approximation of
f
within the subspace of
\mathcal{G}_{P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}}
-measurable functions.
Such functions can be characterized by the fact that
f(m)=g(P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}m)
for some measurable function
g
, i.e., these are ridge functions that do not depend on the null space of
P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}
.
We can quantify the effectiveness of the input dimension reduction strategy by inspecting this
L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}
error between
f
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m}}[f|\mathcal{G}(P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})]
.
To this end, let
\mathcal{W}:=\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}}
denote the latent space for the output.
We then have the following definition.

Definition 3.1

(Subspace Poincaré inequality)
.

We say a Borel probability measure
\mu_{m}
satisfies the Subspace Poincaré inequality
if for any
f\in C^{1}(\mathcal{M};\mathcal{W})
with
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m}}[\|D_{m}f\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W})}^{2}]<\infty
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m}}[\|f-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f|\mathcal{G}(P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})]\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}]\leq c_{\mu_{m}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m}}[\|D_{m}f(I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W})}^{2}],

(45)

where
c_{\mu_{m}}>0
is a Poincaré constant that is independent of
f
and
P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}
.

The canonical example of a distribution satisfying a Subspace Poincaré inequality is the Gaussian distribution; we remark on this case below.
Other examples and characterizations are available, though these are typically given for finite-dimensional input spaces.

Now consider any differentiable
f:\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}
.
Since we are only interested in dimension reduction of
m
,
we study the conditional expectation of
u
with respect to
m
taken pointwise in
z
,

\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}}\left[f\right](m,z):=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m}}[f(\cdot,z)|\mathcal{G}(P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})](m).

(46)

and study the approximation error of
\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathcal{M},r}}\left[f\right](m,z)
.
Then, the product measure
\mu=\mu_{m}\otimes\mu_{z}
satisfies a partial subspace Poincaré inequality whenever

\mu_{m}
satisfies the subspace Poincaré inequality.
In particular, since
z
is not truncated, the result does not rely on
\mu_{z}
satisfying the subspace Poincaré inequality, and the resulting Poincaré constant depends only on the subspace Poincaré constant for
\mu_{m}
.

Proposition 3.3

(Subspace Poincaré inequality for partial dimension reduction)
.

Suppose
\mu_{m}
satisfies a subspace Poincaré inequality,

\mu_{z}
is a Borel probability measure on
\mathcal{Z}
, and
f\in C^{1}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{Z};\mathcal{W})
satisfies the moment bounds for
\mu=\mu_{m}\otimes\mu_{z}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|f\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|D_{m}f\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W})}^{2}]<\infty.

(47)

Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|f-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}}\left[f\right]\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}]\leq c_{\mu}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|D_{m}f(I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W})}^{2}].

(48)

Proof.

This follows directly from the subspace Poincaré inequality.
Recall that for any
z
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|f(m,z)-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}}\left[f\right](m,z)\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}]\leq c_{\mu}\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|D_{m}f(m,z)(I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W})}^{2}]

(49)

Taking expectations now with respect to
z\sim\mu_{z}
yields the desired result.
∎

This allows us to quantify the effectiveness of the partial AS given in (
43
)–(
44
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose
\mu_{m}
satisfies a subspace Poincaré inequality and
\mu_{z}
is a Borel probability measure over
\mathcal{Z}
,
and
u\in C^{1}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{Z};\mathcal{U})
and satisfies the moment bounds for
\mu=\mu_{m}\otimes\mu_{z}

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|u\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|D_{m}u\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{U})}^{2}]<\infty.

(50)

Let
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r_{m}}

=
\{\psi_{i}^{\mathrm{AS}}\}_{i=1}^{r_{m}}

be the AS basis
and
P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{m}}\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{T}
. Then, the conditional expectation given the projection
P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}

satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}[\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}(u(m,z)-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}}\left[u\right](m,z))\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}]\leq c_{\mu}\sum_{i>r_{m}}\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{AS}}.

(51)

Proof.

We make use of the subspace Poincaré inequality from above applied to the mapping
f(m,z)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}u(m,z)
.
That is, for any
P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}[\|f(m,z)-\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}}\left[f\right](m,z)\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}]\leq c_{\mu_{m}}\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}[\|D_{m}f(m,z)(I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W})}^{2}].

In particular, the upper bound can also be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|D_{m}f(I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W})}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}((I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[D_{m}f^{*}D_{m}f](I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}((I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})\mathcal{H}_{m}(I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})).

For
P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{m}}\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{T}
, the trace term simplifies to

\mathrm{Tr}((I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})\mathcal{H}_{m}(I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}))=\sum_{i>r_{m}}\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{AS}},

which yields the desired result.
∎

This result shows that there exists a ridge function, namely
\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}}\left[u\right]
, whose average errors are bounded by the sum over trailing eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{m}
.
While this is not immediately connected with the approximation theory developed in the previous section (which makes use of the ridge function
u_{r}(m,z)=P_{\mathcal{U},r_{u}}u(P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}m,z)
instead of the conditional expectation), it does provide intuitive justification that the subspace represented by
P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}
captures the important directions of the input space.
We do note that under further regularity assumptions on
u
and
\mu_{m}
, such as Lipschitz continuity of
u
and
Du
and Gaussian
\mu_{m}

we can show that
\bar{u}_{r}
is also continuously differentiable with Lipschitz continuous derivatives,
and hence develop approximation errors of the RBNO based on a neural network approximation of
\bar{u}_{r}
,
analogous to the approach used in Section 2.
A similar approach is taken in
[
9
]
.

Finite- and infinite-dimensional Gaussian inputs

In our numerical examples, we will mostly consider discretizations of the Gaussian random fields for the uncertain parameter
\mathcal{M}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}}
is finite dimensional and
\mu_{m}=\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{C}_{m})
is a Gaussian with a non-degenerate covariance
\mathcal{C}_{m}
.
In such cases, it is customary to equip
\mathcal{M}
with the precision-weighted inner product. To avoid confusion, we will write
\mathcal{E}
to denote
\mathcal{M}
equipped with this inner product, such that

\langle m_{1},m_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}=\langle\mathcal{C}_{m}^{-1/2}m_{1},\mathcal{C}_{m}^{-1/2}m_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}
.
For Gaussian
\mu_{m}
, conditional expectations with respect to
\mathcal{G}(P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})
have an explicit expression,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m}}[f|\mathcal{G}(P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})]=\int f(P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}}m+(I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})\eta)d\mu_{m}(\eta).

(52)

Moreover,
\mu_{m}
satisfies the subspace Poincaré inequality

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m}}[\|f-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m}}[f|\mathcal{G}(P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})]\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m}}[\|D_{m}f(I-P_{\mathcal{M},r_{m}})\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{U})}^{2}],

(53)

with
c_{\mu_{m}}=1
.
In this case, the adjoint in (
43
) is given by
D_{m}u(m,z)^{*}=(D_{m}u(m,z))^{*}_{\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{C}_{m}(D_{m}u(m,z))^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}
,
where
(\cdot)^{*}_{\mathcal{E}}
refers to the adjoint with respect to the Cameron–Martin space inner product and
(\cdot)^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}
denotes the adjoint with respect to the original inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}
.
The corresponding eigenvalue problem (
44
)
can then be written as a generalized eigenvalue problem,

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{m}\psi_{i}^{\mathrm{AS}}=\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{AS}}\mathcal{C}_{m}^{-1}\psi_{i}^{\mathrm{AS}},\qquad i\in\mathbb{N},

(54)

where

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{m}=\mathbb{E}_{(m,z)\sim\mu}[(D_{m}u(m,z))_{\mathcal{M}}^{*}\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}D_{m}u(m,z))],

(55)

Importantly, the subspace Poincaré constant
c_{\mu_{m}}=1
, and therefore the error bound in
Proposition

3.4
, does not explicitly depend on the discretization dimension
d_{m}
.

Although
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{E}
are equivalent in finite dimensions,
this is no longer the case in infinite dimensions.
Specifically,
\mathcal{C}_{m}^{-1/2}
can be an unbounded operator on
\mathcal{M}
, and hence the
\mathcal{E}
norm is only well-defined on the Cameron–Martin space
\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{C}_{m}^{1/2})
, which does not necessarily coincide with
\mathcal{M}
. Nevertheless, the reduced bases constructed from (
44
)
can still be a well-defined form of input dimension reduction for infinite-dimensional Gaussians (see
[
60
,
52
,
53
]
).
This can be viewed as the limiting case of
d_{m}\rightarrow\infty
provided (
54
) and (
55
) have meaningful limits.

3.4.3
Sample-Based Approximations for Reduced Basis Computation

In practice, both the POD and AS need to be computed from a finite sample-based approximation of the expectations.
For the POD, we take the mean estimator

\widehat{u}:=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{POD}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{POD}}}u(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})

(56)

and the unbiased covariance estimator

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{u}:=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{POD}}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{POD}}}(u(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})-\widehat{u})(u(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})-\widehat{u})^{*}

(57)

where
(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})\sim\mu
,
i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{POD}}

are i.i.d. samples from the input distribution.
Then, the empirical POD is given by the dominant eigenvectors
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{u}
,

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{u}\widehat{\phi}_{j}^{\mathrm{POD}}=\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{\mathrm{POD}}\widehat{\phi}_{j}^{\mathrm{POD}},\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}

(58)

where we take
\{\phi_{j}\}_{i=1}^{r_{u}}=\{\widehat{\phi}_{j}^{\mathrm{POD}}\}_{j=1}^{r_{u}}

and use
b_{u}=\hat{u}
for the bias in the RBNO.

For the AS, we take the derivative-covariance estimator

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{m}:=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{AS}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{AS}}}D_{m}u(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})^{*}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{r_{u}}{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{r_{u}}^{*}D_{m}u(m^{(i)},z^{(i)}),

(59)

where
(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})\sim\mu
,
i=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{AS}}

are again i.i.d. samples from the input distribution.
The empirical AS is given by the dominant eigenvectors of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
,

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{m}\widehat{\psi}_{j}^{\mathrm{AS}}=\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{\mathrm{AS}}\widehat{\psi}_{j}^{\mathrm{AS}},\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}

(60)

where we take
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r_{m}}=\{\widehat{\psi}_{j}^{\mathrm{AS}}\}_{j=1}^{r_{m}}
.
Since we assume that the mean
\bar{m}
of
\mu_{m}
is known, we simply take
b_{m}=\bar{m}
to be the bias.

4
Implementation Details

4.1
Diffeomorphic Deformations via Basis Expansions and Elastic Extensions

In this section, we discuss choices for
\chi_{z}
and the associated distribution
z\sim\mu_{z}
such that
\chi_{z}
remains a diffeomorphism for all choice of
z
.
Specifically, we will consider representations of
z\mapsto\chi_{z}
that itself utilizes a basis expansion,

\chi_{z}(X)=X+\sum_{i=1}^{d_{z}}z_{i}\mathfrak{d}_{i}(X),

(61)

where
\{\mathfrak{d}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d_{z}}
are a set of smooth displacement basis functions
\mathfrak{d}_{i}:\Omega_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We have that if

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{d_{Z}}z_{i}\nabla_{X}\mathfrak{d}_{i}-I\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{0})}<1,

(62)

then
\chi_{z}
given by (
61
) is a diffeomorphism.
However, as noted in
[
68
]
, the condition (
62
) is often overly conservative and in practice, a larger admissible set
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
can be permitted.
In our numerical examples, we typically select
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
to be constrained by box bounds
z_{l}\leq z_{i}\leq z_{u}
that ensures the resulting deformations
\chi_{z}
are diffeomorphic, and subsequently choose
\mu_{z}
to be a uniform distribution over
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
.

The formulation of (
61
) accommodates several parametric shape representations commonly used in shape optimization.
This includes domain deformations given by a Fourier basis expansion, which enables the design of boundaries as the graphs of arbitrary functions
[
69
,
70
]
.
It also includes shape representations via free-form deformations
[
71
]
,
in which
\mathfrak{d}_{i}
are typically chosen from an expressive class of locally-supported basis functions such as Bernstein polynomials or B-splines.
In our numerical experiments, we consider shape optimizations using the Fourier representation in a Poisson PDE in
Section

5.3

and the free-form deformation approach in steady-state Navier–Stokes flow in
Sections

5.4
and
5.5
.
Details of the shape parameterizations are presented in
Sections

5.3.2
and
5.4.2
for the two approaches, respectively.

In many other design problems, the shape boundaries are prescribed as graphs of basis expansions without an explicit extension to the interior of the domain.
This is the case when using domain-specific bases on non-trivial domains,
such as the Hicks–Henne bases
[
72
,
73
,
74
]
for airfoil design
and Lewis forms
[
75
]
for ship hull design.
In such cases, the displacement basis
\mathfrak{d}_{D}^{(i)}
is prescribed only on the designed boundaries
\Gamma_{D}\subset\partial\Omega_{0}
. Nevertheless, we can still pose the problem using (
61
) by extending the displacement basis into the entire domain
\Omega_{0}
via elastic extensions,
a commonly-used method for mesh updating in shape optimization

[
76
,
77
]

and fluid structure interaction
[
78
,
79
,
80
]
.
To do so, we obtain
\mathfrak{d}_{i}
as the solution of the linear elasticity PDE with
Dirichlet boundary condition
\mathfrak{d}_{i}|_{\Gamma_{D}}=\mathfrak{d}_{D}^{(i)}
and
\mathfrak{d}_{i}|_{\partial\Omega_{0}\setminus\Gamma_{D}}=0
,

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\left(a_{\mu}(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})+a_{\lambda}(\nabla\cdot u)I\right)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\Omega_{0}

(63a)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{d}_{D}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{D}

(63b)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{i}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{0}\setminus\Gamma_{D},

(63c)

where
(\nabla u^{T})_{ij}=(\nabla u)_{ji}
corresponds to the matrix transpose of the displacement gradient.
The Lamé parameters
a_{\lambda}
and
a_{\mu}
can be defined in a spatially-varying manner (e.g., based on distance to boundaries) to improve the conditioning of the deformation gradient
F_{\chi_{z}}(X)=\nabla_{X}\chi_{z}(X)
.
The displacement bases are then numerically computed by solving (
63
) using, say, the finite element method.

In fact, even when using free-form deformations where
\mathfrak{d}_{i}
are prescribed on the entire
\Omega_{0}
,
we will still use
\mathfrak{d}_{i}
to generate a new set of displacement basis functions
\tilde{\mathfrak{d}}_{i}
by using
\mathfrak{d}_{i}|_{\partial\Omega_{0}}
as the boundary conditions.
This does not change the motion of the shape boundaries when used in (
61
), but can improve the conditioning of the deformation gradient and hence the quality of the deformed mesh in the resulting displacement basis functions.

Remark 4.1

(Regularity of elastic extensions)
.

Theoretically, for a deformation map
\chi_{z}
to be a diffeomorphism, it is required to be of class
C^{1}
. However, when such maps are generated by solving the equations of linear elasticity with the finite element method, the resulting weak solutions belong to Sobolev spaces such as
W^{1,\infty}
as opposed to
C^{1}
.
This level of smoothness is often sufficient to define pushforward/pullback operations and ensure differentiability in shape optimization (e.g.,
[
61
, Section 11]
).
Moreover, invertibility of the numerical deformations can be verified by ensuring the deformation gradient has a positive determinant at the element level.
Nevertheless, we remark that elastic extensions are not strictly required for free-form deformations approaches, and can be omitted if smoothness of the deformation maps is of concern.

4.2
Computational Workflow for Operator Learning

We now summarize the overall workflow for constructing shape-DINO surrogates for PDE-constrained OUU, which largely follows that described in
[
9
]
.
We then elaborate on the details of the training data generation and neural operator training.

1.

State data generation:

Sample parameter–control pairs

\{(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}}
from the prescribed training distribution.
For each sample, solve the high-fidelity PDE to obtain the corresponding state
solutions
\{u^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}}
.

2.

State-space reduction via POD:

Compute the sample mean
\widehat{u}
over a subset of
n_{\mathrm{POD}}
training samples and perform POD on the centered snapshots
\{u^{(i)}-\widehat{u}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{POD}}}

following (
56
)–(
58
). Then, select a reduced basis
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r_{u}}
of rank
r_{u}
to represent the state space.

3.

Sensitivity data generation:

For each training sample, compute reduced-output parameter sensitivities

\{\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}D_{m}u^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}}

and shape sensitivities

\{\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}D_{z}u^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}}

using the adjoint method.

4.

Parameter-space reduction via active subspaces:

Compute an active subspace for the random parameters from

n_{\mathrm{AS}}
reduced-output parameter sensitivities
\{\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}D_{m}u^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{AS}}}

following (
59
)–(
60
)
and select a reduced basis
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r_{m}}
of rank
r_{m}
.

5.

Latent-space encoding:

Project parameters, states, and Jacobians onto their respective reduced bases,

m_{r}^{(i)}=\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}\,m^{(i)},\qquad u_{r}^{(i)}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}\,(u^{(i)}-\widehat{u}),\qquad J_{m,r}^{(i)}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}D_{m}u^{(i)}\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}},\qquad J_{z,r}^{(i)}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}D_{z}u^{(i)}.

6.

Neural operator training.

Train the neural network
g_{\theta}
via empirical risk minimization using the
reduced training dataset

\{(m_{r}^{(i)},z^{(i)},u_{r}^{(i)},J_{m,r}^{(i)},J_{z,r}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}},

thereby learning a surrogate model that maps parameters and shapes to reduced states and sensitivities in latent space.

4.2.1
Details and Computational Costs

State data generation

State training data are generated by solving the underlying PDE
\mathcal{R}(u^{(i)},m^{(i)},z^{(i)})=0
for
u^{(i)}
given the sampled inputs
(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})
.
This is typically the dominant cost in training data generation.
When
\mathcal{R}
corresponds to a linear PDE (i.e.,
\mathcal{R}
is linear in
u
), the resulting computational cost is solving a linear system of equations that scales with the discretization dimension
d_{u}
.
On the other hand, when
\mathcal{R}
corresponds to a nonlinear PDE, the solution
u^{(i)}
may require successively solving several linear PDEs, as in Newton or Picard iteration.

POD computation

Once the state data
\{u^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}}
is generated,
the POD can be computed by solving the eigenvalue problem using
a sample-based estimate of the covariance using
n_{\mathrm{POD}}
of the
n_{s}
training samples,
as in (
56
)–(
58
). In particular, the eigenvalue problem (
58
)
can be solved efficiently in a matrix-free manner via iterative methods such as Lanczos
[
81
]
or randomized methods
[
82
]
, and requires no additional PDE solves.

Derivative data computation

Recall that VJPs can be computed by the adjoint sensitivity equations (
7
).
Thus, for a given input sample
(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})
, once the state
u^{(i)}
has been computed, the cost of generating the VJPs along each of the
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{POD}}}
POD basis directions involves solving
r_{u}
linear systems of equations.
Notably, the
r_{u}
linear systems share the same operator (left-hand side),
and only differ in their right-hand sides.
This allows amortization of the solution costs, either by re-using the matrix factorizations when using direct methods (e.g., LU/Cholesky factors),
or by re-using expensive preconditioners when using iterative methods.
In our numerical examples, we consider problems for which direct methods are tractable.
In this case, the cost of generating the
r_{u}
VJPs (including both
m
and
z
Jacobians) is asymptotically equivalent to that of solving just the first linear system, since cost of applying the matrix factors to the remaining
r_{u}-1
right-hand sides is negligible compared to the cost of performing the matrix factorization.

Active subspace computation

In our workflow, the AS can be directly computed from the sampled VJPs
\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}D_{m}u(m^{(i)},z^{(i)})
.
Again, the eigenvalue problem in (
44
) can be solved
through randomized methods
without explicitly forming
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
,
and requires no additional PDE solves once the sensitivity data has been generated.
Here, we note when using the conventional
L^{2}_{\mu}
loss (
35
),
Jacobian data only needs to be generated for the first
n_{\mathrm{AS}}
input samples to compute the active subspace since it is not used elsewhere in training.
However, in this setting,
it may be preferable in practice to use an input PCA for dimension reduction of
\mathcal{M}
to avoid computing derivatives altogether.
This approach is investigated in
[
9
]
,
but we do not adopt it in order to retain consistency in our comparisons between Shape-DINO and Shape-NO.

4.2.2
Neural Network Training

For neural network training, with given reduced bases, we can approximate (
3.3
) via Monte Carlo using a finite training data corpus

\left\{(m_{r}^{(i)},z^{(i)},u_{r}^{(i)},J_{m,r}^{(i)},J_{z,r}^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}}.

In particular, we will adopt the loss function using a normalized form of (
3.3
)

\min_{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\frac{\|u_{r}^{(i)}-g_{\theta}(m_{r}^{(i)},z^{(i)})\|^{2}_{2}}{\|u_{r}^{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}}+\alpha_{D}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\left(+\frac{\|J_{m,r}^{(i)}-D_{1}g_{\theta}(m_{r}^{(i)},z^{(i)})\|^{2}_{2}}{\|J_{m,r}^{(i)}\|^{2}_{F}}+\frac{\|J_{z,r}^{(i)}-D_{2}g_{\theta}(m_{r}^{(i)},z^{(i)})\|^{2}_{2}}{\|J_{z,r}^{(i)}\|^{2}_{F}}\right).

(64)

Here, we take
\alpha_{D}=1
for derivative-informed training (Shape-DINO) and
\alpha_{D}=0
for conventional training (Shape-NO).

5
Numerical Results

In this section, we evaluate the accuracy and sample efficiency of shape-DINO across three distinct PDE-constrained shape OUU problems:
(i) a flux-tracking problem for a Poisson equation with a shape-parametrized top boundary and an uncertain log-permeability field, (ii)
dissipation reduction for 2D steady-state Navier–Stokes
flow around an object under uncertain inflow conditions,
and (iii) bending-moment and force reduction for 3D steady-state Navier–Stokes flow around a tower under uncertain inflow conditions.
In each example, our goal is to assess the effectiveness of Shape-DINOs (and Shape-NOs) through
(1) their generalization accuracies for output and derivative predictions and
(2) their accuracies in producing solutions to the OUU problems compared to PDE-based solutions using similar computational budgets.
Note that for the 2D problems, we can afford to compare the approximate OUU solutions to an accurate PDE-based reference solution computed using a large sample size.
On the other hand,
for the 3D case,
producing an accurate reference solution is computationally prohibitive,
and instead the purpose of the experiment is to demonstrate scalability of our surrogate-based approach.

For the numerical results, we use FEniCS
[
83
]

to implement finite element discretizations for the PDEs
and
PyTorch
[
84
]
to construct the neural network approximations.
Additionally, data generation is implemented using hIPPYlib
[
85
]
and hIPPYflow
[
86
]
, and the OUU problems are formulated and solved using SOUPy
[
87
]
.
CPU-based timings are carried out on a single compute node of the TACC VISTA system using an NVIDIA Grace CPU Superchip,
while neural network training and deployment are performed with an NVIDIA H200 GPU.

5.2
Cost accuracy comparison of the neural operator

We train Shape-DINOs and Shape-NOs for a range of sample sizes. The trained neural operators are then used to solve the OUU problems via SAA of the objective function using a large sample size. We denote the resulting optimal solutions by the neural operators by
z^{\dagger}_{\text{NN}}
. For comparison, we also solve the OUU problems directly using the governing PDE and SAA with varying sample sizes, chosen to reflect computational budgets comparable to those required for training the neural operators. The corresponding PDE-based optimal solutions are denoted by
z^{\dagger}_{\text{PDE}}
. All optimization problems are solved using gradient-based methods. For the linear elliptic Poisson example with box constraints, we employ the L-BFGS-B algorithm. For the 2D and 3D Navier–Stokes shape OUU problems, we use a trust-region constrained method with box constraints.

The accuracies of the approximations are assessed relative to the reference solutions obtained by solving the PDE-based OUU problem using a large sample size. The resulting reference optimal solution is denoted by
z^{\star}_{\text{ref}}
. Approximate optimal solutions are compared against
z^{\star}_{\text{ref}}
using the relative optimal cost error defined as

\frac{|\mathcal{J}(z^{\dagger}_{\text{approx}})-\mathcal{J}(z^{\star}_{\text{ref}})|}{|\mathcal{J}(z^{\star}_{\text{ref}})|},

(65)

where
\mathcal{J}(z^{\dagger}_{\text{approx}})
and
\mathcal{J}(z^{\star}_{\text{ref}})
are evaluated using the true objective function, estimated via an accurate Monte Carlo estimator with a large number of samples. Here,
z^{\dagger}_{\text{approx}}
denotes either the neural-operator-based solution
z^{\dagger}_{\text{NN}}
or the PDE-based solution
z^{\dagger}_{\text{PDE}}
, depending on the method under consideration.

Computational efficiency is measured in terms of the total number of state PDE solves, that is, the evaluation of
u(m,z)
required to obtain the optimal solution. This quantity represents the dominant computational cost of the optimization process. For neural-operator-based optimization, this cost corresponds to the total number of training samples used. For PDE-based optimization, it is given by the product of the total number of objective function evaluations and the SAA sample size.

In
Section

5.6
, we compare the computational cost of solving for the forward state and its sensitivity with respect to the shape parameter using PDE-based solvers and neural operators. Because these two operations dominate the overall optimization cost, we focus exclusively on them.
Across our numerical experiments, we show that neural operators can provide orders-of-magnitude speedups over high-fidelity PDE solves, making large-scale OUU problems computationally feasible.

5.3
Flux-tracking design in a Poisson Problem under Uncertain Permeability Fields

5.3.1
Poisson Problem with Varying Top Boundary

For our first numerical example, we consider a Poisson PDE on a domain
\Omega_{z}
in the form of a rectangle with a top boundary that we are free to design.
We assume that
\Omega_{z}
is related to the reference domain
\Omega_{0}=(0,4)\times(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
through the family of diffeomorphisms
\chi_{z}

This example is analogous to the setup considered in
[
69
]
.
Here, the PDE is given in spatial coordinates as

\displaystyle-\nabla_{x}\cdot(\exp(\widetilde{m})\nabla_{x}\widetilde{u})

\displaystyle=f\qquad x\in\Omega_{z}

(66)

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\widetilde{u}\cdot n

\displaystyle=0\qquad x\in\Gamma_{L,z}\cup\Gamma_{R,z}

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}

\displaystyle=0\qquad x\in\Gamma_{B,z}

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}

\displaystyle=1\qquad x\in\Gamma_{T,z}

where

We represent the shape of the top boundary using a Fourier expansion with respect to the horizontal coordinates,

5.3.3
Reference Domain Formulation

The PDE can be formulated on the reference domain through a change of coordinates in its weak form. On the spatial domain, we have the trial space

\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z}):=\{\widetilde{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{z}):\widetilde{u}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{B,z}\text{ and }\widetilde{u}=1\text{ on }\Gamma_{T,z}\}

and the test space

\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{z}):=\{\widetilde{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{z}):\widetilde{v}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{B,z}\text{ and }\widetilde{v}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{T,z}\}.

The weak form is then given for any
\widetilde{v}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{z})
as

\langle R_{z}(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{m}),\widetilde{v}\rangle_{\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{z})^{\prime}\times\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{z})}=\int_{\Omega_{z}}\exp(\widetilde{m})\nabla_{x}\widetilde{u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\widetilde{v}\;dx-\int_{\Omega_{z}}f\widetilde{v}\;dx.

(69)

We then apply the coordinate transformations from
A.1
to the integrals,
which yields the weak form of the PDE residual expressed in terms of the reference domain quantities.
To this end, we consider reference domain state and test spaces,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})

\displaystyle:=\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega_{0}):u=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{B}\text{ and }u=1\text{ on }\Gamma_{T}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{0})

\displaystyle:=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega_{0}):v=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{B}\text{ and }v=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{T}\}.

where
\Gamma_{T}
and
\Gamma_{B}
are the top and bottom boundaries of
\Omega_{0}
, respectively.
We then have

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{R}(u,m,z),v\rangle_{\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{0})^{\prime}\times\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{0})}=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\exp(m)(F_{\chi_{z}}^{-T}\nabla_{X}u)\cdot(F_{\chi_{z}}^{-T}\nabla_{X}v)\det F_{\chi_{z}}dX-\int_{\Omega_{0}}(T_{\chi_{z}}f)v\det F_{\chi_{z}}dX.

(70)

We observe that the shape variable

F_{\chi_{z}}=\nabla_{X}\chi_{z}
and the pullback

As previously discussed, we treat (

This is discretized using a Galerkin finite element method with continuous piecewise-linear elements for bot the state and random parameter space, leading to
d_{m}=d_{u}=4,257

5.3.4
Flux-tracking Shape Optimization under Uncertainty

For the OUU problem, we consider a tracking-type objective
for the flux across the bottom boundary.
That is, given a target flux profile

\tau_{\mathrm{target}}\in L^{2}(\Gamma_{B,z})
,
we seek to minimize the
L^{2}(\Gamma_{B,z})
misfit between the
normal flux
-e^{m}\nabla_{x}u\cdot n
and the target,

Q_{z}(u,m)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{B,z}}(-e^{m}\nabla_{x}u\cdot n-\tau_{\mathrm{target}})^{2}ds.

(71)

This transforms to the following integral in reference coordinates

Q(u,m,z)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{B}}\left(-\exp(m)F_{\chi_{z}}^{-T}\nabla_{X}u\cdot N-\tau_{\mathrm{target}}\right)^{2}dS,

(72)

where we have made the simplification using the fact that

\chi_{z}(X)=X
on
\Gamma_{B}

and therefore the unit normal vector remains unchanged, i.e.,

n(X)=\frac{\det F_{\chi_{z}}(X)F_{\chi_{z}}^{-T}(X)N(X)}{|\det F_{\chi_{z}}(X)F_{\chi_{z}}^{-T}(X)N(X)|}=N(X).

For the penalization, we consider an
\ell_{2}
norm on
z

\mathcal{P}_{\ell_{2}}(z;\alpha):=\alpha|\Omega_{0}|\|z\|_{2}^{2},

(73)

where
\alpha>0
is a penalization weight
and the factor
|\Omega_{0}|
, which denotes the Lebesgue measure of
\Omega_{0}
, is used for ease of implementation in FEniCS.

We consider two example risk measures for our objectives. In the first example, we consider the risk neutral case using the expectation as the optimization objective,

\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\rho^{\mathrm{mean}}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}(Q(u(m,z),m,z)+\mathcal{P}_{\ell_{2}}(z;\alpha).

(74)

Our second example considers the risk averse case using CVaR as the optimization objective,

\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\rho^{\mathrm{CVaR},\beta}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}Q(u(m,z),m,z)+\mathcal{P}_{\ell_{2}}(z;\alpha),

(75)

with
\beta=0.95
,
which we solve using the smoothed CVaR formulation (
17
).
In both cases, the objective functions include the

\ell_{2}
penalization on the shape variables,
where we take
\alpha=0.001
for the mean objective
and examine both
\alpha=0.001
and
\alpha=0.1
for the CVaR objective.

5.4
Design for Dissipation Reduction in 2D Flow under Multiple Uncertain Inflow Conditions

5.4.1
Navier–Stokes Equations with a Variable-Shape Object

Next we consider a steady incompressible Navier–Stokes problem on the channel domain

\Omega_{\text{channel}}=(0,2)\times(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

in which there is an initially circular object

\Omega_{\text{object}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:(x_{1}-0.5)^{2}+(x_{2}-0.5)^{2}<0.1^{2}\}

with radius
r=0.1

centered at
(0.5,0.5)^{T}
.
The goal is to design the shape of the object to minimize the viscous dissipation through free-form deformations,
where the shape of the object is transformed through a family of diffeomorphic deformations
\chi_{z}

to obtain the design
\Omega_{\mathrm{object},z}=\chi_{z}(\Omega_{\mathrm{object}})
.
This setting is often used as a benchmark configuration to assess shape-induced drag reduction and dissipation control in confined flows
[
91
]
.

Here, we introduce an additional difficulty by
considering two independent inflow scenarios,
in which the object faces either a horizontally-directed inflow (eastward flow) or a vertically-directed inflow (northward flow),
serving as a prototype for shape design problems under different wind loading directions.
We formulate this problem by considering two sets of PDEs whose domains differ by a reflection of the object along the line
x_{1}=x_{2}
.
For the eastward flow case, we take the object in its default orientation,
\Omega^{\mathrm{East}}_{\mathrm{object}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{object},z}
,
for which the flow domain becomes

\Omega_{z}^{\mathrm{East}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{channel}}\setminus\mathrm{cl}\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{object}}^{\mathrm{East}}\right),

where
\mathrm{cl}\left(\cdot\right)
denotes the closure.
For the northward flow, we reflect the orientation of the object,
\Omega_{\mathrm{object}}^{\mathrm{North}}=\mathrm{Reflect}(\Omega_{\mathrm{object},z})

such that the flow domain is

\Omega_{z}^{\mathrm{North}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{channel}}\setminus\mathrm{cl}\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{object}}^{\mathrm{North}}\right),

where
\mathrm{Reflect}(\cdot)
denotes a reflection about the line
x_{1}=x_{2}
.
Note that this operation amounts to a 90-degree clockwise rotation about the center
(0.5,0.5)^{T}
followed by a reflection along the line
(x_{1},0.5)
.
This additional reflection will not affect our problem definition, since we will subsequently consider inflow distributions that are invariant to this reflection.

We now formulate the PDEs for the two scenarios on the domains
\Omega_{z}^{s},s\in\{\mathrm{East},\mathrm{North}\}
,
for which their states in spatial coordinates are given by the velocity-pressure pairs
\widetilde{u}^{s}=(\widetilde{v}^{s},\widetilde{p}^{s})

Using the Cauchy stress tensor,

\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}(\widetilde{u}^{s}):=2\nu\widetilde{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{v}^{s})-\widetilde{p}^{s}I,

(76)

where

\widetilde{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{v}^{s}):=\mathrm{sym}(\nabla_{x}\widetilde{v}^{s})=\frac{\nabla_{x}\widetilde{v}^{s}+(\nabla_{x}\widetilde{v}^{s})^{T}}{2}

(77)

is the strain rate tensor, we can write the governing equations as

\displaystyle(\widetilde{v}^{s}\cdot\nabla_{x})\widetilde{v}^{s}-\nabla_{x}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}(\widetilde{u}^{s})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\Omega_{z}^{s},

(78a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\cdot\widetilde{v}^{s}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\Omega_{z}^{s},

(78b)

\displaystyle\widetilde{v}^{s}-(\widetilde{v})_{\text{in}}^{s}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\text{in}},

(78c)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}(\widetilde{u}^{s})n

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\text{out}},

(78d)

\displaystyle\widetilde{v}^{s}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\text{no-slip}},

(78e)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}(\widetilde{u}^{s})n\cdot e_{t}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\text{free-slip}},

(78f)

\displaystyle\widetilde{v}^{s}\cdot n

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\text{free-slip}},

(78g)

where

is the unit tangent vector on the boundary and

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{in}}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\partial\Omega_{z}^{s}:x_{1}=0\},\qquad

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{no-slip}}

\displaystyle=\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{object}}^{s},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{out}}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\partial\Omega_{z}^{s}:x_{1}=2\},\qquad

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{free-slip}}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\partial\Omega_{z}^{s}:x_{2}=0\text{ or }x_{2}=1\}.

The inflow profile is determined by the uncertain model parameter through

(\widetilde{v})_{\text{in}}^{s}(x)=\exp(\widetilde{m}^{s}(x))e_{1},\qquad e_{1}=(1,0)^{T}.

We define the distributions of
\widetilde{m}^{s}
Gaussian random fields on the reference domain

\Omega_{0}:=\Omega_{\mathrm{channel}}\setminus\mathrm{cl}\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{object}}\right)

such that
m^{s}\sim\mu_{m}^{s}=\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{C}_{m}^{s})
with a different Bi-Laplacian covariance operator

\mathcal{C}_{m}^{s}

for each scenario
s\in\{\mathrm{East},\mathrm{North}\}

In particular,
\mathcal{C}_{m}^{s}
is given by (
67

and
\delta^{s},\gamma^{s}

such that the correlation length is
0.25

5.4.2
Shape Parametrization using Free-Form Deformations

The admissible shapes variations are represented using a
free-form deformation approach where
\chi_{z}
are given by Bernstein polynomials.
This free-form deformation strategy has been widely applied in PDE-constrained optimization due to its flexibility, smoothness, and relatively low-dimensional parametrization of boundary motion
[
71
,
10
,
11
,
14
]
.
We first present the approach based on
[
92
]
and then show how this parametrization can be interpreted in the form of (
61
).

To this end,
we consider the unit square
\Omega_{\mathrm{control}}:=(0,1)^{2}
containing the object
\Omega_{\mathrm{object}}

along with a lattice of control points
\xi_{k,l}=(k/K,l/L)^{T}
, for
k=0,\dots,K
and
l=0,\dots L
, where
K,L
are integers.
We then define the Bernstein polynomials, which for a maximum degree
n_{p}
are given by

b_{j}^{n_{p}}(t):=\binom{n_{p}}{j}t^{j}\left(1-t\right)^{n_{p}-j},\quad j=0,\ldots,n_{p}.

(79)

In 2D, we can construct the tensor-product Bernstein polynomials from the 1D Bernstein polynomials

b_{k,l}^{K,L}(t_{1},t_{2})=b_{k}^{K}(t_{1})b_{l}^{L}(t_{2}),\qquad k=0,\dots,K,\qquad l=0,\dots,L.

(80)

Next, we let
\delta_{k,l}=((\delta_{k,l})_{1},(\delta_{k,l})_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

be the displacement of the control point
\xi_{k,l}
,
such that its displaced location is given by
\widetilde{\xi}_{k,l}=\xi_{k,l}+\delta_{k,l}
.
We let the motion of the control points define the deformation map, such that

\chi(X):=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{l=0}^{L}b_{k,l}^{K,L}(X_{1},X_{2})\widetilde{\xi}_{k,l}.

(81)

Evidently, the control point displacements
\delta_{k,l}
parametrize the deformations.
To connect this with deformations of the form (
61
), we recognize that by the property
\sum_{k=0}^{K}kb_{k}^{K}(t)=Kt
of Bernstein polynomials,
we have that

\chi(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{l=0}^{L}b_{k,l}^{K,L}(X_{1},X_{2})(\xi_{k,l}+\delta_{k,l})=X+\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{l=0}^{L}b_{k,l}^{K,L}\delta_{k,l}.

Thus, we can equivalently define
\chi_{z}
in the form of
(

)

using the displacement basis functions

\mathfrak{d}^{1}_{k,l}(X)=b_{k,l}^{K,L}(X_{1},X_{2})e_{1},\quad\mathfrak{d}^{2}_{k,l}(X)=b_{k,l}^{K,L}(X_{1},X_{2})e_{2},

where
e_{1}=(1,0)^{T}
and
e_{2}=(0,1)^{T}
, along with the shape variables
z_{k,l}^{j}=(\delta_{k,l})_{j}
for
k=0,\dots K
,
l=0,\dots L
, and
j=1,2
.

In our problem, we will adopt a
K=10
by
L=10
lattice of control points over the region
\Omega_{\mathrm{control}}
.
However, we exclude the first and last control point from our optimization variables to ensure that the outer boundaries do not move,
which also allows us to extend all remaining displacement basis functions by zero beyond
X_{1}\geq 1
.
Thus, our resulting admissible deformations consists of the displacement bases
\mathfrak{d}^{j}_{k,l}(X)
and shape variables
z_{k,l}^{j}
for
k=1,\dots K-1
,
l=1,\dots L-1
, and
j=1,2
, giving a total of
d_{z}=162
degrees of freedom.
These displacements are also preprocessed using the linear elasticity equations (

\Omega_{0}

5.4.3
Reference Domain Formulation

We formulate the Navier–Stokes equations (
78a
) in weak form, where we use Nitsche’s method to weakly impose the inflow boundary conditions involving the model parameters.
In the spatial domain, we define the function spaces for the velocity and pressure as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{v}(\Omega_{z}^{s}):=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega_{z}^{s}):v=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{\text{no-slip}},\,v\cdot n=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{\text{free-slip}}\},\qquad\mathcal{U}_{p}(\Omega_{z}^{s}):=L^{2}(\Omega_{z}^{s}),

such that the trial space is given by

\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z}^{s}):=\mathcal{U}_{v}(\Omega_{z}^{s})\times\mathcal{U}_{p}(\Omega_{z}^{s})

and the test space is given by
\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{z}^{s}):=\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{z}^{s})
.
For any
\widetilde{y}^{s}=(\widetilde{w}^{s},\widetilde{q}^{s})\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{z}^{s})
,
we have

r_{z}^{s}(\widetilde{u}^{s},\widetilde{m}^{s},\widetilde{y}^{s}):=\langle\mathcal{R}_{z}(\widetilde{u}^{s},\widetilde{m}^{s}),\widetilde{y}^{s}\rangle_{\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{z}^{s})^{\prime}\times\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{z}^{s})}=r_{\mathrm{flow}}^{s}(\widetilde{u}^{s},\widetilde{m}^{s},\widetilde{y})+r_{\mathrm{Nitsche}}^{s}(\widetilde{u}^{s},\widetilde{m}^{s},\widetilde{y})

(83)

where

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{flow}}^{s}(\widetilde{u}^{s},\widetilde{m}^{s},\widetilde{y}^{s})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{z}^{s}}\left((\widetilde{v}^{s}\cdot\nabla_{x})\widetilde{v}^{s}\right)\cdot\widetilde{w}^{s}dx+\int_{\Omega_{z}^{s}}2\nu\;\widetilde{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{v}^{s}):\widetilde{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{w}^{s})dx

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\Omega_{z}^{s}}\widetilde{p}^{s}\left(\nabla_{x}\cdot\widetilde{w}^{s}\right)dx+\int_{\Omega_{z}^{s}}\left(\nabla_{x}\cdot\widetilde{v}^{s}\right)\widetilde{q}^{s}dx

and

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{Nitsche}}^{s}(\widetilde{u}^{s},\widetilde{m}^{s},\widetilde{y}^{s})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}\widetilde{w}^{s}\cdot(\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}(\widetilde{u}^{s})n)ds-\int_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}(\widetilde{v}^{s}-(\widetilde{v})^{s}_{\text{in}})\cdot(2\nu\widetilde{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{w}^{s})n+\widetilde{q}^{s}n)ds

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}\widetilde{w}^{s}\cdot(\widetilde{v}^{s}-(\widetilde{v})_{\text{in}}^{s})(\widetilde{v}^{s}\cdot n)ds+\int_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}c_{d}\nu(\widetilde{v}^{s}-(\widetilde{v})_{\text{in}}^{s})\cdot\widetilde{w}^{s}ds.

Here,
c_{d}
is the constant that is chosen to ensure the correct convergence rate of the Dirichlet boundary condition.
We discuss its choice below.

We then transform the weak forms above into the reference domain forms.
Since the reference domain is the same for the two flow scenarios, we can use the same trial and test spaces for both, i.e.,

\mathcal{U}(\Omega_{0})=\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{0})=\mathcal{U}_{v}(\Omega_{0})\times\mathcal{U}_{p}(\Omega_{0})
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{v}(\Omega_{0}):=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega_{0}):v=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{object}},\,v\cdot n=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{\text{free-slip}}\},\qquad\mathcal{U}_{p}(\Omega_{0}):=L^{2}(\Omega_{0})

Using the variable transformations for the strain rate and stress tensors,

\displaystyle\varepsilon(v^{s})

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{sym}(\nabla_{X}v^{s}F^{-1}),

(84)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}(u^{s})

\displaystyle:=2\nu\varepsilon(v^{s})-p^{s}I.

(85)

we have that for any
y\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega_{0})
,

r(u^{s},m^{s},z,y):=\langle\mathcal{R}(u^{s},m^{s},z),y\rangle_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{V}}=r_{\mathrm{flow}}(u^{s},m^{s},z,y)+r_{\mathrm{Nitsche}}(u^{s},m^{s},z,y),

(86)

where

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{flow}}(u^{s},m^{s},z,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\left(\nabla_{X}v^{s}F^{-1}v^{s}\right)\cdot w^{s}\det FdX+\int_{\Omega_{0}}2\nu\varepsilon(v^{s}):\varepsilon(w^{s})\det FdX

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\Omega_{0}}p^{s}\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla_{X}w^{s}F^{-1})\det FdX+\int_{\Omega_{0}}(\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla_{X}v^{s}F^{-1})q^{s}\det FdX,

(87)

and

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{Nitsche}}(u^{s},m^{s},z,y)=-\int_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}w^{s}\cdot\mathbf{T}(u^{s})NdS-\int_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}(v^{s}-v^{s}_{\text{in}})\cdot(2\nu\varepsilon(w^{s})+q^{s}I)NdS

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}w^{s}\cdot(v^{s}-v^{s}_{\text{in}})(v^{s}\cdot N)dS+\int_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}c_{d}\nu(v^{s}-v^{s}_{\text{in}})\cdot w^{s}dS

(88)

Note that we have again made simplifications based on the fact that the left boundary remains fixed under the free-form deformations such that the unit normal does not change.
Moreover, under this representation, the inflow profile is simply given by
v^{s}_{\text{in}}(X)=\exp(m^{s}(X))e_{1}
.

The PDEs are discretized in the reference domain using a mixed Galerkin formulation on a triangular mesh. We employ Taylor–Hood elements for the state
u=(v,p)
, consisting of continuous piecewise-quadratic elements for the velocity
v
and continuous piecewise-linear for the pressure
p
, leading to a state dimension
d_{u}=86{,}205
. Continuous piecewise-quadratic elements are also used for the random parameter field, yielding

d_{m}=201
. For the penalty term in the Nitsche method, we choose
c_{d}=10^{5}/h_{e}
,
where
h_{e}
is the element size corresponding to each triangular element of the reference domain.
The control variable is represented in a finite-dimensional Euclidean space with dimension
d_{\mathcal{Z}}=162
.

5.4.4
Dissipation-reducing 2D Shape Design under Uncertain Inflows

Our optimization problem seeks to simultaneously minimize the viscous dissipation rate induced by each of the two distinct inflow configurations
s\in\{\mathrm{East},\mathrm{North}\}
,
a measure that is physically related to the drag forces acting on the object. We write the dissipation QoI in spatial coordinates as

Q_{z}^{s}(\widetilde{u}^{s}):=\int_{\Omega_{z}^{s}}2\nu\widetilde{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{v}^{s}):\widetilde{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{v}^{s})\,dx.

(89)

We can pose this in the reference domain by the change of variables for the strain tensor,

Q(u^{s},m^{s},z)=\int_{\Omega_{0}}2\nu\varepsilon(v^{s}):\varepsilon(v^{s})\det FdX.

(90)

To quantify performance under uncertainty, we define a weighted entropic risk objective that combines the two flow scenarios in a weighted sum,

\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{weighted}}(z):=\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{East}}(z)+0.3\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{North}}(z)+\mathcal{P}_{\ell_{2}}(z;\alpha),

(91)

where we take

\mathcal{J}^{s}(z):=\rho^{\mathrm{entropic},\beta}_{m^{s}\sim\mu_{m}^{s}}(Q(u^{s}(m^{s},z),m^{s},z)),

(92)

to be the entropic risk measure for each inflow scenario
s\in\{\mathrm{East},\mathrm{North}\}

and
\mathcal{P}_{\ell_{2}}
to be the
\ell_{2}
penalization functional (
73
).
Here, we use
\alpha=0.001
for the penalty weight
and
\beta=1
for a moderate level of risk aversion.

We then consider the shape optimization problem subject to the governing PDE constraints and box constraints on the shape variables
z
(
73
). In addition, we impose a volume constraint to prevent unrealistic shrinkage or growth of the object by requiring the volume to remain between
30\%
and
120\%
of its initial value
V_{0}:=|\Omega_{\mathrm{object}}|=\pi\cdot 0.1^{2}
.
That is, we solve the minimization problem

\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{weighted}}(z),\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{ad}=\{z\in[-1,1]^{d_{z}}:\mathrm{Vol}(z)\in[0.3V_{0},1.2V_{0}]\},

(93)

where the volume functional is implemented as

\mathrm{Vol}(z)=|\Omega_{\mathrm{channel}}|-\int_{\Omega_{0}}\det FdX.

5.5.1
Problem Formulation

In this problem, we consider two different optimization QoIs that both capture notion of the drag forces on the object.
These are (i) the total horizontal force on the object and (ii) the total bending moment on the object with respect to the center of its base. These QoIs are defined separately for each inflow scenario indexed by
s\in\{\mathrm{East},\mathrm{North}\}
.
For each case, let
u^{s}=(v^{s},p^{s})

denote the velocity–pressure solution of the steady incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in spatial coordinates
The
force and bending moments acting on the tower boundary

\Gamma_{\mathrm{object}}
with outward unit normal
n
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{z}^{s}(\widetilde{u}^{s},\widetilde{u}^{s})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{object}}^{s}}\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}(\widetilde{u}^{s})\,n\,ds,

(95)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{z}^{s}(\widetilde{u}^{s},\widetilde{m}^{s})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{object}}^{s}}(x-O)\times\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}(\widetilde{u}^{s})n\right)\,ds,

(96)

where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{T}
are the spatial coordinates,
O=(0.5,0,0.5)^{T}
is the center of the base of the structure, and
\times
denotes the cross product.
We let

\mathbf{F}^{s}(u^{s},m^{s},z)

and

\mathbf{M}^{s}(u^{s},m^{s},z)

denote their respective forms when the input coordinates are in the reference domain (i.e., the force and moment vector are still expressed in spatial coordinates).
Writing
\mathbf{F}^{s}=(F_{1}^{s},F_{2}^{s},F_{3}^{s})

and
\mathbf{M}^{s}=(M_{1}^{s},M_{2}^{s},M_{3}^{s})^{T}
,
the two scalar QoIs that we will consider are the total horizontal force

Q_{\mathrm{force}}^{s}(u^{s},m^{s},z):=(F_{1}^{s})^{2}+(F_{2}^{s})^{2},

(97)

where the vertical component,
F_{2}^{s}
, is omitted,
and the total bending moment

Q_{\mathrm{bend}}^{s}(u^{s},m^{s},z):=\|\mathbf{M}^{s}(u^{s},m^{s},z)\|_{2}^{2}=(M_{1}^{s})^{2}+(M_{2}^{s})^{2}+(M_{3}^{s})^{2}.

(98)

In addition to the optimization QoIs, we incorporate penalization terms to regularize the design and enforce physical consistency. Again, two types of penalization are considered: (i) an
\ell_{2}
penalty on the shape control coefficients (
73
), and (ii) a gravity moments penalization.
For the gravity moment, we assume the structure itself has a uniform solid density of
\rho_{\mathrm{object}}=2{,}000
.
The moment about the center of its base is then given by

\mathbf{M}^{g}(z)=\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{object}}}(x-O)\times\rho_{\mathrm{object}}g\;e_{2}\;dx,

(99)

where
g=9.81
is the gravitational acceleration constant and
e_{2}=(0,-1,0)^{T}
is the unit vector in the vertical direction.
Similar to the integrals above, we can formulate this gravitational moment integral in the reference domain.
We then take

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{grav}}(z;\alpha):=\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\mathbf{M}^{g}(z)\|^{2}_{2}.

(100)

The objective functions for design under uncertainty are then given
by the weighted sum of entropic risk measures across the two flow scenarios
supplemented by an appropriate penalization term.

For the horizontal force reduction, we adopt the objective function

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{force}}(z)=0.75\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{force}}^{\mathrm{East}}(z)+0.25\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{force}}^{\mathrm{North}}(z)+\mathcal{P}_{\ell_{2}}(z;\alpha)

(101)

where for each flow scenario
s\in\{\mathrm{East},\mathrm{North}\}
, we have

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{force}}^{s}(z)=\rho^{\mathrm{entropic},\beta}_{m^{s}\sim\mu_{m}^{s}}(Q^{s}(u^{s},m^{s},z)).

(102)

Here, the penalization
\mathcal{P}_{\ell_{2}}
is the
\ell_{2}
penalization used in (
73
), for which we take
\alpha=2\times 10^{-5}
.

For bending-moment reduction, we adopt the objective function

5.6
Comparison of timings

In this section, we report the computational cost of evaluating the state and subsequent VJPs using either the PDE solver or the neural operator approximation. Execution times are measured using device-appropriate timers: CPU timings are measured as wall-clock elapsed time using Python’s

Since the state and VJP evaluations account for the majority of the computational cost in both training-data generation, neural operator training, and the evaluation of the objective function and its gradients in OUU, we use these timing results to contextualize the sample-efficiency comparisons from the previous sections and to highlight the online speedups achieved by the Shape-DINO approach.

Recall that for the PDE-based approach, state evaluation corresponds to solving the state PDE, which itself may consist of solving a sequence of linear systems when the state PDE is nonlinear, while the VJP requires solving a single linear system corresponding to the adjoint PDE.
We therefore report the single-core timings of the solving the state and adjoint PDEs, where we use the direct solver MUMPS
[
94

as the underlying linear solver.
For surrogate-based methods, we simply report the timing for the forward (state) and backward (VJP) passes of the neural operator; we present per-sample timings for both single sample and batched (1000 sample) evaluations.
Because Shape-NO and Shape-DINO share the same network architecture, these results are reported as one single time.
The results for the Poisson, 2D and 3D Navier-Stokes examples are shown in Table

We first focus on the PDE-based timings. In the Poisson example, where the state PDE is linear, the costs of the state and adjoint solves are indeed comparable.
In fact, when direct solvers are used, this additional adjoint solve can actually be computed at negligible cost by reusing the transpose of the matrix factorization for the state PDE.
On the other hand, in the Navier–Stokes examples,
the cost of the adjoint solves are on average
0.2

Moreover, since direct solvers allow multiple VJPs to be computed at the cost of a single VJP,

of the cost of the state data (negligible amount in the linear Poisson case and

0.25\times
in the Navier–Stokes case).
Thus, compared to the orders-of-magnitude gain in sample efficiency seen in the previous sections, the benefits of derivative-based training far outweigh this small additional cost per sample.

In terms of strictly online costs in optimization, neural operators provide substantial speedups over PDE solvers when used to evaluate the QoI
(m,z)\mapsto Q(u(m,z)m,z)
and its gradients with respect to the shape variable
z
.
For state solves, single sample neural operator evaluations are more than two orders of magnitude faster for the Poisson problem and more than four orders of magnitude faster for the 2D Navier–Stokes problem. For batched evaluations the speedups exceed five and six orders of magnitude for the Poisson and 2D Navier–Stokes problems, respectively.
This speedup is particularly noteworthy because batched execution is native to single-GPU systems. In contrast, achieving comparable throughput for CPU-based PDE solves in a bulk-synchronous setting typically requires additional hardware, whereas neural operator evaluation typically does not. Similar advantages are observed for VJP/adjoint solve used to evaluate the
z
gradient of the QoI. In the single sample case, the neural operator yields nearly four orders of magnitude speedup in the Poisson problem and three orders of magnitude speedup for the 2D Navier–Stokes problem. In the batched case the speedups exceed four orders of magnitude for both problems.

The advantage becomes even more pronounced in the 3D Navier–Stokes setting, where the computational cost of high-fidelity PDE solves increases dramatically due to both the higher discretization dimension and the increased complexity of sparse factorizations in 3D relative to 2D.
For this problem the neural operators state solve speedups exceed six and eight orders of magnitude for the single sample and batched evaluation respectively. The VJP speedups likewise exceed five and seven orders of magnitude for the single sample and batched evaluations, respectively.
These results demonstrate that replacing repeated PDE solves with neural operator surrogates yields significant computational savings, making large-scale optimization problem feasible at a cost that can be prohibitive using only PDE solvers.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we have presented a novel surrogate-based framework, Shape-DINO, for accelerating PDE-constrained shape OUU problems. By representing geometric variability through diffeomorphic mappings to a fixed reference domain and utilizing reduced-basis neural operator architectures with derivative-informed training, the proposed framework jointly learns the solution map and its derivatives with respect to both the random parameters and the design variables. This framework directly addresses the coupled challenges of geometric variability, sampling complexity, fast online evaluation, and gradient accuracy that arise in risk-based shape optimization.

Beyond the methodological contributions, to the best of our knowledge, this work provides the first end-to-end theoretical analysis of RBNO surrogates for optimization under uncertainty. We proved the universal approximation properties (Theorem
3.1
) of the multi-input RBNO and established bounds on the induced optimization error in the risk neutral setting (
Theorem

3.2
), providing a rigorous justification for surrogate-based optimization.

Through a collection of numerical experiments, we have demonstrated that derivative-informed neural operators can efficiently solve a range of shape OUU problems. In all cases, incorporating Jacobian information during training led to substantial improvements in state prediction accuracy, gradient fidelity, and most importantly, reliable optimal designs. These observations are consistent with the a priori error bounds derived in Section
2
, which show that optimization errors are directly controlled by errors in both the solution and its shape derivatives. As a result, Shape-DINO consistently produced more robust solutions and more reliable optimization behavior than non-derivative neural operators. Moreover, the reduced-basis architecture enabled fast inference, while surrogate-based SAA allowed large-batch sampling that would be computationally infeasible using PDE solves alone.

Our experiments further showed that Shape-DINO achieves significantly better cost–accuracy trade-offs than PDE-based approaches. For comparable levels of accuracy, Shape-DINO required several orders of magnitude fewer PDE solves than direct SAA-based PDE optimization, while enabling fast online evaluation once trained. This performance aligns with the approximation results established in Section
3
, which provides theoretical guarantee that reduced-basis multi-parameter neural operators can approximate both the solution and its derivatives arbitrarily well in appropriate
C^{1}
norms. In practice, this allows the offline training cost to be amortized across multiple optimization runs involving different objectives, risk measures, or regularization choices, enabling repeated design queries, interactive design-space exploration, and near-real-time shape optimization under uncertainty.

In addition, we demonstrated that the proposed framework scales to complex 3D problems, where fully PDE-based optimization is computationally prohibitive. These results highlight the potential of Shape-DINO for novel applications involving high-dimensional geometry, uncertainty, and complex physical models.

We also acknowledge the limitations of our framework. When the optimization objective is relatively simple, i.e. accurately approximated with few PDE solves, our framework may be unnecessarily complex. In such settings, classical PDE-based optimization may be more appropriate. Shape-DINO is more advantageous in regimes where geometric variability, uncertainty, and sampling complexity make traditional approaches computationally infeasible. Also, our framework relies on diffeomorphic mesh deformation and elastic extensions, which are effective for moderate geometric variations. However, the chosen shape parametrization and training distribution may not adequately cover large or extreme deformations. If the optimization explores shapes far outside this regime, mesh distortion can occur and can affect both PDE accuracy and surrogate training.

Finally, the results of this work suggest several directions for future research, including extensions to incorporation of higher-order derivative information, adaptive sampling strategies guided by optimization progress, and applications to more complex geometric parameterizations. Moreover, rather than serve as proxies for the PDE solves in evaluating the cost functional, surrogates can act as control variates to be employed along with small numbers of PDE-based evaluations to accelerate estimation of risk measures within a variance reduction framework
[
95
,
96
]
. This opens the door to methods that are intermediate between neural operator-based and PDE-based cost functional evaluation, and to studying optimal tradeoffs between PDE solves during Shape-DINO training versus PDE solves during control variate-based optimization.

More broadly, this work demonstrates that incorporating shape and parametric sensitives into neural operator training is essential for reliable and scalable OUU, positioning Shape-DINO as a powerful tool for complex PDE-constrained design optimization workflows. Beyond risk-based shape optimization, we envision that the proposed shape derivative informed operator learning framework can be employed in a broad range of settings, including PDE-based inverse problems for geometric and shape reconstruction, Bayesian inversion under geometric uncertainty, and near real time parametric deterministic shape optimization. This highlights the potential of Shape-DINO as a general computational framework for design, inference, and decision making for complex engineered systems.

(115)

Moreover,
there exist constant
M_{u}
and
M_{u_{z}}

such that

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{p}_{\mu_{m}}(\mathcal{M};\mathcal{U})}

\displaystyle\leq M_{u},

(116)

\displaystyle\|D_{z}u(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{p}_{\mu_{m}}(\mathcal{M};\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U}))}

\displaystyle\leq M_{u_{z}},

(117)

for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
.

Proof.

First, for any
z\in\mathcal{B}
, (
24a
) of
2.1
yields

\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{p}\leq(\|u(0,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\kappa_{u}\|m\|_{\mathcal{M}})^{p},

such that
\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu}[\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{p}<\infty
since
\mu_{m}
has finite
p^{th}
moments.
Moreover, for any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
,

\|u(\cdot,z_{2})-u(\cdot,z_{1})\|_{L^{p}_{\mu_{m}}}^{p}=\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|u(m,z_{2})-u(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{p}]\leq\kappa_{u}^{p}\|z_{2}-z_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Z}}^{p},

which shows that
z\mapsto u(\cdot,z)
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
\kappa_{u}
.
An analogous application of (

) yields Lipschitz continuity of
z\mapsto D_{z}u(\cdot,z)
with constant
\kappa_{u_{z}}
.
This continuity combined with compactness of the admissible set
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}\subset\mathcal{B}
then implies the existence of the upper bounds in (

) and (
117
).
∎

On the other hand, the Lipschitz continuity of
D_{u}Q
and
D_{uz}Q
from

implies the following bounds.

Lemma B.1

(Quadratic bounds)
.

Suppose
Q
satisfies
2.2
with the compact admissible set
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
and open set
\mathcal{B}
.
Then, for every

,

, and

|Q(u_{2},m,z)-Q(u_{1},m,z)|\leq\|D_{u}Q(u_{1},m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}\|u_{2}-u_{1}\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{u}}}{2}\|u_{2}-u_{1}\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}

(118)

and

\|D_{z}Q(u_{2},m,z)-D_{z}Q(u_{1},m,z)\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\leq\|D_{uz}Q(u_{1},m,z)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Z}^{\prime})}\|u_{2}-u_{1}\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{uz}}}{2}\|u_{2}-u_{1}\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}.

(119)

Proof.

Consider first (

Taking a Taylor expansion for

Q(u_{2},m,z)-Q(u_{1},m,z)=\int_{0}^{1}D_{u}Q(u_{1}+th,m,z)hdt,

where

\displaystyle|Q(u_{2},m,z)-Q(u_{1},m,z)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|D_{u}Q(u_{1}+th,m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}\|h\|_{\mathcal{U}}dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left(\|D_{u}Q(u_{1},m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}+\kappa_{Q_{u}}t\|h\|_{\mathcal{U}}\right)\|h\|_{\mathcal{U}}dt

\displaystyle\leq\|D_{u}Q(u_{1},m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}}\|h\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{u}}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}.

For

∎

Corollary B.1
.

Consider the setting of

Proof.

B.2.1
Differentiability of the Quantity of Interest

As discussed, for any fixed
z
, the quantity
Q(u(m,z),m,z)
is a random variable depending on the realization of
m\in\mathcal{M}
.
Thus, we can treat the mapping
z\mapsto Q(u(\cdot,z),\cdot,z)
as a mapping from the variable
z
to the space of random variables.
Under
2.1
and
2.2
, we can show that this is a continuously differentiable mapping from

\mathcal{B}
to
L^{1}_{\mu_{m}}
.

Proposition B.2
.

Suppose
u
satisfies
2.1
and
Q
satisfies
2.2
with the Borel probability measure
\mu_{m}
, admissible set
\mathcal{Z}_{ad}
, and open set
\mathcal{B}
for some
p\geq 2
.
Then, the map
f_{Q}:z\mapsto Q(u(\cdot,z),\cdot,z)
is a continuously differentiable function from
\mathcal{B}
to
L^{1}_{\mu_{m}}(\mathcal{M};\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Well-defined:

We begin by verifying
f_{Q}(z)
is in
L^{1}_{\mu_{m}}
for any
z\in\mathcal{B}
.
By (
122
) of
Corollary

B.2
, we have

\|f_{Q}(z)\|_{L^{1}_{\mu_{m}}}=\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu}[|Q(u(m,z),m,z)|]\leq\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}\left[M_{Q}+M_{Q_{u}}\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{u}}}{2}\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}\right].

Since
u(\cdot,z)\in L^{p}_{\mu_{m}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{U})
with
p\geq 2
, the resulting expectation is finite such that
f_{Q}(z)\in L^{1}_{\mu_{m}}

Differentiability:

Since
Q
and
u
are both continuously differentiable, the derivative is formally given by

D_{z}f_{Q}(z)=D_{u}Q(u(\cdot,z),\cdot,z)D_{z}u(\cdot,z)+D_{z}Q(u(\cdot,z),\cdot,z).

We verify this is indeed the derivative. In particular, we consider the residual

r(z,h):=\|Q(u(\cdot,z+h),\cdot,z+h)-Q(u(\cdot,z),\cdot,z)-D_{z}f_{Q}(z)h\|_{L^{1}_{\mu_{m}}}

is
o(\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}})
.
We begin by letting
A(m,z):=D_{u}Q(u(m,z),m,z)D_{z}u(m,z)+D_{z}Q(u(m,z),m,z)
, such that

\displaystyle\frac{r(z,h)}{\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}}=\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}\left[\frac{|Q(u(m,z+h),m,z+h)-Q(u(m,z),m,z)-A(m,z)h|}{\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}}\right].

In particular, by the differentiability of
Q
and
u
, the integrand converges to zero pointwise in
m
as
h\rightarrow 0
.
We now seek a dominating function on the integrand.
To this end, define

\mathsf{A}_{1}:=D_{u}Q(u(m,z),m,z)D_{z}u(m,z),\qquad\mathsf{A}_{2}:=D_{z}Q(u(m,z),m,z)

such that
A=\mathsf{A}_{1}+\mathsf{A}_{2}
, and

\mathsf{D}_{1}:=Q(u(m,z+h),m,z+h)-Q(u(m,z),m,z+h),\quad\mathsf{D}_{2}:=Q(u(m,z),m,z+h)-Q(u(m,z),m,z),

such that
Q(u(m,z+h),m,z+h)-Q(u(m,z),m,z)=\mathsf{D}_{1}+\mathsf{D}_{2}
.
We can bound each term separately.

1.

For
\mathsf{A}_{1}
, we make use of the Lipschitz continuity of
D_{u}Q
, such that

\frac{|\mathsf{A}_{1}(m,z)h|}{\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq\|\mathsf{A}_{1}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\leq M_{Q_{u}}\|D_{z}u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}+\kappa_{Q_{u}}\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}\|D_{z}u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}.

(126)

2.

For
\mathsf{A}_{2}
, we make use of (
123
) from
Corollary

B.2
, such that

\frac{|\mathsf{A}_{2}(m,z)h|}{\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq\|\mathsf{A}_{2}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\leq M_{Q_{z}}+M_{Q_{uz}}\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{uz}}}{2}\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}.

(127)

3.

For
\mathsf{D}_{1}
, we make use of (
120
) from
Corollary

B.1
, such that

\displaystyle|\mathsf{D}_{1}(m,z)|

\displaystyle\leq(M_{Q_{u}}+\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}})\|u(m,z+h)-u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{u}}}{2}\|u(m,z+h)-u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(M_{Q_{u}}+\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}})\kappa_{u}\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{u}}\kappa_{u}^{2}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}^{2},

where this latter inequality follows from the Lipschitz continuity of
u
from
2.1
.
Therefore,

\frac{|\mathsf{D}_{1}(m,z)|}{\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq(M_{Q_{u}}+\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}})\kappa_{u}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{u}}\kappa_{u}^{2}}{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}.

(128)

4.

For
\mathsf{D}_{2}
, we first make use of the mean value theorem, such that

|\mathsf{D}_{2}(m,z)|\leq\|D_{z}Q(u(m,z),m,\zeta)\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}},

where
\zeta=z+th
for some
t\in[0,1]
.
For sufficiently small
h
, we have that
\zeta\in\mathcal{B}
.
This allows us to apply (
123
) from
Corollary

B.2
to obtain

\frac{|\mathsf{D}_{2}(m,z)|}{\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}}\leq\|D_{z}Q(u(m,z),m,\zeta)\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\leq M_{Q_{z}}+M_{Q_{uz}}\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{uz}}}{2}\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}.

(129)

In each case, the upper bounds are quadratic forms of
\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}
and
\|D_{z}u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}
(or equivalently
\|D_{z}u(m,z)\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}
),
both of which are
L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}
functions.
Thus, the integrand is dominated by an
L^{1}_{\mu_{m}}
function.
The dominated convergence theorem then allows passing to the limit such that

\frac{r(z,h)}{\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}}\rightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }h\rightarrow 0.

Continuity of the derivative:

To see continuity, consider any
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
.
We have that

\|D_{z}f_{Q}(z_{2})-D_{z}f_{Q}(z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\leq\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|\mathsf{A}_{1}(m,z_{2})-\mathsf{A}_{1}(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}+\|\mathsf{A}_{2}(m,z_{2})-\mathsf{A}_{2}(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}].

Continuity of
D_{u}Q
,
D_{z}Q
,
u
, and
D_{z}u
implies that the integrand converges to zero for every
m
.
Again, it remains to find a dominating function that allows the limit
z_{2}\rightarrow z_{1}

to be taken outside of the expectation.
In particular, (
126
) yields

\|\mathsf{A}_{1}(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\leq M_{Q_{u}}\|D_{z}u(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}+\kappa_{Q_{u}}\|u(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{U}}\|D_{z}u(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}.

Making use of the Lipschitz continuity of
u
and
D_{z}u
, we also have
for
h=z_{2}-z_{1}

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{A}_{1}(m,z_{2})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq M_{Q_{u}}\|D_{z}u(m,z_{2})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}+\kappa_{Q_{u}}\|u(m,z_{2})\|_{\mathcal{U}}\|D_{z}u(m,z_{2})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}

\displaystyle\leq M_{Q_{u}}\left(\|D_{z}u(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}+\kappa_{u_{z}}\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\kappa_{Q_{u}}(\|u(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\kappa_{u}\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}})(\|D_{z}u(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}+\kappa_{u_{z}}\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}}),

For sufficiently small
h
, both

\|\mathsf{A}_{1}(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}

and

\|\mathsf{A}_{1}(m,z_{2})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}

are dominated by a common
L^{1}_{\mu_{m}}
function.
Similarly, for
\mathsf{A}_{2}
, (
127
) yields

\|\mathsf{A}_{2}(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\leq M_{Q_{z}}+M_{Q_{uz}}\|u(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{uz}}}{2}\|u(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2},

while Lipschitz continuity of
u
and
D_{z}u
imply

\|\mathsf{A}_{2}(m,z_{2})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\leq M_{Q_{z}}+M_{Q_{uz}}(\|u(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\kappa_{u}\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}})+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{uz}}}{2}(\|u(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\kappa_{u}\|h\|_{\mathcal{Z}})^{2}.

such that

\|\mathsf{A}_{2}(m,z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}

and

\|\mathsf{A}_{2}(m,z_{2})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}

are also dominated by a
L^{1}_{\mu_{m}}
function.
The dominated convergence theorem then allows passing to the limit, such that

\|D_{z}f_{Q}(z_{2})-D_{z}f_{Q}(z_{1})\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\rightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }z_{2}\rightarrow z_{1},

which shows the continuity of
D_{z}f_{Q}
.
∎

A corollary of this result is that the cost function defined by the expectation of
Q
is continuously differentiable.

Corollary B.3
.

Consider the setting of
Proposition

B.2
.
Then, the function
\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

\mathcal{J}(z):=\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[Q(u(m,z),m,z)]

(130)

is continuously differentiable, and its derivative is given by

D_{z}\mathcal{J}(z)=\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[D_{u}Q(u(m,z),m,z)D_{z}u(m,z)+D_{z}Q(u(m,z),m,z)].

(131)

Proof.

This follows from the chain rule, and the fact that the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{m}}[\cdot]
is a continuous linear functional on

, and therefore is continuously differentiable.
∎

B.2.2
Proof of Proposition
2.2

We now present the proof for

Proof.

Recall that by

z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}

For
\mathsf{D}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq M_{Q_{u}}+\kappa_{Q_{u}}\|u(m,z)-u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\kappa_{Q_{u}}\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}.

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|\mathsf{D}_{2}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle\qquad+\kappa_{Q_{u}}\|u(\cdot,z)-u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}\|D_{z}u(\cdot,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}.

Note that by Young’s inequality,
ab\leq\tfrac{a^{2}}{2}+\tfrac{b^{2}}{2}
, we can write this without cross terms as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|\mathsf{D}_{2}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle\leq(M_{Q_{u}}+\kappa_{Q_{u}}M_{u})\|D_{z}u(\cdot,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{u}}}{2}\|u(\cdot,z)-u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}^{2}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{u}}}{2}\|D_{z}u(\cdot,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}^{2}.

Finally, for

,

\|\mathsf{D}_{3}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\leq(M_{Q_{uz}}+\kappa_{Q_{uz}}\|u(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}})\|u(m,z)-u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{uz}}}{2}\|u(m,z)-u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}.

Again, taking expectations and using the Cauchy–Schwarz along with the upper bound from
Proposition

B.1
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|\mathsf{D}_{3}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}]\leq\left(M_{Q_{uz}}+\kappa_{Q_{uz}}M_{u}\right)\|u(\cdot,z)-u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}+\frac{\kappa_{Q_{uz}}}{2}\|u(\cdot,z)-u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}^{2}.

Combining the above, we have

\displaystyle\|D_{z}\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{mean}}(z)-D_{z}\mathcal{J}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{mean}}(z)\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(\|u(\cdot,z)-u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}+\|D_{z}u(\cdot,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{2}\left(\|u(\cdot,z)-u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}^{2}+\|D_{z}u(\cdot,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}^{2}\right),

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are constants depending on
M_{Q_{u}},M_{Q_{uz}},\kappa_{Q_{u}},\kappa_{Q_{uz}},M_{u}
but not
u_{\theta}
.
∎

Appendix C
Universal Approximation Results

In this section, we develop the proofs for the universal approximation property of RBNOs in terms of both the outputs and the derivatives (
Theorem

3.1
), and its subsequent application to risk neutral optimization problems (
Theorem

3.2
). In order to prove
Theorem

3.1
, we decompose the approximation errors of an RBNO into the dimension reduction errors associated with projecting the inputs and outputs and the neural network approximation error in the resulting latent space.
We bound each source of the approximation errors separately, beginning with the latent space neural network in
Section

C.1
followed by the dimension reduction in
Section

C.2
, before combining them into a proof of
Theorem

3.1
in
Section

C.3
.
The proof of
Theorem

3.2
then follows from
Theorem

3.1
along with
Theorem

2.1
and
Proposition

2.2
.

Within this section, we will deviate from the notational conventions introduced in
Remark

2.1
relating to the change of coordinates between spatial and reference domains.
Namely,
x
will be used to denote generic vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
, and
\widetilde{\cdot}
will not carry any implications about whether it is a spatial or reference domain quantities.

C.1
Finite-Dimensional Neural Network Approximation

We first prove a variant of the universal approximation theorem for neural networks in finite dimensions, which we state as follows.

Theorem C.1

(Universal approximation on semibounded sets)
.

Let
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathcal{B};\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}})
, where
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is open
and let
\mu_{x}
be a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
.
Suppose that for some
p\geq 1
,

1.

the mapping
\mathcal{B}\ni y\mapsto g(\cdot,y)\in L^{p}_{\mu_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}})
is continuous, and

2.

the mapping
\mathcal{B}\ni y\mapsto D_{y}g(\cdot,y)\in L^{p}_{\mu_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}\times d_{y}})
is continuous, where
\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}\times d_{y}}
is equipped with the Frobenius norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}
.

Then for any
\epsilon>0
and any compact set
K_{y}\subset\mathcal{B}
,
there exists a GELU neural network
g_{\theta}
such that

\sup_{y\in K_{y}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu_{x}}\left[\|g(x,y)-g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}^{p}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu_{x}}\left[\|D_{y}g(x,y)-D_{y}g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}^{p}\right]<\epsilon^{p}.

The result differs from conventional universal approximation results in two ways.
First, the approximation accuracy considers both the output and the derivative error.
Second, instead of considering approximation errors as either uniform over compact sets or integral quantities over distributions (with potentially unbounded support),
we decompose the inputs into two sets of variables, here denoted
x
and
y
, where integration is performed over the former while the supremum is taken over the latter.
This anticipates the differing roles of the two inputs for the solution operator
(m,z)\mapsto u(m,z)
, where integration is with respect to
m
while optimization is with respect to
z
.

In this work, we prove
Theorem

C.1
following a cutoff argument used in
Yao et al. 2025
.
That is, we use conventional universal approximation results of feedforward neural networks over compact sets to construct a neural network
\tilde{g}
with high accuracy over a compact subset
K_{x}\times K_{y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathcal{B}
.
We then construct a cutoff function in the form of a second neural network such that when applied to
\tilde{g}
, controls the approximation error outside of the compact set
K_{x}\times K_{y}
.
Finally, we show that this construction can be taken to the limit as
K_{x}
approaches the entire
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
, allowing the
L^{p}_{\mu_{x}}
error over
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
to be made arbitrarily small uniformly over
y\in K_{y}
.

We present proof of
Theorem

C.1
follows.
First, we show how an appropriate cutoff function can be constructed as a neural network (
Lemmas

C.2
,
C.3
and
C.4
).
We then show how the cutoff function can be used in combination with standard universal approximation results to construct approximations of
g
that are accurate within a compact set while having controlled growth rates outside of the compact set (
Lemmas

C.5
and
C.6
).
Finally, in Section
C.1.3
, we complete the proof for
Theorem

C.1
by combining the aforementioned results through the limiting argument taking
K_{x}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
.

C.1.1
Neural Network Constructions

In order to construct the cutoff function, we first require an approximation of the norm function
\|\cdot\|
as a neural network.
As shown in
[
Yao et al. 2025
, Lemma D.4]
, we can use GELU activation functions to construct an approximation of the absolute value function
|\cdot|
.
We summarize this result as follows.

Lemma C.1

(Absolute value function)
.

Given
\theta>0
, define for
x\in\mathbb{R}

f_{\mathrm{abs}}(x;\theta):=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}(\theta x)+\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}(-\theta x)}{\theta},

(132)

Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\theta>0
such that

\left|f_{\mathrm{abs}}(x;\theta)-|x|\right|\leq\epsilon

(133)

and
f_{\mathrm{abs}}(x;\theta)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

We can then use this to construct an approximation to the
\ell_{1}
norm as follows.

Lemma C.2

(
\ell_{1}
norm)
.

Given
\theta>0
, define for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}

f_{\ell_{1}}(x;\theta):=\mathbf{1}^{T}\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}(\theta x)+\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}(-\theta x)}{\theta},

(134)

where
\mathbf{1}:=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\theta>0
such that

\left|f_{\ell_{1}}(x;\theta)-\|x\|_{1}\right|\leq\epsilon

(135)

and
f_{\ell_{1}}(x;\theta)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
.

Proof.

As shown in
Lemma

C.1
, the GELU activation function can be used to construct an approximation of the absolute value function on
\mathbb{R}
.
Then, for any
\epsilon
, there exists
\theta>0
such that

f_{\mathrm{abs}}(t;\theta):=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}(\theta t)+\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}(-\theta t)}{\theta},

(136)

satisfies

f_{\mathrm{abs}}(t;\theta)\geq 0
and
|f_{\mathrm{abs}}(t;\theta)-|t||\leq\epsilon/d_{x}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
This implies that for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
, we have
f_{\ell_{1}}(x;\theta)\geq 0
and

|f_{\ell_{1}}(x;\theta)-\|x\|_{1}|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d_{x}}\left|f_{\mathrm{abs}}(x_{j};\theta)-|x_{j}|\right|\leq\epsilon.

∎

The GELU activation function can also be used to construct a cutoff function
\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}
on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(t)\approx 1
for
t\leq R
while
\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(t)\approx 0
for
t\gg R
.
The following is a simplified statement of
[
Yao et al. 2025
, Lemma D.5]
.

Lemma C.3

(Cutoff function)
.

Given
\theta,b>0
, define for
x\in\mathbb{R}

\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(x;\theta,b):=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}(\theta(x-b-1))-\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}(\theta(x-b))}{\theta}+1.

(137)

Then, for any
\epsilon>0
and
R>1
,
there exist
\theta,b>0
such that the following hold:

\begin{cases}|\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(x;\theta,b)-1|\leq\epsilon&0\leq x\leq R,\\
|\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(x;\theta,b)|\leq\epsilon&x\geq 4R,\\
\end{cases}

(138)

Additionally, there exists
M>0
independent of
\theta,b
such that

|\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(x;\theta,b)|\leq M
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Lemmas

C.2
and
C.3
can then be used to construct a cutoff function based on the
\ell_{2}
norm of the input.

Lemma C.4

(Cutoff function using
\ell_{2}
norm)
.

There exists
M>0
such that
for any
\epsilon>0
and
R>1
,
there exists a two-layer GELU neural network
f_{\mathrm{cut}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\begin{cases}|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)-1|\leq\epsilon&0\leq\|x\|_{2}\leq R,\\
|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)|\leq\epsilon&\|x\|_{2}\geq 5(\sqrt{d_{x}}+1)R,\\
|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)|\leq M&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}.\end{cases}

(139)

Proof.

We define
f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)=f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x;\theta_{\ell_{1}},\theta_{\mathrm{cut}},b_{\mathrm{cut}})
as the composition of
f_{\ell_{1}}
and
\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}
, i.e.,

f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x;\theta_{\ell_{1}},\theta_{\mathrm{cut}},b_{\mathrm{cut}})=\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(f_{\ell_{1}}(x;\theta_{\ell_{1}});\theta_{\mathrm{cut}},b_{\mathrm{cut}}).

(140)

We will choose the cutoff radius for
\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}
to account for the conversion for
\ell_{1}
to
\ell_{2}
, i.e.,
\|x\|_{2}\leq\|x\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{d_{x}}\|x\|_{2}
.

To this end, let
\epsilon,R
be given. We construct
f_{\ell_{1}}
to satisfy

|f_{\ell_{1}}(x)-\|x\|_{1}|\leq R,\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}},

and
\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}
to satisfy

\begin{cases}|\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(x;\theta,b)-1|\leq\epsilon&0\leq x\leq\tilde{R},\\
|\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(x;\theta,b)|\leq\epsilon&x\geq 4\tilde{R},\\
|\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(x;\theta,b)|\leq M&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}},\\
\end{cases}

where
\tilde{R}:=(\sqrt{d_{x}}+1)R
.

We then consider the following cases.

1.

\|x\|_{2}\leq R
. We have

f_{\ell_{1}}(x)\leq\|x\|_{1}+R\leq(\sqrt{d_{x}}+1)R=\tilde{R},

implying that
|\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(f_{\ell_{1}}(x))-1|\leq\epsilon
.

2.

\|x\|_{2}\geq 5(\sqrt{d_{x}}+1)R
. We have

f_{\ell_{1}}(x)\geq\|x\|_{1}-R\geq 5(\sqrt{d_{x}}+1)R-R\geq 4\tilde{R},

implying that
|\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}(f_{\ell_{1}}(x))|\leq\epsilon
.

Finally,
|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)|\leq M
holds for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
with the same
M
from
Lemma

C.3
.
Since, both
\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}
and
f_{\ell_{1}}
can be interpreted as single-layered neural networks, their resulting composition
f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)
is a two-layered neural network.

∎

C.1.2
Neural Network Approximations with Controlled Growth

Universal approximation theorems, such as
[
Pinkus 1999
, Theorem 4.1]

show that single-layer neural networks are able to approximate continuously differentiable functions arbitrarily-well in terms of both their output value and their derivatives over compact input domains.
As a corollary, we can show that for GELU activation functions, the resulting approximant can be constructed to have globally bounded outputs and derivatives despite the fact that GELU itself is unbounded.

Lemma C.5

(Bounded approximation on compact sets)
.

Suppose
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathcal{B};\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}})

where
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is open.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0
and compact sets
K_{x}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and
K_{y}\subset\mathcal{B}
,
there exists a single-layer GELU neural network
g_{\theta}
such that
simultaneously

\sup_{(x,y)\in K_{x}\times K_{y}}\|g(x,y)-g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}\leq\epsilon,

(141)

and

\sup_{(x,y)\in K_{x}\times K_{y}}\|D_{y}g(x,y)-D_{y}g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}\leq\epsilon.

(142)

Moreover,
g_{\theta}
and its derivatives are bounded, i.e., there exists
M=M(\epsilon)>0
such that simultaneously

\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}\|g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}\leq M

(143)

and

\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}\|D_{y}g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}\leq M.

(144)

Proof.

First, consider the function

\sigma(x)=\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}(x+1)-\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}(x-1)
.
It can be shown that
\sigma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\sigma^{(k)}(x)|<\infty
for all
k\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
. That is,
\sigma
is a smooth, non-constant function with bounded output and derivatives.

Now, let
\xi:=(x,y)
and
K:=K_{x}\times K_{y}
.
By standard universal approximation theorems, such as
[
Pinkus 1999
, Theorem 4.1]
,
there exists a single-layered neural network of the form

g_{\theta}(\xi)={W}_{2}\sigma({W}_{1}\xi+{b}_{1})+{b}_{2}

(145)

such that simultaneously

\sup_{\xi\in K}\|g(\xi)-g_{\theta}(\xi)\|_{2}\leq\epsilon

(146)

and

\sup_{\xi\in K}\|D_{\xi}g(\xi)-D_{\xi}g_{\theta}(\xi)\|_{F}\leq\epsilon.

(147)

We recognize that
g_{\theta}(\xi)
can instead be written as

g_{\theta}={W}_{2}(\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}({W}_{1}\xi+{b}_{1}+1)-\sigma_{\mathrm{GELU}}({W}_{1}\xi+{b}_{1}-1))+{b}_{2},

(148)

which can be thought of as a single-layer GELU neural network. Moreover, since
\sigma
has bounded values and derivatives, we have that
D_{\xi}g_{\theta}(\xi)
is bounded for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}.

Finally, it suffices to recognize that

\|D_{y}g(x,y)-D_{y}g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}\leq\|D_{\xi}g(\xi)-D_{\xi}g_{\theta}(\xi)\|_{F}.

∎

Remark C.1
.

Within the proof above, we have referenced universal approximation theorems that consider the approximation of functions defined on the entire input space,
e.g.,
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}})
, while we have only assumed that
g
is defined on the open subset
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathcal{B}
.
This is largely inconsequential for the following reason.
Since
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathcal{B}
, where
\Subset
denotes a compact embedding,
there exist open sets
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}
such that
K\Subset\mathcal{B}_{1}\Subset\mathcal{B}_{2}\Subset\mathcal{B}
.
Moreover, for the closure
\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{B}_{1})
, we consider a smooth external approximation to the indicator function,

\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{B}_{1})}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}};\mathbb{R})
,
such that
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{B}_{1})}|_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}=1
and
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{B}_{1})}|_{\mathcal{B}_{2}^{c}}=0
.
This can be used to define
\tilde{g}(x,y)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{cl}(\mathcal{B}_{1})}(x,y)g(x,y)
, which we extend by
0
to
y\notin\mathcal{B}
.
The extension
\tilde{g}
is therefore in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}})
and its outputs and derivatives coincide with
g
over the set
K
.
It suffices then to consider a neural network approximation
g_{\theta}
of
\tilde{g}
instead of
g
when using the conventional universal approximation theorems.

Lemma C.6

(Bounded approximation on semi-bounded sets)
.

Suppose
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathcal{B};\mathbb{R}^{d_{g}})

where
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is open. Then, for any compact subset
K_{y}\subset\mathcal{B}
,
\epsilon>0,R>1
, and
p\geq 1
,
there exists a three-layer GELU neural network
g_{\theta}
such that simultaneously

\sup_{\|x\|_{2}\leq R,y\in K_{y}}\|g(x,y)-g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}\leq\epsilon,

(149)

and

\sup_{\|x\|_{2}\leq R,y\in K_{y}}\|D_{y}g(x,y)-D_{y}g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}\leq\epsilon.

(150)

Moreover,
g_{\theta}
can be chosen such that

\|g(x,y)-g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}^{p}\leq C_{p}(\|g(x,y)\|_{2}^{p}+1)

(151)

and

\|D_{y}g(x,y)-D_{y}g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}^{p}\leq C_{p}(\|D_{y}g(x,y)\|_{F}^{p}+1)

(152)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and
y\in K_{y}
,
where
C_{p}>0
is a fixed constant that does not depend on
\epsilon
or
R
.

Proof.

Our strategy is as follows. We first use
Lemma

C.5
to construct a bounded approximation
\tilde{g}_{\theta}
to
g
on a compact set. We then control the growth of the approximation
\tilde{g}_{\theta}
in the
x
direction by (approximately) multiplying it by a cutoff function
\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}
.

To this end, let
g,\epsilon,R
be given.
For any
\epsilon_{g}>0
,

Lemma

C.5
allows us to construct
\tilde{g}_{\theta}
, a bounded approximation of
g
over the compact sets
K_{x}:=\{\|x\|_{2}\leq 4(\sqrt{d_{x}}+1)R\}
and
K_{y}
, such that

\sup_{(x,y)\in K_{x}\times K_{y}}\|g(x,y)-\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}\leq\epsilon_{g},\qquad\sup_{(x,y)\in K_{x}\times K_{y}}\|D_{y}g(x,y)-D_{y}\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}\leq\epsilon_{g},

and

\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}\|\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}\leq M_{g},\qquad\sup_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}\|D_{y}\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}\leq M_{g},

noting that
M_{g}=M_{g}(\epsilon_{g})
.
For an arbitrary
\epsilon_{\mathrm{cut}}>0
,
we also consider the cutoff function
f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)
from
Lemma

C.4
satisfying

\begin{cases}|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)-1|\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{cut}}&\|x\|_{2}\leq R\\
|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)|\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{cut}}&\|x\|_{2}\geq 5(\sqrt{d_{x}}+1)R\\
|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)|\leq M_{\mathrm{cut}}&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}},\end{cases}

where
M_{\mathrm{cut}}
does not depend on
\epsilon_{\mathrm{cut}}
or
R
.
Finally,
we consider a single-layered neural network approximation
f_{\times}
of the multiplication function
\times(t_{1},t_{2})=t_{1}t_{2}
, such that

\|f_{\times}-\times(\cdot,\cdot)\|_{C^{1}(B)}\leq\epsilon_{\times},

for an arbitrary
\epsilon_{\times}>0
, where

B=[-M_{\mathrm{cut}},M_{\mathrm{cut}}]\times[-M_{g},M_{g}]
.
This is again possible by standard universal approximation results
Pinkus 1999
.
We then take

g_{\theta}(x,y):=f_{\times}(f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x),\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y))\approx f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y),

which is a three-layer neural network since
\tilde{f}_{\mathrm{cut}}
is a two-layer neural network.
This has the derivative

D_{y}g_{\theta}(x,y)=D_{2}f_{\times}(f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x),\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y))D_{y}\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y),

where
D_{2}
refers to partial differentiation with respect to the second variable.
Note that this construction where
f_{\times}
is chosen based on the output range of
f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)
and
\tilde{g}
ensures that

\|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)\tilde{g}(x,y)-f_{\times}(f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x),\tilde{g}(x,y))\|_{2}\leq\epsilon_{\times}

and

\|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)I-D_{2}f_{\times}(f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x),\tilde{g}(x,y))\|_{F}\leq\epsilon_{\times}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
.

We now consider the following cases for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
given that
y\in K_{y}

1.

\|x\|_{2}\leq R
. By the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|g(x,y)-g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\|g(x,y)-\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}+\|(1-f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x))\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}+\|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)-f_{\times}(f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x),\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y))\|_{2}.

Since
\|x\|_{2}\leq R
, we have
\|g(x,y)-g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}\leq\epsilon_{g}

and
|1-f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)|\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{cut}}
.
Thus,

\|g(x,y)-g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}\leq\epsilon_{g}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{cut}}M_{g}+\epsilon_{\times}.

By analogous reasoning, we have

\displaystyle\|D_{y}g(x,y)-D_{y}g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|D_{y}g(x,y)-D_{y}\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|(1-f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x))D_{y}\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)D_{y}\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)-D_{2}f_{\times}(f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x),\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y))D_{y}\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{g}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{cut}}M_{g}+\epsilon_{\times}M_{g}.

2.

\|x\|_{2}\geq 5(\sqrt{d_{x}}+1)R
. We have

\displaystyle\|g(x,y)-g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|g(x,y)\|_{2}+\|g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|g(x,y)\|_{2}+|f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)|\|\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}+\|f_{\times}(f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x),\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y))-f_{\mathrm{cut}}(x)\tilde{g}_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|g(x,y)\|_{2}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{cut}}M_{g}+\epsilon_{\times}

and

\displaystyle\|D_{y}g(x,y)-D_{y}g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}\leq\|D_{y}g(x,y)\|_{F}+\|D_{y}g_{\theta}(x,y)\|_{F}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|u(m,z)-u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{p}]+\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|D_{z}u(m,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}^{p}]

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2^{p-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|u(m,z)-u_{r}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{p}]+\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|D_{z}u(m,z)-D_{z}u_{r}(m,z)\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}^{p}]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2^{p-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|u_{r}(m,z)-u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{p}]+\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|D_{z}u_{r}(m,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}^{p}]\right).

(163)

By
Lemma

C.9
, there exist
r_{u}
and
r_{m}
such that

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|u(m,z)-u_{r}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{p}]+\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}[\|D_{z}u(m,z)-D_{z}u_{r}(m,z)\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}^{p}]\leq\frac{\epsilon^{p}}{2^{p}}.

(164)

We now seek an approximation of
u_{r}
for the chosen
r_{u}
and
r_{m}
.
To this end,we define the mapping
g:\mathbb{R}^{r_{m}}\times\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}}
,

g(x,z)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}u(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}x,z),

(165)

such that

u_{r}(m,z)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}g(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}m,z).

(166)

We can verify that
g
satisfies the assumptions in
Theorem

C.1
.
First,
g
is continuously differentiable since
u
is continuously differentiable.
Moreover, the mappings
\mathcal{B}\ni z\mapsto g(\cdot,z)\in L^{2}_{\mu_{r}}(\mathbb{R}^{r_{m}};\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}})

and
\mathcal{B}\ni z\mapsto D_{z}g(\cdot,z)\in L^{2}_{\mu_{r}}(\mathbb{R}^{r_{m}};\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}\times d_{z}})

are Lipschitz continuous with respect to
z
,
where
\mu_{r}=\left(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}\right)^{\sharp}\mu_{m}
denotes the pushforward measure of
\mu_{m}
under the projection
\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}
.
To see this, consider the
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
, and the difference

\|g(\cdot,z_{2})-g(\cdot,z_{1})\|_{L^{p}_{\mu_{m}}}^{p}=\int\|g(x,z_{2})-g(x,z_{1})\|_{2}^{p}d\mu_{r}(x).

Using the definition of
g
and the
change of variables
x=\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}m
with the pushforward measure
\mu_{r}
, we have

\int\|g(x,z_{2})-g(x,z_{1})\|_{2}^{p}d\mu_{r}(x)=\int\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}u(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}m,z_{2})-\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}u(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}m,z_{2})\|_{2}^{p}d\mu_{m}(m).

Since
\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}
is an orthonormal basis, we have
\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U},\mathbb{R}^{r_{u}})}=1
, such that

\int\|g(x,z_{2})-g(x,z_{1})\|_{2}^{p}d\mu_{r}(x)\leq\int\|u(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}m,z_{2})-u(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}m,z_{2})\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{p}d\mu_{m}(m)\leq\kappa_{u}^{p}\|z_{2}-z_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Z}}^{p}.

This implies that
z\mapsto g(\cdot,z)
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to
z
.
An analogous procedure can be used to show that
z\mapsto D_{z}g(\cdot,z)
is also Lipschitz continuous with respect to
z
.

This allows us to apply the result of
Theorem

C.1
. That is, given
\epsilon>0
, there exists a neural network approximation
g_{\theta}
of
g
satisfying

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu_{r}}[\|g(x,z)-g_{\theta}(x,z)\|^{p}]+\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu_{r}}[\|D_{z}g(x,z)-D_{z}g_{\theta}(x,z)\|_{F}^{p}]\leq\frac{\epsilon^{p}}{2^{p}}.

(167)

Then the error between
u_{r}(m,z)
and the RBNO
u_{\theta}(m,z)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*},z)

can be written directly in terms of
g
and
g_{\theta}
.
That is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}\left[\|u_{r}(m,z)-u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}\left[\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}\left(g(\Psi_{r_{m}}^{*}m,z)-g_{\theta}(\Psi_{r_{m}}^{*}m,z)\right)\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu_{r}}\left[\|g(x,z)-g_{\theta}(x,z)\|_{2}^{p}\right],

(168)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}\left[\|D_{z}u_{r}(m,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(m,z)\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{m\sim\mu_{m}}\left[\|\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}\left(D_{z}g(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}m,z)-D_{z}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}m,z)\right)\|_{\mathrm{HS}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U})}^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu_{r}}\left[\|D_{z}g(x,z)-D_{z}g(x,z)\|_{F}^{p}\right].

(169)

Combining the bounds
(
164
)
and
(
167
),
along with (
168
) and (
169
)
yields the desired approximation error when substituted into (
163
).

When
b_{m}\neq 0
,
we have

\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}_{r_{m}}m=\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}_{r_{m}}(m-b_{m})+\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}_{r_{m}}b_{m}.

Thus, for the neural network
g_{\theta}
constructed using the procedure above,
we have

g_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}_{r_{m}}m,z)=g_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}_{r_{m}}(m-b_{m})+\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}_{r_{m}}b_{m},z)=\tilde{g}_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}_{r_{m}}(m-b_{m}),z),

where
\tilde{g}_{\theta}
is also a GELU neural network whose input layer bias is shifted by
\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}_{r_{m}}b_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{m}}
.
Thus, the RBNO has the desired representation

u_{\theta}(m,z)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{m}}\tilde{g}_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}^{*}_{r_{m}}(m-b_{m}),z).

Finally, when
b_{u}\neq 0
, we can construct the approximation
\tilde{u}_{\theta}(m,z)
for the shifted operator
u(m,z)-b_{u}
using the same procedures above, noting that

u(m,z)-b_{u}
also satisfies
2.1
.
The resulting approximation of
u(m,z)
is then given by the affine shift,

u_{\theta}(m,z)=\tilde{u}_{\theta}(m,z)+b_{u}=\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}g_{\theta}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}^{*}(m-b_{m}),z)+\bar{u}.

∎

C.4
Universal Approximation for Optimization under Uncertainty

We can now combine
Theorem

3.1
with
Theorem

2.1
to prove
Theorem

3.2
,
which guarantees the existence of an RBNO with sufficiently accurate output and derivatives such that when used as a surrogate in expectation minimization (assuming the objective is convex), its stationary points are accurate.

Proof.

(Of
Theorem

3.2
)
First, we note that any GELU neural network
g_{\theta}
is smooth and its derivatives of all orders are globally bounded by the nature of the GELU activation functions.
Thus, given any reduced bases
\mathbf{\Phi}_{r_{u}}
and
\mathbf{\Psi}_{r_{m}}
, the resulting RBNO is also
u_{\theta}
constructed from
g_{\theta}
is also smooth with bounded derivatives.
This implies that
u_{\theta}
satisfies
2.1
with the same open set
\mathcal{B}
.

Consider now any
0<\epsilon_{u}<1
.
By the universal approximation result of
Theorem

3.1
,
there exists an RBNO such that

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\|u(\cdot,z)-u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}\leq\epsilon_{u},

(170)

and

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\|D_{z}u(\cdot,z)-D_{z}u_{\theta}(\cdot,z)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu_{m}}}\leq\epsilon_{u}.

(171)

Since both
u
and
u_{\theta}
satisfy
2.1
while
Q
satisfies
2.2
,
Proposition

2.2
implies that there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
u_{\theta}
such that

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}\|D_{z}\mathcal{J}(z)-D_{z}\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(z)\|_{\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\leq C\epsilon_{u},

(172)

where the linear error term dominates for
\epsilon_{u}<1
.
By
Theorem

2.1
, we have bounds for both the optimization error and the optimality gap, given by

\displaystyle\|z^{\star}-z^{\dagger}\|_{\mathcal{Z}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\lambda}\epsilon_{u},

(173)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(z^{\dagger})-\mathcal{J}(z^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{2}}{2\lambda}\epsilon_{u}^{2}.

(174)

From this, we see that it suffices to construct
u_{\theta}
using
\epsilon_{u}
satisfying

\epsilon_{u}\leq\min\left\{\frac{\lambda\epsilon}{C},\frac{\sqrt{2\lambda\epsilon}}{C},1\right\}.

∎
