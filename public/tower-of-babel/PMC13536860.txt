Bloodstream infections and antimicrobial resistance patterns among under-fives children with suspected bloodstream infections attending the pediatric clinic of Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital in Bahir Dar, northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Alemayehu Abate, Melkamnesh Azage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntibiotic-resistant bloodstream infections are a major public health concern, particularly in children under five years of age. The objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of bloodstream infections and assess the antimicrobial resistance profiles and their associated factors among febrile children under five years of age in Bahir Dar, northwest Ethiopia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from November to December 2025 among 281 children under five years of age who attended the pediatric clinic of Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital in Bahir Dar. Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected using a structured questionnaire through face-to-face interviews with the parents or guardians. Approximately 3 mL of venous blood was aseptically collected and cultured using standard microbiological techniques. Bacterial identification was performed based on colony characteristics and biochemical tests. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method. Data were entered into EpiData version 3.1 and analyzed using SPSS version 26. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with bloodstream infections, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe prevalence of bloodstream infection was 19.2% (54/281; 95% CI: 14.0%–24.4%). The most common isolate was Klebsiella pneumoniae (31%), followed by Enterobacter cloacae (11%), Escherichia coli (9%), and Acinetobacter baumannii (9) %. Gram-negative bacteria accounted for 80% of the isolates. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed high resistance rates to commonly used antibiotics, particularly ampicillin (87.0%, 40/46), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (86.3%, 44/51), and cefepime 42/54 (77.8). Resistance was also high to ciprofloxacin (68.5%, 37/54), tobramycin (68.6%, 35/51), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (66.0%, 31/47), gentamicin (63.3%, 31/49), tetracycline (61.1%, 33/54), and chloramphenicol (60.5%, 26/43). In contrast, lower resistance rates were observed for cefotaxime (37.0%, 20/54) and meropenem (20.4%, 11/54). Significant associated factors of blood stream infections included age < 1 year (AOR = 2.11; 95% CI: 1.46–2.86), fever duration > 7 days (AOR = 2.74; 95% CI: 1.22–6.15), partially immunized children (AOR = 3.21; 95% CI: 1.08–9.51; p = 0.036), and non-immunized children (AOR = 9.87; 95% CI: 3.61–26.9; p < 0.001).ConclusionThe prevalence of bloodstream infections among febrile children under five years of age was high. Younger age, fever duration > 7 days, and partially immunization and non-immunization were significant predictors of bloodstream infection. Strengthening antimicrobial stewardship, improving immunization coverage, and enhancing early diagnosis are essential to reduce the burden of bloodstream infections and combat antimicrobial resistance.

Introduction

Blood stream infection is a major public health problem, particularly in pediatric populations under five years of age [1]. If this infection fails to be detected and treated promptly, it may cause severe systemic inflammation and sepsis [2]. Due to their undeveloped immune systems, which are less able to mount effective defenses against invasive infections, bacteremia in children is clinically significant [3]. The disease can worsen quickly, leading to significant morbidity, marked by extended hospital stays, invasive procedures, and possibly long-term health consequences [4]. The mortality rate from pediatric bacteremia is still alarmingly high, especially in low- and middle-income nations, where resources for diagnosis and treatment may be scarce [5].

Blood stream infection continues to be one of the most serious health issues in the world, with an estimated 31.5 million cases and 5.3 million fatalities per year [6]. The epidemiology of bacteremia shows significant regional differences impacted by healthcare facilities, socioeconomic status, and common infections [7]. Bacteremia has serious consequences in young children in sub-Saharan Africa, a region burdened by infectious diseases and systemic healthcare delivery challenges [8]. According to World Health Organization (WHO) predictions, children under the age of five accounted for 41% of all sepsis cases worldwide in 2020, and 42% of intensive care patients with septicemia died [9].

In Ethiopia, the prevalence of bloodstream infections ranges from 12.84 to 27.78% [10]. Common bloodstream infections, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Acinetobacter baumannii, have shown increasing levels of resistance, with numerous isolates demonstrating resistance to third-generation cephalosporins and first-line antibiotics, such as ampicillin and cotrimoxazole [11, 12]. Bacterial isolates from the bloodstream of children have been found to have high rates of resistance to first-line antibiotics, such as ampicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, tetracycline, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole [13]. Treatment options are complicated by multidrug resistance (MDR) and extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) production in Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, and other gram-negative isolates [14].

Despite the severity of the infection, little is known about the prevalence of bacteremia and patterns of antibiotic resistance among children under five years of age in Bahir Dar, a major urban center in the Amhara region, which serves a large catchment population with diverse healthcare needs, making it an important setting for investigating the epidemiology of bloodstream infections. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of bloodstream infections and assess the antimicrobial resistance profiles and their associated factors among febrile under-five children in Bahir Dar, northwest Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study design and period

A cross-sectional prospective study was conducted among under five children attending the pediatric clinic of Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH) in Bahir Dar, northwest Ethiopia. The study was conducted between November and December 2025. Bahir Dar is the capital city of the Amhara National Regional State, which is approximately 565 km northwest of Addis Ababa. The hospital serves as a referral center for more than 7 million people in the region and provides comprehensive diagnostic and treatment services, including pediatric diagnosis and treatment.

Study population

The study population consisted of all children (aged < 5 years) who were clinically suspected of having bloodstream infections and had blood culture investigations performed during the study period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All children with a clinical suspicion of bloodstream infection whose parents/guardians provided consent and who had blood samples collected for culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were included. Children whose specimens were improperly collected or contaminated were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling procedure

A single population proportion formula was used to calculate the sample size. A 95% confidence level (Z₁₋α/₂ = 1.96), 5% margin of error (d = 0.05), and prevalence of 21% were considered [1]. The final sample size was n-final = 255 + 26=281 after a 10% non-response rate was added: 255 + 26=281. Hence, the final sample size for this study was 281 children. A consecutive sampling technique was used, and all eligible pediatric patients who met the inclusion criteria during the study period were recruited until the required sample size was reached.

Data collection tool and procedure

A standardized questionnaire provided by the interviewer was used to collect data. The questionnaire was adapted from previous studies conducted on a similar topic [1, 3, 10, 11] and customized accordingly. To ensure validity and reliability, the questionnaire was evaluated for the relevance, clarity, consistency, and comprehensiveness of each item. A pretest was conducted on 5% (n = 14) of the sample size among participants from a population similar to the study population but not included in the final study. The pretest was used to evaluate the clarity, wording, sequence, and appropriateness of the questions. Revisions were made accordingly before the actual data collection. The tool included laboratory findings, clinical history, and sociodemographic characteristics. Face-to-face interviews with parents or guardians were used to collect data. Trained laboratory personnel aseptically collected approximately 3 ml of venous blood samples for culture and antibiotic susceptibility testing after obtaining informed consent from the parents/guardians. The venous puncture site was thoroughly cleaned with 2% iodine tincture and 70% alcohol.

Operational definitions

Age

The age of the child at the time of data collection, categorized as < 1 year, 1–3 years, and 3–5 years.

Sex

The biological sex of the child, categorized as male or female.

Residence

The place of usual residence of the child, categorized as urban or rural.

Referral Status

A child was considered referred if he/she was sent from another health facility for further evaluation or treatment; otherwise, the child was classified as non-referred.

Duration of fever

The length of time the child had experienced fever before presentation to the health facility, categorized as < 3 days, 3–7 days, or > 7 days.

Recent hospitalization

A history of admission to a health facility for at least 24 h within the previous three months before the current visit. Responses were categorized as yes or no.

Use of invasive devices

History of exposure to any invasive medical device, such as an intravenous cannula, urinary catheter, or other device penetrating the skin or body cavity before sample collection. Responses were categorized as yes or no.

Prior antibiotic use

Receipt of any systemic antibiotic treatment within the three months preceding data collection. Responses were categorized as yes or no.

History of chronic illness

Presence of a physician-diagnosed chronic medical condition requiring ongoing care or treatment, such as congenital heart disease, asthma, diabetes mellitus, or other long-term illnesses. Responses were categorized as yes or no.

History of skin wound infection

Presence or documented history of a skin wound infection before enrollment in the study. Responses were categorized as yes or no.

Educational level of mother/guardian

The highest level of formal education completed by the child’s mother or guardian, categorized as no formal education, primary school, secondary school, and college and above.

Occupation of mother/guardian

The current employment status of the child’s mother or guardian, categorized as government employee or non-government employee.

Family income

The average monthly household income reported by the parent or guardian, categorized as < 5,000 Ethiopian Birr, 5,000–10,000 Ethiopian Birr, and > 10,000 Ethiopian Birr.

Immunization status

The vaccination status of the child according to the national immunization schedule. Children who received all age-appropriate vaccines were classified as fully immunized, those who missed one or more recommended doses were classified as partially immunized, and those who had not received any vaccine were classified as not immunized [1, 3, 7–11, 20, 21, 31–33, 35].

Sample processing and culturing for identification of bacterial isolates

Blood samples were inoculated into sterile blood culture bottles containing appropriate enrichment broth. After gently mixing the inoculated bottles, they were incubated for up to seven days at 35–37 °C. Every day, the bottles were checked for indications of microbial growth, such as hemolysis, gas generation, and turbidity. Subcultures were carried out on blood agar, MacConkey agar, and chocolate agar where growth was suspected or indicated.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method. Fresh three to five pure colonies of bacteria were selected and transferred to a tube containing 4–5 mL of sterile normal saline. The mixture was gently mixed to homogenize the suspension. Three to five pure colonies of bacteria were selected and transferred to a tube containing 4–5 mL of sterile normal saline. The mixture was gently mixed to form a homogeneous suspension. The prepared suspension was incubated at 35–37 °C until the turbidity matched the 0.5 McFarland standard. A sterile cotton swab was dipped into the prepared suspension, and excess fluid was removed by pressing it against the inner wall of the test tube. The suspension was evenly distributed on the entire surface of Mueller-Hinton agar for non-fastidious bacteria and on Mueller-Hinton agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood for fastidious bacteria. The inoculated plates were left at room temperature for 3–15 min before placing the antibiotic discs. The antibiotics used were amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (AMC, 20 µg), ampicillin (AMP, 10 µg), chloramphenicol (C, 30 µg), ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg), cefepime (CFP, 30 µg), ciprofloxacin (CPR, 5 µg), ceftriaxone (CTR, 30 µg), cefoxitin (CXT, 30 µg), gentamicin (GM, 10 µg), meropenem (MER, 10 µg), tetracycline (TC, 30 µg), and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP–SMX, 1.25/23.75 µg). In accordance with the standardized CLSI 2021 recommendations, the zone of inhibition was classified as sensitive, intermediate, or resistant [15]. The antimicrobial agents included in the susceptibility testing panel were selected based on their availability and frequent use for the treatment of bacterial infections in the study setting. Although ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, and tetracycline are not routinely recommended as first-line therapies for pediatric patients, they are prescribed under close clinical supervision for their potential side effects. Therefore, these antibiotics were included in the susceptibility testing panel to provide clinically relevant information on local antimicrobial resistance patterns.

Data quality assurance

Data collectors and laboratory personnel received training on study protocols, ethical issues, sample collection, safe specimen handling, testing for bloodstream infection, participant counseling, and safety and confidentiality issues. The lead investigator conducted daily monitoring and pre-tested the questionnaire. To guarantee data quality, two skilled supervisors rigorously reviewed the data collected daily. They focused on evaluating the completeness, internal consistency, validity, and integrity of the data collection tool. Both internal and external quality controls were implemented, and laboratory supplies were examined for expiration.

Data processing and analysis

The data were coded and entered into EpiData version 3.1 (EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark). The cleaned and coded data were exported to the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages were used to summarize the socio-demographic, clinical, blood stream bacterial infections, and antimicrobial susceptibility characteristics of the study participants. The prevalence of bloodstream infection and the distribution of bacterial isolates were presented using tables and figures. Antimicrobial resistance patterns were analyzed by calculating the proportion of resistant isolates for each antimicrobial agent and were presented as frequencies and percentages. Bivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with bloodstream infection among under-five children with suspected bloodstream infections. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Crude odds ratios (CORs) and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to determine the strength and direction of associations with blood stream infections. Statistical significance was declared at a p-value < 0.05. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF values < 10 indicating no significant multicollinearity. The fitness of the final multivariable logistic regression model was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and a p-value > 0.05 was considered indicative of an adequate model fit. This study followed the recommendations of the STROBE guidelines [16].

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research and Ethics Committee of Bahir Dar University (protocol number 34/2025). Permission to conduct the study was granted by the Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. Prior to data collection, the parents/guardians were fully informed about the study objectives, procedures, and their rights, and written informed consent was obtained. Participation was voluntary, and the participants were free to withdraw at any time without consequence. Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout the study period.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants

A total of 281 participants were included in this study. The mean age ± SD of the study participants was approximately 2.27 ± 1.42 years; 82 (29%) were younger than one year old, 100 (36%) were between the ages of three and five. Of the study participants, 144 (51%) were female. The majority of the 142 (51%) were from rural areas. More than half of the participants (148 [53%]) had a referral history from other health facilities. The majority of the participants, 102 (36%), presented with a fever duration of 3–7 days. A history of recent hospitalization was reported in 106 (38%) participants. Similarly, 78 (28%) participants had a history of invasive device use during previous admissions (Table 1).

Distribution of organisms isolated

The prevalence of bloodstream infection in this study was 54/281 (19%, 95% CI: 14.0% – 24.4%). Klebsiella pneumoniae was the most common isolate in our sample, accounting for 17/54 (31%, 95% CI; 18.9% – 43.9%). Enterobacter cloacae was next with 6/54 (11%, 95% CI; 2.7% – 19.5%). Escherichia coli and Acinetobacter baumannii were equally prevalent, each accounting for five isolates (9% each). Antimicrobial resistance of bacterial isolates

Klebsiella pneumoniae was sensitive to ceftazidime (34%) and meropenem (14%); however, it was resistant to ampicillin (100%), ceftriaxone (88%), co-trimoxazole (88%), gentamicin (82%), and tobramycin (70%). Escherichia Coli was sensitive to Meropenem (17%), while it demonstrated strong resistance to Ampicillin (100%), Tobramycin (92%), Co-trimoxazole (92%), Chloramphenicol (75%), Ceftazidime (75%), and Ciprofloxacin (75%).

Acinetobacter baumannii was sensitive to cefoxitin (12%), tetracycline (35%), and meropenem (29%); however, it was highly resistant to ceftriaxone (77%). Enterobacter aerogenes was susceptible to cefoxitin (13%) and meropenem (25%) but highly resistant to ampicillin (100%), tobramycin (100%), co-trimoxazole (100%), ceftriaxone (77%), and chloramphenicol (75%). Pseudomonas aeruginosa was responsive to tobramycin (29%), ceftazidime (14%), gentamicin (29%), tetracycline (14%), and meropenem (14%); however, it was highly resistant to ceftriaxone (71%). Factors associated with bloodstream infection among study participants

Children aged less than one year had more than twofold higher odds of developing bloodstream infection than those aged 3–5 years (AOR = 2.11; 95% CI: 1.46–2.86; p = 0.02). Children with fever lasting > 7 days were significantly more likely to develop bloodstream infections (AOR = 2.74; 95% CI: 1.22–6.15; p = 0.015). Partially immunized children (to a complete routine immunization schedule) had over three times higher odds of bloodstream infection (AOR = 3.21; 95% CI: 1.08–9.51; p = 0.036), while non-immunized children had nearly tenfold increased odds (AOR = 9.87; 95% CI: 3.61–26.9; p < 0.001) compared with fully immunized children (Table 4).

Discussion

Antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections are becoming a more common cause of global morbidity and mortality [17]. It is a major public health concern with significant clinical and economic consequences [18]. The objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of bloodstream infections and assess the antimicrobial resistance profiles and their associated factors among febrile children under five years of age in Bahir Dar, northwest Ethiopia.

In In the current study, the prevalence of bloodstream infections was 54/281 (19.2%, 95% CI: 14.0% – 24.4%). This finding was similar to a study from Gondar, Ethiopia, which reported that the prevalence of bloodstream infections was 20.7% [19], and another study from Ethiopia that reported that 21.6% of cases had culture-positive bacteria that caused pediatric sepsis [20].

In the current study, Klebsiella pneumoniae was the most common isolate, accounting for 17/54 (31%, 95% CI; 18.9% – 43.9%). This finding was similar to of that a study from Tanzania, which indicated that the prevalence of Klebsiella pneumoniae was 26.9% [21]. The high prevalence of K. pneumoniae may be due to its strong nosocomial potential, capacity to endure in hospital settings, and increasing resistance to antibiotics, which makes treatment difficult. In this study, other detected BSI include Acinetobacter baumannii, Enterococcus spp., Enterobacter cloacae, Coagulase-negative Staphylococci (CONS), Escherichia coli, K. oxytoca, K. ozaenae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, S. aureus, and Enterobacter aerogenes. These findings were similar to those of a study conducted in Ethiopia that reported S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, Enterobacter species, and E. coli as the cause of bloodstream infections in children [22]. The dominance of organisms such as A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, Enterobacter spp., and CONS suggests a substantial contribution of healthcare-associated infections, likely linked to invasive procedures, prolonged hospital stays, and possible gaps in infection prevention and control practices. Similarly, the presence of S. aureus and E. coli indicates that both community-acquired and hospital-acquired sources may be contributing to the burden of pediatric BSIs.

In the current study, the majority of isolates were Gram-positive bacteria 43/54, (80%; 95% CI: 69.3%-90.7%, and gram-negative bacteria were 11/54 (20%; 95% CI: 9.3%-30.7%). These findings are similar to those of studies that demonstrated that gram-positive bacteria are more likely to cause BSI than gram-negative bacteria [23, 24]. This finding reflects a mixed epidemiology of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms, with a predominance of opportunistic and hospital-associated pathogens.

In our study, Klebsiella pneumonia was susceptible to ceftazidime and meropenem but was highly resistant to ampicillin, ceftriaxone, cotrimoxazole, gentamicin, and tobramycin. This finding was similar to that of a study from Rwanda, which indicated that 100% of the Klebsiella species exhibited ampicillin resistance.33.3% were resistant to cefotaxime, 77.3% to gentamicin, and 42.8% to ciprofloxacin [25]. This multidrug resistance pattern is alarming, as it significantly limits empirical treatment options in pediatric BSIs and suggests the possible presence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing strains.

In the current study, E. coli demonstrated significant resistance to ampicillin, tobramycin, co-trimoxazole, chloramphenicol, ceftazidime, and ciprofloxacin; however, it was susceptible to meropenem. These findings are consistent with a Rwandan study that found resistance rates to ampicillin and tetracycline of nearly 90%, amoxicillin–clavulanic acid of 83.3%, cefadroxil of 79.3%, and ceftazidime of 78.6% [26]. This finding emphasized increasing antimicrobial pressure and possible misuse or overuse of antibiotics in both community and hospital settings. In this study, Acinetobacter baumannii was sensitive to cefoxitin, tetracycline, and meropenem but resistance to ceftriaxone. Enterobacter aerogenes showed high resistance to ceftriaxone (76.5%) and chloramphenicol (75%) and total resistance (100%) to ampicillin, tobramycin, and co-trimoxazole. On the other hand, partial sensitivity to meropenem (25%) and cefoxitin (12.5%) was detected. These results were comparable with a study from Ethiopia that reported Ampicillin (90.9%), Amoxicillin (100%), Ceftriaxone (62.5%), Chloramphenicol (60%), Ciprofloxacin (36.4%), Gentamycin (75%), cotrimoxazole (100%), and Tetracycline (80%) were all resistant to E. aerogenes [19]. The observed resistance patterns underscore a critical need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship, routine susceptibility testing, and stricter antibiotic use policies to curb the further spread of resistant bloodstream pathogens.

In the current study, Enterococcus spp. was sensitivity to tobramycin and ceftriaxone but showed high resistance to co-trimoxazole, ciprofloxacin, ampicillin, and gentamicin. This finding was consistent with a study done in Myanmar that reported Enterococcus spp. was resistance to ampicillin ranged from 68% to 89%, and high resistance to erythromycin, tetracycline, and ciprofloxacin [27]. Additionally, a Bangladeshi study found that Enterococcus isolates had high levels of ampicillin and ciprofloxacin resistance [28]. This multidrug resistance pattern is clinically important, as ampicillin is traditionally considered a key agent for treating enterococcal infections.

In our study, s. aureus was sensitive to Tetracycline, however it showed high resistance to Ciprofloxacin and Co-trimoxazole and Coagulase Negative Staphylococcus spp. was sensitive to Cefoxitin, Gentamicin and Tetracycline, nevertheless it showed high resistance to Co-trimoxazole and Ciprofloxacin. These findings were similar with a study from Ethiopia that reported Staphylococcus aureus was resistance to Penicillin (92.3%), Ampicillin (84.6%), Co-trimoxazole (61.5%), and Tetracycline (53.8%). Isolates of coagulase Negative Staphylococcus spp. showed 100% resistance to ampicillin and 81.8% resistance to penicillin and co-trimoxazole,100% resistance to ampicillin, 91.7% resistance to gentamicin, tetracycline, and co-trimoxazole [29]. The similarity between the current and previous studies suggests a persistent and widespread resistance problem among Gram-positive BSI pathogens in Ethiopia and neighboring regions. These findings underscore the urgent need for continuous antimicrobial surveillance, rational antibiotic use, and strengthened infection prevention strategies to limit further dissemination of resistant organisms.

In our study, children aged less than one year had more than twofold higher odds of developing bloodstream infection compared to those aged 3–5 years (AOR = 2.11; 95% CI: 1.46–2.86; p = 0.02). This finding was similar with studies that reported neonates and infants had the highest prevalence of bloodstream infections, whereas older children have much lower rates [30]. Additionally, being younger than 12 months is an independent risk factor for infections particularly bloodstream infections [31]. The increased risk of blood stream infections among infants may be due to immature immune systems, decreased maternal antibody protection over time, and increased exposure to invasive procedures or hospitalization. In this study, children who were referred had higher risk of bloodstream infection compared to non-referred children (AOR = 1.88; 95% CI: 0.98–3.61; p = 0.038). This finding was similar with studies that reported the severity of a child’s illness was associated with their referral [32], and referred children had a 2.3- compared to direct admissions [33]. This association may reflect delayed presentation, prior treatment failure, or progression of disease severity before arrival at referral facilities, all of which increase the risk of bloodstream infection. These findings highlight the importance of early recognition and timely management of illness in young children, as well as strengthening primary healthcare systems to reduce delayed referrals and subsequent complications.

In our study, children with fever lasting more than 7 days were significantly more likely to develop bloodstream infection (AOR = 2.74; 95% CI: 1.22–6.15; p = 0.015). This finding was similar with studies that reported in children under five, a fever lasting more than seven days increased the incidence of BSI by 2.9 times (95% CI 2.1–4.0) [34], and prolonged fever is a sign of systemic infection and increased mortality risk [35]. This emphasizes the importance of early diagnostic evaluation of persistent fever to rule out bloodstream infections and initiate timely treatment. In this study, children with a history of antibiotic use had nearly twofold increased risk of BSI (even though not statistically significant) (AOR = 1.89; 95% CI: 0.99–3.61; p = 0.043). This finding was similar with studies that reported antimicrobial resistance and a higher incidence of bacteremia are closely associated with prior antibiotic exposure [36], and children with prior antibiotic treatment are more likely to develop infections caused by BSI [37]. This highlights the importance of rational antibiotic use and adherence to antimicrobial stewardship principles to reduce selection pressure and resistance development.

In our study, partially immunized children had over three times higher odds of bloodstream infection (AOR = 3.21; 95% CI: 1.08–9.51; p = 0.036), while non-immunized children had nearly tenfold increased odds (AOR = 9.87; 95% CI: 3.61–26.9; p < 0.001) compared to fully immunized children. This finding was similar with studies that reported under-immunized and non-immunized children were disproportionately affected by invasive bacterial infections [20], and the risk of blood stream infections necessitating hospitalization is greatly increased by insufficient vaccination status, with children who are not immunized being the most vulnerable [38]. In the current study. age-specific vaccination schedules for children younger than one year was considered that strengthens the assessment of factors associated with bloodstream infections by ensuring that vaccination status was evaluated according to age-appropriate eligibility. These findings highlight the critical role of early fever evaluation, rational antibiotic use, and full adherence to childhood immunization schedules in preventing bloodstream infections among children.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional study design limits the ability to establish a causal link between identified risk factors and bloodstream infections. Second, the use of the conventional blood culture method, which is relatively old and has limited sensitivity may fail to detect low-level bacteremia, reduce the yield, and led to underestimation of bloodstream infection prevalence. Third, prior antibiotic use was based on caregiver self-report, which may introduce recall bias and could affect the accuracy of this variable. Similarly, some clinical and exposure-related data may be subject to information bias. Fourth, although several factors showed statistically significant associations with the outcome, some of the corresponding confidence intervals were relatively wide. Wide confidence intervals indicate a lower level of precision in the estimated effect sizes and may reflect limited sample sizes, low event frequencies in certain subgroups, or substantial variability among the included studies. Finally, anaerobic bacteria and viral causes of febrile illness were not investigated, meaning that some cases of bloodstream infection or sepsis may have been missed, potentially underestimating the true burden of infection.

Conclusion

In this study, the prevalence of bloodstream infection was 19.2%. The most common bacterial isolate was Klebsiella pneumoniae, followed by Enterobacter cloacae, Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Enterococcus species and coagulase-negative staphylococci. The antimicrobial susceptibility pattern showed a high level of resistance to commonly used antibiotics, particularly ampicillin, cotrimoxazole, gentamicin, and cefepime, while relatively better sensitivity was observed for meropenem and ceftazidime. Several factors were significantly associated with bloodstream infections, including younger age (< 1 year), prolonged fever (> 7 days), referral status, and incomplete or non-immunization status. The findings of this study emphasized bloodstream infections caused by bacteria is a serious public health challenge in the study area. Strengthening infection prevention and control measures, promoting antimicrobial stewardship, and ensuring complete childhood immunization are essential to reduce the burden of these infections and improve child health outcomes.
