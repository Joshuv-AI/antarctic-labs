Bilateral symmetrically symptomatic cervical Hirayama disease diagnosed with dynamic magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Rajat Gupta, Nityanand Pandey, Basanti Mazumdar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Hirayama disease is a rare, self-limiting cervical myelopathy characterized by juvenile-onset distal upper-limb unilateral or asymmetrical weakness and wasting. Bilateral and nearly symmetrical involvement is rare and may mimic motor neuron disease. The diagnosis is typically established based on dynamic flexion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).Case Description: A 23-year-old male presented with a 6-year history of progressive distal upper-limb weakness and atrophy that initially involved the right side and later the left. INTRODUCTION

Hirayama disease is a rare cervical flexion myelopathy characterized by insidious onset, distal upper-limb weakness, and wasting in young males. It is usually unilateral or asymmetrical and is due to posterior cervical cord compression occurring during repeated neck flexion, secondary to anterior displacement of the posterior dural sac, leading to repetitive compression of the lower cervical spinal cord.[1,2] Bilateral symmetrical involvement is uncommon and may mimic motor neuron disease, resulting in diagnostic delay.[3] Dynamic flexion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays a key role in confirming the diagnosis. Once diagnosed, progressive cases may be effectively treated with cervical stabilization.

We report a rare case of bilateral symmetrical Hirayama disease diagnosed with dynamic MRI and successfully managed with posterior cervical stabilization.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 23-year-old male presented with a 6-year history of progressively worsening weakness and wasting of the distal upper limbs. The symptoms initially involved the right side and progressed to the left over time. The weakness significantly impaired activities of daily living. There were no sensory symptoms, neck pain, radicular complaints, or bowel and bladder involvement.

On examination, there was marked bilateral distal upper-limb wasting, more pronounced on the right, with relative preservation of brachioradialis muscle bulk (oblique amyotrophy). Proximal muscle strength and sensory examination were normal. Clinical photographs demonstrating bilateral distal upper-limb wasting are shown in Figure 1.

(a) Clinical photograph showing bilateral symmetrical wasting of intrinsic hand muscles. Dorsal view demonstrates atrophy of interossei and thenar muscles with relative sparing of proximal forearm muscles, producing the characteristic pattern seen in Hirayama disease. (b) Clinical photograph showing bilateral distal upper-limb involvement. Palmar view demonstrates relative thinning of distal forearm muscles with sparing of proximal musculature, consistent with involvement of C7–T1 myotomes in Hirayama disease.

Electromyography revealed chronic denervation in the C7–T1 myotomes with preserved sensory nerve action potentials. Neutral cervical MRI demonstrated lower cervical cord atrophy. Dynamic flexion MRI revealed posterior cervical cord compression due to anterior displacement of the posterior dural sac, along with a crescent-shaped enhancing posterior epidural space containing multiple flow voids, consistent with Hirayama disease.[2,3] The dynamic MRI findings are shown in Figure 2.

(a) Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the cervical spine in extension showing disappearance of previously noted posterior epidural enhancing crescent-shaped lesion and restoration of normal dural alignment, consistent with dynamic venous engorgement that resolves in extension, characteristic of Hirayama disease. (b) Dynamic flexion MRI of the cervical spine. Sagittal images obtained in flexion position demonstrate anterior displacement of the posterior dural sac with resultant compression of the lower cervical spinal cord, along with prominent posterior epidural space containing flow voids, consistent with engorged venous plexus characteristic of Hirayama disease. (c) MRI of the cervical spine in neutral position. Axial T2-weighted images demonstrate focal thinning of the lower cervical spinal cord with bilateral symmetric hyperintense foci in the anterior horns (“snake-eye appearance”), consistent with chronic anterior horn cell degeneration in Hirayama disease. (d) Contrast-enhanced MRI in flexion showing prominent crescent-shaped posterior epidural enhancement due to venous plexus engorgement, with anterior shifting of the posterior dura and resultant dynamic cervical cord compression at C4–C6, characteristic of Hirayama disease. (e) Axial MRI of the cervical spine in flexion. Sequential T2-weighted axial images demonstrate flattening of the cervical spinal cord with anterior displacement of the posterior dura and associated posterior epidural space prominence. The cord shows bilateral symmetric hyperintensities in the anterior horn cells, producing the characteristic “snake-eye” appearance, suggestive of chronic ischemic changes. (f) Dynamic cervical spine MRI in extension (noncontrast) showing restoration of normal dural position without anterior displacement and resolution of posterior epidural space expansion, confirming the positional (flexion-dependent) nature of spinal cord compression in Hirayama disease.

Due to progressive symptoms, the patient underwent posterior cervical stabilization using transfacetal cortical screw fixation. Postoperatively, disease progression stabilized with continued functional improvement on follow-up. The patient was followed for 1 year after surgery. Disease progression remained arrested throughout follow-up. Right-hand grip strength improved from approximately 60% preoperatively to 80% at 1 year, while left-hand grip strength remained stable at 40%. No formal functional outcome scores (mJOA, Frankel grade, or other standardized scales) were recorded. Postoperative radiographs are shown in Figure 3. DISCUSSION

Hirayama disease is a self-limiting cervical flexion myelopathy affecting young males and characterized by distal upper-limb amyotrophy due to dynamic cervical cord compression during neck flexion.[1,2] The underlying pathophysiology involves disproportionate growth between the vertebral column and the dural sac, leading to a tight dural canal. During neck flexion, anterior displacement of the posterior dura results in repeated compression of the lower cervical cord and microcirculatory ischemia, predominantly affecting anterior horn cells.[4]

Although typically unilateral or asymmetrical, bilateral symmetrical involvement is rare and may mimic motor neuron disease, leading to diagnostic uncertainty.[3,7] Clinical features such as oblique amyotrophy and sparing of the brachioradialis muscle provide important diagnostic clues.

Dynamic flexion MRI is the gold standard for diagnosis, demonstrating anterior dural displacement, posterior cervical cord compression, and engorgement of the posterior epidural venous plexus.[2,3,5] Neutral MRI may only show cord atrophy, making dynamic imaging essential.

Management includes cervical collar immobilization in early stages; however, surgical intervention is indicated in progressive cases.[6] Posterior cervical stabilization techniques, including transfacetal cortical screw fixation, limit flexion-induced cord compression and provide favorable outcomes.[2]

In the present case, surgical intervention resulted in arrest of disease progression and functional improvement, supporting its role in selected patients. In the present case, posterior cervical stabilization halted further neurological decline and resulted in measurable improvement in right-hand grip strength over 1 year of follow-up, although standardized functional scores were not obtained.

CONCLUSION

Bilateral symmetrical cervical Hirayama disease is rare and may lead to diagnostic confusion with motor neuron disease. Dynamic flexion MRI is essential for accurate diagnosis. Posterior cervical stabilization is an effective treatment in progressive cases to halt disease progression and improve functional outcomes.
