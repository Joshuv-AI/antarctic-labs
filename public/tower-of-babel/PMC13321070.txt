Exploring memory effects: Sparse identification in vector-borne diseases
Authors: Dimitri Breda, Muhammad Tanveer, Jianhong Wu, Xue Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceVector-borne diseases pose growing public health risks, yet reliable forecasting is often hindered by limited data and complex, delayed transmission processes. This study introduces a data-driven framework that uncovers hidden transmission dynamics with memory directly from time series data relying on sparse discovery, i.e., using only few basis functions. The approach enables accurate forecasting of disease risk under data constraints and further insight certifies its reliability and robustness.

Projecting tick-borne infection risk to humans under substantial uncertainties of tick population size and activity, environmental condition, and human–tick interaction imposes significant computational challenges since the data we observe, i.e., the human incidence of the relevant diseases, is only the final outcome of a complex and multiyear tick–host pathogen transmission and tick–human contact processes. Data-driven short-term forecasting requires rigorous computational and data fitting techniques to estimate parameters of the underlying transmission dynamics model. Long-term scenario analysis (for example, under projected climate scenarios) demands identifying the first-principle mechanisms that govern the patterns of tick–host and host–human contact for the human case reported. The challenge is amplified by the limited availability of time series of human incidence and environmental conditions despite a large body of fields and laboratory studies on ticks and hosts.

To address these short-term forecasting and long-term projection challenges, we employ the Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) framework (1) that automatically discovers governing equations from time series data of some state variables. SINDy was pioneered for mechanistic models using ordinary differential equations (ODEs) (1, 2), and was later extended to models involving spatial variation such as partial differential equations (PDEs) (3), stochastic variation such as stochastic differential equations (4), and more recently, development and feedback delay such as differential equations with constant (discrete) time delays (5–8). These advancements highlight the flexibility and applicability of the framework, reinforcing its significance as a pivotal technique in the rapidly expanding field of data-driven scientific discoveries.

Despite this substantial progress, a significant gap that is highly relevant to projection of vector-borne disease burden in human persists in the application of SINDy to systems characterized by complex relationships between variables and by distributed memory effects. Distributed delays and renewal-type Volterra integral equations involving integration over a continuum of past states to model distributed memory dynamics are key ingredients in modeling biological, ecological, and engineering systems in which the evolution of the system relies on a range of past states (9–11). Examples include age-structured population models (11), maturation processes in cell populations (10), tick-borne disease transmission dynamics (12) and genetic regulatory networks with complex time lags (13).

Building on the recently developed SINDy extension for distributed memory (14), we show how effective the SINDy framework is for short-term forecasting of tick-borne disease transmission risk in a temporally changing environment. Tick-borne diseases present an ideal test case as they involve distributed memory effects across multiple biological timescales, from tick maturation and pathogen incubation periods to human immune response dynamics. Traditional compartmental models (12, 15) struggle to capture these continuous, distributed processes without explicitly specifying many intermediate biological stages and transition rates and without fitting to human incidence data. By contrast, our data-driven approach automatically discovers these distributed delay structures directly from human incidence data without requiring prior specification of tick life stages, vector-pathogen dynamics, or disease reporting mechanisms. Our method combines integral-based formulations (16–20) that improve noise robustness with structured candidate library construction that explicitly includes distributed delay variables and quadrature approximations for memory kernel integration. There is also growing interest in optimizing SINDy models over additional free parameters using multiobjective or Pareto-based strategies, which is closely related to our treatment of behavioral and memory parameters (21).

While our framework is readily transferable to other vector-borne systems characterized by nonlinear interactions and delayed transmission, we take Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome (SFTS) as an example to demonstrate the SINDy methodology, applying it to the most affected city Dalian of the Liaoning Province, China. SFTS is an emerging tick-borne infectious disease caused by the Bunyavirus/SFTS virus (SFTSV) and transmitted primarily by Haemaphysalis longicornis (22). With fatality rates ranging from 12 to 50% in East Asia (23), SFTS has been recognized by the World Health Organization as a priority emerging zoonosis since its first identification in China in 2009. The disease remains severe in central China, has been expanding to higher latitudes and has recently emerged in northeastern provinces such as Liaoning, where incidence shows a clear upward trend. Historically, nearly 90% of reported SFTS cases in Liaoning since 2011 were concentrated in Dalian and Dandong. Recent epidemiological trends reveal a significant shift: During the period from 2020 to 2022, Dalian experienced three years of COVID-19 control restrictions. During this period, restrictions were eased just before each summer, leading to a rapid rebound in summer tourism. This establishes Dalian as the current epicenter of SFTS within the province of Liaoning. Given this background of intensifying and localized transmission, we apply our data-driven method to SFTS case data from Dalian. Our method directly optimizes predictive accuracy and identifies distributed delay effects without requiring explicit specifications of intermediate biological processes and tick–human contact patterns.

The remainder of this work is organized as follows: Section 1 reviews the mathematical foundation of renewal equations (REs) and our adopted method for identifying distributed delay systems. This section also details the construction of a library based on quadrature and the formulation of sparse regression. Section 2 provides further background discussions of SFTS and describes the epidemiological data used in the case study in Section 2.1. Section 2.2 demonstrates the performance of our method on SFTS transmission data via numerical experiments, focusing on examining model identification accuracy, robustness, and parameter sensitivity. Section 1 provides details on the materials and methods. Finally, we conclude in Section 4 with a discussion of the wider implications of this study and outline future research directions for data-driven discoveries in infinite-dimensional systems arising from biological systems.

SINDy for Distributed Delay Renewal Equations

Many dynamical systems in practice depend not only on discrete delays but also on the cumulative history of the state. Rather than the current value of a system responding to its value at one or more specific time points in the past, the current behavior and behavioral change reflects a weighted average of how the system evolved. This distributed memory where the present evolves from contributions across the entire past history is fundamentally different from systems with discrete, isolated delays and leads naturally to REs and related integral formulations. REs and their generalizations through PDEs (for those interested in models of structured populations, we suggest (24, 25) for further reading) provide powerful frameworks for modeling such systems, particularly in structured population dynamics.

The core structure of an RE captures this distributed dependence through a functional relationship as[1]x(t)=G(xt)

which connects the current value directly to its history xt defined as xt := (s)x(t+s) for s∈[−τ,0], where τ>0 is the maximum delay. Here, G:X→Rn is a function operating on a Banach space X of history functions mapping [−τ,0] to Rn with appropriate regularity [typically measurable for REs (26)]. The function G accounts for how the system aggregates influence from its past. In many applications, G is defined by an integral [2]G(φ)∫−τ0g(s,φ(s))ds,φ∈X,

where the kernel function g:[−τ,0]×Rn→Rn is a typically nonlinear map that assigns weights to past states. The kernel g is the decisive element; it determines the character of the model by specifying which parts of the history matter (first argument of g) and how much they matter at each state value (via the second argument of g). As we clarify, this nonautonomous dependence of g on s is a key aspect for its reconstruction from observed data.

Identifying the kernel g, the first principle, from observed data is the central challenge in reconstructing REs from measurements. Following the SINDy framework (16–20), here we recall from ref. 14 a recently introduced approach based on quadrature approximations that allows us to adapt the SINDy methodology to identify the right-hand side Eq. 2 of an RE Eq. 1. In practical applications, current values are observed at discrete times, often accompanied by measurement noise. The available data consist of m snapshots organized in a data matrix X∈Rm×n as follows [3]X=(X1,…,Xn)x1(t1)⋯xn(t1)⋮⋱⋮x1(tm)⋯xn(tm).

The first step in using SINDy to find the kernel g is to use a suitable quadrature formula to approximate the integral G(xt) in Eq. 1. Thus the integral is transformed into a weighted sum of kernel values: [4]∫−τ0g(s,x(t+s))ds≈∑k=1Kwkg(sk,x(t+sk)).

Here, K denotes the number of quadrature nodes sk∈[−τ,0] with associated weights wk for k=1,…,K. The choice of quadrature rule [e.g., rectangle, trapezoidal, or Clenshaw-Curtis (27–29)] should be based on the smoothness properties of the kernel and the available data resolution.

The SINDy framework assumes that the kernel g can be expressed as a sparse linear combination of basis functions from a candidate library Θ (where sparsity means that only few basis functions will be active). Because g depends on both the delay variable s and the state value x(t+s), the library must be constructed to capture both of these arguments. Importantly, the explicit dependence of the kernel on the delay variable s (nonautonomous dependence) is a key feature that must be preserved during identification, as it determines how memory effects can vary across the delay interval.

For each quadrature node sk, k=1,…,K, we construct a polynomial library Θk of candidate functions that depend on the node sk and the time-shifted state values x(t+sk) as Θk := Θ(sk,X(·+sk))=1,sk,X1(·+sk),…,Xn(·+sk),sk2,skX1(·+sk),…,Xn2(·+sk),…skd,skd−1X1(·+sk),…,Xnd(·+sk),

where polynomial terms are included up to a specified degree d. Users can augment this library with additional nonlinear functions based on prior knowledge (e.g., either esk, sin(Xj(·+sk)), etc.). The notation Xj(·+sk) represents the j-th state variable evaluated at time-shifted instants ti+sk for i=1,…,m. Such shifted values can be recovered through piecewise linear interpolation when necessary. Even though the basis functions originate from a shared functional template, we retain the notation Θk to highlight that the library is assessed at delay values sk specific to each node, resulting in design matrices that are numerically distinct across quadrature nodes.

The complete SINDy library for identifying the RE Eq. 1 is formed by taking a weighted sum from these libraries Θk, k=1,…,K, with weights given by the quadrature weights wk. The corresponding identification problem reads[5]Xj≈∑k=1KwkΘ(sk,X(·+sk))ξj

for each state component j=1,…,n. In this context, in Eq. 5 the vector ξj∈Rp represents a sparse vector of coefficients that determines which library terms are actively involved in the kernel function for the j-th equation component. Each of these sparse vectors is derived by solving the regularized least-squares regression problem[6]ξj=arg minξ∈ℝp{‖Xj−(∑k=1KwkΘ(sk,X(·+sk)))ξ‖2+λ‖ξ‖0}.

for each j=1,…,n. This represents a standard convex optimization problem that can be effectively addressed using tools such as sequentially thresholded least squares (STLS, as we adopt in this work) (2) or least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) (30). By assembling all the resulting sparse vectors into a matrix Ξ := (ξ1,…,ξn)∈ℝp×n, we finally identify Eq. 1 with x(t)≈∫−τ0Θ(s,xT(t+s))Ξds, thereby uncovering the fundamental analytical structure of the kernel g in Eq. 2. This approach allows practitioners to derive interpretable, sparse functional forms that control distributed memory effects in REs and makes explicit the nonautonomous structure of the kernel.

Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome in Dalian

SFTS is an emerging disease transmitted from ticks to humans, first identified in central China in 2009. Since then, it has spread to South Korea, Japan, and other East Asian countries. The disease is caused by the SFTS virus (SFTSV), which belongs to the genus Bandavirus in the family Phenuiviridae. Infected patients experience high fever, gastrointestinal symptoms (vomiting and diarrhea), severe reductions in platelet count and white blood cell count, and elevated liver enzymes indicating liver damage. In severe cases, the disease can lead to failure of multiple organs, with a fatality rate of 12 to 50% (23). This high mortality rate makes SFTS a significant public health concern in endemic regions. SFTSV can infect various animal species including rodents, dogs, and goats, which typically show no symptoms. These asymptomatic animals serve as viral reservoirs that maintain circulation in natural ecosystems. Humans become infected primarily through tick bites and human-to-human transmission can occur through direct contact with infected blood or body fluids (15).

Data Collection.

This study uses environmental and epidemiological (deidentified) data related to SFTS in Dalian, Liaoning Province, China. Monthly confirmed human SFTS cases from 2011 to 2022 were obtained from the Liaoning Province Center for Disease Control and Prevention, providing a community-level view of transmission dynamics. Monthly mean temperature over the same period was extracted from the National Climatic Data Center (NCDC) Integrated Surface Database (data).

Fig. 1 compares seasonal patterns of temperature and SFTS incidence, showing the familiar association that how climatic factors, particularly temperature, influence tick activity, and human exposure (18). The local ecological setting in Dalian, characterized by a high abundance of H. longicornis ticks and typical host species (rodents for nymphs and goats for adults), makes this region well suited for studying tick-borne transmission dynamics (12).

All datasets were aligned by aggregating them to a common monthly time grid to ensure that epidemiological and environmental variables were temporally synchronized for model fitting (12). This curated dataset enables the construction of high-dimensional, temporally aligned feature matrices suitable for training and validating SINDy-based models.

SINDy-Based Distributed Delay Model Discovery.

Mechanistic compartmental model.

The reference paper (12) develops a mechanistic, stage-structured compartmental model formulated as a system of ODEs to understand SFTS transmission dynamics in Dalian. The ODE system comprises sixteen state variables representing tick populations at different life stages and infection classes, plus rodent, and goat host populations, yielding a coupled system of twenty differential equations for the complete tick–host–virus dynamics. This model explicitly incorporates the complete life cycle of the primary vector, H. longicornis, tick populations across eight distinct life stages: eggs, questing larvae, feeding larvae, questing nymphs, feeding nymphs, questing adults, feeding adults, and egg-laying adults. Each of these life stages is further subdivided into subcategories (susceptible or infected), recognizing that different developmental stages exhibit distinct infection dynamics, host-seeking behavior, and transmissibility. This stage-structured approach is biologically motivated by the fact that immature ticks (larvae and nymphs) predominantly feed on rodents, while adult ticks preferentially parasitize larger hosts such as goats, creating distinct ecological roles and transmission opportunities that a simplified, stage-structured model would necessarily overlook.

A key feature of the mechanistic model is its explicit treatment of three coexisting transmission pathways through which the SFTSV circulates within the tick–host system. The model incorporates systemic transmission that occurs when susceptible ticks acquire infection while feeding on infected animal hosts or when infected ticks infect susceptible hosts during feeding. Cofeeding transmission, also included in the model, is a nonsystemic route wherein susceptible and infected ticks simultaneously feed on the same host, enabling virus transmission through host-mediated mechanisms. The model formulation also incorporates transovarial transmission that represents vertical transmission from infected adult female ticks to their offspring through eggs, providing a mechanism for virus persistence even when host infections are low.

Temperature dependence is encoded by making most biological rates functions of ambient temperature: tick development rates, mortality rates at each life stage, and host-seeking activity all follow temperature-dependent functional forms fitted to experimental data (12). This is crucial because the tick life cycle shapes both the size and timing of infected tick populations available to transmit the virus to humans. The model also includes logistic birth dynamics for rodent and goat populations, natural mortality at each stage, and a one-month reporting delay to represent the lag between tick exposure and confirmed human SFTS diagnosis.

The crucial quantity connecting tick-host dynamics to observable human cases is the monthly case incidence, which is computed through an integral equation that aggregates the contributions of infected questing nymphs and infected questing adults over a one-month surveillance period, weighted by their respective temperature modulated attachment rates to humans. Specifically, the predicted number of newly reported human SFTS cases during month t is expressed as [7]C(t)=∫t−σt(ΛNpNNqi+ΛApAAqi)(u)du,

where σ denotes the surveillance interval, Nqi and Aqi represent the densities of infected questing nymphs and infected questing adults at time u, respectively, ΛN and ΛA are temperature-dependent host attachment rates for nymphs and adults and pN and pA are temperature-dependent proportion of active questing nymphs and adult ticks. These attachment rates are formulated as Λβ=αβeωT, where αβ represents temperature-dependent strength coefficients, ω encodes the strength of human outdoor activity and T denotes ambient temperature. This formulation represents the key biological insight that human case incidence depends not merely on the current value of the system but rather on the accumulated history of infected tick populations weighted by their behavioral activity, a concept intimately connected to the distributed delay framework employed in the subsequent SINDy-based analysis.

Baseline SINDy models: Incorporating incidence, temperature, and outdoor activity only.

This section demonstrates how the SINDy framework of Section 1 is used to identify the kernel of a distributed delay model for SFTS transmission in Dalian, overcoming the complexity of existing first-principles compartmental models as the one described in Section 2.2.1. Our aim is to infer the underlying transmission dynamics and to forecast future SFTS incidence from available multivariate observational data. We employ a data-driven approach using SINDy to identify sparse dynamical relationships directly from observational time series. The dataset includes only monthly human case counts and environmental temperature. Abundance estimates for tick nymphs and adults, derived from the mechanistic transmission model in ref. 12, are used later on only to test whether adding mechanistic covariates enhances the predictive performance of the SINDy model (and this is not the case as we show in Section 2.2.3).

A polynomial library of potential kernel functions is created, beginning with terms that include human cases (denoted as C), temperature data (denoted as T) and their combinations according to the baseline model [8]C(t)=∫−σ0f(s,C(t+s),T(t+s))ds.

Next, an exponential term representing the impact of outdoor activity, expressed as eωT, is added to the database modifying Eq. 8 into [9]C(t)=∫−σ0f(s,C(t+s),T(t+s),eωT(t+s))ds.

According to Section 1, in order to account for the distributed memory effect, both models are approximated using numerical quadrature, where the time-delay interval [−σ,0] is discretized to establish the regression matrix, and sparse regression is employed to identify the minimal set of functional terms needed to reflect the time-series behavior observed in prior cases.

The delay σ is set to one month, consistent with the human case reporting period used in ref. 12. We use past observations of monthly cases and temperature over the reporting window as inputs to the right-hand side, while current observations of monthly cases act as inputs to the left-hand side.

The monthly records of SFTS cases from Dalian spanning 2011 to 2022, along with daily temperature data, serve as input for model identification. The data are divided into training and validation subsets, with the first eight years (96 data points) dedicated to model training and parameter estimation, while the remaining years are set aside for validation and forecasting evaluation. In all experiments, polynomial SINDy libraries were developed alongside trapezoidal quadrature (K=100). Since the quadrature nodes do not align with the 96 monthly observation times, time-shifted state variables are determined through linear interpolation between the monthly data points. We explored two different values for the sparsity-promoting parameter: λ=10−5 and λ=10−10. Regardless of the value chosen, the algorithm successfully recovered an autonomous kernel, the resulting equations remained sparse, with only a few candidate terms in the polynomial library having nonzero coefficients (Tables 1 and 2).

Incorporating tick abundance mechanistic covariates.

Expanding on the results of the previous analysis, we extend the models by integrating mechanistically derived data of infected tick populations with human case records and temperature data. We introduce this extension solely to test whether incorporating the multivariate nature of the transmission system can improve the forecasting, acknowledging that the dynamics of SFTS are closely linked to the presence of infected vectors at various life stages enhances predictive accuracy. Data on infected nymph and adult tick populations are obtained from simulations of the mechanistic transmission model detailed in Section 2.2.1. As in the previous section, we use the same dataset temporal division. By incorporating these complementary data streams into the SINDy framework, we investigate whether this hybrid approach can capture the complex interdependencies between tick population dynamics, environmental factors, and human infection rates yielding any further predictive gain over the simple baseline model Eq. 8. The mechanistic reference model we compare against is the incidence Eq. 7, which expresses monthly human cases as an integral over infected questing nymph and adult tick densities weighted by temperature-dependent attachment rates according to ref. 12. This relationship can be expressed through the formulation [11]C(t)=∫−σ0f(s,C(t+s),T(t+s),eωT(t+s),INq(t+s),IAq(t+s))ds,

where INq and IAq represent infected questing nymph and adult tick populations, respectively. An additional variant that directly aligns with the mechanistic structure of Eq. 7, acting as a targeted test to see if SINDy can explicitly recover the underlying transmission parameters, is [12]C(t)=∫−σ0f(s,eωT(t+s),INq(t+s),IAq(t+s))ds.

Fig. 4 presents the comparison of the trajectories produced by the mechanistic model Eq. 7 and SINDy discovered models Eq. 8 (using data that include only human cases and temperature), and Eqs. 11 and 12 (using data that include also additional infected nymph and adult tick populations data) with sparsity-promoting parameter λ=10−10 and polynomial library of degree one. The model derived from Eq. 8 strikes an optimal balance between simplicity and predictive accuracy. It effectively captures the observed seasonal patterns and the significant rise in incidence during 2020–2022, all while maintaining a sparse structure and low validation error (Fig. 2 and Table 1). Incorporating mechanistically derived tick populations in model Eq. 11 does not yield any predictive improvement over this simple baseline model Eq. 8. Furthermore, model Eq. 12 performs significantly worse than all other models, primarily because it lacks the crucial dependence on human cases (C) and ambient temperature (T). RMSE values in Tables 1 and 2 although they are not extremely small, are quite reasonable given the noisy surveillance data and the limited number of training points, and the corresponding trajectories in Figs. 2–4 reproduce the observed seasonality and major peaks.

Parameter Sensitivity Analysis.

Sensitivity analysis plays a key role in both validating and interpreting data-driven epidemiological models. It shows how model predictions change when parameters are perturbed, which helps assess whether the identified parameters represent real characteristics of the transmission dynamics or numerical artifacts of the optimization procedure. It also improves interpretability by revealing which parameters most strongly influence predictions, thereby highlighting the primary processes in SFTS transmission in Dalian.

The SINDy distributed delay model Eq. 9 involves two parameters that require sensitivity analysis. The outdoor activity parameter ω (in the exponential term eωT) captures how temperature affects human exposure to infected ticks through changes in outdoor behavior. The integration window σ (defining the reporting period) specifies the time span over which past environmental conditions and vector populations contribute to current human infections.

From an epidemiological perspective, the model performance and model-based prediction are expected to be sensitive to both parameters. The parameter ω should lie within a realistic range. The parameter σ should be consistent with the characteristic timescales of the tick life cycle and infection process in the study area.

In this section we examine how the model behaves when the behavioral parameter ω and the reporting-window parameter σ are varied; these analyses are optional refinement steps and are not required to obtain the baseline predictive models presented in Section 2.2. If one wishes to optimize ω (and, when varied, σ), we use PS as described in SI Appendix, section A and Algorithm 2, minimizing the Eq. 10 between observed and predicted incidence. Materials and Methods

In this research, the monthly counts of confirmed SFTS cases from 2011 to 2022 were obtained from the Liaoning Province Center for Disease Control and Prevention, and monthly mean temperature data were sourced from the NCDC Integrated Surface Database. The first eight years of data (96 monthly observations) were used for model training and parameter estimation, with the remaining four years reserved for validation and forecasting evaluation. The SINDy framework was applied to distributed delay renewal equations using trapezoidal quadrature with 100 nodes, allowing the models to approximate the integral over a one-month reporting period. Polynomial candidate libraries of degrees one and two were constructed, and sparse regression was performed using STLS with sparsity-promoting parameters λ=10−5 and λ=10−10, and the outdoor activity parameter ω was optimized through PS using a bilevel method. Full algorithmic details of the SINDy identification procedure and parameter optimization are provided in SI Appendix, section A. For full mechanistic baseline model details, refer to the SI Appendix, section B.

Conclusions

Predicting the human burden of vector-borne diseases in a changing environment from limited time series data, such as human incidence and temperature records, requires the identification of hidden mechanisms underlying disease transmission in the vector–host zoonotic cycle and vector–human contact processes that generate the observed human incidence patterns. These mechanisms are typically highly nonlinear, arising from complex interactions among vectors, hosts, and humans. Moreover, they often incorporate memory effects due to delays in pathogen transmission, vector development, and human case reporting.

The Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) framework, pioneered in ref. 1, provides a powerful data-driven approach for uncovering such hidden mechanisms from time series data and has become an important tool in the rapidly expanding field of scientific discovery driven by data. Unlike previous extensions of the SINDy framework, our study explicitly targets epidemiological systems with delayed transmission. In particular, we demonstrate that an appropriate extension of the SINDy framework to systems with distributed memory effects can effectively uncover transmission dynamics of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), a tick-borne disease, using limited human incidence and local temperature time series from the study region (Dalian, Liaoning Province, China), without imposing predefined mechanistic assumptions on the human case reporting process.

The SINDy framework adapted for distributed memory effects assumes that memory spans a finite time interval and that the kernel function can be expressed as a sparse linear combination of basis functions from a candidate library. Both the integration interval and selected candidate functions may involve parameters that must be optimized through error-based estimation. In our case study, these include the exponent governing human activity effects and the temporal extent of the distributed memory kernel. We show that the proposed SINDy-based framework facilitates systematic sensitivity analysis, yielding comprehensive profiles that quantify the influence of key parameters across the expanded model space and identify their impact on predictive accuracy. We should emphasize that our framework is readily transferable to other vector-borne systems characterized by delayed transmission and reporting.

While this data-driven approach prioritizes predictive performance, it may trade mechanistic transparency for computational efficiency and interpretability. As a result, inferred models are sometimes best compared through their dynamical trajectories rather than through explicit mechanistic interpretation. Although we address the issue of uncertainty in our sensitivity analysis, future work should focus on incorporating uncertainty quantification for probabilistic forecasting, adding further environmental drivers, and developing multiregional models applicable to endemic settings.
