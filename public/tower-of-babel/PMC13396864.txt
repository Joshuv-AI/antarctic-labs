Rethinking GABAergic therapy in neonatal seizures: Beyond Scylla and Charybdis
Authors: Raffaele Falsaperla, Vincenzo Sortino, Georgia Ramantani
Published: 2026

To the Editors,

We read with interest the review by Thompson and colleagues on neonatal seizures and GABAergic antiseizure medications (ASM), framed through the metaphor of Scylla and Charybdis. 1 The authors highlight an important concern: in the immature brain, GABAA receptor activation may be depolarizing because of developmental differences in chloride homeostasis, 2 raising the possibility that GABAergic ASM could have paradoxical effects in some neonatal settings. This creates an important therapeutic dilemma for clinicians managing neonatal seizures: treatment with GABAergic ASM might worsen neuronal excitability, whereas withholding treatment might expose the developing brain to seizure‐related injury. 3 We agree that this issue deserves careful consideration, but presenting neonatal seizure treatment mainly as a choice between two opposing dangers oversimplifies the current understanding of developmental GABA signaling and its clinical implications.

The classical concept of a uniformly depolarizing action of GABA in the neonatal brain has evolved substantially over the past decades. Early experimental studies demonstrated that the high intracellular chloride concentration observed in immature neurons—largely driven by NKCC1 activity and limited KCC2 expression—can produce depolarizing GABA responses during early development. 4 However, subsequent work has shown that the functional consequences of these depolarizing responses are highly context‐dependent. In many settings, GABAergic transmission may still exert inhibitory effects at the network level through mechanisms such as shunting inhibition and increased membrane conductance. 5 , 6

Moreover, the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of chloride transporter expression across brain regions, neuronal subtypes, and developmental stages further complicates the interpretation of GABAergic pharmacology in neonates. Electrophysiological and imaging studies have demonstrated that the chloride reversal potential varies considerably between neuronal populations and across developmental time points, challenging the notion of a uniform depolarizing GABA state during the neonatal period. 7 , 8 This heterogeneity suggests that the response to GABAergic ASM may differ across brain regions and pathological contexts rather than representing a simple global paradox.

From a clinical perspective, the available evidence does not justify the conclusion that GABAergic ASM are broadly ineffective or harmful in neonates. Phenobarbital and related agents remain the most widely used first‐line therapies for neonatal seizures. Although their efficacy is limited, their use is supported by decades of clinical experience, safety data, and current international recommendations. 9 , 10 At the same time, experimental concerns raised in specific models should not be ignored, 1 but neither should they be generalized uncritically to all neonatal seizures and all clinical settings. More immediate and better‐established risks arise from uncontrolled seizures and status epilepticus in the neonatal brain, which are associated with excitotoxic injury, disturbed network maturation, and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes.

In this context, the metaphor of Scylla and Charybdis, while compelling, may inadvertently reinforce a dichotomous view of neonatal seizure management. In classical mythology, however, Odysseus survives not by accepting a simple binary choice, but by navigating the danger through judgment and adaptive strategy. 11 Similarly, neonatal seizure management may benefit less from a binary view of GABAergic therapy and more from etiologic stratification and mechanism‐based treatment, including metabolic evaluation, pyridoxine trials when indicated, and early targeted therapy in suspected genetic epilepsies. 12

From this perspective, the current challenge is not simply choosing between two unavoidable dangers, but improving our mechanistic understanding of neonatal seizures and translating this knowledge into more targeted therapeutic strategies. Continued integration of developmental neurobiology, experimental models, and clinical research will be essential to move beyond metaphor and toward a more precise framework for the treatment of neonatal seizures.

We commend Thompson and colleagues for drawing attention to this important and complex topic and for stimulating further discussion on how developmental GABA signaling should inform the management of neonatal seizures.
