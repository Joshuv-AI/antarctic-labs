Response of yield and quality of wheat with different gluten content to altitude above sea level and nitrogen treatment
Authors: Yujiao Wang, Xuhong Chang, Demei Wang, Yushuang Yang, Yanjie Wang, Xiwei Liu, Shubing Shi, Guangcai Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) yield and processing quality varied across environments, especially along altitudinal gradients. This study evaluated the combined effects of altitude above sea level and nitrogen (N) topdressing on yield and quality traits of winter wheat with strong, medium and weak gluten content. Six cultivars were tested for two growing seasons at Renqiu (4 m), Beijing (40 m) and Lhasa (3688 m). Raw-data verification showed that Lhasa reduced mean grain yield by 44.1% compared with Beijing, mainly through reductions in spike number, grains per spike and thousand-grain weight. High altitude increased total grain protein by 17.5% compared with Beijing, especially through increases in albumin and globulin fractions, but this increase did not translate into better processing quality. Strong and medium gluten wheat showed reduced gluten index, shorter dough stability time and higher weakening degree at Lhasa, whereas weak gluten wheat maintained relatively better stability under high-altitude stress. Nitrogen responses differed among gluten types: strong gluten wheat benefited more from 240 kg/ha N, medium gluten wheat performed similarly under 180–240 kg/ha N, and weak gluten wheat showed a more stable response under high-altitude conditions. Multivariate analyses separated the Lhasa environment from Renqiu and Beijing and indicated a clear yield-quality trade-off. These results suggested that cultivar selection and N management should be adjusted jointly according to altitude above sea level and gluten type.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the world’s major staple crops and provides dietary energy, protein, fibre and mineral nutrients1. Demand for wheat with stable processing quality has increased with the development of the flour and food-processing industries2. Under population growth, limited arable land and climate variability, improving both yield and quality through suitable cultivar deployment and field management has become an important issue for food security3–5. Altitude above sea level integrates multiple environmental gradients, including temperature, radiation, precipitation, atmospheric pressure and soil properties, and therefore affects wheat physiology, yield formation and grain quality6,7. Wheat cultivars with different gluten content may respond differently to such environmental changes because of differences in protein composition, starch properties and end-use requirements8,9. However, previous studies have usually focused on a single environmental factor or a single gluten type. A systematic evaluation of altitude, N management and gluten type is still needed.

Altitude above sea level is a useful indicator of environmental heterogeneity in mountainous and plateau regions. With increasing altitude, air pressure decreases, solar radiation usually increases, diurnal temperature range becomes wider, and effective accumulated temperature often decreases. These changes can affect wheat growth and yield formation directly or indirectly10,11. Wheat yield is determined mainly by spike number per unit area, grains per spike and thousand-grain weight, all of which are sensitive to temperature and radiation during vegetative and reproductive growth12,13. Low temperature around jointing can suppress tiller development and reduce effective spike number14, whereas adequate accumulated temperature and assimilate supply before and after flowering support grain filling and thousand-grain weight15. Consequently, the yield response to altitude is not simply linear, and it may differ among gluten types because their phenology and source-sink relationships differ.

Grain quality depends on protein concentration, protein composition, gluten strength and starch characteristics, which are jointly regulated by genotype and environment. Altitude above sea level can influence these traits through temperature, radiation and water conditions. Temperature affects assimilate transport, metabolic enzyme activity, cell division and N uptake, thereby influencing N accumulation and grain protein formation16,17. Radiation and sunshine duration regulate photosynthesis and the supply of carbohydrates and proteins to the developing grain18. Water status can also alter gluten content and dough strength19. Previous studies reported that grain protein and lysine contents varied across altitudinal ranges20, suggesting that wheat quality responses to altitude may be strongly genotype dependent.

Strong gluten wheat, medium gluten wheat and weak gluten wheat differ in breeding objectives, end use and cultivation requirements. Protein quality formation is especially sensitive to N supply and temperature during grain filling21. Low-temperature events can reduce protein and starch accumulation and alter grain quality formation22. Previous studies have also shown that altitude-related stress affects wheat yield, quality and metabolite profiles23. Therefore, different gluten types may have different adaptive ranges across altitudinal environments. The key knowledge gap is how altitude above sea level, N topdressing and gluten type interact to regulate yield and quality in a unified field experiment.

This study aimed to: (i) quantify yield and quality responses of six winter wheat cultivars representing strong gluten wheat (S-W), medium gluten wheat (M-W) and weak gluten wheat (W-W) under two N topdressing rates across three altitudes above sea level over two growing seasons; (ii) identify the main environmental and management factors associated with yield and quality variation; and (iii) clarify how altitude, gluten type and N management jointly affected the yield-quality trade-off. The results provide a basis for regional cultivar deployment and precision N management in wheat production across complex terrains.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Six winter wheat cultivars were used. Strong gluten wheat (S-W) included Zhongmai 578 (ZM-578) and Zhongmai 5051 (ZM-5051), medium gluten wheat (M-W) included Zhongmai 82 (ZM-82) and Zhongmai 89 (ZM-89), and weak gluten wheat (W-W) included Yangmai 15 (YM-15) and Yangmai 24 (YM-24). Seeds were obtained from the wheat breeding and experimental materials maintained by the Institute of Crop Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences and the collaborating regional experimental stations. Gluten-type classification followed the cultivar release information and the Chinese wheat quality classification criteria, which use grain protein content, wet gluten content and dough properties to distinguish strong, medium and weak gluten wheat.

The three experimental sites exhibited distinct climatic characteristics due to differences in geography and elevation above sea level (Table 1). Renqiu (4 m) features a warm temperate subhumid continental monsoon climate, Beijing (40 m) a warm temperate semihumid to semiarid monsoon climate, and Lhasa (3688 m) a plateau temperate semiarid monsoon climate. Across the two growing seasons, Lhasa consistently received much higher solar radiation (4888.5–5238.1 MJ/m²) and sunshine hours (2682.6–2765.2 h) than Renqiu (2937.5–3119.1 MJ/m²; 1563.9–1671.2 h) and Beijing (3031.0–3033.0 MJ/m²; 1728.4–1756.6 h). However, Lhasa also showed a larger average daily temperature range (13.4–14.0 °C) and lower daily minimum temperatures (down to − 11.1 °C), indicating stronger diurnal thermal fluctuations. In contrast, Renqiu and Beijing had higher daily maximum temperatures (up to 37.8 °C and 36.3 °C, respectively) and more days with maximum temperature ≥ 33 °C (up to 14 and 5 days). Annual precipitation varied substantially, with Lhasa receiving 210.9–285.1 mm, Renqiu 73.1–183.2 mm, and Beijing 123.8–168.3 mm. These climatic contrasts—particularly in radiation, temperature range, and thermal extremes—provided the environmental basis for evaluating altitude-related effects on wheat yield and quality.

Experimental design

The experiment compared yield and quality responses of three gluten types of winter wheat under two N topdressing rates at three sites over two years. A randomized complete block design was used at each site, and each treatment had three replicated plots. Urea was used as the N fertilizer. Two N treatments were applied: N240, with 120 kg/ha basal N and 120 kg/ha topdressing N at jointing, and N180, with 120 kg/ha basal N and 60 kg/ha topdressing N at jointing. Basal N was band-applied at 5 cm depth within 24 h after sowing, and topdressing was broadcast and incorporated at the jointing stage (Feekes stage 6, plant height about 30 ± 2 cm). broadcast and incorporated 150 kg P₂O₅/ha and potassium sulfate at 120 kg K₂O/ha before sowing. Each plot was 12 m², with 20 cm row spacing and 50 cm between plots. The target seedling density was 375 plants/m². Irrigation of 60 mm was applied at overwintering, after jointing fertilization and early grain filling, respectively. Field management was standardized across sites. Imidacloprid (75 g of active ingredient/ha) was applied for aphid control at stem elongation, and tebuconazole (125 g of active ingredient/ha) was applied for fungal disease prevention at anthesis.

Crop measurement

Grain yield and yield components

Wheat was harvested at physiological maturity when the grains had turned completely golden. The whole plot was used for management, and a 3.6 m² harvest area in the centre of each plot was used for yield determination to avoid border effects. After seedling emergence, a fixed 1 m row section was selected to count spike number. Ten plants were randomly selected from each plot to measure plant height, spike length, total spikelet number, fertile spikelet number and grains per spike. Thousand-grain weight was determined from 1000 randomly selected grains from each plot. Test weight was measured with a grain test weight meter (with readings recorded to the nearest 0.1 g/L). After harvest, grain samples were stored for three months, milled with a Quadrumat Junior Test Mill (Brabender GmbH & Co. KG, Duisburg, Germany), and then used for flour quality analyses.

Nitrogen and protein content determination

Mature grains from each plot were mixed and ground with a cyclone mill (FOSS CT293 Cyclone sample mill) to obtain whole wheat flour. The N content of 0.100 ± 0.001 g whole wheat flour was determined using the Kjeldahl method with an Automatic Kjeldahl Nitrogen Analyzer 9860 (Jinan Haineng Instrument Co., Ltd., Shandong, China) following standard procedures25. Whole grain protein content was calculated as N content × 5.7, as commonly used for wheat protein conversion26. Each biological replicate was measured independently, and technical duplicates were used when instrument measurements were required.

Grain protein fractionation and physical properties

Albumin, globulin, gliadin and glutenin were extracted successively using the classical Osborne fractionation procedure and its later modifications for wheat proteins26,27. Wheat grain hardness was measured using a JYDX100 × 40 Wheat Hardness Tester (Wuxi Xiliang Machinery Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China).

Flour gluten characteristics

Total gluten content and dry and wet gluten contents were determined with a Perten GM2200 gluten instrument (Perten Instruments Inc., Hägersten, Sweden) according to AACC Method 38-12.02. Sedimentation value was determined according to AACC Method 56–61.0226.

Dough rheological properties

Water absorption, dough development time, stability time, weakening degree and farinograph index were determined using a Farinograph-E with a USB port (Brabender GmbH & Co. KG, Duisburg, Germany) according to AACC Method 54-50.0126.

Phenological and meteorological analysis

The following phenological stages and indices were calculated from field observation dates. M was the whole growth period from seedling emergence to maturity. DVG was the number of days from emergence to regreening, DVR was the number of days from regreening to heading, and DRG was the number of days from heading to maturity. Regreening was defined as the stage when overwintered seedlings resumed visible growth and new green leaves appeared after winter dormancy. RVG was the ratio of vegetative growth duration to the whole growth period, RRG was the ratio of reproductive growth duration to the whole growth period, and RR/V was the ratio of reproductive growth duration to vegetative growth duration.

Meteorological data were obtained from the local meteorological stations. Sunshine duration (SM, SVG, SVR and SRG), accumulated temperature (TM, TVG, TVR and TRG), and precipitation (P) were calculated for the whole growth period, vegetative growth period, overlapping vegetative-reproductive period and reproductive growth period.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.3.0). Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (P < 0.05) was used to compare initial soil properties and meteorological factors among sites. For yield and quality traits, year, site/elevation above sea level, gluten type, cultivar and N treatment were included according to the experimental design. Biological replicates corresponded to field plots (n = 3 per treatment), and technical replicates were used for laboratory instrument measurements when applicable.

PLS-PM quantified the pathways linking environmental drivers to grain yield. Standardized path coefficients (β) were used to compare effect sizes among variables. Reproductive-stage accumulated temperature (TRG) had a direct negative path to thousand-grain weight (β = −0.68, P < 0.01) in the fitted model, while reproductive-stage sunshine duration and precipitation also contributed indirectly through yield components (Fig. 4).

For significant interaction terms, post-hoc contrasts were performed to compare the effects of site/elevation above sea level, year, gluten type and N treatment. Because only three altitude levels were included, relationships with altitude were interpreted as site-based descriptive trends rather than as a universal altitude-response model.

Figures and graphical summaries were prepared using Microsoft Excel 2019 and Origin 2022. Response surface analysis was used to visualize the joint response of gluten index to altitude above sea level and N rate; because only three altitudes were tested, the fitted surfaces were interpreted descriptively. Descriptive statistics, including maximum, minimum, range, mean, SD and coefficient of variation (CV), were calculated. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess associations among meteorological factors, yield components, protein fractions and quality parameters.

Results

Differences in planting sites and the wheat growth cycle

Meteorological conditions differed clearly among the three sites (Table 3). Lhasa had the highest sunshine duration and radiation but a lower and more variable thermal environment than the low-altitude sites. Beijing showed relatively favourable accumulated temperature and precipitation during the whole growth period, whereas Renqiu had relatively high reproductive-stage sunshine duration. These site differences provided the environmental basis for comparing altitude-related responses.

Across the two growing seasons, Beijing and Renqiu represented low-altitude environments (40 and 4 m), while Lhasa represented a high-altitude plateau environment (3688 m). The meteorological data indicated that altitude above sea level changed the balance among light, temperature and water conditions. In particular, the Lhasa site combined strong radiation with lower effective thermal resources during key growth stages.

Because gluten groups differed in phenology, the accumulated sunshine duration, temperature and precipitation experienced by S-W, M-W and W-W also differed. W-W generally had a longer growth period and accumulated more meteorological resources, whereas M-W completed reproductive growth more rapidly. These differences were considered when interpreting yield and quality responses.

Within each site, the three gluten types exhibited consistent phenological rankings. Weak gluten wheat (W-W) tended to have a relatively longer reproductive period than the other two types, especially at Lhasa. Medium gluten wheat (M-W) generally completed emergence and reproductive growth earlier than S-W and W-W, resulting in a shorter whole growth period. These consistent differences suggest that gluten types possess distinct developmental adaptations to high-altitude conditions. The prolonged reproductive phase at high altitude, particularly in W-W, may represent a compensatory strategy under low-temperature stress, but it did not translate into higher yield, as shown in later sections.

ANOVA indicated that planting site had a highly significant effect on the wheat growth cycle (P < 0.01; Table 4). The growth duration at Lhasa (3688 m) was longer than at Beijing (40 m), which was consistent with the lower thermal conditions at the plateau site (Tables 1 and 3).

Grain yield and yield components

Mixed linear model and ANOVA results showed that site/elevation above sea level was a dominant source of variation in grain yield and yield components (Tables 5 and 6; Fig. 4). The effect size of site/elevation above sea level was larger than that of N treatment and several interaction terms, indicating that environmental differences among sites strongly shaped yield formation.

Mean grain yield followed the order Beijing (794.9 g/m²) > Renqiu (728.2 g/m²) > Lhasa (444.4 g/m²). Because only three altitudes above sea level were tested, the dashed trend lines in Fig. 3A were interpreted as descriptive trends across sites rather than as formal evidence of a significant linear correlation with altitude. Yield reduction at Lhasa was more evident in S-W and M-W than in W-W, suggesting that high-altitude stress restricted high-yield and high-quality types more strongly.

Further analysis of yield components indicated that the yield penalty at Lhasa was caused by simultaneous reductions in spike number, grains per spike and thousand-grain weight. Based on the raw yield dataset, mean grain yield followed Beijing (794.9 g/m²) > Renqiu (728.2 g/m²) > Lhasa (444.4 g/m²). Mean spike number was 559.6, 524.7 and 431.9 spikes/m² in Beijing, Renqiu and Lhasa, respectively. Mean grains per spike were 82.4, 72.2 and 53.0, and mean thousand-grain weight was 34.9, 31.4 and 27.5 g, respectively. The year (Y) × site (S) interaction was significant for several traits, but the overall trend of lower yield components at Lhasa was consistent across years (Tables 5 and 6).

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) presented in Table 5 revealed that year (Y), cultivar or gluten type (C), site/elevation above sea level (S), and their interactions all exerted highly significant effects on grain yield, yield components, and agronomic traits of winter wheat. Among the three main factors, site (S) and year (Y) generally showed the largest F-values across most traits, indicating that environmental conditions, particularly altitude-associated changes in temperature, radiation, and water availability, were the dominant drivers of yield variation. For example, site effects on yield (F = 741.23), plant height (F = 2367.05), and spike number (F = 173.29**) were exceptionally strong.

Cultivar or gluten type (C) also significantly influenced all traits, though with generally smaller F-values than site or year, confirming that genetic background modulates how plants respond to environmental gradients. More importantly, several two-way and three-way interactions were significant. The S × C interaction was significant for all traits (e.g., yield: F = 35.06**), suggesting that the relative performance of strong, medium, and weak gluten wheats changed across sites. The Y × C interaction was significant for most traits except grains per spike, indicating that year-to-year climate variability affected gluten types differently.

The three-way interaction (S × Y × C) was significant for yield and most yield components (e.g., yield: F = 36.21**), highlighting that the combined effect of site, year, and gluten type was not simply additive. Collectively, these results demonstrate that spike formation, grain set, and final grain yield are jointly regulated by site-specific environmental conditions and genotype. Therefore, optimizing wheat production across altitudinal gradients requires simultaneous consideration of seasonal weather patterns, cultivar selection based on gluten type, and their complex interactions.

Analysis of variance presented in Table 6 revealed that cultivar or gluten type (C), site/elevation above sea level (S), and nitrogen topdressing rate (N) all significantly influenced wheat yield and yield components, though with marked differences in effect sizes. Site (S) exhibited the strongest effects across almost all traits, with F-values for yield (234.48), spike length (438.77), and total spikelet number (116.30) being particularly high. This confirms that environmental conditions associated with altitude were the dominant drivers of yield formation. Cultivar (C) also significantly affected all traits, with F-values ranging from 4.90 for yield to 59.53** for fertile spikelets, indicating that genetic background consistently modulated agronomic performance across environments.

Nitrogen treatment (N) significantly increased spike number, grains per spike, thousand-grain weight, and grain yield (F = 48.92**), demonstrating a positive effect of higher N supply on productivity. However, N did not significantly affect spike length, plant height, or spikelet numbers, suggesting that nitrogen primarily influenced sink capacity rather than vegetative or spike architecture.

Importantly, the S × C interaction was significant for all traits (e.g., yield: F = 11.09**), indicating that the relative performance of strong, medium, and weak gluten wheats varied across altitudinal environments. In contrast, the S × N interaction was not significant for any trait, and the three-way interaction (S × C × N) was also non-significant. These results indicate that the main effect of site was stronger than the differential nitrogen response among sites, and that nitrogen management could be optimized independently of gluten type across altitudes to a large extent.

Partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM) quantified the causal pathways linking environmental drivers to grain yield. Altitude exerted a direct negative effect on thousand-grain weight (β = −0.10), while simultaneously promoting spike number through a positive indirect pathway (β = +0.21). During the reproductive phase, accumulated growing degree days showed a significant negative influence on grains per spike (β = −0.10). Conversely, solar radiation played a crucial role in enhancing spike density with a strong positive effect (β=+0.25). Ultimately, all components contributed to final yield, with grains per spike and spike number showing the strongest positive associations (β=+0.51 and β=+0.41). The model confirms that altitude acts as a primary limiting factor, negatively impacting yield both directly and by reducing thousand-grain weight (Fig. 5).

The multivariate panels in Fig. 7 further showed that the Lhasa yield gap was linked mainly to lower spike number, fewer grains per spike and reduced thousand-grain weight. Because the study included only three altitude levels, the matrix in Fig. 7F was interpreted as a descriptive association among variables rather than as a stand-alone proof of altitude-yield correlation.

Altitude × Nitrogen interaction on gluten quality

Response surface analysis visualized the joint effect of altitude above sea level and N rate on gluten index in strong gluten wheat. At low altitudes (< 500 m above sea level), N240 tended to increase gluten index compared with N180. At high altitude (> 3000 m above sea level), gluten index remained lower even under N240, indicating that additional N could not fully offset the quality limitation imposed by the Lhasa environment.

Discussion

High-altitude effects on phenology and morphology: beyond simple delay

Our findings confirmed that high altitude delayed wheat development and reduced plant stature (Fig. 7; Table 4), consistent with the temperature-limitation hypothesis11. The differential responses among gluten types suggested that W-W had a more stable developmental strategy under plateau conditions. Low temperature and large diurnal temperature fluctuation can influence starch synthesis and grain filling22. Reduced spike number per m² at Lhasa indicated impaired tiller initiation or survival during the jointing stage, whereas reductions in spike length and fertile spikelet number indicated constraints on spike development.

Deciphering the yield-quality trade-off: Altitude × nitrogen × genotype

The yield penalty at Lhasa was driven by limitations in sink size (spikes/m² and grains per spike) and sink strength (thousand-grain weight). Low temperature can restrict photosynthetic capacity during grain filling and reduce assimilate transport and starch deposition15,28. S-W required higher N input to express its yield and gluten-quality potential, whereas M-W had a wider N optimum. The idea of a gluten-type-specific altitudinal adaptation range was supported by genotype × site interactions, but it should be considered a working hypothesis rather than a fully established concept because this study tested only three altitudes above sea level.

The high-altitude protein paradox: Composition matters more than quantity

The high-altitude-induced increase in total grain protein (+ 17.5% compared with Beijing, Table 7; Fig. 11) was consistent but potentially misleading. Fractionation showed that the increase was driven mainly by albumin and globulin. Although these fractions contribute to nutritional value, they contribute less to the viscoelastic gluten network required for bread-making than gliadin and glutenin29,30. In this manuscript, the term “high-altitude protein paradox” is used as our descriptive interpretation of this pattern: total protein increased at high altitude, but gluten functionality and dough processing quality decreased. The reduced glutenin/gliadin balance and poor dough rheology at Lhasa showed that protein composition and polymerization quality were more important than total protein content alone.

Climate drivers and management implications: Towards precision agriculture

The correlation and SEM analyses linked specific climatic variables to phenotypic responses (Figs. 14 and 15). The associations between reproductive-stage meteorological factors and yield components suggested that the high-altitude response was driven by the combined effects of thermal resources, radiation and water conditions. These analyses should be interpreted within the tested sites rather than extrapolated to all altitudinal zones.

These findings support the hypothesis of gluten-type-specific altitudinal adaptation. W-W showed better stability under high-altitude stress, whereas the quality advantages of S-W were expressed more fully at low-altitude sites with adequate N input. This genotype-environment-management framework highlights the need to optimize cultivar selection and N management together.

Cultivar selection should be prioritized according to altitude above sea level and production target. W-W was more suitable for high-altitude production when stability was emphasized, whereas S-W was more appropriate for low-altitude production aimed at stronger gluten quality.

Precision N management is required rather than blanket N recommendations. For high-altitude regions near 3000 m or above, W-W combined with 180 kg/ha N provided a comparatively stable yield-quality balance in this study, with mean yield of approximately 409.8 g/m² and grain protein content of approximately 11.7%. For low-altitude plains, S-W with 240 kg/ha N maintained stronger gluten quality. For M-W, the 180–240 kg/ha range provided more flexibility. High-altitude S-W cultivation is not recommended when the target is strong bread-making quality.

Future breeding should target genotypes with enhanced tolerance to low-temperature stress, improved N use efficiency and stable storage protein composition, especially high-molecular-weight glutenin subunits that help maintain dough strength under environmental stress. Recent work on diverse winter wheat evaluated with both traditional measurements and machine-learning-assisted phenotyping also supports the value of integrating field traits, image-based traits and computational analysis for climate-stress adaptation31. These targets may help reduce the risk of high protein but low processing quality under plateau conditions.

This study was limited to three altitudes above sea level (4 m, 40 m and 3688 m) and did not include transitional zones between 2000 and 3000 m. Future studies should include more intermediate altitudes, additional years and molecular analyses of gluten protein polymerization to test the proposed adaptation hypothesis more rigorously.
