Police Reporting After Sexual Assault: Victim and Assault Characteristics That Predict Reporting Among Victims Who Seek Medical Forensic Care
Authors: Rachael Goodman-Williams, Samantha Smith, Jessica Volz, Anthony Hanna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Many sexual assault victims choose not to report their assault to police, and identifying characteristics that predict non-reporting can highlight areas for improvement in the criminal legal system’s response to sexual assault. In the current study, we analyzed 655 medical forensic records collected at a hospital-based forensic nursing program to determine what victim and assault characteristics predict victims’ reporting decisions after sexual assault. Using logistic regression, we determined that increased victim age (OR = 1.02, p = .02), receiving the medical forensic exam within 24 hr of the assault (OR = 2.51, p < .001), anogenital injury (OR = 1.60, p = .008), and physical non-anogenital injury (OR = 1.47, p = .04) predicted increased odds of police reporting. While intermittent memory loss did not significantly relate to reporting, complete memory loss predicted a significantly lower likelihood of reporting the assault (OR = 0.45, p < .001). There was no significant difference in reporting between White victims and Black or African American victims, but victims who were Hispanic or Latine (OR = 1.76, p = .02) or whose race and ethnicity was reflected in a category inclusive of Asian or Pacific Islander, Arab or Middle Eastern, Bi- or Multiracial, and other identities (OR = 2.01, p = .02) were significantly more likely to report than White victims. Physical force, presence of a weapon, and the presence of multiple perpetrators did not relate to police reporting. Findings suggest that victims may make reporting decisions, in part, based on perceptions of evidentiary strength. Law enforcement agencies committed to investigating all reported assaults should explore ways to communicate to victims that evidence can be an outcome of a police investigation and is not a prerequisite to reporting sexual assault.

Introduction

Sexual assault is pervasive, with roughly 1 in 4 women (26.8%), 1 in 26 men (3.8%), and 1 in 3 transgender and gender-diverse individuals (33.6%) experiencing completed or attempted rape at some point in their lives (Basile et al., 2022; Langenderfer-Magruder et al., 2016). Yet despite the frequency with which it occurs, sexual assault is one of the most underreported crimes (Spohn, 2020). The 2018 National Crime Victimization Survey found that 75% of rapes are not reported to police (Morgan & Ouderkerk, 2019), with some studies finding non-reporting rates as high as 95% (see Lonsway & Archambault, 2012, for a review). These low rates of police reporting have been identified as a public safety issue (Wolitzky-Taylor et al., 2011), particularly as many individuals who perpetrate sexual assault do so multiple times (Campbell et al., 2020; Lovell et al., 2017).

Yet most reported sexual assaults are closed with only minimal investigation (Tasca et al., 2013; Venema, 2016) and there has been extensive documentation of victims’ re-traumatization within the criminal legal system (Campbell, 2024; Zweig et al., 2014). Qualitative research has explored victims’ reasons for not reporting, common among them the perceptions that reporting an assault to police will not help and may make things worse (Goodman-Williams, Volz, & Fishwick, 2024; Patterson et al., 2009). Quantitative studies, too, have analyzed victim, offender, and assault characteristics in samples of reporting and non-reporting cases to identify variables that predict reporting. The current study builds on this literature by exploring victim and assault characteristics that predict police reporting among victims who seek a post-assault medical forensic exam. Because most sexual assault victims do not seek any formal post-assault services (Rennison, 2022; Thompson, 2024), identifying predictors of reporting among victims who seek post-assault medical care and have forensic evidence collected but do not report their assault to police at that time has the potential to highlight unique concerns that victims may have about reporting to police specifically, rather than concerns about engaging with formal help-seeking systems more broadly.

Literature Review

Predictors of Police Reporting After Sexual Assault

For decades, community activists have criticized cultural reliance on rape stereotypes, also called rape myths, to distinguish serious cases of sexual assault from supposed false allegations or harmless misunderstandings (Burt, 1980; Lonsway & Fitzgerald, 1994; Shaw et al., 2017; Smith & Skinner, 2017). These rape myths rely on the idea that, with few exceptions, victims could prevent sexual assault if they truly wanted to and, therefore, most reported sexual assaults are regretted consensual sex. The few exceptions thought to indicate “real rape” are characterized by stranger or multiple assailants, physical force, and victim injury (Edwards et al., 2011; Lonsway & Fitzgerald, 1994). Similarly, in the aftermath of “real rape,” victims are expected to respond in a uniformly traumatized manner and immediately seek help (Carr et al., 2014; Hackett et al., 2008). These myths have been documented in police reports and tied to law enforcement’s response to sexual assault (Salerno-Ferraro & Jung, 2022; Venema, 2016).

It is therefore unsurprising that variables associated with rape myths have been found to predict whether victims report sexual assault to police. Repeated studies have found that being assaulted by a stranger is linked to significantly greater odds of reporting than being assaulted by a known perpetrator (Stokbaek et al., 2021; Wieberneit et al., 2024; Zinzow et al., 2022), so much so that in their analysis of National Women’s Study data, Wolitzky-Taylor et al. (2011) identified the association between victim-offender relationship and police reporting as a “pattern [that] has remained consistent since the 1980s” (p. 823). Qualitative research has explored this relationship further, with victims expressing concern that reporting known offenders could lead to physical, financial, or relational retaliation (Goodman-Williams, Volz, & Smith, 2024).

Rape stereotypes related to victims’ abilities to resist the assault have also been found to predict police reporting. Experiencing forcible rape, assault with a weapon, or an assault that involved multiple perpetrators are all associated with reporting to police (Schei et al., 2003; Varline, 2024; Zinzow et al., 2022). Physical and anogenital injury are predictive of reporting, as well (Downing et al., 2022; Varline, 2024; Wolitzky-Taylor et al., 2011, with one study finding that 52% of victims who sustained injuries chose to report their assault to police compared to only 31% of those who had not sustained injuries (Ceelen et al., 2019). Collectively, these findings suggest that victims are more likely to report the assault when there are clear markers of their resistance or readily identifiable explanations for why they were unable to resist the assault.

While victim incapacitation due to alcohol or drugs is a legally recognized indicator of non-consent (Department of Justice, 2014), research has consistently found that sexual assault victims are less likely to report if they consumed alcohol or drugs prior to the assault (Clay-Warner & Burt, 2005; Kilpatrick et al., 2007). In their analysis of victims who had forensic evidence collected with and without reporting to police, Downing et al. (2022) found that not having voluntarily consumed alcohol prior to the assault increased the odds of reporting by more than four times, in contrast to victims who had voluntarily consumed alcohol. Proposed explanations for this relationship have primarily focused on victim-blaming stigma, as victims who consume drugs or alcohol prior to an assault often are assigned more blame than non-intoxicated victims (Grubb & Turner, 2012). Studies examining police perspectives also have found that victim intoxication at the time of the assault is linked to officer skepticism and decreased case advancement (Wieberneit et al., 2024).

However, other studies have not found a relationship between substance use and police reporting. Reflecting on substance use’s lack of statistical significance in their model, Stokbaek et al. (2021) noted that pre-assault substance use was highly confounded with memory loss, a variable that did significantly predict reporting, and speculated that “alcohol intake reported in earlier studies could in fact be a marker for amnesia” (p. 401). In support of this hypothesis, Goodman-Williams, Volz, and Smith (2024) found that victims who reported drug or alcohol consumption prior to the assault were significantly more likely to state that lacking information about the assault was a reason they chose not to report the incident to police. Kane et al. (2024), too, found that uncertainty about whether an assault had occurred significantly reduced the likelihood of reporting. Relatively few studies have examined memory loss quantitatively; however, potentially because assault characteristics that victims cannot recall are often coded as missing and those cases are excluded from analysis.

Finally, despite the reality that many victims do not seek help immediately after an assault (Carr et al., 2014), the time between the assault and seeking medical forensic care has consistently been associated with police reporting. Research studies have operationalized delayed care seeking at 24 hr (Schei et al., 2003; Stokbaek et al., 2021), 32 hr (Downing et al., 2022), and 72 hr (Baert et al., 2023) and, in all cases, victims who wait longer to seek care are less likely to report the assault to police. These effects may be compounded or explained by related factors. In one study, victims who sustained physical injuries, whose assailants used a weapon, or who were assaulted by a stranger sought medical care sooner than did victims whose assaults did not reflect those stereotypes (Millar et al., 2002). These complex, potentially interdependent relationships highlight the need to further study how assault characteristics relate to each other and collectively predict police reporting.

Theoretical Frameworks: Rape Culture and Focal Concerns Perspective

As discussed above, much of the sexual assault reporting literature has focused on variables associated with rape culture. As a theoretical construct, rape culture describes a sociocultural context in which all but the most egregious acts of sexual violence are normalized and excused (Estrich, 1987; Jordan, 2022). The few assaults deemed worthy of attention typically reflect what Christie (1986) termed an “ideal victim” who was engaged in respectable behavior at the time of victimization and was verifiably harmed by a clearly dangerous offender. Importantly, Christie suggests that only these ideal victims are “given the complete and legitimate status of being a victim” (p. 12). These concepts provide a theoretical underpinning to this study, emphasizing that rape victims regularly are met with skepticism and, as a result, are forced to evaluate their help-seeking decisions through a lens of disbelief.

The study is also guided by focal concerns perspective (FCP), a framework used to highlight areas of focus that “command widespread and persistent attention and a high degree of emotional involvement” (Miller, 1958, p. 6). Steffensmeier et al. (1998) applied FCP to judicial decision making, proposing that judges’ sentencing decisions are influenced by three focal concerns: the offender’s blameworthiness and the degree of harm caused to the victim, community safety and the need for protection from the offender, and practical implications of the sentence imposed. Through this lens, factors such as crime seriousness, dangerousness of the offender, and likelihood of repeat offending are paramount. Though she did not reference FCP explicitly, Frohmann (1991) set the stage for application of FCP to decision making in sexual assault cases by highlighting the degree to which prosecutors’ charging decisions in sexual assault cases are shaped by convictability concerns having to do with “assumptions about relationships, gender, and sexuality” (p. 224). Spohn et al. (2001) further developed FCP for application to sexual assault prosecutions, arguing that prosecutors’ focal concerns in sexual assault cases are uniquely shaped by victim credibility concerns and, as a result, their evaluations of convictability are particularly vulnerable to stereotypes about “real rape” and “real victims.” This framework has been applied to police officers’ investigative decisions in sexual assault cases, as well, with findings again indicating that concerns about convictability encourage reliance on rape stereotypes throughout the criminal legal process (Spohn et al., 2014).

FCP has been applied to victims’ decision making by Kaiser et al. (2017), who used FCP to explore sexual assault victims’ cooperation with police, and by Kelley et al. (2022), who explored how focal concerns variables shape victims’ reporting decisions. Kelley developed three categories of variables aligned with FCP but specific to victim reporting: blameworthiness of the offender (e.g., offense severity, physical force), protection of the community (e.g., location of the assault, weapon use), and practical considerations (e.g., other help-seeking behaviors, victim injury). Their analysis identified significant predictors in each category, offering support for the application of FCP to victims’ reporting decisions. Congruent with other FCP theorists, Kelley also incorporated victim and offender characteristics, such as age and race, into their analysis in recognition of the “perceptual shorthand” criminal legal system actors often incorporate into judgments of guilt and innocence. The current study is broadly guided by theories of rape culture and ideal victimhood, with more concrete direction for included variables drawn from Kelley et al.’s (2022) application of FCP to victim reporting.

Current Study

The purpose of the current study was to identify victim and assault characteristics related to police reporting among victims who chose to have a sexual assault kit (SAK) collected as part of a post-assault medical forensic exam. Medical forensic exams are comprehensive examinations that include recording the patient’s history of the assault, the examiner’s assessment of trauma and injury, treatment of minor injuries and associated medical concerns, and collection of physical samples that may have evidentiary value in the SAK (Department of Justice, 2024). If victims choose to report the assault, they can release their SAK to police for forensic analysis and investigation. If they choose not to report their assault at that time, their SAK (often then termed a “non-report SAK”) is held anonymously for a designated period before being destroyed (Zweig et al., 2014).

There are multiple advantages to exploring victim reporting decisions using medical forensic data. First, medical forensic exam records are extensive, containing 15 to 20 pages of detailed questions and diagrams. Second, in addition to the structured questions, medical forensic exam forms include the victim’s subjective narrative of the assault. The narrative is either written by the victim or recorded as close to verbatim as is possible by the forensic examiner and it contains details that allow for the coding of nuanced variables. Third, medical forensic exams are typically obtained within 120 hr of the assault (Department of Justice, 2014), limiting the potential impact of memory decay. Finally, because most victims who have an SAK collected also choose to report the assault to police, there may be particular salience to variables that predict non-reporting among victims who have forensic evidence collected.

For this study, we coded victim and assault characteristics from 11 years of full report and non-report SAK medical forensic records. Although we had different variables available to us than did Kelley et al. (2022), the assault characteristics we coded fit the rape culture-informed focal concerns they laid out in their analysis. In our model, we included variables that reflected the blameworthiness of the perpetrator (physical force), protection of the community (multiple perpetrators, weapon use), and practical considerations for pursuing the case (bodily injury, anogenital injury, obtaining the medical forensic exam within 24 hr, and memory loss). These variables allowed us to answer the research question: Do variables related to blameworthiness of the perpetrator, protection of the community, and practical considerations for pursuing the case predict whether victims who have an SAK collected report their assault? We hypothesized that victims would be more likely to choose the full report SAK if their assault characteristics aligned with these focal concerns, specifically hypothesizing that reporting the assault to police would be more likely among victims who: (a) experienced excessive physical force during the assault, (b) were assaulted by multiple perpetrators, (c) were assaulted by a perpetrator who had a weapon, (d) sustained physical non-anogenital injury, (e) sustained anogenital injury, (f) obtained the medical forensic exam within 24 hr of the assault, and (g) retained complete memory of the assault. Congruent with the perceptual shorthand variables included in Kelley’s model, we also included victim age and victim race/ethnicity in the analysis. Based on stereotypes about the unreliability of young victims (Greeson et al., 2016) and congruence with the perceptual shorthand aspect of FCP, we hypothesized that older victims would be more likely to report the assault. Due to mixed findings in previous research (Downing et al., 2022; Wolitzky-Taylor et al., 2011), we included race/ethnicity as an exploratory variable without a formal hypotheses.

Method

Sample and Setting

Data for the analysis came from a large, hospital-based forensic nursing program in a suburban area of the mid-Atlantic United States. The program includes two nurse practitioners, seven registered nurses, and two certified nurse assistants who provide medical forensic care to sexual assault and other crime victims. The forensic nursing program is located in a state in which patients have three options when seeking medical services after sexual assault: (1) full report SAK, in which forensic evidence is collected and associated with a police report, (2) non-report SAK, in which forensic evidence is collected and stored anonymously with no association to a police report unless and until the victim “converts” their kit into a full report SAK, and (3) medical only exam, in which the patient is treated medically but no physical or biological samples are collected for evidentiary purposes.

Data were extracted from medical forensic records associated with full report and non-report SAKs collected from 2010 to 2021. Because there were fewer non-report than full report SAKs, we first searched electronic and paper records to identify all non-report SAKs associated with patients aged 18 or older and collected during the study period. This search resulted in the identification of n = 379 non-report SAK records. We then collected a systematic random sample of full report SAKs, selecting the same number of full report SAKs as we had non-report SAKs from each year. This produced an initial sample of n = 758 cases.

Hard copies of each patient record were printed for data coding and each record was labeled with an anonymized research identification number. While coding the data, we determined that n = 71 cases should not have been included in the sample due to reflecting a mandatory reporting scenario (n = 22), forensic evidence that had been collected for a crime other than sexual assault (n = 7), a file in which the patient had chosen the medical only exam option (n = 6), a follow-up visit rather than initial SAK collection (n = 2) or being a duplicate file of another included case (n = 34). We additionally excluded victims who were cisgender men (n = 28) or transgender (n = 4) due to their small numbers and potential differences in reporting behavior (Canan et al., 2024; Thomas & Kopel, 2023). These exclusions resulted in a final sample of n = 655, with 346 non-report SAK cases and 309 full report SAKs.

Data Coding and Analysis

Institutional Review Board approval was granted by both Wichita State University and the hospital that provided access to the medical forensic records. After formalizing a data use agreement between the university and hospital, the lead researcher was allowed to code directly from the medical forensic records, provided the files were not removed from the medical forensic unit and no identifying patient information was recorded. The outcome variable was police reporting at the time of the medical forensic exam, which was operationalized as having a non-report SAK collected (defined as not reporting) or a full report SAK collected (defined as reporting).

Victim age was recorded in the number of years at the time of the medical forensic exam. Victim race and ethnicity were originally coded in eight categories based on victim self-identification: (1) White Non-Hispanic or Latine, (2) African American or Black, (3) Hispanic or Latine, (4) Asian or Pacific Islander, (5) Native American or Alaskan Native, (6) Middle Eastern, (7) Multiracial or Biracial, or (8) Other. Categories that included less than 5% of the sample were collapsed for analysis, such that the final victim race/ethnicity categories were: (1) White Non-Hispanic or Latine, (2) African American or Black, (3) Hispanic or Latine, and (4) Other. Assault characteristics included anogenital injury (0 = no, 1 = yes), physical non-anogenital injury (0 = no, 1 = yes), presence of multiple perpetrators (0 = no, 1 = yes), presence of a weapon (0 = no, 1 = yes), whether the victim sought out the medical forensic exam within 24 hr of the assault (0 = no, 1 = yes), whether excessive physical force was used against the victim during the assault (defined as physical blows, strangulation, burns, or injury with a weapon; 0 = no, 1 = yes), and whether the victim reported memory loss (0 = none, 1 = fuzzy/intermittent, 2 = complete). For binary variables, the reference group was the absence of the characteristic (e.g., the reference group reflected cases in which there was no injury, no multiple perpetrators, no weapon use, no excessive force). For multi-level categorical variables, reference groups were no memory loss and White Non-Hispanic or Latine race/ethnicity.

For the three predictor variables that relied on a victim’s memory of the assault (multiple perpetrators, presence of a weapon, and physical force), the “no” category included both victims who explicitly reported that an assault characteristic did not occur and those who were unable to recall whether it occurred (e.g., if the victim had no memory of physical force being used, it was coded as 0). This decision was made for both conceptual and methodological reasons. Conceptually, this approach reflects the similarity between victims who are certain that a given characteristic did not occur and those who lack memory of it, as both groups can be understood as not having subjectively experienced that feature as part of their assault narrative. Methodologically, treating “does not recall” as part of the “no” category for these variables allowed cases with memory loss to remain in the analytic sample while avoiding the problematic levels of multicollinearity that would occur if “does not recall” responses were coded separately and thereby conflated with the broader memory loss variable. Assumptions of logistic regression were assessed prior to the analysis. Multicollinearity was not an identified issue, with no VIF values reaching 5 and no pairwise comparisons above 0.7. Ten outliers for victim age were identified using Mahalanobis Distances. All were confirmed to be valid values. Age was not linearly related to the log odds (B = 0.25, p = .57), and the regression was run twice, once with outliers included and once with them excluded. No results changed in significance or direction, therefore the decision was made to retain these 10 individuals in the analysis. Missing data were minimal (n = 25 cases, 3.8%) and listwise deletion of these cases resulted in an analytic sample of n = 630 patient records (n = 334 non-report SAKs, n = 296 full report SAKs). All coding was completed by the first author, with uncertainties resolved through consultation and consensus between the first and third authors.

Results

The omnibus chi-square test indicated that the logistic regression model was significantly better at predicting whether victims chose to report the assault than was the null model (χ2 = 82.16 [DF = 12], p < .001). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test did not show evidence of poor fit (p = .29), indicating that the model fits the data adequately, and the Nagelkerke R2 value of .16 demonstrated a moderate level of fit and predictive strength relative to a null model (Hosmer et al., 2013). ROC analysis produced a value of 0.703, which is at the low end of acceptable discrimination (Hosmer et al., 2013). Collectively, these fit indices indicate that the model improves prediction significantly over chance, albeit with substantial variability not explained by the included variables. As hypothesized, receiving the exam within 24 hr of the assault significantly increased the odds of reporting (OR = 2.49, p < .001), as did experiencing physical non-anogenital injury (OR = 1.46, p = .04) and anogenital injury (OR = 1.61, p = .01). Memory loss significantly predicted reporting (p = .003), though not at all levels. There was no significant difference in reporting between victims who had intermittent memory loss and those with no memory loss (OR = .80, p = .29), but victims with complete memory loss did show a significantly lower odds of police reporting than did victims with no memory loss (OR = .45, p < .001). Victim age also predicted police reporting, such that each additional year of age was associated with 2% increased odds of reporting the assault (OR = 1.02, p = .02). Our hypotheses related to physical force, weapon use, and presence of multiple perpetrators were not supported.

In regard to patient race and ethnicity, which was included as an exploratory variable without a defined hypothesis, we did identify a significant relationship with police reporting (p = .04). While there was not a significant difference between White Non-Hispanic or Latine victims and Black or African American victims (OR = 1.38, p = .14), we found that those who identified as Hispanic or Latine were more likely than White Non-Hispanic or Latine victims to report the assault (OR = 1.77, p = .02), as were victims whose race and ethnicity was reflected in the “other race/ethnicity” category that included people who described their race as Asian or Pacific Islander, Native American or Alaskan Native, Middle Eastern, Multi- or Biracial, or another racial or ethnic group (OR = 2.03, p = .02).

Discussion

Substantial resources have been devoted to improving law enforcement’s response to sexual assault in recent decades, with the simultaneous goals of improving victims’ experiences and encouraging more victims to report their assaults to police (Lovell & Langhinrichsen-Rohling, 2022). Rates of sexual assault reporting remain low, however, and may be trending downward over time (Morgan & Ouderkerk, 2019). Sexual assault victims’ reporting decisions have been a frequent topic of study, but because help-seeking decisions are made in tandem with one another it is difficult to disentangle variables that predict reporting an assault to police from variables that predict engaging with formal help-seeking systems more broadly. Using medical forensic exam records as a data source, the current study narrowed the focus to sexual assault victims who engage with formal help-seeking systems shortly after an assault and identified variables that predict whether these victims also report that assault to police.

In line with the FCP theoretical framework and Kelley et al.’s (2022) operationalization of focal concerns to sexual assault victims’ reporting decisions, we explored reporting through the lens of offender blameworthiness, community safety, and practical considerations for pursuing the case. Interestingly, while Kelley found that sexual assault victims considered all three focal concerns when making a reporting decision, we did not find variables related to blameworthiness of the offender or community safety to predict victim reporting. More specifically, our hypotheses that physical force, weapon use, and the involvement of multiple perpetrators would predict reporting the assault at the time of evidence collection were not supported. All four variables associated with practical considerations for pursuing the case, on the other hand, did predict police reporting. This differentiation along theoretical dimensions suggests that among victims who have decided to engage with formal help-seeking systems, it may be practical considerations about pursuing the case that most strongly shape their decision to report the assault to police.

The most impactful of these practical considerations was seeking a medical forensic exam within 24 hr of the assault, with the odds of reporting to police 2.5 times higher for victims who had an SAK collected within 24 hr. This relationship has been documented repeatedly in the research literature (Baert et al., 2023; Downing et al., 2022; Stokbaek et al., 2021), with proposed explanations for the relationship focusing on acknowledgment of the assault as a crime, perceived utility of the medical forensic exam among victims leaning toward or decided on reporting, and encouragement to seek an immediate exam among victims whose first contact is law enforcement (Downing et al., 2022). When seen through the FPC lens of practical considerations for pursuing the case, it is possible that victims are more optimistic about the exam’s likelihood of recovering forensic evidence when conducted within 24 hr of the assault. Forensic evidence is recoverable multiple days after an assault, however, and this information should be proactively communicated to victims (Speck & Ballantyne, 2015). It is also possible that victims who wait multiple days to seek care are concerned that this decision will lead police to question their credibility, and here it is crucial that police be educated about the many reasons victims may delay help-seeking and the lack of relationship between delayed help-seeking and credibility (Bicanic et al., 2015; DePrince et al., 2020).

It is also worth considering what complete memory loss might mean for victims seeking SAK collection. Lacking information about the assault has been documented as the most common reason given for not reporting among victims who had a non-report SAK collected (Goodman-Williams, Volz, & Smith, 2024) and the differentiation between partial and complete memory loss in this analysis clarifies the concern. Whereas intermittent memory loss may not be perceived by victims as overly detrimental to an investigation, complete memory loss may be perceived as an insurmountable barrier. Reducing the degree to which complete memory loss is an actual and perceived barrier to reporting is therefore an important task for communities that want to increase sexual assault reporting rates. Relevant actions could include expanding forensic testing options for non-report SAKs, such that individuals who are unsure if an assault occurred could have their SAK forensically tested before deciding whether to make a report. This option is available in a small but growing number of states (Goodman-Williams et al., 2025) and its expansion could be particularly meaningful to victims who lack memory of the assault. Additionally, individuals without memory of an assault often worry that they will be criticized or even prosecuted for making a false report if an investigation does not return supporting evidence (Avalos, 2017), and police agencies that would not charge reports of sexual assault made in good faith should communicate this policy to their communities.

Presence of anogenital and physical non-anogenital injuries also predicted reporting. This is an area of mixed findings in the research literature, with some studies finding a positive association between injury and reporting (Downing et al., 2022; Zinzow et al., 2022), others finding no association (Kane et al., 2024; Stokbaek et al., 2021), and others finding univariate but not multivariate significance (Schei et al., 2003; Wolitzky-Taylor et al., 2011). The context of our study is most similar to Downing et al.’s (2022), as both studies were based on medical records and specific to victims who had forensic evidence collected. The significance of victim injury in both studies, despite substantial differences in other variables included in the models, suggests that injury may be a particularly impactful factor for victims who seek post-assault medical care. It is possible that being told there is no observable injury is interpreted by victims as a refutation rather than more accurately as a non-finding. Forensic nurses can look for opportunities to educate their patients that sexual assault does not always result in visible injury (Kane et al., 2024; Larsen et al., 2015), and police should be prepared to investigate reported assaults in a manner capable of uncovering other types of evidence.

Two victim characteristics, included through the perceptual shorthand lens of FCP, were also found to be associated with reporting. First, each year increase in victim age predicted 2% increased odds of reporting the assault to police. This is consistent with previous research findings that young adults have particularly low rates of reporting (Bosick et al., 2012; Stokbaek et al., 2021). Qualitative research has explored the reporting concerns and help-seeking pathways of adolescent victims (Greeson et al., 2016; Lemaigre et al., 2017), and studies of unique concerns among young adult victims should be similarly pursued. Second, we found that while there was no statistical difference in reporting between Black or African American victims and White victims, both Latine or Hispanic victims and other racial or ethnic minority victims showed higher rates of reporting than White victims. Shaw and Lee (2019) found that a majority of studies about the criminal legal system’s response to sexual assault coded race into only two categories, most often differentiating White from Black or African American victims, or White from all non-White victims. Our findings highlight that reporting patterns vary across racial and ethnic groups with more nuance than a binary variable can capture and encourage the inclusion of race as a multi-level categorical variable whenever it is statistically feasible to do so. Additionally, although we did not formally code for identities related to immigration, we noted that the race and ethnicity categories with higher rates of reporting included substantial representation of immigrant communities. These communities may have different levels of knowledge about reporting options or comfort seeking formal care without reporting the assault. Sexual assault response systems should proactively communicate the full range of options, and the protections associated with accessing those options, to all communities.

There are multiple limitations to our study. First, we were limited to including variables that were documented in the medical forensic records, which left us unable to include other variables of interest, such as socioeconomic indicators. Second, we were limited to the variables we could include while including memory loss. We were unable to include victim-offender relationship, for example, because coding instances in which victims were unsure of who assaulted them as missing would have resulted in cases of memory loss being excluded from the analysis and coding them as a discrete category (i.e., identity unknown) would have created problematic levels of multicollinearity with the memory loss variable. This was similarly true of assault location. We decided these tradeoffs were worthwhile given the relative lack of exploration into memory loss, particularly at multiple levels, but we nevertheless recognize the limitation of not including these variables. Relatedly, we chose to treat assault characteristics that relied on victims’ memories as not endorsed when victims stated that they did not remember it occurring, rather than treating those instances as missing data. This decision allowed us to retain these assault characteristics alongside memory loss in the analysis, and we see it as conceptually congruent with victims’ subjective understandings of their assaults. Nevertheless, it is a methodological decision that should be kept in mind when interpreting our findings.

Finally, there are limitations to the generalizability of our findings. The medical forensic program we partnered with is an unusually expansive program. Whereas many forensic nursing programs rely on on-call staff and lack adequate coverage (Clowers, 2018), this unit has forensic nurses on-site for 20 hours each day, 7 days a week, with an administrator on-call during gaps in on-site coverage. These programmatic strengths have the potential to positively impact patient experiences, including increased time available to discuss reporting options. It is unknown how these impacts might affect these data, but it is nevertheless important to consider limitations to generalizability based on the expansiveness of the study site.

Not all victims will choose to report sexual assault to police, and a victim-centered framework does not suggest that all should do so. Rather, a victim-centered approach situates victims as the experts on their experiences and seeks to create a system that offers victims choices, with support for whatever they decide (Campbell, 2024). From that perspective, these results suggest that non-reporting options should be shared widely so that all victims know they can access medical forensic care with or without reporting to police. Simultaneously, stakeholders should consider how to maximize opportunities for justice within the criminal legal system for victims who worry they lack the evidence to support a criminal case, potentially due to delayed help-seeking, memory loss, or lack of visible injuries. While upstream barriers to case progression in these scenarios may remain, forensic examiners and police agencies can take meaningful steps to demonstrate an understanding of sexual assault in all circumstances, particularly those in which victims are hesitant to engage with the criminal legal system.
