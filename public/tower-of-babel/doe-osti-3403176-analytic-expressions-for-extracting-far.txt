 ABSTRACT

Magnetic-confinement DT pilot plants and DEMO-class devices will employ thin breeding blanket first
walls with substantially lower steady-state power-handling margins than ITER-like thick walls, making
reliable prediction of main-chamber particle and heat loads increasingly critical. This report addresses that
need by developing analytic tools to interpret far scrape-off layer (far-SOL) plasma–wall interaction
measurements in divertor tokamaks, with the goal of extracting effective cross-field transport coefficients
from “window-frame” experiments and related diagnostics.

This report establishes the physical and geometrical framework linking far-SOL transport to main-
chamber recycling (MCR) and its consequences (enhanced wall sputtering by ions and charge-exchange
neutrals, altered fueling and neutral penetration, and potentially significant main-wall power deposition).
The report formulates and clearly distinguishes poloidal magnetic flux expansion (FXpol), used primarily
to map radial decay lengths between poloidal locations, from total magnetic flux expansion (FXtot), used
to map parallel flux densities. This report derives compact expressions for FXpol and FXtot, shows when
each is applicable, and demonstrates how deposited flux densities depend on both magnetic expansion
and incidence angle. It also analyzes how protection limiters — toroidal or effectively toroidal sets of
discrete poloidal limiters — can bound the far-SOL and create well-defined measurement/control
volumes.

This report defines the window-frame method as a particle (and potentially power) accounting technique
that infers the radial outflux toward the main wall from measurements of the parallel flux deposited on
facing limiter surfaces that bound a flux-tube “window.” The method is motivated by the practical and
interpretive difficulty of directly measuring fluxes on surfaces aligned with B. The report lays out the
required assumptions (notably negligible ionization/recombination within the window-frame volume,
negligible back-surface losses, and limited net poloidal exchange across side surfaces), and it provides the
geometric relations connecting flux-tube cross-sectional area 𝐴∥,SOL to plasma-wetted area 𝐴wet.

This report presents closed-form expressions for extracting far-SOL cross-field transport coefficients
from measured radial profiles (typically from Langmuir probe arrays embedded in window-frame limiter
surfaces, supplemented by temperature measurements). For the case where profiles are well-approximated
by exponentials and coefficients are radially constant, the report derives formulas for (i) diffusive
transport 𝐷⊥ from a measured 𝜆𝑛, and (ii) convective transport 𝑣⊥ from 𝜆𝑛, explicitly incorporating
poloidal variation of flux-tube geometry via magnetic “shape integrals” and using FXpol/FXtot to simplify
and interpret the dependence on equilibrium geometry and connection length. Parallel connection length
expressions are likewise written in integral form, enabling consistent treatment of noncircular shaping and
poloidal flux expansion. The section then extends the method to electron and ion heat transport, deriving
relations to obtain 𝜒⊥𝑒, 𝜒⊥𝑖 (and/or energy convection speeds) from measured 𝜆𝑛, 𝜆𝑇𝑒 , 𝜆𝑇𝑖 together with
sheath transmission factors, while emphasizing the need for reliable 𝑇𝑖 estimates in the far-SOL.

Finally, an approach is presented for radially varying transport coefficients when measured 𝑛(𝑟) is not
exponential. It provides inversion expressions to reconstruct 𝑣⊥(𝑟) (and/or 𝐷⊥(𝑟)) from the measured
profile shape, highlighting that an additional boundary condition — knowledge of the escaping cross-field
flux at the outer radius of the window-frame region — is required. The report discusses practical
experimental strategies for constraining that boundary flux (including closely spaced limiter diagnostics)

and notes that when ionization and/or parallel gradients are non-negligible, interpretive modeling with
extended numerical tools (outlined in later appendices) is needed.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 Magnetic confinement DT fusion devices following ITER, including Pilot Plants and DEMOs, will
need to breed their own tritium. This poses a new challenge for the design of the blanket first wall not faced
by ITER since they must be very thin, of order mms, to achieve a Tritium Breeding Ratio, TBR, > 1 [1].
By contrast, the ITER blanket wall thickness is of order 10 cm [2] and is designed to be able to handle up
to ~ 5 MW/m2 of deposited plasma power flux density – an order of magnitude higher than a thin breeder
blanket wall is expected to be able to handle [1, 3].
 Better knowledge of wall fluxes of particles and power is therefore essential for the design of wall
armor for devices of this class. In the early 2000’s, plasma contact with the main vessel walls of tokamaks
– “main chamber recycling”, MCR – became an active research area [4-12]. The near-SOL region – the
first few mm outside the separatrix carrying most of the power to the divertors, see Fig. 1 – has quite
appropriately been a major focus of tokamak boundary research for many years and it is now an area that
is becoming relatively well in hand [13], albeit with some significant questions remaining. By contrast,
tokamak far-SOL research, i.e. on the plasma interaction with the main walls of the chamber, MCR, remains
much less explored, a situation that needs to be rectified with some urgency.
 The near-SOL and far-SOL of divertor tokamaks were first defined on C-mod based on radial profiles
of 𝑛𝑒 [4, 5, 6, 12] where it was found that with increasing 𝑛𝑒, a ‘density shoulder’ developed in the SOL,
increasing plasma contact with the main walls, i.e. the magnitude of MCR. An example is shown in Fig. 1.
 As noted above, tokamak far-SOL research, i.e. on the plasma interaction with the main walls of the
chamber, remains little explored, a situation that needs to be rectified with some urgency if current plans
for pilot plants and DEMOs are to be realized.

 2. PLASMA-WALL INTERACTIONS AND FAR-SOL GEOMETRY IN A TOKAMAK

 Recent studies of cross-field filamentary transport, in both natural and laboratory magnetized
plasmas, have motivated renewed interest in the far-SOL region of tokamak edge plasmas and the resulting
plasma contact with the main walls [15, 16, 17, 18]:
 A coherent description linking filament (blob) models, perpendicular/parallel transport balance,
and divertor detachment has recently been found that provides a consistent picture in which many
experimental findings are brought together. “These findings may turn out to be of great practical
importance for the design and operation of next generation tokamaks: since these devices will operate at
high densities and with detached divertors, transport in the SOL can be expected to be dominated by
filaments in the high density regime described in this work. Therefore, the extrapolation of SOL transport
based on experimental observations conducted in present tokamaks with attached divertors and low
collisionalities may be seriously underestimating the future particle and heat fluxes onto the main chamber
wall.” [15]
 “The remarkable similarity of AUG and JET results supports the prediction of a shoulder formation
in ITER.” [16]… which “….could be of great practical importance, since it could substantially increase
the particle flux arriving to the first wall, as well as contribute to the spread of particle and power fluxes
on the divertor target. Both effects would have relevant consequences on the sputtering yield of the PFCs
of both regions, thus changing the predicted lifetimes of several components.” [17]
 “Since convection can carry a substantial fraction of PSOL tens of mm away from the separatrix,
SOL transport associated to filaments may have a relevant impact on the distribution of heat fluxes onto
PFCs… this effect could be enhanced in future machines, in which the increase of divertor collisionality
leading to an enhanced particle flux might not be immediately correlated to a decrease in the SOL
temperature, as seems to be the case in present day machines.” [18]
 As the foregoing reports make clear, the implications and consequences of increased PWI, Plasma
Wall Interactions, are potentially of major significance:
 (a) Increased particle fluxes to the main wall will increase wall sputtering, both directly and
 indirectly; directly due to higher wall fluxes of ions; indirectly by increased sputtering by
 higher wall fluxes of charge exchange neutrals generated by the increased recycling at the
 walls, i.e. MCR.
 (b) The increased MCR will increase fueling of the confined plasma.
 (c) The increased MCR will increase fueling of the SOL plasma, potentially resulting in positive
 feedback that increases the MCR further.

 (d) The increased MCR will increase the neutral density in the pedestal, potentially degrading
 confinement.
 (e) The increased power load on the main wall, while unlikely to be a problem for (typically thick)
 non-breeding walls, may be a significant issue for thin breeding walls.

2.1 DEVELOPMENT AND CONSEQUENCES OF HYDROGENIC MAIN CHAMBER
 RECYCLING

 In DIII-D as the plasma density is raised toward divertor detachment, i.e. toward reactor-relevant
edge conditions, the total ion flux to the main chamber walls is found to become greater than the total ion
flux to the divertor targets and can approach levels comparable to the ion flux to the target during attachment

 5

[9], see Fig. 2. Similar behavior was first reported in C-mod and was termed Main Chamber Recycling,
MCR [5].
 The C-mod Authors identified 4 closed loops of recycling fuel particles, see Fig. 3(a) reproduced
from [7]. Loop 1 accounts for ions incident on the divertor target, with the recycling neutrals then being
ionized within the divertor and returning to the target. Loop 2 indicates neutrals that recycle from the
divertor target, enter the core and travel back to the divertor plate as ions; this is one part of Divertor
Leakage Recycling, DLR. Loop 3 indicates neutrals created outside the divertor at the main-wall surfaces
that circulate through the SOL and core and return to the main-wall surfaces; this is Main Chamber
Recycling, MCR. Loop 4 accounts for the fraction of divertor recycling that escapes the divertor as neutrals
through bypasses in the cryopump cavity and is ionized in the SOL and core, returning to the divertor as
ions; Loop 4 is thus another part of DLR. Divertor Recycling, DR, is the sum of Loops 1, 2 and 4. Most
tokamaks don’t have a significant flux in Loop 4, and the schematic can then be simplified to 3 closed
Loops, see Fig.3(b) which also indicates the part of each loop where the particles are neutrals and where
ions. It is important that this type of schematic should employ only closed loops. This greatly aids
understanding because closed loops satisfy two simplifying constraints (i) particle conservation, and (ii)
steady-state, therefore “ion flux to each solid/liquid surface = neutral flux from the same solid/liquid
surface”. Other types of loops do not satisfy these constraints and consequently may be confusing or even
misleading. Satisfying these constraints does not mean that the very same neutral that was released from a
specific surface -- main-wall or target -- returns to that specific surface as an incident ion. When individual
particles are tracked with a code, significant particle swapping occurs between loops; however, the total

2.2 TOTAL AND POLOIDAL FLUX EXPANSION IN TOKAMAKS
 It is important to distinguish between poloidal magnetic flux expansion, FXpol, and total magnetic
flux expansion, FXtot, since they relate to fundamentally different aspects of the SOL magnetic
configuration. FXpol is primarily used to relate the radial decay length, 𝜆 [m], of a plasma quantity such as
𝑛𝑒 [particles/m3], at a reference location, say the Outside Midplane, OMP, to its value at another poloidal
location along the SOL, say a divertor target, while FXtot, is primarily used to relate a parallel flux density,
[#/s/m2], in the SOL such as the parallel particle flux density 𝛤|| [particles/m2/s] or the power flux density
𝑞|| [W/m2], at a reference location to its value at another poloidal location along the SOL. Thus, FXpol and
FXtot, relate to fundamentally different aspects of the SOL magnetic configuration: the value of 𝜆𝑞||, for
example, may change between two poloidal locations, with no change in 𝑞||, and vice versa.
 Flux expansion has been a much-discussed topic in the literature [23]. Unfortunately, a distinction
between poloidal and total flux expansion is often not made clearly, or at all. Expressions for FXpol and
FXtot, abbreviations which appear not to have been used before, are often quoted without derivation. In
some cases, sophisticated expressions appropriate for numerical code analysis are given, usually with

 7

little/no derivation and not providing much insight for potential users. Consideration is rarely given to the
question of when FXpol and FXtot are useful concepts and when not. Distinctions are usually not made
between the exact expressions for FXpol and FXtot and approximate ones, nor are the different
approximations involved and their typical magnitudes discussed. Here, heuristic derivations that start from
first principles are given with the aim of addressing this situation. Alternative derivations in terms of flux
tube coordinates or numeric metric coefficients are presented in Appendices A and B, respectively.
 In this section, we first consider the concepts and quantifications of FXpol and FXtot, then apply them
to aid understanding and modelling of the SOL plasma. The first part of this section deals with the magnetic
fields only, without reference to any solid surfaces, and therefore the results apply to both divertor and
limiter tokamaks, and to both the confined plasma, i.e. inside the separatrix, as well as to the scrape-off
layer, i.e. outside the separatrix – as well as to both the near- and far-SOL.

2.3 ROLE OF PROTECTION LIMITERS IN POLOIDAL-DIVERTOR TOKAMAKS

 A distinction is made between “protection limiters” and “main limiters”. Main limiters are used in
limiter-configured tokamaks to remove most of the plasma power flux in the SOL. Protection limiters are
used in divertor-configured tokamaks to protect the walls and need only remove a small fraction of the
plasma power flux in the SOL, typically. It is usually necessary to protect the main walls of the vessel of
poloidal-divertor tokamaks from far-SOL fluxes of power and particles. This is especially important on the
LFS (Low-Field Side) because of the presence of antennae, edges of port openings, etc. The latter may have
low power-handling capability and/or may contain materials that could contaminate the plasma with
problematic impurities. Protection is achieved by installing limiters that stand proud of the main wall which
are designed to handle the expected deposited power loads and which are armored with materials having
tolerable sputtering and plasma contamination properties. The protection limiters act as particle and power
sinks for the far-SOL plasma, thereby intercepting a fraction of the loads that would otherwise reach the
main walls. In addition, when appropriately designed, a set of protection limiters also shortens the radial
decay lengths of 𝑛𝑒 and 𝑇𝑒 in their ‘shadow’, i.e. in the region radially outboard of their tips, which enables
the limiters to increase the fraction of the loads they capture for the same radial span of the limiters. This
feature may help reduce the size, and thus capital cost, of reactor-scale tokamak devices [22]. Protection of
the entire main vessel wall requires that such shadowing exist at all poloidal-toroidal locations, which is
most simply achieved using a toroidal limiter, or a set of toroidal limiters, or a set of poloidal limiters that
approximate a toroidal limiter, see Sec. 2.3.2.

 Thus far we have assumed a single toroidal limiter located at 𝜗 = 0. The ‘window-frame’ studies
 carried out to date in DIII-D [7, 8, 9, 10, 11] have involved, in effect, a set of toroidal ‘limiters’ which

 3. PLASMA OUTFLUXES IN THE FAR-SOL

 Since we want to know the outflux of particles and power to the main wall, it raises the question of
why we should settle for measuring these outfluxes merely toward the main wall. Why couldn’t Langmuir
probes be installed in the main walls with their collection surfaces facing radially inward to directly measure
radial fluxes depositing on the main wall? Firstly, there is no generally accepted interpretation for Langmuir
probes with collection surfaces parallel to B, see e.g. Sec. 25.2 of [23]. Secondly, practical limitations on
achieving extremely high alignment of probes in tokamaks, means that their collection surfaces are virtually
never actually parallel to B anyway; however, for glancing angles the magnitude of the deposited fluxes is
highly sensitive to the exact value of the angle between the surface and B making measurements uncertain
 We are therefore obliged to settle for measuring far-SOL radial outfluxes toward the main wall.
This is generally done using one of two different methods:
 (a) a fluctuation probe is typically mounted on a reciprocating drive at some particular poloidal-
 toroidal location, to sample the far-SOL radially out to near the main wall; radial decay lengths of
 plasma quantities are then derived by using a model of intermittent transport mediated by filaments,
          see e.g. [8, 10, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 30, 31];
 (b) particle and/or power balance, aka the “window-frame” method: limiters are placed at specific
 locations in the far-SOL to create a well-defined geometrical region where the total radial outflux
 entering the region can, with certain assumptions, be taken to equal the total parallel flux deposited
 on the sides of the limiters facing into the region, the latter being measured directly by probes
 mounted in the limiter faces or calculated using data from a nearby reciprocating probe.
The present report applies to the ”window-frame” method only, and is described in the next section.

 It is very important that the plasma is not ‘aware’ of whether a SOL flux tube is terminated by a solid
surface which is orthogonal to B, or is at some other angle, 𝜃⊥, because the Bohm-Chodura boundary

detailed shape and tilt of the surface of a toroidal limiter or the divertor target of a poloidal divertor, the
resulting SOL plasma is 2D toroidally symmetric, which greatly aids understanding and modelling of it.
By contrast, other limiter or divertor configurations generally create 3D SOLs that are not toroidally
symmetric and so have more complex plasma properties, although in special circumstances a set of discrete
limiters can act effectively as a toroidal limiter, see Sec. 2.3.2.

 APPENDIX C. THE NEED FOR MEASUREMENTS OF 𝑻𝒆 AND 𝑻𝒊 IN THE FAR-SOL

The principal diagnostic for window-frame studies are radial arrays of Langmuir probes built into the
window-frames, facing into the LSOL defined by the window-frame. The ion saturation current to the
probes, 𝐼+
         𝑠𝑎𝑡(𝑟) = 𝑒𝛤||(𝑟), measures the parallel (poloidal) outflux of ions from the LSOL which, in the

absence of local ionization/recombination, equals the flux that entered it radially through the window-pane
spanning the inner edge of the window-frame. It is also necessary to know 𝑛𝑒(𝑟) which can be obtained
from 𝐼+
 𝑠𝑎𝑡(𝑟) = 𝑐𝑠(𝑟)𝑛𝑒(𝑟), where 𝑐𝑠(𝑟) = 𝑒(𝑇𝑒(𝑟) + 𝑇𝑖(𝑟)/𝑚𝑖) is the plasma sound speed. The

Langmuir probes also provide measurements of 𝑇𝑒(𝑟), but they do not measure 𝑇𝑖. One of the most reliable
methods for measuring 𝑇𝑖 in a tokamak edge plasma is an RFA, Retarding Field (Energy) Analyzer, see
e.g. [26] and references therein; however, RFAs employ delicate grids and are rarely if ever built into
limiters, since they tend to require frequent maintenance. A more robust measuring method would use a
radial array of robust SETCs, Surface Eroding Thermocouples, see e.g. [27] and references therein, built
into the window-frames to measure the local deposited power flux density, 𝑞𝑑𝑒𝑝(𝑟). From the measured 𝑇𝑒
(𝑟) and 𝑞𝑑𝑒𝑝(𝑟), a value of 𝑇𝑖(𝑟) can be extracted using the known dependence of the sheath heat
transmission coefficient on 𝑇𝑒 and 𝑇𝑖, see e.g. [28].
