Awareness of active ageing and its association with quality of life among older adults in a tertiary care setting in Saudi Arabia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Halah F Alenizi, Eman Alanazi, Ali Alzahrani, Aiman Y Alwadi, Sadeq B Abu-Dawas, Fouad F Jabri, Meshal Alqahtani, Haneen Alotaibi, Peter MB Cahusac, Abdullah I Alsharif, Mohammad H Rajab, Abdullah Alkhenizan, Noara K Alhusseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation ageing is increasing globally, highlighting the need to promote not only longevity but also optimal quality of life (QoL) in later life. The World Health Organization’s Active Ageing framework emphasizes health, participation, and security as key determinants of well-being in older age. However, evidence on awareness of active ageing and its association with QoL remains limited in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among adults aged ≥ 60 years attending Family Medicine Clinics at a tertiary care hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Participants completed the World Health Organization Quality of Life–BREF (WHOQOL-BREF) and the Active Ageing Awareness Questionnaire (AAAQ). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and questionnaire scores. Bivariate and multivariable regression analyses were performed to examine associations between active ageing awareness and QoL domains while adjusting for relevant sociodemographic and health-related factors.ResultsA total of 351 older adults participated (mean age 68.2 ± 7.1 years). Most participants (79.8%) reported no prior awareness of the term “active ageing.” Nevertheless, higher active ageing awareness scores were significantly associated with higher QoL across all four WHOQOL-BREF domains (physical, psychological, social, and environmental; p < 0.001). In fully adjusted multivariable models, each one-point increase in the AAAQ awareness score was independently associated with higher odds of better QoL in the physical (adjusted OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03), psychological (aOR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03), social relationships (aOR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00-1.03), and environmental (aOR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03) domains. The strongest associations were observed in the psychological and environmental domains. Higher education level and better financial status were also independently associated with improved QoL outcomes.ConclusionsThe study findings showed a low level of awareness of the concept of active ageing among older adults in a tertiary care clinic in Saudi Arabia. Greater awareness of active ageing was positively associated with better quality of life among older adults, despite limited familiarity with the concept. These findings highlight the importance of integrating active ageing education into healthcare services and public health initiatives to support healthier and more fulfilling ageing trajectories.Keywords: Active ageing; quality of life; older adults; Saudi Arabia; tertiary care setting, cross-sectional

Introduction

Population ageing is accelerating worldwide and is increasingly shaping healthcare needs, social systems, and national economies [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines ageing as a process involving progressive molecular and cellular changes that reduce physiological reserves and increase vulnerability to disease and death [2]. Globally, the proportion of people aged ≥ 60 years is projected to rise markedly over coming decades, with older adults expected to outnumber young children in many settings [2, 3]. These demographic shifts pose substantial challenges for health systems including increasing demand for care services, straining social protection systems, and necessitating policy redesign to support ageing populations [1].

Saudi Arabia is experiencing a similar demographic transition, with national strategies explicitly targeting improvements in longevity and population well-being [4, 5]. As per the 2025 statistics report from the Saudi General Authority for Statistics (GSTAT), the older adults population has reached 1.7 million representing 4.8% of the total population [6]. Although this proportion remains lower than in many high-income countries and broadly comparable to other Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states, Saudi Arabia is part of a wider Arab regional demographic transition that is occurring at one of the fastest paces worldwide. The shift from an “ageing” to an “aged” society is projected to be compressed into roughly 13–40 years in Arab countries, compared with the 50–150 years observed historically in European settings [7]. In Saudi Arabia specifically, the old-age dependency ratio is expected to nearly double between 2020 and 2030 [8], underscoring the urgency of preparing health systems and communities for population ageing. Life expectancy at birth has increased steadily, and the Health Sector Transformation Program aligned with Saudi Vision 2030 aims to raise life expectancy to 80 years by 2030 [4]. While these gains reflect improvements in healthcare and living conditions, they also coincide with a growing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) among older adults. Recent modelling studies project substantial increases in NCD prevalence and healthcare expenditures in the Kingdom as the population ages, underscoring the importance of preventive and health-promoting strategies tailored to older populations [8].

In response to the global challenges of population ageing, the WHO introduced the Active Ageing policy framework, defining active ageing as “the process of optimizing opportunities for health, participation, and security in order to enhance quality of life as people age” [9]. This framework emphasizes that “active” extends beyond physical activity to include continued participation in social, economic, cultural, spiritual, and civic affairs [9]. Active ageing thus shifts the focus from disease-centered models of ageing to approaches that emphasize autonomy, engagement, and security throughout later life. Complementing this framework, the United Nations and WHO launched the Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030) to accelerate evidence-based actions that maintain functional ability, reduce ageism, and strengthen health and social care systems for older adults [1, 10]. Progress reports from the Decade highlight important advances, but also reveal persistent gaps in implementation, particularly in low- and middle-income settings [10].

Quality of life (QoL) is a central outcome in geriatric research and policy. The WHO conceptualizes QoL as an individual’s perception of their position in life within the context of their culture and value systems, incorporating physical health, psychological state, social relationships, level of independence, and environmental context [9]. International studies consistently demonstrate that active ageing-related factors, including social participation, perceived security, and health-promoting behaviors, are positively associated with QoL and well-being in older adults [11, 12]. In Saudi Arabia, studies assessing QoL among older adults indicate that QoL varies significantly according to sociodemographic and health-related factors, such as education, income, marital status, and chronic disease burden [13–15]. However, most local studies have focused on QoL outcomes without explicitly examining awareness of active ageing as a potential determinant [16].

Despite increasing policymakers’ attention to healthy and active ageing, evidence remains limited in Saudi clinical settings regarding how awareness of active ageing relates to QoL among older adults. Understanding awareness is particularly important, as knowledge of active ageing principles may influence individuals’ health behaviors, engagement with services, and perceptions of ageing itself. To address this gap, the present study aimed to assess awareness of active ageing and examine its association with QoL among adults aged ≥ 60 years attending a tertiary care setting in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, while accounting for key sociodemographic and health-related factors.

Methods

Study design and setting

A descriptive clinic-based cross-sectional study was conducted among adults aged ≥ 60 years attending the Family Medicine Clinics at King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Center (KFSHRC) in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. KFSHRC is a major tertiary care center that serves patients from different regions of the Kingdom, facilitating the recruitment of older adults with diverse sociodemographic characteristics. The clinic-based setting enabled access to older adults with varying health conditions who routinely engage with healthcare services.

Study population and sampling

Participants were eligible if they were aged ≥ 60 years, male or female, Saudi or non-Saudi, Arabic native speakers or English, literate or illiterate, and attending or accompanying patients at the Family Medicine Clinics during the study period. Individuals who declined participation were not enrolled. The estimated annual older adult population attending the Family Medicine Clinics was approximately 4,000. Using a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 351 participants. Consecutive sampling was used, whereby all eligible individuals encountered during the data collection period were invited to participate until the target sample size was reached. This non-probability sampling approach was selected to maximize feasibility and enrollment within the clinical setting.

Data collection procedures

Data collection took place between January 2024 and March 2025 following Institutional Review Board approval. Participants were approached in clinic waiting areas or contacted by telephone if they had previously attended the clinics. Contact details were used solely for recruitment and were not included in the study dataset. After providing verbal informed consent as the researchers obtained waiver for the signed informed consent form, participants completed the questionnaires either through self-administration or interviewer administration in English or Arabic, depending on literacy level and personal preference. Questionnaires were administered using paper-based forms, QR-code-linked online surveys, or structured telephone interviews to enhance accessibility and encourage participation among older adults. The use of multiple modes of administration aimed to reduce participation barriers and minimize nonresponse bias No identifiable personal information was collected in any questionnaire or retained for research purposes.

Study instruments

Quality of life: Quality of life was assessed using the World Health Organization Quality of Life–BREF (WHOQOL-BREF), a validated instrument widely used among older adults and in cross-cultural settings [17]. Participants completed the WHOQOL-BREF in either Arabic or English depending on their language preference. The Arabic version administered was the official WHO Arabic translation, developed by the WHOQOL Group through their standardized cross-cultural translation methodology (independent forward translation, back-translation, and expert panel review) [17]. The WHOQOL-BREF consists of 26 items, including two general items assessing overall QoL and general health, and 24 items covering four domains: physical health (7 items; example item: “Do you have enough energy for everyday life?“), psychological health (6 items; example item: “How much do you enjoy life?“), social relationships (3 items; example item: “How satisfied are you with your personal relationships?“), and environment (8 items; example item: “How safe do you feel in your daily life?“). Items were scored on a five-point Likert scale. Negatively worded items were reverse-coded according to WHO scoring guidelines. Raw domain scores were transformed to a 0-100 scale, with higher scores indicating better QoL [17].

Total scores ranged from 14 to 56 and were transformed into a 0-100 scale, with higher scores indicating greater awareness of active ageing principles [18]. Validity and reliability

The AAAQ was translated into Arabic through a forward translation performed by a bilingual member of the research team, followed by an expert review of the translated items by a geriatric medicine specialist, who assessed both linguistic accuracy and content validity. No items required substantive modification, and the original content of all items was preserved. A pilot test was then conducted on 22 participants aged ≥ 60 years (both male and female) prior to data collection, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.74, indicating acceptable internal consistency [19]. This level of reliability supports the use of the Arabic version of the AAAQ for assessing active ageing awareness among older adults in this population. The WHOQOL-BREF demonstrated good reliability and validity across diverse populations [17]. The Arabic version of the WHOQOL-BREF has also been previously validated in an Arab general population, with Cronbach’s alpha exceeding 0.70 across all four domains and confirmatory factor analysis supporting the four-domain structure [20], supporting its use in Arabic-speaking older adults in our setting.

Variables

The primary independent variable was awareness of active ageing (AAAQ score). The primary outcome was QoL, assessed using WHOQOL-BREF domain scores. Covariates included age, sex, marital status, region of residence, education level, occupational status, income status, and self-reported chronic health conditions, ascertained via a closed checklist of common chronic conditions among older adults in Saudi Arabia (hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, diabetes, heart disease, cancer, thyroid disease, bone disease, and pulmonary disease), each recorded as present or absent. These covariates were selected based on prior literature demonstrating their associations with quality of life and ageing-related outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using Stata statistical software. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ characteristics and questionnaire scores: categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms; given the non-normal distribution of several QoL and active ageing awareness scores, non-parametric tests were applied where appropriate (Wilcoxon rank-sum and Kruskal-Wallis tests for group comparisons, and Spearman’s rank correlation for associations between continuous variables). For the primary multivariable analysis, ordinal regression models were used to examine the association between active ageing awareness and each WHOQOL-BREF domain score, since this approach preserves the ordered structure of the domain scores. As a sensitivity analysis, binary logistic regression models with domain scores dichotomized at the median were fitted to confirm that the direction and significance of the associations did not depend on model specification; we acknowledge that this dichotomization reduces statistical efficiency relative to the ordinal models [21]. All multivariable models were adjusted for sociodemographic covariates (age, sex, region of residence, education level, occupational status, and financial status). Chronic health conditions were assessed in bivariate analyses against the AAAQ awareness score only and were not retained as covariates in the multivariable models. Categorical covariates were modelled using their original response categories without post hoc collapsing, in order to preserve subgroup transparency. The implications of small cell sizes for the precision of certain subgroup estimates are addressed in the discussion. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Center, Riyadh (RAC No. 223450). Participants were informed about the study objectives and procedures prior to enrollment. Data collection was conducted anonymously, and no personally identifiable information was recorded. Participation was voluntary, and respondents were free to decline or withdraw at any stage without consequence.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 351 older adults aged ≥ 60 years participated in the study. The mean age was 68.19 ± 7.08 years, and 56.13% were male. Most participants were married (76.92%) and resided in the Central region of Saudi Arabia (89.74%). The most commonly reported chronic conditions were diabetes (55.17%) and hypertension (54.89%). Regarding education, 25.64% held a bachelor’s degree and 23.36% completed high school, while 10.54% were illiterate. The majority of participants were retired (60.97%), and 74.07% reported having a sufficient fixed income. Awareness of active ageing

A striking finding of this study was that the large majority of participants, nearly four in five (79.77%), reported that they had never previously heard of the term “active ageing.” Despite this very low level of explicit familiarity, the median active ageing awareness score was nonetheless relatively high at 80.95 (interquartile range [IQR]: 64.29–92.86) on a 0-100 scale, indicating that participants endorsed active ageing principles even when the term itself was unfamiliar to them. Awareness scores demonstrated variability across sociodemographic and health-related characteristics. Significant differences in awareness scores were observed by education level, region of residence, financial status, and selected health conditions. Participants with higher educational attainment demonstrated the highest awareness scores, whereas those with primary education had the lowest (p = 0.020). Individuals reporting sufficient fixed income had significantly higher awareness scores compared with those reporting insufficient income (p = 0.019). Regional differences were observed, with participants residing in the Central region showing higher awareness scores than those in other regions (p = 0.030). With respect to health conditions, participants with hypercholesterolemia demonstrated significantly higher awareness scores than those without this condition (p = 0.002), while participants with bone diseases showed lower awareness scores (p = 0.033). No statistically significant differences in awareness scores were observed by age, sex, marital status, or occupational status. These findings are summarized in Table 2.

Association between active ageing awareness and quality of life

Active ageing awareness was positively correlated with all four QoL domains. Statistically significant associations were observed between awareness scores and the physical, psychological, social, and environmental QoL domains (p < 0.001 for all). The strongest correlations were observed for the psychological and environmental domains (Spearman’s rank correlation rs = 0.40), followed by the physical health and social relationships domains (rs = 0.30). These associations indicate a moderate positive relationship between greater awareness of active ageing principles and higher perceived quality of life across multiple domains. The correlations are presented in Table 5 and illustrated in Fig. 1, which displays scatter plots of AAAQ awareness scores against each WHOQOL-BREF domain score. A consistent positive linear trend is evident across all four panels, with the steepest slopes observed for the psychological and environmental domains, consistent with their higher Spearman correlation coefficients.

Multivariable analysis

In multivariable ordinal regression models adjusting for age, sex, education level, financial status, region of residence, and chronic health conditions, higher active ageing awareness remained independently associated with higher QoL scores across multiple domains. Education level and financial status also emerged as significant independent predictors of QoL, particularly within the psychological and environmental domains. The persistence of these associations after adjustment suggests that awareness of active ageing contributes to quality of life beyond the effects of sociodemographic characteristics and comorbidity burden (Tables 6 and 7).

Discussion

This study assessed awareness of active ageing and its association with quality of life among older adults attending a tertiary care setting in Saudi Arabia. The findings demonstrate a statistically significant positive association between active ageing awareness and all four QoL domains, even after adjusting for key sociodemographic and health-related factors. These results suggest that awareness of active ageing principles is meaningfully linked to how older adults perceive their physical, psychological, social, and environmental well-being. Despite the majority of participants reporting that they had never heard of the term “active ageing,” higher awareness scores were significantly associated with better QoL outcomes.

Active ageing awareness among older adults

A key finding of this study is the low level of familiarity with the term “active ageing,” with nearly four out of five participants reporting no prior awareness. This contrasts with findings from European settings, where awareness of active ageing concepts is substantially higher, likely reflecting long-standing public health campaigns, social policies, and community-based ageing initiatives [12, 22]. The limited explicit awareness observed in this study may indicate that the concept of active ageing has not yet been systematically incorporated into health education or public discourse targeting older adults in Saudi Arabia. Interestingly, despite low familiarity with the term, participants demonstrated relatively high awareness scores on the AAAQ. This apparent paradox suggests that many older adults may implicitly practice or value elements of active ageing, such as maintaining independence, social engagement, and health-seeking behaviors, even without formal recognition of the framework itself. Several factors may help explain this dissociation between explicit term-recognition and endorsement of active ageing principles. First, the AAAQ assesses the extent to which respondents agree with statements reflecting active ageing principles on a Likert scale and does not require recognition of the WHO terminology itself; tacit endorsement of these principles can therefore coexist with low awareness of the term. Second, several core elements of the active ageing framework (maintaining health, remaining socially engaged, contributing within family and community life, and being supported in later life) align closely with cultural and religious values that are deeply embedded in Saudi society, including Islamic teachings that emphasize the care, respect, and continued contribution of older adults [23]. Older adults in this setting may therefore have internalized practices consistent with active ageing through lived cultural experience rather than through formal health-policy discourse. Third, the absence of the Arabic term “active ageing” in public health communication and media in Saudi Arabia may explain low term-recognition, even when the underlying values are widely shared. A practical implication is that health-promotion efforts may be most effective when they explicitly name and reinforce existing beliefs and behaviors that are already culturally endorsed, rather than introducing active ageing as an unfamiliar or imported concept. Similar observations have been reported in other settings, where older adults endorse active ageing-related behaviors despite limited conceptual awareness [16]. Education level and financial status were significant correlates of awareness in this study, consistent with previous research showing that socioeconomic factors influence access to health information and engagement with health-promoting concepts [16, 22]. These findings highlight the need for targeted educational strategies that reach older adults with lower educational attainment and limited financial resources.

Association between active ageing awareness and quality of life

The positive association between active ageing awareness and QoL across all four domains aligns with international evidence indicating that knowledge, engagement, and perceived control over ageing-related processes are associated with better well-being in later life [24–26]. In this study, the strongest associations were observed in the psychological and environmental domains. Psychological well-being may be particularly sensitive to awareness of active ageing principles, as understanding that aspects of ageing are modifiable could foster a sense of autonomy, purpose, and self-efficacy among older adults. This interpretation is supported by prior studies linking active engagement and positive ageing perceptions with improved mental well-being [26]. The association observed in the environmental domain may reflect greater awareness of resources, safety, accessibility, and support systems that enable independent living and participation in society. Older adults who are more aware of active ageing principles may be better positioned to recognize and utilize environmental supports that enhance daily functioning and quality of life. These findings are consistent with research emphasizing the importance of security and supportive environments as core determinants of quality of life in older populations [25, 27].

Sociodemographic and health-related factors

Consistent with previous studies conducted in Saudi Arabia and the wider region, quality of life in this study varied by education level and financial status, and prior literature has shown similar variation by chronic disease burden [13–15]. Participants with higher education and sufficient income reported better QoL, particularly in the psychological and environmental domains. Chronic conditions were prevalent in our sample, although we did not formally examine within-study associations between individual chronic conditions or multimorbidity and the WHOQOL-BREF domains, which we acknowledge as a limitation. Importantly, the association between active ageing awareness and QoL persisted after adjustment for sociodemographic factors, suggesting that awareness may play a complementary role alongside structural and other determinants of well-being. No significant differences in active ageing awareness were observed by sex or age group. This finding differs from some international studies reporting gender-based disparities in active ageing indices [22], and may reflect contextual factors specific to Saudi society, such as strong family support structures and relatively equitable access to healthcare services for older adults. Cultural norms emphasizing family care and intergenerational support may mitigate disparities observed in other contexts.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings of this study have important implications for public health policy and geriatric care in Saudi Arabia. Enhancing awareness of active ageing principles represents a potentially low-cost and scalable strategy to improve quality of life among older adults. Rather than broad system-level statements, several specific and feasible delivery formats can be considered. First, brief active ageing counseling can be integrated into routine primary-care and family-medicine encounters, where older adults are seen regularly and where a trusted clinician-patient relationship already exists. Second, active ageing messages can be embedded within existing chronic disease management pathways, a particularly relevant channel given that more than half of our participants reported hypertension or diabetes, both of which generate recurring clinical touchpoints. Third, culturally tailored printed and digital educational materials (e.g., short booklets, infographics, and short videos in colloquial Arabic) can be made available in primary health care (PHC) waiting areas and through patient portals. Fourth, community-based health-promotion sessions can be delivered through trusted community settings such as mosques, social and senior centers, and family gatherings, which align with how health information is shared in Saudi society. Fifth, training programs for PHC physicians and nurses can incorporate brief active ageing counseling competencies into continuing medical education. These formats are consistent with the primary-care strengthening goals of the Saudi Health Sector Transformation Program and the Model of Care under Vision 2030 [4]. Integrating active ageing education into primary care services, family medicine clinics, and community health programs could support national goals aligned with Saudi Vision 2030 and the WHO Decade of Healthy Ageing [4, 9, 10]. Educational interventions should be culturally tailored and delivered in accessible formats to ensure inclusivity across educational and socioeconomic groups, particularly for older adults with limited formal education or financial resources.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including the use of validated instruments, an adequate sample size, and the simultaneous assessment of active ageing awareness and quality of life within a Saudi clinical context. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. With respect to study design, the cross-sectional nature precludes causal inference, and the single-center, clinic-based setting may limit generalizability to older adults who do not regularly access healthcare services. Reliance on self-reported data may also introduce recall or social desirability bias, and consecutive sampling may have favored individuals who are more engaged with healthcare. With respect to measurement, although the Arabic version of the AAAQ demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in our pilot, the translation procedure consisted of a single forward translation followed by expert review rather than a full Beaton-style cross-cultural adaptation with independent back-translation and committee review. The awareness scores should therefore be interpreted with this in mind. In terms of sample composition, certain sociodemographic categories had small cell sizes. Most notably participants reporting being single (n = 3) and those residing in non-Central regions which reflect genuine cultural and structural characteristics of the study population (the strongly family-oriented context of Saudi society and the predominantly Central catchment of the tertiary recruitment site, respectively) rather than sampling artefacts. Although these small cells did not affect the primary association between AAAQ awareness and quality of life, the corresponding subgroup estimates for marital status and non-Central regions in the multivariable model have wide confidence intervals and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, with regard to analytical scope, a range of self-reported chronic conditions was assessed only in bivariate analyses against the AAAQ awareness score; the multivariable models did not adjust for chronic conditions, and we did not operationalise a composite multimorbidity variable (e.g., a count of conditions or an “any vs. none” indicator). Future studies should therefore examine multimorbidity as a discrete construct in the relationship between active ageing awareness and quality of life. Despite these limitations, this study provides novel and policy-relevant evidence on active ageing awareness in Saudi Arabia, addressing a critical gap in the regional geriatric literature.

Conclusion

Although the great majority of older adults in this tertiary care setting in Saudi Arabia had never heard of the term “active ageing,” higher endorsement of active ageing principles, as measured by the AAAQ, was independently and positively associated with quality of life across all four WHOQOL-BREF domains, even after adjustment for sociodemographic and health-related factors. The central message of this study is that awareness of active ageing, understood as familiarity with its underlying principles rather than recognition of the term itself, matters for the quality of life of older adults. Together with the observed socioeconomic gradients in quality of life, these findings support culturally tailored, primary-care-anchored efforts to name and reinforce active ageing concepts among older adults in Saudi Arabia. Longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to establish whether enhancing such awareness causally improves quality of life.
