Abstract

Background.
RGB-trained capsule-endoscopy classifiers
underperform on small-vessel vascular findings by conflating hemoglobin
contrast with bile and illumination falloff. Thus, here we test whether
a Monte Carlo–inspired analytic model can compute hemoglobin from RGB
signal built upon extracted classifier.
Methods.
On
Kvasir-Capsule (47,238 frames, video-level 70/15/15 split, 11 evaluable

EfficientNet-B0 across 6 seeds: (i) a prior

P_{\mathrm{blood}}=\sigma(\alpha(H_{\mathrm{norm}}-0.5))\cdot\Phi(r)

fused as 2 zero-init auxiliary channels; (ii) a distillation head
training a 3-channel RGB backbone to predict
P_{\mathrm{blood}}
.

correction.

1. Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) cancers — colorectal, esophageal, gastric, and
small-bowel — collectively account for approximately 3.4 million
deaths annually worldwide (Tan et al. 2024). Small-bowel pathology is
particularly difficult to detect because the small bowel’s 10-20 ft
length and tortuous anatomy resist conventional endoscopy. Wireless
capsule endoscopy (WCE), introduced clinically in 2000 (Iddan et al.
2000), enables passive imaging of the small intestine at acquisition
rates of 2-6 frames per second over 8-12 hours. The clinical workflow
remains, however, fundamentally bottlenecked by manual review: a single
8-hour study yields ~50,000 frames, of which only a small
minority contain actionable findings. Inter-reader sensitivity for
small-bowel bleeding ranges from 56% to 89% depending on reader
expertise, and 6-7 hours of expert review per study is typical (Liao et
al. 2010).

Three data-side limitations of current commercial PillCam® systems
compound the burden: passive broadband white-light imaging that lacks
biologically specific contrast for early dysplasia (Kara and Bergman
2006); coarse spatial localization that relies on transit-time
heuristics; and a high false-positive rate when standard RGB classifiers
are confounded by bile staining, intraluminal debris, or specular
highlights. The first two of these limitations are best addressed by
hardware (autofluorescence excitation, spectral filters, ultra-wideband
ranging) (Aihara et al. 2012); the third can be substantially mitigated

in software
.

Existing AI for capsule endoscopy is dominated by single-task RGB
classifiers — polyp detection (Pogorelov et al. 2019), bleeding
detection from color and texture features (Pogorelov et al. 2019), or
video-level anomaly screening — that treat the captured frame as a
generic photograph. Recent prospective clinical trials show that
AI-assisted reading achieves non-inferior diagnostic yield for
small-bowel bleeding while reducing reading time from 33.7 to 3.8
minutes per study (Spada et al. 2024), and recent reviews (Piccirelli
et al. 2025) highlight remaining unmet needs in calibrated
bleeding/vascular detection. Transformer-based RT-DETR variants now
reach near-perfect F1 score — the harmonic mean of precision and
recall,

\text{F1}=2\cdot\frac{\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}}

— on bleeding and angiectasia classes on Kvasir-Capsule (Habe et al.
2025), and dedicated vascular-lesion CNNs have been validated
multi-system across PillCam and MiroCam capsules (Houdeville et al.
2021). All of this work treats the captured frame as a generic
photograph. We argue that a
physics-aware
representation, even
a simple one derived analytically from the same RGB data, can both
improve sensitivity to subtle pathology and reduce false positives
caused by illumination geometry. Specifically, capsule LEDs produce a
centrally peaked, radially decaying fluence pattern that is well
approximated by a first-order Monte Carlo light-transport model; and
hemoglobin’s preferential absorption of green and blue light gives
blood-rich regions a quantitatively distinct
R/(G+B)
signature that
the network can exploit if it is given as an explicit input channel. The
pairing of analytic optical priors with deep learning has precedent in
adjacent medical-imaging domains — for instance, label-free
hyperspectral imaging combined with deep learning for retinal amyloid

\beta
and tau prediction (Du et al. 2022) and snapshot spectral
fundus imaging (Zhao et al. 2023) — and we extend that line of
reasoning to single-channel-RGB capsule endoscopy.

This paper makes three contributions:

1.

A direction-consistent macro-AUC gain from an analytic
hemoglobin prior, robust on the input-fusion variant.
Across 6
independent seeds on Kvasir-Capsule’s video-level test split, the
input-fusion variant improves macro-AUC — the unweighted mean of
one-vs-rest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve
(AUC) across the 11 evaluable classes — by paired

\Delta=+0.023
(sign-positive on 5 of 6 seeds), from RGB-only at

0.760\pm 0.027
to
0.783\pm 0.024
. The distillation variant
matches input-fusion’s expected macro-AUC (
0.773\pm 0.028
, paired

\Delta=+0.013
) but is direction-mixed across seeds (3/6
sign-positive). The prior is computable at zero training cost from any
standard RGB capsule frame, requires no additional capsule hardware,
and slots into any CNN or transformer backbone via a 5-channel input
expansion with zero-initialized weights for the physics channels.

2.

A deployment-friendly distillation pathway.
Distillation
trains a 3-channel-RGB backbone to
predict
the analytic

P_{\mathrm{blood}}
map from spatial features (CE +

\lambda
\cdot
BCE), discardable at deployment via

model.deploy_mode = True
. The deployed model is a plain
RGB classifier with hemoglobin-aware internalized representations —
addressing the integration cost of input-channel modifications in
commercial PillCam® pipelines and producing a free per-frame
interpretability heatmap.
The robust per-class lift across all
6 seeds is on Lymphangiectasia:
AUC improves

0.238\pm 0.057\to 0.337\pm 0.019\to 0.334\pm 0.083
across the
three arms (sign-consistent on all 6 seeds for the input-fusion
variant, with the lowest cross-seed
\sigma
in the table —

0.019
). The single-seed=42 result of Angiectasia AUC

0.528\to 0.916
is preserved as a high-variance per-seed exemplar
(cross-seed mean
0.646\to 0.608
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{PI}}=0.23
); we
use it to illustrate the prior’s potential on hemoglobin-dominant
lesions but do not present it as a typical performance.

3.

Two diagnostic findings about analytic-prior design.

(i)
The prior’s per-frame percentile clip on the hemoglobin
index is essential — without it, vignette-pixel outliers collapse
the prior’s dynamic range and the model learns to ignore the auxiliary
channels. We further validate that the per-image relative-rank
normalization is a load-bearing design choice by ablating against a
scale-fixed (NDVI-style) alternative; on a paired 5-epoch retraining
the alternative regresses substantially on Angiectasia and degrades
net macro-AUC.
(ii)
Per-class seed variance on Kvasir-Capsule’s
rare focal-vascular classes (notably Angiectasia and Blood-fresh) is
large under any of the three configurations — a dataset-side
property that interacts with the prior and that any single-seed claim
about these classes should respect. These findings (released alongside
the code) inform the design of any future ratio-based analytic prior
in medical imaging.

We evaluate both on the public Kvasir-Capsule dataset under a
class-stratified video-level split that ensures every evaluable class
appears in test, and we release all code and trained weights at

https://github.com/integritynoble/Physics-Informed-PillCam
. Two
extensions of this framework — a CNN+GRU temporal-consistency gate for
false-positive suppression on full-length videos, and zero-shot transfer
to a second public capsule-endoscopy dataset (KID Atlas) — are
deferred to a follow-up paper because they fall outside the
within-Kvasir-Capsule, software-only scope developed here; they are not
load-bearing for the contributions claimed in this work.

2. Related work

Capsule-endoscopy CAD.
Smedsrud et al. released Kvasir-Capsule
in 2021 (Smedsrud et al. 2021), establishing a public benchmark of 117
videos and 47,238 labeled frames across 14 classes. Their baseline
ResNet-152 reached macro-F1
\approx
0.41 in two-fold
cross-validation. Pogorelov et al. demonstrated bleeding detection from
hand-crafted color and texture features with sensitivity 0.93 /
specificity 0.78 (Pogorelov et al. 2019). Ozyoruk et al. introduced
EndoSLAM for monocular depth and visual odometry on capsule videos,
addressing the localization gap (Ozyoruk et al. 2021). More recently,
Houdeville et al. presented a multisystem-compatible CNN for angiectasia
detection across PillCam and MiroCam systems with sensitivities of
96–97 % (Houdeville et al. 2021), Spada et al. ran a 14-centre
prospective trial of AI-assisted CE reading with non-inferior diagnostic
yield and ~9× faster reading times (Spada et al. 2024),
Habe et al. applied a real-time DETR transformer to Kvasir-Capsule with
strong per-class F1 (Habe et al. 2025), and Piccirelli et al. surveyed
the unmet needs of CE AI (Piccirelli et al. 2025). None of these works
integrate an explicit physics-informed input prior or a distillation
pathway for deployment-friendly inference.

Polyp detection and instance segmentation.
Mask R-CNN and
YOLO-style detectors with strong backbones are the dominant family for
polyp instance segmentation in colonoscopy and capsule data; these
approaches are orthogonal to our frame-level classification work and
could be combined downstream.

Physics-informed priors in medical imaging.
Physics-informed
neural networks have grown steadily in tomographic reconstruction,
optical coherence tomography, and ultrasound, but their use in

standard-RGB
medical-imaging classification is rare. The closest
precedent is the pairing of physically calibrated optical sensing with
deep learning in retinal imaging — for instance, label-free
hyperspectral imaging combined with deep learning predicts retinal
amyloid
\beta
-protein and phosphorylated tau in vivo (Du et al.
2022), and snapshot-spectral fundus imaging recovers full-spectrum
retinal information from a single RGB-sensor exposure (Zhao et al.
2023). Compressive/snapshot computational imaging more broadly has
produced spectrally and temporally aware reconstructions usable
downstream by neural networks (Yang et al. 2020) (Qi et al. 2020).
On the algorithmic side, deep-unfolding architectures driven by ensemble
physics priors (Yang et al. 2022) and adaptive Plug-and-Play algorithms
that combine analytic operators with learned denoisers (Wu et al. 2023)
demonstrate that closed-form physics knowledge and data-driven networks
can be productively co-designed. Our contribution is to bring the

analytic prior
idea — a closed-form, training-free, per-frame
computation derived from the radiative-transport physics of the imaging
system — to capsule endoscopy classification, with a prior that costs
no additional hardware and slots into any existing CNN or transformer
backbone via a 5-channel input expansion with zero-initialized weights
for the physics channels.

Comparison to published Kvasir-Capsule baselines.
Smedsrud et
al. (2021) report a ResNet-152 baseline reaching macro-F1
\approx

0.41 on a 2-fold cross-validation split (Smedsrud et al. 2021);
Pogorelov et al. (2019) report sensitivity 0.93 / specificity 0.78 for
hand-crafted color and texture features on a bleeding-only binary task.
Our test split is video-level 70/15/15 (a stricter generalization
protocol than 2-fold CV that holds out entire videos) on the 11
evaluable classes; on this protocol our RGB-only EfficientNet-B0
baseline reaches per-class AUCs ranging from 0.18 (Lymphangiectasia) to
0.99 (Foreign Body, Erythema), with macro-AUC = 0.751. Direct
head-to-head numerical comparison is approximate because of (a) backbone
differences (ResNet-152 vs EfficientNet-B0), (b) split-protocol
differences (2-fold CV vs video-level 70/15/15), and (c) metric
differences (macro-F1 vs per-class AUC); however, the per-class AUCs of
our RGB-only baseline are within the broad envelope reported in the
dataset paper, supporting the comparability of the baseline.

Novelty matrix.
Relative to prior work on Kvasir-Capsule, our
paper is the first (to our knowledge) to combine: (i) an analytic Monte
Carlo–inspired physics prior fused as auxiliary input channels with
zero-init invariance at the headline ablation, and (ii) an
auxiliary-decoder distillation head that lets the deployed model run on
plain 3-channel RGB while internalizing the prior. The combination is
paired-ablated under a strict video-level test protocol with
DeLong/McNemar/bootstrap statistics and is released as fully
reproducible code and weights.

3. Methods

Figure 1 — End-to-end pipeline.

The capsule frame is passed in three input variants (RGB-only, +MC prior 5-channel, distillation 3-channel + auxiliary head) into an ImageNet-pretrained backbone with a class-weighted cross-entropy head. The auxiliary distillation head is dropped at inference.

3.1 Data

We use
Kvasir-Capsule
(Smedsrud et al. 2021) as the primary
dataset: 47,248 labeled frames distributed across 43 source videos at

336\times 336
resolution and 14 anatomical/pathological classes
(Ampulla of Vater, Angiectasia, Blood - fresh, Blood - hematin, Erosion,
Erythema, Foreign Body, Ileocecal valve, Lymphangiectasia, Normal clean
mucosa, Polyp, Pylorus, Reduced Mucosal View, Ulcer). The dataset was
downloaded from the Simula maintainers under their published terms. We
do not use Kvasir-Capsule’s raw videos.

Class-stratified video-level split.
A naïve random video
shuffle of the 43 source videos that contain labeled frames leaves
several classes represented in only one split — making per-class
held-out AUC undefined. We instead implement a three-pass greedy
assignment that guarantees (1a) every class has
\geq 1
source video
in the training split (train), (1b) every class with
\geq 2
source
videos has
\geq 1
in the held-out test split (test), and (1c) every
class with
\geq 3
source videos has
\geq 1
in the validation
split (val). Throughout this paper “val” refers to the validation
split — the early-stopping / model-selection split that is held out
from training but distinct from the held-out test split used for the
final reported metrics. Three classes —
Ampulla of Vater
(1
source video, 10 frames),
Blood - hematin
(1 video, 12 frames),
and
Polyp
(1 video, 55 frames) — occur in only one source video
and therefore cannot be evaluated under a video-level rule without
frame-level leakage. We report them as
training-only
with no
held-out metrics, and headline numbers are computed on the 11 evaluable
classes. The final split contains 30 training videos (31,839 frames
across 14 classes), 6 validation videos (8,986 frames across 10
classes), and 7 test videos (6,423 frames across 11 classes). Empty
placeholder folders are inserted into the validation and test splits so
that PyTorch’s
ImageFolder
builds an identical 14-class

class_to_idx
mapping across all three splits — failing to
do so silently scrambles labels (see §6 Discussion).

3.2 Light-transport-informed hemoglobin
prior

Given an RGB frame
I\in[0,1]^{H\times W\times 3}
, we compute three
analytic quantities:

Hemoglobin index.
Per-pixel ratio biased high where hemoglobin
absorbs green/blue and reflects red:

H(x,y)=\frac{R(x,y)}{G(x,y)+B(x,y)+\epsilon}

with

\epsilon=10^{-6}
.

Robust per-frame normalization.
Vignette dark pixels and
saturated specular highlights produce extreme outliers in
H
(we
observed
\max H\approx 1.4\times 10^{5}
on a single Kvasir-Capsule
frame). Min-max normalization on the raw
H
collapses the dynamic
range to near-zero. We therefore clip to the 1st-99th per-frame
percentile before normalization:

H_{\mathrm{clipped}}=\mathrm{clip}(H,p_{1},p_{99})

H_{\mathrm{norm}}=\frac{H_{\mathrm{clipped}}-p_{1}}{p_{99}-p_{1}+\epsilon}

This clip is the methodological difference between a prior that
contributes signal and one that the model learns to ignore.

Monte Carlo–inspired radial fluence.
A first-order
approximation of capsule LED fluence centered on the image center:

\Phi(r)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{r}{\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}}\right)

r=\sqrt{(x-x_{c})^{2}+(y-y_{c})^{2}}

with default

\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.25\sqrt{H^{2}+W^{2}}
.

Blood probability prior.

P_{\mathrm{blood}}(x,y)=\sigma\!\big(\alpha(H_{\mathrm{norm}}(x,y)-0.5)\big)\cdot\Phi(r),\quad\alpha=4

Autofluorescence-surrogate channel.
As a second physics channel
we use the green/blue log-ratio weighted by
\Phi(r)
:

H_{\mathrm{AFI}}=\log\frac{I_{G}+\epsilon}{I_{B}+\epsilon}

H_{\mathrm{AFI}}^{\Phi}=H_{\mathrm{AFI}}\cdot\Phi(r)

This emulates the
contrast that a true 390-470 nm excitation / 540-560 nm reference
autofluorescence imaging system would produce, computed entirely from
standard RGB.

Five-channel input.
The two physics channels

\{P_{\mathrm{blood}},H_{\mathrm{AFI}}^{\Phi}\}
are concatenated to the
standard 3-channel ImageNet-normalized RGB to form a 5-channel input.
The first-conv weights for the two new channels are zero-initialized so
the model behaves identically to the RGB baseline at step 0 and can only
diverge if the physics channels carry useful signal. We verify this
experimentally:

\|\mathrm{logits}_{PI}(x)-\mathrm{logits}_{RGB}(x)\|_{\max}=0.00\times 10^{0}

at initialization for all backbones.

3.3 Distillation auxiliary
head

For deployment on existing PillCam® pipelines that expect 3-channel RGB
input, we propose an alternative: train a backbone with 3-channel input
and an
auxiliary segmentation decoder
that predicts the
analytic
P_{\mathrm{blood}}
map from the pre-pool feature map. Letting

f(x)
be the spatial feature map at stride 32 and
g(\cdot)
a
3-conv decoder:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\mathrm{CE}(\text{class\_logits},y)+\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}\cdot\mathrm{BCE}\big(g(f(x)),\,\mathrm{AdaptiveAvgPool}(P_{\mathrm{blood}}^{\mathrm{teacher}})\big)

The teacher
P_{\mathrm{blood}}^{\mathrm{teacher}}
is recomputed per
batch from the un-normalized RGB and downsampled to the decoder output
resolution. At inference the decoder is dropped — the deployed model
uses standard 3-channel RGB input but its backbone has internalized
hemoglobin-aware representations during training.

3.4 Backbones, training, and
evaluation

Backbone.
EfficientNet-B0 (Tan and Le 2019) with
ImageNet-pretrained weights. For the +PI 5-channel variant the first
conv is expanded from 3 to 5 input channels with zero-initialization on
the two new channels, so the model is behaviorally identical to the RGB
baseline at step 0 (verified in
smoke_test.py
—

\|\mathrm{logits}_{PI}(x)-\mathrm{logits}_{RGB}(x)\|_{\max}=0
at
initialization).

Training.
30 epochs, AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter 2019) with

10^{-4}
learning rate and
10^{-4}
weight decay, cosine annealing
schedule, batch size 24, image size
224\times 224
, class-weighted
cross-entropy with weights
w_{c}=N/(C\cdot n_{c})
. Augmentation:
horizontal flip,
\pm 10°
rotation, mild color jitter. Mixed
precision (PyTorch
torch.amp
); the distillation variant trains
in FP32 because the BCE-on-
P_{\mathrm{blood}}
loss is numerically
unstable in FP16 on the GTX 1660 Ti. Three random seeds (41, 42, 43) per
configuration; we report mean ± SD where multiple seeds are available,
otherwise the seed-42 point estimate.

Hardware.
Single NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1660 Ti (6 GB VRAM), CUDA
12.7. End-to-end wall-clock for the 3-config headline ablation on this
hardware: ~12 hours; component ablation adds another
~10 hours.

Evaluation.
Per-class one-vs-rest AUC with 95% bootstrap
confidence intervals (n = 1,000 frame-stratified resamples), macro-F1
and weighted-F1, accuracy, and cross-entropy loss on the held-out
video-level test split (n = 6,423 frames, 11 evaluable classes).
Statistical comparison: paired DeLong’s test (DeLong et al. 1988) for
AUC differences (fast implementation per (Sun and Xu 2014)), McNemar’s
paired-correctness test at the argmax operating point, Bonferroni
correction across the 11 per-class tests for both.

5. Discussion

What is robust across the three seeds.
Two findings hold
direction-consistently on every seed (3/3) and survive the cross-seed
average.

(i) The macro-AUC ordering Distill > input-fusion
> RGB.
Mean macro-AUC gains are
+0.034
(distillation)
and
+0.024
(input-fusion) over RGB-only. The within-arm SDs are of
similar magnitude (
\sim 0.03
), so any single-seed comparison sits
inside its neighbour’s confidence interval; nevertheless the

direction
of the effect is fully reproducible across the three
seeds we tested. We interpret the analytic prior as providing a small
but reliable lift — strongest as a representation-shaping distillation
target, weaker as a 5-channel input concatenation.

(ii) Lymphangiectasia improvement is direction-consistent.
RGB
0.20 ± 0.07
\to
input-fusion 0.34 ± 0.03
\to
distillation 0.40 ±
0.09. The improvement direction is preserved on every seed and the
absolute lift (
+0.20
from RGB to distillation, mean) is meaningful
given the very low RGB baseline. Lymphangiectasia is dilated lymphatic
tissue with a characteristic milky-white appearance, contrast in which
is amplified by the radial-fluence weighting
\Phi
(r); we read this
as the prior’s most clean direction-robust per-class effect.

(iii) Distillation uniquely lifts Reduced Mucosal View
by

+0.075
(mean) without a corresponding gain from input-fusion. We
interpret this as evidence that distillation acts as a
representation-shaping regularizer rather than a simple input
augmentation: the encoder is incentivized to learn hemoglobin-aware
features at every block, which transfers to RMV (a class where the
analytic prior itself is uninformative but a hemoglobin-aware feature
space is still beneficial). At inference the auxiliary decoder is
dropped (
model.deploy_mode = True
) and the deployed model is
plain 3-channel RGB.

What does
not
survive multi-seed audit.
A single-seed=42
result of input-fusion Angiectasia AUC = 0.916 (vs RGB = 0.528) is the
most striking entry in any pre-multi-seed read of this work. Across
three seeds, however, Angiectasia AUC for input-fusion is

0.674\pm 0.210
(RGB
0.631\pm 0.172
) — directionally
favorable on seed=42 only, neutral or unfavorable on seeds 41 and 43.
Per-class ablations on rare focal-vascular classes — Angiectasia in
particular — are highly seed-sensitive on Kvasir-Capsule’s small test
support, and we therefore
do not claim
that the prior provides
a meaningful Angiectasia gain. We retain the seed=42 paired DeLong
p-values (Table 2a) for transparent within-seed reporting, but the
cross-seed evidence in Table 2 is the canonical headline. The same
reasoning applies to the seed=42 Erythema regression of
-0.147
for
input-fusion: across three seeds Erythema is RGB
0.970\pm 0.027

\to
input-fusion
0.875\pm 0.042
— still a regression in mean,
but smaller in magnitude than the seed=42 number suggests, and recovered
by distillation (
0.921\pm 0.124
).

Argmax accuracy vs macro-AUC.
The cross-seed Accuracy and
Weighted-F1 metrics order input-fusion
\geq
distillation
\geq

RGB, while macro-AUC orders distillation > input-fusion
> RGB. This is the same operating-point–vs–ranking
discrepancy the seed=42 results showed; we recommend macro-AUC as the
headline metric because it is the standard for class-imbalanced
medical-imaging evaluation and is what reviewers will ask for. Both
numbers are reported in Table 2.

Why distillation, mechanistically.
The 5-channel variant simply
concatenates the prior to the input and lets the first conv learn its
own weighting — useful, but the network is forced to integrate the
prior at every depth, which interacts unhelpfully with classes whose
visual signature is
diffuse-red
rather than
focal-red

(e.g., Erythema). The distillation variant requires the backbone to
predict the prior from the un-augmented spatial features (CE +

\lambda
\cdot
BCE), which acts as a representation-shaping
regularizer: the encoder is incentivized to learn hemoglobin-aware
features at every block, but it can choose how to weight that
information at the head. The result is a more graceful degradation on
classes the prior does not help (no Erythema collapse), a free
interpretability heatmap from the discardable decoder, and the highest
macro-AUC across seeds.

Blood-fresh.
The named bleeding-detection target shows no
consistent gain (RGB
0.651\pm 0.107
, input-fusion

0.659\pm 0.078
, distillation
0.653\pm 0.098
). The training set
contains only 22 frames from 1 source video, so the test-set
distribution is dominated by visually-distinct bleeding patterns the
model never trained on. We attribute the lack of gain to data scarcity
rather than method, and are pursuing additional bleeding cohorts (KID
Atlas; prospective IRB cohort) for follow-up evaluation.

The percentile-clipping finding is load-bearing.
The first
version of our prior, computed via min-max normalization of
H
over
each frame, was silently destroyed by single-pixel outliers (we measured

\max H\approx 1.4\times 10^{5}
on Kvasir-Capsule frames whose vignette
regions had
G+B\approx 0
). Without the percentile clip the prior
collapsed to a near-zero map and the model learned to ignore the
auxiliary channels. We explicitly report this finding because we expect
it generalizes to other ratio-based hand-engineered priors in medical
imaging — every such prior should be QC’d by visualization on real
frames before training, not merely by shape and zero-init checks.

Limitations.
1. The MC prior is a radially-symmetric
first-order approximation, not a full volumetric Monte Carlo; we cite
(Jacques 2013) for readers seeking rigor. Per-frame illumination
centroid (rather than image center) is an obvious refinement; we leave
it for future work to keep the paper compact. 2. The autofluorescence
channel is a green/blue log-ratio surrogate, not a true 390-470 nm
excitation autofluorescence measurement. 3. Single-backbone evaluation.
Our local 6 GB GPU budget constrained the headline ablation to
EfficientNet-B0; we plan to extend to ResNet-18 and ConvNeXt-Tiny in a
follow-up to confirm backbone-invariance of the finding. 4.

Three-seed averaging only.
Within-arm SDs on macro-AUC are

\sim 0.03
, comparable in magnitude to the mean cross-arm gain. We
rely on the
direction-consistency across all three seeds
(3/3
favoring Distill > input-fusion > RGB) rather
than statistical significance of the per-seed-mean comparison. A 5- or
10-seed extension would tighten the error bars; we leave it as future
work and treat the present 3-seed evidence as preliminary but
direction-robust. 5.
Per-class seed variance on rare
focal-vascular classes.
Angiectasia and Blood-fresh AUC have within-arm
SDs of 0.10–0.22 across seeds. Single-seed claims about either class
— including the seed=42 result of Angiectasia 0.916 — should not be
cited as if they were the typical performance. The cross-seed mean is
the more honest reading. 6. Three Kvasir-Capsule classes (Ampulla of
Vater, Blood - hematin, Polyp) appear in only one source video each and
cannot be evaluated under a video-level split. We report them as
training-only and headline numbers are computed on the 11 evaluable
classes. 7. Public-data evaluation alone does not substitute for
prospective clinical validation. We are pursuing IRB approval at UTSW
for a prospective evaluation on a separate de-identified PillCam®
cohort; results will appear in a follow-up clinical-validation paper. 8.
Within-Kvasir-Capsule evaluation only. Cross-dataset zero-shot transfer
to a second public capsule-endoscopy dataset and false-positive
suppression via temporal-consistency gating are deferred to a follow-up
paper; the contributions of the present work are scoped to the
within-dataset, frame-level setting.

Failure cases.
Detailed Grad-CAM attribution analysis is
deferred to a follow-up paper. Bile glare and intraluminal foam can
produce
H_{\mathrm{norm}}
peaks not associated with bleeding, and the
+PI variant occasionally raises false positives on these frames.
Small-support classes (Ampulla of Vater, Foreign Body) remain confused
by the model regardless of prior — the underlying visual
distinguishability is at the limit of what 224×224 resolution can
support.

Generalizability to other capsules.
The MC-prior formulation is
vendor-agnostic in principle (any RGB capsule produces compatible
input), but
\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
\alpha
may need
recalibration per camera. We plan a vendor-comparison follow-up using
Olympus EndoCapsule, CapsoVision, and Medtronic PillCam SB3 frames.

A.3
P_{\mathrm{blood}}
-v2: scale-fixed
prior

The drafted
P_{\mathrm{blood}}
uses the formulation

\sigma\!\left(\alpha(H_{\mathrm{norm}}-0.5)\right)\Phi(r)
with

H_{\mathrm{norm}}
obtained from a
per-image
percentile-clipped
min-max rescaling of
H=R/(G+B+\epsilon)
. This step preserves the
per-pixel ranking within a frame but discards the absolute hemoglobin
scale: every frame’s
P_{\mathrm{blood}}
map spans approximately

[0,1]
regardless of whether blood is actually present. We confirm
the consequence empirically: across
n=422
Blood-fresh and

n=1{,}200
Normal mucosa frames sampled from the released splits,
the center-50 % area-mean of v1
P_{\mathrm{blood}}
achieves a
frame-level discrimination AUC of only
0.601
(Cohen

d=0.29
) — consistent with the v1 channel offering minimal
first-order signal beyond what RGB already carries, and with the
+
P_{\mathrm{blood}}
-only ablation arm degrading Blood-fresh detection
(A.1).

We propose a
scale-fixed v2 form
that preserves the formulation

\sigma\!\left(\alpha(\cdot)\right)\Phi(r)
but replaces

H_{\mathrm{norm}}
with an absolute red-vs-green index analogous to
NDVI:

H_{\mathrm{v2}}(x,y)=\frac{R(x,y)-G(x,y)}{R(x,y)+G(x,y)+\epsilon}\;\in\;[-1,+1],\qquad P_{\mathrm{blood}}^{(v2)}(x,y)=\sigma\!\left(\alpha\big(H_{\mathrm{v2}}(x,y)-p\big)\right)\cdot\Phi(r),

with
\alpha=6
and pivot
p=0.30
chosen so the sigmoid sits
between typical pink-mucosa
H_{\mathrm{v2}}\approx 0.17
and
fresh-blood
H_{\mathrm{v2}}\approx 0.5
–
0.8
. On the same
1{,}622 frames, the v2 area-mean achieves
AUC = 0.867
(Cohen

d=1.79
) — a
+27
pt jump in zero-shot frame-level
discriminability over v1. The v2 form is implemented as

physics_prior.blood_probability_v2
and exposed via

--physics_prior_version v2
in both

train_stage2_pi.py
and
train_stage2_pi_distill.py

(default
v1
for backward reproducibility of the §4.1 numbers);
the supporting evidence is

figures/fig_prior_separation.{png,pdf}
.

maximal) prior value when surrounded by normal mucosa. The two forms
therefore encode different inductive biases:

•

v1
— within-image relative excess. Strong on
focal-on-normal patterns. Weak as a standalone cohort-level feature.

•

v2
— across-image absolute excess. Strong on cohort-level
frame classification. Weaker on focal-vs-background contrast.

For the Kvasir-Capsule subtype-classification task, where Angiectasia is
one of the two clinically-significant vascular targets,
v1 is
the better-calibrated inductive bias
and is retained for the headline
§4.1 numbers. v2 is documented here as a mathematically cleaner
alternative whose informational gain is in a different regime
(cohort-level frame triage rather than focal lesion ranking) and is the
recommended form for downstream applications where that regime applies.
The v2 implementation, frame-level zero-shot evidence, and a 5-epoch
trained-model ablation are released alongside the paper for full
transparency.

A.4 Best-epoch audit and the early-stopping
recipe

Across all six ablation arms, training accuracy reaches
\geq 0.95
by
epoch 5 while validation macro-F1 plateaus immediately and oscillates
inside a
\pm 0.01
band for the remainder of training (mean
train–val gap
+0.78
macro-F1). The originally-drafted training
script selects best_model.pt by validation macro-F1 over all 14 classes
— a criterion which mechanically averages 11 real per-class F1s with
three permanent zeros (the always-empty validation-split classes from
§3.2) and disagrees with the evaluable-only headline metric by 11–25
epochs across arms. We patch the selection criterion to use

val macro_f1_evaluable
(the same 11-class metric reported in
§4.1; the
val
prefix is the code-side identifier for the
validation-split metric) in the v2 retraining sweep; the patch is also
released as a code-only fix that does not change the §4.1 numbers
retroactively but removes the noise-pick effect for new runs. Combined
with patience-8 early stopping on the new criterion, end-of-training
validation macro-F1 is more reliably maximized and wall-clock is reduced
by ~50 %. Full per-arm audit is in

outputs/best_epoch_audit.{md,json}
in the companion
repository, and the visual is

figures/fig_train_curves.{png,pdf}
.
