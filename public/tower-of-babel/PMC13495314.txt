Delayed retirement and low self-worth among older adults: evidence from CLASS
Authors: Guofeng Guan, Hangyu Zhang, Lu Wang, Qing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgainst the background of population aging and ongoing debate over retirement-age reform in China, this study examines whether delayed-retirement behaviour is associated with low self-worth among older adults. Using panel data from the 2018 and 2020 waves of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS), the paper investigates the association between delayed retirement and low self-worth among older adults and explores the mechanisms underlying this relationship. The empirical results show that delayed retirement is associated with a lower likelihood of low self-worth among older adults. This finding remains robust across a series of alternative specifications and supplementary robustness checks. Heterogeneity analysis further indicates that the association is more pronounced among older women and older adults engaged in informal employment. Mechanism analysis suggests that delayed retirement may be linked to a lower likelihood of low self-worth through stronger social interactions, greater economic participation, and enhanced intergenerational economic support.These findings enrich the literature on the subjective welfare consequences of delayed retirement and may inform policy discussions on later-life work and ageing in China.Keywords: Delayed retirement, Low self-worth, Social interactions, Economic participation, Intergenerational economic support

Background and introduction

Population aging, delayed retirement, and the policy context

Population aging has become one of the most important structural challenges facing countries at advanced stages of development, and China is no exception. According to the classification criteria established by the United Nations in Population Ageing and Its Socioeconomic Consequences, a country or region enters an aging society when the proportion of individuals aged 65 and above exceeds 7%. China crossed this threshold in 2000, and the share of older adults in the population has continued to increase since then. As shown in Fig. 1, the aging process has accelerated since 2004, and China is expected to enter a stage of advanced population aging by around 2035. This demographic shift has generated mounting pressure on social welfare systems, raised concerns about pension sustainability, and may constrain long-term economic growth.

China’s population aging trend

Against this background, delayed retirement has become an increasingly important policy discussion in China. During the study period, China’s statutory retirement ages were 60 for men, 50 for female workers, and 55 for female cadres. It should also be noted that the gradual reform adopted in 2024 and effective from 1 January 2025 will raise these ages over time to 63 for men, 55 for female workers, and 58 for female cadres. Accordingly, this paper examines delayed-retirement behaviour observed under the pre-reform institutional setting, rather than the causal effect of the post-2025 statutory reform itself.

The current statutory retirement system, established under the Interim Measures on the Retirement and Resignation of Workers issued by the State Council in 1978, is increasingly mismatched with the profound socioeconomic transformations China has experienced over the past several decades. As life expectancy rises and the age structure changes, extending working lives is now widely discussed as one possible policy instrument for responding to population aging, pension sustainability pressures, and changes in the age structure of the labour force. Because statutory retirement ages in China are closely linked to pension eligibility and labour-force exit in the formal institutional setting, changes in these thresholds may have broader implications not only for employment decisions, but also for income timing and family arrangements in later life.

The idea of delayed retirement was formally raised during the Third Plenary Session of the 18th Central Committee in 2013. Subsequent policy documents further clarified the direction of reform. In particular, the Fifth Plenary Session of the 19th Central Committee explicitly proposed the gradual increase of the statutory retirement age, and the 14th Five-Year Plan reiterated the principles of gradual adjustment, flexible implementation, category-specific arrangements, and coordinated planning. The urgency of this reform became even more apparent when the Standing Committee of the 14th National People’s Congress announced on September 13, 2024, that the delayed retirement policy would take effect in 2025.

At the same time, the significance of delayed retirement extends beyond pension finance and labor allocation. With continued improvements in living standards, public and academic discussions in China have increasingly extended beyond material conditions to include broader concerns about subjective well-being, dignity, and quality of life [1]. In this context, the consequences of delayed retirement should not be assessed only in terms of fiscal sustainability or labor supply. It is equally important to examine whether continued work after the statutory retirement age can shape the subjective well-being of older adults themselves, especially their psychological sense of value and social worth.

Self-worth as a subjective welfare dimension

Self-worth is an important dimension of subjective well-being. It is generally understood as an individual’s perception and evaluation of the self as a socially recognized and valued being. Huang and Yang define self-worth as an individual’s perception and evaluation of the self as a social object, together with the positive emotional experiences associated with self-recognition and self-evaluation [2]. As a core psychological construct, self-worth has a pervasive influence on cognition, emotions, and behavior.

Existing studies show that self-worth has both motivational and emotional significance. Individuals often pursue higher self-worth by continuously demonstrating and proving their abilities, suggesting that self-worth can serve as a motivational force that supports effort and achievement [3]. At the same time, lower levels of self-worth are associated with less positive evaluations of events and a greater susceptibility to emotional disturbance [4]. By contrast, stronger self-worth can foster positive psychological change, enhance one’s sense of social recognition, and reinforce the perception of being a valuable and contributing member of society [5, 6].

The conceptualization of self-worth has also evolved over time. Early research often treated it as a unidimensional construct, emphasizing a general positive or negative evaluation of the self. For example, Morris defined self-worth as an individual’s positive or negative attitude toward the self, encompassing self-acceptance and self-respect [7]. However, later studies increasingly questioned this simplified view and emphasized the multidimensional nature of self-worth [8]. In particular, Leary distinguishes between global self-worth and domain-specific self-worth, arguing that individuals may evaluate themselves differently across social, intellectual, and physical domains [9].

More recent research has shifted attention toward the determinants of self-worth, highlighting the importance of health, social relationships, personality traits, and broader social conditions. These studies suggest that self-worth can be shaped by both individual resources and relational contexts, and they seek to identify pathways through which self-worth may be enhanced [10–13]. Batra and Ghoshal similarly emphasize that self-worth is closely linked to an individual’s subjective recognition of their own identity [14].

This perspective is especially relevant in the context of aging and retirement. For many older adults in China, decades of work are not merely a source of income; they are deeply intertwined with social identity, daily routines, and interpersonal recognition. Once individuals leave the labor force, they may experience not only a decline in earnings, but also a weakening of social roles and a diminished sense of purpose, although the extent of these changes may vary across institutional and cultural settings [15]. In this sense, retirement may affect subjective well-being not only through material channels, but also through identity-related and relational channels.

Research gap and contributions

Beyond the literature on pension sustainability and labour supply, a broader body of research has shown that retirement and later-life work may affect older adults through multiple health, psychological, and behavioural pathways. Existing studies suggest that retirement may influence physical health, mental health, health behaviours, and subjective well-being [15–17], although the direction and magnitude of these effects vary across institutional settings, empirical strategies, and population groups. For example, evidence from Germany indicates that retirement may improve health through reduced work-related stress and strain, increased sleep duration, and more frequent physical activity. Evidence from China suggests that the overall effects of retirement on physical and mental health may be limited on average, while important heterogeneity exists across gender and education groups. Recent evidence from Brazil further shows that retirement may affect health-related behaviours such as physical exercise, smoking, alcohol consumption, and diet. Taken together, these studies indicate that the consequences of retirement are not confined to economic outcomes, but also extend to broader dimensions of health, behaviour, and well-being.

However, the existing literature has paid relatively limited attention to low self-worth as a distinct psychological outcome in later life. Compared with broad indicators such as subjective well-being or life satisfaction, low self-worth captures a more specific dimension of how older adults perceive their own usefulness, value, and social role. This distinction is especially important in the context of ageing, because feelings of uselessness may reflect not only emotional well-being, but also whether older adults feel that they are still needed by society and by their families. Studies on self-perceived uselessness among older adults in China have shown that this perception is associated with a wide range of demographic, psychosocial, behavioural, and health-related factors, and that it is also related to broader ageing outcomes such as successful ageing [18, 19].

In the Chinese context, the relevance of low self-worth is likely to be particularly salient because later-life identity is closely connected not only to work roles, but also to family contribution and intergenerational support. Existing research has shown that greater financial resources are associated with lower self-perceived uselessness among older adults in China, and that culturally embedded intergenerational transfers, especially downward transfers to younger generations, may also shape whether older adults feel useful or useless [20]. This suggests that the consequences of delayed retirement may extend beyond employment and income as such, and may also depend on whether continued work helps older adults maintain a meaningful contributive role within both society and the family.

A closely related recent study by Guan et al. (2025) examines the association between delayed retirement and the subjective well-being of older adults in China using CFPS data and identifies social capital, sense of achievement, and reduced grandchild care as key mechanisms [21]. The present study differs from and extends that contribution in several important respects. First, the two papers use different datasets and sample constructions. Guan et al. rely on CFPS data across multiple waves, whereas this paper uses the 2018 and 2020 waves of CLASS and constructs a more tightly defined older-adult sample based on the availability of the low self-worth measure. Second, the empirical specifications differ. Guan et al. estimate models with year and province fixed effects, while this paper adopts an individual fixed-effects framework with time fixed effects, thereby placing greater emphasis on within-individual changes over time. Third, the dependent variables are conceptually and operationally different. Guan et al. measure subjective well-being using self-rated happiness, whereas this paper focuses on low self-worth, operationalized through the direct experience of feeling useless. This distinction is substantively important because subjective well-being is a broad evaluative outcome, while low self-worth captures a more specific psychological dimension related to perceived usefulness, social value, and role relevance in later life. Fourth, the mechanism analyses highlight different pathways. Guan et al. emphasize social capital, sense of achievement, and the trade-off created by reduced grandchild care, whereas this paper examines social interactions, economic participation, and intergenerational economic support. The latter places greater emphasis on older adults’ continued contributive roles within both society and the family. Finally, the theoretical contribution of this paper lies in shifting the analytical focus from whether delayed retirement is associated with greater overall happiness to whether delayed-retirement behaviour is associated with a lower likelihood of feeling useless or socially devalued under China’s institutional and family context.

Accordingly, this paper contributes to the literature in three ways. First, it extends research on delayed retirement from broad well-being and health outcomes to the more specific psychological outcome of low self-worth. Second, it enriches the public-health and ageing literature by showing that the implications of delayed retirement may include not only economic and behavioural consequences, but also older adults’ perceptions of their own usefulness and value. Third, by focusing on China, this paper highlights how the meaning of later-life work may be shaped by institutional retirement arrangements and by family-based intergenerational roles, thereby offering evidence on the contextual boundaries of delayed-retirement effects.

The model also implies heterogeneous effects. This intuition is consistent with the paper’s subsequent heterogeneity analysis by gender and employment type.

Overall, the stylized OLG framework suggests that delayed retirement is not merely a labor-supply decision. It also reshapes the social identity, economic engagement, and intergenerational role of older adults, thereby affecting their self-worth. Guided by this framework, the following empirical analysis examines whether delayed retirement is associated with a lower likelihood of low self-worth among older adults and whether this association operates through social interactions, economic participation, and intergenerational economic support.

Research design

Data and sample selection

This paper uses data from the 2018 and 2020 waves of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS). CLASS is a nationally representative survey targeting individuals aged 60 and above. The baseline survey was conducted in 2014, followed by follow-up waves in 2016, 2018, and 2020. It covers 462 village or neighborhood committees across 28 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities in China, providing rich information on older adults’ economic conditions, family relations, social participation, and subjective well-being.

Because this study focuses on the relationship between delayed retirement and low self-worth among older adults, the 2014 and 2016 waves are excluded due to the absence of relevant survey items needed to construct the dependent variable. Accordingly, the empirical analysis relies on the 2018 and 2020 waves only. In line with the institutional background discussed earlier, the statutory retirement age is 60 for men, 50 for female workers, and 55 for female cadres, and the gradual postponement reform will raise these thresholds over time.

To reduce potential bias arising from severe health limitations and diminished self-care capacity among older adults, this paper excludes severely disabled respondents and those aged 70 or above. After further removing observations with missing values for key variables, the final sample consists of 4,849 valid observations. This sample construction helps improve comparability across individuals and reduces interference from extreme old-age health constraints.

Variables and descriptive statistics

Dependent variable: low self-worth

The dependent variable in this paper is low self-worth. Although self-worth is generally understood as an individual’s perception and evaluation of their own value in social life, the empirical measure used here captures its negative manifestation. Specifically, this paper uses the survey question, “In the past week, have you felt useless?” as a proxy for low self-worth among older adults. Responses of “No” are coded as 0, while responses of “Sometimes” or “Often” are coded as 1. Therefore, a higher value indicates a higher likelihood of experiencing low self-worth. This treatment keeps the empirical measure consistent with the original questionnaire wording and avoids reversing the coding of the outcome variable.

Key explanatory variable: delayed retirement

The key explanatory variable is delayed retirement. Because the delayed retirement policy had not yet been formally implemented during the sample period, this paper adopts a substantive rather than a direct policy-based definition. Following the original coding logic, individuals who have exceeded the statutory retirement age and continue working are classified as experiencing delayed retirement, whereas those who have completed retirement procedures and are no longer working are classified as not experiencing delayed retirement. The statutory thresholds are defined as 60 for men, 50 for female workers, and 55 for female cadres.

This definition is intended to capture a behavioral status rather than the causal effect of the 2025 reform itself. In other words, the paper examines whether continued paid work beyond the statutory retirement age is associated with a lower likelihood of low self-worth among older adults.

Control variables

To reduce omitted-variable bias, this paper includes control variables at both the individual and household levels. Individual-level controls include age, gender, pension status, health condition, personal net income last year, educational attainment, and marital status. Household-level controls include co-residence with children, total monthly household expenditure, and place of residence. These variables are included to capture demographic characteristics, economic resources, family context, and living environment that may jointly affect delayed-retirement decisions and low self-worth.

In terms of coding, health condition is treated as a binary variable, with responses of “very healthy,” “healthy,” or “fairly healthy” coded as 1 and other responses coded as 0. Educational attainment equals 1 for respondents with high school education or above, and 0 otherwise. Marital status equals 1 for married respondents and 0 otherwise. Pension status, co-residence with children, and place of residence are also coded as binary variables following the original questionnaire-based classification. Personal net income last year refers to the respondent’s total personal income over the past 12 months, measured in RMB and adjusted to 2014 constant prices. Total monthly household expenditure refers to the average monthly total expenditure of the respondent’s co-residing household, also measured in RMB and adjusted to 2014 constant prices. Both variables enter the regressions in natural logarithmic form.

To assess potential multicollinearity, we examined pairwise correlations and variance inflation factors for the main regressors. The correlation between personal income and household expenditure is moderate (r = 0.490), and the corresponding variance inflation factors are 1.42 and 1.44, respectively, suggesting that multicollinearity is not a serious concern.

A note on variable interpretation

Because the dependent variable is coded from the negative survey item “have you felt useless?”, the empirical results should be interpreted as effects on low self-worth rather than on positive self-worth. Accordingly, a negative coefficient on delayed retirement indicates that delayed retirement is associated with a lower likelihood of low self-worth, that is, a lower probability that older adults feel useless.

This interpretation ensures consistency between the coding scheme and the regression results.

This pattern may be understood from several related perspectives. First, retirement often involves the weakening or loss of socially recognised roles, whereas delayed retirement allows older adults to remain engaged in work and social life, thereby reducing the sense of uselessness associated with role exit. Second, continued work provides ongoing labour income, which may help relieve financial stress and strengthen economic independence. Third, delayed retirement may also allow older adults to maintain a contributive role within the family, particularly when continued work supports their ability to assist children or other family members. These interpretations are broadly consistent with the mechanism analysis reported later in the paper.

The control variables also suggest that economic resources and economic burdens may operate differently. In the baseline fixed-effects specification, better health status and higher personal income are associated with a lower likelihood of low self-worth, which is consistent with the idea that stronger individual resources may reduce financial stress and reinforce feelings of independence and competence. By contrast, higher household expenditure is positively associated with low self-worth. This should not be interpreted mechanically as indicating greater welfare. In this context, household expenditure may partly reflect medical spending, dependency burdens, caregiving-related costs, or broader household financial stress, all of which may be associated with a greater likelihood of low self-worth among older adults.

In addition, age is positively associated with low self-worth in the fixed-effects specification, suggesting that as individuals grow older, they may become more vulnerable to feelings of uselessness. The coefficient on place of residence becomes statistically insignificant after individual fixed effects are included, implying that time-invariant residential differences are largely absorbed by the fixed-effects estimator.

Overall, the results are consistent with the baseline finding that delayed retirement is associated with a lower likelihood of low self-worth among older adults.

First, given that the dependent variable is binary, a Probit model is used as an alternative specification. To further address these concerns, this paper uses an instrumental-variable strategy as a supplementary robustness check. The instrument is constructed as the leave-one-out provincial delayed-retirement rate, excluding the individual’s own delayed-retirement status from the provincial average, so as to reduce mechanical reflection. Heterogeneity analysis

Although delayed retirement is generally associated with a lower likelihood of low self-worth among older adults, this association is not uniform across groups. Table 5 reports the heterogeneity analysis by gender and employment type. The results suggest that the effect of delayed retirement is more pronounced among older women and among older adults engaged in informal employment.

Third, regarding intergenerational economic support, the paper constructs a binary variable based on whether the respondent or the co-residing spouse provided money, food, or gifts to children in the past 12 months. Column (3) of Table 6 shows that delayed retirement significantly increases the likelihood of providing intergenerational economic support. In the Chinese family context, being able to support adult children is not only economically meaningful but also symbolically important, as it reinforces older adults’ sense of purpose, responsibility, and value within the family, thereby helping reduce low self-worth.

Overall, the mechanism analysis indicates that delayed retirement does not affect psychological well-being solely by extending labor supply. Rather, it appears to reduce low self-worth through a broader process involving stronger social interactions, greater economic participation, and enhanced intergenerational economic support. These findings are consistent with both the theoretical framework and the baseline empirical results.

Discussion and implications

This paper examines the association between delayed retirement and low self-worth among older adults using data from the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS). During the sample period, China had not yet implemented a mandatory delayed-retirement reform; accordingly, this paper focuses on delayed-retirement behaviour rather than the causal effect of the post-2025 statutory reform itself. The empirical results show that delayed retirement is associated with a lower likelihood of low self-worth among older adults. The mechanism analysis further suggests that this association operates through social interactions, economic participation, and intergenerational economic support.

These findings should be understood in relation to the broader literature on retirement, health, and well-being. Previous studies have shown that retirement may affect physical health, mental health, health behaviours, and subjective well-being, although the direction and magnitude of these effects differ across institutional settings and empirical designs. Research from Germany suggests that retirement may improve health through relief from work-related stress, greater sleep, and increased physical activity [15]. Evidence from China indicates that the average health effects of retirement may be limited, but that important heterogeneity exists across social groups [16]. Evidence from Brazil further suggests that retirement may change health behaviours such as smoking, drinking, physical activity, and diet [17]. In this sense, the present study is broadly complementary to the wider public-health literature: rather than focusing on broad health or subjective well-being outcomes, it highlights low self-worth as a more specific psychological dimension through which later-life work may matter. The results suggest that the consequences of delayed retirement are not limited to earnings or pension-related outcomes, but may also extend to how older adults perceive their own usefulness, social role, and personal value.

The present findings are closely related to, but distinct from, those reported by Guan et al. (2025) [21]. Guan et al. show that delayed retirement is positively associated with subjective well-being among older adults and identify social capital, sense of achievement, and reduced grandchild care as relevant mechanisms. By contrast, the present study focuses on low self-worth rather than broad subjective well-being, uses CLASS rather than CFPS data, and adopts an individual fixed-effects framework to emphasize within-individual variation over time. This distinction is not merely methodological; it also reflects a different theoretical emphasis. Low self-worth is not simply the inverse of happiness, but a more role-specific psychological outcome that captures whether older adults continue to perceive themselves as useful, capable, and socially valued. The mechanism results further show that delayed retirement is associated with a lower likelihood of low self-worth through stronger social interactions, greater economic participation, and enhanced intergenerational economic support. Compared with the mechanisms emphasized by Guan et al., this study therefore places greater weight on economic agency and contributive family roles as pathways linking delayed-retirement behaviour to psychological outcomes in later life. In this sense, the current paper extends the literature by moving from broad subjective well-being to a more specific role-based psychological dimension of ageing and retirement.

The results are also consistent with the emerging literature on self-perceived uselessness among older adults in China. Prior studies have shown that self-perceived uselessness is associated with a wide range of socioeconomic, psychosocial, behavioural, and health-related factors, and that it is negatively related to successful ageing [18, 19]. This broader evidence helps situate low self-worth as an important public-health concern rather than a peripheral psychological feeling. In this sense, the present study contributes by showing that delayed-retirement behaviour may be one factor associated with a lower likelihood of such negative self-perceptions.

The mechanism results also underscore the importance of the Chinese institutional and family context. In China, the meaning of later-life work is shaped not only by income needs, but also by continued social participation and the ability to contribute to the family. Existing research suggests that greater financial resources are associated with lower self-perceived uselessness among older adults, and that intergenerational transfers—especially downward transfers to younger generations—may reinforce feelings of usefulness [20]. This helps explain why delayed retirement may reduce low self-worth through intergenerational economic support: continued work may allow some older adults to maintain a meaningful contributive role vis-à-vis their children and family members.

The findings should also be interpreted in light of the institutional and cultural context of China. In a setting where work, family responsibility, and intergenerational support remain closely connected to later-life identity, continued work beyond the statutory retirement age may carry meanings that extend beyond earnings alone. The psychological meaning of continued work may also be shaped by cultural expectations: in the Chinese context, later-life self-worth may be tied more closely to continued contribution, family responsibility, and being perceived as still useful to others. This may help explain why delayed retirement is associated with a lower likelihood of low self-worth through social interactions, economic participation, and intergenerational economic support. At the same time, these mechanisms may be context-specific. In settings where retirement is more strongly associated with personal autonomy, leisure, or the pursuit of individual preferences rather than continued contribution to family and society, the relationship between delayed retirement and low self-worth may differ substantially. The findings should therefore not be assumed to generalise automatically across cultural and institutional contexts.

These findings have several policy implications. First, the results suggest that the effects of delayed retirement should not be evaluated solely in terms of pension sustainability or labour supply. Policymakers should also consider the psychological and social consequences of later-life work, including whether continued employment may help some older adults maintain a stronger sense of usefulness and value. Second, as the effects of delayed retirement are likely to be context-dependent, policy design should remain flexible and attentive to heterogeneity across social and family circumstances. Third, if delayed-retirement policies are to be expanded, complementary measures that support age-friendly workplaces, social participation, and family-work balance for older adults may help maximise the potential benefits while reducing possible tensions.

Several limitations should be noted. First, although the fixed-effects and robustness analyses strengthen confidence in the findings, the study remains observational and caution is needed in making strong causal claims. Second, the meaning of low self-worth may be shaped by specific institutional and cultural conditions in China, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other contexts. Third, although the mechanism analysis suggests plausible channels, the available data do not allow a fully exhaustive account of all possible pathways linking delayed retirement to low self-worth.

Despite these limitations, this study adds to the growing literature on retirement and later-life well-being by showing that delayed retirement may matter not only for income, health, or broad happiness, but also for whether older adults continue to feel useful and socially valued. In doing so, it contributes a more psychologically specific perspective to the study of delayed retirement in China and highlights the importance of considering both institutional context and family-based social roles when evaluating retirement transitions.
