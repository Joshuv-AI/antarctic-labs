What do Tanzanian Research Ethics Committees prioritise? A quantitative analysis of reviewer comments using Emanuel’s framework
Authors: David Machaku, Bruno Sunguya, Elizabeth Bukusi, Richard Waddell, Connie M Ulrich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch Ethics Committees (RECs) are essential for protecting participants, yet their practical priorities in resource-limited settings remain underexplored. This study assesses how Tanzanian RECs apply Emanuel et al.'s eight-principle ethical framework during protocol review.MethodsA quantitative content analysis was conducted on reviewers’ comments from 67 clinical trial protocols submitted between 2021 and 2023 in three Tanzanian RECs. Comments were categorized using a modified ethics framework, and frequencies were calculated to identify patterns.ResultsRECs demonstrated a dominant focus on protocol structure (28.4% to 43.7%) and scientific validity (7.5% to 23.4%). Principles such as independent review (1.6% to 3.0%) and social value (2.4% to 6.0%) were consistently underemphasized. REC 1 and REC 2 placed greater emphasis on procedural and scientific elements, while REC 3 showed stronger attention to collaborative partnerships. However, REC 3 reviewed only two protocols; its percentages are highly unstable and should be interpreted as illustrative only.ConclusionTanzanian RECs showed selective conformity to Emanuel et al.’s framework, prioritizing procedural and scientific elements over equity-oriented principles. This means they might approve studies that are scientifically sound but ethically inadequate. Recommendations include the implementation of balanced-review templates, enhanced reviewer training, and institutional accountability mechanisms to promote comprehensive ethical oversight.Keywords: Research Ethics Committees, Emanuel et al.’s ethical framework, Clinical trial protocols, Ethical oversight, Tanzania

Introduction

Research Ethics Committees (RECs) safeguard the rights and welfare of participants by ensuring that research protocols meet established ethical standards [1, 2]. Globally, RECs have evolved in response to historical misconduct in research, such as the Tuskegee Syphilis Study and unethical clinical trials in vulnerable populations [3]. While international guidelines such as the Declaration of Helsinki and the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) framework provide broad principles [4, 5], the way RECs apply these standards in practice, particularly in low-resource settings, remains underexplored. Emanuel et al.’s eight-principle framework (2004) offers a systematic lens for evaluating ethical review practices. This framework underscores scientific validity, social value, fair participant selection, favorable risk–benefit ratio, independent review, informed consent, collaborative partnerships, and respect for participants [6].

Across Africa, the growth of clinical trials and collaborative research has heightened the importance of RECs [7]. Studies in Africa applied Emanuel et al.’s framework to evaluate reviewers’ comments across multiple RECs. In a South African REC, reviewers placed particular emphasis on consent forms and scientific validity [8]. Similarly, in Zimbabwe, RECs found that comments most frequently addressed consent forms and scientific validity [9]. In Uganda, however, scientific validity emerged as the dominant concern, followed by consent forms [10]. Beyond these two areas, reviewers also raised issues related to risk–benefit assessment, fair subject selection, and community engagement, dimensions that are often underemphasized. Additional concerns included administrative matters and issues outside the eight principles of Emanuel et al.’s framework. Collectively, these findings suggest a need to examine how comprehensively Emanuel et al.’s framework is applied in African contexts.

In sub-Saharan Africa, Tanzania has been one of the hotspots of clinical trials and clinical research, particularly in HIV/AIDS, malaria, and maternal health, making REC oversight increasingly vital. This oversight is coordinated through the National Institute for Medical Research (NIMR), institutional RECs, and National Regulatory Authorities such as the Tanzania Medicines and Drug Authority (TMDA) [11, 12]. Despite alignment with international standards, Tanzanian RECs face challenges, including limited funding, high protocol volumes, and shortages of trained ethicists [13, 14]. These constraints may shape the priorities of reviewers, influencing which ethical principles receive the most attention.

This study aimed to evaluate the distribution of REC reviewers’ comments on clinical trial protocols in relation to Emanuel et al.’s ethical framework. It aimed to analyze Tanzanian REC practices, to identify strengths, gaps, and areas for capacity building. Despite the RECs’ centrality in the research ecosystem, questions persist about what RECs truly prioritize when reviewing protocols. Emanuel et al.’s framework provides a comprehensive lens through which to examine these priorities.

Methodology

Study design

A quantitative content analysis was conducted to examine reviewers’ comments from Tanzanian RECs. This approach allowed systematic categorization of textual data into predefined ethical principles, enabling descriptive comparison across committees while minimizing researcher bias [15, 16].

Study setting and sample

The research was conducted in three Tanzanian RECs: two in Dar es Salaam and one in Zanzibar, representing a public academic institution, a private research institution, and a government-affiliated institution. The RECs had an established track record in clinical trial oversight, extensive experience in broader health research, and implemented a digitalized ethics review process for clinical trials.

The study sample included all approved clinical trial protocols from 2021 to 2023 to capture the post-pandemic transition, during which many RECs migrated to digital platforms for ethical review and oversight. This convenience approach was feasible because the volume of archival data from the RECs’ records was finite and manageable, minimizing sampling bias and maximizing representativeness.

Data collection and coding

Comments from REC reviews were categorized based on predefined codes derived from a modified version of Emanuel et al.’s ethics framework supplemented with categories identified in prior African studies (South Africa, Uganda, and Zimbabwe). In addition to the eight principles, two parallel categories that included protocol structure and additional structural elements, were retained to capture administrative and procedural concerns [8–10].

Each comment was coded using a binary approach (1 = present, 0 = absent), indicating whether a given ethical principle or sub-item was addressed within the reviewer’s comments for each protocol. Percentages represented the proportion of binary-coded occurrences for a given category out of all coded occurrences across the total comment pool for that REC.

This quantizing process, the conversion of qualitative data into numerical values, allowed systematic comparison across protocols and RECs [17, 18]. It is important to note that “Protocol Structure” and “Additional Structural Elements” are not part of Emanuel et al.’s eight principles; they were retained as analytically distinct categories because previous comparable studies in South Africa, Zimbabwe, and Uganda similarly documented a significant volume of reviewer comments falling outside the eight principles [8–10]. Including these categories enabled direct cross-study comparability and avoided artificially inflating the proportional weight of the ethical principles. All percentage distributions for the eight principles were therefore interpretable relative to comments within the principles alone, with protocol structure and additional structural elements treated as a separate, parallel layer of review activity. The frequencies and rank of the comments were analyzed to tally occurrences of sub-items within each category, enabling comparisons between RECs and identification of dominant, moderate, and underemphasized areas.

Coding process and reconciliation

The reviewers’ written comments were coded into an Excel sheet that was developed by the first author (DM) to automate tallying and frequency distribution analysis. When reviewer comments addressed multiple ethical principles, such as a comment on ‘community engagement’ that also touched on ‘informed consent’, dual coding was applied. Dual coding occurred in approximately 8% of all coded comments (n ≈ 58 of 726). This dual coding may have marginally inflated frequencies in those specific categories; while the overall effect was considered minimal given the total comment volume, readers should be aware that frequency counts for collaborative partnerships, informed consent, scientific validity, and protocol structure may be slightly higher than they would be under single-coding rules.

The researchers collaborated with REC administrators to code the data at their respective offices. As gatekeepers of the reviewers’ remarks, the administrators supervised the single-session process to ensure strict confidentiality of the data. To ensure the reliability of the coding process, intercoder reliability was assessed using a randomly selected subset of 20% of the reviewer comments (n ~ 145 comments), independently coded by a statistician who was not involved in the primary coding. Agreement was calculated using Cohen’s Kappa (κ). An overall Kappa of κ = 0.79 was achieved, indicating substantial agreement [19]. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus between the coders, and where disagreement persisted, the statistician served as arbitrator. This process was done to minimize systematic bias and strengthen the validity of the frequency-based comparisons across RECs.

Ethical considerations for using archived data

Ethical approval and permission to conduct the study were obtained from the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences-REC (MUHAS-REC-03-2024-2069), the National Health Research Ethics Committee (NatHREC) (NIMR/HQ/R.8a/Vol.IX/4771), and the Zanzibar Health Research Ethics Committee (ZaHREC)(ZAHREC/02/ST/MAR/2025/261). The study accessed archived reviewer comments, which constitute institutional data and not human subjects research. This study did not require informed consent from participants, as permission to access data and to publish was provided by the National Institute for Medical Research. Protocol and reviewer identifiers were not included in the analysis and the manuscript. Confidentiality was keenly observed.

Findings

The number of clinical trial protocols approved from 2021 to 2023

There were 67 clinical trial protocols analyzed in the period from January 2021 to December 2023. REC 2 had the highest overall number of protocols (n = 39), followed by REC 1 with 26. REC 3 was minimal, recording only 2 instances exclusively in 2023. Annual totals showed a declining trend in the subsequent years. Notably, REC 3 had zero activity in 2021 and 2022, with a small number in 2023 (Table 1).

Demographics of clinical trial protocols

Of the 67 studies across the three RECs, the vast majority (98.5%) employed treatment/intervention designs. By the trial phase, Phase III studies were most common (46.3%). REC 2 had the most studies overall (58.2%). Most Phase I trials appeared in REC 2, whereas Phase III trials were more evenly distributed between REC 1 and REC 2. Five studies had no designated phase, primarily in REC1. Adults-only studies comprised the largest studied group (55.2%), predominantly in REC 2. Children-only studies (23.9%) were also well represented and mainly distributed between all three RECs. Notably, REC 3 included only clinical trials involving children; however, given that REC 3 reviewed only 2 protocols, this observation is purely descriptive and should not be generalised (Fig. 1).

The number of initial reviewers’ comments in the RECs

Each REC’s reviewer comments were tallied across three a priori variable categories derived from the modified Emanuel et al. (2004) framework: (1) the eight ethical principles as indicated above; (2) protocol structure items; and (3) additional structural elements. The total frequency (TF) for each REC was calculated by summing binary-coded occurrences (1 = present, 0 = absent) across all sub-items within these three categories. Across the three RECs, a total of 726 individual comments were generated, ranging from 279 to 380.

Comparing the three RECs

Across the three RECs, key similarities included a dominant focus on protocol structure and scientific validity, which together comprised the highest comment proportions. Fair participant selection (Principle 4) received moderate, consistent attention across all RECs, while independent review (Principle 6) remained consistently underemphasized.

However, significant differences emerged: REC 1 prioritized structural and scientific issues, applying stricter scrutiny to document form and scientific foundations, whereas REC 2 and particularly REC 3 emphasized participant-centered ethical principles. Notably, REC 3 showed the strongest focus on collaborative partnerships; however, a caveat to this is that REC 3 reviewed only 2 protocols. Hence, the percentages in REC 3 are not statistically comparable to RECs 1 and 2. Overall, all RECs demonstrated some degree of conformity on core technical aspects (Figs. 2 and 3).

Discussion

This study analyzed 726 reviewer comments across 67 clinical trial protocols reviewed by three RECs between 2021 and 2023. The findings highlight how written reviewers’ comments are distributed across Emanuel et al.’s eight-principle framework and document variation in emphasis between the RECs. Interpretations in this section are based on descriptive patterns and not normative indictments of REC practices. Frequency data cannot establish the quality, depth, or importance of deliberations on any principle, as RECs also conduct committee meetings where substantive discussions occur orally without generating written comments.

The finding that protocol structure and scientific validity dominated the written comments across all three RECs aligns with previous African REC studies. South African RECs emphasized consent forms (27.4%) and scientific validity (21.3%) [8], Zimbabwean RECs showed similar patterns (23% and 13% respectively) [9]. While this study found a comparable focus on scientific validity across Tanzanian RECs, the RECs place unprecedented emphasis on protocol structure, far exceeding the 9.5% administrative concerns reported elsewhere [10]. Several alternative explanations for these patterns can be drawn. First, RECs may treat scientific validity and informed consent as threshold conditions, where, if these are not attained, then reviewing other conditions loses relevance. Second, structured submission and review templates, or local and national guidelines, may direct written comments towards certain categories regardless of the reviewer’s values or training. Third, some principles, such as independent review, may generate fewer comments by design because they concern the review process itself rather than protocol structure. Fourth, reviewers may consider some principles satisfied through other documentation, hence they won’t be flagged in comments. These explanations must be considered before attributing comment patterns to reviewer training gaps or a culture of procedural compliance.

One of the RECs showed more importance on collaborative partnerships compared to the other two RECs, suggesting institutional culture may shape ethical emphasis more than national guidelines alone would predict. The consistent underemphasis of independent review and social value may reflect multiple factors, including resource constraints [20], prioritization of immediate regulatory compliance [21], or limited reviewer training in assessing long-term social impact [22]. We should keep in mind that this dataset contains no information on protocol sponsorship (Global North vs. local), funding flows, benefit-sharing agreements, or post-trial access provisions. This opens up the opportunity to design studies that could pair frequency analysis with document review of funding arrangements or ethnographic observation of REC meetings, rather than the conclusions of the present study. Our findings apply specifically to written reviewer comments on clinical trials reviewed by three Tanzanian RECs. The study did not examine observational studies, social science research, or other protocol types; 46.3% of protocols were Phase III trials, which may present different ethical profiles. The findings should not be generalised to all REC activities, all protocol types, or to other African countries without appropriate caution.

All Tanzanian RECs follow the same national guidelines established by the nation’s regulatory authorities [11, 12]. These authorities are also involved in the coordination of accreditation and training of the RECs [23]. Yet, comment patterns vary between institutions, pointing to a role for institutional culture and local practices alongside national standards. Future mixed-methods research should examine reviewer deliberations, pilot balanced-review templates, and longitudinally track whether strengthened attention to underemphasized principles translates to more comprehensive oversight.

Study limitations

This analysis has important limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the binary coding approach flattens qualitative nuance: for example, a minor formatting note and a substantive concern about conflict of interest are counted equally. No weighting for importance or severity was applied, meaning that high-impact remarks did not count more than low-impact ones. This limits the degree to which frequency counts can be equated with ethical thoroughness. Second, the study does not provide a denominator for weighting frequencies against the total number of possible comments per principle. Percentages reflect the share of coded occurrences within each REC’s total comment pool; they do not indicate whether all applicable principles were scrutinised for every protocol. Third, dual coding (approximately 8% of comments) may marginally inflate frequencies in categories at the intersection of principles. The precise impact of this has not been fully quantified and should be considered when interpreting frequencies for collaborative partnerships, informed consent, scientific validity, and protocol structure. Fourth, the small sample for REC 3 (n = 2) renders its percentages highly sensitive to individual variation and statistically unstable, limiting them to exploratory insights rather than firm comparisons with REC 1 (n = 26) and REC 2 (n = 39). Fifth, generalizability is constrained by examining only clinical trial protocols, and therefore, findings should not be assumed for other types of research reviewed by the same RECs. The predominance of Phase III trials (46.3%) may further influence the comment distribution, as safety and efficacy concerns are particularly salient in late-phase trials. Sixth, relying exclusively on archival written comments means that oral deliberations during REC meetings are not captured. Principles such as social value or collaborative partnerships may be discussed extensively in meetings without generating many written comments. Written comments, therefore, represent an incomplete proxy for the full scope of REC deliberation. Finally, the study evaluated written reviewers’ comments from three RECs in one country, though they offer some institutional diversity.

Nonetheless, this study has several strengths that support its credibility. The study minimized sampling bias by analyzing all clinical trials submitted during the study period, providing a robust picture of clinical trial research in Tanzania. Subjective categorization errors were also minimized by using intercoder reliability calculations. The modified Emanuel et al.’s framework was grounded in prior research in Africa and supported the use of this framework to capture REC practices. And, the study captures actual reviewers’ comments through archival data that helps to explain the components of research review and its value.

Conclusion

This study describes how written reviewer comments are distributed across Emanuel, et al.’s 8 -principle framework in three Tanzanian RECs. The findings identify a written emphasis on some principles, such as scientific validity and informed consent, while an underemphasis on others, such as social value, independent review, and collaborative partnerships. Protocol structure has been, by far, the most emphasized category in the written comments. To support more comprehensive ethical oversight, RECs could consider implementing structured review checklists requiring explicit written commentary on all eight principles, investing in reviewer training focused on social value and equity dimensions, and conducting periodic audits of comment distribution. Future research, particularly mixed-methods designs combining comment analysis with direct observation of REC deliberations, would help clarify whether patterns in written comments reflect broader gaps in review practice or simply the nature of written versus oral deliberation.

Implications

This study highlights the strengths and gaps in ethical oversight practice in Tanzanian RECs. The consistent flagging of protocol structural organization and scientific validity demonstrates strong procedural and technical rigor. However, the comparatively limited attention to social value, collaborative partnerships, and independent review suggests areas where RECs may risk underemphasising dimensions related to equitable partnership and long-term community benefit. Addressing these shortcomings is important to ensure that clinical trials are both scientifically sound and ethically responsive to local populations.

One practical implication is the implementation of review checklists and submission formats, which can steer reviewers towards both structural, methodological, and ethical issues. This could encourage more comprehensive oversight of clinical trials. In addition, targeted training for reviewers on assessing social value, benefit-sharing, and partnership quality would strengthen REC practices and help committees move beyond compliance towards a more holistic ethical review. Institutional culture also matters. The stronger emphasis on collaborative partnerships observed in one REC, though based on a very small sample, suggests that committee norms and leadership priorities can shape ethical emphasis. This will foster peer learning and consistent consideration and application of Emanuel’s framework.

Finally, the findings point to directions for future research. Studies incorporating qualitative data, such as observation of REC deliberations or interviews with reviewers, would provide richer insight into how principles are applied beyond written comments. Comparative work across African countries could clarify whether the Tanzanian patterns reflect broader regional trends or unique national contexts.

Data availability

The data analysed in this study consist of quantized reviewer comments obtained from RECs. The original comments are the property of the respective RECs and are confidential. Due to these ownership, confidentiality, and contractual restrictions, the raw reviewer comments are not publicly available and cannot be shared without explicit permission from the RECs. The authors had access to the original comments only for the purpose of this one-time content analysis (quantification of themes, frequencies, and ranks) under the supervision of the REC.
