A fatty acid amide activates myeloid cells and improves neurovascular outcomes in retinal degeneration
Authors: Guoqin Wei, Shreyosree Chatterjee, Qinglin Yang, Sanahan Vijayakumar, Daisuke Ogasawara, Sarah Giles, Katie Biscocho, Peter Westenskow, Junhua Wang, Ruhan Fan, Helena Pham, Edith Aguilar, Jacob Robinson, Ayumi Usui-Ouchi, Roberto Bonelli, Kevin Eade, Gary Siuzdak, Benjamin Cravatt, Michael J. Sailor, Dale Boger, Martin Friedlander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neurovasculoglial cross-talk underlying breakdown of the neurovascular unit is a central, yet poorly understood, component of many neurodegenerative disorders of the CNS, including retinal disease. Primary fatty acid amides have been identified to regulate this cross-talk between vasculature and neuronal tissues, but specific molecules and mechanisms remain unresolved. Here we show, using an unbiased high-resolution metabolomics screen, that erucamide, a 22:1 monounsaturated omega-9 fatty acid amide, is highly dysregulated during photoreceptor degeneration in mice. In vivo delivery of erucamide using organosilane-modified porous silicon nanoparticles activated retinal myeloid cells, leading to the upregulation of angiogenic and neurotrophic cytokines that limited vascular and neuronal degeneration. We identified TMEM19 as a binding protein for erucamide that is crucial for myeloid cell activation and subsequent neuroprotection. These findings reveal a previously unknown primary fatty acid amide pathway that modulates neuroimmune interactions during retinal degenerative diseases. We propose erucamide and analogs as candidate therapeutics.

Main

The vast majority of diseases that lead to catastrophic loss of vision do so as a result of breakdown in the neurovascular and glial unit of the retina. Retinal pigmented epithelial (RPE) cell malfunction and subsequent photoreceptor degeneration can lead to vision loss, exemplified by conditions such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD)1. Other leading causes of vision loss are inherited retinal degenerations such as retinitis pigmentosa2,3 and neurovascular retinal diseases such as diabetic retinopathy4. These diseases either do not have a clear genetic basis (for example, diabetic retinopathy) or are genetically heterogeneous (for example, retinitis pigmentosa)5. While these diseases have different underlying causes, they share similar pathologies with functional loss of the interconnected vasculature, glia, photoreceptors and RPE, leading to vascular anomalies and photoreceptor death.

Photoreceptors convert light into electrical signals through a collaboration between cells of the neurosensory retina and the RPE and choriocapillaris. This phototransduction process begins in the photosensitive outer segments of rod and cone photoreceptors. The RPE plays a critical role in this process by interdigitating with the photoreceptor outer segments where it, among other things, phagocytoses shed outer segments and recycles isomerized opsins. Its functions are necessary for the activity and survival of photoreceptors6. Tightly controlled lipid homeostasis is crucial to regulate the turnover between photoreceptors and RPE cells and to maintain their cellular functions7.

Distinct lipid mediators, such as specific fatty acid amides, are also essential for maintaining retinal health and function. For example, ceramides are key players in various forms of photoreceptor cell death and are potential drug targets for retinal degenerative diseases8. Anandamide, also known as N-arachidonoylethanolamide, is a well-characterized neurotransmitter that regulates the endocannabinoid signaling pathway and can modulate visual signal processing by interacting with cannabinoid receptors in the retina9. However, little is known about the specific roles of primary fatty acid amides (PFAMs) in the retina. As a direct extension of the CNS, the retina shares many characteristics with brain tissue. A better understanding of the biological and pathological functions of PFAMs in the retina is also likely to shed light on their roles in the brain and other neuronal tissues.

PFAMs10,11 represent a family of endogenous chemical messengers and signaling molecules in the mammalian nervous system. Structurally, PFAMs consist of a long hydrocarbon chain with an amide group (–CONH2) at one end. PFAMs like palmitamide, oleamide and the other three PFAMs were first discovered in 1989 in luteal phase plasma12. Although their biological functions were initially unclear, interest increased when oleamide was identified as an endogenous sleep-inducing molecule in mammalian cerebrospinal fluid10. Another PFAM, erucamide, was detected in the plasma and cerebrospinal fluid of pig brain13 and was first isolated from bovine mesentery. It was reported to be pro-angiogenic over 34 years ago14,15, although its complete biological function remains unclear.

Given the critical role of PFAMs in the nervous system and the known importance of other fatty acids in the retina, we undertook an untargeted metabolomic and lipidomic screen of normal and degenerating retinas. We found that one of the most profound changes in the retinal lipid profile occurred with a PFAM, erucamide. Erucamide levels decreased drastically in degenerating retinas, indicating a possible neuroprotective function. To address the challenge of in vivo delivery of the hydrophobic erucamide molecule, we utilized organosilane-modified porous silicon nanoparticles (pSiNPs). Intravitreal injections of erucamide nanoparticles led to widespread activation of GS-lectin-positive cells, which were identified as CD11b+ myeloid cells. This activation, driven by TMEM19 protein binding, was accompanied by the upregulation of angiogenic cytokines and growth factors, as well as neuroprotection, suggesting a new therapeutic function for erucamide in retinal diseases.

Results

Erucamide is downregulated during retinal degeneration

Using high-resolution global mass spectrometry-based metabolomics, we cataloged the metabolites dysregulated during retinal degeneration in an animal model of RPE-mediated retinal degeneration, the Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) rat. Dysregulated features were identified using our data processing technology (XCMS)16 combined with a database we developed (METLIN)17,18. Of all the untargeted metabolites, PFAMs emerged as one of the most severely dysregulated classes of metabolites in degenerating retinas (Fig. 1a). Erucamide is present in the CNS of many species10,15,19 and has been shown to regulate angiogenesis and water balance in skeletal muscle and the intestine14,15,19. However, its function in the CNS, biosynthetic pathway, site of action and potential signaling cascades are all unknown. In addition, detection of erucamide in human retinal tissue has not been previously demonstrated. Using liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (LC–MS), we detected picogram quantities of erucamide in peripheral retinal tissue from postmortem human donor eyes (n = 6, age range 62–85 years) from Lions NSW Eye Bank, Sydney Local Health District Ethics Review Committee (2020/ETH03339), suggesting that it is ubiquitously expressed in the retina across multiple species (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c). As erucamide is highly hydrophobic, direct retinal injection is not possible because it aggregates locally and fails to disperse within the retina after intravitreal injection. Consequently, we generated functionalized pSiNPs for the delivery of erucamide both in vivo and in cultured tissues. The pores of these particles were functionalized with hydrophobic n-octadecylsilane groups to facilitate loading of erucamide, while the overall particles remain sufficiently hydrophilic to allow full dispersion in aqueous solutions.

pSiNPs are biocompatible, showing low toxicity in multiple endothelial cell types (Extended Data Fig. 2a, average viability exceeded 85% at all tested concentrations), and can biodegrade into silicic acid end products that are readily excreted from the body20. In addition, their tunable pore size and their readily functionalized surface make them amenable to the loading of a wide range of small-molecule and biologic therapeutics. As a result, pSiNPs have been used extensively to deliver drugs to sensitive and hard-to-reach tissues21, including the delivery of small-molecule drugs to the vitreous22,23. Chemical surface functionalization of pSiNPs with n-octadecylsilane (H3Si (CH2)17CH3, referred to here as C18) imparts a hydrophobic character24 to the mesopores (10–15 nm), and thus were chosen for loading of erucamide (Fig. 1g). The nanoparticles maintained desirable sizes (~200 nm) and zeta potential25,26 (−33 mV) after erucamide loading, as shown by the transmission electron microscopy images, dynamic light scattering and zeta potential (ζ-potential) measurements (Extended Data Fig. 2b–d). The loading of erucamide was confirmed by the attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectrum of the erucamide-loaded C18-pSiNPs (Eru-C18-pSi), which displayed the characteristic C = O (at 1,690 cm−1) and N-H (at 3,310 cm−1) stretching bands of erucamide. The C-H scissor and stretching modes (at 1,465 cm−1 and 2,849 cm−1, respectively) are assigned to both erucamide and the C18 surface modification27,28, while a band at 1,080 cm−1 is assigned to the Si-O-Si stretching modes from silicon oxides on the surface of the pSiNPs (Extended Data Fig. 2e). Thermogravimetric analysis measurements indicated that the particles were composed of approximately 6% (wt%) erucamide, based on the mass change upon oxidative combustion at 950 °C (Extended Data Fig. 2f,g).

Erucamide delivery via pSiNPs rescues retinal degeneration

Next, we investigated the potential therapeutic function of erucamide in the mammalian retina. rd10 mice were used in these experiments as vaso-degeneration and photoreceptor degeneration start at P16–P20 and occur in a stereotypical temporal sequence29, and erucamide levels are spontaneously attenuated by P12 (Extended Data Fig. 1a). We intravitreally injected 214 ng of erucamide into the vitreous of rd10 at P12 and P22 through Eru-C18-pSi (Fig. 2a). We observed that the extent of photoreceptor atrophy was significantly reduced in rd10 mice at P32 with erucamide. The thicknesses of both the outer nuclear layer (ONL)30 and the inner nuclear layer (INL) were rescued by erucamide. (Fig. 2b,c). To assess retinal function, we performed electroretinography (ERG) recordings across a range of stimulus intensities. Erucamide-treated rd10 mice exhibited increased scotopic B-wave amplitudes compared with vehicle controls across intensities, reaching statistical significance at higher flash strengths (50 cd s/m2; Fig. 2d,e). These results indicate a rod-dominated, intensity-dependent functional rescue in a genetically degenerating retina. Additionally, erucamide significantly protected the intraretinal deep plexus vasculature from vaso-obliteration compared to vehicle-injected controls (Fig. 2f,g). Strikingly, we also observed significantly increased numbers of CD11b+ myeloid cells across the retinal layers, especially in the ONL after erucamide treatment. Our findings are consistent with prior work showing that the neuroprotective effect of IGF-1 in rd10 mice also requires CD11b+ microglia, which infiltrate the ONL and support photoreceptor survival31. Moreover, we extended our analysis to the RhoP23H model to test whether this pathway applies across different genetic contexts. Unlike rd10, which carries a missense mutation in exon 13 of phosphodiesterase 6B (Pde6b)29, the RhoP23H model carries the p.Pro23His mutation in the rhodopsin gene and shows progressive retinal degeneration similar to that seen in patients with autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa32. As shown in Extended Data Fig. 3, Eru produced clear rescue effects on INL thickness and vessel density, and showed intensity-dependent functional improvement in ERG responses. In this advanced disease stage, vascular density was quantified as a combined all-layer metric due to limited anatomical separation of individual plexuses. Together, these complementary models support the broad applicability of our approach across multiple forms of inherited retinal degeneration.

Erucamide targets CD11b+ myeloid cells

To evaluate the effects of erucamide on healthy ocular tissue and to determine the cellular and biological target of erucamide, it was intravitreally administered into 1-month-old wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J mouse retinas. Eru-C18-pSi injected mice showed an infiltration of GS-lectin+ cells at 3 days after injection, while retinas injected with C18-pSiNPs maintained a normal appearance similar to the non-injected control group (Fig. 3a). Further staining with collagen IV33 and CD11b34 revealed that the GS-lectin+ active signals were CD11b+ myeloid cells but not collagen IV+ endothelial cells (Fig. 3b). These results suggest that erucamide might directly target CD11b+ myeloid cells in the retina. We synthesized a fluorescent dye (BODIPY)-labeled erucamide (BDPErucamide; Fig. 3c) to directly observe the cellular targets. Strikingly, all BDPErucamide was taken up by CD11b+ cells, providing additional evidence that CD11b+ cells are the direct target of erucamide in the retinas (Fig. 3d). As observed in rd10 mice, CD11b+ cells in WT C57BL/6J retinas were activated across the retinal vascular layers by erucamide; however, unlike rd10 retinas, erucamide-induced CD11b+ cell accumulation in the ONL was not significant in WT eyes (Fig. 3e,f). To further explore the potential effects of erucamide on the target cells, we isolated CD11b+ cells by flow cytometry and evaluated the expression of pro-angiogenic genes within the cells by quantitative PCR (qPCR). We found that the expression of PDGFα, CTGF, PDGFβ, CX3CL1, Ang1 and BDNF was higher in CD11b+ cells of erucamide-treated animals (Fig. 3g). Furthermore, the findings translated to human tissue. Treatment of human induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cell-derived macrophage precursor cells (MPCs)35 with erucamide led to significantly higher levels of PDGFα, VEGF, FGF-2, IGF-1 and BDNF compared to controls (Fig. 3h). Taken together, these data suggest that CD11b+ myeloid cells are the primary target of erucamide both in vivo and in vitro resulting in the secretion of pro-angiogenic and neurotrophic factors.

TMEM19 is the binding protein mediating erucamide’s effects

These results raised the question of whether there is a specific receptor or binding protein for erucamide at the cellular level. We used a photoaffinity labeling (PAL) method36 to further explore and define the potential binding proteins of erucamide in a human microglia cell line, HMC3 cells. As shown in Fig. 4, we generated ‘fully functionalized’ lipid probes of erucamide (Eru.da) and the closely related PFAMs, which are also naturally present in the retina (oleamide and palmitamide, Ole.da and Pal.da), as control probes36. A photoreactive element (diazirine group) was introduced into the carbon chain of erucamide, oleamide and palmitamide to convert reversible small-molecular-protein interactions into stable, covalent adducts upon ultraviolet (UV) light irradiation. Introduction of a terminal alkyne handle allows late-stage conjugation to rhodamine or biotin tags by click chemistry (Fig. 4a,b). Erucamide, oleamide and palmitamide probes showed overlapping but distinct protein interaction profiles in HMC3 cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Following PAL (3 h incubation with probes), biotin conjugation and affinity enrichment, multiplexed proteomics of all three probes provided a unique list of erucamide-specific binding proteins that are involved in specific biological processes like lipid metabolism, signaling and cellular processes. However, a large number of these proteins were uncategorized as membrane traffic-related proteins (Extended Data Fig. 4b,c). From the list of proteins identified, a transmembrane protein, TMEM19, was the most likely candidate to be an erucamide binding protein (Fig. 4c) because it was the most highly enriched protein in PAL of erucamide diazirine probes compared to control probes. We confirmed the interaction between erucamide and TMEM19 by overexpressing TMEM19 in HEK293T cells and observed that overexpressed TMEM19 is prominently labeled by erucamide probes compared to control probes (Fig. 4d). TMEM19-dependent downstream signaling mediates neuroprotection

TMEM19 is predicted to have six transmembrane domains; however, its function remains unknown (Fig. 5a). We further investigated the effect of erucamide on myeloid cells through TMEM19 by analyzing bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in iPS cell-MPCs treated with erucamide with TMEM19 knocked down. TMEM19 was knocked down in iPS cell-MPCs using siTMEM19 or scrambled short interfering RNA (Scr siRNA) as control, then cells were treated with either empty nanoparticles (C18-pSi) or erucamide (Eru-C18-pSi). Differential expression analysis was performed to compare the treatment combinations. We first performed the comparisons between Scr cells with and without erucamide (Extended Data Fig. 5a) and siTMEM19 cells separately (Extended Data Fig. 5c); subsequently, we compared differences from the two comparisons against each other in Fig. 5b. By directly comparing erucamide-induced transcriptional changes in Scr versus siTMEM19 MPCs, this analysis isolates the component of the erucamide response that specifically requires TMEM19. Enrichment analyses on these results revealed that the most significantly decreased pathway involves cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions (Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 5a–c). A number of them (for example, PDGFRB, TNFSF18, TNFSF13B and TNFRSF8) are reported to be highly associated with angiogenesis and vascular homeostasis37–39 (Fig. 5c). qPCR confirmed that the effective downstream cytokines/growth factors (VEGFa, PDGFα, FGF, TNF, IGF-1 and BDNF) of these pathways are upregulated by erucamide, but they were dysregulated when we knocked down TMEM19 (Fig. 5d). This aligns well with our results indicating an upregulation of pro-angiogenic genes in erucamide-injected retinas. To determine if TMEM19 is essential for the activation of MPCs by erucamide stimulation, we knocked down another two top candidates from the same PAL list. It was only when TMEM19 was knocked down that we observed that most of the growth factors (for example, FGF-2, VEGFa and PDGFα) triggered by erucamide were not upregulated or activated (Extended Data Fig. 5d,e). Discussion

In recent years, growing evidence has highlighted that neurovascular and glial cross-talk is essential for maintaining healthy, functional neurons, blood vessels and glia in the CNS40, including the retina. Therapeutic strategies aimed at preventing neuronal degeneration and vision loss have focused on modulating neurotrophic and angiogenic signaling pathways as well as using iPS cell-derived retinal tissues to replace damaged RPE cells/photoreceptors, stabilizing retinal structure and visual function41–44. Several studies have shown that stem cell transplantation into the eyes of retinitis pigmentosa animal models can partially improve visual function45,46. However, concerns remain regarding the long-term efficacy of iPS cell-derived RPE therapies, particularly due to the risk of oncogenic mutations and tumorigenesis reported in both preclinical models and clinical observations47,48. For example, a pilot clinical study that administered mesenchymal stem cells, adipose-derived stem cells and platelet-rich plasma into the suprachoroidal space of 21 patients with retinitis pigmentosa did not show significant improvements in visual performance up to 6 months after treatment49.

The limited survival rates and potential immune responses pose important hurdles to the widespread clinical application of stem cell therapies in treating retinal degenerative diseases like AMD and retinitis pigmentosa. Given these limitations, we explored whether small molecules expressed by iPS cell-derived retinal tissue could provide neurovasculotrophic rescue in models of retinal degeneration. Metabolomic and lipidomic screens of degenerating retinas revealed a PFAM, erucamide, with paracrine neurotrophic activity that exerted a profound rescue effect on models of retinal neurovascular degeneration. Our approach involved the use of advanced nanoparticles to allow effective in vivo delivery of erucamide, circumventing its hydrophobic nature and enhancing its bioavailability in the retina.

Furthermore, we identified CD11b+ myeloid cells as primary targets of erucamide within the retina. Consistent with prior studies implicating myeloid cells in photoreceptor support, erucamide treatment promoted CD11b+ cell activation across retinal layers, with significant ONL accumulation observed specifically in erucamide-treated degenerating rd10 retinas. This context-dependent ONL accumulation of CD11b+ cells is consistent with support of neurotrophic signaling to degenerating photoreceptors and paracrine angiogenic support of the adjacent deep vascular plexus. Further mechanistic studies using PAL, click chemistry for biotin-derived affinity enrichment and multiplexed quantitative proteomics alongside closely related PFAMs, revealed TMEM19 as a new transmembrane protein serving as a major and selective binding partner for erucamide. Our findings define erucamide–TMEM19 signaling as a regulator of retinal homeostasis through the coordinated modulation of neuronal, glial and vascular compartments. Rather than acting solely as a neuroprotective or anti-inflammatory molecule, erucamide orchestrates a broader ‘cross-talk’ between neural, vascular and glial elements of the retina. Activation of TMEM19 on microglial cells stimulates the secretion of cytokines and growth factors (including VEGF, PDGF, FGF-2, IGF-1 and BDNF) that support photoreceptor integrity and maintain vascular networks.

Although erucamide-treated rd10 mice showed significant B-wave recovery under scotopic conditions, A-wave amplitudes exhibited only modest, nonsignificant increases, consistent with partial preservation of photoreceptors that remain genetically compromised due to the Pde6b mutation. This incomplete A-wave recovery suggests that the enhanced B-wave likely reflects not only residual photoreceptor input but also neurotrophic modulation of inner retinal cells. Supporting this, erucamide treatment elevated CD11b+ myeloid infiltration and upregulated trophic factors such as FGF, IGF-1 and BDNF, which may enhance synaptic function or resilience of bipolar and Müller cells. These findings point to a dual mechanism of action: preservation of rod photoreceptors and functional support to inner retinal circuits, underlying the observed rescue of visual signaling.

TMEM19 is a new transmembrane protein with no reported function in any other cell type or tissues. Using single-cell RNA-seq databases from the Spectacle Platform, we found that TMEM19 has low expression in retinas across different databases for humans and mice, with signals mainly from the microglia50, which is consistent with our observations. Future investigations will more closely examine the precise mechanisms regulated by TMEM19 and explore additional applications of erucamide in both in vitro and in vivo models. Furthermore, the optimization of erucamide analogs to enhance solubility and prolong in vivo half-life could potentially offer novel small-molecule-based therapies for AMD, retinitis pigmentosa and other neurovasculoglial degenerative diseases. Conversely, the development of erucamide antagonists or TMEM19 inhibitors could provide strategies to mitigate pathological angiogenesis associated with conditions like diabetic retinopathy and neovascular AMD.

In conclusion, our findings support the use of small-molecule-based therapies targeting the erucamide–TMEM19 pathway as a promising therapy for the treatment of retinal degenerative diseases, offering potential advantages in terms of efficacy and clinical applicability. This discovery expands our understanding of PFAMs beyond their established roles in the CNS, shedding light on their potential therapeutic applications in retinal degenerative diseases. By elucidating the molecular underpinnings of erucamide’s neurotrophic effects, this study may advance therapeutic strategies that preserve vision and improve quality of life for patients affected by these devastating conditions worldwide.

Methods

Animal studies

Animals were used in strict accordance with ethical guidelines of the Scripps Research Institute (Scripps Research IACUC protocol no. 08-0045). RCS rats were obtained from M. LaVail, UCSF. rd10 mice (B6. CXB1-Pde6brd10/J), WT mice (C57BL/6J) and RhoP23H mice (B6.129S6(Cg)-Rhotm1.1Kpal/J) were obtained from the Jackson Labs. All animals were housed under a 12-h light–12-h dark cycle at an ambient temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and 45–65% relative humidity, with ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and water. All intravitreal injections were performed under anesthesia to ensure animal welfare and procedural precision. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg per kg body weight) and xylazine (10 mg per kg body weight). For neonatal mice (<P14) with unopened eyes, the eyelids were carefully separated using blunt forceps to create a small fissure along the natural eyelid margin. To establish an entry site, a 0.5-ml 31-gauge insulin syringe (Sol-Millennium, 1653151B-PK) was first inserted and withdrawn at the limbus, followed by injection using a 5-μl Hamilton syringe fitted with a 33-gauge, 0.5-inch sharp needle (point style 4; Hamilton, 7803-15). The needle was advanced toward the center of the vitreous cavity while avoiding contact with the lens. A total of 0.5 μl of solution was injected into the vitreous cavity for mice younger than P16, and 1 μl for mice older than P16, using minimal, constant pressure over 10 s. The needle was maintained in position for an additional 10 s to minimize reflux, then withdrawn slowly over another 10 s (ref. 52). Success injections were verified by the absence of leakage and maintenance of ocular integrity under a dissecting microscope. Both male and female mice were included in all experimental groups, and no sex-dependent differences were observed in their responses to Eru treatment.

For C18-pSi and Eru-C18-pSi injections in rd10, WT and RhoP23H mice, bilateral injections were performed. Within the same littermate, pups were randomized to control (C18-pSi) or treatment (Eru-C18-pSi) groups.

For BDPErucamide injection, bilateral injections were performed for C18-pSi and BDPEru-C18-pSi groups as the original Eru-C18-pSi design.

For in vivo knockdown experiments, all littermate mice received intravitreal injections of AAV2 viruses. The left eye was injected with an AAV2 carrying a scrambled shRNA control (AAV2-Scr, 2.02 × 1012 genome copies (GCs) per ml), while the right eye received an AAV2 expressing shRNA targeting TMEM19 (AAV2-shTMEM19, 2.14 × 1012 GCs per ml). Five days after the injection, mice were randomly assigned to two treatment groups and bilaterally injected with either C18-pSi or Eru-C18-pSi nanoparticles. The shRNA sequences used were as follows: Scr-shRNA, 5′-CAACAAGATGAAGAGCACC-3′; shTMEM19, 5′-ACGTGCAAAAGGTATATAAAG-3′. AAV2 vectors and other custom reagents are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

For in vivo neutralizing antibody inhibition experiments, the following antibodies were bilaterally co-injected with either C18-pSi or Eru-C18-pSi in rd10 mice (vendor, catalog number and concentration indicated): TNF (R&D systems, MAB410, 250 ng μl−1), Rat IgG1 Isotype Control (control for TNF, R&D systems, MAB005, 250 ng μl−1), FGF-2 (05-117, Sigma-Aldrich, 1 μg μl−1), Mouse IgG1 Isotype Control (control for FGF, R&D systems, MAB002, 1 μg μl−1), VEGF164 (R&D systems, AF-493-NA, 200 ng μl−1) and Goat IgG Control (control for VEGF164, R&D systems, AB-108-C, 200 ng μl−1).

Ocular metabolite extraction and sample storage

Metabolite extractions were performed using either whole eyes (for RCS and WT rats) or isolated retinas (for rd10 and WT mice, and human donor samples), as specified below. For RCS and WT rat samples, rodents were euthanized, and whole eyes were immediately enucleated, rinsed in ice-cold PBS, snap-frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80 °C until extraction. For rd10 and WT mouse samples, eyes were enucleated and placed in PBS on ice. Under a dissection microscope, the retina was carefully separated from the rest of the eyeball and immediately processed for extraction. For human donor samples, peripheral retina from postmortem eyes was dissected and processed similarly. Tissue samples were transferred into 2-ml glass homogenizers (Wheaton) and homogenized in a cold extraction solvent consisting of methanol–chloroform–water–acetic acid (60:25:14:1, vol/vol), a fully miscible mixture optimized for broad-spectrum metabolite recovery. The initial homogenization lasted 30 s, followed by the addition of 200 μl chilled extraction solvent (4 °C) and further homogenization for 90 s. This was followed by two additional homogenization cycles using 600 μl and 400 μl extraction solvent, respectively. After each cycle, the homogenizer was rinsed thoroughly to minimize sample loss. The combined homogenate (~1,200 μl total, including eye fluids) was transferred to a pre-chilled microcentrifuge tube and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 1 min. All homogenates were kept on dry ice during processing. After thawing on ice (4 °C), samples were centrifuged at 16,000g for 10–15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and optionally stored overnight at −20 °C to allow further precipitation. Samples were then dried using a SpeedVac concentrator without heating. The dried extracts were reconstituted in acetonitrile–water (1:0.8, vol/vol) containing 5% methanol, 5% chloroform and 0.1% formic acid. Samples were vortexed vigorously or sonicated for 1 min, incubated at 4 °C for ~1 h and centrifuged again to remove debris. Supernatants were transferred to LC–MS vials for metabolomic analysis.

LC–MS

For untargeted metabolites in RCS and WT rats, analyses were performed using a 1260 HPLC system (Agilent Technologies) coupled to a 6538 UHD Accurate-Mass Q-TOF (Agilent Technologies) operated in positive (ESI+) and negative (ESI−) ionization modes. Standards or eye extracts were separated using a Waters Xbridge C18, 3.5 mm, 135 A, 150 mm × 1.0 mm; that is, when the instrument was operated in positive ionization mode, the solvent system was A = 0.1% formic acid in water, and B = 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient elution starts with 100% A for injection at 0 min, increases to 80% A at 5 min and reaches 0% A at 43 min, then decreases B from 100% to 98% from 43 min to 55 min, then drops to 5% at 60 min. The injection volume was 4 μl. ESI conditions included: gas temperature, 325 °C; drying gas, 11 l min−1; nebulizer, 30 psig; fragmentor, 124 V; and skimmer, 65 V. The instrument was set to acquire over the m/z range of 25–1,500, an acquisition rate of 1.3 spectra per second. MS/MS was performed in auto mode, and the instrument was set to acquire over the m/z range of 25–1,500, an acquisition rate of 2 spectra per second for MS, 1.5 spectra per second for MS/MS with an iso width (the width half-maximum of the quadrupole mass bandpass used during MS/MS precursor isolation) of 1.3 m/z. MS/MS scans a maximum of five precursor ions at the same time. It stops after three scans for the same ions, then released after 0.4 min. The cycle time is 3.9 s. For erucamide detection in rd10 mice and human peripheral retina, analyses were performed on a Waters I-Class LC and a Waters SQD2 single-quadrupole MS operated in positive ESI mode. A Waters Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm) with an 85–99% B gradient lasting 2.4 min (A: 0.1% aqueous formic acid, B: 0.1% formic acid in methanol, 0.6 ml min−1 flow rate, 55 °C column temperature) was used. The product identity and relative quantification were determined by mass spectrometry (Waters Unispray, positive SIRs).

LC–MS data processing and statistics

LC–MS data from the retina extract were processed using the bioinformatics analysis software XCMS Online (https://xcmsonline.scripps.edu/). The experimental MS/MS spectral data were compared with the reference erucamide MS/MS spectra from the METLIN database (both at a collision energy of 20 eV)17,18,53,54. MS/MS spectra were manually processed by extracting ion chromatograms using Qualitative Analysis of MassHunter Workstation (Agilent Technologies). pNLC and BPC extractions were performed using MassHunter.

Preparation of pSi

Freestanding pSi flakes were purchased from Trutag, prepared using a previously developed ‘perforated etch’ method55. The pSiNPs were obtained by immersing the pSi flakes in ethanol in a sealed vial, which was then immersed in an ultrasonication bath (Ultrasonic bath, 97043-960, VWR International) and subjected to ultrasonic fracture conditions for 24 h. The nanoparticle suspension was centrifuged at 1,500g for 20 min to remove large particles at the bottom of the tubes. The nanoparticle suspensions were then centrifuged at high speed (10,000g, 20 min) to pelletize the nanoparticles. The supernatant was discarded to eliminate the smallest nanoparticle fragments that remained in the suspension, and the pellet was resuspended in fresh ethanol. The high-speed centrifugation/resuspension procedure was carried out two more times to thoroughly wash the nanoparticles. The resulting pSiNPs displayed an average hydrodynamic size of ~170 nm.

Surface modification of pSiNPs

The collected pSiNPs were washed and centrifuged (15,000g, 15 min) twice with toluene to remove residual ethanol. The pSiNPs were then resuspended in toluene and transferred into a glass vial, and n-octadecylsilane was added. The final concentration of pSiNPs and n-octadecylsilane was 3 mg ml−1 and 0.6 mg ml−1, respectively. A reaction vessel containing the pSiNPs and n-octadecylsilane was heated in a 95 °C oil bath for 24 h with magnetic stirring. The mixture was sonicated occasionally during the reaction to ensure full suspension of the nanoparticles. The particles were subsequently washed and centrifuged three times with toluene and three times with ethanol and finally resuspended in 100% ethanol for storage.

Characterization of pSiNPs

Hydrodynamic diameter (size) and the ζ-potential (or surface charge) measurements were obtained on a Zetasizer, Zs90 (Malvern Instruments). All pSiNP, C18-pSiNP and Eru-C18-pSiNP size measurements and ζ-potential measurements were measured with particles dispersed in deionized water. For hydrodynamic size measurements, 400 μl of 10 µg ml−1 of particles were used while for ζ-potential, 800 μl of 10 µg ml−1 of particles were used. The FTIR spectra of the particles were obtained with a Thermo Scientific Nicolet 6700 FTIR instrument fitted with a Smart iTR diamond ATR fixture. TEM images were obtained using a JEOL 1400 plus electron microscope (JEOL USA) operating at 80 kV and subsequently imaged with a Gatan Oneview camera (Gatan). Thermogravimetric analysis data were obtained using a TA Instruments Discovery SDT 650. Nanoparticle samples were heated from 30–950 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C min−1 in a pure oxygen atmosphere. Both weight (%) change and derivative heat flow values were assessed, and erucamide content was determined by the difference in mass loss between empty and erucamide-loaded organosilane-modified pSiNPs. Dye-loaded particle constructs were assessed using a Molecular Devices SpectraMax iD5 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader. N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms were obtained using dry nanoparticles at a temperature of 77 K using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 instrument.

Loading of erucamide to pSiNPs

A 10 mg ml−1 stock solution of erucamide was prepared by dissolving erucamide in ethanol overnight. A 3.4 mg ml−1 sample of C18-pSiNPs in ethanol was centrifuged (15,000g, 10 min) to remove free ethanol and the particles were re-dispersed in 1 ml of the 10 mg ml−1 erucamide stock solution. The particles were then subsequently allowed to mix at 20 °C overnight. The particles were then centrifuged (15,000g, 10 min) and washed three times with PBS to remove any unbound erucamide and were resuspended in PBS or media and were used immediately after resuspension.

Cell culture

The HMC3 cell line (CRL-3304) was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured with Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM, 30-2003, ATCC). The HEK293T cell line (CRL-3216) was purchased from the ATCC and cultured with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, 30-2002, ATCC). For transfection with mTMEM19-Flag plasmid, HEK293T cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells per cm2 in 2 ml complete medium and grown for 24 h until 80% confluent. Transfection complexes of plasmid (Mouse TMEM19 Gene ORF cDNA clone expression plasmid, C-Flag tag, SinoBiological, MG55126-NF) and Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, 11668030) were formed at room temperature in serum-free medium before drop-wise addition to the cells, followed by incubation for 5 h and replacement with complete medium for 48 h. IPS cell MPCs were derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells from a female donor with informed consent. The cell reprogramming was performed by the Harvard iPS core facility utilizing a Sendai virus for reprogramming factor delivery. iPS cells were maintained on Cultrex (R&D systems)-coated plates immersed with mTesR+ medium (STEMCELL Technologies). The cells were passaged every 3 days around 80% confluence. Colonies containing clearly visible differentiated cells were marked and manually removed before any subsequent passaging. All MPCs were generated using previously described methods34,35. Primary human umbilical vein endothelial cells (PCS-100-010) were purchased from the ATCC and cultured with Vascular Cell Basal Medium (PCS-100-030) with Endothelial Cell Growth Kit-VEGF (PCS-100-041). Human choroidal endothelial cells (36052-03) were purchased from Celprogen and cultured with Human Choroidal Endothelial Cells Complete Growth Media with Serum (M36052-03S). Human retinal endothelial primary cells (36205-33) were purchased from Celprogen and cultured with Human Retinal Endothelial Cell Culture Media with Serum (M36205-33S). All cells were obtained with verified normal karyotype and were free from contamination.

PAL method

HMC3 cells and HEK293T cells were treated with vehicle (ethanol) or a lipid probe for 3 h in the incubator. HMC3 cells were then UV irradiated (10 min) and lysed, and lipid probe-labeled proteins were conjugated to biotin azide by CuAAC reaction. The biotin-labeled proteins were then enriched with streptavidin beads and trypsin digested, and the digested peptides were labeled with TMT, followed by combination, HPLC fractionation and LC–MS/MS analysis36. HEK293T cells were then UV irradiated then UV irradiated and lysed, and lipid probe-labeled proteins were conjugated to Rhodamine by CuAAC reaction. The labeled proteins were then used for visualizing by western blot.

Western blotting

HMC3 cells and HEK293T cells were treated with vehicle (ethanol) or a lipid probe for 3 h in the incubator. Cells were then UV irradiated and lysed, and lipid probe-labeled proteins were conjugated to Rhodamine dye by CuAAC reaction. Around 25 μl samples were fractionated on 10% Tris-Glycin SDS–PAGE gel at 275 V (constant) for 240 min, followed by standard western blotting analysis. The following antibodies were used for western blotting: ANTI-FLAG M2 antibody, Mouse monoclonal (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804), horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse NA9310, GE, 1:5,000 dilution).

Retinal myeloid cell isolation by flow cytometry

A postnatal neural dissociation kit (Miltenyi, 130-092-628) was used to prepare a single-cell suspension from mouse retinas. Cells were centrifuged at 150g for 5 min at 4 °C. The digested tissue was resuspended in 100 μl of 4% FBS in PBS containing an FITC antibody to CD11b (1:100 dilution; BioLegend, 101206) and incubated for 20 min on ice. The cells were washed and suspended with 1 ml of 4% FBS/PBS containing DAPI (1:2,000 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 62248). Labeled retinal myeloid cells (CD11b positive) were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (MoFlo Astrios EQ; Beckman Coulter) at the Scripps Flow Cytometry Core Facility. Sorted cells were resuspended in 350 μl of RLT buffer from RNeasy Micro Kit (QIAGEN) and stored at −80 °C.

RNA isolation, real-time PCR and RNA-seq

For whole retina and culture cells, single retinas were collected in 500 μl of TRIzol and total RNA was isolated using a PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Seven hundred and fifty nanograms of RNA was used for RT–qPCR using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For flow-sorted cells, total RNA was isolated from sorted cells using the RNeasy Micro Kit (QIAGEN) and reverse transcribed using Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit for RT–qPCR (Thermo Scientific). qPCR was performed using Power-up SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and primers on a Quantstudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). β-actin (Actb) was used as the reference gene for all experiments. Levels of mRNA expression were normalized to those in controls as determined using the comparative CT (ΔΔCT) method. RNA samples were used for RNA-seq in Scripps Genetic Cores. Quality control of raw sequencing data was performed using FastQC and MultiQC56. Raw reads were aligned to the reference genome using RSubread57. Gene expression levels were quantified using featureCounts. Post-alignment quality control included assessing the distribution of reads across genomic features (for example, exons, introns) and checking for potential batch effects using principal component analysis. Differential expression analysis was carried out using the limma-voom pipeline1. Data were filtered and normalization was performed using the trimmed mean of M-values method. Linear modeling and empirical Bayes moderation were applied to identify differentially expressed genes. Genes with a false discovery rate-adjusted P value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Overrepresentation analysis and functional enrichment analysis were performed with the clusterProfiler58 package using Gene Ontology and KEGG to define biologically relevant pathways and enrichment analysis was conducted.

Cryosection and immunohistochemistry

For cryosections, eyecups were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 4 h. Under a dissecting microscope, a cut was carefully made around the cornea, and the eyecups were transferred to 20% sucrose in PBS at 4 °C for overnight. Samples were embedded in OCT compound (Tissue-Tek, 4566) with the eyecup oriented dorsal side up and the optic nerve positioned at the bottom of the cryomold, then snap-frozen in chilled isopentane on dry ice. Once frozen solid, blocks were wrapped and stored at –80 °C until sectioning. For cryosections, blocks were equilibrated in the cryostat chamber (–20 °C) for 1 h, mounted dorsal side down (optic nerve at the bottom), and sectioned at a thickness of 10 µm. Sections were air-dried and processed for immunohistochemistry using standard protocols59. Briefly, slides were permeabilized and blocked in 2% BSA in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The following primary antibodies were used: Recoverin (rabbit; Millipore, AB5585; 1:500 dilution), isolectin GS-IB4 (Griffonia simplicifolia, Alexa Fluor 568 conjugated; Thermo Fisher Scientific, I21412; 1:1,000 dilution). Secondary antibodies were obtained from Life Technologies, and the DAB staining kit was purchased from Vector Laboratories.

Retinal whole-mount staining

Retinal whole mounts were prepared as previously described with minor modifications52. Eyecups were fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 1 h, then transferred to individual wells of a 48-well plate containing PBS. The cornea was carefully removed using fine forceps and scissors, followed by gentle separation of the sclera and choroid from the retina, avoiding direct contact with the retinal tissue to prevent damage. The lens and hyaloid vasculature were removed, and the isolated retina was used for immunohistochemistry. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Antibodies of collagen IV (Millipore, AB756P; 1:200 dilution) and CD11b (BD Biosciences, 550285; 1:500 dilution) were used. The following day, tissues were washed and incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h and then DAPI staining at room temperature. Following final PBS washes, each retina was transferred to a six-well Teflon-coated slide with 8-mm diameter wells (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 63423-08), positioning the optic nerve head at the center. Four to six evenly spaced radial incisions were made from the periphery toward the center to flatten the retina into a ‘flower-like’ configuration. Retinal petals were gently unfolded and flattened with the vitreous surface facing upward. Excess buffer was removed, and a drop of SlowFade mounting medium (Molecular Probes) was added before applying a coverslip. Slides were imaged immediately or stored at 4 °C in the dark until confocal microscopy.

Choroid flat-mount staining

Eyecups were fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 1 h and transferred to individual wells of a 48-well plate containing PBS. The cornea was removed with fine forceps and scissors, followed by careful separation of the RPE–choroid–sclera complex from the rest of the eyeball. To remove RPE cells, the remaining tissue was incubated in freshly prepared 1% EDTA in PBS for 2 h at room temperature. For melanin bleaching, tissues were incubated in 10% hydrogen peroxide (Sigma, H1009) diluted in PBS at 55 °C for 90 min in a water bath. After bleaching, tissues were washed three times in PBS and twice in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100. Immunohistochemistry staining was then performed using the same procedure as described for retinal whole mounts.

Confocal microscopy and quantification

All images were acquired using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (LSM710, Carl Zeiss) and processed with ZEN 2010 software (Carl Zeiss) and ImageJ/Fiji (National Institutes of Health or NIH). For retinal thickness analysis, recoverin- and DAPI-labeled cryosections were imaged at high resolution. Thickness was measured at eight equidistant radial points extending from the optic nerve toward the peripheral retina on both the nasal and temporal sides. Mean values were calculated for each eye. Eyes injected with erucamide or vehicle (n ≥ 5 per group) were compared. The full procedure was repeated in four independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA, and data were plotted with GraphPad Prism 10. For vascular density analysis and CD11b+ cell quantification, z-stacks of three-channel confocal images (collagen IV, CD11b and DAPI) were acquired at 5-μm step intervals across the full retinal thickness to capture the superficial, intermediate and deep plexus layers using retinal whole-mount preparations. Maximum intensity projections were generated using ImageJ/Fiji. Images were split into individual grayscale channels and thresholded to isolate vascular structures. Vascular density was calculated as the percentage of vessel-covered area relative to the total selected retinal area using the ‘Threshold’ and ‘Vascular density’ plugins in ImageJ/Fiji. CD11b+ cells were quantified from merged three-channel images using the ‘Cell Counter’ plugin in ImageJ/Fiji. Cell counts were performed independently in each vascular layer as well as the ONL.

Ganzfeld electroretinography

Mice were adapted to dark overnight, and all procedures were conducted under red light60. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg per kg body weight) and xylazine (10 mg per kg body weight), and pupils were dilated with 1% tropicamide and 2.5% phenylephrine. Five minutes after dilation, mice were placed on a temperature-controlled heating pad to maintain body temperature throughout the recording. Silver needle electrodes were positioned as the reference (central joules) and ground (skin above the tail), and full-field ERG responses were recorded from both eyes using active contact lens electrodes (Mayo). Recordings were obtained using an electronically controlled Ganzfeld dome (Espion E2 with Colordome; Diagnosys). Scotopic responses were recorded in darkness using a series of white flashes at increasing intensities (0.01, 25 and 50 cd s/m2). After 5 min of light adaptation under a 30 cd/m2 background, photopic responses were recorded using a 25 cd s/m2 flash, followed by flicker responses at 10 Hz (25 cd s/m2) stimuli. ERG signals were filtered at 0.3–300 Hz, and signal averaging was applied to improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

Probe synthesis

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Unless otherwise specified, data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation or standard error of the mean. Comparisons between two groups were made using two-tailed unpaired t-tests. For two-factor experimental designs, two-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s LSD post hoc test was applied. For multiple-group comparisons, the two-stage, step-up method (Benjamini, Krieger and Yekutieli) was used for controlling the false discovery rate in multiple unpaired t-tests. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data points represent individual biological replicates, and no statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. All experiments were repeated independently at least three times.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. For in vivo experiments using rd10, WT C57BL/6J and RhoP23H mice, animals within the same litter were randomly assigned to treatment groups (C18-pSi vehicle or Eru-C18-pSi). For the AAV2 knockdown experiments, each mouse received contralateral eye injections (AAV2-Scr in the left eye, AAV2-shTMEM19 in the right eye), with subsequent bilateral assignment to vehicle or erucamide treatment randomized within litters. Investigators were not blinded to group allocation during in vivo experiments; however, quantification of retinal layer thickness, vascular density, CD11b+ cell counts and ERG analysis were performed using standardized automated or semiautomated methods to minimize subjective bias. For in vitro experiments (iPS cell-MPC treatments, PAL assays, qPCR), treatments were performed in parallel under identical conditions. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times with similar results, except where otherwise stated in the figure legends.
