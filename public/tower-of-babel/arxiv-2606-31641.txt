Invariant λ \lambda -translators in the Heisenberg group Mathematics Subject Classification: Keywords :


Abstract
We study oriented surfaces in the Heisenberg space Nil 3 \mathrm{Nil}_{3} whose mean curvature H H at each point is H = ⟨ N , ∂ z ⟩ + λ H=\langle N,\partial_{z}\rangle+\lambda , where N N is the unit normal, ∂ z \partial_{z} is the vertical Killing vector field and λ ∈ ℝ \lambda\in\mathbb{R} . These surfaces are known as λ \lambda -translators and generalize, among others, minimal and positive constant mean curvature surfaces, and also translating solitons of the mean curvature flow. The objective in this paper is to classify λ \lambda -translators invariant by the following one-parameter groups of isometries of Nil 3 \mathrm{Nil}_{3} : left-translations, rotations and helicoidal motions.



Antonio Bueno

1  Introduction

Let
M

(\mathcal{M},g)
and
\{\Psi_{t}\}
a smooth variation of

\Psi_{t}
evolves by mean curvature flow if

\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_{t}}{\partial t}(p,t)\right)^{\bot}=H_{t}(\Psi_{t}(p))N_{t}(\Psi_{t}(p)),

where

evolves by mean curvature flow under a one-parameter group of ambient isometries. In such a case, if

is the Killing vector field associated to the group of isometries, then

For the particular but important case that
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{3}

[
19
,
20

[

Definition 1.1

Given

The Heisenberg group has a 4-dimensional isometry group, generated by three linearly independent left-translations and rotations about a vertical geodesic. The left-translations along a vertical geodesic will be referred as

\lambda

For each

\lambda

2  Preliminaries

The Heisenberg space
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
is defined as
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with usual coordinates
(x,y,z)
, endowed with the metric

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle=dx^{2}+dy^{2}+(dz+\tau(ydx-xdy))^{2}.

(2.1)

This space is a Lie group when we define the inner product

(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})*(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2})=(x_{1}+y_{1},x_{2}+y_{2},z_{1}+z_{2}+\tau(x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1})).

For each
p_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})\in\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
we define the
left-translation
based on
p_{0}
as

L_{p_{0}}(x,y,z)=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})*(x,y,z).

The left-translations are isometries and in particular
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
is a homogeneous space. If
e_{1}=\partial_{x},\ e_{2}=\partial_{y},\ e_{3}=\partial_{z}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then

E_{1}=\partial_{x}-\tau y\partial_{z},\quad E_{2}=\partial_{y}+\tau x\partial_{z},\quad E_{3}=\partial_{z}

(2.2)

are the only orthonormal (for the metric (
2.1
)), left-invariant vector fields satisfying
(E_{k})_{\textbf{0}}=e_{k}
, where
\textbf{0}=(0,0,0)
. The left-translations and the vector fields
E_{k}
are related as follows. The flow of isometries of the Killing vector field
E_{3}
is the one-parameter group
t\mapsto L_{(0,0,t)}
, which are commonly known as the
vertical translations
in the
\partial_{z}
-direction and will be denoted by
V_{t}
. The flow of isometries of
E_{1}
is the one-parameter group
t\mapsto L_{(t,0,0)}
while the one of
E_{2}
is
t\mapsto L_{(0,t,0)}
. At this point, is worth-mentioning the remaining isometries of
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
. Besides the left-translations, we find the rotations about a vertical geodesic. If such a geodesic is the one passing through
0
, then the rotations are given by usual Euclidean rotations

\rho_{t}:\mathrm{Nil}_{3}\to\mathrm{Nil}_{3},\qquad\rho_{t}\left(\begin{matrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\cos t&-\sin t&0\\
\sin t&\cos t&0\\
0&0&1\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{matrix}\right).

For instance, the flow of
E_{1}
and of
E_{2}
agree after a rotation, hence the left translations
L_{(t,0,0)}
and
L_{(0,t,0)}
differ by a rotation.

Since
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
is not a space form and there are not homogeneous 3-manifolds with isometry group of dimension five, the aforementioned isometries describe the isometry group of
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
, being four-dimensional. Note that there are no reflection-type isometries and as a paramount consequence there is no an analog to the famous Alexandrov reflection technique. Finally, given a line
t\mapsto(tx_{0},ty_{0},z_{0})
, the rotation of angle
\pi
about this line is also an isometry.

2.1  The mean curvature equation

We compute an expression for the mean curvature of an immersed surface. Given
\psi=\psi(s,t):M\to\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
a parametric surface, we express the basic vector fields
\psi_{s},\psi_{t}
in the basis
\{E_{k}\}
,

\psi_{s}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}a_{k}(s,t)E_{k}(\psi(s,t)),\qquad\psi_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}b_{k}(s,t)E_{k}(\psi(s,t)).

We drop the dependence on the variables
(s,t)
in order to clarify the notation. The coefficients of the first fundamental form are
E=\langle\psi_{s},\psi_{s}\rangle,\ F=\langle\psi_{s},\psi_{t}\rangle
and
G=\langle\psi_{t},\psi_{t}\rangle
. A vector field orthogonal to
T_{\psi}M
is

\eta=(a_{2}b_{3}-a_{3}b_{2})E_{1}(\psi)+(a_{3}b_{1}-a_{1}b_{3})E_{2}(\psi)+(a_{1}b_{2}-a_{2}b_{1})E_{3}(\psi),

hence a unit normal vector field is
N=\eta/\sqrt{\langle\eta,\eta\rangle}
. The covariant derivatives are

\displaystyle\nabla_{\psi_{s}}\psi_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\partial a_{k}}{\partial s}E_{k}(\psi)+a_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{j}\nabla_{E_{j}(\psi)}E_{k}(\psi),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\psi_{s}}\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\partial b_{k}}{\partial s}E_{k}(\psi)+b_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{j}\nabla_{E_{j}(\psi)}E_{k}(\psi),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\psi_{t}}\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\partial b_{k}}{\partial t}E_{k}(\psi)+b_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{3}b_{j}\nabla_{E_{j}(\psi)}E_{k}(\psi),

thus the coefficients of the second fundamental form are

e=\langle\nabla_{\psi_{s}}\psi_{s},N\rangle,\quad f=\langle\nabla_{\psi_{s}}\psi_{t},N\rangle,\quad g=\langle\nabla_{\psi_{t}}\psi_{t},N\rangle.

For the computation of the covariant derivatives, we use that the Levi-Civita connection on the global basis (
2.2
) is

\displaystyle\nabla_{E_{1}}E_{1}=0,

\displaystyle\nabla_{E_{2}}E_{1}=-\tau E_{3},

\displaystyle\nabla_{E_{3}}E_{1}=-\tau E_{2},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\nabla_{E_{1}}E_{2}=\tau E_{3},

\displaystyle\nabla_{E_{2}}E_{2}=0,

\displaystyle\nabla_{E_{3}}E_{2}=\tau E_{1},

\displaystyle\nabla_{E_{1}}E_{3}=-\tau E_{2},

\displaystyle\nabla_{E_{2}}E_{3}=\tau E_{1},

\displaystyle\nabla_{E_{3}}E_{3}=0.

Finally, the mean curvature is

2H=\frac{Eg-2Ff+eG}{EG-F^{2}}.

3
\lambda
-grim reapers

We begin by investigating
\lambda
-translators that are invariant under left-translations, which are the analogs of cylindrical surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Recall that a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is cylindrical if it is invariant by a one-parameter group of translations. If such translations have direction
\vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then a cylindrical surface can be parametrized as
(s,t)\mapsto\alpha(s)+t\vec{v}
, where
\alpha(s)
is a planar curve in some plane
\Pi
. In general,
\vec{v}
and
\Pi
are commonly chosen to be orthogonal, but there is no a priori relation between both of them. If
\vec{v}
and
\Pi
are not orthogonal, the cylindrical surface is said to be
tilted
.

Cylindrical translators in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are fully classified: up to a change of coordinates they are the grim-reaper cylinder, whose base curve in the
xz
-plane is the graph
z=-\log\cos x
, its tiltings and any vertical plane. For
\lambda\neq 0
a classification of cylindrical
\lambda
-translators in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
was achieved independently in
[
12
,
25
]
, where explicit parametrizations were obtained, and in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

[
7
,
23
]
. In the latter, such cylindrical surfaces were also referred to as grim reapers. A way to generate cylindrical surfaces in
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
is by means of the submersion

\pi:\mathrm{Nil}_{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad\pi(x,y,z)=(x,y),

whose fibers, i.e.
\pi^{-1}(q),\ q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are geodesics everywhere tangent to the vector field
\partial_{z}
. Given a curve
\alpha\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the
vertical cylinder over
\alpha
is defined as
C_{\alpha}=\pi^{-1}(\alpha)
, which is everywhere tangent to
\partial_{z}
and whose mean curvature is
H=\kappa_{\alpha}/2
, where
\kappa_{\alpha}
is the curvature of
\alpha
. Conversely, any surface invariant under vertical translations must be a vertical cylinder
\pi^{-1}(\alpha)
, for some curve
\alpha
. In particular, the unit normal of any vertical cylinder is everywhere orthogonal to
\partial_{z}
. As a straightforward consequence, we deduce the following.

Proposition 3.1

A
\lambda
-translator invariant by vertical translations is a vertical cylinder over a circle of constant curvature
\kappa_{\alpha}=2\lambda
.

Note that
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be identified with the minimal plane
z=0
(although not isometrically) and define analogously
C_{\alpha}=V_{t}(\alpha),\ t\in\mathbb{R}
. Under this analogy, we give the following definition.

Definition 3.2

A
\lambda
-grim reaper is a surface in
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
invariant by a one-parameter group of non-vertical left-translations.

The situation here changes as there are no totally geodesic surfaces in
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
that are analogous to planes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Nonetheless, we consider a
\lambda
-grim reaper as the image under a left-translation of a planar curve
\alpha(s)
contained in some vertical plane. After a rotation, we assume that
\alpha(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
and translate
\alpha(s)
under the one-parameter group of left-translations
t\mapsto L_{(0,t,ct)}
, where the parameter
c\geq 0
denotes the tilting. Only if
c>0
we explicitly refer as a tilted
\lambda
-grim reaper. Thus, a parametrization of a
\lambda
-grim reaper is

\psi(s,t)=L_{(0,t,ct)}(\alpha(s))=(x(s),t,-\tau tx(s)+z(s)+ct).

(3.1)

We drop the dependence on the variable
s
unless explicitly needed. The global orthonormal basis (
2.2
) on
\psi
is

E_{1}(\psi)=(1,0,-\tau t),\quad E_{2}(\psi)=(0,1,\tau x),\quad E_{3}=(0,0,1),

and the vector fields
\psi_{s},\psi_{t}
have coordinates in
E_{k}

\psi_{s}=x^{\prime}E_{1}(\psi)+z^{\prime}E_{3}(\psi),\quad\psi_{t}=E_{2}(\psi)+(c-2\tau x)E_{3}(\psi).

Now we compute the covariant derivatives using (
2.3
). For the sake of clarity, we do explicitly the first one. Recall that
\psi_{s}=x^{\prime}E_{1}(\psi)+z^{\prime}E_{3}(\psi)
, hence
a_{1}=x^{\prime},a_{2}=0
and
a_{3}=z^{\prime}
. We also drop the dependence of
E_{k}
on
\psi
.

\displaystyle\nabla_{\psi_{s}}\psi_{s}=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial a_{1}}{\partial s}E_{1}+a_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{j}\nabla_{E_{j}}E_{1}+\frac{\partial a_{3}}{\partial s}E_{3}+a_{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{j}\nabla_{E_{j}}E_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}E_{1}-2\tau x^{\prime}z^{\prime}E_{2}+z^{\prime\prime}E_{3}=(x^{\prime\prime},-2\tau x^{\prime}z^{\prime},-\tau(2\tau xx^{\prime}x^{\prime}+tx^{\prime\prime})+z^{\prime\prime}).

An analog computation shows

\nabla_{\psi_{s}}\psi_{t}=\tau z^{\prime}E_{1}+\tau(2\tau x-c)x^{\prime}E_{2}-\tau x^{\prime}E_{3},\qquad\nabla_{\psi_{t}}\psi_{t}=2\tau(c-2\tau x)E_{1}.

The unit normal is

N=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1+(c-2\tau x)^{2})x^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}}}\left(-z^{\prime}E_{1}-(c-2\tau x)x^{\prime}E_{2}+x^{\prime}E_{3}\right),

whose denominator,
D
, is the square root of the determinant of the first fundamental form of
\psi
. The coefficients of the second fundamental form are

e=\frac{z^{\prime}(2\tau(c-2\tau x)x^{\prime 2}-x^{\prime\prime})+x^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime}}{D},\quad f=\frac{\tau(-1+(c-2\tau x)^{2})x^{\prime 2}-\tau z^{\prime 2}}{D},\quad g=\frac{-2\tau(c-2\tau x)z^{\prime}}{D}.

Thus, the mean curvature of a
\lambda
-grim reaper in terms of the coordinates of the base curve is

2H=\frac{z^{\prime}(-x^{\prime\prime}-(c-2\tau x)(-2\tau x^{\prime 2}+(c-2\tau x)x^{\prime\prime}))+(1+c^{2}+4\tau x(-c+\tau x))x^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime}}{((1+(c-2\tau x)^{2})x^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2})^{3/2}}.

From the expression of the determinant of the first fundamental form, we consider the arc-length parameter

x^{\prime}=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1+(c-2\tau x)^{2}}},\qquad z^{\prime}=\sin\theta,

where
\theta
is a smooth function. With this arc-length parameter, the mean curvature and angle function of
\psi
are

2H=\sqrt{1+(c-2\tau x)^{2}}\theta^{\prime},\qquad\langle N,E_{3}\rangle=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1+(c-2\tau x)^{2}}},

and from the
\lambda
-translator equation we conclude

\theta^{\prime}=\frac{2}{1+(c-2\tau x)^{2}}\left(\cos\theta+\lambda\sqrt{1+(c-2\tau x)^{2}}\right).

We have proved that the coordinates of the base curve of a
\lambda
-grim reaper satisfy the ODE system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 5.69046ptx^{\prime}=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1+(c-2\tau x)^{2}}},\\
\vskip 5.69046ptz^{\prime}=\sin\theta,\\
\theta^{\prime}=\dfrac{2}{1+(c-2\tau x)^{2}}\left(\cos\theta+\lambda\sqrt{1+(c-2\tau x)^{2}}\right).\end{array}\right.

Conversely, for given initial conditions
(x_{0},z_{0},\theta_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, any solution to this initial value problem gives rise to a planar curve in the
xz
-plane which is the generating curve of a
\lambda

Finally, note that the second function of this system is dependent on the first and the third, due to the invariance of both the mean curvature and angle function of a surface moving under vertical translations. Hence we study the solutions of this system by means of the first and the third equations.

For each

4  Rotational
\lambda

In this section we focus on
\lambda
-translators invariant under a one-parameter group of rotations. Given a planar curve
\alpha(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
, the rotational surface with

\alpha(s)
is parametrized by

\psi(s,t)=(x(s)\cos t,x(s)\sin t,z(s)).

A unit normal vector field is

N=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1+\tau^{2}x^{2})x^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}}}\left(-(\tau\sin txx^{\prime}+\cos tz^{\prime})E_{1}+(\tau\cos txx^{\prime}-\sin tz^{\prime})E_{2}+x^{\prime}E_{3}\right),

and the covariant derivatives are

\displaystyle\nabla_{\psi_{s}}\psi_{s}

\displaystyle=(2\tau\sin tx^{\prime}z^{\prime}+\cos tx^{\prime\prime})E_{1}+(-2\tau\cos tx^{\prime}z^{\prime}+\sin tx^{\prime\prime})E_{2}+z^{\prime\prime}E_{3},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\psi_{s}}\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=(-\sin t(1+\tau^{2}x^{2})x^{\prime}+\tau\cos txz^{\prime})E_{1}+(\cos t(1+\tau^{2}x^{2})x^{\prime 2}+\tau\sin txz^{\prime})E_{2}-\tau xx^{\prime}E_{3},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\psi_{t}}\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=-\cos tx(1+2\tau^{2}x^{2})E_{1}-\sin tx(1+2\tau^{2}x^{2})E_{2},

hence we compute the coefficients of the second fundamental form

e=\frac{-z^{\prime}(2\tau^{2}xx^{\prime 2}+x^{\prime\prime})+x^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{(1+\tau^{2}x^{2})x^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}}},\quad f=\frac{\tau x(\tau^{2}x^{2}x^{\prime 2}-z^{\prime 2})}{\sqrt{(1+\tau^{2}x^{2})x^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}}},\quad g=\frac{x(1+2\tau^{2}x^{2})z^{\prime}}{\sqrt{(1+\tau^{2}x^{2})x^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}}}.

Similarly to the case of
\lambda
-grim reapers, we consider the arc-length parameter

x^{\prime}=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}x^{2}}},\qquad z^{\prime}=\sin\theta,

and the mean curvature and angle function of
\psi

2H=\frac{\sin\theta}{x}+\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}x^{2}}\theta^{\prime},\qquad\langle N,E_{3}\rangle=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}x^{2}}}.

From Eq. (
1.1
) we conclude that the coordinates of the base curve of a rotational
\lambda
-translator fulfill the following ODE system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 5.69046ptx^{\prime}=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}x^{2}}},\\
z^{\prime}=\sin\theta,\\
\theta^{\prime}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}x^{2}}}\left(2\left(\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}x^{2}}}+\lambda\right)-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{x}\right).\end{array}\right.

(4.1)

Conversely, given
(x_{0},z_{0},\theta_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

4.2  The behavior of the orbits

Once we have determined the structure of the phase plane and described its monotonicity regions, we focus on the motion and behavior of the orbits in it. The next result proves that an orbit cannot stay forever in a monotonicity region.

Proposition 4.5

An orbit
\gamma(s)=(x(s),\theta(s))
cannot stay in a monotonicity region with
x(s)\to\infty
as
|s|\to\infty
.

Arguing by contradiction, assume that
\gamma(s)=(x(s),\theta(s))
is an orbit such that when
s\to\infty

\gamma(s)
is strictly contained in some monotonicity region with

(the case

\theta(s)\neq\pi/2+k\pi
for any

; without losing generality, assume that
\gamma(s)\subset\{\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)\}
. By assumption,
\gamma
can be expressed as a vertical graph
\theta=\theta(x)
for
x\geq x_{0}
and some
x_{0}>0
. The inverse function theorem yields

\theta^{\prime}(x)=\frac{d\theta}{dx}=\dfrac{d\theta/ds}{dx/ds}=\frac{1}{\cos\theta(x)}\left(2\left(\frac{\cos\theta(x)}{\sqrt{1+\tau^{2}x^{2}}}+\lambda\right)-\frac{\sin\theta(x)}{x}\right),

and in this region we have
\theta^{\prime}(x)>0
for any
x>x_{0}
. Since
\theta(x)
is strictly increasing and bounded from above by
\theta=\pi/2
, there exists a sequence
x_{n}\to\infty

\theta(x_{n})\to\theta_{0}\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2]
and
\theta^{\prime}(x_{n})\to 0

Next, we prove the non-existence of closed orbits in
\mathcal{R}
, and its proof follows from Bendixson-Dulac theorem.

Proposition 4.6

There do not exist closed orbits in
\mathcal{R}

Let us express Eq. (
4.2

\gamma

2.

\gamma

3.

or
\gamma

\lambda
-translators, as stated next.

Theorem 4.11

There do not exist closed rotational
\lambda
-translators.

Before proving the result, we think that few comments must be done. The existence of certain closed surfaces in
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
is a challenging task even for the widely studied case of CMC surfaces. For instance, the uniqueness of the rotational embedded CMC spheres among closed, embedded surfaces in
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
is still an outstanding open problem. In
[
4
]
, the author generalized the classification of rotational CMC surfaces to the case when the mean curvature depends on the unit normal. It was proved that for certain choices of the prescribed function, the rotational embedded prescribed mean curvature spheres constructed were not unique in the Alexandrov sense, as it was exhibited the existence of embedded tori which were even rotational. Note that rotational CMC tori do not exist in
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
in virtue of the classification in
[
35
]
.

Regarding the non-existence of closed
\lambda
-translators, in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
it is a consequence of the elliptic equation
\Delta h=2H\langle N,\partial_{z}\rangle
fulfilled by the restriction of
height function

h
to an immersed surface, and the fact that any closed surface is null-homologous in the second homology group of the ambient space, hence is the boundary of a 3-chain. Then we conclude by Stoke’s theorem; see e.g.
[
13
,
26
]
. The product structure in both spaces makes this height function readily available, but there is not an analog to this function in
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
. This has as consequence the non-existence of sharp height estimates for CMC graphs, which differs from the scenario in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the product spaces
\mathbb{M}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Although the non-existence of a general closed
\lambda
-translator with any further assumption on its symmetries or topology seems to the author an interesting, and far for being trivial, problem to address, we restrict ourselves to the non-existence of rotational ones since the main purpose of the paper is to study invariant
\lambda
-translators and in particular rotational ones. Note that any closed rotational surface in
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
must have genus 0 or 1, since
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a connected planar curve cannot introduce multiple disjoint cycles needed for adding higher genus. Thus, a rotational closed
\lambda
-translator must be either diffeomorphic to a sphere or a torus. After all the background regarding closed surfaces, we prove Thm.
4.11
.

The proof is by contradiction. Let
\alpha(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
be the base curve of a rotational closed
\lambda
-translator. Thus,
\alpha(s)
either stays at a positive distance to the rotation axis and is a closed curve (hence generating a torus), or intersects the rotation axis orthogonally (hence generating a sphere).

First, assume that
\alpha(s)
generates a torus and thus its image is a closed curve, possibly with self-intersections. Thus, there exists a minimum
T>0
such that
\alpha(s)=\alpha(s+T)
for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, both the
x
and
z
-coordinates are
T
-periodic functions. Let us denote by
\gamma(s)=(x(s),\theta(s))
to the orbit corresponding to
\alpha(s)
in a fundamental interval
[s_{0},s_{0}+T]
; the closeness of
\alpha(s)
implies that
\gamma(s)
is a periodic orbit. In case that
\alpha(s)
does not self-intersect before closing, then after a translation of
2\pi
in the
\theta
-direction we have that
\gamma(s)
lies contained in the region
\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]
. However, this is impossible due to Cor.
4.10
. If
\alpha(s)
self-intersects then it does a finite number of times and thus
\gamma(s)
is again a periodic orbit but this time lies contained in some strip
\theta\in[(2k-1)\pi,(2m+1)\pi]
, with
k<m
. In any case, we contradict again Cor.
4.10
after invoking it a finite number of times. This concludes the non-existence of rotational
\lambda
-translators with the topology of a torus.

If
\alpha(s)
generates a sphere, the proof is similar. Let be
\gamma(s)
the orbit corresponding to
\alpha(s)
and assume by contradiction that they generate a
\lambda
-translator
S_{0}
with the topology of a sphere. Let
p_{0},q_{0}\in S_{0}
the points of intersection of
S_{0}
with the rotation axis. After a reparametrization of
\alpha(s)
we assume
\alpha(0)=p_{0}
and
\alpha(s_{0})=q_{0}
with
s_{0}>0
and thus
x(s)
increases for
s>0
small enough and decreases for
s<s_{0}
close enough to
s_{0}
. In particular,

and

. This implies that the orbit
\gamma(s)
is a compact arc having as endpoints
(0,2k\pi)
and
(0,(2m+1)\pi)
, with

. We can assume, up to a discrete translation in the
\theta
-direction of
\mathcal{R}
, that
\gamma(0)=(0,0)
and
\gamma(s_{0})=(0,(2m+1)\pi)
with
m\geq 0
.

Start by assuming that
m=0
and thus
\gamma
has as endpoints
(0,0)
and
(0,\pi)
and passes through some
(x_{0},\pi/2)
with
x_{0}>0

is embedded since

for every

This proves the non-existence of closed rotational

5  Helicoidal
\lambda
-translators

In the final section of this paper, we classify helicoidal
\lambda
-translators. An helicoidal motion in
\mathrm{Nil}_{3}
of
pitch

h>0
is the one-parameter group of isometries defined as the composition of a rotation
\rho_{t}
and a vertical translation
V_{ht}
,

\varpi_{t}=\rho_{t}\circ V_{t}(x,y,z)=(x\cos t+y\sin t,x\sin t-y\cos t,z+ht).

As in the rotational case, a helicoidal surface is defined as the image of a planar curve
\alpha(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
, called the base or generating curve, by the one-parameter group of helicoidal motions
\varpi_{t}
. Thus, a parametrization is

\psi(s,t)=(x(s)\cos t,x(s)\sin t,z(s)+ht).

Although the intermediate computations will be omitted, we highlights the main features. The first fundamental form has determinant

\langle\psi_{s},\psi_{s}\rangle\langle\psi_{t},\psi_{t}\rangle-\langle\psi_{s},\psi_{t}\rangle^{2}=((h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2})x^{\prime}+x^{2}z^{\prime 2},

hence we consider the arc-length parameter

x^{\prime}=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}},\qquad z^{\prime}=\frac{\sin\theta}{x}.

With this arc-lenght parameter, the mean curvature and angle function are

2H=\frac{\sin\theta}{x}+\theta^{\prime}\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}},\qquad\langle N,E_{3}\rangle=\frac{x\cos\theta}{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}}.

Consequently, the following system is fulfilled by the coordinate curves of a helicoidal
\lambda
-translator.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 5.69046ptx^{\prime}=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}},\\
z^{\prime}=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{x},\\
\theta^{\prime}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}}\left(2\left(\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}}+\lambda\right)-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{x}\right).\end{array}\right.

5.1  The phase plane of helicoidal
\lambda
-translators

As in the previous section, we restrict to the
2D
-system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 5.69046ptx^{\prime}=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}},\\
\theta^{\prime}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}}\left(2\left(\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}}+\lambda\right)-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{x}\right),\end{array}\right.

(5.1)

and define the phase plane
\mathcal{R}
as the solutions (orbits) of Eq. (
5.1
). We study its properties and its monotonicity regions by means of the implicit curve

\Gamma=\{(x,\theta)\in\mathcal{R}\colon F(x,\theta)=0\},

where

F(x,\theta)=2x\left(\lambda+\dfrac{x\cos\theta}{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}}\right)-\sin\theta.

We see again that
e_{0}=(\frac{1}{2\lambda},\frac{\pi}{2})
is the unique equilibrium of (
5.1
), whose corresponding helicoidal surface is just the vertical CMC cylinder of radius
1/(2\lambda)
.

Although the existence of the parameter
h
complicates in substantial ways the study of the global properties of
\Gamma
, we are still able to prove its main properties. First, recall that for fixed datum
\tau,\lambda,h
, the equation
F(x,\theta)=0
does not admit as solution a sequence
(x_{n},\theta_{n})\in\Gamma
with
x_{n}\to\infty
, hence there exists
C=C(\tau,\lambda,h)>0
such that
\Gamma\subset\{0\leq x\leq C\}
. Since
F(x,\theta)=0
has no solution for
\theta\in(3\pi/2,2\pi)
, we conclude that
\Gamma
is contained in the rectangle
[0,C]\times[0,2\pi]
and since
\Gamma
is closed we deduce that
\Gamma
is compact, with finitely-many connected components. Furthermore, if
\lambda>1
then the constant
C
can be chosen to be independent of
h
.

Recall that
F(0,0)=F(0,\pi)=0
and

\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}(x,\theta)=2\left(\lambda+\frac{x(2h(h-\tau x^{2})+x^{2})\cos\theta}{((h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2})^{3/2}}\right),\qquad\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}(x,\theta)=-\cos\theta-\frac{2x^{2}\sin\theta}{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}},

hence

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}(0,0)=-1,\qquad\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}(0,\pi)=1,

and
\Gamma
is expressed locally as a vertical graph
\theta=\theta(x)
around
(0,0)
and
(0,\pi)
. Moreover, around
(0,0)
(resp. around
(0,\pi)
) we have
\theta^{\prime}(0)=2\lambda
(resp.
\theta^{\prime}(0)=-2\lambda
) and thus both components of
\Gamma
lie in the region
\theta\in(0,\pi)
locally around both
(0,0)
and
(0,\pi)
, regardless of the value of
\lambda
. This is a first difference with the rotational case, since there for
\lambda<1

\Gamma
had points above the line
\theta=\pi
around
(0,\pi)
.

We begin by proving that for each

such that

. This implies in particular that
\Gamma
can be expressed in such a region for

as a horizontal graph

. So, fix

F(0,\theta_{0})=-\sin\theta_{0}<0,\quad\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x,\theta_{0})=\infty,\quad\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}F(x,\theta_{0})>0,

hence the existence of a unique

Now we focus on the region

\Gamma
with the line

F(x,\pi)=2x\left(\lambda-\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}}}\right).

Thus:

•

, we find no solutions of

and the other two near
(x_{1}^{\pm},\pi)
.

•

If
\lambda<1
and
x>0
, then
F(x,\pi)=0
has two well-defined real solutions

x_{\lambda}^{\pm}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}+2h\tau\lambda^{2}\pm\sqrt{(1-\lambda^{2})(1-\lambda^{2}+4h\tau\lambda^{2})}}}{\lambda\tau\sqrt{2}}.

(5.2)

We can also express
\Gamma
as a vertical graph
\theta=\theta(x)
around each of such points, and this time
\theta^{\prime}(x_{\lambda}^{-})>0
and
\theta^{\prime}(x_{\lambda}^{+})<0
. We will deduce later that the component
\Gamma\cap\{\theta\in(\pi,3\pi/2)\}
is a connected vertical graph that joins the points
(x_{\lambda}^{-},\pi)
and
(x_{\lambda}^{+},\pi)
and thus can be described by the function
\theta(x)
. Furthermore,

\lim_{h\to\infty}x_{\lambda}^{+}-x_{\lambda}^{-}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}}{\lambda\tau}.

Thus, even if
h\to\infty
, these points of intersection do not collapse to the same point.

We investigate the points of
\Gamma
such that their
x
-coordinate has a local extrema. Around such points we can express
\Gamma
as a horizontal graph
x=x(\theta)
and
(x,\theta)\in\Gamma
satisfies
x^{\prime}(\theta)=0
if and only if
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}(x,\theta)=0
. Thus, points with local extrema at the
x
-coordinate are located at the intersection of
\Gamma
with the graph of the function

f(x)=\pi-\arctan\frac{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}}{2x^{2}},\qquad x\geq 0.

We introduce the following notation: given a function
f
, we denote by
\mathcal{G}_{f}=\{(x,f(x))\colon x\in\mathrm{Dom}(f)\}
to the graph of
f
. Note that
f
extends smoothly to
x=0
as
f(0)=\pi/2
and
f^{\prime}(0)=0
. Furthermore,
f^{\prime}(x)=0
for
x>0
if and only if
x=\frac{\sqrt{2}h}{\sqrt{2h\tau-1}}
. This value is well-defined whenever
2h\tau>1
. Thus, if
2h\tau\leq 1
then
f
is strictly increasing and otherwise has a local maximum whose value on
f
is

f\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}h}{\sqrt{2h\tau-1}}\right)=\pi-\arctan\frac{\sqrt{4h\tau-1}}{h}.

In any case,
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=\pi-\arctan\frac{\tau}{2}
and in particular
\mathcal{G}_{f}
lies contained in the region
\theta\in(\pi/2,\pi)
. By compactness of
\Gamma
, we ensure the existence of at least a point
(x_{0},\theta_{0})\in\Gamma
such that
x^{\prime}(\theta_{0})=0
. Furthermore, if
\Gamma
is connected then it intersects the
\mathcal{G}_{f}
at a unique point
(x_{0},\theta_{0})
and
x_{0}
must be a global maximum. Indeed, at any local extrema one has
x^{\prime\prime}(\theta)=-\frac{F_{\theta\theta}}{F_{x}}
and since

\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial\theta^{2}}(x,\theta)=-\frac{2x^{2}\cos\theta}{\sqrt{(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}+x^{2}}}+\sin\theta>0,\quad\theta\in(\pi/2,\pi),

we deduce that
x
cannot have inflexion points, only local maxima and minima. Thus, if
\Gamma
is connected it can only intersect
\mathcal{G}_{f}
at a unique point. We also deduce that for any connected component of
\Gamma\cap\{\theta\in[\pi,3\pi/2)\}
, the points with largest and lowest
x
-coordinate are precisely located at the line
\theta=\pi
. In particular, any such component can be expressed as a vertical graph
\theta=\theta(x)
. Let us study in more detail the existence of such points. The conditions
F(x,\theta)=0
and
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}(x,\theta)=0
yield

l(x):=\frac{1}{2x}+\frac{2x^{3}}{x^{2}+(h-\tau x^{2})^{2}}=\frac{\lambda}{\sin\theta},

and by solving
\theta
we define
L(x)=\pi-\arcsin\frac{\lambda}{l(x)}
. Thus, the existence of points in
\Gamma
with local critical values of the
x
-coordinate are located in the intersection of the graphs
\mathcal{G}_{f}
and
\mathcal{G}_{L}
. See Fig.
9
.

Figure 9:
Left: the graph of the function
l(x)
for different values of the parameter
h
, and a horizontal line
y=\lambda
for
\lambda=1
. Right, the graphs
\mathcal{G}_{f}
and
\mathcal{G}_{L}
in the phase plane
\mathcal{R}
. Every time that they intersect, the curve
\Gamma
has a point with critical
x
-coordinate.

We study the properties of
L
by means of the ones of
l
. First of all, recall that
L
is only defined whenever
\lambda\leq l(x)
. Note that
\lim_{x\to 0}l(x)=\infty
and
L
can be extended to
x=0
as
L(0)=\pi
, and
\lim_{x\to\infty}l(x)=0
. Thus, for fixed
\lambda
and
x
sufficiently large
L(x)
fails to be defined. Furthermore, there exists at least a value
x_{0}>0
such that
l(x_{0})=\lambda
and therefore
\mathcal{G}_{L}
passes through the point
(x_{0},\pi/2)
. Since
L(0)>f(0)
,
\mathcal{G}_{f}
and
\mathcal{G}_{L}
intersect at least once and we deduce again the existence of at least a point of
\Gamma
with critical
x
-coordinate. Lastly,
L^{\prime}(x)=0
if and only if
l^{\prime}(x)=0
and if
x_{0}
is such that
L^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
, then
L
has a local maximum (resp. a local minimum) if and only if
l
has at
x_{0}
a local maximum (resp. a local minimum).

In Fig.
9
, left, we can see the behavior of
\mathcal{G}_{l}
(plotted in black) for different values of the parameter
h
and the line
y=\lambda
for
\lambda=1
. In Fig.
9
, right, we can see
\mathcal{G}_{L}
for the corresponding values of
h
and also
\mathcal{G}_{f}
. Each time that
l(x)
intersects a horizontal line
y=\lambda
at some
(x_{0},l(x_{0}))
, we find that
\mathcal{G}_{L}
in
\mathcal{R}
intersects the line
\theta=\pi/2
at the point
(x_{0},\pi/2)
, and the values of
x
for which
l(x)<\lambda
are not in the domain of
L
. In particular,
\mathcal{G}_{L}
has at most two connected components. We distinguish depending on the values of
\lambda
, starting from the limit case
\lambda=0
and increasing it.

•

If
\lambda>0
is small enough such that
l(x)=\lambda
has only a solution
x_{0}
, then
\mathcal{G}_{L}
is a connected arc joining the point
(0,\pi)
and
(x_{0},\pi)
and that intersects
\mathcal{G}_{f}
precisely at the unique point of
\Gamma
with maximum
x
-coordinate.

•

If
l(x)=\lambda
has two solutions
x_{0}<x_{1}
, then the line
y=\lambda
is tangent to
\mathcal{G}_{l}
at its local minimum point and thus
\mathcal{G}_{L}
is a connected arc that joins the points
(0,\pi)
and
(x_{0},\pi/2)
, then
bounces
and joins the points
(x_{0},\pi/2)
and
(x_{1},\pi/2)
.

•

If
l(x)=\lambda
has three solutions
x_{0}<x_{1}<x_{2}
, then
\mathcal{G}_{L}
has two connected components: one,
\mathcal{G}_{L}^{1}
, joining the points
(0,\pi)
and
(x_{0},\pi/2)
and the other,
\mathcal{G}_{L}^{2}
, joining
(x_{1},\pi/2)
and
(x_{2},\pi/2)
.

•

When
\lambda
further increases, the values
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
both converge to the value
x_{+}
where
l
reaches its local maximum. The component
\mathcal{G}_{L}^{2}
tends to be smaller, then collapses at the point
(x_{+},\pi/2)
and finally disappears.

Fix
\lambda>0
and assume for a moment
h=0
, i.e. we are in the case of rotational surfaces. The monotony properties of
l
depend on
\tau
. For instance,
l^{\prime}(x)=0
if and only if
\tau\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
at the points

x_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-\tau^{2}\pm\sqrt{4-8\tau^{2}}}}.

Hence, for
\tau\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
the function
l
is strictly decreasing, and if
\tau<1/\sqrt{2}
then
l
has a local minimum at
x_{-}
and a local maximum at
x_{+}
. Now, for
h>0
close enough to zero we have that
l
has the same properties as for
h=0
due to the continuity dependence of
h
. Let us denote by
x_{\pm}^{h}
to the values where
l
reaches its maximum and minimum, for given
h>0
. We find that as
h
increases then
l(x_{-}^{h})
decreases and
l(x_{+}^{h})
increases; furthermore, we also see that
x_{-}^{h}
and
x_{+}^{h}
both increase and thus for fixed
\lambda>0
the connected component
\mathcal{G}_{L}^{2}
always exists and has unbounded
x
-coordinate as
h
increases.

Now, for fixed
\tau
and
\lambda
and making
h
vary, the behavior of
f
and
L
determines two different scenarios. In the first one, the graphs
\mathcal{G}_{f}
and
\mathcal{G}_{L}
intersect only once for every
h>0
. Thus,
\Gamma
is connected and we are in an analog situation to rotational surfaces. In the second, the graphs
\mathcal{G}_{f}
and
\mathcal{G}_{L}
intersect only once for
h>0
small enough; this always happens since for
h=0
we only have one critical
x
-coordinate of
\Gamma
(Fig.
10
, left). As
h
increases the local minimum of
\mathcal{G}_{L}
decreases and then intersects
\mathcal{G}_{f}
again, hence
\Gamma
shrinks at such intersection (Fig.
10
, center). For
h
slightly greater we find two compact components of
\Gamma
: one,
\Gamma^{1}
, containing the points
(0,0)
and
(0,\pi)
, and other,
\Gamma^{2}
(Fig.
10
, right).

Figure 10:
The behavior of the curve
\Gamma
and the graphs
\mathcal{G}_{f}
and
\mathcal{G}_{L}
for fixed
\tau,\lambda
and varying
h
.

When
h
further increases the component
\mathcal{G}_{L}^{2}
has greater
x
-coordinate and thus if
\lambda>1
there exists
h_{0}>0
such that the graph
\mathcal{G}_{L}
intersects
\mathcal{G}_{f}
tangentially one last time, making
\Gamma^{2}
shrink and then disappears (Fig.
11
, left).

If
\lambda=1
, there is always the point
(\sqrt{h/\tau},\pi)
and
\Gamma^{2}
is always locally a graph
\theta=\theta(x)
around such point. However, as
h\to\infty
the component
\Gamma^{2}
tends to disappear. Indeed, assume that there exists
\theta_{0}\in(\pi/2,\pi)
such that for every
h_{n}>0
there exists
x_{n}>0
big enough such that
(x_{n},\theta_{0})\in\Gamma^{2}
. Hence,

2x_{n}\left(1+\frac{x_{n}\cos\theta_{0}}{\sqrt{x_{n}^{2}+(h_{n}-\tau x_{n}^{2})^{2}}}\right)=\sin\theta_{0}.

The right-hand side is fixed; it can be as small as we want, but is fixed. If
h_{n}\to\infty
and so
x_{n}\to\infty
, we do not mind if the factor
(h_{n}-\tau x_{n}^{2})^{2}
converges to some non-negative constant, even zero, or diverge, as the left-hand side eventually diverges, a contradiction; the fact that
\theta_{0}
is fixed is crucial. Thus,
\Gamma^{2}
tends to shrink but never disappears for a finite
h>0
(Fig.
11
, center).

If
\lambda<1
we know that there always exist the points
x_{\lambda}^{\pm}
of intersection between
\Gamma^{2}
and
\theta=\pi
, given by Eq. (
5.2
). This connected component will be unbounded if we make
h\to\infty
. Since the difference of the points
x_{\lambda}^{+}-x_{\lambda}^{-}
is bounded as
h\to\infty
, this component
\Gamma^{2}
never disappears (Fig.
11
, right).

); center: it becomes smaller but does not disappear (

).

5.2  Proof of Thm.
1.5

The proof is immediate from the properties deduced in Section
4.1
about the phase plane and the properties of the curve
\Gamma
proved in this section. The existence of a second connected component of
\Gamma
, which is a closed curve, only makes that an orbit entering its inner region changes the monotonicity of its
\theta
-coordinate, but the orbit eventually escapes from this region and then behaves as in Section
4.2
. The existence of the orbits
\gamma_{\pm}
and their behaviors are proved the same as for the case
h=0
, thus details are skipped; see Fig.
12
, top, the orbits in blue and red, respectively.

Figure 12:
The phase plane for helicoidal
\lambda
-translators. Here,
\lambda<1
.

Let
(x_{+},\pi/2)
and
(x_{-},\pi/2)
with
x_{+}<x_{-}
the points through which pass the orbits
\gamma_{+}
and
\gamma_{-}
, respectively. To determine the rest of the orbits, fix some
x_{0}>0
and let
\gamma(s)
be the orbit passing through
(x_{0},\pi/2)
at
s=0
. If
x_{0}<x_{-}
, then
\gamma(s)\to e_{0}
as
s\to\infty
, and when
s\to-\infty
, then
\gamma
lies at the left-hand side of
\gamma_{-}
and its
x
-coordinate diverges. See Fig.
12
, top, the orbit in orange. If
x_{0}>x_{-}
, then if
s\to-\infty
then
\gamma
lies at the right-hand side of
\gamma_{-}
and its
x
-coordinate diverges as well, while if
s\to\infty
then the
x
-coordinate of
\gamma
decreases. Furthemore, let
(x_{n},(2n-1)\pi)
the intersection of
\gamma
with
\theta=(2n-1)\pi
and consider the translation
\gamma_{n}=\gamma-(0,2(n-1)\pi)
restricted to
\theta\in[0,\pi]
; observe that
\gamma_{1}=\gamma
in this region. A gluing argument as in Thm.
1.3
ensures that some
\gamma_{n_{0}}
lies at the left-hand side of
\gamma_{-}
and thus
\gamma_{n_{0}}(s)\to e_{0}
as
s\to\infty
. Consequently,
\gamma(s)
ends up converging to the equilibrium
e_{0}+(0,2(n_{0}-1)\pi)
as
s\to\infty
; see Fig.
12
, top, the orbit in purple. Similarly as for rotational
\lambda
-translators not-intersecting the rotation axis and by
2\pi
-periodicity, the fact that
x_{0}<x_{-}
or not is irrelevant as any orbit will eventually converge to some equilibrium as
s\to\infty
.

In Fig.
12
, bottom, we see three helicoidal
\lambda
-translators. The first and the second correspond to the orbits with endpoints
(0,0)
and
(0,\pi)
plotted in blue and red, respectively. The third corresponds to an orbit converging to
e_{0}
and not reaching the
\theta
-axis, hence the profile curve does not intersect the rotation axis.
