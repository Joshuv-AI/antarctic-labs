Does the obesity paradox truly exist? A study on the relationship between different degrees of obesity and stroke incidence and mortality based on the UK Biobank
Authors: Ling Shi, Siqi Zhai, Jing Shi, Fan Zhang, Zhiren Zhang, Zhenwei Pan, Maoyi Tian, Zhongling Zhang, Zengxiang Dong, Yue Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity has been associated with increased incidence and severity of various cardiovascular risk factors, as well as an elevated risk of stroke. However, the evidence of its effect on outcomes in stroke patients has been equivocal. Previous studies have documented the obesity paradox in stroke, characterized by a negative correlation between BMI and stroke mortality. To address the limitations of relying solely on BMI in previous investigations, notably the documented obesity paradox linking BMI to stroke mortality, our study incorporates additional obesity metrics beyond BMI. We aimed to comprehensively evaluate the relationship between obesity and stroke outcomes, thereby providing a more holistic understanding of their association.MethodsThe present study included 502,128 participants from the UK Biobank database. Cox proportional hazard models were performed to determine the associations of indicators, including BMI, WC, WHR, WHtR and BFP with the incidence and mortality of stroke. ResultsIncreased BMI was associated with elevated stroke incidence and mortality, with the lowest risk observed in the BMI range of 18-24 kg/m². Both lower BMI (17≤BMI<18.5 kg/m²) and higher BMI (BMI≥25 kg/m²) were linked to higher incidence and mortality of stroke. In contrast, other obesity-related indicators (WC, WHR, WHtR and BFP) exhibited more pronounced linear relationships with the incidence of stroke, all-cause mortality in the total population, stroke mortality in the total population and all-cause mortality in stroke patients. However, no significant differences were found in stroke-specific mortality among underweight, normal weight, overweight and obese groups in stroke patients across various obesity indicators, suggesting no obvious association between obesity and stroke-specific mortality in stroke patients under the current definitions and adjustments in this study.Notably, stroke severity, acute care, and complications were not available in the analysis, and residual confounding from these unmeasured factors cannot be ruled out. Furthermore, our analyses on the relationships between several obesity subtypes with different metabolic health statuses and stroke incidence and mortality revealed that, regardless of normal weight or not, individuals with metabolic abnormalities had significantly higher incidence and mortality of stroke compared to those with normal metabolism. This highlights the importance of metabolic status in the onset and prognosis of stroke.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that stroke-specific mortality in stroke patients was not associated with obesity severity when assessed using multiple obesity indicators including BMI, WC, WHR, WHtR and BFP. This is inconsistent with previous observations of the obesity paradox. Meanwhile, the observation that individuals with metabolic abnormalities exhibit significantly higher stroke incidence and mortality compared to those with normal metabolism further confirms the adverse impact of metabolic disturbances on stroke risk and prognosis, underscoring the pivotal role of metabolic health in stroke management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28239-7.Keywords: Obesity, Obesity paradox, Metabolic subtypes of obesity, Stroke

Introduction

The global burden of obesity continues to expand: the number of overweight or obese individuals worldwide rose from 0.929 billion in 1990 to 2.6 billion in 2021, with the obesity prevalence more than doubling in both adult males and females [1]. Among high-income countries, the United States has the highest obesity rate, affecting over 40% of adults [2]. In China, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among adults has reached 50.7% and is projected to climb to 65.3% by 2030 [3], imposing substantial social, clinical, and economic impacts [4].

Obesity is associated with an increased risk of premature mortality [2] and is a well-established risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, as well as certain cancers[5–8]. Stroke, ranking as the second leading global cause of death, is characterized by high incidence, high mortality, and its role as a primary driver of long-term disability [9, 10]. Although extensive research highlights the adverse effects of elevated body mass index (BMI) on post-stroke survival [11], evidence supporting the obesity paradox suggests that overweight and even obesity may have a protective effect on all-cause mortality and prognosis of ischemic stroke (IS) [12–14], a trend that also applies to those with hemorrhagic stroke (HS) [15, 16]. These contradictions may stem from BMI’s vulnerability to disease-related influences [17]. For instance, individuals with subclinical conditions may lose weight due to muscle mass loss prior to clinical diagnosis [17], even as their body fat percentage increases and fat distribution remains unaltered. Additionally, BMI confounds fat mass and lean mass, potentially misclassifying individuals with high lean mass (e.g., athletes) as having obesity [18]. It also lacks precision in characterizing fat distribution [19]; for example, central obesity assessed by waist circumference correlates more strongly with visceral fat than BMI does [20]. Beyond BMI, the paucity of robust data linking other body composition metrics to long-term outcomes further perpetuates and reinforces the persistence of conflicting findings [21]. Thus, caution is warranted when using BMI alone to assess obesity for evaluating stroke risk and prognosis.BMI should be integrated with other indices such as waist circumference (WC), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), body fat percentage (BFP) and visceral fat area (VFA) for comprehensive assessment.

More importantly, simultaneous attention should be paid to metabolic status (e.g., blood glucose, blood lipids, blood pressure) during obesity assessment. Obesity can be classified into metabolically healthy normal weight (MHNW), metabolically unhealthy normal weight (MUNW), metabolically healthy obesity (MHO), metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO), normal weight obesity (NWO) and sarcopenic obesity (SO) based on metabolic status [22]. Notably, metabolic abnormalities are typically linked to elevated risks of cardiovascular diseases [23, 24], stroke [25, 26], type 2 diabetes [27] and other conditions. This strong association underscores the importance of metabolic health in the management of obesity-related complications. However, despite having normal metabolic parameters, MHO patients still require active intervention for obesity to reduce the risk of long-term adverse cardiovascular events and prevent progression to MUO [28, 29]. NWO is a relatively new category of obesity that is characterized by the presence of high body fat despite having a normal BMI [30].

Individuals with NWO have a higher risk for cardiometabolic diseases than individuals with normal weight and without high body fat [31]. In summary, addressing metabolic abnormalities may be more effective than simple weight loss in reducing the risk of common obesity-related complications. For individuals with metabolically healthy obesity or high body fat, early interventions involving diet and physical activity are warranted to reduce their long-term disease risk.

This study aims to utilize the UK Biobank database to investigate the longitudinal associations of multiple body composition indices (including BMI, WC, WHR, WHtR and BFP) with the incidence and mortality of IS and HS. Furthermore, this study also focuses on evaluating the incidence and mortality of stroke among obese and non-obese populations across different metabolic statuses.

Methods

Data source

We used data from the UK Biobank (Registration No.532351), a large-scale prospective cohort study that enrolled over 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years from across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. In this study, stroke incidence and mortality were ascertained during the follow-up period from the baseline assessment (2006–2010) until 31 March 2024 (censoring date). This database provides comprehensive data including demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, clinical measurements and biological samples to support research on disease aetiology and outcomes. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki (revised in Helsinki in 2024) and approved by the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (REC: 06/MRE08/65). Participants attended baseline assessment centers across England, Scotland, and Wales, where they provided electronically signed consent. Consistent with the UK Biobank Ethics and Governance Framework, no further informed consent was sought from participants for this secondary data analysis, as the research purpose aligns with the scope of the original consent provided by all cohort participants.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) The study population included patients with IS and HS; (2) Patients with complete follow-up data on all-cause mortality and stroke-specific mortality. The inclusion criteria based on metabolic classification of obesity: metabolically healthy obesity (MHO): BMI ≥ 30 and metabolically healthy; metabolically unhealthy obesity (MUO): BMI ≥ 30 and metabolically unhealthy; metabolically unhealthy normal weight (MUNW): 18.5 ≤ BMI ≤ 24.9 and metabolically unhealthy; metabolically healthy normal weight (MHNW): 18.5 ≤ BMI ≤ 24.9, metabolically healthy; normal weight obesity (NWO): 18.5 ≤ BMI ≤ 24.9, BFP > 25% in males or > 35% in females, and metabolically unhealthy [32]. Diagnostic criteria for metabolic abnormality: two or more of the following conditions: triglyceride (TG) ≥ 150 mg/dL; high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) < 40 mg/dL (males) or < 50 mg/dL (females); fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 100.8 mg/dL; systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 85 mmHg [33].

Participants who met fewer than two of the above criteria were classified as metabolically healthy. Exclusion criteria: (1) Failure to meet the above inclusion criteria; (2) participants with pre-existing stroke, cancer, or severe coronary heart disease at baseline were excluded; (3) patients without data on BMI, WC, WHR, WHtR and BFP; (4) underweight participants (BMI<17) (individuals with a BMI<17 were excluded from the analysis, as such low values typically reflect severe undernutrition, occult underlying illnesses or measurement errors, which could introduce substantial bias). Exclusion criteria for obesity subtypes based on metabolic classification: (1) missing data for BMI or BFP;. (2) participants with pre-existing stroke; (3) missing enrollment date.

Study cohort

502,128 UK Biobank participants were included in the analysis. We excluded 3,103 participants without BMI information, 106 without WC information, 37 without hip circumference information, and 7,504 missing BFP data. After excluding participants with missing data on confounding factors (n = 4,013) or enrollment dates (n = 186), two separate analytical cohorts were constructed. For the IS cohort, individuals with prevalent IS (n = 1,714), cancer (n = 43,287), or coronary heart disease (CHD, n = 16,293) at baseline were excluded, leaving 417,981 participants. For the HS cohort, those with prevalent HS (n = 105), cancer (n = 43,477), or CHD (n = 16,593) at baseline were excluded, yielding a final sample of 419,100 participants. For the analysis of stroke incidence and mortality according to different obesity phenotypes, we excluded 3,103 participants with missing BMI data, 7,546 with missing BFP data, 195 without enrollment date and 7,054 individuals with stroke prior to enrollment. Finally, 484,230 participants were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were the mortality events in stroke patients under assessment of different obesity indicators, including all-cause mortality in the total population, stroke mortality in the total population, all-cause mortality in stroke patients and stroke-specific mortality in stroke patients. Stroke incidence was analyzed as the secondary outcome. Data on stroke events and mortality were ascertained through linkage to UK Biobank hospital inpatient records, primary care data, and death registries, where stroke was coded as the underlying or contributory cause of death. We defined outcomes according to the International Classification of Disease, 10th Revision (ICD-10): I63 for ischemic stroke (IS) and I61-I62 for hemorrhagic stroke (HS). Only first-ever strokes were included in this study, and recurrent strokes were not analyzed as independent events. Participants who died from other causes were not classified as stroke deaths, but were included in the assessment of all-cause mortality. All participants were retained in the analysis to ensure sample completeness.

Other variables

In the present study, we included covariates that could confound the relationship between obesity and stroke, including age, sex, tobacco smoking (never smokers, current smokers, and former smokers), alcohol consumption (never drinkers, current drinkers, former drinkers) and Townsend deprivation index (a measure of deprivation based on four census variables: unemployment, non-car ownership, non-house ownership, and household overcrowding), as well as potential mediators including diabetes mellitus (DM, only patients with type 2 diabetes were included, E11), hypertension (HTN, only those with primary hypertension were included, I10), and atrial fibrillation (AF, only patients with atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter were included, I48).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as percentages, and comparisons between two categorical variables were performed using the chi-square (χ²) test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and for comparisons between two continuous variables that conformed to a normal distribution, the t-test was applied. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between different obesity levels and stroke incidence and mortality, as evaluated by BMI, WC, WHR, WHtR, and BFP. Cox proportional hazards models were adjusted for the confounders including age, sex (model 1), smoking status, drinking frequency and Townsend deprivation index (model 2). Hypertension, DM and AF were included as potential mediators in model 3. In analyses of obesity subtypes according to metabolic status, missing values for TG, HDL, SBP and DBP were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to minimize potential bias in the metabolic subgroup analyses. Prior to metabolic phenotype classification, we first evaluated missingness for all metabolism-related variables in the total study sample of 502,128 participants.

The counts and proportions of missing values were summarized as follows: triglycerides (TG): 38,425 missing observations (7.65%); high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C): 72,574 missing observations (14.45%); fasting blood glucose (FBG): 72,878 missing observations (14.51%); first systolic blood pressure (SBP1): 34,429 missing observations (6.86%); second systolic blood pressure (SBP2): 41,198 missing observations (8.20%); first diastolic blood pressure (DBP1): 34,416 missing observations (6.85%); second diastolic blood pressure (DBP2): 41,194 missing observations (8.20%). No missing data were found for the sex variable.

Missing values of these continuous metabolic variables were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), with predictive mean matching (PMM) adopted as the imputation algorithm. We generated five imputed datasets, and the MICE algorithm ran for five iterations. A fixed random seed of 123 was specified to ensure analytical reproducibility. After imputation, the complete() function was used to extract the pooled dataset for subsequent analyses. Using the imputed values of TG, HDL-C, FBG, SBP and DBP, we performed unit conversion, calculated individual metabolic abnormality scores, and further categorized participants into distinct metabolic health statuses and obesity-related metabolic phenotypes.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the stability and reliability of our findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding stroke events within the first 2 years of follow-up. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4).

Results

Baseline characteristics

In the IS cohort, participants who developed incident IS were significantly older (60.87 ± 6.84 vs. 55.89 ± 8.07 years, P < 0.001) and more likely to be male (55.76% vs. 45.66%, P < 0.001). The distribution of smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, DM, and AF also differed significantly between IS and controls (all P < 0.001). In the HS cohort, individuals who developed incident HS were significantly older (59.37 ± 7.37 vs. 56.02 ± 8.08 years, P < 0.001) and had substantially higher baseline prevalence of hypertension (97.26% vs. 61.55%), DM (99.73% vs. 91.63%), and AF (99.37% vs. 92.66%) than controls (all P < 0.001). The Townsend deprivation index was significantly higher in both IS and HS cases relative to their respective controls (both P < 0.001), suggesting a potential association between lower socioeconomic status and incident stroke (Table 1).

Associations of BMI, WC, WHR, WHtR and BFP with stroke incidence and mortality

Over a median follow-up of 15.6 years (IQR: 14.8–16.3 years), 7,913 incident cases of IS were recorded. The median time from baseline to ischemic stroke onset among affected participants was 9.4 years (IQR: 5.8–11.9 years). A total of 37,815 all-cause deaths occurred during follow-up, including 419 deaths attributed to IS. The total person-years of follow-up for the IS incidence analysis was 6,333,399. Tables 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 present the incidence and mortality rates of IS events across each obesity measurement indicator. Individuals with a lower BMI (underweight, 17 ≤ BMI < 18.5 kg/m²) were associated with higher incidence of IS and the lowest risk of IS was exhibited at a BMI of 18.5–25 kg/m²(normal weight). Thereafter, the incidence of IS increased with rising BMI. However, for the remaining adiposity measures (WC, WHR, WHtR and BFP), the associations with the incidence of IS events were linear, with higher adiposity corresponding to a greater risk of IS events (Table 2). For the associations between five obesity indicators and mortality, lower BMI and BFP correlated with higher all-cause mortality in the total population. Mortality risk was lowest when BMI fell within the normal weight range, and it gradually increased as BMI rose, exhibiting a J-shaped association. WC, WHR, and WHtR generally showed a linear correlation with all-cause mortality in the total population, and mortality risk rose significantly as these indices increased (Table 3). This same trend was also observed for IS mortality in the total population (Table 4). Additionally, we observed that BMI and WC had a J-shaped association with all-cause mortality in IS patients.

In contrast, WHR, WHtR and BFP exhibited a linear association with all-cause mortality in this cohort (Table 5). Notably, none of the five obesity indicators showed a significant association with stroke-specific mortality in IS patients (Table 6). We further performed survival analyses using Cox proportional hazards regression with three sequential adjustment models to examine associations between diverse adiposity indicators and incident IS. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 was additionally adjusted for smoking, drinking and Townsend deprivation index; Model 3 was further adjusted for hypertension, DM and AF. Notably, each obesity indicator was fitted into a separate Cox model, and thus all tabulated HRs represent independent estimates from individual models rather than outputs from a single multivariate model containing multiple correlated obesity indices. All adiposity measures were significantly and positively associated with the risk of incident ischemic stroke (all P < 0.001). Although hazard ratios (HR) were slightly attenuated with progressive covariate adjustment, the associations remained statistically significant, demonstrating robust results (Table 7). To assess the stability of the overall results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding IS events within the first 2 years of follow-up (Table S1). This analysis yielded consistent results and confirmed the robustness of our results.

Previous studies have identified obesity as a risk factor for HS, linking it to an elevated risk of HS onset [34]. However, a study published in Neurology demonstrated that higher BMI correlates with reduced mortality and improved functional outcomes in intracerebral hemorrhage, validating the existence of the obesity paradox in HS as well [35]. Within the UK Biobank population, the median follow-up duration from baseline was 15.6 years (IQR: 14.9–16.4 years), with a total of 6,390,668 person-years accumulated for incidence analysis. During follow-up, 3,355 incident HS events were recorded. The median time from baseline to HS onset was 14.7 years (IQR: 10.9–15.9 years) among affected participants. A total of 38,127 all-cause deaths occurred, including 895 deaths related to HS. Based on these prospective data, our study demonstrated that all five adiposity indicators showed significant positive linear trends with HS incidence, all-cause mortality in the total population and stroke mortality in the total population as obesity severity increased. Central obesity measures (WC, WHR and WHtR) showed stronger associations with these outcomes than did BMI and BFP. Importantly, only WHR remained significantly associated with both all-cause mortality and stroke-specific mortality in HS patients, underscoring the need to prioritize its assessment in stroke prevention and post-stroke risk management (Tables 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12). Notably, in our staged multivariable models, the association patterns between adiposity indicators and HS differed substantially across adjustment levels.

Incidence and mortality of stroke in different obesity subtypes

We analyzed the incidence and mortality rates across several obesity phenotypes classified by metabolic status. Statistically significant differences in stroke incidence and mortality were observed among the five metabolic subgroups (p < 0.001). Overall, regardless of weight status, individuals with metabolic abnormalities had significantly higher stroke incidence than those with normal metabolism (MHNW vs. MUNW, p < 0.001; MHO vs. MUO, p < 0.001). Notably, the highest stroke incidence was observed in the MUNW, MUO and NWO groups, at 2.93%, 3.68% and 3.48%, respectively (Fig. 2A). Additionally, these groups exhibited elevated levels of all-cause mortality in the total population, stroke mortality in the total population, all-cause mortality in stroke patients and stroke-specific mortality in stroke patients (Fig. 2B-E). Previous follow-up data from 1,131 middle-aged Finnish men showed that even after adjusting for traditional risk factors such as smoking and family history, individuals with metabolic syndrome had a 2.41-fold higher risk of ischemic stroke and a 2.05-fold higher risk of all-cause stroke [36]. Furthermore, a study conducted in a Chinese population validated this association, demonstrating that metabolic syndrome increases the risk of ischemic stroke by 5.1-fold and the risk of hemorrhagic stroke by 3.3-fold [37]. The results of the present study are consistent with those of previous cohort studies, indicating that individuals with metabolic abnormalities have a higher risk of stroke.

In the context of normal metabolism, stroke incidence, all-cause mortality in the total population and stroke mortality in the total population were all higher in the metabolically healthy obese (MHO) group than in metabolically healthy normal weight (MHNW) group (p < 0.001), indicating that obesity is an independent risk factor distinct from common metabolic abnormalities (Fig. 2A-C). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of all-cause mortality in stroke patients (p = 0.193) and stroke-specific mortality in stroke patients (p = 0.456) (Fig. 2D-E). Among individuals with metabolic abnormalities, the metabolically unhealthy obese (MUO) group had higher stroke incidence (p < 0.001), and all-cause mortality in the total population (p < 0.001) than the metabolically unhealthy normal weight (MUNW) group. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of stroke mortality in the total population (p = 0.220), all-cause mortality in stroke patients (p = 0.108) or stroke-specific mortality in stroke patients (p = 0.146) (Fig. 2C-E). Notably, the MUNW group exhibited persistently elevated stroke incidence and mortality rates, and its stroke-specific mortality in stroke patients even surpassed that of the MUO group, although the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 2E). These findings underscore the high-risk profile of MUNW, potentially attributed to its tendency toward underdiagnosis due to normal weight status, which leads to delayed interventions. Furthermore, the MUNW population is often complicated with sarcopenia or increased visceral adiposity [38], which exacerbates injury in stroke patients and ultimately contributes to high mortality rates (Fig. 2C-E). Discussion

Obesity can be assessed through multiple anthropometric indicators, each with distinct advantages and inherent limitations. BMI is the most widely used measure of general obesity but only reflects overall body mass without capturing fat distribution. By contrast, WC, WHR and WHtR are important indicators for evaluating central obesity, whereas BFP serves as a more precise measure for assessing total body fat [39]. In this study, we systematically examined the associations of five metrics with stroke incidence and mortality. Our results revealed divergent association patterns across different obesity indices: BMI exhibited a classic J-shaped relationship with stroke incidence and mortality for both stroke subtypes, except for stroke-specific mortality in stroke patients. In comparison, WC, WHR, WHtR, and BFP were predominantly linearly and positively associated with stroke-related adverse outcomes. Such discrepancies are attributable to the distinct physiological implications of each indicator. The J-shaped association observed for BMI arises from dual adverse effects of underweight and excess body weight. Low BMI is associated with pulmonary infections [40], disability [41], unplanned hospital discharge [42], and delayed rehabilitation [41] in stroke patients. Potential mechanisms may involve the association of underweight status with endothelial dysfunction and systemic inflammation, which promote platelet aggregation and adhesion, thereby increasing the risk of subsequent stroke or systemic embolism [43, 44]. Additionally, underweight status is associated with abnormal circulating adipokine levels, which may contribute to eating difficulties and malnutrition, further exacerbating adverse outcomes in stroke patients [45]. Conversely, elevated BMI and excessive fat accumulation contribute to vascular remodeling, metabolic dysregulation and chronic inflammation, which progressively increase stroke risk. The synergistic adverse effects of underweight and overweight/obesity ultimately generate a typical J-shaped association, with the lowest stroke risk observed at the normal BMI range and elevated risks at both extreme ends. In contrast, WC, WHR, WHtR, and BFP directly reflect the degree of fat accumulation. Increased visceral and total body fat triggers sustained inflammatory activation, disrupts blood pressure and lipid homeostasis, and consequently elevates stroke incidence and mortality in a linear manner. Distinct results were observed in Cox proportional hazards regression analyses between HS and IS. All five obesity indicators served as robust independent risk factors for IS across sequentially adjusted models. For HS, only WHR maintained consistent statistical significance after successive covariate adjustment. Other obesity indicators lost statistical significance in Model 2 and became significant again only in the fully adjusted Model 3. This pattern implies that the relationship between WHR and HS risk is independent of factors including hypertension, DM and AF, reflecting an intrinsic and stable association. As a reliable and specific predictive biomarker, WHR has great potential to facilitate targeted prevention for populations at high risk of HS.

Furthermore, obesity status assessed using the five indices showed no significant association with stroke-specific mortality in stroke patients. Our findings are inconsistent with the traditional obesity paradox, which posits that overweight or obesity is associated with lower mortality risk and improved functional outcomes from stroke. A similar phenomenon has also been observed in cardiovascular diseases and cancer [46, 47]. Indeed, this is paradoxical: before disease onset, obesity is a risk factor for its development, but once the disease is present, obesity becomes protective. Numerous researchers have reassessed the obesity paradox. Jawad H. Butt et al. found that the paradox is absent in heart failure patients when alternative anthropometric measures such as WHR are used instead of BMI [48].

Analysis of prior literature on heart failure has shown that obese patients are significantly younger than their normal-weight counterparts; additionally, obese patients exhibit higher systolic blood pressure (SBP), which confers a survival advantage [49]. Charnigo R et al. noted that failure to adjust for certain unmeasured factors may lead to the erroneous inference that obesity is truly associated with a favorable prognosis, whereas it may instead represent a statistical artifact [50]. Such unmeasured factors may reflect the combined influence of beneficial phenotypic traits, including the synergistic interplay of younger age, higher left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), better renal function, higher systolic blood pressure and superior nutritional status [51].

Our review of prior studies indicates that such inconsistencies in the obesity paradox may arise because most investigations relied exclusively on BMI to assess obesity.

Given that the association between BMI and clinical outcomes may be mediated by multiple factors, comprehensive evaluations encompassing renal function, metabolic health, and nutritional status are necessary [52]. BMI has inherent limitations in obesity assessment, as it fails to reflect an individual’s body fat percentage; accordingly, incorporating additional metrics such as WC, WHR and BFP is warranted [53].

Furthermore, Yang et al. employed multiple metrics including arm circumference, thigh circumference, maximum calf circumference, triceps skinfold and subscapular skinfold for comprehensive obesity assessment to validate the associations with cardiovascular disease mortality [54], aiming to more accurately evaluate the clinical value of obesity-related risk. Therefore, sole reliance on BMI’s association with stroke outcomes to support the “stroke obesity paradox” is contentious. Additionally, several moderators and confounders such as age, stroke severity, stroke subtype, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, alcohol consumption and lifestyle may underlie this apparent paradox [55].

Analyses of five obesity subtypes with distinct metabolic profiles revealed that, regardless of weight status, metabolically unhealthy individuals consistently exhibited significantly elevated stroke incidence and mortality rates. Previous studies have also demonstrated that metabolically unhealthy individuals face a higher risk of developing cardiovascular diseases [23] and type 2 diabetes [27], further underscoring the non-negligible role of metabolic health across various pathologies. Individuals with NWO are often overlooked due to their normal weight, yet many of them have concomitant metabolic disorders and thus require close attention. Notably, metabolically healthy populations still harbor potential health risks, warranting increased attention to prevent the progression from metabolic health to dysfunction.

Additionally, metabolically healthy obese individuals show higher stroke incidence and mortality, indicating that even with metabolic health, proactive weight management remains necessary to delay or prevent the metabolic deterioration and to reduce long-term disease risk.

Limitation

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the UK Biobank lacks data on the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS), a key tool for assessing stroke prognosis, limiting our evaluation of stroke outcomes to mortality alone. Secondly, although visceral obesity is a primary metabolic and CVD risk predictor in obesity and computed tomography (CT) is the gold standard for measuring visceral fat area to diagnose abdominal obesity, we did not conduct further analysis of the relevant imaging data. Finally, in classifying obesity subtypes based on metabolic status, sarcopenic obesity could not be further evaluated. This is because the UK Biobank provides only grip strength data and lacks dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) or bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) data, both of which are necessary to accurately assess appendicular skeletal muscle mass, making it impossible to determine sarcopenic obesity status. Additionally, our exclusive reliance on UK Biobank data, without incorporating an in-house cohort to further explore the associations between obesity and stroke incidence and mortality, may restrict the robustness and generalizability of our findings. Besides the limitations mentioned above, insufficient clinical details on stroke may hinder the interpretation of associations between obesity indicators and stroke outcomes. In the present study, we comprehensively evaluated associations between multiple obesity indicators (including WC, WHR, WHtR and BFP) and the incidence and mortality of IS and HS, detecting no statistical evidence in favor of the obesity paradox.

Nevertheless, several inherent limitations of the dataset weaken the certainty of our conclusions. The UK Biobank lacks detailed clinical data on stroke severity, acute treatment and major post-stroke complications, all of which are critical prognostic factors for stroke outcomes. These unmeasured clinical covariates may introduce residual confounding and bias the observed associations.

Conclusion

In summary, we found that the stroke incidence, stroke mortality in the total population and all-cause mortality in stroke patients increased progressively with the obesity severity, whereas stroke-specific mortality in stroke patients showed no significant association with obesity severity. No supportive evidence for the obesity paradox was observed in our cohort. These findings do not corroborate the traditional notion that overweight or obesity improves prognosis in stroke patients.

Nevertheless, limited by the lack of key clinical data in the UK Biobank, we are unable to definitively reject the obesity paradox in broader stroke populations. Moreover, beyond metrics such as BMI and WC, greater focus on metabolic health may offer more effective strategies to enhance survival, reduce the risk of adverse outcomes and improve quality of life in individuals with obesity.
