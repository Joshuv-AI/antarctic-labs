Quartic reductions and elliptic obstructions for perfect Euler bricks
René Peschmann

Abstract
. We show that the perfect Euler brick (perfect cuboid) problem is equivalent to the following elementary question: do there exist coprime integers a , b , m , n a,b,m,n such that the two expressions ( 2 ​ ( a 2 − b 2 ) ​ m ​ n ) 2 + ( ( a 2 + b 2 ) ​ ( m 2 − n 2 ) ) 2 and ( 4 ​ a ​ b ​ m ​ n ) 2 + ( ( a 2 + b 2 ) ​ ( m 2 − n 2 ) ) 2 \bigl(2(a^{2}{-}b^{2})mn\bigr)^{2}+\bigl((a^{2}{+}b^{2})(m^{2}{-}n^{2})\bigr)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad(4abmn)^{2}+\bigl((a^{2}{+}b^{2})(m^{2}{-}n^{2})\bigr)^{2} are simultaneously perfect squares? Despite their near-identical structure (differing only in the first summand), no solution has ever been found. We reduce this quartic pair to a one-parameter family of genus-3 hyperelliptic curves C A : w 2 = λ 8 + A ​ λ 4 + 1 C_{A}\colon w^{2}=\lambda^{8}+A\lambda^{4}+1 and develop obstructions on the distinguished elliptic quotient E A E_{A} : the Kummer character χ f \chi_{f} is non-trivial on the 4 4 -torsion, and 2 2 -descent arguments exclude several families of square classes. Computationally, we verify that no solution exists for parameters up to 10 3 10^{3} . These results do not yet exclude perfect Euler bricks unconditionally; the remaining gap and possible approaches (including a genus-5 covering obstruction and connections to ℚ ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) ) are discussed.

1.
Introduction

A
perfect Euler brick
(also called
perfect cuboid
) is a
rectangular parallelepiped with positive integer edges
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}

such that all three face diagonals

d_{ij}=\sqrt{e_{i}^{2}+e_{j}^{2}}
and the space diagonal

D=\sqrt{e_{1}^{2}+e_{2}^{2}+e_{3}^{2}}
are also positive integers:

(1)

e_{i}^{2}+e_{j}^{2}=d_{ij}^{2}\quad(1\leq i<j\leq 3),\qquad e_{1}^{2}+e_{2}^{2}+e_{3}^{2}=D^{2}.

While Euler bricks (satisfying only the face-diagonal conditions)
exist (the smallest has edges
(44,117,240)
), but no perfect Euler
brick has ever been found, and the question has remained open since
at least the 18th century; see Guy
[
9
]
and
Sharipov
[
13
]
for the related cuboid polynomial approach.

This paper does not claim a proof of non-existence of perfect Euler
bricks. Rather, it develops a new reduction framework and derives
several unconditional obstructions. The novelty lies in packaging
the quartic pair into a genus-3 curve
C_{A}
, reinterpreting the
square conditions through a rational function
f
on the elliptic
quotient
E_{A}
, and analysing the resulting Kummer character
\chi_{f}

on the torsion subgroup.
The key bridge (Corollary
5.2
) is that non-degenerate
rational points on
C_{A}
correspond precisely to points

P\in E_{A}(\mathbb{Q})
with
f(P)
a nonzero rational square;
the problem thus becomes a square-value problem on an elliptic curve.

Our main contributions are:

(i)

A reduction of the problem to a symmetric quartic
pair (
11
), and a forward implication showing
that any brick produces a non-degenerate rational point on a
genus-3 curve
C_{A}
(Sections
2
–
4
).

(ii)

A computation of the Kummer character
\chi_{f}
on the

2
-torsion of
E_{A}
and a proof of non-triviality on the

4
-torsion (Section
5
).

(iii)

Algebraic arguments via
2
-descent that exclude
several families of square classes, together with computational
verification for all parameters up to
10^{3}

(Sections
6
–
7
).

Status of results.

Unconditional:
any perfect Euler brick yields
a non-degenerate rational point on
C_{A}
(Lemma
3.1
);
the character
\chi_{f}
satisfies
\chi_{f}(T_{4})\neq 1
for every

4
-torsion point (Theorem
5.4
); and for points
with
\delta_{3}=1
, the value
f(P)
is not a square
(Theorem
6.2
).

Computational:
for all parameters up to
10^{3}
,
the product
f_{1}f_{2}
is never a perfect square
(Section
7
).

Open:
these obstructions do not yet cover all
descent classes; see Section
8
for a detailed
assessment.

2.
From Euler bricks to quartic pairs

2.1.
The Pythagorean framework

By Euclid’s formula, every primitive Pythagorean triple
(odd leg, even leg, hypotenuse) can be written as

(m^{2}-n^{2},\;2mn,\;m^{2}+n^{2})

for coprime integers
m>n>0
with
m-n
odd.
We write
U=m^{2}-n^{2}
,
V=2mn
,
W=m^{2}+n^{2}
for these
three components, so that
W^{2}=U^{2}+V^{2}
.

Choose a Euclid pair
(a,b)
with
a>b>0
,
\gcd(a,b)=1
,

a-b
odd, and set

U_{1}=a^{2}-b^{2},\quad V_{1}=2ab,\quad W_{1}=a^{2}+b^{2}.

A second pair
(m,n)
gives

U_{2}=m^{2}-n^{2}
,
V_{2}=2mn
,
W_{2}=m^{2}+n^{2}
.
Equating the common edge
e_{1}
from both triples
(
e_{1}=k_{1}U_{1}=k_{2}U_{2}
) determines
k_{2}=k_{1}U_{1}/U_{2}
,
and setting
k_{1}=U_{2}
clears denominators
(cf. Spohn
[
16
]
).
The edges become

(2)

e_{1}=U_{1}U_{2},\qquad e_{2}=V_{1}U_{2},\qquad e_{3}=U_{1}V_{2},

up to a common positive rational factor, with automatic face diagonals

d_{12}=W_{1}U_{2}
and
d_{13}=U_{1}W_{2}
.
The remaining conditions are:

(a)

Third face diagonal:

(3)

V_{1}^{2}\,U_{2}^{2}+U_{1}^{2}\,V_{2}^{2}=\square\qquad\text{(the \emph{Master condition})}.

(b)

Space diagonal:

(4)

W_{1}^{2}\,U_{2}^{2}+U_{1}^{2}\,V_{2}^{2}=\square\qquad\text{(the \emph{$H$-total condition})}.

Remark 2.1
.

Since
W_{1}^{2}=U_{1}^{2}+V_{1}^{2}
, the difference

(W_{1}^{2}-V_{1}^{2})U_{2}^{2}=(U_{1}U_{2})^{2}
is always a perfect square.
In terms of the Pythagorean ratios
R_{i}=V_{i}/U_{i}
, the Master
condition reads
R_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2}=R^{2}
for a further ratio
R
:
a Pythagorean triple of Pythagorean ratios.

2.2.
Conic reduction

Setting
\rho=t-1/t
(where
t=m/n
), the two conditions and
the rationality of
t
become three simultaneous square conditions
on
\rho
:

(5)

\displaystyle Y^{2}

\displaystyle=W_{1}^{2}\,\rho^{2}+4U_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{($H$-total)},

(6)

\displaystyle X^{2}

\displaystyle=V_{1}^{2}\,\rho^{2}+4U_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{(Master)},

(7)

\displaystyle S^{2}

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}+4

\displaystyle\text{(rationality of $t$)}.

Condition (
5
) is a conic with rational point

(\rho,Y)=(0,2U_{1})
. Parametrising via

(8)

\rho=\frac{4U_{1}\lambda}{W_{1}(1-\lambda^{2})},\qquad Y=\frac{2U_{1}(1+\lambda^{2})}{1-\lambda^{2}}

and substituting into (
7
)–(
6
) gives:

Proposition 2.2
.

A perfect Euler brick exists if and only if there exist coprime
positive integers
a>b
with
a-b
odd, and a rational

\lambda\notin\{0,\pm 1\}
, such that both

(9)

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=W_{1}^{2}(\lambda^{2}-1)^{2}+4U_{1}^{2}\lambda^{2},

(10)

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=W_{1}^{2}(\lambda^{2}-1)^{2}+4V_{1}^{2}\lambda^{2}

are perfect squares.

Proof.

Given a brick, the two Euclid pairs determine
\rho
and hence

\lambda
; the Master condition becomes
f_{2}=\square
and the
rationality of
t
becomes
f_{1}=\square
.
Conversely,
f_{1}=r^{2}
and
f_{2}=x^{2}
recover
\rho

via (
8
); then

t=(\rho+\sqrt{\rho^{2}+4})/2
is rational since

f_{1}=\square
ensures
\rho^{2}+4=\square
; the Master
condition holds by
f_{2}=\square
; and
H
-total is automatic from
the conic parametrisation.
∎

2.3.
The normalised quartic family

Setting
s=a/b
and dividing by
W_{1}^{2}
:

(11)

r^{2}=\lambda^{4}+2c\,\lambda^{2}+1,\qquad x^{2}=\lambda^{4}-2c\,\lambda^{2}+1,

where

(12)

c=c(s)=\frac{s^{4}-6s^{2}+1}{(1+s^{2})^{2}}=\frac{U_{1}^{2}-V_{1}^{2}}{W_{1}^{2}}.

The product is
f_{1}f_{2}=\lambda^{8}+A\lambda^{4}+1

with
A=2-4c^{2}
.

Remark 2.3

(Three sums of squares)
.

With
\tilde{L}_{1}=2U_{1}\lambda
,
\tilde{L}_{2}=2V_{1}\lambda
,

\tilde{L}_{3}=W_{1}(\lambda^{2}-1)
:

f_{1}=\tilde{L}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{L}_{3}^{2},\qquad f_{2}=\tilde{L}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{L}_{3}^{2},\qquad\tilde{L}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{L}_{2}^{2}=(2W_{1}\lambda)^{2}.

The third sum is automatic. By the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity,
the product decomposes as

f_{1}f_{2}=(\tilde{L}_{1}\tilde{L}_{2}-\tilde{L}_{3}^{2})^{2}+\tilde{L}_{3}^{2}\,(\tilde{L}_{1}+\tilde{L}_{2})^{2},

a sum of two squares whose second summand is itself a perfect square.
In
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, each
f_{i}
is a norm:

f_{i}=N(\tilde{L}_{i}+i\tilde{L}_{3})
.
Being a perfect square requires that for every split prime

p=\pi\bar{\pi}
(
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
), the sum

v_{\pi}(z_{i})+v_{\bar{\pi}}(z_{i})
is even, where

z_{i}=\tilde{L}_{i}+i\tilde{L}_{3}
.
(Primes
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
automatically divide any norm to an
even power.)

Remark 2.4

(Unit-circle structure)
.

Define
\kappa=4ab(a^{2}-b^{2})/(a^{2}+b^{2})^{2}
.
Then
c^{2}+\kappa^{2}=1
, and
the normalised quartics decompose as

(13)

\frac{f_{1}}{W_{1}^{2}n^{4}}=(\lambda^{2}+c)^{2}+\kappa^{2},\qquad\frac{f_{2}}{W_{1}^{2}n^{4}}=(\lambda^{2}-c)^{2}+\kappa^{2},

differing only in the sign of
c
.
The summand
\kappa^{2}
is the entire obstruction:
for
\kappa=0
(i.e.
a=b
, degenerate)
each
f_{i}
reduces to a perfect square.
Setting
\varphi=4s/(1+s^{2})
,
\psi=2(s^{2}-1)/(1+s^{2})
,
so that
\varphi^{2}+\psi^{2}=4
,
the product factorises over
\mathbb{Q}(s)[\lambda]
into four
positive-definite quadratics:

(14)

\frac{f_{1}f_{2}}{W_{1}^{4}n^{8}}=\prod_{\pm}(\lambda^{2}\pm\varphi\lambda+1)\,(\lambda^{2}\pm\psi\lambda+1).

The three involutions

\lambda\mapsto-\lambda
,

\lambda\mapsto 1/\lambda
,

\lambda\mapsto-1/\lambda

pair these factors as

f_{1}=P_{1}P_{2}
,
f_{2}=P_{3}P_{4}

and two further pairings, recovering the
three elliptic quotients
E_{A}
,
E^{\prime}_{A}
,
E^{\prime\prime}_{A}

of §
4.1
.

3.
From quartic pairs to the genus-3 curve

Lemma 3.1
.

If a perfect Euler brick exists, then for the corresponding
s
,
the genus-3 hyperelliptic curve

C_{A}\colon w^{2}=\lambda^{8}+A(s)\,\lambda^{4}+1

has a non-degenerate rational point.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.2
, a brick gives
f_{1}=r^{2}
and

f_{2}=x^{2}
for some
\lambda\notin\{0,\pm 1\}
.
Then
f_{1}f_{2}=(rx)^{2}=\lambda^{8}+A\lambda^{4}+1
,
so
(\lambda,rx)
is a rational point on
C_{A}
with

\lambda\neq 0,\pm 1
.
∎

This forward implication is the only direction needed for
non-existence: if
C_{A}
has no non-degenerate rational points,
no brick can arise from the parameter
s
.

Remark 3.2

(Function field descent)
.

The converse, that every non-degenerate point on
C_{A}
yields a
brick, would require
f_{1}f_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
to imply

f_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
. Over the function field
\mathbb{Q}(s)(\lambda)
,
this holds because
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are coprime in the UFD

\mathbb{Q}(s)[\lambda]

(
\gcd(f_{1},f_{2})\mid\gcd(4c\lambda^{2},2(\lambda^{4}+1))=1

for
c\neq 0
).
At specific rational values, primes dividing
c(s_{0})
can
introduce common factors, so the specialised converse requires
additional verification. We do not use this converse.

4.
The genus-3 curve and its elliptic quotients

The curve
C_{A}\colon w^{2}=\lambda^{8}+A\lambda^{4}+1
has genus
3

(degree-
8
polynomial with no odd-degree terms).
Its
degenerate
rational points are

(\lambda,w)\in\{(0,\pm 1),(\pm 1,w_{0})\}
.

4.1.
Quotients and Jacobian decomposition

The curve carries a Klein four-group of involutions:

\iota_{1}\colon\lambda\mapsto-\lambda,\qquad\iota_{2}\colon\lambda\mapsto 1/\lambda,\qquad\iota_{3}=\iota_{1}\iota_{2}\colon\lambda\mapsto-1/\lambda.

Quotient by
\iota_{1}
.

Setting
\mu=\lambda^{2}
gives the genus-1 quartic

(15)

\mathcal{E}\colon w^{2}=\mu^{4}+A\mu^{2}+1.

Using the rational point
(\mu,w)=(0,1)
, this transforms to the
Weierstrass model

(16)

E_{A}\colon y^{2}=(x+A)(x-2)(x+2).

The back-transformation from Weierstrass coordinates to the quartic
model is

(17)

\mu^{2}=\frac{4(x+A)}{x^{2}-4},

valid for
x\neq\pm 2
.

Quotient by
\iota_{2}
.

Since
\iota_{2}(\lambda)=1/\lambda
sends
w^{2}=\lambda^{8}+A\lambda^{4}+1
to

(w/\lambda^{4})^{2}=\lambda^{-8}(\lambda^{8}+A\lambda^{4}+1)
, the correct invariants are

\eta=\lambda+1/\lambda
and
u=w/\lambda^{2}
. Dividing
w^{2}=\lambda^{8}+A\lambda^{4}+1

by
\lambda^{4}
gives
u^{2}=\lambda^{4}+A+\lambda^{-4}
, and since

\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}=\eta^{2}-2
:

(18)

E^{\prime}_{A}\colon u^{2}=\eta^{4}-4\eta^{2}+(A+2).

Quotient by
\iota_{3}
.

With invariants
\sigma=\lambda-1/\lambda
and
v=w/\lambda^{2}
, and using

\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}=\sigma^{2}+2
:

(19)

E^{\prime\prime}_{A}\colon v^{2}=\sigma^{4}+4\sigma^{2}+(A+2).

Proposition 4.1
.

The Jacobian of
C_{A}
decomposes (up to isogeny) as

J(C_{A})\sim E_{A}\times E^{\prime}_{A}\times E^{\prime\prime}_{A},

a product of three elliptic curves (
\dim J=g(C_{A})=3
).

Sketch.

The Klein four-group
\langle\iota_{1},\iota_{2}\rangle
acts on

J(C_{A})
, decomposing it into eigenspaces for the three non-trivial
characters (cf. Kani–Rosen
[
10
]
).
Each eigenspace is the image of the Prym variety of the
corresponding double cover
C_{A}\to C_{A}/\langle\iota_{k}\rangle
, which
is an elliptic curve since
g(C_{A}/\langle\iota_{k}\rangle)=1
for
each
k
. The three quotient maps

C_{A}\to\mathcal{E}
,
C_{A}\to E^{\prime}_{A}
,
C_{A}\to E^{\prime\prime}_{A}

induce an isogeny
J(C_{A})\to E_{A}\times E^{\prime}_{A}\times E^{\prime\prime}_{A}

with finite kernel.
∎

4.2.
The bridge: from
C_{A}
to
E_{A}

Let

\mathcal{E}\colon w^{2}=\mu^{4}+A\mu^{2}+1

be the quartic quotient of
C_{A}
.
Under the birational map
\mathcal{E}\dashrightarrow E_{A}
, a rational
point
P=(x,y)\in E_{A}(\mathbb{Q})
with

corresponds to a
rational point
(\mu,w)\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{Q})
, and

M(P):=\frac{4(x+A)}{x^{2}-4}=\mu^{2}.

Thus a non-degenerate rational point on
C_{A}
is equivalent to a
rational point
(\mu,w)\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{Q})
with

\mu
a nonzero rational square with
\mu\neq 1

(excluding
\mu=1
, which gives the degenerate points
\lambda=\pm 1
).
In Section
5
we recast this square condition on
\mu

in terms of the function
f
on
E_{A}
.

.

Let

or

5.
The Kummer character

Define the rational function on
E_{A}
:

(20)

f(P)=\frac{2y(P)}{(x(P)-2)(x(P)+2)}.

Lemma 5.1
.

\displaystyle f(P)^{2}=M(P)=\frac{4(x+A)}{x^{2}-4}
.

Proof.

f^{2}=\frac{4y^{2}}{((x-2)(x+2))^{2}}=\frac{4(x+A)(x-2)(x+2)}{(x^{2}-4)^{2}}=\frac{4(x+A)}{x^{2}-4}.\qed

In summary, the substitutions
\mu=\lambda^{2}
and
M=\mu^{2}
yield

\lambda\;\mapsto\;\lambda^{2}=\mu\;\mapsto\;\mu^{2}=M,\qquad M(P)=f(P)^{2},

connecting the genus-3 coordinate
\lambda
to the Weierstrass
function
f
. Concretely,

f(P)=\pm\,\lambda^{2}

(after a sign choice for
y
).

Corollary 5.2
.

C_{A}
has a non-degenerate rational point if and only if there
exists
P\in E_{A}(\mathbb{Q})
such that
f(P)
is a perfect square
in
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
with
f(P)\neq 1
.

Proof.

Let
P\in E_{A}(\mathbb{Q})
correspond to
(\mu,w)\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{Q})
.
By Lemma
5.1
,

f(P)^{2}=M(P)=\mu^{2},

so
f(P)=\pm\mu
.
Changing the sign of
y(P)
changes
f(P)
by a factor of
-1
,
hence we may arrange
f(P)=\mu
.
Therefore
f(P)
is a nonzero rational square different from
1

if and only if
\mu\in\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
with
\mu\neq 1
,
and the latter is equivalent to

\mu=\lambda^{2}
for some rational
\lambda\neq 0,\pm 1
, i.e. to a
non-degenerate rational point on
C_{A}
.
∎

Theorem 5.3

(Divisor of
f
)
.

\mathrm{div}(f)=(T_{1})-(T_{2})-(T_{3})+(O)
.

Proof.

The function
y
vanishes at
T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}
with a pole of

3
at

. The function

has a double zero at

and

; similarly

. Hence

\mathrm{div}(f)=(T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}-3O)-(2T_{2}-2O)-(2T_{3}-2O)=(T_{1})-(T_{2})-(T_{3})+(O)
.
∎

The character
\chi_{f}
on torsion

For a torsion point
T\in E_{A}(\mathbb{Q}(s))_{\mathrm{tors}}
, define

\chi_{f}(T)=\left[\frac{f(\cdot+T)}{f(\cdot)}\right]\;\in\;\frac{\mathbb{Q}(s)(E_{A})^{*}}{\mathbb{Q}(s)(E_{A})^{*2}}.

Theorem 5.4
.

The character
\chi_{f}
satisfies:

(a)

\chi_{f}(T_{1})=[-1]
and
\chi_{f}(T_{2})=[-1]

(non-trivial on
T_{1},T_{2}
),

(b)

\chi_{f}(T_{3})=[1]
(trivial on
T_{3}
),

(c)

\chi_{f}(T_{4})\neq 1
for any rational point
T_{4}

of order
4
.

Proof.

(a)

For
T_{2}=(2,0)
: using the addition formula for

P+T_{2}
on
E_{A}
with
P=(x_{1},y_{1})
, one computes

x(P{+}T_{2})=\frac{2(x_{1}+2A+2)}{x_{1}-2},\qquad y(P{+}T_{2})=\frac{-y_{1}\,(x(P{+}T_{2})-2)}{x_{1}-2}.

Substituting into
f(P+T_{2})/f(P)
and simplifying:

\frac{f(P+T_{2})}{f(P)}=-\frac{x_{1}^{2}-4}{4(x_{1}+A)}=-\frac{1}{f(P)^{2}},

using
f(P)^{2}=4(x_{1}+A)/(x_{1}^{2}-4)
.
In
\mathbb{Q}(s)(E_{A})^{*}/\mathbb{Q}(s)(E_{A})^{*2}
, this gives

\chi_{f}(T_{2})=[-1/f^{2}]=[-1]
.
For
T_{1}=(-A,0)
: similarly,

f(P+T_{1})/f(P)=-1
,
so
\chi_{f}(T_{1})=[-1]
.

(b)

For
T_{3}=(-2,0)
: the analogous computation gives

\frac{f(P+T_{3})}{f(P)}=\frac{x_{1}^{2}-4}{4(x_{1}+A)}=\frac{1}{f(P)^{2}},

so
\chi_{f}(T_{3})=[1/f^{2}]=[1]
.

(c)

We carry out the computation for
2T_{4}=T_{1}
(the other
cases are analogous).
The torsion group
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
has generators

T_{4}
(order
4
) and
T_{2}
(order
2
), so

T_{1}=2T_{4}
and
T_{3}=2T_{4}+T_{2}
.
Translation by
-T_{4}
in the group law sends

T_{1}\mapsto T_{4},\quad T_{2}\mapsto T_{2}-T_{4}=3T_{4}+T_{2},\quad T_{3}\mapsto T_{3}-T_{4}=T_{4}+T_{2},\quad O\mapsto-T_{4}=3T_{4}.

From
\mathrm{div}(f)=(T_{1})-(T_{2})-(T_{3})+(O)
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\bigl(f(\cdot+T_{4})/f(\cdot)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}(f(\cdot+T_{4}))-\mathrm{div}(f)

\displaystyle=\bigl[(T_{4})-(3T_{4}{+}T_{2})-(T_{4}{+}T_{2})+(3T_{4})\bigr]

\displaystyle\quad-\bigl[(2T_{4})-(T_{2})-(2T_{4}{+}T_{2})+(O)\bigr].

Listing all
8
torsion points with their net coefficients:

\begin{array}[]{c|cccccccc}&O&T_{4}&2T_{4}&3T_{4}&T_{2}&T_{4}{+}T_{2}&2T_{4}{+}T_{2}&3T_{4}{+}T_{2}\\
\hline\cr\text{coeff.}&-1&+1&-1&+1&+1&-1&+1&-1\end{array}

Every coefficient is
\pm 1
(odd).
If
f(\cdot+T_{4})/f(\cdot)=c\cdot g^{2}

for some
c\in\mathbb{Q}(s)^{*}
and
g\in\mathbb{Q}(s)(E_{A})^{*}
, then

\mathrm{div}(c\cdot g^{2})=2\,\mathrm{div}(g)
would have all even
coefficients, which is a contradiction.
Hence
\chi_{f}(T_{4})\neq 1
.

∎

Remark 5.5
.

Part (a) shows that translation by
T_{1}
or
T_{2}
flips the sign
of
f
modulo squares: if
f(P)\in\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
then

f(P+T_{1})\in-\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
, and vice versa.
Part (c) is the deeper structural obstruction:

4
-torsion translation generically prevents
f
from lying in

\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
at both
P
and
P+T_{4}
.

6.
Algebraic obstructions via
2
-descent

The
2
-descent map on
E_{A}

(see
[
4
,
15
]
; for descent on cuboid-related curves
see also
[
14
]
) sends a non-torsion point
P=(x,y)

to a triple of classes in
\mathbb{Q}^{*}/\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
.
Concretely, write
x=u/v^{2}
with
u\in\mathbb{Z}
,
v\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,

\gcd(u,v)=1
. For each root
r_{i}
(where
r_{1}=-A
,

r_{2}=2
,
r_{3}=-2
), define
\delta_{i}
to be the squarefree
part of the integer
v^{2}(x-r_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then

\delta(P)=(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\neq 0})^{3}\quad\text{with}\quad\delta_{1}\delta_{2}\delta_{3}\in\mathbb{Q}^{*2}.

We write
p\mid\delta_{i}
to mean that
p
divides the squarefree
integer
\delta_{i}
, equivalently
v_{p}(x-r_{i})
is odd.

Proposition 6.1
.

Over
\mathbb{Q}(s)
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle:=(r_{1}-r_{2})(r_{1}-r_{3})\equiv-1\pmod{\mathbb{Q}(s)^{*2}},

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}

\displaystyle:=(r_{2}-r_{1})(r_{2}-r_{3})\equiv 1\pmod{\mathbb{Q}(s)^{*2}}.

Proof.

\Delta_{1}=(-A-2)(-A+2)=A^{2}-4=(2-4c^{2})^{2}-4=16c^{2}(c^{2}-1)
.
Since
(1-c^{2})=16s^{2}(s^{2}-1)^{2}/(1+s^{2})^{4}
is a square in
\mathbb{Q}(s)
,
we get
\Delta_{1}\equiv-1
.
Similarly
\Delta_{2}=(2+A)\cdot 4=4(A+2)=4(4-4c^{2})=16(1-c^{2})\equiv 1
.
∎

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
P\in E_{A}(\mathbb{Q})
be a non-torsion point with
2
-descent class

(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3})
as defined above.

(a)

If
\delta_{3}=1
: then

f(P)\equiv 2\pmod{\mathbb{Q}^{*2}}
, hence
f(P)
is not a square.

(b)

If
p\mid\delta_{3}
(odd prime) then
p\mid\delta_{1}
or

p\mid\delta_{2}
.

Proof.

(a)

\delta_{3}=1
implies
\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}
(since

\delta_{1}\delta_{2}\delta_{3}=1
in
\mathbb{Q}^{*}/\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
).
Write
x+A=d\alpha^{2}
,
x-2=d\beta^{2}
,
x+2=\gamma^{2}

with
d
squarefree.
Then
y^{2}=d^{2}\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}\gamma^{2}
, so
y=d\alpha\beta\gamma

(up to sign), and

f=2y/((x-2)(x+2))=2d\alpha\beta\gamma/(d\beta^{2}\gamma^{2})=2\alpha/(\beta\gamma)\equiv 2\pmod{\mathbb{Q}^{*2}}
.
Since
2
is not a rational square,
f(P)\notin\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
.

(b)

From
y^{2}=(x+A)(x-2)(x+2)
we have

v_{p}(y^{2})
even.
If
v_{p}(x+2)
is odd, then
v_{p}(x+A)+v_{p}(x-2)
must be odd,
so at least one of
v_{p}(x+A)
,
v_{p}(x-2)
is odd, hence positive.
Therefore
p\mid\delta_{1}
or
p\mid\delta_{2}
.

∎

Remark 6.3
.

Computationally, a stronger statement holds: when both

v_{p}(x-2)
and
v_{p}(x+2)
are odd (i.e.
p\mid\delta_{2}
and

p\mid\delta_{3}
), the valuation
v_{p}(f(P))
is observed to be
odd in all tested cases, so
f(P)\notin\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
.
A full proof of this parity claim would require a more detailed

p
-adic analysis.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
p
be an odd prime dividing

c=(s^{2}-2s-1)(s^{2}+2s-1)/(1+s^{2})^{2}
.
Then
p
does not contribute to the
2
-Selmer rank of
E^{\prime}_{A}
.

Proof.

The Weierstrass form of
E^{\prime}_{A}
(obtained from the quartic
model (
18
) via the partition

\{\alpha,-\alpha\}\mid\{\beta,-\beta\}
of the four roots,
where
\alpha=2(s^{2}-1)/(1+s^{2})
and
\beta=4s/(1+s^{2})
)
has Weierstrass roots
0
,
-(\alpha-\beta)^{2}
,
-(\alpha+\beta)^{2}
.
The three root-difference products are:

(\alpha-\beta)^{2}(\alpha+\beta)^{2}=(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})^{2}

(always a square),
and
(\alpha\pm\beta)^{2}\cdot 4\alpha\beta
, whose squarefree
part is that of

4\alpha\beta=32s(s^{2}-1)/(1+s^{2})^{2}\equiv 2s(s^{2}-1)\pmod{\mathbb{Q}(s)^{*2}}
.
Since
\gcd(s^{2}\pm 2s-1,\;s(s-1)(s+1))=1
in
\mathbb{Q}[s]

(the roots
-1\pm\sqrt{2}
of
s^{2}+2s-1
are not in
\{0,\pm 1\}
),
every prime
p\mid c(s_{0})
divides the root-difference products
to an even power. Hence the local condition at
p
is
trivially satisfied and
p
does not enlarge the Selmer group.
∎

Remark 6.5

(Gaussian arithmetic)
.

Since each
f_{i}
is a norm in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
(Remark
2.3
),
primes
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
divide
f_{i}
to an even power
automatically. Only split primes
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
can obstruct

f_{i}
from being a square: the condition is that

v_{\pi}(z_{i})+v_{\bar{\pi}}(z_{i})
be even for each Gaussian prime

\pi\mid p
.

Computational verification

Recall from §
2.1
that the quartic pair
f_{1},f_{2}

depends on two Euclid pairs
(a,b)
and
(m,n)
: the first determines
the parameter
s=a/b
(and hence

), and the second
enters through
\lambda=m/n
in the normalised
forms (

).
All computations were performed with
PARI/GP
[

]
(rank certifications via

)
and SageMath
[
12
]
(curve construction and point searches).
Scripts are available at

.

(1)

For all
1\leq b<a\leq 1000
and
1\leq n<m\leq 1000

with
\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(m,n)=1
,
a-b
and
m-n
odd:

is never a perfect square.
(The coprimality and parity filters reduce the raw

1000^{4}
loop to roughly

tuples actually tested.)

(2)

For the five specialisations where all Mordell–Weil

(

,
\tfrac{18}{47}
,
\tfrac{23}{59}

\tfrac{23}{64}

\tfrac{29}{65}

a modular search over
175{,}418
lattice points with
45

finds zero candidates for

.

(3)

For every tested tuple
(a,b,m,n)
, there exists a prime

with

odd (a

), preventing

from being a square.
No single prime works universally; the blocker varies with the

8.
Discussion and further directions

Summary of proved results

The following hold unconditionally:

(a)

A perfect Euler brick produces a non-degenerate rational point
on
C_{A}
(Lemma
3.1
).

(b)

The Kummer character satisfies
\chi_{f}(T_{1})=\chi_{f}(T_{2})=[-1]
,

\chi_{f}(T_{3})=[1]
, and
\chi_{f}(T_{4})\neq 1
for every rational
point
T_{4}
of order
4

(Theorem
5.4
).

(c)

If
\delta_{3}=1
, then
f(P)\notin\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
; moreover,
every odd prime dividing
\delta_{3}
also divides
\delta_{1}

or
\delta_{2}

(Theorem
6.2
).

Computational evidence

For all parameters up to
10^{3}
, the product
f_{1}f_{2}
is never
a perfect square. For every tested parameter tuple, there exists an
obstructing prime for
f_{1}f_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
; it is either
p=2
or

p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, and no prime
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
appears in the
computations. The obstructing prime varies with the parameters (no
single prime works universally). In addition, the rank computations
produce
42
certified rank-
0
specialisations for
E_{A}
and
54

for
E^{\prime}_{A}
; by Proposition
4.2
, any injective
specialisation among these would force the corresponding generic rank
to be
0
.

The remaining gap

The results above establish several necessary conditions for a
perfect Euler brick, but do not constitute a complete proof of
non-existence. Specifically: Theorem
6.2
excludes
points with
\delta_{3}=1
and constrains primes dividing
\delta_{3}
,
but does not rule out the case where
\delta_{3}\neq 1
and
all odd prime factors of
\delta_{3}
divide
\delta_{1}
(but
not
\delta_{2}
). Rational points of
E_{A}
in this residual
descent class could in principle yield
f(P)\in\mathbb{Q}^{*2}
, and
no unconditional argument currently excludes them.
The computational verification covers all parameters up to
10^{3}

but is inherently finite.

A genus-5 covering obstruction

The non-triviality
\chi_{f}(T_{4})\neq 1

(Theorem
5.4
(c)) implies that the double cover

C_{T_{4}}\colon z^{2}=f(\cdot+T_{4})/f(\cdot)

of
E_{A}
is a curve of genus
5
(by Riemann–Hurwitz:
8
branch
points on a genus-
1
base give
g=2\cdot 1-1+8/2=5
).
By Faltings’ theorem
[
8
]
,
C_{T_{4}}
has at most finitely
many rational points. If moreover

\mathrm{rk}\,J(C_{T_{4}})(\mathbb{Q})<5
,
Chabauty–Coleman
[
5
,
17
,
18
]

would make this finiteness effective;
see
[
3
]
for computational experiments on
genus-
3
curves and
[
2
]
for the quadratic
Chabauty extension to higher-rank cases.
In combination with the relation

z^{2}=f(\cdot+T_{4})/f(\cdot)
, this may constrain points for which

f(P)
and
f(P+T_{4})
are simultaneously squareclasses.

For all tested specialisations with
a,b\leq 80
, the rank data
are consistent with this Chabauty hypothesis (the maximum observed
rank sum
\mathrm{rk}\,E_{A}+\mathrm{rk}\,E^{\prime}_{A}
is
4
, at
s=18/47
).
However, the Jacobian
J(C_{T_{4}})\sim E_{A}\times\mathrm{Prym}_{4}

involves an abelian
4
-fold that we do not decompose further, and
for larger parameters, rank sums exceeding
5
cannot be excluded.
A full development of this approach would require
either an explicit Prym decomposition or a uniform rank bound.

Connection to
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})

The numerator of
c(s)
factors as

s^{4}-6s^{2}+1=(s^{2}+2s-1)(s^{2}-2s-1)

This factorisation appears in Asiryan’s work

Adapting that approach from irreducibility to simultaneous
representability remains open.

This appears to be the most natural next step within the

from torsion to the full

(a,b,m,n)

at which

Find a Brauer–Manin obstruction on the surface

;
see
[
6
]
for methods on hyperelliptic
curves and
[

]
for a geometric analysis of the
cuboid surface.

The problem in simplest form

In its most elementary formulation
(cf. Remark
2.3
):

Do there exist coprime positive integer pairs
a>b>0
and

m>n>0
, each of opposite parity, such that
L_{1}^{2}+L_{3}^{2}
and

L_{2}^{2}+L_{3}^{2}
are simultaneously perfect squares, where

L_{1}=2(a^{2}-b^{2})mn,\quad L_{2}=4abmn,\quad L_{3}=(a^{2}+b^{2})(m^{2}-n^{2})\,?

Since
L_{1}^{2}+L_{2}^{2}=(2mn(a^{2}+b^{2}))^{2}
is automatically a
perfect square, the question asks whether two Pythagorean triples
sharing a common leg
L_{3}
, whose other legs form a third
Pythagorean triple, can simultaneously close.
