Misdiagnosis of ameloblastoma: A case report and literature review
Authors: Yihan Huang, Qingqing Ding, Weiming Zhang, Min Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ameloblastomas are slow-growing benign tumors that mostly occur in the mandible, and the most conventional histopathological subtype is follicular. This report describes the case of a 60-year-old man with an ameloblastoma in the right maxilla. In March 2012, the patient was diagnosed with basal cell adenocarcinoma at a local hospital. In April 2012, imaging and clinical assessment performed at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University confirmed the diagnosis of basal cell adenocarcinoma. A sinus coronal enhanced scan showed a well-defined lesion in the right maxilla. A total right maxillary osteotomy was performed under general anesthesia to remove the tumor. The postoperative histopathology report showed that the mass was an ameloblastoma. A literature review was conducted to review age and sex predisposition, histopathological subtypes, and initial clinical symptoms of maxillary ameloblastoma and to compare previous findings with those of the present case. In addition, we explored the potential causes of misdiagnosis.

Introduction

Ameloblastoma, which generally occurs in the vicinity of the mandibular molars or ramus, is a benign and locally aggressive odontogenic tumor. 1 The tumor arises from residual odontogenic epithelium or stratified squamous epithelium of tumors occurring in the mandible and maxilla. 2 Approximately 80% of ameloblastomas occur in the mandible, primarily in the third molar region; the remaining 20% occur in the maxilla. 3 They are characterized by painless facial swelling and slow but aggressive progression. Patients usually seek medical advice for other symptoms, such as dental problems, incidental findings during an examination, or significant facial swelling.

The 2017 World Health Organization classification categorizes ameloblastoma into four types, including ameloblastoma, unicystic ameloblastoma (UA), extraosseous/peripheral ameloblastoma, and metastasizing ameloblastoma. 4 In addition, as basal cell myeloblastoma is an odontogenic variant (benign), it may have pathological manifestations similar to those of basal cell carcinoma (BCC) or basal squamous cell carcinoma (malignant). Compared with ameloblastoma, immunohistochemistry shows that the expression levels of Ki-67 and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) are significantly higher in BCC. However, it is difficult to differentiate the two using current histopathological methods. This report describes the case of a patient with maxillary ameloblastoma (a rare occurrence) that was previously misdiagnosed as basal cell adenocarcinoma. We explored the uncommon basal cell ameloblastoma that had occurred in the maxilla and reviewed the previously reported cases of maxillary ameloblastoma.

Case report

The patient, a 60-year-old man, developed facial swelling in April 2010; he first experienced right-sided nasal bleeding in March 2012. On the right cheek, there was a round swelling that could be palpated without any tenderness. There was recurrent bleeding from the right nasal cavity that occurred approximately 10 times each month; the average bleeding amount was approximately 100 mL per session. The bleeding could be stopped by self-application of cold compresses and nasal tamponade. He visited a local hospital, where sinus computed tomography (CT) revealed that his right maxillary sinus had a mass causing bone destruction (Figure 1). In March 2012, histopathological examination at the local hospital suggested basal cell adenocarcinoma. In April 2012, sinus coronal enhanced CT at The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University revealed imaging findings similar to those obtained at the local hospital. Pathology consultation at the hospital established a diagnosis of basal cell ameloblastoma of the right maxillary sinus.

Preoperative (a and b) axial and (c) coronary CT showing a tumorous mass in the right maxillary sinus, with destruction of the rear outer walls, spreading to the pterygopalatine fossa, infratemporal fossa, ethmoid sinus and the anterior part of the masseter. CT: computed tomography.

As a result of the preoperative nasal endoscopy, viscous, bloody discharge was observed in the right middle nasal meatus. None of the other preoperative tests revealed an absolute contraindication to surgery. The patient underwent a total maxillectomy with lateral nasal incision on the right side under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, the anterolateral wall of the right maxillary sinus was destroyed, and a dark-red mass bulged into the labiogingival sulcus, involving the second premolar to the third molar, right infratemporal fossa, and pterygopalatine fossa. During the procedure, the right maxillary bone and swelling were completely removed. The operation was uneventful, with intraoperative bleeding of 300 mL as well as transfusion of 400 mL whole blood and 200 mL plasma.

The excised tissue was sent for histopathological examination. The excisional biopsy report revealed that the tumor was an ameloblastoma with dimensions of 6 × 3 ×2.5 cm. The epithelial cells were arranged in islands or follicles surrounded by the connective tissue (Figure 2). No immunohistochemical (IHC) evaluation was performed for the tumor.

Basaloid cells in the follicle lacking reverse polarity of nuclei. Hyperchromatic basaloid cells were detected in the center of the follicle, with a lack of stellate reticulum-like cells.

At 7 months postoperatively, the patient presented to the Burn and Plastic Surgery Department for reconstruction of the right side of the nose using a facial pedicle musculocutaneous flap. Although the patient was not followed up, he was alive at the time of writing this manuscript. The reporting of this study conforms to the Case Report (CARE) guidelines. 5

Discussion

In this report, we presented the case of a 60-year-old man with maxillary ameloblastoma. We considered that the patient had basal cell ameloblastoma because it had been previously diagnosed as basal cell adenoma and basal cell adenocarcinoma. Histopathologically, ameloblastoma has seven variants: follicular, plexiform, acanthomatous, basal cell, granular cell, keratopapillary, and desmoplatic ameloblastoma. The follicular subtype represents the most conventional type of ameloblastoma. Basal cell ameloblastoma is one of the least common types of ameloblastoma, which is difficult to distinguish from BCC. A basal cell ameloblastoma is characterized by islands of uniform baseloid cells in a mature fibrous connective tissue stroma. Peripheral cells are columnar and exhibit reversal of polarity. The cells exhibit intense basophilic staining and nearly uniform staining intensity. In some islands, the central portion is completely replaced with baseloid cells, while in others, there are scanty stellate reticulum-like areas. Baseloid cells are hyperchromatic without palisading of nuclei. 6 The majority of ameloblastoma cases occur in the mandible, with fewer occurring in the maxilla. Furthermore, ameloblastoma of the maxilla generally occurs in the posterior region of the molars, consistent with the intraoperative findings in this case. In the present case, basal cells were present in the maxilla, which is a very rare occurrence.

We conducted a PubMed search for the keywords “(maxilla) AND (ameloblastoma) AND (case report).” We obtained a total of 67 case reports (involving 75 cases) from the PubMed database (Table S1). In these reports, we found some typical clinical features of ameloblastoma. First, most patients were female; young, especially teenagers aged 10–19 years; and more likely to develop an ameloblastoma (Figure 3). Second, maxillary ameloblastomas are most commonly found in the anterior part of the maxilla, from the central incisors to the second premolar; therefore, patients usually consult their dentist if they discover a mass in the maxilla. Invasive masses can extend into the maxillary sinuses and even into the orbits. Third, the number of case reports has increased over time. As a result of modern diagnostic methods (biopsy and histopathology examination), the diagnosis rate of ameloblastoma has improved, based on the number of cases reported in different generations of patients, with both diagnostic methods exhibiting a similar use rate. Fourth, the most common histologic subtype in our study was desmoplastic ameloblastoma. Six cases have been reported; all patients were female, tended to be young, had a good prognosis, and had no recurrence during the follow-up period. In the remaining cases, UA, odonto ameloblastoma, and basal cell ameloblastoma were the most common histological subtypes, all of which had a clear female predominance. Fifth, all the histological subtypes had odontogenic epithelial islands and connective tissue stroma. Sixth, currently, IHC evaluation is less commonly performed, and only two of these case reports employed this technique. However, many recent studies have found that immunohistochemistry can be helpful for tumors that are difficult to identify using histopathological examination using markers such as Ki-67, which may help differentiate BCC from its nearest benign mimic, trichoepithelioma. 7 MMP-2 expression is significantly higher in both epithelial and stromal components of ameloblastoma, and Jawad et al. reported a significant difference in favor of BCC for the epithelial expression of MMP-9. 8 Seventh, the recurrence rate was 17.4%, and in most cases, recurrence occurred frequently within a few years with no specific histological subtype predilection. Furthermore, curettage and lumpectomy appeared more likely to cause ameloblastoma recurrence.

Age- and sex-based distribution of the reported cases of ameloblastoma.

Several clinical characteristics are associated with ameloblastoma, including unilateral facial deformity, toothache, headache, nasal obstruction, epistaxis, and visual disturbance. 9 Our patient was an adult male, not a young man, when he experienced the symptoms. The first symptom was painless facial swelling, consistent with the clinical features of an ameloblastoma. However, the most unique aspect of this case is that the patient had recently experienced frequent rhinorrhea on the right side of his nose, which may have been caused by the tumor in the maxillary sinus. It may also have been responsible for the previous misdiagnosis of BCC, which often manifests as erosion, scabs, or bleeding in the central part of the lesion. A similar predilection site may also account for the misdiagnosis of BCC. All ameloblastomas occur on the face, while 70% of BCCs occur on the face, most commonly on the nose and eyelids, and the rest on the trunk and extremities. On radiological examination, the right maxillary mass in this patient showed an isosignal with the surrounding connective tissue and possible adjacent bone destruction on CT; however, BCC commonly presents as a skin lesion with low metastatic potential and appears isoattenuating or hypoattenuating compared with nearby muscle and bone tissue. Therefore, the two conditions could not be distinguished on CT. The pathogenesis of this case is that the epithelial cells are arranged in islands or follicles surrounded by the connective tissue. In addition, the nodular type, which is the most common form of BCC, shows basaloid cells with peripheral nuclei arranged in a palisading or picket-fence pattern. 10 Many similarities exist between the ameloblastoma in this case and BCC in the absence of IHC markers such as Ki-67, MMP-2, and MMP-9.

Radical resection is the recommended, standard therapy for ameloblastoma. It is essential to obtain surgical margins free of neoplasia to reduce the likelihood of local recurrence, which depends on the histopathological subtype and surgical technique. 11 Basal cell ameloblastoma is associated with a higher local infiltration rate. Maxillary ameloblastomas are more difficult to manage than mandibular tumors owing to the thin and fragile bone of the maxilla and its proximity to the nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, orbit, and vital structures of the skull base. 12 Among the reported cases of basal cell ameloblastoma of the maxilla, the recurrence rate remains unknown, mainly owing to the low number of documented cases. Our patient underwent a total right maxillary osteotomy with adequate surgical excision, and there were no signs of recurrence at 7 months postoperatively, consistent with the lower recurrence rates following radical treatment reported in other studies. 11

This study has a significant limitation in that this case report lacks follow-up data, making it impossible to compare our findings with those of other cases. Second, we did not perform IHC analysis of the ameloblastoma. Although there are currently no IHC markers for ameloblastoma diagnosis, there exist relevant markers for basal cell adenocarcinoma detection, such as pancytokeratin, cytokeratin 7, carcinoembryonic antigen monoclonal, p63, p40, S100, calponin, and cytokeratin 5/6.

In conclusion, basal cell ameloblastoma occurring in the maxilla is relatively uncommon. Our case study provides an overview of symptoms, imaging results, diagnosis, and treatment of ameloblastoma. In addition, we conducted a literature review to summarize the histopathological subtypes as well as age- and sex-based predisposition to maxillary ameloblastoma.
