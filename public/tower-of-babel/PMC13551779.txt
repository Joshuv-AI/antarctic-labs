Physiological and transcriptomic analyses elucidate the effects of nitrogen form ratios on strawberry growth and development
Authors: Shen Chen, Han Gao, Yongqi Sheng, Yu Cheng, Jinshun Hua, Weiwen Wu, Huanxiang Ma, Qijia Chen, Hongsheng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In protected strawberry cultivation, the application of a mixture of ammonium (NH₄⁺) and nitrate (NO₃⁻) nitrogen typically yields better plant growth and development compared to treatments using either nitrogen form alone. To elucidate the underlying physiological and molecular mechanisms, this study employed ‘Benihoppe’ strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa Duch.) as plant material. Under a constant total nitrogen concentration (6 mmol/L), treatments with different ammonium-to-nitrate ratios (NH₄⁺:NO₃⁻) were established. Through the determination of physiological indices and endogenous hormone levels, combined with transcriptome analysis, we systematically investigated the regulatory role of nitrogen form ratios on strawberry growth. The 7:3 NH₄⁺:NO₃⁻ ratio treatment (CL₂) significantly promoted plant height and petiole growth, increased fruit yield, but had no significant effect on fruit size. During the green fruit stage, this treatment notably increased the levels of auxin, cytokinin, and gibberellin. Transcriptome analysis revealed a significant enrichment in plant hormone signal transduction pathways, with related differentially expressed genes (AUX/IAA, SAUR, GH3, CRE1, and B-ARR) being markedly regulated. This study proposes that a 7:3 ammonium-to-nitrate ratio helps regulate strawberry growth and improve yield, with the underlying mechanism likely involving the coordinated regulation of plant nitrogen uptake and hormone signaling, thereby providing a theoretical basis for precise nitrogen fertilization management in protected strawberry cultivation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09054-z.

Introduction

Strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa), also known as the garden strawberry, is a perennial herbaceous plant belonging to the family Rosaceae [1]. The plant typically grows in clumps and exhibits characteristic morphological features including a shortened stem and stolons [2]. Its root system is fibrous, primarily concentrated within the top 15 cm of the soil layer [3], resulting in a limited capacity for nutrient uptake from deeper soil horizons. Therefore, cultivation commonly requires loose, fertile, slightly acidic soil with a pH between 5.5 and 6.5 [4]. Benihoppe strawberry, an early-maturing and elite cultivar developed from the cross between ‘Akihime’ and ‘Sachinoka’, is characterized by several advantageous agronomic traits. These include strong environmental adaptability, shallow dormancy, high natural fruit-setting ability, large fruit size, and excellent fruit quality. This cultivar prefers slightly acidic sandy loam soil. When planted in September, the fruits typically reach maturity by mid-to-late December.

From a nutritional perspective, plants predominantly rely on inorganic nitrogen forms during growth and development, with only a limited amount of organic nitrogen being directly absorbed [5]. Inorganic nitrogen is primarily taken up and utilized as nitrate (NO₃⁻) and ammonium (NH₄⁺) [6]. Studies have shown significant differences among plant species in their efficiency of absorbing, assimilating, and transporting these two nitrogen forms. Such variation exists not only between different plant families (grasses vs. legumes) but also within the same species across developmental stages or under varying environmental conditions. For instance, under waterlogged conditions, plant roots often exhibit a heightened preference for ammonium [7].

In recent years, the regulatory mechanisms of nitrogen form ratios on plant growth and development have become a research focus. Numerous studies indicate that different nitrate-to-ammonium ratios significantly alter plant growth dynamics and the accumulation of secondary metabolites by influencing nitrogen uptake and utilization efficiency.In terms of growth regulation, Pyrus betulifolia seedlings showed optimal shoot biomass accumulation at a NO₃⁻:NH₄⁺ ratio of 3:1, while a 1:1 ratio promoted the best overall physiological performance [8]. For orchids such as Anoectochilus roxburghii and Anoectochilus yunnanensis, tissue-cultured plantlets exhibited optimal stem growth under a low NH₄⁺:NO₃⁻ ratio, though field cultivation required a relatively higher proportion of nitrate [9]. In flue-cured tobacco, a 1:1 nitrate-ammonium ratio combined with deep placement at 25 cm significantly improved plant growth in southern Henan [10]. Notably, Cyrtanthus sanguineus (Amaryllidaceae) displayed the smallest leaf area under pure nitrate treatment, while a 1:1 nitrate-ammonium ratio resulted in maximal leaf area [11]. Species-specific responses are also evident in loquat seedlings, which show a preferential uptake and utilization of ammonium [12]. Ginkgo seedlings exhibited developmental stage-dependent responses to nitrate-ammonium ratios, with both one-year-old seedlings and two-year-old half-sib families performing best under a 3:1 ratio [13]. Mongolian oak (Quercus mongolica) seedlings demonstrated differentiated responses in biomass accumulation and photosynthetic characteristics: a 2:6 ratio favored biomass accumulation, whereas a 4:4 ratio significantly enhanced photosynthetic pigment and soluble protein content [14]. These studies collectively reveal that plant responses to nitrogen forms are highly species-specific and developmentally regulated. For Ginkgo biloba seedlings, a nitrate-to-ammonium ratio of 75:25 resulted in a total flavonoid yield of 33.93 mg per plant and a terpene lactone yield of 27.88 mg per plant, demonstrating a significant metabolic regulatory effect [15]. This indicates that ammonium and nitrate nitrogen play distinct roles in promoting the synthesis of medicinal components and secondary metabolites in plants. Before application, it is essential to understand both the plant’s adaptability to different nitrogen forms and the target products intended for synthesis, thereby selecting the appropriate nitrogen form and exploring its optimal ratio. Furthermore, studies on the effects of nitrate-ammonium ratio fertilization on crop yield and quality also extend to other plants such as tomato [16] and pomegranate [17], sometimes investigated in interaction with other factors (light intensity, calcium chloride spraying). In summary, clarifying the species-specific nitrogen form preference is a prerequisite for precise nitrogen fertilization management in crop cultivation.

Recent molecular studies have employed RNA-seq to analyze differential gene expression in axillary buds of woodland strawberry (Fragaria vesca) wild-type and mutant plants. The mutant showed significant upregulation of cytokinin biosynthesis and signaling genes and downregulation of TCP transcription factors, revealing that the transcription factor MYB117a inhibits axillary bud elongation by modulating CK homeostasis. This finding provides a theoretical basis for optimizing plant architecture and yield in strawberry [18]. Another study combined transcriptomics with yeast one-hybrid screening to identify the upstream transcription factor FaSCL8, which regulates the vacuolar phosphate transporter gene FaVPT1 in strawberry. FaSCL8 activates FaVPT1 expression, promoting phosphorus and sugar accumulation in fruits, thereby accelerating ripening and anthocyanin synthesis. Transcriptome data further indicated that FaSCL8 overexpression significantly upregulates ripening-related genes [19]. Additionally, transient transformation and transcriptomic analysis revealed that the MADS-box transcription factor FaMADS6 negatively regulates anthocyanin accumulation, influencing fruit color development [20]. Transcriptome profiles across different fruit developmental stages provide essential data resources for strawberry genetic improvement [21].

This study addresses the central issue in nitrogen form management for protected strawberry cultivation: while the integrated application of ammonium (NH₄⁺) and nitrate (NO₃⁻) nitrogen is generally superior to using either form alone [22], and the specific ratio significantly affects plant morphology and fruit quality, the molecular mechanisms by which it regulates fruit development remain unclear. Particularly against the backdrop of widespread adoption of fertigation technology, determining how to achieve precise regulation of the ammonium-to-nitrate ratio based on molecular mechanisms to simultaneously enhance both yield and quality represents a key technical challenge for current industry efforts aimed at improving efficiency and product value. To address this challenge, this study employed ‘Benihoppe’ strawberry as plant material and systematically established treatments with different ammonium-to-nitrate ratios under a constant total nitrogen concentration. The core strength of this research lies in its integrated analytical strategy, which combines physiological measurements, comparative transcriptomics, and targeted quantification of plant hormones via HPLC. The study aims to achieve the following objectives: (1) Elucidate the physiological effects: Clarify the specific impacts of different ammonium-to-nitrate ratios on plant growth (plant height, petiole length) and fruit quality traits (sugar-acid content, soluble solids); (2) Decipher the dynamic regulatory network: Through correlation analysis of transcriptomic and hormonal data, reveal the interplay between endogenous hormone levels and genome-wide expression patterns during fruit development; (3) Uncover the core mechanism: Identify the key signaling pathways through which nitrogen form ratios regulate fruit development, thereby providing a solid molecular biological foundation for the precise management of nitrogen fertilization in protected strawberry cultivation. This research not only holds promise for clarifying the theoretical mechanisms by which nitrogen forms regulate quality formation, but its integrated approach also offers a novel perspective for the molecular design of nutrient management in crops.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and experimental design

Our experiment was conducted in a multi-span connected greenhouse, a large-scale environmentally controlled greenhouse for commercial protected horticulture) at the Institute of Modern Horticultural Industry, Yangzhou University, located in the Xijiang Ecological Park, Shatou Town, Guangling District, Yangzhou City. The plant material consisted of virus-free seedlings of the strawberry cultivar ‘Benihoppe’, which were propagated through tissue culture. The virus-free status of seedlings was confirmed via reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) detection of four major strawberry viruses: Strawberry Mottle Virus (SMoV), Strawberry Mild Yellow Edge Virus (SMYEV), Strawberry Crinkle Virus (SCV) and Strawberry Vein Banding Virus (SVBV); only seedlings with negative results for all viruses were used for the experiment (Supplementary Fig. 3). Under normal cultivation conditions, the average weight of primary fruits is 25–30 g, with a maximum fruit weight exceeding 50 g; at commercial maturity, the fruit has a soluble solids content of 11%–14%, soluble sugar content of 7%–9%, and titratable acidity of 0.6%–0.8%, with a balanced sugar-acid ratio, rich aroma, and excellent fresh-eating quality. The plants were grown in rectangular containers filled with a substrate composed of peat, vermiculite, and perlite mixed in a ratio of 3:1:1. The substrate was characterized by loose texture, good aeration, uniform particle size, and adequate buffering capacity, with a pH of 5.5–6.5 and an EC value below 0.5 mS・cm⁻¹. The plants were maintained under a daily light duration of 8–10 h and relative humidity of 70%-80%. Temperature was controlled as follows: during the vegetative growth period, day temperature 20–25 °C, night temperature 10–15 °C, with a day-night temperature difference of 8–12 °C; during the fruit growth and development period, day temperature 18–23 °C, night temperature 8–12 °C, with a day-night temperature difference of 10–12 °C.

Based on a modified Yamazaki nutrient solution formula [23], the total nitrogen concentration was fixed at 6 mmol·L⁻¹ for all treatments, with five NH₄⁺:NO₃⁻ molar ratios set as the experimental variables: 10:0 (CL1, pure ammonium control), 7:3 (CL2, high ammonium ratio treatment), 5:5 (CL3, equal ratio control), 3:7 (CL4, high nitrate ratio treatment), and 0:10 (CL5, pure nitrate control). The above one-to-one correspondence between CL labels and NH₄⁺:NO₃⁻ ratios applies to the full text. Each treatment included 100 plants. The NH₄⁺:NO₃⁻ ratio was controlled by adjusting the dosage of ammonium nitrogen sources ((NH₄)₂SO₄, NH₄H₂PO₄) and nitrate nitrogen sources (Ca(NO₃)₂·4 H₂O, KNO₃), with the total nitrogen concentration unchanged. The specific concentrations of macronutrient sources are provided in Table 1; the micronutrient components (stock solution C) were identical across all treatments, and the detailed formula has been moved to Supplementary Table S1. Stock solutions were prepared and diluted prior to use. To prevent precipitation, three separate stock solutions (A, B, and C) were prepared: A and B were concentrated 100-fold, and C was concentrated 1000-fold. The pH of the nutrient solution was adjusted using a 10% phosphoric acid solution and maintained within the range of 5.5–6.5. The EC value was initially set to approximately 0.7 mS·cm⁻¹ during the seedling stage, increased to around 1.0 mS·cm⁻¹ as plant growth accelerated, and gradually raised from 1.0 to 1.2 mS·cm⁻¹ from fruit expansion through maturation.

Determination of plant growth and yield parameters

Determination of vegetative growth indicators of plants

Plant height, petiole diameter, and petiole length were measured at three key growth stages: 45 days after transplanting (vegetative growth stage), full flowering stage (10 days after initial flowering), and green fruit stage of the first crop of fruits (fruit expansion stage). For each treatment, 15 plants with consistent growth vigor were randomly selected, with 5 plants as one plot, totaling 3 biological replicates. Specifically, plant height was measured as the vertical height from the rhizome base of the plant to the top of the latest fully expanded leaf. For petiole diameter and petiole length, the petiole of the second fully expanded functional leaf of the plant was selected: petiole diameter was measured at the widest part of the middle of the petiole, and petiole length was measured from the base of the petiole to the insertion point of the leaf blade.

Determination of fruit morphology, single fruit weight and yield indicators

Fruit longitudinal diameter, transverse diameter, and single fruit weight were measured at the commercial maturity stage of each crop of fruits (when more than 80% of the fruit surface turns red, meeting the commercial harvesting standard). For each batch, 30 commercially mature fruits with consistent development, no diseases or pests, and no mechanical damage were randomly selected from each treatment, divided into 3 biological replicates. The longitudinal diameter of the fruit was measured as the vertical length from the fruit apex to the base of the calyx, and the transverse diameter was measured as the horizontal diameter at the widest part of the fruit, both determined with a digital vernier caliper. The fresh weight of a single fruit was weighed using an electronic analytical balance with a precision of 0.01 g, and the average single fruit weight and maximum single fruit weight were calculated. For yield determination, fruits that reached the commercial maturity standard from each plant in each treatment were harvested in batches throughout the entire harvest period. The total yield per plant was accumulated and recorded, and the average total yield of 3 biological replicates for each treatment was calculated.

Measurement of fruit quality indicators

Core quality indicators including soluble sugar content, titratable acid content, and soluble solids content of fruits were sampled and determined at the commercial maturity stage of the first crop of fruits. For each treatment, 15 commercially mature fruits with no diseases or pests and uniform development were randomly selected. After removing the fruit stalk, achenes and calyxes, the mesocarp tissue was taken and mixed evenly, divided into 3 biological replicates, with 3 technical replicates set for each sample.

Soluble sugar content was determined by the anthrone-sulfuric acid colorimetric method; titratable acid content was determined by the NaOH neutralization titration method; soluble solids content was determined with a hand-held digital refractometer. Before detection, the flesh tissue was ground into homogenate in liquid nitrogen, centrifuged at 4000 r/min for 10 min, and the supernatant was taken for measurement.

RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing

Sampling protocol

Samples for transcriptome sequencing were collected at three key developmental stages of strawberry fruits: green fruit stage (15 days after anthesis of the first crop of fruits, the fruit is completely green with no sign of color change), color turning stage (25 days after anthesis, 30%–50% of the fruit surface turns red), and commercial maturity stage (red ripening stage, 35 days after anthesis, more than 80% of the fruit surface turns red).

For each developmental stage of each treatment, fruits from 6 plants with consistent growth vigor were randomly selected. After removing the fruit stalk, achenes and calyxes, the mesocarp tissue was quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and transferred to an ultra-low temperature refrigerator at -80 °C for storage. A mixed sample of fruits from 3 plants was set as one biological replicate, with 3 biological replicates for each treatment at each developmental stage.

Sequencing process

Total RNA was extracted from the samples using the Plant RNAprep Pure Micro Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). The concentration, purity and integrity of RNA were detected using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). High-quality RNA with OD260/280 between 1.8 and 2.1, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, and RIN value ≥ 6.5 was screened for subsequent library construction.

3 µg of high-quality RNA was used as the starting template, and the cDNA library was constructed according to the instructions of the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The library was purified using the AMPure XP bead system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, CA, USA) to enrich cDNA fragments of approximately 200 bp in length. The adapter-ligated fragments were amplified by 15 cycles of PCR with Illumina-specific primers. After the PCR products were purified again with the AMPure XP system, the concentration and fragment size distribution of the library were evaluated using an Agilent high-sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent, Suzhou, China). Finally, the qualified libraries were sequenced in paired-end mode on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform.

Identification of differentially expressed genes

Clean reads were aligned to the strawberry reference genome (assembly v4.0.a2; https://www.rosaceae.org/species/fragaria_vesca/genome_v4.0.a2, accessed on 28 February 2025) using HISAT2 software. Uniquely mapped reads were then assigned to corresponding gene features based on the genome annotation file with feature Counts. Gene expression levels were normalized and quantified as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of exon per Million mapped fragments). To identify expression differences among sample groups, differential expression analysis was performed using the edgeR [24] package in R. Transcripts with an adjusted *p*-value (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute log₂ fold change > 1 were defined as significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

RNA-seq data analysis and functional annotation

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted on the annotated gene sets, with statistically significant terms filtered at a threshold of *p* < 0.05. The analysis was performed using the cluster Profiler R package (version 3.8.1). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database serves as a key resource for systematically understanding higher-order functions and biological utilities within cellular systems, organisms, and ecosystems. Its knowledge base integrates molecular-level data derived from genomic sequencing and various high-throughput technologies (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/; accessed on 28 February 2025). To identify biologically relevant pathways, the statistical enrichment of differentially expressed genes in KEGG pathways was also evaluated using the clusterProfiler package [25].

Targeted phytohormone quantification via UPLC-MS/MS

Sampling protocol

The sampling period, sampling site, and biological replicate settings for the determination of endogenous phytohormone content were completely consistent with those for transcriptome sequencing sampling. Targeted phytohormone quantification was mainly performed on strawberry fruit samples at the green fruit stage, while covering the color turning stage and commercial maturity stage. During sampling, fruits with consistent development were selected. After removing the fruit stalk, achenes and calyxes, the mesocarp tissue was frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into uniform powder, which was stored in an ultra-low temperature refrigerator at -80 °C for later use, with 3 biological replicates for each treatment at each stage.

Detection process

100 mg of powder sample was weighed, and endogenous phytohormones in the sample, including auxin (IAA), cytokinin (CTK), gibberellin (GA), abscisic acid (ABA), and jasmonic acid (JA), were extracted by low-temperature methanol extraction. After purification by a solid-phase extraction column and drying under nitrogen blowing, the sample was redissolved to 1 mL with chromatographic methanol, filtered through a 0.22 μm organic phase filter membrane, and quantitatively detected by ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). Isotope internal standard correction was set during the detection process, with 3 technical replicates for each sample. Finally, the absolute content of endogenous phytohormones in each treatment sample was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Gene expression data were analyzed using the 2 − ΔΔCT method for relative quantifi-cation. GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA) was used for graphical presentation. All statistical comparisons were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics 30 (IBM Corp., USA). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted on Z-score normalized FPKM values using the prcomp function in R (v4.1.2). Visualization plots, including PCA score plots, box plots, volcano plots, and functional enrichment bar plots, were generated with the ggplot2 package (v3.3.4). Metabolite profile correlations were visualized as a heatmap using the corrplot package (v0.92), and metabolomic heatmaps were created with ComplexHeatmap (v2.9.4). Differences in phytohormone metabolite levels were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc tests, with *p* < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of different nitrogen form ratios on phenotypic development of strawberry plants

Plant height showed significant differences among different NH₄⁺:NO₃⁻ ratio treatments (Fig. 1B). The 7:3 NH₄⁺:NO₃⁻ ratio treatment (CL₂) resulted in the highest plant height, which was significantly higher than that of the pure ammonium treatment (CL1, 10:0) and pure nitrate treatment (CL5, 0:10) (p ≤ 0.05). No significant difference in plant height was observed between CL1 and CL5 treatments. Overall, plant height exhibited a trend of initial increase and subsequent decrease with the rising proportion of NO₃⁻ in the nutrient solution, with the maximum value observed in the CL2 treatment (Fig. 1A). These results indicate that mixed nitrogen source application at an appropriate ratio is more effective for promoting strawberry vegetative growth than single nitrogen form supply. Petiole diameter and length of strawberry leaves showed consistent variation trends among treatments (Fig. 1C and D). For petiole diameter, the CL2 treatment achieved the maximum value of 6.46 mm, which was 26.42% higher than that of the CL1 treatment (5.11 mm), and was significantly higher than that of CL1, CL3, CL4 and CL5 treatments (p ≤ 0.05). The CL1 treatment showed significant differences in petiole diameter with CL2, CL3 and CL4 treatments, but had no significant difference with the CL5 treatment. For petiole length, the maximum value was 18.37 cm in the CL2 treatment, which was 32.15% higher than that of the CL1 treatment (13.90 cm), and was significantly higher than that of the other four treatments (p ≤ 0.05). Similarly, the petiole length of the CL1 treatment was significantly different from that of CL2, CL3 and CL4 treatments, with no significant difference observed between CL1 and CL5 treatments.

Strawberry fruits exhibited the plumpest phenotype under the CL2 and CL3 nitrogen treatments, with a faster coloration rate during the turning stage (Fig. 2A).

For fruit internal quality indicators, the CL₂ treatment (7:3) resulted in the highest contents of soluble sugars, titratable acids and soluble solids among all treatments (Fig. 2B). Specifically, the soluble sugar content of the CL2 treatment was 46.6% higher than that of the pure nitrate treatment (CL₅), with a significant difference between the two groups (p ≤ 0.05). The titratable acid content of the CL2 treatment was 22.9% higher than that of the CL5 treatment, showing a statistically significant difference (p ≤ 0.05). Meanwhile, the soluble solids content of the CL2 treatment was 13.6% higher than that of the CL5 treatment, with a significant difference between groups (p ≤ 0.05). The CL1 and CL5 treatments (single nitrogen form supply) showed significantly lower contents of soluble sugars, titratable acids and soluble solids compared with the CL2 treatment (p ≤ 0.05).

For strawberry fruit yield, the highest value was observed in the CL2 treatment at 213.67 g per plant, which was significantly higher than that of CL1 and CL5 treatments (p ≤ 0.05). The CL1 treatment showed a significant difference in yield with CL2, CL3, and CL4 treatments, but no significant difference was observed compared with CL5 (Fig. 2C). To systematically investigate the transcriptional responses of strawberry to different nitrogen forms, construct a gene regulatory network (GRN) of nitrogen-mediated fruit development, and identify key gene modules and hub genes associated with strawberry fruit development and quality formation, we performed WGCNA [27]based on the full transcriptome dataset. A total of 45,016 filtered genes with non-zero variance were used for network construction via the blockwiseModules function, and nine distinct co-expression modules were generated after merging similar modules with a cut-height threshold of 0.15 (Fig. 8A). The number of genes in each module ranged from 74 (pink module) to 17,896 (turquoise module, the largest module).

Discussion

Effects of different nitrogen form ratios on strawberry growth and development

Research on nitrogen uptake in strawberry plants indicates that mixed nitrogen sources are more conducive to plant growth than single-form nitrogen supply. The results of this experiment support the hypothesis that when the ammonium-to-nitrate ratio in the nutrient solution is 7:3, strawberry plant height, petiole length, and petiole diameter are all higher than in other treatment groups. This observation is consistent with previous reports in tomato, where a 7:3 ammonium-to-nitrate ratio was reported to enhance root activity by 22% and promote cytokinin synthesis, thereby driving stem elongation [28]. Similarly, studies on blueberry have demonstrated that supplementing NH₄⁺ with an appropriate amount of NO₃⁻ promotes growth and development; at an ammonium‑to‑nitrate ratio of 4:2, plant height, stem diameter, leaf area, and chlorophyll content were all significantly increased [29]. Comparable trends were also found in cauliflower, where a 5:5 ammonium‑to‑nitrate ratio yielded the highest total chlorophyll content during the curding stage [30]. Prolonged exposure to pure ammonium nitrogen (NH₄⁺) can lead to toxicity in roots due to the accumulation of high ammonium concentrations [31]. Prolonged exposure to pure ammonium nitrogen (NH₄⁺) can lead to root toxicity due to the accumulation of high ammonium concentrations (Rare, 1990). In the present study, the growth stagnation observed in the pure ammonium treatment (CL₁) during the later growth stages may be associated with such ammonium toxicity. When NH₄⁺ concentration exceeds 2 mM, it can inhibit the mitochondrial electron transport chain and trigger a reactive oxygen species burst [32]. Conversely, the weaker growth performance under pure nitrate treatment (CL₅) may be attributed to higher energy costs for nitrogen assimilation. Studies have shown that NO₃⁻ assimilation requires 15–20% more ATP compared to NH₄⁺ assimilation [33], which could explain the slower biomass accumulation observed in the CL₅ group in this experiment.In summary, compared to single nitrogen source applications, an ammonium‑to‑nitrate ratio of 7:3 is suggested to be the most effective for promoting strawberry vegetative growth and development, potentially indicating a regulatory link between nitrogen form and plant developmental efficiency.

Effects of different nitrogen form ratios on strawberry fruits

The efficiency of nitrogen uptake and utilization by strawberry plants varies depending on the nitrogen form, leading to corresponding changes in fruit quality and traits. Analysis of fruit quality indicators in this experiment revealed that strawberries treated with mixed nitrogen sources exhibited superior quality compared to those treated with a single nitrogen source, demonstrating the optimized allocation of photosynthetic products under mixed nitrogen supply. For instance, NH₄⁺ promotes proton pump activity, thereby enhancing transport efficiency to the fruits [34]. Meanwhile, NO₃⁻ upregulates the expression of sucrose synthase genes such as SUS3, increasing sink strength [35]. This synergistic effect of the two nitrogen forms likely mitigates the inhibitory effect on photoassimilate partitioning caused by the high energy cost associated with assimilating a single nitrogen source (NO₃⁻ or NH₃⁺) [36]. The optimized sugar-acid ratio (characterized by high sugar and high acid) observed in the CL2 treatment may stem from NH₄⁺-induced accumulation of organic acids like malate and citrate, acting in concert with NO₃⁻-driven sugar metabolism. This finding is highly consistent with the established mechanisms underlying strawberry flavor quality formation [37]. Furthermore, the CL2 treatment group in this experiment showed significantly higher values in yield, fruit weight, and size compared to other treatments. This reflects the precise regulation of fruit development by nitrogen forms: nitrate nitrogen promotes cytokinin synthesis to regulate the cell division phase, while ammonium nitrogen induces auxin synthesis affecting the cell expansion phase [38, 39]. At an ammonium-to-nitrate ratio of 7:3, the synergistic action of these two hormones may optimize the spatiotemporal coordination of cell division and expansion in the fruit, a mechanism supported by similar findings in tomato [40]. The lowest marketable fruit rate under the pure nitrate treatment (CL₅) may be related to its inhibitory effect on calcium and magnesium uptake, leading to physiological disorders in the later stages of fruit development [41].

RNA-seq analysis under different nitrogen form ratios

Fruit ripening is a crucial stage of fruit development, regulated by a multi-layered network involving environmental factors, plant hormones, transcription factors, and epigenetic modifications. Nitrogen plays a vital physiological role in plant growth and development, influencing the ripening of both climacteric and non-climacteric fruits [42]. Research on key genes involved in plant nitrogen uptake has extensively focused on nitrate transporters (NRTs) and ammonium transporters (AMTs) [43, 44]. However, the regulatory networks connecting nitrogen with other hormones remain insufficiently elucidated. To unravel the underlying molecular mechanisms, this study employed RNA-seq technology to systematically analyze gene expression patterns in mature strawberry fruits subjected to different nitrogen treatments. However, since this study used octoploid strawberry plants, which have a complex genome, we were unable to perform qRT-PCR analysis to validate the expression levels of the selected genes; therefore, this study has certain limitations.

Based on the proportion of up- and down-regulated DEGs across treatments, the CL2 (7:3) treatment promoted the upregulation of a substantial number of DEGs. GO enrichment analysis revealed a significant enrichment of genes associated with plant hormone signal transduction, indicating that nitrogen plays an important role in modulating hormonal signaling during strawberry fruit ripening. Further KEGG pathway analysis showed that the DEGs were primarily enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway and plant hormone signal transduction pathways. These findings are consistent with studies reporting that the expression and activity of NPF genes are regulated by various factors, including nitrate concentration, hormonal signals, and environmental stress [45], as well as with KEGG pathways enriched in Chinese cabbage [46] and tomato [47]. This suggests that nitrogen forms primarily promote strawberry coloration, sugar accumulation, and early ripening by influencing pathways such as MAPK signaling and hormone signal transduction.

Synergistic interaction between nitrogen and the auxin pathway

Phytohormones play a key role in environmental responses by regulating plant developmental processes and metabolic adaptation [48]. Transcriptomic studies have revealed that different hormone signaling networks act in concert to modulate plant growth and development, with mineral nutrient stress significantly affecting the biosynthesis and transport of endogenous hormones [38]. As a core phytohormone, auxin (IAA) plays an important regulatory role in key developmental stages such as embryogenesis, root architecture, floral organ development, and fruit ripening. Under nitrogen deficiency, IAA content in functional leaves of cucumber was significantly lower than in normally fertilized controls, suggesting that nutrient availability directly affects auxin homeostasis in plants [49]. Notably, studies have found that nitrogen stress not only activates the expression of genes related to IAA biosynthesis but also enhances the polar transport capacity of IAA from shoots to roots [50]. At the molecular level, the AUX/IAA gene family encodes short-lived nuclear proteins that serve as early auxin response elements [51]. By forming heterodimeric complexes with auxin response factors (ARFs), they function as transcriptional repressors in the auxin signal transduction pathway [52]. In the present study, during the green fruit stage, AUX/IAA‑encoding genes were upregulated in the CL2‑G and CL3‑G comparison but downregulated in CL1‑G. Under mixed nitrogen supply, the downregulation of AUX/IAA genes in the placenta reduced repression of auxin signaling, thereby facilitating auxin signal transduction. Some SAUR proteins also interact with AUX/IAA proteins, strengthening the repression of ARF transcription factors and indirectly reducing the expression of IAA synthesis‑related genes [52]. In this study, during the mature green stage, both up‑ and down‑regulation of SAUR‑encoding genes were observed under the mixed nitrogen treatment compared to pure ammonium or pure nitrate treatments. This expression pattern suggests that the SAUR gene family may be subject to context‑dependent and potentially complex regulation under different nitrogen regimes. Although the transcriptomic data indicate a strong association, further functional studies are needed to validate the specific regulatory mechanisms involved.

Regulatory networks of nitrogen sources influencing cytokinin and gibberellin metabolic pathways

CRE1, a core cytokinin receptor, directly promotes cytokinin signal transduction when its activity is enhanced, thereby positively regulating cell division. Increased expression or activity of CRE1 allows for more efficient perception of cytokinin signals, triggering a downstream phosphorelay cascade that activates B-ARR and induces the expression of cell cycle-related genes, thereby driving cell division. For example, overexpression of CRE1 in Arabidopsis enhances meristem activity and promotes the proliferation of lateral roots and shoot apical meristems [53]. In rice, overexpression of the homologous gene OHK1 leads to increased tillering [54]. Conversely, loss of CRE1 function results in disrupted cytokinin signaling, failure to activate B-ARR, and suppressed expression of cell division-related genes. The CRE1 mutant exhibits shortened primary roots, sparse lateral roots, and a significantly reduced cell division rate in the shoot apical meristem [55]. Similarly, in maize, mutation of the homologous gene ZmHK1 reduces kernel number per ear [56]. In the present study, during the mature green stage, genes encoding CRE1 were upregulated in CL2 and downregulated in CL3, indicating that mixed nitrogen sources favor cytokinin production.

The core of the gibberellin signaling pathway is the degradation mechanism of DELLA proteins, which act as negative regulators. DELLA proteins accumulate when gibberellin is scarce and inhibit the expression of downstream genes. When gibberellin is present, it binds to the receptor GID1, triggering the ubiquitination and degradation of DELLA proteins, thereby relieving the inhibition of downstream transcription factors [57]. Gibberellin affects plant height and tillering by regulating the stability of DELLA proteins. For instance, the “Green Revolution” genes Rht1 (wheat) and Sd1 (rice) achieved semi-dwarf breeding and significantly increased grain yield by reducing gibberellin activity [58]. In this study, during the green fruit stage, most genes in the CL2-G group were downregulated. This pattern suggests that, compared to pure nitrate treatment, mixed nitrogen sources may suppress the activation of DELLA proteins, which could in turn promote endogenous gibberellin production. This potential regulatory relationship, however, requires further functional validation to establish a direct causal link.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the effects of different ammonium-to-nitrate ratios on strawberry growth, yield and their underlying regulatory mechanisms through physiological, hormonal, transcriptomic and gene co-expression network analysis. The results showed that under the condition of total nitrogen concentration of 6 mmol/L, the NH₄⁺:NO₃⁻ ratio of 7:3 (CL2) is the optimal ratio for protected strawberry cultivation. This ratio significantly promoted the vegetative growth of strawberry plants (plant height, petiole growth), increased fruit yield, but had no significant effect on fruit size.
