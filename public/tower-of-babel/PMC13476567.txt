Advancing transplant ethics: ISHLT statement and implementation framework
Authors: Peter Hopkins, Olivia Kates
Published: 2026

In 2022, the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) published a Statement on Transplant Ethics, approved by the Board of Directors in April of that year. This statement acknowledged and endorsed the principles of other ethics statements, including the World Medical Association Statement on Organ and Tissue Donation and the Declaration of Istanbul. Emphasis was placed on the ethical principle of donor autonomy and freedom from coercion, and the threats to this principle from the wrongful practices of procuring organs from executed prisoners, transplant tourism, and organ trafficking.

Transplant medicine has continued to evolve and grow in complexity, international collaboration is expanding and the ISHLT has recognised the need to clearly articulate the to give full expression to of its longstanding ethical principles whilst providing a transparent, pragmatic governance framework for their implementation. For example, the 2022 ISHLT Statement on Transplant Ethics declared that transplant science submitted from jurisdictions where ethical transplant practices could not be independently verified should be excluded from scientific presentation, and that giving instruction in clinical or transplant-related skills to transplant professionals in such jurisdictions should be prohibited. The practical implementation of such pointed mandates has proven challenging, inviting technicality rather than nuance.

The updated 2026 Statement on Transplant Ethics published herein represents the culmination of nearly 12 months of thoughtful deliberation involving the ISHLT Board of Directors, the Ethics Committee, and the Leadership Advisory Forum. This included a dedicated ethics forum incorporated into the annual ISHLT Leadership Meeting in January 2026. This Statement is grounded in three fundamental principles, which may be operationalised through a range of ethical transplant practices:•Respect for Persons, operationalized through voluntary organ donation free from the possibility of coercion or exploitation.•Utility, operationalized through responsible and transparent allocation processes.•Justice, operationalized through fair access to transplantation according to clinical need without discrimination.

The Statement does not introduce new ethical principles but rather articulates familiar principles through the lens of the ISHLT’s foundational mission as an international society dedicated to the advancement of care for patients with advanced heart and lung disease. It does not relegate the essential commitment to organ donors’ autonomy. The Statement embraces respect and concern for all persons, including organ donors, donor family members, recipients, caregivers, transplant candidates, and clinicians delivering healthcare. The Statement also gives fuller expression to core tenets of seeking a collective good, prioritising scarce resources, and advancing justice through the considered distribution of benefit or burden. Procedural considerations, including a commitment to cultural humility, further contextualize these shared ethical values within a culturally diverse organization.

Accompanying the Statement on Transplant Ethics is the ISHLT Implementation Framework, which modernises and makes transparent the procedures for consistently applying these principles across the Society’s scientific programs and educational content. Whereas the Statement on Transplant Ethics can have myriad applications across the breadth of procedures involved in transplantation, the specific purpose of this framework is to guide determinations about whether scientific submissions earmarked for presentation under the ISHLT name reflect the ethical principles that the ISHLT upholds. The framework calls for:•Author attestation that their submission complies with the ISHLT Statement on Transplant Ethics, The Declaration of Istanbul, the Declaration of Helsinki, and other applicable institutional ethics body requirements.•Reasonable confidence on the part of the ISHLT and its representatives that research in the field of transplantation was conducted in a manner consistent with our Statement on Transplant Ethics.•Enhanced ethics review of certain submissions involving, though not necessarily restricted to The Ethics committee, Scientific Program Committee, Editorial Leadership, and The Board of Directors where genuine questions remain, with further information sought from the author and/or the submitting institution.•The Society reserves the right to decline submissions where reasonable confidence is not established.

Research involving human organs or tissues procured in the People’s Republic of China on or before the 1st of January 2015 will not be accepted for presentation or publication within ISHLT programs or networks. This is the date on which the Chinese government introduced national reforms intended to end the wrongful practice of procuring organs from executed prisoners and transitioned to a voluntary, coordinated donation program. The Board appreciates the ongoing debate regarding verification of these reforms, and the broader challenge of ensuring the additive application of both local and international ethical requirements for transplantation in any jurisdiction. Our implementation framework will provide scrutiny over all submissions to uphold our ethical principles. With these procedures, ISHLT members, authors, reviewers, session moderators, editors, and annual meeting participants can expect a more transparent, consistent, and globally applicable approach to ethics reviews across the programs we offer.

Together, our Statement on Transplant Ethics and Implementation Framework position the Society to be a global leader in transplant ethics, science, and education, by elevating the trustworthiness of translational and clinical research whilst also supporting fruitful collaborations with international scientific partners. Our ethical values represent a guiding North Star, but our approach to implementation will remain nimble, responsive to the changing landscape of transplantation practices. This is not the final word, but rather the first chapter of an ongoing conversation.
