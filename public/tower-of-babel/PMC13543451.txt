How mathematics anxiety relates to mathematical ability in AI agent-supported instruction: the moderating role of executive function
Authors: Guoye Lin, BingQing Tang, Yubai Niu, Jianwei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMathematical ability is widely recognized as a core indicator of students’ cognitive development and academic achievement. However, in classroom environments where artificial intelligence (AI)-supported instruction is increasingly integrated into routine teaching practices, how mathematics anxiety shapes the development of mathematical ability over time remains insufficiently understood. To address this gap, this study examines the longitudinal relationship between state mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability among primary school students in AI agent-supported instructional contexts, with particular attention to the moderating role of executive function. Using a convenience sampling approach, a total of 494 students from Grades 1, 3, and 5 participated in routine AI agent-supported mathematics instruction over a six-month period, with AI-supported lessons implemented approximately twice per week on average. A two-wave longitudinal design was adopted, and cross-lagged panel analysis was conducted to examine the directional relationships between the key variables. The results indicated an asymmetric relationship, in which mathematics anxiety significantly predicted subsequent mathematical ability, while the reverse effect was not supported. Specifically, higher levels of mathematics anxiety predicted subsequent declines in overall mathematical ability, mathematical computation ability, and logical-spatial ability, whereas prior mathematical ability and its subdimensions did not significantly predict later mathematics anxiety. In addition, executive function moderated the relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability outcomes in a domain-specific manner. Higher levels of executive function strengthened the negative association between mathematics anxiety and both overall mathematical ability and mathematical computation ability, but not logical-spatial ability. A similar pattern was observed for inhibitory control, whereas working memory and cognitive flexibility did not show significant moderating effects. This study contributes new longitudinal evidence on students’ mathematical development in AI agent-supported classrooms and advances current understanding of how mathematics anxiety and executive function jointly shape learning outcomes, while also informing the design of more responsive instructional support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-04994-5.Keywords: Mathematics ability, Mathematical anxiety, Executive function, Cross-lagged panel analysis, AI agent-supported instruction, Primary school students

Introduction

Mathematical ability represents a foundational psychological construct that underpins individuals’ capacity to successfully engage in mathematical activities and solve problems efficiently [29]. It encompasses multiple interrelated components, which are commonly reflected in students’ computational performance and logical-spatial reasoning [44, 56]. Computational proficiency refers to the ability to perform calculations accurately and efficiently, whereas logical-spatial ability involves the use of logical reasoning, pattern recognition, spatial representation, and visual tracking to interpret mathematical information and solve problems [59]. Mathematical ability is therefore operationalized as a composite construct comprising mathematical computation ability and logical-spatial ability, consistent with the measurement instrument used [55]. Strong mathematical ability is not only essential for academic achievement in mathematics but also serves as a predictor of broader cognitive development and problem-solving competence [21, 46].

With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), AI-supported instruction has increasingly been integrated into mathematics classrooms, offering new opportunities to enhance students’ learning processes [31, 61]. AI technologies, particularly intelligent tutoring systems and AI agents, are capable of providing adaptive feedback, personalized learning pathways, and real time scaffolding, which may support students’ cognitive engagement and improve mathematical performance [40]. Compared with conventional digital tools, AI agents are characterized by their capacity to interact with learners in more responsive, dialogic, and context sensitive ways [15, 57]. They can interpret students’ inputs, provide tailored prompts, guide step by step reasoning, and adjust instructional support according to learners’ ongoing performance and needs [24, 26]. In mathematics learning, such features are especially valuable because they may help students clarify problem conditions, externalize reasoning processes, and sustain engagement during challenging tasks. Emerging evidence suggests that these technologies can facilitate the development of mathematical skills by promoting individualized practice, strengthening immediate instructional support, and reducing unnecessary cognitive load during complex problem solving [3, 32]. In this sense, AI-supported learning environments, particularly those involving AI agents, have the potential to reshape how mathematical ability is developed and assessed. At the same time, adaptive instructional support may change how mathematical tasks are presented, but students may still experience uncertainty, errors, and performance pressure during problem solving, making mathematics anxiety a relevant concern in AI-supported learning environments.

However, mathematical performance is not solely determined by cognitive abilities. A substantial body of research has demonstrated that affective factors, particularly mathematics anxiety, play a critical role in shaping students’ mathematical outcomes [8, 53]. Mathematics anxiety refers to feelings of tension, worry, or fear that interfere with the manipulation of numbers and the solving of mathematical problems [16]. High levels of mathematics anxiety have been consistently associated with lower mathematical achievement, as anxiety can consume working memory resources and hinder efficient cognitive processing [12, 42]. This negative relationship has been observed across different age groups, including primary school students, suggesting that mathematics anxiety may emerge early and exert long-term effects on learning trajectories.

Despite extensive research on the relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability, the underlying mechanisms that explain this relationship remain insufficiently understood, particularly in technology-enhanced learning contexts. One promising explanatory variable is executive function, which refers to a set of higher-order cognitive processes, including working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility, that regulate goal-directed behavior [4]. Executive function has been shown to be closely linked to both mathematical performance and emotional regulation. On the one hand, strong executive function supports efficient problem solving and mathematical reasoning [7, 30]. On the other hand, it may buffer the negative impact of anxiety by enabling individuals to regulate attention and suppress intrusive thoughts [2, 52]. However, limited research has examined the moderating role of executive function in the relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability, especially within AI-supported instructional environments.

Therefore, this study addresses two key gaps in the existing literature. First, although prior research has established a robust association between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability, the temporal ordering of this relationship remains unclear, particularly in primary school contexts. Longitudinal evidence is still limited regarding whether mathematics anxiety functions as an antecedent condition influencing later performance, or whether mathematical ability shapes subsequent emotional responses over time. Second, while executive function has been widely recognized as relevant to both mathematical learning and emotional regulation, its role in shaping the relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability remains underexplored. In particular, it is unclear whether executive function uniformly attenuates the negative effects of anxiety or whether its influence is conditional on specific cognitive or emotional demands.

To address these gaps, this study adopts a longitudinal design and cross-lagged panel analysis to examine how state mathematics anxiety relates to mathematical ability among primary school students in an AI-supported instructional context. In addition, the study investigates whether executive function moderates this relationship. As AI agents are increasingly embedded in routine mathematics teaching through adaptive feedback, real-time guidance, and individualized scaffolding, it is important to examine whether established anxiety-performance relationships remain evident in these technology-enhanced classrooms. By situating mathematics anxiety, mathematical ability, and executive function within an AI agent-supported instructional environment, this study aims to provide longitudinal evidence on children’s mathematical development under contemporary classroom conditions where AI-supported instruction forms part of regular teaching practice. Literature review

Theoretical foundation: attentional control theory

Attentional control theory (ACT) posits that anxiety disrupts the balance between goal-directed (top-down) and stimulus-driven (bottom-up) attentional systems, thereby impairing individuals’ ability to efficiently allocate cognitive resources during task performance [19]. Specifically, anxiety is associated with increased attentional bias toward task-irrelevant or threat-related stimuli, as well as diminished inhibitory control over intrusive thoughts, which collectively reduce processing efficiency [54]. Rather than directly impairing task effectiveness, anxiety primarily compromises the efficiency of cognitive processing, leading individuals to expend greater mental effort to achieve comparable performance outcomes [18].

Drawing on ACT, mathematics anxiety can be conceptualized as a cognitive-emotional state that interferes with students’ attentional regulation during mathematical tasks, thereby undermining performance. In this framework, executive functions, particularly inhibitory control, play a crucial role in managing the extent to which anxiety-related interference affects task execution. Students with higher levels of executive control may be more actively engaged in regulating attention and suppressing irrelevant thoughts [37]. However, under heightened anxiety, this regulatory effort may also intensify cognitive resource competition, potentially amplifying performance decrements [13, 35]. This perspective provides a theoretical basis for examining executive function as a moderator in the relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability. Because AI-supported mathematics learning often involves continuous feedback, error correction, and guided problem solving, ACT is useful for explaining why students’ anxiety and attentional regulation may remain important even in technology-enhanced instructional environments.

Mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability

Mathematics anxiety has been consistently associated with lower mathematical performance across different age groups, including primary school students [16]. Prior research has shown that students with higher levels of mathematics anxiety tend to perform more poorly on mathematics tasks, particularly those involving computation, problem solving, and sustained cognitive engagement [5, 14]. This association is theoretically consistent with ACT, which suggests that anxiety interferes with performance by reducing the efficiency of attentional control and information processing during mathematical tasks [23].

Despite this well-established negative association, the directionality of the relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability remains unclear. Much of the existing literature has relied on cross-sectional designs, which are insufficient for determining whether mathematics anxiety predicts later declines in mathematical ability or instead develops as a consequence of poor performance (e.g., [11, 50]. Longitudinal studies have offered valuable evidence, but the findings remain mixed, with some suggesting that anxiety precedes later performance difficulties and others indicating that poor performance may contribute to subsequent anxiety [27, 39]. In addition, limited attention has been paid to whether this relationship differs across distinct dimensions of mathematical ability, such as computation and logical-spatial performance.

This gap is particularly important in primary school, where both mathematical competence and anxiety are still developing. Therefore, longitudinal evidence is needed to clarify the temporal relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability and to examine its potential variation across mathematical domains.

Executive function as a cognitive mechanism

Executive function refers to a set of higher-order cognitive processes, including working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility, that regulate goal-directed behavior [4]. These components support attention regulation, information maintenance and updating, and adaption to changing task demands [38]. A substantial body of research has demonstrated that executive function plays a critical role in mathematical learning and performance, particularly in tasks that require sustained attention procedural accuracy, and multi-step problem solving [10, 17]. Among these components, inhibitory control is especially relevant because it enables students to suppress irrelevant information and resist interference during mathematical processing.

Executive function is also closely related to attentional regulation, making it a theoretically relevant factor in understanding how mathematics anxiety influences performance [9]. Students with stronger executive function may be better able to regulate anxiety-related distraction during mathematical tasks. However, emerging evidence suggests that executive function does not always mitigate the negative effects of anxiety, indicating that its role may depend on the specific cognitive and emotional demands involved. Despite increasing interest in this issue, relatively little research has examined how different components of executive function shape the relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability, particularly among primary school students.

AI-supported mathematics learning context

With the rapid development of artificial intelligence, AI-supported instructional environments have become increasingly prevalent in mathematics education. Technologies such as intelligent tutoring systems and AI-based pedagogical agents are capable of providing adaptive feedback, personalized learning pathways, and real-time scaffolding, thereby supporting students’ engagement and mathematical performance [40]. These systems can dynamically adjust task difficulty, offer step-by-step guidance, and reduce unnecessary cognitive load during problem solving, which may facilitate the development of mathematical skills [3, 32]. As a result, AI-supported mathematics learning environments are increasingly viewed as promising tools for enhancing both learning processes and outcomes.

However, existing research has primarily focused on the cognitive and performance-related benefits of AI-supported learning, with relatively limited attention to learners’ emotional experiences, particularly mathematics anxiety, within such environments. This gap is important because AI-supported instruction may change the immediate conditions of mathematical problem solving, but students’ anxiety and regulatory capacities may still shape how they engage with mathematical tasks in these environments. In this regard, individual differences in executive function may remain important in understanding students’ mathematical performance within AI-enhanced environments. Therefore, AI agent-supported mathematics classrooms provide a timely context for examining whether mathematics anxiety remains associated with students’ mathematical development over time and whether executive function conditions this association.

Method

Participants

Participants were recruited through convenience sampling from a public primary school located in southeastern coastal China. A total of 510 students initially participated in the study. After excluding 16 students whose scores fell beyond three standard deviations from the mean, the final sample consisted of 494 students (263 boys) (53.24%) and 231 girls (46.76%), with an overall mean age of 9.42 years (SD = 1.79). The effective response rate was 96.86%.

The sample included students from three grade levels: Grade 1 (n = 159, 81 boys and 78 girls; M age = 7.20, SD = 0.58), Grade 3 (n = 167, 86 boys and 81 girls; M age = 9.62, SD = 0.68), and Grade 5 (n = 168, 96 boys and 72 girls; M age = 11.32, SD = 0.55). All participants were enrolled in regular mathematics classes at the time of data collection.

Prior to participation, informed consent was obtained from both students and their parents or legal guardians. Participation was voluntary, and all data were collected anonymously. Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Zhejiang Sci-Tech University. All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Research procedure

This study adopted a two-wave longitudinal design. Data were collected at two time points across an approximately six-month observation period, spanning September 2025 to March 2026. Time 1 (T1) data were collected during the autumn school term, and Time 2 (T2) data were collected after students had returned for the spring term. Although the overall interval between measurements included the winter vacation, both waves of assessment were administered during regular school terms. At each time point, participants completed measures of state mathematics anxiety, executive function, and mathematical ability. This design enabled the examination of the longitudinal relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability, as well as the moderating role of executive function.

Data were collected during regular school hours in classroom settings. All assessments were administered under the supervision of trained researchers in collaboration with classroom teachers to ensure standardized procedures. Instructions were delivered in a consistent manner, and students completed the tasks independently. Each assessment session lasted approximately 30–40 minutes, and all responses were recorded anonymously.

The study was conducted in digitally equipped classrooms where AI agent-supported instruction was routinely integrated into mathematics teaching through Seewo smart classroom systems. Within this environment, AI-based pedagogical agents were embedded in the instructional platform and used to support learning through functions such as real-time feedback, step-by-step problem-solving guidance, adaptive prompting, and error diagnosis. During AI-supported lessons, mathematical tasks were presented via the digital classroom system, and students interacted with the platform to submit responses, receive immediate guidance, and revise their solution strategies. Teachers simultaneously used the system to monitor student progress and provide targeted instructional support based on students’ ongoing performance.

Students attended one mathematics lesson per school day, and AI agent-supported instruction was implemented in approximately two mathematics lessons per week on average. Instructional planning, including the use of AI-supported lessons, was coordinated through grade-level lesson preparation groups to ensure consistency in teaching approaches. The mathematical content and curriculum pacing followed the standard syllabus and did not differ from those implemented in comparable schools.

The AI-supported instructional environment reflected routine teaching practice rather than a researcher-designed intervention. In this setting, AI agent-supported instruction was embedded as part of regular classroom teaching and provided a shared learning context for all participating students. This naturalistic design allowed the study to examine the longitudinal relationships among mathematics anxiety, executive function, and mathematical ability under authentic classroom conditions in which AI-supported instruction had become part of everyday mathematics learning.

Measurement

Math ability

Mathematical ability was assessed using a standardized instrument developed by [22], which has been widely used to evaluate children’s mathematical competencies. The instrument comprises two major domains: mathematical computation and logical-spatial ability, consisting of a total of 12 subtests. One subtest (number copying) served as a warm-up task and was not included in scoring.

The mathematical computation subscale includes six subtests (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, fill-in tasks, and magnitude comparison), designed to assess students’ understanding of mathematical concepts, computational speed, and calculation accuracy. The logical-spatial subscale consists of four subtests (length estimation, block counting, figure counting, and number connection), which measure students’ logical reasoning, pattern recognition, spatial visualization, and visual tracking abilities. Scores were calculated based on the number of correctly solved items across subtests, with higher scores indicating better mathematical performance.

The overall scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.889). The subscales also showed acceptable reliability, with Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.896 for mathematical computation and 0.701 for logical-spatial ability. Higher scores indicated better mathematical performance.

State mathematics anxiety

State mathematics anxiety was measured using a self-report questionnaire adapted from [43]. The scale assesses students’ situational feelings of tension, worry, and nervousness when engaging in mathematical tasks. Participants were asked to rate the extent to which they experienced anxiety in specific mathematics-related situations (e.g., “I feel nervous when solving math problems” and “I feel worried when I have to complete math tasks under time pressure”) on a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The questionnaire was administered collectively in a classroom setting under standardized instructions.

The scale demonstrated high internal consistency in the present study (Cronbach’s α = 0.898). A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using Mplus, and the results indicated a good model fit: χ2/df = 3.218, RMSEA = 0.067, CFI = 0.950, TLI = 0.936, and SRMR = 0.045, suggesting satisfactory construct validity.

Executive function

Executive function was assessed using the Yellow–Red Test developed by [49]. This computerized assessment consists of six game-based tasks (i.e., Cat–Dog, Arrows, Flies, Matching, Farm, and Find-the-Same), designed to evaluate four core components of executive function: inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, working memory, and overall executive functioning.

The test is suitable for children aged 6 to 12 years and typically requires 15–30 minutes to complete. In the present study, students completed the tasks individually on mobile devices in a quiet classroom setting. The instrument has demonstrated good reliability in prior research (Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.80 to 0.86).

All raw scores were standardized into T-scores for analysis. Specifically, the Cat–Dog task score was used as an indicator of overall executive function. The average T-scores of the Arrows and Flies tasks represented inhibitory control, while the average scores of the Matching and Farm tasks indicated working memory. Cognitive flexibility was assessed using the T-score of the Find-the-Same task.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.3. Prior to the main analyses, data screening procedures were conducted. Cases with extreme values exceeding three standard deviations were excluded, and missing data were examined and found to be minimal. The normality of the variables was assessed using skewness and kurtosis indices, all of which fell within acceptable ranges, indicating that the data met the assumptions for subsequent parametric analyses.

To investigate the longitudinal relationships between state mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability, a cross-lagged panel model was conducted using Mplus 8.3 [1]. Specifically, mathematical ability and state mathematics anxiety at Time 1 (T1) and Time 2 (T2) were included in the model. Autoregressive paths (e.g., T1 → T2 for the same variable) and cross-lagged paths (e.g., T1 anxiety → T2 ability; T1 ability → T2 anxiety) were simultaneously estimated to examine the directional influences between the two constructs over time.

To further examine the role of executive function, moderation analyses were conducted [25]. Executive function was included as a moderator in the relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability. Interaction terms were created by standardizing the relevant variables and computing their product terms. The significance of interaction effects was tested, and simple slope analyses were performed to interpret significant moderation effects.

Results

Cross-lagged analysis of mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability

All variables were mean-centered prior to analysis. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted to examine the longitudinal relationships between primary school students’ state mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability across two time points, with grade level and gender included as control variables.

All three models were saturated, meaning that the number of estimated parameters was equal to the number of elements in the covariance matrix, yielding zero degrees of freedom. Accordingly, model fit indices were not interpreted, and the analyses focused on the path coefficients [51].

As shown in Figure 1, 2 and 3, a consistent asymmetric pattern emerged across all three models. Baseline state mathematics anxiety significantly and negatively predicted mathematical ability six months later (β = −0.223, p < 0.001), whereas baseline mathematical ability did not significantly predict state mathematics anxiety six months later (β = −0.081, p > 0.05).

The same pattern was observed for mathematical computation ability. Baseline state mathematics anxiety significantly and negatively predicted mathematical computation ability six months later (β = −0.221, p < 0.001), whereas baseline mathematical computation ability did not significantly predict state mathematics anxiety six months later (β = −0.084, p > 0.05).

Similarly, for logical-spatial ability, baseline state mathematics anxiety significantly and negatively predicted logical-spatial ability six months later (β = −0.214, p < 0.001), whereas baseline logical-spatial ability did not significantly predict state mathematics anxiety six months later (β = −0.032, p > 0.05). Taken together, these findings indicate that state mathematics anxiety predicted later mathematical outcomes across domains, whereas the reverse paths from prior mathematical ability to later mathematics anxiety were not statistically supported.

The moderating role of executive functions

To improve readability, this section first reports the moderation results for overall executive function and inhibitory control, followed by the results for working memory and cognitive flexibility. Overall, significant moderating effects were observed for overall executive function and inhibitory control, whereas working memory and cognitive flexibility did not significantly moderate the relationship between state mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability.

Specifically, the interaction block was significant for overall mathematical ability, F(2, 486) = 15.943, p <.001, mathematical computation ability, F(2, 486) = 13.522, p <.001, and logical-spatial ability, F(2, 486) = 3.851, p =.022. Simple slope analyses indicated that state mathematics anxiety significantly and negatively predicted later mathematical ability across all three grade levels, but the magnitude of this association varied developmentally. For overall mathematical ability, the negative association was strongest in Grade 1, B = −1.771, p <.001, followed by Grade 5, B = −1.272, p <.001, and Grade 3, B = −0.631, p =.001. A similar pattern was found for mathematical computation ability, with significant negative associations in Grade 1, B = −1.372, p <.001, Grade 5, B = −1.073, p <.001, and Grade 3, B = −0.512, p =.001. For logical-spatial ability, the association was also significant in Grade 1, B = −0.512, p <.001, Grade 5, B = −0.382, p <.001, and Grade 3, B = −0.252, p =.001.

Additional analyses were conducted to examine whether the moderating role of executive function differed across grade levels. Three-way interaction terms among state mathematics anxiety, executive function, and grade level were added to the moderation models. The three-way interaction block was not significant for overall mathematical ability, F(2, 481) = 0.081, p =.919, mathematical computation ability, F(2, 481) = 0.053, p =.947, or logical-spatial ability, F(2, 481) = 1.241, p =.290. These results suggest that, although the longitudinal association between mathematics anxiety and later mathematical outcomes varied by grade level, the moderating role of executive function did not differ significantly across Grades 1, 3, and 5.

Discussion

RQ1: The longitudinal relationships between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability

The first aim of this study was to examine the longitudinal relationship between state mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability among primary school students in an AI-supported mathematics learning context. The findings showed that state mathematics anxiety at Time 1 significantly and negatively predicted overall mathematical ability six months later, as well as its two subdimensions, namely mathematical computation and logical-spatial ability.

In contrast, prior mathematical ability and its subdimensions did not significantly predict later state mathematics anxiety (e.g., [34]. These findings suggest that the temporal relationship observed in the present study is better characterized as an asymmetric longitudinal pattern in which anxiety predicts later mathematical performance, but prior performance does not predict subsequent anxiety. Additional developmental analyses further indicated that this negative longitudinal association was observed across Grades 1, 3, and 5, although its magnitude varied by grade level. This suggests that mathematics anxiety may be relevant across different stages of primary schooling, while its influence on later mathematical outcomes may not be developmentally uniform.

This pattern highlights the developmental significance of mathematics anxiety as an antecedent factor in children’s mathematical learning. One plausible explanation is that mathematics anxiety functions as a form of cognitive-emotional interference that disrupts students’ engagement with mathematical tasks over time [36]. According to Attentional Control Theory [19], anxiety impairs the efficiency of top-down attentional regulation by increasing sensitivity to task-irrelevant thoughts and reducing the capacity to inhibit distracting internal stimuli. In mathematics learning, such interference may be especially detrimental because many mathematical tasks require sustained concentration, sequential reasoning, and accurate monitoring of intermediate steps. This interpretation is also relevant in AI agent-supported classrooms, where students may receive adaptive prompts and immediate feedback but still need to interpret problems, monitor solution steps, and respond to errors under evaluative pressure. When students repeatedly experience anxious arousal in mathematics-related situations, part of their cognitive resources may be diverted away from task processing and toward worry regulation, which in turn undermines later performance [28]. The longitudinal effect observed here therefore suggests that mathematics anxiety is not merely a concurrent emotional reaction to mathematics, but a factor that may shape subsequent learning trajectories.

The fact that mathematics anxiety significantly predicted not only overall mathematical ability but also both mathematical computation and logical-spatial ability is also noteworthy. This finding indicates that the negative influence of anxiety may not be restricted to speeded or calculation-based aspects of mathematics. For mathematical computation, the mechanism is relatively intuitive: anxious students may be more likely to lose attentional focus, make procedural errors, or allocate excessive resources to self-monitoring rather than actual calculation [20]. For logical-spatial ability, the pathway may be somewhat different but still meaningful. Tasks involving pattern detection, spatial representation, and visual-logical coordination also rely on stable attentional engagement and efficient information selection [47]. In AI-supported mathematics lessons, these demands may remain present because the availability of step-by-step guidance does not remove the need for students to maintain attention, select relevant information, and make sense of visual or procedural feedback. In this sense, mathematics anxiety may exert a broader disruptive influence on mathematical cognition than is sometimes assumed, affecting not only procedural fluency but also higher-order reasoning and visual-spatial processing.

Another important implication of this finding lies in the non-significant reverse path. Prior mathematical ability did not significantly predict later mathematics anxiety, which suggests that, at least within the present sample and time interval, lower performance did not automatically translate into increased anxious feelings over time. This result may indicate that mathematics anxiety in primary school is not simply a delayed emotional consequence of poor performance [44, 56]. Instead, it may be more directly shaped by students’ immediate emotional experiences during mathematical engagement, such as tension under evaluation, worry during task completion, or anticipatory unease in mathematics-related situations. Put differently, emotional responses may be relatively proximal and situational, whereas mathematical ability develops more cumulatively [41]. This asymmetry may help explain why anxiety showed predictive power over later ability, but not vice versa.

Taken together, the findings extend mathematics anxiety research by showing that the anxiety-performance relationship remains salient within AI agent-supported mathematics instruction. The AI-supported context is not treated here as a causal variable, but it provides an important contemporary setting in which established psychological processes can be examined [6]. The results suggest that adaptive feedback and instructional scaffolding may coexist with anxiety-related attentional interference, making mathematics anxiety a continuing concern in technology-enhanced mathematics classrooms. Even in environments where instructional support is adaptive and immediate, students’ emotional states may still shape how they engage with available support. Students experiencing heightened anxiety may be less able to allocate attention efficiently to AI-based prompts or feedback, because anxiety-related interference can occur during problem interpretation and solution monitoring [60].

RQ2: The moderating role of executive function in the relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability

The second aim of this study was to examine whether executive function moderates the relationship between state mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability. The results showed that overall executive function significantly moderated the association between mathematics anxiety and both overall mathematical ability and mathematical computation ability, but not logical-spatial ability. More specifically, the negative predictive effect of mathematics anxiety was stronger among students with higher levels of executive function. A similar pattern was found for inhibitory control, whereas working memory and cognitive flexibility did not show significant moderating effects. Developmental robustness analyses showed that this moderating pattern did not differ significantly across grade levels, suggesting that the role of executive function was relatively stable across the primary school grades included in this study. Although these interaction effects reached statistical significance, their magnitudes were relatively small (βs = -.035 to -.052). Therefore, the moderation findings should be interpreted as evidence of modest but meaningful boundary conditions rather than large practical differences in students’ mathematical performance.

This finding differs from much of the prior literature, which has generally treated executive function as a protective or buffering resource that mitigates the adverse effects of anxiety on academic performance (e.g., [9]. However, this difference does not necessarily contradict the functional value of executive control. Rather, it suggests that the role of executive function depends on the level of anxiety and the type of cognitive demand involved in mathematical tasks. This pattern is especially meaningful in AI agent-supported mathematics classrooms because students are not only solving mathematical problems, but also responding to system prompts, feedback, and error information. Such environments may offer instructional support, but they may also require students to continuously regulate attention while processing both mathematical content and AI-generated guidance.

At first glance, this pattern may seem counterintuitive, because executive function is often conceptualized as a protective cognitive resource. However, the present findings suggest that the relationship between anxiety and executive control may be more complex than a simple buffering model implies. A more theoretically grounded interpretation can be derived from Attentional Control Theory, which distinguishes between performance effectiveness and processing efficiency. From this perspective, executive control may help students maintain task goals and regulate attention, but anxiety increases the amount of effort required to do so [7, 38]. Students with stronger executive or inhibitory control may therefore remain highly engaged in task monitoring, error checking, and interference suppression. Under low-anxiety conditions, these regulatory processes are likely to support performance. Under heightened anxiety, however, the same processes may become more effortful because students must simultaneously manage task demands and anxiety-related intrusive thoughts. The stronger negative association observed among students with higher executive function may therefore reflect increased regulatory cost under anxious conditions rather than a harmful effect of executive function itself.

This interpretation aligns with the central proposition of Attentional Control Theory, namely that anxiety primarily disrupts processing efficiency rather than task goals per se. For students with high executive control, the issue may not be a lack of regulatory capacity, but rather a greater dependence on effortful control processes that become vulnerable under anxious states [54]. This may be particularly evident in mathematical computation tasks, which require procedural accuracy, sustained concentration, and rapid suppression of irrelevant thoughts. In AI-supported computation tasks, students may additionally need to coordinate their own solution steps with adaptive hints, corrective feedback, or scaffolded prompts. These forms of support can be helpful, but they still require active interpretation and attentional regulation. Under such conditions, inhibitory control becomes especially salient. The significant moderation effect observed for overall executive function and inhibitory control, but not for working memory or cognitive flexibility, suggests that the key mechanism may lie in interference suppression rather than in information updating or set shifting.

The specific role of inhibitory control deserves particular attention. Inhibitory control refers to the ability to suppress irrelevant information, impulsive responses, or competing mental content [4]. In mathematics-anxiety contexts, intrusive worry thoughts are likely to function as internal distractors that interfere with ongoing processing. The present results suggest that inhibitory control may be central because the disruptive component of mathematics anxiety is closely tied to interference management. Students with stronger inhibitory control may be more likely to engage actively in suppressing worry-related thoughts and monitoring errors. Yet suppression is not cost-free. When anxiety is high, the effort required to inhibit intrusive thoughts may compete with the resources needed for calculation accuracy and step-by-step reasoning [37]. Thus, the stronger negative association among students with higher inhibitory control should not be interpreted as evidence that inhibitory control is maladaptive. A more cautious interpretation is that inhibitory control supports performance under ordinary conditions, but its benefits may be reduced when anxiety increases the cost of maintaining control.

By contrast, neither working memory nor cognitive flexibility significantly moderated the anxiety–performance association. This distinction is theoretically meaningful. Working memory is essential for holding and manipulating information, and cognitive flexibility supports switching strategies or perspectives, but the present results suggest that these functions may not be the primary boundary conditions through which state mathematics anxiety shapes mathematical performance in this sample [48]. Instead, the disruptive impact of anxiety may be more directly linked to students’ ability to regulate interference at the attentional level. This finding helps refine broad accounts of executive function by showing that its components do not contribute equally to the anxiety–mathematics relationship.

The domain-specific nature of the moderation findings is also important. Executive function and inhibitory control significantly moderated the relation between mathematics anxiety and mathematical computation, as well as overall mathematical ability, but not logical-spatial ability. This suggests that the regulatory demands associated with anxiety may be especially relevant for tasks involving sequential operations, rule application, and response accuracy. Computation tasks may place heavier demands on immediate attentional control and error inhibition, making them more sensitive to anxiety-related interference and its interaction with executive regulation [23]. In contrast, logical-spatial tasks may rely more on representational reasoning and less on continuous suppression of intrusive cognitive material, which could partly explain the absence of a significant moderation effect.

The findings also have implications for understanding learning in AI-supported mathematics environments. AI tools can provide real-time prompts, adaptive feedback, and procedural scaffolding, but these supports do not operate in a psychological vacuum. Students’ responses to AI-based support may depend on how well they can regulate attention and manage internal interference while interacting with the system [58]. The present results suggest that even in AI-supported settings, executive regulation does not uniformly shield students from anxiety-related difficulty. Instead, students with stronger regulatory capacities may also become more sensitive to the cognitive costs of maintaining control under pressure. This reinforces the idea that effective mathematics learning in AI-enhanced environments depends not only on instructional support, but also on how emotional and cognitive regulatory processes interact during task engagement. At the same time, given the relatively modest size of the observed interaction effects, this implication should be understood as theoretically informative rather than as evidence of large differences in students’ learning outcomes.

Implications

Theoretical implications

A key theoretical contribution of this study lies in clarifying the temporal ordering between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability. Existing research has often treated the two constructs as reciprocally related, yet the present findings suggest a more asymmetrical pattern in primary school students. State mathematics anxiety significantly predicted later mathematical ability, whereas prior mathematical ability did not significantly predict subsequent anxiety. This finding refines current understandings of the anxiety–performance link by suggesting that mathematics anxiety may operate less as an outcome of poor performance and more as an antecedent emotional risk factor that shapes later mathematical development [44].

Another theoretical contribution concerns the role of executive function in the anxiety–performance relationship. Rather than functioning simply as a direct predictor of achievement, executive function emerged as an important boundary condition that shaped the strength of the negative association between mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability. More importantly, the moderating pattern observed in this study complicates the conventional assumption that stronger executive function necessarily buffers the effects of anxiety [16]. The results indicate that higher executive function, particularly inhibitory control, may under some conditions intensify the negative influence of anxiety, thus pointing to a more nuanced and conditional account of cognitive regulation in mathematics learning.

The findings also contribute to theory by examining the anxiety–performance relationship within an AI agent-supported instructional context. Much of the existing literature on AI in mathematics education has emphasized adaptive feedback, personalization, and performance improvement, but has given less attention to how emotional and regulatory characteristics shape students’ responses to such environments [6]. The present study extends this line of inquiry by showing that established psychological factors, such as mathematics anxiety and executive function, remain theoretically relevant in classrooms where AI agent-supported instruction forms part of routine mathematics teaching. This contribution is not to establish the causal effect of AI agents, but to broaden mathematics anxiety research into a contemporary instructional context increasingly shaped by human–AI interaction. In this sense, the study helps align research on mathematics anxiety with current developments in AI-supported education while maintaining a learner-centered theoretical focus.

Practical implication

The findings highlight the importance of addressing mathematics anxiety early in primary education. Because mathematics anxiety predicted later mathematical ability across domains, it should not be viewed merely as a temporary emotional reaction during task performance. Instead, it appears to have developmental significance for subsequent learning. This suggests that classroom practice should place greater emphasis on creating emotionally supportive learning environments, reducing excessive performance pressure, and normalizing mistakes as part of the learning process [45]. Such efforts may help prevent anxiety from becoming an enduring obstacle to mathematical development.

The results also point to the need for a more differentiated understanding of executive function in educational practice. While executive function is often treated as an asset that should be strengthened to improve academic performance, the present findings suggest that stronger executive control does not automatically protect students from anxiety-related difficulties. In particular, the prominent role of inhibitory control indicates that students may require not only cognitive regulation skills but also support in managing the cognitive burden associated with anxiety. Interventions should therefore combine the cultivation of executive skills with strategies for emotional regulation, rather than assuming that cognitive training alone will be sufficient.

Implications also emerge for the design of AI-supported mathematics learning environments. Although AI tools can provide individualized prompts, real-time feedback, and adaptive scaffolding, these affordances may not be equally beneficial for all learners [24, 26]. Students’ emotional readiness and regulatory capacity appear to shape how effectively they can engage with such support. Given that the present study did not directly test specific AI design features, these implications should be interpreted cautiously. Rather than implying that particular system functions would necessarily reduce mathematics anxiety, the findings suggest that future AI-supported mathematics environments may benefit from attending to learners’ emotional and regulatory differences. For example, adaptive pacing, supportive feedback, and carefully sequenced scaffolding could be explored in future design-based or experimental studies as possible ways to make AI-supported mathematics learning more responsive to students’ anxiety and attentional regulation [33].

Conclusion

This study examined the longitudinal relationship between state mathematics anxiety and mathematical ability among primary school students in an AI agent-supported mathematics learning context, and further explored the moderating role of executive function. Using a cross-lagged panel model, the findings revealed that mathematics anxiety significantly and negatively predicted later mathematical ability, including both mathematical computation and logical-spatial ability, whereas prior mathematical ability did not predict subsequent anxiety. In addition, executive function, particularly inhibitory control, moderated the relationship between mathematics anxiety and mathematical performance, with stronger negative effects observed among students with higher levels of executive function. Together, these results highlight the importance of mathematics anxiety and executive regulation in shaping children’s mathematical development over time.

In contrast to prior studies that have emphasized reciprocal relationships between mathematics anxiety and performance or have treated executive function as a protective cognitive resource, the present study provides evidence for a unidirectional effect of anxiety on later mathematical ability and a more conditional role of executive function. The core contribution of this study lies in offering a more nuanced account of how mathematics anxiety and executive function jointly relate to mathematical development in contemporary classrooms where AI agent-supported instruction is routinely embedded. By integrating longitudinal and moderation analyses, the study extends existing research beyond static associations and demonstrates that the effects of anxiety on learning are both temporally structured and contingent on individual differences in cognitive regulation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample was drawn from a single region, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other educational contexts. Second, the study relied on two time points, which constrains the ability to capture more fine-grained developmental trajectories. In addition, although the cross-lagged panel model provides useful evidence regarding temporal ordering, the two-wave design does not allow for strong causal inference. It may also conflate between-person differences with within-person changes, meaning that the observed pathways should be interpreted as longitudinal associations rather than definitive causal effects. Third, although multiple measures were used, the reliance on questionnaire data for mathematics anxiety may introduce potential self-report bias.

Fourth, AI agent-supported instruction was examined as the instructional context rather than as an experimentally manipulated condition. Although all participants learned in classrooms where AI agents were routinely integrated into mathematics teaching, AI usage was not experimentally manipulated or directly measured. The study also did not include a non-AI comparison group or collect process data on students’ interactions with specific AI functions. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of the relationships among mathematics anxiety, executive function, and mathematical ability within an AI agent-supported instructional context, rather than as evidence of the effectiveness of AI agent-supported instruction itself. Future research could address these limitations by including more diverse samples across regions or educational systems, adopting multi-wave longitudinal designs, such as random-intercept cross-lagged panel models or latent growth models, to distinguish within-person changes from stable between-person differences, and incorporating multimethod assessments such as behavioral or physiological measures of anxiety. In addition, future studies may compare AI-supported and non-AI-supported classrooms or use learning process data to examine how specific AI functions interact with mathematics anxiety and executive function during problem solving.
