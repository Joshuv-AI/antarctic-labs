Executive Summary
In this project, AvCarb, LLC evaluated the baseline performance metrics of commercial carbon
veils and their corresponding Gas Diffusion Layers (GDLs) with the goal of establishing an
optimized, vertically integrated production system for wet-laid nonwoven substrates used in gas
diffusion media for electrochemical energy storage and conversion devices. Mechanical testing
and microstructural characterization were conducted and used to develop a multiscale
computational model capable of simulating and predicting the performance of GDLs in fuel cells.
Although the project successfully generated foundational transport and modeling data, it was
terminated prior to identifying the critical GDL design parameters necessary for full
optimization.

The program aimed to improve carbon veil fabrication through enhanced fiber dispersion,
fiber-fiber adhesion control, and improved web formation, enabling the production of
high-quality, uniform substrates. Simulations were intended to guide mixing and solution
delivery system design and process conditions, followed by production-scale trials to evaluate
fiber dispersion, web uniformity, and mechanical robustness. At full deployment, the proposed
production line would have been capable of producing approximately 650,000 m² of carbon veil
annually. This capability remains strategically important, as the United States currently lacks a
domestic source of wet-laid nonwoven carbon substrates that satisfy the stringent quality
requirements for fuel-cell GDLs and electrolyzers representing an ongoing supply-chain
vulnerability. Beyond supply-chain benefits, the project established a robust benchmarking
dataset for existing commercial carbon veils while advancing next-generation material concepts
targeting improved performance and manufacturing consistency.

Task 1 established baseline physical, mechanical, and microstructural characteristics of five
commercial carbon veils and their resulting GDLs. These metrics such as uniformity, structural
integrity, porosity, tortuosity, tensile strength were correlated with electrochemical
performances to understand how substrate attributes affect final GDL functions.

Task 2 focused on developing and validating a comprehensive multiscale computational model
to predict the performance of gas diffusion layers (GDLs) in fuel cells. xCT-derived
microstructure data from Task 1 were incorporated into simulations to determine permeability,
tortuosity, pore-size distributions, and water-transport behavior. These models linked microscale
fiber-level features to macroscale fuel-cell behavior, enabling predictive simulations of water
and gas transport and establishing a quantitative foundation for future material design. The
impact of the modeling work performed during the first three quarters provides the necessary
grounds for transport studies of any sample of interest for which xCT images have been
collected, and this data has already been successfully implemented into the macroscale modeling
of PEMFC to enable predictive performance simulations. This capability is instrumental in
identifying material development priorities.

Task 3 supported the optimization of carbon veil fabrication processes by investigating fiber
dispersion, adhesion, and mechanical behavior. Modeling was used to refine mixing-system
design and operating conditions, and production-scale trials began to assess veil uniformity and
mechanical robustness. These activities were intended to guide the final design of a production
line capable of manufacturing high-quality, uniformly dispersed carbon veils.

Overall, the characterization, modeling, and preliminary process-development efforts completed
during the project created the technical foundation needed for future optimization of domestic
carbon veil and GDL manufacturing. Although the project ended before full optimization could
be achieved, its results provide a clear path forward for continuing development of
next-generation GDL materials and associated production technologies.

Introduction
AvCarb, with over six decades of developing and providing engineered carbon substrates, has built a
portfolio of materials that specifically enhance the operations and economics of electrochemical
systems like fuel cells, electrolyzers, and energy storage systems. Our extensive expertise in carbon-
based materials technically enabled the creation of the DOE-funded initiative titled “Establishment of
a Vertically Integrated Domestic Manufacturing Process for Production of Substrates Needed for
Manufacture of Gas Diffusion Layers.”

This initiative was proposed to leverage AvCarb LLC’s materials and manufacturing expertise to
establish a domestic production line capable of producing at least 650,000 m2 of carbon veil substrates
annually. These carbon veils, also referred to as carbon nonwoven papers, serve as the base substrate
to produce Gas Diffusion Layers (GDLs) for both fuel cell and electrolyzer applications. By enabling
domestic production of these critical materials, the project was intended to reduce supply chain risks,
mitigate cost volatility associated with imports, and establish a robust and scalable U.S. based supply
chain for advanced electrochemical technologies.

Beyond strengthening the domestic supply chain for clean hydrogen technologies, this initiative was
designed to optimize the performance of carbon veils through rigorous studies and modeling of both
the substrates and their resulting Gas Diffusion Layers (GDLs). These studies aim to advance
understanding of these critical materials, identify opportunities for cost savings, and improve
downstream manufacturing efficiencies, ultimately enabling broader deployment of Proton Exchange
Membrane Fuel Cells (PEMFCs) in medium- and heavy-duty vehicles.

Project objectives
The primary objective of this project is to develop a fully domestic, vertically integrated production
system for wet-laid nonwoven substrates used in the manufacture of gas diffusion media. The project
specifically aimed to advance domestic production capabilities for carbon veil Gas Diffusion Layers
(GDLs), which are critical to hydrogen fuel cells and electrolyzers. Working alongside three university
partners, AvCarb aimed to:

 • Establish the baseline physical structure and critical properties of both the existing carbon veils
 and the GDL products made from these veils.
 • Incorporate these characterizations results into initial models comparing the structure and
 performance of commercially-sourced versus domestically produced veils.
 • Identify the critical process parameters required to manufacture high-quality carbon veils.
 • Validate these parameters through pilot-scale fabrication trials experimental trials that
 demonstrate the feasibility of carbon veil production on a pilot line and then with an external
 commercial vendor.
 • The final objective of Phase I is to match the existing baseline material with the material made
 at this external vendor.

Phase II was intended to design, build, and install a production line capable of producing ≥ 650,000 m2
of substrate annually. Concurrently with the installation, AvCarb will work closely with the
subrecipients to test different carbon veil designs to improve the uniformity and performance of the gas
diffusion media under various operating conditions and validate these designs via short trial rolls.

Summary of Technical Accomplishments by Tasks
The project was structured into two phases, with a Go/No-Go decision point at mid-project. Phase I
focused on research and development activities, including establishing baseline uniformity of
commercial carbon veils, characterizing their physical and mechanical properties, modeling their
behavior, and validating these findings through laboratory testing as seen on Figure 1. This work was
intended to identify which veil characteristics most strongly influence GDL performances. Although
significant progress had been made and many Phase I milestones were approaching completion, the
Department of Energy (DOE) terminated the project for convenience on October 9, 2025.

Task 1. Baseline Evaluation of Carbon Products
Task 1 established the baseline performance metrics for commercial carbon veils, focusing on
uniformity, structural integrity, and performance indicators such as resistivity and stiffness. Because
these veils form the primary structure of the GDL substrates, modifying their properties is hopefully a
great way to adjust GDL performance. These characteristics were then measured to correlate them with
the final electrochemical performance.

Subtask 1.1: Down Web & Cross Web Sampling at AvCarb
Standardized sampling and mechanical testing protocols were successfully developed and validated to
ensure data consistency, repeatability, and traceability across all samples. This work provided a
statistically rigorous foundation for comparative analyses of carbon veil designs and morphologies in
the future.

A comprehensive sampling of five different carbon veils was completed during this subtask, both every
linear meter down-web for the first and last 15 meters and every 12” cross-web. Table 2 presents a
legend describing the 5 carbon veil (CV) rolls and lot numbers used during the project. Figure 2
describes the sampling layout along and across each roll.

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that carbon veil type significantly influenced most
physical characteristics. Distinct letter groupings in Table 4 confirm statistically significant differences
among most veil pairs for each mechanical property. Means in the same column followed by different
letters are significantly different (p < 0.05). Thickness and basis weight despite exhibiting the largest
F-values (149.8 and 1304.1, respectively), are primarily governed by manufacturing and forming
conditions rather than compositional differences.

The binder content differed significantly across all veil rolls (F = 267.9), with CV5 exhibiting the
highest level (23.4%) and CV3/CV4 the lowest. Tensile properties exhibited clear anisotropy, with
machine direction (MD) values consistently exceeding cross-machine direction (CMD) values across
all veil types. Generally, the MD tensile strength increased with increasing binder content, with CV5
showing the highest MD tensile (3365 N/m). While increased binder content enhanced MD tensile
strength, CMD tensile strength showed a weaker response, suggesting that binder preferentially
reinforces fiber alignment. Wet MD tensile strength generally follows the dry MD trends but at reduced
magnitudes, reflecting the effect of water on the carbon veil strength. Carbon veil rolls with higher
binder content show superior wet tensile retention as expected. The skeletal density varied
independently of the tensile performance, implying that this property alone is not a reliable predictor
of mechanical strength.

Overall, binder content seems to be the dominant variable influencing mechanical performance.
Directional tensile behavior and wet tensile strength further highlight the importance of binder
distribution, rather than bulk structural parameters alone, in tuning the performances and
manufacturability of the subsequent substrates derived from these carbon veils.

A comparison between samples collected at the start and end of the roll was performed for CV3 and
CV4 to evaluate uniformity in the machine direction. For CV3, the thickness differed significantly
between the beginning of the roll and end of the roll, whereas all other measured properties were
similar. For CV4, significant differences were observed in basis weight and wet MD tensile strength
between both ends of the roll. Notably, all tensile properties increased at the end of the roll, which
could be explained by the process reaching steady state.

The same analysis was performed to assess the cross-web uniformity, in which samples from the left,
center and right positions (collected at 12-inch intervals) were tested and compared using one-way
ANOVA. No significant differences were observed for any of the responses evaluated (all p-value >
0.2), confirming that each roll was uniform in the cross-machine direction.

Results:
The sampling and testing protocols developed in this subtask provided consistent, traceable data across
all five commercial carbon veils. Baseline measurements of thickness, basis weight, tensile strength,
wet tensile strength, and binder content were collected. Statistical analysis showed that veil type
significantly affected most properties, with binder content emerging as the primary driver of
mechanical performance. Down-web assessments revealed some variation at the start and end of rolls
for CV3 and CV4, whereas cross-web measurements showed no significant differences, confirming
good uniformity across the width of each carbon veil. Overall, the results established a reliable baseline
and demonstrated that binder distribution, more than bulk structural parameters, governs veil
performance and manufacturability. Because some of the end samples were not tested on every roll,
this tasks’ completion is 85%.

Subtask 1.2. Validation of Carbon Veil at University of California, Irvine (UCI)
The samples collected in Subtask 1.1 were evaluated with X-ray computed tomography (xCT)
technique to determine critical physical properties of the carbon veils made from different carbon fibers.
The calibration and validation of image processing techniques were performed to ensure the dimensions
measured are precise and accurate, and to obtain an optimal separation of the different layers in the
substrate stack. These efforts were critical in developing the analytical foundation for assessing veil
microstructure, including fiber diameter distribution, pore size, porosity, and tortuosity. By quantifying
these structural parameters, the project team has generated a database that allows meaningful cross-
comparisons among carbon veil configurations. The development of image processing techniques and
data collected was incorporated into the modeling activities under Task 2, providing empirical inputs
for simulation and predictive modeling of GDL performance.

The xCT for carbon veils was performed at beamline 8.3.2 of the Advanced Light Source at Lawrence
Berkeley National Laboratory. For sample preparation, a circle with a diameter of 3 mm was cut from
each of the 10 carbon veil samples. As shown in Figure 3, the samples were split into 2 groups of 5 and
placed into acrylic tubes with 5 mil Kapton used as spacers between the carbon veil samples. Samples
were collected from the center of the roll, at meter 3 (M3) and meter 9 (M9) down-web from the start.

 Figure 3. Sample preparation for the X-ray CT measurements
 of the carbon veil samples.

At an X-ray energy of 17 keV and an optical zoom of 5x, it was possible to view all 5 samples per
acrylic tube in the scanning window at one time (5 samples imaged per 1 scan). The resulting pixel size
for these xCT scans was 1.27 μm per pixel. Thicknesses of the carbon veils were measured using the
line tool in ImageJ on 9 different sections of each carbon veil throughout the full image stack and taking
the average value.

From the resulting image stacks, FIJI ImageJ was used to isolate and align each sample horizontally
before a section of 1500 μm by 1500 μm by the full sample thickness was selected to perform the
characterization. Each cropped section was then thresholded to yield a binary image stack (fiber and
pore phases only). The ImageJ plugin BoneJ was used to extract average pore size and pore size
distribution data for each veil. A code that measures the percent of each image slice that is the pore
domain was used to calculate the porosity for the veils. All 10 samples are very porous (~95%) and

Milestone 4 (#1.1.4): Microstructural Evaluation and Baseline performance of GDL materials

Results:
The work performed for Task 1.4 provided critical baseline data that will help select an optimal carbon
veil/process combination for the GDL product. The data collected during this project presents a trade-
off between mechanical robustness and electrochemical performance/gas transport depending on the
carbon veil used. If the primary design goal is peak power density and efficient water management,
CV1 could be the material of choice due to its better transport properties (lower tortuosity and OTR).
However, if the primary design goal is product durability and handling, CV4 could be the better option
due to its superior mechanical strength, particularly in CMD. The preliminary electrochemical testing
revealed a limited correlation between GDL properties and electrochemical performance, supporting
the theory that Microporous Layer (MPL) and Catalyst Layers (CL) are the main factors in the
performance bottleneck and can easily mask the full benefits derived from variation in GDL design.
Consequently, future work should integrate MPL and CL optimization with each GDL design.

Task 2. Fabrication of a Multi-Scale Model
During the project, University 2 developed and built the frame of a multiscale computational fluid
dynamics (CFD) model to predict GDL performances in PEMFCs. Specifically, they built microscale
and macroscale models using the characterization data generated during Task 1 with the goal of
extracting the most critical GDL design parameters to guide future designs and improve PEMFC
performances.

Subtask 2.1 – Creation of the CFD Model
To establish the microscale model, University 2 utilized three-dimensional GDL microstructure images
provided by University 1. xCT data from MB-30 and EP-40 substrates were analyzed to extract key
structural characteristics, which were then used to calculate transport properties, including
permeability, porosity, and tortuosity, for input into the macroscale PEMFC model. MB-30 represents
the final GDL configuration incorporating the microporous layer (MPL), while EP-40 is the
corresponding substrate without an MPL used in the fabrication of MB-30. In addition to property
extraction, the reconstructed microstructure images were used to investigate liquid water transport
behavior within the GDL and across the MPL/Substrate interface for microscale simulations. The
images were reconstructed to 3D geometries through image analysis and imported into the
computational fluid dynamics platform Simcenter STAR-CCM+ to perform transport simulations
directly within the resolved microstructure. In addition to the effective transport properties, key water
management metrics including water breakthrough pressure and liquid water transport pathways were
also investigated.

The calculations of the tortuosity (bottom left) and permeability (top left) are summarized in Figure 17.
On the right side of this graphic are the computation meshes for both EP-40 and MB-30.

was used. Temperature gradients were computed across two computational domains: one representing

solid obstruction). The ratio of the effective thermal conductivities obtained from these two domains
was then used to estimate the tortuosity of the porous structure. Table 10 reports the values of this

Task 3. Modeling of Carbon Veil Production Parameters
This task investigated the relationship between fiber–fiber friction, dispersion behavior, and fiber
mechanical properties to support modeling and optimization of wet-lay nonwoven manufacturing of
carbon veil and carbon paper. The dispersion time and agitation intensity (shear force) required to
disperse the carbon fiber-bundles depend on the frictional (adhesion) forces between the individual
fibers in the bundles. The adhesion force between the fibers depends on fiber surface roughness, fiber
hydrophobicity, fiber length, fiber diameter, and other factors such as wet end additives. The

information obtained from this Task will be used in Subtasks 3.2 and 3.3 to determine the agitation
intensity needed for successful dispersion of the fiber-bundles in a mixing tank.

The frictional behavior of carbon fibers was systematically evaluated in several fluid environments to

critical role of water chemistry control during processing, as variations in ionic content can directly
affect the uniformity and microstructural quality of the final carbon veil or paper.

Subtask 3.2 Optimal Baffle Mixer & Flow Conditions Defined for Existing Carbon Fiber
The raw carbon fibers used to make the carbon veil substrates come in bundles (flakes) of
predetermined lengths and these bundles need to be individualized during the mixing process to obtain
proper veil formation. There are two types of defects expected during this process: logs and rope. Logs
are undispersed fiber bundles while Ropes defects are dispersed fibers that are wrapped around one
another. Rigid fibers do not usually form ropes but can form elongated logs that may behave as such.
This task goals are to identify the best processes and equipment’s design to disperse the carbon fibers
without creating defects.

In order to predict the optimal baffle design and flow conditions necessary to disperse the fiber bundles
into single fibers and lay them down uniformly, a computational model was developed to simulate fiber
dispersion in a mixing tank. In the CFD-DEM simulation, the water flow field inside the tank is
simulated and used to track the trajectory of the fiber bundles as they break up into individual fibers
and disperse in the tank as a function of time. Figure 41 shows a magnified view of the flow field from
a simulation using flexible fibers.

Milestone 8 (#3.3.2): Optimal Baffle Mixer & Flow Conditions Defined for Existing Carbon Fiber

Results:
The flow pattern was mainly influenced by the baffle and the rotor (impeller–shaft assembly).
Employing four blades per impeller with a 45° blade angle, a shaft length of 15 cm, and multiple
impellers mounted along a single shaft increased the turbulent intensity, shear rate, turbulent kinetic
energy, and fluid velocity, which in turn promoted effective dispersion of carbon fibers in the tank.
Using the DEM solver, the average contact force (adhesive, normal, and tangential) was determined to
be acting on the carbon fiber bundles, as well as the drag force exerted by the fluid on
the individualized fibers. These forces gradually decreased as mixing progressed. Once the fibers
became fully dispersed, the adhesive forces between them reached a minimum and remained the same.
Additionally, increasing the impeller’s rotational speed enhanced the fluid velocity, turbulent intensity,
shear rate, and turbulent kinetic energy, which collectively promoted the breakup
of the fiber bundles and improved their dispersion in the tank. The dispersion behavior was also
strongly influenced by the optimal design of the mixing system (specifically the baffle shape, number
of impeller blades, blade angle, shaft height, and the number of impellers mounted on a single shaft).
Other factors impacting fiber dispersion include impeller speed, mixing time, surface energy, and fiber
length. It was observed that higher impeller rotational speed, lower carbon fiber surface energy, a four-
blade impeller configuration, multiple impellers on a single shaft, and a 45° blade angle significantly
reduced the time required to achieve complete carbon dispersion. The dispersed fibers were collected
onto a moving belt to virtually fabricate a carbon veil. This step was performed after ensuring complete
mixing and dispersion of all fiber bundles. The simulations are presently about 75% completed as they
need to be calibrated with the hands-on experiments.

Validation: Our CFD-DEM computational model is now operational, and it can simulate fiber
dispersion but requires calibration.

Comparative analyses of optical microscope images, fiber orientation distributions, dominant angles,
anisotropy ratios, and fiber diameter measurements for CV1–CV5 samples collected from the start and
end of the production rolls revealed no significant differences, aside from a few isolated outliers. The
consistent microstructural features and fiber alignment patterns indicate stable process conditions
throughout roll production. Minor variations observed in dominant angle and anisotropy ratios are
likely attributed to localized deposition or handling effects rather than systematic process drift.

The average size of fiber bundles, or “log bundles,” across all analyzed samples was found to be
approximately three to four fiber diameters, exhibiting a relatively high standard deviation. This
variation suggests localized differences in fiber packing density and alignment within the veil structure.
However, comparisons between samples collected from the start and end of the production rolls
revealed no meaningful differences in bundle size, indicating consistent fiber deposition and web
formation throughout the manufacturing process. Minor variations observed are within the expected
range for commercial-scale production and are unlikely to impact the overall structural or mechanical
performance of carbon veils.

The surface morphology of the carbon veil samples was examined using a Hitachi SU3900 scanning
electron microscope (SEM). Representative SEM images of surface fibers are shown in Figure 55 to
illustrate log formation. The analysis revealed the presence of a possible surface finish (sizing agent)
or residue between adjacent fiber bundles, suggesting that special attention is needed to make sure this
sizing agent is fully removed/dissolved, during the mixing or dispersion process. Without removing
this agent, certain regions within the veil could contain partially unseparated fiber clusters.

Conclusion

The work completed under Award DE-EE0011353 established a strong technical foundation for the
future development of a fully domestic, vertically integrated manufacturing process for carbon veils
and gas diffusion layers (GDLs) used in PEM fuel cells and electrolyzers. Although the project was
terminated prior to Phase II, the technical accomplishments across characterization, modeling, and early
process development represent substantial progress toward removing a critical supply-chain
vulnerability for the United States. Collectively, the data, methodologies, and modeling tools generated
provide a robust, science-based framework that future efforts can build on to advance domestic
production capabilities.

A comprehensive evaluation of five commercially sourced carbon veils formed the backbone of the
project. AvCarb established rigorous sampling protocols, mechanical testing procedures, and statistical
analyses that revealed the relationships between binder content, mechanical strength, anisotropy, and
down-web variability. University partners expanded this foundation through high-resolution xCT,
extracting pore-scale features such as porosity, pore radius, and tortuosity. These datasets—combined
with water contact angle and tensiometry results—offered a detailed understanding of water-transport
behavior, wettability, and microstructural uniformity across veil types. The transformation of this
foundational data into substrates and finished GDLs enabled further correlations between carbon veil
properties, GDL mechanical robustness, diffusivity, permeability, and electrochemical performance.
These analyses revealed the complex and often non-linear interactions between carbon veil structure,
MPL intrusion, and fuel-cell water management, highlighting the need for integrated
materials-and-process optimization.

One of the most significant accomplishments of the project was the successful development of a
multiscale computational modeling framework by University 2. For the first time, xCT-derived GDL
microstructures were integrated directly into micro- and macroscale CFD simulations capable of
predicting water saturation, breakthrough behavior, gas transport resistance, and full-cell polarization
performance under varying humidity and pressure conditions. The team produced 14
microstructure-resolved CFD models across carbon veils and GDLs, enabling quantitative links
between structural parameters such as tortuosity, porosity, permeability, and MPL intrusion depth with
the resulting cell-level performance. These models not only validated experimental trends—such as the
strong influence of MPL structure on mass-transport limitations—but also established a predictive
capability essential for guiding future veil-design strategies, manufacturing parameter optimization,
and material down-selection.

In parallel, University 3 established a detailed scientific understanding of fiber–fiber interactions that
directly govern carbon-veil manufacturability. Through friction testing, solvent and dispersant studies,
scanning electron microscopy, mixing-tank experiments, and CFD-DEM simulations, the team
quantified adhesion forces, dispersion behavior, fiber-bundle breakup, and the impact of mixing system

geometry. These results identified the critical role of fluid chemistry, impeller design, blade angle,
rotational speed, and fiber surface energy in achieving stable, uniform fiber dispersion—knowledge
essential for scaling wet-laid veil production. Additionally, commissioning of a pilot wet-laid line
demonstrated the feasibility of producing carbon-fiber mats and established a practical platform for
future optimization of binder formulations, dispersion systems, and web-forming parameters.

While the project did not progress to Phase II construction and demonstration of a commercial-scale
production line, the achievements of Phase I have clearly defined the pathway forward. The project
delivered: (1) a validated, statistically robust baseline for current commercial carbon veils and GDLs,
(2) a predictive multiscale CFD modeling capability that connects microstructure to fuel-cell
performance, (3) a scientific understanding of fiber-dispersion physics and the parameters controlling
wet-laid manufacture, and (4) initial pilot-line validation for future process development. These
outcomes collectively reduce technical risk and significantly accelerate the development of a domestic
GDL substrate manufacturing capability.

The groundwork laid by this project positions future efforts to focus on optimizing veil microstructures,
advancing MPL integration strategies, refining dispersion and mixing conditions, and ultimately
transitioning from pilot-scale trials to full commercial production. As hydrogen technologies expand
and fuel-cell adoption accelerates, the insights and tools generated here will play a central role in
enabling secure, high-quality, U.S.-based supply of critical GDL materials.
