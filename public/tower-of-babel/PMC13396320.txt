Extended Preoperative Immunotherapy Duration Reduces the Risk of Myasthenia Gravis Exacerbation for Patients with Thymoma: A Multicenter Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jialin Chen, Hongxi Chen, Xianglin Chu, Jie Song, Nana Zhang, Huahua Zhong, Zhangyu Zou, Hui Wu, Liewen Pang, Chongbo Zhao, Hongyu Zhou, Zhitao Gu, Sushan Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPatients with thymoma-associated myasthenia gravis (TAMG) frequently have a higher risk for clinical deterioration. Previous studies primarily focused on postoperative myasthenic crisis (MC), leaving intermediate and long-term MG outcomes after thymectomy largely unexplored. This study aimed to identify predictors of postoperative acute exacerbation within 1 year after thymectomy and explore the potential benefits of preoperative immunotherapies.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study enrolled 347 patients with TAMG from 1756 individuals between February 2008 and October 2024. The primary endpoint was defined as the occurrence of acute exacerbation within 1 year postoperatively. Statistical evaluation employed logistic regression, Cox proportional hazards models, receiver operating characteristic analysis, and Kaplan–Meier curves.ResultsOf the study cohort, 96 patients (27.7%) had postoperative acute exacerbation of MG within 1 year. Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified that preoperative Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) III–V was an independent risk factor for postoperative acute exacerbation (hazard ratio, HR 3.913, P < 0.001); preoperative immunotherapy duration > 14 days was associated with a protective effect (HR 0.419, P < 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified preoperative MGFA III–V (odds ratio, OR 0.392, P < 0.05), reduced rate of R0 resection (OR 0.440, P < 0.05), and postoperative acute exacerbation (OR 0.245, P < 0.001) as independent risk predictors for achieving minimal symptom expression (MSE) at 1 year postoperatively.ConclusionA preoperative immunotherapy duration exceeding 14 days is crucial for improving postoperative outcomes for TAMG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-026-00988-y.

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disease characterized by antibody-mediated functional impairment of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), leading to fluctuating weakness and fatigue of skeletal muscles [1, 2]. Approximately 50–70% of patients with MG present thymic hyperplasia, while thymoma occurs in 10–20% of such patients [3, 4]. In contrast, thymoma-associated myasthenia gravis (TAMG) accounts for approximately 30% of patients with thymoma, who present earlier onset, more severe symptoms, higher mortality, and a predilection for generalized symptoms with bulbar and respiratory involvement compared to those without thymoma [4–6]. Clinically, nearly all patients with TAMG are positive for anti-acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibodies and exhibit a heightened susceptibility to therapeutic resistance and myasthenic crisis (MC) [1, 7, 8].

Thymectomy plays a crucial role in the treatment of TAMG and achieves favorable long-term neurological outcomes even in patients with severe MG [9]. However, a substantial proportion of patients with TAMG may suffer from worsening and relapse of MG or develop postoperative myasthenic crisis (POMC) after thymectomy [3, 10]. Postoperative MG exacerbation often results from multiple synergistic mechanisms. Crucially, thymectomy cannot eliminate the pre-existing peripheral autoreactive T cells that escaped impaired negative selection in the thymoma. Additionally, thymoma resection reduces regulatory T cells, inducing an immune imbalance [3], while surgical stress triggers inflammatory cytokines that directly damage neuromuscular junctions and exacerbate complement-mediated destruction [11]. Consequently, these factors frequently lead to postoperative acute exacerbations or MC, necessitating adjunctive perioperative immunotherapy to control residual pathogenic activity [12].

Recently, a new therapeutic goal to achieve the minimal symptom expression (MSE) or full symptom-free remission with a minimal daily oral corticosteroid dose (≤ 5 mg) was proposed by an international Delphi consensus [13]. Despite this, clinical practice lacks a standardized consensus regarding the optimal administration and duration of perioperative immunotherapies, particularly for patients with TAMG presenting with milder preoperative symptoms. Given these clinical challenges, it is essential to define predictive factors for long-term efficacy in this population to mitigate postoperative risks and explore the potential benefits of preoperative immunotherapies.

Therefore, this study aims to identify the clinical predictors of postoperative acute exacerbation and the achievement of MSE within the first year following thymectomy in a multicenter TAMG cohort. By delineating these predictive factors, we hope to facilitate the development of individualized, evidence-based preoperative management strategies.

Methods

Study Design, Subjects, and Data Collection

This was a multicenter, retrospective observational study. We included patients with TAMG who underwent thymectomy at Huashan Hospital Fudan University, Shanghai Chest Hospital of Shanghai Jiao Tong University, West China Hospital of Sichuan University, and Fujian Medical University Union Hospital between February 2008 and October 2024.

Diagnosis of MG: patients presented with typical clinical symptoms of fluctuating muscle weakness and fatigue, combined with at least one positive result from the following objective evaluations: pharmacological testing (intramuscular neostigmine test), electrophysiological studies (repetitive nerve stimulation [RNS] or single-fiber electromyography [SFEMG]), or serological testing for anti-AChR antibodies (tested by commercial radioimmunoassay kit, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, or cell-based assay) [14, 15].Pathologically confirmed thymoma.Postoperative follow-up period ≥ 1 year.

Clinical data were retrospectively reviewed, including gender, age at MG onset, age at thymectomy, disease duration from MG onset to thymectomy, preoperative immunotherapy regimens, preoperative Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) classification and MG Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) score, comorbidities, World Health Organization (WHO) histologic subtypes and tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging, surgical approach, intraoperative blood loss, and completeness of resection. During postoperative follow-up, patients were evaluated using the MG-ADL and the Myasthenia Gravis Composite (MGC) Scale, and the treatment was recorded simultaneously [16, 17]. Permission to use the MG-ADL scale in this study was obtained from Mapi Research Trust, Lyon, France (https://eprovide.mapi-trust.org). All procedures involving human subjects were performed in accordance with the ethical standards formulated by the institutional and/or national research committees, as well as the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments or comparable ethical standards. The study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Huashan Hospital (2019-441, 2020-999 and 2022-913). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their inclusion in this study, including the permission for the use of MG-ADL score and MGFA classifications.

According to the previous studies, stable high-dose corticosteroid therapy administered 2 to 4 weeks preoperatively reduces the incidence of POMC in patients with severe clinical symptom subtypes [18]. In clinical practice, patients with well-controlled preoperative symptoms may not require such prolonged treatment courses or high-dose corticosteroids. The 14-day cutoff for preoperative treatment was determined based on the clinical pharmacological characteristics of corticosteroids in myasthenia gravis. The onset of clinical efficacy for corticosteroids typically requires approximately 2 weeks. Furthermore, corticosteroid-induced transient worsening of myasthenic symptoms predominantly occurs within the first 2 weeks of administration. Therefore, we reasoned that a preoperative treatment duration of > 14 days provides an optimal and secure window to achieve clinical stabilization and effectively prevent postoperative acute exacerbations.

To further clarify the impact of preoperative immunotherapy on postoperative outcomes, we further stratify the participants according to the duration of immunotherapy use: (1) the extended immunotherapy group, defined as the stable use with corticosteroid for more than 2 weeks before thymectomy, with or without concurrent administration of immunosuppressants, plasma exchange (PE), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG); (2) the control group, defined as no immunotherapy or the duration of immunotherapy less than 2 weeks before thymectomy. Patients who received preoperative IVIG or plasmapheresis alone without baseline corticosteroid therapy were excluded from these specific treatment categories. Notably, no such patients were present in our study.

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint was defined as the occurrence of postoperative acute exacerbation within 1 year, while the secondary endpoint included (1) the achievement of MSE at 1 year postoperatively; (2) the comparison of 1-year clinical outcomes stratified by preoperative myasthenia gravis severity (mild vs. moderate-to-severe); and (3) the rate of secondary generalization in patients initially presenting with ocular myasthenia gravis (OMG). Postoperative acute exacerbation was defined as a clinical deterioration of non-ocular symptoms, manifesting as an increase of greater than 3 points in the non-ocular items of the Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) score, concurrently requiring an adjustment of the treatment regimen to alleviate the symptoms. MSE is defined as an MG-ADL score of 0 or 1 [19]. Patients were grouped as MSE and non-MSE groups based on the achievement of MSE at 1 year after thymectomy.

Clinical Definitions

Preoperative disease severity was assessed using the MGFA clinical classification [20]. As referenced from a previous study [21], patients with a preoperative MGFA classification (I or II) were defined as “mild TAMG”. In contrast, patients with MGFA III or higher were defined as “moderate-severe TAMG.” Thymic epithelial tumors were histologically classified according to the WHO criteria [22] and TNM staging system [23]. The surgical approach was divided into open surgery and minimally invasive surgery. Thymoma resection status was categorized into two types: category 1 refers to R0 resection (complete resection), and category 2 includes R1 resection (microscopically incomplete resection) or R2 resection (macroscopically incomplete resection) [24].

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data was expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using the t test when the data showed normal distribution. Data not conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed by the Mann–Whitney test for intergroup differences. Categorical data were expressed as percentages, and intergroup differences were evaluated using the chi-square test (χ2 test) or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with P < 0.1 in comparisons between the MSE and non-MSE groups were included in the multivariate logistic regression model of predicting MSE at 1 year after thymectomy. The model performance was quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). In the logistic regression model, an AUC value > 0.7 was considered indicative of a good model.

For time-to-event analysis, the interval from thymectomy (T0) to the onset of postoperative acute exacerbation was recorded in months. The influencing factors of postoperative acute exacerbation were assessed by univariate Cox regression analysis. Variables with P < 0.1 were included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis. Cumulative incidence of postoperative acute exacerbation was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and the log-rank test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for two-sided tests. In the Cox proportional hazards model, censoring was defined as patients who did not experience postoperative acute exacerbation by the end of the 1-year follow-up period, or those who were lost to follow-up or died from other causes. Additionally, complete data were available for all variables of interest in our final study cohort; therefore, no missing data imputation methods were required. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Mac version 26.0.

Results

Preoperative Clinical Features and Overall Outcomes

From a total of 1756 individuals with thymoma who were initially screened, we finally enrolled a total of 347 patients with TAMG. The detailed demographic and clinical baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. All participants were AChR antibody-positive, with no MuSK antibody positivity or double-seronegative myasthenia gravis were identified. Patients were stratified according to preoperative MGFA classification as follows: class I (n = 103), class II (n = 147), class III (n = 83), class IV (n = 8), and class V (n = 6) (Fig. 1b). Among these patients, 137 (39.5%) were male and 210 (60.5%) were female, with a median age at MG onset of 46 years. The median age at thymectomy was 47 years, and the median interval from MG onset to thymectomy was 3 months. The median preoperative MG-ADL score was 3 (IQR 2, 6), and the median MGC score was 4 (IQR 2, 13). Additionally, a total of 298 (85.9%) patients achieved R0 resection. A total of 221 patients (63.7%) received immunotherapy for more than 2 weeks prior to surgery, among whom 52 patients were concomitantly administered PE or IVIG, and 50 patients were given immunosuppressants in combination. The preoperative median daily prednisone dose before the surgery was 42.22 ± 5.36 mg. Among patients who received preoperative IVIG or PE (n = 52), 37 were classified as preoperative MGFA III–V and 15 were categorized as MGFA I–II (71.2% vs. 28.8%). Compared with patients who did not receive preoperative IVIG or PE, those who had advanced treatments are less likely to achieve MSE at 1 year postoperatively (57.7% vs. 71.9%, P = 0.04), while no significant difference in the incidence of postoperative acute exacerbation between these two groups (36.5% vs. 26.1%, P = 0.121).

Among all enrolled participants, 287 (82.7%) patients underwent minimally invasive surgery, while 60 (17.3%) underwent open surgery. Type B2 (WHO) was the most prevalent (139 cases, 40.1%), followed by B3 (100 cases, 28.8%), AB (42 cases, 12.1%), B1 (41 cases, 11.8%), A (24 cases, 6.9%), and C (1 case, 0.3%) subtypes. According to the TNM staging, there were 232 (66.9%) patients with stage I, 52 (15%) patients with stage II, 19 (5.5%) patients with stage III, and 44 (12.7%) patients with stage IV. Ninety-two patients received postoperative radiotherapy and 21 received chemotherapy.

At 1 year postoperatively, 242 patients (69.7%) achieved MSE, the median time to MSE was 3 months (IQR 2–5). Ninety-six (27.7%) patients developed postoperative acute exacerbation, 52 (15%) of whom had postoperative MC or impending MC (IMC). Thirty-six (10.4%) patients suffered from MC or IMC within 1 month after thymectomy. Among the 96 patients who experienced postoperative acute exacerbations, the median MG-ADL score during the exacerbation episode was 13.0 (IQR 7.75–17.0), and the median MGC score was 32.5 (IQR 15.0–38.0). Furthermore, 52 patients (54.1%) required admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) for close monitoring or respiratory support.

Risk Factors and Prediction Model for Postoperative Acute Exacerbation

The cumulative incidence of postoperative acute exacerbation exhibited a progressive increase over the follow-up period. In the Cox proportional hazards model, time-to-event was calculated from thymectomy to the onset of acute exacerbation or censored at 1 year. Univariate Cox regression analysis (Table 2) revealed no significant associations between postoperative acute exacerbation and gender, age at onset, age at thymectomy, disease duration from MG onset to thymectomy, surgical approach, intraoperative blood loss, comorbidities, or WHO histopathology. The P values for preoperative MGFA class, preoperative immunotherapy regimen, TNM staging, and completeness of resection were all < 0.1. These variables were subsequently included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis. Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified preoperative MGFA III–V and immunotherapy duration > 14 days as independent predictors for postoperative acute exacerbation within 1 year after surgery. The risk of acute exacerbation was significantly increased in patients with preoperative MGFA III–V (HR 3.913, P < 0.001). The Kaplan–Meier curves showed poorer outcome in MGFA III–V (log rank P < 0.05, Fig. 2a). Risk Factors and a Prediction Model for MSE Within 1 Year After Thymectomy

The clinical characteristics of the two groups (MSE vs. non-MSE) are displayed and compared in Table 1. No significant differences were observed regarding gender, age at MG onset, age at surgery, disease duration from MG onset to thymectomy, preoperative immunotherapy duration, comorbidities, WHO histologic subtypes, and TNM staging. Similarly, surgical approach and intraoperative blood loss exhibited no significant differences. However, 46.7% of patients in the non-MSE group, while 19.8% of patients in the MSE group were classified as preoperative MGFA class III or higher (P < 0.001). The non-MSE group exhibited a significantly higher proportion of reduced rate of R0 resection (21.9% vs. 10.7%, P < 0.05). Furthermore, the incidence of postoperative acute exacerbation within 1 year was markedly higher in the non-MSE group (52.4% vs. 16.9%, P < 0.001). The median daily corticosteroid dose at 1 year was 5 mg/day in the MSE group, which was significantly lower than the 20 mg/day observed in the non-MSE group (P < 0.001).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis (Table 3) identified MGFA III–V (OR 0.392, P < 0.05), reduced rate of R0 resection (OR 0.440, P < 0.05), and postoperative acute exacerbation (OR 0.245, P < 0.001) as risk factors for not achieving MSE at 1 year after surgery. The regression model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.735 (P < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 1). Patients with Preoperative MGFA III–V are Associated with Poor Prognosis

Preoperative MGFA III–V had a negative effect on the clinical outcome. Next, we compared the neurological outcome of patients with preoperative MGFA I–II (mild TAMG, n = 250) versus MGFA III–V (moderate-severe TAMG, n = 97). There was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of sex, age at onset, age at thymectomy, disease duration, tumor stage and histology, completeness of resection, or comorbidities.

One year after thymectomy, the proportion of patients with preoperative MGFA III–V who achieved MSE was significantly lower than that of patients with mild TAMG (48/97, 49.5% vs. 194/250, 77.6%, P < 0.001). Conversely, the proportions of patients receiving preoperative IVIG or PE (38.1% vs. 6.4%, P < 0.001), as well as those receiving corticosteroids or immunosuppressants (73.2% vs. 61.6%, P < 0.05), were higher in the moderate-to-severe TAMG group than in the mild TAMG group, with a correspondingly higher median daily dose of corticosteroids. The moderate-severe TAMG group exhibited a markedly higher incidence of acute exacerbation (50.5% vs. 18.8%, P < 0.001), IMC or MC (33% vs. 8%, P < 0.001), and mortality (6.2% vs. 0%, P < 0.001) within the first year after thymectomy (Fig. 3). Secondary Generalization in Ocular TAMG

The conversion from OMG to generalized myasthenia gravis (GMG) in patients with TAMG is of significant interest in clinical practice. In our cohort, there were 103 patients presenting with OMG (MGFA class I), comprising 47 (45.6%) men and 56 (54.4%) women, with a median age at thymectomy of 49 years. During the first year following thymectomy, the rate of secondary generalization was 25.2%, with 26 patients developing GMG at a median conversion time of 2 months. Overall, 77 (74.8%) of these initial patients with OMG achieved MSE at 1 year postoperatively. Notably, patients who converted to GMG showed a significantly lower proportion of achieving MSE compared to those who remained as pure OMG (50% vs. 83.1%, P < 0.05).

Discussion

To date, no consensus has been reached on the perioperative treatment of TAMG. By utilizing a multicenter retrospective cohort with thymoma, our study explored the risk factors associated with clinical outcome after thymectomy, highlighting the importance of preoperative MG control and the use of extended immunotherapies.

Previous studies have shown that steroid therapy is negatively correlated with prognosis after thymectomy for TAMG, primarily due to the inclusion of patients with severe preoperative MG and the intervention was carried out when their symptoms worsened [25, 26]. In contrast, patients in the more severe stage who received continuous high-dose prednisolone for 2 to 4 weeks preoperatively had a reduced incidence of POMC and a greater proportion of complete stable remission (CSR) and pharmacologic remission (PR) compared with those who did not receive prednisolone [18]. Several cohort studies reported the perioperative efficacy and safety of IVIG, PE, efgartigimod, or eculizumab for controlling symptoms in TAMG [7, 27–29]. However, most studies enrolled patients with moderate to severe symptoms, with a paucity of ocular-type or mild TAMG. These fail to truly reflect the scenario of patients with TAMG with stable preoperative MG. Gamez et al. proposed that IVIG may be unnecessary in patients with well-controlled disease, as the incidence of POMC was extremely low and comparable between the placebo and IVIG groups [28]. A meta-analysis also indicated PE may produce little or no difference in patients with mild clinical expression of the disease [29]. In our study cohort, with the exception of patients presenting with severe preoperative symptoms, the majority of patients had well-controlled preoperative symptoms, including those with OMG. Subgroup analyses consistently demonstrated that initiating immunotherapy 14 days before thymectomy was associated with a significantly decreased risk of postoperative acute exacerbation. Consequently, we propose that preoperative immunotherapy should be considered for all patients with TAMG, including those with mild symptoms or OMG. This therapy should be initiated more than 2 weeks prior to surgery and continued postoperatively with subsequent tapering dosages to clinical manifestations and oncological status. It is worth noting that corticosteroids prior to invasive surgery have historically been associated with an increased risk of postoperative infections and delayed wound healing [30, 31]. However, in our cohort, the majority of patients were managed with a low-to-moderate maintenance dose of oral corticosteroids (≤ 30 mg/day). At these optimized dosages, the preoperative regimen was generally well tolerated and we did not observe a clinically significant increase in the incidence of severe postoperative infections or wound healing complications. This finding suggests that achieving preoperative clinical stabilization using an optimized, lower-dose corticosteroid strategy can effectively balance the benefits of neuromuscular symptom control with the mitigation of surgical risks.

Preoperative MGFA III–V is another independent predictor for developing acute exacerbation. Consistent with previous findings, our multicenter study provides substantial validation indicating that preoperative MGFA class III–V correlate with poor prognoses in patients with TAMG after surgery [32–35]. Patients with preoperative MGFA class III–V exhibited more severe clinical phenotypes and inferior prognoses compared to those with class I–II, characterized by a lower proportion of achieving MSE and an elevated risk of MC, IMC, and mortality. These findings underscore the critical imperative for optimizing preoperative symptom control in TAMG [2, 36]. We recommend that patients with inadequate control of MG symptoms should undergo surgery after their symptoms have been stabilized through adequate medication treatment.

In our cohort of 103 patients with preoperative pure OMG, 26 (25.2%) of them developed GMG after surgery. Similarly, Zhang et al. reviewed 58 patients with OMG who underwent thymectomy, finding that 13 (22.4%) developed GMG [37]. The patients in their study not only had thymoma but also included those with thymic hyperplasia. Among the 44 patients with TAMG, 12 patients (27.2%) developed GMG, while only 1 out of 14 patients (7%) with thymic hyperplasia developed GMG. Another study reported consistent findings: 28.4% of patients with TAMG progressed to GMG postoperatively, whereas the proportion was only 11.1% among non-thymoma patients [38]. This suggests that the risk for generalization from OMG to GMG is higher in patients with thymoma than those without thymoma. Consequently, a critical clinical dilemma is whether adding or prolonging preoperative immunotherapy is justified for these mild cases. Our multivariate analysis directly addresses this query: even after adjusting for baseline MGFA status, an optimized duration of preoperative immunotherapy remained an independent protective factor against adverse outcomes. This strongly implies that proactive preoperative pharmacological stabilization is not redundant for patients with OMG. It acts as a crucial prophylactic shield that potentially disrupts the systemic autoimmune cascade, thereby reducing post-thymectomy instability and mitigating the high risk of secondary generalization.

A total of 242 patients (69.7%) achieved MSE at 1 year after thymectomy with a median time of 3 months after thymectomy (IQR 2–5). This highlights the importance of thymectomy in patients with TAMG. Subsequent analyses were performed to explore the predictive factors of achieving MSE. Preoperative MGFA III–V, reduced rate of R0 resection, and postoperative acute exacerbation were identified as risk factors for not achieving MSE. Previous studies have shown that the staging and classification of thymoma are closely associated with overall survival and recurrence-free survival (RFS) [34, 39], while the relationship with the prognostic outcomes of MG remains controversial. Some studies report no significant correlation [25, 40], whereas others implicate WHO type B histology and advanced Masaoka staging in unfavorable therapeutic outcome [32, 33, 37, 39]. In our cohort, WHO pathological classification and TNM staging have no association with the clinical prognosis of MG. Instead, reduced rate of R0 resection emerged as independent negative predictors of 1-year clinical outcomes. The median interval from the onset of MG to thymectomy was only 3 months, and most patients achieved R0 resection in the study. This suggests that even with variations in tumor histology and stages, a favorable prognosis may still be achieved if R0 resection was accomplished. Furthermore, for patients with TAMG, early surgical intervention is recommended once their clinical symptoms are well controlled.

This multicenter retrospective study represents the first comprehensive analysis of postoperative predictors in TAMG, demonstrating a beneficial effect of preoperative immunotherapy on clinical outcomes. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces inherent susceptibility to confounding factors and information bias. A head-to-head comparison among different specific modalities of immunotherapy was precluded by selection bias. In this real-world cohort, fast-acting advanced therapies such as IVIg and PE were strictly utilized as emergency rescue measures tailored for patients with critical baseline neurological weakness or impending crisis, creating a severe baseline imbalance where a direct retrospective correlation would unreliably reflect true pharmacological efficacy. Second, recruitment from tertiary referral centers may limit the generalizability of findings to the broader TAMG population due to selection bias. Third, the focus on 1-year outcomes may not fully capture long-term outcomes. Moreover, we did not differentiate the timing of postoperative acute exacerbations in the primary outcome assessment. Early exacerbations are mainly caused by surgical stress and acute inflammation, while late ones are closely linked to disease fluctuation and dose reduction of immunosuppressive treatments. Further prospective studies are required to classify exacerbations chronologically and facilitate stratified clinical intervention. Finally, while novel biological agents (such as FcRn inhibitors and anti-complement drugs) are reshaping the therapeutic landscape, their limited utilization in this cohort necessitates future investigation. Nevertheless, given the economic constraints associated with biologics, conventional immunotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment for most patients.

Despite of these limitations, our study underscores the critical prognostic relevance of preoperative symptom control in TAMG. Preoperative immunotherapy duration of more than 14 days was independently associated with a decreased risk of postoperative acute exacerbation and MGFA III–V was associated with a significantly increased risk of acute exacerbation. Preoperative MGFA III–V, incomplete R0 resection, and postoperative acute exacerbation are negative predictors for achieving MSE.

Conclusion

A preoperative immunotherapy duration exceeding 14 days is a promising strategy for patients with TAMG in terms of reducing the incidence of acute exacerbation and providing favorable outcomes.
