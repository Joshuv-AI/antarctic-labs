Eco‐Friendly Extraction and Valorization of Cucumis metuliferus E. Mey. ex Naud. Peel and Pulp: Optimization of Phenolic Recovery and Evaluation of Biological Activities
Authors: Wala Ben Amor, Hajer Riguene, Wissal Ayouni, Sinine Choura, Mohamed Chamkha, Chaima Mouffouk, Soumia Mouffouk, Hamada Haba, Ridha Ben Salem, Ghayth Rigane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study focused on optimizing the ultrasound‐assisted extraction process for both the peel and pulp of Cucumis metuliferus E. Mey. ex Naud. grown in Tunisia, using response surface methodology. The optimization targeted total phenolics content, total flavonoids content, as well as antioxidant activities evaluated by ferric reducing antioxidant power and 1, 1‐diphenyl‐2‐picrylhydrazyl assays. A central composite design was employed to investigate the influence of three key parameters: extraction time, extraction temperature, and liquid‐to‐solid ratio. The ultrasound‐assisted extraction (UAE) parameters were set as follows: extraction time ranging from 6.59 to 23 min, ultrasound temperature from 33.18°C to 66.81°C, and liquid‐to‐solid ratio from 13.18 to 46.81 mL/g. The optimal parameters for pulp extraction were determined to be a 15.76‐min extraction time, a temperature of 50.50°C, and a solvent‐to‐solid ratio of 30.54 mL/g. High‐performance liquid chromatography quantification identified gallic, chlorogenic, and caffeic acids as the main phenolic compounds, and the corresponding extracts exhibited potent, dose‐dependent anti‐inflammatory and antimicrobial effects. This study highlights a sustainable and eco‐friendly process that supports the circular economy and offers a promising approach for the industrial production of antioxidant‐rich Cucumis metuliferus extracts.Keywords: antimicrobial, antioxidant, caffeic acid, central composite design, chemistry, chlorogenic acid, industrial production, response surface methodology

Introduction

Cucumis metuliferus E. Mey. Ex Naudin, commonly known as kiwano or horned melon, belongs to the Cucurbitaceae family. The fruit is characterized by its ellipsoid shape, transitioning from green to an orange hue as it ripens. Native to sub‐Saharan tropical Africa, it has gained global recognition as a specialty horticultural product and is now cultivated in regions such as New Zealand, Australia, and the United States. Various parts of Cucumis metuliferus have been reported to exhibit diverse culinary and medicinal properties, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, antifungal, and antiviral activities [1]. In Tunisia, where climatic conditions vary from arid to semi‐arid, C. metuliferus thrives under specific agro‐climatic conditions. The fruit is widely consumed for its refreshing taste and its rich medicinal attributes, which are attributed to its phytochemical and nutritional composition [2]. These include high levels of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, vitamins, amino acids, and other bioactive compounds [3]. Furthermore, kiwano plays a critical role in addressing micronutrient deficiencies, often being consumed in significant quantities due to its nutritional richness. Recent advancements in extraction technologies have emphasized the importance of green and sustainable methods for isolating bioactive compounds. Among these, microwave‐assisted extraction (MAE) and ultrasound‐assisted extraction (UAE) have shown great potential. These methods are characterized by reduced extraction times, higher yields, improved selectivity, and better preservation of plant secondary metabolites compared to conventional techniques [4].

This study investigates the green extraction of phenolic compounds from Cucumis metuliferus cultivated in Tunisia using (UAE. The research focuses on process optimization and the evaluation of the antioxidant activity of the extracted compounds, highlighting their potential applications in health and food industries. To optimize the extraction parameters for polyphenolic compounds, response surface methodology (RSM) and central composite design (CCD) were applied in the present study. The extraction variables investigated included extraction time (6.59–23.40 min), extraction temperature (33.18°C–66.81°C), and liquid‐to‐material ratio (13.18–46.81 mL/g).

Results and Discussion

Experimental Data for Total Phenolic Content (TPC), Total Flavonoid Content (TFC), DPPH, and FRAP Assays From Cucumis metuliferus Peel and Pulp

Table 1 clearly shows significant variability in the bioactive compounds present in the peel extracts of C. metuliferus. Specifically, the total phenolic content varied from 67.06 to 197.40 mg GAE/g DW, and the total flavonoids content ranged between 38.59 and 115.40 mg QE/g DW. Additionally, the antioxidant activity evaluated via IC50 values spanned from 143.603 to 200.162 µg/mL, while the FRAP values fluctuated from 63.788 to 246.736 µM BHT/g DW. Optimal extraction of the peel was achieved at 50.50°C, using a 30.01 mL/g liquid‐to‐solid ratio for 15.93 min. For Cucumis metuliferus pulp, the total phenolic content was found to range between 71.50 and 235.27 mg GAE/g DW, while total flavonoids level varied from 39.140 to 160.190 mg QE/g DW. Antioxidant activity, as measured by the DPPH assay, showed values between 40.52 and 81.96 µg/mL, and FRAP values spanned from 152.644 to 233.206 µM BHT/g DW. Additionally, the optimal extraction conditions for the pulp were determined to be at 50.50°C with a liquid–solid ratio of 30.54 mL/g and an extraction time of 15.76 min. To validate the optimization model, the predicted values were compared with the experimental values obtained under the optimal extraction conditions. The experimental responses were in good agreement with the predicted values, confirming the adequacy and reliability of the developed quadratic models for predicting TPC, TFC, DPPH, and FRAP responses.

Optimization of the Extraction Process

A study focusing on the optimization of UAE parameters to enhance the yield of phenolic compounds from Sideritis raeseri. Using a CCD, the researchers evaluated the effects of key variables, including extraction time, temperature, and solvent‐to‐solid ratio, on extraction efficiency [5]. The statistical significance of the second‐degree polynomial model was assessed through analysis of variance (ANOVA), with p‐values below 0.05 indicating the significance of the model terms. The findings confirmed the model's validity, as evidenced by the low p‐values, highlighting the effectiveness of the selected parameters in optimizing the UAE process [6]. The predicted values for the bioactive components of Cucumis metuliferus peel, as shown in Table 1, ranged as follows: total phenolics (TPC) from 67.434 to 197.411 mg GAE/g DW, total flavonoids (TFC) from 38.799 to 115.032 mg QE/g DW, DPPH values from 42.252 to 89.023 µg/mL, and FRAP values from 144.982 to 200.347 µM BHT/g DW. Similarly, for the pulp extracts, the predicted ranges were 73.517 to 235.383 mg GAE/g DW for TP, 41.405 to 160.067 mg QE/g DW for TF, DPPH values between 40.522 and 81.957 µg/mL, and FRAP values ranging from 152.644 to 233.206 µM BHT/g DW.

Variables Effects on the Total Phenolic Content (TFC)

The mathematical model describing the extraction of total flavonoids (TFC) is expressed in the form of an equation that illustrates the relationship between the different parameters: extraction time (t), temperature (T), and liquid‐to‐solid ratio (R), and their influence on TF yield:

TFC (mg QE/g DW) = −788.3 + 15.11 t (min) + 22.09 T (°C) + 14.132 R (mL/g) −0.6546 t (min) × t (min) −0.2150 T (°C) × T (°C) −0.2347 R (mL/g) × R (mL/g) + 0.0533 t (min) × T (°C) + 0.1199 t (min) × R (mL/g) −0.03326 T (°C) × R (mL/g)

The ANOVA results of the regression coefficients reveal the influence of the extraction parameters extraction time (t), temperature (T), and liquid‐to‐solid ratio (R) on total flavonoid content (TFC). The linear effect of time was found to be highly significant (p < 0.001), while both temperature and the liquid‐to‐solid ratio also demonstrated statistically significant effects on TF levels. Furthermore, the quadratic effects of all three variables (t2, T2, and R2) were found to be highly significant, indicating that the response of TFC to these parameters is not purely linear but involves curvature in the response surface. This highlights the importance of optimizing the levels of each factor to achieve the maximum TFC yield. Significant interaction effects were also observed between the variables. The interactions between time and temperature (t × T), temperature and ratio (T × R), and time and ratio (t × R) all had meaningful impacts on the TFC, suggesting that the combined influence of these factors plays a critical role in the extraction process.

The response surface analysis, as illustrated in Figure 1, graphically demonstrates the significant interactions among the studied variables and their influence on TFC yield. These 3D plots help visualize how changes in two variables simultaneously can affect the extraction outcome, while the third variable is kept constant at its central value. Regarding the specific effect of extraction time, a positive linear influence and a negative quadratic effect were observed. As shown in Figure 1c, increasing the extraction time up to 22 min led to a noticeable improvement in TFC. However, beyond this point, TFC yield began to decline. This behavior is consistent with findings by Nurcholis et al. [10], who reported that prolonged extraction enhances flavonoids content up to certain duration, beyond which degradation or saturation effects may occur. However, temperature exhibited a similar trend. Its linear effect on TFC was positive, while the quadratic term had a negative impact. This can be explained by the fact that the solubility of flavonoid compounds increases with temperature, facilitating mass transfer between the solvent and plant material [11]. However, beyond an optimal temperature particularly near or above the boiling point of the solvent the solvent may begin to evaporate, reducing the effective solvent volume and negatively affecting the extraction efficiency. This thermal sensitivity of flavonoids underscores the importance of temperature control. Finally, the liquid‐to‐solid ratio demonstrated a clear positive linear effect on the extraction of flavonoid compounds from Cucumis metuliferus. As shown in Figure 1d,e, increasing the solvent volume relative to the plant material enhanced TFC yield, likely due to improved solvent penetration and mass transfer. This finding reinforces the role of the liquid‐to‐solid ratio as a key parameter in maximizing flavonoid extraction efficiency.

Variables Effects on DPPH

The mathematical model for DPPH is expressed as:

DPPH (µg/mL) = 478.0 −1.09 t (min) −11.527 T (°C) −7.872 R (mL/g) + 0.2365 t (min) × t (min) + 0.10903 T (°C) × T (°C) + 0.12366 R (mL/g) × R (mL/g) −0.0706 t (min) × T (°C) −0.0965 t (min) × R (mL/g) + 0.03496 T (°C) × R (mL/g)

The optimization of antioxidant activity, evaluated through the DPPH radical scavenging assay, was carried out using RSM under UAE conditions. The DPPH values (expressed as IC50) obtained from 30 experimental runs were used to build a second‐order polynomial model, allowing the study of the effects of three variables: extraction time (t), temperature (T), and solvent‐to‐solid ratio (R). The response values obtained experimentally were in good agreement with the predicted values, indicating the adequacy of the model.

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) confirmed that the model was statistically significant, with a high F‐value of 60.00 and a p‐value < 0.001. The coefficient of determination (R 2) was 99.08%, while the predicted R 2 reached 97.43%, demonstrating strong predictive accuracy and high model reliability. These results confirm that the proposed model can effectively describe the influence of extraction parameters on DPPH activity. Regarding individual effects, the linear effect of the solvent‐to‐solid ratio (R) was the most significant (p < 0.001), followed by extraction time and temperature, which were also statistically significant. All three variables exhibited strong quadratic effects (t2, T2, and R2), with p‐values < 0.001, suggesting a curvilinear relationship between each factor and DPPH activity. This highlights that both excessively low and high levels of each variable can negatively affect antioxidant performance. Moreover, interaction effects were notable. The interaction between time and ratio (t × R) had the strongest influence on DPPH values, while time–temperature (t × T) and temperature–ratio (T × R) interactions also showed significant contributions (p < 0.05). These results underline the necessity of simultaneous optimization of all three parameters to achieve maximal antioxidant activity. From the response surface analysis, the extraction time was found to have a negative linear and a positive quadratic effect on DPPH. This indicates that antioxidant activity decreases with prolonged extraction time up to a point, beyond which it may slightly recover. This behavior can be attributed to the potential degradation of phenolic compounds over long durations, which reduces radical scavenging capacity. Therefore, it is essential to maintain an optimal extraction period that is long enough to extract the maximum of bioactive molecules but not too long to make them the subject of degradation process [12]. Similarly, extraction temperature also displayed a negative linear and positive quadratic effect on DPPH values, as shown in Figure 1f. This suggests that moderate heating can enhance extraction efficiency, but high temperatures may lead to thermal degradation of active compounds. The balance between improved solubility and compounds stability is thus critical. Finally, the antioxidant activity of Cucumis metuliferus extracts, as measured by DPPH, is largely attributed to their phenolics content. These compounds can donate easily protons to neutralize free radicals, a mechanism well‐documented previously in the literature [13, 14].

Variables Effects on FRAP Activity

The analysis of antioxidant activity by the FRAP method is based on the electron transfer involved in the reduction of ferric ions (Fe3 +) to ferrous ions (Fe2 +). The polynomial equation representing this response is given as:

FRAP (µM BHT/g DW) = −409.3 + 8.89 × t + 14.136 × T + 11.420 × R −0.3648 × t2 −0.14066 × T2 −0.17799 × R2 + 0.0637 × t × T + 0.0220 × t × R −0.02456 × T x R where t is extraction time (min), T is extraction temperature (°C), and R is the solvent‐to‐solid ratio (mL/g). All three factors (t, T, and R) exhibited significant linear effects on FRAP values. However, the quadratic effects of these individual factors were not statistically significant. As for interaction effects, the interaction between time and temperature (t × T) and between temperature and ratio (T × R) exhibited significant influence on FRAP (p‐values = 0.003 and 0.010, respectively), whereas the interaction between time and ratio (t × R) was not significant (p‐value = 0.133). Moreover, the extraction time had a positive linear effect and a negative quadratic effect on FRAP values of Cucumis metuliferus. Similarly, extraction temperature demonstrated both a positive linear effect and a negative quadratic effect on the antioxidant activity (FRAP) of kiwano. As shown in Figure 1, the optimal conditions were reached at 16 min of extraction and a temperature of 53°C. This indicates that the combination of time and temperature enhanced the FRAP response. The highest FRAP values were recorded at moderate temperatures between 50°C and 53°C. These findings are consistent with those of Ahmed et al. [15], who reported that extraction temperature significantly affects the antioxidant activity (FRAP) of pomegranate extracts. An increase in temperature and sonication time, up to certain levels, improves the solubility and extractability of phenolic compounds into the solvent of extraction. However, excessively high sonication temperatures or prolonged times beyond the optimal range may lead to the degradation of heat‐sensitive phenolic compounds, thereby reducing the antioxidant capacity of the extract, as reported by Almusallam et al. [16]. Thus, the solvent‐to‐solid ratio (R) had both a positive linear and quadratic effect on the antioxidant activity (FRAP) of Cucumis metuliferus, as shown in Figure 1h.

The linear, quadratic, and interaction effects of the independent variables extraction time (t), temperature (T), and liquid‐to‐solid ratio (R) on the total phenolics content (TPC) were analyzed and summarized. According to Table 2, the linear effect of extraction time (t) is highly significant on total phenolics content (TPC), followed by temperature (T) and the solvent‐to‐solid ratio (R), with F‐values of 1064.82, 806.28, 184.91, and 49.48, respectively. These results indicate that all three factors contribute significantly to the extraction efficiency, with time having the strongest impact. In addition, Table 2 reveals that the quadratic effects of time (t2), temperature (T2), and ratio (R2) are also highly significant, with p‐values below 0.001. This indicates that a moderate combination of time and temperature promotes effective extraction. Similarly, the solvent‐to‐solid ratio also shows a positive linear and negative quadratic effect. Increasing the ratio up to 40 mL/g leads to a significant rise in TPC, which reaches a maximum of 235.27 mg GAE/g DW (Figure 2j). This behavior can be explained by improved solvent penetration and mass transfer, which enhance the diffusion of phenolic compounds. However, excessive plant material in low‐ratio conditions may increase viscosity of the medium, reducing then the extraction efficiency. These findings are consistent with results reported in previous studies using similar plant matrices.

Effects of Independent Parameters on Total Flavonoid Content (TFC)

This model was developed to assess the impact of each factor and their interactions on flavonoids content:

TFC (mg QE/g DW) = −1362.5 + 30.38 t (min) + 35.85 T(°C) + 24.31 R (mL/g) −1.1748 t (min) × t (min) −0.3447 T(°C) × T(°C) + 0.0447 t (min) × T(°C) + 0.1471 t (min) × R (mL/g) −0.0499 T(°C) × R (mL/g)

ANOVA was applied to the regression coefficients to evaluate the significance of each factor. The results showed that the linear effects of extraction time, temperature, and solvent‐to‐solid ratio were all statistically significant, with p‐values < 0.001 for time and temperature, and p = 0.005 for the ratio. In addition, the quadratic terms (t2, T2, and R2) were all highly significant, indicating a nonlinear influence of these variables on TFC yield. Regarding interaction effects, the interaction between time and temperature (t × T) was not statistically significant (p = 0.109), suggesting no meaningful combined influence. However, the other interactions, namely time with ratio (t × R) and temperature with ratio (T × R), were highly significant, highlighting a synergistic effect between these variables. To visualize these effects, 3D response surface plots were generated, as mentioned in Figure 2. These plots illustrate the interactions between the variables and their influence on flavonoids extraction. As shown in Figure 2k. TFC yield increased with extraction time up to approximately 22 min, followed by a decrease as temperature continued to rise. This indicates a favorable effect of moderate extraction durations, while excessive heating might lead to flavonoids degradation. Figure 2l illustrates the combined effect of temperature and solvent‐to‐solid ratio. TFC increased with both parameters initially, particularly with rising temperature, suggesting a strong influence of thermal energy on extraction. However, further increases in temperature beyond optimal levels led to a decline in flavonoids yield, while increasing the ratio no longer produced a significant effect. Regarding the solvent‐to‐solid ratio, both linear and quadratic effects were significant. The extraction process was carried out using ratios between 13.18 and 46.81 mL/g. According to Figure 2k,l, TFC yield increased significantly (p < 0.05) with the ratio, reaching a maximum value of 160.19 mg QE/g DW. This confirms that optimizing the ratio improves solvent penetration and facilitates the release of flavonoids from the fruit's matrix.

The p‐values for the various factors (time, temperature, and solvent‐to‐solid ratio) are very small, confirming that these factors have a highly significant effect on the DPPH assay. Furthermore, the quadratic effects of all the factors are highly significant, with p‐values less than 0.001. This indicates a non‐linear relationship between the variables and the DPPH radical scavenging activity. Additionally, all interactions time–temperature (t × T), time–ratio (t × R), and temperature–ratio (T × R) significantly influence the DPPH scavenging activity. These interactions highlight the complex relationship between the factors in determining antioxidant capacity. To better understand the effects of these factors, response surface plots were used. The extraction time has a negative linear effect and a positive quadratic effect on DPPH scavenging activity. As shown in Figure 2m, there is a significant interaction between time and temperature on DPPH activity, suggesting that the optimal conditions for maximum antioxidant activity are achieved when both time and temperature are carefully controlled. Similarly, temperature also displays a negative linear effect and a positive quadratic effect on DPPH scavenging activity. The results indicate that the IC50 value decreases to its minimum (IC50 = 40.65 µg/mL) as the temperature increases, reaching its optimum at approximately 66.81°C as given in Figure 2n,o. This suggests that higher temperatures enhance the extraction of antioxidants up to a certain point. In contrast, the solvent‐to‐solid ratio (R) exhibits a negative linear effect and a positive quadratic effect on DPPH scavenging activity. Increasing the ratio improves the extraction of antioxidants, but an excessive amount of extraction solvent could reduce efficiency after reaching an optimal point.

Effects of Independent Parameters on FRAP

The antioxidant activity assessed using the FRAP method is grounded in an electron transfer mechanism, specifically through the reduction of ferric ions (Fe3 +) to ferrous ions (Fe2 +). To optimize this response, a polynomial regression model was established as follows:

FRAP (µM BHT/g DW) = −722.7 + 15.55 t + 23.05 T + 15.171 R −0.6218 t2 −0.2179 T2 −0.2601 R2 + 0.0109 t × T + 0.1530 t × R −0.02884 T × R where t is extraction time (min), T is temperature (°C), and R is solvent‐to‐solid ratio (mL/g).

All three independent variables exerted a highly significant linear influence on the FRAP values. Moreover, their quadratic effects were also found to be statistically significant, confirming the existence of optimal conditions within the studied ranges. In terms of interaction effects, the combinations of time and ratio (t × R), as well as temperature and ratio (T × R), demonstrated significant impacts (p < 0.001 and p = 0.031, respectively). However, the interaction between time and temperature (t × T) was not statistically significant (p = 0.601), indicating a minimal synergistic effect between these two factors on antioxidant activity. Analyzing the response surface further reveals that extraction time has a positive linear and a negative quadratic effect, indicating that antioxidant activity increases with time up to a certain point before declining. Similarly, temperature follows the same trend initially enhancing FRAP activity before reaching a peak and then decreasing. Notably, the optimal condition was observed at approximately 22 min and 66.81°C, suggesting a synergistic effect when both parameters are appropriately balanced, Figure 2p,q. On the other hand, the liquid‐to‐solid ratio exhibited both positive linear and quadratic effects, implying a continuous increase in FRAP activity as the solvent volume increases, likely due to enhanced mass transfer and compounds solubilization. These findings align with previous studies, such as those by Yim et al. [14], who demonstrated that optimizing UAE parameters namely temperature, time, and solvent ratio significantly improved antioxidant activity in plant‐based systems through mechanisms involving both linear and nonlinear effects.

Identification and Quantification of Phenolic Compositions in Cucumis metuliferus Peel and Pulp Extract Using HPLC–DAD

HPLC–DAD analysis was applied to detect phenolic compounds in Cucumis metuliferus extracts. The results showed that several phenolic compounds could be identified and quantified in the peel extract (Table 3 and Figure 3), whereas no targeted phenolic compounds corresponding to the available reference standards were identified in the pulp extract under the HPLC–DAD conditions employed (Figure 4). First, the heterogeneous distribution of secondary metabolites in fruits is a key phenomenon. The peel, acting as a protective barrier, naturally accumulates higher concentrations of phenolic compounds. This trend is confirmed by a recent review on cucurbits, which consistently highlights that the peel is a richer reservoir of phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity than the pulp [17]. Conversely, the pulp, characterized by its high water and sugar content, has an inherently lower phenolics content. According to López et al. [18], the high amount of sugar in the fruits interferes with the extraction and analysis of other compounds, making them harder to detect.

Furthermore, a substantial fraction of polyphenols may exist in bound or non‐extractable forms (NEPs). Our standard extraction protocol, without a hydrolysis step, is not designed to release these compounds. As explained by, These NEPs, which are often the majority fraction, remain invisible to conventional analyses without prior hydrolysis [19]. Their absence in our results could therefore indicate an underestimation of the actual phenolics content, direct experimental support for this idea comes from a very recent study on a cucurbit: Panozzo et al. [20] demonstrated that applying acid or enzymatic hydrolysis to squash peel released a significantly richer and more diverse phenolic profile than solvent extraction alone. In addition, the HPLC–DAD method also has limitations. It only detects compounds that match its reference standards. If certain compounds have a different UV spectra or retention time, or are present in too small quantities, they will not be detected. Other types of polyphenols, such as phenol amides or lignans, often require specific standards or more sensitive techniques to be identified [12, 21]. Moreover, it is important to combine several analytical techniques. While HPLC–DAD mainly identifies non‐volatile polyphenols, other studies such as that performed by Vieira et al. [13] using GC–MS have found other bioactive compounds in the pulp, including volatile substances. To obtain a more complete picture, the use of LC–MS/MS would be very useful, as this method is more sensitive and can detect many compounds, even without reference standards [13, 18].

In summary, the small number of compounds found does not mean that the fruits contain few, but rather that the method used does not allow everything to be detected. These results show that it is essential to choose extraction and analysis methods that are adapted to the complexity of the fruits and the wide diversity of phenolic compounds.

Ovalbumin Denaturation Assay

The in vitro anti‐inflammatory effects of both peel and pulp extracts prepared from the fruits of Cucumis metuliferus using optimized UAE with RSM and the CCD were evaluated by protein denaturation inhibition assay, and the results are presented in Figure 5. Based on the obtained results, all the tested samples inhibit thermally induced protein denaturation in a concentration‐dependent manner. However, kiwano hydroalcoholic extracts showed excellent anti‐inflammatory potential, with IC50 values estimated at 24.03 ± 0.68 and 30.77 ± 0.34 µg/mL for the peel and pulp extracts, respectively. This activity was significantly better than that exerted by the non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drug at the majority of the tested concentrations (IC50 = 366.03 ± 0.56 µg/mL).

Anti‐inflammatory effects of crude extracts prepared from the fruits of C. metuliferus using optimized ultrasound‐assisted extraction methods.

The interesting ability of C. metuliferus crude extracts to prevent protein denaturation and thus possess possible anti‐inflammatory effects could be strongly associated with their high content of phenolic compounds. Especially to the richness of the extracts in chlorogenic, caffeic, and gallic acids, these molecules are well documented for their potential to inhibit protein denaturation and to interfere with the biosynthesis and the excretion of inflammatory mediators by modulating several cellular signaling pathways [14, 15, 16]. Many factors may explain the higher anti‐denaturation potential of the tested extracts compared to the reference non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drug. The strong anti‐denaturation potential of the extract may reflect synergistic interactions among diverse classes of phytochemicals that prevent protein denaturation, rather than the effects of individual constituents acting independently. Additionally, the reference drug used in this study was an injectable diclofenac formulation rather than analytical‐ grade diclofenac sodium, therefore, the potential influence of formulation excipients and solubility related factors on the assay outcome cannot be completely excluded. Under the experimental conditions, these formulation components may have altered the physicochemical environment of the reaction mixture, thereby affecting protein stability and consequently the apparent inhibitory activity of diclofenac. Nevertheless, the results of the present study are consistent with literature data, where it has been reported that different parts of Kiwano fruits exert significant in vitro immunomodulatory, antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties [17, 18, 19]. Furthermore, the protein denaturation assay evaluates only one aspect of the inflammatory process and does not necessarily predict therapeutic efficacy in vivo. Therefore, more detailed investigations using cell based and in vivo models are needed to confirm the anti‐inflammatory effects of C. metuliferus and to identify the bioactive compounds responsible for the observed effects.

Antimicrobial Activity

The antimicrobial activity of C. metuliferus peel extract was assessed in vitro against a variety of bacterial and fungal microorganisms and the results are shown in Table 4. According to the findings, the peel extract exerted a strong antimicrobial effect against all the tested microbial strains and significantly inhibited their growth in a dose‐dependent manner with inhibition zones ranging from 21.66 to 37.2 mm and minimum inhibitory concentrations between 62.5 and 500 µg/mL. which demonstrates the ability of the extract to possess strong antimicrobial potential even at low concentrations. This activity was relatively lower than that exerted by the tested aminoglycoside antibiotic gentamicin, except for the strains K. pneumoniae ATCC70063 and M. morganii ATCC25830, where the C. metuliferus peel extract had a more prominent inhibitory effect than the reference antibiotic. Indeed, the results of the present study concord with the findings of several previous reports that underscore the antimicrobial potential of this fruit against several clinical and phytopathogenic strains [20, 22, 23, 24].

The antimicrobial potential of Kiwano could be strongly associated with its chemical composition. This connection could be confirmed by the study of Zhang et al. [25], which reported that C. metuliferus biosynthesizes its biomolecules in response to various biotic stresses, such as infections and insect attacks. Recent reports confirmed the richness of this species in carotenoids and pigments [26, 27]. These molecules are documented to exert strong bacteriostatic and bactericidal effects in vivo and in vitro [28]. Furthermore, the novel fatty acids isolated and characterized from the fruits of this species could justify its potent antimicrobial properties [29]. This class of metabolites is considered the possible next‐generation of antibiotics with a broad spectrum of antimicrobial activities that may exert their effects by altering the genetic material replication, affecting the bacterial cell wall structure, inhibiting protein biosynthesis, disturbing the cell metabolism and membrane efflux [30].

On the other hand, a recent report documented the ability of many phenolic acids and hydroxybenzoic acid derivatives isolated from the peel of this plant to exert antibacterial properties against the multidrug‐resistant strain Pseudomonas aeruginosa in vitro and in silico [30]. Additionally, this strong activity could be related to the existence of other classes of primary and secondary metabolites such as steroids, flavonoids, tannins, glycosides, and terpenoids [2, 31]. Therefore, the fruits kiwano may serve as a valuable source of biomolecules that could constitute potential antibiotic drugs against this variety of pathogenic microorganisms considered as responsible of severe nosocomial and post‐operative infection.

In fact, recent reports conducted on this fruit confirm its safety [32, 33], which suggests that C. metuliferus may exert its therapeutic potential without causing possible toxicity or adverse side effects.

Conclusion

The UAE method developed in this study for extracting polyphenols from Cucumis metuliferus peel and pulp was successfully optimized using RSM combined with CCD. The optimal extraction conditions for polyphenols and their antioxidant activities from the peel were achieved at a liquid‐to‐solid ratio of 30.01 mL/g, under a temperature of 50.50°C for 15.93 min. Under these conditions, the maximum values for total phenolics content (TPC), total flavonoids content (TFC), DPPH radical scavenging activity, and FRAP were 198.52 mg GAE/g DW, 116.26 mg QE/g DW, 41.73 µg/mL, and 201.84 µM BHT/g DW, respectively. Furthermore, the optimal concentrations of polyphenols and their antioxidant activities in the pulp extract were as follows: TPC (236.72 mg GAE/g DW), TFC (161.12 mg QE/g DW), DPPH (39.86 µg/mL), and FRAP (234.83 µM BHT/g DW), obtained under conditions of 15.76 min extraction time, 50.50°C temperature, and a liquid‐to‐solid ratio of 30.54 mL/g. The phenolic composition and antioxidant activities were further examined using HPLC–DAD. The main phenolic compounds detected in the peel extracts of C. metuliferus were gallic acid, chlorogenic acid (the predominant compound), and caffeic acid. Biological evaluations revealed that the optimized extracts are not only highly enriched in antioxidants but also exhibit strong bioactive potential. They displayed remarkable anti‐inflammatory properties, with a significantly higher inhibition of protein denaturation compared to the reference drug diclofenac. Moreover, the extracts demonstrated broad‐spectrum antimicrobial activity against a range of pathogenic strains. This research successfully demonstrates that ultrasound‐assisted extraction is an effective method for obtaining potent and multifunctional extracts from kiwano, confirming its potential as a valuable source of natural compounds for the pharmaceutical and food industries.

Experimental Section

Sample Collection

The fruits of Cucumis metuliferus E. Mey. ex Naud. used in this study were harvested from Sousse, Eastern Tunisia, in March, 2023. The botanical material was formally identified and authenticated by Dr Maher Boukhris from the University of Sfax. A representative voucher specimen of Cucumis metuliferus E. Mey. ex Naud. was deposited at the Laboratory of Organic Chemistry LR17ES08, Faculty of Sciences of Sfax, University of Sfax, Tunisia, under the registration number CMMN032023. The harvested fruits were thoroughly washed with distilled water to remove surface contaminants before the peel and pulp were separated. The isolated peel was subsequently dried in a laboratory oven at a constant temperature of 50°C until reaching a constant weight. Finally, the dried peel was finely ground using an electric grinder to obtain a homogenous powder for further extraction.

Ultrasound‐Assisted Extraction

The UAE method was performed following a previous study with some modifications. The sample was then extracted using an ultrasonic bath, with extraction time, temperature, and ultrasound power adjusted according to the experimental design. To optimize the extraction process and ensure a successful outcome, it is crucial to consider several key variables, the main ones being the applied ultrasonic power, the frequency (20 KHz), the extraction temperature [0°C–120°C], the reactor characteristics, Pulsation: ON cycles 0.2 –600 s, OFF cycles 0.3–600 s; Amplitude control:10%–100 %. A preliminary study was carried out to identify the parameters influencing the response of interest and to define their minimum and maximum levels. For this, the extraction method was applied by varying only one parameter at a time, while the others remained constant. The peel and pulp were placed in a beaker, then mixed with a specified amount of extraction solution to obtain a liquid–solid ratio between 10 and 50 mL/g. The extraction time was adjusted between 5 and 25 min, while the ultrasonic temperature has been changed between 30°C and 65°C. This simple method can be applied with any type of solvent. The setup uses an Elma Sonic ultrasonic bath that operates with high‐frequency mechanical waves (in KHz). The system includes a helical blade rotating at 24 rpm, immersed in a bath containing plant material and a suitable solvent. The plant biomass is mixed with a hydroalcoholic mixture (water + ethanol) in a heat‐resistant container, under specific conditions. A total of 30 extracts were prepared 15 as powders and 15 as pulps each with defined mass, extraction time, temperature, and solvent‐to‐solid ratio. After extraction, the mixture is filtered and subjected to liquid–liquid extraction using ethyl acetate in a separating funnel. The aqueous phase is discarded, and the organic phase is concentrated using a rotary evaporator. Ethanol is then added to the dried extract.

Experimental Design

RSM utilizing CCD was employed to optimize the extraction conditions and evaluate the relationship between independent variables and the resulting outcomes. Four independent variables were studied: sample‐to‐solvent ratio (13.18–46.81 mL/g), temperature (33.18°C–66.81°C) and extraction time (6.59 – 23.40 min). A total of 30 experimental runs were performed to optimize the total phenolics content (TPC), total flavonoids content (TFC), DPPH scavenging activity, and FRAP activity. ANOVA was applied to determine the individual linear, quadratic, and interaction regression coefficients (β) using Minitab 16.0 statistical software. The coefficient of determination (R 2) was used to assess the fit of the polynomial equations to the observed data. A low p‐value and high F‐value, with a 95% confidence level, were considered to evaluate the significance of the independent variables in the model [2]. A second‐order polynomial equation was developed to predict the optimal extraction conditions and to construct the response surfaces (RSM).

Determination of the Total Phenolics Content

The total phenolics content (TPC) in the extracts was measured using a modified version of the Folin–Ciocalteu method, as adapted by Aroua et al. [34]. The results are reported as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents (GA) per gram of dry weight (mg GA/g DW). All analyses were conducted in triplicate.

Determination of Total Flavonoids Content

The total flavonoids content in the extracts was determined using spectrophotometry, following the method outlined by Aroua et al. [34]. This method relies on the formation of an aluminum–flavonoid complex, which has a maximum absorption at 510 nm. The flavonoid concentration was expressed in terms of quercetin equivalent per gram of extract (mg QE/g dry weight). The experimental procedure was as follows: 1 mL of extract was mixed with 300 µL of 5% sodium nitrite (NaNO2) at time zero. After 5 min, 300 µL of 10% aluminum chloride (AlCl3) was added, followed by 2 mL of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) one minute later. The solution was then diluted with distilled water to a final volume of 10 mL, and the optical density of each sample was measured at 510 nm. For quercetin, the absorbance–concentration relationship was described by the equation: y = 1.0909 x + 0.0059, with a correlation coefficient (R 2) of 0.9879.

Antioxidant Activity

The antioxidant activity of the extracts was measured using the DPPH (2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl) method, as described by Aroua et al. [34]. This assay is based on the ability of antioxidant compounds to neutralize the DPPH free radical, resulting in a decrease in absorbance measured at 517 nm. The extracts were tested at various concentrations, and their radical‐scavenging effectiveness was determined based on the reduction in the optical signal of DPPH. The results revealed a significant antioxidant activity, which increased with the concentration of the extracts, showing an activity comparable to that of sodium ascorbate, used as a positive control.

Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power Assay (FRAP)

The FRAP test was conducted based on the method outlined by Aroua et al. [34], with several modifications. The preparation of the solution for measuring antioxidant activity using the FRAP method begins with dissolving a precise amount of FeCl3·6H2O to obtain a Fe3 + concentration of 2 × 10−2 M. Then, 1 mL of HCl (1 M) is added, and the solution is diluted to a final volume of 50 mL with distilled water. Meanwhile, a TPTZ (2,4,6‐tri(2‐pyridyl)‐s‐triazine) solution is prepared by dissolving an appropriate amount of TPTZ in 96% ethanol to achieve a concentration of 10− 2 M, and the solution is adjusted to 50 mL. A sodium acetate solution is prepared by dissolving 3.1 g of sodium acetate in 16 mL of acetic acid, then diluting the solution to 1 L with distilled water. The FRAP solution is then prepared by mixing 10 mL of the sodium acetate/acetic acid solution, 1 mL of the TPTZ solution, and 1 mL of the FeCl3·6H2O solution. For testing the antioxidant activity of the extracts, the concentration of the solution to be analyzed should range between 0.3 and 1 mM. The reaction mixture consists of 3 mL of the FRAP solution, 100 µL of the extract to be tested, and 300 µL of distilled water. After a six‐minute incubation, the absorbance is measured at λ = 595 nm, with 96% ethanol used as the blank. To determine the antioxidant activity, a calibration curve is constructed using BHT.

HPLC–DAD Analysis

Chromatographic analysis was performed according to the procedure outlined by Jebli et al. [35], using an Agilent 1260 series HPLC system equipped with a diode array detector (DAD) (Agilent, Waldbronn, Germany). Compound separation was carried out on a ZORBAX Eclipse XDB‐C18 column (4.6 mm internal diameter, 250 mm length, 3.5 µm particle size). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% acetic acid in water) and solvent B (pure acetonitrile). A gradient elution was applied at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, with an injection volume of 10 µL, and the column temperature maintained at 40°C. The gradient program was as follows: 10%–50% B from 0 to 22 min; 50%–100% B from 22 to 32 min; held at 100% B from 32 to 40 min; and decreased back to 10% B from 40 to 44 min, followed by a 6‐minute re‐equilibration period. The DAD scanned wavelengths ranging from 190 to 400 nm, with compound detection monitored at 254, 280, and 330 nm. Peaks were identified by congruent retention times and UV spectra, and compared with those of the standards. Quantification was carried out using a four‐point calibration curve (r 2 = 0.989), employing authentic external standards when available.

The ability of the ultrasound‐assisted extracts prepared from C. metuliferus to prevent protein denaturation was assessed using the egg albumin denaturation test [35]. Antimicrobial Activity

The antimicrobial effect of the peel extract obtained from C. metuliferus was evaluated using the agar disk diffusion test against eight microbial strains: one yeast (Candida albicans ATCC 90029), five reference strains (Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC70063, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, and Morganella morganii ATCC25830), and two clinical bacteria (Streptococcus D, and Enterococcus sp.) [36, 37]. All the microorganisms were graciously provided by the Institute of Pasteur (Algeria) and the Laboratory of Bacteriology, University Hospital Center‐Batna (UHC). While the clinical bacterial strains were initially isolated from patients and identified in the Laboratory of Microbiology at the University Hospital Center‐Batna through microscopic examination based on staining reaction, Gram character, morphology, colony appearance, oxygen requirements, physiological and biochemical characteristics, and serological methods. The strains were purified by streaking in four quadrants under sterile conditions at optimal temperatures for 24 h, according to the specific strains. The microbial suspension was prepared by transferring one or more colonies from each pure culture into 5 mL of nutrient broth. The mixture was homogenized and incubated at 37°C for 10–24 h. The optical density of each 1 mL inoculum was measured at 625 nm and adjusted to match a 0.5 McFarland standard. Petri dishes containing Mueller–Hinton agar were inoculated with the standardized microbial suspensions using the swabbing technique. Whatman paper discs were carefully placed on the inoculated agar surface with sterile forceps after being impregnated with 10 µL of the tested extract at various concentrations (1000, 500, 250, 125, 62.5, and 31.25 µg/mL) or reference antibiotic (10 µg of gentamicin/disc). The dishes were incubated at 37°C for 24 h. All assays were carried out in triplicate, and the antimicrobial effect was expressed as the diameter in mm of the inhibition zones formed around the discs. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC values) were determined by identifying the lowest concentrations of the tested extracts that still exerted observable antimicrobial effect.

Statistical Analysis

The results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on three replicates conducted during the analytical procedure. Statistical differences were evaluated using one‐way ANOVA, followed by Student's t‐test for pairwise comparisons. A p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
