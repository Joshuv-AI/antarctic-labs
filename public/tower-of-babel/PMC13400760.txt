Indian Nephrologists’ Approaches to Shared Decision-Making, Advance Care Planning, and Palliative Care
Authors: Komal Kashyap, Raj Kanwar Yadav, Vishwajeet Singh, Shyam Bihari Bansal, Anand Chellappan, Anitha Swamy, Asheesh Kumar, Mythri Shankar, Narayan Prasad, Priya Alexander, Raja Ramachandran, Suceena Alexander, Swaminathan Sundaraman, Vijay Kher, Vinay Rathore, Brajesh Kumar Ratre, Joris Gielen, Sushma Bhatnagar
Published: 2026

Introduction

As patients undergoing kidney care approach kidney failure, they face complex decisions regarding dialysis initiation, transplantation, or foregoing disease-directed treatment in favor of palliative and supportive care. In this context, shared decision-making (SDM), advance care planning (ACP),1 and palliative care (PC)2 have emerged as essential components of high-quality, patient-centered kidney care.

The benefits of SDM, ACP, and PC are generally recognized in the literature and existing guidelines from organizations such as Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes and the Renal Physicians Association favor their integration into nephrology care.3 However, the integration of SDM, ACP, and PC into routine nephrology practice remains a significant challenge globally.4, 5, 6 Understanding how nephrologists perceive and practice ACP, SDM, and PC is therefore critical, as it will help understand how their integration can be enhanced. This study examines the attitudes and practices of the members of the Indian Society of Nephrology toward these 3 essential components of patient-centered care and analyzes potential conflicts between attitudes and practices.

The Methods have been described in the Supplementary Methods section.

Results

A total of 257 nephrologists submitted responses to the survey. Their demographic data are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. The margins of error of the survey items assessing attitudes and practices related to SDM, ACP, and PC ranged from 2.24% to 6.88% at the 95% confidence level. As shown in Table 1, a large majority of nephrologists endorsed SDM. For instance, more than 9 in 10 (90.5%) supported a physician-patient-caregiver partnership, and a similar proportion (92.4%) emphasized providing adequate information at all stages of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Nephrologists also recognized the importance of ACP, as illustrated by the observation that 96.7% agreed that asking about patients’ values and preferences is essential. Support for integrating PC was high, too, as almost all favored collaboration between nephrology and PC (97.6%) and supported including PC training in postgraduate programs (96.2%).

Attitudes and practices of nephrologists

ACP, advance-care planning; CKD, chronic kidney disease; PC, palliative care; SDM, shared decision-making.

Although there was large support for SDM, ACP, and PC, their use in practice was uneven. More than 91.6% of nephrologists reported that they often or always ensured patients had adequate information about treatment choices; however, only 71.9% consistently explored broader aspects of quality of life, including psychological and social domains, which are essential components of SDM. ACP practices were inconsistent as well; for example, only approximately half (49.7%) routinely discussed ACP when prognosis was poor. Referrals to PC were infrequent. More than half (51.4%) even stated that they never or rarely referred patients to PC.

For each of the 3 domains, a latent class analysis was fitted, and the 2-class model was identified as the superior solution for each domain. Supplementary Figure S1 presents the conditional item response probabilities of all classes. An extensive description of all classes is presented in the Supplementary Description of Latent Classes in the Supplementary Materials. In the SDM domain, 92.4% belonged to the class of “shared-decision-makers” and 7.6% were “paternalists.” In the ACP domain, 39% were “ambivalent to ACP,” whereas 61% were “supporters.” In the PC domain, 50.8% were “active PC advocates” and 49.2% were “passive PC advocates.”

Class membership within each of 3 domains was compared with demographic variables and class membership in the other domains. The statistically relevant associations have been reported in Table 2. These results show that in comparison to nephrologists who are ambivalent toward ACP, ACP supporters are more likely to practice in a health care setting where palliative and supportive care services are available. When compared with passive PC advocates, active PC advocates are more likely to have had PC training, practice in a health care setting where palliative and supportive care services are available, and be ACP supporters.

Discussion

The incorporation of SDM, ACP, and PC into routine nephrology remains a critical, albeit often incomplete, standard of care globally.4, 5, 6 The findings of this study reflect this persistent challenge. Although nephrologists overwhelmingly endorsed SDM, ACP, and PC in principle, their reported practices revealed substantial inconsistencies. The latent class analyses further highlighted this gap between attitudes and clinical behavior.

Most respondents belonged to SDM Class 1, the “shared decision-makers” (92.4%), who strongly supported a collaborative model in which physicians, patients, and caregivers jointly decide on treatment options. A smaller but notable minority (7.6%) formed the “paternalists,” who claimed to provide adequate, understandable information but believed that patients did not require comprehensive information. Earlier studies on much smaller samples of nephrologists have documented similar discrepancies between nephrologists’ stated beliefs and their actual SDM practices.7, 8, 9,S1,S2 This discrepancy may partly result from social desirability bias. Nephrologists may report stronger support for SDM than they apply in routine practice. However, the respondents did not know the latent class categories, such as shared decision-makers and paternalists during survey completion, because the study team created these categories afterward through latent class analysis.

A comparable gap emerged in the ACP domain. The majority class, the “ACP supporters” (61%), represents the cohort most likely to translate SDM ideals into practice. In contrast, the “Nephrologists Ambivalent to ACP” (39%) supported ACP conceptually but were hesitant to practice it, particularly in early CKD stages. This ambivalence mirrors findings from the Danish survey by Frandsen et al.S3 and may reflect limited training in PC in which clinicians develop skills in ACP discussion. In this study, PC training was associated with more support for ACP.

The PC classes further illustrated the attitude-practice divide. Both “active PC advocates” (50.8%) and “passive PC advocates” (49.2%) agreed that PC should be initiated early and integrated into postgraduate training. However, passive PC advocates rarely referred patients to PC services, despite endorsing its value. This disconnect has been noted in previous studies,S4,S5 and underscores the challenge of operationalizing PC principles in routine care. This lack of clinical action is not necessarily because of limited commitment to PC. For instance, active PC advocates were more likely to have had PC training. Yet, limited training may not fully explain the low referral rate. Limited availability of palliative and supportive care services may also contribute to this gap. Many nephrologists practiced in settings without PC services, and the availability of such services was strongly associated with being an active PC advocate and an ACP supporter. Previous research similarly suggests that physical distance between nephrology and PC may be a major barrier to PC access.S5–S7

The observed gaps between nephrologists’ attitudes and practices suggest that many patients with advanced CKD may not be receiving timely SDM, ACP, or PC. Although nephrologists overwhelmingly endorse these approaches, inconsistent exploration of quality-of-life domains, delayed initiation of ACP, and infrequent PC referrals indicate that patients may be making treatment decisions without adequate values-based guidance or symptom support. The strong associations between ACP support, PC training, and the availability of PC services highlight that institutional capacity and clinician preparedness are central determinants of whether patient-centered care is delivered. These findings underscore the need for health systems and training programs to ensure that nephrologists are equipped and supported to translate their stated commitments into routine clinical practice.

Overall, the attitude-practice gap identified in this study is consistent with findings from smaller international samples, indicating that this is a global phenomenon rather than one unique to India. With data from 257 nephrologists, this study provides more robust evidence that bridging this gap requires more than attitudinal change. Institutional capacity building, practical training in ACP and PC, and improved access to PC services are essential to support nephrologists in delivering the patient-centered care they overwhelmingly endorse.

This study has some limitations, which have been described in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Description of Study Limitations).
