Spatial interrelationships between flood risk and ecosystem services in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region at multiple scales
Authors: Meng-hao Xi, Hao-xuan Zhang, Peng Yao, Ui-ming Li, Yi Tang, Long-sheng Huang, Luo-lan Yao, Hong-yu Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Against the backdrop of global climate change and rapid urbanization, the coexistence of intensifying flood risk and ecosystem service degradation in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) region has emerged as a major constraint on regional sustainable development. This study develops an integrated framework of “multi-scale assessment–association analysis–mechanism exploration,” and comprehensively applies the InVEST model, the RUSLE model, random forest algorithms, spatial autocorrelation analysis, and coupling coordination degree models to systematically investigate the complex relationships and scale effects between flood risk and key ecosystem services—namely water yield and soil conservation—in the BTH region. The results indicate that: (1) the spatial patterns of both flood risk and ecosystem services exhibit pronounced scale dependence, with high-risk areas concentrated in piedmont plains and urban built-up zones, while high ecosystem service areas are stably distributed within the Yanshan and Taihang ecological barrier regions, resulting in a clear spatial mismatch characterized by “high risk–low service”; (2) the relationship between water yield service and flood risk demonstrates a scale reversal, shifting from a weak negative correlation at the micro-scale to a significant positive correlation at meso- and macro-scales, whereas soil conservation service consistently exhibits a stable negative correlation with flood risk at meso- and macro-scales, with the strength of this relationship increasing with scale; and (3) bivariate spatial analysis identifies spatial synergy zones of “high water yield–high flood risk” and spatial trade-off zones of “high soil conservation–low flood risk,” while coupling coordination degree assessment reveals the coexistence of coordinated and maladjusted states across the region, with relatively poorer coordination between soil conservation and flood risk. From a multi-scale perspective, this study deepens the understanding of the coupling mechanisms between flood risk and ecosystem services, and provides a scientific basis for ecological restoration, resilient urban planning, and coordinated watershed governance in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region.

Introduction

The interplay between global climate change and rapid urbanization is profoundly reshaping regional hydrological cycles and land-use patterns1,2. Under this context, the increasing frequency and intensity of extreme precipitation events have made urban flooding a severe challenge threatening urban safety and sustainable development worldwide3–5. As the core area of China’s Capital Economic Circle, the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) region is characterized by a high concentration of population and economic activities, and its urban agglomeration is facing growing pressure from flood risks6,7. However, the escalation of flood risk is not solely driven by climatic factors,it is also closely linked to profound human-induced modifications of natural systems. Large-scale urban expansion and the rapid increase in impervious surfaces have directly encroached upon key natural ecosystems such as forests, wetlands, and rivers and lakes8, leading to significant degradation of ecosystem regulating services—particularly those related to runoff regulation, rainfall retention, and flood mitigation9. This degradation weakens the inherent ecological capacity for disaster prevention and mitigation, often forming a vicious cycle of increased risk exposure, declining ecological regulation capacity, and rising system vulnerability10, thereby severely constraining the achievement of regional ecological security and climate resilience goals11.

In recent years, substantial progress has been made in both flood risk modeling and ecosystem service assessment. In the field of flood risk research, approaches have evolved from traditional statistical analyses to high-resolution simulations based on hydrodynamic models, such as SWMM and MIKE12, as well as comprehensive risk assessments integrating multi-source data13–15. Meanwhile, in ecosystem service studies, tools such as the InVEST and SWAT models have been widely applied to quantify regulating services including water yield, flood regulation, and water conservation16. Despite these advances, several critical limitations remain. First, existing studies often treat flood risk and ecosystem services as relatively independent research domains, resulting in a clear conceptual and methodological separation17. Although some studies have highlighted the disaster mitigation benefits of green infrastructure18, systematic and quantitative investigations into the intrinsic and dynamic interactions between ecosystem services and flood risk—particularly from the perspective of ecosystem service supply–demand matching—are still insufficient19. Second, the lack of an explicit scale perspective remains a prominent gap. Most studies focusing on the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region are confined to single administrative units (e.g., individual cities) or homogeneous spatial scales6, and thus fail to systematically reveal how the relationships between flood risk and ecosystem services vary across spatial scales, from local parcels or communities to watersheds and the entire urban agglomeration20. This lack of a multi‑scale integrated perspective may lead to the following problems. Management strategies derived from local scales, when applied to promote regional comprehensive planning, often become inefficient. Even worse, these strategies may conflict with each other21. It should be noted that the definition of “flood risk” in existing studies is often ambiguous. From a hydrological perspective, urban pluvial flooding is primarily associated with short-duration intense rainfall, the proportion of impervious surfaces, and urban drainage capacity, whereas fluvial flooding is more governed by upstream runoff generation and concentration processes, river channel conveyance capacity, and topographic conditions. In the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region, these two types of flood processes frequently interact: cities located in the piedmont plain face not only the threat of flash floods induced by rapid runoff concentration from upstream mountainous areas but also an increased risk of waterlogging due to the expansion of impervious surfaces in high-density built-up areas. Therefore, this study adopts a “compound flood risk” assessment framework that incorporates both of the above flood processes into a unified risk measurement system, aiming to more comprehensively reflect the characteristics of regional compound flood hazards and to lay the groundwork for subsequent investigations into the regulatory mechanisms of ecosystem services (water yield and soil retention) on flood risk.

In light of these limitations, this study takes the highly coupled human–environment system of the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei urban agglomeration as a representative case and aims to construct an integrated research framework of “multi-scale assessment–association analysis–mechanism exploration” to comprehensively investigate the interactions between flood risk and key ecosystem services, namely water yield and soil conservation. The core scientific questions addressed in this study include: (1) At multiple scales, what are the characteristics of the spatial differentiation patterns of flood risk and key ecosystem services in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region? (2) At different scales, what statistical associations (e.g., correlation analysis, spatial bivariate clustering, and coupling coordination relationships) exist between the supply capacity and spatial patterns of ecosystem services and flood risk? (3) Under the combined pressures of rapid urbanization and climate change, what scale dependence characterizes the interactive relationship between flood risk and ecosystem services? By addressing these questions, this study seeks to deepen the theoretical understanding of the ecological mechanisms underlying flood formation within coupled human–environment systems, and to provide robust scientific support for cross-scale ecological restoration, resilient urban planning, and coordinated watershed governance in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region, thereby contributing to high-quality and secure regional development.

Regional overview and data sources

Study area

The Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) region (115°42′E–119°51′E, 36°05′N–42°40′N) is located in the northern part of the North China Plain and spans the middle and lower reaches of the Haihe River Basin, covering a total area of approximately 218,000 km2. The region is characterized by a temperate semi-humid continental monsoon climate, with a mean annual precipitation of 400–600 mm, of which about 60–75% occurs during the summer months (June–August), predominantly in the form of short-duration, high-intensity rainfall events. Such precipitation patterns make the region particularly susceptible to mountain torrents and urban flooding22. In terms of topography, the northwestern part of the region is dominated by the Taihang and Yanshan Mountains and associated hilly terrain, which constitute critical areas for water conservation and ecological security. In contrast, the southeastern part consists mainly of extensive alluvial plains characterized by low elevation and poor drainage conditions, where flood disasters have historically occurred frequently6.

As a key national strategic region, the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei urban agglomeration accommodates more than 110 million inhabitants and hosts a highly concentrated distribution of cities, industries, and infrastructure. Over recent decades, rapid urbanization and land-use change have led to a dramatic expansion of impervious surfaces and a continuous shrinkage of natural wetlands and river–lake systems, substantially altering regional runoff generation and flow-concentration processes and weakening the natural regulating and buffering capacity of ecosystems (Li et al., 2020). Methods

Flood risk assessment

In this study, “flood risk” is defined as the potential adverse impacts on population, economy, and infrastructure, along with their probability of occurrence, caused by surface water accumulation (urban waterlogging) or river water level rise (fluvial flooding) under specific spatiotemporal scales. This definition integrates two types of flood processes—urban pluvial flooding and watershed fluvial flooding—considering both the risk of urban drainage system overloading driven by short-duration heavy rainfall and the regional flood risk controlled by upstream runoff generation and concentration as well as river channel conveyance capacity. Based on this definition, this study develops a comprehensive risk assessment framework that incorporates hazard, exposure, and vulnerability.

Identification of flood-inundated areas (training sample generation)

To identify flood‑inundated areas, this study utilized Sentinel‑1 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) data from the COPERNICUS/S1_GRD dataset on the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform (Tang & Li, 2020). VV polarization was selected for its sensitivity to surface water changes. Images were acquired for two periods: the pre‑flood phase (June 29—July 29, 2023) and the flood‑event phase (July 29—August 29, 2023), corresponding to the extreme rainfall event that severely affected the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region in late July and early August 2023.

The Otsu thresholding method was applied to each image to generate binary water/non‑water maps. By comparing the two classified images, pixels that changed from non‑water to water were labeled as flooded (positive) samples, while pixels remaining non‑water throughout were labeled as non‑flooded (negative) samples. To ensure spatial representativeness, we randomly selected 50,000 positive and 50,000 negative samples using a stratified strategy based on elevation, land‑use type, and distance to river networks, covering all major geomorphic units of the study area. These samples were used for training and validation of the random forest model. All other input features for flood risk assessment (see Table 1) were obtained and processed as described in "Data Sources" section.

Hazard index (HI) modeling: random forest classifier

Input features: Only indicators directly related to natural hazard susceptibility were selected, including elevation, precipitation, slope, aspect, and soil moisture (see Table 1, “Hazard” group). These features reflect the controlling effects of topography, climate, and underlying surface conditions on runoff generation and water accumulation.

Model training: A total of 50,000 sample points each from inundated and non-inundated areas were extracted (70% for training, 30% for testing) to construct a random forest classifier23. Ten-fold cross-validation was applied to optimize hyperparameters (number of trees = 500, maximum depth = 10, minimum leaf node samples = 5). The model output for each grid cell was the flood inundation probability (range 0–1), which was defined as the flood Hazard Index (HI). Model evaluation metrics: accuracy = 0.92, precision = 0.89, recall = 0.91, F1 score = 0.90, AUC = 0.96 (based on the test set)24.

Feature importance interpretation: SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were used to calculate the mean absolute contribution of each input feature to the model’s predictions. SHAP values were used only to understand the relative importance of each factor for hazard, not as direct weights for subsequent risk index synthesis.

Exposure index (EI) and vulnerability index (VI) construction

Exposure and vulnerability involve socio-economic attributes and were computed independently of the hazard model.

Exposure index (EI): Three indicators were included: land use type, GDP density, and population density (see Table 1, “Exposure” group). After range normalization (positive transformation), a weighted sum with equal weights was applied:

EI = 1/3 × GDP + 1/3 × Pop + 1/3 × LUCC.

Vulnerability index (VI): Three indicators were included: distance to the nearest hospital (inverted to represent “medical service accessibility”), dependency ratio, and proportion of compulsory-education population (see Table 1, “Vulnerability” group). After positive transformation and range normalization of all indicators, an equal-weighted sum was applied:

VI = 1/3 × Hosp + 1/3 × Dep + 1/3 × Edu.

The use of equal weights follows common practice in multi-indicator comprehensive risk assessment25,26. Weight sensitivity is further analyzed in the discussion section (Sects. Results,Discussion).

Synthesis of the comprehensive flood risk index (CFRI)

The three components were multiplied to obtain the comprehensive flood risk index for each grid cell (30 m resolution):

CFRI = HI × EI × VI.

The CFRI is a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater flood risk. All subsequent multi-scale aggregations (30 m grid → 10 km grid → county level) and correlation analyses with ecosystem services (water yield and soil retention) are based on the CFRI.

Ecosystem service assessment

An increasing body of literature has explored the classification of ecosystem services27. In previous studies, ecosystem services are commonly categorized into four fundamental types: provisioning services (e.g., food production, raw materials, and water supply), regulating services (e.g., carbon storage, water purification, waste treatment, and soil conservation), supporting services (e.g., biomass production and biodiversity maintenance), and cultural services (e.g., education, recreation, and non-material benefits). Compared with other ecosystem services, reductions in soil conservation and water conservation (water yield) services can substantially weaken the water-holding capacity of surface soils, causing rainfall intensity during extreme precipitation events to far exceed soil infiltration capacity and thereby facilitating the rapid formation of flood disasters. Accordingly, this study focuses on the interactions between soil conservation, water yield services, and urban flood risk.

Scale selection

Due to the inherent non-uniformity in the spatial distribution of geographical elements, spatial heterogeneity is ubiquitous, and the use of spatial units with different sizes and shapes often leads to distinct statistical relationships and spatial distribution patterns. Therefore, it is essential to explicitly consider scale effects and to conduct statistical analyses and spatial relationship investigations across multiple spatial scales. Given the practical characteristics of the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) region, the administrative scale was defined based on county-level divisions. For the grid scale, a fishnet approach was employed to construct regular spatial units, and the choice of grid size directly influences the accuracy of flood risk assessment. A grid resolution of 10 km × 10 km was selected based on the following considerations: (1) this resolution represents a meso-scale that effectively balances the need for spatial detail with the identification of regional spatial patterns20; (2) it is well matched to the spatial extent of the BTH region, ensuring a sufficient number of spatial units for statistical analysis while capturing the spatial heterogeneity of natural and socioeconomic factors within the region; and (3) this scale has been widely applied in ecosystem service and risk assessment studies in comparable regions14,15,30, providing strong methodological continuity and comparability.

To examine the influence of grid-cell size selection on key analytical results, we constructed three grid scales (5 km, 10 km, and 20 km) for a comparative sensitivity analysis. Three core metrics were quantitatively evaluated: the Pearson correlation coefficient (*r*) between flood risk and water yield service, the Pearson correlation coefficient (*r*) between flood risk and soil retention service, and the bivariate spatial autocorrelation Moran’s I for water yield service. The results showed that, at the 5 km, 10 km, and 20 km scales, the risk–water yield correlation coefficients were 0.241, 0.268, and 0.281, respectively; the risk–soil retention correlation coefficients were − 0.089, − 0.097, and − 0.112, respectively. Both sets of correlation coefficients were marked with **, indicating significance at the *p* < 0.01 level. For the three scales, the corresponding risk–water yield Moran’s I values were − 0.003, − 0.009, and − 0.015, none of which passed the significance test (|Z|< 1.96). Overall, all three metrics exhibited monotonic trends with increasing grid scale, and neither the sign of the correlation coefficients nor the significance levels reversed. This indicates that the analytical conclusions obtained at the 10 km grid scale are robust within the 5–20 km range. Therefore, this study adopts 10 km as the core analytical grid scale, which balances the dual requirements of capturing fine‑scale local spatial details and identifying broad regional patterns, while also ensuring comparability with existing studies in the same field.

Bivariate spatial correlation analysis

To identify local spatial association patterns between flood risk and ecosystem services in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) region, this study employed a bivariate spatial correlation analysis approach. This method quantifies the bivariate local spatial autocorrelation index (Bivariate Local Moran’s I) to reveal the spatial clustering characteristics and dependency relationships between two geographical phenomena (e.g., flood risk and water yield or soil conservation) across spatial units. Based on the sign and significance of the bivariate local Moran’s I, spatial units were classified into four types of spatial clusters: high–high (HH) clusters, where areas with high flood risk are spatially associated with high ecosystem service values; low–low (LL) clusters, where low flood risk coincides with low ecosystem service values; high–low (HL) clusters, indicating spatial adjacency between high flood risk and low ecosystem service areas; and low–high (LH) clusters, representing spatial associations between low flood risk and high ecosystem service areas.

Coupling coordination degree model

To systematically evaluate the overall level of interaction and coordinated development between the two subsystems—flood risk and ecosystem services—in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) region, this study employed the coupling coordination degree model. This approach goes beyond simple correlation analysis by comprehensively quantifying the coordination relationship between subsystems from two dimensions: interaction intensity and developmental balance.

Results

Flood risk patterns

Using the natural breaks (Jenks) classification method, flood risk assessment results were presented at three spatial scales—the pixel scale (30 m × 30 m), the 10 km × 10 km grid scale, and the county-level administrative scale—thereby systematically revealing the multi-scale spatial differentiation characteristics of flood risk in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) region (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Multi-scale spatial patterns of flood risk in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region:(a) raster scale (30 m × 30 m); (b) grid scale (10 km × 10 km); (c) county-level administrative scale. The figure was created by ArcGIS Desktop 10.7. software; https://www.esri.com.

Multi-scale spatial patterns of flood risk in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region:(a) raster scale (30 m × 30 m); (b) grid scale (10 km × 10 km); (c) county-level administrative scale. The figure was created by ArcGIS Desktop 10.7. software; https://www.esri.com.

At the pixel scale (Fig. 2a), the assessment results provide a fine-grained depiction of intra-regional heterogeneity in flood risk. High-risk areas exhibit pronounced point-like and linear distribution patterns and are mainly concentrated in two types of regions. The first includes the piedmont sloping plains and parts of the low hilly areas along the Taihang and Yanshan Mountains, where pronounced topographic relief, concentrated precipitation, and rapid runoff convergence contribute to high natural hazard potential. The second type comprises built-up areas of major cities such as Beijing, Tianjin, and Shijiazhuang, where high-intensity land use has led to a substantial increase in impervious surfaces, elevated surface runoff coefficients, and intensified pressure on urban drainage systems, resulting in high exposure and pronounced engineering vulnerability. In contrast, low- and medium-risk areas are widely distributed across the northwestern mountainous regions and the southeastern plains. This scale highlights the direct control of local topography, drainage infrastructure, and land-use patterns on flood risk and provides refined spatial support for the layout of urban flood control facilities and the implementation of sponge city strategies.

After aggregation to the 10 km grid scale (Fig. 2b), the macroscopic spatial patterns of flood risk become more evident, with local heterogeneity smoothed and spatial clustering effects significantly enhanced. High-risk grids form three major contiguous belts: the southern foothills of the Yanshan Mountains–Tangshan coastal belt, the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains, and the middle and lower plains of major river systems such as the Haihe, Yongding, and Daqing Rivers. This pattern indicates that at broader spatial scales, flood risk is not only influenced by local underlying surface conditions but is also closely associated with meso-scale meteorological systems (e.g., storm center migration paths), watershed-scale hydrological responses, and the spatial extent of river floodplains. The identification of these high-risk continuous zones at the grid scale provides critical guidance for basin-wide flood management and cross-administrative coordination at the regional level.

At the county-level administrative scale (Fig. 2c), flood risk values represent the overall flood threat faced by each administrative unit. Counties with high and relatively high flood risk are primarily concentrated in the low-lying plains of southeastern Hebei Province, including Hengshui, Cangzhou, and parts of Langfang, as well as counties along the lower reaches of the Haihe River in Tianjin. These areas are characterized by flat terrain and dense river networks, where the combined risks of pluvial waterlogging and fluvial flooding are particularly pronounced. Counties with moderate flood risk are widely distributed across the transitional zones along the foothills of the Yanshan and Taihang Mountains and in suburban areas of Beijing and Tianjin, where risk levels are strongly influenced by local micro-topography and urban drainage capacity. Low- and very low-risk counties are mainly located in northern Beijing, northern Hebei’s Yanshan mountainous regions, and the Bashang Plateau in the northwest, where higher elevations and less developed river systems result in relatively limited flood threats. Overall, flood risk exhibits a stepwise decline from the southeastern plains toward the northwestern mountainous regions, and the county-level risk pattern shows strong consistency with topographic elevation, river network density, and historical flood records.

Ecosystem service patterns

Water yield service

Based on the InVEST water yield module, this study systematically evaluated the spatial characteristics of water yield in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) region by integrating multi-source data, including land use, soil properties, meteorological variables (precipitation and evapotranspiration), and topography. The assessment results were presented at three spatial scales—the grid scale (30 m × 30 m), the 10 km × 10 km grid scale, and the county-level administrative scale—thereby revealing the scale-dependent spatial patterns of water yield across the region (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Multi-scale spatial patterns of water yield in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region:(a) raster scale (30 m × 30 m); (b) grid scale (10 km × 10 km); (c) county-level administrative scale. The figure was created by ArcGIS Desktop 10.7. software; https://www.esri.com.

Multi-scale spatial patterns of water yield in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region:(a) raster scale (30 m × 30 m); (b) grid scale (10 km × 10 km); (c) county-level administrative scale. The figure was created by ArcGIS Desktop 10.7. software; https://www.esri.com.

At the grid scale (Fig. 3a), the assessment results provide a fine-grained representation of the spatial heterogeneity of water yield. High-value areas exhibit pronounced belt-shaped distributions, primarily located along the windward slopes and piedmont hilly zones of the Yanshan and Taihang Mountains. These areas benefit from orographic uplift effects that enhance annual precipitation, and are predominantly covered by forests and shrublands, which promote strong soil infiltration capacity and thus generate high potential water yield. In contrast, low-value areas are mainly concentrated in the Bashang Plateau in the north, the agricultural plains of central Hebei, and major urban built-up areas. The Bashang Plateau is characterized by relatively low precipitation and high evapotranspiration, resulting in limited water yield capacity. Although the agricultural plains receive comparatively higher precipitation, the dominance of water-intensive crops leads to high actual evapotranspiration, thereby reducing surface runoff and groundwater recharge32. In urban areas, extensive impervious surface coverage causes precipitation to be rapidly converted into surface runoff and discharged, reducing soil water retention and infiltration,consequently, the water yield process is artificially truncated, and its ecological regulation function is markedly diminished33. Overall, results at this scale accurately reflect the integrated control of local climate, vegetation type, soil characteristics, and land-use patterns on hydrological processes.

When water yield was aggregated to the 10 km grid scale (Fig. 3b), micro-scale fluctuations were smoothed, and macro-scale zonal patterns became more distinct. High-value zones (mean > 0.66) are mainly concentrated along the eastern and southern piedmonts of the Yanshan and Taihang mountain ranges, forming two prominent arc-shaped high-value belts. This distribution closely corresponds to long-term mean precipitation patterns in the region, indicating that atmospheric precipitation is the primary driving factor of spatial differentiation in water yield34. Medium-value areas (mean 0.58–0.66) are primarily located in the transitional zones between mountain fronts and plains, as well as in some low hilly regions, reflecting a gradual transition from high-elevation water-yielding areas to low-elevation consumption or outflow zones. Low-value areas form contiguous patches in the northwestern arid and semi-arid regions and in the core urban agglomerations of the southeastern plains. At this scale, the spatial transfer and attenuation of water yield services from mountainous ecological barrier zones to plain consumption areas are clearly revealed, providing meso-scale evidence for identifying key water conservation areas and delineating ecological protection redlines.

At the county-level administrative scale (Fig. 3c), spatial patterns of water yield are further integrated, reflecting the overall water supply and conservation capacity of each administrative unit. Counties with high water yield values (mean > 0.72) are mainly distributed in mountainous areas of Chengde, northern Zhangjiakou, and western Baoding. These areas constitute critical ecological barriers and water conservation zones in the BTH region, and their high water yield capacity is of strategic importance for maintaining regional water balance35. In contrast, counties with low water yield values mainly include most of Tianjin Municipality, agricultural counties in Cangzhou and Hengshui, and the urban cores of industrial cities such as Tangshan and Shijiazhuang, which function as major water consumption and demand centers. This scale of analysis directly supports decision-making related to ecological compensation, water allocation quotas, and inter-regional water transfer based on administrative units, and reveals a pronounced spatial mismatch in water yield services across the BTH region—characterized by “northern supply and southern consumption, western conservation and eastern use.” This mismatch underscores the urgency of strengthening regional ecological compensation mechanisms and coordinated water resource management30.

At the 30 m grid scale (Fig. 4a), soil conservation capacity exhibits a highly heterogeneous spatial distribution. High-value areas (> 12,000 t·km⁻2·a⁻1) are mainly distributed along the southeastern slopes of the Yanshan Mountains, the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains, and in some hilly regions. These areas share several common characteristics: (1) pronounced topographic relief (slope > 15°), which provides strong driving forces for potential soil erosion; (2) dominant vegetation types consisting of natural secondary forests and shrublands, with forest coverage generally exceeding 40%, resulting in effective canopy interception and root reinforcement; and (3) land-use types dominated by forest land, with relatively low levels of human disturbance. In contrast, low-value areas (< 2000 t·km−2·a−1) are primarily distributed across the Bashang Plateau, the central Hebei plains, and major urban built-up areas. In the Bashang Plateau, terrain is relatively flat but vegetation cover is sparse, leading to limited soil conservation capacity. In agricultural plains, although vegetation cover shows pronounced seasonal variation, intensive cultivation practices reduce topsoil stability. In urban areas, the expansion of impervious surfaces has resulted in a near-complete loss of soil conservation function. At this scale, the results provide a fine-grained depiction of the local control mechanisms exerted by micro-topography and surface characteristics on soil conservation capacity.

After aggregation to the 10 km grid scale (Fig. 4b), the macro-scale spatial differentiation patterns of soil conservation become more apparent. High-value grid cells (mean > 10,000 t·km−2·a−1) are concentrated along the Yanshan–Taihang ecological barrier zone, forming two distinct high-value belts with a northeast–southwest orientation that closely align with the regional mountain systems. This pattern reflects the synergistic effects of topography and climate: windward mountain slopes receive abundant precipitation and provide favorable conditions for vegetation growth, resulting in well-developed ecosystems with strong soil and water conservation capacity. Medium-value grid cells are mainly distributed in the transitional zones between mountain fronts and plains, as well as in hilly areas, where soil conservation capacity shows a gradual declining trend. Low-value grid cells are predominantly located in the northwestern arid and semi-arid regions and in the densely urbanized areas of the southeastern plains. This scale of analysis facilitates the identification of key soil conservation function zones at the regional level and provides spatial support for delineating ecological protection redlines and constructing ecological security patterns.

At the county-level administrative scale (Fig. 4c), soil conservation capacity exhibits an overall spatial pattern characterized by “higher in the north and west, lower in the south and east.” Counties with high soil conservation values (mean > 20,000 t·km−2·a−1) are mainly located in northern Chengde, eastern Zhangjiakou, and the mountainous areas of western Baoding. These regions constitute critical water conservation and soil conservation function zones in the BTH region, and their ecosystem service value is of strategic importance for regional ecological security. Counties with moderate soil conservation values are primarily distributed in piedmont plains and some hilly counties, where soil conservation capacity is strongly influenced by agricultural activities and urban–rural development. Low-value counties are concentrated in the plains of Tianjin, Cangzhou, and Hengshui, as well as in the urban cores of cities such as Tangshan and Shijiazhuang. This scale of analysis directly addresses ecological management needs based on administrative units and reveals a pronounced spatial mismatch between the supply and demand of soil conservation services: high-supply areas are concentrated in the northern mountainous regions, whereas high-demand areas (e.g., urban and agricultural zones) are mainly located in the central and southern plains. Consequently, there is an urgent need to establish cross-regional ecological compensation mechanisms and to promote a coordinated governance model characterized by “mountain conservation and plain beneficiaries.”

At the same time, well-developed vegetation and soil structures enhance precipitation infiltration and regulate runoff generation processes, which in turn attenuate flood peaks and reduce downstream flood pressure at the watershed scale. The stronger negative correlation observed at the county scale further suggests that, from the perspective of administrative units as integrated systems, enhancing soil conservation capacity constitutes a critical ecological pathway for systematically reducing regional flood risk.

Discussion

Spatial characteristics of flood risk and ecosystem services

Based on a multi-scale assessment framework, this study reveals a distinctive spatial pattern of flood risk in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) region, characterized by “micro-scale heterogeneity, meso-scale clustering, and overall pressure at the administrative-unit level.” This finding represents a meaningful extension of existing research. First, this study confirms the spatial tendency of high-risk areas to concentrate in the piedmont plains of the Taihang and Yanshan Mountains as well as in major urban built-up areas, which is consistent with the macro-scale conclusions of flood risk zoning in the BTH region reported by Liu Jiahong (2020). However, through fine-grained grid-scale analysis, this study further demonstrates that macro-scale high-risk zones are internally composed of numerous discrete high-risk “points” (e.g., urban waterlogging hotspots) and “corridors” (e.g., flood conveyance pathways). Such micro-scale heterogeneity is often obscured in traditional administrative-unit analyses. This finding corroborates the argument advanced by Wu20 that understanding urban flood risk requires a multi-scale landscape perspective, and indicates that single-scale analyses may fail to fully capture the complex spatial mechanisms underlying flood risk formation.

Second, the identification of continuous high-risk belts along the “piedmont plains–downstream river reaches” not only confirms that impervious surface expansion driven by rapid urbanization is a key driver of increasing flood risk6,12, but more importantly, multi-scale association analysis clarifies that these areas represent typical zones where three types of pressure—high natural hazard potential, high socioeconomic exposure, and high infrastructural vulnerability—are superimposed. This advances previous studies that tended to attribute flood risk to driving factors in a relatively generalized manner, and provides more precise spatial targets for risk-oriented intervention strategies.

The multi-scale assessment of water yield and soil conservation services further reveals that, although both services exhibit similar overall spatial differentiation patterns in the BTH region—characterized by “higher values in mountainous areas and lower values in plains”—their formation mechanisms and scale effects differ substantially. With respect to water yield service, this study confirms the classical conclusion that its spatial distribution is primarily controlled by topography and precipitation patterns34, with high-value areas stably located on the windward slopes of the Yanshan and Taihang Mountains. This finding is consistent with the results of14,15) regarding water conservation functions in the region. In contrast, the RUSLE-based assessment of soil conservation service reinforces the consensus that mountainous ecological barriers constitute key soil and water conservation function zones35. The strong spatial correspondence between high soil conservation values and the orientation of the two major mountain systems further confirms the dominant role of topography and vegetation cover. The key contribution of this study lies in elucidating the hierarchical effects of soil conservation through scale analysis: at the local (grid) scale, soil conservation primarily manifests as on-site erosion control benefits, whereas at regional (grid and county) scales, its downstream disaster mitigation effects—achieved through reducing sediment delivery and maintaining river conveyance capacity—become increasingly prominent. This finding aligns with Wu’s (2021) assertion that landscape services exhibit cross-scale flow characteristics, highlighting that assessments of the disaster mitigation value of soil conservation must transcend administrative boundaries and adopt a watershed-scale perspective.

The above multi-scale pattern characteristics are further corroborated by quantitative indicators and representative locations. Taking the correlation coefficients as an example (Table 3), the relationship between water yield service and flood risk was weakly negative at the 30 m grid scale (*r* = − 0.068, *p* < 0.05), but became significantly positive at the 10 km grid scale (*r* = 0.268, *p* < 0.01), and remained significantly positive at the county scale (*r* = 0.246, *p* < 0.01). The absolute value of the correlation coefficient jumped from less than 0.07 to above 0.25, an increase of more than 250%, clearly demonstrating the strength of the “scale reversal”. In contrast, the negative correlation between soil retention service and flood risk intensified markedly with increasing scale: from non‑significant at the grid scale (*r* = 0.012) to − 0.097 at the 10 km grid scale (*p* < 0.01), and further to − 0.445 at the county scale (*p* < 0.01)—an almost four‑fold increase in absolute value, indicating that the risk‑reducing effect of soil retention is systematically amplified at larger spatial units.

From the perspective of spatial clustering patterns (Table 4; Fig. 5, Fig. 6), the bivariate Moran’s I also exhibits clear scale sensitivity. The water yield service and flood risk did not pass the significance test at the 10 km grid scale (Moran’s I = − 0.009, Z = − 1.105), suggesting that their spatial distributions are relatively independent at this scale. At the county scale, however, the Moran’s I became significantly positive (Moran’s I = 0.188, Z = 5.216, *p* < 0.01), indicating that the spatial synergy pattern of “high water yield—high risk” emerges after aggregation to administrative units. The reason for this transition lies in the fact that high‑water‑yield areas (e.g., Laiyuan County in Baoding, Weichang County in Chengde) and high‑risk areas (e.g., Taocheng District in Hengshui, Yunhe District in Cangzhou) are not directly adjacent at the grid or mesh scale, but through aggregation by administrative units, upstream water supply capacity and downstream risk exposure become significantly associated at the county level.

Comparing typical locations across scales further elucidates the above patterns. Taking a 30 m grid cell in Mentougou District, Beijing (located in a forested area on the eastern foot of the Taihang Mountains) as an example: its water yield service value is high (normalized value > 0.8), while its flood risk value is low (< 0.2), exhibiting a micro‑scale characteristic of “high water yield—low risk”. When aggregated to the 10 km grid scale, however, the grid cell containing this area also includes some piedmont urban construction land, raising the risk level to approximately 0.5, resulting in a medium‑risk–high‑water‑yield combination. Further aggregation to the county scale of Mentougou District—where the entire area is located in the upstream runoff generation zone—still yields a relatively high mean water yield service value (0.74), whereas the high risks in the downstream plain areas (e.g., Haidian, Fengtai) are not included in this district’s statistics. Thus, at the county scale, Mentougou exhibits “high water yield—medium‑to‑low risk”. This case intuitively demonstrates how administrative boundaries “cut off” upstream‑downstream hydrological connectivity, leading to fundamental changes in statistical relationships across scales.

Another example is a 10 km grid cell in Jinnan District, Tianjin, located in the lower reaches of the Haihe River plain. With a high proportion of impervious surfaces, the normalized flood risk value reaches 0.89, while the soil retention service value is only 0.07, representing a typical “high risk—low soil retention” trade‑off zone. At the county scale, the mean soil retention of Jinnan District remains very low (0.09), while the risk level stays high (0.85), making the negative correlation between the two even more pronounced. This case shows that in plain areas where soil retention service is inherently weak, the lack of risk‑reduction function is not “smoothed out” by aggregation; instead, it becomes more evident as the spatial unit enlarges, further confirming the pattern of a scale‑strengthened negative correlation between soil retention and risk.

In summary, through the quantitative evolution of correlation coefficients, changes in significance of bivariate spatial autocorrelation tests, and the specific numerical changes of typical locations (the Mentougou mountain grid cell, the Jinnan plain grid cell) across three scales, the multi‑scale effects in this study are systematically supported by the data. These lines of evidence indicate that the reversal of the relationship between water yield service and risk with changing scale originates from the hydrological separation between upstream water‑yielding areas and downstream risk zones. Meanwhile, the scale‑strengthened negative correlation between soil retention and risk reflects its stable and positive risk-reduction spillover effect at the watershed scale.

Interactions between flood risk and ecosystem services

By integrating correlation analysis, bivariate spatial analysis, and the coupling coordination degree model, this study systematically reveals the nonlinear and scale-dependent complexity of the relationships between flood risk and ecosystem services.

First, the scale dependence identified through correlation analysis confirms that the relationship between ecosystem services and flood risk is not fixed, but varies substantially with the scale of observation. For example, water yield service exhibits a weak negative correlation with flood risk at the micro-scale, but shifts to a significant positive correlation at meso- and macro-scales. This phenomenon of “scale reversal” challenges the conventional perception of ecosystem services as uniformly acting disaster mitigation factors.

Particularly worthy of in‑depth discussion is the “scale reversal” phenomenon observed between water yield service and flood risk: a weakly negative correlation at the 30 m grid scale (*r* = − 0.068) turns into significantly positive correlations at the 10 km grid and county scales (*r* = 0.268 and 0.246, respectively). This seemingly contradictory result actually reveals a profound linkage between hydrological processes and spatial scale.

At the micro-scale, areas with high water yield service values are typically associated with high vegetation cover such as forests and shrubs. These areas have stronger soil infiltration capacity and higher surface roughness, which can prolong runoff concentration time and reduce the peak surface runoff locally, thereby mitigating the waterlogging risk of the grid cell itself. In other words, at the local scale, high water yield capacity often coincides with favourable infiltration and storage conditions, leading to a weak negative correlation.

However, when the observation scale expands to the meso‑scale (10 km grid) and macro‑scale (county), this relationship undergoes a fundamental reversal. The high‑value areas of water yield service spatially coincide with regional high‑precipitation belts—namely the windward slopes of the Yanshan and Taihang Mountains. These areas are the main “water source areas” and upstream runoff generation zones of the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region. The substantial surface runoff and lateral flow generated here are transported downstream along river systems to the plains. Downstream areas are characterised by dense populations, economic agglomeration, and high proportions of impervious surfaces; while receiving upstream water, they also have limited drainage capacity, creating a spatial mismatch pattern of “high water yield in the upstream, high risk in the downstream”. Consequently, when aggregated at administrative or grid scales, counties with high water yield capacity (e.g., mountainous counties) and counties with high flood risk (e.g., downstream plain counties) do not overlap spatially, but through hydrological connectivity they form a statistically positive correlation.

The core insight of this finding is that the impact of water yield service on flood risk exhibits a strong “spatial spillover effect”: the water yield service supplied upstream does not reduce risk locally but rather “transfers” risk downstream via runoff concentration processes. Therefore, at the macro‑scale, water yield service not only fails to mitigate flood risk but instead becomes one of the important driving factors of risk formation. This mechanism is highly consistent with the classic “source–sink” theory in hydrology and further confirms that ecosystem service assessment must transcend administrative boundaries and incorporate a watershed connectivity perspective.

In contrast, soil retention service and flood risk show a consistently negative correlation across different scales, with the correlation strengthening as the scale increases. This reflects a fundamental difference in the mechanisms by which the two services regulate flood risk. Soil retention primarily functions by reducing sediment transport, maintaining river channel conveyance capacity, and enhancing slope infiltration. Its risk‑reduction benefits are not limited to the local area but can also benefit downstream regions by improving watershed hydrological conditions, thus exhibiting a positive, cross‑scale “spillover effect”. This comparison further reinforces the core argument of this study—that the relationship between ecosystem services and flood risk is not fixed but depends on the complex interaction of service type, spatial scale, and hydrological connectivity.

Second, bivariate spatial analysis (Moran’s I) further quantifies the heterogeneity of these relationships from a spatial clustering perspective. The results indicate that water yield service and flood risk exhibit “high–high” spatial synergy at the county scale, whereas soil conservation service demonstrates a clear “high–low” spatial trade-off pattern. These findings are consistent with the argument advanced by Peng et al.17 that ecosystem services and human activities exhibit complex spatial matching relationships, and they provide concrete empirical examples of “risk–service” interactions. In particular, the identification of “high–high” synergy zones highlights spatial conflict areas that are simultaneously ecologically important and flood-risk hotspots. This offers precise spatial targets for implementing the most urgent and targeted strategies of “protective restoration” or “adaptive management,” moving beyond the relatively generalized descriptions of spatial relationships in previous studies.

Third, the introduction of the coupling coordination degree model extends beyond simple association analysis by evaluating the system from a comprehensive perspective that integrates interaction intensity and developmental balance. The results indicate that the overall coordination between flood risk and water yield service in the BTH region is stronger than that between flood risk and soil conservation service. This finding reflects fundamental differences in the roles and mechanisms through which these two ecosystem services regulate flood risk. Water yield is more closely associated with the “quantity” and dynamics of hydrological processes, resulting in a more complex and variable relationship with flood risk, whereas soil conservation is directly linked to the “quality” and stability of disaster mitigation processes, and its degradation more directly leads to systemic maladjustment. This insight aligns with the framework proposed by Cui et al.10, who approached ecological security pattern construction from the perspective of flood regulation services.

In summary, through a multi-dimensional analytical approach, this section demonstrates that the relationship between flood risk and ecosystem services is scale-dependent, spatially heterogeneous, and service-specific. The findings not only confirm the critical role of ecosystem services in flood risk regulation, but more importantly, reveal the complexity, conditionality, and spatial specificity of these regulatory effects.

It should be noted that the statistical associations reported in this study (correlation, bivariate spatial autocorrelation, and coupling coordination) are derived from cross‑sectional data and thus reflect spatial covariation rather than strict causal relationships. For instance, the positive correlation between water yield and flood risk at the county scale does not imply causation; rather, it may arise from shared drivers (e.g., precipitation, topography) and hydrological connectivity. Expressions such as “regulating effect” or “mitigation effect” are therefore used as mechanistic inferences based on eco‑hydrological principles, not as statistical proof of causality. Future research should employ process‑based hydrological models or quasi‑experimental designs to formally test these causal pathways.

Innovations and contributions of this study

The innovations of this study are mainly reflected in three aspects. First, a multi-scale integrated analytical framework was developed. By overcoming the limitations of previous single-scale studies, this framework systematically reveals the scale dependence of the relationships between flood risk and ecosystem services, and provides a methodological reference for understanding the cross-scale transmission mechanisms of geographical processes. Second, this study deepens the understanding of the coupling mechanisms between “risk” and “service.” Through the application of the coupling coordination degree model, the interaction strength between flood risk and ecosystem services was quantitatively assessed, while the balance of their co-development was simultaneously evaluated. This system-oriented perspective elucidates the ecological pathways through which regional flood disasters are formed under coupled human–environment systems. Third, a spatial governance perspective based on “supply–demand” matching was proposed. By identifying the spatial mismatch and alignment between areas with high ecosystem service supply and areas with high flood risk demand, this study provides a scientific basis for cross-regional ecological compensation mechanisms and coordinated flood risk management.

The theoretical contribution of this study lies in enriching the application of coupled human–environment system science and landscape sustainability science in the field of disaster risk research. From a practical perspective, the findings provide multi-scale, operational spatial decision support for territorial ecological restoration, resilient urban planning, and integrated watershed risk management in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region.

Based on the results of bivariate spatial clustering (Fig. 5, 6) and coupling coordination degree assessment (Fig. 7, 8), this study proposes the following spatially explicit countermeasures:

Limitations and future research directions

In terms of methodological validation, this study has the following limitations. First, the training and validation of the random forest model were based on remotely sensed flood inundation samples (50,000 points each, with 70% for training and 30% for testing). The overall model AUC reached 0.96, indicating good classification performance. However, due to the lack of independent ground‑based flood event observations, the extrapolation robustness of the model still requires further testing. Second, the InVEST water yield module and the RUSLE model employ empirical formulas and simplified assumptions (e.g., neglect of subsurface lateral flow, river channel storage processes, etc.) to simulate hydrological and erosion processes, which may deviate from actual conditions. Third, spatial bivariate autocorrelation analysis is sensitive to the specification of the spatial weight matrix. This study used a combination of Queen contiguity and distance threshold; future research could test alternative weighting schemes (e.g., k‑nearest neighbors, distance based on watershed networks) to assess the robustness of the results.

Future research could consider coupling hydrodynamic models (e.g., SWMM, MIKE) with ecosystem service models to achieve process‑based simulation of flood dynamics. Introducing time‑series data (multi‑period remote sensing and reanalysis data) for dynamic correlation analysis would also be beneficial. Moreover, causal inference methods (e.g., panel data fixed‑effects models, instrumental variable approaches) could be employed to further test the causal effects of ecosystem services on flood risk.

Conclusions

Taking the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei (BTH) region as a case study, this research constructed an integrated framework of “multi-scale assessment–association analysis–mechanism exploration.” By comprehensively applying spatial analysis, statistical models, and coupling coordination degree methods, the study systematically reveals the complex interactions and scale effects between flood risk and key ecosystem services, namely water yield and soil conservation. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:

The spatial patterns of both flood risk and ecosystem services exhibit pronounced scale dependence. At the 30 m grid scale, both variables display high spatial heterogeneity, finely controlled by local topography, land-use patterns, and other surface characteristics. When aggregated to the 10 km grid and county-level scales, macro-scale zonal patterns become evident: high flood risk areas form continuous belts along the “piedmont plains–downstream reaches of major rivers,” whereas high ecosystem service areas are stably concentrated within the Yanshan and Taihang ecological barrier zones. This results in a clear spatial mismatch between “high-risk–low-service” plain urban agglomerations and “low-risk–high-service” mountainous regions. The relationships between flood risk and the two ecosystem services differ fundamentally and exhibit systematic evolution across scales. The relationship between water yield service and flood risk demonstrates a “scale reversal,” shifting from a weak negative correlation at the micro-scale to a significant positive correlation at meso- and macro-scales. This indicates that at regional scales, areas with high water yield capacity largely coincide with zones of high precipitation and upstream runoff generation. The surface runoff generated in these areas converges toward downstream exposure zones, thereby potentially exacerbating overall flood risk and highlighting the “source–sink” effects inherent in hydrological processes. In contrast, soil conservation service maintains a consistently significant negative correlation with flood risk at meso- and macro-scales, with the strength of this relationship increasing with scale. This finding confirms the irreplaceable ecological disaster mitigation value of soil conservation, which systematically reduces flood risk at the watershed scale by stabilizing soils, reducing sedimentation, and maintaining river conveyance capacity. Spatial clustering and system coordination analyses further underscore the complexity of these interactions. Bivariate spatial autocorrelation analysis identifies spatial synergy zones characterized by “high water yield–high flood risk” (e.g., piedmont areas and downstream plains) and spatial trade-off zones characterized by “high soil conservation–low flood risk” (e.g., ecological barrier zones), providing precise spatial targets for differentiated governance strategies. Coupling coordination degree assessment indicates that the region as a whole is characterized by the coexistence of coordinated and maladjusted states. Moreover, the coordination between flood risk and water yield service is relatively stronger than that between flood risk and soil conservation service, reflecting a more severe coupling dilemma in rapidly urbanizing areas where soil ecological function degradation and increasing disaster risk are closely intertwined.
