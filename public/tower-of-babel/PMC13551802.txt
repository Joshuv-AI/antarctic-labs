Latent profiles of fear of falling and their determinants among community-dwelling older adults with sarcopenia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hui-qi Kang, Yan-feng Wang, Wu-hong Deng, Xiu-fen Yang, Si-jia Zhou, Chao-ran Qu, Hui Si, Ting Wu, Wei-xiang Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFear of falling (FoF) is prevalent among older adults with sarcopenia, frequently causing activity-restricting fear and requiring targeted interventions. Although FoF is influenced by individual factors like impaired balance or weakness of limbs, few studies have comprehensively analyzed its multidimensional factors. This study examines the relationships between FoF latent classes and multilevel factors (individual, familial, societal) in community-dwelling older adults with sarcopenia.MethodsThis cross-sectional study utilized convenience sampling to recruit 356 participants (age ≥ 60 years) diagnosed with sarcopenia between December 2023 and December 2024. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire, along with the Falls efficacy scale international (FES-I), the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), the Family APGAR index, and the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS). Latent profile analysis (LPA) identified distinct FoF classes. Multivariable logistic regression and a decision tree model were employed to identify the determinants of FoF profiles in older adults with sarcopenia.ResultsFour latent profiles were identified: Low Fear of falling (22.0%), Medium Fear of falling (40.7%), High Fear of falling (26.8%), Severe Fear of falling (10.5%). To identify the associated factors distinguishing these profiles, a CART decision tree was constructed. The analysis revealed that physical activity level, family APGAR index, and anxiety were the critical determinants for classifying FoF profiles. Multinomial logistic regression further quantified the associations between physical activity level and FoF profile membership, with physical activity level showing higher odds of membership in the lower-severity FoF profiles relative to the Severe Fear of Falling profile.ConclusionOur study found that FoF among community-dwelling older adults with sarcopenia exhibits significant heterogeneity and offers valuable insights to more effectively customize targeted programs to reduce fear severity and related burdens.Keywords: Older adults, Sarcopenia, Fear of falling, Latent profile analysis, Decision tree analysis

Background

Sarcopenia is defined as an age-related decline in skeletal muscle mass and function, and is characterized by reduced muscle strength and physical performance [1]. Older individuals with sarcopenia are more vulnerable to falls because of compromised lower-limb strength and impaired postural stability, which often lead to fractures, injuries, mortality, and fear of falling [2]. Fear of falling (FoF) is a multidimensional concept [3, 4], that represents a cognitive assessment of one’s own motor performance and on the other hand reflects emotional reactions to fall events in the sense of posttraumatic stress [5]. Moreira et al. [6] demonstrated that individuals with FoF are more likely to adopt fear-driven activity restrictions, which lead to physical deconditioning, reduced muscle mass, and diminished balance, thereby increasing the risk of future falls. Beyond the physiological consequences, FoF also results in decreased independence, lower quality of life, and greater susceptibility to anxiety, depression, and social isolation among older adults with sarcopenia [7, 8]. Surveys have indicated that the prevalence of FoF in community-dwelling older individuals with sarcopenia is significantly greater than that in nonsarcopenic older adults, ranging from 67.7% to 93.3% [9, 10], and the associated adverse outcomes not only increase the burden on families and society but also may cause more healthcare burdens and pose significant safety risks.

FoF is a heterogeneous condition, and older adults with sarcopenia may exhibit distinct patterns of fear depending on their individual, familial, and social circumstances. Previous research has focused predominantly on demographic and clinical characteristics (e.g., age, sex, comorbidities, and fall history) in relation to FoF [11]. Clinical observations suggest that two individuals with similar physical function and fall history may exhibit markedly different FoF profiles depending on their emotional state (e.g., whether they tend to worry or catastrophize) and their social environment (e.g., whether they have supportive family members or an active social network) [12]. While these factors provide important context, they offer limited insight into the psychological and social mechanisms that may underlie different fear patterns. Anxiety and depression may amplify the perceived threat and trigger avoidance [13], family support may buffer against fear by providing encouragement and practical assistance, and broader social networks may facilitate continued community engagement [14]. However, those studies have focused mainly on physical or psychological factors in isolation, with limited attention given to their potential interactions, and no study to date has used a person-centered approach such as latent profile analysis to explore the heterogeneity of FoF and its related influencing factors.

Therefore, to capture the full spectrum of factors potentially contributing to FoF heterogeneity, this study assessed not only routine demographic and clinical variables but also three key psychosocial constructs: anxiety and depression (using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale), family functioning (using the Family APGAR Index), and social support (using the Social Support Rating Scale). These instruments were selected on the basis of their established reliability and validity, their widespread use in geriatric research, and their coverage of distinct yet complementary domains relevant to FoF. Moreover, no study has used a person-centered approach similar to LPA to explore the heterogeneity of FoF and its related influencing factors. Therefore, a cross-sectional study that explores these factors from multiple perspectives is essential for guiding interventions targeting FoF. This work aims to elucidate FoF heterogeneity patterns of FoF and their factors, facilitating personalized community-based interventions to mitigate FoF and its associated adverse outcomes.

Methods

This cross-sectional investigation was conducted between December 2023 and December 2024 at two community health centers in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province. We recruited older adults with sarcopenia for a comprehensive study of FoF.

Sample size

In this cross-sectional study, the sample size was calculated on the basis of the sample size estimation method. The sample size should be at least 10 times the number of independent variables [15]. It included a total of 26 independent variables, considering a 10% noneffect response rate. The minimum sample size for the study was at least 286 cases: n = 10×(k)×(1 + r) = 10 × 26 × (1 + 0.10) = 286.

Measures

The structured questionnaire was designed on the basis of a literature review and included sex, age, BMI index, marital status, personal monthly income, living arrangement, educational level, history of falls in the past year, physical activity level (It was assessed using the short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF), a validated instrument that captures the intensity, duration, frequency, and type of all physical activities performed over the preceding 7-day period. Total weekly physical activity was expressed in MET-minutes and calculated separately for moderate physical activity (4.0 MET-min/min), vigorous physical activity (8.0 MET-min/min), and walking (3.3 MET-min/min), using the formula: MET (a certain intensity) × duration per day (minutes/day) × frequency per week (days/week) [16], pain (measured by the pain dimension of the EQ-5D-5 L scale) [17], the use of walking aids and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [18]. The final physical activity variable used in the analyses was total weekly physical activity expressed as MET-min/week.

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was developed by Zigmond and Snaith in 1983 [19], and consists of 14 items, with 7 items for the anxiety subscale (HADS-A) and 7 items for the depression subscale (HADS-D). It uses a 4-point Likert scale, with each item scored from 0 to 3. The total score for both subscales ranges from 0 to 21, with a threshold of 8 indicating the presence of positive symptoms. A higher total score indicates a greater level of anxiety or depression. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.807, indicating that the HADS is a reliable screening tool for anxiety and depression.

The Family APGAR Index (APGAR) was initially developed by Smilkstein in 1978 on the basis of family system characteristics and is mainly used to identify family functional deficits [20]. It covers five core dimensions: adaptation, partnership, growth, affection, and resolve. Each dimension corresponds to a single item, and a 3-point Likert scale is used: “Often” is assigned 2 points, “Sometimes” is assigned 1 point, and “Rarely” is assigned 0 points. The total score criteria are as follows: 7 to 10 points indicates good family care, 4 to 6 points suggests moderate family care deficits, and 0 to 3 points indicates severe family care deficits. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this questionnaire in this study was 0.915.

The Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) was developed by Xiao Shuiyuan in 1986 and revised in 1990 [21]. The scale consists of three modules: objective support, subjective support, and utilization of support, and is composed of 10 items. The final score is the sum of the scores of each item, and the score range is positively correlated with the intensity of social support. According to the scoring criteria, a total score between 12 and 22 indicates a lack of social support, 23 to 44 reflects a certain level of social support, and 45 to 66 indicates relatively abundant support resources. This scale has good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.935 in this study.

The Falls Efficacy Scale-International (FES-I) was developed by Yardley et al. in 2005 to assess an individual’s concern about falling and its impact on daily activities [22]. FoF was measured using the FES-I which contains 16 items covering daily activities ranging from simple to complex, such as walking, shopping, and going up and down stairs. Each item is rated on a 4-point scale (1 = not concerned, 4 = extremely concerned), with a total score ranging from 16 to 64. A higher score indicates a greater fear of falling in the individual. It is widely used among older adults at high risk of falls and is an important tool for assessing psychological factors related to falls [23]. The FES-I has good reliability and validity. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.982.

To ensure the accurate diagnosis of sarcopenia, this study employed a standardized measurement protocol. First, the muscle strength of the research subjects was assessed using a CAMRY electronic handgrip dynamometer (three measurements were conducted, with the maximum grip strength value taken as the standard). Simultaneously, the skeletal muscle mass index of the limbs (ASMI) was measured using an InBody device (BIA). In accordance with the diagnostic criteria of the 2019 Asian Working Group on Sarcopenia (AWGS) [24], if the participants met both of the following conditions, they were diagnosed with sarcopenia: muscle strength indicators (maximum grip strength test), for men < 28 kg and for women < 18 kg; and ASMI: for men < 7.0 kg/m2 and for women < 5.7 kg/m2.

Eligibility

Inclusion criteria: ①age ≥ 60 years old; ②confirmed sarcopenia according to the AWGS 2019 guidelines [24]; ③possessing of normal language communication and cognitive abilities; and ④voluntarily signed a written informed consent form to confirm participation in the study.

Exclusion criteria: ①limited lower limb joint movement or contraindications for orthopedic surgery; ②participants who had already participated in other similar projects. After participants with missing data were excluded, the final sample included 356 older adults with sarcopenia.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 26.0, Mplus version 7.0 and R version 4.5.2. The data analysis was divided into four subsections. First, a descriptive analysis of the sociodemographic characteristics of the study subjects was performed. For continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, the mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD) are typically used to represent the data. Otherwise, the median and IQR were used for all continuous variables. The independent samples t-test was employed for between-group comparisons. Second, we investigated models that identified one to six latent profiles and employed several fit indicators to determine the optimal number of profiles. The Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC) were used to compare models with different numbers of profiles, with smaller values indicating a better model fit [25]. The information entropy index (Entropy) was used as a classification validity indicator, and a value of 0.8 or above is considered to indicate clear category discrimination ability. The Lo-Mendell-Rubin (LMR) test was used to compare models with increasing numbers of profiles. A significant p value (p < 0.05) for the LMR test indicates that a model with k profiles provides a significantly better fit to the data than a model with k − 1 profiles does, supporting the selection of the more complex model. Additionally, the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) was applied to assess whether a model with k profiles significantly outperformed a model with k-1 profiles, with a significance level of p < 0.05 indicating that the k-profile model was superior. Furthermore, the entropy index was used to evaluate the classification accuracy of the models, with values closer to 1 indicating more precise categorization [26]. To identify determinants of FoF profiles in older adults with sarcopenia, we employed the multivariable logistic regression combined with a classification and regression tree (CART) model. In the model, FoF was designated as the dependent variable and all statistically significant factors from the logistic regression analysis were designated as the independent variables. The CART was chosen for its efficiency in handling categorical variables, detecting interaction effects, and generating an intuitive tree diagram that aligns with logistic regression, providing a complementary view of how risk factors interact to shape FoF profiles. The parameters of the CART decision tree model were set as follows: The cp. index (complexity parameter) is the core parameter that controls the growth and pruning of the decision tree. It prevents overfitting by controlling the complexity of the decision tree and helps us to find a balance between the goodness of fit of the model and its simplicity. Through 10-fold cross-validation for parameter tuning, it was determined that when cp. = 0.012, the error rate of the model was relatively low and overfitting could be avoided.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

The questionnaires were distributed to 371 eligible participants, with 356 valid responses retained for analysis, yielding a response rate of 98.38%. Since the continuous variables do not follow a normal distribution, the median and IQR are used for presentation. The sample was predominantly female (55.1%). With respect to pain and discomfort, 105 individuals (29.5%) reported “No pain,” 181 individuals (50.8%) reported “slight,” pain and only 19 individuals (< 10%) were classified as “severe” or “unable to/extreme.” To reduce the standard error in subsequent data analysis, we combined the “moderate” (51 individuals), “severe” (6 individuals), and “unable to/extreme” (13 individuals) groups, renaming them the “Moderate to extreme” group, totaling 70 individuals (19.7%). Latent profile analysis

Latent profile analysis was conducted on the FoF in 356 older adults with sarcopenia. In this study, the model fit of 1 to 6 potential classes was tested. The fitting parameters of each type are detailed in Table 2. Among the 1 to 6 fitted models, the model selection process is as follows: ① Initial screening by information criteria: As the number of latent classes increased from 1 to 6, all the information criterion indicators monotonically decreased. This phenomenon indicates that more complex models have better statistical fit, and thus models with only 1 to 3 latent classes were excluded by statistical significance test: Among the 4 to 6 class models, the LMR test P-values of the 5-class and 6-class models exceeded the critical threshold of 0.05, indicating that their statistical improvements were not significant, thus, they were eliminated. ③Validation of classification validity: For the 4-class model, the entropy value was greater than 0.8, meeting the classification quality requirements, confirming that this model can clearly distinguish different latent classes. Therefore, the optimal latent class model for this study ultimately selected the model with 4 latent classes (Table 3).

The four latent profiles were characterized and named on the basis of their response patterns across the 16-item FES-I. Profile 1 (n = 79, 22.0%): Despite these elevations, the absolute scores remained lower than those of all the other profiles. High confidence was reported during most indoor/outdoor activities with context-specific vigilance in fall-prone contexts. Therefore, we named this category as “Low Fear of falling”. Profile 2 (n = 144, 40.7%): The largest subgroup exhibited uniformly low FoF scores across all the items. It had minimal interitem variability, with all scores lower than those of Profiles 3 and 4. Therefore, we named it the “Medium Fear of Falling Group”. Profile 3 (n = 96, 26.8%): The scores exceeded those of Profiles 1 and 2 but remained below those of profile 4. Pronounced score heterogeneity with peak concerns for Item 9 (Visiting friends/relatives), Item 10 (Attending social gatherings), and Item 11 (Walking outside) were revealed. This profile was named the “High Fear of Falling Group”. Profile 4 (n = 37, 10.5%): The smallest subgroup demonstrated uniformly high scores across all the items, comprising the smallest proportion. The scores of each item in this category were high, and the differences between the scores of each item were minimal, indicating that the patients had fears and concerns about all activities both indoors and outdoors. Therefore, we named it the “Severe Fear of Falling Group”. The distinct response patterns across profiles are shown in Fig. 1.

Group comparison

FoF latent profiles served as the grouping variable. Given the skewed data distribution, the results of the Kruskal‒Wallis nonparametric tests revealed statistically significant differences across sociodemographic and disease-related characteristics: sex (H = 8.372, P = 0.039), marital status (H = 28.125, P < 0.001), monthly income (H = 11.957, P = 0.008), living arrangement (H = 45.513, P < 0.001), education level (H = 19.176, P < 0.001), BMI (H = 10.484, P = 0.015), fall history (H = 122.712, P < 0.001), physical activity level (H = 84.145, P < 0.001), pain/discomfort (H = 94.155, P < 0.001), assistive device use (H = 81.886, P < 0.001), and CCI score (H = 24.421, P < 0.001). Specifically, females were significantly more represented in the Severe Fear of falling profile; from the “Low Fear of falling” to “Severe Fear of falling profiles”, decreasing proportions of married individuals and higher-income earners were observed, while increasing proportions of those living alone, lacking formal education, experiencing moderate-to-severe pain/discomfort, and utilizing assistive devices were evident, as detailed in Table 4.

Regression analysis

The factors with statistical significance from the group comparison analysis, including sex, BMI, marital status, personal monthly income, living arrangement, educational level, history of falls in the past year, physical activity level, pain/discomfort, assistive device use, and the CCI, were considered independent variables. Compared with “Severe Fear of falling” group, higher physical activity level was associated with higher odds of being in any of the three FoF groups (P < 0.001), whereas pain/discomfort and fall history were consistently linked to lower odds (P < 0.001). Not using assistive devices strongly increased the odds of belonging to the three FoF groups (P < 0.001). With respect to education, college/university education had protective effects across all groups (P < 0.001). Additionally, widowed or divorced status and higher CCI scores were significantly associated with the categories, as detailed in Table 5.

Decision tree model for different FoF categories

We selected the significant variables (p < 0.05 ) from the logistic regression and included them in the decision tree. Additionally, although the results of BMI, CCI score, anxiety, depression, family care, and social support were not significant, we found through literature review that these six variables are factors influencing FoF [10, 27–30]. Therefore, we also included them in the decision tree. The CART decision tree model identified physical activity level (MET-min/week) as the most important associated factor (root node). Anxiety, family care, social support, living arrangement, fall history, and CCI score were identified as internal nodes. The main determinants of categories were as follows: ①Participants with a physical activity level ≥ 3935 MET-min/week or an anxiety score < 5 were predominantly classified into the “Low Fear of Falling” group. ②Participants with a physical activity level < 3935 MET-min/week, an anxiety score ≥ 5, a social support score < 41, and no fall history during the past year were predominantly classified into the “Medium Fear of Falling” category. ③Participants were predominantly classified into the “High Fear of Falling” group if they had an anxiety score ≥ 5, a social support score < 41, a fall history ≥ 2 times in the past year, and a CCI score ≥ 1; or if they had a physical activity level < 993 MET-min/week, a social support score ≥ 13, and a Family APGAR Index score < 8.④Participants with a physical activity level < 993 MET-min/week and a social support score < 13 were predominantly classified into the “Severe Fear of Falling” group. Discussion

Through LPA, community-dwelling older adults with sarcopenia were classified into four distinct FoF profiles: “Low Fear of falling”, “Medium Fear of falling”, “High Fear of falling”, and “Severe Fear of falling”. Our study is consistent with the Fear-Avoidance Model, which conceptualizes FoF as a dynamic process involving interactions between physiological vulnerability, cognitive appraisals, and behavioural outcomes [31]. According to this model, accurate threat appraisal (e.g., recognizing genuine fall risk) is adaptive, but excessive fear can trigger avoidance behaviours, leading to physical deconditioning, social withdrawal, and a self-perpetuating cycle of disability and further fear.

This typology, which is grounded in a high-risk physiological group, validates and extends the Fear-Avoidance Model by illustrating a progression from situational caution to pervasive psychological distress. These findings underscore that FoF is not solely determined by physiological factors; rather, self-efficacy appraisals and emotional temperament play significant modulatory roles. Delbaere et al. [23] classified older individuals on the basis of their physiological risk factors and FES-I scores. While their “worried” and “aware” subtypes resonate with our “Severe Fear of falling” profiles, our study reveals more nuanced psychological heterogeneity, particularly the critical “High Fear of falling” profile, which may represent a unique interplay of physical vulnerability and social withdrawal.

Anxiety emerged as a predominant factor associated with more severe FoF profiles, whereas depression was not a significant influencing factor, which is consistent with the findings of Luo et al. [32]. This distinction may be attributed to the differing characteristics of these conditions: anxiety is a spiraling emotion marked by excessive worry and restlessness, whereas depression is a down repressing state characterized by low mood and diminished interest [33]. Thus, in the context of sarcopenia management, proactively screening and treating anxiety may be critical for preventing FoF escalation and interrupting the potential downstream development of depression [34].

Emotional support, attentive care, and companionship from family members can mitigate FoF to some extent. Patterson et al. [35] reported that older adults with close familial relationships exhibited better mental health and greater social engagement than those lacking such ties did, which aligns with our findings. Our decision tree analysis identified family dysfunction as the primary internal node, highlighting the central role of social support systems. Patients with insufficient family interaction often lack the motivation to participate in social activities, leading to muscle disuse atrophy.

In a survey of older individuals in Vietnam with varying levels of social support, Vo et al. [36] found that those with low or moderate support reported higher levels of FoF, which supports our findings. Additionally, a prospective cohort study demonstrated that social support is negatively correlated with sarcopenia risk [37]. High-quality social support and active social participation can help reduce FoF and promote healthy lifestyles, including balanced nutrition and regular physical activity level, and can exert protective effects against sarcopenia [38].

In conclusion, the profile-based analysis presented in this study provides a strategic framework for implementing stratified interventions among older adults with sarcopenia. The findings indicate that weekly physical activity level is a significant determining factor of FoF. Nogueira et al. found that sedentary time is significantly associated with FoF in older adults with sarcopenia. Previous evidence has linked sedentary behavior and low physical activity with FoF and sarcopenia risk. Our findings extend this evidence by showing that lower physical activity level, measured in MET-min/week, was associated with more severe FoF profiles [39]. This variable plays a critical role in objectively identifying the presence of FoF in clinical populations. People-centered care should be individualized according to distinct FoF profiles. For individuals classified within the “Low Fear of falling” and “Medium Fear of falling” subgroup, interventions should prioritize fall prevention education to enhance self-efficacy [40]. For the “High Fear of falling” profile, we recommend initiating family-centered psychosocial support for the “High Fear of falling” profile. For the “Severe Fear of falling” group, we suggest integrated interventions that combine structured physical activity level with psychological therapies (e.g., CBT), as multimodal treatment strategies have demonstrated superior outcomes [41]. All individuals exhibiting high levels of FoF, particularly those in the “Severe Fear of falling” profile, should be incorporated into community-based high-risk monitoring systems. Ultimately, a comprehensive strategy involving initial screening, ongoing dynamic assessment, and multidisciplinary collaboration holds promise for disrupting the fear-disability cycle and enhancing overall quality of life in this population.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this study is the first to employ a person-centered approach to examine the heterogeneity of FoF and its multidimensional associations, overcoming the limitations of traditional single-dimensional attribution. However, this study has some limitations. First, the data were collected from only two community hospitals in Shenzhen, a relatively affluent and highly urbanized city with strong social infrastructure, which limits the generalizability of the findings to rural areas, less developed regions, or other provinces where healthcare access and cultural contexts may differ. Second, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, as participants who voluntarily enrolled may differ from the broader population in terms of health awareness or socioeconomic status. Third, the sample was comprised of community-dwelling older adults, thus the findings may not apply to institutionalized or hospitalized populations. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and cannot disentangle the bidirectional relationships among FoF, anxiety, physical activity level, and family function; longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these dynamics. Fifth, the high internal consistency of the FES-I (Cronbach’s α = 0.982) may indicate item redundancy, although this does not affect the validity of the latent profile analysis. Sixth, Profile 4 (Severe FoF) included only 37 participants (10.5% of the sample), which may affect the stability and generalizability of the findings for this subgroup. Future research should employ multicenter, stratified sampling strategies across diverse geographic regions and settings to enhance representativeness.

Conclusion

This study reveals discernible heterogeneity in fear of falling among community-dwelling older adults with sarcopenia. Healthcare providers should implement profile-specific interventions informed by distinct profiles’ characteristics and their modifiable factors to mitigate FoF.
