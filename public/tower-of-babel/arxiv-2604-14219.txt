3.
The companion generating series as an Eichler integral

Write

D=q\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}q}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}.

For a holomorphic
q
-series
f(\tau)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}q^{n}
, define its normalized weight
-2
Eichler integral by

(3.1)

\mathcal{E}_{f}(\tau):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{3}}q^{n}=-\frac{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{3}}{2}\int_{\tau}^{\mathrm{i}\infty}f(z)(z-\tau)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z.

Then

(3.2)

D^{3}\mathcal{E}_{f}=f.

Define

(3.3)

E(\tau):=\frac{\mathcal{B}(t(\tau))}{Y(\tau)}.

Applying the variation-of-constants formula in Yang
[
12
, Lemmas 1–2, and the example following Lemma 2]
to the
\theta
-form operators (
2.2
)–(
2.3
) gives

(3.4)

D^{3}E(\tau)=\Phi(\tau):=\left(\frac{Dt(\tau)}{t(\tau)}\right)^{3}\frac{t(\tau)}{Y(\tau)\bigl(1-24t(\tau)+16t(\tau)^{2}\bigr)}.

Lemma 3.1
.

One has the identity

(3.5)

\left(\frac{Dt}{t}\right)^{2}=Y^{2}(1-24t+16t^{2}).

Proof.

Both sides belong to
M_{4}(\Gamma_{0}(8))
. For the left-hand side:
t
has no zeros on
\mathfrak{H}
(its zeros lie at the cusps
\infty
and
0
), so
(Dt)/t
is holomorphic on
\mathfrak{H}
with weight
2
, and its square has weight
4
. Cusp holomorphy follows from
\ord\bigl((Dt)/t\bigr)=0
at every cusp. For the right-hand side:
Y
is an eta-quotient with no zeros or poles on
\mathfrak{H}
, and
1-24t+16t^{2}
is holomorphic on
\mathfrak{H}
; the cusp orders are
\ord(Y^{2})=(0,0,2,2)
and
\ord(1-24t+16t^{2})=(0,0,-2,-2)
, so the product is holomorphic at cusps.

A direct computation gives the
q
-expansions

\left(\frac{Dt}{t}\right)^{2}=1-16q+48q^{2}+64q^{3}+624q^{4}+O(q^{5}),

Y^{2}(1-24t+16t^{2})=1-16q+48q^{2}+64q^{3}+624q^{4}+O(q^{5}).

The difference lies in
M_{4}(\Gamma_{0}(8))
and vanishes through
q^{4}
, hence is identically zero by the Sturm bound.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

The function
\Phi
belongs to
M_{4}(\Gamma_{0}(8))
.

Proof.

Because
t
is a weight-
0
modular function on
\Gamma_{0}(8)
, the logarithmic derivative
(Dt)/t
has weight
2
. Since
Y
has weight
2
, the expression (
3.4
) transforms with weight
4
.

We verify holomorphy on
\mathfrak{H}
. Using the identity (
3.5
), the formula (
3.4
) simplifies:

(3.6)

\Phi=\left(\frac{Dt}{t}\right)^{3}\frac{t}{Y(1-24t+16t^{2})}=\frac{Dt}{t}\cdot\frac{Y^{2}(1-24t+16t^{2})}{Y(1-24t+16t^{2})}\cdot t=Y\cdot Dt.

Since
Y
and
Dt
are both holomorphic on
\mathfrak{H}
, so is
\Phi
.

It remains to check holomorphy at the cusps. Let
u
be a local parameter at a cusp of width

, and suppose

with

so
\ord\bigl((Dt)/t\bigr)=0
. Using Proposition

, we obtain

At the cusps

The Fricke involution acts on

as follows.

Lemma 4.1
.

One has

(4.1)

g_{8}|_{4}W_{8}=-g_{8}.

Proof.

For each divisor

(E_{4}(m\tau)|_{4}W_{8})(\tau)=8^{2}(8\tau)^{-4}E_{4}\!\left(-\frac{m}{8\tau}\right).

Using

with
u=8\tau/m
, we obtain

E_{4}(m\tau)|_{4}W_{8}=\frac{64}{m^{4}}E_{4}\!\left(\frac{8\tau}{m}\right).

Hence

\displaystyle E_{4}(\tau)|_{4}W_{8}

\displaystyle=64E_{4}(8\tau),

\displaystyle\qquad E_{4}(2\tau)|_{4}W_{8}

\displaystyle=4E_{4}(4\tau),

\displaystyle E_{4}(4\tau)|_{4}W_{8}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}E_{4}(2\tau),

\displaystyle\qquad E_{4}(8\tau)|_{4}W_{8}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{64}E_{4}(\tau).

Substituting these identities into (
3.7
) gives (
4.1
).
∎

\Gamma(s)\Gamma(1-s)=\pi/\sin(\pi s)
, yields

\Gamma(s)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{-s}\zeta(s+3)\zeta(s)=-2^{3s+3}\Gamma(-s-2)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{s+2}\zeta(1-s)\zeta(-s-2).

Multiplying by the Euler factors gives

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{-s}L(g_{8},s+3)

\displaystyle\qquad=\Gamma(-s-2)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{s+2}\zeta(1-s)\zeta(-s-2)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\Bigl(-2^{3s+3}(1-2^{-s-3})(1-2^{-s-1})(1-2^{1-s})\Bigr).

A direct simplification shows that the bracket equals

(1-2^{s-1})(1-2^{s+1})(1-2^{s+3})
: indeed, writing

2^{3s+3}=2^{s+3}\cdot 2^{s+1}\cdot 2^{s-1}
and using
2^{a}(1-2^{-a})=-(1-2^{a})

for
a=s+3
,
s+1
,
s-1
turns the bracket into
-(-1)^{3}
times the claimed product.
This is exactly the Euler factor of
L(g_{8},1-s)
. Hence (
4.4
) follows.
∎

The next identity is the weight
-2
Bol identity specialized to
W_{8}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every holomorphic function
F
on
\mathfrak{H}
one has

(4.5)

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{3}}\bigl(8\tau^{2}F(W_{8}\tau)\bigr)=\frac{1}{64\tau^{4}}F^{\prime\prime\prime}(W_{8}\tau).

Equivalently,

D^{3}(F|_{-2}W_{8})=(D^{3}F)|_{4}W_{8}.

Proof.

Since

W_{8}^{\prime}(\tau)=\frac{1}{8\tau^{2}},\qquad W_{8}^{\prime\prime}(\tau)=-\frac{1}{4\tau^{3}},\qquad W_{8}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\tau)=\frac{3}{4\tau^{4}},

a direct differentiation of
8\tau^{2}F(W_{8}\tau)
shows that all terms involving
F
,
F^{\prime}
, and
F^{\prime\prime}
cancel in the third derivative. The surviving term is

8\tau^{2}\bigl(W_{8}^{\prime}(\tau)\bigr)^{3}F^{\prime\prime\prime}(W_{8}\tau)=8\tau^{2}(8\tau^{2})^{-3}F^{\prime\prime\prime}(W_{8}\tau)=\frac{1}{64\tau^{4}}F^{\prime\prime\prime}(W_{8}\tau),

which is (
4.5
). Dividing by
(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{3}
gives the formulation with
D
.
∎

We now compute the period polynomial explicitly.

Proposition 4.4

(Explicit period polynomial)
.

Let

(4.6)

P_{8}(\tau):=(E|_{-2}W_{8})(\tau)+E(\tau).

Then
P_{8}
is a polynomial of degree at most
2
, and in fact

(4.7)

P_{8}(\tau)=\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3)(8\tau^{2}+1).

Equivalently,

(4.8)

(E|_{-2}W_{8})(\tau)+E(\tau)=\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3)(8\tau^{2}+1).

Proof.

Set

H(\tau):=(E|_{-2}W_{8})(\tau)+E(\tau).

By Lemma
4.3
, Proposition
3.3
, and Lemma
4.1
,

D^{3}H=(D^{3}E)|_{4}W_{8}+D^{3}E=g_{8}|_{4}W_{8}+g_{8}=0.

Hence
H
is a polynomial of degree at most
2
.

To determine it explicitly, restrict to the
W_{8}
-invariant geodesic

(4.9)

\tau(y):=\frac{\mathrm{i}y}{2\sqrt{2}}\qquad(y>0).

Then
W_{8}\tau(y)=\tau(1/y)
, and (
3.12
) gives

(4.10)

F(y):=E(\tau(y))=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{3}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi ny}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

Fix
1<c<2
. Mellin inversion yields

(4.11)

F(y)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{(c)}\Gamma(s)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{-s}L(g_{8},s+3)y^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}s,

because

\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi ny}{\sqrt{2}}\right)y^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}y=\Gamma(s)\left(\frac{\pi n}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{-s}\qquad(\Re(s)>0).

By Stirling’s formula, the integrand in (
4.11
) decays exponentially on vertical lines. We may therefore shift the contour to
\Re(s)=-c-2
. In the strip
-c-2<\Re(s)<c
, the integrand
M(s)y^{-s}
has simple poles only at
s=0,-1,-2
(from
\Gamma(s)
); the pole of
\Gamma
at
s=-3
is cancelled by the zero
L(g_{8},0)=0
. The residue at
s=-1
vanishes because
L(g_{8},2)=0
.

Write

M(s):=\Gamma(s)\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{-s}L(g_{8},s+3).

Using the functional equation (
4.4
) and the substitution
u=-s-2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{(-c-2)}M(s)y^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{(c)}M(u)y^{u+2}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=y^{2}F(1/y).

Hence the residue theorem gives

(4.12)

F(y)-y^{2}F(1/y)=\Res_{s=0}\bigl(M(s)y^{-s}\bigr)+\Res_{s=-2}\bigl(M(s)y^{-s}\bigr).

The residues are

\Res_{s=0}\bigl(M(s)y^{-s}\bigr)=L(g_{8},3)=\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3),

\Res_{s=-2}\bigl(M(s)y^{-s}\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}L(g_{8},1)y^{2}=-\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3)y^{2}.

Substituting into (
4.12
) yields

(4.13)

F(y)-y^{2}F(1/y)=\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3)(1-y^{2}).

On the geodesic (
4.9
),

(E|_{-2}W_{8})(\tau(y))=8\tau(y)^{2}E(W_{8}\tau(y))=-y^{2}F(1/y),

so (
4.13
) becomes

H(\tau(y))=\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3)(1-y^{2}).

Since
8\tau(y)^{2}+1=1-y^{2}
, this is exactly

H(\tau(y))=\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3)(8\tau(y)^{2}+1)\qquad(y>0).

Both sides are polynomials of degree at most
2
in
\tau
and they agree on the infinite set
\{\tau(y):y>0\}
, so they are identical. This proves (
4.7
) and (
4.8
).
∎

5.
Singularity analysis at the dominant critical value

The fixed point of
W_{8}
in the upper half-plane is

(5.1)

\tau_{*}:=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\sqrt{2}},\qquad W_{8}(\tau_{*})=\tau_{*},\qquad 8\tau_{*}^{2}+1=0.

Set

(5.2)

t_{0}:=t(\tau_{*}).

Along the invariant geodesic (
4.9
), let
q=\exp(-\pi y/\sqrt{2})\in(0,1)
. From (
2.5
) one obtains the product formula

(5.3)

t(\tau(y))=q\prod_{n\geq 1}\left(\frac{1+q^{4n}}{1+q^{n}}\right)^{8}.

Hence
0<t(\tau(y))<1
for all
y>0
, and in particular

(5.4)

0<t_{0}<1.

The singular point
t_{0}
can now be identified exactly.

Proposition 5.1
.

One has

(5.5)

t_{0}=\frac{3-2\sqrt{2}}{4}.

Proof.

Differentiate the Fricke-invariance relation (
2.10
) at
\tau=\tau_{*}
. Since
W_{8}(\tau_{*})=\tau_{*}
and
W_{8}^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=-1
, we obtain

t^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=t^{\prime}(\tau_{*})W_{8}^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=-t^{\prime}(\tau_{*}),

so
t^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=0
.

On the other hand, differentiating (
2.11
) at
\tau_{*}
gives

(5.6)

Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}\,Y(\tau_{*}),

so
Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})\neq 0
because
\eta
has no zeros on
\mathfrak{H}
. If
\mathcal{A}
were holomorphic at
t_{0}
, then by (
2.6
) the chain rule would give

Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(t_{0})t^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=0,

contrary to (
5.6
). Therefore
t_{0}
is a finite singularity of
\mathcal{A}
, and hence a finite singular point of the differential equation (
2.4
). The nonzero finite singularities of (
2.4
) are exactly the roots of
16z^{2}-24z+1
, namely

\frac{3\pm 2\sqrt{2}}{4}.

By (
5.4
), only the smaller root lies in
(0,1)
. Therefore

t_{0}=\frac{3-2\sqrt{2}}{4}.

This proves (
5.5
).
∎

We next determine the local exponents at the dominant singularity.

Proposition 5.2
.

At
z=t_{0}
the local exponents of (
2.4
) are

0,\qquad\frac{1}{2},\qquad 1.

Consequently there exist constants
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\beta_{0},\beta_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

(5.7)

\mathcal{A}(z)=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\Bigl(1-\frac{z}{t_{0}}\Bigr)^{1/2}+O\!\Bigl(1-\frac{z}{t_{0}}\Bigr),

(5.8)

\mathcal{B}(z)=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\Bigl(1-\frac{z}{t_{0}}\Bigr)^{1/2}+O\!\Bigl(1-\frac{z}{t_{0}}\Bigr).

Proof.

The coefficient of
y^{\prime\prime\prime}
in (
2.4
) is
z^{2}(16z^{2}-24z+1)
, so
z=t_{0}
is a regular singular point. Put

z=t_{0}(1-\varepsilon),\qquad y=\varepsilon^{r}.

Since
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}z=-t_{0}^{-1}\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\varepsilon
, substitution into (
2.4
) shows that the coefficient of
\varepsilon^{r-2}
is

-8\sqrt{2}\,r(2r-1)(r-1).

Therefore the indicial equation is
r(2r-1)(r-1)=0
, and the exponents are
0
,
1/2
, and
1
.

We derive the expansions (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) from the modular parametrization. Since
t^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=0
(Proposition
5.1
) and
t
is nonconstant, there exists
m\geq 2
with
t(\tau)-t_{0}=c_{m}(\tau-\tau_{*})^{m}+O((\tau-\tau_{*})^{m+1})
and
c_{m}\neq 0
. We claim
m=2
. Indeed, the Wronskian identity (
3.5
) gives
(Dt/t)^{2}=Y^{2}(1-24t+16t^{2})
. The left-hand side vanishes to order
2(m-1)
at
\tau_{*}
(since
Dt/t
vanishes to order
m-1
), while on the right-hand side
Y(\tau_{*})^{2}\neq 0
and
1-24t+16t^{2}=16(t-t_{0})(t-t_{1})
vanishes to order exactly
m
(from the factor
t-t_{0}
, as
t(\tau_{*})\neq t_{1}
). Equating orders gives
2(m-1)=m
, hence
m=2
.

Therefore
u:=(1-t(\tau)/t_{0})^{1/2}
is a local holomorphic parameter at
\tau_{*}
with a simple zero. The function
Y(\tau)=\mathcal{A}(t(\tau))
is holomorphic at
\tau_{*}
, so
\mathcal{A}
is a holomorphic function of
u=(1-z/t_{0})^{1/2}
near
z=t_{0}
. Expanding in powers of
u
gives (
5.7
). Likewise,
\mathcal{B}(t(\tau))=Y(\tau)E(\tau)
by Proposition
3.3
, and both
Y
and
E
are holomorphic at
\tau_{*}
(the latter as a convergent Eichler
q
-series). Hence
\mathcal{B}
is also a holomorphic function of
(1-z/t_{0})^{1/2}
near
z=t_{0}
, which gives (
5.8
). In particular, neither expansion contains logarithmic terms.
∎

The next lemma shows that the square-root term in (
5.7
) is nonzero.

Lemma 5.3
.

One has
\alpha_{1}\neq 0
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.2
,
t(\tau)-t_{0}=c(\tau-\tau_{*})^{2}+O((\tau-\tau_{*})^{3})
with
c\neq 0
. Substituting into (
5.7
) gives

Y(\tau)=\mathcal{A}(t(\tau))=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\lambda(\tau-\tau_{*})+O\bigl((\tau-\tau_{*})^{2}\bigr)

for some
\lambda\neq 0
. Since (
5.6
) shows that
Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})\neq 0
, we conclude
\alpha_{1}\neq 0
.
∎

The dominant asymptotics now follow from standard singularity transfer.

Lemma 5.4
.

One has
\alpha_{1}\neq 0
(Lemma
5.3
), and the coefficients satisfy

s_{n}\sim-\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\,t_{0}^{-n}n^{-3/2},\qquad B_{n}^{(8)}=-\frac{\beta_{1}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\,t_{0}^{-n}n^{-3/2}+O(t_{0}^{-n}n^{-2}).

Therefore

(5.9)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{B_{n}^{(8)}}{s_{n}}=\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}.

Proof.

The other finite singularity of (
2.4
) is

\frac{3+2\sqrt{2}}{4}>1>t_{0},

so
t_{0}
is the unique singularity of smallest modulus. Since the differential equation (
2.4
) is Fuchsian with no other singularities on the circle
|z|=t_{0}
, the functions
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
extend analytically to a
\Delta
-domain at
z=t_{0}
(an indented disk of radius exceeding
t_{0}
, slit along
[t_{0},\infty)
). The local behavior at
t_{0}
is algebraic with exponent
1/2
, so the transfer theorem of Flajolet–Odlyzko (see
[
6
, Theorem VI.1]
) applied to (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) gives the stated asymptotics. Dividing them yields (
5.9
).
∎

To identify the ratio
\beta_{1}/\alpha_{1}
, we differentiate at the Fricke fixed point.

Lemma 5.5
.

One has

(5.10)

\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}=\frac{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{B}(t(\tau))\big|_{\tau=\tau_{*}}}{Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.2
,
t(\tau)-t_{0}=c(\tau-\tau_{*})^{2}+O((\tau-\tau_{*})^{3})
with
c\neq 0
, so

\left(1-\frac{t(\tau)}{t_{0}}\right)^{1/2}=\lambda(\tau-\tau_{*})+O\bigl((\tau-\tau_{*})^{2}\bigr)\qquad(\lambda\neq 0).

Substituting this into (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) gives

Y(\tau)=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\lambda(\tau-\tau_{*})+O\bigl((\tau-\tau_{*})^{2}\bigr),

\mathcal{B}(t(\tau))=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\lambda(\tau-\tau_{*})+O\bigl((\tau-\tau_{*})^{2}\bigr).

Differentiating at
\tau=\tau_{*}
yields

Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=\alpha_{1}\lambda,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{B}(t(\tau))\Big|_{\tau=\tau_{*}}=\beta_{1}\lambda,

which is exactly (
5.10
).
∎

The Fricke period polynomial gives the final derivative identity.

Proposition 5.6
.

At the fixed point
\tau_{*}
one has

(5.11)

E(\tau_{*})+\frac{E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}{2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}}=\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3),

(5.12)

Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}\,Y(\tau_{*}).

Proof.

Differentiate (
4.8
). Since
W_{8}(\tau_{*})=\tau_{*}
,
W_{8}^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=-1
, and
8\tau_{*}^{2}=-1
, the derivative of
(E|_{-2}W_{8})(\tau)=8\tau^{2}E(W_{8}\tau)
at
\tau_{*}
is

16\tau_{*}E(\tau_{*})-8\tau_{*}^{2}E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=4\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}\,E(\tau_{*})+E^{\prime}(\tau_{*}).

Adding the derivative of
E(\tau)
and differentiating the right-hand side of (
4.8
) gives

4\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}\,E(\tau_{*})+2E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=16\tau_{*}\cdot\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3)=\frac{7\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}}{8}\zeta(3),

which is equivalent to (
5.11
).

Now differentiate (
2.11
). Evaluating at
\tau_{*}
gives

-Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=-16\tau_{*}Y(\tau_{*})-8\tau_{*}^{2}Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*}).

Since
-8\tau_{*}^{2}=1
, this becomes

2Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})=16\tau_{*}Y(\tau_{*})=4\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}\,Y(\tau_{*}),

which is exactly (
5.12
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

From (
3.13
) we have

\mathcal{B}(t(\tau))=Y(\tau)E(\tau).

Differentiating at
\tau=\tau_{*}
and using (
5.12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathcal{B}(t(\tau))\big|_{\tau=\tau_{*}}}{Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}

\displaystyle=\frac{Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})E(\tau_{*})+Y(\tau_{*})E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}{Y^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}

\displaystyle=E(\tau_{*})+\frac{E^{\prime}(\tau_{*})}{2\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}}=\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3)

by (
5.11
). Therefore Lemma
5.5
gives

\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}=\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3).

Finally Lemma
5.4
yields

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{B_{n}^{(8)}}{s_{n}}=\frac{7}{32}\zeta(3).

This is exactly (
1.5
).
∎

6.
The continued-fraction normalization

We now give the elementary continuant bridge from the Apéry limit to the Batut–Olivier/Ramanujan-Machine normalization. For the continued fraction (
1.4
), define

a_{0}:=1,\qquad a_{n}:=(2n+1)(3n^{2}+3n+1)\quad(n\geq 1),\qquad b_{n}:=-n^{6}.

Let
P_{n},Q_{n}
be the continuants determined by

P_{-1}=1,\quad P_{0}=1,\qquad Q_{-1}=0,\quad Q_{0}=1,

(6.1)

U_{n}=a_{n}U_{n-1}+b_{n}U_{n-2}\qquad(n\geq 1),

where
U_{n}
stands for either
P_{n}
or
Q_{n}
. Then
P_{n}/Q_{n}
is the
n
th convergent of (
1.4
).

Proposition 6.1
.

For every
n\geq 0
one has

(6.2)

P_{n}=\frac{(n+1)!^{3}}{4^{n+1}}s_{n+1},\qquad Q_{n}=\frac{(n+1)!^{3}}{4^{n}}B_{n+1}^{(8)}.

Consequently,

(6.3)

\frac{P_{n}}{Q_{n}}=\frac{s_{n+1}}{4B_{n+1}^{(8)}},\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{P_{n}}{Q_{n}}=\frac{8}{7\zeta(3)}.

Proof.

Set

\widetilde{P}_{n}:=\frac{(n+1)!^{3}}{4^{n+1}}s_{n+1}.

Using (
2.1
) with index
n
, we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+1)!^{3}}{4^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{(2n+1)(12n^{2}+12n+4)s_{n}-16n^{3}s_{n-1}}{(n+1)^{3}}

\displaystyle=(2n+1)(3n^{2}+3n+1)\frac{n!^{3}}{4^{n}}s_{n}-n^{6}\frac{(n-1)!^{3}}{4^{n-1}}s_{n-1}

\displaystyle=a_{n}\widetilde{P}_{n-1}+b_{n}\widetilde{P}_{n-2}.

Also
\widetilde{P}_{0}=1
and
\widetilde{P}_{1}=20=a_{1}+b_{1}P_{-1}
, so
\widetilde{P}_{n}=P_{n}
for all
n
.

The same calculation with
B_{n}^{(8)}
in place of
s_{n}
shows that

\widetilde{Q}_{n}:=\frac{(n+1)!^{3}}{4^{n}}B_{n+1}^{(8)}

satisfies the same recurrence (
6.1
). Since
\widetilde{Q}_{0}=1
and
\widetilde{Q}_{1}=21=a_{1}
, we have
\widetilde{Q}_{n}=Q_{n}
. This proves (
6.2
). The limit in (
6.3
) follows immediately from Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

By construction,
P_{n}/Q_{n}
is the
n
th convergent of the continued fraction (
1.4
). Proposition
6.1
shows that these convergents tend to
8/(7\zeta(3))
. Hence

\PCF\bigl((2n+1)(3n^{2}+3n+1),-n^{6}\bigr)=\frac{8}{7\zeta(3)}.

∎

7.
Concluding remarks

The theorem-level identities treated here have earlier proof routes: the
level-
8
Apéry constant through Golyshev’s Beukers–Atkin–Lehner method and
through the Golyshev–Kerr–Sasaki motivic/normal-function framework, and
the continued fraction through Batut–Olivier. The expository contribution
of the present derivation is the explicit eta-product/Eichler-integral
computation with the Wronskian identity reducing the inhomogeneous companion
to a concrete weight-
4
Eisenstein combination, the Mellin–Barnes residue
calculation giving the explicit Fricke period polynomial, and the elementary
continuant calculation identifying the resulting Apéry limit with the
Batut–Olivier/Ramanujan-Machine continued fraction.
