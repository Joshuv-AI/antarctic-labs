Histidine metabolic reprogramming drives oxidative stress induced mtDNA release to promote necroptosis and airway inflammation in severe asthma
Authors: Ying Chen, Junwen Huang, Zhaoqian Gong, Yuemao Li, Yaoxin Chen, Keke Fan, Bang Zhu, Yanyan Ma, Dapeng Hu, Shuyu Huang, Jie Yang, Xiaoqian Peng, Wenqu Zhao, Haijin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a critical contributor to asthma pathogenesis. Emerging clinical and metabolomic evidence has implicated histidine metabolism in asthma; however, whether histidine metabolic reprogramming contributes to severe asthma pathogenesis and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we integrated clinical cohort analyses, multi-omics profiling, primary human airway epithelial cell experiments, and both toluene diisocyanate (TDI)- and house dust mite/lipopolysaccharide (HDM/LPS)-induced severe asthma murine models to systematically delineate this relationship. Histidine levels were markedly elevated in induced sputum from asthma patients and were strongly associated with disease severity, airflow limitation, and inflammatory indices. Integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses revealed a pathogenic reprogramming of histidine metabolism, characterized by enhanced histamine biosynthesis and depletion of the cytoprotective carnosine, thereby amplifying airway inflammatory responses. Pharmacological blockade of histidine metabolism significantly alleviated airway hyperresponsiveness, inflammation, and structural remodeling in both TDI- and HDM/LPS-induced severe asthma models. Mechanistically, histidine metabolic dysregulation drives oxidative stress mediated mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to mtDNA release and subsequent activation of mt-ND6/FPR2 signaling, ultimately triggering necroptotic epithelial cell death. Collectively, these findings define a histidine-driven oxidative stress–mtDNA–necroptosis axis as a central mechanism of airway inflammation in severe asthma, offering new therapeutic opportunities through metabolic targeting.Keywords: Asthma, Airway inflammation, mtDNA, Histidine metabolism, Oxidative stress, Necroptosis

Introduction

Asthma is a heterogeneous chronic airway disorder characterized by persistent airway inflammation, hyperresponsiveness, mucus hypersecretion, and structural remodeling, affecting over 300 million people globally. Although standard pharmacological therapies, including inhaled corticosteroids and bronchodilators, achieve adequate symptom control in most patients, a subset develops severe asthma (SA). This severe phenotype is characterized by corticosteroid resistance, frequent exacerbations, and progressive airway remodeling, posing a significant clinical and therapeutic challenge [1,2]. Although current therapies primarily target inflammation, they often fail to prevent disease progression in corticosteroid-resistant severe asthma, highlighting the urgent need to elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms. House dust mite (HDM)/lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced asthma is a classical allergen-driven model of severe asthma [3,4], whereas toluene diisocyanate (TDI), a widely used industrial chemical and major cause of occupational asthma, induces severe asthma through chemical sensitization [5,6]. Importantly, both HDM/LPS- and TDI-induced models exhibit corticosteroid-insensitive airway inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness, key features of severe asthma. Our previous studies demonstrated that the TDI-induced asthma model exhibits airway epithelial oxidative stress and corticosteroid insensitivity, key features of severe asthma [7,8]. Therefore, employing both models enables the identification of molecular mechanisms shared across distinct severe asthma settings.

Metabolic reprogramming has recently emerged as a central regulator of chronic inflammatory diseases, shaping immune cell activation, differentiation, effector function, and tissue homeostasis [9,10]. Dysregulated cellular metabolism, encompassing lipid, glucose, and amino acid pathways, has been increasingly implicated in chronic airway inflammation and structural remodeling [11,12]. Specifically, pulmonary metabolic dysregulation, including abnormal purine metabolism, has been shown to contribute to the pathophysiological changes associated with TDI induced airway inflammation in SA [13]. Nevertheless, the key metabolic drivers that initiate and perpetuate airway inflammation and remodeling in SA remain poorly defined. In particular, the contribution of specific amino acid metabolic pathways and their mechanistic links to disease progression are still largely unexplored. Accumulating evidence suggests a potential role for histidine metabolism in asthma. Multi-omics and metabolomic studies have reported altered histidine metabolism in patients with asthma and demonstrated associations with airway inflammatory phenotypes [14,15]. In addition, a recent Mendelian randomization study suggested a potential link between histidine levels and asthma risk [16]. However, whether histidine metabolic reprogramming contributes to severe asthma pathogenesis and the underlying mechanisms remain largely unknown.

Mitochondria are central hubs integrating metabolic and inflammatory signaling. Beyond their well-established role in ATP production, these organelles regulate redox homeostasis, apoptosis, and innate immune activation [17]. Previous studies reported significant mitochondrial dysfunction in airway epithelial cells of TDI-induced severe asthmatic mice and demonstrated that targeting mitochondrial oxidative stress significantly attenuated airway inflammation [18,19]. However, the mechanistic relationship between metabolic reprogramming and mitochondrial dysfunction remains unclear. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), a circular genome that encodes essential oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) subunits, can be released into the cytosol during mitochondrial stress or damage, where it acts as a potent damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP). Cytosolic mtDNA activates innate immune receptors and downstream inflammatory cascades [20,21]. Emerging evidence indicates that abnormal mtDNA release and transcriptional dysregulation play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of several inflammatory diseases, including asthma [[22], [23], [24]]. Previous clinical studies have shown that mtDNA copy number is elevated in patients with asthma and correlates with inflammatory parameters [25,26]. Furthermore, excessive mtDNA release has been shown to exacerbate airway inflammation in combined house dust mite (HDM) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced asthma models [27]. Consistent with these observations, studies in TDI-induced murine SA models have shown that mtDNA translocation into the cytoplasm through the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) amplifies airway inflammation by activating inflammatory signaling pathways [28]. Despite these findings, the precise upstream metabolic drivers of mtDNA release and the specific downstream signaling mechanisms by which mtDNA exacerbates airway inflammation in SA remain elusive.

Necroptosis, a regulated necrotic cell death mediated by receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1), receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (RIPK3), and the mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL), has emerged as a critical mechanism linking mitochondrial dysfunction to inflammation [29]. Unlike apoptosis, necroptosis culminates in plasma membrane rupture and the release of DAMPs, which amplifies tissue inflammation. Recent findings indicate that mitochondrial stress and mtDNA release can activate necroptotic signaling, forming a feed-forward loop that exacerbates epithelial injury and inflammatory response [30]. Increased expression of necroptosis-related molecules, including RIPK3 and phosphorylated MLKL (p-MLKL), has been reported in patients with asthma [31]. Moreover, necroptosis has been implicated in airway epithelial injury, neutrophilic inflammation, and corticosteroid resistance, and its inhibition alleviates airway inflammation in experimental models [32]. However, the metabolic and mitochondrial mechanisms driving necroptosis in asthma remain largely elusive.

Therefore, we hypothesized that histidine metabolic reprogramming contributes to severe asthma pathogenesis by promoting mitochondrial dysfunction, mtDNA release, and downstream necroptotic signaling. To test this hypothesis, we integrated clinical analyses, multi-omics approaches, primary human airway epithelial cell experiments, and two distinct murine models of severe asthma to investigate the role of histidine metabolism in airway inflammation and remodeling and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

This study enrolled adult asthma patients from the Respiratory Clinic at Nanfang Hospital, Guangzhou, China. Ethical approval was obtained from the hospital's Ethics Committee (Approval No. NFEC-2023-259), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion. A total of 114 subjects were gradually enrolled after being diagnosed with asthma based on the 2021 Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) criteria. Individuals who had experienced respiratory infections or acute disease exacerbations within four weeks before the baseline visit were excluded.

Severe asthma cases were identified according to GINA steps 4-5, consistent with the classification method previously outlined by Giacco et al. [33]. The control group included 44 healthy volunteers with no prior history of pulmonary disorders and normal lung function confirmed by spirometry. Sputum induction and processing followed standardized protocols, as detailed in our earlier work. The concentration of

histidine and mt-ND6 were determined using ELISA kits supplied by CUSABIO (CSB-EL015082HU, China) and MLBIO (LM8K2804H, China).

Reagents

Toluene-2,4-diisocyanate (TDI, ≥ 98.0%, 89870), methacholine (PHR1943), and acetone (179124) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Histidine-deficient diet was bought from Xietong Inc (XTI01CR, Jiangsu, China). HDM was gained from Greer Laboratories (421175, Lenoir, NC, USA). LPS (HY-D1056), l-Histidine (HY–N0832), l-Carnosine (HY–W013494), the Histidine decarboxylase (HDC) inhibitor, Pinocembrin (HY-N0575), the mPTP inhibitor, Cyclosporin A (CsA, HY-B0579), the mtDNA inhibitor, Dnase I (HY-108882A), the Formylpeptide receptor 2 (FPR2) inhibitor, WRW4 (HY-P1119), and the necroptosis inhibitor, GSK872 (HY-101872) were bought from Medchemexpress (New Jersey, USA). ELISA kits for total IgE (88-50460-86), IL-4 (88-7044-88), IL-5 (88-7054-88), IL-13 (88-7137-88), IL-17A (88-7371-22) and TNF-α (BMS607-3) were obtained from Thermo Scientific (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Anti-HDC was bought from HUABIO (ER65495, Hangzhou, China). Anti-mtND1 (A17967), anti-mtND2 (A17968), anti-mtND3 (A17969), anti-mtND4 (A9941), anti-mtND5 (A12465) and anti-mt-ND6 (A17991) were bought from Abclonal (Wuhan, China). Anti-DNA was bought from Progen (AC30-10, Heidelberg, Germany). Anti-Carnosine Synthase 1 (CARNS1) (25725-1-AP), anti-FPR2 (30989-1-AP), anti-MLKL (66675-1-Ig), anti-RIPK3 (17563-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (Chicago, USA). Anti-p-MLKL (EPR9515) and anti-p-RIPK3 (EPR9516) were bought from Abcam (Cambridge, UK).

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Southern Medical University and complied with the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals Act. Male BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, 20-24 g) were obtained from Southern Medical University. The animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at 24 °C with 40%–70% humidity, a 12-h light/dark cycle, and ad libitum access to standard chow and water. All procedures adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines to ensure ethical and standardized reporting of animal research.

For TDI-induced severe asthmatic model. According to our previous researches [7,8,18,34], with 10 mice each, a total of 80 mice were randomly divided into the following groups: (1) AOO (a mixture of 2 vol of acetone and 3 vol of olive oil)-sensitized, AOO-challenged and DMSO-treated (Control group); (2) TDI-sensitized, TDI-challenged, and DMSO-treated (TDI group); (3) TDI-sensitized, TDI-challenged, and histidine-deficient diet (HDD) treated (TDI + HDD group); (4) TDI-sensitized, TDI-challenged, and high-histidine diet (HHD) treated (TDI + HHD group); (5) TDI-sensitized, TDI-challenged, and Pinocembrin treated (TDI + Pinocembrin group); (6) TDI-sensitized, TDI-challenged, and Carnosine treated (TDI + Carnosine group); (7) TDI-sensitized, TDI-challenged, and WRW4 treated (TDI + WRW4 group); (8)TDI-sensitized, TDI-challenged, GSK872 treated (TDI + GSK872 group). Based on the TDI-induced model, mice in the high- and low-histidine intervention groups were respectively received 2 g/L histidine administered via drinking water or fed a histidine-deficient diet [35]. And in the carnosine intervention group, carnosine was administered by adding it to the drinking water at a concentration of 3 g/L [36]. Mice in the other intervention groups were administered pinocembrin (25 mg/kg) [37], WRW4 (2.2 mg/kg) [38] or GSK872 (5 mg/kg) [39] via intratracheal instillation after each TDI challenge. Comparatively, sham mice were given the same amount of vehicle.

To further validate the generalizability of our findings, a classical HDM/LPS-induced murine model of severe asthma was established. On day 1 and 8, mice were intranasally sensitized with either sterile saline (20 μL) or HDM (20 μg). One day 15, mice were challenged intranasally with a mixture of HDM extract (20 μg) and LPS (5 μg). From day 16 to day 19, mice received daily intranasal challenges with HDM (20 μg) to induce airway inflammation. The experimental grouping was conducted as described in the preceding section.

Metabolomic profiling

Approximately 100 mg of lung tissue was homogenized in liquid nitrogen, followed by mitochondrial isolation prior to metabolomic profiling. Metabolomic profiling was performed using UHPLC-MS/MS operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes (ESI+ and ESI−). Raw mass spectrometry data were first converted to mzXML format using MSConvert in ProteoWizard (v3.0.8789) and processed with the XCMS package (v3.12.0) in R for peak detection, retention time correction, and alignment (parameters: ppm = 15, peakwidth = c(5, 30), mzdiff = 0.01, method = “centWave”). The data were normalized by the total ion signal to minimize systematic variations. Metabolite identification was based on accurate mass and MS/MS spectra by matching against multiple databases, including HMDB [40], MassBank [41], LipidMaps [42], mzCloud [43], KEGG [44], and an in-house standard library (Panomix Biomedical Tech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). Molecular formulas were predicted from parent ion m/z, mass error (ppm), and adduct information, while MS/MS spectra were further compared with reference fragment ions for confident metabolite annotation. Multivariate statistical analyses, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) were initially performed separately for the positive and negative ionization datasets using the R ropls package (version 1.34.0) to evaluate group separation, with permutation tests conducted to prevent model overfitting. The datasets obtained from both ionization modes were subsequently integrated for downstream metabolomic analyses, including differential metabolite identification, hierarchical clustering, pathway enrichment, and network analysis. Candidate biomarkers were screened based on a combined criterion of P < 0.05, Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) > 1, and fold change thresholds. Hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package (version 1.0.12). Relative metabolite abundances were normalized by row-wise Z-score transformation to facilitate comparison across samples. Clustering of metabolites and samples was performed based on Euclidean distance using the complete linkage method. Differential metabolites were subsequently subjected to functional enrichment and pathway topology analysis using MetaboAnalyst, and the results were mapped and visualized through Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Mapper to facilitate biological interpretation.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Mouse lung tissues were collected after euthanasia and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted from mouse lung tissues using the AccuraCode cracking mixture reagent (Singleron, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. RNA integrity and purity were evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Sequencing libraries were generated with the Illumina TruSeq library preparation kit and subsequently sequenced on an Illumina platform. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package (version 1.42.0) [45], with an adjusted P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All sequencing data have been deposited in the BioStudies database under accession number S-BSST2238.

Airway Hyperresponsiveness (AHR)

On day 25, AHR was evaluated by quantifying lung resistance (RL) using a plethysmographic system (Buxco Electronics, Troy, NY). Mice were positioned within a barometric whole-body plethysmograph and initially exposed to aerosolized sterile saline to establish a baseline. Following this, the mice were subjected to escalating concentrations of methacholine (3.125, 6.25, 12.5, and 25 mg/mL). RL measurements were obtained at 5 min intervals subsequent to each nebulization, in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. The RL values at each methacholine concentration were expressed as a percentage relative to the saline baseline.

Serum IgE levels

Blood samples were collected and allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Total serum IgE levels were quantified using an ELISA kit, following the manufacturer's instructions. IgE concentrations were determined by comparison with a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of the supplied standards.

Analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)

Total cell counts in the BALF were determined prior to centrifugation (1000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The supernatants were subsequently stored at −80 °C for ELISA quantification of cytokines, including IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IL-17A, and TNF-α, according to the manufacturers' protocol. For differential cell analysis, cytospin preparations were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). A total of 200 non-degenerate, morphologically intact cells per sample were examined under a light microscope to determine the proportions of macrophages, eosinophils, neutrophils, and lymphocytes, with damaged cells and debris excluded from the count.

Lung histopathology

The left lung lobes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. Histological analyses were performed using H&E, Masson's trichrome, and PAS staining to evaluate airway inflammation, collagen deposition, and mucus production. Perivascular and peribronchial inflammatory cell infiltration were semiquantitatively scored based on a previously reported method [7]. Inflammatory severity was graded as follows: 0 = no inflammation; 1 = occasional peribronchial/perivascular inflammatory cell cuffing; 2 = mild infiltration (1-10 cells thick); 3 = moderate infiltration (10-20 cells thick); and 4 = severe infiltration (>20 cells thick).

Transmission electron microscopy

Lung tissue samples were dissected into small cubes and initially fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h. Samples were then post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30% to 100%), and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (∼70 nm) were prepared and subjected to double-staining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Ultrastructural analysis was performed using a transmission electron microscope (JEOL JEM-1010).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining of lung sections for HDC and CARNS1 was conducted following deparaffinization and antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with H2O2 for 15 min. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against HDC and CARNS1 at the recommended dilutions. After washing with PBS, the samples were treated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 20 min at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using DAB as the chromogenic substrate.

Analysis of apoptosis by TUNEL histology

Apoptosis in lung tissue was assessed by TUNEL assay on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections using a commercial colorimetric kit (C1098, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Airway epithelium gene expression dataset analysis

Airway epithelial cell transcriptomic datasets from asthma patients (GSE43696 [46], GSE67940 [47], GSE76227 [48], and GSE89809 [49]) were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The data were downloaded in MINiML format, containing complete platform, sample, and record information for each GSE. Raw datasets were log2-transformed if not already normalized. When necessary, quantile normalization was performed using the preprocessCore package (version 1.62.0). Probe IDs were mapped to gene symbols based on platform annotations, probes corresponding to multiple genes were removed, and multiple probes for the same gene were averaged. For datasets with batch effects, these were corrected using the removeBatchEffect function in the limma package (version 3.58.1) [50]. In cross-dataset or multi-platform analyses, only common gene symbols were retained, and datasets/platforms were treated as separate batches before batch effect removal. Data preprocessing quality was assessed by boxplots for normalization and PCA plots comparing results before and after batch correction.

TDI-HSA conjugates

The synthesis of the TDI-HSA conjugate was performed based on a previously reported protocol, with modifications adapted from the method [51]. In brief, TDI was gradually added to HSA solution prepared in PBS under continuous stirring. After the reaction, the mixture was centrifuged at 3000×g for 20 min at room temperature to eliminate any unreacted TDI. The resulting conjugate was subsequently dialyzed against PBS at 4 °C for three days using cellulose membrane dialysis tubing (Sigma Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO, USA).

DNase I treatment

DNase I was transfected into the cytosol using the PULSin™ protein delivery reagent (501-01, Polyplus Transfection) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, DNase I was complexed with PULSin™ following the supplier's instructions. After washing the cells three times with PBS, they were incubated with the DNase I/PULSin™ complex for 4 h at 37 °C. The mixture was then removed, and the cells were cultured in fresh complete medium for an additional 24 h before being collected for subsequent analyses.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, C6005, NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release

LDH release was measured using a commercial LDH cytotoxicity assay kit (C0019S, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Oxidative stress levels detection

Total antioxidant capacity (TAC), malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, and the glutathione (GSH)/oxidized glutathione (GSSG) ratio were measured using corresponding commercial assay kits in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols.

Cell culture

Primary human bronchial epithelial cells (HBECs) were obtained from ATCC and cultured in PneumaCult™-Ex Plus Medium. When the cells reached approximately 85% confluence in tissue culture flasks (NEST Science, 707001, Shanghai, China), they were subcultured and seeded into appropriate culture plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/cm2. Cells were subsequently passaged at the same density into T25 flasks or seeded onto 12 mm Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore polyester membranes, CLS3402, Costar). The cultures were maintained at 37 °C until full confluence was achieved (typically within 2-4 days). Upon confluence, the medium in the inserts was removed and cells were rinsed with D-PBS. PneumaCult™-air-liquid interface (ALI) Maintenance Medium was then added to the basal chamber only, leaving the apical side exposed to air to establish an ALI. The maintenance phase under ALI conditions was continued for 28 days according to the manufacturer's protocol. Cells within five passages were used for all experiments. After exposure, cells were collected for protein extraction, and the basal supernatants were harvested 24 h later. Mycoplasma testing was performed before each passage and sample collection to ensure contamination-free cultures. For stimulation experiments, cultures were treated with various concentrations of TDI-HSA conjugate (0-150 μg/mL), with or without treatment using pinocembrin (10 μM), carnosine (20 mM), WRW4 (10 μM) or GSK872 (5 μM). Cells in the control group received an equivalent volume of PBS.

Detection of cell permeability

Cell permeability was detected by FITC-labeled Dextran (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to manufacturer's protocols.

Transfection

The siRNA were synthesized by Guangzhou Hanyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The sense sequence for si-FPR2 (human) was GCACUGGACCGCUGCAUUUTT AAAUGCAGCGGUCCAGUGCTT. The siRNA and corresponding scramble control (NC-siRNA) were introduced into cells using Lipofectamine 3000. The culture medium was replaced after 6 h, and cells were incubated for an additional 24 h before stimulation with TDI-HSA.

Protein extraction and Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from whole lung tissues and cultured cells using a protein extraction kit (KeyGEN Biotechnology Co., KGB5303, Nanjing, China). The samples were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer and subjected to 10% SDS-PAGE for protein separation, followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against HDC, CARNS1, mt-ND1, mt-ND2, mt-ND3, mt-ND4, mt-ND5, mt-ND6, FPR2, MLKL, p-MLKL, RIPK3 and p-RIPK3 at appropriate dilutions. After washing, membranes were incubated with IRDye® 680RD-conjugated secondary antibodies (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). Protein bands were visualized using the Odyssey imaging system and quantified with ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence

To evaluate the release of mtDNA, cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were seeded onto glass-bottom culture dishes (705001, NEST, China). After adherence, cells were stimulated with TDI-HSA (150 μg/mL) for 12 h, with or without treatment using pinocembrin (10 μM) or carnosine (20 mM) for 1 h. Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked using 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature. Mitochondria were stained using MitoTracker™ Red (873315-86-7, Yeasen, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary anti-DNA antibody, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Fluorescence signals were visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Confocal LSM 980).

Mitochondrial superoxide and mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial oxidative stress and membrane potential were assessed using MitoSOX™ Red (1003197-00-9, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) and JC-1 (T3208, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) staining, respectively. Briefly, cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well on glass-bottom culture dishes and cultured overnight. Cells were treated with TDI-HSA (150 μg/mL) for 6 h at 37 °C, with or without treatment using pinocembrin (10 μM) or carnosine (20 mM). For MitoSOX Red staining, live cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with 5 μM MitoSOX Red in serum-free medium for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. For JC-1 staining, after PBS washes, cells were incubated with JC-1 working solution (5 μM) for 60 min at 37 °C. After staining, fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Confocal LSM 980). For each condition, images from five randomly selected fields were analyzed to ensure representative quantification.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the RNAex Pro Reagent Kit (AG21101, Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from mRNA using a reverse transcription kit (R333, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Q221-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a standard qPCR platform.

The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min, during which fluorescence signals were collected. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. The specific primer sequences used are listed in Table S6.

Subcellular fractionation and analysis of cytosolic mtDNA

HBECs were harvested following stimulation for the assessment of cytosolic and total mtDNA levels. Briefly, collected cells were divided into two aliquots. One aliquot was processed directly for total DNA extraction, while the other was subjected to subcellular fractionation using a mitochondrial isolation kit (SM0020, Solarbio, China) to obtain the cytosolic fraction. DNA from both whole-cell lysates and cytosolic fractions was extracted using a DNA extraction kit (18700ES50, Yeasen, China). The extracted DNA samples were subsequently analyzed by qPCR. Cytosolic mtDNA levels were normalized to the corresponding total mtDNA content for each sample.

Mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate

Mitochondrial respiration was assessed using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (103015-100, Agilent Technologies) on a Seahorse XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer. Cells were seeded into XF96 cell culture microplates at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well and incubated overnight to allow adherence. Following the manufacturer's protocol, sequential injections of oligomycin (1 μM), FCCP (0.5 μM), and a mixture of antimycin A and rotenone (each at 0.5 μM) were performed during the assay. Key parameters, including basal oxygen consumption rate (OCR), maximal respiration, ATP production-linked OCR, and spare respiratory capacity, were calculated using the Seahorse Wave software in accordance with the standard analysis method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc correction. When normality assumptions were not satisfied, appropriate nonparametric tests were applied. Spearman's rank correlation was used to assess associations due to the non-normal distribution of most clinical variables. Diagnostic performance of candidate biomarkers was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. The optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index. Airway hyperresponsiveness curves were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc testing. All tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Metabolomic and transcriptome profiling reveals histidine metabolic disturbance in severe asthma model

Emerging evidence has implicated metabolic dysregulation in asthma pathogenesis [10,12]. However, most metabolomic studies have focused primarily on whole tissues or isolated cells, often neglecting the metabolic contributions of subcellular organelles such as mitochondria. To elucidate mitochondrial-specific metabolic alterations associated with SA, mitochondrial fractions were isolated from lung tissues of TDI-induced severe asthmatic mice and analyzed using non-targeted high-throughput metabolomic profiling. Base peak chromatograms demonstrated good metabolite coverage in both positive and negative ionization modes (Fig. 1A, E). PCA and OPLS-DA analyses revealed clear separation between the control and TDI groups, indicating substantial metabolic reprogramming following TDI exposure (Fig. 1B, C, F, G). Consistent with the PCA and OPLS-DA results, differential metabolite analysis identified 1797 significantly altered metabolites in the positive ionization mode, including 973 upregulated and 824 downregulated metabolites, and 1160 significantly altered metabolites in the negative ionization mode, including 443 upregulated and 717 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 1D, H). On these basis, the results obtained from the positive and negative ionization modes were subsequently integrated for downstream metabolite analysis. Hierarchical clustering analysis corroborated these findings, identifying distinct metabolic signatures with significant enrichment of l-histidine and related metabolites in the TDI group (Fig. 1I). Complementary volcano plot and Z-score analyses identified l-histidine, pyrrole-2-carboxylic acid, and l-prolinamide as the key contributors to the observed metabolic shift (Fig. 1J and K). Metabolic network mapping further identified l-glutamic acid and l-histidine as central nodes integrating multiple dysregulated metabolic pathways (Fig. 1L), suggesting that these amino acids play crucial roles in driving mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming. Subsequent pathway enrichment analysis highlighted histidine metabolism as one of the most significantly perturbed pathways in TDI-induced asthma (Fig. 1M). This dysregulation was characterized by elevated l-histidine and N,N-Dimethylhistidine levels alongside reduced carnosine levels (Fig. 1N), collectively indicating significant histidine metabolic reprogramming associated with mitochondrial dysfunction in TDI-induced asthma.

To further substantiate this conclusion, lung tissues from TDI-induced severe asthmatic mice were simultaneously collected and subjected to transcriptomic analysis. Transcriptomic profiling revealed extensive gene expression alterations in lung tissues following TDI exposure. Volcano plot analysis revealed extensive transcriptional alterations in the TDI group, including 1969 significantly upregulated genes and 1743 significantly downregulated genes compared with the control group (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, PCA demonstrated a clear separation between control and TDI-treated samples, indicating distinct global transcriptomic profiles and substantial transcriptional reprogramming following TDI exposure (Fig. 2B). KEGG enrichment analysis identified significant activation of cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, chemokine signaling, TNF signaling and glutathione metabolism pathways (Fig. 2C). Heatmap analyses further showed robust induction of neutrophilic inflammation related genes, eosinophilic inflammation associated genes and oxidative stress related genes (Fig. 2D–G, Table S1). Expression profiling of histidine metabolism related genes revealed a consistent upregulation pattern in TDI-exposed lungs compared with controls. Other catabolic genes in the histidine pathway, including Hal and Uroc1, also showed elevated expression in several TDI samples, whereas the expression levels of histamine-degrading enzymes and carnosine-synthesizing enzymes showed no significant alterations (Fig. 2H). Overall, these metabolomic and transcriptomic findings consistently demonstrate that TDI exposure drives a pronounced shift toward enhanced histidine catabolism and histamine biosynthesis, accompanied by increased oxidative stress, underscoring histidine metabolic reprogramming as a key molecular feature of severe asthma.

Histidine accumulation correlates with airway obstruction and inflammation in asthma patients

To determine the clinical significance of histidine metabolic dysregulation in human asthma, histidine concentrations were quantified in induced sputum samples obtained from a well-characterized cohort comprising 44 healthy control (HC), 78 patients with mild-to-moderate asthma (MMA), and 36 patients with SA (Fig. 3A). Baseline demographic characteristics, including age, sex, and body mass index, were comparable among groups (Table 1), minimizing confounding effects. Pulmonary function progressively worsened with increasing disease severity. SA patients exhibited significantly reduced forced vital capacity (FVC% predicted), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1% predicted), FEV1/FVC ratio, peak expiratory flow (PEF), and maximal expiratory flow (MEF) indices (MEF75, MEF50, MEF25, MEF75/25; all P < 0.001) compared with the HC and MMA groups, reflecting significant airflow limitation in SA.

Clinical assessments further corroborated these findings. Asthma Control Test scores were significantly lower in the SA group than in the MMA group (15.8 vs. 17.6, P = 0.002), whereas fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) levels were elevated (62.0 vs. 37.0 ppb, P = 0.045), indicating poor disease control and heightened airway inflammation. Sputum cytology further revealed a stepwise increase in neutrophil and eosinophil proportions from HC to SA (both P < 0.001), providing a clinical basis for subsequent analysis of histidine metabolism in relation to airway obstruction and inflammation.

Consistent with mitochondrial metabolomic findings in the murine model, sputum histidine (HIS) concentrations were significantly elevated in both the MMA and SA groups compared with HC (P < 0.01 and P < 0.0001, respectively; Fig. 3B). Correlation analysis revealed significant negative correlations between HIS levels and key lung function parameters, including FVC% predicted (r = −0.234, P = 0.012), FEV1% predicted (r = −0.299, P = 0.001), and PEF% predicted (r = −0.294, P = 0.002) (Fig. 3C–E). In parallel, HIS concentrations positively correlated with sputum neutrophil proportions (r = 0.442, P < 0.001; Fig. 3F). These findings collectively suggest an association between elevated histidine concentration and both functional airway obstruction and neutrophilic airway inflammation. Given the established relationship between histidine dysregulation and mitochondrial dysfunction, the study next evaluated whether elevated HIS levels were accompanied by mitochondrial damage in asthma patients. Previous studies have identified mtDNA as a key DAMP contributing to epithelial injury [28]. Specifically, mt-ND6, a mitochondrially encoded subunit of complex I, has been implicated in inflammatory signaling through its N-formyl peptide derivatives [52]. Therefore, this study evaluated mt-ND6 concentrations in sputum samples. The result revealed significantly elevated mt-ND6 levels in both the MMA and SA groups compared with HC (Fig. 3G). Additionally, HIS levels showed a positive correlation with mt-ND6 expression (r = 0.382, P < 0.001; Fig. 3H), suggesting a mechanistic link between histidine accumulation and mitochondrial perturbation in asthma. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis further confirmed the diagnostic potential of these biomarkers. HIS and mt-ND6 independently distinguished asthma patients from HC, yielding area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.731 and 0.827, respectively. Combined assessment of HIS and mt-ND6 improved diagnostic performance, yielding an AUC of 0.847 (all P < 0.001; Fig. 3I–K, Table S3). In multivariate logistic regression analysis, increased Histidine (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.02-1.12, P = 0.016) and elevated mt-ND6 (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02-1.05, P < 0.001) were identified as independent risk factors for asthma (Table S4). On this basis, multiple linear regression analysis demonstrated that both Histidine and MTND6 were independently and negatively associated with PEF. The overall model was statistically significant (F = 23.332, P < 0.001), explaining approximately 23.1% of the variance in PEF (R2 = 0.231, adjusted R2 = 0.221). Higher levels of Histidine (B = −1.064, β = −0.289, t = −3.669, P < 0.001) and mt-ND6 (B = −0.141, β = −0.276, t = −3.494, P < 0.001) were both significantly associated with lower PEF values (Table S5). Collectively, these findings indicate that elevated histidine and mt-ND6 levels reflect underlying metabolic and mitochondrial perturbations in asthma and may serve as clinically useful biomarkers for disease diagnosis, severity stratification, and treatment response.

TDI exposure significantly increased airway resistance upon methacholine challenge, a response aggravated by histidine supplementation and attenuated by histidine deprivation (Fig. 4B). Total and differential cell counts in BALF revealed increased inflammatory cell infiltration following TDI exposure, characterized by eosinophil and neutrophil recruitment, with the most pronounced inflammatory burden observed in the high-histidine group (Fig. 4C, D and E). This inflammatory phenotype was accompanied by a significant increase in Th2-related cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13), TNF-α, IL-17A, and serum IgE levels. These responses were further amplified by exogenous histidine administration (Fig. 4F), indicating heightened type 2 and mixed granulocytic airway inflammation.

Histidine metabolic reprogramming regulates airway inflammation in HDM + LPS induced severe asthmatic mice

To further validate the role of histidine metabolic reprogramming in airway inflammation and SA pathophysiology, a second murine model was established using BALB/c mice sensitized and challenged with HDM and LPS. Mice received dietary and pharmacological interventions, including a high-histidine or histidine-deficient diet, intratracheal pinocembrin, or l-carnosine supplementation. HDM + LPS exposure significantly increased airway hyperresponsiveness compared with the control group. This functional impairment was further exacerbated by histidine supplementation but significantly attenuated by histidine deprivation, HDC inhibition, or carnosine treatment (Fig. S2A). Cytological and histological analyses revealed extensive airway inflammation in HDM + LPS-induced mice, characterized by macrophage, neutrophil, eosinophil, and lymphocyte infiltration (Fig. S2B–D). These inflammatory responses were significantly attenuated following each intervention. Consistent with cellular findings, serum IgE and BALF cytokines IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IL-17A, and TNF-α were significantly elevated in HDM + LPS mice but suppressed by histidine deprivation, pinocembrin, or l-carnosine supplementation (Fig. S2E and F). Histopathological analyses further revealed that HDM + LPS-induced airway epithelial thickening, mucus hypersecretion, and collagen deposition were significantly alleviated by these treatments (Fig. S2G–I). Collectively, these findings reinforce that histidine metabolic reprogramming is a critical driver of airway inflammation and remodeling in SA. Restoration of metabolic balance, either through inhibition of HDC or supplementation with carnosine, effectively mitigates airway hyperresponsiveness and tissue injury, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the histidine–histamine–carnosine axis in SA management.

Histidine metabolic reprogramming contributes to airway inflammation by regulating mitochondrial oxidative stress and mtDNA release

Building on transcriptomic and clinical findings indicating a close link between histidine metabolism and mitochondrial regulation, we further investigated changes in mitochondrial structure and function. Transmission electron microscopy revealed mitochondrial swelling and loss of cristae in airway epithelial cells of lung tissues from TDI-induced asthmatic mice (Fig. 7A). qPCR analysis showed downregulation of genes associated with mitochondrial biogenesis-related genes (including Nrf-1, Nrf-2, Cox1, and Tfam) (Fig. 7B). Notably, pharmacological inhibition of HDC with pinocembrin or exogenous supplementation of carnosine effectively alleviated these alterations. Moreover, targeted inhibition of HDC or supplementation with carnosine significantly increased TAC and SOD activity and GSH/GSSG ratio while reducing MDA levels (Fig. 7C–F). In vitro, TDI-HSA stimulation of primary human bronchial epithelial cells resulted in decreased cell viability (Fig. 7G), increased LDH release (Fig. 7H), disruption of airway epithelial structural integrity (Fig. 7I), enhanced epithelial permeability (Fig. 7J), and upregulation of epithelial alarmins (IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP) (Fig. 7K), accompanied by reduced expression of barrier-related molecules (ZO-1 and OCCLUDIN) (Fig. 7L). Seahorse metabolic analysis further demonstrated a reduction in basal and maximal respiration, ATP production, and overall respiratory capacity following TDI-HAS exposure (Fig. 7M–Q). MitoSOX and JC-1 staining showed that TDI-HSA increased mitochondrial superoxide production and decreased membrane potential (Fig. 7R–T), while Western blot revealed upregulated NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) expression (Fig. 7U). Notably, targeted inhibition of HDC with Pinocembrin or supplementation with carnosine effectively alleviated these alterations, indicating that histidine metabolism may contribute to airway inflammation in severe asthma by regulating mitochondrial oxidative stress.

Histidine metabolic reprogramming enhances mtDNA release, leading to upregulation of FPR2 expression

Given that mt-ND6 contains an N-formyl peptide-like structure analogous to bacterial peptides and can be released under pathological stimuli such as inflammation to activate downstream signaling pathways [52,54], the expression patterns of formyl peptide receptors (FPRs) in asthma were assessed to identify potential mediators of mtDNA-driven signaling. Transcriptomic analysis of airway epithelial samples revealed significant upregulation of FPR2 in SA compared with HC, whereas FPR1 and FPR3 expression remained unchanged (Fig. 9A–C). Additionally, qPCR analysis of airway tissues from TDI-HSA induced severe asthmatic mice showed a significant upregulation of FPR2 expression, while FPR1 and FPR3 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 9D and E). To determine whether mitochondrial injury and mtDNA release directly drive FPR2 induction, cytosolic mtDNA was selectively degraded using DNase I or its mitochondrial release was inhibited using CsA. Both interventions significantly suppressed TDI-HSA induced FPR2 upregulation at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 9F and G), indicating that mtDNA release is a critical upstream regulator of FPR2 expression in airway epithelial cells. Consistent with these findings, transcriptomic profiling of lung tissues from TDI-induced asthmatic mice likewise revealed a marked upregulation of FPR2, whereas the expression of FPR1 and FPR3 remained largely unchanged (Fig. 9H). Correlation analysis further revealed a significant positive association between FPR2 and HDC expression (Fig. 9I). In addition, TDI-HSA induced FPR2 upregulation was attenuated by pinocembrin or carnosine, whereas exogenous mtDNA further enhanced FPR2 expression and partially reversed these effects (Fig. 9J). Collectively, these findings identify mtDNA release as an important upstream driver of FPR2 upregulation, linking histidine metabolic reprogramming to FPR2 mediated signaling in airway epithelial cells during severe asthma.

FPR2 inhibition attenuates airway inflammation and remodeling in TDI induced SA model

To functionally validate the role of FPR2 signaling in SA pathogenesis, the selective FPR2 antagonist WRW4 was administered through intratracheal instillation to TDI-induced asthmatic mice (Fig. 10A). Pharmacologic inhibition of FPR2 significantly attenuated airway hyperresponsiveness, accompanied by significant reductions in both total and differential inflammatory cell counts in BALF (Fig. 10B–E). WRW4 treatment significantly suppressed TDI-induced elevations in serum IgE and Th2/Th17-associated cytokines, including IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IL-17A, and TNF-α (Fig. 10F). Histopathological analyses further showed significant improvements in TDI-induced peribronchial and perivascular inflammation, mucus hypersecretion, and collagen deposition following WRW4 treatment (Fig. 10G–I). Collectively, these findings identify FPR2 as a key mediator of airway pathology in severe asthma and highlight the mtDNA–FPR2 axis as a potential therapeutic target.

FPR2 inhibition attenuates necroptosis in TDI-induced asthma model

Necroptosis is a caspase-independent, pro-inflammatory form of programmed cell death activated by severe cellular stressors such as DNA damage and inflammatory stimuli [55]. Unlike apoptosis, which maintains membrane integrity, necroptosis promotes inflammation through the release of DAMPs, thereby amplifying tissue injury [56]. Recent studies suggest that necroptosis contributes to asthma pathogenesis [31,32], although the upstream regulatory mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Given the established association between FPR2 signaling and mitochondrial DAMPs, including mtDNA, this study examined the potential role of FPR2 in regulating necroptosis. Transcriptomic correlation analysis of airway epithelial samples from asthma patients (derived from the GEO datasets analyzed in previous bioinformatics analysis above) revealed strong positive correlations between FPR2 expression and key necroptotic mediators, including RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL (Fig. 11A). Consistent with these findings, correlation analysis of metabolomic data from asthmatic mouse lung tissues likewise revealed a significant association between FPR2 and necroptosis-related pathways (Fig. 11B). At the protein level, TDI-HSA exposure significantly increased p-RIPK3 and p-MLKL expression levels in airway epithelial cells (Fig. 11C), indicating activation of the necroptotic pathway. Functional assays further demonstrated that TDI-HSA treatment significantly reduced epithelial cell viability (Fig. 11D) and increased LDH release (Fig. 11E), reflecting membrane disruption characteristic of necroptosis. TUNEL staining together with cleaved caspase-3 immunohistochemistry in lung tissues from TDI-exposed mice further confirmed enhanced apoptosis in airway epithelial cells (Fig. 11F and G). Notably, treatment with the FPR2 antagonist WRW4 significantly reversed these pathogenic effects. To further substantiate this observation, FPR2 expression was silenced in airway epithelial cells using siRNA (Fig. 11H). The in vitro knockdown results closely mirrored those observed in vivo, confirming the same regulatory pattern (Fig. 11I–K). Collectively, these findings suggest that FPR2 activation promotes necroptosis in TDI-induced SA, whereas its inhibition confers cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects by limiting necroptotic cell death.

Necroptosis inhibition attenuates airway inflammation and remodeling in TDI induced SA model

To further delineate the role of necroptosis in asthma pathogenesis, the RIPK3 inhibitor GSK872 was administered to TDI-induced asthmatic mice (Fig. 12A). Pharmacologic inhibition of RIPK3 significantly attenuated airway hyperresponsiveness, as evidenced by reduced lower respiratory resistance to methacholine (Fig. 12B). Consistent with improved airway physiology, total and differential cell counts in BALF revealed significant reductions in eosinophil and neutrophil infiltration following GSK872 administration, while macrophage and lymphocyte counts remained unchanged (Fig. 12C–E). In parallel, GSK872 treatment significantly suppressed TDI-induced elevations in serum IgE and BALF cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IL-17A, and TNF-α) (Fig. 12F). Histopathological analyses further revealed that GSK872 effectively mitigated TDI-induced peribronchial inflammation, goblet cell hyperplasia, and collagen deposition, consistent with reduced inflammation and mucus scores (Fig. 12G–I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that necroptosis is a key driver of airway inflammation, mucus hypersecretion, and structural remodeling in the TDI-induced SA model. Targeted inhibition of RIPK3 mitigates these pathogenic features, thereby highlighting necroptosis as a promising therapeutic target for SA.

FPR2 and necroptosis inhibition attenuates airway inflammation and remodeling in HDM + LPS induced SA model

The roles of FPR2 signaling and necroptosis in SA were further validated using an independent HDM + LPS-induced SA model (Fig. S4A). HDM + LPS exposure significantly increased airway hyperresponsiveness and elevated total inflammatory cell count in BALF, while treatment with the FPR2 antagonist WRW4 or the RIPK3 inhibitor GSK872 significantly reduced both parameters (Fig. S4B–C). Cytospin analyses confirmed significantly decreased macrophages, neutrophils, eosinophils, and lymphocytes infiltration following administration of either inhibitor (Fig. S4D–E). Correspondingly, HDM + LPS-induced elevations in serum IgE and Th2/Th17-related cytokines were significantly suppressed by WRW4 and GSK872 (Fig. S4F). Histological examination further revealed that both WRW4 and GSK872 alleviated epithelial thickening, mucus hypersecretion, and subepithelial collagen deposition (Fig. S4G–I), reflecting significant improvements in airway remodeling. Collectively, these findings indicate that pharmacological inhibition of FPR2 or RIPK3 effectively mitigates airway inflammation, mucus production, and structural remodeling in HDM + LPS-induced severe asthmatic mice, reinforcing a central mechanistic role for the FPR2-necroptosis axis in SA pathogenesis.

Discussion

This study identified a novel pathogenic signaling axis in SA in which histidine metabolic reprogramming induces mitochondrial oxidative stress, mtDNA release, FPR2 activation, and necroptosis, culminating in chronic airway inflammation. By integrating clinical metabolomic profiling with mechanistic in vivo and in vitro analyses, the study establishes histidine as an active pro-inflammatory mediator rather than a secondary metabolic byproduct. The results highlight the histidine–mtDNA–FPR2–necroptosis axis as a central contributor to SA pathogenesis and as a promising therapeutic target.

Clinically, sputum histidine concentrations were positively correlated with asthma severity, airway obstruction, and neutrophilic inflammation. These findings are consistent with previous metabolomic studies reporting elevated histidine levels in serum and sputum from asthma patients [14,15,[57], [58], [59]]. The present study advances this knowledge by providing a mechanistic link between dysregulated histidine metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction. The observed increase in mt-ND6, a mitochondrially encoded OXPHOS subunit, and its strong correlation with sputum histidine levels further support a causal relationship, suggesting that dysregulated histidine metabolism disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis and promotes mitochondrial stress signaling in SA.

Mechanistic analyses revealed that histidine metabolism undergoes a pro-inflammatory reprogramming in SA, characterized by enhanced HDC-mediated histamine synthesis and depletion of carnosine, an endogenous antioxidant and carbonyl scavenger. While histamine has been consistently implicated as an important mediator of airway hyperresponsiveness and inflammation in asthma [60,61], the present study highlights its broader pathogenic role in severe, corticosteroid-resistant asthma. The concurrent reduction in carnosine likely exacerbates mitochondrial oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, thereby increasing susceptibility to mitochondrial damage and facilitating mtDNA leakage [62,63]. This metabolic imbalance exacerbates epithelial injury and airway inflammation. Notably, pharmacologic inhibition of HDC with pinocembrin or carnosine supplementation significantly ameliorated airway hyperresponsiveness, inflammation, and tissue remodeling, underscoring the critical regulatory balance between the histamine and carnosine pathways in maintaining airway homeostasis.

MtDNA, a circular genome encoding key OXPHOS subunits, functions as a potent DAMP when released aberrantly into the cytosol. Under oxidative stress conditions, cytosolic mtDNA fragments engage innate immune receptors, promoting inflammasome formation, type I interferon signaling, and chronic inflammation [20]. Consistent with these biological functions, previous findings have shown that cytosolic mtDNA accumulation was significantly elevated in the TDI-induced SA model and that targeted inhibition of mtDNA release effectively restored mitochondrial homeostasis and suppressed airway inflammation [28]. However, the upstream metabolic regulators of mtDNA efflux remain elusive. This study established histidine metabolic reprogramming as a critical upstream regulator of mtDNA release. The findings revealed that TDI exposure or histidine supplementation promoted mPTP opening, facilitating mtDNA efflux into the cytosol. These findings align with previous reports demonstrating that oxidized mtDNA fragments can be transported through mPTP- and VDAC-dependent channels to activate downstream inflammasome and interferon pathways [53]. Furthermore, the selective upregulation of mt-ND6 among mtDNA-encoded transcripts suggests that histidine-induced metabolic perturbation not only enhances mtDNA release but also alters mitochondrial gene expression, thereby exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction and airway inflammation.

FPRs are a family of G protein-coupled receptors that recognize bacterial and mitochondria-derived formyl peptides, acting as key molecular sentinels linking microbial invasion, tissue injury, and inflammation [64]. Among these receptors, FPR2 has emerged as a context-dependent modulator capable of mediating both pro-inflammatory and pro-resolving signals depending on the ligand milieu [65]. Mitochondrial proteins such as mt-ND6 contain N-formyl methionine residues analogous to those in bacterial formyl peptide. Under mitochondrial stress, proteolytic fragments of mt-ND6 can serve as endogenous FPR2 agonists, thereby activating innate immune responses [52]. Consistent with this mechanistic framework, the present study demonstrated that mtDNA release together with mt-ND6 upregulation acts in concert to activate FPR2 signaling in SA. Transcriptomic analyses of human airway epithelial cell datasets, supported by experimental validation in murine models, revealed selective FPR2 induction, whereas FPR1 and FPR3 remained largely unchanged. Furthermore, inhibition of mtDNA release or enzymatic degradation of cytosolic DNA significantly suppressed FPR2 upregulation, suggesting a causal relationship between mitochondrial stress and FPR2 activation. Functionally, pharmacological inhibition of FPR2 with WRW4 significantly ameliorated airway hyperresponsiveness, inflammatory cytokine production, and airway remodeling in both the TDI- and HDM + LPS-induced SA models. Consistent with our findings, previous clinical studies have reported altered FPR2 signaling in patients with severe asthma, and increased FPR2 expression has been observed in bronchoalveolar lavage macrophages from severe asthma cohorts [66,67]. Collectively, these findings highlight FPR2 as a critical downstream effector of histidine metabolism-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction, suggesting it as a promising therapeutic target in SA.

Necroptosis, a regulated form of necrotic cell death mediated by RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL, has emerged as a key contributor to chronic airway inflammation. Unlike apoptosis, necroptosis compromises plasma membrane integrity, leading to DAMP release that amplifies tissue inflammation. Recent studies have implicated necroptosis in airway epithelial injury, barrier dysfunction, neutrophilic inflammation, and corticosteroid resistance in SA [31,32]. The present study advances this understanding by identifying FPR2 as an upstream activator of necroptotic signaling in the asthmatic airway. Transcriptomic analyses revealed a strong positive correlation between FPR2 expression and necroptosis-related genes, suggesting that mitochondrial stress-derived signals converge on the FPR2-necroptosis axis. Consistent with this regulatory mechanism, pharmacological inhibition of necroptosis significantly reduced airway hyperresponsiveness, cytokine production, and tissue remodeling, confirming its pathogenic role in mediating chronic airway inflammation. Collectively, these findings highlight necroptosis as a key downstream effector through which histidine metabolism–induced mitochondrial dysfunction and FPR2 activation drive chronic airway inflammation, establishing necroptosis signaling as a potential therapeutic target in SA.

Despite the promising findings, this study has several limitations. First, while the TDI and HDM + LPS models have been well established for studying SA, they may not fully capture the complexity and heterogeneity of human asthma endotypes. Additional validation in alternative models, such as IL-13 overexpression or Alternaria-induced airway inflammation, would further strengthen the translational relevance of the findings. Second, this study focused primarily on airway epithelial cell-intrinsic responses; consequently, the contributions of immune cell populations—including macrophages, neutrophils, and innate lymphoid cells—to this metabolic-mitochondrial axis remain to be elucidated and warrant further investigation. Third, the mechanistic conclusions of this study were primarily supported by pharmacological interventions. Although consistent results were obtained using multiple compounds targeting distinct steps of the proposed pathway, potential off-target effects cannot be completely excluded. Future studies employing genetic approaches, such as knockout mouse models, will be important to further validate the causal role of histidine metabolic reprogramming in severe asthma. Finally, although our analyses of patient-derived samples and independent transcriptomic cohorts support the clinical relevance of histidine metabolic reprogramming in severe asthma, the current clinical evidence remains largely cross-sectional. Moreover, our patient cohort was not stratified according to sensitization status or disease etiology, precluding direct evaluation of whether histidine metabolic alterations are shared across distinct severe asthma subtypes. Larger independent cohorts and prospective longitudinal studies with detailed clinical phenotyping will therefore be required to validate these findings, strengthen the link between clinical observations and mechanistic insights, and determine the prognostic value of sputum histidine and mt-ND6 levels for exacerbation risk, disease progression, and therapeutic responsiveness. Furthermore, future translational and interventional studies will be needed to determine whether modulation of the histidine–histamine–carnosine axis can provide therapeutic benefit in severe asthma.

In conclusion, this study established histidine metabolic reprogramming as a key upstream mediator of mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to FPR2-mediated necroptosis and severe airway inflammation. These findings expand current understanding of asthma pathogenesis beyond classical immunologic paradigms and highlight the histidine–mtDNA–FPR2–necroptosis axis as a promising therapeutic target for SA management.
