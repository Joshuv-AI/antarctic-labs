Embolic MINOCA From Mechanical Aortic Valve Thrombosis

Coronary embolism is an uncommon and often underrecognized cause of acute myocardial infarction (MI), accounting for approximately 2% to 3% of cases, and is most frequently associated with atrial fibrillation, cardiomyopathy, or native valvular heart disease.1 Embolic MI originating from mechanical prosthetic valves is rare, and presentation as MINOCA (myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries) is particularly exceptional.

There is a paucity of studies directly linking prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) to prosthetic valve thrombosis. Nevertheless, indirect and mechanistic evidence suggests that PPM may promote prothrombotic conditions through the induction of abnormal transvalvular flow and adverse hemodynamic alterations.2,3

We report a case of distal embolic MI originating from a mechanical aortic valve, in which PPM likely contributed to thrombus formation and increased ischemic susceptibility, illustrating the diagnostic challenges of coronary embolism in patients with mechanical valves.

History of Presentation

A 64-year-old woman presented with acute chest pain lasting several minutes, occurring 9 hours before admission. She reported similar transient episodes over the preceding 3 days.

On admission, blood pressure was 123/76 mm Hg and heart rate 72 bpm. Cardiac auscultation revealed a diminished aortic prosthetic click. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with 2-mm ST-segment elevation and T-wave inversion in septal and apical leads (Figure 1). Past Medical History

She had no conventional cardiovascular risk factors but a history of double mechanical valve replacement (mitral ATS 29 and aortic St Jude 17) performed 17 years earlier for rheumatic heart disease.

Laboratory testing demonstrated elevated troponin I (2,073 ng/L, decreasing to 1,884 ng/L), mild inflammatory response (C-reactive protein 16.7 mg/L), normal renal function, and an international normalized ratio of 2.19.

Management

The patient was managed with therapeutic anticoagulation and underwent redo aortic valve replacement with a larger prosthesis.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postoperatively, transprosthetic gradients improved significantly. At 1-month follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic, and echocardiography showed normal prosthetic valve function with normalized transaortic gradients.

Discussion

Acute MI is defined by the Fourth Universal Definition as clinical and electrographic evidence of acute myocardial ischemia accompanied by a rise and/or fall of cardiac troponin. In the present case, troponin levels peaked at approximately 2,000 ng/L. Although significantly elevated, this relatively modest rise is consistent with a small infarct size reflecting limited myocardial necrosis confined to a restricted territory.

Plaque rupture, microvascular dysfunction, vasospasm, and coronary dissection are potential causes. The patient's low cardiovascular risk, absence of prior angina, dual prothesis replacement, and focal transmural enhancement at the apex were more suggestive of coronary embolism. An autopsy study by Prizel et al4 showed that atherosclerotic infarctions are usually proximal, resulting in larger areas of ischemia often limited to the subendocardium due to collateral circulation. In contrast, embolic infarctions tend to involve distal occlusions, producing smaller, often transmural necrosis in the absence of collaterals.

Although the diagnostic criteria proposed by Shibata et al5 for coronary embolism typically involve multiple coronary territories, the diagnosis in this case was supported by 2 minor criteria: the presence of a major embolic risk factor (mechanical prosthetic valve) and nonculprit vessels showing <25% stenosis. Shibata emphasized that coronary emboli are often transient, dissolving or migrating before coronarography, which explains the absence of angiographic thrombus in a substantial proportion of cases. The patient's intermediate anticoagulation status likely facilitated the transient embolic event. In addition, early initiation of dual antiplatelet therapy and therapeutic enoxaparin may have contributed to rapid embolus resolution before coronary angiography.

Intracoronary imaging and stress-CMR are important to assess alternative causes of MINOCA but were not available in our center. CMR confirmed transmural necrosis, ruling out Takotsubo cardiomyopathy and myocarditis. Microvascular dysfunction typically demonstrates diffuse subendocardial perfusion abnormalities.6 Coronary artery dissection was considered in view of coronary tortuosity on angiography; however, no angiographic features suggestive of dissection including intimal flap, double lumen, contrast staining, or diffuse smooth narrowing were observed.7 The clinical course was rapidly favorable with anticoagulation without recurrence.

The diagnosis of prosthetic obstruction and leaflet restriction was primarily supported by echocardiography showing elevated transprosthetic gradients, reduced dimensionless velocity index, and prolonged acceleration time and was confirmed by coronary computed tomography angiography exhibiting associated thrombus. Fluoroscopy suggested asymmetric leaflet motion of the mechanical aortic prosthesis but was considered complementary given its angle-dependent limitations. Overall, these findings support embolization originating from the dysfunctional prosthetic valve.

In this patient, prosthetic valve thrombosis was likely driven predominantly by prosthesis-related factors rather than subtherapeutic anticoagulation. Although direct clinical evidence remains limited, Levesque et al8 support a mechanistic link between PPM and prosthetic valve thrombosis. The small aortic prosthesis size with chronically elevated gradients likely generated turbulent flow and localized stasis, which favor platelet activation promoting thrombus formation.9 In addition, PPM may have indirectly increased ischemic vulnerability by reducing coronary flow reserve (CFR), increasing LV wall stress, and lowering the ischemic threshold, thereby amplifying the necrotic impact of a small distal embolic event.10 In fact, Pibarot et al11 demonstrated that CFR impairment is more closely tied to the effective orifice area after surgery than to LV mass alone. Hence, persistent PPM may significantly attenuate CFR improvement following valve replacement, potentially perpetuating myocardial ischemia.

From a therapeutic perspective, contemporary practice emphasizes optimization of anticoagulation as the initial therapy, while the need for surgical intervention is guided by hemodynamic status, severity of prosthetic obstruction, and embolic risk. In the present case, although the patient was hemodynamically stable, the presence of significant prosthetic obstruction and a documented embolic event supported an early surgical strategy during hospitalization.

The present case illustrates a rare intersection of mechanical prosthetic thrombosis and MINOCA due to embolism and highlights the need for further systematic research to clarify the role of chronic PPM in thromboembolic events.

Conclusions

We described an uncommon presentation of embolic MINOCA in a patient with mechanical aortic prosthesis dysfunction. Beyond anticoagulation status, prosthesis-related factors such as a small valve size and PPM may contribute to abnormal flow dynamics and increased thrombotic risk, as well as heightened myocardial ischemic vulnerability. Key Message

In MINOCA with mechanical valves, assess coronary embolism and assess prosthetic function carefully. Early surgery may be required in obstructive cases.
