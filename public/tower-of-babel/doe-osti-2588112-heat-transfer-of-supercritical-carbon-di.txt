EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This technical report describes measurements performed at the National Energy Technology
Laboratory’s (NETL) Heat Exchanger Experimental Test (HEET) facility located in Morgantown,
WV, using supercritical carbon dioxide (sCO2) as the working fluid. Testing utilized 3D-printed
tubes with internal ribs and yttria stabilized zirconia (YSZ) exterior coatings that were employed
in the heat exchanger. Heat transfer and friction factor enhancements from the internal ribs were
characterized over a range of flow conditions. Measured thermal performance factors ranged from
1-1.6, which were greater than other previously studied 3D-printed tubes. Additionally, the thermal
resistance from the YSZ coating was characterized for both a freshly applied coating and a coating
subjected to thermal aging.

1. INTRODUCTION
The direct supercritical carbon dioxide (sCO2) power cycle is a technology that has the potential
to outperform current state-of-the-art power systems with carbon capture and storage (CCS) on a
capital cost ($/kW) and a cost-of-electricity basis (Weiland and White, 2019). The direct sCO2
power cycle, also called the Allam or Allam-Fetvedt cycle, can achieve high-efficiency power
conversion with inherent carbon capture using high-pressure CO2. As shown in Figure 1, CO2 and
water are extracted from this semi-closed power cycle, and a significant fraction of the CO2 is
recirculated to dilute the oxy-fuel combustor for temperature control. A significant amount of heat
is recovered from the turbine exhaust and used to preheat the recycled CO 2. The turbine extracts
power from the high-temperature gases produced in the combustor, and this turbine block is the
focus for this report. Thermal management of the turbine components is critical for achieving the
high efficiency and low cost-of-electricity in previous techno-economic studies (Weiland and
White, 2019).

 Figure 1: Schematic of the direct-fired sCO2 power cycle (Searle et al., 2024).

In a previous study, Searle et al. (2024) developed a simplified model to screen cooling
technologies for sCO2 turbine applications. One of the pathway technologies identified in this
previous study included a combination of thermal barrier coatings and internal cooling passages
with heat transfer enhancements. The objective of the current study is to fabricate an additively
manufactured lab-scale test article with internal heat transfer enhancements with and without
thermal barrier coatings to assess the thermal performance. In addition, the durability of the
thermal barrier coating material will be investigated by accelerated thermal cycling to determine
whether the thermal performance of the thermal barrier coating changes.

2. TUBE DESCRIPTION
The National Energy Technology Laboratory’s (NETL) Heat Exchanger Experimental Test
(HEET) facility, located in Morgantown, WV, consists of a shell and tube heat exchanger with
sCO2 as the working fluid (Black et al., 2020). Heat flows from the high-temperature annular sCO2
in the shell, through the 3D-printed tube, and into the lower temperature sCO2 in the tube. The
shell and tube flows are each characterized by steady-state temperature and pressure measurements
at the respective inlets and outlets, along with the respective mass flow rates.
Various tube designs have been tested in the HEET facility (Black et al., 2020; Searle et al., 2020).
In the present study, a 3D-printed Inconel 718 tube was tested. This tube had a round exterior of
12.7 mm in diameter and a square inner channel with angled ribs extending into the flow from the
walls of the inner channel. Details of the tube geometry are shown in Figure 2. The entire tube was
assembled from five tube sections with ribbed features plus two featureless extensions. The tube
sections were orbitally welded together. The first and last of the sections with features included
round-to-square and square-to-round transitions, respectively, over a length of 17.1 mm.
Coated and uncoated tubes were tested in the current work. The coated tubes had an yttria-
stabilized zirconia (YSZ) material deposited over the length of each tube section, except for a
1-inch gap at each section’s end to allow for welding. In this study, the unmodified tube will be
referred to as the “uncoated” tube, the coated but unaged tube as the “coated” tube, and the coated
and aged tube as the “aged” tube.
The shell consisted of a stainless-steel tube 1 inch in outer diameter with 0.12 inch wall thickness.
Insulating wrap encased the shell along the length of the heat exchanger to reduce heat loss to the
room. Specific parameters of the experimental setup are shown in Table 1.

3. DATA ANALYSIS
This section documents the data analysis steps required to extract relevant heat transfer
enhancement and frictional loss metrics from the experimental data. These techniques have been
used in several previous publications to extract similar parameters (Black et al., 2020; Searle et
al., 2020).

3.1 HEAT TRANSFER ENHANCEMENT
The heat transfer enhancement was obtained from the slope of a Wilson plot (Fernández-Seara et
al., 2007). A Wilson plot graphs the total thermal resistance for heat transfer through a pipe versus
the theoretical thermal resistance at the surface of interest. The total thermal resistance was
obtained from Equation 1. In Equation 1 a temperature difference was calculated as the log-mean
temperature difference across the tube wall, and the total heat transfer was calculated from the
enthalpy difference along the length of the tube. CoolProp (Bell et al., 2014) or Refprop (Lemmon
et al., 2018) were used to obtain enthalpies from the measured inlet and outlet temperatures and
pressures. The theoretical thermal resistance at the inner surface of the tube was calculated
according to Equation 2. In Equation 2 the convective heat transfer coefficient was calculated from
the Nusselt number (Nu), fluid thermal conductivity, and a characteristic dimension of the tube. In
this work the hydraulic diameter of the tube was used as the characteristic dimension. The Nusselt
number was obtained using the Dittus-Boelter (DB) correlation shown in Equation 3, where the
Reynolds (Re) number and Prandtl numbers (Pr) were calculated at the average temperature and
pressure along the tube length.

3.3 THERMAL PERFORMANCE FACTOR
The heat transfer and friction factor metrics were then combined into a single parameter according
to Equation 7. This metric is termed the thermal performance factor (TPF). The thermal
performance factor provides a measure of the heat transfer enhancement benefit per cost of
frictional pressure loss. The values for these tubes ranged from 1 to 1.75, as shown in Figure 8.
Compared to other experimental data for conventional and additively manufactured (AM) tubes
from Searle et al. (2020), the current tubes have noticeably higher thermal performance factors in
the range of Reynolds numbers studied. The thermal performance factors of the current tubes also
differ from previous studies in that the current tubes exhibit an increase in thermal performance
factor with increasing Reynolds number, whereas previously studied tubes had the opposite, or a
nearly constant, trend.

3.4 THERMAL RESISTANCE
Total thermal resistance across the tube is shown in Error! Reference source not found. for the
uncoated, coated, and aged tubes. The uncoated tube was found to have a lower resistance than the
other tubes, which was expected due to the lack of coating. The coated and aged tubes were found
to have indistinguishable thermal resistance, indicating that the aging process had no significant
effect on the YSZ thermal barrier coating.
The thermal aging process consisted of inserting and removing the tubes from a tube furnace. The
tube furnace was held at a steady temperature of 1050 °C. An argon bath was used in the furnace
to reduce oxidization of the YSZ coating. The tubes were inserted into the furnace for 50 minutes
and then were allowed to cool outside of the furnace under a benchtop fan for 10 minutes per
thermal cycle. The tubes were subjected to 70 thermal cycles over a 2-week period.

A stack up of the theoretical thermal resistance for the convective heat transfers on the shell and
tube sides, as well as the thermal resistance through the tube wall, for the coated and uncoated
tubes are shown in Figures 11 and 12. These theoretical resistances were calculated using the
Dittus-Boelter correlation to obtain convective heat transfer coefficients. The measured overall
resistance, shown plotted on top of the stack ups, falls below the sum of the theoretical thermal
resistances. This was expected due to the decrease in thermal resistance created by the heat transfer
enhancement of the tube. However, the measurements were not expected to fall below the sum of
the shell side convective resistance and tube conductive resistance. This was observed at larger
Reynolds numbers. This discrepancy indicates that some heat transfer enhancement may have
occurred on the shell side of the tube which was not accounted for in this analysis. This shell side
heat transfer enhancement may be of interest to future work.

4. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
This report summarizes experiments performed at the HEET facility at NETL in Morgantown,
WV, using 3D-printed Inconel 718 tubes with angled internal ribs and external YSZ thermal barrier
coatings. Heat transfer and friction factor enhancements relative to theoretical correlations were
measured for single-pass counterflow heat exchange with sCO2 as the working fluid. The tubes
were found to have comparable heat transfer enhancements, ranging from 2-4. Lower friction
factor enhancements (8-15), relative to previously studied tubes, led to higher thermal performance
factors (1-1.6) than the previously studied tubes.
