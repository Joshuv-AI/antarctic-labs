CXCL14 Enhances Myocyte Fusion and Activates the FAK-ERK5 Signaling Pathway in Skeletal Muscle Stem Cells and Myoblasts
Authors: Nurkyz Alymkulova, Younjeong Oh, Jennifer Fransisca, Bagus Sarmito, Yongsung Hwang, Jeong Kyo Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skeletal muscle regeneration is essential for maintaining muscle function throughout life and is regulated by numerous growth factors, cytokines and their associated signaling pathways. In this study, we investigated the role of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 14 (CXCL14) in myogenic differentiation using skeletal muscle stem cells (MuSC) and the mouse myoblast C2C12 cell line. Notably, CXCL14 significantly enhanced myocyte fusion. To elucidate the underlying regulatory mechanisms, we examined fusion-associated signaling pathways and found that CXCL14 activates the focal adhesion kinase–extracellular regulated protein kinase 5 (FAK-ERK5) signaling pathway. Our findings identify the CXCL14-FAK-ERK5 signaling axis as a positive regulator of myocyte fusion. Furthermore, by establishing fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) as the primary source of CXCL14 and demonstrating its pro-fusion activity, this study provides new insight into the paracrine regulation from FAPs to MuSCs during muscle regeneration.

Introduction

The primary functions of skeletal muscle include locomotion, postural control, and respiration (1). The maintenance of functional skeletal muscle relies on its remarkable capacity for regeneration (1). Muscle regeneration is a complex process that primarily depends on nascent muscle formation from skeletal muscle stem cells (MuSCs), also known as satellite cells, coordinated by interactions with other muscle resident cells. MuSCs reside on the surface of myofibers in healthy muscles, where they remain largely quiescent but become activated in response to injury. Upon activation, MuSCs proliferate and give rise to myocytes and subsequently myofibers (1).

In recent years, fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), a muscle-resident population of mesenchymal stromal cells, have emerged as key regulators of skeletal muscle regeneration and muscle mass (2, 3). Notably, ablation of FAPs in skeletal muscle followed by an injury showed delayed muscle regeneration (3). Additionally, ablation of FAPs in uninjured muscles leads to severe atrophy in mice (3). Recent single-cell transcriptome analyses have identified two major FAP subpopulations in resting, non-injured muscle, characterized by expression of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 14 (Cxcl14) and dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (Dpp4), respectively (3).

Chemokines are small signaling proteins that primarily regulate immune cell behavior but are also involved in skeletal muscle regeneration and homeostasis (4). Although CXCL14 is predominantly expressed in FAPs, its role in skeletal muscle has remained poorly understood. A recent study has identified CXCL14 as a positive regulator of skeletal muscle mass (5), while others have reported a negative role in myogenesis (6). In this study, we demonstrate that CXCL14 promotes myocyte fusion via activation of the focal adhesion kinase-extracellular regulated protein kinase 5 (FAK-ERK5) signaling pathway during myogenic differentiation, revealing a novel function of FAP-derived CXCL14 in regulating MuSCs.

Materials and Methods

Mice and cell culture

All mouse experiments were approved by the Soonchunhyang University Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility on a 12 hours light/dark cycle and fed a standard chow diet. MuSCs were isolated from the gastrocnemius muscle of 6 to 8-week-old male ICR (Institute for Cancer Research) mice by enzymatic digestion. Cells were cultured in BIO-AMP-2 medium (Biological Industries, 01-194-1A) on collagen type I-coated culture dishes. Fibroblasts were removed using a pre-plating method, in which cells were plated onto uncoated culture dishes for 10 minutes. To induce myogenic differentiation, cells were cultured for 6 days in differentiation medium consisting of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Corning 10-013-CVRC) supplemented with 2% heat-inactivated horse serum. FAP cells were isolated from 6 to 8-week-old male ICR mice hindlimb muscles using enzymatic dissociation followed by magnetic-activated cell sorting for PDGFRα＋ Sca1＋CD31−CD45−cells. Cells were used for experiments immediately after isolation.

C2C12 mouse myoblast cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Cells were maintained in growth media consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Myogenic differentiation was induced by culturing cells for 4 days in differentiation medium consisting of DMEM supplemented with 2% heat-inactivated horse serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Recombinant mouse CXCL14 (R&D Systems, 730-XC-025) was used at concentrations and for durations as indicated in the figure legends.

RNA isolation, droplet digital PCR, quantitative real-time PCR, and single-cell RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using a ProtoScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (BioLab, E63006) with random primer. Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) was performed using ddPCR EvaGreen Supermix (Bio-Rad, 1864034) on the Bio-Rad QX200 Droplet Digital PCR System. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using PerfeCTa SYBR Green FastMix (Quanta, 013940) on the Bio-Rad Real-time PCR System.

For single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), single cells were isolated from whole leg muscles in 9-week-old male C57BL/6J mice and encapsulated into droplets using the 10x Chromium Single Cell Controller (10x Genomics), targeting 10,000 cells per mouse. ScRNA-seq libraries were generated using the NextGEM Single Cell 3'Library & Gel Bead Kit v3.1 (10x Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform to a depth of approximately 500 million reads per library. Raw sequencing data were processed using the Cell Ranger analysis pipeline (10x Genomics), including alignment to mouse reference genome (mm10, GENCODE vM23/Ensembl 98) and generation of gene-cell count matrices. Processed data were analyzed and visualized in Loupe Browser (10x Genomics) for clustering and marker gene expression. Clusters were consolidated into ten groups using K-means clustering (k=10) and annotated based on canonical marker gene expression. Expression of genes of interest was subsequently examined across annotated cell types in Loupe Browser.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in freshly prepared 1x Laemmli buffer. Proteins were separated by 8% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Amersham Hybond). Membranes were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃ followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature. Primary anti-phospho-p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK; T180/Y182) (3D7, CST, 9215S), anti-p38 MAPK (CST, 9212), anti-phospho-Erk5 (Thr218/Tyr220) (CST, 3371L), anti-Erk5 (CST, 3372S), anti-β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, A5441), anti-paired box protein 7 (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, DSHB), anti-myoblast determination protein 1 (MyoD) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 32758), anti-myogenic factor 5 (C-20, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 302), anti-myogenin (MYOG) (5FD, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 52903), anti-sarcomere myosin heavy chain (MF-20), anti-myosin heavy chain type I (BA-D5, DSHB), type IIA (SC-71, DSHB), type IIB (BF-F3, DSHB). Secondary antibodies were HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (CST, 7074) and anti-mouse IgG (CST, 7076). Protein signals were detected using Amersham ECLTM 600 (GE Healthcare).

Histology, immunofluorescence staining and EdU incorporation assay

Tibialis anterior (TA) skeletal muscles of 8-week-old ICR male mice were harvested, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Tissue sections were incubated with primary and secondary antibodies and mounted using Fluoroshield containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma, F6057). Primary antibodies included anti-CXCL14 (Invitrogen, PA5106402) and anti-PDGFRα (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 398206). Secondary antibodies were Alexa Fluor 488-Donkey anti-rabbit (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, 711-545-152) and Cy3-Donkey anti-mouse (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, 715-165-150).

Cultured cells were fixed, permeabilized, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃. Primary antibodies used were anti-phospho-FAK (Invitrogen, 700255), anti-vinculin (7FD, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 73614), anti-MYOG (5FD, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 52903), and anti-sarcomere myosin heavy chain (MF-20, DSHB, University of Iowa). Secondary antibodies included Cy3-donkey-anti-mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, 711-165-150), Alexa Fluor 488-donkey-anti-rabbit IgG (Jackson 711-545-152), and Alexa Fluor 647-donkey-anti-mouse IgG (Jackson 715-605-150). F-actin was visualized using Alexa Fluor 568 Phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A12380), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

EdU incorporation was assessed using a Click-iTTM EdU Imaging kit (Invitrogen, C10337) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Exponentially growing myoblasts were labeled with EdU (10 μM in 250 μL of growth medium) for 1 hour for MuSCs and 30 minutes for C2C12 cells.

Images were acquired using a Nikon DS-i2 digital SLR camera attached to a Nikon Eclipse inverted microscope or a Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscope and processed using ImageJ and ZEN imaging software (Carl Zeiss). Myogenic differentiation indices were calculated as the number of nuclei in the marker-positive cells divided by the total number of nuclei. Myocyte fusion indices were calculated as the percentage of myosin heavy chain (MyHC)–positive myofiber distribution based on the nuclei number inside each myofiber. The myofibers were categorized as follows: mononuclear, 2∼4 nuclei, and more than 5 nuclei. Corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF) was calculated as integrated cell density–(area of selected cell×mean fluorescence of background readings) for quantification of membrane-localized phosphorylated FAK (p-FAK).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least two independent replicates. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software). Data are presented as mean±standard error the mean. Statistical significance between two groups was determined using two-tailed Student’s t-test. p-values are indicated as *p<0.05, **p<0.01, and ***p<0.001, respectively.

Results

FAPs are the predominant source of CXCL14 in skeletal muscle

ScRNA sequencing was performed to examine gene expression profiles in FAPs in normal skeletal muscle. Cxcl14 expression was found to be highly enriched in FAPs (Fig. 1A) consistent with a previous report. Next, we isolated MuSCs (also known as satellite cells) and FAP from mouse hindlimb muscle and analyzed Cxcl14 expression in these cell populations by ddPCR analysis. Cxcl14 expression was significantly higher in FAPs (Fig. 1B). These findings were further supported by immunofluorescence staining analysis, showing co-staining of FAP-specific marker, PDGFRα, and CXCL14 proteins in FAP cells within TA skeletal muscle (Fig. 1C). Taken together, these results strongly suggest that CXCL14 secreted from FAPs may act as a paracrine factor to regulate MuSCs during muscle regeneration.

CXCL14 significantly enhances myocyte fusion

We first examined the effect of CXCL14 on MuSCs proliferation and differentiation. MuSCs were cultured in the presence or absence of CXCL14 for 24 and 48 hours and subjected to EdU incorporation assay. CXCL14 treatment significantly increased MuSC proliferation (Supplementary Fig. S1A); however, qRT-PCR analysis of cell cycle-related genes revealed no significant difference between control and CXCL14-treated cells (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

To assess myogenic differentiation, MuSCs were cultured in differentiation medium with or without CXCL14 for up to 6 days. Differentiation indices were assessed at day 2 by myogenin (MyoG), and at day 6 by MyHC immunostaining (Supplementary Fig. S2A). At both time points, differentiation indices were comparable between CXCL14-treated and control cells. Consistently, Western blot analysis showed no significant changes in the expression of myogenic differentiation markers following CXCL14 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Despite having no detectable effect on myogenic differentiation, CXCL14 significantly reduced the portion of mononuclear myocytes while increasing the number of myotubes containing more than five nuclei, indicating enhanced myocyte fusion in MuSC cultures (Fig. 2A). A similar increase in myocyte fusion was observed in C2C12 cells at day 4 as assessed by MyHC immunostaining (Fig. 2B).

CXCL14 may increase myocyte fusion through the FAK-ERK5 pathway

To investigate the mechanism underlying CXCL14-induced myocyte fusion, we first assessed the expression of key myofusion-related genes by ddPCR. No significant changes were observed in the expression of myofusion-related genes (Fig. 3A). We next examined candidate signaling pathways involved in myocyte fusion including p38 MAPK (7) and ERK5 (8). CXCL14 treatment markedly increased ERK5 phosphorylation (Fig. 3B).

Because ERK5 is one of the downstream targets of FAK (9) and activation of FAK facilitates cell-cell junction during myogenesis (10), we examined activation of FAK by CXCL14 treatment. Differentiating C2C12 cells were stained for p-FAK and other cellular structures, followed by quantification of membrane-localized p-FAK using CTCF. CXCL14 treatment significantly increased membrane localization of p-FAK in differentiating C2C12 myocytes (Fig. 3C). Collectively, these results suggest that CXCL14 enhances myocyte fusion through activation of the FAK-ERK5 signaling pathway.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrate that the chemokine CXCL14 is predominantly produced by FAPs in skeletal muscle. It is important to note that the conventional scRNA sequencing approach used in this study has inherent limitations in capturing multinucleated myofibers. Therefore, although our analysis identifies FAPs as a major source of CXCL14, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that myofibers also contribute to CXCL14 production.

CXCL14 enhances myocyte fusion without exerting discernible effects on the myogenic differentiation of MuSCs or C2C12 myoblasts. Activation of the FAK-ERK5 signaling pathway by CXCL14 suggests that this pathway mediates its pro-fusion effects. FAK is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase that plays a critical role in regulating cell adhesion, migration, and survival (11). Notably, conditional deletion of FAK in MuSCs markedly impairs muscle regeneration following injury and leads to the formation of myofibers with reduced diameters (12). Consistently, primary myogenic cells lacking FAK are defective in fusion, but retain differentiation capacity, indicating that FAK is essential for proper myocyte fusion, but not for myogenic differentiation (12).

ERK5 has also been identified as a key signaling pathway involved in myocyte fusion (8), and its activation has been reported to depend on FAK signaling (9). Furthermore, inhibition of ERK5 activity has minimal effects on the expression of MyoD, MEF2, and other myogenic differentiation markers (8). Previous studies have also demonstrated that myoblast differentiation can be mechanistically separated from myoblast fusion (10). For example, pharmacological inhibition of p38 suppresses both myogenic differentiation and myocyte fusion, whereas the promyogenic factor IGF-1 rescues differentiation, but not fusion.

Collectively, these findings suggest that myogenic differentiation and myocyte fusion are regulated by distinct, and at least partially uncoupled, mechanisms. In this context, our results establish a CXCL14-FAK-ERK signaling axis that specifically regulates myocyte fusion without affecting myogenic differentiation. We further suggest that FAP-derived CXCL14 promotes myocyte fusion through this pathway during muscle regeneration. Further in vitro and in vivo studies are warranted to validate this mechanism.

How CXCL14 transmits the signal upstream of FAK remains unclear. Previous studies have reported that CXCL14 interacts with a variety of receptors in a cell context-dependent manner. CXCL14 inhibits CXCL12 (SDF1) signaling through CXCR4 in peripheral mononuclear cells (13), and the insulin-like growth factor receptor 1 (IGF-1R) is identified as a CXCL14 receptor in human embryonic stem cells (14). In addition, CXCL14 has been reported to interact with atypical chemokine receptor 2 in breast (15) and non-small cell lung cancer cells (16), G-protein-coupled receptor 85 in mammary fibroblasts (17), and more recently, low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) in immune cells (18). To date, none of these receptors has been directly investigated for its role in mediating CXCL14-enhanced myocyte fusion. Notably, activation of LRP1 by lactoferrin has been shown to promote myotube hypertrophy in vitro (19), raising the possibility that LRP1 may mediate the pro-fusion effects of CXCL14. Consistent with this hypothesis, FAK activation is reduced in LRP1-deficient cells (20), suggesting that CXCL14 may activate FAK-ERK5 signaling via LRP1.

Although we did not observe any apparent effects of CXCL14 on myogenic differentiation in MuSCs and C2C12 cells, a recent study reported that Cxcl14 gene knockdown accelerates myogenic differentiation in C2C12 cells, and muscle regeneration in mice, identifying CXCL14 as a negative regulator of myogenesis (6). Given that Cxcl14 is predominantly expressed in FAPs and likely function as a paracrine factor acting on MuSCs and myogenic cells, a Cxcl14 gene knockdown strategy limited to C2C12 cells may not accurately reflect the paracrine action of CXCL14. In contrast, lentiviral delivery of Cxcl14 siRNA in vivo may target multiple cell types at injury site, including FAPs, making the reported negative regulatory effects of CXCL14 on muscle regeneration is particularly intriguing. Although not extensively examined in the present study, we observed that CXCL14 promotes MuSC proliferation. Initial analyses of cell cycle-related gene expression did not reveal clear mechanistic insights. Therefore, more definitive approaches such as MuSC- or FAP-specific Cxcl14 gene deletion will be necessary to delineate the precise, cell type-specific role of CXCL14 during muscle regeneration.
