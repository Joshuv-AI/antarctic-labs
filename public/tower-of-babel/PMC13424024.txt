Fast Progressors in Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma: A 5-Year Visual Field Index-Based Classification in a Real-World Cohort
Authors: Chengjie Feng, Jing Sun, Tingting Liu, Jingjing Zhao, Xiang Fan, Jing Hong, Lingling Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe purpose of this study was to investigate the effect of visual field index (VFI) of identifying especially “fast visual field (VF) progressors” in patients with primary open angle glaucoma (POAG) and to analyze the risk factors.MethodsFast progression was defined as VFI decline worse than −4.6%/year or mean deviation (MD) worse than −1 decibel (dB)/year. Moderately rapid progression (MRP) was defined using cohort-derived cutoffs (VFI worse than −1.016%/year; MD worse than −0.44 dB/year). Proportions of fast progression and MRP were compared between VFI- and MD-based criteria and across baseline disease stages. Longitudinal VFI change was further analyzed as a continuous outcome using linear mixed-effects models (LMM) with random intercepts and slopes for eyes nested within patients.ResultsA total of 162 participants (315 eyes) were included; 94 (58.02%) were male and 68 (41.98%) were female. VFI indicated a fast progression rate of 0.92% and MRP rate of 20.63%, whereas MD yielded 2.75% and 23.49%, respectively. The proportion of eyes classified as VFI-defined MRP increased markedly with advancing disease stage (p < 0.001). MD-confirmed MRP showed a concordant stage-dependent trend but did not reach statistical significance. In multivariable analysis, worse baseline MD, thinner central corneal thickness, lower spherical equivalent, and older age were independently associated with a steeper annual VFI decline.ConclusionsIn this long-term treated POAG cohort, VFI-defined fast progression was rare; however, VFI-based criteria provided statistically significant stage-dependent discrimination of clinically relevant progression. The results support incorporating longitudinal VFI modeling into routine-care datasets to better identify eyes at higher risk of meaningful functional loss over time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01417-x.Keywords: Glaucoma, Open-angle, Visual field tests, Disease progression, Linear models, Longitudinal studies

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) is a chronic, largely asymptomatic disease; identifying eyes with fast functional deterioration is essential to prevent irreversible vision loss.In real-world cohorts, “fast progression” is often defined using MD-based criteria, whereas the clinical profile and stage-dependent performance of visual field index (VFI)-defined fast/rapid progression criteria are less clearly characterized.To report fast progression rate on the basis of VFI in a real-world cohort under standard treatment, to compare the fast progression rate between POAG and normal tension glaucoma (NTG), and to investigate which baseline factors predict faster long-term VFI decline.What was learned from the study?VFI-defined fast progression rate was 0.92%. VFI captured stage-dependent moderately rapid progression, with VFI-only MRP increasing with advancing baseline stage (p < 0.001).Using all longitudinal VFI measurements in linear mixed-effects models, older age and worse baseline functional damage were associated with steeper VFI loss; thinner central corneal thickness (CCT) and refractive status (spherical equivalent) also showed independent associations, while mean intraocular pressure (IOP) did not remain independently associated after multivariable adjustment in this treated cohort.

Introduction

Glaucoma is the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, affecting nearly 80 million people [1, 2]. Approximately 60–70% of these patients were diagnosed with open angle glaucoma (OAG). The prevalence of OAG is 2.1% among Chinese individuals older than 50 years [3, 4]. Primary open angle glaucoma (POAG) typically has a gradual onset with slow deterioration of the optic nerve and visual field. Therefore, by the time the symptoms appear, the disease is usually at a moderate or advanced stage, with blindness rates exceeding 15–20% [5, 6]. Thus, visual field (VF) testing is critical for both diagnosis and progression assessment.

Visual field severity was usually assessed using mean deviation (MD) and visual field index (VFI). MD reflects the overall depression of visual field sensitivity, whereas VFI represents the percentage of remaining visual field function. Assessing the whole central VF defects points equally, however, MD lacks specificity in the evaluation of glaucoma VF defects [7, 8]. MD, based on total deviation values from Humphrey perimetry, can be influenced by other factors such as cataract progression. In contrast, the VFI uses pattern deviation data and assigns greater weight to central points, thereby reducing the influence of media opacities. This makes VFI more sensitive to glaucomatous damage.

Numerous cohort studies have investigated glaucoma prognosis. More recently, attention has turned to the especially “rapid” progression of VF defects in glaucoma by using MD. Jackson et al. (2023) defined “fast progression” as a decline in MD worse than −1 dB per year [9].

The above threshold is clinically important because even patients with early stage glaucoma who show such rapid deterioration are likely to reach advanced stages within a decade and face blindness within another 10 years.

Although some patients do not meet “fast progression” criteria, the VF decline rates of this population are still faster than average progression rate. This clinical oversight shows the necessity of defining a “moderately rapid progression” category for improved risk stratification.

While previous longitudinal studies and clinical trials have primarily relied on MD slopes or event-based MD criteria to define progression, limited data are available regarding the distribution and clinical profile of VFI-defined fast progressors in a real-world cohort.

Aptel et al. (2015) reported MD change of −0.54 dB/year (or −2.35% /year in VFI) in advanced OAG [10]. Given the correlation between MD and VFI, a decline of worse than −1 dB/year in MD (“fast progression” defined by Jackson et al. (2023) [9]) corresponds to a VFI reduction of more than −4.6% per year, which could also be regarded as indicative of fast progression.

Previous landmark trials compared overall visual field progression in normal tension glaucoma (NTG) and POAG using MD-based or event-based endpoints, without specifically characterizing the subset of fast progressors or using VFI-based definitions [11, 12]. Later cohort studies of treated glaucoma reported risk factors for field loss but likewise did not distinguish fast progressors within POAG versus NTG [13, 14].

Therefore, this long-term real world cohort study aimed to investigate the effect of VFI of identifying especially “fast visual field progressors” in patients with POAG and to analyze the risk factors.

Methods

Study Population

This study was a single-center longitudinal study encompassing patients diagnosed with POAG from 2004 to 2020 at Peking University Third Hospital Department of Ophthalmology. All patients underwent examination and diagnosis by two independent glaucoma specialists.

Inclusion criteria: All of the patients diagnosed with POAG at our center between 2004 and 2020 who received standard treatment for POAG and were followed up for a minimum duration of 5 years and underwent a minimum of five visual field examinations.

A diagnosis of primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) was made according to the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma Preferred Practice Pattern (PPP) [15]. It required: (1) an open anterior chamber angle on gonioscopy; (2) characteristic glaucomatous optic neuropathy (abnormal cup-to-disc ratio, optic disc hemorrhage, or nerve fiber layer defect, etc.); and (3) a typical glaucomatous visual field defect concordant with the structural damage. Secondary causes of open-angle glaucoma (e.g., pseudoexfoliation syndrome, pigment dispersion syndrome, uveitis, trauma/angle recession, and corticosteroid-related glaucoma) were excluded [13].

Exclusion criteria: a diagnosis of intraocular diseases other than POAG (corneal diseases, retinal diseases etc.) or other ocular diseases; a best-corrected visual acuity of less than 0.5; a follow-up period of less than 5 years; fewer than five VF examinations and eight clinical visits; an inability to perform VF tests or unreliable VF test results; having undergone myopic surgery; very high myopia with SE < −8.0 Diopter (D) [16, 17].

Patients diagnosed with POAG were divided into two types: (1) POAG with elevated IOP > 21 mmHg, which will be referred to as POAG in the narrower sense in this article [18]; and (2) normal tension glaucoma (NTG), whose intraocular pressure (IOP) was never beyond the normal limit (≤ 21 mmHg).

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital [IRB no.: IRB00006761-M2022308] and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Information collection

The study collected patient medical information through two distinct methods: paper medical records and the hospital’s electronic medical record system.

Clinical History Documentation: Clinical histories were systematically collected for all enrolled patients during their initial visit through structured interviews. Data acquisition encompassed: demographic parameters (gender, age); POAG-specific history (diagnosis timeline, IOP records of each follow-up visit (via Goldmann applanation tonometry (GAT)), IOP-lowering medication regimens, prior glaucoma surgeries); coexisting ocular pathologies (cataract, refractive errors); systemic comorbidities (hypertension, hypotension, cardiovascular/cerebrovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, psychiatric disorders); behavioral factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption patterns); and familial predisposition to glaucoma.

Basic ophthalmic examination: All patients underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examinations, including IOP, spherical equivalent, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), and findings from slit-lamp biomicroscopy. The methodologies used for assessing OAG conditions were consistent with our previous practices [19, 20].

Specialized ophthalmic examinations: All baseline factors were evaluated at the time of baseline visit. Central corneal thickness (CCT) was determined using ultrasound pachymeter. Baseline IOP was recorded as the highest pretreatment measurement obtained via GAT. Analysis of follow-up IOP was summarized at the timepoint of 5 years. Optic nerve structural integrity such as vertical cup-to-disc ratio (VCDR), peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer thickness, and disc hemorrhage (DH) were assessed using fundus photographs.

Treatment experiences: Changes in treatment during the follow-up period were documented, including adjustments in medication (both changes in drug type and dosage, referred to as the “drug change” variable), whether the patient received laser treatment (such as selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT) or other types, indicated by the “laser treatment” variable), and any glaucoma surgeries performed (denoted as the “surgery” variable).

Follow-Up Endpoint Definition

Because routine follow-up visits did not always occur exactly at 5.0 years, the time window of endpoint was defined as the first eligible follow-up visit after 5 years (and before 7 years), and rates of progression (MD/VFI) and other information (such as follow-up IOP) were summarized using data from baseline to this landmark endpoint.

Visual Field Testing

Given the extremely high outpatient volume and the need for consistent longitudinal comparability, our center routinely used Humphrey 30–2 SITA Fast (Humphrey, Carl Zeiss Meditec Inc, Dublin, CA) as the standard perimetry protocol during the study period. All patients underwent refractive correction to achieve best corrected visual acuity prior to VF examinations. Only tests with ≤ 20% fixation losses, ≤ 15% false positive errors, and ≤ 33% false negative errors were considered reliable and included for the analyses, consistent to our prior study [19, 20].

The VFI and MD values, as calculated by the Humphrey Field Analyzer, were documented on the basis of the results of each VF examination. Baseline VFI and MD were defined as the values from the second of two consecutive reliable VF tests closest to the baseline visit.

Glaucoma severity was classified on the basis of the modified Hodapp–Parrish–Anderson criteria using mean deviation (MD) values from standard automated perimetry: early stage (MD > −6 dB), moderate stage (−12 dB ≤ MD ≤ −6 dB), and late stage (MD < −12 dB) [17].

Rate of VFI and MD change were estimated at the follow-up endpoint. All longitudinal VFI measurements across the entire follow-up were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models.

Definition of Different Progression Patterns

Fast progression and moderately rapid progression were defined using the rates of change calculated at the follow-up endpoint.

“Fast progression” was characterized by a reduction in VFI worse than −4.6%/year, or worse than −1/year in the global measure of MD (according to previous literature reporting “fast progression” using MD [9] and reporting the correlation between MD and VFI. [10])

“Fast progression” was characterized by a reduction in VFI worse than −4.6%/year, or worse than −1/year in the global measure of MD (according to previous literature reporting “fast progression” using MD [9] and reporting the correlation between MD and VFI. [10])

“Moderately rapid progression (MRP)” was defined as a reduction in VFI worse than −1.016%/year, or worse than −0.445 dB/year in the global measure of MD, approximate to previous studies defining slow or moderate progressors [21, 22]. The cutoff values for rate of change in VFI and MD were determined by the average of all patients with progressive VF impairment (negative rate of VFI/MD change).

“Moderately rapid progression (MRP)” was defined as a reduction in VFI worse than −1.016%/year, or worse than −0.445 dB/year in the global measure of MD, approximate to previous studies defining slow or moderate progressors [21, 22]. The cutoff values for rate of change in VFI and MD were determined by the average of all patients with progressive VF impairment (negative rate of VFI/MD change).

Statistical Analysis

The follow-up data for each patient were initially recorded in the Case Report Forms and subsequently entered into the REDCap system for systematic collation.

RStudio (R version 4.5.1) and SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) were used for descriptive analyses. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables were reported as mean (SD) or median (IQR) accordingly. Continuous variables were compared using the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test, Fisher’s exact test, or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. As a supplementary analysis, we quantified the magnitude of the stage effect using Cohen’s w for the 3 × 2 χ2 tests and reported the corresponding observed power under the study sample size.

Regression analyses were conducted in RStudio (R version 4.5.1). The linear mixed-effects models were fitted using all available VFI measurements over the entire follow-up for each eye, with random intercepts and slopes for eyes nested within patients. First, univariable mixed-effects models were fitted for each baseline factor. Candidate predictors for the multivariable model were then selected on the basis of their univariable association (p < 0.10) and clinical relevance. Time was expressed in years and centered at the mean follow-up. Collinearity was assessed before multivariable modeling using variance inflation factors calculated from corresponding marginal linear models, with VIF < 2 taken to indicate no important collinearity. Model assumptions were checked by visual inspection of residuals and random-effects distributions.

VF Progression Between Patients of Different POAG Stages

Both MD and VFI criteria indicated a gradual increase in proportion of fast progression and MRP with increasing disease severity (Table S3). However, the differences between stages were not statistically significant using MD criteria. Conversely, when it comes to VFI-confirmed MRP, a statistically significant difference emerged between patients with early, moderate-, and late-stage glaucoma (p < 0.001) (Table S3). Specifically, the proportion of patients exhibiting MRP was markedly higher in the late-stage group, with nearly half meeting this criteria. In contrast, only about one-fourth of patients with moderate glaucoma met the criteria of MRP.

A distribution analysis of rate of change in VFI stratified by baseline stage (Fig. 3) showed that early stage eyes were tightly clustered around 0%/year, whereas late-stage eyes had a broader, left-shifted density curve. Patients with late-stage glaucoma contributed a substantially larger proportion of eyes with relatively fast VFI progression speed (e.g., worse than −1.5%/year) than patients with early or moderate-stage glaucoma, with moderate-stage eyes lying between these two extremes. Additionally, late-stage POAG also exhibited greater spread toward positive slopes.

Comparative analysis result of patients with NTG and POAG with VF-related clinical characteristics is presented in Table S4. The baseline VF metrics and rate of change in VF progression do not reveal any statistically significant differences between patients with NTG and POAG. Discussion

This study examined VF progression in Chinese patients with POAG undergoing consistent treatment. Patients with a baseline VFI of 95% had an overall annual VF progression rate of −0.3%/year. Fast progression, defined via a VFI decline of −4.6%/year, was noted at 0.917% overall: 0.5% in early stage, 1.5% in moderate-stage, and 2.9% in late-stage glaucoma. That means that under current treatments, roughly 1% of early/moderate cases risk progressing toward blindness over a decade, with this risk tripling for individuals with late-stage glaucoma.

Starting from a baseline VFI of 95%, VFI reduction greater than −4.6% annually would reduce VFI to below 50% within 10 years—comparable to MD levels of −15 to −20 dB [23], a range typically indicative of late-stage glaucoma and significantly impaired vision-related quality of life [24, 25].

Our results showed that less than 1% of patients reached blindness-risk thresholds over a decade, demonstrating the efficacy of current treatment protocols. Few studies worldwide have used VFI thresholds; for example, Zhang et al. (2019) reported a 15.3% fast progression rate [26] with a VFI cutoff of −1%/year. The variation mainly reflects differences in threshold selection. Notably, our cohort’s VFI cutoff of −1.016%/year is similar to Zhang et al.’s fast progression threshold, suggesting it may serve as a preliminary screening parameter, though further calibration is needed to refine patient risk stratification.

Although MD is widely used to assess glaucoma severity, it has certain limitations—it represents a global average and is easily influenced by cataract-related media opacity. In contrast, the VFI utilizes pattern deviation analysis and emphasizes central visual field weighting, leading to more effective detection of glaucomatous damage while minimizing the effects of lens opacity. These features make VFI a valuable complementary indicator for long-term visual field monitoring.

Approximately one-quarter of patients meeting VFI-based progression criteria were not identified using MD and these eyes had more severe baseline VF defects and later disease stages, supporting the view that VFI is more sensitive for monitoring progression in advanced glaucoma [26]. The enhanced specificity of VFI arises from: (1) the functional prioritization of central VF regions through nonuniform topographic weighting and (2) reduced susceptibility to cataract-induced measurement artifacts.

Subgroup analyses further demonstrated that while MD-confirmed MRP showed a similar stage-dependent increasing trend (20.9%, 27.3%, and 35.3%), this did not reach statistical significance in our cohort (p = 0.140), and larger studies are needed to verify its stage discrimination. In contrast, VFI-confirmed MRP increased more steeply across stages (15.2%, 25.8%, and 47.1%) and was statistically significant (p < 0.001), supporting VFI as a more robust discriminator of stage-related VF progression in this dataset (Table S3). Patients with late-stage POAG exhibited the fastest progression, consistent with a U-shaped progression curve described previously [26].

To strengthen the interpretation of the stage-stratified comparisons, we quantified the magnitude of the stage effect using Cohen’s w for the 3 × 2 χ2 tests. Cohen’s w indicated a larger stage effect for VFI-confirmed MRP (w = 0.249), with high observed power in our sample (~0.98). In contrast, the MD-confirmed MRP effect was smaller (w = 0.112; power ~0.41), and a larger cohort is needed to confirm stage discrimination under MD criteria.

Notably, the late-stage group also showed greater spread toward positive VFI slopes, which likely reflects the well-described increase in perimetric test–retest variability with greater disease severity and reduced sensitivity of global VF indices near end-stage damage (a floor effect), rather than true functional improvement. [27, 28]

These results affirm VFI’s superior capacity to capture nonlinear progression patterns and its alignment with the disease’s pathophysiology.

The study also found comparable VF progression rates between NTG and POAG identified by VFI. While using MD, earlier studies reported slower progression in NTG from POAG population [29, 30], others demonstrated no significant intergroup differences in progression rates [31–34], with NTG preferentially exhibiting central/paracentral defect [34, 35]. However, in our study, patients with NTG showed reduced responsiveness to IOP-lowering treatments and less treatment intensification. This observed therapeutic conservatism, potentially driven by clinicians’ perceptions of “acceptable” IOP levels in NTG, might inadvertently permit ongoing optic nerve damage despite seemingly controlled IOP.

Previous studies, using MD decline of worse than −1 dB/year as a threshold, reported fast progression rates of 5–12% [10, 22, 36–40]. In contrast, our subjects with similar baseline MD value had lower fast progression rate of 2.75%, which possibly could be explained by population and case-mix characteristics, including ethnic differences, the predominance of early stage disease, and the inclusion of a proportion of normal-tension glaucoma—which is more prevalent in Asian populations with POAG and is often associated with slower progression.

Although only 1–3% of patients were classified as fast progressors by either VFI or MD criteria, about 20% showed VF deterioration above the population mean. These intermediate-risk patients may not face immediate blindness but require proactive management to slow progression.

Using longitudinal VFI change as a continuous outcome, the LMM results suggest that structural and age-related susceptibility may be at least as important as IOP in driving long-term VFI loss. The associations of older age and worse baseline MD with faster progression are consistent with large longitudinal cohorts, where reduced retinal ganglion cell reserve and cumulative vascular/mitochondrial damage in older eyes have repeatedly predicted steeper field decline despite treatment [12–14]. Thinner CCT is a well-established risk factor for conversion and progression, likely reflecting a more compliant connective-tissue phenotype: a “soft” cornea often coexists with a more deformable lamina cribrosa and may also lead to chronic IOP underestimation by applanation tonometry [41, 42]. The link between higher (more hyperopic) spherical equivalent and faster VFI loss is less well documented; it may relate to short, crowded discs that are more vulnerable to non-IOP mechanical or vascular stress, but this requires confirmation in independent cohorts. In contrast, the lack of an independent IOP effect after adjustment mirrors findings from treated glaucoma populations, where residual progression appears largely determined by age- and tissue-level susceptibility rather than mean IOP alone [11, 13, 14].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center analysis based on historical data, it is subject to potential confounding and selection biases, as well as challenges related to follow-up consistency and data completeness. Second, although the sample size exceeds that of many previous studies on visual field progression, it may still be inadequate to support strong predictive modeling, particularly for analyses stratified by clinical subtypes. Third, even with 203 POAG and 112 NTG eyes, our predominantly mild baseline loss may limit power to detect very small POAG–NTG differences. Lastly, continuous ≥ 5-year follow-up at a single tertiary center may be influenced by referral patterns and geography. As a national tertiary referral center, many patients travel from other provinces for diagnosis and initial management, after which routine follow-up and prescription refills are often undertaken at local tertiary hospitals to reduce travel burden. Therefore, follow-up duration at our center is influenced by referral pathways and access factors, and our findings warrant validation in multicenter cohorts.

Conclusions

This 17-year real-world study demonstrated a fast progression rate of 0.917% on the basis of VFI and 2.75% on the basis of MD in patients with POAG. The rate of MRP defined by VFI increased significantly with disease severity, whereas MD-based criteria demonstrated a similar trend that did not reach significance and warrants validation in larger cohorts. In addition, several predictors for VF progression based on VFI were identified. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate fast visual field deterioration in an Asian population using both VFI and MD, highlighting the potential of VFI as a more sensitive tool for identifying high-risk patients.
