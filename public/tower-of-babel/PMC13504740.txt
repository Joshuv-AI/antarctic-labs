Justice in the governance of personal health data spaces: stakeholder perspectives in Belgium
Authors: Erik Laes, Yvonne Denier, Elfi Goesaert, Cato van Schyndel, Nathalie Lambrechts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContemporary health data governance in Europe operates within a persistent tension between the protection of individual rights and the collective benefits that can be achieved through data sharing. Recent legislative initiatives such as the Data Act, Data Governance Act and the European Health Data Space attempt to reconcile these values by promoting trustworthy data reuse while safeguarding individual rights. However, these frameworks often rely on models of citizens as passive data donors, without ensuring citizen empowerment or equitable benefit sharing. This study examines how institutional stakeholders in Flanders understand the ethical conditions under which a Personal Health Data Space could be governed legitimately for secondary health-data use. We use literature on distributive, procedural, and recognition justice to interpret these expectations and to identify design considerations for participatory health-data governance.MethodsThe study employed an embedded research design within the We Are project, a Personal Health Data Space system under development in Flanders (Belgium). Empirical data were collected through stakeholder interviews and complemented by a purposive conceptual literature review on justice in health data governance. Data were thematically analysed through the lens of the three justice dimensions.ResultsFindings indicate that stakeholders support data solidarity, entailing expectations of reciprocity, transparency, and accountability. Distributive justice was associated with fair benefit-sharing mechanisms and reinvestment of data-derived value into public goods. Procedural justice was understood as continuous citizen participation and oversight, rather than one-time consent. Recognition justice emerged as the need to value diverse forms of knowledge and to include marginalised or underrepresented groups through inclusive data stewardship.ConclusionsThe study concludes that both literature and stakeholder views support data solidarity as a more ethically robust foundation for health data governance than data altruism. Collective and participatory governance arrangements can operationalise solidarity by embedding fairness, inclusivity, and accountability in data reuse. These findings provide practical and conceptual guidance for the ethical design of Personal Health Data Space systems.Trial registrationThis study is not a clinical trial. It is a conceptual and empirical qualitative / ethical analysis study and therefore clinical trial registration is not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01531-2.

Background

A rapidly expanding ecosystem of health and lifestyle technologies such as wearables, sensors, online platforms, and genetic testing tools, nowadays generates and processes excessively large volumes of health data. The integration of personal lifestyle and wellbeing data with clinical health information holds significant promise for advancing personalised and preventive healthcare (PPH) [1]. By coupling personal health-related data (PHD) from wearables, mobile health applications, and other lifestyle technologies with official medical records, it becomes possible to generate more comprehensive and dynamic health profiles, thereby enhancing both individual care and population-level health insights [2]. However, much of this potentially valuable lifestyle and wellbeing data remains confined within proprietary corporate databases, where access and interoperability are constrained by commercial interests and opaque governance practices [3, 4]. Although health data today make up about 30% of worldwide digital data (and many health data are not yet digitised, including in Belgium), recent OECD findings report that only 3% is used for actual decision-making [5].

Recent regulatory initiatives in the European Union (EU), such as the Data Governance Act (DGA)1, Data Act (DA)2 and the European Health Data Space (EHDS)3, seek to promote greater sharing and reuse of data (including PHD), through harmonised infrastructures and new mechanisms for citizen control. The DGA introduces data intermediaries to mediate data exchanges between individuals or organisations and third parties, the DA gives access and data portability rights to users of connected devices, while the EHDS aims to standardise access to official health records (e.g., electronic health records, results of laboratory tests, medical imaging, etc.) across the EU, granting individuals rights to annotate, restrict, or opt out of sharing their official health records. While these initiatives represent important progress in enhancing citizen empowerment, facilitating improved healthcare delivery, and promoting greater accessibility of health data across the EU, they still leave the issue of coupling privately held lifestyle and wellbeing data to clinical health data in official records largely unresolved.

Against this backdrop, personal data space (PDS) systems propose a paradigmatic shift in data governance. PDS systems enable individuals to have more control and transparency over the sharing of their personal digital data [6, 7]. This decentralised, user-governed control makes it easier to combine official healthcare records (e.g., from hospitals or general practitioners) with data generated through wellbeing and lifestyle apps. Furthermore, PDS systems give individuals granular, real-time control over how their data are shared and reused, offering a technological foundation for more participatory data ecosystems [8, 9]. Third parties can access specific sets of personal data, with permission, through a secure link for a specific task (e.g., research and development, delivering a personalised ad, communicating preventive health measures, etc.). The widespread adoption of PDS systems in PPH thus holds the promise of promoting interoperability, transparency, and portability of PHD across healthcare systems.

At present however, the sharing and reuse of personal data continue to elicit public concern about privacy, accountability, and the trustworthiness of data infrastructures [10, 11]. Establishing a trusted framework that responds to citizens’ concerns and promotes a collaborative approach to data sharing will be essential for the successful implementation of any data space initiative [12]. Trust and the perceived legitimacy of data sharing are furthermore closely linked to the expectation that such practices will advance the common good [13, 14]. Despite proponents’ claims that PDS technology provides the foundation for trustworthy data-sharing environments, its practical implementation raises important ethical questions [15–17]. In particular, Janssen et al. [17] criticise PDS for placing too much responsibility on individuals to manage complex privacy decisions, potentially reinforcing digital inequalities and failing to ensure meaningful consent. Moreover, these authors argue that PDS systems do not adequately address broader power imbalances, collective data rights, or the systemic nature of data exploitation, thereby limiting their ethical effectiveness.

This article argues that such ethical challenges should not be seen as intrinsic limitations of the PDS model, but rather as governance issues that must be explicitly addressed within the institutional frameworks in which these systems are embedded. To advance this perspective, we examine the governance architecture underpinning the We Are initiative, initiated by a multi-stakeholder partnership in the Flanders region of Belgium.4 We Are is an independent citizen-centric initiative that aims to empower individuals to control their PHD through a trusted and transparent PHDS system. The system allows citizens to selectively share data with third parties (such as academia, civil society organisations, public authorities, and private companies) for purposes like research & development, innovation, and personalised services. We Are integrates a technological layer (i.e., data storage centred on the individual), functionalities for data access and communication (i.e., the interface towards the user), and a governance layer (i.e., the framework of rules, processes, and legal and ethical responsibilities that ensures the management, access, and use of the data on the system – the focus of the present study) (cf. Figure 1).

Through our investigation of the We Are initiative, we explore to what extent governance design choices could address the ethical concerns raised in the context of emerging personal health data space (PHDS) infrastructures. This requires a methodological approach that goes beyond system-level functionality and addresses the institutional conditions under which PHDS systems can be governed in an ethically well-founded manner. Methods

Embedded research

The authors of the present study are actively involved in the co-development and implementation of the We Are initiative. As embedded researchers, we are directly involved in the participatory design of the We Are system’s institutional architecture, including the drafting of governance models, the facilitation of stakeholder meetings, and the alignment of system goals with broader policy objectives in Flanders, Belgium and the EU. This embeddedness brings both strengths and limitations: while it enables deep contextual insight and sustained access to key deliberative processes and stakeholders, it also necessitates rigorous critical reflection on our own positionality and potential normative commitments. To mitigate risks of bias and maintain analytical distance, we employed iterative cycles of reflexive documentation, including (but not limited to) debriefing notes from meetings and critical discussion sessions among co-authors and the We Are consortium members (cf. footnote 4). In addition, we adopted a scientifically validated qualitative and interpretive research methodology grounded in the principles of Value Sensitive Design (VSD), while deliberately extending its scope beyond technological artefacts to include the institutional and governance dimensions of health and wellbeing data systems.

Expanding value sensitive design to institutional contexts

Originally developed within the field of human-computer interaction, VSD is an approach that integrates ethical values into the design of technology from the early stages of development [18]. VSD combines conceptual, empirical, and technical investigations to identify stakeholder values and translate them into design requirements for technologies. While VSD traditionally focuses on user-interface design and system functionality, recent work has extended its principles to broader sociotechnical and governance contexts [19]. Our methodological approach aligns with this broader trajectory, treating the governance framework of the We Are system itself as a design object. In this study, VSD thus served as a guiding lens rather than a prescriptive protocol: it oriented our empirical work toward uncovering how ethical values such as autonomy, fairness, and solidarity are understood by different actors and how these can be embedded in the design and governance of PHDS systems. As such, the stakeholder interviews functioned as the empirical investigation within the VSD process, enabling us to articulate justice-related design principles that respond to concrete stakeholder concerns.

Analytical framework: justice in health data governance

Drawing on the growing literature on data justice [20–22] and inspired by the work of Schlosberg on environmental justice [23], we conceptualised three interrelated dimensions of distributive, procedural, and recognition justice as the overarching framework for our study. Taken together, these three dimensions capture three analytically distinct, but interdependent governance questions raised by P(H)DS systems. Distributive justice asks how benefits, burdens, risks, and opportunities arising from data reuse and/or sharing are allocated. Procedural justice asks who participates in decisions, how decisions are made, and what forms of accountability and recourse are available. Recognition justice asks whose identities, capabilities, vulnerabilities, and knowledge forms are acknowledged in the design and governance of data infrastructures.

The literature review was designed as a purposive conceptual review rather than a systematic review. Its purpose was to develop an analytical framework for developing the interview guide and interpreting stakeholder interviews, not to provide an exhaustive synthesis of all literature on PHD governance. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar using combinations of terms including ‘health data governance’, ‘data justice’, ‘data solidarity’, ‘secondary use of health data’, ‘social licence’, ‘benefit sharing’, ‘public value’, and ‘participatory data stewardship’. We also used backward and forward citation tracking from key papers. The results of the literature review are discussed in relation to the findings from the stakeholder interviews in the Discussion section (Section "Discussion").

Stakeholder interviews: mapping perspectives on just governance

Between January and May 2024, an external consultant commissioned by the We Are consortium conducted nine semi-structured interviews with purposively selected stakeholders. Potential participants were identified through a stakeholder mapping of the Flemish and Belgian health data ecosystem [24]. Selection aimed to capture a range of institutional perspectives relevant to PHDS governance, including public digital-health infrastructure, health insurance and reimbursement, healthcare-provider representation, patient/citizen and consumer interests, legal and data-protection expertise, digital innovation, community care, and social inclusion. The purpose was not statistical representativeness, but the inclusion of information-rich participants with formal responsibilities, recognised expertise, or societal relevance in relation to health-data governance and secondary use of health data. Interview participants

The interview guide was developed based on the conceptual literature review, the VSD orientation of the study, and the specific governance questions raised by the We Are initiative. It covered themes such as citizen control, trust, consent, benefit-sharing, commercial access, public value, accountability, exclusion, digital literacy, and governance oversight. The guide was discussed and tested internally before being finalised. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital Antwerp/University of Antwerp, reference B300201940638. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

We did not aim for statistical representativeness or claim thematic saturation. Rather, we assessed data sufficiency through the concept of information power: the sample was small but information-rich, given the narrow focus on PHDS governance, the participants’ direct expertise in health-data governance, and the theoretically guided analysis. Participants were recruited by e-mail, with follow-up phone calls where needed. Interviews lasted between 45 and 90 min, were conducted via secure video conferencing, and were audio-recorded and transcribed with informed consent. All transcripts were pseudonymised for analysis. Initial coding was inductive [25], allowing for the emergence of salient themes without predefining categories [26]. Two researchers (including co-author CvS) conducted the initial inductive coding of all transcripts, and disagreements or ambiguities were discussed in analytic meetings with the wider author team. Reflexive memos were used to document analytic decisions, especially given the authors’ embedded position in the We Are project. The inductively identified themes were subsequently interpreted in relation to distributive, procedural, and recognition justice by the corresponding author (EL), with further discussion among the author team. For example, concerns about unequal digital literacy were linked to recognition justice, while debates over data access rules and benefit-sharing were related to distributive justice. Throughout the analysis, we paid particular attention to how stakeholders interpreted key governance terms such as “control”, “trust”, “consent”, “benefit”, and “accountability”, since these terms indicate important points of ethical and institutional contestation. Our intention was not to resolve these design choices by abstract reasoning from first principles, but to treat them as concrete institutional design concerns to be explored and refined through empirical research and stakeholder engagement.

Results of stakeholder interviews

Distributive justice

Stakeholders discussed distributive justice as a crucial dimension of the ethical governance of PHDS systems, focusing on the fair allocation of benefits and burdens arising from data sharing. One central theme was the need for reciprocity: citizens contributing their data should see tangible returns. As noted, “People are willing to help others, but they also want to know: what’s in it for me? If big companies benefit but ordinary citizens receive nothing in return, then the system is not fair.” (P3) This sentiment was echoed by others who worried that data solidarity could inadvertently legitimise asymmetrical flows of value toward powerful actors: “I hear a lot about data solidarity, but in practice that often means citizens giving and others taking. Where is the reciprocity? Where is the collective benefit?” (P5) For many, the concept of solidarity needed to be underpinned by concrete mechanisms to ensure equitable sharing of benefits.

Other stakeholders linked distributive justice to broader social solidarity traditions in Belgium, drawing parallels with the health insurance system: “In our social security model, everyone contributes according to their means, and everyone receives according to their needs. Maybe data governance should learn from that, so we can build structures that ensure proportional contribution and benefit.” (P7) This analogy to existing welfare systems was seen as a potential way to embed distributive fairness into new PHDS systems.

The interviews also revealed tensions around using data for PPH: “It is often easier to convince people to share data for treatment of an acute condition, where the personal benefit is clear. But for prevention, where the advantage is more collective and long-term, it is much harder. People don’t see the direct return, so they hesitate.” (P4) This reflects what is commonly known as the ‘prevention paradox’: while preventive measures yield significant benefits at the population level, the individual incentive can seem diffuse or abstract.

Several stakeholders referred to the potential of PHDS systems to create more equitable models of benefit sharing, but they cautioned that this potential depends on deliberate governance choices: “It’s great that people can decide who accesses their data, but if the system is designed mainly to serve the interests of large companies or institutions, the benefits will still flow upwards. You need to build rules that make sure citizens and communities gain something concrete, not just empty promises.” (P8) Another concern involved how data benefits are defined and measured: “When we talk about benefits, who decides what counts? A pharmaceutical company might see benefit in using data to develop expensive drugs, but is that benefit shared with the people who provided the data?” (P2) This comment highlights the contested nature of ‘benefit’, underscoring the need for participatory processes in defining what counts as fair return. Transparency around how benefits are distributed was also viewed as critical: “If citizens do not know who profits from their data, they will quickly lose trust. There needs to be transparency about how revenue is shared, how research priorities are set, and who really benefits.” (P6) This call for openness was tied to deeper questions of power, with multiple stakeholders expressing concern about unequal bargaining positions between ordinary citizens and large institutions or commercial partners.

Stakeholders further noted that distributive justice is intertwined with procedural and recognition justice: “If the rules about benefit sharing are made without citizen input, they will never be seen as legitimate.” (P1) This perspective reinforces the idea that distributive and procedural justice must be developed in tandem. Some interviewees also touched on the issue of digital divides and structural inequalities, emphasising that distributive justice must consider differences in who can meaningfully participate: “If data sharing becomes a condition for accessing better care, then people who are less digitally literate or more concerned about privacy could be left behind. That would deepen existing inequalities instead of reducing them.” (P4).

Finally, stakeholders stressed the need for long-term strategies to monitor and adjust benefit-sharing mechanisms as the system evolves: “We can’t just build a fair system once and then forget it. You need to keep checking: is the distribution of benefits still fair? Are some groups being left out? Is the system still working for everyone?” (P5) This commitment to ongoing reflection and adjustment was seen as vital to maintaining trust and ensuring that distributive justice remains at the forefront of governance practice.

In sum, the interviews reveal that stakeholders view distributive justice as a practical, political, and ethical challenge requiring clear rules, transparency, continuous evaluation, and genuine citizen involvement: “It’s simple: if the benefits are not shared fairly, then the whole promise of citizen-controlled data systems becomes just another way to exploit people. We have to do better.” (P3).

Procedural Justice

From the stakeholder interviews, a strong consensus emerged that procedural justice cannot be reduced to the provision of individual consent mechanisms alone: “Consent is important, but it is not enough. People need to be able to follow what happens with their data afterwards, and to speak up if something goes wrong.” (P4) This view reflects a broader apprehension that overly legalistic or technical consent procedures risk placing the burden on individuals while ignoring structural power asymmetries. Likewise, several stakeholders stressed that fair decision making is not a one-time commitment, but rather a continuous process of dialogue and adaptation: “We cannot just ask people once at the beginning. You need to check in with them along the way, show them what is happening with their data, and listen to their questions and doubts.” (P5) Building such iterative forms of participation, according to stakeholders, is central to sustaining trust over the longer term.

For many stakeholders, procedural justice also implies establishing collective structures of oversight based on structural participation: “If decisions are made only by the organisations building the system, how can we be sure the interests of citizens are protected? There must be a way for users and civil society groups to really have influence.” (P3) Other stakeholders mentioned the risk of governance capture by large commercial players: “If commercial actors are too powerful in the system, then the voices of citizens will always come second. We need rules that guarantee a balance of power.” (P6) Here, the need for participatory mechanisms goes beyond citizen consultation or periodic feedback rounds; it requires embedding citizen voices into the core governance structures with real authority over strategic choices. Stakeholders linked this directly to the design of procedural safeguards that ensure civil society organisations such as patient groups and community representatives have meaningful and protected seats at the decision-making table. Mechanisms of accountability and recourse were also identified as indispensable: “There should be an independent council or committee that can intervene when there are abuses or conflicts. People need to know they have somewhere to go.” (P7) Without such structures, many felt that procedural justice would remain hollow, leaving citizens with no practical tools to challenge or appeal decisions that they perceive as unjust.

Beyond formal structures, stakeholders underscored the need for a culture of openness and accountability: “Transparency is not just about putting documents online. It’s about explaining decisions in clear language, being honest about uncertainties, and admitting when mistakes happen.” (P1) In this view, procedural justice implies fostering an institutional culture that encourages responsiveness and genuine dialogue with citizens. However, some stakeholders described how existing institutional cultures often resist transparency when it threatens internal interests. Accordingly, it was referred to the fact that hospitals sometimes are reluctant to allow outside scrutiny of their practices because such scrutiny is perceived as ‘threatening’: “Then they [i.e., citizens] will get into our data, and once they do, they might draw their own conclusions… and we [i.e., hospitals] don’t want that.” (P1) This example illustrates how procedural justice is not just about citizen participation but also about challenging institutional power and enabling accountability across all levels of the system.

Finally, stakeholders spoke of the importance of procedural justice not only at the outset of system development but also as a continuous thread throughout the lifecycle of the project: “It’s easy to launch with big promises about citizen control. The real challenge is keeping those promises alive five or ten years later, when there are new pressures, new partners, and new technologies.” (P5) This reflects a broader recognition that procedural justice is not static but must evolve along with the system and the communities it serves.

In sum, the interviews reveal that stakeholders see procedural justice as an essential cornerstone of ethical governance for PHDS systems. It demands more than the individual consent frameworks typically emphasised in privacy regulation: it requires building institutional mechanisms for ongoing participation, independent oversight, and meaningful recourse. “If you want people to trust the system, you have to show them they matter in how it is run, not just in how their data is used.” (P1).

Recognition Justice

Stakeholders consistently argued that recognition justice requires acknowledgment and respect for the diversity of citizens’ lived experiences, identities, capabilities, and social positions. One of the most frequently voiced concerns was the risk of digital exclusion. Importantly, stakeholders warned that even well-designed systems can become exclusionary if their assumptions about user capabilities are not matched by infrastructure: “You have to take into account that not everyone has the same level of digital literacy… that affects how people understand and act on consent.” (P4) One stakeholder gave a personal example involving their elderly parents: “If you ask my parents to actively manage data… how on earth will we organise that if there’s no one at the counter who can help them in a way they understand?” (P1) Several other interviewees noted that assumptions of digital fluency underpin many health data system designs, yet this fluency is far from evenly distributed: “You create a system where those who are most vulnerable are the least able to participate.” (P6) The risk here is that systems intending to increase user control may, in practice, reinforce disparities by presuming capacities that not all users possess. Stakeholders also discussed recognition justice in terms of privacy and visibility, particularly for socially marginalised groups: “For small or stigmatised groups, it can be dangerous to share data, because you are more easily identifiable.” (P3) This comment highlights a paradox: while PHDS systems offer individuals more control over their data, such control may be illusory for communities whose small size or social status increases their vulnerability to reidentification and discrimination when data is shared. In this case, recognition must involve anticipating and mitigating differential risks by designing protective mechanisms that are especially robust for those with heightened exposure to harm.

Beyond digital capacity and privacy risks, multiple stakeholders pointed to deeper epistemic injustices within healthcare systems: “The data you collect as an individual is hugely relevant, but when you sit in front of a doctor, you get nothing meaningful or useful in return.” (P2) This experience exemplifies the undervaluation of patient-generated data and lay knowledge, which are often excluded from clinical decision-making processes. PHDS systems, if designed with care, can begin to address this gap by validating the experiential insights of citizens and enabling them to participate as knowledge contributors.

While many stakeholders expressed hope that PHDS systems could shift power relations in more equitable directions, this optimism was tempered by concerns about institutional inertia and the dominance of large actors: “I hope that We Are can capture the needs of the community, but the question is whether it is strong enough against the big players.” (P6) Here, recognition is not only about identity or capacity, but about political and economic power. Without mechanisms to put the perspectives of communities and patients (and particularly those with fewer resources) at the centre of attention, PHDS systems risk tokenistic engagement while decision-making remains concentrated among institutional or commercial actors.

Stakeholders repeatedly returned to the role of communication as a medium for recognition: “The communication must be adapted so that everyone can follow, not just the digitally skilled.” (P7) Recognition in this sense requires accessible language, culturally sensitive framing, and outreach strategies that consider linguistic diversity, disability, and education levels. Several interviewees noted that public information on data rights or system use is often highly technical and inaccessible, alienating citizens who might otherwise be engaged. Others connected recognition to the structural supports necessary to enable participation: “Without support, training, and public awareness, the promise of data control remains hollow.” (P8) This was echoed by other stakeholders who described health data governance as currently over-reliant on individual responsibility, without sufficient collective infrastructure to ensure equitable participation.

Recognition was also seen as potentially empowering when grounded in authentic control: “The idea that you as a citizen can decide who sees your data gives a sense of recognition.” (P5) However, this sense of agency depends heavily on how much influence users truly have over system governance and the outcomes of their data-sharing decisions: “If people feel that their perspective matters, then you get trust.” (P1) Several interviewees made it clear that gaining trust requires long-term commitment, not one-off inclusion efforts: “Inclusion is not just about inviting people to the table. It’s about changing the table: who sets the agenda, who defines what counts as relevant, and who has the final say.” (P8) Recognition, therefore, must be embedded in lasting governance structures and feedback loops.

In conclusion, the stakeholder interviews demonstrate that recognition justice is a foundational aspect of ethical PHDS system governance. At its core, recognition justice demands that data governance systems not only include but affirm different ways of knowing, being, and interacting with digital technologies and healthcare institutions. Without deliberate attention to these dimensions, PHDS systems risk reinforcing the very inequalities they claim to challenge.

Discussion

This study examined how institutional stakeholders in Flanders understand the conditions under which a PHDS could be governed legitimately for secondary uses of PHD. The interviews do not support an unconditional claim that PHDS systems are inherently just. Rather, stakeholders treated justice as a governance achievement: data sharing can serve the common good only if reciprocity, participation, and recognition are built into the institutional arrangements surrounding the technology. This finding qualifies the common promise of PDS systems. Although PDS architectures may strengthen transparency and individual control, interviewees repeatedly warned that individual control alone can shift responsibility onto citizens and leave commercial and institutional power asymmetries intact. This concern aligns with critical literature on PDS systems [17] and with studies showing that public support for health-data reuse is conditional on trust, purpose, privacy protection, transparency, accountability, and perceived public benefit [10, 11]. The main contribution of this study is therefore not to show that PHDS systems such as We Are are inherently ‘more just’ than other health data architectures, but to specify the governance conditions under which stakeholders considered such systems ethically acceptable.

Reciprocity and benefit-sharing as conditions for distributive justice

The clearest distributive concern in the interviews was that citizens may contribute data while other actors capture most of the value. Participants expressed this concern directly: if “big companies benefit but ordinary citizens receive nothing in return” (P3), or if solidarity means “citizens giving and others taking” (P5), data sharing will not be regarded as fair. These remarks show that stakeholders did not understand benefit-sharing as a secondary issue to be addressed after data access has been granted. Rather, benefit-sharing was treated as a precondition for legitimate access.

This empirical finding resonates with literature on data solidarity, which argues that legitimate data governance should enable socially valuable data use, prevent or mitigate data-related harms, and return part of the value generated from data to the public domain [27, 28]. It also aligns with work on public value in digital health, which asks who creates value, who captures it, and how governance can prevent the public value of health data from being displaced by narrower institutional or commercial interests [14]. Hummel et al. similarly argue that demands for data ownership often express deeper concerns about redistribution, transparency, and control rather than a simple wish to commodify personal data [29]. In our interviews, this point was visible in stakeholders’ repeated insistence that citizens should know “who really benefits” (P6) and who decides what counts as a benefit (P2).

The concept of benefit should therefore be made explicit in PHDS governance. Benefits may include direct individual feedback, improved healthcare services, familial or community-relevant knowledge, public-health planning, scientific knowledge, health-system efficiencies, commercial innovation, and equity gains. These benefits do not necessarily align. A commercial product, for example, may generate scientific or economic value while offering limited return to the people or communities whose data contributed to its development. Conversely, some public-health benefits, especially in prevention, may be collective and long-term rather than immediately visible to individuals. This helps explain stakeholders’ concerns about the prevention paradox: people may support data use for acute care where individual benefit is clear, but be more hesitant when the benefit is diffuse, delayed, or primarily collective. A practical implication is that PHDS access requests should include a public-value and benefit-sharing assessment. Applicants should be required to specify the expected benefits, the likely beneficiaries, the burdens or risks for particular groups, whether commercial value will be generated, and how results or value will be returned to citizens, patient groups, communities, or public systems. This does not require every data use to produce direct individual return, but it does require a transparent justification of why a proposed use is fair. Without such explicit criteria, data altruism risks becoming a one-directional model of donation, and well-resourced actors may be better positioned to frame their activities as serving the public interest, thereby reproducing a Matthew effect in access to data-derived value [30].

Several stakeholders linked distributive justice to Belgian social-security traditions, where citizens contribute according to capacity and receive according to need. This analogy is useful because welfare-state legitimacy often rests on norms of solidarity and generalised reciprocity [31]. However, the analogy also has limits. Social-security redistribution is largely mandatory and centrally organised, whereas PHDS governance must also preserve meaningful citizen agency and contestability. Data solidarity should therefore not mean surrendering control to a central authority. It should mean that individual control is combined with collective mechanisms for defining public value, distributing benefits, and monitoring whether promised benefits materialise.

Beyond consent: participation, oversight, and recourse as procedural safeguards

Stakeholders were equally clear that procedural justice cannot be reduced to consent. “Consent is important, but it is not enough” (P4), because citizens also need to follow what happens after data have been shared and to “speak up if something goes wrong.” Similarly, P5 argued that citizens cannot simply be asked once at the beginning; governance must “check in with them along the way.” These findings are consistent with the literature showing that conventional informed consent is poorly suited to dynamic, secondary health-data uses [32]. Dynamic consent, meta-consent, and broad-but-deep consent each respond to this problem by giving individuals more flexible ways to express preferences over time [33–35]. Yet the interviews also support critiques that more sophisticated consent models remain insufficient if they leave citizens alone to navigate complex data ecosystems or fail to address structural asymmetries between data subjects and data controllers [36].

The relevant governance question is therefore not which consent model should replace all others, but how consent can be embedded within wider structures of accountability. Stakeholders proposed independent councils, citizen and civil-society representation, appeal mechanisms, and public reporting of access decisions. These proposals correspond to work on biomedical big data and data cooperatives, where legitimacy depends on governance arrangements that allow citizens and patient representatives to question, shape, and contest institutional practices [37, 38]. They also echo relational approaches to trust in precision medicine, which argue that participation from diverse communities requires institutional trustworthiness, sustained communication, and responsiveness over time rather than one-off authorisation [39, 40].

This point is important for interpreting our proposal for participatory PHDS governance. The argument is not that existing democratic institutions, data-protection law, research ethics committees, or EHDS access bodies are illegitimate or should be replaced. Rather, PHDS systems occupy a governance space that is not fully covered by these existing arrangements. This is particularly relevant for lifestyle and wellbeing data generated through wearables, apps, sensors, and other consumer technologies, which are often collected outside official healthcare organisations and remain largely governed by commercial platforms or fragmented private arrangements. PHDS systems may therefore prove especially valuable where they create accountable pathways for bringing such data into health-related research, innovation, prevention, or care without leaving decisions solely to individual consent or corporate terms of service. Such arrangements inevitably add procedural complexity, and this is a legitimate concern. The answer is not to multiply committees without clear authority. Participatory mechanisms should be proportionate to risk, have defined mandates, include appropriate expertise and citizen or patient representation, and be periodically evaluated. In low-risk cases, streamlined review and standard transparency obligations may be sufficient. In cases involving commercial use, sensitive groups, high reidentification risk, lifestyle data from non-clinical sources, or contested public value, more deliberative review would be justified. Procedural justice thus requires more than formal openness; it requires institutions capable of explaining decisions, responding to challenge, and revising governance as technologies, users, data sources, and social expectations change.

Recognition, digital inclusion, and epistemic justice

Recognition justice was most visible in stakeholders’ concerns about digital exclusion, differential vulnerability, and the undervaluation of patient-generated knowledge. Participants warned that PHDS systems can become exclusionary if they assume that all citizens have the same digital literacy, time, confidence, or access to support. P1’s example of elderly parents who would need “someone at the counter” to help them manage data choices illustrates a broader concern: a system designed to increase control may unintentionally benefit those already best able to exercise control. This concern is especially salient in Belgium, where a substantial share of the population is at risk of digital exclusion, particularly older adults, people with lower education, and socio-economically disadvantaged groups [41]. It also aligns with capability-based accounts of health justice, which emphasise that formal opportunities are insufficient if people lack the real means to use them [42], and with work on scarcity showing how precarious circumstances constrain attention, planning, and choice [43].

Recognition also concerns differential exposure to harm. As P3 noted, members of small or stigmatised groups may be more easily identifiable and may face higher risks from data sharing. In such cases, equal rules may not produce equal protection. PHDS governance should therefore require explicit assessment of group risks, including reidentification, profiling, discrimination, and the use of data for forms of normative prevention that may pressure individuals or groups to conform to dominant health norms [44, 45]. Recognition justice thus connects directly to distributive and procedural justice: unequal vulnerability must affect both the conditions of access and the procedures through which access is reviewed.

A further recognition concern was epistemic. P2’s remark that individually collected data can be “hugely relevant” while producing “nothing meaningful or useful in return” in clinical encounters points to the undervaluation of patient-generated data and experiential knowledge. This resonates with the literature on epistemic injustice, which shows how some forms of knowledge are discounted because of social position or institutional hierarchy [46]. PHDS systems could help correct this imbalance by enabling citizens and patients to contribute data, questions, and priorities to knowledge production. However, this will not occur automatically. Studies on patient-generated health data show that sharing such data can support more patient-centred care only when there is clear communication, appropriate clinical integration, and attention to barriers faced by people living with long-term conditions [47].

Inclusive data stewardship therefore requires more than accessible interfaces. It requires plain-language communication, non-digital routes for participation, delegated or supported decision-making where appropriate, outreach to underrepresented communities, and governance criteria that ask whether proposed data uses reduce or reinforce health and digital inequalities. Frameworks such as the CARE Principles are useful here not because they can be transferred uncritically to the Belgian context, but because they show how data governance can make collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, and ethics explicit audit points [48]. Similarly, participatory data stewardship and data-cooperative models show how recognition can be linked to decision-making authority and benefit-sharing rather than reduced to symbolic inclusion [49].

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its embedded qualitative design within a PHDS initiative under development, which enabled access to concrete governance questions rather than abstract views about data sharing. The combination of stakeholder interviews, VSD, and justice-oriented analysis allowed us to connect empirical concerns with practical design considerations. The study also has limitations. First, the sample was small and composed of institutional stakeholders with relatively high expertise in health-data governance; it should not be interpreted as representing citizens or patients generally. Second, our findings should not be read as representing a singular public [50]. Attitudes toward secondary health-data use vary by purpose, actor, perceived public benefit, trust in institutions, privacy concerns, health status, digital literacy, and experiences of marginalisation [10, 11, 51]. The Belgian and Flemish context matters: traditions of social security and solidarity may make certain forms of collective benefit-sharing intuitively meaningful, while linguistic, institutional, and socio-economic diversity may shape expectations of representation and trust. Other European contexts may therefore produce different views about acceptable commercial access, state involvement, opt-out arrangements, or citizen participation. Third, as the We Are initiative was still under development, our article identifies governance expectations and design considerations rather than evaluating implemented outcomes. Finally, the literature component was conceptual and purposive rather than systematic.

Conclusion

This study shows that institutional stakeholders in Flanders conditionally support the development of PHDS systems such as We Are. Their views suggest that PHDS systems should not be regarded as inherently just. Rather, their ethical value depends on whether they are governed in ways that make reciprocity, participation, and recognition operational. PHDS systems may be particularly valuable in relation to lifestyle and wellbeing data generated outside official healthcare organisations, where existing governance structures are often fragmented and commercial terms of service play a prominent role. However, this potential will only be realised if PHDS governance is embedded within existing legal and ethical frameworks while adding proportionate mechanisms for public-value assessment, benefit-sharing review, inclusive participation, and equity monitoring. Particularly, three commitments follow from our analysis. First, distributive justice requires transparent assessment of who benefits from data reuse and how value is returned to citizens, communities, patient groups, or public systems. Second, procedural justice requires governance arrangements that go beyond one-off consent by providing participation, independent oversight, accountability, and routes for complaint or appeal. Third, recognition justice requires active attention to digital exclusion, group vulnerability, and the epistemic value of patient and citizen knowledge.

These findings should be interpreted as analytically transferable design considerations rather than as evidence for a single governance model. Future research should test these mechanisms through direct engagement with citizens, patients, digitally excluded groups, and low-trust communities, and should compare how different institutional and welfare-state contexts shape expectations of legitimate health-data reuse.
