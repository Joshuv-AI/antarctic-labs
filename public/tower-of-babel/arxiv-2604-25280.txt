The optimal betting wealth growth rate
Ashwin Ram, Aaditya Ramdas

Abstract
This paper characterizes the best possible rate of growth of wealth in a Kelly betting game when repeatedly betting against a general i.i.d. null hypothesis 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , but the data are drawn i.i.d. from an arbitrary alternative Q Q . We prove that it equals lim n → ∞ n − 1 ​ inf P ∈ ( 𝒫 n ) ∘ ⁣ ∘ KL ⁡ ( Q n , P ) \lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1}\inf_{P\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n},P) , where 𝒫 n = { P n : P ∈ 𝒫 } \mathcal{P}^{n}=\{P^{n}:P\in\mathcal{P}\} and ( 𝒫 n ) ∘ ⁣ ∘ (\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ} is its bipolar, i.e. this rate is achievable and one cannot do better. This quantity is in general smaller than a more popular quantity in the literature, KL inf ​ ( Q , 𝒫 ) := inf P ∈ 𝒫 KL ⁡ ( Q , P ) \mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q,P) . If KL inf ​ ( ⋅ , 𝒫 ) \mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(\cdot,\mathcal{P}) is weakly lowersemicontinuous (w.l.s.c.) at Q Q , we show that the two quantities are equal; in particular, this happens when 𝒫 \mathcal{P} is weakly compact. For simple alternatives, we provide the first matching necessary and sufficient condition for when power-one sequential tests exist (without assumptions on 𝒫 , Q \mathcal{P},Q ). We also derive the optimal worst-case growth rate against composite 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} . We emphasize that test supermartingales on reduced filtrations suffice for all iid testing problems, and more general e-processes are not required. We thus completely generalize the recent results of Larsson et al. [ 14 ] to the sequential setting.

Introduction

Consider a forecaster who asserts that all observations in a data stream are drawn i.i.d. from some unknown distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}

Q

The skeptic’s accumulated wealth represents the evidence gained against the null: the larger the wealth, the more the evidence. These wealth processes are nonnegative supermartingales, or, more generally,

Our main contribution is characterizing the optimal asymptotic growth rate of evidence under the alternative.

Related Work.

A number of works have established martingale arguments for constructing tests that remain valid under optional stopping
[
27
,
8
,
22
]
. At the same time, Kelly’s criteria and later improvements connected expected log-wealth growth to relative entropy in simple settings and settings with the existence of a dominating reference measure
[
12
,
5
,
4
]
. In contrast, our work shows what will replace the
\mathrm{KL}
identity when dealing with composite nulls and without a dominating reference measure (all the while ensuring validity at all stopping times).

More recently, a number of works on
e
-values and
e
-processes reframe nonnegative supermartingales as anytime-valid measures of statistical evidence against the null
[
24
,
11
,
28
,
19
,
13
]
. As a consequence, issues with testing and reproducibility have (at least partially) been resolved. That is, we have both safe continuously monitored testing and calibration and merging of evidence across different analyses. Further, in this setting, general-purpose constructions and optimality notions like admissibility have been developed
[
21
,
9
]
. Our work is the first assumption-free converse of this: we provide an achievability theorem for i.i.d. composite null testing. In doing so, we identify the maximal per-sample log-growth rate possible. Furthermore, for i.i.d. problems, we prove that optimizing over test supermartingales suffice in the sense that they already attain the full
e
-process envelope.

More specifically, in this safe-testing literature, growth-rate optimal (
\mathrm{GRO}
)
e
-variables for composite hypotheses have been established, along with its strong duality characterization terms in terms of the reverse information projection
[
9
,
10
,
14
]
. Our work here is the asymptotic analogue of these finite-horizon results. We show that the sequential limiting growth rate (for i.i.d. data) is a normalized reverse
\mathrm{KL}
to the bipolar
(\mathcal{P})^{\circ\circ}
. We emphasize here the evident gap between this bipolar rate and the well-studied
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. We emphasize that, in general, the correct maximal growth rate of the
inf-KL
is only equal to the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
under certain topological conditions, such as weak lower semicontinuity.

For composite hypotheses without a dominating reference measure,
[
15
]
have completed Le Cam’s foundational program
[
16
]
of when nontrivial fixed-sample tests exist; in particular, one must pass to the weak-
\star
closures in the dual space of finitely additive charges. Separately, recent work has also presented weak compactness of the null class as a general sufficient condition that guarantees existence of a power-one sequential test against the entire complement of the composite null
\mathcal{P}

[
18
]
. For such tests, it has been established that the sharp general lower-bound for any sequential procedure in the low type I error regime (ie as
\alpha\downarrow 0
) is
\log(1/\alpha)/\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}

[
1
]
. This bound has been further analyzed in terms of what constructions asymptotically match this
[
26
]
. However, none of these works provide an assumption-free condition for existence of power-one tests that is both
necessary and sufficient
for point-alternatives. We explicitly do this (along with sufficient conditions for composite alternatives) in terms of strict positivity of
a_{n}(Q)
. In addition, we clarify under what restrictions the very-well studied (yet unfortunately incorrect)
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
term is the governing complexity measure for sequentially testing composite nulls.

7
and conclude and present open problems in Section

Preliminaries

Let
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})
be a measurable space.
We let
\mathcal{M}_{1}
be the set of probability measures on
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})
. (If needed, we may assume that
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})
is a Polish space with its Borel
\sigma

Definition 2.1
.

For any measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
and nonempty set of probability measures
\mathcal{S}
on this space,
define its polar as

\mathcal{S}^{\circ}:=\Bigl\{E:\Omega\to[0,\infty]:\sup_{P\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[E]\leq 1\Bigr\}.

Following
[
14
]
, the bipolar is then defined as

\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}:=\Bigl\{R\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega):\mathbb{E}_{R}[E]\leq 1\ \text{ for all }E\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ}\Bigr\}.

Note that
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
, because if
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
E\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ}
, then
\mathbb{E}_{P}[E]\leq\sup_{P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{P^{\prime}}[E]\leq 1
.
We use
\Omega
above instead of
\mathcal{B}
because, for example, we will also use it on product spaces like
(\mathsf{X}^{n},\mathcal{B}^{\otimes n})
. For
P\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
, let
P^{n}
be the
n
fold product measure on
(\mathsf{X}^{n},\mathcal{B}^{\otimes n})
.

For
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
and
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
,
recall
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)
and
define

\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n}):=\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q^{n},(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ})\in[0,\infty].

It is shown that the above infimum is attained by a subprobability measure
P_{n}^{\star}
(unique up to
Q
null sets) called the
reverse information projection
(RIPr) of
Q^{n}
onto
(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}

[
14
]
.
One can show that for
n\geq 1
:

\frac{1}{n}\,\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})\leq\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

We will later show that in general the above inequality can be strict, but that equality holds under some simple structural assumptions on
\mathcal{P}
, even without assuming a dominating reference measure.

Definition 2.2
.

For
M,N\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
we denote the KL divergence as

\mathrm{KL}(M\|N):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{X}}\log\left(\frac{dM}{dN}\right)dM,&\text{if }M\ll N,\\[6.00006pt]
+\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For
p,q\in[0,1]
, the binary KL divergence is defined as

D(p\|q):=p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\log\frac{(1-p)}{(1-q)},

with the following conventions: if
q\in[0,1]
,
0\log(0/q):=0
,
and if
p>0
, we take
p\log(p/0):=+\infty
, while
\log 0:=-\infty
. If
Z:\Omega\to[0,\infty]
is measurable and
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega)
, then

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\log Z]:=\sup_{M\geq 1}\int_{\Omega}\log(Z\wedge M)\,d\mu\in[-\infty,\infty].

Weak convergence of measures is denoted by
\Rightarrow
.
The map
(M,N)\mapsto\mathrm{KL}(M\|N)
from
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})\times\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
to
[0,\infty]
is lower semicontinuous for the weak topology on both coordinates.

To further clarify,
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
is endowed with the weak topology, which is the coarsest topology for which the
P\mapsto\int fdP
is continuous for every bounded continuous
f\in C_{b}(\mathsf{X})
. Equivalently,
P_{n}\Rightarrow P
if and only if for every

\int f\,dP_{n}\to\int f\,dP.

We say that a subset
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
is weakly compact if it is compact in this topology. Because
\mathsf{X}
is Polish, the weak topology on
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
is metrizable; for example, by the bounded Lipschitz metric. From this, one can interpret weak compactness sequentially. That is, every sequence
(P_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{P}
has a subsequence
P_{n_{k}}\Rightarrow P
for some
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Prokhorov’s theorem implies that

\mathcal{P}
being weakly closed and tight (for every

such that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P(K_{\varepsilon}^{c})\leq\varepsilon
). Examples of weakly compact
\mathcal{P}
include all laws supported on a compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(eg: distributions on
[0,1]
with mean
\leq 0.5
), or exponential families where the parameter is in a closed set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Our proof of asymptotic exactness will need a way to choose a weak neighborhood of

\Phi(R):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
does not drop substantially below
\Phi(Q)=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})

\Phi

\mathcal{P}
.
We say that
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
lower semicontinuous at

\Phi
is lower semicontinuous at
Q
for the weak topology. Meaning that if for every sequence
R_{k}\Rightarrow Q
, we have also
\Phi(Q)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\Phi(R_{k})
.
As an important sufficient condition, if
\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact in
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
, then
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
lower semicontinuous at every
Q

We will now clarify some important conventions and the filtrations we are working on. Unless otherwise stated, all the sequential processes in this work are defined on
\Omega=\mathsf{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
with coordinate maps
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
, and we set
\mathcal{F}_{0}:=\{\varnothing,\Omega\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})

. We write
\mathbb{F}:=(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
for the original filtration. We define (strictly) increasing sequences of time indices as

\mathsf{T}:=\Big\{\mathbf{t}=(t_{k})_{k\geq 0}:=0=t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots,\,t_{j}\in\mathbb{N}\text{ for all $j\geq 1$}\Bigr\}.

For
\mathbf{t}\in\mathsf{T}

\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}:=(\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
. Therefore, a
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
stopping time is a map
\tau:\Omega\to\{t_{k}:k\geq 0\}
such that
\{\tau\leq t_{k}\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}
for every
k\geq 0
. Note that when
\mathbf{t}=\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}=\mathbb{F}
, so the blockwise filtrations recover the original filtration as a special case.

More generally, if
\mathbb{G}=(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a filtration on the same space we write
\mathbb{G}\preceq\mathbb{F}
whenever
\mathcal{G}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
. For
\mathbf{t}\in\mathsf{T}
, we write
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbf{t}}:=(\mathcal{G}_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
as the corresponding reduced blockwise filtration.

Definition 2.3

(E-processes (on possibly reduced filtrations))
.

We say that a nonnegative adapted process
E=(E_{s})_{s\in\mathbf{t}}
is an
e
-process for
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbf{t}}
if for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
and every finite-valued
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbf{t}}
-stopping time
\tau
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[E_{\tau}]\leq 1
. The class of all such processes is denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{G}_{\mathbf{t}}}(\mathcal{P})
.
The elements of
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P}):=\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathcal{P})

are simply called
e
-processes
, while those in some generic
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}}(\mathcal{P})
are called
blockwise e-processes
. A blockwise e-process
E
(on some
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
) can be lifted to an e-process (on
\mathbb{F}
) by defining
E_{j}=0
for
j\notin\mathbf{t}
.

An e-process corresponds to the wealth process of a gambler who bets on every observation (technically, they can bet separately against each
P\in\mathcal{P}
, but their net wealth is the infimum of their wealths in each game, see
[
20
]
for details).
A blockwise e-process arises because a gambler does not wish to bet on every observation one by one, but bets on blocks of observations instead. An important class of (blockwise) e-processes are (blockwise) test supermartingales, defined next.

Definition 2.4

(Blockwise test supermartingale)
.

Let
\mathbf{t}=(t_{k})_{k\geq 0}\in\mathsf{T}
. A blockwise test supermartingale for
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
is a nonnegative process
(S_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
such that
S_{t_{0}}=1
, each
S_{t_{k}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}
measurable and for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
we have that for all
k\geq 1
almost surely under
P^{\infty}
,

\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[S_{t_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}]\leq S_{t_{k-1}}.

Due to the optional stopping theorem, every blockwise test supermartingale on
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
is a blockwise
e
-process, hence an element of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}}(\mathcal{P})
.

Definition 2.5

(Wealth processes)

For any subfiltration
\mathbb{G}\preceq\mathbb{F}
we define the class of wealth processes on

\mathcal{W}_{\mathbb{G}}(\mathcal{P}):=\bigcup_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{G}_{\mathbf{t}}}(\mathcal{P}).

Therefore, a wealth process is either an

-process on
\mathbb{G}
or an
e
-process on some reduced block filtration
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbf{t}}
. In the special case of
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{F}
, we write
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P}):=\mathcal{W}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathcal{P})
. Each
W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathbb{G}}(\mathcal{P})
is understood together with its indexing filtration.

Wealth processes are named as such because they correspond to the sequences of wealths that any gambler can possibly achieve by defining various betting games in which they can bet against the null
\mathcal{P}
(or more accurately, against
\mathcal{P}^{\infty}
).

Throughout this work, all our statements about processes on the original filtration will refer to an
e
-process on
\mathbb{F}
. All statements about reduced filtrations will refer to a blockwise
e
-process, i.e. an
e
-process on some
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
.

3
Point
Q
: Intrinsic Growth Rate and Sequential Testability

We will now explicate something seemingly contradictory to the literature. Often, the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
is seen as the key quantity in optimal bounds. However, we will show that in general,
e
-processes cannot grow any faster than the
inf-KL
, and the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
may not ever be achieved.

Recall the shorthands of
\mathcal{P}^{n}:=\{P^{n}:P\in\mathcal{P}\}
and
a_{n}:=\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n}):=\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\in[0,\infty]
, where
(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
is the bipolar.
For a fixed horizon
n
, define the
e
-power, i.e. the expected log capital as

\operatorname{EPow}_{n}(Q,\mathcal{P}):=\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E].

The main result of
[
14
]
is the following strong duality, with no restrictions on
Q,\mathcal{P}
.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
14
]
)
.

\operatorname{EPow}_{n}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)
.

We will now explicate a very useful observation about these
n
-step bipolars.

Lemma 3.2
.

For each
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
, the sequence
(a_{n}(Q))_{n\geq 1}
is superadditive. Meaning for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{n+m}(Q)\geq a_{n}(Q)+a_{m}(Q)
. So it follows that by Fekete’s lemma,

d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(Q)=\sup_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(Q)\in[0,\infty].

We will now present an impossibility result for
e
-processes on any filtration
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{t}=(t_{k})_{k\geq 0}\in\mathsf{T}
and let
W=(W_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}}(\mathcal{P})
be a blockwise
e
-process for
\mathcal{P}
. Now, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, recall that
a_{n}:=\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\in[0,\infty]
. Then it follows that for every
k\geq 1
we have that

\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q^{t_{k}}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]\leq a_{t_{k}}.

And so consequently we have that

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}.

At this point, we are ready to present our achievability proposition, proving that an
e
-process can attain the
inf-KL
rate arbitrarily closely. In particular, let us denote the asymptotic
inf-KL
rate by

\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})\in[0,\infty].

And, if this limit does indeed exist, we will denote it by
d_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}
. In words: what our next proposition tells us that
\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}
is not at all an upper bound only, rather it is achievable arbitrarily closely by a blockwise
e
-process.

Proposition 3.4
.

Assume that
\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}<\infty
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\mathbf{t}=(t_{k})_{k\geq 0}\in\mathsf{T}
and a blockwise test supermartingale
(E_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
such that under
Q^{\infty}
,

\boxed{\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]\geq\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}-\varepsilon.}

And moreover,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]\quad Q^{\infty}\text{-a.s.}

Thus, suppose
d_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})
exists and is finite. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a blockwise test supermartingale on some
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
with asymptotic growth rate at least
d_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}-\varepsilon
both almost surely and in expectation.

Proof.

Take some
\varepsilon>0
and assume that
\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}<\infty
. Just for notational convenience, recall we let
a_{m}:=\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{m},\mathcal{P}^{m})
. Necessarily by definition of
\limsup
and the fact that
d_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}<\infty
, there exists some integer block length
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{m}<\infty
and that

\frac{1}{m}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{m},\mathcal{P}^{m})\geq\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(1)

The first thing we must do is choose a single
m
-sized block
e
-variable with a near optimal e-power. By Proposition
3.1
at horizon
m
,
a_{m}=\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E]
. Therefore, there exists some
E^{\star}:\mathsf{X}^{m}\to(0,\infty)
with
E^{\star}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\geq a_{m}-\frac{\varepsilon m}{2}.

(2)

Note here that we are just using the fact that a supremum can be approximated arbitrarily closely. We now need to build an
e
-process by repeating this same
m
-block e variable. To this end, we will partition the coordinates into consecutive blocks of length
m
. Then, block
k
consists of
(X_{(k-1)m+1},\dots,X_{km})
. And, define
t_{k}:=km
, then
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}:=\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{t_{k}})
is
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
. Now define
E_{t_{0}}:=1
and for
k\geq 1
,

E_{t_{k}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}E^{\star}\bigl(X_{(j-1)m+1},\dots,X_{jm}\bigr).

We claim this satisfies the definition of blockwise
e
-process for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Because of the fact that
P^{\infty}=P^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
is iid, it must be the case that the
k
-th block is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}
and distributed as
P^{m}
. Hence for all
k\geq 1
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[E_{t_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}\left[E_{t_{k-1}}\cdot E^{\star}\bigl(X_{(k-1)m+1},\dots,X_{km}\bigr)\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=E_{t_{k-1}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P^{m}}[E^{\star}].

Now, note that
E^{\star}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
. As such, it is clear that we have by definition that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P^{m}}[E^{\star}]\leq 1
. So in particular,
\mathbb{E}_{P^{m}}[E^{\star}]\leq 1
. Therefore we get that

\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[E_{t_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}]\leq E_{t_{k-1}}.

Therefore, clearly,
(E_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
is a test supermartingale for
\mathcal{P}
, i.e. it satisfies Definition
2.4
. Now, we’re going to compute the expected log growth rate under
Q^{\infty}
. Under
Q^{\infty}=Q^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
, we know that the blocks are iid with law
Q^{m}
. Given this, for
j\geq 1
define the iid random variables

Y_{j}:=\log E^{\star}\bigl(X_{(j-1)m+1},\dots,X_{jm}\bigr).

So,
\log E_{t_{k}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}Y_{j}
and
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[Y_{j}]=k\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]
. If we divide by
t_{k}=km
we get that

\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]=\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}].

(3)

Now, then applying (
2
) followed by (
1
) we get that

\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\geq\frac{a_{m}}{m}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\geq\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}-\varepsilon.

Taken in conjunction with (
3
), this proves

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]\geq\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}-\varepsilon.

It remains to now prove the almost sure convergence of the realized log growth rate. Because
a_{m}<\infty
and
E^{\star}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
, Proposition
3.1
entails
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\leq a_{m}<\infty
. Therefore, taking this with (
2
),
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\in\mathbb{R}
. So,
Y_{1}=\log E^{\star}(X_{1:m})
is integrable. Meaning,
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[|Y_{1}|]<\infty
. By the strong law of large numbers applied to the iid sequence
(Y_{j})
we get that almost surely under
Q^{\infty}
,

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}Y_{j}\to\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[Y_{1}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}].

Recall that
t_{k}=km
. Dividing by
m
proves that almost surely under
Q^{\infty}
,

\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}\to\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}].

Therefore, alongside (
3
), we have shown that the almost sure limit indeed equals the expected log limit, proving the claim.
∎

We can summarize the intrinsic maximal expected-log growth with the following theorem. Since each
W\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})
actually lies in
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}}(\mathcal{P})\text{ for some }\mathbf{t}\in\mathsf{T}
, we define its point-alternative expected log-growth functional as

\mathcal{R}_{Q}(W):=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}],\text{ where }\mathbf{t}\in\mathsf{T}\text{ is such that }W\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}}(\mathcal{P}).

Recall that here,
\mathbf{t}
is the unique block schedule that is associated with
W
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}\in[0,\infty]
. Then it follows that

\boxed{\sup_{W\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(W)=\ \overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}.}

Proof.

We will start with the upper bound. Suppose that
W\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})
. If
W\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathcal{P})
, we can apply Lemma
3.3
with
t_{k}=k
. If
W\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}}(\mathcal{P})
for some
\mathbf{t}=(t_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, we can apply Lemma
3.3
using this
\mathbf{t}
. In either case it follows that

R_{Q}(W)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}=\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}.

If we take the supremum over all
W\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})
, that proves the upper bound. We will now prove the
lower bound,
i.e. the achievability. We are first going to handle the case where
\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}=+\infty
. To this end, let
M>0
be arbitrarily chosen. By definition of
\limsup
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{m}=\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{m},\mathcal{P}^{m})\geq mM+1
. Note that this inequality is understood to be in
[0,\infty]
. We emphasize this because it allows the bipolar
a_{m}=+\infty
in particular. Proposition
3.1
yields
a_{m}=\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E]
. So there exists an
e
-variable
E^{\star}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\geq mM
. Let us now partition the data into consecutive blocks of length
m
and set
t_{k}:=km
. Then, define

W_{t_{k}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}E^{\star}\bigl(X_{(j-1)m+1},\dots,X_{jm}\bigr).

We have that
(W_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
is a test blockwise test supermartingale along
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
. Therefore,
W\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})
. Thus under
Q^{\infty}
by independence of the blocks we get

\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]=\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\geq M.

Notice how
M>0
was arbitrary. Hence, the supremum of achievable expected log growth rates is indeed
+\infty
. Now, in the case where
\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}<\infty
, Proposition
3.4
already gives us for every
\varepsilon>0
, a blockwise test supermartingale
W\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})
such that

R_{Q}(W)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]\geq\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}-\varepsilon.

Then, letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
proves the lower bound, completing the proof.
∎

Therefore, Theorem
3.5
tells us that unfortunately the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
isn’t the intrinsic maximal expected log growth rate of
e
-processes in general unfortunately. The right maximal rate is in fact the asymptotic
inf-KL
rate
\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}=\limsup_{n}\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})
. Meaning,
\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}
is always achievable arbitrarily closely and it upper bounds all
e
-processes.
As we will see in our Proposition
5.4
, we have that
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=0
for all
n
, so
\overline{d}_{\textnormal{inf-KL}}=0
. Hence the above upper bound that we showed implies that no
e
-process (in the most general sense) can have positive asymptotic expected log growth under
Q^{\infty}
. And here we see the constant process
E_{t_{k}}\equiv 1
achieves rate
0
. So we can have cases where the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
while the maximum achievable
e
-process rate is
0
.

Remark 3.6
.

Now, note that the supremum over wealth processes will be equal to the supremum over
e
-processes on the original filtration
\mathbb{F}
. In particular, more generally, our zeroing argument will work for any subfiltration
\mathbb{G}\preceq\mathbb{F}
. Suppose that
W=(W_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{G}_{\mathbf{t}}}(\mathcal{P})
is a blockwise
e
-process for some
\mathbf{t}=(t_{k})_{k\geq 0}\in\mathsf{T}
. Define the zeroed process
\widehat{W}=(\widehat{W}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
by

\widehat{W}_{n}:=\begin{cases}W_{t_{k}},&n=t_{k}\,\text{ for some $k\geq 0$},\\
0,&n\notin\{t_{k}:k\geq 0\}.\end{cases}

It follows then that
\widehat{W}\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{G}}(\mathcal{P})
. Indeed, suppose that
\tau
is any finite valued
\mathbb{G}
-stopping time. Then, the ceiling time
\tau^{+}:=\min\{t_{k}:t_{k}\geq\tau\}
will again be a
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbf{t}}
-stopping rule. So, we will have
\widehat{W}_{\tau}\leq W_{\tau^{+}}
pointwise. Therefore, for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
,

\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[\widehat{W}_{\tau}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[W_{\tau^{+}}]\leq 1.

Thus, through zeroing, we can embed every blockwise
e
-process on
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbf{t}}
into an
e
-process on
\mathbb{G}
. In particular, with
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{F}
and our convention that takes
\log 0=-\infty
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log\widehat{W}_{n}]=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}].

Now since every
e
-process on
\mathbb{G}
is already a wealth process on
\mathbb{G}
, this proves that

\sup_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathbb{G}}(\mathcal{P})}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(W)=\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{G}}(\mathcal{P})}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{n}].

In particular, for the original filtration we have

\boxed{\sup_{W\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})}\mathcal{R}_{Q}(W)=\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{n}].}

One other idea would be to extend the blockwise
e
-process with the left-constant interpolation of
\widehat{W}_{n}:=W_{t_{k}}
whenever
t_{k}\leq n<t_{k+1}
. Unfortunately, this will fail to be an
e
-process on
\mathbb{F}
. The issue is that a
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time can use additional information that is revealed inside the current block and then decide to stop immediately or (strategically) wait until the next block time. We will show a situation where this happens in the following example.

Example 3.7
.

Let
\mathsf{X}=\{0,1\}
. Let
P\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1/2)
. Let the block times be
t_{0}=0,t_{1}=2,t_{2}=4
respectively. Now define
W_{0}:=1
,
W_{2}:=1
, and
W_{4}:=2\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathsf{X}_{3}=1\}}
. We claim that
(W_{t_{k}})
is an
e
-process along
\{0,2,4\}
: a block-wise
e
-process. To see this, suppose
\sigma
is any stopping rule taking values in
\{0,2,4\}
. Then,
\{\sigma=4\}=\{\sigma>2\}\in\mathcal{F}_{2}
. Now,
W_{4}
depends only on
\mathsf{X}_{3}
, which is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{2}
. Further,
\mathbb{E}_{P}[W_{4}]=1
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[W_{\sigma}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma=0\}}W_{0}+\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma=2\}}W_{2}+\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma=4\}}W_{4}]

\displaystyle=P(\sigma=0)+P(\sigma=2)+\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma=4\}}W_{4}]

\displaystyle=P(\sigma=0)+P(\sigma=2)+P(\sigma=4)\mathbb{E}_{P}[W_{4}]

\displaystyle=1.

Therefore, it follows that the block-wise
e
-property holds. In particular,
(W_{t_{k}})
is an
e
-process on the reduced filtration. Let us now form the left-constant interpolation
\widehat{W}_{n}:=W_{t_{k}}
for
t_{k}\leq n<t_{k+1}
. Then,
\widehat{W}_{3}=W_{2}=1
and
\widehat{W}_{4}=W_{4}=2\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{3}=1\}}
. Now consider the
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time

\tau:=\begin{cases}3,&\mathsf{X}_{3}=0,\\
4,&\mathsf{X}_{3}=1.\end{cases}

This will be an
(\mathcal{F}_{n})
-stopping rule. In addition,

\widehat{W}_{\tau}=\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathsf{X}_{3}=0\}}\widehat{W}_{3}+\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathsf{X}_{3}=1\}}\widehat{W}_{4}=\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathsf{X}_{3}=0\}}+2\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathsf{X}_{3}=1\}}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{P}[\widehat{W}_{\tau}]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 1+\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2=\frac{3}{2}>1.

It follows that
\widehat{W}
cannot be a
e
-process on the original filtration
\mathbb{F}
.

In Example
3.7
, we are viewing what occurs if one “peeks” into the next block. Upon seeing that
X_{3}=1
, one can wait until time
4
to capitalize on the larger
W_{4}
. However, if
X_{3}=0
, one can stop immediately at time
3
, keeping the previous capital
W_{2}
. The blockwise
e
-process definition does not allow such peeking into the block and then choosing. This is why our embedding of the
e
-processes on
\mathbb{F}
is doing so with zero off the block times, instead of holding it constant between the times.

3.1
A necessary condition for sequential testability

Suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is a class of probability measures on some measurable space and that
Q
is any probability measure. In particular, we will allow
Q\in\mathcal{P}
, which will correspond to
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=0
.
For each
n
, we let
\mathcal{P}^{n}:\{P^{n}:P\in\mathcal{P}\}
will be the corresponding i.i.d. product class on
n
samples, and
(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
is its bipolar. We recall a lemma that gives intuition on the event probabilities over the bipolar, proved in
[
18
]
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{S}
is a nonempty set of probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
and let
A\in\mathcal{F}
. Then it follows that

\sup_{R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}}R(A)=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{S}}P(A).

Now with this defined, we will now present a lemma that tells us that if
Q^{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
for all
n
, then power one tests are in fact impossible.

Lemma 3.9
.

Assume that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have that
Q^{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
on
(\mathsf{X}^{n},\mathcal{B}^{\otimes n})
. Then for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
and every stopping time
\tau
,
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
implies that
Q^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
.
Said differently, if a power-one test exists for
Q
against
\mathcal{P}
, then it must be the case that for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
Q^{k}\notin(\mathcal{P}^{k})^{\circ\circ}
.

Proof.

By definition for each
n
, the event
\{\tau\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
depends only on the first
n
samples. Hence
Q^{\infty}(\tau\leq n)=Q^{n}(\tau\leq n)
. Now since
Q^{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
, Lemma
3.8
gives

Q^{n}(\tau\leq n)\leq\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{n}(\tau\leq n)\leq\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha.

Now taking the
\sup_{n}
gives us by continuity of measure that
Q^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=\sup_{n}Q^{\infty}(\tau\leq n)\leq\alpha
.
∎

Interestingly, non-membership in the bipolar will lead to a
\mathrm{KL}
gap that is strictly positive. We will formalize this idea in Proposition
5.3
. That is, we will show that for every nonempty
\mathcal{S}
and
Q\notin S^{\circ\circ}
,
\inf_{R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|R)>0
. However, for us to better understand this, we will defer this proof until we prove that the bipolar is closed under the total variation limits in Lemma
5.1
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Take some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and let
\tau
be an
(\mathcal{F}_{n})
-stopping time. Assume that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
and that
Q^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=1
. Then, it must follow that there exists a time
N<\infty
such that for all
n\geq N
,
Q^{n}\notin(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
.

Proof.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let us define the event
B_{n}:=\{\tau\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
. Note that
(B_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is increasing and that
\bigcup_{n\geq 1}B_{n}=\{\tau<\infty\}
. As a result, we have

Q^{\infty}(B_{n})=Q^{\infty}(\tau\leq n)\uparrow Q^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=1.

(4)

That means there exists some
N
such that for all
n\geq N
,

Q^{\infty}(B_{n})>\alpha.

(5)

Having done this, take some
n\geq 1
and note that for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
, we have that
P^{\infty}(B_{n})=P^{\infty}(\tau\leq n)\leq P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
. By definition
B_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
and hence depends only on
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
. Therefore, we have that
P^{\infty}(B_{n})=P^{n}(B_{n})
. Therefore,

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{n}(B_{n})\leq\alpha.

(6)

Assume now for the sake of contradiction that for some
n\geq N
we have that
Q^{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
. By definition of
(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
and
B_{n}\in\mathcal{B}^{\otimes n}
, we can use Lemma
3.8
with
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{P}^{n}
. Therefore,
\sup_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}R(B_{n})=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{n}(B_{n})
. In particular because
Q^{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
by assumption, we have that

Q^{n}(B_{n})\leq\sup_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}R(B_{n})=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{n}(B_{n})\leq\alpha.

Note that in the last inequality, we just used (
6
). However, we just argued that
Q^{n}(B_{n})=Q^{\infty}(B_{n})>\alpha
by (
5
). This is a clear contradiction. Hence it certainly must be the case that
Q^{n}\notin(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
for all
n\geq N
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Necessary and Sufficient Conditions for Sequential Testability

Our next corollary will show how non-membership in the bipolar actually propagates forward in
n
. However, to get there and make the proof more straightforward, we will first present a lemma to show that bipolar membership is preserved under marginalization.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
1\leq n\leq k
and let
\pi_{1:n}:\mathsf{X}^{k}\to\mathsf{X}^{n}
be the projection onto the first
n
coordinates. If
R\in(\mathcal{P}^{k})^{\circ\circ}
, then the marginal

R_{1:n}:=R\circ\pi_{1:n}^{-1}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}.

Proof.

Consider some
E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
. Define the map
\widetilde{E}:\mathsf{X}^{k}\to[0,\infty]
by
\widetilde{E}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}):=E(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. If we use the product form
P^{k}=P^{n}\otimes P^{k-n}
we have that for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
\mathbb{E}_{P^{k}}[\widetilde{E}]=\mathbb{E}_{P^{n}}[E]\leq 1
. By Definition
2.1
we have that
\widetilde{E}\in(\mathcal{P}^{k})^{\circ}
. Since by assumption
R\in(\mathcal{P}^{k})^{\circ\circ}
,
\mathbb{E}_{R}[\widetilde{E}]\leq 1
. But notice how
\mathbb{E}_{R}[\widetilde{E}]=\mathbb{E}_{R_{1:n}}[E]
by definition of the marginal. Thus,
\mathbb{E}_{R_{1:n}}[E]\leq 1
for every
E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
, which is exactly in fact the condition that
R_{1:n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
, completing the proof.
∎

Now, with this lemma shown, the proof of the corollary that nonmembership propagates forward in
n
will be fairly simple.

Corollary 3.12
.

If
Q^{n}\not\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
for some
n
, then for every
k\geq n
, we have that
Q^{k}\not\in(\mathcal{P}^{k})^{\circ\circ}
.

Proof.

Take some
k\geq n
. Let us assume to the contrary that
Q^{k}\in(\mathcal{P}^{k})^{\circ\circ}
. Thanks to Lemma
3.11
, we have that the marginal of
Q^{k}
on the first
n
coordinates belongs to
(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
. However, that marginal is exactly
Q^{n}
. So that would mean that
Q^{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
, which contradicts the assumption.
∎

Finally, the last result we will present will specifically show that if we have weak compactness in conjunction with the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}>0
, that necessarily implies eventual non-membership also.

Proposition 3.13
.

Assume that
\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact and that
d:=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
. Then, there exists some
N<\infty
such that for all
n\geq N
,
Q^{n}\notin(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
.

Proof.

Because of the fact that
\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact by assumption, we can immediately use the fact that
[
18
, Lemma 3]
tells us that
\Phi(R):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
is weakly lower semicontinuous everywhere. And in particular, it is at
Q
. Now, since
d=\Phi(Q)\in(0,\infty)
, thanks to Theorem
4.1
we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}=d
. Let us now choose
N
such that for all
n\geq N
, we have that
a_{n}/n\geq d/2
. Therefore,

a_{n}\geq\frac{d}{2}n>0.

(7)

Now consider some
n\geq N
. If
Q^{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
, that would mean that
Q^{n}
is feasible in the infimum defining
a_{n}
. Therefore that means that
a_{n}=\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\leq\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|Q^{n})=0
. But this contradicts (
7
). So,
Q^{n}\notin(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
for all
n\geq N
.
∎

All that we have developed so far leads to this theorem on necessary and sufficient characterizations of existence of power-one sequential tests for arbitrary composite nulls versus point alternatives.

Theorem 3.14
.

The following statements are equivalent.

1.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists a level-
\alpha
sequential test of
\mathcal{P}
against
Q
with power one.

2.

There exists some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Q^{k}\notin(\mathcal{P}^{k})^{\circ\circ}
.

3.

There exists some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{k}(Q)>0
.

4.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)>0
.

And moreover, if these statements hold and
k
is as in statement
(2)
above, for every
n\geq k
,
Q^{n}\notin(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
and
a_{n}(Q)>0
.

An important point to make is that in Theorem
3.14
, we do not require the one step condition, i.e.
a_{1}(Q)>0
. We can see this with a counter-example as follows: this is sufficient, but not necessary.

Example 3.15
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{X}=\{0,1\}
. Let
\mathcal{P}=\{\delta_{0},\delta_{1}\}
and
Q=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1}
. Then
a_{1}(Q):=\inf_{R\in\mathcal{P}^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|R)=0
since
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{\circ\circ}
. However, suppose that
A:=\{(0,1),(1,0)\}\subseteq\mathsf{X}^{2}
. Then, it follows that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{2}(A)=0
, while
Q^{2}(A)=1/2
. Thanks to Lemma
3.8
, it follows that
Q^{2}\notin(\mathcal{P}^{2})^{\circ\circ}
, and hence
a_{2}(Q)>0
. Therefore, thanks to Theorem
3.14
, a power-one sequential test exists. Therefore, the correct assumption should be that there is positivity at some horizon
k
, namely
d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
.

4
When the Bipolar Rate Equals
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}

For each
n
, let us define the class
\mathcal{P}^{n}:=\{P^{n}:P\in\mathcal{P}\}
on
(\mathsf{X}^{n},\mathcal{B}^{\otimes n})
. Now let
(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
be the bipolar. Now define

\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n}):=\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\in[0,\infty].

Thanks to
[
14
]
, the infimum is always attained. Thus we can denote any minimizer as
P_{n}^{\star}
and call it therefore the RIPr of
Q^{n}
onto
(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
. We will now present our main result of when the
inf-KL
and
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
coincide.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
. Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
be nonempty. Assume that
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)<\infty
and that
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
lower semicontinuous at
Q
. Then it follows that

\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}(Q,\mathcal{P}).}

And, this conclusion will hold whenever
\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact.

To prove this theorem, we will use a KL lower bound that follows from the data processing inequality on a binary
\sigma
-field.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be measurable and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega)
and let
A\in\mathcal{F}
. Now, define
p:=\mu(A)
and
q:=\nu(A)
. Then it follows that

\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu)\geq p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}.

In addition, an important lemma for us will be a convex-set empirical upper bound. Note that this is essentially a nonasymptotic version of Sanov, and we can attribute it to Csiszár
[
7
]
. Importantly, his assumption of almost complete convexity holds with our assumptions. It is crucial to note that under convexity alone, the lemma would not hold, as explained by Csiszár in his work.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
and let
C\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
be convex and weakly closed. Define
A_{n}:=\{\widehat{Q}_{n}\in C\}\subseteq\mathsf{X}^{n}
. Then it follows that for every
n\geq 1
,

P^{n}(A_{n})\ \leq\ \exp\Bigl(-n\inf_{R\in C}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)\Bigr).

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Write the shorthand
d:=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)\in[0,\infty)
. Note that if
d=0
, then for every
n
our universal lower upper bound below will imply that
0\leq\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})\leq nd=0
. So it would follow that
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=0
for all
n
and so our desired limit would hold. Hence we can assume going forward without loss of generality that
d>0
. We will first prove universal upper bound. Note that this holds under no assumptions and holds for any measurable space (we do not need to assume Polish structure). Now, because
\mathcal{P}^{n}\subseteq(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
,

\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\leq\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|P^{n}).

We also have that

\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|P^{n})=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}n\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)=n\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P),

Therefore, for all
n
we have that
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})\leq nd
, and hence

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})\leq d.

We now show the matching lower bound to show tightness. Take a particular
\varepsilon\in\bigl(0,\tfrac{d}{4}\bigr)
. Now define
\Phi(R):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
. Note that our second assumption in this theorem entails that
\Phi
be lower semicontinuous at
Q
. Or equivalently, for this specific
\varepsilon
, there exists a weakly open neighborhood
O_{\varepsilon}
of
Q
such that
\Phi(R)\geq d-\varepsilon
for all
R\in O_{\varepsilon}
. Now consider a particular bounded Lipschitz metric
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
on
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
, which necessarily metrizes the weak topology on Polish
\mathsf{X}
. Because
O_{\varepsilon}
is weakly open and contains
Q
, there exists
r>0
such that the open
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
-ball

B_{r}^{\mathrm{BL}}(Q):=\{R\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X}):d_{\mathrm{BL}}(R,Q)<r\}

is contained in
O_{\varepsilon}
. In addition set
\delta:=r/2
and define the closed ball

C:=\overline{B}_{\delta}^{\mathrm{BL}}(Q):=\{R\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X}):d_{\mathrm{BL}}(R,Q)\leq\delta\}.

Clearly, then
C
is weakly closed. And because
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
is induced by a norm on signed measures, it follows that
C
is convex. In addition,
C\subseteq B_{r}^{\mathrm{BL}}(Q)\subseteq O_{\varepsilon}
, so
\inf_{R\in C}\Phi(R)\geq d-\varepsilon
. Define the event
A_{n}:=\{\widehat{Q}_{n}\in C\}\subseteq\mathsf{X}^{n}
. Since
d_{\mathrm{BL}}(\widehat{Q}_{n},Q)\to 0
almost surely under
Q^{\infty}
, it follows that
Q^{n}(A_{n})\to 1
. Define
\beta_{n}:=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{n}(A_{n})
. Thanks to Lemma
4.3
, for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
P^{n}(A_{n})\leq\exp\Bigl(-n\inf_{R\in C}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)\Bigr)
. Therefore taking the supremum, we have

\beta_{n}\leq\exp\Bigl(-n\inf_{R\in C}\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)\Bigr)=\exp\Bigl(-n\inf_{R\in C}\Phi(R)\Bigr)\leq\exp\bigl(-n(d-\varepsilon)\bigr).

Equivalently for all
n\geq 1
,

\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{\beta_{n}}\geq d-\varepsilon.

(8)

Now take any particular
n\geq 1
and any
R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
. By Lemma
3.8
applied to
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{P}^{n}
and the event
A_{n}
we have that
R(A_{n})\leq\sup_{S\in\mathcal{P}^{n}}S(A_{n})=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{n}(A_{n})=\beta_{n}
. Now let
p_{n}:=Q^{n}(A_{n})
and
q_{n}:=R(A_{n})
. Then
p_{n}\to 1
and
q_{n}\leq\beta_{n}
. Thus by Lemma
4.2
we get that

\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\geq p_{n}\log\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}+(1-p_{n})\log\frac{1-p_{n}}{1-q_{n}}.

(9)

We can use the fact that
\log(1-q_{n})\leq 0
and therefore that
-(1-p_{n})\log(1-q_{n})\geq 0
to give us a simpler bound. That is,

\displaystyle p_{n}\log\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}+(1-p_{n})\log\frac{1-p_{n}}{1-q_{n}}

\displaystyle=p_{n}\log p_{n}+(1-p_{n})\log(1-p_{n})-p_{n}\log q_{n}-(1-p_{n})\log(1-q_{n})

\displaystyle\geq-H(p_{n})+p_{n}\log\frac{1}{q_{n}},

(10)

where
H(p):=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)
is the binary entropy. Now since
p_{n}\to 1
, it must be the case that
H(p_{n})\to 0
. Also, since
q_{n}\leq\beta_{n}
, it must be the case that
\log(1/q_{n})\geq\log(1/\beta_{n})
. Together then both (
9
) and (
10
) give us that

\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\geq-H(p_{n})+p_{n}\log\frac{1}{\beta_{n}}.

Dividing by
n
and taking the
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
, and taking the infimum over
R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
gives us for all
n\geq n_{0}(\varepsilon)
therefore that
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})\geq-H(p_{n})+p_{n}\log\frac{1}{\beta_{n}}
. Now let
c_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{\beta_{n}}\geq 0
, which is because
\beta_{n}\leq 1
. Then it follows that

\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})\geq-\frac{H(p_{n})}{n}+p_{n}c_{n}.

Now
H(p_{n})\leq\log 2
. Therefore,
H(p_{n})/n\to 0
, and
p_{n}\to 1
. Now by (
8
), we have that
c_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{\beta_{n}}\geq d-\varepsilon
for all
n
. Therefore,
\liminf_{n\to\infty}c_{n}\geq d-\varepsilon
. Since
c_{n}\geq 0
and
p_{n}\to 1
, it follows that
\liminf p_{n}c_{n}\geq\liminf c_{n}
. Therefore, we get

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})\geq d-\varepsilon.

Recall that
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary. So,
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})\geq d
, completing the proof.
∎

One thing to note is why we need some regularity assumption like lower semicontinuity of
R\mapsto\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
at
Q
. If we did not have this, then there could be a sequence
R_{k}\Rightarrow Q
with
\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R_{k}\|P)
much smaller than
\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)
. Suppose that we are in one of these cases. Then we can easily build very rich classes
\mathcal{P}
which are noncompact and very oscillatory such that the bipolar
(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
becomes large enough to where
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})
grows sublinearly in n, while the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
stays strictly postive. Hence our desired asymptotic equality would fail. This case of failure is the motivation for weak compactness of
\mathcal{P}
or any other condition that implies our lower semicontinuity assumption.

Let us now once again compare the two quantities
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P}):=\inf_{R\in\mathcal{P}^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|R)
and
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)
. We will write,

\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B}):=\{f:\mathsf{X}\to\mathbb{R}:f\text{ is bounded and }\mathcal{B}\text{-measurable}\}.

In addition, we will use the notation
L(Q,f):=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]-\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f)
and
\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f):=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\log\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{f}]
. We will use a shorthand for the very well known
\mathrm{GRO}
value as
R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)
. Of course,
[
14
]
show that the
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})=R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)
. We now show that
R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})}L(Q,f)
.

Now for some particular
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
, let us define

R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q):=\sup_{X\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log X]\in[0,+\infty].

Note that though
R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)
is defined by optimizing over all the
e
-variables, we can represent it by bounded measurable functions. We can have a bounded variational representation of the point alternative value. To this end, for
f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}
define,

\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f):=\log\Bigl(\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{f}]\Bigr),\quad L(Q,f):=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]-\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f).

Since
f
is bounded,
e^{f}
is bounded. Therefore,
\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f)\in\mathbb{R}
is well-defined.

Theorem 4.4
.

For every
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
, we have
R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}}L(Q,f)
. More explicitly,

\sup_{X\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log X]=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]-\log\Bigl(\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{f}]\Bigr)=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Eff}}}H(Q\mid P).

Proof.

First, we start with the simpler direction; we will show that
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}}L(Q,f)\leq R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)
. To begin, take an arbitrarily chosen
f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}
and set
X_{f}:=e^{f-\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f)}
. It follows that for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
we have

\mathbb{E}_{P}[X_{f}]=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[e^{f-\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f)}\right]=e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f)}\,\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{f}]\leq e^{-\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f)}\,\sup_{P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P^{\prime}}[e^{f}]=1.

Thus,
X_{f}\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})
, implying

\displaystyle L(Q,f)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]-\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\log\bigl(e^{f-\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f)}\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log X_{f}]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{X\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log X]

\displaystyle=R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q).

Taking the supremum over all
f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}
gives us that
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}}L(Q,f)\leq R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)
. So the first direction is proved.

It remains to now show that
R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}}L(Q,f)
. To this end, let us take some particular but arbitrary
X\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})
. We need to show that
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log X]\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}}L(Q,f)
. First suppose that
E_{Q}[(\log X)^{-}]=\infty
. In this case, by convention it follows that
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log X]=-\infty
, so the bound follows. Thus, let us assume wlog from this point on that
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\log X)^{-}]<\infty
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let us define the bounded truncation
f_{n}:=(\log X\wedge n)\vee(-n)\in\mathcal{B}_{b}
. Therefore, it follows that,

e^{f_{n}}=\exp\bigl((\log X\wedge n)\vee(-n)\bigr)=(X\wedge e^{n})\vee e^{-n}\leq X+e^{-n},

which holds pointwise on
\Omega
. Therefore, since
X\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})
, we have that for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
,

\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{f_{n}}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}[X+e^{-n}]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]+e^{-n}\leq 1+e^{-n}.

Taking the supremum over all
P\in\mathcal{P}
and then taking the logarithm gives us

\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{n})\leq\log(1+e^{-n}).

(11)

On the other hand, the positive and negative parts of
f_{n}
are simply
f_{n}^{+}=(\log X)^{+}\wedge n
and
f_{n}^{-}=(\log X)^{-}\wedge n
respectively. By the monotone convergence theorem, it follows that
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{n}^{+}]\uparrow\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\log X)^{+}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{n}^{-}]\uparrow\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\log X)^{-}]<\infty
(we assumed earlier wlog that the negative limit is finite). And so therefore it is appropriate for us to subtract these limits. Therefore we get that
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{n}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{n}^{+}]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{n}^{-}]\to\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\log X)^{+}]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\log X)^{-}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log X]
. Hence, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{n}]\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log X].

(12)

Now (
11
) and (
12
) yield

L(Q,f_{n})=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{n}]-\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{n})\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f_{n}]-\log(1+e^{-n})\to\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log X].

Since
f_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{b}
, we therefore get

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}}L(Q,f)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log X].

This holds for every
e
-variable
X
. So, it follows that
R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)=\sup_{X\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{P})}\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\log X]\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}}L(Q,f)
, concluding the proof.
∎

It is interesting to note how the
inf-KL
and
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
relate nonasymptotically. We will show in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.5
.

For every class
\mathcal{P}
of probability measures and every probability measure
Q
, we always have that

\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})\leq\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

If in addition,
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})
is the Borel space of a metric space and
\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact and convex, then

\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

Proof.

We will first show the first part that
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})\leq\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
always holds. We first show that
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{\circ\circ}
. Suppose that
P\in\mathcal{P}
. To show that
P\in\mathcal{P}^{\circ\circ}
, we need to only show that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]\leq 1
for every
X\in\mathcal{P}^{\circ}
. But, this is just the definition of
X\in\mathcal{P}^{\circ}
. Therefore,
P\in\mathcal{P}^{\circ\circ}
, and hence
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{\circ\circ}
. Now let us take the infimum of the
\mathrm{KL}(Q\|\cdot)
over both sets, yielding

\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\inf_{R\in\mathcal{P}^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|R)\leq\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

It remains to now show that if
\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact and convex, then the reverse inequality will hold also. To this end, assume
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})
is the Borel space of a metric space and that
\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact and convex. Thanks to
[
26
]
we know that

\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\sup_{f\in C_{b}(\mathsf{X})}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]-\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\log\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{f}]\right\}=\sup_{f\in C_{b}(\mathsf{X})}L(Q,f).

(13)

On the other hand, using the fact that
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})=R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)
by our previous bounded-representation result we have that

\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})=R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})}L(Q,f).

(14)

Every bounded continuous function is bounded and measurable so
C_{b}(\mathsf{X})\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})
. This enlargement of the function classes can only increase the supremum. Therefore, from (
13
) and (
14
),

\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\sup_{f\in C_{b}(\mathsf{X})}L(Q,f)\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})}L(Q,f)=\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

And, taken together with the bound where we lower bounded the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
, we get that
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. This completes the proof.
∎

5
Counterexamples and Structural Examples

We will first see what happens without regularity. We will show a counterexample of what happens when the second assumption in our Theorem
4.1
fails. But we will first prove that total variation limits lie in the bipolar. This will help us better intuit the counter-example.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be measurable and let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega)
be nonempty. Suppose that in fact there exists a sequence
(S_{m})_{m\geq 1}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
such that
S_{m}\to S
in total variation. Meaning,
\|S_{m}-S\|_{\mathrm{TV}}:=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}|S_{m}(A)-S(A)|\longrightarrow 0
. Then, we have that
S\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
.

Proof.

Let
E\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ}
. Then,
\mathbb{E}_{S_{m}}[E]\leq 1
for all
m
. Now, for
M\geq 1
define the truncation
E^{(M)}:=E\wedge M
so that both
0\leq E^{(M)}\leq M
and
\mathbb{E}_{S_{m}}[E^{(M)}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{S_{m}}[E]\leq 1
. Total variation convergence means convergence of the integrals of bounded measurable functions. Hence we get that

\mathbb{E}_{S}[E^{(M)}]=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{S_{m}}[E^{(M)}]\leq 1.

Now letting
M\to\infty
and applying the monotone convergence theorem, we can see that
\mathbb{E}_{S}[E]\leq 1
. Because this holds for all
E\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ}
, it indeed must be the case that
S\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
.
∎

We now present another proposition that entails that the bipolar contains the total-variation closed convex hull. It’s immediate from our Lemma
5.1
and from Theorem 3.4 of
[
15
]
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be measurable and let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega)
be nonempty. Denote
\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{S})
for its convex hull, the set of all finite convex combinations of elements of
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
denote the total variation distance. Then, it follows that
\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
is convex and closed under
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
-limits. Let us denote
\overline{(\cdot)}^{\mathrm{TV}}
to mean closure in total variation. Then we have that,
\overline{\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{S})}^{\mathrm{TV}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
.

Now, we will present the proof of we deferred earlier: non-membership in the bipolar implies a strictly positive
\mathrm{KL}
gap.

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose that
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
is measurable. Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega)
be nonempty. And let
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega)
. If
Q\notin\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
, then it follows that
\inf_{R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|R)>0
.

Proof.

Thanks to Proposition
5.2
, it follows that
\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
is closed in total-variation limits. This means that it is a closed subset of the metric space
(\mathcal{M}_{1}(\Omega),\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{TV}})
. By definition,
Q\not\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
, so the
\mathrm{TV}
-distance from
Q
to
\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
is strictly positive. Namely,
\delta:=\inf_{R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}}\|Q-R\|_{\mathrm{TV}}>0
. Therefore by Pinsker’s inequality for every
R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
,
\mathrm{KL}(Q\|R)\geq 2\|Q-R\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\geq 2\delta^{2}
. Then, taking the infimum over
R\in\mathcal{S}^{\circ\circ}
proves the claim.
∎

With all this proved, we are now ready to present our proposition. Specifically we will show failure of Theorem
4.1
when the second assumption fails.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\mathsf{X}=[0,1]
with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra and let
Q
be the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let us define
\varepsilon_{k}:=2^{-k}
,
A_{k}:=[0,\varepsilon_{k}]
, and
a_{k}:=\exp(-1/\varepsilon_{k})=\exp(-2^{k})
. Further, define
b_{k}:=\frac{1-\varepsilon_{k}a_{k}}{1-\varepsilon_{k}}\in(1,\infty)
and
\frac{dP_{k}}{dQ}:=a_{k}\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}+b_{k}\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}^{c}}
. Now let
\mathcal{P}:=\{P_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Then the following hold.

1.

First,
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
. Indeed, notice that
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})\geq 1+\tfrac{1}{2}\log\tfrac{1}{2}>1/2.

2.

Second,
\Phi(R):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
is not lower semicontinuous at
Q
for the weak topology. Thus, Theorem
4.1
’s second assumption fails.

3.

Third, for every
n\geq 1
, we have that
Q^{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
and thus
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=0
.

As a consequence, the KL rate identity in Theorem
4.1
unfortunately fails. That is,

\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=0\quad\text{while}\quad\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0.}

Proof.

We will begin our proof by first showing that
P_{k}\Rightarrow Q
and even
P_{k}\to Q
in total variation. Note that
dP_{k}/dQ
is
Q
-ae equal to
a_{k}
on
A_{k}
and
b_{k}
on
A_{k}^{c}
. Therefore we get that

\displaystyle\|P_{k}-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]}\Bigl|\frac{dP_{k}}{dQ}-1\Bigr|dQ

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\varepsilon_{k}(1-a_{k})+(1-\varepsilon_{k})(b_{k}-1)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\varepsilon_{k}(1-a_{k})+\varepsilon_{k}(1-a_{k})\Bigr)=\varepsilon_{k}(1-a_{k})\leq\varepsilon_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0.

Specifically,
P_{k}\Rightarrow Q
. We now show that
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
. For each
k
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{k})

\displaystyle=\int\log\Bigl(\frac{dQ}{dP_{k}}\Bigr)dQ

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{k}\log\frac{1}{a_{k}}+(1-\varepsilon_{k})\log\frac{1}{b_{k}}

\displaystyle=1+(1-\varepsilon_{k})\log\Bigl(\frac{1-\varepsilon_{k}}{1-\varepsilon_{k}a_{k}}\Bigr).

Note that
a_{k}\leq 1
and because of this, we have that
1-\varepsilon_{k}a_{k}\leq 1
. Therefore we get that

\log\Bigl(\frac{1-\varepsilon_{k}}{1-\varepsilon_{k}a_{k}}\Bigr)\geq\log(1-\varepsilon_{k}).

So clearly
\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{k})\geq 1+(1-\varepsilon_{k})\log(1-\varepsilon_{k})
. Note that
\varepsilon_{k}\in(0,1/2]
. And, the function
g(\varepsilon):=1+(1-\varepsilon)\log(1-\varepsilon)
is decreasing on
(0,1/2]
. Because
g^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=-\log(1-\varepsilon)-1<0
. Hence it follows that the minimum over
k\in\mathbb{N}
is attained at
\varepsilon_{1}=1/2
. This gives us that
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{k})\geq 1+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{2}>\frac{1}{2}
. Hence the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
is greater than
0
. We now prove that assumption two fails. Define the same
\Phi(R):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
. For every
k
, choosing
P=P_{k}
gives us
\Phi(P_{k})=0
. However,
P_{k}\Rightarrow Q
while
\Phi(Q)=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
as we just showed. So
\Phi
cannot be lower semicontinuous at
Q
. Let us now finish our proof by showing that
Q^{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
and
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=0
. Take some arbitrary but particular
n\geq 1
. We have

\|P_{k}^{n}-Q^{n}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq 1-(1-\|P_{k}-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}})^{n}\leq n\|P_{k}-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0.

So
P_{k}^{n}\to Q^{n}
in total variation. So we can use Lemma
5.1
respectively with
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{P}^{n}
, giving
Q^{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
. Therefore we get that

\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\leq\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|Q^{n})=0.

So, we have that
\inf\text{-}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=0
for all
n
, hence the rate identity in the theorem will fail.
∎

We will now revisit the example from
[
18
]
. We will now make clear why weak lower semicontinuity is sufficient, but not necessary to get our rate identity also.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\mathsf{X}=[0,1]
on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Let
Q=\delta_{0}
and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define
P_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1/k}
. Then let
\mathcal{P}:=\{P_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Then,

1.

\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\log 2>0
.

2.

\Phi(R)=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(R\|P)
is not weakly lower semicontinuous at
Q
.

3.

For every
n
,
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=n\log 2
, and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}=\log 2=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
.

4.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists a level
\alpha
sequential test with power-one against
Q
.

5.

There exists an e-process
(E_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{F}
for
\mathcal{P}
with exact growth rate
\log 2
under
Q
.

Proof.

All other statements save for the third was proven in
[
18
]
. We will prove that the rate identity still holds, however. Consider some particular
n
and define
A_{n}:=\{x^{n}:x_{1}=\cdots=x_{n}=0\}
. It must be the case that
Q^{n}(A_{n})=1
and for every
k
,
P_{k}^{n}(A_{n})=\bigl(P_{k}(\{0\})\bigr)^{n}=2^{-n}
. Therefore by Lemma
3.8
it follows that
\sup_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}R(A_{n})=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{n}(A_{n})=2^{-n}
. For any
R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
, we can write
q:=R(A_{n})\leq 2^{-n}
and
p:=Q^{n}(A_{n})=1
and then apply Lemma
4.2
to give us that

\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\geq p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}=\log\frac{1}{q}\geq\log\frac{1}{2^{-n}}=n\log 2.

Therefore,
a_{n}\geq n\log 2
. Conversely let us choose any
k
with
R=P_{k}^{n}\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
. Therefore,
a_{n}\leq\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|P_{k}^{n})=n\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{k})=n\log 2
. Hence,
a_{n}=n\log 2
for every

and thus
\frac{1}{n}a_{n}=\log 2
.
∎

A natural question is is weak compactness alone enough to give us a non-asymptotic rate identity? Turns out, the answer is no. We will formalize that in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.6
.

There exists a weakly compact class
\mathcal{P}
and a probability measure
Q
such that,

\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})<\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

Therefore, under weak compactness alone the identity
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
is false in general.

Proof.

Consider the two-point space
X=\{0,1\}
with the discrete
\sigma
-algebra. Define
P_{1}=(1/4,3/4)
,
P_{2}=(3/4,1/4)
,
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},P_{2}\}
, and
Q=(1/2,1/2)
. Clearly, since
\mathcal{P}
is finite, it must be weakly compact. However, it is not convex. We first compute
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. As we already noted, we have in general that

\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})=R_{\mathcal{P}}(Q)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})}L(Q,f).

Because we are on a two-point space, every bounded measurable function
f
is determined by two real numbers,
a=f(0)
and
b=f(1)
. Therefore it follows that

\displaystyle L(Q,f)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]-\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{a+b}{2}-\log\max\left\{\frac{1}{4}e^{a}+\frac{3}{4}e^{b},\,\frac{3}{4}e^{a}+\frac{1}{4}e^{b}\right\}.

Let us upper bound this quantity. The maximum of two numbers is at least their average. Hence,

\displaystyle\max\left\{\frac{1}{4}e^{a}+\frac{3}{4}e^{b},\,\frac{3}{4}e^{a}+\frac{1}{4}e^{b}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\frac{1}{4}e^{a}+\frac{3}{4}e^{b}\right)+\left(\frac{3}{4}e^{a}+\frac{1}{4}e^{b}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(e^{a}+e^{b}).

By the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality,

\frac{1}{2}(e^{a}+e^{b})\geq\sqrt{e^{a}e^{b}}=e^{(a+b)/2}.

Therefore, it follows that

\max\left\{\frac{1}{4}e^{a}+\frac{3}{4}e^{b},\,\frac{3}{4}e^{a}+\frac{1}{4}e^{b}\right\}\geq e^{(a+b)/2}.

Taking logarithms, it follows that
\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(f)\geq\frac{a+b}{2}
. Hence for every
f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})
,
L(Q,f)\leq\frac{a+b}{2}-\frac{a+b}{2}=0
. On the other hand, we can easily get equality here by any constant function (i.e.
f\equiv c
) because
L(Q,f)=c-\log(e^{c})=0
. Thus,
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})}L(Q,f)=0
. Therefore,
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})=0
. We now compute the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. By symmetry,

\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\min\{\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{1}),\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{2})\}=\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{1}).

Let us compute this. Meaning,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1/2}{1/4}+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1/2}{3/4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log 2+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{2}{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{4}{3}

\displaystyle>0.

Therefore,
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
. Thus, even though
\mathcal{P}
was weakly compact, we have
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})=0
while
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
. Hence,

\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})<\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Notice how the class
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},P_{2}\}
is not convex and
Q
lies exactly in between the two nulls,
Q=\frac{1}{2}P_{1}+\frac{1}{2}P_{2}
. Essentially, the bipolar enlargement is convexifying the null and therefore pushing the
inf-KL
down to
0
. On the other hand, the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
still is measuring distance to the original nonconvex class
\{P_{1},P_{2}\}
, and this is strictly positive still. Hence the two conditions that together give
\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
are weakly compactness and convexity. Weak compactness alone is not enough in this non-asymptotic case.

6
Composite Alternatives and the Uniform Growth Rate

In Section
3
, we considered a particular alternative
Q
. We showed that the intrinsic maximal asymptotic expected log growth rate of an
e
-process against a point alternative
Q
is inherently dependent on the per-sample
inf-KL
rate. In this section, we will broaden this idea to a composite alternative class
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
. A single
e
-process must be able to achieve this growth simultaneously for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Just to be complete here, we will recall all notation. Throughout this section,
(\mathsf{X},\mathcal{B})
is Polish,
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
and
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
are nonempty. And,
\mathcal{P}^{n}:=\{P^{n}:P\in\mathcal{P}\}
. Recall that
(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}
is the bipolar associated with nonnegative tests, as in Definition
2.1
. For each
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
we will use the shorthand
a_{n}(Q):=\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})=\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\in[0,\infty]
.

The composite case is much more subtle than the singleton alternative
Q
case. There are at least two composite extensions of
inf-KL
in this case (two natural counterparts of
a_{n}(Q)
). The first is the worst-case pointwise reverse-projection, which we define as follows.

Definition 6.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
worst-case
pointwise reverse-projection value as

a_{n}(\mathcal{Q}):=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}a_{n}(Q)=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\in[0,\infty].

We define its corresponding asymptotic rate as

\overline{d}_{\mathrm{wc}}:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})\in[0,\infty].

One can see that
a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})
is an extension of the
inf-KL
. It is really measuring the smallest reverse-projection value among all the alternatives. We know that every
e
-process needs to work for the worst alternative, hence it follows that
a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})
gives us a universal upper bound on what any single
e
-process may guarantee uniformly over the entire
\mathcal{Q}
. But, the key point here is that this may not even be achievable uniformly, hence a loose bound. Intuitively, the
e
-variable optimal for one
Q
may be worse under another alternative
Q^{\prime}
. To resolve this, it has been shown that the
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
must be inside the actual
e
-power optimization at each horizon
[
9
]
. We will formalize that idea of the robust
e
-power at horizon
n
with the following definition.

Definition 6.2
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let us define

b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P}):=\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E]\in[0,\infty].

The constant
E\equiv 1
lies in
(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
, so
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\geq 0
. We define the corresponding asymptotic robust rate as

\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\in[0,\infty].

One can interpret
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
as the value in a finite-horizon zero-sum game. The statistician will choose an
e
-variable
E
; nature will choose
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. The payoff is
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E]
. However, with the worst-case term
a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})
, the order of optimization is switched here. That is, we maximize for each
Q
and then take the worst
Q
. This is the intuition for why we always have the minimax inequality
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\leq a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})
. And this gap is exactly the difficulty in the composite case, which we will attempt to deal with throughout this section. To begin, we will first formalize the idea that the robust
e
-power is dominated by the worst case
inf-KL
.

Lemma 6.3
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\leq a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})
. And, consequently, we have that
\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}\leq\overline{d}_{\mathrm{wc}}
.

We will formalize what exactly it means for a single
e
-process to grow uniformly over all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Specifically, along those deterministic times where the
e
-process is defined, we will measure the uniformity.

Definition 6.4
.

Each
W\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})
lies in
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}}
for some
t\in\mathsf{T}
. As such, we define the corresponding uniform expected-log growth rate for each
W
as

\mathsf{G}(W;\mathcal{Q}):=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}],\text{ for some }\mathbf{t}\in\mathsf{T}\text{ such that $W\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}}$}.

Here, recall that
\mathbf{t}
is the unique block schedule associated with
W
. Given this, we define the intrinsic maximal uniform rate as

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P}):=\sup_{W\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})}\mathsf{G}(W;\mathcal{Q})\in[0,\infty].

We are ready to present our main theorem. The theorem can be seen as the analogue of Theorem
3.5
for composite alternatives. More importantly, our theorem is completely general, as we do not require compactness, domination, semicontinuity, convexity, or any other such assumption.

Theorem 6.5
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P}
are nonempty. Then it follows that

\boxed{\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\Bigl(\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E]\Bigr)}

Note that in full generality we will not claim a relationship between
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0
and the existence of a uniformly power-one sequential test. We formalize a sufficient condition to do so under weak compactness along with a finite
\mathcal{Q}
corollary.

Remark 6.6
.

By our zeroing argument in Remark
3.6
, we can get the same value of
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
if the supremum is taken over only
e
-processes on the original filtration
\mathbb{F}
. We only use
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})
to keep both
e
-processes on
\mathbb{F}
and blockwise
e
-processes on
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
in one single notation.

Proof of Theorem
6.5
.

We will prove this for wealth processes
W\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})
. Thanks to Remark
3.6
, this is equivalent to using only
e
-processes on
\mathbb{F}
. We will prove the upper bound
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\leq\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}
. We will then show achievability up to some arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
. We will start with the
upper bound
. Consider any
W\in\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{P})
. If
W\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathcal{P})
, we can use the schedule
t_{k}=k
. And if
W\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}}(\mathcal{P})
already, we can use its associated schedule
\mathbf{t}=(t_{k})_{k\geq 0}
. Take some particular
k\geq 1
and set
n=t_{k}
.
W_{t_{k}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}
-measurable and
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}=\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=\pi_{n}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}^{\otimes n})
with
\pi_{n}(\omega):=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
. Because of this, there exists a
\mathcal{B}^{\otimes n}
-measurable map
w_{k}:\mathsf{X}^{n}\to[0,\infty]
such that for all
\omega\in\mathsf{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
W_{t_{k}}(\omega)=w_{k}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
. Because
\tau\equiv t_{k}
is a
(\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}})
-stopping rule, Definition
2.3
gives for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
\mathbb{E}_{P^{n}}[w_{k}]=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[W_{t_{k}}]\leq 1
. Therefore by definition of the bipolar,
w_{k}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
. And so by Definition
6.2
we have

\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log w_{k}]\leq\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E]=b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P}).

Note that
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log w_{k}]
. Thus, for each
k
,

\frac{1}{t_{k}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]\leq\frac{1}{n}b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\frac{1}{t_{k}}b_{t_{k}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P}).

Consequently taking the
\limsup_{k\to\infty}
gives us

\mathsf{G}(W,t;\mathcal{Q})\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}b_{t_{k}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}.

Taking the supremum over all
e
-processes gives
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\leq\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}
. It remains to now prove the
lower bound
; the achievability. We first consider the case where
\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}=+\infty
. In this case, take some arbitrary
M>0
. By definition of
\limsup
, there exists some
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
b_{m}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\geq mM+1
that is in
[0,\infty]
. By definition of
b_{m}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
, there exists some
E^{\star}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
such that
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\geq mM
. Hence, let us now repeat this same block construction that we had in Proposition
3.4
. Only this time, we will use
E^{\star}
. Let us also define
t_{k}:=km
and

W_{t_{k}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}E^{\star}\bigl(X_{(j-1)m+1},\dots,X_{jm}\bigr).

Note that
E^{\star}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
, so we can use our same conditional expectation calculation that we did in Proposition
3.4
. If we do this, we can clearly see that
W_{t_{k}}
is a
e
-process for
\mathcal{P}
. And moreover, for any
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
because we have independence of blocks under
Q^{\infty}
we have that

\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]=k\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\geq kmM=t_{k}M.

Therefore,
\mathsf{G}(W,t;\mathcal{Q})\geq M
. And since
M>0
was arbitrary it follows that
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=+\infty=\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}
in this case. We will now consider the other case, where
\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}<\infty
. Take some
\varepsilon>0
. By definition of
\limsup
we know that there exists some
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\frac{1}{m}b_{m}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\geq\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(15)

By definition of
b_{m}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
, there exists some
E^{\star}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
such that

\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\geq b_{m}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})-\frac{\varepsilon m}{2}.

(16)

Let us now build the same product test supermartingale with
t_{k}=km
and
W_{t_{k}}=\prod_{j\leq k}E^{\star}(\text{block }j)
. Then, for each of the
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
we have that

\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]=k\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\geq k\inf_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\prime m}}[\log E^{\star}].

If we just take the infimum over
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
on the left hand side and use (
16
), we get

\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]\geq k\Bigl(b_{m}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})-\frac{\varepsilon m}{2}\Bigr).

Then dividing by
t_{k}=km
gives us that

\frac{1}{t_{k}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]\geq\frac{1}{m}b_{m}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Now, if we take the
\limsup_{k\to\infty}
, the rhs is unchanged. So doing this followed by applying (
15
) gives us that
\mathsf{G}(W,t;\mathcal{Q})\geq\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}-\varepsilon
. Recall
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary. Hence,
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\geq\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}
. Together with the upper bound, this tells us that
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}
. And, the inequality
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\leq\overline{d}_{\mathrm{wc}}
is immediate from Lemma
6.3
. Finally, thanks to Remark
6.6
, the supremum will not change if we take it over all
e
-processes rather than the blockwise ones.
∎

Having shown this, let us explain a little bit on the almost sure growth under each
Q
. In this above achievability construction, take some
m
and
E^{\star}
. For each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
where
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[|\log E^{\star}|]<\infty
, the block increments
\log E^{\star}(\text{block }j)
are i.i.d. integrable under
Q^{\infty}
. Therefore, by the strong law we have that with probability 1 under
Q^{\infty}
,

\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log W_{t_{k}}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}].

Thus, whenever we have integrability, the achieved uniform expected-log rate is also realized almost surely under
Q
along the block times.

6.1
When do the robust and worst-case pointwise rates actually match?

Theorem
6.5
completely characterizes the maximal expected log growth rate in terms of the robust finite-horizon values
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
. That being said, this
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
can indeed be arduous to compute. A more explicit and intuitive quantity is the worst-case pointwise reverse-projection
a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})
from Definition
6.1
. In general, this will only give us an upper bound. Meaning, by Lemma
6.3
, we have that
b_{n}\leq a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})
always. This gap, therefore, is a serious composite-alternative mini-max gap. However, we will give broad conditions under which said gap is going to vanish at the asymptotic per-sample rate level. To begin, we will start with the finite-class case. That is, for finite
\mathcal{Q}
, this asymptotic mini-max gap is negligible.

Proposition 6.7
.

Assume that
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{M}\}
is finite. Then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\eta>0
that,
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\geq a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})-\log M-\eta
. Consequently,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(\mathcal{Q}).

Proof.

Take some particular but arbitrarily chosen
n\in\mathbb{N}
throughout this proof. The first case we will consider is the case where
a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})=+\infty
. Clearly, then
a_{n}(Q_{i})=+\infty
for every
i\in\{1,\dots,M\}
. Now, let us fix some
L>0
. For each
i
, since
\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{i}^{n}}[\log E]=+\infty
, we may choose some
E_{i}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{Q_{i}^{n}}[\log E_{i}]\geq L+\log M.

Now let
E:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}E_{i}
. By linearity of expectation under each
P^{n}
, it follows that
E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
. In addition, we have that
E\geq\frac{1}{M}E_{i}
pointwise. So
\log E\geq\log E_{i}-\log M
pointwise. Therefore, for each
i
we get that

\mathbb{E}_{Q_{i}^{n}}[\log E]\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q_{i}^{n}}[\log E_{i}]-\log M\geq L.

So,
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E]\geq L
. Hence,
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\geq L
. But,
L
was arbitrary, which means that
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=+\infty
, and so therefore the claim inequality is trivial. It remains to now prove the case where
a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})<\infty
. Let
d:=a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})=\min_{1\leq i\leq M}a_{n}(Q_{i})
and take some particular
\eta>0
. For each
i
,
a_{n}(Q_{i})\geq d
. Therefore by the definition of the supremum, there must exist
E_{i}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{Q_{i}^{n}}[\log E_{i}]\geq d-\eta.

Now define
E:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}E_{i}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
. As we already argued in the first case, we have that
\log E\geq\log E_{i}-\log M
pointwise. Hence for each
i
that we have that

\mathbb{E}_{Q_{i}^{n}}[\log E]\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q_{i}^{n}}[\log E_{i}]-\log M\geq d-\eta-\log M.

Now if we take the
\inf_{i\leq M}
we get that
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E]\geq a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})-\log M-\eta
. Then, finally to conclude we can just take the
\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}}
over the left hand side, giving
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\geq a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})-\log M-\eta
.
∎

Notice how in this above proposition we have shown that not knowing which
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
holds creates a cost at most an additive
\log M
in the finite horizon. And, this disappears in such per-sample asymptotics. However, the question becomes what about in the case of possibly uncountable
\mathcal{Q}
. That will be the motivation for our following theorem under which a sub-exponential complexity covering condition makes the same conclusion hold. In other words, if we have subexponential uniform approximability, that will imply that
b_{n}
and
a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})
will match asymptotically.

Theorem 6.8
.

Assume that there exist sequences
(\Delta_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset[0,\infty)
and integers
(N_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that the following hold.

1.

For each
n
, there exist
E_{n,1},\dots,E_{n,N_{n}}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
with the uniform approximation property; meaning, that,

\forall Q\in\mathcal{Q},\quad\exists j\in\{1,\dots,N_{n}\}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E_{n,j}]\geq a_{n}(Q)-\Delta_{n}.

2.

Second, the approximation error and complexity are sublinear. That is,

\frac{\Delta_{n}}{n}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\log N_{n}}{n}\longrightarrow 0.

Then it once again follows that,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})

Proof.

Consider some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and defining the uniform mixture,
E_{n}:=\frac{1}{N_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}E_{n,j}
. The same linearity argument that we did in the Proposition
6.7
gives
E_{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
. Now, consider any
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
and choose an index
j(Q)\in\{1,\dots,N_{n}\}
which satisfies the approximation property. Pointwise, we have
E_{n}\geq\frac{1}{N_{n}}E_{n,j(Q)}
. Therefore,
\log E_{n}\geq\log E_{n,j(Q)}-\log N_{n}
. Hence,

\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E_{n}]\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E_{n,j(Q)}]-\log N_{n}\geq a_{n}(Q)-\Delta_{n}-\log N_{n}.

Taking the
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
, we get

\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E_{n}]\geq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}a_{n}(Q)-\Delta_{n}-\log N_{n}=a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})-\Delta_{n}-\log N_{n}.

Necessarily
E_{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
. So the definition of
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
tells us that

b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\geq a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})-\Delta_{n}-\log N_{n}.

All we need to do now is take this result together with the universal upper bound, that
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\leq a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})
from Lemma
6.3
. Let us do this, divide by
n
and take the
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
. By assumption, we have that
\Delta_{n}=o(n)
and
\log N_{n}=o(n)
. This thereby forces the lower bound to match the upper bound in the limsup, which proves our claim.
∎

Theorem
6.8
has a small requirement that at a horizon
n
, we should be able to find a subexponential family of
e
-variables which have expected log-payoff that approximates the individually optimal payoff
a_{n}(Q)
up to
o(n)
, but uniformly over all the
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. This is the smallest way we can give substance to the idea that
\mathcal{Q}
not be too large from the perspective of uniform log-growth. If
N_{n}
is exponentially large, then the mixture penalty
\log N_{n}
will be linear in
n
. As a consequence, we will have a genuine rate gap. However, we once again emphasize that we can analyze the intrinsic uniform rate without any assumptions through the robust horizon values
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
.

6.2
When will the Intrinsic Uniform Growth Rate be Positive?

Let us state what we can get from the fact that these pointwise
\mathrm{KL}
quantities are positive. Before getting there, note that
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
depends on the robust finite-horizon values
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
. Throughout this section, note that all statements also apply for
\mathbb{F}
-
e
-processes. On the other hand, the items we will expound on in this section will be those build from the pointwise values,

a_{n}(Q):=\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R),

where
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
. In full generality we can only get necessary and sufficient statements with additional assumptions. However, we will give some necessary implications also, which hold in full generality. For each
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
, recall that we showed the sequence
(a_{n}(Q))_{n\geq 1}
is superadditive. So, as in Lemma
3.2
, we may define the asymptotic pointwise rate as

d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P}):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(Q)=\sup_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(Q)\in[0,\infty],

where the limit exists by Lemma
3.2
. Therefore, in full generality, it will always hold that for any arbitrary
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P}
,

\boxed{\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0\Longrightarrow\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q^{n},(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ})>0\Longrightarrow\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0.}

(17)

We will show that this holds in Theorem
6.9
. The converses are in general not true without additional structure. However, as an example, suppose that
\mathcal{Q}
is finite and that
\Phi(R)
is weakly lower semicontinuous at
Q
. Then, we will have that (which will be proven in Corollary
6.12
),

\boxed{\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0\Longleftrightarrow\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q^{n},(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ})>0\Longleftrightarrow\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0.}

(18)

We are now ready to present our theorem on necessary positivity implications. These will be fully general and consequently prove (
17
).

Theorem 6.9
.

Suppose
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P}
are nonempty. Then,
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\leq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. This means that if
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0
, then
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
. In addition, for every
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
,
d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})\leq\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. Hence,
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0
also implies that
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
.

Proof.

Consider any
e
-process
(W_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
along deterministic sample sizes
t_{k}
. Take some
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. For each
k
, set
n:=t_{k}
. Since
W_{t_{k}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}
-measurable, there exists a
B^{\otimes n}
-measurable function
w_{k}:\mathsf{X}^{n}\mapsto[0,\infty]
such that
W_{t_{k}}(\omega)=w_{k}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
. Each deterministic
t_{k}
is a stopping time, hence by the definition of
e
-process, for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[W_{t_{k}}]\leq 1
. However, since
W_{t_{k}}=w_{k}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, it follows that this is equivalently
\mathbb{E}_{P^{n}}[w_{k}]\leq 1
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
, so we must have
w_{k}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
. Therefore, thanks to Proposition
3.1
at the horizon
n=t_{k}
we get

\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log w_{k}]\leq\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E]=a_{n}(Q)=a_{t_{k}}(Q).

(19)

Therefore it follows that

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}(W,t;Q)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\inf_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\prime\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log W_{t_{k}}]

(20)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}a_{t_{k}}(Q)

(21)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(Q)

(22)

\displaystyle=d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

Here, (
20
) is obvious. From (
19
), (
21
) is immediate. Lastly, (
22
) holds because a limsup along some subsequence
(t_{k})
is bounded by the full limit of
a_{n}(Q)/n
. Now this bounds holds for any
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, so it follows that
\mathsf{G}(W,t;\mathcal{Q})\leq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. Taking the supremum over all
e
-processes
W_{t_{k}}
gives us that

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\leq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

So our first claim is proved. It remains for us to show that for each
Q
,
d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})\leq\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

a_{n}(Q)=\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)\leq\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|P^{n})=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}}n\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)=n\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

Dividing this by
n
and letting
n\to\infty
gives
d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})\leq\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. Taking the infimum over all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
completes the proof.
∎

As we mentioned, to get sufficient conditions, there needs to be more structure. One immediate one is if we suppose that there is no asymptotic minimax gap. Because the robust rate is the best achievable growth rate of the
e
-process, implying that this growth rate will also be equal to worst-case rate if there is no asymptotic gap. Then, one-step bipolar positivity becomes sufficient. We will formalize that idea as follows. Recall that
a_{n}(\mathcal{Q}):=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}a_{n}(Q)
.

Corollary 6.10
.

Assume in addition that

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\overline{d}_{\mathrm{wc}}:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(\mathcal{Q}).

This will hold for example, under Proposition
6.7
. And it will also hold under any assumption that gives
\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}=\overline{d}_{\mathrm{wc}}
.
Then it follows that
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}^{{\circ}{\circ}})>0
implies that
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0
. And in particular, all the single directions in (
17
) become double directions, i.e. both necessary and sufficient as in (
18
).

We will now specialize. First, we will give a condition that gives an if and only if statement. It is for finite
\mathcal{Q}
.

Corollary 6.11
.

Assume that
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{1},Q_{2},...,Q_{M}\}
is finite. Then it follows that

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q^{n},(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{{\circ}{\circ}}).

Therefore
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0
implies that

\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q^{n},(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{{\circ}{\circ}})>0.

Lastly, to get a if and only if condition with respect to the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
, we will need both finite
\mathcal{Q}
and pointwise weak lower semicontinuity. We will formalize that as follows.

Corollary 6.12
.

Assume that
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{M}\}
is finite and that for each

the map

is weakly lower semicontinuous at

. Then, for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. Hence,
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. Therefore

\boxed{\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0\Longleftrightarrow\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}d^{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0\Longleftrightarrow\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0.}

We would like to make an important note on the testability problem now. Thanks to Theorem
3.14
, we always have a necessary and sufficient condition for characterizing the existence of power one sequential tests for arbitrary composite nulls against point alternatives. However, one question remains here: do we have such a statement for arbitrary composite alternatives? Is
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0
both necessary and sufficient for the existence of a level-
\alpha
power one sequential test of
\mathcal{P}
against
\mathcal{Q}
? The answer turns out to be no. We will convince ourselves of this with a very simple (countable) counterexample.

Example 6.13
.

Consider the null
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{0}\}
, where
P_{0}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1/2)
. Now, let
\mathcal{Q}:=\{Q_{i}:i\geq 1\}
, where
Q_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1/2+2^{-i-2})
. Here, each
Q_{i}
will be individually power-one against
\{P_{0}\}
thanks to Theorem
3.14
, since
\mathrm{KL}(Q_{i}\|P_{0})>0
. Now let us choose level allocations
\alpha_{i}:=\alpha 2^{-i}
. And let
\tau_{i}
be a level-
\alpha_{i}
power one test against
Q_{i}
. Define the stopping rule
\tau:=\inf_{i\geq 1}\tau_{i}
. It follows then that

P_{0}^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\sum_{i\geq 1}P_{0}^{\infty}(\tau_{i}<\infty)\leq\sum_{i\geq 1}\alpha_{i}=\alpha.

However, for each
i
,
Q_{i}^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=1
since
\tau<\tau_{i}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{Q}
is clearly power-one sequentially testable against
\mathcal{P}
in this case. But recognize that since
\mathrm{KL}(Q_{i}\|P_{0})\downarrow 0
we have that, thanks to Theorem
6.9
,

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\leq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})\leq\inf_{i\geq 1}\mathrm{KL}(Q_{i}\|P_{0})=0.

Thus, we have a power one test for arbitrary
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P}
albeit
\Gamma=0
, proving that
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})
being strictly positive is not necessary to have a power one test exist.

6.3
Pointwise Power-One Test Characterization for composite
\mathcal{P}
against composite
\mathcal{Q}

The most fully general characterization of the optimal uniform growth rate is already presented in Theorem
6.5
. However, the case that is most easy to see is the weakly compact one. In this regime, we already have a uniform positive lower bound on the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
over all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. From it, we have a single test supermartingale that works simultaneously for all alternatives. To be clear, we say that a level-
\alpha
sequential test is pointwise power one against
\mathcal{Q}
if
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
and for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
Q^{\infty}(\tau<\infty)=1
.

Theorem 6.14
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{X}
is Polish. Suppose that
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
are weakly compact. And suppose that
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{c}
. Recall that
d_{\min}:=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. Suppose that
d_{\mathrm{min}}>0
. Then it follows that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,d_{\mathrm{min}})
, there exist deterministic times
t_{k}\uparrow\infty
and a single test supermartingale
(E_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
such that for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
wp 1 under
Q^{\infty}
,

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}\geq d_{\min}-\varepsilon.

And,

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]\geq d_{\min}-\varepsilon.

As a consequence, for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the stopping time
\tau_{\alpha}:=\inf\{{t_{k}}\geq 1:E_{t_{k}}\geq 1/\alpha\}
is a power-one level-
\alpha
test of
\mathcal{P}
against
\mathcal{Q}
.

The takeaway here is that weak compactness allows us the lower semicontinuity, which in turn will control the
\mathrm{KL}
uniformly over
\mathcal{Q}
. Our assumption that
d_{\mathrm{min}}>0
entails that the entire alternative class is uniformly separated from the null. Hence we get a single test supermartingale that grows at a strictly positive asymptotic rate no matter which
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
is true. And if we threshold that process, we will get a sequential test that is pointwise power-one.

Now, we will upgrade our earlier Corollary
6.12
to a more interpretable one that also gives us a uniform power one test.

Corollary 6.15
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{X}
is Polish. And suppose that
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
is weakly compact. Assume that
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{M}\}
is finite. Then, the following conditions are equivalent:

1.

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0
.

2.

\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})>0
.

And if these equivalent conditions hold, for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists a power-one level-
\alpha
test of
\mathcal{P}
against
\mathcal{Q}
.

Now, we will make this more concrete by upgrading this corollary. We will present two general sufficient conditions for power-one sequential tests against
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
.

Theorem 6.16
.

Let
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
be nonempty and nonintersecting. Suppose that either of the following two conditions hold:

1.

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0
.

2.

\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact.

Then, for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists a level
\alpha
pointwise power-one sequential test of
\mathcal{P}
against
\mathcal{Q}
.

Notice that throughout for this composite alternative case, we have not presented any condition that is both necessary and sufficient. Now, if a level
\alpha
pointwise power one test of
\mathcal{P}
against
\mathcal{Q}
were to exist, thanks to Theorem
3.14
applied pointwise to
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,

d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\inf_{R\in(\mathcal{P})^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q^{n}\|R)>0.

In other words, this condition is necessary (pointwise). However, it is not sufficient in general for a single test to work against all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
without some additional structure.

th block
e
-variable as

E^{(k)}:=\begin{cases}1,&k<k_{0},\\[3.99994pt]
(1-\gamma_{k})\exp(m_{k}r)\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}+\gamma_{k},&k\geq k_{0}.\end{cases}

Now, for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
k\geq k_{0}
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P^{m_{k}}}[E^{(k)}]

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma_{k})\exp(m_{k}r)P^{m_{k}}(A_{k})+\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\gamma_{k})\exp(m_{k}r)\beta_{k}+\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\gamma_{k})\exp(-m_{k}\eta)+\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\gamma_{k})\cdot 1+\gamma_{k}=1.

We know that
\mathbb{E}_{P^{m_{k}}}[E^{(k)}]=1
for
k<k_{0}
. Therefore the blockwise product
E_{t_{0}}:=1
and

E_{t_{k}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}E^{(j)}\bigl(X_{t_{j-1}+1},\dots,X_{t_{j}}\bigr),

is in fact a test supermartingale
\mathcal{P}
, exactly as in Proposition
7.1
. Now take some
Q\in U
. Because
U
is weakly open and contains
Q
, the complement
U^{c}
is weakly closed and does not contain
Q
. In other words, if there existed a sequence
(R_{n})\subset U^{c}
with
\mathrm{KL}(R_{n}\|Q)\downarrow 0
, by Pinsker’s inequality,
R_{n}\Rightarrow Q
is forced to occur. But that contradicts the fact that
U^{c}
is weakly closed and excludes
Q
. So it must be the case that
c_{Q}(U):=\inf_{R\in\cl(U^{c})}\mathrm{KL}(R\|Q)
satisfies
c_{Q}(U)>0
. Now let us use Sanov’s theorem under
Q
. Doing this gives us

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\log Q^{m}(\widehat{Q}_{m}\in U^{c})\leq-c_{Q}(U).

In particular,
Q^{m_{k}}(A_{k}^{c})\leq\exp(-m_{k}c_{Q}(U)/2)
for all large
k
. Hence it follows that
\sum_{k}Q^{m_{k}}(A_{k}^{c})<\infty
. Then thanks to Borel Cantelli Lemma 1,
A_{k}
occurs eventually
Q^{\infty}
almost surely. Let us work on the event that
A_{k}
holds for all sufficiently large
k
. On this event we have that for
k\geq k_{0}
,

\log E^{(k)}\in\Bigl[m_{k}r+\log(1-\gamma_{k}),\;m_{k}r\Bigr].

Therefore, summing and dividing by
t_{k}=\sum_{j\leq k}m_{j}
, we have that

r+\frac{1}{t_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log(1-\gamma_{j})\leq\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}\leq r+\frac{C(\omega)}{t_{k}}.

Here, recall that
C(\omega)
is just some finite random constant for finitely many exceptional blocks. However, since
\sum_{j}|\log(1-\gamma_{j})|<\infty
and
t_{k}\to\infty
, both of the correction terms must vanish. Therefore we get that almost surely under
Q^{\infty}
,

\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}\to r,

which was our first claim. In addition, the expected log growth claim follows exactly as in Proposition
7.1
. Meaning, from linearity of expectation,
\mathbb{E}[\log E_{t_{k}}]=\sum_{j\leq k}\mathbb{E}[\log E^{(j)}]
. Then, the Borel-Cantelli lemma gives us a lower bound
\mathbb{E}[\log E^{(k)}]\geq(1-o(1))m_{k}r+O(1)
. Moreover,
\log E^{(k)}\leq m_{k}r
for all

\log E_{t_{k}}\leq rt_{k}
. As such,
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]\leq rt_{k}
. And this in conjunction with the lower bound tells us that indeed
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]/t_{k}\to r
.
∎

Now, we are ready for our main theorem for weakly compact
\mathcal{Q}
. Our theorem will prove the existence of a pointwise near-optimal composite blockwise test supermartingale through rate level covers.

For intuition, if we take
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q\}
, thanks to Theorem
7.5
below, we get for every
\varepsilon>0
a test supermartingale with

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}\geq d(Q)-\varepsilon,

almost surely under
Q^{\infty}
. And the analogous expected log bound will also hold. So it will recover the lower bound direction of Proposition
7.1
in the singleton case. However, Proposition
7.1
will be much sharper as it gives an exact limit
d(Q)
rather than a near-optimal lower bound.

Theorem 7.5
.

Assume that
\mathsf{X}
is Polish and that
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
is weakly compact. Also, let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
be weakly compact. Assume that
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{c}
. As before, let us define
d(Q):=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\Phi(Q)\in(0,\infty]
for
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Now take some
\varepsilon>0
. Then it follows that there exist deterministic block lengths
(m_{k})
with
t_{k}:=\sum_{j\leq k}m_{j}\to\infty
and a
test-supermartingale

(E_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
such that for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
with
d(Q)<\infty
,

\boxed{\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}\geq d(Q)-\varepsilon\quad Q^{\infty}\text{-a.s.}}

In addition,

\boxed{\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]\geq d(Q)-\varepsilon.}

Proof.

Take a particular
\varepsilon>0
. For
m\geq 1
, let us define the rate grid as

r_{m}:=\frac{m\varepsilon}{2},\qquad s_{m}:=r_{m}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\frac{(m-1)\varepsilon}{2}.

Then, for each
m\geq 1
let us define the superlevel set as
K_{m}:=\{Q\in\mathcal{Q}:d(Q)\geq r_{m}\}
. Now, since
\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact by assumption, by
[
18
, Lemma 3]
it follows that
\Phi
is weakly lower semicontinuous everywhere. Therefore,
d=\Phi
is weakly lower semicontinuous on
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
. So
K_{m}
must be weakly closed in
\mathcal{Q}
, and since
\mathcal{Q}
is weakly compact that must mean that each of the
K_{m}
are weakly compact. Now take some particular but arbitrarily chosen
m\geq 1
. For each
Q\in K_{m}
we have that
d(Q)\geq r_{m}
. Hence that in conjunction with weak lower semicontinuity of
\Phi
at
Q
tells us that the set
O_{m,Q}:=\{R\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X}):\Phi(R)>r_{m}-\varepsilon/4\}
is weakly open and contains
Q
. Recall that
\mathsf{X}
was Polish, and so it follows that the weak topology is metrizable on
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathsf{X})
. Now consider the bounded Lipschitz metric
d_{\mathrm{BL}}
. Recall that
O_{m,Q}
is weakly open and contains
Q
. Therefore, we can choose
\delta_{m,Q}>0
such that

\overline{B}^{\mathrm{BL}}_{\delta_{m,Q}}(Q)\subseteq O_{m,Q}.

Now, let us set
U_{m,Q}:=B^{\mathrm{BL}}_{\delta_{m,Q}}(Q)
. It follows that
\cl(U_{m,Q})=\overline{B}^{\mathrm{BL}}_{\delta_{m,Q}}(Q)
is convex and weakly closed. In addition,

\inf_{R\in\cl(U_{m,Q})}\Phi(R)\geq r_{m}-\varepsilon/4.

The family
\{U_{m,Q}:Q\in K_{m}\}
is an open cover of the compact set
K_{m}
, so it must have a finite subcover. Let us denote this subcover as

K_{m}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{J_{m}}U_{m,j},

where for
j=1,\dots,J_{m}
, we have that
\inf_{R\in\cl(U_{m,j})}\Phi(R)\geq r_{m}-\varepsilon/4
. Because
s_{m}=r_{m}-\varepsilon/2<r_{m}-\varepsilon/4\leq\inf_{R\in\cl(U_{m,j})}\Phi(R)
, we can use Lemma
7.4
with
U=U_{m,j}
and
r=s_{m}
. To this end, we get for each pair
(m,j)
a test-supermartingale
(E^{(m,j)}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
for
\mathcal{P}
(and note that, they are all constructed on the same block schedule; for example by taking the same
m_{k}=k^{2}
in the lemma). And such that this blockwise test-supermartingale satisfies for every
Q\in U_{m,j}
,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E^{(m,j)}_{t_{k}}=s_{m},

almost surely under
Q^{\infty}
, and likewise for the expected log-growth necessarily. Now let us choose weights
(w_{m,j})
such that
\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{m}}w_{m,j}\leq 1
. Just to be concrete let us set
w_{m,j}:=\frac{2^{-m}}{J_{m}}
. Now then let us define the mixed process where
k\geq 0
as

E_{t_{k}}:=\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{m}}w_{m,j}E^{(m,j)}_{t_{k}}.

Each of the
E^{(m,j)}
is a blockwise
e
-process for
\mathcal{P}
. And, conditional expectation is linear, which means that
(E_{t_{k}})
is also a blockwise
e
-process for
\mathcal{P}
. Meaning,
\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[E_{t_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}]\leq E_{k-1}
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Now consider some particular
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
with
d(Q)<\infty
. Let us choose
m
so that
r_{m}\leq d(Q)<r_{m+1}=r_{m}+\varepsilon/2
. Clearly then by that choice, it follows that
Q\in K_{m}
, which means
Q\in U_{m,j}
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,J_{m}\}
. For this particular
(m,j)
we have that pointwise
E_{t_{k}}\geq w_{m,j}E^{(m,j)}_{t_{k}}
, and so it follows that
\log E_{t_{k}}\geq\log w_{m,j}+\log E^{(m,j)}_{t_{k}}
. All we need to do now is divide by
t_{k}
and take the
\liminf
and we get that almost surely under
Q^{\infty}
,

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}\geq\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E^{(m,j)}_{t_{k}}=s_{m},

as
\log(w_{m,j})/t_{k}\to 0
. In addition, note that our choice of
m
above implies that
s_{m}=r_{m}-\varepsilon/2\geq d(Q)-\varepsilon
. This proves the almost sure lower bound. The expected log bound follows with the same inequality applied and taking expectations. That is,

\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]\geq\log w_{m,j}+\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E^{(m,j)}_{t_{k}}].

Then, dividing by
t_{k}
and taking the
\liminf
and using the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\log E^{(m,j)}_{k}]/t_{k}\to s_{m}
we get that

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]\geq s_{m}\geq d(Q)-\varepsilon.

This completes the proof.
∎

We will now give a uniform version of this theorem.

Corollary 7.6
.

Under the same setup as Theorem
7.5
, define,

d_{\min}:=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}d(Q)=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})\in(0,\infty].

Then it follows that for every
\varepsilon>0
, wp 1 under
Q^{\infty}
for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
with
d(Q)<\infty
, the same test supermartingale will satisfy

\boxed{\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}\geq d_{\min}-\varepsilon.}

And it also satisfies

\boxed{\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[\log E_{t_{k}}]\geq d_{\min}-\varepsilon.}

Proof.

For each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, thanks to Theorem
7.5
we have that with probability one under
Q^{\infty}
,

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}\geq d(Q)-\varepsilon.

Now, for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
d(Q)\geq d_{\mathrm{min}}
. So our almost-sure claim is immediate. The expected-log claim follows the same way: just from taking the infimum over
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
in the lower bound from Theorem
7.5
.
∎

Throughout this paper, we have used blockwise constructions for achievability (through blockwise test supermartingales on a reduced filtration). For impossibility, it holds for arbitrary
e
-processes. For sequential testing, the processes on
\mathbb{F}_{\mathbf{t}}
are enough already, since if we threshold at times
t_{k}
, we have a stopping rule in the original filtration. Thus, albeit the evidence process still updates on a schedule, the power-one implications we prove are still meaningful. We will formalize this more as follows.

Remark 7.7
.

Our blockwise constructions we use throughout this paper are structurally important. They allow us to convert a finite horizon
e
-variable into a sequentially valid process. Note that by duality at horizon
m
, we have an
m
-sample
e
-variable
E^{\star}:\mathsf{X}^{m}\to[0,\infty]
,
E^{\star}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
. It has a large expected log under the target law
Q^{m}
. However, note that this is not an
e
-process on
\mathbb{F}
, rather it is terminal at time
m
. However, our blockwise construction solves this problem. We partition the data into disjoint lengths of length
m
. We define
t_{k}:=km
and set

E_{t_{k}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}E^{\star}\bigl(X_{(j-1)m+1},\dots,X_{jm}\bigr).

Then we have for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
that

\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[E_{t_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}]=E_{t_{k-1}}\mathbb{E}_{P^{m}}[E^{\star}]\leq E_{t_{k-1}}.

This holds since the future block is independent of the past and has law
P^{m}
, and since
E^{\star}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
. So,
(E_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
is a test supermartingale on the reduced filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}
. Under
Q^{\infty}
, the block log-increments are i.i.d. and hence both the expected log growth and almost sure growth are immediate.

In addition, one cannot in general hold the process constant between
t_{k}
and
t_{k+1}
. As indicated in Example
3.7
, a
\mathbb{F}
-stopping rule can exploit the information that is revealed inside the current block, which will violate the
e
-process property. This can be saved by setting the process equal to
0
off the block times, and it will still be a valid process. However, this will only preserve in full-generality the
\limsup
(of the expected
\log
objective) but will not preserve any almost sure or full
\liminf
statement.

One should therefore look at blockwise processes as the constructive objects for lower-bound and achievability arguments. These will certainly be
e
-processes, but on a reduced filtration. However, this is already sufficient for sequential testing applications since
\tau_{\alpha}:=\inf\{t_{k}:E_{t_{k}}\geq 1/\alpha\}
is a
(\mathcal{F}_{n})
-stopping rule in the original filtration.

8
Summary

In this work, we proved that the intrinsic maximal growth rate for any sequential procedure under absolutely no assumptions is the normalized limit of the
inf-KL
, rather than the
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
, the latter in general being larger. We provided a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of power one sequential tests (in terms of positivity of the
k
-step bipolar rate) for composite
\mathcal{P}
and singleton
Q
, greatly generalizing prior work. We provided general conditions under which the maximal achievable growth rate
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})
actually equals
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}
. We also proved the maximal intrinsic growth rate in the composite alternative setting (under no assumptions whatsoever), which turns out to be a limiting variant of the
\mathrm{GROW}
value, and provide some general conditions under which this reduces to a
\mathrm{KL}
type expression. In addition, we also provided general conditions for the existence of power one sequential tests for composite alternatives and related that to strict positivity of the intrinsic maximal growth rate in the composite case.

. To see that note that for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
, the product structure of
P^{n+m}=P^{n}\otimes P^{m}
tells us that

\mathbb{E}_{P^{n+m}}[G]=\mathbb{E}_{P^{n+m}}\left[E_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})F_{m}(x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{P^{n}}[E_{n}]\mathbb{E}_{P^{m}}[F_{m}]\leq 1\cdot 1\leq 1.

This follows from the independence of the first
n
coordinates and the last
m
coordinates under
P^{n+m}
. And, the inequality follows because
E_{n}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
and
F_{m}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
. So we get that
G\in(\mathcal{P}^{n+m})^{\circ}
, which was our claim. Hence once again thanks to Proposition
3.1
we get

\displaystyle a_{n+m}(Q)

\displaystyle=\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n+m})^{\circ}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n+m}}[\log E]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n+m}}[\log G]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n+m}}\Big[\log E_{n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})+\log F_{m}(X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+m})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E_{n}]+\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log F_{m}]

\displaystyle\geq a_{n}(Q)+a_{m}(Q)-2\varepsilon.

(30)

Here, in (
30
), we once again use independence under
Q^{n+m}=Q^{n}\otimes Q^{m}
along with the fact that the former part only will depend on the first
n
coordinates and the latter on the last
m
. And so since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we may indeed conclude superadditivity whenever both
a_{n}(Q)
and
a_{m}(Q)
are finite. That is,

a_{n+m}(Q)\geq a_{n}(Q)+a_{m}(Q).

Suppose one of
a_{m}(Q)
or
a_{n}(Q)
were
+\infty
. In this case, our same argument here applies, but using lower targets that are arbitrarily large instead of
a_{m}(Q)-\varepsilon
or
a_{n}(Q)-\varepsilon
. Therefore, it follows that
(a_{n}(Q))_{n\geq 1}
is superadditive in any case. So the existence of the limit is immediate now from Fekete’s lemma. Meaning,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}(Q)}{n}=\sup_{n\geq 1}\frac{a_{n}(Q)}{n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
6.3
.

Take some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and take any
E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}
. For every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,

\inf_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\prime n}}[\log E]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E]\leq\sup_{E^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E^{\prime}].

Then, by Proposition
3.1
, i.e. the finite horizon strong duality, we know that the rightmost term in our above expression is simply
a_{n}(Q)=\textnormal{inf-KL}(Q^{n},\mathcal{P}^{n})
. If we then take the
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}
over the inequality we therefore get that

\inf_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\prime n}}[\log E]\leq\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}a_{n}(Q)=a_{n}(\mathcal{Q}).

Finally, if we take the
\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}}
over the lhs we get that
b_{n}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})\leq a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})
. Now all we need to do is divide by
n
and take the
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
. This will certainly preserve the inequality. And as such, we will get that
\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}\leq\overline{d}_{\mathrm{wc}}
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
6.10
.

First, note that
\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}^{\circ\circ})=\inf_{R\in\mathcal{P}^{\circ\circ}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|R)=a_{1}(Q)
. Let us set
c:=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}a_{1}(Q)=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}^{\circ\circ})
. By assumption, it follows that
c>0
. Now let us take some particular
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Thanks to Lemma
3.2
, it follows that the sequence
(a_{n}(Q))_{n\geq 1}
is superadditive. So we get that for every
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle a_{n}(Q)

\displaystyle\geq a_{n-1}(Q)+a_{1}(Q)

\displaystyle\geq a_{n-2}(Q)+2a_{1}(Q)

\displaystyle\,\,\vdots

\displaystyle\geq na_{1}(Q)

\displaystyle\geq nc.

(31)

Then taking the infimum over all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
gives us that
a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}a_{n}(Q)\geq nc
. Therefore,

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\overline{d}_{\mathrm{wc}}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}(nc)=c>0,

proving our claim.
∎

Proof of Corollary
6.11
.

Since
\mathcal{Q}
is finite, it follows that by Proposition
6.7
and Theorem
6.5
that

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\overline{d}_{\mathrm{wc}}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(\mathcal{Q}).

Note that by definition,
a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})=\min_{1\leq i\leq M}a_{n}(Q_{i})
. Thus it follows that

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\min_{1\leq i\leq M}a_{n}(Q_{i})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\min_{1\leq i\leq M}\frac{a_{n}(Q_{i})}{n}.

(32)

Now, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,M\}
, by Lemma
3.2
we have that
a_{n}(Q_{i})/n\to d_{\star}(Q_{i},\mathcal{P})
. Note that the minimum of finitely many convergence real sequences will converge to the minimum of the limits necessarily. So by (
32
) we have that

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\min_{1\leq i\leq M}d_{\star}(Q_{i},\mathcal{P})=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

(33)

So by definition of
d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})
we get that

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q^{n},(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ\circ}),

and thus the equivalence follows, completing our proof.
∎

Proof of Corollary
6.12
.

Our rate identity shows that pointwise weak lsc implies that for each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. So together with Corollary
6.11
it follows that,

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}d_{\star}(Q,\mathcal{P})=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

So, it follows that the strict positivity equivalence holds, completing our proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.14
.

Thanks to Corollary
7.6
, it follows that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,d_{\mathrm{min}})
, there exist deterministic times
t_{k}\uparrow\infty
and a single test supermartingale
(E_{t_{k}})
such that for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
wp 1 under
Q^{\infty}
,

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}\geq d(Q)-\varepsilon.

Recall that
d(Q)=\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P})
. Now since
d(Q)\geq d_{\mathrm{min}}
for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
, we immediately have that
Q^{\infty}
almost surely for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}\log E_{t_{k}}\geq d_{\min}-\varepsilon.

The expected-log bound follows from the second conclusion of Corollary
7.6
. Now take some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\tau_{\alpha}
as we defined. Since
d_{\mathrm{min}}-\varepsilon>0
, thanks to our almost sure lower bound we have that
E_{t_{k}}\to\infty
wp 1 under
Q^{\infty}
for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Therefore it follows that for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
Q^{\infty}(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)=1
. All we need to now is show the level-
\alpha
correctness under the null.
(E_{t_{k}})
being a test supermartingale entails it is also an
e
-process. So for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[E_{\tau_{\alpha}}]\leq 1
. Now, on the event
\{\tau_{\alpha}<\infty\}
, we have that
E_{\tau_{\alpha}}\geq 1/\alpha
. So it follows that

1\geq\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[E_{\tau_{\alpha}}]\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}P^{\infty}(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty).

Therefore,
P^{\infty}(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)\leq\alpha
for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
, completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Corollary
6.15
.

Since
\mathcal{Q}
is finite, thanks to both Proposition
6.7
and Theorem
6.5
we have that,

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(\mathcal{Q}),

where
a_{n}(\mathcal{Q})=\min_{1\leq i\leq M}a_{n}(Q_{i})
here. Now,
\mathcal{P}
is weakly compact. Thus from Theorem
4.1
applied to each
Q_{i}
we have that

\frac{1}{n}a_{n}(Q_{i})\longrightarrow\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q_{i},\mathcal{P}).

Then if we take the minimum over these finitely many indices we have that

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\min_{1\leq i\leq M}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q_{i},\mathcal{P})=\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathrm{KL}_{\mathrm{inf}}(Q,\mathcal{P}).

Hence, the equivalence is shown. The existence of the uniformly power-one test follows immediately, thanks to Theorem
6.14
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.16
.

The second claim of the theorem is the result of
[
18
, Theorem 1]
. So it remains for us to prove the first claim. Assume that
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})>0
. Thanks to Theorem
6.5
, we have

\Gamma(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P})=\overline{d}_{\mathrm{rob}}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left(\sup_{E\in(\mathcal{P}^{n})^{\circ}}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{n}}[\log E]\right).

Therefore, there exist some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, where for some
c>0
and an
m
-sample
e
-variable
E^{\star}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
such that

\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\geq mc>0.

Now set
t_{k}:=km
. Take
t_{0}:=0
. Define the blockwise process
S
where
S_{t_{0}}:=1
and for
k\geq 1
,

S_{t_{k}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}E^{\star}(X_{(j-1)m+1},\ldots,X_{jm}).

For each
P\in\mathcal{P}
, the blocks are i.i.d. with law
P^{m}
. And since
E^{\star}\in(\mathcal{P}^{m})^{\circ}
,
\mathbb{E}_{P^{m}}[E^{\star}]\leq 1
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[S_{t_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}]=S_{t_{k-1}}\mathbb{E}_{P^{m}}[E^{\star}]\leq S_{t_{k-1}},

proving that
(S_{t_{k}})_{k\geq 0}
is a blockwise test supermartingale for
\mathcal{P}
. Let us now consider some
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. Under
Q^{\infty}
, the block log-increments

Y_{j}:=\log E^{\star}(X_{(j-1)m+1},\ldots,X_{jm})

are i.i.d. and satisfy
\mathbb{E}_{Q^{\infty}}[Y_{1}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\geq mc>0
. By the extended strong law of large numbers (i.e. allowing the mean to be
+\infty
) it holds that
Q^{\infty}
almost surely

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}Y_{j}\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}_{Q^{m}}[\log E^{\star}]\in(0,\infty].

Therefore,
\frac{\log S_{t_{k}}}{t_{k}}=\frac{1}{km}\sum_{j=1}^{k}Y_{j}
will have strictly positive
\liminf
with probability 1 under
Q^{\infty}
. In particular for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
S_{t_{k}}\to\infty
almost surely. Now define

\tau_{\alpha}:=\inf\{t_{k}:k\geq 0,\,S_{t_{k}}\geq 1/\alpha\}.

Then, it holds that
\tau_{\alpha}
is a
\mathbb{F}
-stopping rule, and since
S_{t_{k}}
diverges with probability one, this implies that for every
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
.
Q^{\infty}(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)=1
. Now
\alpha
-correctness is immediate from Ville’s inequality applied to the blockwise test supermartingale. That is, for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
, we have that
P^{\infty}(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)\leq\alpha
. Equivalently, we can apply optional stopping to
\tau_{\alpha}\wedge t_{N}
, giving us

1\geq\mathbb{E}_{P^{\infty}}[S_{\tau_{\alpha}\wedge t_{N}}]\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}P^{\infty}(\tau_{\alpha}\leq t_{N}).

Then letting
N\to\infty
gives us
P^{\infty}(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)\leq\alpha
. So it follows that
\tau_{\alpha}
is a level-
\alpha
pointwise power-one test, competing the proof.
∎
