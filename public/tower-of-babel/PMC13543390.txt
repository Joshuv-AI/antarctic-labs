Biocontrol of Angelica sinensis root rot and degradation of neosolaniol toxin by a novel Trichoderma asperellum M2 strain
Authors: Yang Liu, Rongrong Mu, Jiaxin Zheng, Xiaofan Xie, Xinyi Yang, Zhaoyu Li, Lu Liu, Yanli Zheng, Jiaxing Zhao, Fugui Pan, Wensuo Ye, Zheng Zhang, Yonghong Zhu, Yali Wang, Yongqiang Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoot rot is a very destructive soil-borne disease severely affecting the quality and yield of common name Angelica sinensis in its main production region (Gansu Province, China). Here, a new Trichoderma asperellum strain M2 with broad-spectrum antifungal activity was assessed for its biocontrol effects against Angelica root rot. This fungal strain produces various hydrolases enzymes, notably cellulase (highest activity), and secreted 12.8 mg/L of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) in a medium enriched with tryptophan. Scanning electron microscopy showed that M2’s mycelium surrounded or parasitized the pathogen’s mycelium, leading to the pathogens hyphae collapse, morphological fracture, and shrinkage. Pathogenicity test confirmed that M2 had significant control effect against Fusarium acuminatum, with its prior inoculation being more effective (maximal inhibition rate: 57.61%). Compared with the control, treatment with strain M2 significantly increased the plant height, root length, fresh weight, and dry weight of A. sinensis seedlings by 30.2%, 67.0%, 33.5%, and 61.8%, respectively, while also increasing their enzyme activity for catalase (CAT), phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), peroxidase (POD), and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) by 12.82, 0.52, 1.57, and 3.7 times, respectively. A fermentation broth of strain M2 was highly effectively at degrading the neosolaniol (NEO) toxin, achieving a remarkable degradation rate of 87.14% after 11 days of treatment. Degradation ratios of the active cell component (49.40%) and extracellular component (33.6%) both significantly exceeded that of the intracellular component (24.2%), suggesting NEO removal mainly via biodegradation. Four products were identified by liquid chromatography/time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC/TOF-MS) for NEO’s degradation by the strain M2. This study’s findings provide a fresh perspective and strategy for applying multi-functional T. asperellum M2 to the biological control of Angelica root rot.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05163-9.Keywords: biological control, Fusarium acuminatum, root rot disease, toxin degradation, Trichoderma asperellum

Introduction

Common name Angelica sinensis (Oliv. Diels), a perennial herbaceous plant in the Apiaceae family, is widely prized in traditional Chinese medicine for its dried root material, which is used as an anti-inflammatory agent and to promote blood circulation. It is also now a popular dietary supplement used in Asia and other Eastern countries [1]. The cultivation area of A. sinensis in Gansu Province, China, grown in about 44,000 hectares in 2018, with approximately 150,000 tons of root produced annually, generating revenue of 2.2 billion yuan [2]. The high-quality development of Angelica industry has profoundly influenced the local economy in several ways: by fostering a sustainable increase in farmers’ incomes, by stimulating the prosperity of county economies, and by offering robust support for the revitalization of rural industries. But the yield and quality of A. sinensis is threatened by climate change, continuous cropping, and the misuse and overuse of fungicides, these creating favorable conditions for plant disease outbreaks. Of these, Fusarium root rot is among the most hazardous soilborne diseases, driving sharp declines in both A. sinensis yield and quality [3].

The Fusarium genus includes widely recognized phytopathogenic fungi, which can lead to diverse diseases in different host crops in addition to releasing economically relevant mycotoxins [4, 5]. Research on the pathogens responsible for root rot in A. sinensis has a long and well-documented history. Early investigations identified Fusarium as the primary pathogenic genus implicated in this disease, with multiple species—namely F. tricinctum, F. avenaceum, F. oxysporum, F. solani, F. redolens, F. incarnatum, F. equiseti, and F. acuminatum being associated with host infections [6]. Among those, F. tricinctum is known to exhibit the highest pathogenicity, followed by F. oxysporum and F. solani [7]. F. avenaceum not only causes root rot but also responsible for triggering leaf spot disease in A. sinensis [8]. Among the others, F. acuminatum can reduce the germination rate and vegetative vigor of durum wheat seedlings, and is a causal agent of root rot disease in wheat crops [9]; moreover, it is responsible for root rot in many traditional Chinese medicinal plants, such as Codonopsis pilosula [10], Ophiopogon japonicu [11], Astragalus membranaceus [12], and Schisandra chinensis [13]. The main disease symptoms include wilting, necrosis, abnormal root development, brown lesions, and later rotting. But in practical field applications, t indicate that F. acuminatum demonstrates a remarkable ability to infect A. sinensis, achieving a 100% incidence rate in controlled pot experiments. Notably, this fungi produces a variety of fungal toxins, such as neosolaniol (NEO), deoxynivalenol, and T-2 toxins [14]. The presence of these pathogenic fungi capable of releasing multiple toxins not only poses a stark challenge to the prevention and control of root rot disease afflicting A. sinensis, it also calls for greater medicinal safety of its roots products.

Much success has been achieved in plant disease control through a suite of techniques, like the targeted use of agrochemicals, breeding for resistant varieties of plants, using good agronomic and horticultural practices, as well as biological control agents [15, 16]. Nevertheless, long-term use of chemical fungicides will cause environmental pollution problems and may foster resistant pathogens, weakening any control effect; at the same time, the residues of highly toxic [17] and chemical pesticides will inevitably lower the quality of harvested A. sinensis roots and risk the health and safety of its consumers [18]. The application of biocontrol agents to manage soilborne diseases has become an exciting strategy by which to circumvent those major issues. Over the last few years, research has been dedicated to clarifying the pathogen responsible for A. sinensis root rot; screening for antagonistic microorganisms; analyzing disease-suppressing soil properties; and developing culturable antagonistic Bacillus spp. strains into biocontrol agents for controlling Angelica diseases [3, 7, 18]. Fusarium fungi were ihese products unavoidably encounter substantial hurdles with a greater usage time, such as the strains’ diminished colonization ability, their limited functional expression, or inconsistent results [19].

Employing fungi as biological control agents offers an efficient, economically viable, environmentally safe, and long-term strategy for plant disease management that many developed countries are already implementing [20, 21]. Trichoderma spp. are now among the most promising biocontrol agents being used against many plant pathogenic fungi [22]. Trichoderma is known to bolster the health of plants by protecting them via mycoparasitism and anti-microbial secondary metabolite production, as well as local and induced systemic resistance against invading pathogens [23]. Furthermore, studies have shown that Trichoderma fungi are highly tolerant of agrochemicals applied in soil and are capable of modifying the rhizosphere for plants benefits, utilizing any available soil nutrients efficiently, surviving under harsh conditions, and promoting plant growth [24, 25]. The strains of T. asperellum have long been studied for their characterization and antifungal efficacy against a variety of fungal plant diseases [26]. Recent work demonstrated that T. asperellum and its secondary metabolites possess antifungal activity against the pathogens responsible for root rot disease in Aconitum carmichaelii Debx [27]. T. asperellum markedly increased the emergence rate of dry bean grown in greenhouse and field trials as well, and reduced the root rot caused by F. solani [28]. Another study found that, T. asperellum TM11, exerts an adequate control effect against two conspecific pathogens (F. commune and F. oxysporum) and holds promising potential for preventing and controlling root rot in blueberry [29]. While Trichoderma species are being successfully used as biocontrol agents of many crop pathogens, including Fusarium spp., none have yet been tested against root rot in A. sinensis.

The present study focused on Trichoderma spp. selected for their potent antagonistic effects, and had three goals: (1) Evaluate the plant growth promoting (PGP) characteristics of the most potent biocontrol fungal strain and its colonization ability in the plant rhizosphere; (2) Investigate the biocontrol effect of this isolate on Angelica root rot disease and its mechanism of action; (3) Explore the degradation mechanism of this strain vis-à-vis Fusarium toxins, to offer a theoretical basis for clarifying its role in plant disease control. By revealing the multiple functions and mechanisms of Trichoderma strains, this study provides new research perspectives for developing multifunctional biological agents, supporting ‘green’ control for root rot disease in A. sinensis crops, and advancing sustainable agriculture.

Materials and methods

Pathogenic fungi and biocontrol fungal strains

Fusarium fungi were isolated from the root rot tissues of diseased A. sinensis plants [14]. These pathogenic strains consisted of F. acuminatum (OQ948167), F. oxyporum (OQ948164), F. solani (OQ948196), F. equiseti (OQ948170), F. redolens (OQ948165), and F. avenaceum (OQ948171). The selected biocontrol strains were species of Trichoderma fungi, namely T. asperellum M2, T. koningiopsis, and T. viride; these were preserved at the Microbiology Laboratory of Lanzhou Jiaotong University (Lanzhou, China) [30]. To test the ability of T. asperellum M2 to produ All strains were maintained in potato dextrose agar (PDA) slants and stored as glycerol stocks at − 80 °C to ensure their viability and genetic stability [31]. For experimental purposes, agar plugs of Trichoderma strains were inoculated in PDA agar plates, and incubated at 28 ± 2 °C, with 60% humidity and continuous light, for 5 days.

Plant material

The Angelica sinensis (Oliv.) Diels seedlings used in the this study were collected in May 2023 from the Angelica Research Institute in Minxian County, Dingxi City, Gansu Province, and were provided by Pro. Zengxiang Guo. The use of these seedlings and the experimental methods involved in this study comply with relevant national guidelines and local regulations. The plant was identified by Professor K. T. Detailed information related to this plant can be queried on the China Virtual Herbarium shared service platform (www.cvh.ac.cn).

Determination of broad-spectrum antagonistic activity of Trichoderma species

To study the antagonist activity of biocontrol fungi against Fusarium spp., dual culture technique described by researchers was used [32]. Mycelial discs (5 mm diameter) were removed from the growing edge of 1-week-old Trichoderma spp. and 1-week-old Fusarium spp. A given culture was placed on opposite sides of Petri plate (90 mm diameter) at equal distance from the center containing potato-dextrose agar. A complete randomized design was used, with four replicates per antagonist. In control plates (without Trichoderma), the pathogen was instead replaced with a sterile agar disc. All plates were incubated in the dark at 28 ± 2 °C for 5 days. The diameter of mycelial growth was then measured and this value used to determine the percentage inhibition (I) as follows: I = (C–T)/C × 100; where C is the radial growth of pathogen (mm) alone (i.e., control); T is the radial growth of pathogen (mm) in the presence of Trichoderma. The interaction between Trichoderma and phytopathogenic Fusarium fungi can be classified according to the width of their inhibition zones, which we here specifically divided into four types. Type 1: The inhibition zone overlaps entirely (i.e., width of 0 mm), which suggests no significant inhibition. Type 2: The inhibition zone is less ≤ 1 mm in width, implying a relatively weak inhibitory effect and modest degree of interaction. Type 3: The inhibition zone is ≤ 2 mm, indicating a stronger inhibitory effect on the pathogenic fungus from Trichoderma, such that the interaction between the two is now more pronounced. Type 4: The inhibition zone’s width is greater ≥ 3 mm, showing that Trichoderma has a powerful inhibitory effect on the pathogenic fungi, with a significant interaction between them.

Extracellular enzymes and IAA production ability of T. asperellum M2

To test the ability of T. asperellum M2 to produce protease, cellulase, chitinase, and glucanase, we employed chromogenic media to assess these biocontrol characteristics of the strain. The media utilized included amylase, cellulase, chitinase, and glucanase detection media, with the components of the media referenced from the research methodology of (Meng et al., 2023) [33]. These experiments were carried out at 28 ± 2 °C for 5 days. In each, it was observed whether a transparent circle formed around the colonies, then measured its diameter and that of the colonies, and calculated the D-value (= [transparent circle diameter] – [colony diameter]), as the basis for detecting the activity level of cellulose enzymes, chitinase, siderophore, and dextranase.

The generation of IAA by T. asperellum M2 was qualitatively and quantitatively determined using the colorimetric method, as detailed in previous studies [34]. Briefly, strain M2 was cultured in PDB medium containing tryptophan (Macklin, Shanghai, China), or lacking it, for 5 days (at 28 ± 2 °C, 180 rpm). After centrifugation at 12 000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was mixed in a 1:2 ratio with the Salkowski reagent (150 mL of concentrated H2SO4 [Nanjing Reagent, Nanjing, China], 250 mL of dH2O, 7.5 mL of 0.5 M FeCl3·6H2O [FuChen, Tianjin, China]). After incubation at 30 °C in darkness for 30 min, the mixture’s absorbance was measured at 530 nm by a spectrophotometer (UV8000, METASH, Shanghai, China). Finally, the IAA concentration was quantified by comparing these readings against a standard IAA solution (Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA).

Observed colonization of A. sinensis roots by T. asperellum M2

The Agrobacterium-mediated genetic transformation method was used to introduce the pHB-RFP vector carrying the RFP gene into T. asperellum M2 [35]. The experimental procedures including the cultivation of Agrobacterium AGL-1, the collection of conidia from T. asperellum M2, the co-culture and transformation process of Agrobacterium AGL-1 and the M2 strain, as well as the detection of the fluorescence stability of the T. asperellum M2 transformants. To observe the colonization of T. asperellum M2 in the roots of A. sinensis, the GFP-labeled strain M2-GFP was inoculated using the root-drenching method. The T. asperellum M2 -RFP was incubated in 100 mL of PDB medium containing 100 µg/mL Kanamycin (Rhawn, China), for 7 days at 28 ± 2 °C. First, the root surfaces of well-grown A. sinensis plants (1 year old) were disinfected; then they were transplanted into plastic pots (10 × 20 × 15 cm [bottom diameter × top diameter × height]) with sterilized soil. The sterile soil used was silty loam. After that, we treated each with 100 mL of the T. asperellum M2 -RFP culture (105 CFU/mL), or 100 mL of water as a blank control. These experimental plants were kept in a greenhouse under a temperature cycle of 25/15°C (day/night). To see where RFP-T. asperellum M2 successfully colonized A. sinensis roots, tissue samples were randomly collected at 3, 5, 7, 10, 15 days post-inoculation (dpi). Collected roots were washed with water and cut into thin slices (1 mm) for observation under a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Olympus FV3000, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), at an excitation wavelength of 588 nm.

Effect of T. asperellum M2 on the mycelial morphology of F. acuminatum

Dual culture of T. asperellum and F. acuminatum were prepared on PDA at 28 ± 2 °C. After 5 days of co-culturing, fungal hyphae samples were picked from the interaction area of the two strains and placed in a centrifuge tube that contained 1.5 mL of an FAA fixation solution (70% ethanol, 37% formaldehyde, glacial acetic acid, and the volume ratios of each component being 18:1:1). Samples were fixed for 48–72 h at room temperature. These fixed samples were then dehydrated with a series of ethanol solutions (50%, 70%, 80%, 90% and 96%, in turn). Each concentration was treated for 20 min, after which the samples were placed in a critical point-drying chamber for 2 h. Once dried, they were ion spray-coated with gold particles in a high vacuum chamber following the procedure described by [36]. The set of prepared samples were sent to the electron microscope laboratory of Lanzhou Jiaotong University, where each was observed under a scanning electron microscope (ZEISS, GeminiSEM 500, Germany).

Effect of T. asperellum M2 fermentation filtrate on the growth of F. acuminatum

To conduct a culture filtrate-mediated inhibition test, T. asperellum M2 was cultured on PDA at 25 ± 2 °C for 5 days until sporulation. From the resulting spore suspension (containing 1 × 106 spores/mL) 1 mL was taken and inoculated it onto 100 mL PDB, followed by incubation for 5 days (at 25 ± 2 °C, on a shaker at 180 rpm). First, a sterile gauze (three layers) is used to conduct an initial filtration of the fermentation broth of strain M2 to remove the mycelia and residual culture medium. Then, the filtrate obtained from the initial process is passed through a sterile filter (with a pore size of 0.22 micrometers). Finally, the filtrate is diluted with sterile water to obtain samples with fermentation liquor concentrations of 10, 20, 40, or 50% (v/v). Each diluted filtrate was evenly spread on separate PDA medium, PDA supplemented with sterile distilled water served as the control. A mycelial agar plug (5 mm in diameter) of F. acuminatum was inoculated at PDA plate’s center for the culture filtrate treatment, with cultures then incubated in the dark at 25 ± 2 °C for 7 days. Each treatment was performed in triplicate. The inhibition rate (IR) was calculated this way: IR (%) = (1 – [Dcf./Dck]) × 100; where Dcf. is the colony diameter of F. acuminatum in the culture filtrate treatment, and Dck is the colony diameter of F. acuminatum in the control.

Effect of the T. asperellum M2 fermentation filtrate on spore germination of F. acuminatum

To prepare the spore suspension of the pathogenic fungus, 10 mL of sterile water was added to the fungal growth plate previously incubated for 7 days. Hyphae were scraped off the culture medium’s surface with a sterile toothbrush, and the resulting mixed suspension then filtered through sterile gauze. The same approach was used for the preparation of T. asperellum M2 sterile filtrates. The sterile fermentation filtrate of T. asperellum M2 was diluted with sterile distilled water to concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, 5.0, 50.0, and 100.0% (v/v). The F. acuminatum spore suspension and different concentrations of the sterile filtrate of M2 were mixed in a 1:1 ratio (v: v), such that the latter’s respective final contents were 0.25, 0.5, 2.5, 25.0, and 50.0% (v/v) [37]. The control group consisted of F. acuminatum spore suspension treated with sterile water. All mixtures were cultured at 28 ± 2 °C on concave slides. After 24 h, spore germination was microscopically observed (Yongxing N-800 F). Germination was defined to have occurred when the length of a germ tube exceeded half the diameter of the small end of conidia. Each observation was repeated thrice to determine the inhibition rate of spore germination. At least 50/ to 100 spores were examined in each visual field. The spore germination rate (GR) and inhibition rate (IR) were calculated as follows: GR (%) = (G1 – [G2/G1]) × 100, where G1is the total number of spores observed in each treatment group, and G2 is the number of germinated spores observed in each treatment group; IR (%) = (C1–[C2/C1]) × 100, where C1 is the spore germination rate of the blank control group, and C2 is the spore germination rate in each treatment group [38].

Effect of the T. asperellum M2 culture supernatant on membrane permeabilization of F. acuminatum

The impact of the T. asperellum M2 culture’s supernatant on intracellular ions was investigated using electric conductivity measurements [37]. The F. acuminatum mycelia were cultivated in 100 ml PDB medium at 25 °C, 180 rpm/min, in the dark conditions for 7 days. Then, the fungal culture was centrifuged at 8000×g/min for 2 min to obtain the F. acuminatum mycelia. Next, the mycelia were washed three times with 10 ml of sterile PBS buffer, and then resuspended in 10 ml of sterile PBS buffer. Subsequently, 10, 20, or 50% of the sterile culture filtrate of T. asperellum M2 was added to the mycelia in the culture. The culture was carried out under sterile conditions at 28 °C, 180 rpm/min. The cell membrane conductivity of each treatment was measured using an electroporator (INESA, Shanghai, China) every 3 h. The culture without adding the sterile culture filtrate of T. asperellum M2 was used as the control; this experiment was performed in triplicate.

Biocontrol effect of T. asperellum M2 on A. sinensis root rot in the greenhouse

Experimental treatments wer Minxian County (Gansu Province, China). Healthy A. sinensis seedlings were planted in plastic pots inside the greenhouse of Lanzhou Jiaotong University. Each pot had an inner diameter of 10 cm at the bottom, an inner diameter of 20 cm at the top, and a height of 15 cm. All pots were first filled with a mixture of soil and nutrients (Hebei Dewodo fertilizer Co., LTD) in a 1:2 ratio (v/v). Then we watered each pot and loosened the soil regularly to ensure the healthy growth of seedlings for 2 weeks before inoculating the pathogen. Meanwhile, spore suspensions of the F. acuminatum and T. asperellum M2 strains were prepared as follows: First, 10 mL of sterile water was added to the PDA plate where either test fungus had been growing for 10 days. Their hyphae on the medium’s surface were scraped away (using a sterile toothbrush), and the mixed suspension then filtered through sterile gauze. Next, the concentration of conidia in the filtrate was measured with a hemocytometer, with the final inoculation concentration adjusted to 105 conidia/mL by dilution. For inoculation, a sterile scalpel was used to create a small wound, 5 mm deep, on the surface of the taproot. Then, 10 to 15 mL of the conidial suspension of one or both fungal isolates was poured into the root zone of each plant (two seedlings per pot), with three replicates. Seedlings treated with sterile water served as the control group.

To assess the biocontrol efficacy of T. asperellum M2 against F. acuminatum under greenhouse conditions, we assigned the A. sinensis seedlings to four treatments: (T1) treated with sterile water only (healthy control); (T2) F. acuminatum-inoculated seedlings (disease control); (T3) seedlings first inoculated with F. acuminatum, then inoculated with T. asperellum after 48 h (treatment group). (T4) seedlings inoculated first with T. asperellum followed by F. acuminatum after 48 h (prevention group). All treated seedlings were maintained under these greenhouse conditions: 25 ± 3 °C, relative humidity > 70%, 14 h of light per day, irrigated with sterile water every 3 days. The capacity of T. asperellum to suppress disease due to various Fusarium phytopathogens was evaluated by visually assessing the lesions in each seedling, aboveground and belowground. Disease severity was assessed post-inoculation on an integer rating scale of 0 to 4 [39, 40]. Specifically, disease severity was classified and scored as follows: 0 = asymptomatic seedlings; 1 = less than 25% of the total leaf area is yellow; mild water stains and decay near the root wound site; 2 = 25–50% of stem tissue and leaves in each seedling are withered; 25–50% of root tissues have developed dark brown lesions; 3 = 50–75% of stem tissue and leaves in each seedling are withered; greater than 50% of the root tissue is rotten, showing black-brown rot areas and mycelium; 4 = seedling is completely rotted or dead, with root tissue entirely rotten and covered by mycelium. Accordingly, a non-parametric approach was used, by calculating the median, mean rank (ijy), and the relative effects of processing (ij) as proposed by Shah and Madden (2004). The severity of root rot caused by F. acuminatum was evaluated within 95% confidence intervals (CIs). PROC RANK was used to calculate the median and ij values; PROC MIXED was used to calculate ij, and CIs were calculated with the SAS macro OWL, as described by researchers [41]. The control efficiency of A. sinensis root rot was calculated this way: Control efficiency (%) = 1 – ([DI in the treatment/DI in control]) × 100 [43].

Once the pathogenicity assays were finished, pathogen was re-isolated from the roots of symptomatic plants and the morphological characteristics of their cultures compared with known cultures of that species. Also, DNA was extracted from re-isolated pathogens, and amplicons from PCR targeting ITS, EF1-a, and RPB2 were sequenced by the Shanghai Sangon Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The ensuing sequences were checked against those in the NCBI database using BLAST search analyses, to confirm Koch’s postulates.

Beneficial effects of T. asperellum M2 for the growth of A. sinensis

The treatments applied to A. sinensis seedlings were the same as that described for biocontrol effect assessment in the greenhouse. That is, a 50 mL-conidia suspension of T. asperellum—containing 3 × 105 conidia/mL—was drenched into the roots of A. sinensis seedlings, while those treated only with sterile water constituted the control group. After 30 days of potential growth, seedings were measured to determine their height (from the soil surface to the top of the highest panicle of each plant), root length, and shoot and root fresh weights. Samples were then oven-dried at 105 °C for 30 min, and further dried at 80 °C for 48 h or until a constant mass was reached, and their dry weight recorded.

Production of resistant enzymes in A. sinensis induced by T. asperellum M2

Enzymes involved in plant resistance, namely peroxidase (POD), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), catalase (CAT), and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), were examined. For this, the fermentation sterile filtrates of F. acuminatum and T. asperellum M2 was prepared. Experimental treatments were the same as the five treatments used in the biocontrol experiments. The specific amount of fermentation liquid was 50 mL of strain M2 sterile filtrate, 50 mL of F. acuminatum sterile filtrate, 25 mL of F. acuminatum sterile filtrate, and 25 mL of strain M2 sterile filtrate; each was taken and used to irrigate the roots of A. sinensis seedlings. For the blank control, 50 mL of sterile filtrates’ water was used. At 30 days after inoculation treatment, a random sample of 0.5 g of root tissue from each treatment group was selected for analysis. Root tissues were ground for assays assessing the enzymatic activities of POD, PAL, CAT, and PPO; each respectively analyzed using commercial assay kits (AIDISHENG, Yancheng, China), as per the manufacturer’s instructions [44, 45]. To determine the activity of each enzyme, we used a spectrophotometer (UA-2800 A, UNIC, Shanghai, China) to record the absorbance values of the four enzymes (POD, PAL, CAT, PPO) at wavelengths of 470 nm, 290 nm, 510 nm, and 525 nm respectively. Further, the concentration of the products can be calculated based on the change in optical density of the solution before and after the reaction, and then the activity of the enzymes can be determined.

Determination of T. asperellum M2’s degradation of the NEO toxin

This assay was done following complete it [46], but with some modifications. The T. asperellum M2 strain was inoculated to the PDB medium containing 20 ug/mL of NEO, and cultured (under conditions of 28 °C, 180 rpm, and darkness no light). The culture medium was collected at 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11 dpi. The culture medium was filtered through a 0.22-µm filter membrane, and then mixed with an equal volume of chromatographically pure acetonitrile as the sample solution. The NEO content at different treatment times was determined by High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC). The NEO degradation rate (Dr) was calculated as follows: Dr (%) = [(C0 – Ct) / C0] ⋅ 100; where CT and C0 are the average concentration of NEO in the culture medium of the treatment and control group, respectively. The treatment group had three replicates, as did the control group: PDB medium containing NEO but not inoculated with T. asperellum M2. All experiments were repeated three times.

Pattern analysis of NEO removal by T. asperellum M2

To determine functional detoxification process outcome, we analyzed the effects of the culture supernatant, intracellular components, and mycelium of strain M2 on its ability to remove NEO. This assay was carried out as described in [47], albeit with some modifications. The M2 strain was inoculated to PDB medium and cultured for 7 days (28 ± 2 °C, 180 rpm). After centrifugation and filtration, the ensuing supernatant was collected and passed through a 0.22-µm filter membrane; the resulting filtrate was designated the extracellular component (EC) of the strain. After filtration, mycelia were collected and washed twice with PBS buffer solution; a portion of the mycelia was suspended in 100 mL of PBS buffer to serve as the active cell component (AC). The other portions of mycelia were immediately freeze-dried and placed in a mortar, with liquid nitrogen added for grinding. The ground mycelia were resuspended in PBS buffer (about 2.0 mL of PBS per gram of mycelium). Later, the supernatant was ultrasonicated under an ice bath for 30 min. After centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min at low temperature, the filtrate was passed through a 0.22-µm filter membrane, and this filtrate then used as the intracellular metabolic product component (IC). The NEO standard was added to each cell component (EC, AC, IC, and the control sample) containing PBS buffer, with the final concentration of NEO being 20 µg/mL. All samples were then allowed to sit at 28 °C for 72 h, after which 1.0 mL of each component was taken, poured through a 0.22-µm filter membrane, and its NEO concentration determined by HPLC.

Degradation dynamics of NEO by T. asperellum M2’s active cellular components

Using the aforementioned method, the mycelial suspension of strain M2 was prepared and obtained the active cell component (AC). The NEO standard was added to a 10-mL tube, with each tube receiving 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, and 1.1% of the mycelial suspension, respectively, until the final NEO concentration reached 20 ug/ml. Then, samples were moved into a dark environment and cultured at 28 °C for 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 13 days. After that, 1.0 mL of each sample was taken per tube and filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane. HPLC was used to quantify the NEO concentration and to investigate how different inoculation amounts and reaction times influenced the process of NEO degradation.

To investigate two other possible influencing factors, the following steps and procedures were implemented. (i) Take 20 mL of the M2 mycelial suspension and transfer it into a 50 mL test tube. Add the NEO standard to achieve a final concentration of 20 µg/mL. Subsequently, take 3 mL of this preparation and transfer it into a 10 mL test tube. For the control group, replace the NEO standard with PBS buffer as a solvent. Incubate both the samples and the control group at different temperatures (24, 26, 28, 30, or 32 °C) under light-free conditions for 72 h. After incubation, filter all samples, including the control group, through a 0.22-µm membrane, and analyze the NEO concentrations using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), to explore the influence of different reaction temperatures on the degradation effect of strain M2. (ii) Take the M2 mycelium suspension (with a pH value of 7), and adjust its acidity or alkalinity using concentrated hydrochloric acid or concentrated sodium hydroxide respectively to obtain mycelium suspension solutions with pH values of 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9. From each adjusted pH solution, transfer 3.0 mL into separate 10.0 mL test tubes. Add the NEO standard to each tube to achieve a final concentration of 20 µg/mL. For the control group, use PBS buffer instead of the NEO standard. Incubate all samples, including the control group, in the dark at 28 °C for 72 h. After incubation, filter each sample through a 0.22-µm membrane. Analyze the NEO concentration in each sample using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Finally, evaluate how variations in pH affect the degradation of NEO. The formula used here for calculating Dr, the degradation rate of NEO, is: Dr (%) = ([C – S] / C) × 100; where C is the NEO concentration in the control group, and S is the NEO concentration in the treatment group.

Determination of the degradation products of NEO toxins

Following the above method, the M2 mycelial suspension was prepared. The NEO standard substance was added to the suspended sample at a 0.9% inoculation volume, to arrive at a final concentration of 20 µg/mL. The pH was adjusted to 8.0 and the sample then placed in the dark, at 28 °C, for 7 days of culturing. Then we took 1.0 mL of the sample, filtered it through a 0.22-µm membrane, and used liquid chromatography/time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC/TOF-MS) to identify the degradation products of NEO toxins. Liquid chromatography employed an Accucore XL C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm) at a temperature of 25 °C, with an injection volume of 5 µL, and a detection wavelength of 254 nm. The chromatographic column was pre-treated with a 98% mobile phase A solution (deionized H2O, 1% formic acid) and a 2% mobile phase B solution (acetonitrile), at a flow rate of 600 µL/min, for gradient elution, using this schedule: 0–2 min, maintain the 98%A/2%B; at 2–6 min, transition from 98%-A/2%-B to 60%-A/40%-B; at 6–7.5 min, transition from 60%-A/40%-B to 10%-A/90%-B; at 7.5–9.8 min, maintain 10%-A/90%-B; at 9.8–10.5 min, transition from 10%-A/90%-B to 98%-A/2%-B; lastly, at 10.5–12.5 min, maintain 98%-A/2%-B.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and presented as the mean ± SEM. To detect differences among group means, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Tukey’s HSD (honest significant difference) test at a significance level of P < 0.05.

The experimental results showed that as the reaction time increased, the degradation rate of NEO by the mycelial suspension of Trichoderma M2 steadily increased. Determination of the degradation products of NEO

Degradation products of the NEO toxin were determined by LC/TOF-MS, as depicted in Fig. 10A–D. The molecular weight of NEO is 328. In the positive ion mode, based on the molecular structure, the addition of hydrogen (+ 1), sodium (+ 23), and potassium (+ 39) resulted in the detection of peaks at mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) of 383, 405, and 421, respectively. Due to the presence of particular functional groups in the NEO molecule, such as ester, methyl, epoxy, and hydroxyl groups, these reactive constituents may undergo fragmentation during the compound’s reaction process. Based on the cleavage of these chemical bonds, several possible product structures were predicted with observed corresponding peaks in the mass spectrum. Their measured m/z were 219, 153, 179, and 215, respectively. Our study thus identified four products: C14H19O2+ (D1), C13H17O+ (D2), C12H19O (D3), and C11H19O4+ (D4) (Supplementary Fig. 5A–D).

Discussion

The present study began with an in vitro biocontrol evaluation of T. asperellum M2 against F. acuminatum, F. oxysporum, F. solani, F. equiseti, F. redolens, and F. avenaceum. The dual culture assay demonstrated that the T. asperellum M2 strain is capable of effectively inhibiting these Fusarium pathogens. Trichoderma species of fungi are reported to have considerable antagonistic activity, which can be harnessed to control a variety of plant pathogens [48, 49]. Our research results are consistent with a number of other investigations findings that T. asperellum strains harbor strong potential as adaptable biocontrol agents against Fusarium spp. We find that the biological antagonist T. asperellum M2 not only inhibits the pathogenic mycelial growth but could also grow on it, causing mycelium destruction and the cytoplasm leakage from hyphae. Crucially, the tested T. asperellum M2 strain grew considerably faster than the Fusarium strains and quickly overwhelmed these pathogens. A capacity for rapid growth confers to antagonists a key advantage when competing with pathogens for space and nutrients [50]. Hyperparasitism is one of the important biocontrol mechanisms by which Trichoderma inhibits pathogens. When subjected to confrontation assays, Trichoderma recognizes the pathogen’s hyphae through lectins and produces large amounts of chitinase and glucanase to dissolve the pathogen’s cell walls. In our study, T. asperellum M2 secreted cellulase, protease, and chitinase, which can break down fungal cell walls and contribute to mycoparasitism. These findings are consistent with those of many other studies involving Trichoderma strains [51–53], which established a link between their ability to control plant pathogenic fungi and the amount of cell wall-degrading enzymes they secreted.

Inoculation with T. asperellum M2 significantly mitigated root rot disease in A. sinensis seedlings caused by their inoculation with F. acuminatum. However, first inoculating hosts with the T. asperellum M2 strain (i.e., prior to infection) was more effective in controlling disease than when inoculated after exposure to F. acuminatum. Likewise, other research reported that seed-soaking with Trichoderma fungi considerably augmented the vigor of tomato seedlings, in addition to increasing the seed germination rate, by facilitating plant growth and enhancing their tolerance to adverse conditions [54]. In our study, this is probably because T. asperellum M2 had more time to establish and colonize the root zone (rhizosphere) of A. sinensis before the pathogen inoculation; this would have allowed the strain to preferentially occupy the limited niche and compete for nutrients, thereby reducing the space and nutrients available for the pathogen [55]. Certain Trichoderma spp. can colonize the rhizosphere and intercellular root compartments of plants, to sustain symbiotic interactions with them [56]. In our study, stable colonization of A. sinensis seedlings’ roots was confirmed using the RFP-labeled T. asperellum M2. These results showed that the red fluorescence signal was strongest on the 7th day, with it persisting on the 15th day. From this, we may infer that T. asperellum M2 survives well in host tissues. In other research, the T. brevicompactum BF06 strain successfully colonized the root tissues of cucumbers and promoted their lateral root growth, while a green fluorescence-labeled T. asperellum M45a strain effectively colonized the interior of roots (Li et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021). Our latest research findings we can use T. asperellum M2 through fermentation to produce biocontrol agents or bio-organic fertilizers, and further evaluate their potential for disease resistance and growth promotion in field experiments.

As our results show, the activity of CAT, SOD, and POD enzymes in the root tissue of A. sinensis was induced by inoculation with the T. asperellum M2 strain alone, or F. acuminatum alone, or both together, each significantly exceeding that of the water control. This response can be best explained by Trichoderma stimulating the systemic defense responses of growing plants by activating defense enzymes, including peroxidase, polyphenol oxidase, and chitinase [57], combined with Trichoderma’s inhibitory effect on pathogenic fungi to enhance overall host resistance to plant disease [58]. Recent work with blueberry showed greater CAT, SOD, and POD enzyme activities in plant tissue after inoculation with T. asperellum TM11 or T. asperellum TM11 alongside F. oxysporum [29].

Nearly all Fusarium species can produce mycotoxins, defined as toxic secondary metabolites of low molecular weight, which are thermally stable and easily bioaccumulate in crops from sowing to harvesting in the field through the storage and food processing stages [59]. Our study’s results suggest that type A trichothecene NEO is the chief mycotoxin present during Fusarium-induced rot of A. sinensis roots, which can elicit strong toxicity in humans if consumed [60]. We find that F. acuminatum could produce neosolaniol (NEO), deoxynivalenol, and T-2 toxins; of these, the level of NEO produced by F. acuminatum was rather high relative to the other two toxins [61]. The breakdown of mycotoxins, which mainly depends on biodegradation by bacteria, fungi, and yeasts or by their specific enzymes, is arguably the most effective, specific, and eco-friendly solution to decompose aflatoxins into nontoxic or less-toxic compounds in food and feed products [62–64]. Although T. harzianum, T. reesei, T. koningiopsis, and T. asperellum, do have the ability to degrade hydrocarbon compounds such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), crude oil, and resins [65] reports on their biodegradation of small molecule contaminants remain sparse, particularly for NEO. In this study, the fermentation broth of T. asperellum M2 was able to degrade 20 µg/mL of the NEO toxin. Notably, the degradation rate was greatest after 11 days of treatment, reaching 87.14%, which suggests that T. asperellum M2 can prevent F. acuminatum infections and the onset of plant disease by swiftly degrading this potent Fusarium toxin.

To determine those involved dynamics, we also tested the NEO removal ability among various cell components of the T. asperellum M2 strain. The NEO removal ratio for the active component (49.4%) was at least 1/3 higher than for the extracellular component (33.6%) or intracellular component (24.2%). Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that the removal of NEO by T. asperellum M2 is mainly due to biodegradation processes. Moreover, the impact of temperature on degradation efficiency is also important. When the temperature rose from 24 to 28 °C, the degradation efficiency reached 45.8%; but with more heat, the degradation efficiency dropped significantly. Our results suggest the optimal temperature is approximately 28 °C, which is close to the optimal growth temperature of T. asperellum M2 (28–30 °C). NEO’s degradation remained high even under strongly acidic or alkali conditions of pH 3.0 or 9.0, respectively. This implies that when NEO is exposed to highly acidic or highly alkaline conditions, its toxicity may diminish somewhat. Our current findings are consistent with the removal mechanisms and optimal conditions of aflatoxins, as described in two recent articles [66, 67]. NEO is a type of trichothecenes mycotoxin characterized by the presence of an acetyl group (ester group) at the C-3 position [68]. Biocontrol of Angelica se hydrolysis of ester bonds, thereby removing the acetyl group and altering the toxicity of the compound. Research into the enzymatic degradation of mycotoxins, such as zearalenone (ZEA), has demonstrated that esterases can effectively hydrolyze their ester bonds, consequently reducing their toxicity [69]. Furthermore, studies on the degradation of mycotoxins have revealed that certain microbial enzymes can facilitate hydroxylation or demethylation of these toxins. For instance, Bacillus species have been shown to possess the ability to degrade various mycotoxins, likely involving modifications to specific functional groups [70]. The enzymatic cleavage of ester, methyl, epoxide, and hydroxyl groups within the NEO molecule may be a crucial mechanism that promotes the degradation and transformation of mycotoxins. Although this study has identified four degradation products of the NEO toxin, the potential toxicity of these products has not been evaluated. Therefore, future experimental research on its toxicological effects in plants is necessary.

Conclusion

In this comprehensive study, the T. asperellum M2 strain was screened and found to have a pronounced inhibitory effect against F. acuminatum. This strain effectively suppresses the mycelial growth and spore germination of F. acuminatum and substantially enhances the permeability of this pathogen’s fungal cell membrane. Through pot experiments, we show that T. asperellum M2 achieves a control efficacy of 57.61% against F. acuminatum, with the preventive treatment group yielding better results than the therapeutic treatment group. In addition, T. asperellum M2 can colonize the roots of A. sinensis, promote its growth, and enhance plant’s resistance to pathogenic fungi. Follow-up analysis of the components that may harbor detoxification functions revealed that the cell-active components of T. asperellum M2 could efficiently degrade NEO (20 µg/mL), attaining a degradation rate of 49.4%. From the LC/TOF-MS, we identified four potential degradation products of NEO by T. asperellum M2. In sum, the T. asperellum M2 strain not only possesses excellent antagonistic properties but also shows great potential for degrading NEO toxins, indicating its broad application prospects as a safe and effective bioremediation agent in mitigating root rot diseases in crops and protecting agricultural products and food.

Study limitations

This study is limited to controlled greenhouse conditions and has not yet conducted field trials, which may not adequately reflect the true performance of the strains in an agricultural environment. Furthermore, although four degradation products of NEO toxins have been identified, their potential toxicity has not been assessed, resulting in a gap in safety evaluations.
