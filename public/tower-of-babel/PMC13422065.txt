Using social media to highlight inhaled occupational exposures as a cause of lung disease
Authors: David Fishwick, Sara De Matteis, Martie van Tongeren, Emma Thompson, Dick J J Heederik, Saskia Weldam, Ewelina Szmytke, Jessica Denning, Andrew David Curran, Pippa Powell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe risks to lung health from workplace hazards remain incompletely understood and may still be overlooked by workers, employers and healthcare professionals. We used an online tool to collect European population-based data relating to workplace exposures, training and lung health.MethodsA targeted industry-specific digital tool, enquiring about the link between respiratory symptoms and work, was made accessible online over a 24-month period.Results22 393 individuals (mean age 43.6 years) responded; 55.7% were female, 46.7% completed in English, 19.9% in Portuguese, 17.8% in French, 10.5% in Dutch and 5% in German. 47% reported nasal symptoms, 42% cough, 21% chest tightness and 20% wheeze. 8412 (37.6%) reported at least one work-related symptom. The use of workplace training (11%) and guidance (17%) to prevent respiratory ill health was uncommon. 980 (5.9%) had changed their job because it had affected their breathing. Increasing age (OR 1.02 per year age increase), completion language (English (OR 0.66), French (OR 0.39)), workplace (bakery (OR 1.64), mine (OR 1.91), sports centre (OR 3.03) and farm (OR 1.77)) and workplace training (OR 0.71) were significantly associated with this outcome. These workers also had more work-related symptoms (p<0.001) and complained of more wheeze (p<0.001) and nasal symptoms (p<0.001). 919 (5.6%) workers with a more likely diagnosis of occupational asthma were more female, had less workplace guidance and training and had co-workers with health complaints.ConclusionThis tool has reached real-world working populations and these findings identify the need for further awareness raising, education and social media interventions.Keywords: Asthma, Epidemiology, Occupational Health

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

It is estimated that approximately 15% of the burden of all diagnosed asthma is attributed to occupational exposures. A broad range of agents (eg, for example flour dust, isocyanate paints, laboratory animals, metal working fluids, enzymes) and jobs (eg, bakers, vehicle spray painters, machinists) have been associated with occupational asthma but a complete exhaustive list is still unknown.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study adds data from a large population sample and notes that self-reporting of respiratory and nasal symptoms is common in workers across many work sectors, along with the potential causative link between these symptoms and work. Training and guidance offered by workplaces to reduce such respiratory health risks were generally uncommon. A sizeable proportion (5.9%) of participants had changed their job because of difficulty with breathing. A more likely diagnosis of occupational asthma was associated with female sex, less availability of workplace guidance and training and co-workers with similar health complaints. Certain sectors (eg, bakery work) had higher levels of a more likely occupational asthma and lower provision of training and guidance.

Introduction

Inhaled workplace exposures remain an important cause of preventable lung diseases.1 2 However, certain risks to lung health from workplace hazards remain incompletely understood and may be underappreciated or overlooked by workers, their employers and healthcare professionals.3 Additionally, there are only limited and primarily qualitative real-world data relating to the understanding of occupational lung diseases by workers themselves.4 5

The aim of this project was therefore to develop an online tool that would pose such questions to participants in high-risk work sectors recruited by an online survey. The tool was co-created by a set of European stakeholders, including representation from the funder; the European Lung Foundation6 7

We present here the findings of this work, from the first 24 months after the launch of the tool. It aimed to collect information about the worker experience in high-risk sectors, to identify the characteristics of those with a more likely diagnosis of occupational asthma and to quantify education and training offered in the workplace where workplace exposures may increase the risk of developing an occupational lung disease.

Methods

An online tool, designed to collect information about work and breathing problems, was developed in collaboration with European respiratory and occupational experts and respiratory patients (https://yourlungsatwork.europeanlung.org/en/). It consisted of a series of questions about whether a participant’s workplace might be affecting their breathing or lung health. Individual feedback was given to the participants in the form of a summary of their responses and a link to a predeveloped factsheet about their workplace. Given the nature of the tool’s intended use and requirements of the stakeholders, question construct was based on the occupational asthma standard of care8 and others bespoke to the requirement of the team.

Respiratory work disability9 10 was recorded as a response to the question ‘Have you ever had to leave or change your job because of your breathing?’. If a participant responded positively, an opportunity to give a text descriptor was included.

Specifically, participants were asked to record the presence of any respiratory (chest and nasal) symptoms and their work-relatedness; worse at work or better on rest days. Symptoms could be chosen from a preset number of options. Participants were also asked, where relevant, when their symptoms started in relation to their work history.

Participants chose their current workplace from a preset list (later used to assist social media targeting of various a priori defined high-risk areas). These included workplaces with exposures known to constitute a high risk for the development of occupational lung disease, such as a bakery (flour dust), a wood workshop (wood dust) and a garage (paints, resin-based fillers, etc). The respondents were also asked about specific exposures.

Age, gender, smoking status, employment status, whether co-workers had similar symptoms at work and provision of employer-provided training and guidance were also documented.

Targeted Facebook11 advertising was used to promote the survey. No incentives for participation were given. Adverts were designed so that the participant was able to click directly to the occupational tool, and posted 1 month at a time, after which new adverts were posted, the targeted occupation changed in rotation. The Facebook advertising platform was set up so that it is possible to automatically target users of a certain demographic or with certain interests, rather than searching manually for certain occupational groups.

A particular demographic was targeted. This was workers aged between 16 and 65+ years of age and able to speak the languages in which the occupational tool was available (English, French, German, Portuguese and Dutch). Individuals working within certain a priori specified occupations were also targeted using a series of keywords relating either to an area of interest, education or employment.

An a priori definition was used to identify those more likely to have occupational asthma, consistent with agreed8 definitions: (1) chest tightness or wheeze that was; (2) work related (better on days off work) and (3) had occurred either since starting the current job or started recently.

Analyses were performed in SPSS (IBM Corp. Released 2020. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.27.0., IBM Corp) and largely descriptive in nature. Differences in proportion were analysed using χ2 testing and Fisher’s exact testing where appropriate. Differences in mean age were assessed using Student’s t-test within SPSS. Logistic regression was carried out within SPSS and assessed the contribution of a set of potentially predictor variables (language of completion, gender, workplace type, training and guidance availability all as categorical variables, with age added as a continuous variable) on the outcome of respiratory work disability. Statistical significance was taken at 5% unless otherwise stated.

Qualitative analysis was undertaken for the single question permitting a freehand text response to enquiry about leaving a job if it had affected the participant’s breathing. An analysis of emergent themes was conducted using an inductive approach by two researchers (PP and JD), adhering to previously established principles,12 and representative quotations were chosen to illustrate certain focus points and to enrich the quantitative data analysis.

Results

Quantitative data analysis

In the first 24 months, 22 393 individuals participated; of those with available data (n=17 624), 55.7% were female, 46.7% completed in English, 19.9% in Portuguese, 17.8% in French, 10.5% in Dutch and 5% in German. The mean age (of the n=16 551 with data between 18 and 90 years old) was 43.6 years (SD 13.8), and breakdown by age groups was as follows: 16–24 years (11.6%), 25–34 (16.9%), 35–44 (21.3%), 45–54 (24.8%), 55–64 (20.8%) and over 65 years (4.6%).

The most common workplaces reported were factory work (15%), hospitals (11.2%), construction (7.9%), garage (6.1%) and bakery (2.7%). Certain occupations appeared to be more male dominated (factory work, garage work, wood workshop, building and construction work) and others more female dominated (bakery, hair and beauty and hospital/clinic work).

Reported symptoms were common, for example, nasal symptoms 46.8%, cough 42.3%, chest tightness 20.9% and wheeze 20.1%. 8412 (37.6%) individuals reported at least one of these symptoms to be work related (better on days off work), and 5.9% of workers had changed their job because it had affected their breathing.

Workplaces with the highest prevalence (positive symptoms as a proportion of all in that workplace) of wheeze were in a mine (25.4%), construction site (23.8%) and a factory (21.7%), and for nasal symptoms were a factory (49.7%), a garage (47.8) and a bakery (47.1%).

Reported symptoms did also appear to relate to specific exposures in a univariate analysis; for example, wheeze was significantly associated with exposures to animals, aerosols, dyes, asbestos, cleaning agents, latex, moulds, chlorine and fumes.

The use of training (11% overall) and guidance (17% overall) to prevent respiratory ill health was variable. A lesser proportion than average reported receiving training about potential risks in the hair and beauty work sector (3.2%), farms (8.1%), sport centres (9.2%), bakeries (9.3%) and garages (9.9%). Training was most prevalent (27%) in the worksite described as ‘in a site of a fire, building collapse, chemical spill or similar’. Lower than average provision of workplace guidance was reported in hair and beauty work (5.3%), bakeries (10.7%), sport centres (14.6%), hospitals (16.3%) and farms (16.5%).

There were 980 (5.9%) individuals who had ever changed their job because it affected their breathing (with age data available). Logistic regression identified that increasing age (OR 1.023, 95% CI 1.017 to 1.028, per year age increase), completion language (English (OR 0.66, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.85), French (OR 0.39, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.54)), workplace (bakery (OR 1.64, 95% CI 1.15 to 2.35), mine (OR 1.91, 95% CI 1.18 to 3.09), sports centre (OR 3.03, 95% CI 1.98 to 4.64) and farm (OR 1.77, 95% CI 1.2 to 2.62)) and workplace training (OR 0.71, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.94) were significantly associated with this outcome. There was no association with the presence of workplace guidance. These individuals also had more work relation of their current symptoms (p<0.001) and complained of more wheeze (p<0.001) and nasal symptoms (p<0.001).

Table 2 shows the characteristics of those 919 workers (with relevant data available for analysis) who a priori were defined as more likely to have occupational asthma. It is evident that this group were more likely to be female, be in receipt of less workplace guidance and training to prevent work-related lung disease and have co-workers with similar health complaints. There was a clinically significant association between this group and those who had ever had to change their job because it affected their breathing (p<0.001, χ2). Nasal symptoms (not itself part of the definition of occupational asthma) were also more common in this group. Working in a wood workshop or factory was associated with higher levels of more likely occupational asthma compared with the referent ‘other work environment’.

Qualitative analysis

364 answered the free text response to the question: ‘Have you ever had to leave or change your job because of your breathing? (If so why?)’. Those making a comment did not differ significantly in terms of age and gender to those who did not do so.

Nine themes emerged, detailed in table 3. The majority of respondents reported that they were leaving their job due to poor air quality in the workplace. This varied from exposure to dust particles, cooking fumes, chemical detergents and mould. Many people cited leaving work due to more general lung symptoms, such as cough, breathlessness and recurrent chest infections. Quotation Q1 illustrates this; “I was always coughing and had a chest infection”.

Many also reported leaving work due to a specific respiratory diagnosis, often of asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Others commonly cited reasons related to mental ill health, such as anxiety and depression, or various non-lung-related conditions such as heart disease or a physical disability.

A recommendation to leave work by a workplace occupational health or a medical, professional, was not uncommon and in some cases, people were felt to have been ‘pushed’ out of the workplace. Quotation Q2 illustrates this; “…the company claimed my work rate was too slow.” Many people also left their role due to a self-reported inability to do the work required of them. Quotation Q3 illustrates this; “[I was] near retirement age and found it impossible to keep going.”

Less commonly, participants mention protecting their own health, with early signs of lung disease, or when recognising unsafe working practices. Indeed, there were several reports of not trusting the employer to protect them: in one case from exposure to asbestos, and in others from poor air quality in general. Quotation Q4 illustrates this; “Enclosed space [with] no ventilation and dust”.

Finally, some reported that they would have left the workplace, but their lung health deteriorated after they had retired, or they were not in a financially suitable position to leave the workplace.

Discussion

This project was developed primarily to provide further evidence about the worker experience, where workplace exposures may increase the risk of developing an occupational lung disease.

Specifically, it is already known that worker understanding of respiratory symptoms is variable, and there is a reluctance for workers to discuss health issues with those at work. In addition, the understanding of these issues by workers depends to a degree on how actively they look to define their symptoms and an appropriate solution. The completion of this project has shown that a pan-European, internet-hosted, study is logistically possible and reached an appropriate target audience.

The findings significantly add to what is already known about the consequences of occupational lung disorders; particularly relating to the demography, first language and gender of affected workers, the provision of guidance and workplace training, and importantly where this is lacking.

These data also offer further quantitative and qualitative characterisation of those who have had to change jobs because of their breathing or were more likely to have occupational asthma. While occupational lung diseases are already known to have significant negative impacts,13 previous work has specifically highlighted that work respiratory disability is relatively common as defined by ever having to change jobs because of a breathing problem. Blanc et al9 identified this outcome in 4% of a population of European workers (similar to this study at 6%), and it was predicted by occupations that caused asthma, self-reported regular exposure to environmental tobacco smoke at work and self-reported job exposure to vapours, gases, dust or fumes. Also, our study confirmed a positive association of asthma-like symptoms with occupational exposure to cleaning agents as reported before.14 Our work identified that this group, based on our respondents with respiratory disability, has female predominance, complains of more relevant symptoms and lacks, relatively, appropriate workplace training and guidance to reduce the risks of developing lung diseases. Qualitative themes that emerged in this group were by equal measures interesting and controversial. It appeared that workers were potentially being advised to resign from work that was harmful for their health or were ‘forced out’ of work because of repeated sickness. Speculatively, limited options were available for this group; essentially relocation to another presumably lesser exposed work area or job loss. Mental ill health such as stress, anxiety and depression were understandably a significant additional feature in this group and have been previously identified. In addition, an a priori definition of more likely occupational asthma was used to assess differences across workplaces. It appeared that participants working in a wood workshop or a factory were more likely to have occupational asthma than the reference group, although interestingly some categories have unexpectedly lower rates of participants so categorised: building or construction site, garage and hair or beauty salon. Certain of these worksite categories might have been expected to be over-represented in the more likely occupational asthma group.8

Certain differences in responses were seen across language of completion. These may perhaps have reflected real differences (for example in training, guidance use, smoking status) while others perhaps because of the dissemination and recruitment process used and the potential for differential participation bias between languages. Linguistic, translational, cultural and technological reasons could have explained differences in responses seen across language groups although it is not possible to be definitive about why differential responses were seen.

There are a significant set of challenges associated with this approach. First, the methodology does not permit an estimation of the likely denominator. Also, all health data are self-reported and not triangulated by an independent source such as healthcare records. It is also highly likely that participation bias is operational; namely that those who felt they had a particular problem related to work may have preferentially participated in comparison to those with no breathing problem. A detail added to the landing page was that those with work related problems might wish to specifically participate. This is likely to have led to an upward bias in the prevalence data in general, although the relationships between variables may not necessarily be biased, and representativeness may not necessarily be an overriding aim in a study such as this.15

In terms of the overall data, the gender difference in the study was only slightly female predominant, and the age profile similar to that seen in the labour force survey in the UK,16 although at a descriptive level our study did have an over-representation of 35–54 year olds, and fewer participants over 65. Also, levels of reported symptoms, although high, were similar to those seen in population-based studies. For example, wheeze was identified by 20.1% of the study population, and this figure is similar, although historic and not directly comparable, to that described (median of 20.7% in the UK) in a comprehensive global population prevalence study.17

Similarly, reported levels of work-related disability (leaving a job because of a breathing problem) in 5.9% of our participants compare well with the European Community Respiratory Health Survey (ECRHS) overall estimate of 4% (range 1% to 8% within different countries studied).9 Similarly, the ECRHS prevalence estimate of those with respiratory symptoms worse at work of 10% (range 4% to 15%)9 was analogous to the prevalence of our definition of occupational asthma of 5.6%.

The population-based nature of this study also led to significant missing data for certain variables, as participants were able to leave certain variables blank if they wished. These missing data may have also had an effect on the generalisability of the study findings. It was for the aforementioned reasons that data were not imputed, or responses weighted further and thus an essentially descriptive analysis, not adjusted for potential confounders, was displayed. Consequently, a careful interpretation of these findings is needed.

It was not possible also to characterise a specific diagnosis, and no further diagnostic information (eg, a confirmed occupational asthma diagnosis) was recorded. It is probable that most with lung disease had asthma given its population prevalence, and certainly the array of relevant occupations identified as associated would be associated with exposures that could cause asthma. The post hoc definition of those more likely to have occupational asthma was constructed for the purposes of this analysis and has not been validated in other work and may lack sensitivity given the phrasing of the questions relating to timing of onset of a particular symptom (at the start of employment). It is likely that some individuals with work aggravated asthma and COPD may have been included in this definition.

Indeed, the presence of COPD in this study population, the third leading cause for mortality and disability worldwide, may well have related in certain circumstances to workplace exposures that could have caused or contributed to its development,18 is also a significant possibility. The likely smaller contribution from interstitial lung disease is again not possible to quantify using our methodology. Further work would clearly be needed to comment further about whether a respondent was likely to have a specific diagnosis.

It was not possible, given the design and anonymous nature of this approach, to identify what proportion of participants subsequently sought healthcare advice about a possible occupational lung disease. That said, the results appear to align in population terms to what is known about occupational lung diseases, including the typical symptoms, common exposures and workplaces and also the prevalence of those with work disability and more likely occupational asthma identified to be similar to population-based estimates.

The results of this study add significantly to our understanding of occupational lung disorders based on a large population sample, and highlight the need for future education, training and policy initiatives designed to reduce their development.
