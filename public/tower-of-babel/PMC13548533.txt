Accumulation of membrane repair-associated proteins and mature myostatin are novel markers of muscle pathophysiology in Pompe disease
Authors: Candice Babarit, Sabrina Jagot, Cindy Schleder, Johan Deniaud, Tiffany Bourgeton, Lydie Lagalice, Lucie Vezzana, Isabelle Leroux, Aurélie Lardenois, Bertrand Evrard, Laurence Dubreil, Jérôme Amiaud, Franck Verrecchia, Chantal Thorin, Alicia Mayeuf-Louchart, Karl Rouger, Marie-Anne Colle, Françoise Bouhour, Françoise Bouhour, Marie Csanyi, Jean-Baptiste Davion, Teresinha Evangelista, Pascal Laforêt, Armelle Magot, Pascale Marcorelles, Claude-Alain Maurage, Yann Péréon, Nathalie Streichenberger, Céline Tard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pompe disease is an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency, characterized by progressive skeletal muscle weakness and respiratory insufficiency. Affected muscles exhibit glycogen-filled lysosomes, autophagic build-up, and mitochondrial abnormalities. Despite global myofibrillar disorganization, satellite cells (SCs) fail to activate, due to mechanisms that remain unclear. This study aimed to further characterize the muscle phenotype in Pompe disease, with particular attention to proteins associated with membrane repair processes, as membrane damage is a primary trigger for SC activation. Longitudinal transcriptomic analysis of skeletal muscle from a Pompe disease mouse model, combined with immunohistochemical and biochemical approaches, showed early and sustained overexpression of dysferlin (DYSF), annexin A2 (ANXA2), and AHNAK2. These membrane repair-associated proteins displayed abnormal localization during disease progression, with sarcoplasmic accumulation associated with T-tubules, lysosomes and autophagosomes, respectively. Analysis of muscle biopsies from some patients with late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD) identified similar expression patterns in moderately affected cases, whereas these patterns were less evident or absent in the most severe samples, suggesting stage-dependent redistribution associated with advanced myoarchitectural disorganization. Furthermore, in the mouse model, we observed persistent post-transcriptional accumulation of mature myostatin (MSTN), a key negative regulator of muscle growth, alongside a decrease in the phospho-SMAD3/SMAD3 ratio and reduced SMAD7 expression, which point toward a more complex modulation of its canonical bioactivity rather than a simple increase. Altogether, these findings identify modified distribution proteins linked to membrane repair and dysregulation of MSTN as features of muscle remodeling in Pompe disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40478-026-02348-1.

Introduction

Pompe disease, also known as glycogen storage disease type II (OMIM #232300), is a lysosomal storage disorder resulting from mutations in the gene encoding acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA) [19]. GAA is essential for glycogen breakdown within lysosomes, and its deficiency leads to abnormal glycogen accumulation, particularly in skeletal and cardiac muscles, causing progressive muscle weakness and atrophy [6, 23]. Histopathological hallmarks include enlarged and damaged glycogen-filled lysosomes, accumulation of autophagic debris and mitochondrial abnormalities [18, 50, 57]. They are associated with impaired fusion between lysosomes and autophagosomes in muscle fibers, resulting in autophagic build-up and defective autophagic flux [16]. Furthermore, accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria exacerbates oxidative stress and disrupts calcium homeostasis [30]. Collectively, these structural changes contribute to progressive and profound muscle tissue remodeling in both patients and Gaa−/− mice, the primary mouse model of the disease. This remodeling is characterized by vacuolized fibers, myofibril disarray, anisocytosis, nuclear internalization, and fiber splitting [25, 30, 52].

Glycogen overload induces lysosomal stress, as evidenced by increased expression of galectin-3, a cytosolic lectin involved in responses to damaged lysosomes [1, 2, 22, 37, 47]. Galectin-3 can also coordinate the degradation of damaged lysosomes via autophagy and their replacement through biogenesis [22]. Autophagosomes, double-membrane vesicles that sequester cytoplasmic components or organelles, fuse with lysosomes to facilitate their elimination [38]. Specific repair mechanisms are initiated following damage to both lysosomal and plasma membranes. Lysosomal membrane integrity is primarily maintained by the endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT), which are recruited prior to galectin-3 upregulation and the activation of the lysophagy machinery [41, 42, 45]. In cases of severe lysosomal membrane damage, annexins 1 and 2 are involved via an ESCRT-independent mechanism [61]. Annexin A2 (ANXA2) also supports plasma membrane integrity via active repair processes triggered by calcium influx. This influx leads to the recruitment of intracellular vesicles and associated proteins, which form a membrane repair patch that reseals the rupture site. Dysferlin (DYSF), a transmembrane protein with Ca2+-binding C2 domains located on both the plasma membrane and the transverse tubule (T-tubule) membrane, plays a critical role in this process [35]. Autosomal recessive mutations in the dysferlin gene impair cell membrane repair, contributing to the development of muscular dystrophies [43]. Compensatory overexpression of DYSF is also observed in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) [55]. DYSF interacts with AHNAK, S100 calcium binding protein A10 (S100A10), and ANXA2 [21, 28]. AHNAK is a large scaffolding protein involved in sub-sarcolemma cytoarchitecture and calcium homeostasis [20]. It is also a defining feature of enlargeosomes, vesicles that facilitate the reduction of cell surface tension that is required for membrane repair [12, 62]. Like DYSF, AHNAK expression is significantly reduced in the muscles of patients with dysferlinopathy but remains unchanged in patients with other muscular dystrophies, such as DMD [21]. ANXA2 expression is elevated in dysferlinopathies and strongly correlates with the severity of the clinical phenotype [8].

Despite the various architectural changes observed at the muscle fiber level, which are supposed to stimulate satellite cell (SC) activation to repair tissue damage, SCs remain quiescent and muscle fiber regeneration does not occur in both patients and Gaa−/− mice [25, 49]. This is particularly perplexing given that SC number and function are preserved, suggesting the presence of specific, yet unidentified, pathophysiological processes that impair muscle repair in Pompe disease. Given that membrane injury is a major stimulus for SC activation [48], we hypothesized that adaptive membrane repair mechanisms may be activated in muscle fibers in Pompe disease to keep the intensity of damage below this threshold for SC quiescence exit.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive transcriptomic analysis of the biological processes (BP) involved in the pathophysiology of Pompe disease, focusing primarily on the global membrane repair mechanism and the failed activation of SCs, two events that may be linked. To this end, we characterized the gene expression signature over the disease course in Gaa−/− mouse muscle to provide a broad overview of the genetic alterations that underlie the pathophysiological changes associated with Pompe disease. We then correlated these transcriptional profiles with histological and biochemical findings in Gaa−/− mice and some patients suffering from late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD). These data provide new insights into the involvement of membrane repair-associated proteins and regulation of SC activation in Pompe disease pathogenesis. Notably, DYSF, ANXA2, and AHNAK2 have been identified as potential biomarkers of disease progression.

Materials and methods

Human samples

Human samples were obtained from three patients with a molecular diagnosis of Pompe disease (2 women and 1 man aged 31, 38 and 46 years, respectively). A control muscle sample was also obtained from a 30-year-old male subject free of known genetic muscle disease despite a history of muscle pain. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the relevant institutions in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. Patients signed a consent form allowing their biopsy to be used for research, according to French regulation (Ref: RC22-0249, ID-RCB: 2022-A00971-42; Committee for the Protection of Individuals: 22.01632.000094). Patients were categorized into three groups based on age of disease onset and disease severity. A 7.5-year-old DMD patient was also added to the cohort (tissue sample obtained from the Brest Biological Resources Center BB-0033-00037 [“CRB Santé du CHU de Brest”], France). For LOPD patients (Table 1), deltoid muscle samples (10 μm-thick cryosections) were processed for conventional histochemical staining (hematoxylin and eosin [HE] and periodic acid-Schiff [PAS]) (Additional file 1). Mouse model

Gaa−/− mice (GAA KO 6neo/6neo mouse model; RRID: IMSR_JAX:004154) with a targeted disruption of exon 6 in the Gaa gene were used. Breeding heterozygous 6neo/6neo mice were kindly provided by Dr. Nina Raben (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) and maintained in the specific pathogen-free animal facility at Oniris (Nantes, France). Gaa−/− homozygous breeding was performed, and wild-type (WT) littermates were bred as controls. Mice were provided with standard mouse chow and water ad libitum and housed under a 12:12 h dark: light cycle at 22 °C. All animals shared the same 129xC57BL/6 genetic background and were exclusively male to limit the potential risk of variability linked to hormonal regulation in female mice. All experiments were conducted in accordance with European guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee on Animal Experimentation of the Pays de la Loire region, France (permit number APAFIS #1267).

Experimental design

The experimental design incorporated both the Pompe disease patients and the murine model for Pompe disease. A longitudinal pathophysiological study was designed to identify dysregulated biological pathways in the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle of Gaa−/− mice. This study was based on microarray gene expression analysis, supplemented by histological and biochemical investigations. Four time points were selected to compare muscle from Gaa−/− and WT mice, coinciding with a pre-symptomatic age (1.5 months [mo]), the age at the onset of clinical symptoms (4 mo), a lesion establishment (6 mo), and the presence of advanced lesions (9 mo). Four Gaa−/− mice and four WT mice were included per time point. Muscles collected at each age were used to compare the overall molecular signatures of the mouse groups. To specifically investigate changes associated with disease progression, muscles from Gaa−/− mice at different ages were compared, focusing on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified in the initial analysis to exclude age-related genes. In addition, deltoid muscle biopsies from LOPD patients with mild, moderate, or severe disease form were subjected to histopathological examination.

Tissue collection

TA muscles from mice were harvested and divided into three samples. One sample, for transcriptomic study, was collected and immediately placed in RNAlater® (#R0901, Merck, Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines, France) overnight (ON) at 4 °C. The RNAlater® was then removed, and tissue samples were stored at − 80 °C until processing. For histology, another sample was positioned on tragacanth gum, snap-frozen in isopentane prechilled in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until sectioning. Finally, the remaining sample, for biochemical study, was directly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until processing.

RNA isolation

Total RNA was extracted from tissue (approximately 20 mg) preserved in RNAlater®, using the Nucleozol RNA extraction kit (#740406, Macherey–Nagel, Hœrdt, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each sample was treated with DNAse I (#AM2235, Life technologies, Saint-Aubin, France). RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Illkirch, France), and its integrity was determined using the Agilent Eukaryote Total RNA Nano kit (#5067, Agilent Technologies, Les Ulis, France) and the 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies).

Microarray gene expression

RNA (100 ng) was used to generate double-strand cDNA, which was fragmented and biotin-labeled using the Genechip WT Plus Reagent Kit (#900720, Applied Biosystems, Warrington, UK). Fragmented cDNA (2.3 µg) was hybridized to a mouse Clariom S array (#902931, Applied Biosystems) for 16 h at 45 °C. Microarrays were washed and stained with phycoerythrin-conjugated streptavidin in an Affymetrix Fluidics Station 450 (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Fluorescent images were captured using a gene Array Scanner 2500 (Affymetrix). The resulting CEL files were processed using the oligo Bioconductor package (version 1.56.0) from R software (version 4.1.0). Data were normalized and corrected with the RMA method. Raw data were deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository under accession number GSE293648. CEL files were processed using the oligo Bioconductor package (version 1.56.0) in R (version 4.1.0). Data were preprocessed using the standard RMA algorithm, including background correction, quantile normalization and probe summarization. Arrays from different age groups were processed in two batches (1.5/4 months and 6/9 months). Because batch was confounded with age, differential expression analyses were performed using age-specific contrasts (Gaa−/− vs. WT mice sample at each age). Within each age group, WT and Gaa−/− mice samples were balanced and processed in the same batch, thereby avoiding batch confounding of genotype effects. DEGs were identified using the Limma R package (version 3.48.3) with an absolute fold-change ≥ 2 and a Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the topGO R package (version 2.44.0) with the Biological Process (BP) and Cellular Component (CC) annotation databases (BP and CC with org.Mm.eg.db; version 3.13.0). p-values were computed using the “classic” and “elim” method algorithm parameters and “Fisher” as the statistical parameter, according to the reference manual.

Western blot

Muscle samples were lysed with a Precellys® homogenizer (#P000062-PEVO0-A, Bertin Technologies, Montigny Le Bretonneux, France) in RIPA buffer (#9806S, Ozyme, Saint-Cyr-l’École, France) containing a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (#11836153001 and #P0044 respectively, Merck, Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines, France). Total protein lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm (30 min, 4 °C), and supernatants were collected in 2X Laemmli buffer (#LC2676, Thermo Fisher) containing 5% v/v 2-mercaptoethanol. Samples (20 μg) were loaded onto a TGX Mini-PROTEAN SDS-PAGE gel (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-coquette, France). Separated proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (#GE10600001, Amersham, Velizy-Villacoublay, France). Membranes were then treated with blocking buffer (5% nonfat milk), incubated ON with primary antibodies (Abs), washed, and incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary Abs at 1:5000 dilution (polyclonal donkey anti-rabbit [#711-035-152, Interchim, Montluçon, France] or polyclonal rabbit anti-goat [#A5420, Merck]). HRP-chemiluminescence was developed using the Clarity Western Substrate kit (#1705060, Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-coquette, France). Membranes were scanned on an imager (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA) and quantified using ImageJ Software. Band intensity (number of positive pixels per unit area) of the target protein was measured and normalized to the total loaded protein per lane. Uncropped images of blots are provided in Additional file 2. Immunohistochemistry

Cryosections (10 μm-thick) were fixed for 10 min with the appropriate buffer according to the specific Ab used (Table 4) and saturated in blocking buffer containing 5% goat serum in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at room temperature (RT). Sections were then incubated with primary Ab diluted in blocking buffer (ON, 4 °C). After washing in PBS, sections were incubated for 1 h at RT with Alexa® 555 or Alexa® 488 secondary Abs (1:300; #A21429; #A21240; #A21434, #A11008, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After rinsing, nuclei were stained with DAPI (#D1306, Invitrogen) followed by mounting slides with Mowiol (#475904, VWR, Pennsylvania, PA, USA). For some experiments, Alexa® 647-conjugated wheat germ agglutinin (WGA; #W32466, Invitrogen) was used to visualize muscle fiber outlines. Images were acquired using a confocal laser microscope (Nikon, Champigny-sur-Marne, France) with NIS software (Nikon) or with the Axioscan slide scanner (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) using ZenBlue software (Zeiss). Colocalization analysis

Confocal images were acquired at high magnification using a 40 × objective, with acquisition settings adjusted according to Nyquist sampling criteria. For each muscle section, random cropped regions were selected so as to cover as much tissue area as possible while avoiding empty areas or artefacts. Background thresholds for each channel were defined based on the corresponding negative control sections processed without primary Ab, and then applied to the images prior to analysis. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated in Fiji using the Colocalization Threshold plugin to evaluate colocalization between the two fluorescence signals.

Statistical analysis

For western blot analysis, differences between the two groups for each protein were analyzed with the Wilcoxon test and GraphPad Prism v.6.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). For quantification of macrophages, the effects of age and group (WT/Gaa−/−) on CD68+ cells/mm2, type M1 cells/mm2, and type M2 cells/mm2 were analyzed by a two-way analysis of variance followed by multiple comparisons with Tukey’s post-hoc test when the independence and normality of the residuals were validated. Data analysis was performed using R-Software. All data are reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The p-value correction for multiple testing was assessed by the « multcomp» package for Tukey’s test. Otherwise, a Kruskal–Wallis test was performed and followed by multiple testing with Wilcoxon pairwise test and Benjamini–Hochberg correction. For all statistical tests, a p-value of 0.05 or less was considered significant.

Results

Pompe disease mice muscle exhibits a molecular signature rapidly established, selective and stable in time

To comprehensively identify cellular processes disrupted in Pompe disease and to determine how these processes evolve over time, we performed a longitudinal transcriptomic analysis on TA muscle from Gaa−/− and WT mice at 1.5, 4, 6, and 9 months of age. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the gene expression dataset (6021 genes with SD ≥ 0.3) showed global differences in baseline transcriptomes with Dimension 1 (Dim1) and Dim2 explaining 26.9% and 18.8% of the variance, respectively (Fig. 1a). This analysis demonstrated a clear genotype-specific separation. Dim1 distinguished the transcriptome of Gaa−/− mice from that of WT mice. Furthermore, the overall molecular signature of WT mice from 1.5 to 6 months of age was distinct from that of 9-mo-old WT mice and all Gaa−/− mice, regardless of age (Fig. 1a). The cluster corresponding to 1.5-mo-old Gaa−/− mice was distinct from the other Gaa−/− mouse groups, indicating a specific transcriptomic signature for this age. Biplot visualization, using the 25 genes with the highest contribution to the PCA, highlighted specific gene expression patterns correlated with genotype (Fig. 1b): the expression of acid alpha-glucosidase (Gaa), methyltransferases (Mettl21c and Mettl21e), aquaporin-4 (Aqp4), nexilin F-actin binding protein (Nexn), and myostatin (Mstn) was negatively correlated with the Gaa−/− genotype. Conversely, the expression of metallothioneins (MT1, MT2, and MT3), serine peptidase inhibitor clade B member 1a (Serpinb1a), serine peptidase inhibitor clade A member 3N (Serpina3n), TIMP metallopeptidase inhibitor 1 (Timp1), and Ahnak nucleoprotein 2 (Ahnak2) was positively correlated with the Gaa−/− genotype. DEGs were identified using the Limma R package (BH-corrected p-value < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change > 1). Comparisons between Gaa−/− and WT mice were performed at each age. A set of 414 DEGs, including 116 downregulated and 298 upregulated genes in Pompe disease, was identified and represented in a heatmap (Fig. 1c, Additional file 3). GO analysis was performed to identify functional enrichment and cellular localization of all DEGs (Padj < 0.01), revealing significant enrichment in 183 biological processes (BPs) and 26 cellular components (CCs) (Additional files 4 and 5). After excluding duplications and pathways involved in general biological mechanisms, 11 BPs and 6 CCs were specifically identified (Fig. 1d). Among the 414 DEGs, 58 genes displayed changes in expression across disease stages within Gaa−/− mice (Fig. 1e, Additional file 6), indicating that the specific molecular signature of Pompe disease mice muscle is acquired early and remains relatively stable. GO term analysis on these 58 DEGs revealed significant enrichment in 8 BPs (Fig. 1f, Additional file 7), primarily related to the regulation of axonogenesis (GO: 0050770, p = 0.0003; GO: 0050767, p = 0.002; GO: 0030516, p = 0.002) and neurogenesis (GO: 0050767, p = 0.00184), potentially reflecting progressive central nervous system involvement in Pompe disease. Another major BP was circadian rhythm. In summary, these results demonstrate rapid alterations in the molecular profile of Pompe disease mice muscle, with a distinct intermediate state during the first month of life. This alteration involves a restricted set of genes linked to metabolism, inflammation, muscle regeneration and organization as well as the neuromuscular system, and remains relatively stable over the subsequent course of disease progression.

Dysferlin shows increased expression and sarcoplasmic distribution in Pompe disease muscle

We sought to understand the significance of the enrichment of "plasma membrane" and "plasma membrane organization" terms in the BPs and CCs identified by the GO analysis. Among the DEGs identified was dysferlin (Dysf), expression of which was significantly increased in Gaa−/− mice from 1.5 month of age and remained stable over the subsequent disease course (Fig. 2a). Increase of the protein level was observed at 1.5 and 9 months of age (3- and tenfold, respectively; Fig. 2b). DYSF was predominantly localized at the plasma membrane in WT mouse muscle (Fig. 2c, top). By contrast, Gaa−/− mouse muscle showed a much more pronounced and diffuse expression pattern (Fig. 2c, bottom): while DYSF was sometimes observed exclusively in the plasma membrane of certain myofibers, it was predominantly localized in the sarcoplasm in Gaa−/− mouse muscles. Some fibers showed DYSF expression in both locations. To further investigate the role of DYSF in the pathophysiology of Pompe disease, we examined its expression in LOPD patients, specifically those with low, moderate, and high glycogen levels (Additional file 1). DYSF was localized mainly in the plasma membrane of myofibers in mildly affected patient, similar to healthy subjects. In moderately and severely affected patients, DYSF was detected in the plasma membrane and in the sarcoplasm (Fig. 3d). This abnormal accumulation of DYSF was also observed in muscle sections from DMD patients. Severely affected LOPD patient exhibited much more intense sarcoplasmic labeling than those with mild or moderate lesions, particularly in small myofibers, although labeling was also detected in some large myofibers. Furthermore, myofibers exhibiting sarcoplasmic accumulation of DYSF did not correspond to those expressing the developmental isoform of MHC (devMHC), which represent less than 0.2% of total fibers, even in advanced stages of the disease. Overall, these results show increased levels of DYSF and sarcoplasmic accumulation in Pompe disease mice muscle, particularly in type IIb fibers with autophagic debris. Accordingly, abnormal DYSF accumulation was also detected in the biopsies of three LOPD patients, where it appeared distinct from regenerative features.

Discussion

To our knowledge, the present work is the first longitudinal transcriptomic analysis performed in Pompe disease and describes the molecular expression profile of skeletal muscle in the Gaa−/− mouse model from 1.5 to 9 months of age. The altered biological pathways identified in Gaa−/− mouse muscle included those involved in cell metabolism, calcium homeostasis, oxidative stress, inflammation, and muscle regeneration, consistent with transcriptional alterations described in previous transcriptomic studies in mouse models and Pompe disease patients [7, 11, 29, 40, 56]. Our findings also provide compelling evidence of accumulation and redistribution of membrane repair-associated proteins in muscle fibers in Pompe disease. Affected muscles exhibit accumulation of DYSF, ANXA2, and AHNAK2 in T-tubules, lysosomes, and autophagosomes, respectively. This abnormal sarcoplasmic build-up of these different proteins is an early-onset phenomenon that progresses to advanced stages of the disease and correlates with other classically described histopathological alterations, making these proteins potentially valuable markers of Pompe disease progression.

DYSF plays a crucial role in plasma membrane repair and T-tubule integrity [44]. Abnormal sarcoplasmic accumulation of DYSF protein was established in myofibers of Gaa−/− mice, consistent with a previous finding in quadriceps and triceps muscle from 4-mo-old Gaa−/− mice [59]. That study reported a significant reduction in such accumulation following ERT with ATB200/AT221, suggesting that DYSF accumulation is directly linked to the degree of autophagic flux impairment. In line with this observation, we found that sarcoplasmic DYSF expression was predominantly associated with myofibers containing accumulated autophagic debris and with type IIb-enriched myofibers, which are the most resistant to ERT [13]. A similar sarcoplasmic accumulation was also observed in moderately affected LOPD patient, whereas it was no longer evident in the most severe case, possibly because of extensive disruption of fiber architecture and sarcoplasmic disorganization. Similar DYSF redistribution has been described in various forms of limb-girdle muscular dystrophy where it is presented as a direct consequence of defects in proteins required for effective membrane targeting [31, 43]. We can hypothesize that DYSF, like ANXA2 and AHNAK2, fails to reach the plasma membrane in Pompe disease due to sarcoplasmic accumulation of autophagic aggregates that disrupt intracellular trafficking. Thus, well-established autophagic flux impairment in Pompe disease could be at least partially involved in the sarcoplasmic accumulation observed in these proteins. It could prevent their degradation via lysosomal or proteasomal dysfunction but also limit their bioavailability.

An increase of galectin-3 was observed as early as 1.5 month in Gaa−/− mice, supporting the existence of early lysosomal stress in Pompe disease, consistent with previous findings in the gastrocnemius muscle of 4-mo-old Gaa−/− mice [37]. In addition, the colocalization of ANXA2 with LAMP1 observed as early as 1.5 month of age in Gaa−/− mice suggests early lysosomal recruitment of ANXA2 in Pompe disease. That observation aligns with previous study demonstrating the role of ANXA2 in repairing severe damaged lysosomal membranes [61]. In LOPD patients, this pattern may be associated with the degree of myoarchitectural disorganization. Its loss in the most severely affected patient could reflect extensive intracellular remodeling that no longer permits the maintenance or detection of ANXA2 recruitment to lysosomes.

Finally, our findings provide evidence that AHNAK2, a component of the costameric network [33], is also dysregulated in Pompe disease. Although its biological function is not completely defined, it has been suggested to play a significant role in plasma membrane assembly and repair [4, 33]. In Gaa−/− mouse muscle, we found that AHNAK2 is localized to the sarcolemma and to autophagosomes. This expression profile is identical to that observed in moderate LOPD patient, consistent with a possible association between AHNAK2 recruitment and autophagosome accumulation in Pompe disease. By contrast, this autophagosome localization of AHNAK2 is absent in severely affected patient. AHNAK2 may thus be involved in autophagic vesicle dynamics, possibly via its cytoskeletal or membrane repair-related functions, although further functional studies will be required to clarify this hypothesis. Of note, knockdown of ANXA2 is associated with reduced expression and sarcoplasmic localization of AHNAK [4], while an interaction between AHNAK and DYSF has also been reported in myopathic patients [21]. Taken together, the present findings strongly support a link between the expression profiles of AHNAK2, DYSF, and ANXA2. Future research involving a larger number of LOPD patients and Gaa−/− mouse model should focus on elucidating the regulatory pathways controlling their interactions.

Another key finding of our study in Gaa−/− mice is profoundly disrupted expression of myostatin, a well-known negative regulator of muscle growth [3, 9, 34]. This finding is of particular interest when considered alongside our previous study performed in the same muscle and at the same ages in which the expression profile for Pax7, MyoD, and MyoG remained unchanged between Gaa−/− and WT mice, except at 1.5 month of age [25]. In agreement with the observations reported in patients by the Pijnappel’s group [49], these results indicate that the SC pool is preserved during Pompe disease and that no commitment into the myogenic lineage occurs from 4 months of age in Gaa−/− mice, despite worsening structural abnormalities. Within this framework, we can hypothesize that MSTN dysregulation may contribute to the lack of SC activation observed in Pompe disease. Mstn gene was among the most dysregulated genes in our dataset, displaying a significant and consistent decrease in transcript expression. In contrast, the mature MSTN protein was increased at all analyzed ages whereas pro-MSTN, the latent precursor form, remained unchanged compared with WT mice muscle. In the absence of significant changes in transcripts encoding the main processing enzymes Furin and tolloid-like metalloproteinases (BMP-1 and TLL-1/2) [27, 58], these findings are more consistent with a post-transcriptional accumulation of mature MSTN in Pompe disease muscle than with increased transcription or overt alterations in proteolytic processing. Whether this reflects altered protein stability, turnover, or availability remains to be established. Given that MSTN regulation largely occurs at the protein level, [27, 59], further characterization of Furin and TLL1/2 protein expression and enzymatic activity would be informative. The permanent overexpression of Trim63 and Eif4ebp1 transcripts observed in Gaa−/− mice support the notion that MSTN dysregulation could be associated with the activation of an atrophic process rather than representing an isolated alteration. However, the reduced phospho-SMAD3/SMAD3 ratio and the decrease in SMAD7 expression do not support a simple increase in canonical MSTN/TGF-β/activin signaling pathway despite mature MSTN accumulation. Rather, these data indicate that post-transcriptional MSTN accumulation in Pompe disease is associated with altered SMAD-dependent signaling. Given the central role of MSTN and of the TGF-β/activin pathway in muscle homeostasis and myogenesis, these alterations warrant further investigation. In particular, additional analyses of MSTN-responsive downstream targets, antagonists such as follistatin and muscle mass would be required to clarify the functional significance of this imbalance. Such studies will be important to determine whether MSTN dysregulation contributes to the lack of SC activation in Pompe disease [25, 49] as suggested by chronic MSTN overexpression models showing their impaired activation and proliferation [34, 46, 51, 54]. Finally, the increase in mature MSTN may also reflect protein accumulation secondary to impaired autophagic flux, which could favor its persistence in muscle without necessarily indicating increased canonical bioactivity, including toward SC.

Despite an increased density of M1 macrophages at 6 and 9 months in Gaa−/− mice muscle, no significant changes in IL-6 and IL-10 transcript expression were observed, suggesting a possible permissive environment but insufficient to promote SC activation. This may also indicate a dissociation between macrophage polarization and cytokine output. Given that impaired glycogen utilization has been shown to modulate macrophage phenotype by maintaining them in M1 polarization and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, one possible explanation could be that altered glycogen handling affects macrophage inflammatory function [32, 60].

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that Pompe disease is characterized by early accumulation and redistribution of DYSF, ANXA2, and AHNAK2. Their respective localization in the T-tubules, lysosomes, and autophagosomes points to compartment-specific remodeling. In LOPD patients, sarcoplasmic accumulation of ANXA2 and AHNAK2 was detected in moderately affected case but not in the most severe one, suggesting stage-dependent expression patterns that may be lost in advanced disease. These proteins may therefore be considered candidate markers of muscle remodeling in Pompe disease. Finally, Pompe disease was also associated with post-transcriptional accumulation of mature MSTN together with altered SMAD-dependent signaling pathway.
