Genetic and clinical insights into the coexistence of multiple myeloma and diffuse large B cell lymphoma from a case report and systematic review with bioinformatics analysis
Authors: Alireza Zangooie, Moein Piroozkhah, Zahra Ghasemi, Mobin Pirouzkhah, Maryam Sardarkhani Eydgahi, Reza Asgari, Malihe Saberafsharian, Zahra Sanei-Far, Amirhosein Maharati, Zahra Salehi, Abolghasem Allahyari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple myeloma (MM) and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) are B-cell malignancies that rarely coexist in a single patient, presenting significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. While MM primarily involves clonal plasma cells, DLBCL is an aggressive lymphoid neoplasm. Investigating shared genetic mutations and understanding their clinical relevance in both cancers could provide novel insights into their pathogenesis and underlying molecular mechanisms, thereby informing future translational research.Materials and methodsA case report was conducted on a 52-year-old male who presented with abdominal pain and anemia. Imaging revealed lymphadenopathy, and biopsy confirmed high-grade DLBCL with concurrent bone marrow involvement suggestive of MM. Laboratory tests identified monoclonal IgM gammopathy, and the patient was treated with R-CHOP (Rituximab, Cyclophosphamide, Doxorubicin, Vincristine, and Prednisone) chemotherapy for DLBCL followed by autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) for MM relapse. A systematic review of the literature was performed using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases to identify cases of patients diagnosed with both MM and DLBCL. Data on patient demographics, clinical features, treatment regimens, and outcomes were extracted. Additionally, bioinformatics analysis was conducted using publicly available genomic data from cBioPortal and IntOGen to identify driver gene mutations in MM and DLBCL. Functional and pathway enrichment analysis was performed with KEGG and Gene Ontology (GO) databases.ResultsThe case report highlighted a complex clinical course where the patient initially responded well to R-CHOP chemotherapy for DLBCL, achieving remission, but later relapsed with MM, treated with ASCT and lenalidomide. The systematic review revealed 14 eligible studies in which MM and DLBCL often occur in older patients, either simultaneously or sequentially, with variable treatment responses, including complete remission, partial remission, or relapse. The bioinformatics analysis identified several shared function and cancer-related pathways between two cancers including interleukin and cytokine-mediated signaling pathways, regulation of cell cycle, neurotrophin signaling pathway, FOXO signaling pathway, Epstein Barr virus infection, and viral carcinogenesis.ConclusionThis study provides valuable insights into the dual occurrence of MM and DLBCL, emphasizing the importance of tailored treatment approaches. The driver mutations identified highlight overlapping oncogenic pathways rather than implying a shared clonal origin, and may inform future studies exploring their biological and clinical implications. Further research into these shared molecular mechanisms could lead to more effective treatments for patients with coexisting MM and DLBCL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05417-y.

Introduction

MM is a malignant disorder of clonal plasma cells characterized by the excessive production of a single type of abnormal monoclonal protein, and although the underlying pathophysiology linking the simultaneous occurrence of additional neoplasms is not well understood, studies have shown that patients with MM are at increased risk of developing secondary hematologic malignancies, including lymphomas [1–4].

The coexistence of MM and DLBCL in a single patient is exceedingly rare and poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Both malignancies originate from mature B cells; however, they differ markedly in their clinical presentation, disease progression, and treatment strategies. While MM primarily involves clonal plasma cells within the bone marrow, DLBCL is an aggressive lymphoid neoplasm that typically presents with rapidly enlarging nodal or extranodal masses [2, 3].

This case report presents the case of a 52-year-old male with a rare and diagnostically challenging synchronous presentation of DLBCL and MM. The patient's initial workup revealed high-grade DLBCL with concurrent bone marrow involvement suspicious for plasma cell neoplasia. Further immunophenotypic and serological studies confirmed the co-existence of IgM kappa-type multiple myeloma. This case is particularly notable for the sequential evolution of clinical priorities—initially focusing on aggressive lymphoma management with R-CHOP chemotherapy, followed by delayed manifestation and treatment of multiple myeloma, including ASCT and eventual relapse. Materials and methods

Patient clinical assessment

As part of the diagnostic evaluation for unexplained abdominal pain and mild anemia, standard gastrointestinal assessments were conducted, including upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, colonoscopy, and physical abdominal examination, all of which were non-contributory. Subsequently, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen was performed as an advanced imaging modality. In light of findings suggestive of a lymphoproliferative disorder, a core needle biopsy was obtained and analyzed histopathologically. For comprehensive staging, bone marrow aspiration and biopsy were also carried out, followed by microscopic and immunohistochemical evaluation, including staining for CD138 to assess plasma cell infiltration. Additional laboratory investigations, including serum protein electrophoresis and immunotyping, were performed to characterize monoclonal protein expression and support differential diagnosis. All procedures and tests were conducted following the acquisition of informed written consent from the patient, in accordance with ethical standards.

Systematic literature screening

The entire process of study selection and data extraction was carried out in alignment with the PRISMA 2020 [5] recommendations, ensuring a systematic and comprehensive assessment of the available literature. Briefly, three researchers (AZ, ZG, and MP) independently screened the literature on PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (WoS) using predefined keywords tailored to capture three major categories of interest: “multiple myeloma (MM)” AND “diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL)” OR “plasmablastic lymphoma”. The search was carried out on February 17, 2026, with no restrictions applied to publication date and type. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH, subject headings) with free-text keywords to maximize sensitivity. Terms encompassed disease entities and study designs (see Supplementary File 1 for the complete strategy). Additionally, we conducted a grey literature search to identify studies or reports not indexed in the databases listed above, using medRxiv and bioRxiv.

The literature screening was conducted using the Rayyan screening tool, which was developed by the Qatar Computing Research Institute (QCRI) (www.rayyan.ai). Following each screening step (title/abstract and full text), the researchers compared the respective resulting studies. In case of disagreement, we resolve it by discussion, or a fourth (ZS), independent expert in the field chooses whether the study was included or excluded for the next screening step.

In the first stage of screening, studies written in languages other than English were excluded, while preprints, conference abstracts, and letters were not. Titles and abstracts of the remaining records were then reviewed to identify reports describing patients diagnosed with both multiple myeloma (MM) AND diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) OR plasmablastic lymphoma. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were subsequently assessed against predefined inclusion criteria. Eligible studies were required to: (i) involve human subjects; (ii) provide histopathologic or diagnostic confirmation of both conditions; and (iii) contain sufficient clinical information for analysis. Three reviewers (AZ, ZG, and MP) independently evaluated the studies, extracting information on publication year, study type, patient demographics, diagnostic findings, treatment strategies (including medications used), and clinical outcomes. The methodological quality of included studies with the JBI checklists [6] that are specifically tailored to case series and case report studies, which addressed eight (or ten) key domains (Supplementary File 2).

In silico analysis

Identification of driver gene mutations in MM and DLBCL

To identify driver gene mutations between multiple myeloma (MM) and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), we utilized publicly available datasets from cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (https://www.cbioportal.org/). The Multiple Myeloma (Broad, Cancer Cell 2014) study (n = 211) was selected as the MM cohort. Although this dataset includes both 177 whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing (WES and WGS), platform-specific metadata was not available within cBioPortal; thus, all 211 samples were included in the analysis. For DLBCL, we selected the Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma (Duke, Cell 2017) cohort, comprising 1001 samples sequenced using WES. We obtained all mutations of cases in each cohort. Then driver genes in each cancer were downloaded from the IntOGen database (https://www.intogen.org/). Ultimately, shared genes from cbioportal and IntOGen for each cancer were identified and visualized using a Venn diagram (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/).

Functional and pathway analysis

A comprehensive identification of related gene ontology (GO) terms and pathways from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) was conducted for the genes analyzed for enrichment using the Enrichr database (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/). The top 20 GO terms and KEGG pathways, ranked by adjusted P-value, were selected for visualization and further analysis using the “ggplot2” package.

OncoKB

To assess the clinical relevance and potential of the driver mutations between MM and DLBCL, we examined the OncoKB database. OncoKB (www.oncokb.org) is a tumor mutation database and treatment classification system recognized by the FDA, used to assess the clinical actionability of specific genomic variants [7]. OncoKB assigns a level of evidence for drug use based on the therapeutic level of evidence (Supplementary File 3; Table 1). For each gene that overlaps between the two cancers examined in our study, we queried to find (i) its oncogenic classification, (ii) the effect of mutations, (iii) its clinical evidence level (OncoKB levels), and (iv) available targeted therapies supported by the FDA or clinical trials.

Results

Case presentation

A 52-year-old male presented with complaints of abdominal pain and anemia (Hb = 13.8) and was subsequently referred to a gastroenterologist for further evaluation. Comprehensive gastrointestinal assessment, including upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy and colonoscopy, as well as a detailed abdominal examination, yielded unremarkable findings. However, a subsequent abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan, performed with intravenous contrast, revealed a 2.3 cm heterogeneous lymphadenopathy located in the splenic hilum, clearly demarcated from the adjacent splenic parenchyma. In addition, multiple enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes were identified, with the largest measuring up to 3.5 cm in diameter.

An ultrasound-guided biopsy was performed, and the pathology report confirmed high-grade diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) with a reactive T-cell component. Based on this diagnosis, the patient was referred to the hematologist. As part of the staging workup for DLBCL, a bone marrow aspiration was conducted. Microscopic and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis revealed that approximately 30% of the bone marrow cells were positive for CD138 and negative for CD3 and CD20 (Fig. 2). Subsequent serological evaluations were performed due to clinical suspicion of MM included serum protein electrophoresis and immunotyping via capillary electrophoresis. The patient's serum protein electrophoresis revealed the following distribution: albumin (38.3%), alpha-1 globulin (5.3%), alpha-2 globulin (7.7%), beta-1 globulin (6.9%), beta-2 globulin (36.3%), and gamma globulin (5.5%) (Table 1). The albumin-to-globulin (A/G) ratio was significantly reduced at 0.62, indicating a disproportionate increase in non-albumin fractions, particularly within the beta region. This pattern, along with markedly elevated beta-2 globulin, suppressed albumin, and relatively low gamma globulin, raised suspicion for a monoclonal protein—possibly migrating in the beta region—consistent with non-secretory or light chain-predominant multiple myeloma. Further evaluation revealed a markedly elevated serum IgM level at 3409 mg/dL, and immunotyping confirmed a monoclonal IgM (kappa) gammopathy. Bone marrow examination demonstrated 30% plasma cell infiltration that was positive for CD138 and negative for CD3 and CD20. Taken together with the patient’s pre-existing anemia, these findings supported a concurrent diagnosis of MM (Fig. 2).

Due to the clinical priority of treating diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), the patient was initiated on combination chemotherapy with Rituximab, Cyclophosphamide, Doxorubicin, Vincristine, and Prednisone (R-CHOP), administered every 21 days. He completed a total of eight cycles over approximately six months. Follow-up abdominal and pelvic CT scans revealed a significant reduction in the size of the celiac and perigastric lymph nodes, with para-aortic nodes returning to within normal limits. Given the favorable response, R-CHOP therapy was continued through all eight planned cycles. Imaging at the end of treatment demonstrated further lymph node regression, confirming a strong therapeutic response and achieving remission of DLBCL. Changes in protein fractions included an increase in Albumin (from 38.3% to 53.1%), Alpha 1 and Alpha 2 (from 5.3% to 6.9% and from 7.7% to 12.3%, respectively), a significant reduction in Beta 2 (from 36.3% to 11.2%), an increase in Gamma (from 5.5% to 8.1%), an improvement in the A/G ratio (from 0.62 to 1.13), and a slight decrease in Total Protein (from 7.3 g/dL to 6.9 g/dL). Based on the patient’s laboratory changes, R-CHOP therapy appeared to contribute to delayed progression of the patient’s MM (Table 1).

Approximately two years after completing R-CHOP, the patient presented with a pathological fracture of the left intertrochanteric femur (Fig. 2). Further diagnostic evaluation confirmed the fracture to be pathological in nature. Histopathological examination of the bone biopsy revealed a plasmacytoma, consistent with plasma cell neoplasia (Fig. 2). Following this diagnosis, the patient was transitioned to treatment for MM, initiating a combination regimen consisting of bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone (VRD). This clinical course suggests that the patient's MM had delayed progression during the two-year period following completion of R-CHOP therapy.

Given the established role of ASCT in treating MM, the patient's regimen was modified to lenalidomide 10 mg, and he subsequently underwent ASCT. Post-transplant, he continued on lenalidomide monotherapy for three years.

During routine follow-up, a drop in hemoglobin to 9 g/dL was observed. A repeat bone marrow biopsy revealed hypercellular marrow with sheets of plasma cells, consistent with relapse of the plasma cell neoplasm. Consequently, the treatment was modified to a combination regimen of bortezomib, cyclophosphamide, and dexamethasone (VCD), which was continued for one year. However, due to adverse effects associated with cyclophosphamide, particularly pancytopenia, a clinical decision was made to discontinue cyclophosphamide. The patient was subsequently transitioned to a bortezomib and dexamethasone (VD) regimen, which he is currently receiving.

Review of reported cases with coexistence of MM and DLBCL

Study identification

The general structure of the predefined keywords, as well as the complete PRISMA flow diagrams with the resulting number of studies for each screening step is depicted in Fig. 3. According to our research strategy, a total of 380 published papers were identified across PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, besides the 4 extra papers that were obtained from reviewing the reference lists of all the included studies, and the Scopus AI tool. Acknowledging that searching via medRxiv and bioRxiv revealed no eligible preprints. After removing 109 duplicates using Rayyan, 274 titles and abstracts were independently screened by three reviewers, resulting in the exclusion of 255 studies due to irrelevance, mislabeled populations, and language barriers. The remaining 19 articles met the inclusion criteria and underwent full-text assessment by three reviewers. Among these, 3 articles were excluded due to a lack of available full text, and 15 were available in English, either via open access or institutional subscriptions. One additional article was excluded because of the wrong population, leaving 14 articles included in the review.Fig. 3PRISMA Flow Diagram

Risk of bias

Three reviewers (ZG, MP, and AZ) independently assessed the methodological quality of the included studies, resolving disagreements through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer when necessary. Methodological quality assessment for case–control studies is described in Supplementary File 2. The methodological quality of the 14 included case reports was generally high. Nine studies [2, 8–15] fulfilled all 8 JBI criteria, and 4 [16–19] showed minor limitations in 1 domain (e.g., unclear or unreported adverse events or patient history), while 1 study [20] met 6 of 8 JBI criteria. Overall, all case reports had a low risk of bias (Supplementary File 2).

Characteristics of included studies

The included studies, published between 2014 and 2026, were conducted mostly in Asia, as well as the United States, Italy, and Poland. Study designs comprised 13 single-patient case reports and one retrospective case series that investigated the coexistence of MM with other primary malignant tumors (PMTs), including a patient diagnosed with both MM and DLBCL [17]. The patients were mostly older adults between 42 and 94 years of age, with a very slight male predominance (8/14), presenting with a range of symptoms including bone pain, fatigue, weight loss, melena, anemia, and other systemic complaints such as night sweats or fever. Some cases presented with more particular symptoms, such as cutaneous lesions [15] or visual problems (10). In some cases, the diagnosis was made incidentally during unrelated evaluations, such as a screening colonoscopy. The MM was often associated with monoclonal gammopathy, involving IgG or IgA subtypes and kappa or lambda light chains, while some cases exhibited multiple M-proteins concurrently. Bone lesions were present in nearly half of the reported cases. Bone marrow involvement and monoclonal protein presence varied in degree, highlighting diagnostic heterogeneity. The lymphoma types ranged from follicular lymphoma and low-grade B-cell lymphoma to classic DLBCL and plasmablastic lymphoma (PBL), with immunohistochemistry commonly demonstrating CD20, BCL6, PAX5, CD10, and high Ki-67 expression, with negative T-cell markers (e.g., CD3) supporting a B-cell lineage. Several cases reported unique immunoprofiles, such as LMP1 positivity or CD200 coexpression. One case reported paraneoplastic antibody (anti-ZIC4) positivity [9]. Treatment strategies included conventional chemoimmunotherapy (e.g., R-CHOP, bendamustine, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, prednisone), targeted agents (e.g., rituximab, bortezomib, lenalidomide, dexamethasone), autologous stem cell transplant (aSCT), and in two cases, CAR-T cell therapy targeting CD19. In one case, the CAR-T cell therapy was dual, targeting both CD19 and BMCA [22]. Clinical outcomes varied among patients, with some patients achieved partial or complete remission of one or both neoplasms, others showed disease stabilization or persistent monoclonal components, and two patients died from sepsis. Notably, one patient showed complete disappearance of M-protein and undetectable plasma and lymphoma cells in the bone marrow post-treatment, although PET-CT follow-up was not performed due to financial limitations. This series highlights the importance of suspecting dual malignancies in atypical or treatment-resistant hematologic cases, particularly when immunophenotyping or protein analysis reveals mixed or unexpected findings. The exact mechanism of coexistence—whether clonal evolution, transformation, or independent neoplastic events—remains poorly defined, but the therapeutic and prognostic implications are clinically significant. Finally, these cases underscore the necessity of individualized treatment approaches, often requiring integrated multidisciplinary care and sometimes advanced therapies, as well as the need for further molecular and clinical studies to clarify pathogenesis and guide management [22–28].

Using publicly available genomic datasets from cBioPortal and IntOGen in each cancer, we identified 55 and 46 driver genes in DLBCL and MM, respectively (Supplementary File 3; Tables 2, 3). Clinical relevance of driver mutations by OncoKB database

OncoKB annotation revealed diverse levels of genetic actionability across the driver genes (Supplementary File 3; Table 4). Among them, TP53 was annotated as likely oncogenic, with likely loss-of-function mutations classified at Level P1 in plasma cell myeloma. TP53 alterations were further linked to potential sensitivity to Eprenetapopt in solid tumors; however, in hematologic malignancies such as MM and DLBCL, the clinical efficacy of p53-reactivating agents remains investigational. Other recurrently mutated genes in DLBCL, including CARD11 and KMT2D, were annotated as oncogenic (Level Dx3), while IRF4 fusions (e.g., IGH–IRF4, DUSP22–IRF4) were considered likely oncogenic with gain-of-function effects (Dx1/Dx3). Several genes, such as BCL7A, PRDM1, SPEN, and CBLB, were categorized as likely oncogenic with predicted loss-of-function consequences, whereas RB1 and DICER1 harbored both neutral and potentially oncogenic variants. MAP4K4 had no curated annotation, and H1-4 variants were considered inconclusive. ATM mutations were also designated as oncogenic (loss-of-function) with Level Tx1 evidence, associated with prostate cancer, and actionable by PARP inhibitors (Olaparib, Talazoparib ± Enzalutamide; FDA Level 2) for this specific cancer. However, no validated therapeutic annotations for ATM alterations are currently established in hematologic malignancies.

Discussion

In the present case, a 52-year-old male was diagnosed with high-grade DLBCL following the detection of abdominal lymphadenopathy and histopathological confirmation. However, bone marrow evaluation unexpectedly revealed a significant clonal plasma cell population (CD138 +), prompting further investigation. Laboratory studies, including serum protein electrophoresis and immunotyping, demonstrated a monoclonal IgM (kappa) gammopathy, markedly elevated beta-2 globulin, suppressed albumin levels, and a reduced albumin-to-globulin (A/G) ratio. Taken together with clinical findings of anemia and 30% plasma cell infiltration in the bone marrow, these results supported a concurrent diagnosis of MM. Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematologic malignancy marked by clonal plasma cell proliferation in the bone marrow, often leading to bone lesions, renal impairment, anemia, and hypercalcemia. With improved survival rates, the risk of second malignancies, both hematologic (e.g., AML, MDS, non-Hodgkin lymphoma) and solid tumors (e.g., lung, stomach, renal cancers), has become a growing concern, particularly in patients under 70. These secondary cancers may result from myeloma treatments like lenalidomide, especially when used with melphalan or stem cell transplantation, as well as from genetic, immunologic, or environmental factors [29, 30]. The coexistence of MM and DLBCL in a single patient is exceedingly rare and presents a unique clinical challenge. Both are mature B-cell neoplasms, but they arise from different stages of B-cell differentiation and display distinct biological behaviors, clinical features, and treatment approaches. MM originates from terminally differentiated plasma cells, whereas DLBCL is an aggressive lymphoid malignancy derived from germinal center or post-germinal center B cells [2, 18, 31]. The simultaneous or sequential occurrence of these malignancies raises important questions about shared pathogenic mechanisms, clonal evolution, and the biological basis underlying their coexistence.

The patient had a rare IgM-producing multiple myeloma, diagnosed based on 30% bone marrow plasma cell infiltration (CD138⁺, CD3⁻/CD20⁻), markedly elevated serum IgM (3409 mg/dL), and monoclonal IgM-kappa gammopathy on immunotyping. Serum protein electrophoresis showed a reduced A/G ratio, elevated beta-2 globulin, and low albumin, consistent with a monoclonal protein likely migrating in the beta region. IgM-predominant multiple myeloma (IgM MM) is an exceptionally rare subtype, accounting for less than 0.5% of all multiple myeloma cases [32]. Because IgM multiple myeloma is a rare entity, it is important to distinguish it from Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM), as these conditions represent biologically distinct disorders with different diagnostic criteria, clinical manifestations, treatment strategies, and prognosis. Based on the clinical, radiologic, and immunophenotypic findings, this case is most consistent with IgM MM rather than WM. According to IMWG and NCCN guidelines, the diagnosis of MM requires clonal bone marrow plasma cells together with at least one myeloma‑defining event. In this patient, bone marrow biopsy demonstrated CD138+, CD20− clonal plasma cells, supporting a plasma cell neoplasm typical of MM rather than WM. Imaging revealed a destructive lytic bone lesion with a pathologic intertrochanteric femoral fracture, fulfilling the CRAB bone disease criterion and representing a definitive myeloma‑defining event. The presence of true osteolytic bone lesions strongly favors multiple myeloma and is characteristically absent in WM, which typically shows CD20+ lymphoplasmacytic infiltration and lacks destructive bone disease. Although the monoclonal protein is of the IgM isotype, rare cases of IgM MM are recognized when myeloma diagnostic criteria are met. This presentation is also incompatible with monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), which requires < 3 g/dL monoclonal protein, < 10% clonal plasma cells in bone marrow, and absence of myeloma‑defining events or end‑organ damage. So, the presence of a lytic bone lesion and pathologic fracture represents clear end‑organ damage, thereby excluding MGUS. Therefore, based on IMWG and NCCN criteria, the findings support a diagnosis of MM, and additional molecular testing such as MYD88 L265P or t(11;14) is not required for diagnostic differentiation from WM, although such studies may be considered for prognostic or biological characterization [33–37].

The therapeutic approach was necessarily individualized and staged. Given the aggressive nature of DLBCL and its immediate threat to organ function, the patient was prioritized for R-CHOP chemotherapy, which is the standard of care for this lymphoma subtype. The excellent response to R-CHOP, confirmed by radiological regression of nodal disease, suggested remission of DLBCL. Interestingly, during the two-year period post-chemotherapy, there were no clinical or laboratory indicators of MM activity, suggesting that the patient’s plasma cell disorder remained biologically indolent or partially suppressed by cytotoxic agents in the R-CHOP regimen. For the majority of patients newly diagnosed with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), treatment with R-CHOP immunochemotherapy results in complete remission (CR), with 60–70% remaining free of disease progression five years later. Given that the median patient age is 65, it is important to understand how both the disease and its treatment affect long-term healthcare utilization among survivors [38]. A descriptive study was conducted to assess the complete remission (CR) rate in newly diagnosed patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) aged 20–70 who received first-line R-CHOP therapy. A total of 95 patients were included, of whom 57.9% were male, and CR was achieved in 84.2% of cases. Patients aged ≤ 45 years accounted for 45.3%, and in 63.2% of cases, the disease duration was ≤ 3 months. Based on body mass index (BMI), 51.6% of patients were classified within the normal range (18.5–24.9 kg/m2). The high efficacy of R-CHOP in this population was demonstrated, though the clinical heterogeneity of DLBCL and the potential for long-term adverse effects were acknowledged, underscoring the need for further research on prognostic markers and alternative treatment options [39]. Also, we observed that the R-CHOP regimen induced a two-year delayed progression remission in a MM patient after eight cycles—an outcome that, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported. Upon relapse, which presented as a pathological fracture and biopsy-proven plasmacytoma, treatment was shifted to a standard VRD regimen for MM. Following initial control, the patient underwent ASCT, consistent with evidence supporting high-dose chemotherapy followed by stem cell rescue as a consolidative strategy in eligible MM patients. Post-transplant maintenance with lenalidomide was continued for three years, as recommended in current guidelines, which have demonstrated prolonged progression-free survival. Lenalidomide is commonly used as maintenance therapy following ASCT in MM, offering substantial improvements in progression-free and event-free survival. It is usually started within six months post-transplant. Although it has been associated with a heightened risk of secondary malignancies, the survival advantage it provides generally surpasses this potential risk [40]. Following ASCT and initiation of maintenance therapy with lenalidomide, a decline in hemoglobin levels and bone marrow plasma cell infiltration were observed, prompting a shift to the VCD regimen. However, due to hematologic toxicity, particularly pancytopenia, cyclophosphamide was discontinued, and the patient was transitioned to VD. This case highlights the critical need to balance treatment efficacy with tolerability, especially in patients with an extended history of therapy.

The coexistence of multiple myeloma and DLBCL in the same patient raises important questions about shared pathogenic mechanisms. Beyond clinical complexity, this overlap may be influenced by common oncogenic pathways rather than shared clonal origin. Our bioinformatics analysis aimed to identify potentially convergent molecular mechanisms by comparing mutation profiles from published MM and DLBCL cohorts, highlighting shared genes and pathways that could inform future mechanistic studies. We emphasize that this analysis is cohort-level and exploratory, and does not provide direct evidence of shared clonal origin in our patient.

BCL7A acts as a tumor suppressor in MM, and its noncoding mutations or loss of expression are common, leading to unchecked IRF4 transcriptional activity, increased proliferation, and survival of myeloma cells. BCL7A normally restricts IRF4 through direct protein interaction, so its loss removes this key regulatory checkpoint [41]. IRF4 is a master regulator of plasma cell differentiation and is highly expressed in MM. It’s up-regulation controls an aberrant gene expression program in MM cells and correlates with poor prognosis. IRF4 also supports the survival of myeloma cells by driving both B-cell to plasma cell transition and pathogenic Th17 differentiation, fueling MM progression and immune dysregulation [42–44]. In DLBCL, IRF4 gene rearrangements define a unique LBCL-IRF4 subtype, often with strong IRF4/MUM1 expression. IRF4 acts as an oncogene, promoting proliferation and transformation of B-cells, and mutations facilitate germinal center exit and plasma cell differentiation, supporting lymphomagenesis [45–48].

BCL7A, a component of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, also acts as a tumor suppressor in DLBCL. Biallelic inactivation or truncating mutations disrupt chromatin structure, block differentiation, and promote lymphomagenesis, while restoration exerts anti-tumor effects [49]. Mutations in CBLB, a negative regulator of B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling, enhance B-cell activation and survival, increasing signal amplification and persistence, which support lymphoma cell proliferation and immune evasion in DLBCL [50, 51]. TP53 mutations and deletions (commonly del17p) are strong negative prognostic indicators in MM and are found in up to 10% of cases. Loss of TP53 impairs apoptosis in response to DNA damage, fostering genetic instability and clonal evolution. Double-hit TP53 alterations define the highest-risk group [52, 53]. In DLBCL, TP53 mutations lead to loss of p53 function, resulting in defective DNA damage response, impaired apoptosis, chromosomal instability, and poor therapeutic outcomes [54–56].

KMT2D (MLL2/MLL4) is a histone methyltransferase regulating H3K4 methylation and active enhancer marks. Loss-of-function mutations reduce methylation, dysregulate oncogenic pathways such as NOTCH, RAS, and ERK, and are associated with tumor progression in MM [57, 58]. In DLBCL, KMT2D mutations (~ 25% of cases) impair epigenetic regulation required for germinal center B-cell differentiation, thereby sustaining proliferation and increasing the risk of malignant transformation [59, 60]. CARD11, a scaffolding protein in the NF-κB pathway, shows activating mutations or rearrangements that lead to constitutive NF-κB signaling. In MM, this promotes proliferation and survival, whereas in DLBCL, especially the ABC subtype, mutated CARD11 confers apoptosis resistance and uncontrolled B-cell proliferation [61, 62].

SPEN mutations, which impair transcriptional repression and Notch signaling, may contribute to immune evasion and poor therapeutic responses in DLBCL and may also support MM pathogenesis [63, 64]. DICER1, essential for microRNA processing, is downregulated in MM, promoting cellular senescence and a tumor-supporting microenvironment. Senescence of MM-MSCs is marked by reduced proliferation, differentiation, and increased support for MM growth, with Dicer1 knockdown in healthy MSCs reproducing these effects, whereas Dicer1 overexpression reverses them. MM cells can induce MSC senescence by downregulating Dicer1, miR-93, and miR-20a, and upregulating p21, thereby promoting disease progression [65, 66]. In DLBCL, lower Dicer1 expression correlates with the non-GCB subtype, high International Prognostic Index scores, and shorter overall and progression-free survival, making it an independent prognostic factor [67].

PRDM1 regulates plasma cell development and acts as an oncosuppressor in lymphoid cancers. The PRDM1β isoform, an alternative promoter of the PRDM1 gene, can disrupt normal PRDM1α function. In MM, analysis of the cis-regulatory elements and epigenetic status of PRDM1β revealed hypermethylation at PRDM1α and hypomethylation at PRDM1β promoters, with increased H3K4me1 and H3K9me2 enrichment at PRDM1β in non-expressing cell lines. Treatment with DNA methylation inhibitors modified PRDM1β promoter activity but not PRDM1α, suggesting epigenetic drugs may offer strategies to control PRDM1 expression in MM [68]. BLIMP-1 expression is frequently associated with the ABC phenotype and plasmablastic morphology, but 63% of ABC-DLBCL patients were negative for BLIMP-1. In these patients, BLIMP-1 loss correlated with Myc overexpression and reduced p53 pathway molecules. Homozygous PRDM1 deletions and mutations, particularly in exons 1 and 2, were linked to poor prognosis in ABC-DLBCL but not in GCB-DLBCL. Gene expression profiling revealed that PRDM1/BLIMP-1 loss resulted in reduced plasma-cell differentiation and increased BCR signaling and tumor proliferation. These findings highlight the tumor-suppressive role of PRDM1/BLIMP-1 in ABC-DLBCL and suggest its loss contributes to poor prognosis [69].

MM and DLBCL rely on remarkably similar tumor microenvironment (TME) programs despite their distinct sites of origin—marrow versus nodal/systemic. In both, malignant B-lineage cells depend on chemokine–integrin signaling networks, notably CXCL12–CXCR4 with VLA-4 and related integrins, along with adhesion molecules such as CD44 and selectins, to mediate homing, retention, and niche trafficking. Stromal, endothelial, and osteolineage or fibroblastic cells supply essential survival and drug-resistance cues, while chronic interactions with myeloid and lymphoid populations establish an immunosuppressive milieu enriched for tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and exhausted or dysfunctional T cells. These interactions are further reinforced by alterations in antigen presentation (e.g., class I/II MHC disruption in DLBCL) and activation of multiple immune checkpoint pathways and suppressive myeloid circuits in MM. Moreover, both malignancies employ soluble and vesicle-mediated communication—cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and exosomes—to remodel metabolic, angiogenic, and osteo/mesenchymal niches, promoting tumor growth, immune evasion, and therapy resistance. Collectively, these shared TME dependencies underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting conserved axes such as chemokine–integrin networks, myeloid checkpoints (e.g., CD47–SIRPα), and broader immune-suppressive pathways across B-cell malignancies [70, 71].

Limitations

Our bioinformatics analysis has several inherent limitations. The datasets were derived from independent patient cohorts with differences in sequencing platforms, sequencing depth, and disease stages, which may introduce heterogeneity. In addition, mutation selection was based on predefined frequency thresholds, potentially capturing both driver and passenger alterations. Patient-matched genomic data for MM and DLBCL were not available, and therefore direct comparative genomic analysis of our patient’s lymph node and bone marrow samples could not be performed. Likewise, whole-exome sequencing (WES) data were not available for this case, which limited the possibility of a more comprehensive genomic characterization. Consequently, the present in silico analysis does not suggest a shared clonal origin in this patient but instead provides a hypothesis-generating overview of potentially overlapping oncogenic pathways. Furthermore, annotations of therapeutic actionability from resources such as OncoKB should be interpreted with caution in hematologic malignancies, as much of the available evidence is derived from studies in solid tumors. Overall, these findings should be considered exploratory and may help guide future mechanistic or patient-specific investigations.

Conclusion and future prospect

This study provides valuable insights into the coexistence of MM and DLBCL, two distinct B-cell malignancies, within a single patient. The case report highlights the diagnostic complexities and challenges of managing these dual malignancies, emphasizing the importance of tailored therapeutic approaches. The systematic review of existing literature underlines that while such co-occurrences are rare, they often occur in older patients and present a range of clinical features and treatment responses. Additionally, bioinformatics analysis revealed driver genetic mutations between MM and DLBCL, including TP53, KMT2D, and PRDM1, which are involved in key cancer-related pathways and may serve as potential therapeutic targets. The findings suggest that a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying these driver mutations can lead to more effective treatment strategies. Further research into the genetic overlap and cooperative pathways between these two cancers will help refine prognostic tools and identify targeted therapies that could improve outcomes for patients with coexisting MM and DLBCL.
