Conditional Score-Based Modeling of Effective Langevin Dynamics
Ludovico T. Giorgini

Abstract
Stochastic reduced-order models are widely used to represent the effective dynamics of complex systems, but estimating their drift and diffusion coefficients from data remains challenging. Standard approaches often rely on short-time trajectory increments, state-space partitioning, or repeated simulation of candidate models, which become unreliable or computationally expensive for high-dimensional systems, coarse temporal sampling, or unevenly sampled data. We introduce a data-driven calibration method based on a novel relationship between the coefficients of a stochastic reduced model and the conditional score of the finite-time transition density, defined as the gradient of the logarithm of the transition density with respect to the initial state. The resulting identity expresses derivatives of lagged correlation functions as stationary expectations over observed lagged pairs involving this conditional score and the unknown model coefficients. This formulation allows the drift and diffusion structure to be constrained directly from finite-lag statistics, without differentiating trajectories, partitioning state space, or repeatedly integrating candidate reduced models during calibration, yielding a least-squares fitting problem over stationary lagged pairs. We validate the approach on analytically tractable and data-driven nonequilibrium diffusions, demonstrating that the inferred models preserve the invariant statistics while accurately reproducing finite-lag dynamical correlations. The framework provides a scalable route for learning stochastic reduced-order models from data that reproduce prescribed statistical and dynamical properties.

Introduction

A central challenge in the study of high-dimensional multiscale dynamical systems is to construct reduced descriptions that retain the statistical and dynamical influence of unresolved variables. In many applications, the resolved variables represent only a small subset of the full state, while the eliminated degrees of freedom continue to affect their evolution through fast fluctuations, memory effects, and effective transport mechanisms. Projection and coarse-graining formalisms provide a precise framework for this idea: eliminating variables from a high-dimensional dynamics generally produces reduced equations with nonlocal memory terms and fluctuating forcing, even when the original dynamics are deterministic or Markovian in the complete state space

,

,

,

]
. In practice, one often replaces this non-Markovian reduced dynamics by an explicit Markovian stochastic surrogate, typically an effective stochastic differential equation whose drift and diffusion terms summarize the influence of unresolved variables on the observed state. Such stochastic parameterizations provide tractable and interpretable models for the statistics and dynamics of resolved observables, and have played a central role in climate dynamics, turbulence, and empirical model reduction
[
29
,
49
,
48
,
33
,

,

]
.

A stochastic reduced-order model should preserve the statistical and dynamical features of the observed variables that are relevant at the resolved scale. A necessary requirement is the reproduction of the invariant measure, since the stationary distribution encodes the long-time statistics of the resolved observables. This requirement alone, however, is not sufficient. Distinct stochastic dynamics can share the same invariant density while exhibiting different temporal correlations, probability currents, response properties, and effective dynamical couplings. A successful reduced model must therefore reproduce not only the steady-state distribution, but also the dynamical mechanisms by which probability is transported through state space. This distinction is especially important in climate modeling, where one often seeks to predict how coarse observables respond to perturbations, rather than only to reproduce their unforced climatology. Response theory makes clear that forced responses are controlled by the interplay between invariant statistics and dynamical transport, not by the invariant density alone
[
51
,
52
]
. Recent analyses of data-driven climate emulators and reduced models have reinforced this point, showing that accurate stationary statistics do not by themselves guarantee accurate response behavior or physically meaningful coarse dynamics
[
13
,

,
12
,
37
]
. The construction of stochastic reduced models should therefore be formulated as the joint problem of matching invariant statistics and finite-lag dynamical observables.

A first route is calibration by repeated simulation: one specifies a parametric or functional model ansatz, integrates the resulting dynamics for each trial parameter value, estimates from the simulated trajectory the statistical or dynamical observables of interest, compares them with the corresponding targets, and updates the model accordingly. This strategy is closely related to simulation-based inference approaches, in which model parameters are inferred by matching statistics or discrepancies computed from simulated and observed data
[

,

. Although very general, such approaches become computationally inefficient when each model evaluation requires a long integration of a high-dimensional stochastic surrogate. A second route is to infer the local-in-time dynamical law directly from trajectory data. This includes Kramers–Moyal and Fokker–Planck reconstruction methods, which estimate drift and diffusion coefficients from short-time conditional increment statistics, as well as related stochastic-modeling approaches that recover effective tendencies, stability coefficients, or noise amplitudes from finite differences of observed trajectories
[

,

,

,

,

,

Such methods have provided important tools for empirical stochastic modeling, but they are intrinsically tied to the availability of sufficiently resolved time series: when observations are noisy, unevenly sampled, or separated by time intervals much longer than the intrinsic dynamical time scale, finite-difference estimates of local tendencies and short-time conditional moments become unreliable. A third route is to approximate coarse generators, transfer operators, or lagged correlation structures through state-space partitions, clustering, or related operator-theoretic constructions

,

These limitations motivate an inverse formulation in which an effective stochastic generator is constrained directly from stationary samples and lagged pairs, without repeated forward integration during calibration, without estimating instantaneous rates of change from trajectories, and without reconstructing local coefficients by state-space clustering.

The alternative pursued here is to use finite-lag conditional statistics as the primary dynamical object. This viewpoint is motivated by time-delay embedding theory, which shows that, for deterministic systems and under suitable generic
conditions, lagged observations can encode the dynamics on the underlying
attractor
[
59
,
2

. In the stochastic
multiscale setting considered here, however, we do not rely on a deterministic
embedding theorem. Rather, the corresponding object is the finite-time
transition law of the resolved variables, which describes how the present
resolved state statistically constrains future resolved states, even when the
full high-dimensional state is unobserved. The relevant object is therefore not
an instantaneous drift or local tendency estimated from consecutive data points,
but the conditional law of future resolved states given present resolved states.
We represent this conditional law through its score, namely the gradient of the
logarithm of the finite-lag transition density with respect to the present
resolved state. The conditional score provides a local sensitivity
representation of the finite-lag statistical dependence between present and
future states, and thus contains the information needed to constrain the reduced
generator. Recent advances in score matching and score-based generative modeling
make it possible to estimate such score functions directly from high-dimensional
samples without explicit density reconstruction

[

,
60
,
55
,
18
]
.
The purpose of this paper is to derive a direct relation between conditional
scores and the mobility structure of an effective Langevin model, and to use
this relation to infer stochastic reduced dynamics from stationary samples and
lagged pairs, avoiding the limitations of simulation-based calibration,
state-space partitioning, and trajectory differentiation.

The use of score-function estimators developed in generative score modeling has already proved highly effective in nonequilibrium statistical physics and data-driven modeling of nonlinear dynamical systems. One important application is generalized fluctuation–dissipation theory, which relates the response of observables to weak perturbations to correlation functions evaluated in the unperturbed system
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

. In nonlinear nonequilibrium systems, these correlation functions involve derivatives of the invariant density, a quantity that is difficult to estimate directly in high dimension. Learned score functions provide precisely this information, and have enabled accurate data-driven response predictions for nonlinear and high-dimensional dynamical systems
[
23
,
20
,
19
]
. The same principle has also been used to construct score-based Langevin reduced models from data
[
24
,
8
]
. In that framework, the learned stationary score fixes the target steady-state density, while a constant mobility matrix, determined from the short-time derivative of the coordinate autocorrelation, closes the dynamics by replacing the state-dependent transport structure with a mean-field approximation. This construction preserves the steady-state distribution by design and reproduces the leading short-time coordinate correlations, and has proved effective in high-dimensional examples, including the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation and cyclostationary sea-surface-temperature data from PlaSim
[
22

Its limitation, however, is intrinsic to the closure: because the mobility is constant and only the stationary score is used, the model can enforce only the short-time correlation constraints used to determine that constant matrix, and cannot systematically impose more general finite-lag dynamical observables. The present work removes this restriction by introducing the conditional score as the object that links finite-lag transition statistics to the state-dependent mobility of the reduced Langevin generator. This extension turns score-based stochastic modeling from a construction that preserves the invariant density and a restricted set of short-time correlations into a framework in which prescribed dynamical constraints can be imposed directly from data.

2
Method

2.1
Problem setting and modeling objective

We consider a resolved process
\bm{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
obtained from observations. The goal is to construct, directly from data, an effective stochastic model for these resolved variables. The construction is based on four assumptions.

First, the resolved process is assumed to admit a statistically stationary distribution
p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})
. Cyclostationary data can be incorporated by augmenting the state with the phase of the external forcing, while slow nonstationary trends should be removed before applying the method. Second, the process is assumed to be ergodic and sufficiently mixing. Ergodicity is needed to replace ensemble expectations by time averages along a single sufficiently long realization, while mixing ensures that correlations between widely separated samples decay, which stabilizes the empirical lagged statistics entering the inverse problem. Third, the resolved variables are assumed to evolve on slower time scales than the unresolved degrees of freedom, which appear only through their effective stochastic influence on the resolved dynamics. We assume, in the main presentation, that the slow variables are fully observed; if the observations are partial, the same framework can be applied after replacing the state by a delay-embedded state. In that setting, the delay coordinates carry finite-window information about the unobserved components and their memory effects, so that the effective reduced dynamics can again be modeled on an augmented observed state. Finally, the data must have adequate temporal resolution for the dynamical quantities being matched. In particular, we do not require the sampling to be fine enough to estimate instantaneous time derivatives, but it must resolve the finite-lag statistics that will be used as modeling constraints.

We model the resolved dynamics by an Itô diffusion of the form

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}(t)=\bm{F}(\bm{x}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x}(t))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{t},

(1)

where
\bm{F}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
is the drift,

\bm{\Sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
is the noise-amplitude matrix, and
\bm{W}_{t}
is a standard
D
-dimensional Wiener process. We denote the diffusion tensor by

\bm{D}(\bm{x}):=\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x})^{T}.

(2)

The central modeling problem is to infer
\bm{F}
and
\bm{D}
from data in such a way that the resulting diffusion reproduces both the stationary distribution of the observations and selected dynamical features of the resolved process.

To enforce the stationary distribution, we use the score of the observed steady state,

\bm{s}(\bm{x}):=\nabla\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}).

(3)

The stationary score can be estimated directly from samples by denoising score matching; see Appendix
S3.1
and Appendix
S3.2
for the stationary and conditional constructions used throughout the paper.
Following the score-based Langevin representation derived in
[
24
]
, a stationary diffusion with invariant density
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
can be written in the score-based form below. A detailed derivation and the required regularity and boundary assumptions are given in Appendix
S1
.

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}(t)=\left[\bm{M}(\bm{x})\bm{s}(\bm{x})+\nabla\cdot\bm{M}(\bm{x})\right]\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{t},

(4)

where

\displaystyle\bm{M}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\bm{D}(\bm{x})+\bm{R}(\bm{x}),

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{R}(\bm{x})^{T}

\displaystyle=-\bm{R}(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\bm{D}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{M}(\bm{x})+\bm{M}(\bm{x})^{T}}{2}\succeq 0.

Here
(\nabla\cdot\bm{M})_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{D}\partial_{x_{j}}M_{ij}
. The symmetric part of
\bm{M}
determines the diffusion tensor, while the antisymmetric part represents stationary probability currents and therefore allows the reduced model to describe nonequilibrium dynamics. The advantage of (
4
) is that the steady-state density is imposed by construction: once the score
\bm{s}
and the mobility
\bm{M}
are specified, the model has
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
as invariant density.

Matching the stationary density alone, however, is not sufficient. Many stochastic processes can share the same invariant distribution while having very different temporal organization, response properties, and effective transport. We therefore constrain the mobility using lagged statistics of a prescribed family of observables. Let

\phi_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}},\qquad m=1,\dots,M,

(6)

be smooth observables chosen to represent the dynamical features of interest. For each pair
(m,n)
and each lag
t
in a selected set of resolved time scales, we define the observed lagged correlation matrix

\bm{C}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(t):=\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\phi_{n}(\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}.

(7)

The modeling objective is then to construct a diffusion of the form (
4
) such that

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{model}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\approx p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}),

(8)

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{m,n,\mathrm{model}}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\bm{C}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(t).

The lagged-correlation matching is imposed for the selected observables and lags.

This choice of constraints is deliberate. The invariant density fixes the statistics of the reduced process, while the lagged correlations probe the action of the dynamics on observables and therefore encode information about time scales, transport, memory effects, and linear response. At the same time, these quantities are statistically robust: they can be estimated as time averages from observed trajectories, including sparsely or unevenly sampled data by using all available pairs within prescribed lag windows. The remainder of this section shows how the mobility
\bm{M}
can be inferred from the score and from the empirical lagged correlations, without requiring repeated long integrations of candidate reduced models.

2.2
Inferring the mobility from correlation constraints

The preceding subsection reduces the modeling problem to the identification of the mobility field
\bm{M}(\bm{x})
. The steady-state score fixes the invariant density. The mobility then determines the probability currents, time scales, transport properties, and lagged correlations of the reduced process. We now show how
\bm{M}
can be inferred directly from the empirical correlation constraints introduced above. The conditional scores entering this construction are estimated from lagged pairs by the joint-score denoising score-matching procedure summarized in Appendix
S3.2
.
The key object is the finite-lag transition density of the observed resolved process,

p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0}),

(9)

and its score with respect to the initial condition,

\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0}):=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0}).

(10)

This conditional score measures how the likelihood of observing
\bm{x}_{t}
at lag
t
changes under an infinitesimal perturbation of the initial state
\bm{x}_{0}
. It is therefore the finite-time analogue of the stationary score, but applied to transition probabilities rather than to the invariant density.

The central identity of the method is that, for the score-based diffusion
(
4
), the derivative of the lagged correlation matrix can
be written in the equivalent forms

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\nabla(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}(0))\,\bm{M}(\bm{x}(0))\right.

(11)

\displaystyle\left.\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}(t)\mid\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\bm{M}(\bm{x}(0))\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right\rangle.

Here
K_{t}
denotes the Koopman semigroup associated with the reduced
Markov process,
\nabla(K_{t}\phi_{m})
is the Jacobian of the lag-
t

conditional expectation of the observable
\phi_{m}
, and
\nabla\phi_{n}

denotes the Jacobian of the vector-valued observable
\phi_{n}
. The
expectation is over stationary lagged pairs
(\bm{x}(0),\bm{x}(t))
.
The second equality follows from the transition-density representation of

K_{t}\phi_{m}
and from the definition of the conditional score

\bm{s}_{t|0}=\nabla_{\bm{x}(0)}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}(t)\mid\bm{x}(0))
. A
detailed derivation of (
11
) is given in
Appendix
S2
.

The first line of (
11
) makes explicit
the Koopman-theoretic structure of the inverse problem. In Koopman spectral
approaches, one typically represents the action of
K_{t}
through eigenvalues
and eigenfunctions, so that, formally, if

\phi_{m}=\sum_{\ell}\bm{a}_{m,\ell}\psi_{\ell}
and

K_{t}\psi_{\ell}=e^{\lambda_{\ell}t}\psi_{\ell}
, then

\nabla(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x})=\sum_{\ell}e^{\lambda_{\ell}t}\,\bm{a}_{m,\ell}\,\nabla\psi_{\ell}(\bm{x}).

This viewpoint underlies Koopman mode decompositions, dynamic mode
decomposition, extended dynamic mode decomposition, and recent formulations
linking Koopman spectral decompositions with response theory

[
34
,
45
,
3
,
61
,
32
,
53
,
64
,
38
]
.
In that setting, response and correlation operators can be decomposed into
contributions associated with Koopman modes, providing a highly interpretable
description of the dynamical time scales.

For high-dimensional stochastic or coarse-grained systems, however, using this
representation as a practical inverse tool requires estimating sufficiently many
Koopman eigenfunctions and, in the present identity, their spatial gradients.
This is a severe numerical bottleneck: the eigenfunction approximation depends
on the choice of dictionary, kernel, or finite-rank subspace; the required number
of modes can grow rapidly with dimension; and differentiating the learned
eigenfunctions can amplify approximation error. The second line of
(
11
) provides an alternative route. It
retains the same Koopman object,
\nabla(K_{t}\phi_{m})
, but evaluates it through
the conditional transition score instead of through an explicit spectral
resolution of the Koopman operator. Thus, neural score estimation on lagged
pairs supplies the differential information needed by the Koopman-gradient
identity without constructing Koopman eigenfunctions. This is the key practical
advantage of the present formulation: it turns a Koopman response identity into
a data-driven correlation constraint that remains linear in the unknown mobility

\bm{M}
, avoids instantaneous velocity estimation, and can be implemented in
high-dimensional systems using modern conditional score estimators.

In practice, we choose a set of resolved lags
\mathcal{T}
and replace all expectations in (
11
) by empirical averages over observed pairs. This defines the empirical correlation operator

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{m,n,t}[\bm{M}]

\displaystyle:=-\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}_{t})\,\widehat{\bm{s}}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\right.

(12)

\displaystyle\left.\bm{M}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}},\qquad t\in\mathcal{T}.

The mobility is then determined by enforcing

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{m,n,t}[\bm{M}]

\displaystyle\approx\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(t),

(13)

\displaystyle t\in\mathcal{T},\quad m,n=1,\dots,M_{\mathrm{obs}},

together with the structural constraint

\frac{\bm{M}(\bm{x})+\bm{M}(\bm{x})^{T}}{2}\succeq 0.

(14)

For a finite-dimensional parametrization
\bm{M}_{\theta}
, this leads to the least-squares problem

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{m,n}w_{m,n,t}\left\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{m,n,t}[\bm{M}_{\theta}]-\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(t)\right\|_{F}^{2}

(15)

\displaystyle+\lambda\,\mathcal{R}(\theta),

where
\mathcal{R}
denotes regularization. The weights
w_{m,n,t}
may account for sampling uncertainty or for the relative importance of different observables and lags.

The reason for matching correlation derivatives, rather than only the correlations themselves, is that the derivative constraint identifies the generator of the reduced process on the chosen observable class. Since the invariant density has already been imposed by construction, the model and the observations have the same equal-time correlations:

\bm{C}_{m,n,\mathrm{model}}(0)=\bm{C}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(0).

(16)

Therefore, for any lag
t
in the resolved interval,

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{m,n,\mathrm{model}}(t)-\bm{C}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\Big[\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n,\mathrm{model}}(\tau)

(17)

\displaystyle-\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(\tau)\Big]\mathrm{d}\tau.

Thus, if the mobility inferred from (
13
) makes the model correlation derivatives agree with the observed derivatives over the selected lag range, then the corresponding correlation functions agree over that range. More precisely, if

\displaystyle\left\|\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n,\mathrm{model}}(\tau)-\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(\tau)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{m,n}(\tau),

(18)

\displaystyle 0\leq\tau\leq T,

then

\left\|\bm{C}_{m,n,\mathrm{model}}(t)-\bm{C}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(t)\right\|_{F}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon_{m,n}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(19)

The derivative constraint is therefore a weak, data-driven way of enforcing dynamical agreement on the observable library.

We now clarify what it means for the observable library to be sufficiently rich. A strong sufficient
condition is that the selected observables and lags identify the admissible
mobility perturbations. Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the admissible class of
mobility perturbations, for example the tangent space associated with the
chosen parametrization of
\bm{M}
. Define the nullspace

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T},\phi}(\mathcal{H}):=\Bigl\{\bm{H}\in\mathcal{H}:

(20)

\displaystyle\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}_{t})\,\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\bm{H}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}=0,

\displaystyle\text{for all }t\in\mathcal{T},\text{ and }m,n\Bigr\}.

If

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T},\phi}\bigl(\mathcal{H}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\{\bm{0}\},

(21)

then the selected constraints are separating on
\mathcal{H}
: no nonzero
admissible perturbation of the mobility leaves all selected
correlation-derivative constraints unchanged. This restricted-injectivity
condition is therefore sufficient for identifying the mobility on the support
of the invariant measure, up to the resolution of the chosen parametrization.

For the reduced-modeling objective pursued here, however, this condition is
stronger than necessary. The practical requirement is that the observable
library be rich enough that a mobility satisfying the empirical GFDT constraints

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{mn}^{\mathrm{obs}}(t)

\displaystyle\approx-\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}_{t})\,\bm{s}_{t|0}^{\mathrm{obs}}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\right.

(22)

\displaystyle\left.\bm{M}(\bm{x}_{0})\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}.

also yields a model whose corresponding correlation derivatives are close to
the observed ones,

\dot{C}_{mn}^{\mathrm{model}}(t)\approx\dot{C}_{mn}^{\mathrm{obs}}(t),\qquad t\in\mathcal{T}.

(23)

Thus, strict injectivity is an identifiability condition. The weaker condition
needed in practice is observable-level adequacy. In this weaker setting,
different mobilities may be indistinguishable by the selected observables and
lags. That nonuniqueness is harmless if all such mobilities produce the same
resolved correlation dynamics. With finite data, the relevant criterion is not
exact nullspace triviality. It is stability and predictive closure: the
selected constraints should make the inverse problem well conditioned for the
correlation statistics one aims to reproduce.

This is the sense in which the method avoids both local velocity reconstruction and state-space clustering. The dynamical information is extracted from robust finite-lag statistics and from conditional scores of observed pairs. The mobility is then inferred within the chosen admissible class by matching the weak correlation dynamics of the data on a selected family of observables.

2.3
Mean mobility, mobility corrections, and the constant closure

The correlation constraints in Section
2.2
determine the mobility field only through its action on the chosen observable library. We introduce the decomposition

\displaystyle\bm{M}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\bm{\Phi}+\delta\bm{M}(\bm{x}),

(24)

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}

\displaystyle:=\left\langle\bm{M}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}},

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\bm{M}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}.

This separates the mean transport structure, encoded by the constant matrix
\bm{\Phi}
, from deviations around it.

For any matrix field
\bm{A}(\bm{x})
, define the conjugate observable associated with
\phi_{n}
by

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}_{n}[\bm{A}](\bm{x})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})}\nabla\cdot\Big[p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,\bm{A}(\bm{x})\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x})^{T}\Big].

(25)

Using the decomposition (
24
), the correlation identity can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\bm{\gamma}_{n}[\bm{\Phi}](\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right\rangle

(26)

\displaystyle+\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\bm{\gamma}_{n}[\delta\bm{M}](\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right\rangle.

The first term is the contribution of the constant mean mobility, while the second term measures the dynamical effect of state-dependent mobility corrections. The derivation of (
26
) is given in Appendix
S4
.

The mean mobility is fixed by the right derivative at the origin of the coordinate correlation. Taking

\phi_{m}(\bm{x})=\phi_{n}(\bm{x})=\bm{x},

(27)

one obtains

\dot{\bm{C}}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}(0^{+})=-\left\langle\bm{M}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}=-\bm{\Phi}.

(28)

Thus,

\bm{\Phi}=-\dot{\bm{C}}_{\bm{x},\bm{x},\mathrm{obs}}(0^{+}).

(29)

In practice, the derivative in (
29
) is estimated from a smooth short-lag fit of the observed coordinate correlations.

We now keep the coordinate observables and consider finite lags
t>0
. Let

\bm{m}_{t}(\bm{x}_{0}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{x}(t)\,\middle|\,\bm{x}(0)=\bm{x}_{0}\right]

(30)

be the finite-lag conditional mean. Then (see Appendix
S4
)

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{C}}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\bm{x}_{t}\,\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}\bm{\Phi}

(31)

\displaystyle-\left\langle\nabla\bm{m}_{t}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\delta\bm{M}(\bm{x}_{0})\right\rangle.

The second term is the only contribution of the state-dependent mobility correction to the coordinate correlation dynamics. It vanishes whenever the conditional mean is affine in the initial condition, as occurs, for example, when the joint distribution of
(\bm{x}(t),\bm{x}(0))
is multivariate Gaussian. In fact

\bm{m}_{t}(\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{a}(t)+\bm{A}(t)\bm{x}_{0},

(32)

then
\nabla\bm{m}_{t}=\bm{A}(t)
is independent of
\bm{x}_{0}
, and therefore

\left\langle\nabla\bm{m}_{t}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\delta\bm{M}(\bm{x}_{0})\right\rangle=\bm{A}(t)\left\langle\delta\bm{M}\right\rangle=\bm{0}.

(33)

Consequently, when the modeling target is to reproduce the observed invariant density and the coordinate time correlations, and when the observed conditional mean is well approximated by an affine function of the initial condition, the constant-mobility model

\bm{M}(\bm{x})=\bm{\Phi}

(34)

is sufficient at the level of these constraints. This recovers, as a special case, the constant score-based Langevin closure introduced in
[
24
]
. State-dependent corrections
\delta\bm{M}
become necessary when the selected observables, nonlinear correlations, or non-affine conditional means reveal dynamical features that cannot be represented by the constant closure.

When the constant closure is not sufficiently expressive, we represent the state-dependent correction with a neural parametrization of the full mobility. We write

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{\theta}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\bm{D}_{\theta}(\bm{x})+\bm{R}_{\theta}(\bm{x}),

(35)

\displaystyle\bm{D}_{\theta}(\bm{x})^{T}

\displaystyle=\bm{D}_{\theta}(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{\theta}(\bm{x})^{T}

\displaystyle=-\bm{R}_{\theta}(\bm{x}).

A neural network outputs

\bm{h}_{\theta}(\bm{x})=\left(\bm{\ell}_{\theta}(\bm{x}),\bm{r}_{\theta}(\bm{x})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{D(D+1)/2}\times\mathbb{R}^{D(D-1)/2}.

(36)

The vector
\bm{\ell}_{\theta}
fills the entries of a lower-triangular matrix

\bm{L}_{\theta}(\bm{x})
, with positive diagonal entries imposed through a smooth positive map. We then set

\bm{D}_{\theta}(\bm{x})=\bm{L}_{\theta}(\bm{x})\bm{L}_{\theta}(\bm{x})^{T}+\varepsilon\bm{I},\qquad\varepsilon>0.

(37)

The vector
\bm{r}_{\theta}
fills the strictly lower-triangular entries of

\bm{R}_{\theta}
, while the remaining entries are fixed by antisymmetry:

[\bm{R}_{\theta}(\bm{x})]_{ij}=-[\bm{R}_{\theta}(\bm{x})]_{ji},\qquad[\bm{R}_{\theta}(\bm{x})]_{ii}=0.

(38)

This parametrization uses

\frac{D(D+1)}{2}+\frac{D(D-1)}{2}=D^{2}

(39)

outputs, exactly matching the number of degrees of freedom of a general mobility matrix, while guaranteeing

\operatorname{Sym}\bm{M}_{\theta}(\bm{x})=\bm{D}_{\theta}(\bm{x})\succ 0

(40)

on the observed support.

The learned correction is defined relative to the mean mobility fixed by the short-time coordinate correlations:

\delta\bm{M}_{\theta}(\bm{x}):=\bm{M}_{\theta}(\bm{x})-\bm{\Phi}.

(41)

Using the linearity of the empirical correlation operator, the residual constraint takes the form

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{m,n,t}[\delta\bm{M}_{\theta}]\approx\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(t)-\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{m,n,t}[\bm{\Phi}].

(42)

The parameters are determined by minimizing

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\delta M}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{m,n}w_{m,n,t}\Bigl\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{m,n,t}[\delta\bm{M}_{\theta}]\Bigr.

(43)

\displaystyle-\Bigl.\left[\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(t)-\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{m,n,t}[\bm{\Phi}]\right]\Bigr\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\mathrm{mean}}\left\|\left\langle\bm{M}_{\theta}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}-\bm{\Phi}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}\mathcal{R}(\theta).

The mean-matching term enforces

\left\langle\delta\bm{M}_{\theta}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}\approx\bm{0},

(44)

so that the network learns only the state-dependent part not already determined by

\bm{\Phi}
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{t}
be a minibatch of observed lagged pairs

(\bm{x}_{0}^{(k)},\bm{x}_{t}^{(k)})
. The empirical residual operator is then evaluated as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathcal{B}}_{m,n,t}[\delta\bm{M}_{\theta}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{t}|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}\phi_{m}(\bm{x}_{t}^{(k)})

(45)

\displaystyle\times\widehat{\bm{s}}_{t|0}\bigl(\bm{x}_{t}^{(k)}\mid\bm{x}_{0}^{(k)}\bigr)^{T}

\displaystyle\times\delta\bm{M}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{0}^{(k)})\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}_{0}^{(k)})^{T}.

Thus training requires only observed lagged pairs, the estimated conditional scores, the observable gradients, and evaluations of the neural mobility at the initial points. No forward simulation of the learned stochastic model enters the loss.

3
Results

3.1
Analytic warmup: the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross square-root diffusion

We begin with a one-dimensional benchmark for which all objects entering the inverse construction, the stationary score, the conditional transition score, and the lagged correlation functions, are available in closed form. This makes it possible to verify, without approximation error, that the correlation constraints identify the state-dependent mobility once the observable library removes the relevant nullspace.

We consider the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross square-root diffusion
[
7
,
14
]

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\kappa(\theta-X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\gamma X_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad X_{t}>0,

(46)

with parameters
\kappa,\theta,\gamma>0
. We assume the usual Feller condition

\kappa\theta\geq\gamma
, so that the origin is not reached when the process is initialized in the interior. The invariant density is Gamma

p_{\mathrm{ss}}(x)=\frac{\left(\kappa/\gamma\right)^{\kappa\theta/\gamma}}{\Gamma(\kappa\theta/\gamma)}x^{\kappa\theta/\gamma-1}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\kappa x}{\gamma}\right),

(47)

and therefore

s(x)=\partial_{x}\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}(x)=\frac{\kappa\theta-\gamma}{\gamma x}-\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}.

(48)

In the score-based representation, the exact mobility is

M(x)=\gamma x.

(49)

Thus, with the mean-zero decomposition introduced in
Section
2.3
,

\displaystyle M(x)

\displaystyle=\Phi+\delta M(x),

(50)

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=\langle M\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}=\theta\gamma,

\displaystyle\delta M(x)

\displaystyle=\gamma(x-\theta).

For a mobility
correction
\delta\bm{M}=\bm{M}-\bm{\Phi}
, the corresponding residual
equation is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m,n,t}[\delta\bm{M}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{m,n}(t),

(51)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{m,n}(t)

\displaystyle:=\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)-\mathcal{A}_{m,n,t}[\bm{\Phi}].

The coordinate observable
\phi_{1}(x)=x
identifies the constant part immediately. Indeed, using (
28
),

\Phi=-\dot{C}_{1,1,\mathrm{obs}}(0^{+})=\theta\gamma.

(52)

However, this observable does not identify the state-dependent correction. If one restricts the correction to the affine mean-zero family

\delta M_{a}(x)=a(x-\theta),

(53)

then the coordinate channel lies in the nullspace:

\left\langle X_{t}\,s_{t|0}(X_{t}\mid X_{0})\,\delta M_{a}(X_{0})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}=0.

(54)

This degeneracy reflects the affine conditional mean of the CIR process and is precisely the kind of nullspace that the observable library must remove.

To recover the mobility correction, we enlarge the observable library by taking

\phi_{\alpha}(x)=x^{\alpha},\qquad\alpha\neq 1.

(55)

The exact lagged correlation derivative is

\displaystyle\dot{C}_{\alpha,1,\mathrm{obs}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\theta\gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}\right)^{1-\alpha}e^{-\kappa t}

(56)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\kappa\theta/\gamma)}{\Gamma(\kappa\theta/\gamma+1)}.

Using the residual identity (
51
), the inverse problem for the affine correction reduces to

\mathcal{A}_{t,\alpha,1}[\delta M_{a}]=\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,1,\mathrm{obs}}(t),

(57)

where all quantities are known analytically. In Appendix
S5.1
we show that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{t,\alpha,1}[\delta M_{a}]

\displaystyle=-a\,K_{\alpha}(t),

(58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,1,\mathrm{obs}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\gamma\,K_{\alpha}(t),

with
K_{\alpha}(t)\not\equiv 0
for
\alpha\neq 1
. Therefore

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\gamma,

(59)

\displaystyle\delta M(x)

\displaystyle=\gamma(x-\theta),

\displaystyle M(x)

\displaystyle=\theta\gamma+\gamma(x-\theta)=\gamma x.

This example isolates the main mechanism of the method. The short-lag coordinate correlation fixes the constant baseline
\Phi
, while a nonlinear observable removes the nullspace left by the coordinate channel and recovers the full state-dependent mobility exactly.

3.2
A two-dimensional nonreversible diffusion with affine multiplicative noise

We next test the full data-driven pipeline on a two-dimensional nonequilibrium diffusion. In contrast with the CIR benchmark of Section
3.1
, the stationary score, conditional score, and lagged correlation derivatives cannot be obtained analytically. They are all estimated from trajectory data, and the mobility is learned only through the correlation constraints derived in Section
2.2
. The purpose of this example is therefore to assess whether the proposed inverse formulation can recover a nontrivial state-dependent mobility correction from finite-lag statistics, and whether the resulting reduced Langevin model improves dynamical observables without degrading the invariant density with respect to the constant mobility closure baseline.

The reference process is a two-dimensional Itô diffusion

\displaystyle\bm{J}

4
Conclusions

We have introduced a conditional score-based framework for constructing
effective Langevin models from stationary samples and finite-lag observations.
The approach separates the modeling problem into two complementary components:
the stationary score, which encodes the invariant density, and a mobility field,
which determines the dynamical transport structure of the reduced stochastic
model. Once inferred, the mobility specifies both the diffusion tensor and the
drift term required to preserve the observed invariant density, including the
irreversible probability current in nonequilibrium systems.

The main result of the paper is the inverse relation in
Eq. (
11
), which constrains the mobility
directly from lagged correlation derivatives and conditional scores. This
relation is linear in the unknown mobility and therefore provides the basis for
a direct calibration procedure. In contrast with simulation-based calibration,
the method does not require repeated forward integrations of candidate reduced
models. In contrast with local Kramers–Moyal or finite-difference
reconstruction methods, it does not require short-time derivative estimates or
finely resolved trajectories. Instead, it uses finite-lag statistical
information, which can be extracted from stationary lagged pairs and is
therefore naturally suited to data that are temporally coarse, unevenly sampled,
or noisy.

The numerical examples illustrate the scope and interpretation of the method. In
the analytically tractable diffusion benchmark of
Section
3.1
, the framework makes explicit how the choice
of observables determines which components of the mobility are constrained by
the available correlation identities. In the nonequilibrium example of
Section
3.2
, where the stationary and
conditional scores are estimated from data, the learned state-dependent mobility
improves the reproduction of finite-lag dynamical statistics relative to simpler
constant-mobility closures while preserving the target invariant distribution.
These results support the central modeling principle of the paper: for reduced
stochastic modeling, the goal is not necessarily to identify a unique pointwise
mobility throughout state space, but rather to learn a transport structure that
reproduces the relevant stationary and finite-time statistics on the portion of
state space sampled by the data.

A broader implication of the work is the role of the conditional score as a
practical representation of finite-time dynamical information. The central
identity of the paper can be interpreted as a Koopman-gradient relation: it
expresses the gradient of an observable evolved under the Koopman semigroup
through averages over lagged pairs. In traditional Koopman spectral approaches,
such quantities are often accessed by constructing eigenfunction expansions of
the Koopman operator. While this perspective is powerful and interpretable, it
becomes difficult to use as a scalable computational tool in high-dimensional
stochastic systems, where many modes may be required and differentiating learned
eigenfunctions can be unstable. The conditional-score formulation provides an
alternative route: it evaluates the same response-relevant Koopman-gradient
information directly from transition statistics, without requiring an explicit
spectral decomposition.

Conditional scores provide a way to rewrite finite-lag averages in terms of
spatial derivatives of transition statistics, without explicitly reconstructing
the transition density or a spectral representation of the Koopman operator. In
this sense, the framework developed here connects score-based generative
modeling, stochastic reduced-order modeling, and Koopman/response-theoretic
descriptions of dynamical systems. This connection opens a path toward
data-driven stochastic closures that simultaneously preserve invariant
statistics, reproduce finite-lag correlations, and encode response-relevant
transport information in a form that can scale to high-dimensional systems.

Several directions remain open. The present formulation can be extended by using
richer parameterizations of the mobility, by incorporating regularization that
enforces physical structure such as symmetry, positive semidefiniteness,
sparsity, or locality, and by adapting conditional score estimation to strongly
multiscale or partially observed systems. Another important direction is to
develop systematic criteria for choosing observable libraries and lag times so
that the inferred mobility is well constrained for the dynamical features of
interest. Finally, the Koopman interpretation suggests a broader program in
which conditional score networks are used as differential surrogates for
finite-time Koopman or Markov semigroups, providing a scalable alternative to
explicit eigenfunction-based constructions in response theory and stochastic
model reduction.

Appendix

Appendix S1
Score-based representation of stationary diffusions

In this appendix we derive the score-based form of the effective diffusion used in the main text. We work on a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
, either
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{D}
with sufficient decay at infinity or a domain with boundary conditions such that all boundary fluxes vanish. We assume that the stationary density
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
is strictly positive and sufficiently smooth on
\Omega
, and that the drift and diffusion coefficients below are smooth enough for the integrations by parts and differentiations used here to be justified.

Consider the Itô diffusion

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}(t)=\bm{F}(\bm{x}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x}(t))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{t},

(S1)

where
\bm{W}_{t}
is a standard
D
-dimensional Wiener process. We define the diffusion tensor

\bm{D}(\bm{x}):=\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x})^{T}.

(S2)

Because of the convention
\sqrt{2}\,\bm{\Sigma}
in (
S1
), the infinitesimal generator acting on smooth test functions
f
is

\mathcal{L}f=\bm{F}\cdot\nabla f+\bm{D}:\nabla\nabla f=F_{i}\,\partial_{i}f+D_{ij}\,\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f,

(S3)

where repeated indices are summed over. The corresponding Fokker–Planck equation is

\partial_{t}p=\mathcal{L}^{*}p=-\partial_{i}(F_{i}p)+\partial_{i}\partial_{j}(D_{ij}p).

(S4)

Equivalently, it can be written as a conservation law

\partial_{t}p=-\nabla\cdot\bm{J},

(S5)

with probability current

J_{i}=F_{i}p-\partial_{j}(D_{ij}p).

(S6)

Suppose now that
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
is an invariant density for (
S1
). Then

\mathcal{L}^{*}p_{\mathrm{ss}}=0,\qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad\partial_{i}J_{i}^{\mathrm{ss}}=0,

(S7)

where

J_{i}^{\mathrm{ss}}=F_{i}p_{\mathrm{ss}}-\partial_{j}(D_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}}).

(S8)

Thus the stationary probability current is divergence-free. Under the boundary and topological assumptions stated above, any such divergence-free current can be represented as the divergence of an antisymmetric tensor potential. More precisely, we assume that there exists a smooth matrix field
\bm{R}(\bm{x})
satisfying

R_{ij}(\bm{x})=-R_{ji}(\bm{x})

(S9)

such that

J_{i}^{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})=\partial_{j}\!\left(R_{ij}(\bm{x})p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\right).

(S10)

This is the antisymmetric-current representation of the stationary nonequilibrium flux. In Euclidean domains with sufficient decay it follows from the standard vector-potential representation of solenoidal vector fields. In domains with nontrivial topology, one must additionally exclude harmonic-current components or treat them separately.

Using (
S8
) and (
S10
), we obtain

F_{i}p_{\mathrm{ss}}-\partial_{j}(D_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}})=\partial_{j}(R_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}}).

(S11)

Therefore

F_{i}p_{\mathrm{ss}}=\partial_{j}(D_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}})+\partial_{j}(R_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}})=\partial_{j}\!\left[(D_{ij}+R_{ij})p_{\mathrm{ss}}\right].

(S12)

We now define the mobility matrix

M_{ij}(\bm{x}):=D_{ij}(\bm{x})+R_{ij}(\bm{x}),

(S13)

or, in matrix form,

\bm{M}(\bm{x})=\bm{D}(\bm{x})+\bm{R}(\bm{x}),\qquad\bm{D}(\bm{x})=\frac{\bm{M}(\bm{x})+\bm{M}(\bm{x})^{T}}{2},\qquad\bm{R}(\bm{x})=\frac{\bm{M}(\bm{x})-\bm{M}(\bm{x})^{T}}{2}.

(S14)

Since
\bm{D}=\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\Sigma}^{T}
, the symmetric part of
\bm{M}
is positive semidefinite.

Equation (
S12
) becomes

F_{i}p_{\mathrm{ss}}=\partial_{j}(M_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}}).

(S15)

Expanding the derivative gives

F_{i}p_{\mathrm{ss}}=(\partial_{j}M_{ij})p_{\mathrm{ss}}+M_{ij}\partial_{j}p_{\mathrm{ss}}.

(S16)

Since
p_{\mathrm{ss}}>0
, we divide by
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
and introduce the score

s_{j}(\bm{x}):=\partial_{j}\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})=\frac{\partial_{j}p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})}{p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})}.

(S17)

This yields

F_{i}(\bm{x})=M_{ij}(\bm{x})s_{j}(\bm{x})+\partial_{j}M_{ij}(\bm{x}).

(S18)

In vector notation,

\bm{F}(\bm{x})=\bm{M}(\bm{x})\bm{s}(\bm{x})+\nabla\cdot\bm{M}(\bm{x}),

(S19)

where

(\nabla\cdot\bm{M})_{i}:=\partial_{j}M_{ij}.

(S20)

Substituting (
S19
) into (
S1
), we obtain the score-based representation

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}(t)=\left[\bm{M}(\bm{x}(t))\bm{s}(\bm{x}(t))+\nabla\cdot\bm{M}(\bm{x}(t))\right]\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x}(t))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{t},

(S21)

with

\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x})^{T}=\frac{\bm{M}(\bm{x})+\bm{M}(\bm{x})^{T}}{2}.

(S22)

Conversely, suppose that a diffusion is written in the form
(
S21
), with
\bm{s}=\nabla\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}
and with

\bm{M}=\bm{D}+\bm{R}
, where
\bm{D}=\bm{D}^{T}\succeq 0
and

\bm{R}=-\bm{R}^{T}
. The stationary current associated with
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
is

\displaystyle J_{i}^{\mathrm{ss}}

\displaystyle=\left(M_{ij}s_{j}+\partial_{j}M_{ij}\right)p_{\mathrm{ss}}-\partial_{j}(D_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}})

\displaystyle=\left(D_{ij}s_{j}+R_{ij}s_{j}+\partial_{j}D_{ij}+\partial_{j}R_{ij}\right)p_{\mathrm{ss}}-\partial_{j}(D_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}})

\displaystyle=R_{ij}\partial_{j}p_{\mathrm{ss}}+(\partial_{j}R_{ij})p_{\mathrm{ss}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{j}(R_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}}).

(S23)

Taking the divergence,

\partial_{i}J_{i}^{\mathrm{ss}}=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}(R_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}}).

(S24)

Because
R_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}}
is antisymmetric in
(i,j)
while

\partial_{i}\partial_{j}
is symmetric in
(i,j)
, the right-hand side vanishes:

\partial_{i}\partial_{j}(R_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}})=0.

(S25)

Therefore

\mathcal{L}^{*}p_{\mathrm{ss}}=-\nabla\cdot\bm{J}^{\mathrm{ss}}=0,

(S26)

so
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
is invariant for the diffusion.

The decomposition has a direct physical interpretation. The symmetric part
\bm{D}
controls irreversible diffusion and dissipation, while the antisymmetric part
\bm{R}
generates stationary probability currents that preserve the invariant density. If
\bm{R}=0
, the stationary current vanishes and the dynamics satisfy detailed balance. If
\bm{R}\neq 0
, the same invariant density can coexist with nonzero stationary currents, allowing the model to represent nonequilibrium dynamics.

Appendix S2
Conditional-score identity for lagged correlation derivatives

In this appendix we derive the identity

\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)=-\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}(t)\mid\bm{x}(0))^{T}\,\bm{M}(\bm{x}(0))\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right\rangle,

(S27)

used in Section
2.2
. We assume throughout that the process is stationary with invariant density
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
, that
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
is strictly positive and smooth on the domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and that all observables and coefficients are sufficiently regular for the differentiations and integrations by parts below to be justified. Boundary terms are assumed to vanish, either because
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{D}
and the relevant quantities decay sufficiently fast, or because no-flux/periodic boundary conditions are imposed.

Consider the score-based diffusion

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}(t)=\left[\bm{M}(\bm{x}(t))\bm{s}(\bm{x}(t))+\nabla\cdot\bm{M}(\bm{x}(t))\right]\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x}(t))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{W}_{t},

(S28)

where

\bm{s}(\bm{x})=\nabla\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}),\qquad\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x})\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x})^{T}=\bm{D}(\bm{x})=\frac{\bm{M}(\bm{x})+\bm{M}(\bm{x})^{T}}{2}.

(S29)

The associated backward generator acts on smooth scalar functions
f
as

\mathcal{L}f=\left[M_{ij}s_{j}+\partial_{j}M_{ij}\right]\partial_{i}f+D_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f,

(S30)

where repeated indices are summed over. Equivalently, using

M_{ij}=D_{ij}+R_{ij},\qquad R_{ij}=-R_{ji},

(S31)

and the stationarity of
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
, the generator admits the divergence-form adjoint relation

\left\langle\mathcal{L}f\,h\right\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}}=-\left\langle\nabla f\,\bm{M}\,\nabla h^{T}\right\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}}+\text{terms antisymmetric under }f\leftrightarrow h,

(S32)

which we now derive in the form needed for correlation functions.

Let

\phi_{m}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}},\qquad\phi_{n}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}}

(S33)

be smooth vector-valued observables, and define

\bm{C}_{m,n}(t):=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\phi_{n}(\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right].

(S34)

Let
K_{t}
denote the Koopman semigroup of the diffusion,

(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}_{0}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\middle|\,\bm{x}(0)=\bm{x}_{0}\right].

(S35)

By stationarity,

\bm{C}_{m,n}(t)=\int_{\Omega}(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}_{0})\,\phi_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\,p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{0}.

(S36)

Differentiating with respect to
t
gives

\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}_{0})\,\phi_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\,p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{0},

(S37)

where
\mathcal{L}
acts componentwise on
K_{t}\phi_{m}
.

We now compute the
(a,b)
entry of (
S37
). Write

u(\bm{x})=(K_{t}\phi_{m})_{a}(\bm{x}),\qquad v(\bm{x})=[\phi_{n}(\bm{x})]_{b}.

Then

[\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)]_{ab}=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}u(\bm{x})\,v(\bm{x})\,p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(S38)

Using the score-based drift, this becomes

\displaystyle[\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)]_{ab}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left[\left(M_{ij}s_{j}+\partial_{j}M_{ij}\right)\partial_{i}u+D_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u\right]v\,p_{\mathrm{ss}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(S39)

Since
s_{j}p_{\mathrm{ss}}=\partial_{j}p_{\mathrm{ss}}
, the drift part can be written as

\left(M_{ij}s_{j}+\partial_{j}M_{ij}\right)p_{\mathrm{ss}}=\partial_{j}(M_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}}).

(S40)

Therefore

\displaystyle[\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)]_{ab}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{j}(M_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}})\,\partial_{i}u\,v\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+\int_{\Omega}D_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}}\,\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u\,v\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(S41)

Integrating the first term by parts in
x_{j}
gives

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{j}(M_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}})\,\partial_{i}u\,v\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=-\int_{\Omega}M_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}}\left[\partial_{j}\partial_{i}u\,v+\partial_{i}u\,\partial_{j}v\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(S42)

For the second term, use the symmetry of
\bm{D}
:

\int_{\Omega}D_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}}\,\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u\,v\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}D_{ij}p_{\mathrm{ss}}\,\partial_{j}\partial_{i}u\,v\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(S43)

Substituting
\bm{M}=\bm{D}+\bm{R}
into (
S42
), the terms involving
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u
cancel as follows:

-M_{ij}\partial_{j}\partial_{i}u+D_{ij}\partial_{j}\partial_{i}u=-R_{ij}\partial_{j}\partial_{i}u=0,

(S44)

because
R_{ij}
is antisymmetric while
\partial_{j}\partial_{i}u
is symmetric. Hence

[\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)]_{ab}=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{i}u(\bm{x})\,M_{ij}(\bm{x})\,\partial_{j}v(\bm{x})\,p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(S45)

Returning to vector-valued notation, with

\nabla(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times D},\qquad\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}\times D},

we obtain

\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)=-\left\langle\nabla(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}_{0})\,\bm{M}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\right\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}}.

(S46)

We next express
\nabla(K_{t}\phi_{m})
through the conditional score. Let

p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})

(S47)

be the transition density at lag
t
. Then

(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}_{0})=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{m}(\bm{x}_{t})\,p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{t}.

(S48)

Assuming that differentiation under the integral is justified,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{m}(\bm{x}_{t})\,\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{t}

(S49)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{m}(\bm{x}_{t})\,p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})\,\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{t}.

Define the conditional transition score by

\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0}):=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

(S50)

Then

\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}_{0})=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{m}(\bm{x}_{t})\,\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\,\middle|\,\bm{x}_{0}\right].

(S51)

Substituting (
S51
) into
(
S46
) gives

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\left[\int_{\Omega}\phi_{m}(\bm{x}_{t})\,\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\,p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{t}\right]\bm{M}(\bm{x}_{0})\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}_{0})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{0}.

(S52)

Recognizing the joint stationary law

p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}_{0})p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})

(S53)

of the pair
(\bm{x}(0),\bm{x}(t))
, we obtain

\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)=-\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}(t)\mid\bm{x}(0))^{T}\,\bm{M}(\bm{x}(0))\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right\rangle,

(S54)

which is (
S27
).

This identity has three important consequences. First, it expresses the action of the generator on finite-lag correlations without estimating instantaneous time derivatives from trajectory increments. Second, it depends on the transition density only through its score with respect to the initial condition, which can be estimated from lagged pairs by score-matching methods. Third, the identity is linear in the unknown mobility field
\bm{M}
, making it suitable for inverse problems in which
\bm{M}
is represented by a finite-dimensional basis or by a neural network.

Appendix S3
Score estimation by denoising score matching

This appendix describes how the stationary score and the conditional transition score used in the main text can be estimated from data by denoising score matching. The first subsection treats the stationary score

\bm{s}(\bm{x})=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}),

while the second subsection uses the same construction to estimate the conditional score

\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0}).

S3.1
Estimating the stationary score

Let
\bm{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{ss}}
denote a sample from the stationary distribution of the resolved process. The score

\bm{s}(\bm{x}):=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})

(S55)

is the central object needed to impose the observed invariant density in the score-based Langevin representation. Directly estimating (
S55
) from data is difficult because the density
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
is unknown, especially in high dimension. Denoising score matching avoids explicit density estimation by learning the score of a noise-regularized version of the data distribution.

For a noise level
\sigma>0
, define the Gaussian-corrupted variable

\widetilde{\bm{x}}=\bm{x}+\sigma\bm{\xi},\qquad\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{D}).

(S56)

The density of
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
is the Gaussian convolution

p_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,\varphi_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(S57)

where

\varphi_{\sigma}(\bm{z})=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-D/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\|\bm{z}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

(S58)

The corresponding smoothed score is

\bm{s}_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}):=\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{x}}}\log p_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}).

(S59)

The key identity behind denoising score matching is that this score can be written as a conditional expectation involving the clean sample
\bm{x}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{x}}}p_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\int p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{x}}}\varphi_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\int p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,\left(-\frac{\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\varphi_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(S60)

Dividing by
p_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})
gives

\bm{s}_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})=\mathbb{E}\left[-\frac{\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}}{\sigma^{2}}\,\middle|\,\widetilde{\bm{x}}\right].

(S61)

Thus the score of the smoothed density is the optimal least-squares predictor of the denoising vector

-\frac{\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}}{\sigma^{2}}.

(S62)

Let
\bm{s}_{\theta}(\widetilde{\bm{x}},\sigma)
be a parametric model for the smoothed score. For a fixed noise level
\sigma
, denoising score matching minimizes

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta;\sigma)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{ss}}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{D})}\left[\left\|\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x}+\sigma\bm{\xi},\sigma)+\frac{\sigma\bm{\xi}}{\sigma^{2}}\right\|^{2}\right].

(S63)

Equivalently, using
\widetilde{\bm{x}}=\bm{x}+\sigma\bm{\xi}
,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta;\sigma)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\bm{s}_{\theta}(\widetilde{\bm{x}},\sigma)+\frac{\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}}{\sigma^{2}}\right\|^{2}\right].

(S64)

The minimizer satisfies

\bm{s}_{\theta^{\ast}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}},\sigma)=\nabla_{\widetilde{\bm{x}}}\log p_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})\qquad p_{\sigma}\text{-a.e.}

(S65)

This follows directly from the projection property of conditional expectation: the least-squares optimal predictor of

-(\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x})/\sigma^{2}
given
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
is precisely the conditional expectation in (
S61
).

In practice, one usually trains over a distribution of noise levels. Let
\sigma\sim\rho(\sigma)
, with support contained in
[\sigma_{\min},\sigma_{\max}]
. The multi-noise denoising score matching objective is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma\sim\rho}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim p_{\mathrm{ss}}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{D})}\left[\omega(\sigma)\left\|\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x}+\sigma\bm{\xi},\sigma)+\frac{\bm{\xi}}{\sigma}\right\|^{2}\right],

(S66)

where
\omega(\sigma)>0
is a weighting function. For each fixed
\sigma
, the population minimizer is the smoothed score
\nabla\log p_{\sigma}
. The desired stationary score is then obtained in the small-noise limit:

\bm{s}(\bm{x})=\nabla\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})=\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\nabla\log p_{\sigma}(\bm{x}).

(S67)

With finite data one uses a small but nonzero
\sigma_{\min}
, which regularizes the score estimate and avoids fitting sampling noise.

Given stationary samples
\{\bm{x}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{N}
, the empirical version of (
S66
) is

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,\bm{\xi}}\left[\omega(\sigma)\left\|\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x}^{(k)}+\sigma\bm{\xi},\sigma)+\frac{\bm{\xi}}{\sigma}\right\|^{2}\right].

(S68)

After training, the stationary score used in the reduced model is approximated by

\widehat{\bm{s}}(\bm{x})\approx\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x},\sigma_{\min}),

(S69)

or by an average over a small range of low noise levels.

S3.2
Estimating the conditional transition score from joint scores

We now describe how the conditional transition score appearing in the
correlation identity is estimated in the manuscript. The required object is

\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0}).

(S70)

This score is a derivative with respect to the initial state, not the final
state. It measures the sensitivity of the finite-lag transition density to
perturbations of the initial condition
\bm{x}_{0}
.

The data provide stationary lagged pairs

(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})\sim p_{0,t}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})=p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}_{0})\,p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0}),\qquad t\in\mathcal{T}.

(S71)

For each lag
t
, we regard the observed pairs
(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})
as
samples from the joint density
p_{0,t}
. We then estimate the joint score

\bm{s}_{0,t}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t}):=\nabla_{(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})}\log p_{0,t}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t}).

(S72)

Writing this score in block form gives

\bm{s}_{0,t}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{s}_{0,t}^{(0)}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})\\
\bm{s}_{0,t}^{(t)}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})\end{pmatrix},

(S73)

where

\bm{s}_{0,t}^{(0)}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}\log p_{0,t}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t}),\qquad\bm{s}_{0,t}^{(t)}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p_{0,t}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t}).

(S74)

The conditional transition density satisfies

p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})=\frac{p_{0,t}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})}{p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}_{0})}.

(S75)

Taking the logarithm and differentiating with respect to
\bm{x}_{0}
gives

\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}\log p_{0,t}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})-\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}_{0}).

(S76)

Therefore,

\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{s}_{0,t}^{(0)}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})-\bm{s}(\bm{x}_{0}),

(S77)

where

\bm{s}(\bm{x}_{0}):=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}_{0})

(S78)

is the stationary score. Thus, the conditional transition score is obtained
from the
\bm{x}_{0}
-component of the joint score by subtracting the stationary
score.

In practice, the joint score is learned by denoising score matching on the
augmented variable

\bm{z}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{x}_{0}\\
\bm{x}_{t}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}.

(S79)

For a fixed lag
t
, define the corrupted pair

\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}=\bm{z}_{t}+\sigma\bm{\xi},\qquad\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{2D}).

(S80)

Equivalently,

\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0}=\bm{x}_{0}+\sigma\bm{\xi}_{0},\qquad\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t}+\sigma\bm{\xi}_{t},\qquad\bm{\xi}_{0},\bm{\xi}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{D}).

(S81)

Let
p_{\sigma,0,t}
denote the Gaussian-smoothed joint density of

(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})
. The corresponding smoothed joint score is

\bm{s}_{\sigma,0,t}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{t}):=\nabla_{(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{t})}\log p_{\sigma,0,t}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{t}).

(S82)

By the standard denoising identity,

\bm{s}_{\sigma,0,t}(\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[-\frac{\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}-\bm{z}_{t}}{\sigma^{2}}\,\middle|\,\widetilde{\bm{z}}_{t}\right].

(S83)

A neural score model

\bm{q}_{\psi}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{t},t,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}

is trained to approximate this smoothed joint score by minimizing

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{jDSM}}(\psi)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\rho_{\mathcal{T}}}\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t})}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma\sim\rho}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{2D})}\left[\omega(\sigma)\left\|\bm{q}_{\psi}\left(\bm{x}_{0}+\sigma\bm{\xi}_{0},\bm{x}_{t}+\sigma\bm{\xi}_{t},t,\sigma\right)+\frac{\bm{\xi}}{\sigma}\right\|^{2}\right],

(S84)

where
\bm{\xi}=(\bm{\xi}_{0},\bm{\xi}_{t})
and

\rho_{\mathcal{T}}
is the sampling distribution over the selected lag times.
For each fixed
(t,\sigma)
, the population minimizer satisfies

\bm{q}_{\psi^{\ast}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{t},t,\sigma)=\nabla_{(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{t})}\log p_{\sigma,0,t}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{t}).

(S85)

We write the learned joint score in block form as

\bm{q}_{\psi}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{t},t,\sigma)=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{q}_{\psi}^{(0)}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{t},t,\sigma)\\
\bm{q}_{\psi}^{(t)}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0},\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{t},t,\sigma)\end{pmatrix}.

(S86)

The component needed for the correlation identity is the derivative with
respect to the initial state. Combining the learned joint score with the
stationary score estimator from Section
S3.1
, we
therefore use

\widehat{\bm{s}}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})=\bm{q}_{\psi}^{(0)}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t},t,\sigma_{\min})-\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{0},\sigma_{\min}).

(S87)

In the limit of infinite data, infinite model capacity, and

\sigma_{\min}\to 0
, this estimator converges to

\widehat{\bm{s}}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})\longrightarrow\nabla_{\bm{x}_{0}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0}).

(S88)

Given a trajectory sampled at times
\{t_{k}\}
, empirical training pairs are
constructed by selecting

(\bm{x}_{0}^{(k)},\bm{x}_{t}^{(k)})=(\bm{x}(t_{k}),\bm{x}(t_{k}+t))

(S89)

for each lag
t\in\mathcal{T}
. If the data are unevenly sampled, one may
instead use all pairs whose time separation lies in a prescribed lag window.
The empirical joint denoising objective corresponding to
(
S84
) is

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{jDSM}}(\psi)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{(k,t)\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma,\bm{\xi}}\left[\omega(\sigma)\left\|\bm{q}_{\psi}\left(\bm{x}_{0}^{(k)}+\sigma\bm{\xi}_{0},\bm{x}_{t}^{(k)}+\sigma\bm{\xi}_{t},t,\sigma\right)+\frac{\bm{\xi}}{\sigma}\right\|^{2}\right],

(S90)

where
\mathcal{P}
denotes the collection of observed lagged pairs used for
training. The lag
t
can be supplied to the network as an additional input,
allowing a single model to learn joint scores over multiple time separations.

Appendix S4
Mean mobility decomposition and constant-closure limit

This appendix derives the identities used in Section
2.3
. We start from the correlation identity established in Appendix
S2
and then specialize it to the mean–fluctuation decomposition of the mobility.

Let
\phi_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}}
and
\phi_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}}
be smooth observables, and define

\bm{C}_{m,n}(t)=\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\phi_{n}(\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right\rangle.

(S91)

From Appendix
S2
, the correlation derivative can be written equivalently as

\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)=-\left\langle\nabla(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}_{0})\,\bm{M}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\right\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}},

(S92)

where
K_{t}
is the Koopman semigroup,

(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}_{0})=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\middle|\,\bm{x}(0)=\bm{x}_{0}\right].

(S93)

Componentwise, if
u=(K_{t}\phi_{m})_{a}
and
v=(\phi_{n})_{b}
, then

[\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)]_{ab}=-\int\partial_{i}u(\bm{x})\,M_{ij}(\bm{x})\,\partial_{j}v(\bm{x})\,p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(S94)

We decompose the mobility as

\bm{M}(\bm{x})=\bm{\Phi}+\delta\bm{M}(\bm{x}),\qquad\bm{\Phi}=\left\langle\bm{M}\right\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}},\qquad\left\langle\delta\bm{M}\right\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}}=\bm{0}.

(S95)

Substituting (
S95
) into (
S92
) gives

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\nabla(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}_{0})\,\bm{\Phi}\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\right\rangle

(S96)

\displaystyle-\left\langle\nabla(K_{t}\phi_{m})(\bm{x}_{0})\,\delta\bm{M}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\right\rangle.

We now rewrite each term as a correlation with a conjugate observable. For any matrix field
\bm{A}(\bm{x})
, define

\bm{\gamma}_{n}[\bm{A}](\bm{x}):=\frac{1}{p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})}\nabla\cdot\left[p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,\bm{A}(\bm{x})\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x})^{T}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}}.

(S97)

In components,

\bigl[\bm{\gamma}_{n}[\bm{A}](\bm{x})\bigr]_{b}=\frac{1}{p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})}\partial_{i}\left[p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,A_{ij}(\bm{x})\,\partial_{j}\phi_{n,b}(\bm{x})\right].

(S98)

Using integration by parts and assuming the boundary terms vanish,

\displaystyle-\int\partial_{i}u(\bm{x})\,A_{ij}(\bm{x})\,\partial_{j}v(\bm{x})\,p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\int u(\bm{x})\,\partial_{i}\left[p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,A_{ij}(\bm{x})\,\partial_{j}v(\bm{x})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\int u(\bm{x})\,\bigl[\bm{\gamma}_{n}[\bm{A}](\bm{x})\bigr]_{b}\,p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(S99)

Since
u(\bm{x}_{0})=(K_{t}\phi_{m})_{a}(\bm{x}_{0})
, the last expression can be written as a stationary lagged expectation:

\int(K_{t}\phi_{m})_{a}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\bigl[\bm{\gamma}_{n}[\bm{A}](\bm{x}_{0})\bigr]_{b}\,p_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{0}=\left\langle[\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))]_{a}\,\bigl[\bm{\gamma}_{n}[\bm{A}](\bm{x}(0))\bigr]_{b}\right\rangle.

(S100)

Applying this identity first with
\bm{A}=\bm{\Phi}
and then with
\bm{A}=\delta\bm{M}
gives

\dot{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(t)=\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\bm{\gamma}_{n}[\bm{\Phi}](\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right\rangle+\left\langle\phi_{m}(\bm{x}(t))\,\bm{\gamma}_{n}[\delta\bm{M}](\bm{x}(0))^{T}\right\rangle.

(S101)

This is the decomposition used in (
26
).

We next show that
\bm{\Phi}
is determined by the right derivative at the origin of the coordinate correlation. Let

\bm{\iota}(\bm{x})=\bm{x}

(S102)

be the identity observable. Then

\nabla\bm{\iota}(\bm{x})=\bm{I}.

(S103)

Taking
\phi_{m}=\phi_{n}=\bm{\iota}
in (
S92
) yields

\dot{\bm{C}}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}(t)=-\left\langle\nabla(K_{t}\bm{\iota})(\bm{x}_{0})\,\bm{M}(\bm{x}_{0})\right\rangle.

(S104)

At
t=0
,
K_{0}\bm{\iota}=\bm{\iota}
and therefore
\nabla(K_{0}\bm{\iota})=\bm{I}
. Hence

\dot{\bm{C}}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}(0^{+})=-\left\langle\bm{M}\right\rangle=-\bm{\Phi}.

(S105)

Equivalently,

\bm{\Phi}=-\dot{\bm{C}}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}(0^{+}).

(S106)

The right derivative is used because diffusion sample paths are not differentiable, while the semigroup correlation function has a well-defined right derivative under the generator assumptions used above.

Finally, we analyze the contribution of
\delta\bm{M}
to coordinate correlations. Define the finite-lag conditional mean

\bm{m}_{t}(\bm{x}_{0}):=(K_{t}\bm{\iota})(\bm{x}_{0})=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{x}(t)\,\middle|\,\bm{x}(0)=\bm{x}_{0}\right].

(S107)

Then (
S104
), together with the decomposition

\bm{M}=\bm{\Phi}+\delta\bm{M}
, gives

\dot{\bm{C}}_{\bm{x},\bm{x}}(t)=-\left\langle\bm{x}_{t}\,\bm{s}_{t|0}(\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{0})^{T}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}\bm{\Phi}-\left\langle\nabla\bm{m}_{t}(\bm{x}_{0})\,\delta\bm{M}(\bm{x}_{0})\right\rangle.

(S108)

The second term is precisely the state-dependent correction to the coordinate correlation dynamics.

Appendix S5
Technical details for the examples

S5.1
CIR square-root diffusion benchmark

We provide the analytic details for the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross benchmark used in Section
3.1
. Consider

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\kappa(\theta-X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\gamma X_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad X_{t}>0,

(S109)

with
\kappa,\theta,\gamma>0
. We introduce the dimensionless parameters

\nu:=\frac{\kappa\theta}{\gamma},\qquad\beta:=\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}.

(S110)

The stationary density is the Gamma distribution

p_{\mathrm{ss}}(x)=\frac{\beta^{\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}x^{\nu-1}e^{-\beta x},\qquad x>0.

(S111)

Equivalently,

s(x):=\partial_{x}\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}(x)=\frac{\nu-1}{x}-\beta=\frac{\kappa\theta-\gamma}{\gamma x}-\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}.

(S112)

The corresponding Fokker–Planck equation is

\partial_{t}p=-\partial_{x}\!\left[\kappa(\theta-x)p\right]+\partial_{x}^{2}\!\left[\gamma xp\right].

(S113)

At stationarity, imposing zero probability flux gives

\kappa(\theta-x)p_{\mathrm{ss}}(x)-\partial_{x}\!\left[\gamma xp_{\mathrm{ss}}(x)\right]=0.

(S114)

Dividing by
p_{\mathrm{ss}}
yields

\kappa(\theta-x)=\gamma x\,\partial_{x}\log p_{\mathrm{ss}}(x)+\gamma.

(S115)

Therefore the drift can be written as

\kappa(\theta-x)=M(x)s(x)+\partial_{x}M(x),\qquad M(x)=\gamma x.

(S116)

This gives the exact mobility decomposition

M(x)=\Phi+\delta M(x),\qquad\Phi:=\langle M\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}}=\gamma\langle X\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}}=\gamma\theta,\qquad\delta M(x)=\gamma(x-\theta).

(S117)

The exact transition density is known in terms of a modified Bessel function. Let

z:=e^{-\kappa t},\qquad c_{t}:=\frac{\kappa}{\gamma(1-z)}=\frac{\beta}{1-z},\qquad u:=c_{t}zx_{0},\qquad v:=c_{t}x.

(S118)

Then

p_{t}(x\mid x_{0})=c_{t}\exp[-(u+v)]\left(\frac{v}{u}\right)^{(\nu-1)/2}I_{\nu-1}\!\left(2\sqrt{uv}\right),\qquad x>0,

(S119)

where
I_{q}
denotes the modified Bessel function of the first kind. In the original variables this reads

\displaystyle p_{t}(x\mid x_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{\gamma(1-e^{-\kappa t})}\exp\!\left[-\frac{\kappa(e^{-\kappa t}x_{0}+x)}{\gamma(1-e^{-\kappa t})}\right]\left(\frac{x}{e^{-\kappa t}x_{0}}\right)^{\frac{\kappa\theta/\gamma-1}{2}}

(S120)

\displaystyle\times I_{\kappa\theta/\gamma-1}\!\left(\frac{2\kappa\sqrt{e^{-\kappa t}x_{0}x}}{\gamma(1-e^{-\kappa t})}\right).

The conditional transition score used in the inverse identity is

s_{t|0}(x\mid x_{0}):=\partial_{x_{0}}\log p_{t}(x\mid x_{0}).

(S121)

Starting from (
S119
),

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{0}}\log p_{t}(x\mid x_{0})

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x_{0}}u-\frac{\nu-1}{2x_{0}}+\frac{I_{\nu-1}^{\prime}(2\sqrt{uv})}{I_{\nu-1}(2\sqrt{uv})}\partial_{x_{0}}\!\left(2\sqrt{uv}\right).

(S122)

Since

\partial_{x_{0}}u=c_{t}z,\qquad\partial_{x_{0}}\!\left(2\sqrt{uv}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{uv}}{x_{0}},

(S123)

and

I_{q}^{\prime}(r)=I_{q+1}(r)+\frac{q}{r}I_{q}(r),

(S124)

the singular terms cancel and we obtain

s_{t|0}(x\mid x_{0})=c_{t}z\left[-1+\sqrt{\frac{x}{zx_{0}}}\,\frac{I_{\nu}\!\left(2c_{t}\sqrt{zx_{0}x}\right)}{I_{\nu-1}\!\left(2c_{t}\sqrt{zx_{0}x}\right)}\right].

(S125)

We now compute the lagged correlations. For
\phi_{\alpha}(x)=x^{\alpha}
, the conditional moment is

m_{\alpha}(t,x_{0}):=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{\alpha}\mid X_{0}=x_{0}]=c_{t}^{-\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\nu)}{\Gamma(\nu)}{}_{1}F_{1}\!\left(-\alpha;\nu;-c_{t}zx_{0}\right),

(S126)

where
{}_{1}F_{1}
is Kummer’s confluent hypergeometric function. Averaging against the stationary density gives

\displaystyle C_{\alpha,1}(t)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}}\left[X_{t}^{\alpha}X_{0}\right]

(S127)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}m_{\alpha}(t,x_{0})\,x_{0}\,p_{\mathrm{ss}}(x_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\beta^{-(\alpha+1)}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\nu)}{\Gamma(\nu)}\left(\nu+\alpha z\right).

Therefore

\dot{C}_{\alpha,1}(t)=-\kappa\alpha z\,\beta^{-(\alpha+1)}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\nu)}{\Gamma(\nu)}.

(S128)

Using
\nu=\kappa\theta/\gamma
,
\beta=\kappa/\gamma
, and
z=e^{-\kappa t}
, this is

\dot{C}_{\alpha,1}(t)=-\alpha\theta\gamma\left(\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}\right)^{1-\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\kappa\theta/\gamma)}{\Gamma(\kappa\theta/\gamma+1)}e^{-\kappa t}.

(S129)

We next evaluate the two sides of the inverse identity for the affine family

\delta M_{a}(x)=a(x-\theta).

(S130)

First,

\partial_{x_{0}}m_{\alpha}(t,x_{0})=\alpha z\,c_{t}^{1-\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\nu)}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}{}_{1}F_{1}\!\left(1-\alpha;\nu+1;-c_{t}zx_{0}\right).

(S131)

Since

\partial_{x_{0}}m_{\alpha}(t,x_{0})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[X_{t}^{\alpha}s_{t|0}(X_{t}\mid x_{0})\,\middle|\,X_{0}=x_{0}\right],

(S132)

the operator acting on
\delta M_{a}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{t,\alpha,1}[\delta M_{a}]

\displaystyle:=-\left\langle X_{t}^{\alpha}s_{t|0}(X_{t}\mid X_{0})\delta M_{a}(X_{0})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}

(S133)

\displaystyle=a\left\langle(X_{0}-\theta)\partial_{x_{0}}m_{\alpha}(t,X_{0})\right\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}}.

Using the Gamma average of
{}_{1}F_{1}
, this gives

\mathcal{A}_{t,\alpha,1}[\delta M_{a}]=-a\,K_{\alpha}(t),

(S134)

where

K_{\alpha}(t):=\alpha\theta z\beta^{1-\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\nu)}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\left[1-{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(1-\alpha,1;\nu+1;z\right)\right].

(S135)

Here
{}_{2}F_{1}
denotes the Gauss hypergeometric function.

The observed residual appearing in the split identity is

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,1,\mathrm{obs}}(t)=\dot{C}_{\alpha,1,\mathrm{obs}}(t)-\Phi\left\langle X_{t}^{\alpha}s(X_{0})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}

(S136)

By integration by parts with respect to the stationary density,

\left\langle X_{t}^{\alpha}s(X_{0})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}=-\left\langle\partial_{x_{0}}m_{\alpha}(t,X_{0})\right\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}}.

(S137)

Using (
S131
), one obtains

\left\langle X_{t}^{\alpha}s(X_{0})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}=-\alpha z\beta^{1-\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\nu)}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}{}_{2}F_{1}\!\left(1-\alpha,1;\nu+1;z\right).

(S138)

Since
\Phi=\theta\gamma
, combining
(
S129
)
and
(
S138
)
gives

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,1,\mathrm{obs}}(t)=-\gamma K_{\alpha}(t).

(S139)

Therefore the inverse equation

\mathcal{A}_{t,\alpha,1}[\delta M_{a}]=\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,1,\mathrm{obs}}(t)

(S140)

reduces to

aK_{\alpha}(t)=\gamma K_{\alpha}(t).

(S141)

For the coordinate observable,
\alpha=1
, one has

{}_{2}F_{1}(0,1;\nu+1;z)=1,\qquad K_{1}(t)=0.

(S142)

Thus the coordinate channel cannot identify the affine correction. Equivalently,

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\mid X_{0}=x_{0}]=\theta+z(x_{0}-\theta),

(S143)

so that

\displaystyle\left\langle X_{t}s_{t|0}(X_{t}\mid X_{0})\delta M_{a}(X_{0})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\partial_{x_{0}}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}\mid X_{0}]\,a(X_{0}-\theta)\right\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}}

(S144)

\displaystyle=az\left\langle X_{0}-\theta\right\rangle_{p_{\mathrm{ss}}}=0.

For any nonlinear power observable
\alpha\neq 1
, the factor
K_{\alpha}(t)
is not identically zero as a function of the lag. Indeed, its small-
z
expansion is

1-{}_{2}F_{1}(1-\alpha,1;\nu+1;z)=\frac{\alpha-1}{\nu+1}z+\mathcal{O}(z^{2}),\qquad z\to 0.

(S145)

Thus the affine coefficient is uniquely identified:

a=\gamma.

(S146)

Consequently,

\delta M(x)=\gamma(x-\theta),\qquad M(x)=\theta\gamma+\gamma(x-\theta)=\gamma x.

(S147)

This proves exact recovery of the CIR mobility from the correlation constraints once the observable library contains a nonlinear observable that removes the coordinate-channel nullspace.

S5.2
Technical details for the two-dimensional affine multiplicative-noise benchmark

This appendix gives the experiment-specific implementation details behind the
two-dimensional benchmark reported in
Section
3.2
. The reference SDE,
its coefficients, the diagnostic construction of

\bm{M}_{\mathrm{ref}}
, and the two forward-validation Langevin equations are
stated in the main text. Here we collect the remaining details needed to build
the empirical residual targets and to train the neural mobility correction.

The stationary and conditional scores are estimated by the denoising
score-matching constructions of Appendix
S3
. In
the present experiment we use perturbation level
\sigma=0.05
, a
stationary-score network with hidden widths
(128,64)
, and a lag-conditioned
joint-score network with hidden widths
(256,128)
.

S5.2.1
Empirical correlation residuals

Let
\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{K}\}\subset(0,\infty)
denote the discrete positive lag grid used throughout the conditional-score fit and the mobility fit. Correlations are evaluated on
\{0\}\cup\mathcal{T}
, with
\tau=0
entering only through the baseline estimate for
\bm{\Phi}
.

For observables
\phi_{m},\phi_{n}
, the empirical lagged cross-correlation is computed from all admissible lagged pairs:

\widehat{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(\tau)=\frac{1}{N_{\tau}}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{\tau}}\phi_{m}(\bm{x}^{(r)}_{\tau})\phi_{n}(\bm{x}^{(r)}_{0})^{T}.

(S148)

For each matrix entry of
\widehat{\bm{C}}_{m,n}(\tau)
, we fit an entrywise cubic interpolant in
\tau
on the grid
\{0\}\cup\mathcal{T}
. The derivative
\widehat{\dot{\bm{C}}}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(\tau)
is then the analytic derivative of this interpolant evaluated at the positive lags
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
, and the baseline mobility is estimated from the corresponding one-sided right derivative at the origin:

\bm{\Phi}=-\widehat{\dot{\bm{C}}}_{1,1,\mathrm{obs}}(0^{+}),\qquad\phi_{1}(\bm{x})=\bm{x}.

(S149)

For each observable pair, we compute

\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{n}(\bm{x})=\nabla\cdot\left(\bm{\Phi}\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x})^{T}\right)+\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x})\bm{\Phi}^{T}\widehat{\bm{s}}_{\psi}(\bm{x}),

(S150)

and the empirical baseline term

\widehat{\bm{G}}_{m,n}(\tau)=\frac{1}{N_{\tau}}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{\tau}}\phi_{m}(\bm{x}^{(r)}_{\tau})\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{n}(\bm{x}^{(r)}_{0})^{T}.

(S151)

The data-derived residual is then

\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(\tau)=\widehat{\dot{\bm{C}}}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(\tau)-\widehat{\bm{G}}_{m,n}(\tau)

(S152)

This is the quantity that the learned mobility correction must reproduce through the conditional-score operator.

The observable channels used in the experiment are

\phi_{n}\in\{x,y\},\qquad\phi_{m}\in\{x,y,x^{2},xy,y^{2},x^{3},x^{2}y,xy^{2},y^{3}\}.

(S153)

This gives
18
ordered channels. The coordinate observables determine the baseline response sector, while the quadratic and cubic observables probe state-dependent departures from the constant-mobility closure.

S5.2.2
Mobility parameterization and training objective

The learned mobility is written as

\bm{M}_{\theta}(\bm{x})=\bm{D}_{\theta}(\bm{x})+\bm{R}_{\theta}(\bm{x}),\qquad\delta\bm{M}_{\theta}(\bm{x})=\bm{M}_{\theta}(\bm{x})-\bm{\Phi}.

(S154)

In two dimensions, the efficient parameterization uses three scalar outputs for the symmetric diffusion tensor and one scalar output for the antisymmetric circulation. Specifically,

\bm{D}_{\theta}(\bm{x})=\bm{L}_{\theta}(\bm{x})\bm{L}_{\theta}(\bm{x})^{T}+\varepsilon\bm{I},\qquad\bm{L}_{\theta}(\bm{x})=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{11,\theta}(\bm{x})&0\\
\ell_{21,\theta}(\bm{x})&\ell_{22,\theta}(\bm{x})\end{pmatrix},\qquad\varepsilon>0,

(S155)

and

\bm{R}_{\theta}(\bm{x})=r_{\theta}(\bm{x})\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(S156)

Thus the network outputs only the four independent scalar fields

\ell_{11,\theta},\quad\ell_{21,\theta},\quad\ell_{22,\theta},\quad r_{\theta},

rather than a redundant unconstrained
2\times 2
matrix. This enforces positive semidefiniteness of the symmetric part by construction and imposes antisymmetry of the circulation exactly.

For a trial mobility, the predicted residual operator is evaluated by Monte
Carlo as

\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{m,n}^{\theta}(\tau)=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{r=1}^{B}\phi_{m}(\bm{x}^{(r)}_{\tau})\,\widehat{\bm{s}}_{\tau|0}(\bm{x}^{(r)}_{\tau}\mid\bm{x}^{(r)}_{0})^{T}\,\delta\bm{M}_{\theta}(\bm{x}^{(r)}_{0})\,\nabla\phi_{n}(\bm{x}^{(r)}_{0})^{T}.

(S157)

The mobility loss is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mob}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathcal{I}_{\phi}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}w_{\tau}\left\|\frac{\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{E}}}_{m,n,\mathrm{obs}}(\tau)-\widehat{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}_{m,n}^{\theta}(\tau)}{S_{m,n}}\right\|_{F}^{2}

(S158)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\mathrm{mean}}\left\|\frac{1}{B_{\mathrm{anc}}}\sum_{r=1}^{B_{\mathrm{anc}}}\delta\bm{M}_{\theta}(\bm{x}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{anc}})\right\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}\mathcal{R}(\theta).

Here
\mathcal{I}_{\phi}
is the set of the
18
fitted observable channels,
S_{m,n}
is the empirical Frobenius-norm scale of the target residual curve for channel
(m,n)
, and
w_{\tau}
is a prescribed positive lag weight that mildly emphasizes the shortest resolved lags. The mean penalty enforces the decomposition

\left\langle\delta\bm{M}_{\theta}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{obs}}\approx\bm{0}

without subtracting a mini-batch mean from
\bm{D}_{\theta}
, which would generally destroy the positive-semidefinite parameterization.

The mobility network has two hidden layers of width
(128,128)
. It is initialized at the constant baseline and trained for
250
Adam epochs with learning rate
3\times 10^{-4}
, weight decay
10^{-6}
,
32768
sampled lagged pairs per lag, mini-batches of four lag values, and
32768
anchor states for the mean-correction penalty.
