Factors associated with severe acute malnutrition among children aged 2 to 23 months presenting with diarrhoea in urban hospitals in Bangladesh
Authors: Sufia Islam, Shegufta Ahmed Chowdhury, Minhajul Islam Akash, Md Farhad Kabir, Tahmeed Ahmed, Christian Lehmann, Mohammod Jobayer Chisti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere acute malnutrition (SAM) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among children under five years, accounting for over one-third of global child deaths.AimsThis study explored clinical and sociodemographic factors asociated with SAM in children aged from 2 to 23 months with diarrhoea.MethodsThe “Antibiotics for Children with Diarrhoea (ABCD)” study was conducted from July 1, 2017, to July 10, 2019, with Bangladesh as one of the seven-country sites. The data were collected from the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b). This secondary analysis was conducted among children who were excluded from the 35,504 children initially screened in the ABCD trial. Children were excluded if they had dysentery, suspected cholera, or had SAM, had comorbidities or had been treated with antibiotics for the last 14 days. Age and sex matched controls were randomly selected from excluded children without SAM in a 1:3 ratio of SAM children. SAM cases were compared with non-SAM controls using conditional logistic regression to identify associated factors.ResultsOf 2,640 children, 660 were cases and 1,980 controls; 57% were male with a mean age of 10 months. Admission characteristics, including dehydration, fever, vomiting and hospital stays, were higher among SAM children (cases). In conditional logistic regression, dehydration was associated with higher odds of SAM (OR: 5.89, 95% CI: 3.48–9.97; p = 0.001). Cough (OR: 2.23 95% CI: 1.36–3.66) and not being breastfed (OR: 1.64 95% CI: 1.12–2.41) were also associated with increased odds (p < 0.05). Recent antibiotic use was shown to be a protective factor (OR: 0.77, 95% CI 0.63–0.95; p < 0.05). In addition, maternal underweight increased the risk, while higher levels of parental education were protective (p < 0.001).ConclusionThese findings underscore the importance of early detection of dehydration, breastfeeding and targeted interventions for families with lower educational levels to reduce the risk of SAM.

Introduction

The prevalence of SAM is highly concentrated in low and middle-income countries (LMICs), with high socioeconomic disparities, food insecurity, and limited access to health care [1]. According to the 2022 Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS), 24% of the children under five are stunted, with 6% classified as severely stunted. Additionally, 11% are wasted, and 2% are severely wasted. Regarding the weight of children under five, 22% are underweight, while 4% are classified as severely underweight [2].

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) still remains a significant public health issue globally, especially affecting children under five years of age. Annual estimation of child deaths due to undernutrition accounts for 2.7 million (45% of all child deaths). Globally, 150.2 million children under 5 years are affected by stunting. Severe wasting is also observed in 12.2 million out of 42.8 million stunted children. These children have weak immunity, developmental delays, and poor cognitive growth. Severely wasted children have a risk of increased mortality [3]. Bangladesh has been a high-priority country in studying the underlying factors and effects of severe acute malnutrition due to its large burden on public health. Factors such as maternal malnutrition, poor breastfeeding practices, frequent infections, and poor sanitation have all contributed to the high incidence of SAM in this region. A recent study conducted in Bangladesh identified a group of high-risk children suffering from diarrhoeal disease along with diverse nutritional statuses. Out of 1068 children with diarrhoea, severe acute malnutrition was identified in 177 children under 5 years old and severe stunting was identified in 239 children. The remaining 652 children did not suffer from malnutrition. A logistic regression analysis, after adjustment for age and sex, indicated a significant association between SAM and dehydration (aOR 2.42, 95% CI: 1.67–3.52; p = 0.001) [4].

Breastfeeding at an early age is crucial for the child’s health and survival. Recommendations from WHO and UNICEF are to start breastfeeding within one hour of birth. It is also recommended that children be exclusively breastfed for the first 6 months of their lives. Breastfeeding should be continued until age 2, and maybe beyond that [5]. Optimal breastfeeding is critical and can provide nutrition, specific immune support and promote a healthy gut microbiome, resulting in lower morbidity and mortality. Breastmilk contains antibodies which combat infections and the risk of chronic disease. It also provides sole nutrition for the first six months of a child’s life. Therefore, breastfeeding could save over 820 000 under 5 years old’s lives annually [6].

The effects of poverty, low parental education, and environmental adversity also elevate the risk of malnutrition [7, 8]. SAM presents profound metabolic adaptation, immune dysfunction, and organ-specific changes, which include severe muscle wasting, compromised gastrointestinal integrity, and increased vulnerability to infections, leading to complications like dehydration, pneumonia and sepsis that call for timely radical intervention [9, 10].

The approach to addressing SAM must be multifaceted, involving prevention, early detection, and treatment. Despite the development of new treatment protocols, reducing the incidence and mortality rates for SAM remains a formidable challenge, especially in resource-constrained settings [10].

The predictors of SAM in children with diarrhoea in Bangladesh have been discussed in various literature and have established a clear relationship between different factors and malnutrition in children; however, much of the existing data are quite old. To our knowledge, in recent years, no studies have been carried out to identify the socio-demographic and clinical factors associated with SAM in children with diarrhoea. It is evident that the death rates among malnourished children have reduced, mainly due to the high vaccination rates among children, improved economic conditions, which have a greater impact on the community. However, we do not know the recent changes in scenarios on the factors associated with SAM. Therefore, this study is important for identifying the updated clinical and sociodemographic factors associated with SAM in children with diarrhoea in the present context using a matched case–control design .It will provide valuable insights for addressing SAM in these children.

Methods

Study design and context of the parent trial

This study was a matched case-control analysis utilizing the data from the Bangladesh site of the “Antibiotics for Children with Diarrhoea (ABCD)” trial, which was conducted from July 1, 2017, to July 10, 2019. The original study “ABCD trial” was a multi-site randomized double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, where Bangladesh was one of the seven countries that participated in the trial. The other participating countries of this ABCD trial were India, Kenya, Malawi, Mali, Pakistan, and Tanzania. The study was designed to evaluate whether a short course of azithromycin reduces post-discharged mortality and improves growth outcomes among high-risk children with diarrhoeal illness. Both male and female children, aged 2–23 months hospitalized with moderate to severe dehydration caused by diarrhoeal disease (≥ 3 watery stools in the last 24 h) and/or moderate wasting, and/or severe stunting were the participants in this trial [11]. The exclusion criteria included dysentery, severe acute malnutrition, suspected infection of Vibrio cholerae, or signs of other infections requiring alternative antibiotic treatment, recent antibiotic use within 14 days prior to participation, or having an enrolled sibling; and known allergy to azithromycin or inability to complete follow-up [11, 12]. A total of 8,266 children, with 4,133 receiving either 10 mg/Kg/Day azithromycin or a placebo, were included in the ABCD trial across the 7 countries. They were followed up on for 180 days.

The study sites of Bangladesh were both Dhaka Hospital and the Mirpur Treatment Centre of International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease, Bangladesh (icddr,b), located in the capital city of Dhaka. Notably, the icddr,b Dhaka Hospital is recognized as the world’s largest facility specializing in the treatment of diarrheal diseases.

Study population and sampling procedure

The analytic population for this study comprised children aged 2–23 months presenting with diarrhoeal illness who were screened for participation in the ABCD trial at the Bangladesh sites but were not enrolled.

From this screened-but-excluded population, we identified cases and controls based on nutritional status. Cases were defined as children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM), while controls were children without SAM. The final analytic sample was derived by selecting all eligible SAM cases and matching them with non-SAM controls. SAM was defined according to WHO criteria as weight-for-length Z-score below −3 SD, or mid-upper arm circumference < 115 mm, or presence of bilateral pitting oedema [13]. Our secondary analysis was conducted as a case-control design among children aged 2–23 months presenting with diarrhoeal illness who were screened for the ABCD trial at the Bangladesh site but excluded due to several factors [11]. A total of 35,504 children were screened, of whom 2,387 were eligible; of these, 1,431 were enrolled, while 33,117 were excluded for various reasons. From this screened-but-excluded population, we identified 660 children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM) as cases and 1,980 non-SAM as controls using age and sex matching (1:3 ratio).

Control selection and matching procedure

Our analysis employed an individual matched case-control design with a 1:3 case-to-control ratio using the excluded children from the ABCD trial Bangladesh site database. Cases (with severe acute malnutrition (SAM) and controls (without SAM) were matched for age and sex.

For matching of controls, we conducted a random selection of children using the ‘FUZZY’ case-control matching method in SPSS, version 26 (IBM, SPSS Inc., New York, USA) to address the challenges of finding suitable controls for our cases (SAM children) based on variables such as gender and age. We specified a tolerance level of zero for sex and 0.2 for age, indicating that we permitted no deviations for sex but minor deviations for age to maximize the number of appropriate controls. We aimed for a 1:3 ratio in case-control matching, meaning that for one child with SAM, three children without SAM were categorized as controls to ensure an adequate sample size for statistical analyses. Our analysis utilized only the data collected during the initial hospitalization period (from admission to discharge/death), focusing on the clinical characteristics and associated in-hospital outcomes.

Variable of interest

The primary outcome variable was severe acute malnutrition (SAM), defined according to World Health Organization criteria. Explanatory variables were selected based on data availability and prior relevance and included child, clinical, and parental characteristics. Child characteristics comprised age, sex, and breastfeeding status. Clinical characteristics included the presence of cough, fever, dehydration status, and documented antibiotic use within the preceding 14 days. Parental characteristics included maternal nutritional status (underweight), maternal education, and paternal education.

The study children were clinically diagnosed by trained study physicians and research staff. The data were collected using paper-based Case Report Forms (CRFs), and subsequently, entered into the REDCap web-based data management system [14]. As the dehydration assessment was done on these patients, there is no possibility of missing data in this study.

Operational definitions

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM): Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) is a serious form of undernutrition in children, characterized by rapid weight loss or wasting. According to WHO criteria, SAM is diagnosed in children under five years if they have a weight-for-length Z-score less than −3, a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) below 11.5 cm, or bilateral pitting oedema. Children with SAM are at high risk of morbidity and mortality and require urgent nutritional and medical care to prevent life-threatening complications [1].

Breastfeeding Status:

The breastfeeding status of children was classified according to WHO guidelines adopted in Bangladesh. Exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) refers to infants receiving only breast milk for the first six months, with no other liquids or foods except oral rehydration solutions or prescribed vitamins/medicines. Partial or mixed breastfeeding includes infants receiving breast milk along with other liquids or solids, while no breastfeeding refers to infants not receiving any breast milk due to weaning, maternal illness, or other factors. Exclusive breastfeeding is promoted in Bangladesh to support optimal infant health and prevent malnutrition and dehydration [4, 5].

Dehydration: Dehydration was assessed according to WHO guidelines, using four clinical signs: general condition, eyes, drinking/breastfeeding ability, and skin pinch.

This classification follows WHO standards and is appropriate for the Bangladesh context, where diarrheal diseases and limited access to safe water increase the risk of dehydration, especially in malnourished children [12].

Fever, vomiting and rapid breathing

The signs for treating SAM children for fever are a temperature > 39 °C or 102.2ºF measured axillary, or hypothermia, which is a temperature < 35 °C or 95ºF measured axillary. Vomiting occurring ≥3 times per hour is defined as persistent vomiting. According to Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI), rapid breathing is defined for age as follows: <2 months children, ≥ 60 /min; 2–12 months children, ≥ 50 /min; 12–59 months, ≥ 40 /min [15–18].

In this study, maternal education is classified into three categories: below secondary, secondary and higher education [19].

Statistical analysis

The data were extracted from the ABCD trial database and analyzed using STATA (version 15.0; StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and SPSS for Windows (version 26; SPSS Incorporation, Chicago, IL, United States). Given the matched case–control design, analyses focused on assessing the association between selected exposure variables and severe acute malnutrition (SAM) status.

Descriptive analyses were conducted to compare the background and clinical characteristics of children with SAM and non-SAM (controls). The categorical data were thus tested for association with either the chi-square or Fisher exact test. t-test was performed to analyze the mean difference for the continuous, normally distributed data and a Mann-Whitney test was performed for the variables that were not normally distributed.

Conditional Logistic regression was performed to account for the matched case-control design and to estimate odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between SAM and exposure variables. The level of significance was considered 0.05. The numbers (with percentages) were presented for binary and categorical variables, and means (and standard deviations) or medians (with interquartile range) for continuous variables.

In the adjusted conditional logistic regression analysis, children who were not breastfed were associated with increased odds of SAM (aOR 1.64, 95% CI: 1.12–2.41; p < 0.05). Discussion

Children with SAM were less likely to be breastfed, had lower levels of parental education, maternal undernutrition and higher rates of dehydration, cough, fever, and vomiting than their counterparts. They also had longer hospital stays and a greater need for admission to the ICU. Recent antibiotic use was shown to be a protective factor for children with SAM.

Poor nutrient absorption in the intestines and subsequent undernutrition often result from acute infections, which can decrease appetite. Moreover, inflammatory responses during the infection cycle accelerate tissue breakdown, leading to faster nutrient depletion. All these are prevalent across different age groups, particularly among children and immunocompromised people [20, 21]. A recent study in Bangladesh revealed that among 250 children aged 6–59 months, 19 were suffering from acute malnutrition. Of these children, 5.3% were identified to have SAM [22]. Another study highlighted that 57.5% children were severely wasted in the coastal villages of Bangladesh [23].

Breastfeeding plays a vital role in preventing childhood undernutrition, including wasting and stunting. Inappropriate breastfeeding practices emerge as one of the significant risk factors among SAM children [24]. Our data suggest that Non-SAM children were exclusively breastfed, compared with SAM children. Complementary feeding in infants was associated with a higher risk of wasting when compared with exclusively breastfed children [25, 26]. Our research is also in agreement with previous findings that increasing breastfeeding practices can lower the risk of SAM in children.

Over 820,000 under-five children could be saved, and 20,000 breast cancer cases could be prevented through recommended breastfeeding. Achieving at least 50% exclusive breastfeeding rates for infants aged 2 to 6 months is associated with both economic reward through societal benefits and enhanced child survival by the year 2025. Providing adequate maternity benefits and extending leave after childbirth, as well as supporting breastfeeding at the workplace, will enhance breastfeeding. Creating a supportive environment through all these measures ensures safety and minimize the risks of vulnerability in children [6].

A subgroup analysis of a placebo-controlled, randomized trial was conducted with azithromycin, in conjunction with the WHO standard of care for the treatment of acute watery diarrhoea. The authors found that azithromycin was associated with a shorter duration of diarrhoea, decreased mortality rates and hospitalizations [27]. Our current study also demonstrated that recent documented use of antibiotics has a modest protective association, highlighting the importance of understanding its effect on children with SAM and diarrhoea.

Maternal malnutrition is a recognized public health issue, particularly in low and middle-income countries. This has a negative impact on children’s health and well-being. In our current study, we found that maternal undernutrition significantly associated with SAM in children under five years old with diarrhoea. Our findings align with a recent study examining children under 2 years old with diarrhoea, which also identified a strong association of maternal undernutrition and child undernutrition [28].

Parental education levels also engage in the prevention and management of SAM. The risk for SAM is associated with lower parental education levels, p < 0.001, which would indicate that targeted educational interventions at the level of parents can constitute an important approach toward the reduction of the incidence of SAM [10]. Improved education among parents will lead to increased knowledge of nutrition, disease prevention, and caregiving practices [29].

Our study also corroborates the findings of a recent study by David and his co-workers, showing that cough and dehydration are both important risk factors for developing SAM [1]. Both conditions have been equally associated with other infectious diseases and complications that aggravate a state of malnutrition [8]. A high odds ratio for dehydration calls for effective rehydration management in SAM cases [9].

It has been indicated from a previous study that the odds ratio of post-discharge hospitalization in SAM children is 1.50, showing the severity of their state and the follow-up required even after treatment [10], which is in agreement with our present study. That would mean that the complications, and thus the follow-up for a longer period, to ensure recovery and prevention of relapse, are higher for SAM children [30].

Conclusion

SAM presents some associated factors as evidenced by a lower discharge rate, longer hospital stays, a higher risk of complications and a higher death rate as compared to Non-SAM. A multi-approach intervention, including management of dehydration, promotion of breastfeeding and targeted interventions for the parental groups with lower educational levels, maternal undernutrition may reduce the incidence of SAM and save the lives of malnourished children with diarrhoeal disease.
