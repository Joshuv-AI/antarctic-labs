Evidence for a trade‐off between growth rate and xylem embolism resistance in 22 Eucalyptus species
Authors: Tiantian Pan, Vinod Jacob, Travis G. Britton, Julian Schrader, Jennifer M. R. Peters, Jinlong Li, Chongzhe Zhang, Jiahao Wen, Brendan Choat, Ian J. Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Within the growth–survival trade‐off framework, embolism resistance is considered a contributor to survival, yet whether greater embolism resistance inherently limits growth remains unclear.We investigated this relationship among 22 Eucalyptus species spanning wide precipitation and temperature gradients, grown under controlled conditions for 6–10 months. We quantified embolism resistance (drought‐induced stem P50), growth, and wood anatomy.Our experimental results reveal a clear trade‐off between embolism resistance and growth rate, consistently supported by both cross‐species and phylogenetic (correlated‐divergence) analyses. Faster growing species exhibited less negative P50 and higher vessel lumen fraction, driven by wider vessels, enhancing hydraulic conductivity. Conversely, slower growing species had more negative P50, characterised by thicker vessel walls and a greater density of narrower vessels, reducing hydraulic conductivity. P50 and growth rate were significantly related to climate‐of‐origin, with species from drier or colder regions exhibiting greater embolism resistance and slower growth. Furthermore, we conducted a global vote‐counting review of 34 studies, showing weak but context‐dependent support for the growth rate–P50 trade‐off, with no evidence of concurrent faster growth and greater embolism resistance.These findings advance understanding of growth rate–embolism resistance trade‐offs, reveal climatic adaptation mechanisms in woody species, and inform predictions of plant persistence under climate change.

Introduction

Plant growth depends on continuous water transport through xylem conduits (vessels and/or tracheids), yet xylem embolism can severely disrupt this transport and, in extreme cases, lead to tissue desiccation and potentially plant mortality (McDowell et al., 2008; Choat et al., 2018; Losso et al., 2022). Under drought, rising tension in the xylem can trigger the formation and spread of gas emboli in the network of xylem conduits (Tyree & Sperry, 1989). Under extreme cold conditions, embolism may arise through freeze–thaw cycles, in which gas bubbles formed during freezing expand upon thawing (Davis et al., 1999; Hacke & Sperry, 2001), or through freezing‐induced water stress (‘frost drought’), in which freezing of soils, roots, or stems restricts water supply while transpiration continues, driving xylem water potentials to more negative values (Ball et al., 2006; Charra‐Vaskou et al., 2016; Mayr et al., 2020). To withstand these hydraulic risks, plants have evolved a diverse array of xylem anatomical features that enhance embolism resistance. Species with more resistant xylem tend to have thicker pit membranes between adjacent vessels, thicker vessel walls (Li et al., 2016; Thonglim et al., 2021), as well as narrower vessels and denser wood (Hacke et al., 2001, 2022; Jacobsen & Pratt, 2023). Furthermore, current understanding of embolism resistance highlights the key roles of pit membrane thickness in drought resistance (Kaack et al., 2021; Lens et al., 2022) and vessel size in freeze–thaw vulnerability (Davis et al., 1999; Choat et al., 2011).

Within the growth–survival trade‐off framework (Wright et al., 2010), increasing embolism resistance has been proposed as a survival‐related strategy, alongside enhanced defence against biotic attack and greater allocation to carbon and nutrient storage (Coley et al., 1985; Chapin III et al., 1990; Powers et al., 2020). While the growth costs associated with defence and storage are well‐established (Coley et al., 1985; Chapin III et al., 1990), whether variation in embolism resistance is consistently associated with reduced growth remains unclear. Field‐based studies across temperate and tropical forests have reported negative relationships between embolism resistance and growth rate both across (Eller et al., 2018; Smith‐Martin et al., 2022) and within species (Sterck et al., 2012), although other field studies reported no or mixed trends (Schuldt et al., 2016; Rosas et al., 2021; Aritsara et al., 2023). Similarly, controlled‐condition experiments on seedlings and saplings have identified negative relationships between embolism resistance and growth rate, both across (Li et al., 2018) and within species (Cochard et al., 2007; Ducrey et al., 2008), while other studies reported no or mixed relationships (Fichot et al., 2010; Guet et al., 2015; Ziegler et al., 2024). These differences in findings may arise from experimental context; for example, controlled‐condition studies minimise environmental variation and may more directly capture inherent physiological relationships, whereas field‐based studies operate under heterogeneous environmental conditions that may either reinforce or obscure evolutionary trait patterns. Likewise, within‐species and multispecies approaches may yield different insights: Within‐species studies reduce phylogenetic and other sources of confounding but often encompass limited trait variation, whereas multispecies studies capture broader trait variation but may be influenced by phylogenetic relatedness and life‐history differences. Together, these contrasting findings indicate that the relationship between embolism resistance and growth rate remains unresolved, and it is unclear whether the trade‐off between growth rate and embolism resistance represents a general pattern across vascular plants or is context‐dependent.

In principle, rapid plant growth depends on a high capacity for water transport (hydraulic conductivity, K s; Schumann et al., 2019; Aritsara et al., 2023), supporting sustained or relatively high photosynthetic carbon assimilation (Brodribb & Feild, 2000; Santiago et al., 2004; Reich, 2014). Accordingly, variation in growth rate is closely linked to anatomical traits that determine K s. Following the Hagen–Poiseuille law, K s scales with the 4th power of vessel diameter (Sperry et al., 2006). Increasing the number of vessels per cross‐sectional area (higher ‘vessel density’) also enhances K s, but only linearly; consequently, K s is more strongly influenced by variation in vessel diameter than by vessel density (Pfautsch et al., 2016). Vessel diameter and density are often negatively correlated (Sperry et al., 2008), and their product (‘vessel lumen fraction’) can also be used to quantify hydraulic conductivity (Gleason et al., 2012). Wood density is another factor that influences growth rate: lower wood density minimises construction costs per unit volume, thereby supporting faster growth (Chave et al., 2009; Poorter et al., 2010).

If anatomical traits that promote higher growth rate (e.g. wider vessels and lower wood density) inherently conflict with those that enhance embolism resistance (e.g. narrower vessels and higher wood density), a growth rate–embolism resistance trade‐off should emerge through anatomical coupling. However, relatively few studies have explicitly examined how anatomical traits mediate the negative growth rate–embolism resistance relationship. Two multispecies studies have linked wood density to this negative relationship but did not consider other anatomical traits (Eller et al., 2018; Li et al., 2018). Several within‐species studies incorporated anatomical traits but yielded inconsistent results: one identified pith area as important (Johnson & Brodribb, 2023), while others associated vessel/tracheid size or pit membrane thickness with this negative relationship (Sterck et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2025). Thus, the anatomical basis underlying the negative growth rate–embolism resistance relationship remains poorly understood, leaving it uncertain whether this negative relationship constitutes a true ‘trade‐off’ in the mechanistic sense, whereby gains in one function inevitably incur costs in another, rather than a spurious correlation. Resolving this uncertainty requires explicitly evaluating how multiple anatomical traits jointly link growth rate and embolism resistance.

Embolism resistance and associated anatomical traits tend to vary with precipitation. Species in drier sites typically show greater embolism resistance (Choat et al., 2012; Peters et al., 2021), likely reflecting adaptation to water deficit. This pattern is supported by several multispecies common garden studies (Bourne et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Oyanoghafo et al., 2021; Guan et al., 2023). A field study (Pfautsch et al., 2016) and three common environment experiments on Eucalyptus (Bourne et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Pan et al., 2025) reported that species from drier climates generally possess narrower vessels, thicker vessel walls, and denser wood. By contrast, species from wetter climates tend to have higher K s, wider vessels, greater vessel lumen fractions, and lower density wood (Gleason et al., 2012; Pfautsch et al., 2016). But do species from wetter climates inherently also grow faster? Field studies have reported both a higher growth rate in species from wetter sites (Morales et al., 2004; Sarris et al., 2007) and no relationship between growth rate and precipitation (Schuldt et al., 2016; Lazaro‐Lobo et al., 2022). Common garden studies showed similar inconsistency, with some reporting positive relationships between climate‐of‐origin precipitation and growth rate (Bourne et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Torres‐Ruiz et al., 2019) and others finding no relationship (Fletcher et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024).

In addition to precipitation, temperature also plays a significant role in shaping plant traits and ecological strategies (Moles et al., 2014). However, growth rate and hydraulic relationships with site temperature remain less well‐understood than those with precipitation. Field studies reported contrasting relationships between growth rate and temperature, from positive (Lyu et al., 2017) to absent or negative relationships (Lazaro‐Lobo et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2022). Within‐species common garden studies have shown that climate‐of‐origin temperature positively relates with growth rate in European species (Torres‐Ruiz et al., 2019; Kurjak et al., 2024) but not in Arabidopsis thaliana (Fletcher et al., 2022). Field investigations (Gleason et al., 2012; Peters et al., 2021) and large angiosperm datasets (Zhang et al., 2013; Morris et al., 2018; Olson et al., 2020) have reported species in warmer regions exhibit higher K s, wider vessels and greater vessel lumen fractions. However, common garden studies found no or opposite relationships between climate‐of‐origin temperature and K s, vessel diameter or vessel lumen fraction (Hietz et al., 2022; Guan et al., 2023; Kurjak et al., 2024).

Temperature‐related trends in embolism resistance are mixed. A global dataset of woody species showed that species from colder sites tend to exhibit greater embolism resistance (Huang et al., 2024). However, two field studies reported no relationship between embolism resistance and site temperature (Larter et al., 2017; Peters et al., 2021), whereas another reported greater embolism resistance in species from warmer regions (Trueba et al., 2017). Similarly, common garden studies observed weak or inconsistent relationships between climate‐of‐origin temperature and embolism resistance, both across species (Li et al., 2018; Oyanoghafo et al., 2021; Guan et al., 2023) and within species (Sebastian‐Azcona et al., 2018; Torres‐Ruiz et al., 2019; Kurjak et al., 2024). Limited evidence suggests that species in colder regions tend to have narrower vessels and thicker vessel walls (Davis et al., 1999; Choat et al., 2011; Echeverría et al., 2022), patterns further supported by common environment experiments (Jankowski et al., 2019; Pan et al., 2025). Temperature‐related trends in wood density are also inconsistent. Regional datasets reported negative relationships between wood density and temperature in angiosperms (Zhang et al., 2013; Clough et al., 2017) but a positive relationship in gymnosperms (Clough et al., 2017), whereas a field study found no clear trend (Peters et al., 2021). Similarly, common garden studies suggest weak associations between wood density and climate‐of‐origin temperature across (Li et al., 2018; Guan et al., 2023) and within species (Sebastian‐Azcona et al., 2018; Hietz et al., 2022).

Eucalyptus, an angiosperm genus with more than 700 species, is a valuable model for studying climate adaptation in woody plants. Eucalypts are naturally distributed across the Australian continent, spanning ecosystems from arid woodlands to cool temperate rainforests and tropical savannas (Jordan et al., 2023). Here, we test the relationship between growth rate and embolism resistance using 22 Eucalyptus species from diverse climate‐of‐origins grown under controlled conditions. Focussing on species within a single genus reduces phylogenetic and structural differences among distantly related taxa, while common conditions minimise environmental variation and reveal inherent relationships. We measured a variety of growth traits, drought‐induced embolism resistance (stem P50), and wood anatomical traits. Specifically, we investigated two questions: (1) Is there a negative relationship between growth rate and embolism resistance? If so, what wood anatomical traits contribute to the underlying mechanism of this relationship? (2) How do growth rate, embolism resistance, and wood anatomical traits correlate with the climate‐of‐origin among this set of species? Importantly, beyond cross‐species comparisons, we integrated phylogenetic (correlated‐divergence) analyses to test whether coordinated shifts in growth rate, embolism resistance, and wood anatomy have accompanied evolutionary divergence within Eucalyptus. This integrative framework clarifies the mechanistic and evolutionary basis of the growth rate–embolism resistance relationship and its climatic structuring. Furthermore, we placed our results into the context of the existing literature by undertaking a systematic vote‐counting review, an evidence‐synthesis approach that tallied the number of studies reporting effects in different directions or significance categories, rather than estimating a pooled effect size. Using this review, we assessed whether inconsistency among studies was associated with study type (controlled conditions vs field‐based), comparison level (between‐species vs within‐species), clade, woodiness, phenology, growth form, age class, or sample size, thereby providing insight into how often the growth rate–embolism resistance trade‐off is detectable.

Materials and Methods

Species selection

We sought to identify a set of Eucalyptus species that represented diverse climates, chosen as phylogenetically independent contrasts (PICs). Each PIC consisted of two closely related species that differed markedly in either the mean annual temperatures (MAT) or mean annual precipitation (MAP) across their range, but never both. Starting with a Eucalyptus phylogeny encompassing 711 species (Thornhill et al., 2019), and a dataset expressing the average climate experienced across the range of each of these species (Gallagher et al., 2019), we identified six precipitation PICs and five temperature PICs, giving a total of 22 species (Supporting Information Table S1; phylogeny presented as Fig. S1). Precipitation contrasts represented at least a 140 mm per year or 20% increase from the drier to the wetter species (on average 102.4%), at the same time keeping MAT variation as low as practically possible (average difference across all contrasts = 0.1°C). Temperature contrasts represented at least a 2.5°C increase in MAT from the colder to the warmer species, at the same time keeping MAP variation as low as practically possible (average difference across all contrasts = 11.5%). Across the 22 species, MAT ranged from 10.5 to 20°C, MAP from 309 to 1501 mm (Table 1).

Plant material

We sourced all seeds from Australian native plant suppliers (Table S2) and sowed them in seedling trays containing a seed‐raising mix during early‐spring 2023 (austral spring). However, germination was only successful for 16 species, which formed the first group. For the remaining six species that failed to germinate, seeds were resown in mid‐summer 2024 (austral summer), forming the second group. Seedlings were initially grown in a small polytunnel at Western Sydney University (site MAP: 927 mm; site MAT: 17.3°C; 33°33′ S, 150°44′ E, Richmond, Australia, climate data from CHELSA; Karger et al., 2017). After c. 3 months of growth, they were relocated to a larger polytunnel at the same site and transferred into 25‐l bags containing 20 kg of a standard, sand‐rich potting mix suitable for Australian native plants (‘Native mix’ supplied by Turtle Nursery, Rouse Hill, Australia). To help account for potential microclimatic differences within the polytunnel, a randomised block design was implemented. Plants were assigned to spatial blocks with random positioning within each block, and their locations were rotated periodically to further minimise positional effects. We isolated the bags from the ground by placing them on pallets, preventing roots from spreading beyond their pots. Three days post transplant, all plants were provided with a slow‐release, water‐soluble fertiliser designed to supply nutrients for up to 6 months. Soil water was maintained at 100% field capacity throughout the experiment using drip irrigation. The two groups of seedlings experienced distinct seasonal growing conditions: the first group developed under austral summer conditions (higher temperatures and longer day lengths), while the second group experienced the austral winter growing season (lower temperatures and shorter day lengths). To account for seasonal differences, growing degree days were approximately standardised across groups: the summer‐grown group was harvested after 6 months growth, and the winter‐grown group was harvested after 10 months. At the end of their respective growth periods, we successfully grew 7–10 individuals per species, except for Eucalyptus regnans F.Muell. (n = 6), Eucalyptus stricta Sieber ex Spreng. (n = 5), Eucalyptus dawsonii R.T.Baker (n = 4), and Eucalyptus grandis W.Hill (n = 3).

Growth rate measurements

Plant height (H, cm), basal stem diameter (SD, mm), and leaf area (LA, cm2) were recorded periodically during growth periods (January–April 2024 for summer group, April–October 2024 for winter group). We measured basal SD 2 cm above the soil surface and above any protruding lignotubers. Total LA was estimated for each plant by multiplying the average area of a typical leaf by the total number of leaves. Half of the individuals from each group were harvested simultaneously (at 6 months' age for the summer group and 10 months' age for the winter group). We carefully extracted entire plants from the bags, including their root systems, placed them in garbage bags with damp paper towels, and transported them to a cold storage room in the laboratory. From each plant, we collected five healthy leaves for LA analysis and then dried them at 70°C for 48 h before recording their dry mass. Specific LA (SLA, cm2 g−1) was determined as the ratio of fresh scanned LA to dry leaf mass. We also collected a twig segment from each plant, which was positioned c. 20 cm from the branch tip, ensuring it had intact, healthy leaves. A 1‐ to 2‐cm section of the twig was excised and stored in 70% ethanol for subsequent wood anatomical analysis. Additionally, basal stem samples from the same individuals were collected to determine wood density. The bark was stripped away, and the exposed xylem was rehydrated overnight before measurements. We determined the volume of each sample using the water displacement method (Ilic et al., 2000), and then dried them at 70°C for 48 h to obtain the dry mass. We calculated wood density (g cm−3) as the ratio of wood dry mass to wood fresh volume. Further processing involved separating roots, branches, and leaves, which were dried at 70°C for 72 h before their dry masses were recorded. Instead of directly counting leaves (which proved too difficult for larger plants), we estimated the final total LA using the formula SLA × total leaf dry mass. Lastly, total biomass was determined by summing the dry weights of roots, branches, and leaves.

We made growth measurements at several time points for each group. As would be expected (Hunt, 1978), seedling growth per height, diameter or LA followed an approximately exponential trajectory over time, meaning that absolute growth rates are not a fair means of comparison among species, as they change markedly over time. Furthermore, growth differences between species were not always consistent across time, for example Eucalyptus viridis R.T.Baker grew faster than Eucalyptus paniculata Sm. early in the growing season, but this pattern reversed later (Fig. S2), likely due to developmental differences. Consequently, we settled on describing growth ‘rate’ in two broad ways: first, in terms of absolute plant size at the end of the experiment (since seedlings within each group were sown and harvested on the same dates, the final measurements of height, diameter, total LA, and total biomass indicated absolute growth within each group). Second, we calculated relative growth rates (RGR) (per height, diameter, or LA), these being stable, by definition, for plants undergoing exponential growth. For example, mass‐based RGR (RGRM) is the rate of change in plant dry mass per unit time and per unit dry mass at the beginning of the time interval. Graphically, it is the slope of the line that connects the loge‐transformed biomass (M, g) of plants at different measurement times (Hunt, 1978) and hence is most easily calculated as: (Eqn 1) RGRM=logeM2−logeM1t2−t1 where M 1 and M 2 refer to plant mass measured at different times, and t 1 and t 2 refer to different measuring times. Because plant mass was measured only at the final harvest, traditional mass‐based RGR could not be calculated. Instead, mass in Eqn 1 was replaced with other metrics of plant size to obtain height relative growth rate (RGRH, mm mm−1 d−1), diameter relative growth rate (RGRD, mm mm−1 d−1), and leaf area relative growth rate (RGRLA, cm2 cm−2 d−1).

Stem vulnerability curves

Stem hydraulic vulnerability (drought‐induced stem P50) was determined for each species using the optical vulnerability (OV) technique as described in Brodribb et al. (2017) and Gauthey et al. (2020). Measurements were conducted from March to May 2024 (16 species during the summer period) and in October 2024 (six species during the winter period). Whole plants, including roots and soil, were carefully removed from plant bags and sealed in black garbage bags with moist paper towels for transport. We stored samples in a cold room overnight to equilibrate water potential (c. 0 MPa). Samples were then placed on laboratory benches for natural dehydration. For each plant, we selected a suitable branch, typically from the mid‐canopy position; for a few smaller individuals, in which branches were insufficient, we used the main stem instead. We carefully removed a small section of bark to expose the xylem, which we then secured in a ‘Cavicam’ clamp. Cavicams consisted of a clamp equipped with an 8‐megapixel camera, 20× lens, and a Raspberry Pi computer (http://www.raspberrypi.org), capturing images under c. 200 μmol m−2 s−1 LED light via refracted surface light (details: https://github.com/OpenSourceOV). Images were taken every 10 min until full dehydration (when embolism events ceased for > 12 h).

We recorded stem water potentials using two methods. For E. paniculata, Eucalyptus porosa F.Muell. ex Miq., Eucalyptus largiflorens F.Muell., and Eucalyptus moluccana Roxb., measurements were taken with a stem psychrometer (PSY, ICT‐International, Australia). We selected a suitable branch or stem (typically a basal branch or the main stem in smaller individuals) and removed a small section of bark to expose the xylem. Silicon grease was applied to the surface of the psychrometer before securing it to the branches. To prevent leaks, the psychrometer and branches were wrapped together with wax tape (Parafilm M®). The device was programmed to record water potential every 10 min, continuing until the plants had fully dehydrated. Since the stems or branches of many species were too small to fit into the psychrometer chamber, we measured plant water potential for the other 18 species with a Scholander pressure chamber (PMS Instruments, Albany, NY, USA). Readings were taken every 4–6 h until the plants were fully dehydrated.

We analysed xylem embolism images using Fiji (ImageJ; Schindelin et al., 2012), following the guidelines provided on https://github.com/OpenSourceOV. We calculated pixel differences between consecutive images in each sequence to quantify changes in light reflectance, thereby highlighting embolism events. The threshold and noise tools in Fiji were used to isolate embolism events by thresholding the images and removing nonembolism‐related changes, resulting in images that displayed only pixels corresponding to embolism. To calculate the percentage of embolism, we analysed the data using the following formula: (Eqn 2) %Embolism=AembAmax×100 where A emb refers to the cumulative embolised xylem area at a specific time; A max refers to the total embolised area recorded after full sample dehydration. By combining water potential measurements with percent area embolised, optical vulnerability curves for each species were generated (Fig. S3). A total of 63 curves were then fitted using the ‘fitplc’ R package (Duursma & Choat, 2017), from which drought‐induced P50 (the xylem water potential at which 50% of cumulative embolism area occurred; − MPa) was obtained for each sample.

However, in Eucalyptus, wood anatomy is characterised by solitary vessels encircled by vasicentric tracheids (VTs) and paratracheal axial parenchyma. VTs may continue to support low levels of water transport after vessel embolism (Pratt et al., 2023), but embolism in VTs may be difficult to detect using the OV technique, meaning that their contribution to residual conductivity may not be fully captured and may introduce some uncertainty in P50 estimates. Given their small diameters and low fractional area in Eucalyptus (c. 3–9%; Ziemińska et al., 2013; Barotto et al., 2016), their overall contribution to hydraulic conductivity and thus their influence on P50 estimates is likely to be limited. In addition, some pixel changes may also occur in non‐conductive cells (e.g. fibres), which may contribute to slight overestimation of embolism events (Venturas et al., 2019).

Wood anatomical analyses

Anatomical traits were examined using wood transverse sections of branchlets. Thin sections, ranging from 10 to 30 μm in thickness, were prepared with a sledge microtome (Reichert, Vienna, Austria). To enhance contrast, we stained the sections with a mixture containing safranin and Astra Blue (0.35 g Safranin + 0.65 g Astra blue + 35 ml 50% Ethanol + 65 ml distilled water) before mounting them in Euparal. A series of 10–16 digital images were taken for each sample at randomly selected locations by a light microscope (Model BX60; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Images were captured at varying magnifications (4×, 20×, and 40×) with a high‐resolution digital camera (Leica DFC 500; Leica Microsystems) attached to the microscope.

We analysed anatomical traits with Fiji (ImageJ; Schindelin et al., 2012), excluding the bark from the measurements. Because VTs are expected to contribute little to hydraulic conductivity in Eucalyptus (Barotto et al., 2016), they were not included in our anatomical analyses or in the computation of theoretical conductivity. At 4× magnification, two to four images were captured, each representing either half or a quarter of the stem cross section. Within these images, two to four fan‐shaped regions were selected for vessel property analysis (Fig. 1a,b). We measured vessel area (A, μm2) directly via Fiji. To identify large vessels, an initial threshold of ≥ 100 μm2 was applied for automatic detection, while smaller vessels were measured manually. In Eucalyptus, solitary vessels typically have much larger diameters than VTs, making them easier to differentiate based on size and position within the transverse section. Additionally, we identified VTs based on the presence of bordered pits connecting them to adjacent vessels (Fig. 1c,d). Vessel diameter (D, μm) was determined using the following formula: (Eqn 3) D=4A/π We then calculated the hydraulically weighted mean vessel diameter (D h, μm) with the following formula (Peters et al., 2021): (Eqn 4) Dh=ΣD4N14 where N represents the total number of vessels involved. We determined vessel density (VD, n mm−2) by counting the vessels within a given cross‐sectional area. We then estimated theoretical conductivity (K th, kg s−1 m−1 MPa−1) using the following formula (Peters et al., 2021): (Eqn 5) Kth=πDh4/128η·VD·ρ where η (MPa·s) represents water viscosity, while ρ (kg·m−3) represents water density. Using the following formula, we estimated η: (Eqn 6) η=1.95×1014T7 where T represents the temperature (K) of liquid water, with η expressed in units of N s m−2 (Roderick & Berry, 2001). The values for ρ were obtained from table 2 in Bigg (1967). Vessel lumen fraction (VLF) was determined as the proportion of the total vessel area (∑A, μm2) relative to the analysed cross‐sectional area. For each sample, six to 10 randomly selected images were taken at 20× or 40× magnification. By using the straight‐line tool in Fiji, we determined vessel wall thickness (VWT, μm; Fig. 1c,d). We recorded several measurements along each vessel wall and used their average to characterise the overall wall thickness.

Systematic vote‐counting review of the growth rate–embolism resistance (P50 ) relationship

We conducted a systematic vote‐counting review of 34 studies (including the present study), examining relationships between growth rate and embolism resistance (P50; obtained from different vulnerability curve methods), as the number of available studies was insufficient to support a formal meta‐analysis (Table 2). Studies were classified into predefined categories (study type, comparison level, clade, woodiness, phenology, growth form, age class, and sample size). Each study contributed once to each category and was assigned to one group within that category and to one of three outcome classes based on the reported relationship between growth rate and embolism resistance: negative, no trend, or mixed; no study reported an exclusively positive relationship. To facilitate standardised comparisons among groups within each category, percentages were calculated for each group, such that the proportion of studies reporting each outcome was expressed relative to the total number of studies in that group (i.e. outcome percentages within each group sum to 100%). Full details of study selection, classification, and synthesis procedures are provided in Methods S1.

Statistical analyses

We report results from all models with P < 0.1, noting those with 0.05 < P < 0.10 as ‘marginally significant’. All statistical analyses were undertaken in R (v.4.2.1, R Development Core Team, 2022).

Cross‐species regression analyses

To assess trait–trait relationships, we used ordinary least squares linear regression based on species mean values. Because species were planted in two experimental groups that differed in planting time and growth duration, grouping may capture combined effects of growth period and associated seasonal conditions. Accordingly, group was included as a fixed effect (Trait A ~ Trait B + Group) to account for variation associated with growth periods. Group was treated as a fixed rather than a random effect because random‐effects models are generally not recommended with fewer than five levels (in this case, two; Harrison et al., 2018). In addition, interactions between group and the focal trait were explicitly tested (Trait A ~ Trait B × Group) to assess whether trait–trait relationships differed between growth periods. Because sample sizes were imbalanced between groups (Winter n = 6; Summer n = 16), group‐specific slope estimates were associated with reduced statistical power and greater uncertainty. Accordingly, interaction tests and within‐group regressions were treated as robustness checks, while primary inferences were based on simplified models assuming a common slope across groups.

To quantify trait–climate relationships, we used multiple linear regression. As with trait–trait models, group was included as a fixed effect to account for potential differences between growth periods. We first ran the models with interaction terms of MAT and MAP (Trait ~ MAT × MAP + Group). In cases when a significant interaction between MAP and MAT was observed, we visualised the interactive effect using a 3D scatter plot generated using the ‘plotly’ package (Sievert, 2020). However, climate gradients studied in previous research often show strong intercorrelation between temperature and precipitation (García‐Cervigón et al., 2020; Hartill et al., 2023), making it challenging to isolate the individual contributions of climate variables to species adaptations and limiting understanding in this key area. To address this limitation, we also fit a model using main effects only (i.e. Trait ~ MAT + MAP + Group) to evaluate the independent effects of each of MAP and MAT on traits. These results were visualised using partial residual plots, generated using the ‘visreg’ package (Breheny & Burchett, 2017), which depicted the relationship between a trait and MAP while controlling for MAT, or vice versa. For trait–climate relationships, group‐specific climate interaction terms (e.g. Group × MAT or Group × MAP) were not tested. These models already included MAT, MAP, and their interaction terms, and adding group‐specific responses would increase model complexity and reduce parameter stability given limited and imbalanced sample sizes.

Correlated‐divergence analyses

We used correlated‐divergence analyses to examine patterns of trait shifts during evolutionary divergences, and their associations with site climate, based on species‐pairs chosen as MAT and MAP PICs. These analyses test whether implied evolutionary shifts in a trait are more strongly linked to implied evolutionary shifts in another trait (or a climate variable) than would be expected by chance alone. In this context, the focus is on evolutionary divergence events, or ‘contrasts’, occurring at phylogenetic tree tips, rather than on species themselves (Westoby et al., 1998). These evolutionary correlations were illustrated as ‘difference graphs’ in which each data point represents a PIC, calculated by subtracting trait values of species from higher MAP or MAT regions from those of species from lower MAP or MAT regions. There is an inherent symmetry in such data, in that the contrasts could equally have been calculated in the other direction, in which case each data point would fall in the ‘mirror‐image’ location of graph space (e.g. in lower right quadrant instead of top left). Consequently, the appropriate line fitting approach is to force the regression through the origin; that is, the regression equations contain no intercept term (Westoby et al., 1998).

Results

Cross‐species and evolutionary relationships between growth rate and embolism resistance

Relationships between growth rate and embolism resistance (drought‐induced stem P50) were examined both with cross‐species analyses (Fig. 2a–g) and correlated‐divergence analyses (‘difference graphs’; Fig. 2h–n). Across the 22 species, growth ‘rate’ – quantified as H, SD, total LA, or total biomass, and in both absolute and relative terms (except RGRH) – showed clear negative relationships with P50 (all P ≤ 0.05; Fig. 2a–d,f,g). That is, faster growing species were more vulnerable to xylem embolism, exhibiting less negative P50 values. The variation explained by these relationships (partial R 2 after accounting for group fixed effects) ranged from 0.27 to 0.59 for absolute growth, and from 0.04 to 0.29 for RGRD and RGRLA. Group fixed effects were significant for RGRH and RGRD (Table S3), reflecting differences in growth periods; however, because group fixed effects did not influence slope estimates of the growth rate–P50 relationships, these effects are not discussed further. P50 × group interactions were significant in relationships of height, SD, total biomass, and RGRLA (Table S3). Group‐specific analyses further showed that relationships between growth rate and P50 were directionally consistent across groups, with steeper slopes in the Winter group, whereas corresponding relationships in the Summer group were weaker (Fig. S4a–c) and occasionally nonsignificant (Fig. S4d).

Results observed in the correlated‐divergence analyses were highly consistent with those reported in cross‐species analyses: evolutionary shifts in growth rate and P50 were negatively correlated (or marginally so) in every case (all P < 0.10; Fig. 2h–n). Taken together, these results provide clear evidence that resistance to embolism is negatively related to growth rate in Eucalyptus, both for extant species and for trait shifts occurring across evolutionary timescales.

Relationships between wood anatomical traits, growth rate, and embolism resistance

Across the 22 species, vessel wall thickness (VWT) was marginally negatively related to total biomass (P = 0.06; Fig. 3a) and strongly positively related to P50 (P < 0.001; Fig. 3d), indicating that species with thicker vessel walls tend to grow more slowly but are more resistant to embolism. Conversely, theoretical conductivity (K th) showed a positive relationship with total biomass (P = 0.012; Fig. 3b) and a negative relationship with P50 (P = 0.006; Fig. 3e), suggesting that greater hydraulic conductivity supports faster growth but comes at the cost of increased embolism vulnerability. Notably, the variation in P50 explained by VWT and Kth was remarkably high (partial R 2 = 0.59 and 0.35, respectively), and more than the variation in total biomass explained by the two traits (partial R 2 = 0.19 and 0.30, respectively).

The other three vessel traits – hydraulic diameter (D h), vessel density, and vessel lumen fraction (VLF) – also contributed to variation in total biomass and P50, though less strongly than VWT or K th (Fig. S5). D h and VLF were positively related to total biomass (P = 0.027 and 0.031; partial R 2 = 0.24 and 0.23, respectively; Fig. S5a,c) and negatively related to P50 (P = 0.004 and 0.03; partial R 2 = 0.36 and 0.24, respectively; Fig. S5d,f). Vessel density showed a positive relationship with P50 (P = 0.034; partial R 2 = 0.18; Fig. S5e) but was unrelated to total biomass.

Relationships between wood density and growth rate were mixed. Growth rate indexed as total biomass at plant harvest was unrelated to wood density (Fig. 3c). This was also true in the equivalent correlation for height (Fig. S6a). However, two other equivalent growth terms, SD and total LA, showed the expected negative relations with wood density (P = 0.044 and 0.041; partial R 2 = 0.29 and 0.13, respectively; Fig. S6b,c). Also unexpectedly, wood density was unrelated to P50 (Fig. 3f).

Results from correlated‐divergence analyses were strongly consistent with those from cross‐species analyses (Figs 3g–l, S5g–l). Evolutionary increases in K th, D h, and VLF were consistently associated with shifts towards higher total biomass and less negative P50 (Figs 3h,k, S5g,i,j,l). Evolutionary increases in VWT and vessel density were significantly associated with shifts towards more negative P50 and lower total biomass (Figs 3g,j, S5h,k). Evolutionary shifts in wood density were unrelated to those in total biomass and P50 (Fig. 3i,l).

Interactive effects of MAP and MAT on plant traits

MAP and MAT showed significant interactive effects on each of P50, K th, D h, vessel density and VLF (interactive effect P = 0.034, 0.003, 0.002, 0.011, and 0.058, respectively; Figs 4a,b, S7c–e; Table S4). R 2 values for those models with significant interactions ranged from 0.31 (vessel density) up to 0.65 (P50). Species from hotter–wetter regions generally exhibited less negative P50 (Fig. 4a), indicating lower resistance to embolism, whereas those from hotter–drier or colder regions (whether dry or wet) had more negative P50, indicating greater resistance to embolism. Species from hotter–wetter regions exhibited higher theoretical conductivity (Fig. 4b), resulting from the higher VLF (Fig. S7e) made of wider vessels (Fig. S7c). By contrast, species from hotter–drier regions had lower theoretical conductivity, due to smaller VLF (Fig. S7e), resulting from smaller vessel size (Fig. S7c) despite having more vessels per unit area (Fig. S7d).

Independent effects of MAP and MAT on plant traits

As expected, variation in both total biomass and P50 was correlated with climate‐of‐origin, with species originating from drier or colder regions exhibiting slower growth rates (P = 0.003 and 0.009, respectively; Fig. 5a,b) and more negative P50 (both P < 0.001; Fig. 5c,d). A global vote‐counting review of the growth rate–embolism resistance (P50 ) relationship

The vote‐counting analysis (Table 2) showed that several categories had relatively comparable study representation across their groups, including sample size (< 10 species, n = 19; ≥ 10 species, n = 15), phenology (evergreen species, n = 19; deciduous species, n = 10), comparison level (within‐species, n = 23; between‐species, n = 11), and clade (angiosperms, n = 22; gymnosperms, n = 10), whereas mixed phenology and mixed clades were less represented. Other categories showed more uneven distributions, with most studies being field‐based (n = 27) rather than conducted under controlled conditions (n = 7), and most focussing on adult plants (n = 24) relative to saplings (n = 6) and seedlings (n = 4). By contrast, all studies focussed on woody species (n = 34), and most examined trees (n = 32), with one study including shrubs and one examining mixed growth forms.

Across 34 studies, 41.2% reported a negative relationship consistent with a growth rate–embolism resistance trade‐off, 47.1% reported no detectable relationship, and 11.8% reported mixed results; notably, no study reported an exclusively positive relationship. That is, although overall support for a trade‐off was weak, no evidence was found for species that simultaneously exhibited faster growth rate and greater embolism resistance. The frequency of negative relationships varied among different categories. The trade‐off was more evident in controlled‐condition studies (57.1%) than in field‐based studies (37.0%), and in within‐species analyses (43.5%) than in between‐species comparisons (36.4%). Across other biological and sampling dimensions, it was also more evident in evergreen (52.6%) than deciduous species (40.0%); in gymnosperms (50.0%) than angiosperms (40.9%); in studies on saplings and seedlings (both 50.0%) than adult plants (37.5%); and in larger sample studies (≥ 10 species; 46.7%) than smaller sample studies (36.8%).

Discussion

Key results from our experimental study of 22 Eucalyptus species grown under controlled conditions, consistent across both cross‐species and phylogenetic (correlated‐divergence) analyses, revealed the following patterns: (1) there was clear evidence of a negative relationship between growth rate and embolism resistance (P50), consistent with a trade‐off among these emergent plant properties. (2) Variation in both P50 and growth rate was correlated with climate‐of‐origin; plants from drier or colder regions had more negative P50 and slower growth. (3) Vessel diameter and theoretical conductivity (K th) were lower in species from drier or colder regions, and their vessels had thicker walls. (4) Variation in wood anatomical traits, especially VWT and K th, was strongly associated with variation in growth rate and P50. (5) Wood density had mixed relationships with growth rate and was not coordinated with P50.

In addition, our global vote‐counting review of 34 studies revealed generally weak but context‐dependent patterns in the growth rate–P50 relationship, with negative relationships between growth rate and embolism resistance more frequently reported under controlled conditions, within species, in evergreen species, gymnosperms, younger life stages, and studies with larger sample sizes, and no study reported an exclusively positive relationship.

Trade‐off between growth rate and embolism resistance

Across our set of Eucalyptus species, faster growth – whether measured by height, SD, total LA, or total biomass at final harvest, and considered in either absolute or relative growth terms – was consistently associated with greater vulnerability to xylem embolism. Phylogenetic analyses in the form of PICs also indicated that evolutionary shifts towards slower growth have been consistently associated with shifts towards greater embolism resistance. Taken together, these results provide clear evidence that resistance to embolism is negatively associated with growth rate. However, does this relationship reflect a true trade‐off? And what mechanistic constraints underlie the apparent compromise – where improved hydraulic safety comes at the cost of reduced growth rate?

We propose that the negative relationship observed between embolism resistance and growth rate likely reflects coordination among multiple xylem anatomical traits that collectively constrain both hydraulic safety and growth rate. While thicker pit membranes provide a stronger physical barrier to embolism spread during drought, they also increase resistance to water flow between adjacent vessels, thereby reducing hydraulic efficiency and potentially limiting growth rate (Brodribb & Feild, 2000; Kaack et al., 2021; Lens et al., 2022). Similarly, narrower vessels enhance resistance to freeze–thaw embolism, but at the cost of reduced hydraulic conductivity and slower growth (Davis et al., 1999; Choat et al., 2011). Furthermore, constructing embolism‐resistant xylem (e.g. thicker pit membranes and a higher density of narrower vessels for a given sapwood area) requires greater structural investment per unit volume (Sperry, 2003), which may be associated with reduced growth rate. As such, multiple anatomical traits likely interact in complex ways to influence embolism resistance, and it represents an integrated outcome of coordinated anatomical trait variation that ultimately trades‐off against growth rate. Although we did not measure pit‐level traits in this study, we had robust data on vessel traits (Figs 3, S5). We found that P50 was associated with thicker vessel walls, a higher density of narrower vessels, and lower VLF, as well as lower hydraulic conductivity; these anatomical characteristics were in turn associated with slower growth rate, consistent with the hypothesised mechanisms described previously (see ‘Insights from analyses of wood anatomy’ in the Discussion section).

Our results also indicated that variation in both P50 and growth rate was correlated with climate‐of‐origin, with plants from drier or colder sites having more negative P50 and slower growth (Figs 4 and 5). This suggests that variation in embolism resistance and growth rate reflects adaptation to climate. Plants from regions with lower precipitation typically experience more sustained periods of water deficit, resulting in greater negative xylem pressure. In response, these species tend to evolve greater embolism resistance, as shown by field observations (Choat et al., 2012; Peters et al., 2021) and common environment experiments (Bourne et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Oyanoghafo et al., 2021; Guan et al., 2023). Similarly, plants from colder climates may be more frequently exposed to freezing events that impose drought‐like water stress (‘frost drought’), which could contribute to selection for greater embolism resistance (Ball et al., 2006; Charra‐Vaskou et al., 2016; Mayr et al., 2020). This pattern of species originating from colder regions having more negative P50 is further supported by a global dataset across 134 species (Huang et al., 2024). Conversely, plants originating from warmer and wetter climates (in which water stress associated with drought or freezing is reduced) tend to prioritise growth over hydraulic safety, a trend consistently observed in the field (Morales et al., 2004; Sarris et al., 2007) and common garden studies (Bourne et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Torres‐Ruiz et al., 2019; Kurjak et al., 2024).

Insights from analyses of wood anatomy

Cross‐species and phylogenetic results both indicated that variation in vessel traits underpinned variation in growth rate and P50, and usually in opposite directions, which was consistent with the negative growth rate–P50 relationship (Figs 3, S5). Theoretical conductivity was the strongest predictor of growth rate, faster growth scaling with higher conductivity (Fig. 3b,h), consistent with findings from previous field investigations (Schumann et al., 2019; Aritsara et al., 2023) and common environment studies (Hajek et al., 2014; Hietz et al., 2022). Theoretical conductivity was primarily determined by vessel diameter, which contributed to higher VLF (Fig. S5a,c,g,i); also, those vessels had thinner vessel walls (Fig. 3a,g), which may reflect lower structural investment at the conduit level (Sperry, 2003). Both K th and D h were higher in species originating from warmer and wetter areas (Figs 4b, 6e,f, S7c, S8a,b), supporting previous field studies (Gleason et al., 2012; Pfautsch et al., 2016; Peters et al., 2021) and common environment studies (Bourne et al., 2017; Jankowski et al., 2019). Those findings further confirm our hypotheses that a fundamental basis for rapid plant growth is high water transport capacity. In wetter and warmer environments, plants typically produce wider vessels with thinner walls to maximise hydraulic conductivity, as faster water transport supports higher rates of stomatal conductance. This, in turn, enables higher photosynthetic rates and hence, more rapid biomass accumulation (Brodribb & Feild, 2000; Santiago et al., 2004; Gleason et al., 2012; Pfautsch et al., 2016; Peters et al., 2021).

VWT explained the greatest proportion of variation in P50 (R 2 = 0.59; Fig. 3d), compared with other anatomical traits, for which R 2 ranged from 0.18 to 0.36 (Figs 3e, S5d–f). Thicker vessel walls are thought to enhance resistance to embolism, likely through their coordination with thicker pit membranes (Li et al., 2016; Thonglim et al., 2021). In our study, more negative P50 was associated with higher VWT, aligning with findings from both field investigations (Li et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2020) and common garden studies (Guet et al., 2015; Thonglim et al., 2021). We also found that plants from colder and drier sites exhibited thicker vessel walls (Fig. 6c,d), mirroring trends reported in a related common garden study on 58 Eucalyptus species measured at the adult stage (Pan et al., 2025). Together, these results suggest that thicker vessel walls may represent an adaptive advantage in drier or colder climates to reduce the risk of embolism. In addition, another possible benefit of thicker vessel walls is increased resistance to vessel implosion under extreme negative xylem pressures, as suggested by previous studies (Hacke et al., 2001; Jacobsen et al., 2005; Pan et al., 2025).

Vessel diameter and vessel density also played important roles in shaping P50 variation (Fig. S5d–f,i,j–l). A greater number of narrower vessels per unit sapwood area resulted in a lower VLF and reduced hydraulic conductivity but was simultaneously associated with stronger resistance to embolism. Narrower vessels were more common in species from drier or colder climates; this is likely advantageous for resisting drought‐induced embolism (Hacke et al., 2022; Jacobsen & Pratt, 2023) or freeze–thaw‐induced embolism (Davis et al., 1999; Choat et al., 2011). However, the relationship between vessel size and drought‐induced embolism vulnerability is not consistently observed across studies, implying that narrower vessels do not universally confer greater embolism resistance (Lens et al., 2022). Instead, vessel size likely covaries with other anatomical traits, such as pit membrane properties, that more directly govern vulnerability. As another adaptive strategy, plants often tend to develop higher vessel density by producing more numerous, narrower vessels. This structural redundancy allows adjacent vessels to maintain water transport when some become embolised, thereby buffering the loss of hydraulic conductivity (Zanne et al., 2010). However, it is worth noting that building embolism‐resistant xylem (a higher density of narrower vessels with thicker walls per unit sapwood area) is more costly than constructing efficient xylem (fewer, wider vessels with thinner walls), as it requires greater structural investment at the conduit level and often results in reduced conductivity and limited growth rate (Brodribb & Feild, 2000; Sperry, 2003).

Additionally, plants from warmer and wetter regions exhibited higher VLFs (Figs S7e, S8f) composed of larger vessels (Figs S7c, S8b), contributing to increased theoretical conductivity (Figs 4b, 6f), which likely supports adequate water transport under high evaporative demand (Olson et al., 2020). Elevated temperatures typically raise the vapour pressure deficit (VPD), accelerating water loss through evapotranspiration. In response, plants may develop larger vessel diameters, boosting hydraulic conductivity to meet the greater evaporative demand from leaves (Olson et al., 2020).

Relationships between wood density, growth rate and embolism resistance

In contrast to our expectations, in this study, the relationships between wood density and the various growth metrics were mixed. Wood density was correlated with final SD and total LA, but not with total biomass or height (Figs 3c,i, S6a–c). The negative relationships between wood density and SD or total LA are consistent with the trend reported in a global dataset (Chave et al., 2009). Lower wood density (i.e. lower construction costs at xylem tissue level) is often accompanied by larger vessels that enable more efficient water transport to leaves, potentially enhancing plant productivity (Brodribb & Feild, 2000; Santiago et al., 2004; Chave et al., 2009). Here, however, wood density was not related to other aspects of growth rate. A potential explanation for the absence of a clear link between wood density and total biomass or height in this study is that wood density strongly influences mechanical strength (Chave et al., 2009) and that faster growing juvenile eucalypts require sufficiently high wood density to structurally support their larger biomass or taller stems. As a result, particularly at the seedling stage, there may be little or no relationship between wood density and growth rate.

Wood density was also expected to be coordinated with P50; many previous studies have shown this to be the case, including studies involving adults growing in natural habitats (Eller et al., 2018; Peters et al., 2021), as well as common garden seedlings (Guet et al., 2015; Li et al., 2018) and saplings (Hazir et al., 2022). Notably, two of these studies involved eucalypts (Li et al., 2018; Peters et al., 2021). Here however, both nonphylogenetic and phylogenetic analyses indicated no association between wood density and P50 (Fig. 3f,l). This was unexpected but also not without precedent (Cochard et al., 2007; García‐Valdés et al., 2021). It is worth noting that embolism resistance in angiosperms is mainly related to vessel traits, particularly pit structures between adjoining vessels (Lens et al., 2022). By contrast, wood density variation in angiosperms primarily reflects variation in fibre fraction and fibre characteristics (e.g. wall thickness), fibres constituting the bulk of xylem tissue rather than vessels (Ziemińska et al., 2013). For this reason, there is not really a clear mechanistic link between P50 and wood density (Lachenbruch & McCulloh, 2014), despite their common correlation. This suggests that, although wood density itself does not directly influence hydraulic function, the observed relationships likely arise because anatomical traits underlying variation in wood density are also linked, in some way, to embolism resistance (Lachenbruch & McCulloh, 2014). One possible interpretation of the lack of relationship between wood density and P50 in our dataset is that developmental stage influences this relationship. Seedlings and adults often experience contrasting local environmental conditions (Iida & Swenson, 2020), and wood anatomy can vary substantially during ontogeny (Osazuwa‐Peters et al., 2017; Iida & Swenson, 2020). Since the plants in our study were relatively young, variation in wood density may not yet have fully developed to the extent observed in larger plants (Iida & Swenson, 2020), potentially weakening its association with P50.

Additionally, while common garden experiments reduce environmental influences on trait expression (i.e. plasticity), they do not eliminate them. Traits such as wood density may still respond plastically to growth conditions, potentially obscuring associations with growth rate or P50. However, we consider this less likely, as wood density showed a clear and expected relationship with climate‐of‐origin precipitation (Fig. 6a), consistent with well‐documented patterns in previous field studies (Pfautsch et al., 2016; Peters et al., 2021).

Assessing the prevalence of the growth rate–embolism resistance trade‐off

The global vote‐counting review of 34 studies indicated generally weak support for a growth rate–embolism resistance trade‐off, with c. 40% of studies reporting a negative relationship and the majority (c. 60%) reporting no or mixed results (Table 2). Despite this overall weak support, a notable and consistent feature of the literature is the complete absence of studies reporting an exclusively positive relationship between growth rate and embolism resistance. This suggests that, even where a trade‐off is not statistically detected, the simultaneous expression of high growth rates and high embolism resistance may not be achievable, and to that extent a trade‐off may exist.

The frequency of negative growth rate–embolism resistance relationships was context‐dependent, with negative relationships more commonly reported in studies conducted under controlled conditions (Table 2). Under such conditions, reduced environmental variability may allow inherent physiological constraints to be more readily detected. By contrast, more than 60% of field‐based studies reported no or mixed relationships (Table 2), indicating that environmentally driven trait variation in natural settings may weaken or obscure the detection of underlying growth rate–embolism resistance trade‐offs. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that fast‐growing species tend to outcompete slow‐growing species under resource‐rich conditions, whereas under stressful conditions, this advantage could diminish, potentially contributing to weaker or less detectable relationships (Augusto et al., 2025). While the trait–trait and trait–climate relationships observed in this study likely reflect inherent differences among species, rather than plastic responses to growth conditions, we cannot rule out that the favourable, well‐watered conditions of the polytunnel facilitated their detection. Whether similar patterns would emerge under more stressful conditions remains an open question and experiments incorporating a gradient of water availability, from well‐watered to drought‐stressed, would be valuable to test the robustness of these relationships across environments.

Evidence for the growth rate–embolism resistance trade‐off was also more frequently reported in within‐species studies than in between‐species comparisons (Table 2). This pattern suggests that coordinated constraints between growth rate and embolism resistance are more readily detected when phylogenetic and life‐history variation is minimised. Between‐species studies, by contrast, span broader phylogenetic scales, across which lineage‐specific strategies and life‐history differences may dominate trait variation and obscure such coordination. Consistent with this interpretation, the present study examined multiple species within a single genus, thereby reducing phylogenetic noise and facilitating the detection of a clearer growth rate–embolism resistance trade‐off.

Across biological and sampling dimensions, negative growth rate–embolism resistance relationships were more frequently reported in evergreen species, gymnosperms, and younger plants, as well as in studies with larger sample sizes (Table 2). Evergreen species rely more strongly on structurally safer xylem to sustain hydraulic function across seasons (Laughlin et al., 2023), which may promote tighter coupling between growth rate and embolism resistance. By contrast, deciduous species employ phenological avoidance and compensatory strategies, such as deep rooting and greater allocation of wood tissues to storage and renewal functions (Eamus, 1999; Zhang et al., 2022; Laughlin et al., 2023), thereby reducing their reliance on structural reinforcement and potentially weakening the coupling between growth performance and hydraulic safety. Compared with angiosperms, the higher incidence of negative relationships in gymnosperms likely reflects their more conservative hydraulic architecture, in which xylem is composed primarily of tracheids that confer higher hydraulic safety but lower hydraulic efficiency and reduced redundancy in water transport pathways (Pittermann et al., 2010; Crang et al., 2018), thereby constraining opportunities to decouple growth rate from embolism resistance. In addition, stronger evidence for the trade‐off in saplings and seedlings suggests that the trade‐off may be more readily detectable at younger developmental stages. Trait–growth relationships are known to be constrained by ontogeny, with many such relationships being strong in seedlings and saplings but weakening as trees become older (Gibert et al., 2016; Falster et al., 2018). As our study focussed on relatively young individuals, the strength and nature of the relationships observed here may partly reflect this developmental stage. One possible explanation underlying this ontogenetic shift is that wood anatomical traits in younger plants may be under stronger genetic control and exhibit lower plasticity than in adults (Jankowski et al., 2019), whereas increasing plasticity in older individuals may weaken the coupling between traits and growth. Finally, larger sample sizes likely increase statistical power, enhancing the detection of relatively subtle or context‐dependent trade‐offs.

Current evidence is constrained by uneven coverage across experimental designs, functional types and life stages. Expanding controlled‐condition experiments and broadening taxonomic and developmental representation, including shrubs, herbaceous species, and early life stages, will be critical for assessing the generality of this trade‐off. Beyond these limitations, inconsistencies in the growth rate–embolism resistance trade‐off reported in the literature may reflect genuine variation across species and environmental contexts. Recent studies suggest that hydraulic efficiency (K s) and safety (P50) follow a convex rather than linear relationship (Gleason et al., 2016; Kreinert et al., 2026). Under this framework, we would expect the growth rate–P50 trade‐off to be strongest in the tails of the curve, whereas species near the centre may show limited differentiation in growth rate and P50, with environmental conditions playing a stronger role in determining growth performance. However, we did not test this possibility quantitatively, and whether a convex relationship exists between growth rate and P50 remains an open question. Exploring this pattern represents an important direction for future research.

Conclusions

Our experimental study provides strong evidence of a trade‐off between growth rate and embolism resistance in Eucalyptus seedlings and suggests that this trade‐off is adaptive. Species from warmer and wetter regions exhibited higher VLFs composed of wider vessels, enhancing hydraulic conductivity and supporting rapid growth, but at the cost of higher vulnerability to xylem embolism. By contrast, species from colder or drier regions developed more numerous, narrower vessels per unit area and thicker vessel walls, improving embolism resistance but reducing hydraulic conductivity and resulting in slower growth. Since all plants were grown under a controlled environment, the trait–trait and trait–climate relationships observed in this study are likely to reflect inherent, or ‘hard‐wired’ differences between species. This suggests that the traits examined in this study are relatively conserved, potentially governed by strong genetic control or only modestly influenced by genotype × environment interactions.

More broadly, our global vote‐counting review indicates that the detectability of the growth rate–embolism resistance trade‐off is strongly context‐dependent. Although overall support for a trade‐off was weak, the simultaneous expression of high growth rates and high embolism resistance was nonexistent, suggesting that a trade‐off may still exist but is only detectable under certain conditions. Together, these findings advance our understanding of the trade‐off between growth and embolism resistance and underscore how woody species adapt to varying climatic conditions. Such insights are essential for improving predictions of plant performance under future climate scenarios.
