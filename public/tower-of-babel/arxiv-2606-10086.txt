Exploratory Responsiveness and Adaptive Rigidity under AI-Assisted Optimization
Balaraju Battu

Abstract
This paper develops a theory of exploratory adaptation under AI-assisted optimization. The central argument is that the long-run adaptive effects of AI systems depend critically on how predictive assistance interacts with exploratory responsiveness itself. We formalize this mechanism using a dynamical framework in which cognitive, institutional, and technological systems evolve over rugged epistemic landscapes characterized by multiple locally reinforced configurations. A central state variable in the model is adaptive responsiveness, which measures the capacity of a system to traverse unfamiliar conceptual and institutional trajectories under changing conditions. Under convergent predictive regimes, AI systems substitute for exploratory engagement, reducing adaptive responsiveness and generating metastable trapping, hysteresis, premature convergence, and exploration-collapse dynamics in which systems become locally efficient but globally rigid. The framework also identifies contrasting exploration-enhancing regimes in which AI systems amplify exploratory search, conceptual traversal, and adaptive mobility. The effective substitution parameter is therefore responsiveness-dependent: systems possessing weak exploratory routines are more vulnerable to exploratory substitution, whereas systems already possessing high adaptive responsiveness may use AI assistance to expand exploratory mobility across rugged landscapes. The long-run adaptive effects of AI consequently depend not only on AI capability itself, but also on institutional structure, developmental context, and the architecture of human-machine interaction.

A Model of Exploratory Adaptation

This section develops the formal framework linking exploratory activity, adaptive responsiveness, and AI-assisted optimization. The central premise is that long-run adaptation depends not only on the accumulation of information, but also on the preservation of exploratory mobility across cognitive and institutional landscapes. Systems confronting uncertain or rapidly changing environments must repeatedly traverse unfamiliar regions of epistemic and institutional space rather than remain confined near existing attractors. Exploratory activity therefore generates socially valuable adaptive capacity because experimentation, exploratory deliberation, uncertainty navigation, and publicly observable reasoning sustain the mobility required for long-run adaptation.

We represent local adaptive motion using gradient-like dynamics:

dx_{t}=-z(t)\nabla V(x_{t})\,dt+\sigma(t)dW_{t}.

Rather, it reflects the general tendency of adaptive systems to reinforce locally stable trajectories while exploration generates perturbations capable of escaping local attractors. The central state variable in the framework is adaptive responsiveness

High responsiveness permits continued exploration and reconfiguration under changing conditions, while low responsiveness generates rigidity and confinement near existing attractors. The distinction is important because systems may possess substantial accumulated knowledge while simultaneously losing the capacity to adapt effectively to unfamiliar environments. A firm can become highly efficient within an existing production regime while losing the ability to respond to technological disruption, while scientific communities may accumulate extensive knowledge while becoming increasingly conservative in exploratory behavior.

Exploration consequently plays a critical role because deterministic stabilization dynamics alone drive systems toward nearby local minima. Productive struggle, exploratory search, and publicly observable reasoning therefore sustain both exploratory variance and adaptive responsiveness itself. 3
Dynamics of Exploratory Responsiveness

This section studies the dynamic implications of AI-assisted optimization for exploratory responsiveness and long-run adaptive capacity. The results develop progressively. We begin by characterizing the baseline effect of AI assistance on responsiveness. We then show how sufficiently large reductions in exploratory activity can generate responsiveness collapse, metastable trapping, and hysteresis. The analysis concludes by showing how decentralized optimization may underprovide the exploratory activity required to sustain long-run adaptive responsiveness in rugged epistemic environments.

The resulting topology produces multiple locally reinforced configurations separated by barriers of varying steepness and curvature, allowing the simulations to illustrate metastable trapping, exploratory collapse, hysteresis, and path-dependent adaptive dynamics. Specific functional forms and numerical landscape specifications used in the simulations are reported in Appendix A. The qualitative results are robust to moderate parameter variation and alternative rugged landscape specifications.

The central mechanism linking these results is straightforward. AI systems improve local performance by reducing costly exploratory effort. However, exploratory activity also regenerates the adaptive responsiveness required for long-run mobility across epistemic and institutional landscapes. As exploratory engagement declines, systems may therefore become increasingly efficient locally while simultaneously becoming increasingly rigid globally.

Proposition 1.

The implication is not that AI lowers performance in the short run. Predictive systems may substantially improve local task efficiency by reducing costly search, deliberation, and uncertainty navigation. However, the same exploratory practices that become privately unnecessary also contribute to the regeneration of adaptive responsiveness itself. Increasing optimization may therefore improve local performance while gradually reducing the exploratory mobility required for long-run adaptation across unfamiliar conceptual and institutional regions.

This distinction between local optimization and adaptive flexibility is central to the framework. Organizations automating routine analysis may improve short-run productivity because workers solve familiar problems more quickly and coordinate more efficiently within established workflows. At the same time, workers may engage less often in independent diagnosis, exploratory reasoning, adversarial deliberation, or unfamiliar problem solving. The organization consequently becomes increasingly efficient within existing routines while gradually losing the responsiveness required to adapt when environments shift unexpectedly. Similar dynamics may emerge in scientific, educational, and institutional systems. 3.2
Responsiveness Collapse

The previous result establishes that AI assistance can gradually reduce adaptive responsiveness under convergent predictive regimes for which
\alpha>0
. We now show that the resulting dynamics may become qualitatively discontinuous once exploratory engagement falls sufficiently. Define a critical responsiveness threshold

z_{c}\in(0,1)

representing the minimum level of adaptive responsiveness required for sustained exploratory mobility across the landscape. Above this threshold, systems retain sufficient flexibility to traverse alternative regions of epistemic and institutional space and adapt to changing conditions. Below this threshold, exploratory mobility deteriorates sharply and systems become increasingly vulnerable to local trapping, premature convergence, and rigidity. Such thresholds become especially important in rugged environments containing multiple locally stable basins separated by steep barriers. Frontier scientific domains, rapidly changing technological sectors, and fragmented institutional environments generally require substantially greater responsiveness because systems must repeatedly escape established attractors and traverse unfamiliar regions of the landscape.

Proposition 2.

If AI-assisted optimization reduces exploratory activity sufficiently, the system converges toward a low-responsiveness regime characterized by reduced exploratory mobility and increasing rigidity.

The proposition follows directly from the responsiveness dynamics. As AI assistance rises, exploratory activity declines according to

s_{t}=s_{0}-\alpha AI_{t}.

Once exploratory engagement falls below the level required to sustain responsiveness regeneration, the system converges toward a low-mobility regime in which stabilization forces dominate exploratory renewal. The important feature of the result is that responsiveness decline is not merely gradual. Systems may continue exhibiting high local performance even while approaching collapse regions in which adaptive flexibility deteriorates rapidly. A student heavily dependent on automated problem-solving tools may continue performing well on familiar exercises while becoming increasingly unable to solve unfamiliar problems independently. Similarly, institutions optimized around predictive routines may remain highly efficient until environmental conditions shift sufficiently to require exploratory adaptation beyond existing workflows. Under sufficiently exploration-enhancing architectures
(\alpha<0)
, however, the same dynamics may instead preserve or strengthen responsiveness by increasing exploratory traversal rather than compressing it.

The distinction between optimization and adaptability is therefore central to the framework. Systems can remain locally competent even while progressively losing the capacity to respond effectively to novelty, structural change, or conceptual disruption. Figure

Systems operating over relatively smooth landscapes can remain adaptive even with modest responsiveness because nearby trajectories remain accessible. 3.3
Metastable Trapping

The collapse of responsiveness has important implications for dynamics on rugged epistemic landscapes. The system evolves according to

Proposition 3.

The proposition follows from the interaction between declining responsiveness and rugged landscape geometry. Such configurations may correspond to dominant scientific paradigms, established technological standards, organizational routines, institutional practices, or widely shared explanatory systems. These states persist because coordination effects, accumulated learning, institutional reinforcement, and adaptive expectations reduce the local costs of remaining within existing trajectories. Escaping a local basin consequently requires sufficiently high exploratory responsiveness to traverse unfamiliar regions of the landscape. 3.4
Hysteresis and Irreversible Rigidity

The nonlinear responsiveness dynamics naturally generate hysteresis because responsiveness regeneration depends on existing responsiveness itself. Collapse and recovery therefore need not follow identical trajectories. Systems experiencing prolonged periods of low exploratory engagement may remain trapped in low-responsiveness states even after exploratory opportunities partially recover.

Proposition 4.

Temporary periods of reduced exploratory activity can generate persistent rigidity even after exploratory opportunities are restored.

Restoring opportunities for exploration may not immediately restore exploratory responsiveness because recovery depends not only on the availability of exploratory engagement, but also on whether the institutional, cognitive, and organizational structures required for exploratory regeneration remain intact.

3.5
Welfare and the Underproduction of Exploration

The preceding results imply a welfare problem arising from decentralized optimization under AI-assisted prediction. Individual agents adopt AI systems because predictive assistance improves local performance and reduces costly exploratory effort. However, exploratory activity also generates socially valuable adaptive responsiveness that decentralized agents do not fully internalize. The relevant distinction is therefore not merely between effort and convenience, but between obtaining the result of exploration and preserving the capacity to explore itself. Predictive systems may deliver high-quality answers, summaries, code, recommendations, or strategies without requiring agents to traverse the underlying problem space independently. Local output improves, but the behavioral, cognitive, and institutional routines through which exploratory responsiveness is regenerated may gradually deteriorate. A system may therefore accumulate useful answers while simultaneously losing the adaptive mobility required to generate new answers when environments change.

Let aggregate welfare be given by

W_{t}=P(AI_{t})+\beta z(t)^{\nu},

where
P(AI_{t})

\beta>0

\nu>1
captures the idea that highly responsive systems are disproportionately valuable for long-run adaptation. The nonlinear responsiveness term reflects the fact that adaptive losses may become increasingly severe once responsiveness deteriorates sufficiently. Small reductions in exploratory responsiveness may generate modest welfare losses, while deeper collapse can produce disproportionately large reductions in adaptive flexibility. Responsiveness consequently becomes especially valuable in environments characterized by uncertainty, structural change, and high landscape ruggedness, where long-run adaptation requires sustained traversal across competing local basins.

Proposition 5.

When exploratory activity generates positive responsiveness externalities, decentralized AI adoption leads to underinvestment in socially valuable exploration.

The proposition follows because private agents internalize the local productivity gains from AI assistance while only partially internalizing the long-run value of exploratory responsiveness. A worker, student, firm, or platform user may rationally rely on predictive assistance because it reduces immediate effort and improves current output. Yet the foregone exploratory activity also reduces public reasoning, skill formation, institutional memory, conceptual diversity, and collective problem-solving capacity. These losses are partly external to the individual decision-maker. The resulting inefficiency therefore differs from a standard productivity tradeoff. The issue is not simply that agents choose too much convenience or too little effort. Rather, decentralized optimization may gradually erode the system-level capacity required to navigate future uncertainty.

In convergent predictive regimes characterized by strongly positive values of
\alpha
, the long-run equilibrium may approach a low-responsiveness state in which systems retain high local throughput while progressively losing exploratory mobility itself. Such systems may continue generating predictions, recommendations, and locally efficient outputs, yet gradually lose the decentralized exploratory routines required for conceptual revision, institutional adaptation, and traversal across unfamiliar regions of the landscape. In this sense, the relevant welfare problem concerns not only the quantity of information produced, but whether adaptive systems preserve the responsiveness required to escape local basins when environments shift. Highly optimized systems may therefore converge toward socially brittle equilibria characterized by strong local efficiency but weak adaptive resilience.

3.7
Empirical Interpretation and Measurement

Although the framework is stylized, its central variables admit natural empirical interpretations. The model is not intended as a calibrated description of any single institutional or cognitive system. Rather, it provides a dynamical structure linking exploratory activity, adaptive responsiveness, institutional structure, and AI-assisted optimization. The framework consequently generates observable implications that may be examined empirically across organizational, scientific, educational, and collaborative knowledge environments.

Highly exploratory workflows generate irregular and decentralized search patterns, while strongly optimized workflows converge more rapidly toward standardized local solutions.

4
Discussion and Conclusion

This paper develops a theory of exploratory adaptation under AI-assisted optimization. The central argument is not that AI systems inevitably reduce human adaptability, but that the long-run adaptive effects of AI depend critically on how predictive assistance interacts with exploratory responsiveness itself. Exploratory activity generates socially valuable adaptive responsiveness because experimentation, uncertainty navigation, disagreement, publicly observable reasoning, and self-guided problem solving regenerate the mobility required to traverse rugged cognitive and institutional landscapes under changing conditions. Systems confronting uncertainty, fragmentation, and rapid structural change therefore require more than accumulated information. They require sufficient responsiveness to escape local attractors, revise existing routines, and traverse unfamiliar conceptual regions.

The framework studies these dynamics through the interaction between exploratory activity, adaptive responsiveness, institutional structure, and AI architecture. In convergent predictive regimes characterized by strongly positive values of
\alpha
, AI assistance substitutes for exploratory engagement by reducing the need for independent search, deliberation, and uncertainty navigation. Under such conditions, increasing optimization lowers exploratory activity, gradually reducing adaptive responsiveness and increasing rigidity over time. The resulting dynamics generate metastable trapping, hysteresis, premature convergence, and exploration-collapse transitions in which systems remain locally efficient while becoming globally brittle. Rugged epistemic landscapes amplify these effects because fragmented and rapidly changing environments require sustained exploratory mobility to avoid becoming trapped near locally reinforced configurations.

The framework, however, also identifies a contrasting regime that is largely absent from existing automation-oriented literatures. The effective substitution parameter is itself endogenous:

\alpha=\alpha(z,\mathcal{I},\mathcal{A}),

where
z
denotes the existing responsiveness stock,
\mathcal{I}
denotes institutional structure, and
\mathcal{A}

This distinction is central because it implies that exploration collapse is not an inevitable consequence of AI assistance itself. Exploration-enhancing systems may instead preserve or expand adaptive mobility by lowering the cost of conceptual traversal, exposing agents to anomalous hypotheses, facilitating adversarial reasoning, or enabling movement across previously inaccessible epistemic regions. Socratic tutoring systems, adversarial research assistants, open-ended simulation environments, and generative scientific tools capable of translating across disciplinary boundaries may therefore strengthen exploratory responsiveness rather than compress it. In highly rugged environments characterized by strong traversal barriers, such systems may increase the capacity of organizations, scientific communities, and educational systems to adapt under changing conditions.

The framework consequently differs from existing literatures emphasizing informational substitution, labor displacement, or knowledge collapse alone. Existing work on cognitive offloading demonstrates that external systems reducing immediate cognitive demand can weaken independent memory formation and exploratory reasoning

The framework also differs from standard optimization models in economics and machine learning. Conventional approaches typically treat exploration as an exogenous parameter or temporary inefficiency. Here, exploratory responsiveness evolves endogenously and may either deteriorate or strengthen depending on the interaction between AI architecture, institutional structure, and existing responsiveness. This creates the possibility of multiple adaptive regimes. Under strongly convergent predictive dynamics, systems may gradually approach low-responsiveness equilibria characterized by high local throughput but weak adaptive resilience. Under exploration-enhancing regimes, by contrast, AI assistance may preserve or expand exploratory mobility by strengthening the exploratory processes through which responsiveness itself is regenerated.

These results have important implications for institutional design. If exploratory activity generates socially valuable responsiveness externalities, then environments optimized purely around immediate efficiency may become dynamically fragile over time. Educational systems emphasizing productive struggle, scientific systems preserving methodological pluralism, and organizations maintaining decentralized experimentation may therefore possess long-run adaptive advantages even when they appear locally less efficient in the short run. The relevant distinction is therefore not between AI and non-AI systems, but between institutions and architectures that compress exploratory mobility and those that preserve or expand it. Organizations may preserve adaptive responsiveness by maintaining structured forms of productive friction within highly optimized environments. Educational systems can require active reasoning before automated assistance. Scientific institutions can preserve decentralized experimentation, adversarial review, and publicly observable reasoning processes. Firms operating in rapidly changing environments can maintain exploratory conceptual development before predictive optimization.

Preserving exploratory responsiveness therefore becomes an institutional design problem rather than merely a technological one. Systems optimized exclusively around short-run throughput may gradually erode the exploratory routines, disagreement structures, and adaptive perturbations required for long-run mobility across rugged environments. The architecture of AI-assisted systems consequently matters fundamentally for long-run adaptation. Convergent predictive systems optimized for immediate answer generation may compress exploratory variance and accelerate local trapping, while exploration-enhancing systems may expand adaptive mobility by lowering the cost of traversing unfamiliar conceptual regions. The long-run adaptive effects of AI therefore depend fundamentally on whether institutions and AI architectures preserve the exploratory responsiveness required for continued collective adaptation.

The paper has several limitations. The framework intentionally abstracts from strategic interaction, heterogeneous agents, endogenous network formation, and political economy considerations. The responsiveness dynamics are stylized and designed to isolate the core mechanism linking optimization and exploratory adaptation. The model also does not distinguish formally between different AI architectures, although the analysis suggests that institutional structure and workflow design critically shape whether AI systems become substitutive or exploration-enhancing. These limitations point toward several important directions for future research, including heterogeneous responsiveness dynamics across agents and institutions, endogenous landscape formation, network structure and collective learning, strategic coordination, and political economy feedbacks under AI-assisted optimization.

This framework generates empirically testable predictions regarding exploratory variance, domain-shift adaptation, public reasoning activity, and responsiveness recovery following periods of intensive AI assistance. The central question is no longer only how AI affects productivity, labor demand, or information acquisition. Increasingly, the relevant question concerns how predictive systems reshape the long-run geometry of exploration itself. By introducing adaptive responsiveness as an endogenous and socially underprovided stock generated through exploratory activity, and by linking AI-assisted optimization to rugged epistemic landscapes, metastable trapping, hysteresis, and exploration-enhancing regimes, the framework provides a unified account of how systems may either lose or preserve the adaptive mobility required for long-run collective adaptation.

Appendix A
Appendix: Responsiveness Dynamics and Numerical Illustrations

This appendix provides additional details on the responsiveness dynamics and numerical illustrations used throughout the paper. The purpose is not to calibrate the framework empirically, but to clarify how the equations and landscape structure encode the central mechanism of the model. Exploratory activity regenerates adaptive responsiveness, while reduced exploration and stabilization pressures gradually erode the mobility required for long-run traversal across rugged epistemic and institutional landscapes.

A.1
Socioeconomic Interpretations of Rugged Landscapes

The baseline framework uses stylized rugged landscapes to represent the interaction between stabilization pressures and exploratory mobility. The purpose is not to model any single cognitive, organizational, or institutional environment directly, but to characterize the general adaptive geometry governing movement across multiple locally reinforced configurations. Nevertheless, the effective potential function
V(x)
may be interpreted more concretely as a composite representation of socioeconomic and cognitive frictions constraining movement across alternative trajectories.

One possible decomposition is

V(x)=\lambda_{C}C(x)+\lambda_{K}K(x)+\lambda_{I}I(x),

(13)

where
C(x)
captures coordination costs associated with deviating from established standards, routines, or conventions,
K(x)
captures cognitive strain or learning effort associated with unfamiliar conceptual structures, and
I(x)
captures institutional switching costs generated by accumulated infrastructure, organizational routines, legitimacy constraints, or path dependence. The weights
\lambda_{C},\lambda_{K},\lambda_{I}\geq 0
determine the relative importance of each friction in shaping the effective landscape.

Under this interpretation, local minima correspond to configurations minimizing combined coordination, cognitive, and institutional strain. Steep barriers separating basins represent retraining burdens, organizational switching costs, legitimacy pressures, accumulated infrastructure dependence, or cognitive effort associated with traversing unfamiliar conceptual regions. Ruggedness therefore emerges when multiple stabilization mechanisms reinforce competing local configurations simultaneously.

In technological environments, ruggedness may arise through network externalities, installed infrastructure, retraining costs, and standardization pressures. The historical competition between VHS and Sony Betamax illustrates how early coordination advantages can generate self-reinforcing barriers that progressively constrain alternative trajectories
[
6
]
. In scientific environments, ruggedness may reflect cognitive specialization, disciplinary boundaries, and professional incentives that increase the difficulty of traversing distant epistemic regions. Recent evidence documenting substantial “pivot penalties” in scientific research is consistent with this interpretation
[
27
]
. In organizational environments, ruggedness may arise from legacy workflows, accumulated routines, managerial incentives, compliance structures, and the costs of retraining personnel around alternative practices.

This interpretation is not intended to replace the general potential function used in the main text. Rather, it clarifies how the abstract geometry of
V(x)
can be grounded in empirically meaningful frictions. Different empirical settings may place different weights on coordination costs, cognitive strain, and institutional switching barriers, while the general adaptive mechanism remains unchanged: stabilization pressures pull systems toward nearby low-strain configurations, while adaptive responsiveness determines whether systems retain sufficient mobility to traverse the barriers separating alternative regions of the landscape.

A.2
Numerical Landscape Specification

The numerical illustrations use stylized rugged landscapes designed to generate multiple locally stable basins and uneven traversal barriers consistent with the general framework developed in the main text. The simulations are intended to illustrate exploratory traversal, metastable trapping, responsiveness collapse, and hysteresis rather than to calibrate any particular institutional or cognitive environment.

The effective potential function used in the numerical simulations is given by

V(x,y)=0.18\sin(2.5x)\cos(2.2y)+0.12\sin(4.1x+1.3y)+0.08\cos(3.7x-2.4y)+0.015(x^{2}+y^{2}).

The sinusoidal components generate irregular local basins and uneven traversal barriers, while the quadratic term prevents trajectories from diverging without bound. The resulting topology produces a rugged landscape containing multiple locally reinforced configurations separated by barriers of varying height and curvature. Alternative specifications producing comparable ruggedness generate qualitatively similar trapping, collapse, and hysteresis dynamics.

A.3
Bounded Responsiveness Dynamics

The main text normalizes adaptive responsiveness so that

z(t)\in[0,1].

The lower bound
z=0
represents complete rigidity. The upper bound
z=1
represents maximal exploratory responsiveness. This normalization is useful because responsiveness is interpreted as a stock of adaptive mobility rather than an unbounded quantity.

The responsiveness law is

\dot{z}(t)=\eta z(t)^{\gamma}s_{t}(1-z(t))-\rho z(t)(1-s_{t}).

The first term captures responsiveness regeneration through exploratory activity. The factor
s_{t}
means that responsiveness increases only when the system engages in exploratory activity. The factor
(1-z(t))
imposes saturation. When responsiveness is already high, the marginal increase in responsiveness becomes small. This prevents the model from generating unbounded responsiveness.

The second term captures responsiveness erosion. When exploratory activity is low, stabilization, routinization, learned dependence, and institutional lock-in reduce responsiveness. The term
z(t)
implies that erosion is proportional to the existing stock of responsiveness. A system with no remaining responsiveness cannot lose further responsiveness.

The parameter
\gamma\in(0,1]
governs nonlinear regeneration. It determines how existing responsiveness affects the capacity to regenerate future responsiveness. When
\gamma=1
, responsiveness regeneration is proportional to the current responsiveness stock. When
\gamma<1
, regeneration remains active even at relatively low responsiveness levels. This produces smooth but persistent recovery dynamics and allows collapse and recovery paths to differ.

A.4
AI Assistance and Exploratory Activity

Exploratory activity is modeled as

s_{t}=s_{0}-\alpha AI_{t},

where
s_{0}
is baseline exploratory engagement,
AI_{t}
is AI assistance intensity, and
\alpha>0
measures the degree to which AI substitutes for costly exploratory activity.

Substituting into the responsiveness law yields

\dot{z}(t)=\eta z(t)^{\gamma}(s_{0}-\alpha AI_{t})(1-z(t))-\rho z(t)\left[1-(s_{0}-\alpha AI_{t})\right].

This equation gives the central comparative statics. Differentiating with respect to
AI_{t}
gives

\frac{\partial\dot{z}(t)}{\partial AI_{t}}=-\alpha\eta z(t)^{\gamma}(1-z(t))-\alpha\rho z(t).

For
z(t)\in(0,1)
and
\alpha>0
, this derivative is negative:

\frac{\partial\dot{z}(t)}{\partial AI_{t}}<0.

Thus, increasing AI assistance lowers the growth rate of adaptive responsiveness whenever AI assistance substitutes for exploratory activity.

A.5
Steady States and Collapse Regions

For fixed AI intensity, steady states satisfy

0=\eta z^{\gamma}s(1-z)-\rho z(1-s),

where

s=s_{0}-\alpha AI.

The low-responsiveness region becomes relevant when
s
is small enough that erosion dominates regeneration. In that case, responsiveness converges toward low values and the system loses exploratory mobility. The main text represents this by introducing a critical responsiveness level

z_{c}\in(0,1).

When the long-run responsiveness level falls below
z_{c}
, the system enters a low-mobility regime.

The threshold
z_{c}
should not be interpreted as a universal constant. It is a reduced-form representation of the minimum responsiveness needed to traverse the relevant landscape. In smoother environments, the required threshold may be low. In rugged environments with many local basins and steep barriers, the required threshold is higher.

A.6
Rugged Landscapes and Metastable Trapping

The state dynamics are

dx_{t}=-z(t)\nabla V(x_{t})\,dt+\sigma(t)dW_{t}.

The potential
V(x)
represents effective stabilization pressures. Low values of
V(x)
correspond to locally reinforced configurations. High values correspond to unstable or weakly reinforced configurations. The model does not assume that agents maximize
V
or directly optimize a known objective. Rather,
V
summarizes the aggregate effect of local updating, reinforcement, coordination, and institutional stabilization.

In the simulations, the rugged landscape is represented by a stylized two-dimensional potential,

V(x,y)=0.12(x^{2}+y^{2})-1.40\exp\left(-\frac{(x+2.1)^{2}+(y+1.8)^{2}}{0.65}\right)-1.25\exp\left(-\frac{(x-1.4)^{2}+(y-1.0)^{2}}{0.80}\right)

-0.95\exp\left(-\frac{(x+0.3)^{2}+(y-1.7)^{2}}{0.50}\right)+0.18\sin(2.5x)\cos(2.2y).

The quadratic term confines the landscape. The Gaussian terms create local basins. The sinusoidal term adds small-scale ruggedness. This functional form is not intended as a literal empirical landscape. It is a stylized object used to illustrate how low responsiveness increases local trapping.

The numerical trajectory follows the Euler discretization

x_{t+\Delta t}=x_{t}-z\nabla V(x_{t})\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\varepsilon_{t},

where

\varepsilon_{t}\sim N(0,I).

Higher
z
produces greater adaptive mobility. Lower
z
confines the trajectory near local basins.

A.7
Hysteresis Simulation

The hysteresis simulation uses the same bounded responsiveness law,

\dot{z}(t)=\eta z(t)^{\gamma}s_{t}(1-z(t))-\rho z(t)(1-s_{t}).

AI assistance is varied over time. It first rises gradually, then remains high, and then declines. This creates a collapse path and a recovery path. Because responsiveness regeneration depends on existing responsiveness, the recovery path differs from the collapse path.

The simulation uses the common parameter values

s_{0}=1,\qquad\alpha=0.65,\qquad\eta=2.5,\qquad\rho=1.0,\qquad\gamma=0.75.

The AI path is

AI_{t}=\begin{cases}AI_{\max}\dfrac{\tau}{0.40},&\tau\leq 0.40,\\[6.0pt]
AI_{\max},&0.40<\tau\leq 0.60,\\[6.0pt]
AI_{\max}\left(1-\dfrac{\tau-0.60}{0.40}\right),&\tau>0.60,\end{cases}

where

\tau=\frac{t}{T}.

The figure in the main text plots
z(t)
against
AI_{t}
. The increasing and decreasing paths do not coincide because responsiveness is a state variable. This is the source of hysteresis.

A.8
Exploratory Mobility on Rugged Landscapes

The second panel of the hysteresis figure measures exploratory mobility on a generic rugged landscape. The simulation uses the same rugged potential
V(x,y)
as the trapping figure. AI reduces responsiveness according to

z_{t}=\max\{0.05,1-0.70AI_{t}\}.

The lower bound prevents complete numerical freezing. The trajectory is simulated using the stochastic landscape dynamics. Exploratory mobility is measured as the rolling mean of step sizes:

M_{t}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=t-m+1}^{t}\|x_{j}-x_{j-1}\|.

This mobility measure is noisier than the bounded responsiveness stock because it depends on stochastic realizations and local landscape geometry. The purpose of the panel is to show that path dependence also appears in generic rugged landscapes, even when no special double-well structure is imposed.

Figure 6:

Path dependence in exploratory mobility on a stochastic rugged epistemic landscape. AI assistance is gradually increased, held high, and then reduced while adaptive responsiveness scales exploratory traversal across a rugged potential surface containing multiple locally stable basins and uneven traversal barriers. The trajectories illustrate stochastic movement under declining exploratory responsiveness, where reduced mobility progressively confines the system near existing attractors and lowers the probability of successful basin escape. Although noisier than the bounded responsiveness dynamics presented in the main text, the rugged landscape generates qualitatively similar persistence effects: once responsiveness deteriorates sufficiently, exploratory mobility recovers asymmetrically even after AI intensity declines. The figure therefore illustrates that metastable trapping, delayed recovery, and path-dependent rigidity emerge endogenously from rugged adaptive geometry itself rather than requiring a specialized double-well responsiveness structure.

A.9
Welfare Illustration

The welfare illustration uses

W_{t}=P(AI_{t})+\beta z(t)^{\nu}.

The productivity term is

P(AI)=P_{\max}(1-\exp(-kAI)).

This captures increasing but concave local productivity gains from AI assistance.

The responsiveness term is nonlinear:

\beta z(t)^{\nu},\qquad\nu>1.

The exponent
\nu
captures the idea that highly responsive systems are disproportionately valuable for long-run adaptation. A system with high responsiveness can traverse unfamiliar regions of the landscape, respond to shocks, and avoid local trapping. A system with low responsiveness may remain locally efficient but globally fragile.

The private objective assigns a lower weight to responsiveness than the social objective. This captures the externality. Individual agents internalize local productivity gains but do not fully internalize the contribution of exploratory activity to system-level adaptive responsiveness. Consequently, the private optimum generally involves greater AI assistance and lower exploration than the social optimum.

A.10
Interpretation of Numerical Illustrations

The numerical figures are illustrative rather than calibrated. They are designed to show the qualitative implications of the model. The common parameter values are chosen to make the mechanisms visually transparent. The key qualitative patterns are robust to moderate parameter variation.

The figures illustrate five linked mechanisms. First, AI assistance reduces responsiveness by substituting for exploratory activity. Second, sufficiently large reductions in responsiveness push the system toward a low-mobility collapse region. Third, low responsiveness increases trapping on rugged landscapes. Fourth, nonlinear responsiveness dynamics generate hysteresis because recovery differs from collapse. Fifth, decentralized adoption can underprovide socially valuable exploration because private agents do not fully internalize responsiveness externalities.

Together, these mechanisms formalize the main claim of the paper. AI-assisted optimization may improve local performance while reducing the exploratory responsiveness required for long-run collective adaptation.
