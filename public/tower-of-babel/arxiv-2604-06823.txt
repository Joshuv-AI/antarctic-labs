On spectrum of sample correlated matrices from large fold tensor vectors
Wangjun Yuan

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the limiting spectral distribution of the sample correlation matrix, whose sample vectors are k k -fold tensor products of n n -dimensional vectors with i.i.d. entries. We focus on the limiting regime n , k → ∞ n,k\to\infty with k = o ⁡ ( n ) k=o(n) , and we show that the limiting spectral distribution is the Marčenko-Pastur law. As a consequence, we show that the limiting spectral distribution of the Whishart matrix from the k k -fold tensor product of independent uniformly distributed unit vectors in ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} is the Marčenko-Pastur law.

1
Introduction

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\}
be a family of i.i.d. centered random variables with unit variance. Let
\mathbf{y}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a random vector and
\{\mathbf{y}_{\alpha}^{(l)}:1\leq\alpha\leq m,1\leq l\leq k\}
be a family of i.i.d. copies of
\mathbf{y}
.
Here, the notation
\otimes
is the tensor outer product.
For
1\leq\alpha\leq m
, we define the
k
-fold tensor product by
Y_{\alpha}=\mathbf{y}_{\alpha}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{y}_{\alpha}^{(k)}
, which is a multi-dimensional array with entry

\displaystyle\left(Y_{\alpha}\right)_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})}=\prod_{l=1}^{k}\left(\mathbf{y}_{\alpha}^{(l)}\right)_{j_{l}},\quad\forall 1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\leq n.

We refer readers to
[
19
]
for more details of tensor outer product.
We identify each
Y_{\alpha}
as an
n^{k}
-dimensional vector, and we consider the normalized vector
Y_{\alpha}/\|Y_{\alpha}\|
, where
\|Y_{\alpha}\|
is the
L^{2}
-norm of the vector
Y_{\alpha}
. We set
Y=(Y_{1}/\|Y_{1}\|,\ldots,Y_{m}/\|Y_{m}\|)
, which is a
n^{k}\times m
matrix. Let
\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots\}
be a sequence of positive real numbers, and
\Lambda_{m}=\mathrm{diag}(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m})
be the diagonal matrix. We consider the following
n^{k}\times n^{k}
Hermitian matrix

\displaystyle M_{n,k,m}=Y\Lambda_{m}Y^{*}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\dfrac{Y_{\alpha}Y_{\alpha}^{*}}{\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}},

(1.1)

which is the sum of
m
independent rank-
1
Hermitian matrices.

For an
N\times N
matrix
A
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}
, we define the
empirical spectral distribution
(ESD) function of
A
by

\displaystyle F^{A}(x)=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\bf 1}_{\lambda\leq x},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

We are interested in the large dimensional limit of
F^{M_{n,k,m}}
.
Throughout the paper, we always assume that the dimensional parameters
m,k
are functions of
n
, and grow towards infinity following the proportion

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{m}{n^{k}}\to c

(1.2)

for some constant
c\in(0,\infty)
.
We also assume that the sequence
\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots\}
satisfies the following limiting moment condition

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tau_{j}^{q}=m_{q}^{(\tau)}<\infty

(1.3)

with
|m_{q}^{{(\tau)}}|\leq A^{q}q^{q}
for some positive constant
A
, for all
q\in\mathbb{N}
.

We are interested in the limiting ESD function
F^{M_{n,k,m}}
of (
1.1
) when the dimension
n\to\infty
.
Our study of model (
1.1
) is motivated by problems in both statistics and quantum information theory.

In statistics, it is interesting to understand the unknown population distribution from large samples. The theory to address this problem is hypothesis testing.
The asymptotic behavior of large samples is derived under certain hypotheses on the population.
Random matrix theory is a very important tool in multivariate statistics, and the spectral properties in high-dimensional cases are of particular interest.

The application of random matrix theory to hypothesis testing dates back to at least
[
17
]
, where the population covariance matrix is investigated via the sample covariance matrix.
Let’s introduce the
sample covariance matrix
as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{n,k,m}=\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}=\dfrac{1}{n^{k}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}Y_{\alpha}Y_{\alpha}^{*},

(1.4)

where
\widetilde{Y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}^{k}}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m})
.
It is an
n^{k}\times n^{k}
Hermitian matrix.
We would like to remark that the model (
1.4
) is also known as Wishart matrix in some literature.
The paper
[
17
]
considers the simplest case
k=1
, and derives the limiting empirical spectral distribution (LSD), which is known as the Marcěnko-Pastur law. In the case of
\tau_{\alpha}\equiv 1
, the Marcěnko-Pastur law has the density function

\displaystyle p_{MP}(x)=\dfrac{\sqrt{((1+\sqrt{c})^{2}-x)(x-(1-\sqrt{c})^{2})}}{2\pi x}{\bf 1}_{[(1-\sqrt{c})^{2},(1+\sqrt{c})^{2}]}(x)+(1-c)\delta_{0}(dx){\bf 1}_{0<c<1}.

(1.5)

Further details can be found in
[
2
,
4
]
.
Subsequently, numerous works on the sample covariance matrices have emerged in the literature, including
[
5
,
18
,
20
]
, and the references therein.
Eventually, for the case
k=1,\tau_{\alpha}\equiv 1
, the necessary and sufficient condition that the Marčenko-Pastur law serves as the limiting distribution of
F^{\widetilde{M}_{n,k,m}}
is carried out in
[
21
]
.

In addition to the sample covariance matrix, the sample correlation matrix, which reveals the correlation on the population, is another fundamental tool in multivariate statistics.
The model (
1.1
) we are interested in is known as the
sample correlation matrix
, and it provides information of Pearson
correlation for multivariate data.
The study of the sample correlation matrix (
1.1
) can be dated back to
[
14
]
, where the case
k=1,\tau_{\alpha}\equiv 1
is considered. The LSD of (
1.1
) is still the Marcěnko-Pastur law (
1.5
).
Since then, a rich literature on the sample correlation matrices has developed, including
[
12
,
9
,
22
,
23
,
13
,
6
]
and the references therein.
Under the same setting of
k=1,\tau_{\alpha}\equiv 1
, the necessary and sufficient condition for the convergence of
F^{M_{n,k,m}}
to the Marčenko-Pastur law in the isotropic model was carried out in
[
11
]
if the underlying vector
Y_{\alpha}
is a linear combination of a random vector with i.i.d. entries, and in
[
10
]
for the isotropic model.

The cases
k\geq 2
are significantly different from the case
k=1
. The tensor structure appears when
k\geq 2
. Thus, in statistics, the theory with
k\geq 2
may be applied to the samples from a population with tensor structure.

The second motivation to study the models (
1.1
) and (
1.4
) comes from quantum information theory.
We start with a classical ball-to-bin probability problem.
We distribute
m
balls randomly into
n
bins and see how many balls are in each bin. The problem can be rephrased as the spectrum of the
\widetilde{M}_{n,1,m}
when
Y_{\alpha}
is chosen randomly from the canonical basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\tau_{\alpha}\equiv 1
.
The canonical basis corresponds to the bins, while
Y_{\alpha}
is understood as the
\alpha
-th ball.
We refer interested readers to
[
24
]
for more details.
The quantum analog of this classical problem is the spectrum of
\widetilde{M}_{n,1,m}
if the vector
Y_{\alpha}
is picked randomly from the unit sphere
S^{2n-1}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
A modified version of the quantum problem, which was introduced in
[
1
]
, is to consider the matrix
M_{n,k,m}
with the vector
Y_{1}
from the random product states in
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes k}
. When
k
and
m/n^{k}
are fixed, and the base vector
\mathbf{y}
is Gaussian,
[
1
]
established the convergence in expectation of the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) towards the Marčenko-Pastur law (
1.5
) as
n\to+\infty
by computing the moments.

In the realm of quantum physics and quantum information theory, it is natural to explore systems with a multitude of quantum states, i.e. consider the case that
k
is large. See, for example,
[
25
,
7
]
.
Moreover, in mathematics, the tensor structure of
Y_{\alpha}
leads to a dependence on the entries of
Y_{\alpha}
and non-unitary invariance. This phenomenon becomes strong if the
k
is large.

Recently, the LSD for the
k
-fold tensor model (
1.4
) was explored in
[
16
]
for real random vector
\mathbf{y}
when
k
is large with the constraint
k=o(n)
. In the special case where
\tau_{\alpha}\equiv 1
, the LSD is exactly the Marčenko-Pastur law (
1.5
). The fluctuation of the ESD around the LSD is also established for a class of linear spectral statistics following the approach of
[
15
]
when
k=2
.
In a more recent study,
[
8
]
considered the complex case of
\mathbf{y}
with
k=O(n)
for the model (
1.4
). Under the limiting condition
k/n\to d
, the limiting moment sequence of the ESD of
M_{n,k,m}
is carried out by assuming the finiteness of all moments of
\xi_{1}
.
It is interesting that the limiting moment sequence obtained in
[
8
]
involves the fourth moment of
\xi_{1}
if
d>0
and
|\xi_{1}|>1
with positive probability. If
\tau_{\alpha}\equiv 1
, the resulting limiting moment sequence corresponds to the Marčenko-Pastur law (
1.5
) when either
d=0
or
d>0
and
|\xi_{1}|=1
a.s..
In the setting that
\xi_{1}
is complex and
|\xi_{1}|=1
a.s., the restriction of the growth of
k
is removed in
[
24
]
. The LSD of the matrix (
1.4
) is established, and it is exactly the Marčenko-Pastur law (
1.5
) whenever
\tau_{\alpha}\equiv 1
.

The paper focuses on the tensor sample correlation matrix. The number
k
of the tensor fold is large, with the following limiting constraint.

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{k}{n}=0.

(1.6)

To the best of our knowledge, the results in the present paper are the first on the limiting spectrum of the sample correlation matrix from large fold tensor vectors (
k\gg 1
).

The following theorem is the main result of the paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let the limiting conditions (
1.2
), (
1.3
) and (
1.6
) hold. Assume that for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
, the
p
-th moment
m_{p}=\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{1}|^{p}]<\infty
. Then there exists a probability distribution function
F_{c}
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}F^{M_{n,k,m}}=F_{c}

in probability.
Moreover, in the case that
\tau_{\alpha}\equiv 1
, the limiting distribution function
F_{c}
is exactly the Marčenko-Pastur law with density function (
1.5
).

We would like to mention that the two models (
1.1
) and (
1.4
) are normalized in different ways, and (
1.1
) is a case of the quantum analogous problem, noting that the columns of
Y
are unit vectors, while the columns of
\widetilde{Y}
are not.
Thus, the results from the model
\widetilde{M}_{n,k,m}
provide information of the quantum problem.
By choosing
\xi_{1}
to be a standard Gaussian random variable, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let the limiting conditions (
1.2
), (
1.3
) and (
1.6
) hold. Assume that the base random vector
\mathbf{y}
is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere
S^{2n-1}
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We set
Y^{\prime}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m})
and define

\displaystyle M_{n,k,m}^{\prime}=Y^{\prime}\Lambda_{m}Y^{\prime*}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}Y_{\alpha}^{\prime}Y_{\alpha}^{\prime*}.

Then there exists a probability distribution function
F_{c}
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}F^{M_{n,k,m}^{\prime}}=F_{c}

in probability.
Moreover, in the case that
\tau_{\alpha}\equiv 1
, the limiting distribution function
F_{c}
is exactly the Marčenko-Pastur law with density function (
1.5
).

3
Proofs

In this section, we develop the proof of Theorem
1.1
using the strategy of comparing
M_{n,k,m}
with
\widetilde{M}_{n,k,m}
. Then we explain how to deduce Corollary
1.1
from Theorem
1.1
.

We start with the following moment estimates on the norm of the random vector
Y_{\alpha}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
m_{4}=\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{1}|^{4}]<\infty
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}\right]=n^{k},\quad\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{4}\right]=n^{2k}\left(1+\dfrac{m_{4}-1}{n}\right)^{k}.

Proof.

As
\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\}
are i.i.d. centered random variables with unit variance, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{i}|^{2}\right]=n,

(3.1)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{y}\|^{4}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{i}|^{4}\right]+\sum_{i\not=j,i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{i}|^{2}|\xi_{j}|^{2}\right]=nm_{4}+n(n-1)=n^{2}\left(1+\dfrac{m_{4}-1}{n}\right).

(3.2)

Note that for
1\leq\alpha\leq m
, we have

\displaystyle\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}=\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}=1}^{n}\prod_{l=1}^{k}\left|\left(\mathbf{y}_{\alpha}^{(l)}\right)_{j_{l}}\right|^{2}=\prod_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{j_{l}=1}^{n}\left|\left(\mathbf{y}_{\alpha}^{(l)}\right)_{j_{l}}\right|^{2}=\prod_{l=1}^{k}\|\mathbf{y}_{\alpha}^{(l)}\|^{2}.

(3.3)

By independence, (
3.1
), (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}\right]=\prod_{l=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{y}_{\alpha}^{(l)}\|^{2}\right]=n^{k},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{4}\right]=\prod_{l=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{y}_{\alpha}^{(l)}\|^{4}\right]=n^{2k}\left(1+\dfrac{m_{4}-1}{n}\right)^{k}.

∎

The following result is the comparison of the distribution functions of
M_{n,k,m}
and
\widetilde{M}_{n,k,m}
, which is the key part of the proof to Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let the limiting conditions (
1.2
), (
1.3
) and (
1.6
) hold.
Assume that
m_{4}=\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{1}|^{4}]<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}L\left(F^{M_{n,k,m}},F^{\widetilde{M}_{n,k,m}}\right)=0

in probability.

Proof.

We apply Lemma
2.3
for the matrix
A=Y\sqrt{\Lambda_{m}}
and
B=\widetilde{Y}\sqrt{\Lambda_{m}}
, where
\sqrt{\Lambda_{m}}=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\tau_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{\tau_{m}})
. We have

\displaystyle L^{4}\left(F^{M_{n,k,m}},F^{\widetilde{M}_{n,k,m}}\right)\leq\dfrac{2}{n^{2k}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(Y-\widetilde{Y}\right)\Lambda_{m}\left(Y-\widetilde{Y}\right)^{*}\right)\mathrm{Tr}\left(Y\Lambda_{m}Y^{*}+\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}\right).

(3.4)

We handle the right hand side of (
3.4
) term by term.

Step 1.
In this step, we bound the second term

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n^{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(Y\Lambda_{m}Y^{*}+\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}\right)

in (
3.4
).

By (
1.1
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(Y\Lambda_{m}Y^{*}\right)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\dfrac{\mathrm{Tr}\left(Y_{\alpha}Y_{\alpha}^{*}\right)}{\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\dfrac{\mathrm{Tr}\left(Y_{\alpha}^{*}Y_{\alpha}\right)}{\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}.

(3.5)

By (
1.4
), we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}\right)=\dfrac{1}{n^{k}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\mathrm{Tr}\left(Y_{\alpha}Y_{\alpha}^{*}\right)=\dfrac{1}{n^{k}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2},

which together with Lemma
3.1
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left(\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}\right)\right]=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}\right)\right|^{2}\right]=\dfrac{1}{n^{2k}}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{4}\right]+\sum_{\alpha\not=\beta,\alpha,\beta=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\tau_{\beta}\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}\|Y_{\beta}\|^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}^{2}\left(1+\dfrac{m_{4}-1}{n}\right)^{k}+\sum_{\alpha\not=\beta,\alpha,\beta=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\tau_{\beta}=\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\right)^{2}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}^{2}\left(\left(1+\dfrac{m_{4}-1}{n}\right)^{k}-1\right).

Hence, for any
x>0
, by Markov inequality and Lemma
3.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}\right)-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\right|>x\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}\right)-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\right|^{2}\right]=\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}\right)\right|^{2}\right]-\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}^{2}\left(\left(1+\dfrac{m_{4}-1}{n}\right)^{k}-1\right).

(3.6)

Now we choose
x=n^{k}
in (
3
) and use (
3.5
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\dfrac{1}{n^{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(Y\Lambda_{m}Y^{*}+\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}\right)-\dfrac{2}{n^{k}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\right|>1\right)\leq\dfrac{1}{n^{2k}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}^{2}\left(\left(1+\dfrac{m_{4}-1}{n}\right)^{k}-1\right).

By Taylor expansion, when
n\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\left(1+\dfrac{m_{4}-1}{n}\right)^{k}-1\right)=\left(\exp\left(k\ln\left(1+\dfrac{m_{4}-1}{n}\right)\right)-1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\exp\left((m_{4}-1)\dfrac{k}{n}+O(kn^{-2})\right)-1\right)=\left((m_{4}-1)\dfrac{k}{n}+O(k^{2}n^{-2})\right),

(3.7)

which together with the convergence (
1.3
) implies

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\dfrac{1}{n^{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(Y\Lambda_{m}Y^{*}+\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}\right)-\dfrac{2}{n^{k}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\right|>1\right)<\infty.

By Borel-Cantelli’s lemma, with probability one, it holds eventually that

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{1}{n^{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(Y\Lambda_{m}Y^{*}+\widetilde{Y}\Lambda_{m}\widetilde{Y}^{*}\right)-\dfrac{2}{n^{k}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\right|\leq 1.

(3.8)

Step 2.
In this step, we establish the convergence of the first term

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n^{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(Y-\widetilde{Y}\right)\Lambda_{m}\left(Y-\widetilde{Y}\right)^{*}\right)

in (
3.4
).

We apply Lemma
2.2
with
A=(Y_{1}\ldots Y_{m})\sqrt{\Lambda_{m}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(Y-\widetilde{Y}\right)\Lambda_{m}\left(Y-\widetilde{Y}\right)^{*}\right)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}}{n^{k}}-1\right)-2\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{\|Y_{\alpha}\|}{\sqrt{n}^{k}}-1\right).

(3.9)

On one hand, we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{\|Y_{\alpha}\|}{\sqrt{n}^{k}}-1\right)\right|\leq\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\left|\dfrac{\|Y_{\alpha}\|}{\sqrt{n}^{k}}-1\right|\leq\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\left|\dfrac{\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}}{n^{k}}-1\right|.

(3.10)

On the other hand, we can see that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}}{n^{k}}-1\right)\right|\leq\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\left|\dfrac{\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}}{n^{k}}-1\right|.

(3.11)

Substituting (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) to (
3.9
), we get

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(Y-\widetilde{Y}\right)\Lambda_{m}\left(Y-\widetilde{Y}\right)^{*}\right)\right|\leq 3\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\left|\frac{\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}}{n^{k}}-1\right|.

(3.12)

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, Lemma
3.1
and independence, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\left|\dfrac{\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}}{n^{k}}-1\right|\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}^{2}\left|\dfrac{\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}}{n^{k}}-1\right|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\not=\beta,\alpha,\beta=1}^{m}\tau_{\alpha}\tau_{\beta}\left|\dfrac{\|Y_{\alpha}\|^{2}}{n^{k}}-1\right|\left|\dfrac{\|Y_{\beta}\|^{2}}{n^{k}}-1\right|\right]
