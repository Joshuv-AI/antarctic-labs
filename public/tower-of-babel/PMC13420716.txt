Association of Serum Chloride Concentration With All-Cause and Cardiovascular Mortality in Patients Undergoing Peritoneal Dialysis: The Fukuoka Peritoneal Dialysis Registry
Authors: Tomoya Hamashoji, Shoji Tsuneyoshi, Kazuhiro Okamura, Shunsuke Yamada, Kazuhiko Tsuruya, Toshiaki Nakano, Tetsuro Ago, Fukuoka Peritoneal Dialysis Registry (F-PDR) group

ABSTRACT

Mortality among patients receiving dialysis far exceeds that of the general population and remains high for hemodialysis (HD) and peritoneal dialysis (PD).1 In PD, cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death, accounting for approximately half of all deaths.2 Despite the identification of modifiable and potentially preventable risk factors, such as decline in residual kidney function,3 peritonitis,4 protein–energy wasting,5 and hyperphosphatemia,6 overall mortality among PD patients has changed only minimally.2 Therefore, further identification of risk factors is needed.

Chloride is the second most abundant serum electrolyte after sodium and plays a central role in body fluid regulation, electrolyte balance and electroneutrality, and acid–base homeostasis and is considered indispensable in the assessment of many pathological conditions.7 Low serum chloride concentration is associated with high all-cause mortality, even after adjustment for serum sodium concentration, in chronic heart failure,8 nondialysis chronic kidney disease,9 and HD cohorts.10 Previous studies8, 9, 10 have suggested that activation of the with-no-lysine kinase (WNK) pathway may mediate the association between low serum chloride concentration and poor prognosis, although the precise mechanism remains unclear.

In PD, studies in incident cohorts have yielded conflicting findings regarding the association between serum chloride and mortality, with some studies linking low chloride concentration to high mortality risk11 and others linking high chloride concentration to high risk.12 To our knowledge, this association has not been examined in maintenance PD cohorts. Importantly, because fluid status, electrolytes, acid–base parameters, and nutritional markers13, 14, 15 commonly fluctuate early after PD initiation, incident measurements, including serum chloride, may not reflect the steady state of maintenance PD. Accordingly, applying findings from incident PD cohorts to maintenance PD cohorts has inherent limitations. These considerations highlight an important knowledge gap regarding the clinical implications of serum chloride concentrations measured under stable, steady-state conditions in maintenance PD patients.

Clarifying this association is clinically relevant because chloride levels may be affected by dialysate buffer composition,16 diuretic use,7 and nutritional status.17 Identifying clinical characteristics consistently associated with chloride concentration could improve risk stratification and may serve as a basis for future interventional studies.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether serum chloride concentration in maintenance PD patients is independently associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. We hypothesized that lower serum chloride concentration would be associated with higher mortality risks, independently of serum sodium and other confounders.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics

We performed a multicenter retrospective cohort study using data from the Fukuoka Peritoneal Dialysis Registry (F-PDR), which enrolled patients aged ≥15 years treated with PD. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kyushu University Hospital and registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN000018902). All procedures adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from participants or, when applicable, from family members or other legally authorized representatives before registry enrollment. When written consent was unavailable, participation was allowed via an opt-out process announced on the study website.

Study Participants

Registry entry was permitted at any time during PD treatment and was not restricted to dialysis initiation. We included PD patients registered in the F-PDR from January 1, 2011 through December 31, 2020 across 20 centers in Japan. Patients were excluded if they had incomplete data at registry enrollment, experienced an event (death, transition to HD, or kidney transplantation) ≤30 days of PD initiation, or had a PD vintage of <90 days at F-PDR enrollment. Follow-up continued until any of the following: transition to HD, kidney transplantation, death while receiving PD, recovery of kidney function, transfer to a nonparticipating facility with unverifiable outcomes, or censoring on December 31, 2021.

Outcomes and Follow-up

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. The secondary outcome was cardiovascular mortality, defined as death attributable to myocardial infarction, heart failure, sudden cardiac death, stroke, or other cardiovascular causes. Survival time was measured from enrollment to the date of death or to the last known follow-up date for censored participants. Causes of death and outcome data were extracted from the patients’ medical records and adjudicated by 3 board-certified nephrologists. For patients who transferred to nonparticipating centers, outcome status was sought by mailed inquiries when feasible.

Exposure

The exposure was serum chloride concentration at enrollment obtained from routine clinical laboratory testing at each participating center. To balance estimate stability with the interpretability of ‘low, medium, and high,’ serum chloride was categorized into tertiles on the basis of the cohort distribution for analysis.

Demographic and Clinical Variables

Data were extracted from medical records at enrollment. Laboratory measurements were performed at each participating center’s clinical laboratory using routine methods. Demographics and clinical factors comprised age, sex, body mass index, smoking status (current/former/never), PD duration, primary kidney disease, history of CVD (composite of ischemic heart disease, heart failure, stroke, or peripheral artery disease), history of malignancy, diabetes mellitus, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, urine volume, and anuria (defined as urine volume ≤100 mL/d). Dialysis adequacy was assessed as total weekly renal and peritoneal Kt/V urea (K = urea clearance; t = treatment time; V = the patient’s body water volume). Laboratory measurements comprised blood hemoglobin and serum concentrations of total protein, albumin, uric acid, sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphorus, intact parathyroid hormone, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and C-reactive protein. Medication variables comprised the use of antihypertensive agents, diuretics, phosphate binders, icodextrin dialysate, and bicarbonate-buffered dialysate. The electrolyte and buffer composition of the PD solutions used by the participants at enrollment is provided in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as medians with interquartile ranges and categorical variables as numbers with percentages. Trends across serum chloride tertiles were examined using the Jonckheere–Terpstra test for continuous variables and the Cochran–Armitage trend test for categorical variables. Kaplan–Meier survival curves by serum chloride tertile were generated for outcome visualization and compared using the log-rank test.

Missing covariates were handled by multiple imputation by chained equations under a missing at random assumption.18 Imputation models included all variables in the primary analysis. We generated 50 imputed datasets with 20 iterations each and combined estimates using Rubin’s rules. We applied the same imputation model across all covariate-adjusted analyses.

Associations between serum chloride concentration and outcomes were quantified with Cox proportional hazards models, yielding hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three multivariable models were prespecified. Model 1 adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 also included body mass index, log-transformed PD duration, history of CVD, diabetes mellitus, systolic blood pressure, log-transformed urine volume, log-transformed renal Kt/V urea, blood hemoglobin, serum albumin, serum potassium, serum phosphorus, and log-transformed serum C-reactive protein. Model 3 further adjusted for serum sodium. To limit confounding by indication, medication use and PD prescription variables were not included in the primary models. The proportional hazards assumption19 was assessed visually using plots of scaled Schoenfeld residuals over time and log–log survival plots across chloride tertiles. As an additional analysis on an absolute time scale, we estimated restricted mean survival time20 at 1,095 days, adjusted for the same covariates as those in the Cox proportional hazards models.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess robustness. First, Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models were fitted to account for competing events and to estimate subdistribution HRs.21 Competing risks were defined as follows: for all-cause mortality, competing events comprised transition to HD, kidney transplantation, and recovery of kidney function; for cardiovascular mortality, competing events comprised noncardiovascular death, transition to HD, kidney transplantation, and recovery of kidney function. Second, a shared frailty Cox proportional hazards model22 with facility-level random effects was fitted to account for unmeasured center-level heterogeneity. Third, an expanded adjustment model was used for all-cause mortality, incorporating the use of diuretics (loop, thiazide, spironolactone), renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers), calcium-based phosphate binders, icodextrin, and bicarbonate-buffered dialysate, in addition to the model 3 covariates. Fourth, serum chloride concentration was modeled as quartiles and analyzed with Cox and Fine–Gray models.

We prespecified subgroup categories using clinically meaningful cutoffs for age (65 years) and body mass index (25 kg/m2), while other continuous variables were dichotomized at the cohort median. Subgroups included age, sex, body mass index, PD duration, history of CVD, diabetes, anuria, renal Kt/V urea, serum albumin, and serum sodium. Within each subgroup, adjusted HRs and 95% CIs were obtained from model 3 using the highest chloride tertile as the reference. Effect modification was assessed by including multiplicative interaction terms and testing them with a pooled Wald test.

Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing; www.r-project.org). This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement for cohort studies.23

Results

Participants’ Characteristics at Enrollment

Figure 1 shows the selection of the analytic cohort. Of the 1,226 patients registered in the F-PDR, we excluded 5 individuals with incomplete data at enrollment and 4 who experienced an event (death, transition to HD, or kidney transplantation) within 30 days of PD initiation. We further excluded 168 patients whose PD vintage at enrollment was <90 days. The final analytic cohort comprised 1,049 patients. The distribution of serum chloride concentration among the analytic cohort is shown in Fig 2. Using a cutoff value based on a prior study,24 hyperchloremia (≥109 mmol/L) was observed in only 13 patients (1.2%). Patients were categorized into tertiles of serum chloride concentration as follows: T1 (<96 mmol/L, n = 341), T2 (96-99 mmol/L, n = 330), and T3 (> 99 mmol/L, n = 378). Table 1 summarizes the characteristics at enrollment by serum chloride tertiles. The cohort had a median age of 66.0 years (interquartile range, 55.0-73.0), and 66.3% were male. Compared with the highest tertile (T3), patients in the lowest serum chloride tertile (T1) tended to have a longer PD duration, and anuria, the use of loop diuretics, thiazide diuretics, calcium-based phosphate binders, and icodextrin were more common. In contrast, renal Kt/V urea, blood hemoglobin, serum albumin, serum sodium, and serum potassium concentrations were lower, and the use of bicarbonate-buffered PD solutions was less frequent in T1 compared with T3.

Survival Outcomes

Over a median follow-up of 731 days (interquartile range, 365-1,121 days), 179 patients (17.1%) died, including 59 (5.6%) from cardiovascular causes. During follow-up, 405 patients (38.6%) transitioned to HD, 45 (4.3%) received a kidney transplant, and 2 (0.2%) recovered renal function. All-cause and cardiovascular mortality rates increased significantly across decreasing tertiles of serum chloride concentration (Fig 3).

Survival curves for (A) all-cause mortality and (B) cardiovascular mortality by serum chloride tertiles (T1-T3). Survival functions were estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves, and differences among the 3 groups were compared with the log-rank test. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association Between Serum Chloride and Mortality

In the multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards model (model 3), compared with T3 (>99 mmol/L), T1 (<96 mmol/L) was associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality (HR, 2.59; 95% CI, 1.55-4.34) and cardiovascular mortality (HR, 3.33; 95% CI, 1.28-8.66) (Table 2). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed visually using log–log survival plots and Schoenfeld residual plots, and no major departures from proportionality were observed. Restricted mean survival time was estimated at 1,095 days. Compared with T3, T1 had a significantly shorter average survival time to all-cause death by 109.4 days (95% CI, −189.4 to −29.4). For cardiovascular death, the average free-from-cardiovascular-death time was likewise significantly shorter when comparing T1 and T3, by 55.9 days (95% CI, −106.2 to −5.5) (Table S2).

Sensitivity Analyses

In the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models, compared with T3 (>99 mmol/L), T1 (<96 mmol/L) was associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality (subdistribution HR, 2.67; 95% CI, 1.60-4.44) and cardiovascular mortality (subdistribution HR, 2.80; 95% CI, 1.03-7.56) (Table S3). Similar results were observed in the shared frailty Cox proportional hazards models with facility-level random effects; compared with T3, T1 was associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality (HR, 2.58; 95% CI, 1.53-4.36) and cardiovascular mortality (HR, 3.16; 95% CI, 1.20-8.30) (Table S4). Furthermore, when the models were additionally adjusted for treatment-related factors, namely the use of diuretics, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors, calcium-based phosphate binders, icodextrin, and bicarbonate-buffered dialysate, the associations between low serum chloride concentration and high all-cause mortality remained unchanged (Table S5). Finally, when serum chloride concentration was categorized into quartiles and analyzed as for the primary models, the results were again consistent: relative to the highest quartile, the lowest quartile was associated with significantly higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risks (Table S6).

Subgroup Analyses

Associations of serum chloride tertiles with all-cause (Table 3) and cardiovascular mortality (Table 4) were generally consistent across subgroups. No significant interactions were observed for any subgroup when interactions between chloride tertiles and each subgroup factor were formally tested.

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort of maintenance PD patients, lower serum chloride concentration (<96 mmol/L) was independently associated with higher risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality compared with higher serum chloride concentrations. We performed this study in maintenance PD patients because previous reports on the association between serum chloride and survival in PD focused on incident cohorts and produced inconsistent findings; results were not necessarily generalizable to maintenance cohorts. This study adds to the limited evidence by showing an association between serum chloride concentration and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in maintenance PD patients, which remained consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses. Accordingly, serum chloride concentration may serve as a component of risk assessment in maintenance PD.

Although previous studies in PD patients reported associations between serum chloride concentration and mortality, the findings were inconsistent. Some studies reported a high risk with low chloride concentration11 and others reported a high risk with high serum chloride concentration.12 Moreover, because previous studies focused on incident PD cohorts, generalizability of the results to outpatient maintenance cohorts is limited. By enrolling maintenance PD patients from multiple centers, our study addressed these limitations and improved the applicability of the findings to similar clinical settings. The robustness of the main results was further supported by analyses that are sensitive to key assumptions, including Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models and shared frailty models.

In our cohort, PD duration also differed across serum chloride tertiles. Longer PD duration may reflect lower residual kidney function, greater dependence on PD, and more intensive PD prescriptions, all of which could contribute to lower serum chloride concentration and worse outcomes. However, the association between lower serum chloride concentration and the outcomes remained after adjustment for PD duration in model 2, and the overall pattern was broadly consistent in subgroup analyses stratified by PD duration. These findings suggest that the observed association was not explained solely by PD duration.

The discrepancy with reports showing an association between high chloride concentration and high mortality risk may be attributable to differences in case mix; specifically, whether incident or maintenance PD cohorts were analyzed and whether users of diuretics or patients with prior CVD were excluded. Additionally, pathophysiology likely differs by treatment phase: in incident PD, hyperchloremic acidosis may predominate, whereas in maintenance PD, chloride depletion/alkalosis may predominate.25,26 In our cohort, compared with T3 (>99 mmol/L), T1 (<96 mmol/L) comprised higher proportions of patients with a CVD history and loop diuretic use (Table 1). Inclusion of such patients may emphasize pathways in which volume overload and heart failure prompt diuretic use, leading to chloride loss and metabolic alkalosis7 and potentially strengthening observed associations with lower chloride concentration. Furthermore, excluding diuretic users or those with prior CVD could attenuate this confounding or mediating pathway and render risk signals linked to higher chloride, such as hyperchloremic acidosis or sodium chloride load/volume excess,7 more apparent. Overall, differences in study timing and case mix could help explain, at least in part, the divergent directions reported across studies.

The low serum chloride concentration observed in the present study is mechanistically plausible in PD. First, sustained alkali loading from lactate- or bicarbonate-buffered dialysate and exogenous alkali (such as calcium carbonate) can overcorrect serum bicarbonate, lowering chloride on an anion-balance basis and yielding a chloride- (saline-) resistant metabolic alkalosis.7,16,27,28 Second, true chloride depletion may occur through sodium and chloride losses with PD and diuretics, reduced intake or malabsorption, and declining residual renal function.7,17,25,28,29 Third, dilutional hypochloremia can result from excess free water retention, often with worsening residual renal function and heart failure, lowering chloride in parallel with sodium.29,30 These pathways commonly coexist and interact in individual patients. In light of these mechanisms, our findings may serve as a basis for generating hypotheses in future PD management strategies, such as moderating alkali load (through dialysate buffer selection), optimizing diuretic use, and addressing potential chloride depletion, while recognizing that causal effects and the efficacy of such interventions remain to be established.

Assuming patients are non-anuric, several plausible pathways could link low serum chloride to higher mortality risk. (1) WNK pathway activation: Low intracellular chloride disinhibits WNK1/4, activating sporulation-specific protein 1-related proline/alanine-rich kinase (SPAK)/oxidative stress-responsive kinase 1 and upregulating sodium-chloride cotransporter/sodium-potassium-chloride cotransporter (NKCC), which promotes distal renal sodium reabsorption, extracellular volume expansion, and diuretic resistance, which are features implicated in cardiorenal syndrome.31, 32, 33, 34, 35 NKCC1 is also expressed in cardiomyocytes. Excessive WNK signaling may disturb intracellular pH homeostasis, reduce contractility, and promote arrhythmogenesis, consistent with observational links between hypochloremia and incident atrial fibrillation.36, 37, 38, 39 (2) Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activation: Low chloride augments macula densa sodium chloride sensing (via NKCC2) and tubuloglomerular feedback, stimulating renin and downstream renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system components. This activation increases systemic/intraglomerular pressures, sodium/water reabsorption, cardiac hypertrophy/fibrosis, oxidative stress, and proinflammatory signaling.40, 41, 42, 43, 44 (3) Host defenses: Low chloride can limit substrate for neutrophil myeloperoxidase, reducing hypochlorous acid generation and dependent neutrophil extracellular trap formation, potentially impairing bactericidal capacity.45 These cardiac and immune pathways are supported mainly by experimental data; causal links to serum chloride in humans remain unproven, and our findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating.

This study has several strengths. First, the multicenter enrollment captured heterogeneity in case mix and practice patterns, which may support external validity and improved statistical precision. Second, focusing the analysis on maintenance PD patients reduced confounding specific to the PD initiation phase, during which rapid physiological changes and treatment modifications occur, and better aligned the cohort with the steady state clinical context in which serum chloride is typically managed. Accordingly, the interpretation of chloride as a prognostic marker is less susceptible to early-phase biases. Third, the primary findings were consistent across complementary analytical frameworks (restricted mean survival time, Fine–Gray subdistribution models, and shared frailty models). Additionally, the findings remained unchanged after additional adjustment for treatment-related factors (medication use and PD prescriptions), indicating robustness regarding modeling assumptions and potential treatment confounding.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, serum chloride concentration and covariates were measured only at enrollment; thus, time-varying exposures and confounding could not be captured. Second, laboratory tests were not centrally measured, which may have introduced measurement error in serum chloride and other laboratory covariates because of interfacility and temporal variability in assay methods and calibration. Third, serum bicarbonate concentration was unavailable, preventing a full assessment of acid–base status, including metabolic alkalosis or acidosis. Accordingly, we could not evaluate whether acid–base imbalance mediates or modifies the association between serum chloride concentration and the clinical outcomes of interest. However, previous studies suggest that serum chloride concentration may be associated with risk independently of serum bicarbonate concentration.9,11 Fourth, as an observational study, residual and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. Fifth, outcomes (including causes of death) were centrally adjudicated against site determinations on the basis of source documents, which reduced but did not eliminate misclassification.

In conclusion, in this multicenter cohort of maintenance PD patients, compared with high concentration, low serum chloride concentration was associated with higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risks. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and add to the emerging evidence linking low serum chloride concentration to adverse outcomes in maintenance PD. Incorporating serum chloride concentration into routine assessment may be reasonable, but prospective studies are needed to determine the clinical utility.
