Abstract
. Start with four digits, arrange them in both descending and ascending order, subtract, and repeat. This simple process is known as the Kaprekar routine, famous in base ten for sending every nonconstant four-digit string to 6174 6174 . We show that in every odd base B > 3 B>3 , the four-digit Kaprekar map has an unexpectedly rigid structure. After at most three iterations, every nonconstant orbit enters an explicit triangular region 𝒯 B \mathcal{T}_{B} , and on this region the map is conjugate to projective doubling: { [ r ] , [ s ] } ⟼ { [ 2 ​ r ] , [ 2 ​ s ] } . \{[r],[s]\}\longmapsto\{[2r],[2s]\}. This gives a complete finite description of all nonconstant terminal cycles, including an explicit formula for their lengths and counts. In particular, the longest terminal cycle has length at most ( B − 1 ) / 2 (B-1)/2 , and equality can occur only when B B is prime. For primes p > 5 p>5 , equality occurs precisely when the least positive m m with 2 m ≡ ± 1 ( mod p ) 2^{m}\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p} is m = ( p − 1 ) / 2 m=(p-1)/2 . The results proved here were first formulated by Schwartz and Thakur. As a test case for AI-assisted formal mathematics, AxiomProver produced Lean/mathlib formalizations of these results.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Background

Kaprekar’s four-digit routine looks, at first, like a numerical magic trick.
Take four digits, arrange them in decreasing order, subtract the same digits in increasing order, and repeat.
In base ten, the trick is famously dramatic: the process is drawn to the constant
6174
,
which is a fixed point since

7641-1467=6174.

For example, starting with the number
2808
, we have the process

2808\longmapsto 8532\longmapsto 6174\longmapsto 6174.

The surprise is not merely that a fixed point appears,
but that a rule made from sorting digits is secretly governed by modular arithmetic.
This paper explains that hidden arithmetic for four digits in every odd base.

Kaprekar introduced this routine in his study of decimal digit phenomena
[
5
,
6
]
.
In the classical four-digit decimal case, every nonconstant digit string reaches the fixed point
6174
.
For 3 decimal digits, it is
495
.
The decimal case, however, is only one member of a larger family.
In other bases the four-digit routine need not have a single attracting fixed point.
Its terminal behavior may consist of several cycles, and their lengths depend on the arithmetic of the base.

The generalized Kaprekar problem has been studied from several points of view. Moreover, the fixed points of Kaprekar mappings define the integer sequence
OEIS A099009
.
Jordan studied self-producing digit sequences
[
4
]
.
Hasse and Prichett determined the four-digit Kaprekar constants (i.e.,
fixed points for general bases)
[
3
]
.
Prichett analyzed five-digit terminating cycles
[
11
]
.
Thakur developed a broader framework for Kaprekar phenomena in arbitrary bases
and digit lengths
[
12
]
.
Devlin and Zeng studied maximum distances to the four-digit fixed point in the
bases where such a fixed point exists
[
1
]
.

More recently, Yamagami and Matsui developed a detailed theory of
3
-adic
Kaprekar loops and obtained formulas for Kaprekar fixed points

[
13
,
14
]
.
Kay and Downes-Ward extended fixed-point and cycle questions to odd bases using
Kaprekar indices and subgroup/coset methods, and their sequel treats even bases,
where the classification is more intricate
[
7
,
8
]
.
Our purpose here is more explicit.
In the four-digit odd-base case, the right coordinates reveal a particularly simple mechanism:
after a bounded pre-periodic segment, the Kaprekar map is just projective doubling.
The resulting proof is short, but the simplification is quite striking,
and it gives a transparent route to the cycle-length consequences below while
keeping the digit dynamics visible throughout.

1.2.
Main classification result

Given a
4
-digit positive integer in an odd base
B
,
let
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq a_{3}\geq a_{4}
denote its digits in non-increasing order.
Then the next Kaprekar iterate depends only on the two differences

(D)

d_{1}=a_{1}-a_{4}\qquad\text{and}\qquad d_{2}=a_{2}-a_{3}.

Thus
d_{1}
records the outer difference and
d_{2}
records the inner difference.
Accordingly, we define a natural state space

(X)

X_{B}\coloneq\{(d_{1},d_{2}):1\leq d_{1}<B,\ 0\leq d_{2}\leq d_{1}\}.

The Kaprekar routine thus induces a map
K_{B}\colon X_{B}\to X_{B}
,
sending each difference pair to the next difference pair.
Note here we have excluded the trivial case
a_{1}=a_{2}=a_{3}=a_{4}
by requiring
d_{1}\neq 0
.

In
Section

2
we will prove that, after a short pre-periodic segment,
these two differences become positive, unequal, and odd; that is,
(d_{1},d_{2})

will lie in the stable odd region defined by

(S)

\mathcal{T}_{B}\coloneq\{(d_{1},d_{2})\in X_{B}:d_{1}>d_{2}>0\text{ and }d_{1}\equiv d_{2}\equiv 1\pmod{2}\}.

We will also describe how
K_{B}
acts on
\mathcal{T}_{B}
.
To give this description, rather than working with
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
themselves,
we’ll want to work with their half-sum and half-difference:

(R)

r=\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad s=\frac{d_{1}-d_{2}}{2}.

(Note that
r>s>0
, and that
r
and
s
are integers because
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are odd.)

For pairs
(r,s)
we will pass to
projective residue classes modulo
B
,
where each residue is identified with its negative.
Introduce the notation

P_{B}\coloneq\left((\mathbb{Z}/B\mathbb{Z})\setminus\{0\}\right)/\{\pm 1\}

for the nonzero projective residue classes.
For nonzero
x\in\mathbb{Z}/B\mathbb{Z}
, let
[x]\coloneq\{x,-x\}\in P_{B}

denote the corresponding residue class in
P_{B}
.
Finally, let
\binom{P_{B}}{2}
denote the (unordered) two-element subsets of
P_{B}
.

We can now state the main structural result.

Theorem 1.1

(Structural classification)
.

Let
B>3
be odd, and let
K_{B}\colon X_{B}\to X_{B}
be the four-digit
Kaprekar map in difference coordinates.
Then
\mathcal{T}_{B}
, as defined in (
S
), is forward-invariant and

K_{B}^{3}(X_{B})\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{B}.

Moreover, the restricted map
K_{B}\colon\mathcal{T}_{B}\to\mathcal{T}_{B}
is conjugate to the doubling map

\binom{P_{B}}{2}\to\binom{P_{B}}{2}\quad\text{ by }\quad\{[r],[s]\}\longmapsto\{[2r],[2s]\}

under the transformation defined in (
R
).

Here, in the context of finite dynamical systems,
we explicate the term “conjugate” as follows.
Let
f\colon X\to X
and
g\colon Y\to Y
be maps.
We say that
f
and
g
are
conjugate
under the bijection

\Phi\colon X\to Y
if

\Phi(f(x))=g(\Phi(x))\qquad\text{for all }x\in X.

Thus
\Phi
is a relabeling of the states which turns one system into the other.
In the particular case of
Theorem

1.1
,
the map
\Phi\colon\mathcal{T}_{B}\to\binom{P_{B}}{2}

is given by (
R
),
and so
Theorem

1.1
makes an implicit promise that
(
R
) is bijective.

We can thus describe
Theorem

1.1
in English as follows:
After at most three iterations,
every orbit enters our smaller stable region
\mathcal{T}_{B}
.
And on this region
\mathcal{T}_{B}
, the sorting-and-subtracting rule is no longer mysterious!
After the change of variables in (
R
),
it is exactly multiplication by
2
, with signs ignored,
on unordered pairs of nonzero projective residue classes.

Remark
.

Here the case
B=3
needs to be excluded because
\mathcal{T}_{B}
is empty when
B=3
.

1.3.
Cycle lengths

It is clear that for odd
B
the doubling map

\binom{P_{B}}{2}\to\binom{P_{B}}{2}\text{ by }\{[r],[s]\}\longmapsto\{[2r],[2s]\}

is bijective.
Since conjugate systems have the same cycle lengths,
we get the following corollaries as the main consequences.
They are stated separately so that the dynamical statement and the arithmetic
consequences are easy to distinguish.

Corollary 1.2

(Cycle-length bound)
.

If
B>3
is odd and
c_{\max}(B)
is the largest terminal-cycle length for the
four-digit Kaprekar map in base
B
, then we have

c_{\max}(B)\leq\frac{B-1}{2}.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if
B\geq 7
,
B
is prime,
and the least positive solution to
2^{m}\equiv\pm 1\pmod{B}
is
m=\frac{B-1}{2}
.

Remark
.

In the edge case
B=5
, one has
c_{\max}(5)=1<2=(B-1)/2
instead.

Thus
Corollary

1.2
gives a universal ceiling
and describes exactly when it is achieved.
We also have the following result:

Corollary 1.3

(Prime bases and maximal cycles)
.

If equality holds in
Corollary

1.2
(so that
B
is prime),
then the number of terminal cycles of length
c_{\max}(B)
is

\left\lfloor\frac{c_{\max}(B)-1}{2}\right\rfloor.

Remark
.

Our results are a four-digit complement to the broader odd-base theory.
Yamagami and Matsui give a detailed theory in base
3

[
13
]
,
and Kay and Downes-Ward study fixed points and cycles in odd bases using Kaprekar indices,
with regular cycles organized through subgroup and coset data
[
7
]
.
Their framework treats arbitrary digit-counts and is especially well suited to regular cycles.
The present note fixes the digit-count at four.
In this small case, the dynamics are more directly described by the outer and inner digit differences,
and after a bounded pre-periodic segment these two differences give a stable region on
which the map becomes projective doubling.
Therefore, the contribution here is a low-dimensional model that makes the
four-digit odd-base dynamics and their cycle counts explicit.

Examples of the main results

Here are a few examples that illustrate these results.
The cycles are controlled not by decimal accidents,
but by the order of
2
modulo the base, with signs ignored.
We start each example with ordinary four-digit strings,
and only then pass to the difference and projective coordinates.

Example

(The case
B=7
)
.

The sorted four-digit string
3100
has

d_{1}=3-0=3\qquad\text{and}\qquad d_{2}=1-0=1.

Thus

r=\frac{3+1}{2}=2\qquad\text{and}\qquad s=\frac{3-1}{2}=1.

Writing each iterate as a sorted digit string, the orbit of
3100
is

3100\longmapsto 5430\longmapsto 5520\longmapsto 5322\longmapsto 5430\longmapsto\dots,

which in difference coordinates is the terminal cycle

(3,1)\longmapsto(5,1)\longmapsto(5,3)\longmapsto(3,1).

Here
(B-1)/2=3
, and
2
has projective order
3
modulo
7
,
so the largest four-digit Kaprekar cycles have length
3
.
This agrees with the count
\lfloor(3-1)/2\rfloor=1
.

Example

(The case
B=11
)
.

The sorted four-digit string
5100
has
(d_{1},d_{2})=(5,1)
, and hence

r=3\qquad\text{and}\qquad s=2.

Writing each iterate as a sorted digit string, the orbit of
5100
is

5100\longmapsto 9650\longmapsto 9920\longmapsto 9632\longmapsto 7742\longmapsto 7652\longmapsto 9650\longmapsto\dots.

In difference coordinates this is the terminal cycle

(5,1)\to(9,1)\to(9,7)\to(7,3)\to(5,3)\to(5,1).

Since
2^{5}\equiv-1\pmod{11}
and no smaller positive power of
2
is congruent to
\pm 1
,
we have
c_{\max}(11)=5
. There are

\left\lfloor\frac{5-1}{2}\right\rfloor=2

cycles of length five; the other one is

(3,1)\to(9,5)\to(7,1)\to(9,3)\to(7,5)\to(3,1).

Example

(The case
B=9
)
.

The composite base
B=9
shows why the inequality is usually strict.
Starting from the sorted string
5100
, we have
(d_{1},d_{2})=(5,1)
and
(r,s)=(3,2)
.
The terminal behavior in difference coordinates is

(5,1)\to(7,1)\to(7,5)\to(5,1),

with the second cycle

(3,1)\to(7,3)\to(5,3)\to(3,1).

Here
(B-1)/2=4
, but
2^{3}\equiv-1\pmod{9}
, so the terminal cycles have length
3
, not
4
.

Example

(The case
B=17
)
.

Finally, a prime base need not give equality.
For
B=17
, the sorted four-digit string
5100
again has
(d_{1},d_{2})=(5,1)
and
(r,s)=(3,2)
,
but
2^{4}\equiv-1\pmod{17}
. Thus the largest terminal cycles have length
4
, not
(17-1)/2=8
.

introduces the difference coordinates and proves that every orbit

2.
Difference coordinates for four digits

We now turn from the statement of the result to the mechanism behind it.
The first step is to reduce the digit operation to a map on two integers.
This reduction is useful because the middle information in a sorted four-digit
string cancels during subtraction.

2.1.
Difference coordinates

Fix an odd base
B>3
. Let

\overrightarrow{N}=[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}]\qquad\text{with}\qquad a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq a_{3}\geq a_{4}

be the decreasing rearrangement of a four-digit string.
We ignore constant strings, since they map to zero.
As in (
D
), let
d_{1}\coloneq a_{1}-a_{4}

and
d_{2}\coloneq a_{2}-a_{3}
so that

1\leq d_{1}<B\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0\leq d_{2}\leq d_{1}.

Then Kaprekar subtraction depends only on
(d_{1},d_{2})
. Indeed,

\displaystyle(a_{1}B^{3}+a_{2}B^{2}+a_{3}B+a_{4})-(a_{4}B^{3}+a_{3}B^{2}+a_{2}B+a_{1})

\displaystyle=d_{1}(B^{3}-1)+d_{2}(B^{2}-B).

Thus the future orbit after one step is determined by the pair
(d_{1},d_{2})
.

If
d_{2}>0
, ordinary base-
B
subtraction gives the unsorted output digits

(1)

[d_{1},\ d_{2}-1,\ B-d_{2}-1,\ B-d_{1}].

If
d_{2}=0
, the subtraction has one fewer borrow, and the unsorted output digits are

(2)

[d_{1}-1,\ B-1,\ B-1,\ B-d_{1}].

Let
K_{B}
denote the induced map on
X_{B}
as defined in (
X
).
To compute
K_{B}(d_{1},d_{2})
, one sorts the four entries in (
1
)
or (
2
) in decreasing order and then takes the outer and inner differences.
This passage to difference coordinates does not lose any eventual cycle lengths.
Indeed, a digit-level orbit determines a difference-coordinate orbit,
and conversely a periodic orbit in difference coordinates lifts to a digit-level
periodic orbit by the subtraction formulas (
1
) and (
2
).
If two digit strings in such a lifted orbit were equal, their difference pairs would also be equal,
so the period is the same. Thus it is enough to count terminal cycles for
K_{B}
on
X_{B}
.

2.2.
Escaping the pre-period

The rest of this section is a cleanup step.
We show that the boundary phenomena disappear after a bounded number of iterations,
leaving a simple stable region on which the projective model will live.

Lemma 2.1

(Pre-period into the stable odd region)
.

For every odd
B>3
,

K_{B}^{3}(X_{B})\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{B}.

Moreover
\mathcal{T}_{B}
is forward invariant under
K_{B}
.

The proof of
Lemma

2.1
will be divided into a few cases.

Case 2.1.1
.

Lemma

2.1
holds in the case
d_{1}>d_{2}>0
.
In fact:

•

If
d_{1}>d_{2}>0
and
d_{1}\equiv d_{2}\pmod{2}
, then
K_{B}(d_{1},d_{2})\in\mathcal{T}_{B}
.

•

If
d_{1}>d_{2}>0
but
d_{1}\not\equiv d_{2}\pmod{2}
, then
K_{B}^{2}(d_{1},d_{2})\in\mathcal{T}_{B}
.

Proof for
2.1.1
.

Abbreviate the four entries in (
1
) by

A\coloneq d_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad C\coloneq d_{2}-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad D\coloneq B-d_{2}-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad E\coloneq B-d_{1}.

We always have
A>C
and
D\geq E
; also
A\neq E
and
C\neq D
.
We note the case
D>A>C>E
is also impossible,
because it would require both
d_{1}+d_{2}<B-1
and
d_{1}+d_{2}>B+1
.
Hence, we must be in one of the five situations listed in
Table

1
.
Of course, only rows whose displayed differences are nonnegative can actually occur.

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hline\cr\text{Ordering}&K_{B}(d_{1},d_{2})&\text{In $\mathcal{T}_{B}$?}\\
\hline\cr A\geq D\geq E\geq C&(d_{1}-d_{2}+1,\ d_{1}-d_{2}-1)&\text{Iff }d_{1}\equiv d_{2}\bmod 2\\
D\geq E>A>C&(B-2d_{2},\ B-2d_{1})&\text{Always}\\
A\geq D>C\geq E&(2d_{1}-B,\ B-2d_{2})&\text{Always}\\
D\geq A>E\geq C&(B-2d_{2},\ 2d_{1}-B)&\text{Always}\\
A>C>D\geq E&(2d_{1}-B,\ 2d_{2}-B)&\text{Always}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Table 1.
Possible results of
K_{B}(d_{1},d_{2})
when
d_{1}>d_{2}>0
.

We assert the last four rows of
Table

1
always lie in
\mathcal{T}_{B}
.
Indeed, it is clear the entries are odd.
Moreover, they are easily seen to be unequal;
in the third row we have
C\geq E\implies d_{1}+d_{2}\geq B+1

while in the fourth row we have
D\geq A\implies d_{1}+d_{2}\leq B-1
.
Hence in the third and fourth row
d_{1}+d_{2}\neq B
always holds
and the two entries are unequal.

It remains to only deal with the first row,
which lies in
\mathcal{T}_{B}
exactly when
d_{1}\equiv d_{2}\pmod{2}
.
It suffices to show that

(3)

K_{B}(h+1,h-1)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}\quad\text{for each odd}\quad h\geq 1.

Indeed (
3
) can be checked directly:

•

If
h\neq 1
, we apply
Table

1
a second time to the new pair
(h+1,h-1)
.
For
(h+1,h-1)
, all five rows of
Table

1
are in
\mathcal{T}_{B}
and we are done.

•

If
h=1
then in fact
(h+1,h-1)=(2,0)
.
Although
Table

1
does not apply, we can directly compute

K_{B}(2,0)=(B-2,1)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}.\qed

Case 2.1.2
.

We have
K_{B}^{2}(d,d)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}
for any
d>0
.

Proof for
2.1.2
.

Formula (
1
) becomes

[d,d-1,B-d-1,B-d].

Then

K_{B}(d,d)=(h+1,h-1)\qquad\text{where}\qquad h\coloneq|B-2d|.

This is the same as (
3
) from the earlier case,
so we get
K_{B}(h+1,h-1)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}
as desired.
∎

Case 2.1.3
.

We have
K_{B}^{3}(d_{1},0)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}
for any
d_{1}>0
.

Proof for
2.1.3
.

Put

u=d_{1}-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad v=B-d_{1}.

Sorting (
2
) gives

(4)

K_{B}(d_{1},0)=\bigl(B-1-\min(u,v),\ B-1-\max(u,v)\bigr).

If the second coordinate in (
4
) is zero, then
d_{1}=1
and

(1,0)\mapsto(B-1,0)\mapsto(B-2,1)\in\mathcal{T}_{B}.

If the image in (
4
) has positive unequal coordinates,
then
2.1.1
applies.
If the image has equal coordinates, then
2.1.2
applies.
Hence every state with
d_{2}=0
reaches
\mathcal{T}_{B}
within at most three steps.
∎

We can now deduce the main lemma.

Proof of
Lemma

2.1
.

We note that
K_{B}(\mathcal{T}_{B})\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{B}
follows from the first half of
2.1.1
,
while
K_{B}^{3}(X_{B})\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{B}
follows by noting that

2.1.1
,
2.1.2
and
2.1.3
cover all possibilities.
∎

Example

(The pre-periodic segment in base
11
)
.

Let
B=11
.
An example for
2.1.2
is

(3,3)\longmapsto(6,4)\longmapsto(3,1)\in\mathcal{T}_{11}.

An example for
2.1.3
is

(6,0)\longmapsto(5,5)\longmapsto(2,0)\longmapsto(9,1)\in\mathcal{T}_{11}.

These two computations illustrate why a short pre-periodic segment
is needed before the simple projective model appears.

3.
The projective model

The previous section deals with the pre-periodic segment. We now reveal the hidden coordinates on the stable region.
They are not the differences themselves, but the half-sum and half-difference of the two differences,
viewed modulo sign.

Recall that
P_{B}=((\mathbb{Z}/B\mathbb{Z})\setminus\{0\})/\{\pm 1\}
.
Define

(P)

\displaystyle\Phi\colon\mathcal{T}_{B}

\displaystyle\to\binom{P_{B}}{2}

\displaystyle\text{by }(d_{1},d_{2})

\displaystyle\mapsto\left\{\left[\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}}{2}\right],\left[\frac{d_{1}-d_{2}}{2}\right]\right\}.

The two classes are nonzero and distinct, because
d_{1}>d_{2}>0
and
d_{1}<B
.
We first prove this is a bijection.

Lemma 3.1

(Bijection with projective pairs)
.

The map
\Phi\colon\mathcal{T}_{B}\to\binom{P_{B}}{2}
is a bijection.

Proof.

Define the intermediate set

\mathcal{R}\coloneq\{(r,s)\mid r>s>0,\ r+s<B,\ r\not\equiv s\bmod 2\}.

Recall the notation from (
R
).
We first contend that the map

(5)

\mathcal{T}_{B}\to\mathcal{R}\qquad\text{by}\qquad(d_{1},d_{2})\mapsto(r,s)=\left(\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}}{2},\frac{d_{1}-d_{2}}{2}\right)

is a (well-defined) bijection, with inverse given by
d_{1}=r+s
and
d_{2}=r-s
.
Indeed, the conditions on
\mathcal{T}_{B}
and
\mathcal{R}
line up exactly:

\displaystyle d_{1},d_{2}\text{ odd}

\displaystyle\iff r\pm s\text{ odd}

\displaystyle d_{1}>d_{2}

\displaystyle\iff s>0

\displaystyle d_{2}>0

\displaystyle\iff r>s

\displaystyle B>d_{1}

\displaystyle\iff r+s<B.

This establishes (
5
) is indeed a bijection.

So, it remains to prove that the projection

(6)

\mathcal{R}\to\binom{P_{B}}{2}\qquad\text{by}\qquad(r,s)\mapsto\{[r],[s]\}

is also a bijection.
To that end, suppose
\{[u],[v]\}\in\binom{P_{B}}{2}
is an arbitrary
pair of distinct projective classes, with
0<u<B
and
0<v<B
.
Since
[u]\neq[v]
we also have
u\neq v
and
u+v\neq B
.

The main point is that among the four ordered pairs

(u,v)
,
(u,B-v)
,
(B-u,v)
,
(B-u,B-v)

it is easy to see exactly one ordered pair has odd sum less than
B
.
(Indeed, exactly two pairs have odd sum, and the sum of all four numbers is
2B

So WLOG assume (by renaming
u
to
B-u
or
v
to
B-v
if needed)
that
u+v<B
is odd.
In that case,
r=\max(u,v)
and
s=\min(u,v)

is the unique pair in
\mathcal{R}
with image
\{[u],[v]\}
.
Hence (
6
) is also a bijection.

Lemma

3.1
now follows by composing (
5
) and (
6
).
∎

Example

(The bijection in base
11
)
.

In base
11
, consider the two classes
[2]=[9]
and
[5]=[6]
.
The unique

and

, which gives

and

.
Hence the bijection in this case is

\Phi(7,3)=\{[5],[2]\}.

The next proposition is the heart of the paper.
It says that, after the pre-periodic segment,
the digit dynamics are exactly multiplication by
2
on the two projective coordinates.

Proposition 3.2

(Projective doubling)
.

For every
(d_{1},d_{2})\in\mathcal{T}_{B}
,

(7)

\Phi\bigl(K_{B}(d_{1},d_{2})\bigr)=\left\{\left[2\cdot\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}}{2}\right],\left[2\cdot\frac{d_{1}-d_{2}}{2}\right]\right\}.

Equivalently, under
\Phi
, the Kaprekar map on
\mathcal{T}_{B}
is conjugate to

\{[r],[s]\}\longmapsto\{[2r],[2s]\}

on unordered projective pairs.

Proof.

We reuse the earlier
Table

1
from the proof of
2.1.1

(which is applicable because we are in
\mathcal{T}_{B}
),
but rewrite it in
(r,s)
-coordinates.
That is, we calculate
K_{B}(d_{1},d_{2})=K_{B}(r+s,r-s)
in terms of
r
and
s
,
then compute the half-sum and half-difference of the new pair, modulo
B
.
This transforms
Table

1
into
Table

2
below.

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\hline\cr\text{Ordering}&K_{B}(r+s,r-s)&\text{Half-sum mod $B$}&\text{Half-diff mod $B$}\\
\hline\cr A\geq D\geq E\geq C&(2s+1,\ 2s-1)&2s&1\\
D\geq E>A>C&(B-2r+2s,\ B-2r-2s)&-2r&2s\\
A\geq D>C\geq E&(2r+2s-B,\ B-2r+2s)&2s&2r\\
D\geq A>E\geq C&(B-2r+2s,\ 2r+2s-B)&2s&-2r\\
A>C>D\geq E&(2r+2s-B,\ 2r-2s-B)&2r&2s\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Table 2.
Computing
K_{B}(r+s,r-s)
in terms of
r
and
s
instead.

In
Table

2
, we find that in every row except the first,
the two projective classes are exactly
[2r]
and
[2s]
, in some order.
This means that (

) is proved
in every case except
A\geq D\geq E\geq C
.

In the problematic case, we then note that
A\geq D
and

rearrange to

\displaystyle A\geq D

\displaystyle\iff d_{1}\geq B-d_{2}-1\iff 2r\geq B-1

\displaystyle E\geq C

\displaystyle\iff B-d_{1}\geq d_{2}-1\iff 2r\leq B+1.

So in fact this case can only occur when
2r=B-1
or
2r=B+1
.
In either case we have

in

and (

) is still true.
∎

Example

(The conjugacy in base

)
.

Start from

so

At this point

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

By
Lemma

2.1
, every orbit reaches the forward-invariant region
\mathcal{T}_{B}

after at most three steps in difference coordinates.
By
Lemma

3.1
,

\Phi
is a bijection from this region to the unordered two-element subsets of

.
By
Proposition

, this bijection carries one Kaprekar step to one doubling step.
This proves both the pre-periodic statement and the asserted conjugacy on the stable region.
∎

4.
Cycle lengths

We have now replaced the Kaprekar map by projective doubling. The rest of the argument is arithmetic.
The key quantity is the order of
2
after signs are ignored.

Definition

(Projective order of
2
)
.

For odd
B>3
, define

(L)

\lambda(B)=\min\{m>0:2^{m}\equiv\pm 1\pmod{B}\}.

Equivalently,
\lambda(B)
is the order of the class of
2
in the finite group

(\mathbb{Z}/B\mathbb{Z})^{\times}/\{\pm 1\}.

This definition is tailored to
P_{B}
:
multiplying by
2^{\lambda(B)}
brings every projective class back to itself.
Consequently the induced map on unordered pairs cannot have longer cycles.

Lemma 4.1
.

Every cycle of the doubling map on
\binom{P_{B}}{2}
has length at most
\lambda(B)
.

Proof.

By definition,
2^{\lambda(B)}\equiv\pm 1\pmod{B}
.
Therefore multiplication by
2^{\lambda(B)}
fixes every projective class
[x]\in P_{B}
,
including classes represented by non-units.
Hence the induced action on unordered two-element subsets of
P_{B}
is also fixed
by the
\lambda(B)
-th iterate. Every orbit length is therefore at most
\lambda(B)
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

One has

\lambda(B)\leq\frac{\varphi(B)}{2}.

Proof.

The class of
2
lies in the finite group
(\mathbb{Z}/B\mathbb{Z})^{\times}/\{\pm 1\}
.
This group has order
\varphi(B)/2
, because
B
is odd and
B>1
.
The order of an element in a finite group divides the order of the group. Hence
\lambda(B)\leq\varphi(B)/2
.
∎

This lets us prove part of Corollary
1.2
now.

Corollary 4.3
.

If
B>3
is odd, then
c_{\max}(B)\leq\frac{B-1}{2}
.
Moreover, if equality holds then
B
must be prime.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.1
,
every terminal Kaprekar cycle is represented by a cycle of the doubling map on
\binom{P_{B}}{2}
.
Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
give

c_{\max}(B)\leq\lambda(B)\leq\frac{\varphi(B)}{2}\leq\frac{B-1}{2}.

This proves the stated bound.

As for the equality case, suppose that
c_{\max}(B)=(B-1)/2
. Then every inequality in

c_{\max}(B)\leq\lambda(B)\leq\frac{\varphi(B)}{2}\leq\frac{B-1}{2}

must be an equality. In particular,
\varphi(B)=B-1
.
For an integer
B>1
, this is equivalent to
B
being prime.
Hence equality forces prime base.
∎

This does not say that every prime base is extremal. It only says that composite bases are ruled out.
The examples below show both a composite base where the bound is strict and a
prime base where the bound is sharp.

Example

(The order bound in bases
9
and
11
)
.

For
B=9
, the projective order of
2
is
3
, since
2^{3}\equiv-1\pmod{9}
.
Lemma
4.1
therefore forces every terminal four-digit cycle in
base
9
to have length at most
3
,
and the examples in the introduction show that this bound is sharp in base
9
.

For
B=11
, the projective order is
5
,
since
2^{5}\equiv-1\pmod{11}
and no smaller positive exponent gives
\pm 1
.
Thus the order bound permits cycles of length
5
,
and the two length-five cycles displayed in the introduction attain it.

The same projective model also gives an explicit count of all terminal cycles.
We record this as a proposition because it is the most useful practical form of the classification.
For each odd divisor
n>1
of
B
, put

\lambda(n)=\min\{m>0:2^{m}\equiv\pm 1\pmod{n}\}.

For a positive integer
a
, define

(8)

\mu_{B}(a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\mid B,\ n>1\\
\lambda(n)=a\end{subarray}}\frac{\varphi(n)}{2a}.

Thus
\mu_{B}(a)
is the number of
a
-cycles of the doubling map on
P_{B}
itself,
before passing to unordered pairs.

Theorem 4.4

(Explicit cycle counts)
.

Let
N_{B}(L)
be the number of terminal four-digit Kaprekar cycles of length
L
in the odd base
B>3
,
with constant digit strings omitted. Then

(9)

\begin{split}N_{B}(L)={}&\mu_{B}(L)\left\lfloor\frac{L-1}{2}\right\rfloor+\mu_{B}(2L)+\binom{\mu_{B}(L)}{2}L\\
&\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a<b\\
\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=L\end{subarray}}\mu_{B}(a)\mu_{B}(b)\gcd(a,b),\end{split}

where
\mu_{B}(a)
is given by (
8
),
and where
\mu_{B}(a)=0
if no divisor
n
contributes to (
8
).

Proof.

We first compute the cycle structure of doubling on
P_{B}
.
Fix a nonzero residue class
[x]\in P_{B}
, and let
g=\gcd(x,B)
. Write
B=gn
.
Then
x=gy
with
y
a unit modulo
n
.
Multiplication by
2
preserves
g
,
and the projective classes with this fixed value of
g
are naturally identified with

(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}/\{\pm 1\}.

On this set, multiplication by
2
has order
\lambda(n)
.
Hence all cycles in this stratum have length
\lambda(n)
, and the number of such cycles is

\frac{|(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}/\{\pm 1\}|}{\lambda(n)}=\frac{\varphi(n)}{2\lambda(n)}.

Summing over all divisors
n>1
of
B
gives (
8
).

It remains to pass from cycles on
P_{B}
to cycles on unordered two-element subsets of
P_{B}
.
This is elementary cycle combinatorics.
Suppose first that the two elements are chosen from two distinct cycles of lengths
a
and
b
.
If
a<b
, then each choice of the two cycles contributes
\gcd(a,b)
orbits of unordered pairs,
each of length
\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)
. This gives the final sum in (
9
).
If the two distinct cycles have the same length
L
,
then each unordered pair of such cycles contributes
L
orbits of length
L
,
giving the term
\binom{\mu_{B}(L)}{2}L
.

Finally suppose that both elements lie in the same
a
-cycle. Label that cycle cyclically.
An unordered pair is determined, up to rotation, by its cyclic distance

1,2,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{a}{2}\right\rfloor.

For each distance smaller than
a/2
, the orbit has length
a
. This contributes

\mu_{B}(a)\left\lfloor\frac{a-1}{2}\right\rfloor

orbits of length
a
. If
a
is even,
the antipodal distance
a/2
gives one additional orbit of length
a/2
for each
a
-cycle;
this accounts for the term
\mu_{B}(2L)
in the count of length-
L
orbits.

By Theorem
1.1
,
terminal Kaprekar cycles are exactly these cycles of the doubling action on
\binom{P_{B}}{2}
.
This proves the formula.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

The following are true.

(i) For odd

, there are at least two terminating
\lambda(B)
-cycles.

(ii) There is a unique terminating cycle for odd

if and only if

,

or

.
(In other words, these are the only odd bases where
all the non-constant strings have the same eventual behaviour.

Proof.

Put

. Then (

\mu_{B}(L)\geq 1
.
If
B>17=2^{4}+1
, it is immediate from the definition of

that

,
and thus the calculation of the first term of the right side (

implies the claim that

.

5.
Prime bases and the number of largest cycles

The preceding section gives the full cycle-count formula and proves the universal bound.
In this section we finish the proofs of
Corollary

1.2
and
Corollary

1.3
.

5.1.
Proof of
Corollary

1.2

Recalling
Corollary

4.3
,
we suppose
B=p>3
is prime and set
q\coloneq(p-1)/2
.
The simplification is that every nonzero residue is a unit,
so
P_{p}
is a group quotient rather than merely a set of projective residue classes.
Thus the projective set
P_{p}=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}/\{\pm 1\}
has exactly
q
elements.
The doubling map on
P_{p}
has order

\lambda(p)=\min\{m>0:2^{m}\equiv\pm 1\pmod{p}\}.

If
p>5
, then
q\geq 3
. In this case,
the induced action on unordered two-element subsets has a cycle of length
q
if
and only if multiplication by
2
is a single
q
-cycle on
P_{p}
.
Equivalently, this happens if and only if
\lambda(p)=q
. Therefore, for primes
p>5
,

c_{\max}(p)=\frac{p-1}{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lambda(p)=\frac{p-1}{2}.

This proves the equality criterion in Corollary
1.2
.

The prime
p=5
is exceptional for a simple reason.
Here
q=2
, so
P_{5}
has only two elements and
\binom{P_{5}}{2}
has only one element.
Hence the largest induced cycle has length
1
, even though
\lambda(5)=2
.

5.2.
Proof of
Corollary

1.3

It remains only to count the largest cycles in the equality case.
This is a small piece of cycle combinatorics:
unordered pairs on a single cycle are classified by their cyclic distance.

Assume
p>5
and
\lambda(p)=q
. Then doubling is a single
q
-cycle on
P_{p}
.
The induced action on unordered two-element subsets of a
q
-cycle is classified by cyclic distance.
The possible distances are

1,2,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\right\rfloor.

For a fixed distance
j
, all unordered pairs at distance
j
form one orbit under cyclic rotation.
If
q
is odd, every such orbit has length
q
.
If
q
is even, the distance
q/2
orbit has length
q/2
, not
q
, because each pair is antipodal.
Therefore the number of orbits of length
q
is

\begin{cases}(q-1)/2,&q\text{ odd},\\[4.0pt]
q/2-1,&q\text{ even}.\end{cases}

Both cases are equal to

\left\lfloor\frac{q-1}{2}\right\rfloor.

Since
c_{\max}=q
, the number of largest terminal Kaprekar cycles is

\left\lfloor\frac{c_{\max}-1}{2}\right\rfloor.

This completes the proof of Corollary
1.3
.

Example

(Counting largest cycles when
p=11
)
.

For
p=11
, the projective set
P_{11}
has
q=5
elements.
Since
2
has projective order
5
, doubling is a single five-cycle on
P_{11}
.
The unordered pairs of elements of a five-cycle have two possible cyclic distances, namely
1
and
2
.
These two distances give the two length-five Kaprekar cycles displayed in the introduction. This is the count

\left\lfloor\frac{5-1}{2}\right\rfloor=2.

For
p=7
, the projective set has
q=3
elements and only one cyclic distance, so there is one largest cycle,
again as in the introduction.
