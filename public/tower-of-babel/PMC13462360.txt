Computational screening of antimicrobial peptide analogs targeting AdeB efflux transporter protein in Acinetobacter baumannii associated with multidrug resistance
Authors: Shalini Mathpal, Tushar Joshi, Romita Guchhait, Sudha Ramaiah, Anand Anbarasu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOverexpression of the AdeABC efflux pump is crucial in conferring multidrug resistance (MDR) in Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii). Its primary subunit, AdeB, recognizes and pumps out aminoglycosides, carbapenems, and several other classes of antibiotics from the cell, making it an attractive target for drug development. There has been substantial interest in utilizing antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) as potential alternatives to traditional antibiotics. Here, we focused on computer-aided drug design methods to identify effective AMPs against the target AdeB protein. First, we used the Joker algorithm to optimize 45 experimentally validated AMPs that can inhibit the growth of A. baumannii. This created a library of 629 peptide analogs, which were evaluated using a supervised machine learning (ML) pipeline based on 117 descriptors. The CatBoost classifier demonstrated the best performance among the tested model, with an accuracy of 96.5%, precision of 97%, recall of 96.2% and specificity of 96%. Furthermore, 126 predicted AMP were screened and filtered based on their physicochemical and safety criteria. Molecular docking analysis of screened peptides with AdeB transporter identified strong binding affinities for eleven peptide analogs. Post-docking, 200 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (including an independent replicate run) supported the structural stability of three peptide analogs (BMAP27-R9, C20DK-R3, and SAAP148-R1). Binding free energy calculation using MM/PBSA indicated improved score of -61.15 kcal/mol for BMAP27-R9, -28.15 kcal/mol for C20DK-R3 and -41.80 kcal/mol for SAAP148-R1. These findings provided a basis for future studies on efflux-targeting antimicrobial strategies against MDR A. baumannii.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57846-y.Keywords: Efflux transporters, AdeB, AMPs, Peptide designSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Microbiology

Introduction

The overuse of antibiotics has led to the extensive spread of Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii), which has become a major cause of hospital acquired infections such as, bacteremia, pneumonia, urinary tract infections (UTIs) and surgical-site infections1,2. This poses a major threat to modern antibiotic therapy as it continuously evolve and jeopardizes effective treatment3,4. MDR A. baumannii infections have been associated with high mortality rates, ranging from 29% to 73% in affected patients5,6. As a result, the world health organization (WHO) has documented A. baumannii as a most critical priority pathogen that require extensive research and development of potent antimicrobial candidates7. Over the recent years, the A. baumannii stains have become resistant to carbapenems, the last class of antibiotics to control such infections8–10. On this account, MDR isolates are usually treated using the combination therapies that may consist of colistin, polymyxin B and tigecycline. However, the most significant clinical problem is to optimize the combination of these drugs and ensuring their safety11–14. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop alternatives treatment methods to overcome the growing threat of AMR, especially in A. baumannii. The organism maintains its virulence through numerous resistance mechanism, such as production of β-lactamase, target site modifications, overexpression of efflux pumps, and alterations in outer membrane permeability15–17. Among these mechanism, active efflux transporter has been identified as major determinant of MDR and plays a significant role in A. baumannii isolates18–20. The AdeABC efflux system, which is a member of resistance-nodulation-division (RNS) family, is the most relevant efflux pump in A. baumannii. It is composed of the membrane fusion protein AdeA, the inner transporter AdeB, and the outer membrane channel AdeC21–23. The AdeABC efflux system is a three-component transport system in which AdeB is a key component involved in recognizing and transporting substrates. The system captures the antibiotics from cytoplasm or periplasm, and then excluding them using a proton motive force dependent confirmational cycling process, with AdeB acting as a bridge connecting AdeB and AdeC in the periplasmic space21,24. The overexpression of AdeABC system has been shown to confer broad resistance to different classes of antibiotics, while deletion of adeB gene or functional impairment of its product eliminated efflux-mediated resistance, highlighting its essential role in the system. Structural insights obtained from cryo-electron microscopy has also revealed that conformational changes in adeB regulates the substrate binding and transport, making it an attractive molecular target for efflux inhibition strategies25,26.

Even though considerable efforts have been made over the past two decades to discover novel antibiotics to treat MDR Gram-positive pathogens, the available options remain limited. Alternative therapeutic strategies such as, nanoparticle drug delivery system, gene therapy, phage therapy, and immunotherapy, have either proven expensive or are still in the early stages of development27,28. Antimicrobial peptides (AMP) have generated significant interest as an alternative for MDR bacterial infections, including those caused by Gram-negative pathogens due to their rapid killing mechanisms, broad spectrum activity, and lower likelihood of resistance development29–32. For instance, amphipathic α-helical AMPs, such as Mastoparan B, which is a cationic 14 amino acid peptide derived from the venom of Vespa basalis, have been shown to exhibit potent antibacterial activity even at very low concentrations and to act synergistically with conventional antibiotics33. Further studies have identified other AMPs, such as MV6, known for enhancing the efficacy of aminoglycosides and synthetic peptides like PAsmr5-17, which inhibit efflux transporter and restore susceptibility to antibiotics34,35.

The aim of present study was to identify novel AMPs that can target A. baumannii using computer aided peptide design strategy. Initially, known AMPs effective against A. baumannii were used as a parent sequence for optimization using joker algorithm. The Joker generates peptide analogs by modifying the parent sequence in a way that preserves the core AMP motifs while altering the chemical properties associated with activity and compatibility with the host. Hereafter, we developed a machine leaning (ML) model for reliable classification and screening using physicochemical descriptors of experimentally verified AMPs and non-AMPs. The high confidence AMP analogs screened by ML model were subjected to in silico assessment, including prediction of antibacterial, hemolytic, anti-infective properties and toxicity analysis. The shortlisted peptide analogs were further investigated using molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with comprehensive MM/PBSA analysis. In general, this combined computational approach helped us to identify potential, rationally designed AMP candidates against drug resistant A. baumannii.

Materials and methods

Generation of peptide analogs

We identified 45 experimentally validated AMPs with activity against A. baumannii from the literature and publicly accessible databases. These peptide sequences were used as the primary dataset for optimization and screening36. Furthermore, Joker algorithm, a pattern-based mutagenesis tool developed for peptide sequence optimization, was utilized for the generation of AMPs analogs37. This method introduces predefined antimicrobial patterns typically composed of cationic and hydrophobic residues into the parent sequences to improve key physicochemical properties such as net positive charge, amphipathicity, and membrane interaction potential. The algorithm applies rule-based modifications, including localized substitutions and/or insertions, to incorporate these patterns while maintaining the overall structural and functional characteristics of the original peptides. The resulting peptide library was then used for subsequent computational analyses.

Preparation of training and test dataset

To screen possible AMPs using ML, the training set included a negative set compiled based on the dataset reported by Cao et al.,38, and a positive dataset obtained from publicly available online resources, such as APD3 and CAMP39,40. The training dataset had an equal representation of active and inactive AMPs, with 760 and 762, respectively. Further, we used the screened peptide analogs obtained from the Joker as our independent external set and tested our model’s ability to predict the antimicrobial properties against A. baumannii.

Feature extraction and ML model development

We have generated the peptide descriptor using the peptide R package. It utilizes twelve well-known sequence-based descriptor sets, including the ST scales, BLOSUM indices, isoelectric point, VHSE scales, Z scales, ProtFP fingerprints, T scales, MS-WHIM scores, Kiders factors, FASGAI vectors, hydrophobicity indices, and Cruciani properties. The total number of peptide descriptor for each peptide sequence was 117, encompassing compositional, physicochemical, and structural properties associated with each peptide sequence. Data quality and redundancy were improved by eliminating duplicate and unnatural amino acid sequences. Further, to minimize redundancy of highly similar sequences, CD-HIT clustering was used, with a sequence identity cutoff of 0.8 for AMPs and 0.4 for non-AMPs. Preprocessing of data and features normalization was performed using the pandas library in python, and the dataset was split into training and validation sets using the model selection module of scikit-learn. The unsupervised dimensionality reduction technique of principle component analysis (PCA) was then used to analyze the variance structure and distribution of the peptide descriptor dataset. Prior to PCA, all missing value were corrected using the mean of each descriptor, and all descriptors were scaled using z- score normalization. Two principal components were used to visualize the global structure of dataset. An AutoML system was applied to automatically identify the best features, model types, and hyperparameters, resulting in development of most robust classification model. The optimized model was then used to screen the peptide analogs, which identify the potential active AMPs for further study. The model’s classification performance was evaluated based on several key metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, Matthews’s correlation coefficient (MCC) and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). These metrics were calculated using the values of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN).

Structure prediction of target

We used PEP-FOLD server to generate 3D conformations of the designed peptide analogs41. This server is based on the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) framework and utilizes the sOPEP force field for accurate conformation sampling. For each peptide, we selected the highest ranked structural model for the further analysis. The PROCHECK program, available in the SAVES v6.1 server, was used to assess the stereochemical quality of the selected models.

Physicochemical characterization and safety assessment of the AMP analogs

The fundamental physicochemical properties of the predicted AMPs and their analogs were estimated with the aid of widely used online tools, such as APD3 and ExPASy ProtParam40,42. These platforms offer detailed insights on key properties, covering atomic composition, molecular weight, amino acid composition, aliphatic index, isoelectric point (pI), instability index, and estimated half-life. All these properties are collectively assessed to determine the stability, solubility and behavior of peptides under the biological circumstances. The CellPPD server43, a support vector machine (SVM)-based tool was used to predict the cell penetrating ability of peptides based on their sequences. The safety profiles of the peptides were analyzed by estimating toxicity using ToxinPred44, and the allergenicity through AllerTOP server45, both of which utilize ML models and sequence-based features for the predictions. The anti-inflammatory potential of the peptides was examined using PreAIP server46. This server combines primary sequence data with evolutionary and structural descriptors and uses a random forest-based model to predict the tendency of peptides to induce anti-inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β, IL-4, IL-10, IL-13, and IL-22). The peptides were classified according to PreAIP confidence scores such as high confidence AIP (≥ 0.468), medium confidence AIP (0.468 − 0.388), and low confidence AIP (0.388 − 0.342). Antigenicity prediction was carried out using VaxiJen v2.047, which applies alignment-independent method that predicts antigenicity by employing auto cross covariance (ACC) transformation of protein sequences. The hemolytic activity of the peptides was assessed through SVM-based HemoPIv2.0 server48.

Molecular docking studies

Protein-peptide docking was carried out using the selected AMPs’ analogs as ligands and the receptor structure (PDB ID: 7KGG) as the target. Prior to docking, protein and peptide structures were prepared following standard preprocessing protocols, including the addition of hydrogen atoms and removal of steric clashes. The prepared structures were submitted to the ClusPro server, which performs automated preprocessing steps such as assignment of protonation states and energy minimization as part of its docking workflow. Docking was performed using the ClusPro web server49, which used semi-definite Monte Carlo simulations for global rigid-body docking. The server generates 70,000 rotational conformations of the ligand and, for each rotation, systematically translates the ligand along the x, y, and z axes on a grid relative to the receptor. Docking was conducted in a blind manner, allowing global exploration of potential binding sites across the receptor surface. The target receptor was docked with each peptide, both parent and mutant, and the resulting docked models were ordered by their lowest energy score and cluster size. Final pose selection was performed based on a combination of cluster size (dominant clusters), docking energy, and biological relevance, particularly consistency with known substrate-binding regions of AdeB. Cross-validation of the docking protocol was performed using an independent protein–peptide docking server, HPEPDOCK. The docked protein-peptide complexes were visualized using the DIMPLOT module implemented in the LigPlot+ software, allowing detailed assessment of hydrogen and hydrophobic interactions.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation analysis

Preparation of the membrane-embedded simulation system

Since the available crystal structure of AdeB does not provide information about its orientation within the membrane, the protein’s membrane topology was determined using the OPM database50. The entire protein structure was then embedded into a model inner membrane of A. baumannii through the CHARMM-GUI web server (www.charmm-gui.org), resulting in a simulation system with box dimensions of 111 × 111 × 11151,52. A simplified lipid bilayer composed of POPG (20%) and POPE (80%) was used to mimic the bacterial inner membrane environment52,53. The final system consisted of the protein-peptide complex embedded in a lipid bilayer, along with water molecules and ions, totaling 151,199 atoms.

System setup and simulation parameters

The topologies of all peptide-protein complexes were generated using CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder54. Each constructed system was placed in a TIP3P water box, and the appropriate numbers of Na + and Cl- were introduced to neutralize the system and adjust the ionic concentration of 150 mM NaCl. All simulations were executed with GROMACS 2024.2 using the CHARMM36 all atom force field55. The initial energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm for 5000 steps, with a convergence criterion of maximum force below 1000 kJ/mol/nm, to remove steric clashes and stabilize the system. The steepest descent method was chosen due to its efficiency and robustness in the initial relaxation of molecular systems. The energy minimized structures were then equilibrated using a six-step protocol of CHARMM-GUI, which involved gradually reducing position restraints on lipid, protein, and water molecule under NVT and NPT ensembles. A three-dimensional periodic boundary condition was applied to eliminate boundary and edge effects with respect to the system’s energy. Each system was then simulated for 200 ns at constant pressure (1 bar) and temperature of 300 K, which is controlled by Parrinello-Rehman barostat with semi-isotropic coupling. The time constants used in coupling were 1 ps for temperature and 5 ps for pressure. All MD simulations were performed at 300 K, a standard temperature widely used in molecular dynamics studies for protein-ligand systems56,57. The output trajectories were examined to understand the conformational dynamics, flexibility and stability of the protein-peptide complexes. Salt-bridge and residue contact map analysis

Salt-bridge interactions were analyzed using the Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) software package (v1.9). Salt bridges were identified based on interactions between oppositely charged residues, specifically acidic residues (Asp, Glu) and basic residues (Arg, Lys). Both protein-peptide and intra-protein salt bridges were considered to assess their contribution to structural stability.

Contact maps were generated using the gmx mdmat module, which calculates the mean minimum distance between residue pairs over the simulation trajectory. In addition, the time evolution of residue contacts was quantified using the gmx mindist tool by applying a distance cutoff of 0.6 nm, a commonly used threshold for defining residue-level contacts.

Post-simulation binding free energy analysis

The free energy of association between the protein and peptide was determined using the gmx_MMPBSA tool58. Energy calculations were performed for both parent peptides and their analogs bound to the target protein. In binding free energy (∆Gbind) calculations, six determinants describe the energetic basis of protein-peptide complex, including van der Waals (∆VDWAALS), electrostatic (∆EEL), gas phase (∆GGAS), polar solvation (∆EPB), non-polar solvation (∆ENPOLAR), and total solvation (∆GSOLV). The residue-level free energy decomposition analysis was carried out to quantify the energetic contribution of each amino acid residues of AdeB upon binding to wild-type and mutant AMPs.

Results

Generation of peptide analogs

A total number of 629 peptide analogs was generated by Joker algorithm form 45 experimentally validated parent AMPs that targets A. baumannii. Joker calculated the AMP score of each generated sequence on the basis of its scoring system. This score indicates the extent to which the designed peptide matches specific physicochemical and sequence properties that are believed to be associated with antimicrobial activity. The joker scoring suggested significant variations within the generated library, which indicates that the algorithm explored a wide range of the local sequence space around the parent scaffolds. The distribution of joker antimicrobial scores in the generated peptide library is shown in Fig. 2, and the complete list of generated peptide analogs along with their scores is provided in the Supplementary information 1.

Box and scatter plot showing the distribution of antimicrobial fitness scores for the 630 peptide analogs generated by Joker algorithm.

Building a predictive framework through evaluation of various ML technique

To explore the intrinsic structure of the peptide descriptor dataset, PCA was performed. PCA result showed that the first two components captured a large portion of the total variability in the dataset with PC1 accounting for 30.04% and PCA2 contributing an additional 24.8% of the total variance. Visualization of PCA score plot showed a partial overlap between active and inactive peptides, indicating a continuous descriptor space (Fig. 3a).

Machine learning analysis of peptide classification (a) Principal component analysis (PCA) showing the separation between active and inactive peptides based on calculated descriptors (b) Confusion matrix showing the classification performance of the selected model (c) Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve illustrating the predictive performance of the model.

A total of 117 descriptors were generated for the training and test set. After preprocessing and feature selection, several ML algorithms, including XGBoost, CatBoost, Random Forest, Extra Trees, Neural Network, and LightGBM, were trained and evaluated for AMP classification. Their performance was measured using accuracy, sensitivity, precision, balanced accuracy, F1 score, error rates, and MCC.

The ML analysis revealed that CatBoost outperformed other tested algorithms, achieving an accuracy of 0.959, a precision of 0.970, a recall of 0.962, a specificity of 0.969, and an F1 score of 0.966. Besides, it showed the lowest error rates (0.030 for FPR and 0.038 for FNR), along with high balanced accuracy (0.965), which indicate model’s stability and ability to differentiate between AMP and non-AMP sequences. LightGBM also performed well with an accuracy of 0.956, a recall of 0.955, a specificity of 0.963, and a balanced accuracy of 0.959, thus making it a very competitive choice. The remaining classifiers such as Random Forest (accuracy of 0.879 with a balanced accuracy of 0.906) and Extra Trees (accuracy of 0.827 with balanced accuracy of 0.860) also delivered good predictive performance. Likewise, the Neural Network model demonstrated good predictive strength (accuracy 0.857, recall 0.814), while XGBoost performed comparatively weaker, with an accuracy of 0.828 and a balanced accuracy of 0.859. The Decision Tree model achieved very high precision (0.9987) but relatively low recall (0.821), suggesting a tendency to overpredict negative samples and ignore true AMP sequence. Under potential class imbalance, a more reliable assessment of model performance was provided by the MCC, which incorporates all four confusion matrix components, including TP, TN, FP, and FN. The results of the confusion matrix of all six algorithm are illustrated in Fig. 3b. The MCC analysis revealed that CatBoost was the best performing model (0.932), followed by LightGBM (0.919), Random Forest (0.812), Decision Tree (0.801), Neural Network (0.742), XGBoost (0.715), and ExtraTrees (0.712). Moreover, in order to assess the model’s ability to differentiate between classes, an ROC curve was generated for each classifier and is shown in Fig. 3c. The ROC curve provides a visual representation of classifier performance and helps in determining the optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity. The analysis indicated that CatBoost had the best sensitivity-specificity relationship, with the AUC (0.989), followed by LightGBM (0.984) and Random Forest (0.977), whereas XGBoost had the lowest AUC (0.892), which indicates poor classification power. The AUC of Neural Network and Extra Trees performed moderately with the value 0.928 and 0.910, respectively. The Table 1 details the performance of different classification models across multiple statistical evaluation criteria. CatBoost was selected as the best classifier for the downstream peptide screening, on the basis of its consistent better performance across all key evaluation metrics. The model was applied to an independent library of 629 peptide analogs, which identified 126 peptides predicted to be active AMPs at selected probability threshold. The shortlisted high confidence peptide analogs were subsequently used for additional analysis such as molecular docking and MD simulations to assess their therapeutic potential.

Physicochemical Characterization and Safety Assessment of the AMP Analogs

A total of 126 shortlisted AMPs were subjected to further screening based on AMP prediction scores, physicochemical properties, and safety assessments. Eleven peptides (ARV150-R2, BMAP27-R9, C20DK-R2, C20DK-R3, C20DK-R5, C20DK-R6, IKR18-R2, Lynronne1-R4, SAAP148-R1, SAAP148-R2, and SAAP148-R5) together with their respective wild-type forms were selected for detailed evaluation against the AdeB efflux transporter of A. baumannii. Physicochemical characterization of these peptides was carried out in APD3 and ExPASy ProtParam platform. It was observed that the molecular weight of all peptides lies within the expected range (10.6 to 12.5 kDa) for short cationic AMPs. Each peptide carried distinct positive charges (between + 6 to + 11) which facilitates electrostatic binding with the negatively charged outer membrane of A. baumannii. The hydropathicity index (0.62–0.66) and amphipathicity values (-0.13 to 1.25) demonstrated that these peptides have a balanced distribution of hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues, a property crucial for penetrating and disrupting the membrane. In addition, the half-life analysis across various biological systems indicated promising stability of the peptides. Most peptides indicated adequate proteolytic stability which is suitable for their potential biological applications, with predicted half-life surpassing 10 h in human reticulocytes (in vitro), over 20 h in yeast, and more than 10 h in E. coli. The Boman index values ranges from 1.27 to 3.78 kcal/mol, indicating varying protein-binding tendencies, which can affect their docking affinity and stability within the AdeB drug binding pocket. Overall, the peptides exhibited favorable safety profiles, with ToxinPred, AllerTOP, VaxiJan, and HemoPI predicting them to be non-toxic, non-allergenic, non-antigenic, and non-hemolytic, respectively. These findings imply minimal risk of eliciting immunogenic reactions or causing hemolysis, rendering the suitability of these peptides for subsequent therapeutic development. Among the selected peptide candidates, C20DK and SAAP148 series exhibited a favorable balance between charge and hydrophobicity balance, highlighting their ability to disrupt AdeB membrane interactions or block its drug-binding channels. The combined evaluation of peptide analogs revealed their strong antimicrobial properties, high cationicity, moderate hydrophobicity, and balanced amphipathicity, while retaining their favorable toxicity, allergenicity and hemolytic profiles. The complete physicochemical and safety profile of all ML screened peptides is included in Supplementary information 2, while top-performing candidates are summarized in Table 2. These features supported their selection for molecular docking and MD simulation analysis against AdeB transporter.

3D structure preparation and validation of peptides

The receptor protein structure (PDB ID: 7KGG) was generated by eliminated heteroatoms, water molecules, and other irrelevant components to minimize structural noise and ensure accurate docking analysis. The PEP-FOLD3 server was used to generate the 3D structure of the peptide analogs, and Ramachandran plot analysis was employed to assess the stereochemical quality of the models. In most peptides’ model, most residues were observed in the favored regions, with many of them achieving 100% occupancy. Notably, none of the peptides displayed residue in the disallowed regions or poor rotamers, confirming the structural reliability of the predicted models for subsequent docking and MD studies. Ramachandran plots for all the peptides are provided in the Supplementary information 3 Figure S1.

Protein-ligand interaction study

Molecular docking of 11 screened AMPs and their corresponding parent counterparts with the A. baumannii target protein AdeB was performed using the ClusPro server. Docking results demonstrated different binding affinities among the peptide variants. For the ARV peptide series, ARV1502 showed the lowest energy score (-969.7 kcal/mol) compared to ARV1502-R2 (-962 kcal/mol). In the C20DK series, C20DK-R2 showed the strongest interaction with AdeB (-1015.6 kcal/mol), while C20DK, C20DK-R3, C20DK-R5, and C20DK-R6 displayed binding energies of -926.9 kcal/mol, -950.1 kcal/mol, -948.2 kcal/mol, and -867.6 kcal/mol, respectively. For BMAP27, the mutant analog BMAP27-R9 showed better binding (-1199 kcal/mol) than the wild-type (-1139.5 kcal/mol). ClusPro docking also revealed scores of -996.5 and -907.7 kcal/mol for Lynronnel1 and Lynronnel1-R4, respectively. In the IKR18 series, the wild peptide showed significantly higher affinity (-1352.8 kcal/mol) than the mutant IKR18-R2 (-1056.4 kcal/mol). For SAAP148, the wild peptide achieved an energy score (-1108.1 kcal/mol), while the analogs SAAP148-R1, SAAP148-R2, and SAAP148-R5 recorded -1070.7, -1124.9, and -955.9 kcal/mol, respectively.

To further assess the robustness of the docking results, cross-validation of top three peptides was performed using an independent docking tool (HPEPDOCK). The binding energy trends obtained from HPEPDOCK were generally consistent with those observed using ClusPro, supporting the reliability of the predicted interactions. Detailed docking scores are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Taken together, these finding highlighted the strong binding tendency of designed AMPs toward AdeB, suggesting their potential inhibitory activity against A. baumannii. The interaction plots identified that several residues involved in peptide binding correspond to previously reported substrate binding sites in the AdeB binding pockets, or to the regions immediately adjacent to them59,60. In particular, key residues were present in the periplasmic entrance site, distal binding pocket, and proximal pocket of the AdeB transporter domain. For ARV1502 series, wild-type ARV1502 made H bonds with TRP886, GLU884, ALA880, and LEU1026, whereas ARV1502-R2 formed similar number of polar contacts but associated with distinct residues, such as SER1003, TYR356, and PHE509. In the BMAP27 series, mutation resulted in a significant increase in binding affinity, with BMAP27-R9 forming nine H bonds compared to three in the wild-type peptide (Fig. 4a, b, c and b). Among the four C20DK mutants, three showed a favorable hydrophobic interaction pattern. C20DK-R2 and C20DK-R3 formed 21 and 18 hydrophobic contacts, respectively, with six H bonds each. However, C20DK-wild and C20DK-R3 maintained a common set of three H bonds involving ALA881, LEU882 and GLU884 (Fig. 5a and b). The C20DK-R5 mutant presented 24 hydrophobic contacts, while the C2-DK-wild complex had 16 contacts, indicating improved stabilization through a border hydrophobic interface. The IKR18 peptide interacted with AdeB mostly through hydrophobic contacts, while the IKR18-R2 altered the binding interface by forming three H bonds with HIS342 and Glu338. Similarly, Lynronne1 formed only Four H bonds, while the mutant complex Lynronne1-R4 formed nine, strengthening the polar contacts. Among the SAAP148 complexes, SAAP148-R1 formed the most H bonds (10), followed by SAAP148-R2 (8), whereas, SAAP148-R5 and parent SAAP148 has seven H bonds each. Interestingly, GLU884 consistently participated in H bonding in SAAP148, SAAP148-R1, and SAAP148-R5. Furthermore, ALA881 and THR439 were found to maintain H bonding in SAAP148/SAAP148-R1 (Fig. 6a and b) and SAAP148-R5/SAAP148-R1, respectively. Collectively, the interaction studies reveal significant alterations in the AdeB binding interface through distinct polar and hydrophobic contacts, with several mutant peptide complexes achieving robust and more stable interaction patterns compared to their respective wild-type complexes. Molecular docking was carried out for 11 peptides, however, according to subsequent MD simulations, only the BMAP27-R9, C20DK-R3 and SAAP148-R1 consistently maintained stable binding pose and favorable behavior. Investigating molecular behavior through MD simulation

To investigate the structural dynamics and interaction behavior of AdeB with peptides, 200 ns MD simulations were performed in replicates of six systems: the parent peptide complexes (BMAP27, C20DK, and SAAP148) with their respective mutants (BMAP27-R9, C20DK-R3, and SAAP148-R1). The equilibrium of all systems was monitored using temperature, pressure and potential energy profiles, confirming that the systems had properly equilibrated prior to the production run (Supplementary information 3 Figure S7, S8, S9). The deviation of protein backbone was recorded throughout the simulation period to access structural stability. For the BMAP27 complex, the average RMSD values in the parent and mutant systems were found to be 0.44 ± 0.12 nm and 0.46 ± 0.21 nm, respectively (Fig. 7a). Although the parent complex showed a slightly lower mean RMSD, it exhibited greater fluctuations compared to the mutant, while both systems remained overall stable during the simulation. In the case of the C20DK complex, the mean values of RMSD were 0.37 ± 0.08 nm (parent) and 0.45 ± 0.10 nm (mutant), indicating comparable stability across the simulation period (Fig. 7b). For the SAAP148 complex, the average RMSD value was 0.42 ± 0.09 nm for parent and 0.37 ± 0.05 nm for the mutant analog (Table 3). No significant conformational changes were observed during the simulation, indicating stable binding with AdeB protein (Fig. 7c). RMSD replicate results for all 6 complexes are provided in the Supplementary information 3 Figure S10.

Salt-bridge and residue contact map analysis

To investigate the structural stability of the peptide-AdeB complexes, salt-bridge interactions were analyzed throughout the MD simulation trajectories. In the BMAP27 system, no salt-bridge interaction between protein and peptide was observed in the wild-type complex. In contrast, the mutant (BMAP27-R9) formed intra-peptide salt bridges, mainly between ASP10-ARG7 and ASP10-LYS13, with distances in the range of 3.7-7.0 Å. These interactions suggest that the mutation helps stabilize the peptide through internal electrostatic interactions. For the C20DK system, the wild-type complex showed a stable salt bridge between GLU938 (AdeB) and LYS1 (peptide) (2.9–5.7 Å). The mutant (C20DK-R3), however, formed an intra-peptide salt bridge (ASP4-LYS2) that remained present during the simulation, although with some fluctuations (3.1–8.4 Å). This indicates that the mutant maintains stability through internal interactions. In the SAAP148 system, the wild-type complex showed a stable protein–peptide salt bridge between GLU884 (AdeB) and ARG3 (peptide) (3.3–4.2 Å). The mutant (SAAP148-R1) formed multiple salt bridges, including ASP2-LYS5, GLU884-LYS1, and GLU945-LYS1, indicating a more complex interaction network. Although these interactions fluctuated (2.7–8.9 Å), they remained present, suggesting sustained stabilization. In addition to peptide-related interactions, all complexes also maintained several intra-protein salt bridges within AdeB, which remained stable throughout the simulation. These interactions are important for preserving the overall structure of the protein.

To further support these findings, residue contact map analysis was performed (Supplementary information 3 Figure S11 a-f). The contact maps showed clear diagonal patterns, indicating that the backbone structure remained stable, along with consistent off-diagonal contacts representing stable tertiary interactions. The number of contacts (< 0.6 nm) remained relatively constant over time for both wild-type and mutant systems, with no major loss of interactions. Although small fluctuations were seen during the early stages, the systems quickly stabilized and maintained consistent contact levels. Notably, the mutant systems (BMAP27-R9, C20DK-R3, and SAAP148-R1) showed similar or slightly more evenly distributed contact patterns, suggesting better adaptability. This indicates that the mutations do not disrupt stability but instead allow flexible rearrangement of interactions while maintaining the overall structure.

The CD20DK wild type complex covered a broad area on the PCA map, indicating flexible but stable motion. In contrast, the C20DK-R3 mutant formed a tighter cluster. In SAAP148 complex, both wild and mutant peptide formed dense and continuous clusters, suggesting stable and restricted conformational behavior (Fig. 9a, b and c).

Post-simulation binding free energy analysis

We have used MM/PBSA method to calculate the free energy (∆Gbind) of each parent and mutant peptide with AdeB protein. In the BMAP27 and SAAP148 system, the total ∆Gbind of the mutant peptide was generally lower than that of the corresponding parent peptide. For BMAP27, the mutant peptide exhibited a more negative ∆Gbind with a value of -61.15 kcal/mol compared to -31.13 kcal/mol for the parent peptide, indicating a more stable complex. This stabilization was primarily due to the presence of stronger electrostatic interactions. In the C20DK system, the ΔG_bind values of the parent (-28.15 kcal/mol) and mutant (-28.82 kcal/mol) peptides were comparable, with only a slight difference between them. Both van der Waals and electrostatic interactions contributed to the overall binding in this system. For the SAAP148 complex, both the parent and mutant peptides showed a strong affinity for the binding to AdeB; however, the ∆Gbind of the mutant analog was the most negative, at -41.80 kcal/mol, while the for the parent peptide it was − 30.41 kcal/mol, suggesting that mutant complex is stabilized due to favorable non-bonded interactions. The ∆Gbind data is summarized in Fig. 11; Table 4. Overall, MM/PBSA analyses indicate that all peptides formed energetically stable complexes with AdeB and support their potential use as inhibitors of AdeB efflux transporter.

Discussion

In MDR A. baumannii, active efflux mechanism has significantly reduced the effectiveness of existing antibiotics. One such efflux pump crucial for developing resistance to multiple antibiotics is AdeABC, a member of the RND family of efflux pumps, where AdeB (the inner membrane component) appears to be one of the major contributors to resistance development in MDR Gram-negative bacteria61. Upregulated expression of adeB has been observed in MDR stains of A. baumannii. Several findings indicate that increased expression of adeB gene is associated with the elevated MICs of various antibiotics including Tigecycline. Similarly, Rafiei et al., reported that increased adeB gene expression leads to higher levels of resistance to Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole, Ciprofloxacin, and Tigecycline, significantly impacting their MICs62. Due to the crucial role in AdeB an antibiotic efflux and resistance, it has emerged as a high value molecular target for strategies aimed at restoring antibiotic susceptibility and reducing biofilm-associated tolerance in A. baumannii. Therefore, the identification of novel agents capable of interacting with or inhibiting AdeB function has garnered considerable attention as a promising strategy to combat efflux-mediated resistance. Growing experimental evidence suggests that the role of adeB in mediating resistance to cationic AMPs. Deletion of adeB in A. baumannii leads to reduced resistance and increased accumulation of substrates, indicating impaired efflux activity63. Recent studies also suggests that peptides can influence efflux mediated resistance. For example, the cyclic peptide MV6 enhances the efficacy of aminoglycosides in an AdeB dependent manner34. Mastoparan-B has been reported to induce structural changes in the AdeB transporter, downregulate adeB expression, and restore antibiotic susceptibility in MDR strains64. Moreover, AMPs such as Bac7(17) and PAsmr5-17, particularly when used in combination with efflux inhibitors like PaβN, have been found to prevent bacterial growth and biofilm formation35. Furthermore, the AMP Cec4 has demonstrated potent efficacy against MDR A. baumannii in both in vitro and in vivo models65. These findings support the involvement of efflux systems in AMP-mediated activity.

In the current study, we implemented an integrated computational workflow combining ML-based peptide screening, structure prediction, molecular docking, MD simulation as well as binding free energy analysis to explore the potential of AMPs analogs as AdeB targeting candidates. Joker was applied to well characterized A. baumannii active parent peptides, followed by large scale screening using the high performance CatBoost classifier. This approach aligns with and extend recent advancement in peptide therapies, as the Joker has previously been shown to successfully generate novel Mastoparan like AMPs with improved activity66. Similar ML based approaches have been utilized for AMP classification in previous research. For example, to predict potential AMPs against Staphylococcus aureus Khabaz et al., proposed a SVM based model, which achieved an F1 score of approximately 0.8067. In another study, AL-Khudhairy et al., screened potential antibiofilm AMPs from a peptide database, followed by molecular docking and MD simulation against Klebsiella pneumoniae68. In our study, the CatBoost model achieved better predictive performance, with 95.9% accuracy, and F1 score of 0.966, and AUC of 0.989. The model’s high sensitivity and specificity enabled the efficient prioritization of approximately 126 high confidence AMP candidates from the 629 analogs generated by Joker. The molecular and physicochemical parameters suggested that all selected peptides had canonical AMP characteristics. It has been established in literature that the activity of α-helical AMPs is regulated by balance of physicochemical parameters, such as hydrophobicity and net positive charge, which together determine membrane affinity, helical stabilization, and electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged bacterial membrane66. The analogs selected in this study were short and positively charged, with a net charge ranging from + 6 to + 11. They contained a high proportion of hydrophobic amino acid, which helped stabilize the amphipathic alpha-helical structure69,70. Such a balance of hydrophobicity and cationicity is crucial for targeting bacterial membranes. Mastoparan B is a naturally occurring, positively charged (+ 6), amphipathic α-helical peptide derived from the hornet venom. It follows a similar mechanism and exhibits antibacterial properties against a wide range of Gram-negative bacteria71. Likewise, all three selected candidate analogs (BMAP27-R9, C20DK-R3, and SAAP148-R1) had similar biophysical properties, suggesting a common membrane-targeting mode of action. All candidate peptides are predicted to be non-toxic, non-allergenic, and non-hemolytic. This observation revealed that peptide had achieved an optimal amphipathic balance, resulting in reduced toxicity to host membrane while retaining the antibacterial activity, which is crucial criterion for AMP optimization. Furthermore, interaction studies demonstrated that parent and its analogs bind strongly to the AdeB transporter, with the mutant showing the better binding score than the parent peptides. Binding analysis revealed that peptide binding involved multiple hydrogen bonds and a significant number of hydrophobic interactions. AdeB has been shown to utilize a multi-site binding mechanism to accommodate substrate in three regions which includes the entrance to the protein domain, the proximal pocket and the distal hydrophobic pocket. It is noteworthy that, the shortlisted peptides preferentially interacted with functionally important residues in the distal binding pocket and regulatory loop regions of AdeB, which are crucial for substrate recognition and transport by the AdeB protein72. The nature of these interactions provides a potential explanation for how these peptides might interfere with AdeB efflux. Dynamic behavior analysis provided a clearer picture of these results, with three potential analogs identified and validated based on 200 ns MD simulations, along with replicate runs. Low and stabilized RMSD value suggested structural stability, whereas consistent Rg values reflected the formation of a compact structure. Additionally, H-bond analysis indicated that BMAP-R9 and SAAP148-R1 possessed a greater number and more stable H-bonds compared to their original parent peptides. These findings were corroborated using both PCA and FEL analysis, which demonstrated the presence of the parent and mutant peptide complexes in distinct low-energy basins. Furthermore, binding energy calculations showed lower ∆Gbind compared to their respective parent peptide complexes, with electrostatic and van der Waals interactions being the primary drivers. The reported binding free energies are primarily based on enthalpic contributions and are therefore used to compare the relative binding affinities among the studied systems. Experimental evidence on Previously reported parent peptides supports the biological significance of these findings. BMAP27 (bovine neutrophil cathelicidin-derived peptide)73 has been shown to be a potent antibacterial peptide against Gram-negative bacteria, including the clinically significant MDR A. baumannii, which primarily act by forming toroidal pores in the bacterial cell membrane74,75. C20DK, a derivative of the porcine NK-lysin-derived peptide (NK-2), was designed to achieve enhanced stability and activity against bacteria with good hemolytic activity due to its amphipathic α-helical structure in membrane-mimicking conditions76. SAAP148 has been extensively studied and shown to be effective both in vitro and in vivo at low micromolar concentration against antibiotic resistant bacteria and biofilms, including MDR A. baumannii77. Moreover, the combination of SAAP148 derivatives with β-lactam antibiotics such as sulbactam has been reported to restore bactericidal activity against extended spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) producing A. baumannii78. Taken together, BMAP27-R9 (GRFKRFRKKDKLKFIKLSAVIPLLHLG), C20DK-R3 (KKKDRLKLIRISAKILIGKK), and SAAP148-R1 (KDRLKKIVFKALKRIWRQLKKPVR) exhibited enhanced activity compared to their parental forms, inhibiting the inner membrane and exhibiting increased affinity for AdeB efflux transporter. The result suggest that these analogs could be important leads for novel therapies that can modulate efflux-mediated resistance mechanism in A. baumannii. However, it is important to note that these observations are based on computational analysis, and further in vitro and in vivo validation will be necessary to validate their biological activity and mechanism of action.

Conclusion

This study identified potential AMP analogs that target the AdeB efflux transporter in A. baumannii. The pipeline combined Joker-based peptide generation, ML-guided screening, and MD simulations to systematically optimize candidate sequences. From the model analysed, the CatBoost classifier produced reliable outcome, allowing effective selection of potentially active peptide from a large mutant library. Upon assessing the physicochemical and safety profiles, we observed that most candidates exhibited characteristic features of AMPs, such as cationic and balanced amphipathic properties, and relatively low toxicity and hemolytic activity. Analysis of simulation trajectories suggested structural stability and favorable interactions for several AdeB-peptide complexes. In particular, BMAP27-R9, C20DK-R3, and SAAP148-R1 showed better binding scores, stronger hydrogen bonds and more favorable free energy profile than their parent peptides. Thus, based on these results, it can be concluded that modifying the sequence of AMPs in a specific manner can improve the interaction between AMPs and AdeB without compromising their essential antimicrobial characteristics. Furthermore, this work provides an excellent foundation for further in silico optimization of AMPs as potential modulators of efflux-mediated drug resistance. Future experimental testing and rational development of alternative therapeutic approaches targeting MDR A. baumannii infections could benefits from the identified lead peptides.
