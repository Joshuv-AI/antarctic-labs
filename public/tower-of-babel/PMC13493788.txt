Spatiotemporal trends of foot and mouth disease (FMD) in Bangladesh from 2017 to 2023 and their associations with climatic factors and machine learning (ML) based prediction
Authors: Md Jisan Ahmed, Kazi Estieque Alam, Faisol Talukder, Prajwal Bhandari, Md Ismile Hossain Bhuiyan, Mustakim Mamun, Md Arifur Rahman, Ritu Chalise, Md Imran Hossain, Moheuddin Morshed, Mirza Synthia Sabrin, Md Tazul Islam Chowdhury, Md Jahangir Alam, Delower Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFoot-and-mouth disease (FMD) is a highly contagious transboundary viral disease that affects cloven-hoofed livestock, causing substantial economic losses. It is caused by an Aphthovirus under the family Picornaviridae. This study aimed to analyze the spatiotemporal distribution of FMD in Bangladesh, evaluate the influence of meteorological factors, and forecast future disease trends. FMD cases and meteorological variables were obtained from DLS and BMD, respectively. Spatial autocorrelation metrics, including Getis–Ord Gi*, Moran’s I, LISA, and inverse distance weighting, were applied to identify disease clusters and risk zones. Correlation and regression analyses identified significant associations between FMD incidence and climatic factors. Relative humidity and temperature exhibited positive correlations with disease occurrence. Additionally, regression modeling revealed that both relative humidity and wind speed had a significant impact on FMD incidence. Predictive models, including ARIMA, Random Forest, and XGBoost, were applied, with XGBoost providing the most accurate forecasts (RMSE = 153.64), followed by Random Forest and ARIMA. FMD incidence peaked in March, with persistent southeastern hotspots and emerging northern clusters. IDW mapping showed elevated risk in southern regions, particularly during the pre-monsoon period, influenced by climatic factors. These insights can inform targeted surveillance and control strategies to mitigate the burden of FMD in Bangladesh.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57440-2.Keywords: FMD, Spatiotemporal, Time series, Forecast, Climate factors, BangladeshSubject terms: Climate sciences, Diseases, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) is a highly virulent and transmissible viral infection that affects cloven-hoofed animals such as cattle, buffalo, swine, lambs, goats, and over 70 wildlife genera1,2. The causative agent is a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus from the Aphthovirus genus, which is part of the Picornaviridae family3. FMD was first described in Italy in 1514, and nearly 400 years later, Loeffler and Frosch identified its cause as a filterable agent4,5. It is currently a cross-border disease spanning Sub-Saharan Africa, southern Asia, the Arabian Peninsula, and other regions worldwide1. FMD virus (FMDV) is classified into seven serotypes, O, A, C, Asia-1, SAT-1, SAT-2, and SAT-3, on the basis of their immunological properties6, and approximately 65 topotypes have been researched to date7. At the clinical level, the condition is distinguished by elevated temperature, immobility, shivering, salivation, and vesicular sores on the oral cavity, interdigital areas, and udders8. The infection spreads efficiently in restricted, colder, and wetter environments. Climate and environmental factors have a substantial influence on mutational drive9.

Bangladesh reported 930 FMD outbreaks from 1988–1991, with multiple district-level outbreaks still occurring annually8,10. The eastern regions of Bangladesh repeatedly presented higher predicted cumulative incidences of FMD11. Every year, several outbreaks of FMD occur in different districts of Bangladesh, particularly during the winter and pre-monsoon seasons, indicating persistent and recurrent transmission11. FMD is endemic in Bangladesh; earlier reports indicated that serotype O accounts for 80–85% of cases, followed by A (10–15%) and Asia1 (up to 5%)12. However, a more recent study revealed a shift in circulating serotypes, with serotype O remaining the most predominant (35.71%), followed by serotype A (32.14%) and Asia-1 (10.71%)13. Bangladesh has a considerable livestock population comprising 25.07 million cattle, 27.1 million goats, 3.9 million sheep, and 1.5 million buffalo14. Approximately 70% (20% directly and 50% indirectly) of rural people in Bangladesh rely on livestock production. The contribution of livestock to the national economy was 1.8% in 2023–2024, and it is growing at 3.15% annually14. The annual outbreaks of FMD have led to substantial economic losses in the country. Recurring outbreaks each year result in significant economic losses, estimated at approximately US$ 2.22 billion or 188.57 billion Bangladeshi Taka (BDT)15. FMD cases in Bangladesh are made more difficult by an incomplete surveillance system, unintentional immunization campaigns, and unregulated livestock cross-border migration15. Cattle and buffalo migrate from Nepal and India to Bangladesh due to religious differences and favorable market prices. There are no immigration control or livestock quarantine zones in Bangladesh16.

Spatiotemporal cluster analysis is a useful tool in medical and veterinary research for detecting disease trends and clusters among demographics, allowing veterinarians and healthcare experts to respond more effectively17,18. The spatial trend in disease incidence can be characterized as temporary, cyclical, seasonal, or long-term variations, with time series modeling providing an effective tool for examining these trends. Cyclic phenomena refer to constant fluctuations in infection rates, whereas seasonal patterns are tied to certain periods19. In both subtropical and tropical environments, the frequency of infectious conditions varies with the season. In Bangladesh, FMD occurs year-round, with the highest number of cases occurring during the winter and pre-monsoon seasons20,21. Awareness of the seasonal variance of epidemics is crucial for developing effective control methods22.

FMD depends on meteorological and environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, rainfall, and wind speed, which affect virus survival and transmission patterns. It can persist in the atmosphere for several days or weeks at a particular temperature23. A recent study revealed that regions with a warm climate might experience reduced between-farm dissemination levels, poorer epidemic expansion, and easier farm decontamination24. It survives longer in wet conditions than in dry conditions, especially for vegetation at temperatures above 20 °C with 86% humidity, while survival on surfaces under the same conditions is nearly 0%25. In Bangladesh, higher levels of precipitation during the pre-monsoon season were significantly associated with increased FMD cases11. Another survey in Bangladesh reported that winter months (November–February) are linked to more FMD cases, with Dhanbari Upazila reporting a notable 41.56% prevalence26. FMDV is an airborne disease, but there are no data on how wind speed affects the rate of dissemination.

Effective surveillance, appropriate detection, and rapid response are essential components of a robust strategy for managing FMD epidemiology18. For this purpose, spatiotemporal studies and awareness of the impact of climatic influences are essential for planning effective control methods for FMD. In addition, recent advances in machine learning and data-driven modeling have further strengthened the ability to predict outbreak dynamics and identify high-risk areas with improved accuracy and scalability in veterinary epidemiology27. These measures help pinpoint emerging high-risk areas, recognize evolving risk trends, and enhance control and prevention efforts, favoring targeted, risk-based vaccination strategies over mass vaccination. This approach is particularly crucial in resource-constrained environments such as Bangladesh, where optimizing the use of limited resources can significantly slow the spread of the disease18,28. Moreover, FMD transmission is increasingly understood within the broader framework of transboundary animal diseases, in which livestock movement, trade networks, and cross-border animal flows play a central role in disease spread29. Furthermore, climatic variability and environmental change can influence host susceptibility, virus survival, and seasonal disease patterns, thereby shaping the spatial and temporal distribution of outbreaks30.

The spatiotemporal study and predictive modeling of FMD have not yet been published in Bangladesh. However, only one study based on spatiotemporal analysis from 2014 to 2017 was found in Bangladesh31 and a study in Uganda used the Random Forest model32. Additionally, many studies have shown that meteorological factors play a significant role in the incidence and spread of infectious diseases, yet their impact on FMD has not been examined. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the spatiotemporal distribution of FMD in Bangladesh using time-series analysis of reported cases from 2017 to 2023. Additionally, it seeks to develop predictive risk maps for FMD and examine the influence of meteorological and climatic factors on its incidence to forecast potential FMD outbreaks through 2027.

Materials and methods

FMD case data

Bangladesh is divided into 491 sub-districts (also known as Upazila), 64 districts, and 8 divisions. As part of disease surveillance, cases (single cattle or buffalo) that are treated at Upazila veterinary hospitals are regularly documented. The Epidemiology Unit of the Department of Livestock Service (DLS) receives monthly surveillance data from subdistricts, which are then forwarded to districts and divisions. For this investigation, FMD cases in cattle and buffalo from 2017–2023 were obtained from the Department of Livestock Services (DLS), Bangladesh, following official application procedures [https://dls.gov.bd/]. As an official government dataset, it adheres to standardized data collection protocols and quality assurance measures implemented by the DLS. On the basis of only clinical signs and history, field veterinarians can accurately diagnose FMD. However, we only included data where the animals had all of the clinical symptoms of FMD, such as fever, oral lesions, excessive salivation, and lameness, to maximize the positive predictive value of the presumptive diagnosis of FMD33. In Bangladesh, the only disease affecting cattle and buffalo that exhibits all of the symptoms at once is FMD. The final dataset provided by DLS included columns for date, district, and division. Missing or incomplete information in key fields was verified against source records when possible, and any unverified entries were excluded. Dates were standardized to the YYYY-MM format, geographic coordinates were verified against administrative boundaries, and administrative names were corrected to align with official DLS classifications. This workflow ensured a clean, accurate, and standardized dataset suitable for spatial and temporal analyses. The study used reported FMD case counts provided directly by the DLS for each month and year. Population-level denominators were not available, and therefore incidence rates or rate ratios were not calculated; all temporal and spatial analyses were conducted using the reported case counts.

Meteorological data

Meteorological data for this study were obtained from the Bangladesh Meteorological Department (BMD) (https://live7.bmd.gov.bd/). The dataset consisted of time-series observations from 35 meteorological stations distributed across Bangladesh, including automatic, manual, and synoptic stations. These stations provided measurements of rainfall, relative humidity, maximum temperature, minimum temperature, average sunshine duration, and average temperature in CSV format. Because climatic observations were recorded at individual stations and not consistently available at the district level, the station-based time-series data were aggregated to generate a national-level time series representing overall climatic conditions in Bangladesh. Data processing and aggregation were performed using the tidyverse package, and the dataset was reshaped from wide to long format to facilitate data manipulation and statistical modeling.

Statistical analysis

A general descriptive summary and box-and-whisker plots were used to analyze the temporal spread and distribution of FMD cases in Bangladesh. The box plots visually summarize the variability and central tendency of case counts across regions and time periods, highlighting medians, interquartile ranges, and potential outliers.

To determine the features of the global pattern (clustered, dispersed, random) of FMD and to determine whether significant spatial autocorrelation was present, the global Moran’s I statistic was employed as a spatial-correlation measure34,35. Global Moran’s I was calculated using a contiguity-based spatial weights matrix (queen contiguity), where districts sharing a boundary or vertex were considered neighbors. No distance threshold was applied, as contiguity sufficiently defined spatial relationships for areal units. Row-standardization of the weight’s matrix was performed to ensure comparability across districts. All calculations were conducted using the spdep package in R (v4.4.2). There is a positive correlation in the spatial distribution when the Moran’s I index is greater than zero, and a negative correlation in the spatial distribution when the Moran’s I index is less than zero. Moran’s, I index ranges from + 1to − 1. A positive association is more evident when the Moran’s I index value is greater. A random geographical distribution is indicated when Moran’s I index = 036. The formula for Moran’s I is based on Guo et al.37. Global Moran’s I was used to determine whether spatial autocorrelation existed across the entire study region rather than only at specific points in time. The risk of FMD was then evaluated via univariate and bivariate cluster analyses. Local indicators of spatial association (LISA) and univariate global Moran’s I are examples of univariate studies. To find local clusters and evaluate their significance, LISA, the decomposition of univariate global Moran’s I into individual regions, was used38. As an extension of univariate cluster analyses39, bivariate cluster analysis, which includes bivariate global Moran’s I and bivariate LISA, was conducted to determine the spatial correlation between two variables. This analysis was performed with spdep, and the maps were visualized with the ggplot2 package40,41.

The identification of geographical clusters can be simplified via hotspot analysis. To identify the statistically significant high values (hot spots) and low values (cold spots), the analysis uses Getis‒Ord Gi* statistics (Gi*). According to the Gi*34,42, hotspots are spatial clusters with high correlations that are located within a given radius of the entire study region. If there is no discernible global spatial clustering, hotspot detection is sensitive when local spatial events are clustered43. When a disease’s statistics and those of its surrounding conditions are relatively high, it is identified as a hot zone. The confidence intervals are predicted to be 99%, 95%, and 90%, respectively, when the Gi* is determined to be 1, 2, and 3. Here, the Gi*42 is calculated. All contiguity matrices used in this analysis were constructed via R version 4.4.2 and the spdep and ggplot2 packages40,41. Although the Getis–Ord Gi statistic can be implemented using a distance-based spatial weights matrix, this study did not apply a distance-threshold conceptualization. Instead, spatial relationships were defined using a contiguity-based spatial weights matrix (queen contiguity), which does not require the specification of a distance threshold. This approach is widely recommended for analyses involving areal units such as administrative districts, where spatial interaction is more appropriately represented by shared boundaries or vertices rather than Euclidean distance. Distance-based thresholds are more suitable for point data or when spatial interaction decays continuously with distance. Therefore, the absence of a distance threshold in this study reflects a deliberate methodological choice consistent with established spatial analysis. In addition, computing Gi* for each district involves multiple local statistical tests, which may increase the likelihood of Type I errors. These limitations are well recognized for local indicators of spatial association and were considered when interpreting the identified FMD hotspot patterns34,44,45.

Inverse distance weighting (IDW) is a type of deterministic method that assigns weight values on the basis of the neighborhood location and its values46. To perform spatial interpolation of noise levels across Bangladesh, the IDW method was employed. IDW is a deterministic spatial interpolation technique that estimates unknown values on the basis of the weighted average of values at known points, with weights inversely proportional to the distance from the interpolation point46. The interpolation was carried out via geospatial data processed through spatial libraries and packages such as sf, sp, and gstat. To prepare the interpolation grid, a regular grid of 100,000 points was generated on the basis of the extent of Bangladesh’s boundary. The grid coordinates are defined and assigned spatial attributes, followed by gridding and projecting the grid to match the Spatial Reference Systems (SRSs) of the input datasets47–49. In this study, the default bandwidth provided by the IDW implementation in the “gstat” package was used, which determines the number of neighboring points considered for interpolation. The IDW algorithm applied a standard inverse distance kernel with a power parameter of 2 (default), assigning higher influence to closer points and less to distant points. This methodological choice is now explicitly stated to ensure reproducibility of the interpolation procedure50,51. IDW interpolation applies the principle that objects near each other are more similar than objects far from each other. IDW uses the measured values around the prediction location to predict a value for any unmeasured location. The predicted value is more influenced by the measured values closest to the prediction site than by those farther away. IDW assumes that the influence of each measured point on the predicted value decreases as the distance from the prediction location increases. In other words, points closer to the prediction location are assigned higher weights, while points farther away receive lower weights, reflecting the underlying spatial weighting principle of distance decay52.

All spatial datasets were initially processed in the World Geodetic System 1984 (WGS84; EPSG:4326) and subsequently projected to an appropriate Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) coordinate reference system (WGS84 datum) for distance-based interpolation.

The autocorrelation function (ACF) characterizes the average relationship between data points in a time series and the data points that preceded them53. An ACF measures a unitless correlation that ranges from − 1 to + 1. The stationary series is indicated by the abrupt drop in the ACF from 1 to 0, whereas the nonstationary series is indicated by the steady drop in the ACF from 1to 0. This indicates that time series with seasonality or trends are not stationary, whereas stationary series are independent of time. To determine the statistical significance of the ACF at various lags, the Ljung–Box Q (LBQ) test statistic was employed. Using LBQ for autocorrelation, the lag effect up to 5 was examined. After accounting for all correlations at intermediate lags, the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) calculates the correlation between BT epizootics that are separated by k periods. Following the identification of an appropriate order for ARIMA(p,d,q), we use Box and Jenkins’ description of least squares to determine accurate estimates of the model’s parameters. Maximum likelihood, which is asymptotically correct for time series, is used to determine the parameters. For Gaussian distributions, estimators are typically adequate, efficient, and consistent; for several nonGaussian distribution families, they are asymptotically normal and efficient54. The Tseries, forcats, forcast package was used to determine the ACF, PACF, ARIMA and ggplot2 used for visualization41,55–57.

To investigate the associations between the number of FMD cases and meteorological parameters, Spearman’s rank correlation was first employed. This nonparametric method was used to identify potential relationships between FMD cases and weather variables, including rainfall, relative humidity, maximum temperature, minimum temperature, average temperature, and average sunshine. This preliminary analysis facilitated the selection of relevant weather variables as external repressors for further modeling. Multivariate regression techniques, including Poisson regression, quasi-Poisson regression, and negative binomial regression, were subsequently conducted to quantify and model the relationships between FMD cases and the selected meteorological variables. These regression methods account for overdispersion and other potential statistical complexities in the count data, ensuring robust analysis. The dependent variable for multivariate regression was FMD, and the meteorological factors were rainfall, relative humidity, maximum temperature, minimum temperature, average sunshine, and average temperature. The correlation was performed with the corrplot package, and the glm and MASS packages were used to perform the regressions58–60.

In these models, the dependent variable was the number of reported FMD cases, while the independent variables included key meteorological factors: rainfall (mm), relative humidity (%), average temperature (°C), average sunshine duration (hours), and wind speed (m/s). Before model fitting, multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) (Supplementary Table 1). High VIF values indicated multicollinearity, particularly among temperature-related variables (rainfall, average temperature, average sunshine). Although multicollinearity can increase standard errors and reduce the precision of coefficient estimates, it does not bias parameter estimates or compromise overall model validity in generalized linear models (GLMs) such as Poisson, quasi-Poisson, and negative binomial regression61–67. Therefore, rather than excluding theoretically important predictors, which could result in model misspecification and the loss of essential climatic information, all variables were retained in the analysis. Additionally, the models were developed without incorporating time-lagged features, focusing solely on the contemporaneous (immediate) effects of climatic factors on FMD incidence. Although lagged relationships between weather variables and disease occurrence are often relevant, previous evidence indicates that including lag terms can increase methodological complexity and offer limited analytical benefits, especially when sample sizes are relatively small or the temporal lag structure is uncertain. Incorporating lag variables may also reduce model precision with only marginal improvements in explanatory power. Consequently, this study adopted a more parsimonious approach, emphasizing the direct, short-term effects of meteorological conditions on FMD cases68,69.

The VIF results indicated severe multicollinearity for AvgT, MinT, and MaxT, with VIF values of 77.95, 41.05, and 18.93, respectively. Rainfall also showed a relatively high VIF value of 10.63, whereas Avg_sunshine, RH, and W_speed showed comparatively low VIF values. Because AvgT, MinT, and MaxT represent closely related dimensions of temperature, principal component analysis was applied to these three variables to generate a composite temperature index. The first principal component explained 94.39% of the total variation and showed positive loadings for AvgT, MinT, and MaxT, indicating that it effectively captured the shared temperature pattern. The multivariate Poisson, quasi-Poisson, and negative binomial regression models were then re-estimated by replacing the individual temperature variables with this composite temperature index. In addition, reduced-form models were estimated by including AvgT, MinT, and MaxT separately to assess whether the results were sensitive to the choice of temperature variable. Across these alternative specifications, the main findings remained consistent in terms of direction and statistical interpretation. These results suggest that although multicollinearity exists among the temperature-related predictors, it does not materially affect the core conclusions of the study. The PCA results are provided in Supplementary File 2.

Although FMD incidence data were available at the district level, climatic variables were available only as national-level time series for Bangladesh; therefore, spatial regression models (spatial lag, spatial error, or Geographically Weighted Regression) could not be implemented; instead, temporal association analyses were conducted. Consequently, Spearman’s correlation and multivariate regression were used to assess temporal relationships between FMD occurrence and climatic variables, while spatial analyses were limited to hotspot detection and clustering of district-level FMD data.

The random forest algorithm is widely used because of its simplicity in application and the ease with which it is possible to quantify the prediction components. Its foundation is the idea of an enormous number of interrelated decision trees (DTs). To generate precise and trustworthy findings, the random forest builds several trees and then merges them70. To improve accuracy, the random forest method combines the opinions of several trees. Reusing the analytical technique, the random forest is utilized with the GBM to improve the software’s accuracy71. The purpose of bagging is to produce uncertainty. Bootstrap aggregation, or “bagging,” increases the algorithm’s precision and stability. In mathematical terms, b is the number of trees, b = 1, 2, 3 … B; fb = Train a DT fb on Xb, Yb; and X′ is the prediction for the missing data. Random forest was performed via the randomForest package72.

The gradient boosting machine based on regression trees has improved with the extreme gradient boosting (XGB) approach. The technique is based on the idea of “boosting,” which is the process of combining all of the predictions of a collection of “weak” learners to create a “strong” learner via additive training techniques. With this prediction function, XGB’s main goal is to minimize overfitting and underfitting problems while also reducing computing costs73. XG boost was performed via the xgboost package74.

Model evaluation and comparison are determined primarily by the accuracy of the models, reflecting how closely the predicted results align with the actual outcomes. Accuracy is assessed via error metrics, with smaller errors indicating better model performance and fitting. In this study, the evaluation of the XGBoost, autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA), and random forest models included both training and prediction sample assessments. A comprehensive set of evaluation indices was applied, including the mean error (ME), root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean percentage error (MPE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), mean absolute scaled error (MASE), and autocorrelation of errors at lag 1 (ACF1). These metrics quantify the magnitude of the error, where larger values correspond to greater error sizes. Additionally, Theil’s U statistic was used to measure accuracy by comparing the predicted results against minimal historical data predictions. By emphasizing larger deviations through squared errors, Theil’s U aids in identifying methods prone to significant errors. A value of Theil’s U < 1 indicates that the model predictions are superior to baseline expectations, indicating enhanced reliability and precision. All the statistical tests, mapping, and visualization were performed in RStudio and R version 4.4.2, and a statistical significance level of 0.05 was considered.

For comparison with the SARIMA time-series model, Random Forest (RF) and XGBoost (XGB) algorithms were also applied to the raw FMD incidence data. No lagged features (e.g., Cases(t-1), Cases(t-2)) were explicitly generated; instead, the models used the direct monthly case counts as input. While RF and XGB are supervised learning models that do not inherently account for temporal dependencies, these models can still capture patterns and trends in time-series data when trained on sequential raw observations, particularly for short-term forecasting or when the primary objective is predictive performance rather than explicit temporal modeling75–77. This approach provides a parsimonious comparison with the SARIMA model while minimizing additional complexity that may arise from creating lagged features, especially given the limited sample size and the monthly aggregation of FMD cases.

Results

Temporal trend of FMD

FMD is a highly contagious viral disease that primarily affects livestock, and its occurrence is influenced by seasonal patterns, environmental factors, and agricultural practices. Analyzing annual and monthly trends provides critical insights for designing effective control measures. The year-wise trends (Fig. 1) reveal a cyclical pattern, with peaks occurring approximately every two years. The highest spikes were observed in 2019, 2021, and 2023, with cases surpassing 27,000 during these periods, highlighting periodic surges. The monthly trends (Fig. 2) indicate that March consistently records the highest number of FMD cases, followed closely by months in the monsoon season (July to August). This heightened prevalence during monsoon months is likely due to increased humidity, favorable conditions for viral transmission, and challenges in livestock management during wet conditions. Conversely, the winter and summer months present relatively few cases, indicating seasonal variation. Moreover, the changing season has led to an increase in FMD incidence in Bangladesh.

Geospatial hotspot mapping of FMD

From 2017 to 2023, the Gi* maps for FMD outbreaks in Bangladesh revealed distinct spatial patterns over time (Fig. 3). The southeastern region consistently emerges as a significant hotspot (red) across all years, reflecting a persistently high concentration of FMD cases. In contrast, the south-western region begins to show coldspot (blue) patterns starting in 2019, which become more pronounced in 2021 and continue through 2023, indicating a declining intensity of outbreaks in these areas. The central parts of the country remain largely nonsignificant throughout the study period, suggesting minimal clustering in these regions. This year-by-year progression highlights the sustained high risk in the southeast and the gradual stabilization or reduction of FMD activity in the north, pointing to evolving epidemiological trends and spatial dynamics.

The red color represents hotspots (high FMD outbreaks clustered), the blue color represents cold spots (low FMD outbreaks clustered), and the gray color indicates that this dot was not significant. Map visualization was generated using R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org/).

Global spatial autocorrelation was first assessed using Moran’s I for each study year to determine whether FMD incidence exhibited significant spatial dependence. Across all study years (2017–2023), Global Moran’s I values were low and statistically non-significant (p > 0.05), indicating weak to no global spatial autocorrelation in district-level FMD incidence. Although Moran’s I value fluctuated slightly between negative and near-zero values over time, none of the years exhibited a statistically significant departure from spatial randomness (Supplementary Table 2). These results justify the subsequent use of local spatial statistics to explore localized clustering patterns that may not be captured by global measures.

The Getis-Ord Gi Bin (Gi_Bin) maps from 2017 to 2023 reveal notable spatial and temporal variations in FMD outbreaks across Bangladesh, highlighting regions with significant clustering patterns (Fig. 4). In 2017 and 2018, the southeastern region stood out as the primary hotspot, while the rest of the country remained largely nonsignificant. By 2019, secondary clusters had started appearing in the northern regions with lower confidence, indicating the emergence of risks. In 2020, northern hotspots gained prominence alongside continued dominance in the southeast, with additional low-confidence clusters spreading toward the central areas. A significant shift was observed in 2021, where coldspots (low FMD clustering) emerged in some northern regions alongside the development of new northern hotspots at the 95% and 99% confidence levels. By 2022 and 2023, these northern hotspots become more defined, and significant clustering continues to spread westward, with hotspots of varying confidence levels appearing across the northern and central districts. However, the southeastern region remains a constant epicenter of FMD outbreaks with 99% confidence hotspots every year, underscoring its persistent vulnerability.

After conducting LISA analysis, maps representing the spatial clustering of FMD outbreaks were prepared (Fig. 5). Between 2017 and 2020, high-high hotspots were consistently observed in southeastern coastal regions, particularly in Chattogram and southern districts, such as Cox’s Bazar and Khulna, with 95–99% confidence. These findings suggest that these regions remained major epicenters for FMD outbreaks during this period. Conversely, low-outlier coldspots were predominantly located in the northern and northwestern regions, such as Rangpur and Rajshahi, indicating minimal FMD activity in these areas. In 2021, significant hotspots emerged in the northern districts, including parts of Kurigram and Dinajpur, marking a shift from previous patterns. However, hotspots in the southeastern regions remained prominent. This shift indicates the potential spread of FMD outbreaks to previously coldspot regions, highlighting the need for intensified surveillance and intervention in northern Bangladesh. During 2022 and 2023, high-high hotspots became more concentrated in both the northern regions and the southeastern regions, reflecting an expansion in spatial clustering. For example, districts such as Sylhet and Sunamganj in Northeast Bangladesh began to show significant clustering of FMD outbreaks. Low-outlier coldspots persisted in some districts of the northwest, but their extent was lower than that in earlier years. This indicates an overall increase in FMD clustering and activity across multiple regions of Bangladesh. Across the years, hotspots in the southeastern coastal regions, particularly in Chattogram, maintained the highest statistical significance with 99% confidence. Similarly, coldspots in parts of the northwestern districts, such as Rangpur, remained significant at the 95% confidence level for most years. Outliers were sporadically observed in regions such as Sylhet and Dhaka, suggesting localized fluctuations in FMD activity. To synthesize the results from both Getis–Ord Gi* (Fig. 3) and Local Moran’s I (LISA, Fig. 5), districts that consistently emerged as hotspots across both analyses were identified as priority areas for FMD control. The southeastern coastal region, particularly Chattogram, Cox’s Bazar, and Khulna, remained a persistent high-risk hotspot from 2017 through 2023, exhibiting the highest statistical significance (99% confidence in Gi*, high-high in LISA). Northern districts, such as Sylhet, Sunamganj, Kurigram, and Dinajpur, showed increasing clustering in later years (2021–2023), indicating emerging risk areas. In contrast, northwestern districts, such as Rangpur and Rajshahi, consistently appeared as coldspots, reflecting minimal FMD activity. These findings highlight the southeastern coastal region as the main area of concern; while emerging northern hotspots warrant intensified surveillance and preventive interventions. By combining Gi* and LISA results, we provide a robust prioritization of districts based on persistent and statistically significant FMD clustering. These extreme values likely reflect regional outbreaks influenced by factors such as animal movement, market density, or seasonal clustering. While they may slightly affect overall mean densities, the IDW interpolation smooths such variations across neighboring areas, allowing the general spatial and temporal trends to remain accurately represented.

Seasonal ARIMA model selection

To model the temporal trends of FMD cases, the ACF and PACF were initially analyzed to confirm the need for Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (SARIMA) modeling (Fig. 7). The ACF plot exhibited a gradual decay, indicating seasonality, whereas the PACF plot showed significant spikes at lags 1, suggesting the presence of autoregressive terms. A range of SARIMA models with seasonal (P, D, Q) and nonseasonal (p, d, q) parameters was tested to identify the best fit (Table 1). The results of the analysis indicated that the SARIMA (2,1,2) (1,0,1) [6] model was optimal, as it had the lowest RMSE (3197.67), MAPE (17.11), and MAE (2316.17), along with a nonsignificant LBQ p-value (0.900), confirming residual independence. Residual analysis further validated the adequacy of the model, showing no remaining autocorrelation. Alternative models, such as SARIMA (1,1,1) (1,1,1) [6] and SARIMA (1,0,1) (2,1,0) [6], have demonstrated reasonable performance but have outperformed the SARIMA (2,1,2) (1,0,1) [6] model in terms of error metrics and model stability. These findings were supported by detailed model comparison metrics, presented in Table 1, which showed that the BIC values and residual diagnostics provided further evidence of the superior fit of the chosen model.

Predictive modeling comparison

Among the three models, XGBoost showed the best performance, with the lowest RMSE value (153.64), indicating the highest accuracy under the full time-series modeling framework. In the XGBoost output, the actual values and fitted values aligned closely, showing that the model captured the observed FMD case pattern effectively. The projected values followed the general trend of the observed series but appeared smoother, suggesting that XGBoost was able to capture the long-term pattern while reducing sensitivity to short-term fluctuations (Fig. 9A). This reflects the strength of XGBoost in handling complex and nonlinear time-series patterns. For the Random Forest model, different tree sizes were evaluated, including 50, 100, 300, and 500 trees. Among these settings, the model with 100 trees produced the best performance. A fixed random seed of 42 was used to ensure reproducibility. Although the Random Forest model followed the observed values during the fitted period, it produced a considerably higher RMSE value (1469.18) than XGBoost. This indicates weaker predictive performance and lower stability in representing the projected FMD pattern under the same full-series framework. The Random Forest forecast showed less consistency with the XGBoost trend and greater variation over time (Fig. 9B). The ARIMA model showed the weakest performance among the three models, with the highest RMSE value (3197.67). Although ARIMA captured some linear temporal structure, its projected values were less able to reflect the variability and nonlinear pattern observed in the FMD time series (Fig. 9C). Overall, XGBoost outperformed Random Forest and ARIMA, providing the lowest prediction error and the most stable representation of the observed and projected FMD trend (Fig. 9).

Comparison of observed and predicted Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD) cases using three forecasting models: (A) Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), (B) Random Forest (RF), and (C) Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA). The plots compare actual case counts with model predictions for the study period and illustrate the relative predictive performance of the three models.

To evaluate whether the models captured outbreak peaks as well as baseline periods, model performance was assessed separately for peak outbreak months and baseline months. Peak months were identified as March, October, and November based on the monthly distribution of FMD cases. XGBoost showed the best performance in both periods, with the lowest RMSE during baseline months (RMSE = 123.62) and peak months (RMSE = 220.41). Random Forest showed higher prediction errors than XGBoost in both baseline months (RMSE = 1028.96) and peak months (RMSE = 2336.18), indicating weaker performance during high-incidence periods. SARIMA showed the highest errors, particularly during baseline months (RMSE = 126,990.14), and also performed poorly during peak months (RMSE = 4673.00). These results indicate that XGBoost not only captured the overall time-series trend more accurately but also provided better representation of peak outbreak periods compared with Random Forest and SARIMA (Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) dynamics in Bangladesh from 2017 to 2023, combining spatiotemporal distribution patterns, the influence of meteorological factors, and predictive modeling via the ARIMA, random forest, and XGBoost algorithms. While previous studies have explored the geographic and temporal aspects of FMD outbreaks in Bangladesh31, this is the first study to integrate meteorological variables and advanced machine learning techniques to understand disease trends and forecast future occurrences. Our findings reveal distinct seasonal and spatial clustering of FMD cases, with significant associations between disease incidence and climatic factors such as temperature, humidity, and rainfall. The combination of predictive modeling further enhances our understanding by providing a foundation for early warning and control strategies. These results contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the role of environmental and climatic factors in the epidemiology of FMD and offer practical insights for surveillance and intervention planning.

Although FMD is generally not considered to follow a clear seasonal pattern, several time-series analyses have explored its potential seasonal dynamics. These investigations have revealed fluctuations in outbreak frequency, with some studies reporting a pronounced peak during the dry season, particularly in March, and a notable decline during the rainy season, especially in August78. These results align with our findings, which showed the highest incidence of FMD in March and the lowest in June. Similarly, a study conducted in Vietnam reported that the majority of FMD cases occurred during the dry season (November to March)79, which also corroborates our study findings. The fluctuations in FMD incidence may vary for various reasons, such as increased precipitation during the pre-monsoon (March) and monsoon (July or August) seasons, which likely support viral survival, creating an environment where the virus can thrive15. The harvesting of crops in February and March drove the increase in disease occurrence in Bangladesh. Moreover, Eid-ul-Adha plays a critical role in the increase in FMD cases. Many cattle are displayed at live animal markets across the country, facilitating the transmission of FMD among animals. In 2017, the highest number of FMD cases was observed in August and September, which were pre- and post-Eid. A similar pattern was observed in 2018, 2020, 2022, and 2023. Another study revealed that winter (November-February) was the most favorable to the airborne speed of FMD80. We also found that after March (spring), the highest number of FMD cases was observed from October to December, which was supported by Bayir and Gurcan81.

In our study, the southern region, especially the Chittagong division, bordering India and Myanmar, reflected a persistently high concentration of FMD cases from 2017 to 2023. This might be because the extensive cross-border movement of animals from neighboring countries is a significant source of FMDV in Bangladesh82,83. FMD is endemic in India and Myanmar, with prevalence rates of 43% and 56%, respectively84,85. In addition, Chattogram serves as a major hub for livestock trade and transportation, linking border areas, local markets, and other regions of the country. The high density of livestock populations, frequent movement of animals through major trade routes, and the congregation of animals in markets may facilitate virus transmission and contribute to the persistence of FMD hotspots in this region. Similarly, from 2021 to 2022, the northern regions of Bangladesh were hotspots of FMD cases. This might be due to a sudden rise in cross-border cattle trade, increased livestock movement during Eid-ul-Adha, high animal density in northern areas, and low coverage of FMD vaccination due to the low literacy rate among livestock farmers, which increases close contact and makes livestock prone to being affected by FMD15,16,86.

Since 2019, the disease has spread to other regions, such as Khulna, Barisal, Rajshahi, and Sylhet. Among them, all border India except Barisal. However, during Eid, animals from these districts were brought to live animal markets and infected with FMD. In a study conducted in Nepal, the FMD virus was detected in live animal markets. FMD viral RNA from infected animals can persist in market environments even after the animals have left, particularly in settings where cleaning practices are minimal, and animal mixing is common87,88.

Although the queen contiguity matrix is suitable for capturing local spatial dependence among neighboring districts, it may not fully represent long-distance FMD transmission driven by livestock movement across non-adjacent districts. FMD spread can be influenced by animal trade, market networks, transport routes, and informal livestock movement, which may connect geographically distant areas. Therefore, the spatial effects estimated in this study should be interpreted mainly as local adjacency-based spillover. Future research should incorporate livestock movement data, road-network distance, animal market connectivity, or network-based spatial weight matrices to better capture long-distance transmission pathways.

FMDV comprises seven serotypes (O, A, C, SAT-1, SAT-2, SAT-3, and Asia-1) classified on the basis of capsid protein serology and further divided into topotypes by geographic origin and VP1 sequencing. As there is no lasting cross-protection between serotypes, immunity from infection or vaccination against one does not protect against others89. Additionally, antigenic variation within serotypes necessitates vaccines that closely match circulating strains90. In Bangladesh, there is a routine vaccination program; however, coverage has been very low. The country produced only 0.4 million doses of bivalent (O, A) inactivated FMD vaccines20. In Bangladesh, serotype O is prevalent (close to 85%), followed by A (approximately 11%) and Asia-1 (approximately 4%)15. Therefore, FMD is prevalent in this country, and proper vaccination is needed to control FMD. The persistence of FMD hotspots in several regions may also be associated with inadequate vaccine coverage, limited access to veterinary services, and low awareness among livestock farmers regarding FMD prevention and vaccination practices, particularly in rural and border-adjacent areas83,84. These factors can reduce vaccination uptake and increase the susceptibility of livestock populations to FMD outbreaks. The trivalent vaccine will be a good option for producing immunity against all three prevalent serotypes detected in Bangladesh.

In our study, we found positive correlations between FMD cases and rainfall, humidity, temperature, and wind speed. Among the climate factors, only humidity and wind speed were significantly positively associated with FMD incidence in Bangladesh from 2017 to 2023. Mielke and Garabed25 reported that although rising temperatures tend to reduce viral survival by promoting desiccation, elevated relative humidity can counteract this effect by limiting desiccation, thereby enhancing the ability of the virus to survive. The opposite directions observed for average temperature and minimum/maximum temperature in the Poisson model should be interpreted with caution. These temperature variables are highly interrelated, as indicated by the VIF results, and their simultaneous inclusion in the same model can lead to unstable coefficient estimates and sign changes. Therefore, this pattern is more likely a statistical consequence of multicollinearity than evidence of opposing biological effects of different temperature measures on FMD transmission. To reduce this risk of misinterpretation, the temperature effect was further evaluated using PCA-based and reduced-form model specifications. One study reported that the prevalence of FMD is significantly greater in subhumid zones than in semiarid zones91. In relation to wind speed (WS), the observed positive association with FMD incidence contrasts with the findings of Rahman et al.31, who reported no significant relationship. This difference may be attributable to variation in study scale and analytical approach. Although the present study did not estimate spatially varying regression coefficients, integrating temporal regression results with spatial hotspot analyses suggests that wind speed may contribute to the geographical spread and persistence of FMD clusters, rather than influencing only localized transmission intensity. This interpretation is consistent with Gloster, et al.92, who demonstrated that airborne transmission played a dominant role in the early spatial spread of the 1967–1968 FMD epidemic over distances of up to 60 km. In this context, wind speed may act as a facilitator, enhancing spatial connectivity between outbreak areas and thereby reinforcing the persistent hotspots identified through Getis–Ord Gi* and LISA analyses. These findings support the role of meteorological conditions in shaping the spatial dynamics of FMD outbreaks, even when spatially resolved climatic data are unavailable.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the use of passive surveillance data may introduce reporting bias, as reported cases are largely limited to areas near Upazila veterinary hospitals, and active surveillance and widespread vaccination were not implemented during the study period. Nevertheless, passive surveillance data remain valuable for identifying FMD hotspots and informing targeted control strategies. Second, FMD diagnosis was primarily based on clinical signs, with laboratory confirmation being infrequently available. However, due to the characteristic clinical presentation of FMD in cattle and buffalo, experienced field veterinarians in Bangladesh can reliably identify the disease. To enhance diagnostic accuracy, only cases exhibiting all key clinical signs (fever, oral lesions, lameness, and excessive salivation) were included under a strict case definition. Also, the case data were obtained through passive surveillance near Upazila hospitals. This may introduce geographical reporting bias, as areas closer to veterinary or hospital facilities are more likely to report cases than remote areas with limited access to services. Therefore, interpolated risk surfaces may partly reflect reporting accessibility rather than only true FMD occurrence. In remote northern regions, where reporting access may be lower, FMD risk could be underestimated. Future studies using active surveillance, livestock population data, and accessibility-adjusted spatial models would provide a more accurate representation of FMD risk. Third, several important epidemiological factors, including within-country and transboundary animal movement, district-wise pig population, vaccination coverage, and live animal market data, were unavailable. Despite these data gaps, carefully analyzed passive surveillance data can still provide meaningful insights for disease control. Strengthening routine data collection at the Upazila level, particularly given the limited manpower within the DLS for farm-level monitoring, would substantially improve understanding of endemic disease dynamics. Additionally, alternative distance-based spatial weight matrices and threshold sensitivity analyses were not explored in this study. Future research could incorporate these approaches to assess the robustness of identified hotspot patterns. Multiple testing adjustments, such as Bonferroni correction or False Discovery Rate (FDR) control, were also not applied to account for the spatial dependence inherent in the Getis–Ord Gi* statistic. Finally, the unavailability of district-level climatic variables limited the scope of spatial modeling. Consequently, relationships between FMD occurrence and climatic factors were examined using non-spatial temporal analyses, while spatial methods were restricted to identifying clustering patterns. Although nonlinear associations were observed, spatial regression models could not be applied due to the lack of spatially resolved climatic covariates. Future studies should address these limitations by integrating high-resolution climatic and environmental data.

Implications

Our findings offer valuable insights into the temporal dynamics and spatial clustering of FMD in Bangladesh. The results highlight key hotspot areas and peak periods of outbreaks, providing critical information to help policymakers optimize the timing and targeting of national surveillance and control programs. Specifically, our study underscores the importance of seasonally informed intervention strategies, which could increase the effectiveness of FMD prevention efforts. Additionally, identifying high-risk periods and locations can support the strategic allocation of resources. Raising public awareness of biosecurity measures and reinforcing national vaccination programs remain essential for preventing outbreaks and curbing disease transmission.

Conclusion

FMD imposes a significant economic burden on Bangladesh and poses a major obstacle to the growth and development of the country’s livestock sector. Between 2017 and 2023, FMD remained endemic, with peak incidence typically occurring in March and from November to December. Climate plays a role in the occurrence of FMD cases, and border regions in Bangladesh have been identified as FMD hotspots. Targeted surveillance and control in these areas are essential for effective disease management. Furthermore, the DLS passive surveillance system could be enhanced by including detailed farm-level and animal-specific information in reports. This would improve the data quality and support targeted control measures. An approved and implemented roadmap strategy aligned with the FAO-OIE Progressive Control Pathway for FMD is urgently needed.
