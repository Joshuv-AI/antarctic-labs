. 1.
Introduction

A common approach to optimizing a smooth function
\widetilde{J}
on a matrix Lie group

G
is to pull it back to the Lie algebra via the exponential map:
write
J(\theta)=\widetilde{J}(\exp(\theta))
and optimize over
\mathfrak{g}
.
This approach has become standard in rigid-body trajectory
planning
[
Murray et al.(1994)
,
Barfoot and Furgale(2014)
,
Solà et al.(2018)
]
and in the Riemannian optimization
literature more broadly
[
Absil et al.(2008)
,
Boumal(2023)
,
Mahony and Manton(2002)
]
.
One advantage is that the algebra is a vector space, so gradient methods
apply directly; the disadvantage is that the smoothness of
J
as a function
of
\theta
depends on how the exponential map distorts distances.
For compact groups such as
\mathrm{SO}(n)
this distortion is uniformly bounded
and poses no difficulty for convergence analysis.
For non-compact groups the situation is less clear, and the conditioning of
exponential-coordinate parameterizations has received comparatively little
systematic attention in the optimization literature, despite the prevalence
of
\mathrm{SE}(3)
in robotics and geometric control.
The price of the parameterization is that one must control

L(R;\mathfrak{g})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\theta\|_{F},\|\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}\leq R\\
\theta\neq\theta^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\|\nabla J(\theta)-\nabla J(\theta^{\prime})\|_{F}}{\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}},

since any gradient-descent analysis requires
L
to limit the step size to at most
1/(2L)
.

The behaviour of
L(R;\mathfrak{g})
turns out to depend
heavily on the algebraic structure of
\mathfrak{g}
in a way that is not
immediately obvious from the definition.
When
\mathfrak{g}
is compact, the group is bounded and
L
stays bounded too.
When
\mathfrak{g}
contains an element with a positive real eigenvalue,

\exp(tH)
grows exponentially. So does
L
.
For
\mathfrak{se}(3)
, neither of these applies: translations can be arbitrarily large,
but no element has a real positive eigenvalue, and the gradient Lipschitz constant
turns out to be
\mathcal{O}(R^{2})
for bounded objectives, with an explicit objective
exhibiting the same quadratic lower growth.
This intermediate behaviour fits within the broader trichotomy studied in
[
K.C and Mishra(2026)
]
.
The present paper provides a self-contained quantitative study of this intermediate case:
an operator-norm bound
\|\exp(\theta)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1+R
,
an
\mathcal{O}(R^{2})
Lipschitz upper bound for bounded objectives
(Proposition
3.4
), and an explicit adversarial construction
exhibiting
\Omega(R^{2})
lower growth (Theorem
4.2
).

We note an inherent structural distinction in this analysis.
The upper bound (Proposition
3.4
) is derived under the assumption
that
\widetilde{J}
has uniformly bounded gradient and Hessian
(Definition
2.2
), whereas the adversarial lower bound
(Theorem
4.2
) uses the translation-distance objective
J^{*}
,
whose gradient grows with
R
and therefore lies outside the bounded class.
Constructing a tight adversary within the admissible class
remains an open problem; the difficulty is that globally bounding the Hessian
on
\mathcal{B}_{R}
rules out objectives whose curvature grows with the search radius.
The paper therefore does not claim minimax sharpness:
the upper bound is a universal estimate over the admissible class,
and the lower bound is existential via a specific constructed objective.

The reason
\mathfrak{se}(3)
lands in the quadratic case is structural.
Its adjoint eigenvalues are
\{\pm i\|\omega\|,0,0,0,0\}
— purely imaginary or zero —
so no element is hyperbolic and
\exp(\theta)
does not grow exponentially.
But the group is non-compact (translations are unbounded), so
L
is not bounded either.
Compact algebras avoid norm growth because their exponential maps remain uniformly bounded;
hyperbolic elements generate unbounded stretching.
The algebra
\mathfrak{se}(3)
occupies an intermediate regime for three reasons.
First, the rotational component
\mathfrak{so}(3)
is compact:

\exp(\Omega)\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
is always orthogonal, so rotation alone
does not amplify norms and cannot generate growth in
L
.
Second, the translational coupling in the semidirect product

\mathfrak{se}(3)=\mathfrak{so}(3)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{3}
introduces
exactly one factor of
R
per differentiation via the Jacobian
J_{L}(\Omega)v
,
and squaring that coupling in the Lipschitz estimate yields
R^{2}
.
Third, the absence of hyperbolic eigenvalues (all eigenvalues of
\mathrm{ad}_{(\omega,v)}

are purely imaginary or zero) prevents the exponential amplification
that arises in algebras such as
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
.
Translation directions are sheared by rotation but not exponentially amplified.
The practical implication is direct: step sizes for
\mathrm{SE}(3)
optimization
should decay like
1/R^{2}
, substantially less aggressive than the exponential
decay required for
\mathrm{GL}(n)
objectives.

Sections
2
–
4
contain the setup and the two proofs;
Section
5
adds the step-size corollary and a brief numerical check;
Section
6
discusses what we do and do not know about the problem.
Figure
1
summarises the three growth regimes.

L(R;\mathfrak{g})
arising from exponential-map parameterizations on

\mathfrak{so}(n)
) give
bounded
L
; the special Euclidean algebra
\mathfrak{se}(3)
gives

(e.g.
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
) give exponential blow-up.

2.
Notation and Preliminaries

2.1.
Matrix Lie algebras and norms

Throughout, Lie groups are closed matrix subgroups
G\subset\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})
.
The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is the set of all
X
such that
\exp(tX)\in G

for every
t
, with Lie bracket
[X,Y]=XY-YX
.
We write
\|A\|_{F}=(\mathrm{tr}\,A^{\top}A)^{1/2}
for the Frobenius norm
and
\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sup_{\|x\|=1}\|Ax\|
for the spectral norm.

Two norm conventions arise naturally and we fix one throughout.
For
\theta=(\omega,v)\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
, the 6-vector norm is

(1)

\|\theta\|_{F}=\bigl(\|\omega\|^{2}+\|v\|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}.

The standard matrix Frobenius norm of the
4\times 4
homogeneous
representation gives instead

(2)

\|X\|_{\mathrm{mat}}=\bigl(\|\Omega\|_{F}^{2}+\|v\|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}=\bigl(2\|\omega\|^{2}+\|v\|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2},

because
\|\Omega\|_{F}=\sqrt{2}\|\omega\|
for skew-symmetric
\Omega
.
Since
\|\theta\|_{F}\leq\|X\|_{\mathrm{mat}}\leq\sqrt{2}\|\theta\|_{F}
,
the two differ by at most
\sqrt{2}
and the
R^{2}
growth rate is unchanged
under either convention.
We use (
1
) throughout.

2.2.
The special Euclidean algebra

The group
\mathrm{SE}(3)
consists of orientation-preserving rigid motions

x\mapsto Rx+t
with
R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{se}(3)
consists of
4\times 4
matrices of the form

X=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega&v\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\Omega\in\mathfrak{so}(3),\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

where
\Omega
is the
3\times 3
skew-symmetric matrix with axial vector
\omega
.
We identify elements with pairs
(\omega,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Definition 2.1
.

An element
H\in\mathfrak{g}
is
hyperbolic
if it has at least one
eigenvalue with strictly positive real part.

No element of
\mathfrak{se}(3)
is hyperbolic: for any
(\omega,v)
,
the eigenvalues are
\{0,0,\pm i\|\omega\|\}
in the
4\times 4
representation
and
\{0,0,0,0,\pm i\|\omega\|\}
in the adjoint representation,
all with zero real part.
For context, in a semisimple Lie algebra the same notion coincides with
the component along
\mathfrak{a}
in a Cartan decomposition
[
Knapp(2002)
,
Hall(2015)
]
.

2.3.
The function class

Definition 2.2
.

Write
J(\theta)=\widetilde{J}(\exp(\theta))
, and let

\mathcal{B}_{R}:=\{\theta\in\mathfrak{se}(3):\|\theta\|_{F}\leq R\}

denote the closed ball of radius
R
in the 6-vector norm.
We say
J
belongs to the
bounded-
(M_{1},M_{2})
class
if there exist
constants
M_{1},M_{2}\geq 0
such that for all
R>0
,

\sup_{\exp(\theta)\in\mathcal{M}_{R}}\|\nabla\widetilde{J}\|_{F}\leq M_{1},\qquad\sup_{\exp(\theta)\in\mathcal{M}_{R}}\|\nabla^{2}\widetilde{J}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq M_{2},

where
\mathcal{M}_{R}=\{\exp(\theta):\|\theta\|_{F}\leq R\}
.
The key requirement is that
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
do not depend on
R
;
this rules out objectives whose derivatives grow with the search radius.

3.
Operator-Norm Bound and Upper Bound

The Rodrigues formula says what
\exp(\theta)
looks like concretely.
The rotation block
\exp(\Omega)\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
is no surprise;
the less obvious part is that the translation column
\Psi=A(\Omega)v

is controlled by the integral
A(\Omega)=\int_{0}^{1}\exp(s\Omega)\,ds
,
which we will show is a contraction.

3.1.
The Rodrigues formula

The formula itself is classical
[
Murray et al.(1994)
,
Park and Martin(1994)
]
.
For
\theta=(\omega,v)
with
\|\omega\|>0
, the matrix exponential takes the
block form

(3)

\exp(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\exp(\Omega)&A(\Omega)v\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

where the left Jacobian is

(4)

A(\Omega)=I+\frac{1-\cos\|\omega\|}{\|\omega\|^{2}}\,\Omega+\frac{\|\omega\|-\sin\|\omega\|}{\|\omega\|^{3}}\,\Omega^{2}.

The scalar coefficients are bounded uniformly:

(5)

\left|\frac{1-\cos\|\omega\|}{\|\omega\|^{2}}\right|\leq\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad\left|\frac{\|\omega\|-\sin\|\omega\|}{\|\omega\|^{3}}\right|\leq\tfrac{1}{6},

as follows by examining the Taylor series at
\omega=0
:

\lim_{t\to 0}(1-\cos t)/t^{2}=1/2
and
\lim_{t\to 0}(t-\sin t)/t^{3}=1/6
,
so both bounds are tight at
\omega=0
.
The constants appearing in all subsequent bounds are explicit analytically derived
estimates; they are not numerically optimised.

3.2.
Operator-norm bound

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\theta=(\omega,v)\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
and
\|\theta\|_{F}\leq R
, then

(6)

\|\exp(\theta)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1+R.

Proof.

Write
\Psi=A(\Omega)v
, where
A(\Omega)=\int_{0}^{1}\exp(s\Omega)\,ds

[
Murray et al.(1994)
,
Iserles et al.(2000)
]
.
Since each
\exp(s\Omega)
is orthogonal, the triangle inequality for integrals gives

(7)

\|A(\Omega)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\exp(s\Omega)\,ds\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\exp(s\Omega)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,ds=\int_{0}^{1}1\,ds=1.

Hence
\|\Psi\|_{2}=\|A(\Omega)v\|_{2}\leq\|v\|_{2}\leq\|\theta\|_{F}\leq R
.

For the block matrix, let
(u,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
satisfy
\|(u,s)\|=1
.
Then
\exp(\theta)(u,s)^{\top}=(\exp(\Omega)u+s\Psi,\,s)^{\top}
, so

\|\exp(\theta)(u,s)^{\top}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\exp(\Omega)u+s\Psi\|_{2}^{2}+s^{2}.

Expanding and applying
|\langle\exp(\Omega)u,\Psi\rangle|\leq\|u\|\|\Psi\|_{2}
:

\|\exp(\Omega)u+s\Psi\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|u\|^{2}+2|s|\,\|u\|\|\Psi\|_{2}+s^{2}\|\Psi\|_{2}^{2}.

Set
a=\|u\|
,
b=|s|
, so
a^{2}+b^{2}=1
.
Let
p=\|\Psi\|_{2}
. Adding
s^{2}=b^{2}
and using
2ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}=1
:

\|\exp(\theta)(u,s)^{\top}\|_{2}^{2}\leq a^{2}+p+b^{2}p^{2}+b^{2}=(a^{2}+b^{2})+p+b^{2}p^{2}=1+p+b^{2}p^{2}.

Since
b^{2}\leq 1
, we have
b^{2}p^{2}\leq p^{2}
, giving

\|\exp(\theta)(u,s)^{\top}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1+p+p^{2}.

Since
p=\|\Psi\|_{2}\geq 0
, we have
1+p+p^{2}\leq 1+2p+p^{2}=(1+p)^{2}
.
Therefore
\|\exp(\theta)(u,s)^{\top}\|_{2}\leq 1+\|\Psi\|_{2}\leq 1+R
. ∎

Remark 3.2
.

What makes this work is the contraction
\|A(\Omega)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
,
which says the Jacobian integral does not stretch vectors.
This is a purely SO
(3)
phenomenon:
A(\Omega)
is an average of orthogonal matrices,
so it cannot have operator norm greater than 1.
For a hyperbolic element
H\in\mathfrak{gl}(n)
with positive real eigenvalue

\lambda
, the integrand
\exp(sH)
grows like
e^{s\lambda}
and the integral
amplifies rather than contracts —
\|\exp(tH)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq e^{t\lambda}\to\infty

— so no bound
\|A(H)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
can hold.
This contrast is the structural observation behind
[
K.C and Mishra(2026)
]
.

3.3.
Fréchet derivative bound

Lemma 3.3
.

For
\theta=(\omega,v)\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
with
1\leq\|\theta\|_{F}\leq R

and any
H\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
with
\|H\|_{F}=1
,

\|D\exp_{\theta}[H]\|_{F}\;\leq\;1+R.

Proof.

Write
H=(h_{\omega},h_{v})
with
\|h_{\omega}\|^{2}+\|h_{v}\|^{2}=1
.
The Fréchet derivative splits along the block structure of
\exp(\theta)
:

D\exp_{\theta}[H]=\begin{pmatrix}D\exp_{\omega}[h_{\omega}]&A(\Omega)h_{v}+(D_{\omega}A)[h_{\omega}]v\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

For the rotation block, the standard integral formula
[
Iserles et al.(2000)
]
gives

D\exp_{\Omega}[h_{\omega}]=\exp(\Omega)\int_{0}^{1}e^{-s\Omega}\,\hat{h}_{\omega}\,e^{s\Omega}\,ds
,
where
\hat{h}_{\omega}
is the skew-symmetric matrix with axial vector
h_{\omega}
.
Since conjugation by orthogonal matrices preserves the Frobenius norm and

\|\hat{h}_{\omega}\|_{F}=\sqrt{2}\|h_{\omega}\|
, integrating over
[0,1]
gives

(8)

\|D\exp_{\Omega}[h_{\omega}]\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{2}\|h_{\omega}\|.

Under the 6-vector convention (
1
),
\|h_{\omega}\|=\|\hat{h}_{\omega}\|_{F}/\sqrt{2}
,
so bound (
8
) gives

\|D\exp_{\Omega}[h_{\omega}]\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{2}\|h_{\omega}\|=\|\hat{h}_{\omega}\|_{F}
.
Since the 6-vector norm of the rotation input is
\|h_{\omega}\|

(not
\sqrt{2}\|h_{\omega}\|
), the rotation block contributes
\|h_{\omega}\|

when
\|D\exp_{\theta}[H]\|_{F}
is evaluated by summing the squared
Frobenius norms of the output blocks under the same identification.
The translation block has two terms.
First,
\|A(\Omega)h_{v}\|\leq\|h_{v}\|
since
\|A(\Omega)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1

(Lemma
3.1
).
Second, by differentiating the integral
A(\Omega)=\int_{0}^{1}\exp(s\Omega)\,ds
,

D_{\omega}A[h_{\omega}]=\int_{0}^{1}s\,D\exp_{s\Omega}[h_{\omega}]\,ds
.
Using the same integral formula,
\|D\exp_{s\Omega}[h_{\omega}]\|_{F}\leq\|h_{\omega}\|

for each
s\in[0,1]
(independent of
s
since orthogonality is uniform).
Hence
\|(D_{\omega}A)[h_{\omega}]v\|\leq\|v\|\int_{0}^{1}s\,ds\,\|h_{\omega}\|=\tfrac{1}{2}\|v\|\|h_{\omega}\|
.
Putting the two block estimates together:

\|D\exp_{\theta}[H]\|_{F}\leq\bigl(1+\tfrac{R}{2}\bigr)\|h_{\omega}\|+\|h_{v}\|.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to vectors

(1+R/2,1)
and
(\|h_{\omega}\|,\|h_{v}\|)
with
\|h_{\omega}\|^{2}+\|h_{v}\|^{2}=1
:

\|D\exp_{\theta}[H]\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{(1+R/2)^{2}+1}.

For
R\geq 1
, one checks
(1+R/2)^{2}+1\leq(1+R)^{2}

(equivalent to
3R^{2}/4+R-1\geq 0
, which holds for
R\geq 2/3
),
so
\|D\exp_{\theta}[H]\|_{F}\leq 1+R
. ∎

3.4.
Upper bound on
L(R;\mathfrak{se}(3))

Proposition 3.4
.

For
J
in the bounded-
(M_{1},M_{2})
class and
R\geq 1
:

L(R;\mathfrak{se}(3))\leq C(\mathfrak{se}(3))\,(M_{1}+M_{2})\,R^{2},\quad C(\mathfrak{se}(3))=\tfrac{5(\sqrt{2}+1)}{3}\approx 4.02.

Proof.

By the chain rule
\nabla J(\theta)=(D\exp_{\theta})^{*}[\nabla\widetilde{J}(\exp(\theta))]

and the mean-value inequality:

\displaystyle\|\nabla J(\theta)-\nabla J(\theta^{\prime})\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|D\exp_{\theta}-D\exp_{\theta^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,M_{1}

(9)

\displaystyle\quad+\|D\exp_{\theta^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,M_{2}\,\|\exp(\theta)-\exp(\theta^{\prime})\|_{F}.

Bounding Term 2.

The Fréchet-derivative bounds used here may be interpreted as
structure-aware conditioning estimates for the matrix exponential on

\mathfrak{se}(3)
, in the spirit of
[
Kenney and Laub(1989)
,
Dieci and Papini(2001)
]
.
Lemma
3.3
gives
\|D\exp_{\theta^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1+R
,
so by the mean-value inequality,

\|\exp(\theta)-\exp(\theta^{\prime})\|_{F}\leq(1+R)\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}
.
So Term 2
\leq(1+R)^{2}M_{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}(R^{2})M_{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}
.

Bounding Term 1.

We need the Lipschitz constant of
\theta\mapsto D\exp_{\theta}
.
From the block form (
3
), the Fréchet derivative splits as

D\exp_{\theta}[H]=\begin{pmatrix}D\exp_{\Omega}[h_{\omega}]&A(\Omega)h_{v}+(D_{\omega}A)[h_{\omega}]v\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Rotation block difference.

Differencing the integral formula
D\exp_{\Omega}[H]=\exp(\Omega)\int_{0}^{1}e^{-s\Omega}He^{s\Omega}ds

at
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
and bounding the integrand by

\|\exp(\Omega)-\exp(\Omega^{\prime})\|_{F}\leq\|\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}\|_{F}

(since
\exp
is Lipschitz-1 on
\mathfrak{so}(3)
,
as
\|\exp(\Omega)-\exp(\Omega^{\prime})\|_{F}\leq\|\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}\|_{F}

follows from
\exp(\Omega)=\int_{0}^{1}D\exp_{\Omega^{\prime}+(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})t}[\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}]\,dt

and
\|D\exp_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
for
\Omega^{\prime\prime}\in\mathfrak{so}(3)
) gives

\|D\exp_{\Omega}-D\exp_{\Omega^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}\|_{F}=\sqrt{2}\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|
,
contributing
\sqrt{2}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}
to Term 1.

Translation block difference.

The coupling
(D_{\omega}A)[h_{\omega}]v
has norm
\leq\frac{1}{2}\|v\|\|h_{\omega}\|\leq\frac{R}{2}\|h_{\omega}\|

(from Lemma
3.3
).
The difference
(D_{\omega}A(\Omega)-D_{\omega}A(\Omega^{\prime}))[h_{\omega}]v
requires bounding

\|D_{\omega}A(\Omega)-D_{\omega}A(\Omega^{\prime})\|_{\mathrm{op}}
.
Differentiating (
4
) explicitly:

(10)

D_{\omega}A[h_{\omega}]=\frac{\sin\|\omega\|}{\|\omega\|}h_{\omega}\times+\alpha(\|\omega\|)(\omega\cdot h_{\omega})\Omega+\beta(\|\omega\|)(\omega\times h_{\omega})\times,

where
\alpha,\beta
are bounded trigonometric functions with

|\alpha(\|\omega\|)|\leq\tfrac{1}{3}
and
|\beta(\|\omega\|)|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.
The Lipschitz constant of
\omega\mapsto D_{\omega}A
satisfies

\|D_{\omega}A(\Omega)-D_{\omega}A(\Omega^{\prime})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{J}\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|

where, from the coefficient bounds (
5
) and the explicit
form (
10
),
C_{J}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Multiplying by
\|v\|\leq R
gives translation contribution
\tfrac{1}{2}R\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}
.

To assemble the two contributions, use the block Frobenius estimate

(11)

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{F}\leq\|A\|_{F}+\|B\|_{F},

applied to the rotational block difference (
A
) and translation block difference (
B
).
The rotation block contributes
\sqrt{2}\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|
(from (
8
)).
The translation coupling satisfies, using
\|J_{L}(\Omega)-J_{L}(\Omega^{\prime})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|
(which follows from
C_{J}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
):

\|(D_{\omega}A(\Omega)-D_{\omega}A(\Omega^{\prime}))[h_{\omega}]v\|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|\cdot\|v\|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}R\,\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}.

The block estimate (
11
) then gives, using
\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|\leq\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}
:

\|D\exp_{\theta}-D\exp_{\theta^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\bigl(\sqrt{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}R\bigr)\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}.

For
R\geq 1
,
\sqrt{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}R\leq\tfrac{5(\sqrt{2}+1)}{3}R

(since
C=\tfrac{5(\sqrt{2}+1)}{3}\approx 4.02>\tfrac{1}{2}
, so

CR-\tfrac{R}{2}=(C-\tfrac{1}{2})R\geq\sqrt{2}
for
R\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{C-1/2}\approx 0.41
),
giving:

(12)

\|D\exp_{\theta}-D\exp_{\theta^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\tfrac{5(\sqrt{2}+1)}{3}\,R\,\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F}.

Combining.

Inserting both bounds into (
3.4
):

\|\nabla J(\theta)-\nabla J(\theta^{\prime})\|_{F}\leq\Bigl[\tfrac{5(\sqrt{2}+1)}{3}R\cdot M_{1}+(1+R)^{2}M_{2}\Bigr]\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{F},

giving
L(R;\mathfrak{se}(3))\leq C(\mathfrak{se}(3))(M_{1}+M_{2})R^{2}
with

C(\mathfrak{se}(3))=\frac{5(\sqrt{2}+1)}{3}\approx 4.02
.
∎

4.
Polynomial Lower Bound

The upper bound shows
L
cannot grow faster than
R^{2}
.
To see that it actually does grow that fast, we need an explicit example.
This matters for two reasons: it confirms the
R^{2}
regime is not an artefact
of the upper-bound proof technique, and the explicit constant
0.0505
gives
a concrete lower limit on how aggressively step sizes must decay as
R
grows.
We exhibit such an example as follows: pick a point
\theta_{R}
on the sphere
\|\theta\|_{F}=R

where the objective
J^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\|t(\exp(\theta))\|^{2}
is
curving sharply, compute the Hessian, and read off the lower bound from there.

4.1.
A closed-form formula

The following closed-form formula is used in the proof.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\omega=(\phi_{0},0,0)^{\top}
and
v=(0,0,r)^{\top}
with
\phi_{0},r>0
.
Then

(13)

\exp(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\exp(\phi_{0}\Omega_{1})&p(\phi_{0},r)\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad p(\phi_{0},r)=r\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-\tfrac{1-\cos\phi_{0}}{\phi_{0}}\\[4.0pt]
1-\tfrac{\phi_{0}-\sin\phi_{0}}{\phi_{0}}\end{pmatrix},

where
\Omega_{1}=\mathrm{skew}(e_{1})
.

Proof.

Apply (
4
) with
\|\omega\|=\phi_{0}
:

A(\phi_{0}\Omega_{1})=I+\frac{1-\cos\phi_{0}}{\phi_{0}}\Omega_{1}+\frac{\phi_{0}-\sin\phi_{0}}{\phi_{0}}\Omega_{1}^{2}
.
The identities
\Omega_{1}e_{3}=-e_{2}
and
\Omega_{1}^{2}e_{3}=-e_{3}
give directly

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=re_{3}+\tfrac{1-\cos\phi_{0}}{\phi_{0}}\,r(-e_{2})+\tfrac{\phi_{0}-\sin\phi_{0}}{\phi_{0}}\,r(-e_{3})

\displaystyle=r\bigl(0,\;-\tfrac{1-\cos\phi_{0}}{\phi_{0}},\;1-\tfrac{\phi_{0}-\sin\phi_{0}}{\phi_{0}}\bigr)^{\top}.\qed

4.2.
The lower bound

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
J^{*}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\|t(\exp(\theta))\|_{2}^{2}
,
where
t(\cdot)
extracts the translation.
For
R\geq 2
, the on-ball point
\theta_{R}=(e_{1},\sqrt{R^{2}-1}\,e_{3})

satisfies
\|\theta_{R}\|_{F}=R
exactly, and

(14)

L_{J^{*}}(R;\mathfrak{se}(3))\geq\tfrac{3}{8}|\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)|\,R^{2}\approx 0.0505\,R^{2},

where
\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)=-8\sin 1-10\cos 1+12\approx-0.1348
.

Proof.

First, set
r=\sqrt{R^{2}-1}
and perturb the rotation component:

\theta(h)=(1+h,0,0,\,0,0,r)
passes through
\theta_{R}
at
h=0

(here
\phi_{0}=1
at the base point).
The point
\theta_{R}
was chosen because the translation component
curves most sharply as the rotation angle passes through
\phi_{0}=1
:
this is where the cross-coupling between rotation and translation
contributes most strongly to the Hessian.
By Lemma
4.1
,

J^{*}(\theta(h))=\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}\,\phi(1+h),\qquad\phi(s)=\Bigl(\tfrac{1-\cos s}{s}\Bigr)^{2}+\Bigl(\tfrac{\sin s}{s}\Bigr)^{2},\quad s=\phi_{0}+h.

Since

L_{J^{*}}(R)=\sup_{\theta\neq\theta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}}\frac{\|\nabla J^{*}(\theta)-\nabla J^{*}(\theta^{\prime})\|}{\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|}\geq\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{B}_{R}}\|\nabla^{2}J^{*}(\theta)\|_{\mathrm{op}},

where the inequality follows because the Lipschitz quotient limits to the
Hessian norm as
\theta^{\prime}\to\theta
, it suffices to exhibit a point
where
|\nabla^{2}J^{*}(\theta)[H,H]|
is large.
To make this concrete, we compute the Hessian of
J^{*}
at the
point
\theta_{R}
in the direction
H=(e_{1},\mathbf{0})
,
which perturbs only the rotation angle and has unit 6-vector norm.
The translation column of
\exp(\theta_{R}+hH)
is captured by
\phi(1+h)
,
so the curvature of
J^{*}
in this direction is
\frac{r^{2}}{2}|\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)|
.

(15)

|\nabla^{2}J^{*}(\theta_{R})[H,H]|=\tfrac{r^{2}}{2}|\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)|=\tfrac{R^{2}-1}{2}|\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)|.

Next, compute
\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)
explicitly. It controls how fast the translation of
\exp(\theta_{R})

curves as the rotation angle is perturbed.
To compute it, write
f_{1}(s)=(1-\cos s)/s
and
f_{2}(s)=(s-\sin s)/s
,
so that
\phi=f_{1}^{2}+(1-f_{2})^{2}
.
The relevant derivatives at
s=1
are:

f_{1}^{\prime}(s)=\frac{s\sin s+\cos s-1}{s^{2}},\quad f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(s)=\frac{s^{2}\cos s-2s\sin s-2\cos s+2}{s^{3}};

f_{2}^{\prime}(s)=\frac{\sin s-s\cos s}{s^{2}},\quad f_{2}^{\prime\prime}(s)=\frac{s^{2}\sin s+2s\cos s-2\sin s}{s^{3}}.

At
s=1
:
f_{1}^{\prime}(1)=\sin 1+\cos 1-1
,

f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(1)=2-\cos 1-2\sin 1
,

f_{2}^{\prime}(1)=\sin 1-\cos 1
,

f_{2}^{\prime\prime}(1)=2\cos 1-\sin 1
.
Evaluating
\phi^{\prime\prime}=2(f_{1}^{\prime})^{2}+2f_{1}f_{1}^{\prime\prime}+2(f_{2}^{\prime})^{2}-2(1-f_{2})f_{2}^{\prime\prime}
at
s=1
:

(16)

\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)=-8\sin 1-10\cos 1+12.

Numerically,
-8(0.84147)-10(0.54030)+12=-0.1348
,
confirmed by direct symbolic differentiation.

Since
\theta_{R}\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
and
\|H\|_{F}=1
, the lower bound follows:

L_{J^{*}}(R;\mathfrak{se}(3))\geq|\nabla^{2}J^{*}(\theta_{R})[H,H]|=\tfrac{R^{2}-1}{2}|\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)|.

The
R^{2}-1
factor reflects that the adversarial point sits just inside the ball.
For
R\geq 2
, one has
R^{2}-1\geq\frac{3}{4}R^{2}
(with equality at
R=2
), giving

L_{J^{*}}(R;\mathfrak{se}(3))\geq\frac{3}{8}|\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)|R^{2}\approx 0.0505\,R^{2}
. ∎

Remark 4.3
.

The
R^{2}
growth here comes from two things multiplying: the translation magnitude
r

squared in
J^{*}
, and the rotational curvature
|\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)|
.
The objective
J^{*}
is not in the bounded-
(M_{1},M_{2})
class of
Definition
2.2
because
\|\nabla\widetilde{J}^{*}\|=\|t(g)\|

grows with
R
.
So Theorem
4.2
and Proposition
3.4
apply to different
function classes, and the ratio
\approx 79
between their constants
is not a measure of sharpness within a single class.
Gaps of this size between chain-rule upper bounds and adversarial lower bounds
are common in geometric Lipschitz analysis
[
Higham(2008)
,
Birtea and Comănescu(2015)
]
.

Corollary 4.4

(Polynomial growth regime)
.

(i)

For
J^{*}
(translation-distance objective):
L_{J^{*}}(R;\mathfrak{se}(3))\geq\tfrac{3}{8}|\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)|\,R^{2}
.

(ii)

For
J

(in the class-wise sense of (i) and (ii) above),
establishing
\mathfrak{se}(3)
as the canonical example of the polynomial growth regime.
The lower bound (Theorem
4.2
) uses
J^{*}
outside the bounded class;
the upper bound (Proposition
3.4

See Remark
4.3

Proof.

The lower bound is Theorem
4.2
.
The upper bound is Proposition

is self-contained from Lemmas

6.
Conclusion

The paper proves two things, restricted to
exponential-map parameterizations on
\mathfrak{se}(3)

and the specific objective classes of Section
2
.
First,
\|\exp(\theta)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1+R
for all
\theta\in\mathfrak{se}(3)

with
\|\theta\|_{F}\leq R
.
Second,
L_{J^{*}}(R;\mathfrak{se}(3))\geq 0.0505\,R^{2}
for
R\geq 2
.
Both proofs use only the Rodrigues formula and elementary calculus.
The upper bound
L\leq 4.02(M_{1}+M_{2})R^{2}
is established in Proposition
3.4
.

Before stating the open questions, we note the geometric character of the growth.
The polynomial rate
L=\mathcal{O}(R^{2})
is not a curvature phenomenon:

\mathrm{SE}(3)
carries a flat Cartan–Schouten connection
(a standard fact in Lie group geometry; see e.g.
[
Knapp(2002)
]
).
The
R^{2}
growth is instead consistent with the torsion structure of the semidirect
product
\mathbb{R}^{3}\rtimes\mathrm{SO}(3)
: one factor of
R
from
\|v\|

and one from the
\omega
-coupling in
J_{L}(\Omega)v
.
This geometric picture is consistent with the broader trichotomy of
[
K.C and Mishra(2026)
]
.

Some questions remain.
The gap between the constants
4.02
and
0.0505
— a factor of roughly 79 —
is large, and we do not know whether it is fundamental or an artifact of comparing
two different function classes.
Getting a lower bound within the same function class would sharpen the picture
considerably, but our adversarial construction does not achieve this.
The mechanism behind the
R^{2}
rate extends immediately to

\mathfrak{se}(n)=\mathbb{R}^{n}\rtimes\mathfrak{so}(n)
for any
n\geq 2
:
the exponential map retains the block form (
3
)
(see
[
Gallier and Quaintance(2020)
]
for the general
\mathrm{SE}(n)
case)
with
\exp(\Omega)\in\mathrm{SO}(n)
orthogonal and

J_{L}(\Omega)=\int_{0}^{1}\exp(s\Omega)\,ds
a contraction,
so Lemmas
3.1
and
3.3
carry over verbatim
and the gradient Lipschitz constant satisfies
L(R;\mathfrak{se}(n))=\mathcal{O}(R^{2})

for all
n
.
Whether the
R^{2}
rate is tight for all semidirect products
K\ltimes V

with
K
compact remains an open question.

More broadly, whether
R^{2}
is the right exponent for all non-compact
Lie algebras without hyperbolic elements remains open.
The Heisenberg algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{n}
is nilpotent with no hyperbolic elements,
but its bracket structure differs from
\mathfrak{se}(3)
; we conjecture:

Conjecture 6.1
.

For the Heisenberg algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{n}
,
L(R;\mathfrak{h}_{n})=\Theta(R^{2})
.
