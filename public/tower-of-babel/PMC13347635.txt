A Novel Dynamic Principal Component Analysis Based on Block Dynamic Mode Decomposition for Chemical Process Fault Detection
Authors: Pu Yang, Zongquan Xie, Shuqi Sheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dynamic principal component analysis (DPCA) has been widely adopted for process monitoring and fault detection in dynamic systems. However, its application in chemical process industries remains constrained by several limitations, including vulnerability to intermittent disturbances caused by equipment vibrations, inability to effectively capture long-term time dependence, and lack of consideration for subsystem coupling. To overcome these limitations, we propose an enhanced methodological framework. First, a frequency-based clustering technique is introduced to remove intermittent noise caused by equipment vibrations. Second, a novel matrix augmentation strategy is introduced to improve DPCA’s capacity to capture long-term time dependence. Third, block matrix dynamic mode decomposition is introduced to model the subsystem coupling. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves competitive performance under moderate lag settings and superior fault detection performance under large lag settings, with advantages in detecting correlated faults across multiple subsystems. In addition, ablation experiments demonstrate that each improvement plays a positive role, and their integration leads to performance gains in complex industrial environments.

Introduction

With the rapid development of information technology and automation, modern process systems are increasingly equipped with extensive sensor networks, enabling the continuous collection of process data. Such data can provide valuable insights for process monitoring and fault detection. Multivariate Statistical Process Control (MSPC) has thus attracted significant attention as a data-driven approach for monitoring and diagnosing complex industrial processes. By analyzing the statistical correlations and patterns among process variables, MSPC facilitates real-time fault detection, thereby helping to maintain the stability and safety of system. −

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) has long been regarded as an important technique in multivariate statistical analysis and process monitoring. PCA was originally developed to identify orthogonal directions of maximum variance in high-dimensional data and reduces dimensionality. It projects sample data onto a lower-dimensional subspace while retaining as much variability as possible. This data-driven characteristic has made PCA particularly valuable in modern process systems, where large-scale data sets are collected. Over the past few decades, numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of PCA and its variants in fault detection and process monitoring across a wide range of industrial sectors including the chemical industry − and power industry. , Its ability to identify differences in the statistical distribution of process variables has been well established. However, in modern process systems, which are characterized by nonlinearity and time dependence, these assumptions are frequently violated, thereby undermining the diagnostic effectiveness of PCA.

To address these challenges, many extensions and modifications to PCA have been developed. Ku proposed Dynamic PCA (DPCA) to enable the model to capture time dependence by augmenting the input matrix. However, given that the principal components extracted by DPCA may not be inherently dynamic, Dong introduced Dynamic Inner PCA (DiPCA) to extract a set of dynamic latent variables,ensuring the derived principal components are dynamic. Nonlinear extensions of PCA, such as Kernel PCA (KPCA), have also been developed to address nonlinearities in process data. Furthermore, Ren proposed an autoencoder-based KPCA to improve the computational efficiency of traditional KPCA. Inspired by deep learning, Deng developed Deep PCA (DePCA) to enhance fault detection performance via multilayer feature extraction. Notably, these methods for nonlinearity all leverage kernel tricks or neural network-based representations to map input data into higher-dimensional feature spaces, where linear separability is more achievable. Beyond PCA variants, researchers have explored other multivariate methods for specific scenarios: Partial Least Squares (PLS) is employed for cases involving both input and output variables, , while Independent Component Analysis (ICA) is used for fault detection in systems with non-Gaussian data. , In addition, deep learning methods such as convolutional neural networks have been applied to process fault detection by automatically extracting hierarchical features from time-series data. ,

Although numerous new methods have been developed to address PCA’s shortcomings, critical challenges remain in chemical process monitoring. In chemical production processes, the operation of rotating equipment or fluctuations in flow velocity induce the microvibrations of equipment. While small, such vibrations interfere with sensor measurements, thereby misleading the process monitoring models to make a wrong alarm. Although existing denoising algorithms effectively mitigate noise from continuous vibrations, they cannot suppress intermittent, decaying vibrations triggered by flow velocity fluctuations. Moreover, industrial chemical systems typically consist of multiple interdependent subsystems, yet existing approaches rarely account for the coupling between these subsystems, which limits model accuracy and robustness. Additionally, while the sampling period of sensors in chemical process systems operates on the order of seconds or even milliseconds, some process variables such as temperature and pressure change slowly. Given this condition, most approaches need to set the lag to a large value, which leads to a disproportionate increase in the number of model parameters and a decrease in both model accuracy and computational speed.

To address the aforementioned challenges, this paper proposes a novel framework termed DMDPCA. Unlike conventional DPCA-based methods that treat temporal dependence, noise suppression, and subsystem coupling independently, the proposed method provides a unified solution targeting three key limitations in industrial process monitoring.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:(1)A frequency-based clustering (FBC) method is developed to suppress intermittent and nonstationary vibration noise, which cannot be effectively handled by conventional denoising techniques.(2)A transfer entropy-based augmentation strategy is proposed to selectively incorporate informative time-lagged variables, enabling efficient modeling of long-term dependencies while avoiding excessive dimensionality.(3)A block dynamic mode decomposition (Block DMD) framework is introduced to explicitly model subsystem coupling, improving the detection performance for correlated faults.

Methodology

To effectively address the challenges of intermittent noise, long-term time dependence, and subsystem coupling in chemical processes, the proposed framework integrates three dedicated modules. The FBC module is designed to handle nonstationary vibration noise, the transfer entropy-based augmentation module aims to capture long-term dynamic dependencies with controlled model complexity, and the Block DMD module is introduced to explicitly characterize subsystem interactions. Unlike traditional approaches that address these issues separately, the proposed method combines them in a unified framework, where each module targets a specific limitation while complementing the others.

Denoise with Frequency-Based Cluster

Noise induced by equipment vibrations in chemical processes is typically composed of two components: constant noise and intermittent, decaying noise. The former is linked to the sustained operation of rotating equipment, while the latter arises from abrupt changes in flow velocity, such as those triggered by valve adjustments. Most conventional denoising techniques assume noise is constant and follows a normal distribution, rendering them ineffective at suppressing the intermittent, decaying noise. To address this limitation, we design FBC to discriminate between stable intervals (i.e., those without intermittent noise) and disturbed intervals (i.e., those with intermittent noise). A key issue here is that applying clustering directly to raw data typically yields only a few identified outliers, rather than meaningful segmentation of stable and disturbed regions. We think the primary distinction between these interval types resides in their frequency-domain characteristics. Accordingly, we partition the data sequence into multiple intervals and perform clustering on their frequency-domain representations to distinguish between the two. Compared with conventional denoising methods that assume stationary or Gaussian noise, the proposed FBC approach leverages frequency-domain characteristics to distinguish intermittent disturbances, making it more suitable for nonstationary vibration noise commonly observed in industrial processes.

Prior to applying FBC, the raw data are standardized using z-score normalization (mean-centering and scaling to unit variance) to eliminate differences in magnitude and unit across variables. Since the constant noise component appears uniformly across all intervals, it does not affect the frequency-domain clustering and is implicitly handled by the following compensation step. Therefore, no additional denoising is required prior to FBC. The standardized data are then partitioned into p nonoverlapping intervals, each containing q consecutive samples, as illustrated in eq 1 [x1x2⋯xqxq+1xq+2⋯x2q⋯⋯⋯⋯x(p−1)q+1x(p−1)q+2⋯xpq] where p represents the total number of intervals, q represents the number of samples within each interval and x i represents the i-th sample composed of process variables.

Subsequently, a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is applied to each row of the data matrix, converting the time-domain signals into their corresponding frequency-domain representations. Due to the inherent symmetry of the FFT for real-valued signals, only the first q/2 frequency components that contain unique information are reserved. To keep the difference between different intervals while reducing dimensionality, the magnitudes of these components are doubled and used as input for clustering. In the following study, the interval length q is set to 100 samples, providing a frequency resolution of approximately 0.016 Hz at the 1 Hz sampling rate. This value balances the need for sufficient frequency resolution against the requirement for an adequate number of intervals for reliable clustering. The number of clusters C in FCM is set to 7, corresponding to disturbed intervals from 6 process units plus one stable intervals. The fuzziness exponent is set to 2.0, and the algorithm terminates when the change in the objective function falls below 1 × 10–3 or the maximum number of iterations (100) is reached.

Finally, the Fuzzy C-means (FCM) clustering is employed for clustering. FCM partitions the data set into C clusters, allowing each sample to belong to multiple clusters simultaneously. This feature makes it well-suited for handling transitional data segments that lie between stable and disturbed intervals. The cluster containing the largest number of segments is then identified as corresponding to the stable intervals and others are disturbed intervals. The stable intervals are directly transformed into time-domain data through Inverse FFT­(IFFT), while the interference intervals are the same after noise compensation. Let F s and F d denote the cluster centroids of the stable and disturbed classes in the frequency domain, respectively. For each disturbed interval with frequency representation F i, the compensated frequency representation F c is computed as follows: 2 Fc=Fi−(Fd−Fs)

This shifts the frequency profile of the disturbed interval toward that of the stable class, suppressing the intermittent noise component. The compensated interval is then converted back to the time domain via IFFT.

Data Matrix Augmentation

DPCA

In chemical production, some process variables change slowly, such as temperature and pressure. These process variables thus exhibit long-term time dependence, which presents a need for handle time dependence. To address this issue, DPCA augments the input data matrix and conducts PCA analysis on the augmented matrix to handle time dependence. Given a data matrix X as eq 3 X=[x1x2⋯xn]T Where x i is a l × m vector representing a sample, and each column of x i represents a monitored variable, n is the total number of samples. The common PCA extracts principal components from X through the eq 4 X=TPT+Ẽ Where matrix P represents the load matrix and its column vectors are called load vectors, each column of T is the projection of X in the direction of load vectors, E represents the residual matrix. Equation can be solved by singular value decomposition.

While in DPCA, to handle time dependence, time-lagged samples are simply appended to the original sample, forming a new sample that contain time dependence. And the data matrix X aug is augmented as follows 5 Xaug=[x̂1x̂2⋯x̂n−l]Tx̂i=[xixi+1⋯xi+l−1] Where X aug ∈ R (n–l)×ml is the data input in DPCA and l represents the max lag of time dependence. This augmentation strategy leads to a disproportionate increase in the dimension of the data matrix when dealing with long-term time dependence, as it takes all time-lagged variables into account. Especially when the sample frequency of sensors is rather high, the max lag must be increased to capture the long-term time dependence. This disadvantage brings about both performance degradation and heavy computational burden in DPCA when addressing long-term time dependence.

Augmentation Based on Transfer Entropy

Given the weakness of the existing augmentation strategy, we propose a novel strategy for data matrix augmentation. This strategy controls the dimension growth of the data matrix and the number of parameters by selectively retaining time-lagged variables with a high likelihood of long-term dependence, while discarding those with low. It begins by calculating transfer entropy (TE) between every pair of variables. As a directional, information-theoretic measure, TE effectively captures causal interactions and dynamic dependence and is inherently robust to noise. It is employed to identify the variables and their corresponding lags that show strong causal influence on the current variables, contributing to the construction of a more compact and informative augmented matrix. The formal expression for TE is provided in eq . 6 Tx→y(τ)=∑xt,yt,xt−τp(xt,yt,xt−τ)log⁡p(yt|xt,xt−τ)p(yt|xt) Where T x→t (τ) represents the TE from x to y with time lag τ, x t–τ represents the x at time t – τ; p(x t , y t , x t–τ) represents the joint probability of X at t and t – τ, and y at t; p(y t | x t , x t–τ) represents the conditional probability of y t given both x t and x t–τ, p(y t | x t ) represents the conditional probability of y t given only x t without time-lagged X. In practice, the joint and conditional probability distributions in eq are estimated using k-nearest neighbor (KNN) probability estimation, a nonparametric approach well-suited for large-scale industrial time-series data. The number of nearest neighbors is set to 10 to balance estimation accuracy and computational efficiency. For each pair of variables (x, y), the TE is computed across a range of candidate lags from 1 to the largest lag. The lag that maximizes the TE is selected as the candidate lag for that variable pair. If the maximum TE surpasses the threshold, the time-lagged x t–τ variable is appended into the data matrix as an additional column. Otherwise, no augmentation is performed for that variable pair. Notably, the threshold is defined using a surrogate test. For each variable pair, the source time series is randomly shuffled 50 times to generate a set of surrogate TE values. The 95th percentile of these surrogate TE values is taken as the threshold to screen the original TE results. The augmentation strategy is defined in eq 7 XTE=[XV] 8 V=[vτv(τ+1)⋯v(τ+n)]T Where v i represents the i-th sample of the selected variable, τ is the lag that maximize the TE. If multiple variables are selected, the augmentation proceeds in the same way. By selectively appending time-lagged variables, we can effectively control the dimension of the data matrix while maintaining the performance of the model. Unlike traditional lag-based augmentation that blindly includes all time-lagged variables, the proposed strategy selectively retains only those with strong causal influence, thereby significantly reducing redundant information and improving computational efficiency while preserving essential dynamic characteristics.

Block DMD

In previous studies, researchers treated the entire process system as a unified entity, applied PCA or its variants for process monitoring under this assumption. This simplification of model complexity enabled effective monitoring performance in certain scenarios. However, in some chemical process systems that consist of multiple interdependent subsystems, this assumption leads to a deterioration in model performance.

Given the weakness above, we introduce Block DMD to model the subsystem coupling. Block DMD is a structured extension of Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD), which explicitly models the coupling relationships between subsystems by partitioning high-dimensional data matrices into multiple low-dimensional block matrices based on physical modules, temporal scales, or functional correlations. The core concept involves decomposing the global dynamics of complex systems into the superposition of local modes and coupling modes, thereby preserving the linear approximation advantages of DMD while enhancing the capability to characterize complex systems with multisubsystems. Compared with conventional PCA-based methods that ignore subsystem structure, the Block DMD framework explicitly models intersubsystem interactions, enabling the detection of correlated faults that may be overlooked due to the cancellation effects in global modeling approaches.

Given a input data matrix X TE augmented by eq , each row of X TE represents a sample of monitored variables. The first step involves rearranging the column based on the structure of subsystem, such that variables belonging to the same subsystem are grouped into the same block matrix. The rearranged matrix X̂ is represented as eq 9 X̂=[S1,S2,···,Sk] where S k (k ≤ m) represents block matrix composed of k-th subsystems’ monitored variables. The assignment of variables to subsystem blocks is based on the physical structure of the production process. Variables measured within the same process unit are grouped into the same block. In the asphalt production case studied in Section , six blocks are defined corresponding to the heating furnace, electric desalting, initial distillation, atmospheric distillation, vacuum distillation, and oxidation blending units. Variables appended through the TE-based augmentation are assigned to the same block as their original (nonlagged) variable.

Results and Discussion

Asphalt Production Process and Experimental Setup

Asphalt is a crucial material for road construction and waterproof engineering, and its quality directly dictates the durability, high-temperature stability, and low-temperature crack resistance of the resulting infrastructure. Since asphalt quality is closely tied to the operational stability of production equipment, it is necessary to develop methods for detecting equipment faults. The asphalt production process involves multiple disciplines including chemical reaction engineering, thermal process engineering and automatic detection technology.

In the case we study, the asphalt production equipment comprises heating furnace units, electric desalting units, initial distillation units, atmospheric distillation units, vacuum distillation units, and oxidation blending units. These units are susceptible to various faults, which can be categorized into two types: some are local faults, typically mitigated by the unit’s own backup or adjustment mechanisms and posing few impacts on other units. Others are correlation faults, which often affect multiple units and can ultimately lead to production halts. The data set used in this paper includes both fault types, encompassing 18 distinct fault categories. The data set comprises approximately 30,000 normal samples and 3000 fault samples, collected at a 1 Hz sampling rate. Typically, each fault sequence starts with a normal operating segment, followed by the onset and evolution of the fault. Each sample contains 3001 time steps of process data, corresponding to 3000 s. For model development, 25,000 normal samples plus half of the fault samples are used for training, validation, and parameter tuning. The remaining normal samples and fault samples are reserved for testing to evaluate detection performance. The fault set includes 18 distinct fault categories: 8 local faults and 10 correlated faults. The number of test samples per fault category ranges from 104 to 312. A total of 226 process variables are monitored, distributed across 6 subsystem blocks corresponding to the six production units mentioned above. The 226 monitored variables are distributed across the six subsystem blocks as follows: heating furnace (45 variables), electric desalting (25 variables), initial distillation (35 variables), atmospheric distillation (48 variables), vacuum distillation (43 variables), and oxidation blending (30 variables). Average Performance Under Different lag

Figure presents the monitoring curves of the four MSPC methods (DRPCA, DiPCA, OMDPLS, and DMDPCA) on a sample sequence from the test data set. DCNN is excluded from this figure because it operates as a classifier and does not produce continuous monitoring statistics comparable to T 2 and SPE. This sequence exhibits a microvibration around the 500th sample and a F4 fault (local faults) around the 1100th sample. Notably, the factory-provided labels for the data set are not completely accurate; as a result, the time points may earlier than the actual timing of vibration and fault onset. All four models performed well during both the normal stage (1–∼500) and the fault phase (∼1100–2000). During the normal phase, monitoring indicators generally remained below the upper control limit (UCL) at the 99% confidence level (0.99UCL), resulting in few false alarms. In the fault stage, these indicators rose rapidly and quickly exceeded the 0.99UCL, with sustained deviations thereafter. By contrast, during the microvibration stage (∼500–∼1100), the monitoring indicators of DRPCA and DiPCA exhibited larger fluctuations, resulting in numerous false alarms. This arose because equipment microvibrations induced intermittent noise in the data from some contact sensors, and conventional denoising techniques failed to effectively mitigate. Among these, the Q r indicator of DiPCA showed particularly significant fluctuations. This is likely because the Q r indicator is specifically designed to monitor changes in the system’s dynamic components, and data variability was exceptionally high during the microvibration stage. Although the indicators of DMDPCA also exhibited larger fluctuations, they rarely exceeded 0.99UCL, and thus there were fewer false alarms. Similarly, the indicators of OMDPLS showed fluctuations during the microvibration stage that were larger than those of DMDPCA but comparable to DRPCA and DiPCA, as OMDPLS also lacks the FBC-based noise suppression mechanism.

We also examined the impact of lag changes on model performance. Table shows the average FAR, FDR and test time of five models under the different lag. When the lag is 2 or 5, due to the lack of capturing dynamic characteristics, all models perform poorly on FAR and FDR. When the lag is 10, models can capture the dynamic characteristics of rapid processes, and thus the performance has significantly improved, but there are still some dynamic characteristics of medium and slow processes that have not been captured. When lag reaches 20, although the dynamic characteristics of slow processes (such as temperature) are not contained, the performance of DiPCA and DRPCA reaches the best. After that, as the lag increases, the dimension of the data matrix is too large due to the nonlinear growth of matrix dimensions, and DiPCA and DRPCA experience a certain degree of overfitting, with UCL becoming too large, resulting in a decrease in FDR. However, DMDPCA, by selectively augmenting the data matrix, captures the dynamic characteristics of slow processes while avoiding overfitting, and FDR reaches 95.28%. DCNN shows relatively stable performance across lag values due to its learned feature representations, but its FDR plateaus beyond lag = 20 and does not benefit from further increases. OMDPLS exhibits a performance trajectory like DiPCA, with FDR declining at large lag values due to the same dimensionality-related overfitting problem.

Note that the UCL values of each model are different, the vertical axis has been modified to T 2/0.99UCL for a clear show. For the local fault, all models demonstrated similar performance in terms of the time required to detect faults. As for the correlation fault, DiPCA and DRPCA are significantly slower than DMDPCA. As DRPCA and DiPCA do not explicitly model subsystem coupling, when a correlation fault occurs, different subsystems may exert opposing influences on the same principal component and restrain the magnitude of change in that component. This ultimately prevents statistical indicators from detecting the fault-induced changes in the principal component, leading to missed faults in early time. Only when the fault’s impact reaches certain magnitude do the principal components exhibit significant changes, allowing the indicators to detect the fault. OMDPLS, despite having a multiblock structure, does not explicitly model the dynamic coupling between subsystems via DMD and thus shows similar detection latency to DRPCA and DiPCA on correlated faults. Meanwhile, DMDPCA captures the subsystem coupling (including self-coupling) via DMD, constructs principal components on the incremental matrix and directly monitors the changes in the data itself, thereby it can detect correlation faults earlier than DRPCA and DiPCA.

Ablation Experiments

To further validate the effectiveness of each component in the proposed DMDPCA framework, ablation experiments are conducted by systematically removing or modifying individual modules. Specifically, four variants are considered: (1) the baseline DPCA model, (2) DPCA with frequency-based clustering (FBC), (3) DPCA with FBC and transfer entropy-based augmentation (TE), and (4) the full proposed DMDPCA model incorporating Block DMD. All models are evaluated under the same experimental conditions using the asphalt production data set with lag = 100, and performance is assessed in terms of fault detection rate (FDR) and false alarm rate (FAR).

By capturing interactions among different subsystems, the proposed model can detect correlated faults, which are often difficult to identify using global modeling approaches due to the cancellation effects among variables.

Conclusion

In this paper, we present DMDPCA, a novel approach for chemical process monitoring and fault detection. To reduce the impact of microvibration of the equipment, we perform cluster based on the frequency of the sensor data and compensate for the noise based on the differences of the cluster centers. To prevent excessive lag from slowing down the calculation speed and accuracy of the model, we selectively append the time-lagged variables into the data matrix through TE. Finally, we model the coupling of the subsystems using dynamic mode decomposition and construct principal components on the incremental matrix, enabling DMDPCA to detect correlation faults earlier. To verify the effectiveness of the proposed approach, we perform comparison experiments on the asphalt production process. The experimental results demonstrate that DMDPCA outperforms DRPCA, DiPCA, DCNN, OMDPLS in scenarios involving large lag values and correlated faults across multiple subsystems. Under moderate lag settings, the proposed method achieves the lowest false alarm rate while maintaining a fault detection rate comparable to existing methods. The ablation study further confirms that each module contributes to the improvement of overall performance, validating the effectiveness of the proposed framework design. Although the proposed method shows promising results, further investigation is required to evaluate its generalization ability across different industrial processes. In addition, the selection of model parameters still relies on empirical settings, which may limit its adaptability in practical applications. Future work will focus on extending the proposed framework to more diverse industrial scenarios and developing adaptive parameter selection strategies to improve robustness and applicability in real-time monitoring systems.
