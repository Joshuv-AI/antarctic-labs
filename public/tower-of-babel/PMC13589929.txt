SNX3 exacerbates hepatic fibrosis by amplifying TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway in hepatocyte-HSC fibrotic loop
Authors: Jing Zhang, Yuxing Dai, Jiaying Yu, Yilin Chen, Dinghu Ma, Wenjing Yu, Mingxia Peng, Zeyu Li, Jing Lu, Honghai Xu, Suowen Xu, Duanping Sun, Peiqing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatic fibrosis is a chronic disease driven by dysregulated TGF-β1-mediated interactions between hepatocytes and hepatic stellate cells. The molecular mechanisms controlling TGF-β1 degradation versus secretion remain poorly understood. Here, we identify sorting nexin 3 (SNX3), a retromer-associated cargo-binding protein, as a critical regulator of TGF-β1-driven hepatic fibrosis. SNX3 expression was significantly elevated in liver tissues from fibrosis patients and mouse models, correlating positively with TGF-β1 signaling. Functional studies using conditional mouse models demonstrated that hepatocyte-specific SNX3 knockout provided substantial protection against fibrosis, while overexpression induced spontaneous liver fibrosis. Mechanistically, SNX3 binds TGF-β1 and prevents its lysosomal degradation by promoting retention in early endosomes, thereby enhancing secretion. In hepatocytes, SNX3 increases TGF-β1 secretion that activates neighboring hepatic stellate cells through paracrine signaling. In activated hepatic stellate cells, elevated SNX3 expression further amplifies TGF-β1 production, creating a self-perpetuating fibrotic cascade leading to enhanced downstream signaling and transcriptional activation of fibrotic genes. Pharmacological studies validated therapeutic potential, with TGF-β1 signaling inhibition attenuating SNX3-induced fibrosis while pathway activation abolished SNX3 knockout protection, establishing SNX3 as a promising therapeutic target.Key words: SNX3, TGF-β1, Smad, Hepatic fibrosis, Hepatocyte, Hepatic stellate cells, Lysosomal pathway degradation, Fibrotic loop

Introduction

Hepatic fibrosis still represents a major global health burden1, characterized by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins that progressively impair liver function and can ultimately lead to cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma2. Despite decades of research, effective therapeutic interventions remain limited, highlighting the urgent need to identify novel molecular targets and understand the fundamental mechanisms driving fibrogenesis3,4.

The pathogenesis of hepatic fibrosis is fundamentally driven by dysregulated cellular crosstalk between hepatocytes and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), with transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) serving as the key regulator of this fibrotic cascade5. Following liver injury, damaged hepatocytes release TGF-β1, which activates HSCs through paracrine signaling. Activated HSCs subsequently transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts that overproduce ECM components, notably collagen type I and fibronectin, while simultaneously perpetuating their own activation via an autocrine TGF-β1 loop6,7. This self-perpetuating cascade depends critically on Smad2/3 signaling. Following TGF-β1 receptor engagement, Smad2/3 undergoes phosphorylation, forms transcriptionally active complexes with Smad4, and finally translocates to the nucleus. There, these complexes bind promoters of pro-fibrotic genes to initiate transcription8.

While the importance of TGF-β1/Smad signaling in hepatic fibrosis is well-established, molecular mechanisms controlling TGF-β1 bioavailability remain incompletely understood9. TGF-β1 is synthesized as a precursor protein that undergoes complex post-translational processing and intracellular trafficking before secretion. The fate of TGF-β1, whether it undergoes lysosomal degradation or is released through secretory pathways, represents a critical regulatory checkpoint regulating the strength and duration of fibrotic signaling. This molecular switch governing TGF-β1 degradation versus secretion represents an attractive yet understudied therapeutic target for hepatic fibrosis10.

Recent advances in our understanding of intracellular trafficking machinery have identified the sorting nexin 3 (SNX3)–retromer complex as a key regulator of protein sorting and lysosomal trafficking11. While SNX3 participates in various cellular processes, its potential role in regulating growth factor availability and pathological conditions such as organ fibrosis has not been thoroughly investigated12,13. Considering the critical importance of TGF-β1 trafficking and secretion in determining the magnitude of fibrotic responses, we propose SNX3 as a key regulator of TGF-β1 bioavailability in hepatic fibrosis.

This study identifies SNX3 as a critical regulator of hepatic fibrosis by controlling TGF-β1 bioavailability. Elevated SNX3 in clinical and experimental fibrotic livers correlates with disease progression and TGF-β/Smad activation. Hepatocyte-specific SNX3 deletion protects against fibrogenesis, whereas overexpression induces spontaneous and injury-aggravated fibrosis. SNX3 binds TGF-β1, rerouting it from lysosomal degradation to enhance secretion. This amplifies TGF-β1/Smad signaling across hepatocytes and HSCs, establishing a paracrine-amplified fibrotic cascade. Pharmacological validation confirms that SNX3-mediated fibrosis strictly depends on TGF-β1/Smad signaling, as Smad3 inhibition alleviates SNX3-driven pathology whereas Smad activation bypasses SNX3 deletion benefits. SNX3 thus gatekeeps TGF-β1 availability to initiate and perpetuate hepatic fibrosis, highlighting it as a promising target for antifibrotic intervention.

Materials and methods

Human specimens

Human liver tissue samples were obtained from the Department of Pathology, The First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University following institutional ethical approval (approval number 20190196). Normal liver tissues were obtained from paracancerous sites of patients undergoing hepatectomy for hepatic hemangioma. Fibrotic liver samples (F1-F2 staging) were obtained from patients with HBV-related hepatic fibrosis before therapeutic intervention. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee. Commercial human liver fibrosis tissue microarray was purchased from Xi’an Zhongke Guanghua Bioaitech Co., Ltd. (DP087Lv01).

Animal models and experimental design

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University and conducted according to institutional guidelines (approval number 2022003467). All experiments included both male and female mice in approximately equal numbers, housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with 12-h light/dark cycles. Age-matched littermate controls were used for all experiments. Data from male and female mice were pooled for analysis as no significant sex-based differences were observed in any of the measured parameters. Sample sizes (n) indicated in figure legends represent independent biological replicates combining both sexes.

Generation of conditional knockout mice

Snx3flox/flox mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology with the C57BL/6J genetic background by Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China). These mice were crossed with Albumin-Cre transgenic mice (B6.Cg-Tg(Alb-Cre)21Mgn/J) to generate hepatocyte-specific knockout mice (Snx3flox/flox Albcre+/−). Littermate Snx3flox/flox mice served as controls in associated experiments. PCR was used to genotype the mice, and Western blot analysis was used to validate the results.

Generation of transgenic overexpression mice

SNX3 transgenic mice were generated using PiggyBac transposon technology. Mouse SNX3 cDNA was cloned into PiggyBac vector under control of CAG promoter with loxP-STOP-loxP cassette. Founder mice were crossed with Albumin-Cre mice to generate hepatocyte-specific overexpression mice (Snx3Tg Albcre+/−). Littermate Snx3Tg mice served as controls in associated experiments. The genotype of the mice was verified by using PCR and bioluminescence imaging. The primers for genotyping are listed in Supporting Information Table S1.

Fibrosis induction models

For bile duct ligation (BDL), mice (8–10 weeks old) were anesthetized with isoflurane and midline laparotomy was performed under sterile conditions. The common bile duct was identified and double-ligated with 5-0 non-absorbable suture, and the middle portion of bile duct was cut between ligatures. Sham-operated mice underwent identical procedure without ligation14. Mice were sacrificed at 1 or 2 weeks post-surgery. For the carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) model, CCl4 was diluted in olive oil (1:4 ratio) and mice received intraperitoneal injections of 1 mL/kg CCl4 solution twice weekly for 6 weeks. Control mice received equivalent volumes of olive oil15. Mice were sacrificed 48 h after final injection.

Drug administration

For SRI-011381 (MCE, HY-100347, China) treatment, the compound was dissolved in vehicle containing 10% DMSO and 40% PEG300 in saline and administered intraperitoneally at 30 mg/kg every other day for 1 week following BDL. Control mice received equivalent volume of vehicle. This dose was selected based on previous reports demonstrating effective Smad2/3 activation in rodent models16 and our preliminary dose-finding studies confirming robust phospho-Smad2/3 upregulation in liver tissue without overt toxicity. For SIS3 (MCE, HY-13013, China) treatment, SIS3 was dissolved in saline and administered intraperitoneally at 2 mg/kg twice weekly for 1 week. Treatment was initiated in 9-month-old transgenic mice showing spontaneous fibrosis This dosing regimen was based on published studies showing effective Smad3 phosphorylation inhibition in liver fibrosis models17. The twice-weekly administration schedule balances therapeutic efficacy with the compound’s pharmacokinetic profile. Treatment was initiated in 9-month-old transgenic mice showing spontaneous fibrosis to evaluate therapeutic efficacy in established disease.

Cell culture and treatments

Primary hepatocyte isolation

Primary mouse hepatocytes (HCs) were isolated using two-step collagenase perfusion method. Mice were anesthetized and liver was perfused via portal vein with EGTA solution (5 mmol/L EGTA, 0.5 mmol/L HEPES in HBSS) for 5 min, followed by second perfusion with collagenase solution (0.5 mg/mL collagenase type IV in DMEM) for 5–7 min. The liver was disaggregated and hepatocytes purified by low-speed centrifugation (50 g, 3 min). Viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion (>85% viability required) and cells were plated in collagen-coated dishes in DMEM (Gibco, 11965, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, 11965, USA).

Hepatic stellate cell culture

The human HSC cell line LX2 was purchased from Procell (CL-0560, China) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C in 5% CO2 atmosphere. For activation experiments, cells were serum-starved for 24 h before treatment.

Cell transfection

For adenoviral transduction, recombinant adenoviral vectors expressing rat SNX3 (Ad-SNX3, with Flag-tag), or control vectors were generated using conventional methods. Cells were transduced at MOI of 100 for 24 h and efficiency was confirmed by Western blotting. For short hair-pin RNA (shRNA) transfection, SNX3-targeting shRNA or negative control shRNA were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L3000015, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The detailed protocols were described in our previous work18. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed to be 48–72 h post-transfection.

Drug treatments

Cells were treated with the following compounds to investigate TGF-β1 signaling and protein degradation pathways: recombinant TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 100-21-50UG, USA) was used to stimulate fibrogenic signaling for 24–48 h; MG132 (10 μmol/L in DMSO; Sigma–Aldrich, M8600, USA) was applied for 12 h to inhibit proteasomal degradation; chloroquine (CQ, 40 μmol/L in DMSO; Sigma–Aldrich, C6628, USA) was used for 12 h to block lysosomal degradation. DMSO concentration maintained below 0.1% was as vehicle control.

Co-culture experiments

For conditioned medium transfer experiments, primary hepatocytes transfected with Ad-SNX3 or sh-SNX3 were cultured in serum-free DMEM for 4 h, and the collected conditioned medium was applied to serum-starved LX2 cells (1:1 ratio with fresh medium) for 24–48 h. For TGF-β1 neutralization studies, conditioned medium was pre-incubated with anti-TGF-β1 neutralizing antibody (10 μg/mL; R&D Systems, MAB1835, USA) for 1 h before HSC treatment.

Histological analysis

Tissue processing and staining

Fresh liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through graded alcohols, stained with hematoxylin for 3 min, differentiated in acid alcohol, counterstained with eosin for 1 min, dehydrated and mounted with DPX. Masson’s trichrome staining was performed according to standard protocol where collagen appears blue, muscle/cytoplasm red, and nuclei black. For Picrosirius Red (PSR) staining, sections were stained with Sirius Red F3B in saturated picric acid for 1 h, where collagen fibers appear red under bright field microscopy. Quantification was performed using Fiji software19.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) heating in microwave for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H2O2 for 10 min and non-specific binding was blocked with 5% normal serum for 1 h. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C and detection was performed using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAB substrate. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. For immunofluorescence (IF), antigen retrieval and blocking were performed as above. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1 μg/mL) and images were captured using OLYMPUS FV3000 fluorescence microscope. Colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Liver tissues (1 mm3) were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer for 12 h at 4 °C, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol series, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (60–70 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined using Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope.

Protein analysis

Western blotting

Liver tissues or cells were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and protein concentrations were determined using BCA assay. The Western blotting procedure was carried out as previously described18. Detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and quantification was performed using Fiji software with GAPDH as loading control. The detail of primary and secondary antibodies utilized in this study is listed in Supporting Information Table S2.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed in Co-IP buffer (50 mmol/L Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 1% NP-40, protease inhibitors) and 300–500 μg protein was incubated with 2 μg primary antibody (anti-TGF-β1, anti-Flag, or IgG control) overnight at 4 °C. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88802, USA) were added for 2 h at 4 °C, followed by three washes with lysis buffer. Bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by Western blotting.

Subcellular fractionation

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were isolated using NE-PER kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78803, USA) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Fraction purity was confirmed using nuclear (Lamin B1) and cytoplasmic (GAPDH) markers.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, 9109, Asian, Japan) and quantified using NanoDrop 2000 (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using One-step RT kit. Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green-based system (EZBioscience, A0012-R2, USA) with gene-specific primers. PCR conditions were 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s. Relative expression was calculated using 2–ΔΔCt method with GAPDH or β-actin as internal controls. The primers for qPCR utilized in this study are listed in Table S1.

Biochemical assays

Serum biochemistry

Blood samples collected via cardiac puncture and serum separated by centrifugation. ALT, AST, ALP activities and total bile acid levels measured using fully automatic biochemical analyzer (URIT, 8280, China).

Hydroxyproline assay

Hepatic hydroxyproline content measured using commercial kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, A030-2-1, China) according to manufacturer’s protocol.

TGF-β1 ELISA

TGF-β1 levels in cell culture supernatants measured using Human TGF-β1 ELISA Kit (Fine Test, EH0287, China) following manufacturer’s instructions. Samples diluted appropriately and optical density read at 450 nm.

Bioluminescence imaging

Liver tissues from transgenic mice were imaged using IVIS Lumina XR system (PerkinElmer, CLS136340, USA). Bioluminescence intensity quantified using Lumazone Version 2.0 software and expressed as photons/second.

Single-cell RNA-seq data acquisition and analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq data from human liver samples (GSE422832) were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). The dataset includes normal liver samples (n = 5) and MASH patients with stage 4 fibrosis (n = 4). Quality control, normalization, and cell clustering were performed using Seurat (v4.3.0). Data were normalized using SCTransform and integrated across samples. UMAP dimensionality reduction was performed and cell types were annotated based on Panglao database. Differential expression analysis was performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni correction. Statistical significance was set at adjusted P < 0.05.

Protein–protein docking analysis

To predict the binding interface between SNX3 and TGF-β1, we performed computational protein–protein docking. The crystal structure of the human SNX3 PX domain (PDB ID: 1OCS) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank. The structure of TGF-β1 in complex with its receptors was generated using AlphaFold Multimer. Template-free protein–protein docking was performed using the HDOCK server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) with default parameters. The top-ranked docking model based on binding energy was selected for analysis. Interface residues and interactions were identified and visualized using PyMOL (Version 2.5). Residues within 5 Å of the binding partner were classified as interface residues.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analysis performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-sided Student’s t-test was used when data were normally distributed; otherwise, Mann–Whitney test was performed. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Sidak’s post hoc test was used for parametric data. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-sided). Sample sizes (n) represent independent biological replicates as indicated in figure legends.

Results

SNX3 expression is elevated in hepatic fibrosis and correlates with disease progression

To investigate the potential involvement of SNX3 in the pathogenesis of hepatic fibrosis, we first examined its expression in clinical liver samples using publicly available GEO databases and commercially acquired tissue microarrays. Analysis of GSE193080 dataset20 combined with immunohistochemical staining for SNX3 performed on these tissue microarrays revealed a progressive increase in SNX3 expression that corresponded with the advancing stages of liver fibrosis, from normal liver (F0) to mild (F1) and moderate (F2) fibrosis (Fig. 1A and Supporting Information Fig. S1A). Further bioinformation analysis confirmed the distinct profibrotic role of SNX3 (Fig. S1B–S1G). To identify the cellular source of SNX3, we performed immunofluorescence co-staining in human liver tissues obtained from The First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. These results demonstrated that SNX3 was primarily localized in hepatocytes (albumin-positive) in both normal and fibrotic liver tissues, with notably increased expression under fibrotic conditions (Fig. 1B and C). Interestingly, SNX3 exhibited elevated co-localization with activated HSCs (α-SMA-positive) in fibrotic tissues, whereas it was rarely detected in quiescent HSCs, implying a pathological role for SNX3 in the pathological state.

To validate these clinical findings, we utilized two well-established mouse models of hepatic fibrosis: bile duct ligation (BDL) and chronic carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) administration (Fig. 1D). Both experimental models confirmed successful fibrosis induction, as evidenced by comprehensive histological and biochemical characterization (Fig. 1E and F, Supporting Information Figs. S2 and S3). Specifically, both BDL and CCl4 treatments resulted in hepatomegaly, elevated serum enzymatic activity, and increased total bile acid and hydroxyproline content (Fig. S2A–S2D, Fig. S3A–S3E). Histological examination revealed significant liver injury and extensive collagen deposition in the BDL model, which was confirmed by the upregulation of fibrotic markers via Western blot analysis (Fig. S2E–S2K). Similarly, the CCl4 model displayed robust collagen deposition and a corresponding increase in these profibrotic markers (Fig. S3F–S3M).

Importantly, the upregulation of SNX3 paralleled fibrosis development in both models. Western blot analysis demonstrated significant elevation of SNX3 protein levels in both BDL and CCl4-treated livers (Fig. 1G). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed increased SNX3-positive areas in both experimental models (Fig. S2L and S2M, Fig. S3L and S3M). Furthermore, SNX3 expression positively correlated with both the degree of liver fibrosis (Fig. 1H) and phospho-Smad3 levels (Fig. 1I), indicating its association with TGF-β/Smad pathway activation. Co-localization studies with correlation analysis further mirrored the clinical observation, confirming SNX3 expression in both hepatocytes and activated HSCs, with minimal expression in macrophages (F4/80-positive) (Fig. 1J–L, Fig. S2N and Fig. S3N).

To explore which cell type in liver contributes more strongly to the fibrotic phenotype, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from normal liver samples (n = 5) and MASH patients with stage 4 fibrosis (n = 4) from the GSE422832 dataset21 (Fig. 1M–O, Fig. S1H–S1K). UMAP clustering identified major liver cell populations including hepatocytes, hepatic stellate cells, endothelial cells, cholangiocytes, and immune cells in both normal and MASH conditions (Fig. 1M, Fig. S1H). Cell composition analysis revealed that hepatocyte populations decrease while HSC populations expand during fibrosis progression (Fig. S1I), consistent with hepatocyte injury and HSC activation. Comparative analysis between normal and MASH samples demonstrates critical differences: SNX3 expression levels stand steadily in normal hepatocytes, however injured hepatocytes in MASH livers exhibit significantly elevated SNX3 expression (Fig. 1N and O), while HSCs show the most dramatic upregulation (Fig. 1P).

To experimentally validate this discovery, we isolated primary hepatocytes from mice subjected to BDL-induced fibrosis. Western blot analysis confirmed that primary hepatocytes from BDL-treated mice exhibited significantly elevated SNX3 protein levels compared to sham controls, directly validating the human scRNA-seq finding in a mouse disease model (Fig. 1Q). We further performed shRNA-mediated SNX3 knockdown in these primary hepatocytes to functionally validate SNX3’s role. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that SNX3 depletion significantly reduced TGF-β1 expression in primary hepatocytes from fibrotic livers, confirming that SNX3 directly regulates profibrotic signaling in disease-relevant primary cells (Fig. S1L). This cross-species confirmation establishes that hepatocytes respond to fibrotic injury by upregulating SNX3 expression.

Collectively, these findings establish that SNX3 expression is consistently elevated across different models of hepatic fibrosis, with injured hepatocytes serving as primary initiators of SNX3 upregulation, and strongly correlates with fibrosis progression, suggesting its critical involvement in liver fibrogenesis.

Hepatocyte-specific knockout of SNX3 protects against hepatic fibrosis

Integrating the scRNA-seq data with our functional studies, we propose SNX3 in hepatocytes serve as primary initiators function in hepatic fibrosis. We determined the functional role of hepatocyte-derived SNX3 in fibrogenesis using conditional knockout mice (Snx3fl/flAlbcre+/−), generated by crossing Snx3-floxed mice with Albumin-Cre transgenics (Supporting Information Fig. S4A–S4D). Specificity and efficiency of SNX3 deletion were validated by Western blot analysis, showing significant reduction of SNX3 protein expression specifically in liver, without changes in other organs (Fig. S4E). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the loss of SNX3 protein in hepatocytes (Fig. S4F). To investigate the functional impact of hepatocyte-specific SNX3 deletion on fibrosis development, we subjected both Snx3fl/flAlbcre+/−and Snx3fl/fl mice to BDL and CCl4-induced fibrosis models (Fig. 2A).

In the BDL model, while Snx3fl/fl mice developed severely enlarged, darkened livers after treatment, the conditional knockout mice showed healthier liver gross morphology and significantly reduced liver enlargement (Fig. 2B and C). Histological examination confirmed the obvious protection. Snx3fl/fl mice exhibited extensive tissue damage, inflammatory infiltration, and abundant blue-stained collagen deposits characteristic of advanced fibrosis. In contrast, Snx3fl/flAlbcre+/−mice preserved normal liver architecture with less collagen accumulation (Fig. 2D–F, Supporting Information Fig. S5A and S5B). Transmission electron microscopy also showed that hepatocytes from conditional knockout mice retained a normal cellular structure compared to the stressed, damaged appearance of hepatocytes from Snx3fl/fl mice (Fig. 2G). Biochemically, knockouts exhibited lower hydroxyproline (Fig. 2H), reduced serum activities of liver enzymes (Fig. 2I–L), and downregulated fibrosis proteins (Fig. 2M).

A similar protective effect was observed in the CCl4-induced fibrosis model. Hepatocyte-specific deletion of SNX3 resulted in preserved liver architecture with reduced hepatomegaly (Fig. 2N and O). The conditional knockout mice also exhibited a reduction of PSR-positive fibrotic areas, improved liver injury markers, and decreased hydroxyproline accumulation (Fig. 2P–T, Fig. S5C and S5D).

These findings suggest hepatocyte-derived SNX3 critically promotes fibrogenesis, highlighting its therapeutic potential.

Hepatocyte-specific SNX3 overexpression induces and exacerbates hepatic fibrosis

To define the profibrotic role of hepatocyte-derived SNX3, we generated hepatocyte-specific SNX3 overexpression mice (Snx3TgAlbcre+/−) using a conditional transgenic approach (Fig. 3A, Supporting Information Fig. S6A–S6C). In vivo imaging confirmed that SNX3 overexpression was robust and restricted to the liver, validating the specificity of the model (Fig. 3B).

We first investigated whether sustained SNX3 overexpression in hepatocytes was sufficient to initiate fibrosis spontaneously. Long-term monitoring revealed a progressive development of fibrotic changes in Snx3TgAlbcre+/− mice. At 9 months, transgenic mice, in the absence of any external insult, displayed significantly elevated serum levels of ALT and aspartate AST compared to control (Snx3Tg) mice (Supporting Information Fig. S7A and S7B). Molecular analysis confirmed a substantial upregulation of key fibrotic markers, including fibronectin and collagen type I, at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3C and I). Histopathological examination corroborated these findings, showing marked collagen deposition by Masson’s trichrome and PSR staining, alongside an expansion of α-SMA-positive areas, indicative of HSCs activation (Fig. 3D–H, Fig. S7C and S7D).

To determine whether SNX3 overexpression could exacerbate fibrosis progression under pathological stress, we subjected 6-month-old mice to BDL-induced fibrosis. Snx3TgAlbcre+/− mice exhibited a markedly exacerbated response to BDL, presenting with severe hepatomegaly and darkened, congested livers (Fig. 3J). Histological analysis revealed extensive tissue damage, massive inflammatory infiltration, and abundant collagen accumulation in the overexpression mice (Fig. 3K and L). Biochemical markers of liver injury, including serum ALT, AST activities and TBA levels, were significantly higher in Snx3TgAlbcre+/− mice compared to controls (Fig. 3M–O). Western blot analysis confirmed marked upregulation of fibrotic proteins, including fibronectin, and collagen type I in the overexpression mice (Fig. 3P).

These data demonstrate that hepatocyte-specific SNX3 overexpression is sufficient to initiate spontaneous fibrogenesis and accelerates fibrosis progression in response to injury stimuli.

SNX3 potentiates hepatic fibrosis by amplifying TGF-β1/Smad signaling and driving HSCs activation

Having established SNX3 as a critical driver of hepatic fibrosis, we investigated its molecular mechanisms. Observing a strong positive correlation between SNX3 expression and phospho-Smad3 levels across fibrosis models, we proposed that SNX3 exerts its fibrogenic effects via canonical TGF-β1/Smad signaling modulation.

In BDL- and CCl4-induced fibrosis, hepatocyte-specific deletion of SNX3 (Snx3fl/flAlbcre+/−) resulted in a significant reduction in phosphorylated Smad3 levels compared to control (Snx3fl/fl) mice (Fig. 4A). Conversely, hepatocyte-specific SNX3 overexpression (Snx3TgAlbcre+/−) markedly elevated phospho-Smad3 activation compared to Snx3Tg controls (Fig. 4B). These findings provided in vivo evidence that SNX3 positively regulates TGF-β1/Smad pathway activation.

We performed detailed in vitro studies using primary hepatocytes. SNX3 overexpression significantly enhanced TGF-β1-induced Smad3 phosphorylation, while SNX3 knockdown suppressed this response (Fig. 4C). Subcellular fractionation revealed that SNX3 overexpression promoted nuclear translocation of Smad2/3 following TGF-β1 treatment, indicating enhanced transcriptional activation (Fig. 4D). Immunofluorescence analysis of vimentin, a downstream transcriptional target of the Smad2/3, demonstrated that SNX3 overexpression significantly augmented its levels (Fig. 4E), indicating enhanced Smad activity.

Given elevated SNX3 expression in activated HSCs in fibrotic liver, we investigated whether SNX3 regulates TGF-β1/Smad signaling in these cells. In primary HSCs, SNX3 overexpression enhanced and prolonged TGF-β1-induced Smad3 phosphorylation (Fig. 4F). Similar to hepatocytes, SNX3 overexpression in HSCs promoted significant nuclear translocation of Smad2/3 upon TGF-β1 treatment (Fig. 4G and H). Functionally, SNX3 knockdown in HSCs significantly reduced the expression of key profibrotic markers, including fibronectin, COL-Ⅰ, and α-SMA, in response to TGF-β1 stimulation (Fig. 4I). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that SNX3 overexpression markedly upregulated α-SMA expression in activated HSCs (Fig. 4J).

Finally, we explored whether hepatocyte-derived SNX3 drives HSC activation through paracrine mechanisms. To address this, we performed conditioned medium transfer experiments (Fig. 4K). Conditioned medium from SNX3-knockdown hepatocytes significantly attenuated α-SMA expression in HSCs. Conversely, medium from SNX3-overexpressing hepatocytes robustly induced α-SMA expression (Fig. 4L–N). These results support that hepatocyte-derived SNX3 activates HSCs potentially through secreted factors like TGF-β1, establishing a paracrine mechanism of hepatocyte–HSC crosstalk.

Collectively, hepatocyte-derived SNX3 amplifies TGF-β1/Smad signaling in both cell types and drives HSC activation via paracrine mechanisms.

SNX3 promotes TGF-β1 secretion by inhibiting lysosomal degradation in hepatocytes and HSCs

Having established that SNX3 potentiates TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling and drives HSC activation, we investigated the underlying molecular mechanisms. Given SNX3’s role in retromer-associated intracellular trafficking, we hypothesized it regulates the intracellular sorting of important TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling components.

In hepatocytes, SNX3 knockdown reduced TGF-β1 protein levels, while its overexpression increased them, without altering other components of TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway (Fig. 5A and B). These changes in intracellular content were mirrored by corresponding alterations in secreted TGF-β1 found in the culture medium (Fig. 5C). Critically, these effects were not associated with changes in TGF-β1 mRNA expression (Supporting Information Fig. S8A and S8B), indicating post-transcriptional mechanisms rather than transcriptional control. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments subsequently confirmed a physical interaction between SNX3 and TGF-β1 proteins (Fig. S8C), which was further supported by their significant co-localization in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes (Fig. S8D). To gain molecular-level insight into the SNX3–TGF-β1 interaction, we performed computational protein–protein docking using the crystal structure of the SNX3 PX domain (PDB: 1OCS) and an AlphaFold-generated TGF-β1 receptor complex. The docking analysis revealed a well-defined binding interface characterized by extensive electrostatic interactions between positively charged residues in SNX3and negatively charged residues in TGF-β1, complemented by hydrophobic contacts (PHE-46 in SNX3 with LYS-25 in TGF-β1) (Fig. S8E). To experimentally validate that SNX3 functions as part of the retromer complex to regulate TGF-β1, we examined the effect of VPS35 knockdown on SNX3-mediated TGF-β1 upregulation. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that while Ad-SNX3 overexpression markedly increased TGF-β1 levels, this effect was significantly attenuated by si-VPS35 treatment (Fig. S8F), demonstrating that an intact retromer complex is required for SNX3’s regulation of TGF-β1. These computational and experimental findings together provide structural and functional evidence for the SNX3–TGF-β1 interaction mechanism.

We extended this investigation to HSCs, where SNX3 expression is also upregulated during fibrogenesis. While SNX3 modulation had no effect on TGF-β1 levels in quiescent HSCs, its role became apparent upon cellular activation (Fig. 5D). In activated HSCs, SNX3 knockdown reduced TGF-β1 levels, while its overexpression promoted TGF-β1 accumulation and secretion, recapitulating the findings in hepatocytes (Fig. 5E and F).

To confirm that TGF-β1 is the critical paracrine mediator in SNX3-driven activation of HSCs, we utilized conditioned medium experiments. Medium from SNX3-overexpressing hepatocytes, which contained elevated levels of secreted TGF-β1, potently induced α-SMA expression in recipient HSCs (Fig. 5G). Conversely, medium from SNX3-knockdown hepatocytes contained lower TGF-β1 levels and had a diminished capacity to activate HSCs (Fig. 5H). The pro-fibrogenic effect was neutralized by TGF-β1-blocking antibody (Fig. 5I). These mechanistic insights were validated in vivo, with hepatocyte-specific SNX3 knockout mice exhibiting reduced TGF-β1, while SNX3 overexpression mice showed significant TGF-β1 accumulation (Fig. 5J and K).

We finally investigated whether SNX3 inhibits degradative pathway of TGF-β1. Pharmacological proteasome inhibition MG132 did not rescue TGF-β1 levels in SNX3-knockdown cells, suggesting that the proteasomal pathway is not the primary mechanism of SNX3-mediated TGF-β1 regulation (Fig. 5L, Fig. S8G). Conversely, lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) restored TGF-β1 protein levels, indicating that SNX3 prevents TGF-β1 degradation via the lysosomal pathway (Fig. 5M). To provide direct spatial evidence that SNX3 loss redirects TGF-β1 to lysosomes, we performed co-localization analysis in cells treated with sh-SNX3 + TGF-β1 with or without CQ. In sh-SNX3 cells, TGF-β1 showed high co-localization with the lysosomal marker LAMP2. Importantly, CQ treatment not only rescued total TGF-β1 levels, but also significantly increased TGF-β1–LAMP2 co-localization (Fig. S8H), demonstrating that lysosomal inhibition degradation prevents TGF-β1 degradation in the lysosomal compartment. To further elucidate the mechanism, we examined the subcellular localization of TGF-β1 in HSCs. SNX3 overexpression promoted the accumulation of TGF-β1 in early endosomes (EEA1-positive) and concurrently reduced its localization within late endosomes/lysosomes (LAMP1-positive) (Fig. 5N and O).

Together, these results demonstrate that SNX3 diverts TGF-β1 from lysosomal degradation, leading to an enhanced intracellular TGF-β1 pool and increased secretion, thereby promoting paracrine activation of HSCs and driving the fibrotic cascade.

SRI-011381 treatment abolishes the protective effect of hepatocyte-specific SNX3 knockout

Our mechanistic studies demonstrated that SNX3 promotes TGF-β1 secretion and activates Smad signaling in hepatocytes and HSCs. To establish causality and therapeutic potential, we designed pharmacological rescue experiments. Using SRI-011381, a Smad2/3 agonist independent of TGF-β receptor, we tested whether direct Smad activation bypasses SNX3 deletion protection22.

Snx3fl/flAlbcre+/− mice and their Snx3fl/fl littermates were subjected to BDL-induced fibrosis with concurrent administration of SRI-011381 (30 mg/kg, i.p.) or vehicle control for one week (Fig. 6A). As expected, vehicle-treated Snx3fl/flAlbcre+/− mice exhibited significant protection against BDL-induced liver injury, consistent with our previous findings. However, SRI-011381 treatment markedly abolished this protective effect.

Biochemical analysis revealed that SRI-011381 treatment significantly reversed the hepatoprotection conferred by SNX3 deletion. SRI-011381-treated knockout mice exhibited decreased liver injury marker levels comparable to those observed in wild-type BDL mice, effectively reversing the protective effect of SNX3 deletion (Fig. 6B and C, Supporting Information Fig. S9A and S9B). Histopathological examination corroborated these biochemical findings. While vehicle-treated Snx3fl/flAlbcre+/− mice preserved normal liver architecture with minimal collagen deposition and reduced α-SMA-positive areas, SRI-011381 treatment restored extensive tissue damage, inflammatory infiltration, and abundant collagen accumulation characteristic of advanced fibrosis (Fig. 6D–F). The restoration of fibrotic changes was accompanied by a significant increase in hydroxyproline content in the SRI-treated knockout mice (Fig. 6H).

Molecular analysis confirmed the restoration of fibrotic signaling pathways. Western blot analysis demonstrated that SRI-011381 treatment led to marked upregulation of key fibrotic proteins, including fibronectin and α-SMA, in the knockout mice (Fig. 6G). Importantly, SRI treatment effectively restored phospho-Smad2/3 levels and TGF-β1 protein expression in the knockout mice, confirming successful bypass of the upstream TGF-β receptor signaling (Fig. 6I).

These rescue experiments provide compelling evidence that SNX3’s pro-fibrotic effects are mediated specifically through the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway.

SIS3 treatment attenuates SNX3 overexpression-induced hepatic fibrosis

To provide further validation of the mechanistic relationship between SNX3 and TGF-β1/Smad signaling, we performed complementary rescue experiments using SIS3, a selective inhibitor of Smad3 phosphorylation that blocks TGF-β1/Smad downstream signaling17.

Nine-month-old Snx3TgAlbcre+/− mice displaying spontaneous fibrosis were treated with SIS3 (2 mg/kg, i.p., twice weekly) or saline control (Fig. 7A). In direct contrast to the SRI rescue experiment, SIS3 treatment markedly attenuated the fibrotic phenotype induced by hepatocyte-specific SNX3 overexpression.

Comprehensive phenotypic analysis revealed that SIS3 treatment provided substantial therapeutic benefit in transgenic mice. The liver-to-body weight ratio, indicative of hepatomegaly in transgenic animals, normalized with SIS3 treatment (Fig. 7B), reflecting resolution of hepatic inflammation and tissue remodeling. Serum ALT and AST activities, elevated in saline-treated Snx3TgAlbcre+/− mice, were significantly reduced following SIS3 administration (Fig. 7C and D). Histological examination confirmed therapeutic efficacy, with SIS3-treated mice showing markedly improved architecture, reduced collagen deposition, and diminished α-SMA immunoreactivity compared to saline controls (Fig. 7E–G).

Molecular characterization confirmed effective pathway inhibition and revealed coordinated downregulation of the fibrotic network. Importantly, SIS3 effectively suppressed phospho-Smad3 activation, reduced TGF-β1 protein abundance, and coordinately decreased downstream fibrotic effectors (Fig. 7I). The maintenance of elevated SNX3 expression alongside reduced TGF-β1 levels suggests that SIS3 interrupts the positive feedback loop between Smad signaling and TGF-β1 production. Moreover, SIS3 treatment significantly reduced COL-Ⅰ expression while maintaining SNX3 overexpression levels (Fig. 7I).

Collectively, these pharmacological intervention studies provide compelling reciprocal evidence for the mechanistic role of SNX3 in TGF-β/Smad-mediated hepatic fibrosis.

Discussion

Liver fibrosis is an advanced liver disease condition that could progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, with no approved antifibrotic therapy currently available23. Our study identifies SNX3 as a novel therapeutic target potentially overcoming limitations of current TGF-β1-focused strategies.

While TGF-β1 is the central fibrotic driver, clinical targeting faces challenges: inhibitors like galunisertib and pirfenidone exhibit dose-limiting toxicities and off-target effects, and receptor blockade may be insufficient24, 25, 26. Critically, long-term systemic TGF-β1 inhibition risks unacceptable adverse effects due to its essential roles in immunity, homeostasis, and tumor suppression9. This necessitates therapeutic strategies that target more precise regulatory mechanisms without completely ablating TGF-β1 function. Controlling TGF-β1 availability via intracellular trafficking regulation offers a sophisticated alternative to blocking receptor–ligand interactions. The cellular fate decision of newly synthesized TGF-β1, specifically its routing toward lysosomal degradation versus secretion, is a key regulatory node allowing fine-tuning of its activity27, 28, 29.

Recent advances highlight the critical role of retromer complexes in regulating the trafficking of diverse cargoes. Retromer dysfunction impacts diverse cellular processes, and its modulation by specific SNXs opens therapeutic avenues30, 31, 32, 33. Our prior work implicated SNX3 in mediating intracellular trafficking of cargo proteins18,34,35. Here, we demonstrate SNX3 controls the cellular fate of TGF-β1, thereby regulating its contribution to fibrogenesis.

The pathological basis of hepatic fibrosis is excessive ECM deposition by activated HSCs, principally mediated by TGF-β1 released from injured hepatocytes. We reveal SNX3 is predominantly expressed in hepatocytes, with levels specifically increased upon damage. Using genetic approaches, we establish hepatocyte-derived SNX3 as a key driver of fibrosis. Another critical discovery is that activated HSCs significantly upregulate SNX3, establishing a self-sustaining fibrotic loop. In these cells, SNX3 diverts TGF-β1 from lysosomal degradation toward secretion, maintaining autocrine HSC activation while simultaneously activating neighboring quiescent HSCs via paracrine signaling. This dual role of SNX3 in hepatocytes and HSCs explains the progression of fibrosis. Initial liver injury triggers SNX3 upregulation in hepatocytes, increasing TGF-β1 secretion that activates HSCs. These activated HSCs then upregulate their own SNX3 expression, perpetuating TGF-β1-driven HSC activation. Breaking this amplification loop offers multiple intervention opportunities.

Pharmacological rescue experiments provide critical validation of the therapeutic potential of targeting this amplification loop. Treatment with SIS3, a selective Smad3 inhibitor17, effectively attenuated fibrosis in SNX3-overexpressing mice by blocking downstream TGF-β signaling. Conversely, administration of SRI-011381, which directly activates Smad2/3 independent of TGF-β receptors16, abolished the protective effects of SNX3 knockout. These complementary approaches demonstrate that SNX3’s pro-fibrotic effects are specifically mediated through the TGF-β1/Smad pathway and establish the druggability of this regulatory axis.

Targeting SNX3–TGF-β1 axis might offer several advantages over existing antifibrotic strategies. It provides more specific modulation than global TGF-β1 blockade, targeting only the pathological upregulation while preserving basal functions. The predominant upregulation in injured hepatocytes and activated HSCs, with minimal expression in other liver cell types, suggests that SNX3 inhibition would have limited off-target effects. This contrasts with systemic TGF-β inhibitors, which affect all cell types indiscriminately. Moreover, targeting intracellular trafficking rather than extracellular signaling represents a novel therapeutic modality that could complement existing approaches. By controlling TGF-β1 availability at its source, SNX3 inhibition could prevent the initiation of multiple downstream pathways simultaneously.

While our findings establish SNX3 as a promising therapeutic target, several considerations regarding long-term safety and clinical translation warrant comprehensive discussion. First, the cell-type restricted expression pattern of SNX3 in the liver suggests a favorable therapeutic window compared to systemic interventions. Our hepatocyte-specific knockout mice maintained normal liver function during the 9-month observation period without spontaneous pathology, indicating that hepatocyte SNX3 is dispensable for homeostatic liver function. This observation provides preliminary safety evidence and contrasts sharply with complete TGF-β1 blockade, which carries substantial risks of autoimmunity, impaired wound healing, and tumor promotion due to TGF-β1’s pleiotropic roles across multiple organ systems. The predominant upregulation of SNX3 specifically in damaged hepatocytes and activated HSCs, with minimal expression in healthy liver tissue, suggests that SNX3 inhibition would preferentially target pathological processes while sparing physiological TGF-β1 signaling in other tissues.

Second, the trafficking-based mechanism of SNX3 inhibition offers inherent safety advantages over receptor-level interventions. By modulating intracellular TGF-β1 availability rather than completely blocking signaling at the receptor level, SNX3 inhibition would preserve basal TGF-β1 function necessary for tissue homeostasis, immune surveillance, and wound repair while specifically targeting pathological amplification36. This ‘dimmer switch’ rather than ‘off switch’ approach to TGF-β1 modulation may reduce the risk of complete pathway ablation and its associated complications. Moreover, because SNX3 acts intracellularly to regulate TGF-β1 secretion rather than blocking extracellular receptor–ligand interactions, this strategy may avoid some of the compensatory mechanisms and resistance that limit efficacy of receptor antagonists. The finding that SNX3 overexpression alone is sufficient to induce spontaneous fibrosis further validates the importance of this regulatory node and suggests that even partial inhibition could provide therapeutic benefit.

However, several important safety questions remain to be addressed in future studies before clinical translation. First, while acute and sub-chronic SNX3 deletion showed no adverse effects in our models, formal long-term safety studies (>6–12 months) with comprehensive toxicological assessment including histopathology of multiple organs, immune function monitoring, and assessment of tumor susceptibility are necessary. Second, SNX3 belongs to the retromer complex family that regulates trafficking of multiple cargo proteins beyond TGF-β1, including growth factor receptors, nutrient transporters, and other signaling molecules37. Although our functional studies identify TGF-β1 as the primary fibrosis-relevant cargo in hepatocytes and HSCs, potential effects on other retromer-dependent processes in the liver and other tissues require systematic investigation. Studies examining whether SNX3 deletion affects trafficking of other known retromer cargoes such as Wntless would help define the specificity of SNX3’s role in TGF-β1 regulation38. Third, the hepatocyte–HSC specificity observed in fibrotic liver may not exclude SNX3 functions in other pathological conditions or organs, necessitating comprehensive preclinical safety profiling across multiple disease models.

Additionally, the development of specific SNX3 small molecule inhibitors faces significant challenges. Given the chronic progressive nature of liver fibrosis, any SNX3-targeting therapy would likely require extended treatment duration (months to years), making long-term safety, tolerability, and absence of off-target effects critical determinants of clinical viability. The potential for developing resistance through compensatory upregulation of other trafficking pathways or TGF-β1 production mechanisms also requires investigation in chronic treatment models. Despite these considerations, our genetic models provide strong proof-of-concept evidence supporting both the feasibility and potential safety of SNX3 inhibition as a therapeutic strategy.

Several other limitations also warrant acknowledgment: (i) While we focused on hepatocytes and HSCs, SNX3 expression and potential roles in other fibrogenic cell types (Kupffer cells, LSECs, immune cells) require investigation39,40. Moreover, future studies using improved HSC-specific conditional models would be valuable to quantify the specific contribution of HSC-derived SNX3 to fibrosis maintenance versus progression, and to determine whether targeting SNX3 in already-activated HSCs could reverse established fibrosis. Such studies would complement our findings on disease prevention through hepatocyte targeting and provide a more complete picture of therapeutic opportunities across different stages of fibrosis. (ii) While our computational docking provides valuable structural predictions and the VPS35 knockdown validates the functional requirement for the retromer complex, direct experimental confirmation of the specific binding residues through mutagenesis studies would further strengthen these findings. Future studies employing site-directed mutagenesis of the predicted interface residues (such as LYS-61, ARG-45, PHE-46 in SNX3) followed by Co-IP and functional assays would definitively confirm their importance. Additionally, high-resolution structural biology approaches such as X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy of the SNX3–TGF-β1 complex would provide direct experimental evidence for the predicted binding mode. Despite these limitations, the consistency between our computational predictions, VPS35 knockdown experiments, and prior Co-IP/co-localization data provides strong support for the proposed SNX3–TGF-β1 interaction mechanism. (iii) While our experiments definitively establish that SNX3 prevents lysosomal degradation of TGF-β1 through the retromer pathway, live-cell imaging studies tracking TGF-β1 trafficking dynamics in real-time would provide additional temporal resolution. Furthermore, direct biochemical isolation of endosomal fractions (early vs. late endosomes vs. lysosomes) followed by TGF-β1 quantification could provide complementary evidence for the endosomal retention vs. lysosomal degradation model. Despite these potential extensions, the convergent evidence from our chloroquine rescue, VPS35 validation, endosomal localization, and co-localization studies provide strong mechanistic support for the proposed trafficking pathway.

In summary, our work represents the first demonstration that SNX3, a retromer component, controls hepatic fibrosis by regulating TGF-β1 trafficking. SNX3 binds TGF-β1, preventing its lysosomal degradation to shift the balance toward secretion. This extends fibrotic regulation beyond transcriptional control to post-translational trafficking, providing a new framework for disease mechanisms. Targeting these pathways promises new fibrosis therapies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study establishes SNX3 as a critical regulator of hepatic fibrosis through its control of TGF-β1 intracellular trafficking, which expands our understanding of fibrosis pathogenesis and offers hope for developing more effective and safer antifibrotic therapies.
