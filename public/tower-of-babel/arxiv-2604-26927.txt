The most discriminable quantum states in the multicopy regime
Maria Kvashchuk, Polina Chernyshova, Lucas E. A. Porto, Ties-A. Ohst, Lucas B. Vieira, Marco Túlio Quintino

Abstract
This work investigates which sets of quantum states give rise to the highest achievable success probability in minimum-error state discrimination if multiple copies of the unknown state are given. Specifically, we consider uniformly distributed ensembles of the form { 1 N , ρ i ⊗ k } i = 1 N \left\{\frac{1}{N},\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}\right\}_{i=1}^{N} , where N N states in dimension d d are provided in k k identical copies, and derive universal limits in this scenario. For pure state ensembles, we prove that whenever N N is large enough to support a state k k -design, these designs will exactly give rise to the maximally discriminable sets. We further show that when N N exceeds the size required for a k k -design, mixed states can outperform all pure state ensembles. We also analyse the analogue classical discrimination problems, in which states are replaced by probability distributions. We recognise that the problem of most discriminable classical states in the multi-copy regime is in one-to-one correspondence to the concept of the multiplicative Bayes capacity of independent uses of classical channels, a concept that emerges naturally in the context of classical information leakage. This connection allows us to completely solve the classical analogue of our problem when N ≥ ( d + k − 1 k ) N\geq\binom{d+k-1}{k} , and to prove that quantum systems offer a quadratic advantage (in number of copies k k ) over classical ones. Curiously, we also show that this quantum advantage is strongly reduced when one is restricted to real quantum states. Finally, we introduce computational techniques to find sets of most discriminable ensembles, and to obtain rigorous universal upper bounds on the maximal success probability for multi-copy state discrimination in cases that are analytically intractable.

Introduction

Quantum state discrimination is a fundamental task that quantifies the probability of determining a particular quantum state that is drawn from a given ensemble
[
1
,
2
]
. Among various different interpretations, quantum state discrimination may be viewed as a communication task between a sender, Alice, and a receiver, Bob. Alice selects a quantum state from a mutually agreed-upon set and transmits it to Bob, whose objective is to identify the received state through an optimal quantum measurement.
A core aspect of quantum theory relies on the fact that non-orthogonal pure states cannot be perfectly distinguished.
In addition to its foundational value, the existence of non-perfectly discriminable states is central in various applications of quantum theory, such as quantum key distribution
[
3
,
4
]
, quantum query complexity
[
5
]
, quantum cryptography
[
6
,
7
]
, hypothesis testing
[
8
]
, and dimension witnessing
[
9
]
.

For the case of discriminating between two quantum states, the Holevo-Helstrom theorem gives a closed formula for the probability of success
[
10
]
. Also in the case of quantum states with a group symmetry, also referred to as group covariant or geometrically uniform states, the so-called pretty good measurements, which are specified by a simple formula, are proven to be optimal, see e.g., Ref.
[
11
]
. When considering arbitrary states, the problem may be phrased in terms of semidefinite programming (SDP)
[
12
,
13

, but no “closed-form” solution is known in this case.

Given the major role of state discrimination, it is of fundamental importance to understand the limits of this task within quantum theory, and to identify the sets of states that are the most discriminable. This problem was addressed in the single copy regime in Refs.
[
14
,
15
]
, which fully characterise the sets of states that are most discriminable, even in the more general setting of ensembles with non-uniform prior distributions
[
14
]
.

While the discrimination problem with a single copy is of foundational interest, from an information-theoretic perspective, it is often natural and relevant to consider scenarios where one has access to
k

,

]

[

In this work, we introduce the concept of

We analyse this concept on arbitrary (possibly mixed) quantum states and on different relevant subsets of states: general (complex) pure quantum states, classical states, and real quantum states.

2
The
k
-copy discrimination problem

The quantum state discrimination problem can be mathematically formalised as following
[
1
,
2
]
.
Let
\{p_{i},\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be an ensemble of
N

d
-dimensional quantum states, that is,
\rho_{i}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
,
\Tr(\rho_i)=1
and
\rho_{i}\geq 0
, and
p_{i}\geq 0
be the probability of preparing state
\rho_{i}
.
The task of quantum state discrimination consists of guessing which state
\rho_{i}
was prepared. For this goal, one is allowed to perform a quantum measurement and guess which state was prepared depending on the outcome of such measurement. Quantum measurements are described by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM), a set of linear operators
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
which are positive semidefinite,
M_{i}\geq 0

\forall i
, and add up to the identity,
\sum_{i=1}^{N}M_{i}=\mathbb{I}
. The maximal probability of discriminating states sampled from an ensemble
\{p_{i},\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is then given by

\displaystyle p_{s}\Big(\{p_{i},\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\Big)=\max_{\{M_{i}\}_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\Tr(\rho_i M_i),

(1)

where the maximisation is taken over all POVMs
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

In this work, we focus on the case where the states are sampled from an equally distributed ensemble, that is,
p_{i}=\frac{1}{N}

\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Moreover, we are interested in the multicopy regime, where one receives
k
identical copies of the state
\rho_{i}
, that is,
\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}
, as illustrated in
Fig.

1
.
In this case, we study the maximal probability of discriminating states sampled from an ensemble
\{\frac{1}{N},\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
This motivates the definition of
k
-copy discriminability, which is the figure of merit that we will use to quantify how discriminable a set of states is in the multicopy regime.

Figure 1
:
Pictorial illustration of the
k
-copy discrimination scenario considered in this work, where dashed wires
represent classical systems and single wires represent quantum systems. With uniform probability, the state
\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}\in\mathcal{L}({\mathbb{C}^{d}})^{\otimes k}
is prepared. In order to discriminate among a set of
N
states, one may perform a joint measurement described by a POVM
\{M_{j}\}_{j}
, where
M_{j}\in\mathcal{L}({\mathbb{C}^{d}})^{\otimes k}
. The discrimination task is successful when
j=i
, and the maximal success probability is presented in
Definition

1
.

Definition 1
.

The
k
-copy discriminability of a set of
N
qudit states
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Discr}(\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},k):=\max_{\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(\rho_i^{\otimes k} M_i),

(2)

where the maximisation is taken over all positive operator-valued measure (POVM) measurements
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

In other words, the
k
-copy discriminability of a set of states
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is the maximal average probability of correctly guessing a state sampled from an equally distributed ensemble
\{\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
Or, equivalently, it is given by the maximal success probability in the task of minimum-error discrimination of the states
\{\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}\}_{i=1}^{N}

[
2
]
.

We notice that in Refs.
[
15
,
14
]
similar notions have been defined for the single copy case.
In our language, what is defined in Ref.
[
15
]
as the
encoding power
of a set of states
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
reads
\text{EP}(\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}):=\operatorname{Discr}(\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},k=1)N
.
Also, using the definition of encoding power, it holds that
\operatorname{Discr}(\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},k)=\text{EP}(\{\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}\}_{i=1}^{N})N
.

In this work, we are interested in studying the maximal value of
k
-copy discriminability that can be reached among all possible sets of states. Therefore, learning the fundamental limit of the state discrimination problem.

Definition 2
.

The maximal
k
-copy discriminability of
N
qudit states is defined as

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{q}}(d,N,k):=\max_{\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\max_{\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(\rho_i^{\otimes k} M_i),

(3)

where the maximisation is taken over all sets of
d
-dimensional states
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and POVMs
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

Moreover, we are also interested in identifying the sets of states that are the most
k
-copy discriminable.

Definition 3
.

A set of
N
qudit states
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
has maximal
k
-copy discriminability when

\displaystyle\operatorname{Discr}(\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},k)=\Omega^{\textup{q}}(d,N,k).

(4)

In
Definition

2
and
Definition

3
, the optimisation is done over all possible quantum states, leading to an ultimate quantum bound on state discrimination in the multicopy regime. While this quantity is meaningful on its own, we are also interested in analysing and comparing this quantity to quantities corresponding to other relevant subsets of quantum states. In
Section

5
, we introduce
\Omega^{\textup{q}}_{\textup{pure}}(d,N,k)
, for which maximisation is performed among all sets of
d
-dimensional pure states
\{\ket{\psi_i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and in
Section

7
, we introduce
\Omega^{\textup{cl}}(d,N,k)
, for the maximisation over classical states (mathematicality formalised by setting states and measurements diagonal in the computational basis). In
Section

8
, we introduce
\Omega^{\textup{real}}(d,N,k)
, where the optimisation is restricted to states and measurements that make no use of complex numbers, i.e., defined in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
instead of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Mathematical preliminaries

In this section we present the definitions of the symmetric subspace, permutation invariance, and state
k
-designs, concepts that play a major role in this work since the problem is defined in the multicopy regime and we are working with
k
-fold quantum states. For an in-depth discussion about the symmetric subspace, we refer to Refs.
[

3.1
The symmetric subspace

Definition 4

(Symmetric subspace)
.

The symmetric subspace of
\left(\mathbb{C}^{d}\right)^{\otimes k}
is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Sym}_{d}^{k}:=\{

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes k}:

\displaystyle V_{\pi}\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi}\quad\forall\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\},

(5)

where
\mathcal{S}_{k}
denotes the symmetric group on
k
elements, and
V_{\pi}
denotes the permutation operator that is associated to
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
via

\displaystyle V_{\pi}:=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[d]}\ket{i_{\pi^{-1}(1)}, \ldots, i_{\pi^{-1}(k)}}\bra{i_1, \ldots, i_k},

(6)

where
[d]=\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{L}(\operatorname{Sym}_{d}^{k})\subseteq\mathcal{L}({\mathbb{C}^{d}}^{\otimes k})
the set of linear operators acting on
{\mathbb{C}^{d}}^{\otimes k}
with support on
\operatorname{Sym}_{d}^{k}
.

Simply speaking, a state belongs to the symmetric subspace, i.e.,
\ket{\psi}\in\operatorname{Sym}_{d}^{k}
if it is invariant under any permutation.
The symmetric subspace is also often referred to as the bosonic-symmetric subspace.

As an important result,
k
-fold pure quantum states span the symmetric subspace by Theorem 3 from Ref.
[
28
]
in the sense that

\operatorname{Sym}_{d}^{k}=\operatorname{span}\{\ket{\psi}^{\otimes k}:\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\}.

(7)

The projector onto the symmetric subspace can be written as

\Pi_{\textup{sym}}(d,k)=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{k}}V_{\pi}.

(8)

Moreover, the projector can be shown to be equal to the average over all
k
-fold pure states, with respect to the Haar measure
[
28
]

\displaystyle\int_{\textup{Haar}}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes k}\mathrm{d}\ket{\psi}:=

\displaystyle\int_{\textup{Haar}}\Big(U\outerproduct{0}{0}U^{\dagger}\Big)^{\otimes k}\mathrm{d}U

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Pi_{\textup{sym}}(d,k)}{\Tr(\Pi_\textup{sym}(d,k))}.

(10)

Finally, the dimension of the symmetric subspace of
{\mathbb{C}^{d}}^{\otimes k}
can, using Eq. (
6
) and (
8
), be computed as

\displaystyle d_{\textup{sym}}[k]:=\Tr\Big(\Pi_\textup{sym}(d,k)\Big)=\binom{d+k-1}{k}.

(11)

3.2
The permutation invariant subspace

Definition 5

(Permutation invariant space)
.

The permutation invariant space
\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k}
is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k}:=\{

\displaystyle\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes k}:

\displaystyle V_{\pi}\rho V_{\pi}=\rho\quad\forall\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\}.

(12)

That is,
\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k}
is the set of linear operators acting on
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes k}
which are invariant under any permutation of the
k
factors.

One of the key differences between linear operators that act on the bosonic-symmetric subspace
\mathcal{L}(\operatorname{Sym}_{d}^{k})
and linear operators from the permutation invariant space
\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k}
is that if
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\operatorname{Sym}_{d}^{k})
then
\rho=V_{\pi}\rho V_{\sigma}

\forall\pi,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
, and if
\rho\in\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k}
, it is stable only under applying the same permutation from the left and right sides, i.e.,
\pi=\sigma

\rho=\frac{\mathbb{I}}{d^{k}}\in\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k},\notin\mathcal{L}(\operatorname{Sym}_{d}^{k})
.

For any quantum states
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
its
k
-folded version belongs to the permutation invariant space
\rho^{\otimes k}\in\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k}
.

4
Single-copy most discriminable states

Before analysing the general
k
-copy problem, we start our discussion with the simplest case of
k=1
, i.e., the case of single-copy state discrimination.
In this regime, the problem of finding the the maximal discriminability achievable with quantum systems has been thoroughly explored in literature
[
14
,
15
]
, and the sets of maximally discriminable states are known to admit a particularly simple characterisation
[
14
]
.
In this section, we review these results with focus on the case of uniform prior, although we note that in Ref.
[
14
]
the authors fully characterise the sets of
1
-copy maximally discriminable states with respect to any arbitrary prior.

To begin with, when the number of candidate states
N
is at most equal to the dimension
d
of the underlying quantum system, i.e.,
N\leq d
, one natural strategy that achieves perfect discriminability amounts to choosing the states
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
to be orthogonal pure states.
More precisely, by setting
\rho_{i}:=\outerproduct{i}{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, and constructing the measurement
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
by
M_{i}=\outerproduct{i}{i}+\frac{1}{N}(\mathbb{I}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\outerproduct{j}{j})
, one can show that
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(\rho_i M_i)=1
.

Analogously, this configuration also inspires a possible strategy for the case
N>d
, although perfect discrimination is not achieved.
In this instance, setting
\rho_{i}:=\outerproduct{i}{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and for
i>d
taking
\rho_{i}
to be any
d
-dimensional state, one can verify that the measurement
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
defined by
M_{i}=\outerproduct{i}{i}
for
i\leq d
and
M_{i}=0
for
i>d
achieves
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(\rho_i M_i)=\frac{d}{N}
.

For the former case, it is clear that the strategy we described is optimal, since it reaches perfect discriminability.
However, even for the latter case the presented strategy turns out to be optimal
[
14
]
.
That is, when
N>d
, there is no set of
N

d
-dimensional quantum states that can be discriminated with average success probability greater than
\frac{d}{N}
.
Since both strategies can be done using only pure quantum states and, more dramatically, even only using classical ones, this means that

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{q}}(d,N,k=1)

\displaystyle=\Omega^{\textup{q}}_{\textup{pure}}(d,N,k=1)

\displaystyle=\Omega^{\textup{cl}}(d,N,k=1)

\displaystyle=\min\left(1,\frac{d}{N}\right).

(24)

A proof of this fact can be found in Refs.
[
14
,
15
]
(see also
Theorem

4.1
below).

Although these strategies are perhaps the most intuitive way to achieve maximal
1
-copy discriminability with quantum theory, it is interesting to notice that in general there exists several other sets of maximally discriminable states.

is properly normalised, and one can verify that
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(\rho_i M_i)=\frac{d}{N}

In fact, when the number of candidate states

Here, we provide an alternative proof for it, which can also be used to conclude that one cannot achieve a discriminability higher than

Theorem 4.1
.

When
N\geq d
, a set of qudit states
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
has maximal
1
-copy discriminability if and only if it contains a state
1
-design.

Proof.

First, assume that
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
contains a state
1
-design, i.e., that there exists a probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\rho_{i}=\frac{\mathbb{I}}{d}
.
Defining
M_{i}:=dp_{i}\rho_{i}
, one can verify that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(\rho_i M_i)=\frac{d}{N},

(26)

and thus the set
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is maximally discriminable.

Now, we will show that if
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
has maximal
1
-copy discriminability, then it contains a state
1
-design.
To do so, notice that for any set of measurements
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(\rho_i M_i)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\Tr(\rho_i^2)\Tr(M_i^2)}

(27)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\Tr(\rho_i)^{2}\Tr(M_i)^{2}}

(28)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(M_i)=d,

(29)

where the first inequality is a form of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, and the second inequality follows from the identity
\Tr(A^2)\leq\Tr(A)^{2}
, valid for any positive semidefinite operator
A
.

A set of measurements
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
that optimally discriminates the states
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, that is, that achieves
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(\rho_i M_i)=d
, must therefore saturate both inequalities above.
In the first place, to achieve equality in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, if
M_{i}\neq 0
we must have
M_{i}\propto\rho_{i}
.
Additionally, to saturate the identity
\Tr(M_i^2)\leq\Tr(M_i)^{2}
, a nonzero
M_{i}
must be rank-1. Analogous argument ensures that, when
M_{i}\neq 0
, the states
\rho_{i}
must have rank-1, hence, they are pure.
These two facts taken together imply that
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
must contain a 1-copy state design.
∎

Now, coming back to the cases where the number of candidate states is smaller than the dimension of the underlying quantum system

Theorem 4.2
.

When

, a set of qudit states
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
has maximal
1
-copy discriminability if and only if
\rho_{i}\rho_{j}=0
for
i\neq j
.

Proof.

If the states in the set
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
have orthogonal support, each state can be written as
\rho_{i}=\sum_{k}p_{k}^{(i)}\outerproduct*{e_{k}^{(i)}}{e_{k}^{(i)}}
, where
p_{k}^{(i)}>0
and
\innerproduct*{e_{k}^{(i)}}{e_{k'}^{(j)}}=\delta_{ij}\cdot\delta_{kk^{\prime}}
.
Hence, we can set
M_{i}:=\sum_{k}\outerproduct*{e_{k}^{(i)}}{e_{k}^{(i)}}
and check that
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(\rho_i M_i)=1
.

To prove the other direction, assume that the set
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
can be perfectly discriminated, i.e., for some
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, we have
\Tr(\rho_i M_j)=\delta_{i,j}
.
This means that for every

, the support of
\rho_{i}
must be orthogonal to the support of
M_{j}
, that is,

\textup{supp}(\rho_{i})\perp\textup{supp}(M_{j})
.
Additionally, writing
\rho_{i}=\sum_{k}p_{k}^{(i)}\outerproduct*{e_{k}^{(i)}}{e_{k}^{(i)}}
, where
p_{k}^{(i)}>0
, it follows that
\bra*{e_{k}^{(i)}}M_{i}\ket*{e_{k}^{(i)}}=1
for all
k
.
Since
M_{i}
cannot have eigenvalues larger than one, this implies that
M_{i}\ket*{e_{k}^{(i)}}=\ket*{e_{k}^{(i)}}
, which means that the support of
\rho_{i}
is included in the support of
M_{i}
, i.e.,
\textup{supp}(\rho_{i})\subset\textup{supp}(M_{i})
.
This can then be used to conclude that for

,
\rho_{i}
and
\rho_{j}
have orthogonal supports, which leads to
\rho_{i}\rho_{j}=0
.
∎

In summary, the maximal discriminability of quantum states is very well understood in the single-copy regime.
In these scenarios, quantum systems do not provide an advantage over classical ones, and sets of states which are maximally discriminable are well characterised, either in terms of

In the following sections, we will observe that the problem becomes much more difficult in the multicopy regime.

In the following sections, we analyse the discriminability of sets of states within the multicopy regime. Utilising more than one copy of each state increases the discriminability of a set of any non-identical states, i.e.,
\textup{Discr}(\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},k>1)>\textup{Discr}(\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},k=1)

approaches the zero operator when

An upper bound on

We now present a fundamental limitation on the discrimination of
k
copies of arbitrary sets of quantum states that are uniformly distributed. This upper bound holds not only for pure states, but also for mixed ones.

Theorem 6.2

(Upper bound for mixed states)
.

The maximal
k
-copy discriminability of
N
qudit states respects the upper bound

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{q}}(d,N,k)\leq\frac{\binom{d^{2}+k-1}{k}}{N},

(58)

where
\binom{d^{2}+k-1}{k}
is the dimension of the symmetric subspace of
(\mathbb{C}^{d^{2}})^{\otimes k}
.

Proof.

Discriminating between
k
copies of a set of mixed states
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is never easier than discriminating between
k
copies of their purifications.
Since such purifications always exist in dimension
d^{2}
, the upper bound
Eq.

58
follows from
Theorem

5.1
.
∎

It is worth noting that, while this universal upper bound is not tight (and likely not attainable), as discussed in
Section

6.4
, this upper bound is not far from being saturated by pure states when the number of states
k
is large and
N
is large enough to contain a state
k
-design.

6.4
Asymptotic analysis of
\Omega^{\textup{q}}(d,N,k)

As discussed in the previous section, for pure states we have

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{q}}_{\textup{pure}}(d,N\geq N^{\prime},k)\leq\frac{1}{N}\frac{(k+d-1)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}.

(59)

From analogous arguments, the upper bound on
\Omega^{\textup{q}}(d,N,k)
ensures that

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{q}}(d,N\geq N^{\prime},k)\leq\frac{1}{N}\frac{(k+d^{2}-1)^{d^{2}-1}}{(d^{2}-1)!},

(60)

from which we conclude that

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{q}}(d,N\geq N^{\prime},k)=O\left(\frac{k^{d^{2}-1}}{N}\right).

(61)

The lower bound is constructed in
Theorem

6.1
, and we can analyse it for the case of large enough

and sufficiently large

:

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{q}}(

\displaystyle d,N\geq N^{\prime},k)\geq

(62)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{k^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}+1\right)+\frac{1}{N}\frac{(k+d-1)^{d-1}}{d^{k}(d-1)!},

(63)

which allows us to describe our function

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{q}}(d,N\geq N^{\prime},k)=\Omega^{\prime}\left(\frac{k^{d-1}}{N}\right).

(64)

In general, we can say that for a fixed dimension, large

, and sufficiently large

\Omega^{\textup{q}}(d,N\geq N^{\prime},k)\approx\frac{k^{\text{Poly}(d)}}{N}

7
The
k
-copy most discriminable classical states

In this section, we investigate the classical version of the problem, restricting the set of possible states only to classical ones.
Interestingly, we establish a direct connection between the
k
-copy discriminability of classical states and the notion of
multiplicative Bayes capacity
of
k
independent runs of a classical channel, which has been extensively studied in the literature
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
.
This allows us to prove tight bounds on the maximal discriminability of classical states, which highlights the quantum advantages in this setting.

For convenience of notation, we choose to describe classical states within the quantum formalism.
That is, we describe a classical state by a density matrix which is diagonal in the computational basis, and whose diagonal terms correspond to a probability distribution.
In this sense, a set of
d
-dimensional classical states
\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is fully specified by a conditional probability distribution
\{\{p(j|i)\}_{j=0}^{d-1}\}_{i=1}^{N}
via

\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}p(j|i)\outerproduct{j}{j}.

(65)

Analogously, a classical measurement is described by a POVM whose elements are also diagonal in the computational basis.
Therefore, in a similar way we can fully specify a classical measurement
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
by a conditional probability distribution
\{\{q(i|j)\}_{i=1}^{N}\}_{j=0}^{d-1}
, according to

M_{i}^{\textup{cl}}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}q(i|j)\outerproduct{j}{j}.

(66)

Notice, in particular, that a classical measurement can always be viewed as a measurement in the computational basis followed by a classical post-processing.

With this notation, the optimal average probability of correctly discriminating between a set of classical states
\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
sampled with uniform prior, i.e., the discriminability of
\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, has a particularly simple form, given by the following theorem.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be a set of classical states described by
\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}p(j|i)\outerproduct{j}{j}
, where
\{\{p(j|i)\}_{j=0}^{d-1}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is a conditional probability distribution.
It holds that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Discr}(\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},k)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[d]}\max_{i}\prod_{l=1}^{k}p(j_{l}|i).

(67)

When
k=1
, this equation reduces to

\displaystyle\operatorname{Discr}(\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},k=1)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\max_{i}p(j|i).

(68)

Proof.

According to
Definition

1
and
Eq.

66
, the
k
-copy discriminability of
\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
can be written as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Discr}(\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},k)=

\displaystyle\max_{q(i|j_{1}\ldots j_{k})}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[d]}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q(i|j_{1}\ldots j_{k})\prod_{l=1}^{k}p(j_{l}|i),

(69)

where the maximisation is performed among all conditional probability distributions
\{\{q(i|j_{1}\ldots j_{k})\}_{i=1}^{N}\}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[d]}
.
Since for each
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}
, we can choose the distribution
\{q(i|j_{1}\ldots j_{k})\}_{i=1}^{N}
independently of other choices of
j
’s, it follows that
Eq.

67
holds.
∎

In other words, the theorem above provides a simpler way to find the optimal measurement for the discrimination of any given set of classical states
\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, which leads to a more straightforward expression for the discriminability of such states, given by
Eq.

67
.
As we will shortly discuss,
Eq.

67
performs a crucial role for the connection between the discriminability of classical states and a well-studied quantity in the literature of classical information theory.

Our goal in establishing this connection is to compute bounds on the maximal
k
-copy discriminability of classical states, defined as follows.

Definition 10
.

The maximal
k
-copy discriminability of
N
classical states is defined as

\displaystyle\Omega

{}^{\textup{cl}}(d,N,k):=

\displaystyle\max_{\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\max_{\{M_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr({\rho^\textup{cl}_i}^{\otimes k} M_i^\textup{cl}),

(70)

where the maximisation is taken over all sets of
d
-dimensional diagonal states
\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and diagonal POVMs
\{M_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
(see
Eq.

65
and
Eq.

66
).

Equivalently, in light of
Theorem

7.1
, the maximal
k
-copy discriminability can be written as

\displaystyle\Omega

{}^{\textup{cl}}(d,N,k)=\max_{\{p(j|i)\}_{ij}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[d]}\max_{i}\prod_{l=1}^{k}p(j_{l}|i).

(71)

7.1
The
k
-copy pure classical states

Before discussing the general version of the classical problem, it is interesting to observe that if we restrict ourselves to the maximal
k
-copy discriminability of pure classical states, the problem is greatly simplified.
In our notation, pure classical states are simply states of the computational basis, which can be obtained by taking deterministic probability distributions
\{\{p(j|i)\}_{j=0}^{d-1}\}_{i=1}^{N}
on
Eq.

65
.

Since there are exactly
d
pure classical states, the most natural instance of the state discrimination problem is the case
N\leq d
.
In this situation, the set
\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
defined by
\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}=\outerproduct{i}{i},\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
is perfectly discriminable, by a measurement in the computational basis.
In the case
N>d
, any set
\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
contains at most
d
different states (which, by the above reasoning, are perfectly discriminable), and therefore the best discriminability one can achieve is
d/N
.

Notice that this reasoning can be applied independently of the considered number of copies
k
, since a projective measurement in the computational basis always perfectly identifies any classical pure state.
That is, in the classical pure case, considering multiple copies does not provide any advantage over the single copy scenario.

Therefore, we conclude that

\Omega^{\textup{cl}}_{\textup{pure}}(d,N,k)=\min\left(1,\frac{d}{N}\right).

(72)

More formally,
Eq.

72
can also be obtained by restricting the optimisation in
Eq.

71
to deterministic probability distributions, which correspond to pure classical states.

Similarly to the quantum case, randomness (i.e., considering mixed classical states) will also be useful in the multicopy regime.
This is due to the fact that randomness enlarges the set of possible states, and in the limit of arbitrarily many copies, any such states are arbitrarily close to being perfectly discriminable.
In some sense, this is also the reason why mixed quantum states are more
k
-copy discriminable than pure ones, but the enlargement of the state space in the quantum case is somewhat subtle, as it relates to the difference between the symmetric and the permutation invariant subspaces.
In the classical case, this enlargement is more explicit, since there are only finitely many classical pure states, but infinitely many mixed ones.

This discussion is nicely illustrated by a simple example.
Consider the problem of discriminating a set of
N=3
classical states of dimension
d=2
, denoted by
\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}
.
If we restrict the states to be pure, we are thus in the problem of discriminating between three bits.
In this setting, at least two bits will necessarily have the same value, which limits the
1
-copy discriminability to
\Omega^{\textup{cl}}_{\textup{pure}}(d=2,N=3,k=1)=\operatorname{Discr}(\{\outerproduct{0}{0},\outerproduct{1}{1},\outerproduct{1}{1}\},k=1)=\frac{2}{3}
.
It also follows that taking any number
k
of copies of those bits does not alter their discriminability, since we cannot extract new information from having more copies of the same bit, in accordance with
Eq.

72
.
Alternatively, having access to randomness allows us to discriminate within
\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}
better, making the set asymptotically perfectly discriminable.
For example, setting
b_{1}=\outerproduct{0}{0}
,
b_{2}=\outerproduct{1}{1}
and
b_{3}=\frac{1}{2}\outerproduct{0}{0}+\frac{1}{2}\outerproduct{1}{1}
, it follows that
\operatorname{Discr}(\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\},k)=1-\frac{1}{3}\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}
.
Thus, not only
\operatorname{Discr}(\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\},k^{\prime})>\operatorname{Discr}(\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\},k)
for any
k^{\prime}>k
, but also
\operatorname{Discr}(\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\},k)\rightarrow 1
when
k\rightarrow\infty
.

7.2
The
k
-copy most discriminable mixed classical states

We now turn to the general classical version of the problem, of finding the maximal
k
-copy discriminability of arbitrary classical states.
As stated in
Definition

10

d
-dimensional classical states
\rho_{i}
are completely described by a conditional probability distribution
\{\{p(j|i)\}_{j=0}^{d-1}\}_{i=1}^{N}
via the equation
\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}p(j|i)\outerproduct{j}{j}

From a classical information theory perspective, conditional probability distributions represent classical channels, which may be described by its associated channel matrix
4
4

4

Traditionally, literature on classical information theory does not make use of Dirac notation. Hence, the authors often just state the
C
is matrix with matrix coefficients

\displaystyle C_{N\to d}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}p(j|i)\outerproduct{j}{i}.

(73)

Here, we use the sub-index

to emphasise that

is a classical channel which maps distributions with

Therefore, the conditional probability distributions that describe both sets of classical states and classical channels provides a one-to-one correspondence between these concepts.

With this correspondence, it turns out that the

\{\rho^{\textup{cl}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is

the so-called

of the associated classical channel

This quantity is denoted by

[

More generally, the
k
-copy discriminability of classical states

\mathcal{ML}^{\times}(C^{(k)}_{N\to d})

\displaystyle C_{N\to d}^{(k)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\left(\prod_{l=1}^{k}p(j_{l}|i)\right)\outerproduct{j_1 j_2 \ldots j_k}{i}.

This connection is more precisely formalised in the following lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Discr}(\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N},k)=\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{ML}^{\times}(C^{(k)}_{N\to d}).

(75)

Proof.

According to
[

is given by

\mathcal{ML}^{\times}(C^{(k)}_{N\to d})=\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[d]}\max_{i}\prod_{l=1}^{k}p(j_{l}|i),

(76)

which is exactly

(see

7.1
).
∎

For a definition of the multiplicative Bayes capacity in the context where it is more often studied, we refer the reader to Refs.

.
And for discussions of the multiplicative Bayes capacity of multiple independent runs of the same channel, to Refs.

Theorem 7.2

The maximal

-copy discriminability of

\displaystyle\textup{cap}_{d}(k):=\frac{k!}{k^{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{d}\in\mathbb{N}\\

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{cl}}(d,N,k)=\frac{1}{N}\textup{cap}_{d}(k).

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{cl}}(d,N,k)=\frac{1}{N}\max_{C^{(k)}_{N\to d}}\mathcal{ML}^{\times}(C^{(k)}_{N\to d}).

The theorem above thus fully determines the maximal

For

7.3
The
k
-copy most discriminable classical bits

For
d=2
, classical states can be written as
\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}=a_{i}\outerproduct{0}{0}+(1-a_{i})\outerproduct{1}{1}
, where
0\leq a_{i}\leq 1
is the probability of the state being the bit
\outerproduct{0}{0}
.
In this way, each classical state is uniquely identified by a single number
a_{i}
.
For concreteness and for developing a sharper intuition on the problem, we present a proof of
Theorem

7.3
for the case
d=2
in details.

Theorem 7.3
.

The maximal
k
-copy discriminability of
N
qubit classical states respects the upper bound

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{cl}}(d=2,N,k)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}\left(\frac{k-l}{k}\right)^{k-l}\left(\frac{l}{k}\right)^{l}

(83)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\frac{1}{k^{k}}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}l^{l}(k-l)^{k-l}

(84)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\frac{k!}{k^{k}}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{k^{l}}{l!}

(85)

Moreover, when
N\geq k+1
, this upper bound is saturated by a set
\{\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
where
\rho_{i}^{\textup{cl}}=a_{i}\outerproduct{0}{0}+(1-a_{i})\outerproduct{1}{1}
with
a_{i}=(i-1)/(N-1)
.
That is, for
N\geq k+1
,

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{cl}}(d=2,N,k)=\frac{1}{N}\frac{k!}{k^{k}}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{k^{l}}{l!}.

(86)

Proof.

The
k
-fold classical state can be written as:

\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}=\sum_{\vec{x}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}p(\vec{x}|i)\outerproduct{\vec{x}}{\vec{x}},

(87)

where the probabilities given by
p(\vec{x}|i)=a_{i}^{k-\mathrm{hw}(\vec{x})}(1-a_{i})^{hw{\vec{(}x)}}
and
\mathrm{hw}(\vec{x})
are the Hamming weight of each bit string, i.e., the number of 1’s in the bit string. Then each state can be rewritten grouping by the same
p(\vec{x}|i)
in front of each
\vec{x}
:

\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}=\sum_{l=0}^{k}a_{i}^{k-l}(1-a_{i})^{l}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}:\,\mathrm{hw}(\vec{x})=l\end{subarray}}\outerproduct{\vec{x}}{\vec{x}}\right).

(88)

Classical measurements have the following form:

\displaystyle M_{i}=p(i|l)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}:\,\mathrm{hw}(\vec{x})=l\end{subarray}}\outerproduct{\vec{x}}{\vec{x}}

(89)

Then, the maximal
k
-copy discriminability of a classical set can be written as:

\displaystyle\Omega^{\textup{cl}}(d,N,k):=

(90)

\displaystyle=\max_{\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\max_{\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(\rho_i^{\otimes k} M_i)

(91)

\displaystyle=\max_{\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}\max_{\{p(i|l)\}_{i=1}^{N}}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}a_{i}^{k-l}(1-a_{i})^{l}p(i|l).

(92)

The upper bound can be derived from a hypothesis testing argument by fixing a measurement and then optimizing each term
\Tr(\rho_i^{\otimes k} M_i)
independently. Each term is the function
a_{i}^{k-l}(1-a_{i})^{l}
is unimodal, and the optimal value is achieved at
a_{i}=\frac{k-l}{k}
as proven in Appendix C of Ref.
[
41
]
. Plugging in the values will yield the upper bound

\displaystyle f(k):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}\left(\frac{k-l}{k}\right)^{k-l}\left(\frac{l}{k}\right)^{l}\

(93)

We now show that when
N\geq k+1
, there are classical qubits that attain the bound.
When
N=k+1
, each measurement is of the form
Eq.

89
. When
N>k+1
, the first
k+1
measurements are of this form, and the rest
M_{i}:=0,\forall i>k+1
. In both of those cases, the sets with maximal discriminability consist of qubit states with
a_{i}
uniformly spaced in
[0,1]
.

Finally, we will show how to simplify the expression. We start by noticing that

\left(\frac{k-l}{k}\right)^{k-l}\left(\frac{l}{k}\right)^{l}=\frac{(k-l)^{k-l}\,l^{l}}{k^{k}}.

(94)

Substituting back into the definition of
f(k)
, we obtain

f(k)=\frac{1}{k^{k}}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}\,l^{l}(k-l)^{k-l}.

(95)

We can now recognise that
s(k):=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}\,l^{l}(k-l)^{k-l}
is the
Schenker sums with n-th term
, defined as A063170 at the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences
[
42
]
. Moreover,
f(k)
is precisely the function
\xi
defined in Ref.
[
43
]
, which proves that
f(k)=\frac{k!}{k^{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{k^{i}}{i!}
and that
f(k)=1+Q(k)
, where
Q
is the Ramanujan
Q
function
[
44
]
.

∎

We notice that, when
N<k+1
, at least one of the measurements is the sum of at least two measurements
Eq.

89
, which leads to having not uniformly distributed on
Z
-axis states as the optimal set, for numerical details see

8.4
Asymptotic analysis of
\Omega^{\textup{real}}_{\textup{pure}}(d=2,N,k)

We now analyse the asymptotic behaviour of
\Omega^{\textup{real}}_{\textup{pure}}(d=2,N,k)
. In
Theorem

8.2
, we prove that, for
N\geq k+1
,

\Omega^{\textup{real}}_{\textup{pure}}(d=2,N,k)\geq\frac{1}{N}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}\right)^{2}
, and conjecture that this lower bound is tight.

In order to understand the asymptotic behaviour of this bound, we can study Renyi entropy for binomial distributions. In particular, if we set
\omega:=1/2
,
p:=1/2
,
n:=k
, and
k:=j
, Eq. 1 of Ref.
[
47
]
reads as

\displaystyle h_{k}(1/2)=\ln\left(\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}\right)^{2}\right).

(116)

That is,
\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}\right)^{2}=e^{h_{k}(1/2)}
.

Since the standard deviation of a single trial of the binomial distribution is given by
\sigma=1/2
, Thm. 1 ensures that

\displaystyle h_{k}(1/2)=\ln\left(\sqrt{2\pi k}\right)+o(1),

(117)

hence, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}\right)^{2}=\sqrt{2\pi k}\cdot\Theta(1)=\Theta(\sqrt{2\pi k})

(118)

and, for large
k
, the lower bound for rebits behaves as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sqrt{2\pi k}\cdot\Theta(1).

(119)

We believe that the lower bound is defined strictly by
\frac{1}{N}\sqrt{2\pi k}
as shown in the question #573537 at Mathematics Stack Exchange
[
48
]
, which is also consistent with our understanding of the problem.
With this assumption, we can notice that, for large values of
k
, and
N\geq k+1
, classical bits respect
\Omega^{\textup{cl}}(d=2,N,k)\sim\frac{1}{N}\sqrt{\frac{\pi k}{2}}
, and pure rebits respect
\Omega^{\textup{real}}_{\textup{pure}}(d=2,N,k)\gtrsim\frac{1}{N}\sqrt{2\pi k}
. That is, the lower bound for rebits offers only a constant advantage over classical bits.

If the pure rebit lower bound is tight (as we expect it to be), we have also shown that complex pure qubits offer a quadratic advantage over pure real qubits.

9
Numerical methods for upper and lower bounds on the
k
-copy discriminability

9.1
Lower bounds

In this subsection, we present methods used to obtain numerical lower bounds on the maximal
k
-copy discriminability of
N
qudit states.
It is known that, for a given set of states
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, its discriminability can be determined by finding optimal measurements via a semidefinite programming (SDP) approach
[
49
]
. The formulation of the problem, as described in Ref.
[
12
]
, is

Given

\displaystyle\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}

maximize

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr(\rho_i^{\otimes k} M_i)

subject to

\displaystyle M_{i}\geq 0\quad\forall i

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}M_{i}=\mathbb{I}

(120)

This is a convex optimisation of a linear function over affine and positive semidefinite constraints. It involves finding
N
operators of size
d^{k}\times d^{k}
. For this semidefinite program, by Slater’s condition, strong duality holds, and the optimum value is attained. Nevertheless, finding the maximal
k
-copy discriminability of
N
qudit states cannot be solved with a one-shot SDP, since the maximisation over qudit states is not convex. We used several techniques for finding the lower bound of an
\Omega(d,N,k)
.

Grid search with SDP.
For qubit case
d=2
, we can discretize the set of possible states using the Bloch sphere representation and parameterize each state by two angles: a polar angle
\theta_{i}\in[0,\pi]
and an azimuthal angle
\phi_{i}\in[0,2\pi)
. Then the optimisation
Section

9.1
is performed for every possible set of
N
states. This exhaustive grid search scales with the cardinality of a search space of such sets, given by
\binom{m}{N}
, where
m
denotes the number of candidate states determined by the discretisation step size of the polar and azimuthal angles.

The run-time complexity of
Section

9.1
scales exponentially with the number of state copies
k
available, and with the number of discretisation steps. For pure states, there is a possible improvement suggested in Ref.
[
50
,
49
]
, which reduces the size of the standard SDP for the minimum-error state discrimination problem. The approach consists of constructing a Gram matrix for a given set of pure states
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. The Gram matrix is an
N\times N
matrix with entries
G_{ij}=\bra{\psi_i}\ket{\psi_j}^{k}
. The initial problem can thus be reformulated as

Given

\displaystyle\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}

maximize

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bra{\psi_i}W_{i}\ket{\psi_i}

subject to

\displaystyle W_{i}\geq 0\quad\forall i

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}=G.

(121)

This optimisation finds
N
operators of size
N\times N
, and is independent on the number of available copies
k
, providing a substantial decrease in the runtime complexity compared to the previous approach.

Grid search provides a lower bound on the value of a function
\Omega(d,N,k)
, since it relies on the discretisation of the search space. Nevertheless, with this approach, it is easy to control and constrain the search space if needed. Combined with the Gram matrix formulation it is a useful tool for the systematic exploration of possible candidate solutions, which provides a good understanding of the structure of the problem.

Gradient descent.
Another approach to obtaining a numerical lower bound of
\Omega(d,N,k)
is to use gradient descent methods, in particular, the Adam algorithm
[
51
]
. This is a stochastic optimisation suitable for non-convex optimisation problems. In our case, the parameters in the algorithm are defined as
2N
matrices of size
d\times d
for real and imaginary values of every density matrix of a state in the set, and
2N
matrices of size
d^{k}\times d^{k}
for measurements. The parameters are optimised using a finite differences approximation of the first derivative with standard hyperparameters: exponential decay rates
\beta_{1}=0.9
and
\beta_{2}=0.999
, exponentially decreasing learning rate from
\alpha=0.1
to
10^{-8}
,
10^{4}
iterations, and several trials with random initial values. The algorithm performs a search over the whole unconstrained space and provides high precision of the value of the objective function; more details about how we unconstrained the problem are provided in
Appendix

E
.

All of the obtained numerical results are presented in
Table

1
,
Table

2
in the appendix. The code is written in the Julia language and uses the MOSEK solver
[
52
]
, and is available on GitHub
[
53
]
.

9.2
Upper bounds

We now introduce a hierarchy of semidefinite programming relaxations providing upper bounds on the maximum discriminability
\Omega
for several scenarios. This section outlines the SDP formulation, with more details on the implementation found in
Appendix

F
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{S}
and
\mathcal{H}_{M}
be the Hilbert spaces associated with the states
\rho_{i}
and POVM elements
M_{i}
, respectively. In order to optimize over both states and measurements simultaneously, we make use of the identity
\Tr(F_{BA} A \otimes B)=\Tr(A B)
, where
F_{BA}
is the swap or “flip” operator acting on the Hilbert spaces of operators of
A
and
B
. First, we apply the identity to rewrite the trace in
Eq.

3
as

\Tr(\rho_i^{\otimes k} M_i)=\Tr(F_{MS} \rho_i^{\otimes k} \otimes M_i).

(122)

This expression is neither linear nor convex in terms of
\rho_{i}
and
M_{i}
, making it unsuitable in a semidefinite program’s objective function. To address this, we first replace
\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}\otimes M_{i}
with the operators
\{\Phi^{0}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
satisfying the constraints

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi^{0}_{i}\geq 0,\quad\Phi^{0}_{i}\in\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k}\otimes\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k},\\
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr_{S}(\Phi^{0}_{i})=\mathbb{I}_{M},\quad\Phi^{0}_{i}\in\mathrm{Sep}(\mathcal{P}_{1^{k}|k})\end{split}

(123)

where
\mathrm{Sep}(\mathcal{P})\subset\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S}^{\otimes k}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{M})
is the convex cone of separable operators according to the partition
\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{1^{k}|k}=\{(1),(2),\ldots,(k),(k+1,\ldots,2k)\}
.

Note that since
\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}\in\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k}
, only the projection of
M_{i}
onto
\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k}
contributes to the trace. Furthermore, since no constraints on
M_{i}
act nontrivially outside this space, without loss of generality we can also enforce permutation invariance on
M_{i}
.

To approximate the separability constraint in
\Phi^{0}_{i}
, we make use of the Doherty-Parrilo-Spedalieri
[
54
]
(DPS) hierarchy (or, more precisely, the quantum de Finetti theorem
[
55
]
) and the positive partial transpose (PPT) criterion
[
56
]
. This is done by considering instead positive operators
\Phi^{\ell}_{i}\in\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k+\ell}\otimes\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k}
acting as potential extensions of
\Phi^{0}_{i}
with
\ell
additional permutation-invariant copies of the state. By the quantum de Finetti theorem, we then have

\displaystyle\min_{\Phi^{0}_{i}}\|\Tr_{S_{\ell}}(\Phi^{\ell}_{i})-\Phi^{0}_{i}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon(d,k,\ell)

(124)

where
\Tr_{S_{\ell}}
denotes the partial trace over
\ell
copies of the state and
\epsilon(d,k,\ell)
a nonnegative function such that
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\epsilon(d,k,\ell)=0
. Purity constraints and proper normalisation on the state subspace are obtained by introducing a new optimisation variable
\Phi^{\ell}_{\perp i}
corresponding to
\rho_{i}^{\otimes k}\otimes(\mathbb{I}-M_{i})
, and an auxiliary variable
\Psi_{i}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S}^{\otimes k+\ell})
such that
\Phi^{\ell}_{i}+\Phi^{\ell}_{\perp i}=\Psi_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{M}
. The final SDP relaxation becomes:

\begin{split}&\text{maximize}\quad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr[\left(\mathbb{I}_{S_{\ell}}\otimes F_{MS_{k}}\right)\Phi^{\ell}_{i}]\\
&\text{subject to}\\
&\quad\Phi^{\ell}_{i},\Phi^{\ell}_{i\perp},\Psi_{i}\geq 0,\\
&\quad\Phi^{\ell}_{i}+\Phi^{\ell}_{\perp i}=\Psi_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{M},\\
&\quad\Phi^{\ell}_{i},\Phi^{\ell}_{\perp i}\in\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k+\ell}\otimes\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k},\\
&\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Tr_{S}(\Phi^{\ell}_{i})=\mathbb{I}_{M},\quad\Tr(\Psi_i)=1,\\
&\quad(\Phi^{\ell}_{i})^{\mathrm{T}_{n}},(\Phi^{\ell}_{\perp i})^{\mathrm{T}_{n}}\geq 0,\;n=1,\ldots,k+\ell.\end{split}

(125)

Here,
\mathrm{T}_{n}
denotes the partial transpose with respect to
n
copies of
\mathcal{H}_{S}
, which can be chosen arbitrarily due to permutation invariance. For the scenario involving pure states, we let
\psi_{i}=\Tr_{S_{k+\ell-2}}(\Psi_{i})
and further impose
\Tr(F_{S_2 S_1} \psi_i)=1
, corresponding to the condition
\Tr(\rho_i^2)=1
. Upper bounds for rebits can be obtained by enforcing
\mathrm{Im}[\Psi_{i}]=0
. Further details on the implementation can be found in
Appendix

F
.

For this work, we have employed this SDP to obtain upper bounds on
\Omega
for the case
d=2
and several scenarios involving pure and mixed qubits and rebits, with
k+\ell\leq 5
. Results can be found in
Table

3
. Optimisations were performed using Python and CVXPY
[
57
,
58
]
, with MOSEK
[
52
]
and SCS
[
59
]
solvers, running on a Intel Core Ultra 9 285K CPU with 128 GB of RAM. The code is available on GitHub
[
60
]
.

Note that
Eq.

123
and the SDP in
Eq.

125
relax the product form necessary for the substitution in
Eq.

122
. The resulting feasible region for the operators
\Phi^{\ell}_{i}
includes convex mixtures of strategies, effectively allowing for classically correlated state preparations and measurements. It is currently unknown if this additional resource leads to an increased value for the maximum discriminability.

k
-designs

In this section we summarise the results obtained in this work that connect the problem of finding the most discriminable
k
-copy states with the concept of state
k
-designs.

11.1
Pure quantum states

When focusing on pure quantum states (see
Section

5
), the relationship between the problem of finding the most discriminable
k
-copy states and state
k
-designs is very strong. More precisely, as stated in
Corollary

5.1
, when the number of states
N
is large enough to contain a state
k
-design, then they are the most discriminable
k
-copy states. Moreover, when the number of states
N
is large enough to contain a state
k
-design, a set of pure states is most discriminable if and only if it contains a state
k
-design, and the maximal probability of discriminating a set of
d
-dimensional pure states that contain a
k
-design is given by
\operatorname{Discr}(\{\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\}_{i},k)=\frac{1}{N}\binom{d+k-1}{k}
. This ensures that our computational methods to find lower and upper bounds on the function
\Omega^{\textup{q}}_{\textup{pure}}(d,N,k)
are also methods to find state
k
-designs, or to rule out state
k
-designs for some given parameters
(d,N,k)
.

Now, when the number of states
N
is not large enough to contain a state
k
-design, the problem of finding the most discriminable
k
-copy states becomes more subtle. While
Theorem

5.3
shows that
\epsilon
approximate state
k
-designs approximate the most discriminable
k
-copy states, the problem of finding the most discriminable states in this regime is likely a hard computational task. We notice that, for a given number of candidates
N
, dimension
d
, the problem of finding
k
-state designs, or even to ensure weather they exist, is a hard mathematical and computational problem
[
61
]
. This then ensures that, the problem of evaluating the function
\Omega^{\textup{q}}_{\textup{pure}}(d,N,k)
and to find states that saturate this bound is also mathematically and computationally hard, since checking whether
\Omega^{\textup{q}}_{\textup{pure}}(d,N,k)=\frac{1}{N}\binom{d+k-1}{k}
is a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a state
k
-design.

Finally, we mention that, when the number of states
N
is not large enough to contain a state
k
-design, we expect that the pure states in which

\operatorname{Discr}(\{\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\}_{i},k)=\Omega^{\textup{q}}_{\textup{pure}}(d,N,k)
are likely good candidates for good approximations of state
k
-designs, and that they may be useful for applications where state
k
-designs are useful, such as in quantum tomography, quantum cryptography, and quantum error correction.

11.2
Mixed quantum states

As discussed in
Section

6
, when the number of states
N
strictly larger than the required number of states to contain
k
-design, the most discriminable
k
-copy states are not pure, and are not strictly given by a state
k
-design. However, we have shown that, in this regime, some advantageous
k
-copy states are still closely related to state
k
-designs, since they can be obtained by a set of states which contains a
k
-design combined with the maximally mixed state. This construction follows from the intuition that, quantum states
k
-design are the most discriminable pure
k
-copy states, and, since state
k
-desings do not add up to the identity map in
{\mathbb{C}^{d}}^{\otimes k}
, but to the projector onto the symmetric subspace, the associated quantum measurement is not normalised. This allows the possibility of having an extra measurement outcome defined as
M_{N+1}=\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{\textup{sym}}(d,k)
, which can increase the success probability of the discrimination task.

While we do not have a proof that this construction is optimal, for the qubit case we have numerical evidence that it is. From our intuition on this problem, we expect that, at least for
d=2
, this construction may be optimal for any dimension every time that
N
is large enough to contain a state
k
-design and one extra state.

11.3
Real quantum states

As discussed in
Section

8
, dealing with multiple copies of real quantum states is mathematically more complicated to analyse than its complex counterpart. The reason for that is that real state
k
-designs do not add up to a projector, but to an operator with several different eigenvalues. This makes the problem of finding the most discriminable
k
-copy states more subtle. First, while analysing the quantity
\operatorname{Discr}(\{\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\}_{i},k)
when the set of states
\{\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\}_{i}
contains a real state
k
-design is possible (see
Theorem

8.1
), the solution is more complicated than in the complex case, and it is not given by a simple combinatorial formula. Second, since real state
k
-designs do not add up to a projector, the methods used in the complex case to obtain upper bounds do not apply in a straightforward way. And, while we believe that real state
k
-designs are the most discriminable
k
-copy states, when restricted to pure real states, we only have a proof of this fact for the case where
d=2
and
k=2
(see
Theorem

8.3
). We expect that there exists a more general design-based argument of this fact for any
d
and
k
, but we have not been able to find it yet.

We have strong numerical evidence that the regular polygon is always the most discriminable set of pure rebits, regardless of the number of states
N
or the number of copies
k
. While this seems to be intuitively true to us, we were unable to find a proof of this fact.

Lastly, we notice that, as in the complex case, when mixed states are considered, the most discriminable real quantum states are not pure. And, as in the complex case, we expect that, while state
k
-designs are not the most discriminable
k
-copy states, they will be still closely related to the optimal construction.

12
Discussions

We have introduced the concept of
k
-copy discriminability of a set of states, which quantifies the maximum probability of correctly identifying a state sampled from such a set with uniform probability, when one is allowed to measure
k
copies of the sampled state.
We have shown that the maximal
k
-copy discriminability achievable with quantum states is closely related to the concept of state
k
-designs.
In particular, when the number of candidate states
N
is large enough to contain a state
k
-design, a set of pure states is most discriminable if and only if it contains a state
k
-design, and the maximal probability of discriminating a set of
d
-dimensional pure states that contains a
k
-design is given by
\operatorname{Discr}(\{\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\}_{i},k)=\frac{\binom{d+k-1}{k}}{N}
.

When the number of states
N
is not large enough to contain a state
k
-design, the problem of finding the most discriminable
k
-copy states becomes more subtle, and we expect that it is a hard computational problem. The case of mixed quantum states was also considered, and in this case, when the number of states
N
is strictly larger than the required number of states to contain a
k
-design, the most discriminable
k
-copy states are not pure, and are not strictly given by a state
k
-design.
However, the found sets are still closely related to state
k
-designs.

Finally, we have analysed the case where real quantum states are considered. The real state
k
-designs were shown not to add up to a projector onto the symmetric subspace, but to an operator with several different eigenvalues. This makes the problem of finding the most discriminable
k
-copy real states more subtle. We have strong numerical evidence that the regular polygon for
d=2
dimensional system is always the most discriminable set of pure rebits, regardless of the number of states
N
or the number of copies
k
, but we were unable to find a proof of this fact.

Despite the results obtained in this work, we can still enumerate several open questions.

1.

For pure quantum states, we proved that the set of states is the most discriminable when the set is large enough to contain a state
k
-design, while for real quantum states, we do not have an analogous proof.
We expect that there exists a more general design-based argument of this fact for any
d
and
k
, but we have not been able to find it yet.

2.

What is the asymptotic behaviour of the function
\Omega_{\textup{pure}}^{\textup{real}}(d,N,k)
for
d>2
? We have shown that, for
d=2
, it seems that real qubits offer only a constant advantage over classical bits (contrary to complex qubits, where this advantage is quadratic). Do real qudits offer only a constant advantage over classical bits for any dimension
d
?

3.

Is the regular
N
-gon set of rebits always the most discriminable set of pure rebits for any number of copies
k
? Apart from the numerical evidence, we do not have proof of this fact.

4.

For
d=2
and
N=3
, is the regular triangle the most discriminable set of qubits for any number of copies
k
? We could prove it for
k=2
under the assumption of pure real qubits, but it is not clear to us how to extend this proof for any number of copies
k
, or even for complex pure qubits.

5.

For mixed states and
N
large enough to contain a state
k
-design and one more state, is the construction of the most discriminable
k
-copy states from a state
k
-design combined with the maximally mixed state optimal for any dimension? We have numerical evidence that it is true for qubits, but we do not have a proof of this fact. Also, this construction seems to be useful even when a design does not exist (see e.g.,
Section

6.3
that addresses the scenario where
d=2
,
N=4
, and
k=2
.)

6.

The numerical upper bound method presented in this work is not known to converge for every scenario, but, we can verify that it does converge in some cases. We leave for future work the analysis of the convergence of this method, and to understand in which scenarios it converges and in which it does not, and why we have this phenomenon.

7.

Does the quantity
\Omega^{q}(d,N,k)
play a role relevant in the analysis of the information leakage, or any quantum cryptographic protocol? Since its classical analogue is a relevant and well-studied quantity for the analysis of the information leakage in classical cryptographic protocols, we expect that its quantum analogue may also have analogous interpretations and applications.

Given the rich structure of the problem of finding the most discriminable
k
-copy states, we foresee some interesting future research directions.

1.

How would the maximal
k
-copy discriminability function
\Omega(d,N,k)
behave when considering generalised probabilistic theories (GPTs)
[
62
,
63
]
, which are a general framework for describing physical theories, including classical and quantum mechanics. Such investigation was done for the single copy case
[
15
]
, but we expect to find different and interesting results for the multicopy case, given that GPTs with a great freedom on quantum states have severe restrictions on the possibility of joint measurements
[
64
,
65
,
66
]
(see however
[
67
]
). Understanding the behaviour of the function
\Omega(d,N,k)
for GPTs may allow us to understand the role of the structure of the state space in the problem of state discrimination with many copies, and to identify the specific features of quantum mechanics that lead to the observed results.

2.

While here we have restricted our analysis to states, this question is also interesting to analyse for measurements. For instance, we can ask what is the most discriminable
k
-copy measurement. We notice that this question may be nontrivial already for the case of
k=1
, and may be connected to the problem of finding the measurements with most information storability
[
15
,
68
]
, and the most incompatible measurements
[
69
]
.

3.

It would be interesting to analyse the case where the prior distribution is not uniform, and to see how this affects the results obtained in this work.

We hope the results and methods presented in this work will spark further research on the problem of state discrimination with many copies, and its connections to state
k
-designs, and possibly, to find applications and connections to other branches of quantum information.

Note added

During the final stages of this work, we became aware of a related manuscript by Achenbach
et al
, titled “Nonclassical traits in multi-copy state discrimination”
[
70
]
. The two works were carried out independently and address closely related questions.

Appendix C
Proof of
Theorem

8.2

See
8.2

Proof.

When
d=2
, a real state
k
-design exists for every
N\geq k+1
, it is enough to consider a set of states distributed in the ZX-plane as a regular polygon
[
71
]
. Hence, for any
N\geq k+1
, we can apply the lower bound presented in
Theorem

8.1
for the particular case of
d=2
. We now notice that, when
d=2
, the multiplicity

\displaystyle m_{j}=\binom{d+k-2j-1}{d-1}-\binom{d+k-2j-3}{d-1}

(126)

simplifies to

\displaystyle m_{j}=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if }j<\frac{k}{2}\\
1,&\text{if }j=\frac{k}{2}\end{cases}

(127)

(notice that
m_{j}=1
only happens when
k
is even and only for the greatest allowed value
j
).

Let us now analyse the value of
\lambda_{j}
when
d=2
. For arbitrary
d
, we have that,

\lambda_{j}=\frac{k!(d-2)!!}{(2j)!!(d+2k-2j-2)!!}

(128)

For
d=2
, the term
(d-2)!!
becomes
0!!=1
. The denominator term
(d+2k-2j-2)!!
simplifies to
(2+2k-2j-2)!!=(2k-2j)!!
:

\lambda_{j}=\frac{k!}{(2j)!!(2k-2j)!!}

(129)

The mathematical identity
(2n)!!=2^{n}n!
ensures that the terms in the denominator can be re-written as
(2j)!!=2^{j}j!
and
(2k-2j)!!=2^{k-j}(k-j)!
.
Substituting these into the expression, we get

\lambda_{j}=\frac{k!}{(2^{j}j!)(2^{k-j}(k-j)!)}

(130)

Combining the powers of 2 in the denominator, we obtain

\lambda_{j}=\frac{k!}{2^{k}j!(k-j)!}

(131)

We can then recognise the binomial coefficient
\binom{k}{j}=\frac{k!}{j!(k-j)!}
to write

\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\binom{k}{j}.

(132)

We now want to evaluate the quantity
p=S^{2}
where
S=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}m_{j}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}
.

Substituting
\lambda_{j}
into the sum gives:

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}m_{j}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{k}}\binom{k}{j}}

(133)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}m_{j}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}

(134)

We now assume that
k
is even. Given
\lambda_{j}=2^{-k}\binom{k}{j}
, we know that for
j<k/2
, the multiplicity is
m_{j}=2
, and when
j=k/2
, the multiplicity is
m_{k/2}=1
.
We can write the sum by explicitly separating the middle term from the rest:

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k/2-1}m_{j}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\right)+m_{k/2}\sqrt{\lambda_{k/2}}

(135)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k/2-1}2\sqrt{2^{-k}\binom{k}{j}}\right)+1\sqrt{2^{-k}\binom{k}{k/2}}

(136)

Factoring out the common
\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}
term yields:

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{k/2-1}2\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}+\sqrt{\binom{k}{k/2}}\right]

(137)

Using the symmetry of binomial coefficients,
\binom{k}{j}=\binom{k}{k-j}
, we can split the factor of 2 in the summation to represent the “left” and “right” halves of the distribution:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k/2-1}2\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k/2-1}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}+\sum_{j=0}^{k/2-1}\sqrt{\binom{k}{k-j}}

(138)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k/2-1}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}+\sum_{i=k/2+1}^{k}\sqrt{\binom{k}{i}}

(139)

Substituting this back into our equation for
S
, the pieces seamlessly join together to form a complete sum from
0
to
k
:

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{k/2-1}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}+\sqrt{\binom{k}{k/2}}+\sum_{i=k/2+1}^{k}\sqrt{\binom{k}{i}}\right]

(140)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{k}}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}

(141)

Squaring the entire sum gives the final quantity for
p
:

\displaystyle p=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}\right)^{2}.

(142)

The calculation for the case of odd
k
is analogous.
∎

Appendix D
Proof of
Theorem

8.3

See
8.3

Proof.

It follows from
Theorem

8.2
that
\Omega^{\textup{real}}_{\textup{pure}}(d=2,N\geq 3,k=2)\geq\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{3}{2}+\sqrt{2}\right)
. To show that the right hand side is optimal, our goal is to also prove that

\Omega^{\textup{real}}_{\textup{pure}}(d=2,N\geq 3,k=2)\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{3}{2}+\sqrt{2}\right)
.
To attain this goal, we will present a feasible point for the dual problem of the state discrimination task that is independent of the states that are subject to the discrimination. By weak duality
[
49
]
, this dual feasible point gives rise to an upper bound of the corresponding primal problem.

The dual problem of state discrimination for a set of states
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
which is uniformly distributed is given by

\displaystyle\min\Tr(X)

(143)

\displaystyle\textup{s.t. }\frac{1}{N}\sigma_{i}\leq X,\quad\forall i\in[N].

(144)

Let us now focus on the case where
\sigma_{i}=\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}\otimes\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}
, and where
\ket{\psi_i}
is a rebit. Since
\ket{\psi_i}
is a rebit, we can write it as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_i}=O_{i}\ket{0},

(145)

where
O_{i}\in SO(2)
is real qubit unitary operator, that is, a rotation in the XZ-plane of the Bloch sphere. Hence, the problem reads as

\displaystyle\min\Tr(X)

(146)

\displaystyle\textup{s.t. }\frac{1}{N}\Big(O_{i}\otimes O_{i}\Big)\outerproduct{00}{00}\Big(O_{i}\otimes O_{i}\Big)^{\dagger}\leq X,\quad\forall i\in[N],

(147)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\min\Tr(X)

(148)

\displaystyle\textup{s.t. }\frac{1}{N}\outerproduct{00}{00}\leq\Big(O_{i}\otimes O_{i}\Big)^{\dagger}X\Big(O_{i}\otimes O_{i}\Big),\quad\forall i\in[N],

(149)

for some given rebit unitaries
O_{i}\in SO(2)
.

This problem motivates an ansatz for
X
; let us take

\displaystyle X=\frac{1}{N}\left(\alpha\Pi_{\textup{sym}}(2,2)+\beta\outerproduct{\phi^+}{\phi^+}\right),

(150)

where
\ket{\phi^+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})
is the maximally entangled state. Notice that, for any unitary operator
U
, it holds that

\displaystyle\Big(U\otimes U\Big)\Pi_{\textup{sym}}(2,2)\Big(U\otimes U\Big)^{\dagger}=\Pi_{\textup{sym}}(2,2),

(151)

simply due to the fact that
U\otimes U
commutes with any permutation operator
V_{\pi}
.
Also, for every rebit unitary
O\in SO(2)
it also holds that

\displaystyle\Big(O\otimes O\Big)\outerproduct{\phi^+}{\phi^+}\Big(O\otimes O\Big)^{\dagger}=\outerproduct{\phi^+}{\phi^+}.

(152)

This holds because
U\otimes\overline{U}\ket{\phi^+}=\ket{\phi^+}
, but when
U
is real, one also has
\overline{U}=U
. The equations above imply that the operator
X
is
SO(2)
-invariant, i.e.,
X=(O\otimes O)X(O\otimes O)^{\dagger}
for all
O\in SO(2)
.

Now, for the fixed ansatz
X=\frac{1}{N}\left(\alpha\Pi_{\textup{sym}}+\beta\phi^{+}\right)
, the dual problem reduces to

\displaystyle\min\frac{1}{N}\left(3\alpha+\beta\right)

(153)

\displaystyle\textup{s.t. }\outerproduct{00}{00}\leq\alpha\Pi_{\textup{sym}}+\beta\outerproduct{\phi^+}{\phi^+}.

(154)

Notice, that there appears no index
i
anymore due to the
SO(2)
-invariance of
X
. The operator inequality
\outerproduct{00}{00}\leq\alpha\Pi_{\textup{sym}}+\beta\outerproduct{\phi^+}{\phi^+}
holds iff

\displaystyle\alpha\Pi_{\textup{sym}}+\beta\outerproduct{\phi^+}{\phi^+}-\outerproduct{00}{00}\geq 0.

(155)

This is equivalent as having all the eigenvalues of
M:=\alpha\Pi_{\textup{sym}}+\beta\outerproduct{\phi^+}{\phi^+}-\outerproduct{00}{00}
being non-negative.
In computational basis, we are hence interested in the eigenvalues of the matrix

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha+\frac{\beta}{2}-1&0&0&\frac{\beta}{2}\\
0&\frac{\alpha}{2}&\frac{\alpha}{2}&0\\
0&\frac{\alpha}{2}&\frac{\alpha}{2}&0\\
\frac{\beta}{2}&0&0&\alpha+\frac{\beta}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(156)

The matrix
M
is block diagonal, so we just need to calculate the eigenvalues of two-by-two matrices.
Direct calculation shows that these eigenvalues are

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=0

(157)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=\alpha

(158)

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}=\alpha+\frac{\beta-1+\sqrt{\beta^{2}+1}}{2}

(159)

\displaystyle\lambda_{4}=\alpha+\frac{\beta-1-\sqrt{\beta^{2}+1}}{2}.

(160)

First, we must have
\alpha\geq 0
and since
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, the only other constraint that is relevant is
\lambda_{4}\geq 0
, which imposes that

\displaystyle\alpha\geq\frac{-\beta+1+\sqrt{\beta^{2}+1}}{2}.

(161)

Since we want to minimise
3\alpha+\beta
, it makes sense to assume that

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{-\beta+1+\sqrt{\beta^{2}+1}}{2}.

(162)

To then minimise
\Tr(X)
, our problem reads as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\;3\left(\frac{-\beta+1+\sqrt{\beta^{2}+1}}{2}\right)+\beta.

(163)

In order to find the minimum of a real-valued function we just need to derive and find its extremal points of
f(\beta):=3\left(\frac{-\beta+1+\sqrt{\beta^{2}+1}}{2}\right)+\beta
.
Direct calculation shows that the derivative of
f
is given by

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\beta)=\frac{3\beta}{2\sqrt{\beta^{2}+1}}-\frac{1}{2}.

(164)

We then see that
f^{\prime}(\beta)=0
when
\beta=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
, and by substituting
\beta=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
into
f
, we obtain

\displaystyle f\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\right)=\frac{3}{2}+\sqrt{2}.

(165)

For completeness, let us explicitly check that
\alpha\geq 0
, so that the eigenvalues of
X-\outerproduct{00}{00}
are non-negative. Since we took
\alpha:=\frac{-\beta+1+\sqrt{\beta^{2}+1}}{2}
, when
\beta=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
, we have that

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}>0.

(166)

To conclude, we have shown that, for
\alpha=\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}
,
\beta=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}
, and
X=\frac{1}{N}\left(\alpha\Pi_{\textup{sym}}+\beta\phi^{+}\right)
, for any pure rebit
\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\otimes\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}\leq X.

(167)

Hence,
\Tr(X)=\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{3}{2}+\sqrt{2}\right)
is an upper bound to the problem of pure rebit discrimination, i.e,
\Omega^{\textup{real}}_{\textup{pure}}(d=2,N\geq 3,k=2)\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{3}{2}+\sqrt{2}\right)
.

∎

Appendix E
Implementation details of the gradient descent for lower bounds

In order to perform the gradient descent optimisation, we first modify the problem to be unconstrained over its parameter space. To achieve this, instead of optimizing over states
\rho_{i}
and POVM elements
M_{i}
directly, we optimize over unconstrained matrices
A_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
and
B_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{d^{k}\times d^{k}}
, respectively, which we use to construct the valid operators. To obtain valid quantum states, we require for all
i

\displaystyle\rho_{i}\geq 0\quad\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Tr(\rho_i)=1.

(168)

The positivity and unit trace constraints can be fulfilled by setting
\rho_{i}=(A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i})/\operatorname{Tr}(A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i})
. For the POVM
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, it should satisfy

M_{i}\geq 0\quad\forall i,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{N}M_{i}=\mathbb{I}.

(169)

Positivity can be achieved by setting
\tilde{M}_{i}=B_{i}^{\dagger}B_{i}
. Letting
M_{i}=\tilde{M}_{i}/\lambda_{\text{max}}(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{M}_{i})
, where
\lambda_{\text{max}}(Q)
is the maximum eigenvalue of
Q
, we obtain
\sum_{i=0}^{N}M_{i}\leq\mathbb{I}
. This is sufficient for the gradient descent optimisation, as the gradient will naturally favour solutions where
\sum_{i=0}^{N}M_{i}=\mathbb{I}
is satisfied. Indeed, this relaxation provides a less constrained version of the search space to be explored in the initial iterations, which is often beneficial. For pure states, we optimize the vectors
\ket{a_i}
such that
A_{i}=\outerproduct{a_i}{a_i}
, and for rebits, we restrict the matrix
A_{i}
or vectors
\ket{a_i}
to be real.

Appendix F
Implementation details of the SDP for upper bounds

In practice, we have implemented the SDP in
Eq.

125
, for
d=2
, by considering a permutation-invariant basis in terms of the Pauli operators. First, we define a (non-normalised) basis for
\operatorname{Perm}_{d}^{k}
via

b_{k}[t]=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}V_{\mu}(\mathbb{I}^{\otimes t_{\mathbb{I}}}\otimes X^{\otimes t_{X}}\otimes Y^{\otimes t_{Y}}\otimes Z^{\otimes t_{Z}})V^{\dagger}_{\mu}

(170)

where
t
denotes the type
[
28
]
indexing the basis, i.e., a 4-tuple of non-negative integers such that
\sum_{\alpha\in\{\mathbb{I},X,Y,Z\}}t_{\alpha}=k
. Next, we define the joint basis for states and measurements via

B[t,t^{\prime}]=\frac{1}{d^{k+\ell}}b_{k+\ell}[t]\otimes b_{k}[t^{\prime}]

(171)

such that the real coefficients
\phi_{i}[t,t^{\prime}]
define
\Phi^{\ell}_{i}=\sum_{t,t^{\prime}}\phi_{i}[t,t^{\prime}]B[t,t^{\prime}]
, and similarly for
\Phi^{\ell}_{\perp i}
. The Pauli representation allows the constraints to be written directly in terms of elementary operations on these types. For example, the purity constraint can be imposed first by defining
r_{i}[a,b,c]=\frac{1}{d^{k}}\left(\phi_{i}[(k+l-2,a,b,c),(k,0,0,0)]+\phi_{\perp i}[(k+l-2,a,b,c),(k,0,0,0)]\right)
, for
a+b+c=2
, corresponding to the coefficients associated with the quadratic Pauli terms in the two-copy reduced state. The purity condition then reduces to
r[2,0,0]+r[0,2,0]+r[0,0,2]=1
, which is equivalent to imposing a unit Bloch vector for the state.

Ultimately, for the positive semidefinite constraints we opted to construct the
2^{2k+l}\times 2^{2k+l}
matrices directly using this permutation-invariant Pauli basis. Partial transposes were implemented using a direct transposition of the basis elements
B[t,t^{\prime}]
. This is inefficient and renders the SDP optimisation intractable for larger
k
and
\ell
, but it was sufficient as a proof of concept. We note that many further optimisations are possible, such as exploiting Schur-Weyl duality, where
\Phi^{k}_{i},\Phi^{k}_{\perp i},\Psi_{i}
, and their partial transposes, can be written in block diagonal form. Positivity can then be imposed directly on their unique blocks, each corresponding to an irreducible representation of the symmetric group. However, we did not pursue such optimisations here. For further details, see the comments in the code available on GitHub
[
60
]
.
