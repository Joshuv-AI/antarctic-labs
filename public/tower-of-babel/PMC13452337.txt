Pregnant women's experiences during a public health emergency: A cross-sectional analysis of the COVID-19 Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System COVID-19 supplement
Authors: Andrew Williams, Ramona A. Danielson, Michael A. Osei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe COVID-19 pandemic was associated with increased maternal morbidity and mortality, with racially minoritized women having worse outcomes than other women in the United States. However, there is little understanding of pandemic-specific stressors that may contribute to disparate outcomes. Thus, this analysis provides descriptive statistics of COVID-19 pandemic related factors among a sample of women who recently gave birth in the United States that can inform future public health disaster preparedness.Study designCross-sectional analysis of secondary data.MethodsAnalysis of 2020-2021 Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System COVID-19 supplement data (weighted n = 453,231) identified five pandemic experience factors through factor analysis: family household stressors, housing instability, COVID prevention measures, COVID household exposure, and external exposure. Prevalence of each factor was estimated overall and by demographic factors.ResultsFamily household stressors affected 42.1% of women, including increased anxiety (48.5%) and job loss (29.9%). Housing instability impacted 11.3%, while 35.2% struggled accessing prevention supplies. Significant disparities emerged: Black and Hispanic women experienced highest family stress rates (48.5%, 48.0%); American Indian/Alaska Native women faced greatest housing instability (21.6%). Younger, unmarried women and those with lower education, Medicaid enrollment, or pre-existing vulnerabilities experienced disproportionate burdens.ConclusionsFindings necessitate targeted emergency preparedness protocols prioritizing pregnant women's social support infrastructure, including housing assistance programs, expanded childcare services, and guaranteed access to prevention resources. The pandemic exacerbated maternal health disparities, with marginalized communities bearing the greatest burden. Medicaid expansion and culturally-responsive interventions should be implemented to support vulnerable populations during public health crises.

Introduction

The COVID-19 (COVID) pandemic reshaped healthcare systems, limited access to services and worsened stressors (e.g. mental health struggles, housing instability) that impacted maternal health [1]. Maternal morbidity and mortality impacts of the COVID pandemic have been well documented [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Pregnant women had a 1.7-fold higher risk of death compared to nonpregnant counterparts [4], with older women [[2], [3], [4], [5]] and racially minoritized women having higher mortality than younger and non-minoritized women [6].

Non-clinic pandemic experiences of pregnant women are less well documented. Literature on pregnant women's experiences during the COVID pandemic suggest vulnerability across multiple domains, including mental health, economics, and abuse [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Mental health impacts were substantial, with 30% of U.S. pregnant women reporting high levels of pandemic-specific stress [11] and in Canada, over 50% of women reported depression and anxiety10 12. Notably, low-income pregnant women (<$20,000) experienced two-fold higher rates of material hardships (e.g., eviction, going hungry) compared to those earning >$60,000 [8]. Lockdowns and economic strain contributed a 33% increase in domestic violence globally, placing pregnant women at heightened risk for adverse outcomes like miscarriage [12].

The pandemic intensified existing inequalities, with racially and socially marginalized pregnant populations bearing disproportionate hardships [7,8,13]. Most critically, pandemic-related stress among marginalized populations was linked to adverse birth outcomes [10,14], demonstrating how systemic inequalities translated into adverse perinatal health outcomes.

Research on pregnant women's COVID experiences lacks nationally representative U.S. data. While one study recruited a large sample (n = 4451 women) across all 50 states, social media recruitment biased the sample toward educated, white women, and the study did not identify who experienced stressors [11]. One recent study using Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) COVID supplement found an association between pandemic stressors and intimate partner violence [15], and another found pregnant women with disabilities had higher levels of pandemic-related stressors [16], yet neither reported prevalence of pregnant women's experiences among an overall, population-based sample. Other U.S. studies used small convenience samples (n = 183) [8] or single-state samples (n = 820) [13]. International studies included a multi-country sample (42.2% U.S., 41.0% Ireland, 9.9% UK; n = 573) [9], Canadian provincial (n = 265) [10] or social media samples (n = 1987) [17], and Australian qualitative research (n = 7)7, limiting generalizability to the U.S.

To address the lack of data regarding pregnant women's experiences during the COVID pandemic, we examined a population-based sample from the 2020-2021 Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) COVID supplement from 28 jurisdictions. This analysis provides data on how women from diverse demographic backgrounds and of varying health status experienced the COVID pandemic. Documentation of these experiences is critical for future preparation and response to public health crises.

Methods

This study used 2020-2021 PRAMS data, a joint surveillance project between the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and state and local health departments to collect population-based data from women who have recently given birth, including experiences before, during, and shortly after pregnancy [18]. Data are self-reported surveys linked with birth certificate data. During the COVID pandemic, PRAMS included an optional supplement regarding pandemic-related experiences. Of 123,392 participants in the original sample, we excluded those from states that did not complete the COVID supplement or with missing data on the COVID experiences (n = 99,041) and those with missing demographic and health data (n = 16,099) for an analytic sample of 8252 PRAMS participants (weighted n = 453,231) from 28 jurisdictions in the U.S. (Supplemental Table 1).

Experiences during the COVID pandemic

Maternal COVID experiences were derived from two questions. Participants answered “While you were pregnant during the COVID pandemic, did you have any of the following experience?” by self-reporting on 9 experiences (e.g., ‘I had responsibilities or a job that prevented me from staying home,’ ‘I was told by a health care provider that I had COVID’ (COVID-19 Supplement question CV5) [19]. Second, “Did any of the following things happen to you due to the COVID pandemic?” was answered via self-report of 13 experiences (e.g., ‘I lost my job or had a cut in work hours or pay,’ ‘I became homeless.’ (COVID-19 Supplement question CV11) [19]. Sociodemographic characteristics

Demographic variables included race/ethnicity [American Indian/Alaska Native (AIAN), Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic Asian, and other race/ethnicity], age (<20, 20-34, ≥35 years), marital status (Yes, No), and rurality (Urban, Rural). Socioeconomic indicators included Medicaid enrollment prior to pregnancy (Yes, No) and education (High school diploma or less, More than high school diploma).

Health-related factors

Health factors included parity (0 previous live births, ≥1 previous live births), pregnancy intention [Unintended (Late/never/not sure); Intended (Then/sooner], interpersonal violence during pregnancy (Yes, No); depression 3 months prior to pregnancy (Yes, No), diabetes prior to pregnancy (Yes, No); high blood pressure or hypertension prior to pregnancy (Yes, No); pre-pregnancy overweight/obese (Yes, No); and pre-pregnancy chronic conditions considered risk factors for COVID complications (i.e., diabetes, hypertension, overweight/obesity).

Statistical analysis

Factor analysis was conducted using the 22 Yes/No variables reflecting experiences during the COVID pandemic, to identify variables for which respondents responded similarly. Grouping variables in this way provides an opportunity to identify broader constructs with multiple dimensions (i.e., different examples of experiences). Variables within factors with loading scores >0.40 were summed to create factor indices. Each index was calculated from a count of Yes responses of the individual variables comprising the corresponding factor. Indices were then assessed for reliability with Cronbach's α. Each index is referred to by its factor number. Four of the 22 variables related to experiences during pregnancy did not load onto any of the factors. A complete list of variables comprising each factor are provided in Table 1 (with factor loadings) and Table 2 (with frequencies). Supplemental Table 2 provides a description of all variables, including data source, definition, and type.

As all variables were categorical, we used proc surveyfreq commands to account for PRAMS stratification and weighting [20]. Use of proc survey commands and domain analyses better estimates variance and results in more accurate tests of significance (SAS 9.4; SAS Institute 2016). All respondents were included in analysis for appropriate estimates, yet analyses used domain statements to obtain estimates for the analytic sample. For the overall analysis, we obtained percent and weighted frequencies for all variables in the analysis (Table 2). Next, we stratified analysis by the categorized factor variables, allowing us to report if factors differed by sociodemographic, social, and health variables. We used Rao-Scott chi square to estimate statistical significance (α = 0.05), accounting for complex survey design, weighted data, and factor variables (which were derived from multiple questions, violating the independence assumption of the Pearson chi square test [21]).

Results

Factor analysis results

Indices were created for the 5 of the 7 identified factors with factor loadings >0.40 and a meaningful grouping of experiences. Variables comprising each factor are in Table 1 (with factor loadings) and Table 2 (with frequencies). Supplemental Table 2 includes sources and definitions of each variable. Factor 1 (Cronbach's α = 0.74; 9 items) represents “family household stressors due to the pandemic”. Factor 7 (Cronbach's α = 0.46; 2 items) represents “housing instability due to the pandemic”. Factor 2 (Cronbach's α = 0.76; 3 items) represents “Trouble accessing COVID prevention measures while pregnant”. Factor 3 (Cronbach's α = 0.71; 2 items) represents “COVID in the household while pregnant”. Factor 6 (Cronbach's α = 0.42; 2 items) represents “exposure to others outside of the home while pregnant” (Table 2). The results of Cronbach's α for Factors 1, 2, and 3 exceeded 0.7, an acceptable level of internal consistency [22]. The lower Cronbach's α for Factors 6 and 7 reflect that each has only two items [22]. Factor indices were categorized for analysis. Factor 1 has three categories, where “yes” responses to 3+ experiences was considered high exposure, 1-2 experiences was medium exposure, and 0 was no exposure. For Factors 2, 3, 6, and 7, the scores were dichotomized into Yes, they experienced this factor (“yes” to 1+ experiences) or No, they did not experience this factor (“yes” to 0 experiences).

Sample characteristics and experiences

Sociodemographic characteristics are in Table 3. Most participants identified as non-Hispanic White (61.0%), followed by Hispanic (17.0%), non-Hispanic Black (13.3%), non-Hispanic Asian (4.7%), Other races/ethnicities (3.3%), and AIAN (0.7%). The majority of women did not receive Medicaid prior to pregnancy (76.5%), were married (61.1%), had > high school education (67.3%), and lived in urban areas (86.9%). Table 2 provides prevalence of self-reported experiences during the COVID pandemic. Family household stressors due to the pandemic (Factor 1) was the most prevalent factor; 42.1% of women reported a high level and 35.1% reported a medium level. The most common experience was feeling more anxious than usual (48.5%), followed by spending more time caring for children and other family (36.0%) and losing their job or cutting hours (29.9%). Approximately 11% of women had to move or relocate and 2.0% became homeless (Factor 7). More than one-third of women (35.2%) reported at least 1 negative experience with COVID prevention measures (Factor 2). Approximately 1 in 10 women reported COVID in the household (Factor 3). More than two-thirds of women (68.5%) said they had exposure to others outside of their home (Factor 6).

Factors by sociodemographic characteristics

Significant differences in household stressors (Factor 1) and housing instability (Factor 7) were observed across racial/ethnic groups (p < 0.01; Table 3). Black (48.5%) and Hispanic (48.0%) women had the highest prevalence of a high level of household stressors, while Asian women had the lowest prevalence (31.2%). Housing instability was most prevalent among AIAN (21.6%), Black (19.5%), and Hispanic women (15.5%).

Differences by marital status, education, and Medicaid enrollment were observed for Factor 1 and Factor 7. Unmarried women experienced higher household stressors (51.2% vs. 36.8%) and housing instability (17.4% vs. 7.3%) than married women (p < 0.01). Women with high school education or less, those on Medicaid, and urban residents reported higher rates of household stressors and housing instability.

Experience of COVID prevention challenges (Factor 2) were significantly different by race/ethnicity and age(p < 0.01). COVID prevention challenges were most frequently reported among Hispanic women (40.1%), followed by White (36.9%), other race/ethnicity (31.5%), AIAN (27.1%), Black (26.6%), and Asian (22.8%) women. Women aged 20–34 years (36.6%) reported the highest prevalence of challenges with COVID prevention, followed by those 35+ years (30.9%) and then women <20 years (28.8%).

COVID infection within the household (Factor 3) differed by race/ethnicity (p < 0.01). Infection in the household was most frequent among AIAN (22.8%) and Hispanic (19.2%) women, followed by Black (10.7%), White (9.2%) and Asian (8.9%) women (p < 0.01). Additionally, there was a lower prevalence of infection in the household among married women, those with higher education, and those not on Medicaid (all p < 0.01).

Exposure to others outside the home (Factor 6) was highest among White (71.2%), Hispanic (68.3%), and Black (65.6%) women, while AIAN (57.4%) and Asian (45.4%) women had lower exposure (p < 0.01). Also, exposure to others outside the home was most common among women aged 20–34 years (69.6%), followed by women ≥35 years (64.8%) and women <20 years (63.3%) (p = 0.03). Women with a high school education or less, non-Medicaid recipients, and urban residents reported lower prevalence of exposure outside the home (all p < 0.05).

Pandemic-related stress and housing instability differed by maternal health factors. Women experiencing interpersonal violence during pregnancy faced the most severe challenges, with 67.7% reporting high family stress (vs. 41.4% without violence) and 31.6% experiencing housing instability (vs. 10.5%; both p < 0.01). Additionally, women with unintended pregnancies reported greater family stress (52.8% vs. 36.4%), housing instability (15.6% vs. 8.8%), and household COVID exposure (14.3% vs. 9.7%; all p < 0.01). Multiparous women experienced higher family stress than first-time mothers (51.3% vs. 29.5%; p < 0.01).

Women with pre-pregnancy hypertension unexpectedly reported lower family stress (41.8% vs. 54.9%) and reduced household COVID exposure (11.2% vs. 16.6%; p < 0.01). Conversely, those with overweight/obesity experienced higher family stress (44.9% vs. 39.5%), increased household COVID exposure (13.3% vs. 9.2%), but also greater adherence to COVID prevention measures (38.7% vs. 31.2%; all p < 0.01). Those with pre-pregnancy depression reported higher family stress (63.0% vs. 38.2%) and housing instability (19.6% vs. 9.6%; both p < 0.01). Notably, women with pre-pregnancy depression were less likely to report exposure to others outside the home (67.5% vs. 73.3%; p < 0.01).

Discussion

This population-based analysis suggests that family household stressors were the most common challenges faced by pregnant women during the COVID pandemic. These family household stressors - including increased strain on interpersonal relationships and high rates of depression, anxiety and economic pressures - reflect how the pandemic intensified existing pressures within households. Many also reported trouble protecting themselves and their families from COVID exposures, as well as adhering to recommended prevention measures such as avoiding close contact and obtaining masks and hand sanitizer. These compounding factors collectively increased the mental and emotional burden on pregnant women, highlighting the critical need for family-centered public health strategies, interventions and reforms during public health crises [14,17].

Population-based data describing pregnant women's experiences during the COVID pandemic in the U.S. is sparse. One U.S.-based study recruited 4451 geographically diverse pregnant women, but was skewed toward educated, white women due to social media recruitment [11]. Furthermore, this study described only associations between pandemic-related experiences and subsequent stressors, but not who was experiencing stressors. Of the two recent studies using the PRAMS COVID supplement, one study of COVID stressors and intimate partner violence did not report prevalence for the full range of COVID experiences [15], and the other focused on a subset of women with disabilities [16], limiting our understanding of the full range of experiences during the COVID pandemic among a general population-based sample of pregnant women in the U.S. The remaining U.S. studies used small convenience samples (n = 183) [8] or were limited to single states (n = 820) [13]. Other studies included international samples, such as a multi-country sample (42.2% U.S., 41.0% Ireland, 9.9% UK; n = 573) [9] and Canadian studies using provincial samples (n = 265) [10] or social media-based samples (n = 1987) [17].

Family household stress

Family household stressors were the most prevalent challenge. Participants reported heightened anxiety, increased caregiving responsibilities, and financial strain. These stressors were compounded by limited prenatal care access, infection fears, and disrupted support systems. Social distancing protocols led to isolation during pregnancy and postpartum, while concerns about infant safety created psychological distress [14,17]. Of note, women experiencing violence showed substantially higher family stress, reflecting documented increases in domestic violence during lockdowns [11,15].

Racial and ethnic disparities

Significant racial and ethnic disparities in pandemic experiences were observed. Black women reported the highest levels of family stress, external exposure, and housing instability. Hispanic women faced barriers to accessing preventive resources. Asian women demonstrated the highest ability to engage in preventive behaviors, followed by Black women, while Hispanic women showed the lowest rates. These patterns align with national preventive behavior data [23] and confirm that minority communities bore disproportionate pandemic burdens [[24], [25], [26]].

Socioeconomic vulnerabilities

Housing instability is a public health concern [27], including increased risks for low birthweight and birth defects among babies born to mothers experiencing homelessness [28]. While the overall prevalence of homelessness in the U.S. is 0.2% [29], 2% of the current sample experienced homelessness - a 10-fold difference.

Additionally, those with low socioeconomic standing (e.g. age under 20 years, low educational attainment) reported high rates of family stress and poor experiences. These findings align with documented challenges among low-income pregnant populations during COVID [14,30]. The COVID pandemic exacerbated long standing challenges among low socioeconomic status populations [[31], [32], [33], [34]], likely contributing to elevated COVID mortality among low socioeconomic status populations.

Health factors

Reproductive history was an important factor. Multiparous women experienced significantly higher family stress than primiparous women. Additionally, women with unintended pregnancies reported the highest family stress levels and housing instability, consistent with global studies documenting heightened psychological distress in this population [14].

Women with pre-existing health conditions experienced additional stressors. Pre-pregnancy depression was associated with elevated family stressors and housing instability, indicating compounded emotional and socioeconomic challenges [35]. Women with pre-pregnancy obesity demonstrated higher household stress levels, increased preventive behavior engagement, and greater household COVID exposure, suggesting both heightened risk perception and vulnerability [36].

Public health implications

These findings have relevance for public health practice and policy. The predominance of family household stress indicates need for comprehensive family-centered interventions beyond traditional prenatal care models. Identified disparities demand targeted approaches addressing vulnerabilities among Black and Hispanic women, younger mothers, those with unintended pregnancies, and women experiencing violence.

Healthcare systems must integrate mental health screening and support into routine prenatal care, with attention to women with pre-existing depression. Housing stability programs should prioritize pregnant women and their families. The strong association between interpersonal violence and pandemic-related stress necessitates universal violence screening protocols in all prenatal settings. Public health emergency preparedness must address pregnant women's needs, ensuring access to prenatal care, social support, and preventive resources.

Strengths

This study's primary strength stems from the use of population-based, multistate PRAMS data (weighted n = 453,231) from 2020 to 2021, the only representative data regarding pregnant women's COVID experiences. Factor analysis identified clusters of pandemic-related experiences, moving beyond single-issue assessments to capture the interconnected nature of stressors. The comprehensive examination of sociodemographic characteristics and health-related factors provides critical insights into the experiences of pregnant women as well as disparities that can inform targeted public health interventions.

Limitations

Limitations include the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inferences about relationships between characteristics and pandemic experiences. The self-reported nature of data may introduce recall or social desirability bias. The optional COVID supplement module may have resulted in selection bias if participation patterns differed systematically across populations. Additionally, the study period (2020-2021) captures varying pandemic phases but cannot distinguish between different pandemic stages or policy responses. The low internal consistency for housing instability and exposure factors (Cronbach's α < 0.5) reflects their two-item construction but may limit reliability. Finally, the study cannot assess long-term health consequences, highlighting the need for longitudinal studies.

Conclusions

Family household stress emerging as the most pervasive challenge for pregnant women. Sociodemographic disparities emerged, with Black, Hispanic, younger, unmarried, less educated, and Medicaid-insured women experiencing greater pandemic stressors compounding existing inequities.

Public health emergency preparedness requires trauma-informed prenatal care integrating mental health, housing, violence screening, and expanded telehealth. Multifaceted support systems that address social determinants of health are essential for protecting familial well-being and reducing disparities in future public health crises.
