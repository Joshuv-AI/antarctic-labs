Cutoff for the inversion walk on tournaments and the state space of restricted inversions
Jiangdong Ai

Abstract
Given a labelled tournament on [ n ] [n] , inverting a vertex subset X X means reversing every edge with both endpoints in X X . Alon, Powierski, Savery, Scott, and Wilmer [ 2 ] asked for the mixing time of the Markov chain that repeatedly inverts a uniformly random subset of [ n ] [n] . We show that this inversion walk undergoes total-variation cutoff at time n n . More precisely, there is a universal constant C > 0 C>0 such that for all c ≥ 0 c\geq 0 , d n ​ ( n + c ) ≤ C ​ 2 − c d_{n}(n+c)\leq C\,2^{-c} , while for all s ∈ { 0 , 1 , … , n } s\in\{0,1,\dots,n\} , d n ​ ( n − s ) ≥ 1 − 2 n + s ​ log 2 ​ n − ( s 2 ) d_{n}(n-s)\geq 1-2^{\,n+s\log_{2}n-\binom{s}{2}} . In particular, the lower tail threshold lies within O ⁡ ( n ) O(\sqrt{n}) below n n , while the upper tail decays within O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) above n n . As a second result, we characterise the state space of the k k -restricted inversion walk , which inverts a uniformly random k k -subset at each step. For n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 and 2 ≤ k ≤ n − 2 2\leq k\leq n-2 , the reachable states form a coset of a subgroup H k ≤ 𝔽 2 ( n 2 ) H_{k}\leq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}} whose codimension is determined solely by k mod 4 k\bmod 4 .



1
Introduction

A
tournament
on the vertex set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
is an orientation of the complete
graph
K_{n}
: for each unordered pair
\{i,j\}
exactly one of
i\to j
or
j\to i
is present.
There are
2^{m}
labelled tournaments, where
m=\binom{n}{2}
.
For a subset
X\subseteq[n]
,
inverting
X
means reversing all arcs with both
endpoints in
X
.
A single inversion can flip up to
\binom{|X|}{2}
edges, so the operation is simultaneously
local (on small sets) and global (affecting
\Theta(n^{2})
edges for typical
X
).

Alon, Powierski, Savery, Scott, and Wilmer
[
2
]

initiated a systematic study of inversion distance in digraphs and tournaments.
Among other results, they proved that any labelled tournament on
[n]
can be reached from
any other by at most
(1+o(1))n
inversions, establishing that the
inversion diameter

is
\Theta(n)
.
They also showed that a uniformly random symmetric
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-matrix has rank
n-O(\log n)
with
high probability (Lemma 12 of
[
2
]
), and asked for the
mixing time of the natural random process that repeatedly inverts a uniformly random subset
of vertices.

The latter question fits into the rich theory of random walks on groups.
Encoding tournaments relative to a fixed reference identifies the state space with the abelian
group
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
, so the inversion walk is a
Cayley walk
: at each step, a random group
element is drawn from a symmetric generating multiset and added to the current state.
For Cayley walks on abelian groups, Fourier analysis on
G
reduces mixing to an estimate of
spectral gaps, which in turn reduce to character sums.
The distinctive feature here is that the generating distribution arises from quadratic functions on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
(one for each clique), giving rise to Gauss-type character sums over

\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
This connects the mixing problem to the theory of quadratic forms over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and the rank
of alternating bilinear forms.

Note that the state space has size
2^{m}=2^{\Theta(n^{2})}
,
so a naive random walk that moves one edge at a time (the
k=2
case; see
Proposition
6.3
) mixes in
\Theta(m\log m)=\Theta(n^{2}\log n)
steps.
By contrast, the full inversion walk mixes in
\Theta(n)
steps, achieving an exponential
speed-up by exploiting the high-dimensional structure of clique-induced inversions.

The
inversion walk

W_{n}
is the Markov chain on labelled
tournaments on
[n]
defined by: from the current tournament
T
, choose
X\subseteq[n]

uniformly among all
2^{n}
subsets and invert
X
.
(When
|X|\leq 1
the step is the identity, so the chain is automatically aperiodic.)
The stationary distribution is uniform on the
2^{m}
tournaments; write
\pi
for this
distribution and

d_{n}(t):=\max_{T_{0}}\|\mathcal{L}(W_{n}(t)\mid W_{n}(0)=T_{0})-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

for the worst-case total-variation distance at time
t
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for all
n
:

(i)

(Upper tail)
For every integer
c\geq 0
,

d_{n}(n+c)\ \leq\ C\,2^{-c}.

(ii)

(Lower tail)
For every integer
s\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
,

d_{n}(n-s)\ \geq\ 1-2^{\,n+s\log_{2}n-\binom{s}{2}}.

In particular,
d_{n}(t)\to 1
for
t\leq n-(\sqrt{2}+\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}

and
d_{n}(t)\to 0
for
t\geq n+\omega(1)
as
n\to\infty
.
Thus
\{W_{n}\}
undergoes total-variation cutoff at time
n
, with an asymmetric transition:
the lower-tail pre-cutoff scale is
O(\sqrt{n})
, while the upper tail is
O(1)
.

Remark 1.2

(Sharpness of the window)
.

The upper tail (i) gives
d_{n}(n+c)\leq C2^{-c}
, i.e. the distance decays exponentially fast
in the additive offset
c
above
n
. In particular, for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
there exists

c(\varepsilon)=O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
(independent of
n
) such that
\sup_{n}d_{n}(n+c(\varepsilon))\leq\varepsilon
.
The lower tail (ii) gives
d_{n}(n-s)\to 1
once

\binom{s}{2}\gg n
, i.e.,
s\gg\sqrt{2n}
.
We therefore have an asymmetric window: the walk is still far from stationary until roughly

\sqrt{2n}
steps
before

n
, but reaches stationarity essentially immediately

after

n
.
Determining the precise lower-tail window—whether it is
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
or not remains an interesting open problem.

Remark 1.3

(Comparison with the inversion diameter)
.

The inversion diameter of
\Theta(n)
from
[
2
]

matches the mixing time up to constants, but the two results are logically independent.
The diameter bounds the support of
\mu_{t}
, while mixing concerns
the
distribution
of the chain.
The connection is made precise via the inversion-ball volume argument in Section
4
.

Fix
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
and consider the
k
-restricted inversion walk

W_{n,k}
,
which inverts a uniformly random
k
-subset at each step.
In the group encoding,
W_{n,k}
moves by adding the vectors
v_{X}
with
|X|=k
, so it is
irreducible on cosets of the subgroup

H_{k}:=\langle v_{X}:\ |X|=k\rangle\leq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}.

Determining
H_{k}
is the first structural step toward studying the mixing of
W_{n,k}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume
n\geq 4
and
2\leq k\leq n-2
.
Define the degree-parity map
\partial:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
by

\partial(F)_{v}=\deg_{F}(v)\pmod{2}
, and the edge-count parity
e:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
by

e(F)=|F|\pmod{2}
.
Then

H_{k}\;=\;\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m},&k\equiv 2\pmod{4},\\
\ker(e),&k\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
\ker(\partial),&k\equiv 3\pmod{4},\\
\ker(\partial)\cap\ker(e),&k\equiv 1\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

The proof combines elementary parity obstructions with a dimension computation using Wilson’s
diagonal form for inclusion matrices
[
6
]
.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we set up the group encoding and the Fourier
L^{2}
–TV bound.
In Section
3
, we prove the upper tail.
In Section
4
, we establish the lower tail via inversion balls and a rank tail bound.
In Section
5
, we combine the two tails to obtain cutoff.
In Section
6
, we prove Theorem
1.4
, treat boundary cases, and record the
classical hypercube asymptotics for
k=2
.
In Section
7
, we collect open problems.

2
Preliminaries and group encoding

Fix a reference tournament
T_{\mathrm{ref}}
on
[n]
(the specific choice does not matter).
Let
m=\binom{n}{2}
and index coordinates of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
by the
m
unordered pairs

\{i,j\}\subseteq[n]
.
Encode each tournament
T
by
z(T)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
, where the coordinate indexed by
\{i,j\}

equals
1
iff
T
disagrees with
T_{\mathrm{ref}}
on the orientation of
\{i,j\}
.
The map
T\mapsto z(T)
is a bijection from the set of tournaments to
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
.

For
X\subseteq[n]
, define
v_{X}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
by

(v_{X})_{\{i,j\}}=\begin{cases}1,&\{i,j\}\subseteq X,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Since inverting
X
flips exactly the edges of the induced subgraph
K_{n}[X]
,
one checks that
z(\mathrm{inv}_{X}(T))=z(T)+v_{X}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
.
Thus
W_{n}
is a Cayley walk on
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
driven by the step distribution

\nu=\mathrm{Unif}\{v_{X}:X\subseteq[n]\}
(with multiplicity, since

v_{\emptyset}=v_{\{i\}}=0
).
The stationary distribution of any irreducible Cayley walk on a finite abelian group
is uniform, so, here
\pi=\mathrm{Unif}(G)
.

Characters of
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
are
\chi_{A}(z)=(-1)^{\langle A,z\rangle}
for
A\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
,
where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the standard inner product over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
Since each character
\chi_{A}
is a group homomorphism, a direct computation gives

P\chi_{A}=\lambda_{A}\chi_{A}
, so the characters diagonalise the transition operator.
The eigenvalue corresponding to
\chi_{A}
is

\lambda_{A}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathrm{Unif}(2^{[n]})}\!\big[(-1)^{\langle A,v_{X}\rangle}\big],\quad A\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m},

with
\lambda_{0}=1
for the trivial character.
For the chain started at a fixed state
T_{0}
, the distribution at time
t
satisfies

\mu_{t}(\{T\})=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{A\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}}\lambda_{A}^{t}\,(-1)^{\langle A,z(T)\rangle}(-1)^{\langle A,z(T_{0})\rangle}.

(1)

The standard
L^{2}
–TV inequality (see e.g.
[
5
, Proposition 7.14]
) then gives

\|\mu_{t}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{A\neq 0}\lambda_{A}^{2t}}.

(2)

Note that the right-hand side is independent of the starting state
T_{0}
, so (
2
)
bounds the worst-case distance
d_{n}(t)
directly.

Definition 2.1

(Inversion distance and ball)
.

For tournaments
T,T^{\prime}
on
[n]
, the
inversion distance

\mathrm{inv}(T,T^{\prime})
is the minimum
number of inversions needed to transform
T
into
T^{\prime}
.
The
inversion ball
of radius
t
around
T
is

B_{t}(T):=\{T^{\prime}:\ \mathrm{inv}(T,T^{\prime})\leq t\}
.

Note that
\mathrm{inv}(T,T^{\prime})
equals the minimum number of clique vectors
v_{X_{1}},\dots,v_{X_{s}}

(repetitions allowed) with
z(T^{\prime})-z(T)=\sum v_{X_{i}}
in
G
.
In particular, after
t
steps started from
T_{0}
, the chain
W_{n}
is supported on
B_{t}(T_{0})
.

3
Upper tail: spectral decay after time
n

We compute the eigenvalues
\lambda_{A}
in terms of the rank of a quadratic form.

Identify
A\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
with an undirected graph
H_{A}
on
[n]
whose edge-set is the support
of
A
. For
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
(the indicator vector of a set
X\subseteq[n]
) define

q_{A}(x):=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E(H_{A})}x_{i}x_{j}\ \in\ \mathbb{F}_{2}.

Since
|E(H_{A}[X])|\equiv q_{A}(x)\pmod{2}
, we have
\langle A,v_{X}\rangle=q_{A}(x)
, and therefore

\lambda_{A}=2^{-n}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{q_{A}(x)}.

(3)

Thus each eigenvalue is a (normalised) Walsh–Hadamard sum of the quadratic Boolean function

q_{A}:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

The
polarisation
of a quadratic function
q:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
is

B_{q}(x,y):=q(x+y)+q(x)+q(y)+q(0),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}.

If
q
is quadratic, then
B_{q}
is a symmetric bilinear form over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
Moreover, for any
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,

B_{q}(x,x)=2q(x)=0\pmod{2},

so
B_{q}
is
alternating
(equivalently, zero-diagonal).
Write
r(q):=\mathrm{rank}(B_{q})
for the rank of this alternating form, and
r(A):=r(q_{A})
.
Since
B_{q}
is alternating,
r(q)
is always even.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every quadratic
q:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
,

\Big|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{q(x)}\Big|\ \leq\ 2^{\,n-r(q)/2}.

Consequently, the eigenvalues of the inversion walk satisfy

|\lambda_{A}|\ \leq\ 2^{-r(A)/2}.

Proof.

Let
S=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{q(x)}
. Squaring and substituting
z=x+y
:

S^{2}=\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{q(x)+q(y)}=\sum_{x,z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{q(x)+q(x+z)}.

Using the definition of
B_{q}
:

q(x)+q(x+z)=B_{q}(x,z)+q(z)+q(0),

so

S^{2}=(-1)^{q(0)}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{q(z)}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{B_{q}(x,z)}.

The inner sum
\sum_{x}(-1)^{B_{q}(x,z)}
is a character sum over a linear map;
it equals
2^{n}
if
z\in\mathrm{rad}(B_{q}):=\{z:B_{q}(x,z)=0\ \forall x\}
,
and equals
0
otherwise (since when
z\notin\mathrm{rad}(B_{q})
the map
x\mapsto B_{q}(x,z)
is a
non-trivial linear functional, whose
\pm 1
values cancel perfectly). Therefore

|S|^{2}\leq|\mathrm{rad}(B_{q})|\cdot 2^{n}=2^{n-r(q)}\cdot 2^{n}=2^{2n-r(q)}.

Taking square roots:
|S|\leq 2^{n-r(q)/2}
. The eigenvalue bound follows from (
3
).
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For even
r\in\{0,2,\dots,n\}
, the number of alternating bilinear forms on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of rank
r

(equivalently, symmetric zero-diagonal
n\times n

\mathbb{F}_{2}
-matrices of rank
r
) is at most

2^{\,r(n-r)+r+\binom{r}{2}}.

Proof.

Let
V=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. An alternating form
B
of rank
r
has radical
\mathrm{rad}(B)
of dimension
n-r
.
Choose the radical subspace
R=\mathrm{rad}(B)
: the number of
(n-r)
-dimensional subspaces of
V

is the Gaussian binomial coefficient
\binom{n}{n-r}_{2}=\binom{n}{r}_{2}
.
Using the product formula,

\binom{n}{r}_{2}=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\frac{2^{n-i}-1}{2^{r-i}-1}\leq\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\frac{2^{n-i}}{2^{r-i-1}}=2^{\,r(n-r)+r}.

Having fixed
R
, the form descends to an alternating form on
V/R\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
.
The space of alternating bilinear forms on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
has dimension
\binom{r}{2}
, hence at most
2^{\binom{r}{2}}
choices.
Multiplying yields the claim.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

The bound in Lemma
3.2
is an overcount, since not all
2^{r(n-r)+r}
subspaces
arise as radicals, and since
we do not require non-degeneracy of the restricted form.
For the purpose of summing geometric series in rank, this overcount does not matter.

Proposition 3.4
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for all
n
and all integers
c\geq 0
,

d_{n}(n+c)\ \leq\ C\,2^{-c}.

Proof.

By (
2
) and Lemma
3.1
,

d_{n}(t)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{A\neq 0}2^{-r(A)t}}.

Since
r(A)
is the rank of an alternating form on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, it is always even.
Group by rank
r=r(A)
and apply Lemma
3.2
:

\sum_{A\neq 0}2^{-r(A)t}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\text{ even}\\
r\geq 2\end{subarray}}2^{\,r(n-r)+r+\binom{r}{2}}\cdot 2^{-rt}.

Let
t=n+c
. The exponent of
2
in the
r
-th term is

\displaystyle r(n-r)+r+\binom{r}{2}-r(n+c)

\displaystyle=rn-r^{2}+r+\tfrac{r(r-1)}{2}-rn-rc

\displaystyle=-r^{2}+r+\tfrac{r^{2}-r}{2}-rc

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{r^{2}}{2}+\tfrac{r}{2}-rc.

Hence

\sum_{A\neq 0}2^{-r(A)(n+c)}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\text{ even}\\
r\geq 2\end{subarray}}2^{-r^{2}/2+r/2-rc}.

For
r\geq 2
and
c\geq 0
the exponent
-r^{2}/2+r/2-rc\leq-r^{2}/2+r/2\leq-1
.
So,

\sum_{A\neq 0}2^{-r(A)(n+c)}\leq 2^{-2c}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\text{ even}\\
r\geq 2\end{subarray}}2^{-r^{2}/2+r/2}=:C_{0}\,2^{-2c},

where
C_{0}:=\sum_{r\geq 2,\,2|r}2^{-r(r-1)/2}<\infty
.
Therefore
d_{n}(n+c)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{C_{0}}\,2^{-c}
, and
C:=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{C_{0}}
is universal.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

There exists a universal constant
\alpha<1
such that for all
n
,

d_{n}(n-1)\leq\alpha.

In particular, the lower-tail cutoff window cannot be
O(1)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
,

d_{n}(t)\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{A\neq 0}|\lambda_{A}|^{2t}\Big)^{1/2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{A\neq 0}2^{-r(A)t}\Big)^{1/2}.

Grouping by the (even) rank
r=r(A)
and applying Lemma
3.2
gives

\sum_{A\neq 0}2^{-r(A)t}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\geq 2\\
2\mid r\end{subarray}}2^{\,r(n-r)+r+\binom{r}{2}}\,2^{-rt}.

Setting
t=n-1
, the exponent simplifies to

r(n-r)+r+\binom{r}{2}-r(n-1)=-\frac{r(r-3)}{2},

hence

\sum_{A\neq 0}|\lambda_{A}|^{2(n-1)}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\geq 2\\
2\mid r\end{subarray}}2^{-r(r-3)/2}=2+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{512}+O(2^{-20})<2.252.

Therefore
d_{n}(n-1)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2.252}<0.751=:\alpha
.
In particular,
d_{n}(n-1)
does not tend to
1
, so the distance cannot stay near
1

all the way up to
n-O(1)
. Equivalently, any lower-tail transition scale must diverge with
n
.
∎

4
Lower tail: inversion balls and a rank tail inequality

Let
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
denote the set of all symmetric
n\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
We have
|\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})|=2^{m+n}
since such a matrix has
m=\binom{n}{2}
strictly
upper-triangular entries and
n
diagonal entries.

Lemma 4.1

(Rank tail for random symmetric matrices
[
2
, Lemma 12]
)
.

Let
M
be uniformly random in
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
. Then for every integer
s\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
,

\Pr\big(\mathrm{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(M)\leq n-s\big)\ \leq\ 2^{\,s\log_{2}n-\binom{s}{2}}.

Remark 4.2
.

This bound is from
[
2
]
; we reproduce it for convenience since it is the key
probabilistic input. Informally, a uniformly random symmetric
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-matrix typically has
rank
n-O(1)
, and Lemma 4.1 shows that the probability of rank deficiency
s
decays
double-exponentially in
s
.

The following proposition translates inversion-ball volume into a statement about low-rank
symmetric matrices, using the group-algebra structure.

Proposition 4.3
.

Fix a tournament
T_{0}
on
[n]
and write
m=\binom{n}{2}
.
For every integer
s\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
,

|B_{n-s}(T_{0})|\ \leq\ 2^{\,m+n+s\log_{2}n-\binom{s}{2}}.

Consequently, for the uniform distribution
\pi
on tournaments,

\pi(B_{n-s}(T_{0}))\ \leq\ 2^{\,n+s\log_{2}n-\binom{s}{2}}.

Proof.

We embed tournaments into
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
and use the rank-subadditivity of sums of
rank-
1
matrices.

Choose any symmetric matrix
M_{T_{0}}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
whose strict upper-triangular part
encodes
T_{0}
(the diagonal of
M_{T_{0}}
is arbitrary; fix it once and for all, say as zero).
For any subset
X\subseteq[n]
, the matrix

M_{X}:=\mathbf{1}_{X}\mathbf{1}_{X}^{\top}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})

is the all-ones matrix on the
X\times X
block and zero elsewhere, and has rank at most
1
.

Let
T\in B_{n-s}(T_{0})
.
By definition, there exists a sequence
X_{1},\dots,X_{t}
with
t\leq n-s
such that

z(T)=z(T_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{t}v_{X_{i}}
in
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
. Fix one such sequence
X_{1},\dots,X_{t}
for each
T\in B_{n-s}(T_{0})
.
All objects below are defined with respect to this choice.
Consider the symmetric matrix

D:=\sum_{i=1}^{t}M_{X_{i}}\;\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2}).

The strict upper-triangular part of
D
coincides with
v_{X_{1}}+\cdots+v_{X_{t}}=z(T)-z(T_{0})
,
which is the strict upper-triangular part of
M_{T}-M_{T_{0}}
.
We therefore
define
the diagonal of
M_{T}
so that
M_{T}-M_{T_{0}}=D
, i.e.,

\mathrm{diag}(M_{T}):=\mathrm{diag}(M_{T_{0}})+\mathrm{diag}(D)\pmod{2}.

This choice of diagonal is unique given
D
and
M_{T_{0}}
, and it ensures

M_{T_{0}}+M_{T}=D
in
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
.

By subadditivity of rank and
\mathrm{rank}(M_{X_{i}})\leq 1
,

\mathrm{rank}(M_{T_{0}}+M_{T})=\mathrm{rank}(D)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathrm{rank}(M_{X_{i}})\leq t\leq n-s.

The map
T\mapsto M_{T}
defined above is injective: two tournaments
T\neq T^{\prime}
differ on some
edge
\{a,b\}
, so the
(a,b)
-entry of
M_{T}
and
M_{T^{\prime}}
differ, hence
M_{T}\neq M_{T^{\prime}}
.
Consequently,
T\mapsto M_{T_{0}}+M_{T}
is also injective (since
M_{T_{0}}
is fixed), and the
image is contained in
\{N\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2}):\mathrm{rank}(N)\leq n-s\}
.

Therefore

|B_{n-s}(T_{0})|\leq\big|\{N\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2}):\mathrm{rank}(N)\leq n-s\}\big|=|\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})|\cdot\Pr(\mathrm{rank}(M)\leq n-s)\leq 2^{m+n}\cdot 2^{s\log_{2}n-\binom{s}{2}},

using Lemma
4.1
with
M
uniform on
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
.
Dividing by the number of tournaments
2^{m}
gives the bound on
\pi
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

For the inversion walk
W_{n}
and every integer
s\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
,

d_{n}(n-s)\ \geq\ 1-2^{\,n+s\log_{2}n-\binom{s}{2}}.

In particular, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

d_{n}\!\left(n-\Big\lfloor(\sqrt{2}+\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\Big\rfloor\right)\ \longrightarrow\ 1\qquad(n\to\infty).

Proof.

Fix any initial tournament
T_{0}
.
After
t=n-s
steps the chain is supported on
B_{t}(T_{0})
, so

\mu_{t}(B_{t}(T_{0}))=1
, and therefore

\|\mu_{t}-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\geq\mu_{t}(B_{t}(T_{0}))-\pi(B_{t}(T_{0}))=1-\pi(B_{n-s}(T_{0})).

For the second part, let
s=\lfloor(\sqrt{2}+\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}\rfloor
. Then

s^{2}=(2+2\sqrt{2}\,\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2})n+O(\sqrt{n}),

and hence

\displaystyle\binom{s}{2}-n-s\log_{2}n

\displaystyle=\frac{s^{2}-s}{2}-n-s\log_{2}n

\displaystyle\geq\Big(\sqrt{2}\,\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Big)n-O(\sqrt{n}\log n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}+\infty.

Therefore
n+s\log_{2}n-\binom{s}{2}\to-\infty
and the bound tends to
1
.
∎

5
Cutoff at time
n

Definition 5.1

(Total-variation cutoff;
[
1
,
3
]
)
.

A sequence of Markov chains undergoes
total-variation cutoff
at times
t_{n}
with
window
w_{n}
if for every fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{n}(t_{n}-\delta w_{n})=1,\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{n}(t_{n}+\delta w_{n})=0.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Item (i) is Proposition
3.4
, and item (ii) is Corollary
4.4
.

For cutoff, fix any
\delta\in(0,1)
.

•

Below the cutoff:
let
t=(1-\delta)n=n-\delta n
.
For large
n
,
s:=\delta n\gg(\sqrt{2}+\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
so Corollary
4.4
gives
d_{n}(n-s)\to 1
.

•

Above the cutoff:
let
t=(1+\delta)n=n+\delta n
.
Proposition
3.4
gives
d_{n}(n+\delta n)\leq C\,2^{-\delta n}\to 0
.

Hence
\{W_{n}\}
undergoes cutoff at time
n
.
The lower-tail pre-cutoff scale is
O(\sqrt{n})
, while the upper tail is
O(1)
.
∎

6
Restricted inversions

Fix
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.
The
k
-restricted inversion walk

W_{n,k}
chooses a uniformly random
k
-subset

X\subseteq[n]
and inverts
X
.
In the group encoding,
W_{n,k}
is a Cayley walk on
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
driven by

\mathcal{S}_{k}:=\{v_{X}:\ X\subseteq[n],\ |X|=k\},\qquad H_{k}:=\langle\mathcal{S}_{k}\rangle\leq G.

Let
V_{k}
denote the right-hand side subspace in Theorem
1.4
(depending on
k\bmod 4
).
The chain is irreducible on cosets of
H_{k}
, with uniform stationary distribution on each such coset.

6.1
Parity invariants

Identify
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
with the family of edge-subsets
F\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}
under
symmetric difference.
Define two
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear functionals:

\partial:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n},\quad\partial(F)_{v}:=\deg_{F}(v)\pmod{2}\qquad\text{(degree-parity map)},

e:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2},\quad e(F):=|F|\pmod{2}\qquad\text{(edge-count parity)}.

We compute these on generators.
For a
k
-clique
K_{X}
on
X\subseteq[n]
,
\deg_{K_{X}}(v)=k-1
if
v\in X
and
0

otherwise, so

\partial(K_{X})=(k-1)\mathbf{1}_{X}\pmod{2}.

Hence:

•

If
k
is odd, then
k-1
is even, so
\partial(K_{X})=0
for all
X
; thus

H_{k}\subseteq\ker(\partial)
.

•

If
k
is even, then
\partial(K_{X})=\mathbf{1}_{X}\not\equiv 0
; the obstruction

\ker(\partial)
is not automatic.

Also,
e(K_{X})=\binom{k}{2}\pmod{2}
.
Since
\binom{k}{2}=k(k-1)/2
:

•

\binom{k}{2}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
iff
k\equiv 0
or
1\pmod{4}
; in these cases

e(K_{X})=0
for all
X
and
H_{k}\subseteq\ker(e)
.

•

\binom{k}{2}\equiv 1\pmod{2}
iff
k\equiv 2
or
3\pmod{4}
; the edge-count
obstruction does not apply.

Combining:
H_{k}\subseteq\ker(\partial)\cap\ker(e)
when
k\equiv 1\pmod{4}
;

H_{k}\subseteq\ker(\partial)
(but not necessarily
\ker(e)
) when
k\equiv 3\pmod{4}
;

H_{k}\subseteq\ker(e)
when
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
; and no obstruction when
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
.
The content of Theorem
1.4
is that these inclusions are equalities.

6.2
Boundary cases

k=0
or
k=1
.

v_{X}=0
for all
X
with
|X|\leq 1
, so
H_{k}=\{0\}
and the chain does not move.

k=n
.

There is only one
n
-subset, namely
[n]
, so
H_{n}=\langle v_{[n]}\rangle
has size
2
.
Starting from
T_{0}
, the chain alternates between
T_{0}
and its complete reversal
T_{0}^{*}

(the tournament obtained by reversing all arcs).

k=n-1
.

The generators are
\{v_{[n]\setminus\{i\}}:i\in[n]\}
. We claim:

•

If
n
is odd: the
n
generators are linearly independent, so
\dim H_{n-1}=n
.

•

If
n
is even: the
n
generators span a space of dimension
n-1
; there is exactly
one linear relation,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{[n]\setminus\{i\}}=0
.

Proof.

Write
u_{i}:=v_{[n]\setminus\{i\}}
. Edge
\{a,b\}
contributes to
u_{i}
iff
i\neq a,b
,
so
\{a,b\}
appears in
\sum_{i\in S}u_{i}
with coefficient
|S\setminus\{a,b\}|\pmod{2}
.

For the full sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}
: edge
\{a,b\}
appears
n-2
times.
Thus
\sum u_{i}=0
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
iff
n
is even; if
n
is odd the sum is non-zero.

For a proper non-empty sub-sum
\sum_{i\in S}u_{i}
with
\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq[n]
:
it suffices to find an edge
\{a,b\}
for which the coefficient
|S\setminus\{a,b\}|
is odd.
Such an edge always exists:
if
|S|
is even, pick
a\in S
and
b\notin S
, so
|S\setminus\{a,b\}|=|S|-1
is odd;
if
|S|
is odd and
|[n]\setminus S|\geq 2
, pick
\{a,b\}\subseteq[n]\setminus S
,
so
|S\setminus\{a,b\}|=|S|
is odd;
if
|S|
is odd and
|[n]\setminus S|=1
, pick
\{a,b\}\subseteq S
,
so
|S\setminus\{a,b\}|=|S|-2
is odd.
Hence some edge has coefficient
1
and every proper non-empty sub-sum is non-zero.

Hence, for odd
n
there is no linear relation among the
n
generators, giving

\dim H_{n-1}=n
; for even
n
the unique minimal relation is the full sum, giving

\dim H_{n-1}=n-1
.
∎

In both cases the state space of
W_{n,n-1}
is much smaller than
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
(it has
dimension at most
n
, compared to
m=\binom{n}{2}
), so the chain mixes rapidly.

6.3
The main regime
2\leq k\leq n-2

The proof of Theorem
1.4
proceeds by comparing dimensions.
The key tool is Wilson’s diagonal form for inclusion matrices.

Lemma 6.1

(Wilson
[
6
]
; see also Jolliffe
[
4
]
)
.

Let
W_{2,k}(n)
be the
0/1
matrix with rows indexed by
2
-subsets and columns by

k
-subsets of
[n]
, with entry
1
iff the
2
-subset is contained in the
k
-subset.
Then over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
,

\mathrm{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}W_{2,k}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\{0,1,2\}\\
2\nmid\binom{k-j}{2-j}\end{subarray}}\left(\binom{n}{j}-\binom{n}{j-1}\right),\qquad\binom{n}{-1}:=0.

Note that the column span of
W_{2,k}(n)
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is exactly
H_{k}
,
since each column is
v_{X}
for some
k
-subset
X
, and the columns generate
H_{k}
.
Hence
\dim H_{k}=\mathrm{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}W_{2,k}(n)
.

We now evaluate the rank formula by case, determining which
j\in\{0,1,2\}
contribute.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Upper bound
H_{k}\subseteq V_{k}
.

This was established in Section
6.1
.

Dimension computation via Wilson’s formula.

We check which of the three terms
j=0,1,2
contribute to the rank sum.

•

j=2
:

\binom{k-2}{0}=1
, which is odd.
Contribution:
\binom{n}{2}-\binom{n}{1}=m-n
.

•

j=1
:

\binom{k-1}{1}=k-1
, which is odd iff
k
is even.
Contribution when
k
is even:
\binom{n}{1}-\binom{n}{0}=n-1
.

•

j=0
:

\binom{k}{2}=k(k-1)/2
, which is odd iff
k\equiv 2
or
3\pmod{4}
.
Contribution:
\binom{n}{0}-0=1
.

Summing over the active terms:

\dim H_{k}=\mathrm{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}W_{2,k}(n)=\begin{cases}(m-n)+(n-1)+1=m&k\equiv 2\pmod{4},\\
m-n+1&k\equiv 3\pmod{4},\\
m-1&k\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
m-n&k\equiv 1\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

Dimensions of
V_{k}
.

The map
\partial:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is the mod-
2
vertex-edge incidence map of
K_{n}
.
Since
K_{n}
is connected, the rank of this map (over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
) equals
n-1
(Indeed,
\mathrm{Im}(\partial)=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:\langle x,\mathbf{1}\rangle=0\}
for connected graphs,
so
\mathrm{rank}(\partial)=n-1
).
Hence
\dim\ker(\partial)=m-(n-1)=m-n+1
.

The map
e:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
is non-trivial (a single edge has
e=1
), so
\dim\ker(e)=m-1
.

For the intersection: we need
e|_{\ker(\partial)}\neq 0
, i.e., there exists an edge-set

F
with
\partial(F)=0
(all degrees even) and
|F|
odd.
A triangle
\{a,b\},\{b,c\},\{a,c\}
has all vertices of degree
2\equiv 0\pmod{2}
and
has
|F|=3
(odd), so it lies in
\ker(\partial)\setminus\ker(e)
.
Therefore
e|_{\ker(\partial)}\neq 0
, and
\dim(\ker(\partial)\cap\ker(e))=m-n
.

The dimensions of
V_{k}
and
H_{k}
match in every case. Since
H_{k}\subseteq V_{k}
,
we conclude
H_{k}=V_{k}
.
∎

Remark 6.2

(Eigenvalues of
W_{n,k}
)
.

The Fourier eigenvalues of
W_{n,k}
are

\lambda^{(k)}_{A}=\mathbb{E}_{|X|=k}\!\big[(-1)^{|E(H_{A}[X])|\bmod 2}\big],

where
H_{A}
is the graph with edge-set
A
.
For fixed
k
, these are related to Krawtchouk-type polynomials in the degree sequence of
H_{A}
.
For
k\geq 3
a sharp mixing result would require bounding
\sum_{A\neq 0}|\lambda^{(k)}_{A}|^{2t}

over the coset, which appears more delicate than the
k=n
case due to the richer dependence of

\lambda^{(k)}_{A}
on the combinatorial structure of
A
.

6.4
The case
k=2
: the hypercube

When
k=2
, each step flips exactly one uniformly random edge.
In the encoding
z(T)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
, this is the simple random walk on the
m
-dimensional
hypercube
\{0,1\}^{m}
. The non-lazy version has period
2
; we state mixing for the lazy
version
W_{n,2}^{L}
(which remains at the current state with probability
1/2
).

Proposition 6.3

(Hypercube mixing;
[
5
, Ch. 18]
)
.

The lazy
k=2
walk
W_{n,2}^{L}
has

t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\varepsilon)=\frac{m}{2}\ln m+O(m)\qquad\text{for every fixed }\varepsilon\in(0,1),

and undergoes cutoff at time
\frac{m}{2}\ln m=\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2}\ln\binom{n}{2}

with window
\Theta(m)=\Theta(n^{2})
.

Comparing with Theorem
1.1
: the full inversion walk mixes at time
\Theta(n)
, an
exponential improvement over the
\Theta(n^{2}\log n)
mixing time for
k=2
.
This reflects the fact that a typical inversion by a
\Theta(n)
-sized set flips
\Theta(n^{2})

edges simultaneously, achieving in one step what the hypercube walk needs
\Theta(n^{2})

steps to accomplish.

7
Discussion and open problems

Theorem
1.1
establishes cutoff at time
n
for the inversion walk on tournaments,
answering the mixing-time question posed in
[
2
, Section 8]
.
Theorem
1.4
gives a complete structural description of the state space of the

k
-restricted walk for
2\leq k\leq n-2
.

We collect several natural follow-up directions.

(1)

Sharp cutoff window.

Our bounds show
d_{n}(t)\to 1
for
t\leq n-(\sqrt{2}+\varepsilon)\sqrt{n}

and
d_{n}(n+c)\leq C\,2^{-c}
for all
c\geq 0
.
In particular the lower-tail pre-cutoff scale is at most
O(\sqrt{n})
, while the upper tail
is
O(1)
.
Note that Lemma
3.5
rules out an
O(1)
lower-tail window.
Closing this gap would likely require a more precise rank tail for the random matrix in
Lemma
4.1
, or a direct spectral argument for the lower bound.

(2)

Mixing of the restricted walk.

For each fixed
k
, determine the mixing time and cutoff behaviour of
W_{n,k}
on its state
space (the coset of
H_{k}
, whose dimension is now known by Theorem
1.4
).
The eigenvalue formula
\lambda^{(k)}_{A}=\mathbb{E}_{|X|=k}[(-1)^{|E(H_{A}[X])|}]
is a graph-theoretic
character sum that depends on the subgraph structure of
H_{A}
in a subtle way.
For
k=\Theta(n)
, the analysis should be tractable by the same Fourier–rank approach used
here; small fixed
k
appears harder.

(3)

Other digraph families.

The inversion walk and its variants can be defined for general digraphs, not just tournaments.
For digraphs with repeated arcs or for orientations of non-complete graphs, the algebraic
structure is similar but the rank calculations may differ. Extending the cutoff result to
these settings is a natural generalisation.

(4)

Spectral gap.

The spectral gap of
W_{n}
is
\gamma=1-\max_{A\neq 0}|\lambda_{A}|
.
From the proof of Proposition
3.4
(with
c=0,t=n
), a rough bound gives

\gamma\geq 1-2^{-1}=1/2
.
Determining the precise spectral gap and the associated relaxation time is an interesting
spectral problem.
