Thermal Oxidation of Ventilation Air Methane in a Flow Reversal Reactor: Comparison of Heat Recovery between Central Cooling and Hot Gas Withdrawal
Authors: Guanghui He, Zhikai Li, Zhiwei Wu, Bo Lu, Qingling Tian, Zhangfeng Qin, Mei Dong, Weibin Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thermal oxidation of ventilation air methane in a flow reversal reactor has attracted more attention due to its low operation cost and high grade of heat. However, there is still controversy over the waste heat recovery and utilization. A detailed one-dimensional heterogeneous model was designed, and the performances of the two heat recovery schemes, namely, central cooling and hot gas withdrawal, were systematically compared. Numerical results show that in the studied cases, the permissible ventilation air methane flow rate and methane concentration for central cooling are 2000–18,000 m3/h and 0.4–1.2 vol % with heat recovery efficiency around 55%, while those for hot gas withdrawal are 2000–10,000 m3/h and 0.6–1.2 vol % with a heat recovery efficiency of 46–76%. Theoretically, the heat recovery efficiency is independent of the heat recovery scheme. Due to the long switching time of the central cooling, it shows a higher overall methane conversion. Temperature fluctuation at the reactor center is 520 °C for hot gas withdrawal, while that for central cooling is 280 °C, which reduces the thermal shock to the bed and slows down the pulverization of the filler. Thus, central cooling seems a favorable heat recovery scheme in terms of its larger permissible flow rate, wider methane concentration range, higher methane conversion, and less thermal shock.

Introduction

Methane (CH4) is the second most potent anthropogenic greenhouse gas, with a global warming potential (GWP) 21 times higher than that of carbon dioxide (CO2). Coal mining activities contribute approximately 8% of total anthropogenic methane emissions, with ventilation air methane (VAM) representing the dominant fraction at 70%. The effective utilization of VAM is severely hampered by several inherent challenges: exceedingly low methane concentrations (as low as 0.2 vol %), substantial concentration variability, exceptionally high gas flow rates, and near-complete moisture saturation.

Two technologies have emerged in industrial practice for the mitigation and utilization of VAM: catalytic oxidation and thermal oxidation utilizing flow reversal reactor configurations. , The flow reversal reactor demonstrates a notable advantage in enabling autothermal operation even under extremely low methane concentrations. However, the inherent forced unsteady-state dynamics and substantial methane concentration variability pose significant control challenges to the operation of the flow reversal reactor. , Many control strategies have been attempted, including model predictive control frameworks, − linear quadratic regulation, , and logic control architectures. − With the help of these control strategies, the bed temperature can be well controlled under dynamic conditions.

Catalytic oxidation offers the advantage of reduced methane concentration thresholds as low as 0.2 vol %, yet suffers from catalyst deactivation by sintering, particularly under dynamic operating conditions and substantial methane concentration fluctuations. Therefore, the aims are to reduce the cost such as by reducing the loading of precious metals, replacing with nonprecious metals, and improving the sintering resistance, one of which is in situ growth of noble metals on the framework of molecular sieves.

The advantage of thermal oxidation is low operation cost due to the absence of catalyst loading. Due to the fact that thermal oxidation is a chain reaction and has a much higher activation energy than catalytic oxidation, thermal oxidation is highly sensitive to methane concentration. Thus, methane concentration always exceeds 0.5 vol % by mixing ventilation air methane (0.2–0.5 vol %). Recently, the autothermal limit of the methane concentration was extended close to that of catalytic oxidation. Gosiewski et al. and Mao et al. reported that thermal oxidation may sustain when the concentration of VAM is higher than 0.2 vol % in a demonstration plant; Lan et al. reported that the limit of VAM in an industrial-scale multibed thermal flow reversal reactor is 0.22 vol %; Yin et al. and Li et al. showed that thermal oxidation was self-sustainable with VAM concentrations higher than 0.25 vol %. Gosiewski and Pawlaczyk pointed out that when the concentration of VAM is lower than 0.4 vol %, the catalytic flow reversal reactor is an appropriate solution, but no heat can be utilized; with a methane concentration higher than 0.4 vol %, the thermal flow reversal reactor is preferred.

As for recovering partial waste heat, generally there are two ways adopted: central cooling and hot gas withdrawal. For the former one, feed flows through the heat exchanger buried in the reactor (Figure a), while part of hot gas is extracted out of the reactor for the latter one (Figure b). Gosiewski et al. noted that heat recovery by central cooling leads to asymmetry in temperature profiles, potentially causing reaction extinction or damage to the heat recovery system, and hot gas withdrawal is expected to be superior to central cooling. Lan et al. reported an industrial-scale (100,000 N m3/h) thermal oxidation system with heat recovery by hot gas withdrawal, and approximately 76.3% of the energy can be recovered at a methane concentration of 1.0 vol %. However, unoxidized methane was vented with withdrawn gas, reducing the total conversion of the methane. Furthermore, more heat will be accumulated in the middle of the reactor, and thus, the maximum temperature of the bed increases with hot gas withdrawal, which increases the temperature fluctuation. ,

Schematic diagram of heat recovery by (a) central cooling and (b) hot gas withdrawal of thermal oxidation of ventilation air methane in a flow reversal reactor.

Although heat recovery by central cooling can cause a significant temperature gradient after the heat exchanger, it can produce the saturated and superheated steam directly. Li et al. showed an industrial-scale thermal oxidation system with heat recovery by embedding a set of coil heat exchangers in the porous medium, and about 31.61%–46.82% energy can be recovered. Optimizing the distribution of the heat exchanger can reduce the asymmetry of bed temperature. Shi et al. designed a thermal flow reactor with two heat exchangers embedded on both sides of the high temperature zone and found the asymmetry can be depressed.

Our previous results show that the effective utilization of the bed is reduced due to the narrowing of the temperature profile for hot gas withdrawal, and the temperature difference at a specific position of the bed can reach 300–500 °C in a switching cycle, which makes the packed bed vulnerable to pulverization. As for central cooling, the scheme with two built-in heat exchangers is significantly better than the scheme with one built-in heat exchanger. The reason for this is that only the downstream heat exchanger operates during the reaction. When the heat exchanger is placed downstream of the reaction zone, heat removal does not affect the heat in the reaction zone, resulting in a relatively uniform temperature distribution and thus stable operation. In order to elucidate the effects of the heat recovery scheme on reaction performances, hot gas withdrawal and central cooling with two heat exchangers embedded were systematically compared numerically in terms of bed temperature distribution, maximum temperature, methane conversion, heat utilization boundary, and heat recovery efficiency. In particular, the thermal shock at a specific position of the bed is compared, which may provide a basis for the selection of heat recovery schemes.

Physical Model and Numerical Method

Thermal Flow Reversal Reactor

The thermal flow reversal reactor integrated with heat recovery, as schematically illustrated in Figure , is designed for the mitigation and energy utilization of ventilation air methane. The thermal flow reversal reactor has dual-chamber symmetric design, with each chamber having an effective cross-sectional area of 1.8 m2 (length × width = 1.5 m × 1.2 m); the chamber is filled with 1000 mm honeycomb ceramics, and the density, volume density, and porosity of the ceramics are 1683 kg/m3, 423 kg/m3, and 0.75, respectively; the hydraulic diameter of the channel is 2.16 × 10–3 m, and the heat capacity and thermal conductivity of the channel are 1020 J/kg/K and 1.46 W/m/K, respectively.

For central cooling, the number of tubes of the heat exchanger is 500, and the total heat exchange area is 40 m2; the outer diameter of the heat exchange tube is 20 mm, the center distance is 26 mm, and the center distance of the two adjacent tubes is 40 mm (Table ). Water flows inside the tube, and hot air flows outside the tube. The working process is as follows: the heat exchanger on the side of the feed inlet does not work, while the downstream heat exchanger works. This means that only one heat exchanger works at any time, which is beneficial to stable operation. For hot gas withdrawal, a pipe is led out from the top of the bed and into the heat exchanger. The output of hot air is controlled by the tail pump by adjusting the flow rate.

To simulate flow reversal, we used the final state values from the prior simulation run as the initial conditions for the current run. Concurrently, the gas velocity sign was reversed, and the boundary conditions were correspondingly inverted. In order to adapt to different flow and concentration conditions, a dynamic switching time scheme was adopted. Specifically, select the two points (x = 0.6 m for forward and x = 2.0 m for reverse) of the bed. When T < T set, reverse the flow direction to prevent the temperature from falling further. T set should not be lower than 600 °C, which is considered to be the lowest temperature at which thermal oxidation can occur. For thermal oxidation, its ignition temperature T 10 is around 700 °C. When the temperature is below 600 °C, this indicates that this area is no longer playing any reactive role. If the temperature falls below this value, the reversal time will be prolonged, and the reaction zone will significantly shift downstream, which is not conducive to the stability of the bed. Therefore, the 0.6–2.0 m range is the area where thermal oxidation can occur, while other areas are mainly used for heat storage.

Results and Discussion

Comparison of the Dynamic and Fixed Flow Reversal Strategy

Periodically changing the feed direction is a fundamental characteristic of the flow reversal reactor. Meanwhile, switching time is also an operating variable: the longer switching time is beneficial to reduce the frequency of valve switching, but more heat will be discharged from the reactor outlet. The appropriate switching time also needs to be adjusted with variation of the methane concentration or flow rate. With a low methane concentration or high flow rate, it is necessary to reduce the switching time to further accumulate the heat in the center of the reactor. Generally, thermal oxidation adopts the strategy of a fixed switching time. , In order to clarify the impact of switching time on reaction performance, fixed and dynamic flow reversal strategies under different flow rates were compared first.

The concentration of methane is fixed at 1.0 vol %, and the flow rate of VAM is 6000, 10,000, 14,000, and 18,000 m3/h, respectively. For fixed switching time operation, the switching time is set to 300 s; for dynamic flow reversal, flow direction will be changed when the temperature at the selected point of the bed is less than 600 °C, which is considered to be the lowest temperature igniting the thermal oxidation. It can be seen from Figure that when the flow rate increases from 6000 to 18,000 m3/h, the switching time decreases from 420 to 80 s and methane conversion remains above 95% for dynamic flow reversal. For fixed switching time, when the flow rate reaches 18,000 m3/h, a large amount of unreacted methane was directly discharged into the atmosphere from the reactor outlet. In addition, thermal distribution of the bed is uniform for dynamic flow reversal, which is conducive to reducing the temperature fluctuation of the bed. Thus, dynamic switching time can further increase the robustness of the reactor, enabling it to adapt to more complex concentration and flow fluctuations.

Heat Recovery by Central Cooling

Effect of the Amount of Heat Extracted Out of the Reactor

First, the effect of heat extracted on reactor performance was investigated with a methane concentration of 1.0 vol % and the flow rate of 10,000 m3/h. When the heat is extracted out of the reactor, the temperature at the measurement point will be reduced. In order to adapt to this change, one way is to keep the reversible temperature T sw unchanged and move the temperature measurement point to the center of the reactor; the other is to reduce the T sw value. In this part, we choose the latter to reduce T sw from 600 to 400 °C. The maximum bed temperature is about 1400 °C when the average temperature of the cold fluid T c rises from 20 to 300 °C (Figure ). However, the lower the cold fluid temperature, the narrower the temperature distribution of the bed and the less heat flows out from the outlet. In addition, with the increase of the average temperature of the cold fluid, the switching time also increases from 180 to 220 s. Heat recovery efficiency decreases from 64.3% to 41.5%. From the perspective of heat utilization, maintaining the cold fluid temperature between 200 and 300 °C is reasonable and heat recovery efficiency is about 40–50% in this range.

Reaction conditions: methane content 1.0 vol %, flow rate 10,000 m3/h, average temperatures in the exchanger from 300 to 600 K, and T sw of 400 °C.

Effect of Methane Concentration

Reaction performance was investigated under methane concentrations of 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2 vol % and the flow rate of 10,000 m3/h. The maximum bed temperature and the fluctuation of the maximum bed temperature increase with the increase of methane concentration (Figure a). When the methane concentration is 0.6 vol %, the maximum temperature fluctuation is about 100 °C, while when the methane concentration is 1.2 vol %, the maximum temperature fluctuation reaches 250 °C. For the heat recovery with heat exchangers embedded at both ends, the reaction occurs in the high-temperature area between the heat exchangers, which caused the uneven exothermic reaction. In addition, the switching time increased from 130 to 220 s, and the heat recovery efficiency decreased from 60.2% to 54.5% (Figure c,d), which is caused by the fixed temperature of the cold material. For different concentrations, heat recovery scheme should also be adjusted to maximize the heat recovery efficiency.

Reaction conditions: methane content from 0.6 to 1.2 vol %, flow rate from 10,000 m3/h, average temperatures of the cooling water in the exchanger are 200 °C, and T sw of 400 °C.

Effect of the VAM Flow Rate

Flow rate fluctuation is another source of instability in the VAM reduction and utilization. Heat recovery performance was investigated with the flow rate in the range of 6000–18,000 m3/h and a methane concentration of 1.0 vol %. The maximum bed temperature increases with the increase of the flow rate (Figure a). Comparing with Figure a, it is clear that methane concentration is the key factor affecting the maximum temperature fluctuation. When the flow rate increased from 6000 to 18,000 m3/h, the switching time decreased from 300 to 120 s, preventing the temperature profile to move out of the reactor. Heat recovery efficiency decreased linearly from 59% to 53.5%. The reason is that more heat needs to be extracted under high flow rate. Obviously, for varying flow rates, heat recovery schemes should also be adjusted.

Reaction conditions: methane content 1.0%, flow rate from 6000 to 18,000 m3/h, average temperatures of the cooling water in the exchanger are 200 °C, and T sw of 400 °C.

Heat Recovery by Hot Gas Withdrawal

Effect of the Amount of Hot Gas Extracted Out of the Reactor

First, the effect of the amount of hot gas withdrawn on the reaction performance was investigated. Extracting hot gas will not directly reduce the temperature of the adjacent bed but will reduce the flow rate through the downstream bed, leading to the accumulation of heat toward the center of the reactor. This is reflected in the bed temperature distribution as a narrowing of the temperature peak. To prevent the temperature peak from moving out of the center of the reactor during hot gas extraction, it is necessary to shorten the reversing time. Therefore, the temperature at the measurement point is much lower than that of the embedded heat exchangers. To adapt to this condition, we reduced T sw from 600 to 100 °C.

Withdrawing part of hot gas does not reduce the maximum bed temperature but has a great impact on the thermal distribution (Figure a,b). The temperature profile narrows to the center of the reactor, and when the hot gas export is 12%, the temperature profile is about 40% of that without export, indicating that the heat is concentrated in the small area of the reaction center. Switching time decreases from 260 to 65 s, which helps to maintain heat near the center of the reactor, and avoids the outflow of unreacted methane with the exported hot gas. Heat recovery efficiency increases linearly to 70%. When the amount of hot gas withdrawal exceeds 12%, the reaction will be extinguished due to excessive heat export.

Reaction conditions: methane content 1.0%, flow rate of 10,000 m3/h, and T sw of 100 °C.

Effect of Methane Concentration

The effect of the methane concentration on the reaction performance was investigated. When the fraction of hot gas withdrawal is set to 10% with a flow rate of 10,000 m3/h, the minimum methane concentration that can maintain the autothermal operation is 0.6 vol %. Changing methane concentration has little effect on the maximum temperature in the studied range, while the temperature profiles widen with the increase of the concentration (Figure a). The switching time increased from 32 to 100 s, and the heat recovery efficiency decreased from 78.1% to 45.2%. Like that in the built-in heat exchanger, this shows that more heat can be utilized under higher methane concentration.

Reaction conditions: flow rate of 10,000 m3/h, hot gas withdrawal fraction of 10%, and T sw of 100 °C.

Effect of the VAM Flow Rate

When the flow rate increases from 6000 to 18,000 m3/h, the maximum temperature of the bed rises from 1250 to 1500 °C (Figure a). Obviously, as the flow rate increases, more heat released is not fully removed from the reactor, resulting in an increase in the temperature (Figure b). The switching time decreased from 200 to 18 s, which is too short to be used practically. Therefore, when recovering heat by hot gas withdrawal, the maximum VAM flow rate in this reactor does not exceed 10,000 m3/h. The heat recovery efficiency increased from 48.5% to 58.2%, which results from the higher gas temperature.

Comparison between Central Cooling and Hot Gas Withdrawal

According to the previous simulation results, the two heat recovery schemes are compared from the aspects of permissible VAM flow rate, methane concentration, methane conversion, heat recovery efficiency, and bed temperature fluctuation. The permissible VAM flow rate with central cooling can reach 2000–18,000 m3/h, while that for hot gas withdrawal is 2000–10,000 m3/h due to the short switching time at large flow rate (Figure a). Autothermal operation can be sustained with methane concentration in the range between 0.4 and 1.2 vol % for central cooling (considering comparisons under the same conditions, the data for central cooling under 0.4 vol % is only presented in Figure b) and that for hot gas withdrawal is 0.6–1.2 vol %; meanwhile, hot gas withdrawal has higher heat recovery efficiency in 0.6–1.0 vol % (Figure b).

Reaction performances comparison between central cooling and hot gas withdrawal: (a) permissible VAM flow rate; (b) permissible methane concentration; (c) methane conversion; and (d) bed temperature at the center of the reactor in a switching period.

The heat released by the reaction can be divided into two parts. One is used to maintain self-heating, while the remaining can be recovered. The amount of heat used to maintain self-heating is considered to be a fixed value. The amount of recoverable heat is related to the methane concentration. The higher the methane concentration, the greater the recoverable heat. Therefore, as the methane concentration increases, the heat recovery efficiency will gradually increase. Theoretically, heat recovery efficiency is independent of the heat recovery scheme. Therefore, whether it is central cooling or hot gas withdrawal, under rational conditions, the recovery efficiency should be consistent. However, the amount of hot air exported and the temperature of the cold material are an empirical trial-and-error process. Therefore, the heat recovery efficiency is not the most important difference between these two heat recovery schemes.

In all of the studied cases, methane can be completely converted. However, due to the long switching time of the central cooling, less methane is discharged from the reactor outlet within the same time interval, so the overall methane conversion is higher (Figure c). Temperature fluctuation in a switching cycle at the center of the bed is 520 °C for hot gas withdrawal, while that for central cooling is 280 °C (Figure d). Smaller temperature fluctuation is conducive to reducing the thermal shock to the bed and slowing the pulverization. In summary, central cooling with two heat exchangers seems a more favorable heat recovery method because of its larger permissible flow rate, wider methane concentration range, higher methane conversion, and smaller temperature fluctuation. Meanwhile, the heat recovery scheme with central cooling also needs to be further optimized to adapt to changes in reaction conditions.

Special emphasis should be placed on this work, which compares the differences between two heat recovery methods from the perspectives of heat recovery efficiency, reversing time, and stability within the same concentration and flow rate range. Due to the inherent differences between the two heat recovery methods, there are some aspects that cannot be completely consistent. For example, the reversal time cannot be completely consistent. Therefore, this conclusion also has certain limitations. Operating costs have not been considered. Obviously, the hot gas withdrawal method does not require major modifications to the reactor, making the installation and operation simpler. Therefore, this work may have some inapplicable aspects in practical applications.

Conclusion

Two heat recovery methods for VAM utilization in a thermal flow reversal reactor, namely, central cooling and hot gas withdrawal, were compared numerically. A detailed one-dimensional heterogeneous model was adopted, and Matlab and Comsol were combined to accomplish the simulation of the flow reversal reactor, control scheme, and heat recovery strategy. Simulation results show that a dynamic switching scheme that reverses flow direction when the temperature of the selected point is lower than the set value can automatically adjust the switching time and enable the reactor to adapt to complex concentration and flow fluctuations. In the studied cases, the permissible VAM flow rate and methane concentration with central cooling are 2000–18,000 m3/h and 0.4–1.2 vol % with heat recovery efficiency around 55%, while those for hot gas withdrawal are 2000–10,000 m3/h and 0.6–1.2 vol % with a heat recovery efficiency in the range of 46–76%, and it shows higher heat recovery efficiency in 0.6–1.0 vol %. Theoretically, heat recovery efficiency is independent of the heat recovery scheme. Due to the long switching time of the central cooling, less methane is discharged from the reactor outlet within the same time interval, so the overall methane conversion is higher. Bed temperature fluctuation is 520 °C for hot gas withdrawal and 280 °C for central cooling, which means that central cooling can reduce the thermal shock and slow down the pulverization of the filler. Thus, central cooling with two heat exchangers embedded seems a more favorable heat recovery scheme because of its larger permissible flow rate, wider methane concentration range, higher methane conversion, and less temperature fluctuation. It is worth mentioning that the operating costs have not been considered in this work; thus, more work still needs to be considered.
