Final Technical Report:

Unraveling the mechanisms of below- and aboveground liana-tree competition in tropical
 forests

INVESTIGATORS
PI: David Medvigy, University of Notre Dame
Co-PI: Jennifer Powers, University of Minnesota
Co-PI: Peter Tiffin, University of Minnesota

OBJECTIVES
Our project addressed liana-tree competition in tropical dry forests. Across tropical forests,
lianas can constitute up to 25% of aboveground stems, 25% of the woody plant biodiversity, and
10% of the leaf area (Gentry 1991). Yet, mechanistic models of forest dynamics have rarely had
any explicit representation of lianas (Verbeeck et al. 2024). Thus, our overarching goal was to
develop a robust representation of lianas into a forest dynamics model. We had three specific
objectives:
 ●​ Measure belowground and aboveground properties of co‐occurring trees and lianas,
 including above‐ and belowground allocation, rooting depth, fine and coarse root
 biomass, vertical root distribution and function, physiological and hydraulic traits, liana
 crown coverage, and below‐ and aboveground net primary production
 ●​ Incorporate lianas into a mechanistic, individual‐based forest dynamics model. The
 model would ultimately include processes related to liana-tree competition, liana
 colonization of trees, and the shedding of lianas by trees.
 ●​ Convene a working group to plan for the incorporation of lianas into Earth system
 models.

We have made much progress toward meeting these objectives, as we detail below. In
keeping with the DOE MODEX philosophy, many of our papers include both empirical and
modeling components.

RESULTS
Our research included several methodological approaches, including (a) meta-analysis, (b)
modeling, and (c) field work. We used these methodological approaches to organize our results
below.

(a) Meta-analysis
We set out to answer how lianas differed from trees in terms of their physiological traits (Willson
et al. 2022). To this end, we leveraged the increase in liana research over the past two decades
to compile a pantropical database of liana traits. We found that sapwood-specific hydraulic
conductivity (Ks,max ), a plant hydraulic trait describing the maximum amount of water passing
through the xylem and strongly related to C sequestration via leaf-level gas exchange, is
significantly higher on average among lianas than trees.

To identify systematic differences in functional traits between lianas and trees, we compiled a
pantropical database of functional traits from the TRY plant trait database (Kattge et al. 2020).
We selected traits to include multiple plant organs (i.e. leaves, stems, roots, and hydraulic
architecture), represent tradeoffs in allocation and life history strategy (e.g. high specific leaf
area is often correlated with low leaf lifespan), and correspond with standard functional traits in
global vegetation models. According to the dataset that we compiled (Willson and Medvigy
2024), containing 846 liana species and over 12,000 tree species, the most striking differences
between trees and lianas existed in hydraulic traits.

We then carried out a more comprehensive analysis of hydraulic functional traits between
tropical trees and lianas. In this extended meta-analysis, on average, liana K s,max was over
three times greater than tree Ks,max (Fig. 1). Meanwhile, the pressure at which 50% xylem
function is lost (representing hydraulic safety, P 50 ) and the slope of the percent loss of
conductivity curve (representing the sensitivity of the xylem to changing pressure, Slope) were
not statistically or physiologically different.

Tropical ecologists have regarded lianas as having more acquisitive traits (i.e., traits yielding a
quick return on resource investment) than trees (Medina-Vega et al. 2021). By contrast, our
results indicate that lianas are not systematically more acquisitive than trees across plant
organs. One caveat is that relatively few observations of root traits for both tree and liana growth
forms are available. Beyond this project, we suggest prioritizing root trait measurements to
better characterize the differences between trees and lianas.

Our results are unique in three ways. First, we find that differences in hydraulic traits are not
accompanied by differences in root traits. Second, our analysis represents the most
comprehensive pantropical meta-analysis of liana hydraulic traits, demonstrating the
pervasiveness of differences in hydraulic traits between growth forms. Third, our results were
performed on a database of liana hydraulic traits that was compiled with an explicit
consideration of the unique liana xylem anatomy, making the estimates of liana Ks,max and the
difference between growth forms in K s,max more reliable.

(b) Modeling
We developed liana models spanning a broad range of complexity:
 ●​ A relatively simple, custom-built model of a single liana-tree pair.
 ●​ A forest dynamics model that permitted processes such as liana leaf growth and leaf
 turnover, but not whole-organism mortality or recruitment of new individuals.
 ●​ A forest dynamics model that included mortality and recruitment, as well as liana-specific
 processes like colonization, and shedding. At this stage, we considered lianas to be fully
 implemented into the model.
 ●​ As a final step, we also simulated liana-tree competition using a completely different
 model with a simplified representation of forest canopy dynamics.

(i) Simplified model of a liana-tree pair (Willson et al. 2022): Our first approach was to consider a
single tree colonized by a single liana. Our main intention was to follow up our meta-analysis
with a modeling evaluation of how Ks,max influences liana viability. The processes included by
this model included photosynthesis, respiration, stomatal conductance, calculation of net
primary production, and, importantly, water transport. Extensive sensitivity analysis was
conducted. We found that whole-plant hydraulic conductivity had to exceed a minimum
threshold in order for net primary production to be greater than zero. This result held for both
trees and lianas. However, this threshold was much larger for lianas than for trees, suggesting
that viable lianas would have larger hydraulic conductivity than trees. Thus, our modeling results
exactly supported the results of our meta-analysis.

We also used this model to examine the implications of a drying hydroclimate for liana viability
(Fig. 2). We found that, especially as vapor pressure deficit decreased, both lianas and trees
required larger whole-plant hydraulic conductivities to maintain positive net primary production.
However, the change required for lianas was much larger than the change required for trees. As
a result, a changing hydroclimate may lead to disproportionate liana death.

(ii) Incorporation of lianas into a forest dynamics model
We incorporated lianas into the TROLL model (Maréchaux and Chave, 2017). TROLL is a trait-
and individual-based forest growth simulator that operates at 1 m³ voxel resolution. TROLL
simulates full tree life cycles including recruitment, growth, seed production, and mortality.
Photosynthetic assimilation is calculated at half-hourly intervals, with monthly timesteps for
carbon gain. For this analysis, we disabled mortality and focused on canopy leaf dynamics of a
single liana-tree pair over 10 years. To our knowledge, no other model has examined liana-tree
competition at this spatial scale.

We investigated the following questions: (1) Which structural and physiological parameters of
trees and lianas, such as initial liana leaf area, voxel-scale constraints, liana shedding rate, and
host tree size, most strongly influence liana survival and colonization success under varying
light environments? (2) How do fast-growing versus slow-growing tree species affect liana
viability across all field-measured species? (3) Which liana colonization strategy, such as
prioritizing leaf production in the upper versus lower canopy leads to the highest colonization
success?

Global sensitivity analysis showed that sunlight was consistently the most influential parameter
across species, exhibiting the largest first-order Sobol’ indices (Si), followed by Initial_LA and
DBH (Fig. 3). In contrast, LADmax and Shed_median showed near-zero first-order effects
across species. Total-order indices (Ti) were substantially larger than Si for most parameters
excluding Sunlight, indicating that interaction between two parameters and higher-order effects
contribute meaningfully to variance beyond the parameters themselves. Across the seven liana
species, modeled viability varied widely, ranging from 12.0% (Forsteronia spicata) to 43.0%
(Hiraea reclinata). Higher-viability species tended to have higher leaf mass per area (LMA), with
the two most viable species (Hiraea reclinata and Mimosa tenuiflora) showing both high viability
(>40%) and relatively high LMA (72–85 g m⁻²), suggesting a trait–performance relationship.

This study is unique in that it focused on fine-scale liana–tree interactions by quantifying how
liana colonization influences modeled host tree viability at the individual level. Although these
simulations represent a single liana–tree pair and cannot be directly compared to forest-scale
NPP, they provide a mechanistic foundation for understanding how pair-level competition may
scale up to shape forest productivity over time. This analysis also demonstrates the value of
global sensitivity analysis for evaluating complex parameter dependencies in TROLL. While
some parameters exhibited limited first-order influence, Sobol’-based GSA showed that their
effects can emerge through interactions with sunlight, initial leaf area, and host DBH
relationships that traditional one-at-a-time approaches would not capture. Together, these
results highlight a practical framework for identifying key drivers, visualizing interaction structure,
and refining TROLL’s representation of liana–tree competition dynamics.

(iii) We next activated the full set of processes in the TROLL model, including growth and
mortality. We carried out additional sensitivity analyses of this full model. We then applied the
model to the important question of how liana-tree competition will be affected by climate change.
Our research questions included: (1) How does tree and liana NPP change with rising VPD
when the model uses monthly vs. hourly climate forcing? (2) Do small lianas show greater
sensitivity to temporal resolution than large lianas in terms of required hydraulic conductivity with
rising VPD? (3) Under projected VPD scenarios from rising CO2 conditions (up to 400 ppm
increase from baseline ~420 ppm), how do lianas and trees respond differently?

We found that Liana NPP was consistently underestimated when short-term fluctuations of VPD
were neglected (Fig. 4). The difference was most pronounced during the transition seasons.
Across the 12-month simulation period, mean NPP calculated with monthly VPD was 1.525

µmol/m²/month, whereas using daily VPD yielded a mean of 1.727 µmol/m²/month, representing
an approximate 13% increase in productivity when short-term atmospheric variability was
captured. To identify the hydraulic capacity required for positive carbon gain under different
temporal resolutions of environmental forcing, we estimated the minimum liana stem hydraulic
conductivity (K) at which simulated NPP remained greater than zero. The required K values
decreased consistently as temporal resolution increased from monthly to hourly meteorology,
indicating that higher-frequency forcing enables lianas to maintain positive productivity under
lower hydraulic efficiency. For example, a liana with a diameter of 1.86 cm required a
conductivity of 53,118 mmol m⁻¹ s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹ when driven by monthly inputs, compared to 44,100
mmol m⁻¹ s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹ when driven by hourly inputs. This pattern was consistent across all size
classes, suggesting that simulations using coarse temporal averaging may underestimate the
hydraulic efficiency necessary for liana to maintain positive NPP. These results highlighted that
short-term meteorological fluctuations are important to determining liana viability and
productivity.

Figure 4: Comparison of simulated liana NPP under monthly versus daily VPD
inputs. Each point represents mean NPP for a given month. Simulations using
daily -mean VPD (green) capture short-term atmospheric variability and result in
higher NPP values compared to simulations using monthly-mean VPD (black).

(iv) We have also collaborated with other modeling groups. In Meunier et al. (2021), we
evaluated the uncertainty of water and light capture processes of a process-based vegetation
model (ED2) including the liana growth form. We further developed the liana plant functional
type in ED2 to mechanistically simulate water uptake and transport from roots to leaves, and
start the model from prescribed initial conditions. We then used the PEcAn bioinformatics
platform to constrain liana parameters and run uncertainty analyses. It should be noted that ED2
and TROLL operate at different spatial scales. TROLL operates at the 1-meter scale, simulates
individuals, and explicitly resolves the competition between lianas and trees. ED2 operates at
about a 20-meter scale, simulates collections of individuals (cohorts), and the processes related
to liana colonization and competition are smoothed out over this spatial scale.

Baseline runs successfully reproduced ecosystem gas exchange fluxes (gross primary
productivity and latent heat) and forest structural features (leaf area index, aboveground
biomass) in two sites (Barro Colorado Island, Panama and Paracou, French Guiana)
characterized by different rainfall regimes and levels of liana abundance.Model uncertainty
analyses revealed that water limitation was the factor driving the competition between trees and
lianas at the drier site (BCI), and during the relatively short dry season of the wetter site
(Paracou). In young patches, light competition dominated in Paracou but alternated with water
competition between the wet and the dry season on BCI according to the model simulations.
The modelling workflow also identified key liana traits (photosynthetic quantum efficiency,
stomatal regulation parameters, allometric relationships) and processes (water use, respiration,
climbing) driving the model uncertainty. In summary, this work showed that competition for water
plays a larger role in the interaction between lianas and trees than previously hypothesized.

(c) Field work
Our field work was conducted in seasonally dry tropical forests (SDTF) of Guanacaste Province,
Costa Rica In 2008, 18 permanent research plots (0.1 ha each) were established. We annually
measured tree growth and recorded liana presence and used these longitudinal data to evaluate
changes in liana presence and the potential for lianas to affect tree growth and mortality
(Becknell et al. 2022). Specifically, we asked: (1) Did the number of trees with lianas change
over the 11 years of observations in these plots? (2) Did the degree of liana crown presence
change over time? (3) Did liana presence affect tree growth? (4) Did liana presence impact tree
mortality? While these questions are of intrinsic interest, answering them was also critical for the
development of forest dynamics models.

Our data included annual growth measurements and liana observations on 1,745 unique stems
with DBHs from 7 to 96 cm, representing 102 tree species from 86 genera. Averaged across all
years, we found that 49% of trees in our study had no lianas, 16% of trees had lianas that
occupied less than 10% of the tree’s crown, 18% of trees had lianas which occupied 10–50% of
the tree’s crown, and 17% of trees had lianas which occupied more than 50% of a tree’s crown.
The number of trees with no lianas declined over time from 52% in 2010 to 45% in 2017. The
number of trees with lianas occupying more than 10% of the tree’s crown increased over time
from 21% in 2010 to 41% in 2020.

In our analysis of study-long average relative growth rates, we found that trees with liana index
of 0 or 1 (no liana, or liana present in less than 10% of the tree’s crown) had higher mean
relative growth rates than trees with liana index of 2 or more (i.e., lianas present in more than
10% of the crown). Thus, lianas negatively impacted the growth of trees in this forest. This result
is significant because it represents one of a small number of studies on liana increase and
growth effects in a seasonally dry forest. This result is also unique because it provides annually
resolved records of the effects of liana index on growth in each year of our study. A further novel
result was that we found an interaction between liana presence index and annual rainfall; i.e.,
the liana-related reductions in annual growth rates were similar among liana presence classes

in years with low rainfall, but varied more among liana presence classes during years of high
rainfall (Becknell et al, 2022).

We investigated the frequency at which colonized trees shed their lianas. We found that 39 of
490 stems (8% of stems starting with high liana values) showed evidence of liana shedding over
the length of our study, suggesting a liana shedding rate of 0.7% of trees shedding lianas per
year. Understanding the degree to which host trees can shed lianas is an important parameter
for efforts to incorporate lianas into simulation models.

Last, our modeling work identified hydraulic conductivity as one of the key traits that differentiate
lianas from trees and control liana viability under climate change scenarios. To understand how
this important trait varies within and among species, we sampled it for five liana species that
co-occur in wet and dry tropical forests. We sampled key hydraulic and morphological traits for
replicated individuals in both sites, and analyzed the data by grouping species into those that
use active (e.g., tendrils, twining) or passive (e.g., scrambling) mechanisms to ascend to the
tree canopy (Figure 5). We found little evidence for differences in hydraulic conductivity between
species growing in wet or dry habits if they had passive climbing mechanisms. By contrast,
species that use active climbing mechanisms to ascend to the canopy show strong
differentiation in hydraulic conductivity between populations from wet and dry climates. These
data provide strong evidence that climbing mechanism is an important trait that may be useful
for developing multiple liana plant functional types in models and that phenotypic plasticity is
one way that lianas adjust to different climate regimes (Perez-Enriquez et al, in prep)

species are grouped by climbing mechanism with boxes representing three species that use
active climbing mechanisms and to that use passive climbing mechanisms.

In summary, this work provides three key results: (1) lianas are increasing in abundance in
secondary SDTFs we studied, and (2) these lianas have clear, negative effects on the growth of
trees, (3) lianas with active climbing mechanisms show more phenotypic plasticity in response
to climatic conditions that those with passive climbing mechanisms. However, we did not find a
statistically significant effect of lianas on tree mortality. This work demonstrates liana increases
in secondary SDTF and adds support to other findings that lianas negatively impact tree growth
in this forest type. Our observed liana increases may be explained by a combination of
successional patterns, forest structural patterns, and tree mortality disturbance relating to the
drought in the middle of our study. However, more work is needed to confirm this hypothesis and
uncover the mechanisms behind liana impacts on growth, and whether these relationships are
tree and liana-species specific. Our modeling work was able to suggest further hypotheses
toward those ends.
