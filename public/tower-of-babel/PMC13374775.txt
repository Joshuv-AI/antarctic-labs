Rucaparib – drug evaluation: a PARP inhibitor for the treatment of BRCA-mutated metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer
Authors: Alan Haruo Bryce, Paige Nash, Anne Roessler, Divneet Mandair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRucaparib (RUBRACAⓇ) is the first FDA-approved poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor for men with BRCA mutation-associated metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC). Rucaparib’s efficacy in patients with mCRPC associated with DNA damage repair (DDR) deficiency was demonstrated in the TRITON2 and TRITON3 trials. In TRITON2, rucaparib demonstrated meaningful antitumor activity in men with measurable BRCA-mutated disease, with objective responses observed across both germline and somatic mutations. In TRITON3, rucaparib improved radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS) over physician’s choice of therapy (docetaxel or a second-generation androgen-receptor pathway inhibitor [ARPI]). Rucaparib is the only drug to have shown improved rPFS versus docetaxel in mCRPC. Rucaparib demonstrated a consistent and manageable safety profile similar to other PARP inhibitors, with the most common adverse events being fatigue/asthenia, musculoskeletal pain, nausea, and anemia/decreased hemoglobin. Men with mCRPC face prolonged and complex treatments, during which maintaining quality of life, minimizing toxicity, and preserving functional status are major priorities. For patients with BRCA mutations, rucaparib offers a targeted oral alternative to chemotherapy that can delay mCRPC progression while reducing many of the logistical, physical, and clinical burdens commonly associated with chemotherapy. Its oral administration and flexible dosing enable an individualized approach that reduces treatment interruptions and maximizes clinical benefits.KEYWORDS: mCRPC, PARP inhibitors, BRCA mutations, TRITON2 trial, TRITON3 trial, progression-free survival, long-term safety

Introduction/overview of the field

Deleterious BRCA mutations are found in approximately 12% of men with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) (BRCA1, 2%; BRCA2, 10%) [1–3]. Compared with non-BRCA prostate cancer (PCa), BRCA-mutated PCa is known to be more aggressive (e.g., higher risk of nodal involvement and distant metastasis), with poorer prognosis, worse survival, and faster progression on androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) [4–7]. In a targeted screening for PCa in men with germline BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations, the cancers diagnosed were predominantly intermediate and high-risk disease [8]. Therefore, BRCA screenings are crucial to identifying appropriate patients that could benefit from targeted therapies such as poly (adenosine diphosphate [ADP]-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors [9,10].

Along with PARP inhibitors, treatment options for advanced PCa include monotherapy with ADT and treatment combinations with taxane-based chemotherapy, androgen-receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs), or radiopharmaceuticals [9,11]. However, a persistent challenge in PCa treatment is the limited duration of clinical and survival benefit due to the emergence of primary and acquired treatment resistance.

Recent research has provided insight into the molecular landscape of advanced PCa and the mechanisms behind treatment resistance [12–14]. Of note, mutations in genes involved in the deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage repair (DDR) pathway have been reported in patients with advanced PCa [1–3]. This finding provides a unique opportunity to develop therapeutic strategies that leverage a decreased ability of the cancer cells to repair DNA damage [12,15].

PARP inhibitors have demonstrated efficacy in cancer cells harboring BRCA1/2 mutations in mCRPC and more recently, in DDR-deficient cancer cells in hormone-sensitive PCa (mHSPC), while sparing non-mutated healthy cells [16,17]. Rucaparib (RUBRACAⓇ) is the first FDA-approved PARP inhibitor for men with mCRPC through the accelerated approval pathway, after being granted Breakthrough Therapy designation [18,19]. In December 2025, rucaparib received full FDA-approval for the treatment of adult patients with deleterious BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations (germline and/or somatic)-associated mCRPC, who have been previously treated with an ARPI [19]. It is also approved in the United States for the maintenance treatment of patients with deleterious BRCA mutation-associated recurrent ovarian cancer [19]. In Europe, it is approved for the first-line maintenance treatment of advanced ovarian cancer and maintenance treatment of platinum-sensitive relapsed ovarian cancer [20]. In this review, we will discuss the relevant background, pharmacology, clinical efficacy, and safety of rucaparib for the treatment of mCRPC.

Introduction to the compound

Rucaparib (Figure 1) is a potent, small-molecule inhibitor of PARP enzymes that has been shown to be active against tumor cells that have an impaired ability to repair DNA damage [21–24]. Through a mechanism known as synthetic lethality, rucaparib induces cytotoxicity in cancer cells via enzymatic inhibition of PARP proteins in the presence of defects in the DDR pathway (e.g., mutations in BRCA1 or BRCA2) (Figure 2) [23–25]. In vitro studies indicate that rucaparib-induced cytotoxicity may involve increased formation of PARP-DNA complexes (specifically via stabilization of PARP1, PARP2, and PARP3, and to a lesser extent other PARP isoforms) resulting in DNA damage, apoptosis, and cancer cell death [19,21]. In tumor cell lines with BRCA1/2 and other DNA repair gene deficiencies, rucaparib demonstrated increased cytotoxicity and anti-tumor activity compared with tumor cell lines without such DNA repair deficiencies [19,25]. Additionally, preclinical studies have reported chemosensitization and radiosensitization effects, supporting the potential of treatment combinations [22,26].

Pharmacology

Dosage

The recommended dose of rucaparib is 600 mg (two 300 mg tablets) taken orally twice daily with or without food (a total daily dose of 1,200 mg) [19]. If needed, to manage adverse events, treatment interruptions or dose reduction(s) may be considered; recommended rucaparib dose modifications include stepwise reductions to 500 mg twice daily (two 250 mg tablets), 400 mg twice daily (two 200 mg tablets), and 300 mg twice daily (one 300 mg tablet) [19]. Patients receiving rucaparib for mCRPC should also receive a gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analog concurrently or should have had bilateral orchiectomy [19].

Pharmacokinetic profile

The pharmacokinetic (PK) profile of rucaparib monotherapy has been characterized in patients with advanced solid tumors [27].

Pharmacokinetic values of oral rucaparib were obtained from a 3-part, open-label, phase I and II study (NCT01482715) [27]. In part 1, 55 patients with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors who had progressed on standard treatment received doses from 40 mg to 500 mg once daily (QD) and 240 mg to 840 mg twice daily (BID) [27]. Single-dose and steady-state PK were evaluated on day 1 and day 15, respectively [27]. Overall, the plasma concentration of rucaparib demonstrated a dose-dependent increase following a QD or BID dosing schedule [27]. Median time to maximum plasma concentration, Tmax, was 1.5–4 hours and 1.5–6 hours for the QD and BID dosing schedules, respectively, which suggests relatively fast absorption [27]. Steady-state was achieved after 8 days for both the QD and BID dosing schedules [27]. The steady-state accumulation ratio for area under concentration-time curve (AUC) ranged from 1.60–2.33 and 1.47–5.44 following QD and BID dosing schedules, respectively [27].

Steady-state exposure at the recommended dose level of 600 mg rucaparib BID using the commercial tablet formulation was evaluated in 18 patients in part 3 of the study [27]. Arithmetic mean (coefficient of variation %) for steady-state Cmax (Cmax,ss) and AUC0-tau were 1940 ng/mL (54%) and 15,800 ng·h/mL (58%), respectively [27].

Absorption and distribution

Absorption and distribution data for rucaparib were obtained from a separate phase I study (NCT01009190), which enrolled 85 patients with advanced solid tumors [28]. Rucaparib demonstrated a dose-independent mean oral bioavailability of 36% (range 30–45%) based on the ratio of dose-normalized AUC [19,28]. Following administration with a high-fat meal, rucaparib exposure was moderately increased, with a ~ 20% increase in Cmax, a ~ 38% increase in AUC0-24 h, and a delay in Tmax of approximately 2.5 hours compared with fasted conditions [19,27]. However, these changes are not considered clinically significant, and rucaparib may be administered with or without food [27]. Following a single intravenous dose of rucaparib 12–40 mg, rucaparib had a steady-state volume of distribution of 113–262 L [19,28].

Metabolism and excretion

Metabolism and excretion data were obtained from an analysis of PK data from several studies including NCT01009190, NCT01482715, and NCT01891344. At the approved rucaparib 600 mg BID dosage, apparent rucaparib clearance at steady state is 44.2 L/h, and the half-life is 26 hours [19,27,29]. In a population PK study of rucaparib 600 mg BID, no apparent difference in rucaparib PK was observed among patients with different phenotypes of CYP1A2 (normal n = 28; hyperinducer n = 136) and CYP2D6 (poor metabolizer n = 9; normal metabolizer n = 76; intermediate metabolizer n = 71; ultra-rapid metabolizer n = 4) [30]. For patients with mild to moderate renal impairment (creatinine clearance 30–89 mL/min, as estimated by the Cockcroft-Gault method), no dosage modification is recommended [19]. Rucaparib has not been studied in patients with creatinine clearance < 30 mL/min or patients on dialysis [19].

Drug-drug interaction

In vitro studies indicated that the risk of drug-drug interactions impacting rucaparib exposure was low [31]. However, there is potential for drug-drug interactions impacting substrate exposure with rucaparib. If rucaparib is administered concomitantly with certain CYP1A2, CYP3A, CYP2C9, or CYP2C19 substrates, the exposure of the substrate increases [32], which may lead to increased risk of adverse events [19]. To reduce this potential risk, decrease the substrate dose as indicated in its approved prescribing information [19].

Clinical efficacy

Rucaparib’s efficacy in men with mCRPC associated with DDR deficiency was evaluated in the TRITON2 (phase II) and TRITON3 (phase III) clinical trials (Table 1).

TRITON2 (NCT02952534)

Rucaparib received accelerated FDA approval in 2020 based on its meaningful objective response rate (ORR) in patients with measurable disease, the primary endpoint of the TRITON2 clinical trial [18,19]. TRITON2 was an international, open-label, single-arm, phase II study evaluating rucaparib in patients with mCRPC associated with DDR deficiency [33]. Eligible patients had a deleterious germline or somatic mutation in one or more genes: BRCA1, BRCA2, or another prespecified DDR gene that may confer sensitivity to PARP inhibition [33]. Enrolled patients had progressed after one to two lines of second-generation ARPI therapy and one taxane-based chemotherapy for castrate-resistant disease [33]. Patients received a starting dose of 600 mg oral rucaparib twice daily [33].

The TRITON2 study enrolled a total of 277 patients. ORR (primary endpoint), duration of response (DOR; secondary endpoint), and radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS; secondary endpoint) were assessed by independent radiology review (IRR) in 133 evaluable patients. The other secondary endpoints of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) response, clinical benefit rate (CBR), overall survival (OS), and safety were analyzed in all 277 enrolled patients [33]. In the final TRITON2 analysis, within the BRCA subgroup, 37/81 (46%) patients with IRR-evaluable disease had a confirmed objective response (Figure 3), with a median DOR by IRR of 15.5 months (95% CI 6.4-not reached [NR]) [33]. The confirmed ORR was 48% and 30% for patients with a BRCA2 and BRCA1 mutation, respectively (Figure 3) [33]. The ORR was 45% for patients with a germline BRCA mutation and 46% for those with a somatic mutation, respectively [33]. The ORR was similar in patients with or without co-occurring TP53, PTEN, or RB1 mutations [33], although ORR was numerically lower for patients with co-occurring BRCA and RB1 mutations (23%; 95% CI 5.0–54, Figure 3) [33]. In an interim analysis of the TRITON2 BRCA-mutated population, which included 115 patients at the time of data cutoff, the ORR among patients with and without homozygous BRCA loss was 70% (95% CI 34.8–93.3%) and 40% (95% CI 12.2–73.8%), respectively [34].

In the final TRITON2 analysis, a PSA50 response occurred in 92 of 172 patients (53%; 95% CI 46–61%) in the BRCA subgroup (Figure 3), with a median time to PSA progression of 6.5 months (95% CI 5.7–7.5) [33]. PSA50 response rates were 59% and 18% for those with a BRCA2 and BRCA1 mutation, respectively (Figure 3) [33]. Of patients with germline and somatic BRCA mutations, 59% (95% CI 46–70%) and 49% (95% CI 39–60%), respectively, had a PSA50 response [33]. There was no difference in PSA50 response rates among patients with co-occurring mutations in TP53, RB1, or PTEN [33]. Additional outcomes in the BRCA subgroup included a median rPFS of 10.7 months (95% CI 8.7–13), median OS of 17.2 months (95% CI 15–20), and a CBR of 58% at 6 months [33].

There were 4 patients with measurable disease by IRR and a PALB2 mutation, all of whom had a confirmed objective response (100%; 95% CI 40–100), with a median duration of objective response (DOR) of 10.1 months (95% CI NR-NR) [33]. Six of 11 patients (55%; 95% CI 23–83) with a PALB2 mutation had a PSA50 response, with a median time to PSA progression of 7.5 months (95% CI 4.7–12) [33]. Additional outcomes in the PALB2 subgroup included a median rPFS of 13.6 months (95% CI 7.1–25), median OS of 17.7 months (95% CI 7.1-NR), and a CBR of 82% at 6 months [33].

No objective responses by IRR were observed in the ATM, CDK12, or CHEK2 subgroups [33]. Among patients with IRR-evaluable disease in the Other DDR gene subgroup, defined as patients with gene mutations in BARD1, FANCA, NBN, RAD51, RAD51B, or BRIP1, 3 of 12 patients (25%; 95% CI 5.5–57%) had a confirmed objective response [33]. These responders had mutations in FANCA, RAD51B, and BRIP1; however, the patient with a BRIP1 mutation was later found to have a co-occurring homozygous BRCA2 deletion at enrollment [33].

PSA responses were limited across the non-BRCA groups. A PSA50 response occurred in 2 of 59 patients (3.4%; 95% CI 0.4–12%) in the ATM subgroup, with a median time to PSA progression of 3.1 months (95% CI 2.8–3.7) [33]. A PSA50 response also occurred in 1 of 15 patients (6.7%; 95% CI 0.2–32%) in the CDK12 subgroup, 1 of 7 patients (14%; 95% CI 0.4–58%) in the CHEK2 subgroup, and 3 of 13 patients (23%; 95% CI 5.0–54%) in the Other DDR gene subgroup [33].

Across the ATM, CDK12, CHEK2, and Other DDR gene subgroups, median rPFS by IRR ranged from 5.3 to 5.8 months, median OS ranged from 10.5 to 14.6 months, and CBR ranged from 13% to 31% at 6 months and from 0% to 23% at 12 months [33].

The study population of TRITON2 consisted largely of patients with measurable disease burden; measurable visceral disease was observed in patients harboring BRCA (37%), non-BRCA (ATM = 43%, CDK12 = 22%, PALB2 = 50%), and Other DDR gene (17%) mutations [33]. In an interim analysis of the TRITON2 BRCA-mutated population, which included 115 patients at the time of data cutoff, those with visceral disease demonstrated an ORR of 47.6% (10/21; 95% CI 25.7–70.2%) and a PSA50 response rate of 63.6% (14/22; 95% CI 40.7–82.8%) [35].

Overall, results from the TRITON2 trial led to FDA accelerated approval of rucaparib for mCRPC in 2020 based on the ORR and DOR in patients with measurable disease [18,19]. This positioned rucaparib as the first PARP inhibitor to receive FDA approval for the treatment of mCRPC [18,19,36]. Rucaparib was shown to be effective across BRCA mutation types (e.g., germline, somatic, homozygous, and heterozygous), as loss of function enabling synthetic lethality occurs in all cases [33,34]. The presence of co-occurring mutations did not diminish rucaparib’s efficacy [33]. Additionally, TRITON2 enrolled a high proportion of patients with visceral disease [33], indicating that rucaparib is effective for the treatment of patients with measurable disease burden.

TRITON3 (NCT02975934)

In December 2025, rucaparib received FDA approval confirming its 2020 accelerated approval and expanding its indication to a broader population of patients with BRCA-mutated mCRPC, based on the significant improvement in rPFS (the primary endpoint of TRITON3) compared with physician’s choice of therapy in patients with BRCA-mutated disease (11.2 vs 6.4 months; hazard ratio [HR] 0.50; 95% CI 0.36–0.69; p < 0.001) [37]. TRITON3 was an international randomized, controlled, open-label, phase III trial evaluating rucaparib in patients with mCRPC and a BRCA1, BRCA2, or ATM mutation [38]. All patients had a history of disease progression after treatment with one previous second-generation ARPI (abiraterone acetate, enzalutamide, apalutamide, or an investigational agent) [38]. Unlike patients in TRITON2, patients in TRITON3 had not been treated with chemotherapy for castration-resistant disease [38]. Previous taxane-based chemotherapy for castration-sensitive disease was permitted [33,38]. Patients were randomly assigned in a 2:1 ratio to receive oral rucaparib (600 mg twice daily) or a physician’s choice of therapy (docetaxel or a second-generation ARPI [abiraterone acetate or enzalutamide]) [38]. Prior to randomization, investigators chose the next choice of therapy (docetaxel, abiraterone acetate, or enzalutamide) for each patient [38]. Patients randomized to the physician’s choice arm were given the opportunity to cross over to the rucaparib arm after confirmation of disease progression [38]. The primary efficacy outcome was rPFS, and key secondary outcomes included OS and objective response according to independent radiology review [38].

In TRITON3, 270 patients were assigned to the rucaparib arm and 135 were assigned to the physician’s choice arm (intention-to-treat [ITT] population) [38]. Among the patients in the rucaparib arm, 201 had a BRCA mutation (BRCA2 n = 172; BRCA1 n = 29) [38]. In the physician’s choice arm, 101 patients had a BRCA mutation (BRCA2 n = 86; BRCA1 n = 15) [38]. The demographics of patients enrolled in TRITON3 (e.g., gender, age, genomic features) were generally an accurate representation of the real-world population at risk for PCa, except that there were fewer men of African descent compared to the general population with advanced PCa [38].

At 62 months, the median duration of rPFS was significantly longer in the rucaparib arm than in the physician’s choice arm, both in the BRCA subgroup (11.2 vs 6.4 months; HR 0.50; 95% CI 0.36–0.69; p < 0.001) (Figure 4(a)) and the ITT population (10.2 vs 6.4 months; HR 0.61; 95% CI 0.47–0.80; p < 0.001) (Figure 4(b)) [38]. Exploratory efficacy analyses compared rucaparib to each physician’s choice of therapy in the BRCA subgroup. Rucaparib significantly prolonged rPFS compared with docetaxel (11.2 vs 8.3 months; HR 0.53; 95% CI 0.37–0.77) (Figure 4(c)) and with abiraterone acetate or enzalutamide (11.2 vs 4.5 months; HR 0.38; 95% CI 0.25–0.58) (Figure 4(d)) [38]. To date, rucaparib is the only PARP inhibitor to have shown improved rPFS versus a docetaxel-containing arm in mCRPC [38].

After an overall median follow-up of 44 months, median OS in the BRCA subgroup was 23.2 months (95% CI 19.1–25.2) in the rucaparib arm and 21.2 months (95% CI 18.0–23.1) in the physician’s choice arm (HR 0.91; 95% CI 0.68–1.20; p = 0.50) [39]. Median OS in the ITT population was similar between rucaparib and physician’s choice (HR 0.99; 95% CI 0.78–1.26) [39]. The confirmed ORR in the BRCA subgroup was 45% (37/82) in the rucaparib arm and 17% (7/41) in the physician’s choice arm [38]. In the ITT population, the confirmed ORR was 35% (37/106) and 16% (9/55) for the rucaparib and physician’s choice arm, respectively [38]. In the ATM subgroup, no meaningful differences in OS and ORR were observed between treatment arms [38,39].

Additional subgroup analyses of TRITON3 demonstrated consistent benefit with rucaparib across BRCA mutation types, liver metastases status, and in patients who crossed over to rucaparib following progression on physician’s choice of therapy. In the BRCA population, rucaparib improved rPFS compared with physician’s choice in both the germline and somatic mutation groups (germline: HR 0.52; 95% CI 0.32–0.84; somatic: HR 0.38; 95% CI 0.25–0.59), with a median rPFS of 11.2 months with rucaparib in both subgroups compared with 8.4 months for those in the physician’s choice arm with germline mutations and 5.6 months for those with somatic mutations [40]. Median OS was 24.8 vs 22.7 months (rucaparib vs physician’s choice) in the germline subgroup [40]. In patients with somatic BRCA mutations, the survival difference was more pronounced, with median OS of 24.2 months for rucaparib compared with 16.3 months for physician’s choice [40].

In subgroup analyses of patients with BRCA mutations by liver metastases status, rucaparib improved median rPFS compared with physician’s choice in patients with liver metastases (4.8 vs 2.6 months; HR 0.54; 95% CI 0.23–1.25) and those without liver metastases (11.3 vs 7.1 months; HR 0.45; 95% CI 0.32–0.63) [41]. However, the subgroup with liver metastases was small, with 20 patients in the rucaparib arm and 10 patients in the physician’s choice arm [41]. Median OS was similar between treatment arms (~11 months) in patients with liver metastases; patients without liver metastases had a median OS of 24 months with rucaparib and 22 months with physician’s choice of therapy [41]. The ORR was higher with rucaparib than with physician’s choice in patients with and without liver metastases [41]. Among patients with liver metastases, partial responses occurred in 41% (7/17) of those treated with rucaparib versus 22% (2/9) receiving physician’s choice, with no complete responses observed in either arm [41]. Among patients without liver metastases, 46% (30/65) in the rucaparib arm achieved a complete or partial response compared with 16% (5/32) in the physician’s choice arm [41].

Exploratory analyses were conducted in patients with BRCA mutations who crossed over to rucaparib from physician’s choice arm after disease progression [42]. Seventy of the 101 patients with BRCA mutation in the physician’s choice arm were eligible for crossover [42]. Of the 70, 53 crossed over to rucaparib [42]. Response was assessed in 31 patients with measurable disease [42]. Among patients initially treated with docetaxel during randomization phase, ORR was similar with docetaxel and after crossover to rucaparib (35% vs 33%) [42]. Among patients initially treated with an ARPI, ORR was higher following crossover to rucaparib (9% vs 46%) [42]. The median DOR following crossover to rucaparib was 5.4 months among patients who crossed over after initial docetaxel and was not reached among those initially treated with an ARPI [42]. Together, these analyses suggest that rucaparib may remain effective in patients with BRCA-mutated mCRPC who have received at least one ARPI and progressed on another ARPI or docetaxel.

Overall, rucaparib is the only PARP inhibitor evaluated in a randomized phase III study comparing a PARP inhibitor with docetaxel [38]. Clinical benefit was primarily driven by patients with BRCA mutations, which comprised the largest proportion of enrolled patients and demonstrated clear treatment effect with rucaparib, including longer rPFS and higher objective response rates compared with the physician’s choice of therapy [38]. The trial also had a high rate of crossover from physician’s choice to rucaparib after progression, with a majority of eligible patients crossing over; OS was similar between arms, likely reflecting this crossover [39].

Safety and tolerability

Rucaparib demonstrated a consistent and manageable safety profile in patients with mCRPC across TRITON2 and TRITON3 (Tables 2 and 3) [33,38]. The most common adverse events in the pooled safety population of 373 patients with BRCA-mutated mCRPC from TRITON2 and TRITON3 included fatigue/asthenia, musculoskeletal pain, nausea, and anemia/decreased hemoglobin [19,33,38]. Fatal adverse events were rare in both trials. In TRITON2, fatal adverse events occurred in 1.7% of patients who received rucaparib, including acute respiratory distress syndrome and pneumonia (1 patient each) [19,33]. In TRITON3, fatal adverse events occurred in 1.5% of patients treated with rucaparib, including cardiac failure, myocardial ischemia, and sepsis (1 patient each) [19,38].

Dose interruption or dose reduction may be used to manage adverse events with rucaparib, consistent with the recommended dosing guidelines [19]. Rucaparib is available in multiple tablet strengths, allowing clinicians to tailor dosing to patient needs and maintain continuity of therapy over time. Thirty-eight percent of patients (106/277) in TRITON2 and 39% of patients (104/270) in TRITON3 underwent dose reduction at some point during treatment [33,38]. Having multiple tablet strengths available underscores the practicality of a treatment regimen that can be individualized and modified to allow patients to continue deriving benefits from rucaparib treatment rather than discontinuing treatment.

Discontinuation rates were generally low across both clinical studies. In TRITON2, adverse events led to rucaparib discontinuation in 12% (32/277) of patients [33]. The most common reasons for discontinuation in TRITON2 were anemia/decreased hemoglobin and fatigue/asthenia, which are known effects of PARP inhibition and can be easily managed [33]. In TRITON3, adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation were reported in 15% (40/270) of patients receiving rucaparib and 22% (28/130) of patients receiving physician’s choice (32% [23/71] with docetaxel and 8% [5/59] with ARPI) [38]. These patterns further demonstrate that, when needed, dose adjustments can be implemented to support ongoing treatment, thus the availability of multiple tablet strengths is a beneficial advantage of rucaparib in real-world clinical settings.

Current status and clinical considerations

Current status

Rucaparib is FDA-approved for the treatment of adult patients with deleterious BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations (germline and/or somatic)-associated mCRPC, who have been previously treated with an ARPI [19,37]. Rucaparib has demonstrated meaningful antitumor activity in this genetically defined subgroup, including objective responses, PSA declines, and improvements in rPFS across phase II and phase III trials [33,38]. These findings support the role of PARP inhibition as a biomarker-driven therapeutic strategy in advanced PCa.

Both the AUA/SUO and NCCN clinical practice guidelines recommend PARP inhibitors, including rucaparib, for men with BRCA1/2-mutated mCRPC who have progressed on prior systemic therapies [9,10]. Based on the results from TRITON2, the therapeutic positioning for rucaparib within the treatment sequence for patients with BRCA-mutated mCRPC was following one to two lines of ARPI therapy (e.g., enzalutamide or abiraterone acetate) and at least one taxane-based chemotherapy [33]. However, TRITON3 results support the use of rucaparib earlier in the treatment sequence, before or instead of taxane chemotherapy, which led to an expanded approval in December 2025 [34]. Germline and somatic genomic testing is emphasized to identify eligible patients and facilitate integration of targeted therapies into individualized treatment pathways [9]. Treatment selection incorporates mutation subtype, prior therapy exposure, comorbidities, and patient preferences [9].

For patients who progress while receiving rucaparib, chemotherapies and radioligand therapies remain as other approved options in addition to enrollment in clinical trials. As the therapeutic landscape continues to expand, the optimal sequencing of rucaparib with existing and emerging systemic therapies continues to evolve.

Clinical considerations

Based on safety data from the pivotal trials of rucaparib, some clinical considerations must be taken into account for clinicians considering prescribing rucaparib. The most common adverse reactions leading to treatment discontinuation in TRITON3 were anemia, fatigue/asthenia, decreased platelet count/thrombocytopenia, and nausea, all of which are common PARP inhibitor class effect toxicities [19]. Clinicians should educate patients on potential adverse effects associated with rucaparib before starting treatment, monitor and talk to patients to determine whether they are experiencing any adverse effects during treatment, and counsel patients on how to manage adverse effects. To identify anemia or thrombocytopenia, a complete blood count should be performed before starting treatment and every month during treatment [46]. Temporary interruption of treatment may be sufficient to manage grade 2 anemia [46]. For patients with grade ≥3 anemia, treatment interruption may be combined with red blood cell transfusions, depending on individual patient factors, such as symptoms, comorbidities, and personal preferences [46]. Fatigue and asthenia may be managed through lifestyle changes such as consistent and suitable exercise, as well as evaluation and treatment of other contributing factors and underlying mood disorders [46]. Dose interruption should be considered for patients with thrombocytopenia, and platelet transfusions should be limited to those with active bleeding or increased risk of spontaneous bleeding [46]. Nausea and vomiting may be managed with lifestyle adjustments (e.g., eating small frequent meals with room temperature food, taking rucaparib later in the day) or antiemetics [46]. Dose reduction or interruption may be required in more severe cases [46].

Although very rare, MDS/AML have been reported in 2 out of 201 patients with prostate cancer treated with rucaparib [19]. Treatment with rucaparib should not begin until any prior chemotherapy-related hematological toxicity (≤grade 1) has resolved [19]. Complete blood counts should be taken when rucaparib is initiated and then monthly [19]. If hematologic toxicity persists for over 4 weeks, rucaparib treatment should be paused or continued at a reduced dose, with weekly blood count monitoring until recovery [19]. If counts do not improve to ≤grade 1 after 4 weeks, or if MDS/AML is suspected, the patient should be referred to a hematologist for further investigations (e.g., bone marrow analysis and blood sample for cytogenetics) [19]. If MDS/AML is confirmed, rucaparib should be discontinued [19].

Impact for the patient

Men with mCRPC face prolonged and complex treatments, during which maintaining quality of life, minimizing toxicity, and preserving functional status are major priorities. For patients with pathogenic BRCA mutations, rucaparib offers an oral, targeted alternative to chemotherapy that avoids infusion-related logistical and physical burdens. Its oral administration and flexible dosing that allows dose reductions or temporary interruptions for management of adverse reactions provide greater day-to-day treatment control. As highlighted in the Safety section, treatment discontinuation due to adverse events occurred twice as often in the docetaxel arm than the rucaparib arm, further supporting the difference in treatment burden. In clinical trials, rucaparib significantly prolonged rPFS, a robust and established endpoint in the mCRPC setting, compared with both ARPIs (11.2 vs 4.5 months; HR 0.38; 95% CI 0.25–0.58) and docetaxel chemotherapy (11.2 vs 8.3 months; HR 0.53; 95% CI 0.37–0.77) [38].

From a patient perspective, delaying disease progression can translate into longer periods without worsening symptoms, reduced need for additional lines of therapy, and a greater sense of stability. Like other PARP inhibitors [43,44], rucaparib is associated with adverse events such as anemia/decreased hemoglobin, fatigue, and gastrointestinal symptoms, which are typically manageable with dose modification [19,33,38]. In contrast to other PARP inhibitors, rucaparib is not associated with thromboembolic events [33,38,43,44]. For many patients, this predictability and modifiability may be preferable to the fixed dose chemotherapy associated with systemic toxicities. Patient reported outcomes on quality of life were also collected in TRITON3 [38]. For the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Prostate questionnaire, changes from baseline to week 25 were similar for the rucaparib and physician’s choice arms in the BRCA subgroup (difference in least-squares means ± SE: 3.1 ± 2.5; 95% CI −1.8–8.1) and the ITT population (2.4 ± 2.2; 95% CI −1.9–6.6) [38]. Results for the Brief Pain Inventory-Short Form questionnaire and the EQ-5D-5 L Visual Analogue Scale were also similar between the rucaparib and physician’s choice arms [38]. Patients treated with rucaparib reported similar quality of life compared to patients treated with docetaxel, abiraterone, or enzalutamide, which indicates that rucaparib achieves greater efficacy than chemotherapy or a second-generation ARPI in advanced patients without decreasing quality of life [38]. As genetic testing becomes more widespread, increasing numbers of patients can be identified as candidates for PARP inhibition. Rucaparib provides patients with BRCA-mutated mCRPC a personalized, flexible, and convenient treatment option aligned with evolving expectations in precision oncology.

No head-to-head trials have directly compared any of the PARP inhibitors to one another. All clinical trials compared the PARP inhibitors (olaparib, talazoparib, niraparib, and rucaparib) to ARPI therapy; rucaparib also was compared with docetaxel in TRITON3. Thus, any discussion of comparative use of the agents must be general and subject to the many caveats regarding cross study comparisons. Molecular structure

PARP inhibitor molecules share a common structural framework that enables binding to the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) binding site within the catalytic domain of PARP enzymes [47]. Through this interaction, these agents promote trapping of PARP-DNA complexes, thereby inhibiting PARP enzymatic activity and impairing DNA repair mechanisms [47].

Although PARP inhibitors have a broadly similar mechanism of action, they differ in their relative binding affinity to and selectivity for individual PARP isotopes. Rucaparib and olaparib inhibit PARP1, PARP2, and PARP3 while niraparib and talazoparib target PARP1 and PARP2 [19,43,44,48]. These differences have been proposed as potential contributors to variation in the toxicity profiles among agents [47]. However, the extent to which differences in PARP isotope binding affinity and selectivity translate into clinically meaningful differences in efficacy or safety outcomes has not been clearly defined [47].

Pharmacokinetics

All four PARP inhibitors show moderate-to-high interindividual variability in plasma exposure [49]. Higher exposure is associated with an increase in toxicity, mostly hematological toxicity [49]. Olaparib and niraparib are non-preferentially cleared hepatically and renally, whereas rucaparib is primarily cleared by the liver, and talazoparib is primarily cleared by the kidneys [19,43,44,49]. Dose adjustments are necessary in patients with renal dysfunction for olaparib and talazoparib, but no dose adjustments for renal dysfunction are needed for rucaparib [19,43,44,49]. Niraparib should be avoided in patients with moderate or severe hepatic impairment [48].

Indications and major outcomes

Rucaparib is FDA-approved in the US for BRCA-mutated mCRPC in men who have been treated with at least one androgen-receptor directed therapy [19]. Olaparib is FDA-approved in the US for HRR-mutated mCRPC following progression with enzalutamide or abiraterone, and in combination with abiraterone for BRCA-mutated mCRPC regardless of prior therapy [43]. Niraparib is FDA-approved in the US for use in combination with abiraterone and prednisone or prednisolone for BRCA-mutated mCRPC and BRCA2-mutated mCSPC [48]. Talazoparib is FDA-approved in the US for use in combination with enzalutamide for HRR-gene-mutated mCRPC [44]. Additionally, rucaparib, olaparib, and niraparib are indicated for ovarian cancer, olaparib and talazoparib are indicated for breast cancer, and olaparib is indicated for pancreatic cancer in the US [43,44,48].

As clinical trials are conducted under widely varying conditions, the comparison of efficacy and safety data across trials requires caution. Important confounding factors include different patient populations, trial lengths, treatment combinations, etc. The most consequential distinctions include the range of qualifying genes included in the study, the choice of control arm therapy, and the presence or absence of crossover to PARP inhibitor therapy. The results of the primary endpoint (rPFS) from the pivotal trial of each PARP inhibitor are shown in Supplementary Table S1. In clinical trials of patients with mCRPC, median rPFS in the overall efficacy population was 10.2 months for rucaparib, 5.8 months for olaparib, 16.5 months for niraparib with abiraterone, and not reached for talazoparib with enzalutamide [43,44,48]. Overall, each PARP inhibitor effectively treated men with mCRPC associated with specific HRR gene mutations. Rucaparib is a monotherapy option for men with BRCA-mutated mCRPC and has been demonstrated to prolong rPFS compared to docetaxel or ARPIs [19]. Olaparib is another monotherapy option for a wider range of HRR-mutated mCRPC; however, there is no evidence of a clear advantage over docetaxel [43]. Niraparib with abiraterone and talazoparib with enzalutamide are effective first-line combination treatments after prior ADT; however, data in the post ARPI setting is limited [44,48].

Adverse events

PARP inhibitors share some common toxicities as a class, but each PARP inhibitor has a unique AE profile [50]. Hematological toxicities, such as anemia, are a frequent class effect AE [50]. The rate of transfusions to treat anemia varied across clinical trials: 27%, 29%, and 42% of patients treated with niraparib, rucaparib, and talazoparib, respectively, received at least one blood transfusion [38,44,48,51]. Transfusion rates were not reported for olaparib. MDS/AML has been reported in patients receiving PARP inhibitors and is included as a warning and precaution in the prescribing information; therefore, careful monitoring for hematological toxicity is recommended. The long half-life and potential hematologic toxicity associated with talazoparib require careful monitoring and dose adjustments [47]. Gastrointestinal AEs are also common, with nausea being the most prevalent [50]. All PARP inhibitors induce fatigue [50]. Increased creatinine concentrations have been observed in the first few weeks of treatment with rucaparib, before stabilizing with continued dosing [50]. However, it should be noted that elevation of serum creatinine may not indicate a decline in glomerular filtration rate or kidney function [50]. Olaparib prescribing information contains a warning that venous thromboembolism, including severe or fatal pulmonary embolism, occurred in 8% of patients with mCRPC [43]. This warning is not present in the prescribing information for the other three PARP inhibitors [19,44,48].

While the overall AE profile of PARP inhibitor ARPI combinations does not appear to demonstrate synergistic toxicities and is consistent with the combined toxicities of each individual agent, the use of two agents in combination can increase toxicity and may not be warranted for some patients. Additionally, for many patients, financial toxicity may accrue due to the need for multiple prescriptions with combinations such as olaparib plus abiraterone and talazoparib plus enzalutamide. This concern may be mitigated by the availability of a fixed-dose combination tablet of niraparib and abiraterone. However, this formulation may require new prescriptions when dose adjustments are needed, potentially increasing prescribing complexity.

Future directions

Combination treatment with PARP inhibitors and other PCa therapies is being investigated. For example, trials are assessing the potential synergistic antitumor effects of radioligand therapy in combination with PARP inhibitors [52,53]. Ongoing trials are also evaluating PARP inhibitors in combination with DNA-damaging agents, e.g., talazoparib plus temozolomide in metastatic PCa (NCT04019327), where temozolomide acts as an alkylating DNA-damaging chemotherapy. Additionally, recent trials have demonstrated the efficacy of PARP inhibition in combinations with ARPIs in the hormone-sensitive setting, moving PARP inhibition as a potential therapy option earlier in disease course [17].

Conclusion

Rucaparib represents an important advancement in the mCRPC for tumors harboring BRCA1/2 mutations. As a potent oral PARP inhibitor, rucaparib induces selective tumor cell death, and has demonstrated consistent antitumor activity across phase II and III studies, including objective responses, PSA declines, and clinically meaningful improvements in rPFS. Rucaparib is also the only PARP inhibitor to have shown improved rPFS compared with docetaxel in patients with mCRPC. Its safety profile, characterized primarily by manageable hematologic and gastrointestinal side effects, supports long-term administration with dose modification as needed. Clinical guidelines recommend integrating rucaparib into routine care, which underscores the growing role of biomarker-driven therapy in PCa and reinforces the necessity of widespread germline and somatic testing to identify eligible patients. Ongoing trials investigating combination strategies and the integration of PARP inhibitors with other targeted modalities will further define the optimal therapeutic sequencing. For eligible patients, rucaparib offers a personalized, effective, and convenient treatment option that aligns with current principles of precision oncology.
