Determinants of Prolonged Length of Stay and Financial Burden After Burn Injury in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia
Authors: Metasebia Worku Abebe, Hanna Atlabachew, Greta L Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Burn injuries impose substantial burdens in low- and middle-income countries, where prolonged hospitalization occurs due to limited specialized centers, resources, and surgical delays. This study examines factors associated with prolonged length of stay (LOS) and treatment costs relative to catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) thresholds among burn patients treated at a tertiary center in Ethiopia.Methods:Burn patients treated at the Addis Ababa Burn, Emergency, and Trauma Center from January 2023 to 2025 were prospectively enrolled in this observational cohort study. Univariable and multivariable analyses were performed to evaluate factors associated with LOS for the overall cohort and by age subgroup and treatment costs were compared with CHE thresholds.Results:Among 196 patients (55.6% men; mean age 17.6 y), the mean LOS was 33.5 ± 28.8 days. Electrical (36.4%) and flame (35.2%) injuries predominated in adults versus scalds in children (73.1%). Total body surface area and sex distribution were similar across groups, but pediatric burns were more superficial and required fewer surgical procedures (0.6 versus 1.4). The number of procedures and systemic infections remained independently associated with log-transformed LOS in adjusted analyses for all groups (all P < 0.05). LOS independently predicted cost. Facility and out-of-pocket costs averaged 24,428 and 7283 Ethiopian birr, respectively, with out-of-pocket spending by subgroup approaching or exceeding CHE thresholds.Conclusions:Prolonged LOS after burn injury in Addis Ababa is driven by clinical severity and complications, escalating costs toward catastrophic levels. Prevention strategies and age-tailored clinical pathways may shorten LOS, reduce costs, and strengthen financial risk protection in low-resource settings.

Takeaways

Question: What factors influence length of stay (LOS), and what are the financial implications following burn injury in Ethiopia?

Findings: In a prospective cohort of 196 burn patients treated in Addis Ababa, scald injuries predominated among children, whereas electrical/flame burns predominated among adults. The number of procedures and infections were independently associated with LOS across study groups. LOS was consistently associated with increased costs, with patient expenses approaching or exceeding catastrophic thresholds.

Meaning: Prolonged LOS reflects burn severity, operative burden, and inpatient complications and is accompanied by substantial financial risk; early clinical intervention and financial protection strategies are needed to reduce this burden in low-resource settings.

INTRODUCTION

Burn injuries are among the most devastating forms of trauma and represent a major global public health issue owing to their association with significant morbidity, prolonged recovery, and high mortality. Despite advances in prevention and acute management, burns remain a leading cause of disability-adjusted life years lost.1 According to Global Burden of Disease 2021 data, the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates for burn injury are highest among low-income countries (105.0 per 100,000 and 55.1 per 100,000, respectively), representing approximately a 66% higher incidence and a 2–3 times greater mortality rate than in high-income countries.2 This global inequity underscores the need for region-specific data to inform prevention strategies and optimize resource allocation.

The burden of burn injury is particularly acute in sub-Saharan Africa, accounting for nearly two-thirds of the global burden of morbidity and mortality.1 A systematic review of burn care in the region reported an average hospital length of stay (LOS) of 24 days.1 Country-level studies similarly found prolonged LOS following burn injury, with 29% of Malawian patients remaining in hospital for more than 48 days due to inadequate fluid resuscitation and surgical delays,3 whereas in Ethiopia the mean LOS has been reported to range from 17.3 to 44 days, with no studies explicitly exploring contributing factors.4–6 Prolonged admissions substantially increase the financial burden of burn care, resulting in catastrophic health expenditure for patients and their families.7 For example, a study of patients treated for burn injuries at a referral center in Tanzania found that hospital fees routinely exceeded the catastrophic health expenditure threshold.8 However, no studies to date have quantified the predictors and financial implications of prolonged LOS following burn injury in sub-Saharan Africa. Such data are essential to inform evidence-based strategies to shorten hospitalization and reduce costs.

This study aimed to define factors that predict hospital LOS and examine the relationship between LOS and total expenditure for adult and pediatric patients treated at the Addis Ababa Burn, Emergency, and Trauma Center (AaBET). The results of this analysis can inform targeted clinical protocols, optimize burn care delivery, and guide policy interventions to reduce catastrophic financial burden on patients and health systems.

METHODS

Study Design and Setting

This prospective, observational cohort study was conducted at AaBET, a national referral hospital providing specialized burn care in Ethiopia. This study was approved by the institutional review board at the research directorate of Saint Paul’s Millennium Medical College, an academic institution affiliated with AaBET Hospital.

Study Population

All patients admitted to AaBET with an acute burn injury during the study period (January 2023–January 2025) were eligible for inclusion and followed from admission through hospital discharge. Patients were excluded if they were discharged against medical advice, died during hospitalization, were transferred to an external facility, or had incomplete data, as LOS could not be reliably measured in these cases.

Variables and Definitions

The primary outcome was hospital LOS, defined as the number of days from admission to discharge. Independent variables included demographics, clinical and burn characteristics, and inpatient clinical course indicators. Serologic variables included hemoglobin and albumin levels when available. Anemia was defined as a serum hemoglobin of 7.00 g/dL or less according to the treating facility’s threshold for blood transfusion. Hypoalbuminemia (≤2.00 g/dL) was reported descriptively among patients with available data, with the threshold selected according to prior burn studies.9,10

Cost Measurement

Caregiver interviews were conducted by trained data collectors at the time of discharge to determine out-of-pocket (OOP) expenditure. Reported costs were categorized as facility-related expenses and patient-related expenses. Facility-related expenses included direct cash payments made for healthcare services such as wound care supplies, consumables, laboratory investigations, imaging studies, medications, intravenous fluids, and admission or service fees. Patient-related expenses included transportation, food for patients and attendants, and reported loss of wages or income during the admission period. Total OOP expenditure was calculated as the sum of expenses incurred over the entire inpatient stay. Cost variables were analyzed descriptively and in relation to LOS but were not included as predictors in multivariable models due to collinearity with LOS.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized descriptively. Because LOS demonstrated a right-skewed distribution, all inferential analyses were performed using log-transformed LOS [ln(LOS)]. Univariate associations with ln(LOS) were assessed using Welch 2-sided t tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for binary variables, 1-way analysis of variance for categorical variables, and Pearson or Spearman rank correlation for continuous variables.

Multivariable linear regression was performed with ln(LOS) as the dependent variable. Independent variables were selected based on clinical relevance and univariate associations. Model assumptions, residual diagnostics, and collinearity were assessed. Intensive care unit (ICU) admission and comorbidity were excluded due to low prevalence, and cost variables were excluded due to collinearity. Anemia was excluded due to collinearity with transfusion requirement. Variance inflation factors less than 2.5 were considered acceptable. Unstandardized regression coefficients (B) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) are reported, and statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05.

In a secondary analysis, all inferential procedures were reported separately for adult and pediatric subgroups using the same analytical framework. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (Chicago, IL).

RESULTS

A total of 196 patients met the inclusion criteria. Patients were predominantly male, young, and urban-dwelling (Table 1). LOS was prolonged and right-skewed, with a mean of 33.5 ± 28.8 days and a median of 27 days (IQR 27 d). Scald burns were the most common etiology (52.0%), and most injuries were superficial or deep partial-thickness (36.2% and 49.5%, respectively), with a mean total body surface area (TBSA) of 12.6 ± 8.7%. Few patients had comorbid medical conditions (11.7%) or abnormal laboratory values on admission. During admission, 14.8% required transfusion, whereas more than one-third (37.2%) developed systemic infection. Patients underwent an average of 0.9 ± 1.2 procedures, and ICU admission was uncommon (2.6%). Demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Univariate analysis of the entire patient cohort revealed that living condition (P = 0.013), etiology (P < 0.001), and depth (P < 0.001) were strongly associated with ln(LOS). Ln(LOS) differed significantly between patients with and without anemia (Mann-Whitney U = 1114.0, P < 0.001) and systemic infection (Mann-Whitney U = 713.5, P < 0.001), but not comorbid medical conditions (Mann-Whitney U = 1640.5, P = 0.172), hypoalbuminemia among those tested (Mann-Whitney U = 108.0, P = 0.274), or ICU admission (Mann-Whitney U = 423.5, P = 0.666). Ln(LOS) was positively correlated with age (Pearson r = 0.33, P < 0.001) and TBSA (r = 0.22, P = 0.002). A strong monotonic association was observed between the number of surgical procedures and ln(LOS) (Spearman ρ=0.81, P < 0.001). Results of univariate analyses are summarized in Table 1.

The overall multivariable linear regression model was statistically significant and demonstrated good explanatory power (R2 = 0.583, adjusted R2 = 0.562; F(9.185) = 28.7, P < 0.001). Diagnostic evaluation showed no evidence of multicollinearity (all variance inflation factors < 2.5), and residual plots supported the assumptions of linearity, homoscedasticity, and approximate normality. Several factors were independently associated with ln(LOS) (Table 2). After adjustment, greater burn depth (B = 0.157, 95% CI 0.029–0.285, P = 0.016), systemic infection (B = 0.330, 95% CI 0.164–0.496, P < 0.001), and number of surgical procedures (B = 0.310, 95% CI 0.213–0.408, P < 0.001) remained independently associated with ln(LOS). Specifically, each additional procedure showed the strongest association with LOS. Male sex was modestly associated with shorter hospitalization (B = −0.170, 95% CI −0.338 to −0.001, P = 0.048), whereas age (B = 0.005, P = 0.060) and TBSA (B = 0.009, P = 0.059) demonstrated borderline associations. Living condition, burn etiology, and transfusion requirement were not independently associated with ln(LOS).

Adult Group

Eighty-eight (44.9%) patients were adults aged 18 years or older, with a mean age of 32.2 ± 13.4 years and 58.0% male (Table 3). Mean LOS was 41.2 ± 26.4 days (median 36 d, IQR 24.8 d). Electrical and flame burns predominated (36.4% and 35.2%, respectively), and partial-thickness injuries were most common (22.7% superficial, 55.7% deep). Mean TBSA was 13.8 ± 11.0%. Adult patients underwent an average of 1.4 ± 1.2 surgical interventions during admission. Few patients were admitted to the ICU (3.4%). Infection and transfusion occurred in 35.2% and 14.8% of patients, respectively.

Univariate analyses demonstrated that burn severity and inpatient course variables were significantly associated with ln(LOS) among adult patients (Table 3). Burn etiology was significantly associated with ln(LOS) (P = 0.003). Greater burn severity was also associated with longer hospitalization, including higher TBSA (Spearman ρ=0.22, P = 0.040) and increasing burn depth (P < 0.001). Ln(LOS) was strongly and positively correlated with the number of surgical procedures performed (Spearman ρ=0.79, P < 0.001). Ln(LOS) differed significantly between patients with and without anemia (Mann-Whitney U = 164.0, P = 0.008), transfusion requirement (Mann-Whitney U = 202.0, P < 0.001), and systemic infection (Mann-Whitney U = 348.0, P < 0.001). In contrast, ln(LOS) was not significantly associated with age, sex, living condition, comorbidity status, ICU admission, or hypoalbuminemia among those tested.

In multivariable linear regression analysis of adult patients, the overall model was statistically significant (F(8, 78) = 13.4, P < 0.001) and explained a substantial portion of the variance in ln(LOS) (R2 = 0.580, adjusted R2 = 0.537) (Table 4). No evidence of multicollinearity was observed, and residual plots supported model assumptions. After adjustment, the number of surgical procedures (B = 0.33, 95% CI 0.22–0.44, P < 0.001) and the presence of systemic infection (B = 0.40, 95% CI 0.14–0.65, P = 0.003) remained independently associated with longer hospitalization. Age, sex, burn etiology, TBSA, burn depth, and transfusion requirement were not independently associated with ln(LOS) in the adjusted model.

Pediatric Group

A total of 108 (55.1%) patients were younger than 18 years, with a mean age of 5.6 ± 5.2 years and a male majority (53.7%) (Table 3). The mean LOS was 27.2 ± 29.3 days (median 20 d, IQR 22.5 d). Most were urban dwellers (80.6%), and few had comorbid medical conditions (5.6%), anemia (16.7%), or hypoalbuminemia (38.5% of those tested). Pediatric scald burns predominated, and most were partial-thickness (47.2% superficial, 44.4% deep). Mean TBSA was 11.7% ± 6.1%. Pediatric patients underwent an average of 0.6 ± 1.0 procedures, and ICU admission was rare (1.9%). Infection and transfusion occurred in 38.9% and 14.8% of patients, respectively.

In univariate analysis of pediatric patients, ln(LOS) differed significantly by burn etiology (Kruskal–Wallis H = 20.1, df = 2, P < 0.001) and burn depth (H = 92.9, df = 3, P < 0.001) (Table 3). Ln(LOS) was positively correlated with age (Pearson r = 0.373, P < 0.001) and TBSA (Spearman ρ=0.321, P < 0.001). A strong monotonic association was observed between the number of surgical procedures and ln(LOS) (Spearman ρ=0.721, P < 0.001). Ln(LOS) differed significantly between pediatric patients with and without comorbidity (Mann-Whitney U = 122.5, P = 0.014), anemia (U = 313.0, P < 0.001), transfusion requirement (U = 125.0, P < 0.001), and systemic infection (U = 793.5, P < 0.001). No significant differences in ln(LOS) were observed by sex, living condition, ICU admission, or hypoalbuminemia among those tested.

The pediatric multivariable model was statistically significant (F(8, 99) = 16.6, P < 0.001) and explained a substantial proportion of variance in ln(LOS) (R2 = 0.573; adjusted R2 = 0.539) (Table 5). Collinearity was not demonstrated, and residual diagnostics supported model assumptions. After adjustment, older age (B = 0.034, 95% CI 0.006–0.062), greater TBSA (B = 0.020, 95% CI 0.002–0.038, P = 0.032), higher number of surgical procedures (B = 0.220, 95% CI 0.051–0.389, P = 0.011), and systemic infection (B = 0.420, 95% CI 0.192–0.648, P < 0.001) remained independently associated with longer LOS. Burn depth showed a borderline association (B = 0.202, 95% CI −0.006 to 0.410, P = 0.056), whereas transfusion requirement, sex, and burn etiology were not independently associated with ln(LOS).

Catastrophic Expenditure Analysis

Total costs increased proportionally with LOS in all groups (P < 0.001). Average total facility costs were 24,428 ± 21,969 Ethiopian birr (ETB) for the entire patient cohort, ETB 32,096 ± 25,549 for adults, and ETB 18,193 ± 16,176 for pediatric patients. The mean OOP cost was ETB 7283 ± 8188 for the aggregate cohort, ETB 9562 ± 8862 for adults, and ETB 5485 ± 7160 for children. Using Ethiopia’s 2024 gross national income per capita (Atlas method) of ETB 83,163 (US $1010 at an annual average exchange rate of US $1 = 82.3 ETB),11,12 aggregate OOP costs consumed approximately 8.8% of annual income; this corresponded to approximately 11.5% and 6.6% for adult and pediatric subgroups, respectively.

Given substantial currency volatility in 2024 (range, 57–140 ETB per US dollar),12 catastrophic expenditure estimates varied accordingly. Depending on the exchange rate applied, aggregate OOP spending ranged from 5.2% to 12.7% of annual income, adult spending from 7.2% to 18.2%, and pediatric spending from 4.1% to 10.4%. Aggregate, adult, and pediatric OOP expenditure approached or exceeded commonly accepted catastrophic thresholds (10%–25% of annual income),13 indicating substantial financial risk even for a single burn admission.

DISCUSSION

This study identified demographic and clinical factors associated with prolonged LOS in adult and pediatric patients treated for acute burn injury at a specialized burn unit in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Although associations with LOS were similar between demographic groups, subgroup analyses revealed differences in burn etiology and inpatient care use, suggesting a need for age-specific, standardized burn care protocols. Such protocols can reduce inpatient complications and decrease overall LOS, resulting in cost savings for both patients and the healthcare system.

A cross-sectional survey of more than 15,000 patients treated at burn centers across Africa, Asia, and Latin America demonstrated a strong male predominance (63%).14 In Africa specifically, children and young adults are at the highest risk of burn injury, with men more commonly injured.1,15 The results of this study agree with prior reports, as patients admitted to the burn unit were predominantly male with a mean age of 17.6 years. These patterns were consistent across adult and pediatric subgroups.

Similarly, understanding burn etiology is critically important to inform preventive strategies, which result in the greatest mortality and cost-benefit gains.16 In our study, scald burns were the most common overall cause of injury. However, pediatric and adult subgroups revealed significantly different etiological patterns. Scald burns were extremely prevalent among pediatric patients, whereas electrical and flame burns were more common in adults. In the pediatric population, scald burns often result from accidental spills and cooking-related incidents,17 whereas in adult women, flame burns are common due to the use of cooking stoves that rely on solid fuels and can ignite traditional, loose-fitting clothing.18,19 Electrical injuries can occur both in the home and workplace, and increased urbanization is likely to account for the notable proportion of electrical burns in our sample. This epidemiological shift was previously described in Ethiopia, where a 5-fold increase in electrical injuries paralleled increased industrialization.20

Burn injuries result in prolonged hospitalization due to the need for specialized wound care following initial resuscitation. Although several demographic and clinical factors were significantly associated with LOS in univariate analysis, the number of surgical procedures and systemic infection were the only variables independently associated with LOS across all subgroups in multivariable models. In the pediatric subgroup, age and TBSA additionally remained independently associated with LOS after adjustment. Multiple prior studies have reported that age, TBSA, etiology, and depth are significantly associated with hospital LOS in adult and pediatric patients.21–26 The attenuation of these associations in adjusted models likely reflects the interrelated nature of burn severity, operative burden, and inpatient complications, with surgical procedures and infection capturing downstream aspects of clinical course. Multi-institutional studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings across Ethiopia and other low-and middle-income country contexts.

Systemic infections were particularly prevalent and affected more than one-third of study patients. Infectious complications are common in burn wards in low-and middle-income countries, with sepsis serving as a contributing factor to mortality.27–29 Although insufficient evidence exists for comparison with other low-resource settings, studies in high-resource contexts have shown infection to be predictive of prolonged LOS,30,31 which supports our findings. Strengthening infection prevention protocols and staff training may reduce LOS and associated costs.

Although hypoalbuminemia has been shown to predict prolonged LOS in both adult and pediatric populations,32,33 no association was demonstrated in the present study. This likely reflects limited statistical power, as serum albumin was not routinely measured and was available for a small subset of patients. In contrast, anemia was associated with longer LOS in univariate analysis across all groups but was excluded from multivariable models due to collinearity with transfusion requirement, which more directly reflects clinical severity and inpatient resource use. Routine, early serologic testing may improve risk stratification and prognostication in resource-constrained settings.

Finally, there was a robust, independent positive association between LOS and both OOP and facility costs across all study groups. Similar findings have been reported previously.34,35 OOP spending frequently approached or exceeded commonly accepted catastrophic expenditure thresholds,13 demonstrating that even a single burn admission poses substantial financial risk to patients and their families in this setting. This aligns with findings from Tanzania, where hospital fees following burn injury routinely surpassed catastrophic levels.8

Sensitivity analyses accounting for currency volatility demonstrated that OOP spending approached or exceeded catastrophic thresholds under all scenarios, revealing the vulnerability of burn patients in unstable economic contexts. Furthermore, the true financial consequences are likely to be underestimated. Productivity losses, long-term disability, outpatient care, and indirect household costs were incompletely captured, and prior research has indicated that these burdens are substantial for burn survivors in low-income settings.36–38 Thus, reductions in LOS through injury prevention, early operative management, and infection prevention may provide both clinical and economic benefit.

This study has several limitations. Although the sample size was large relative to similar studies, this was a single-institution cohort with overrepresentation of urban patients, limiting generalizability. Exclusion of patients transferred to external facilities may have introduced bias, as such patients may experience longer total LOS and higher costs.39 Furthermore, data on comorbidities, hypoalbuminemia, and inhalation injury were incomplete or absent despite these factors having been associated with poorer clinical outcomes in prior studies.9,10,40–44

Despite these limitations, this study is among the few to examine predictors of prolonged LOS following acute burn injury in a low-income setting. By integrating clinical variables with macroeconomic instability, it provides new insight into how patient- and system-level factors interact to shape health outcomes and affordability. The findings can inform policies targeting prevention, complication reduction, and financial risk protection.

CONCLUSIONS

This study identifies demographic and clinical factors associated with prolonged LOS among patients treated for acute burn injury at a specialized facility in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Age-related differences in burn etiology and inpatient care use suggest the need for age-specific, standardized burn care protocols for adult and pediatric patients. Hospitalization costs were substantial and approached or exceeded catastrophic expenditure thresholds. These findings underscore the importance of prevention strategies, early clinical management, and age-specific treatment guidelines to reduce inpatient complications and LOS, as well as policies that strengthen financial risk protection.
