Barriers and facilitators of brace compliance in adolescents with spinal deformities: a qualitative study
Authors: Faezeh Ghorbani, Hadi Ranjbar, Mohammad Kamali, Zahra Nemati, Hiva Razavi, Taher Babaee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) and Scheuermann’s kyphosis (SK) are common spinal deformities affecting different aspects of teenagers’ lives. Conservative treatments such as bracing and exercise are key for managing these spinal deviations before skeletal maturity. This qualitative descriptive study explored factors influencing adolescents’ adherence to brace treatment for spinal deformities, with the goal of identifying barriers and facilitators to compliance.MethodsSeventy-four participants, including 32 adolescents, their parents (27 mothers and 5 fathers), and healthcare professionals (6 orthotists, 2 physiotherapists, and 2 spine surgeons), were interviewed between September 2020 and May 2021. The participants were invited from related spinal centers. For data analysis in this qualitative study, content analysis was used.ResultsData analysis revealed four main categories, 14 categories, and 94 subcategories across 1521 codes. The findings indicate that while adolescents generally experience a similar path toward brace adherence, each individual’s journey is unique, and influenced by personal circumstances. For each stage of the brace compliance process, some codes were extracted as facilitating and inhibiting factors.ConclusionVarious factors affect adolescents’ compliance with wearing a brace for spinal deformities, with each factor impacting individuals differently. Personalized solutions are essential to overcome barriers and enhance compliance, as each adolescent experiences unique conditions during treatment. Although the path to brace compliance shares patterns, minor differences exist, necessitating customized approaches. Barriers and facilitators identified in the study can be managed through structured follow-up programs, therapeutic incentives, educational videos, and workshops that highlight others’ progress, fostering a positive attitude towards treatment’s health and appearance benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-09817-7.

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) and Scheuermann’s kyphosis (SK) are the most prevalent spinal deformities seen in teenagers, significantly impacting their quality of life [1]. AIS is identified in 0.5% to 5.2% of children aged 10 to 16 [2], while SK is found in 1% to 8% of the same age group [3]. The progression of these curves may lead to uneven posture, misaligned shoulders and hips, noticeable ribs, back pain, and respiratory condition [4]. These progressive spinal deformities also carry psychological and social repercussions [5]. Untreated curves of more than 30 degrees are likely to worsen and will necessitate surgical intervention [6]. Consequently, conservative approaches like braces and exercise are the primary non-surgical options for adolescents with spinal deformities. These methods are crucial for managing curve progression before skeletal maturity is reached.

According to the 2016 SOSORT (the International Scientific Society on Scoliosis Orthopedic and Rehabilitation Treatment) guidelines, observation and exercise are recommended for scoliosis curvatures of 20 degrees or less [7]. For moderate scoliosis, characterized by curvatures between 20 and 35 degrees, brace treatment combined with specific exercises is recommended. For severe scoliosis, characterized by curvatures of more than 45 to 50 degrees, surgical intervention is deemed necessary [7].

Although brace interventions are highly effective in preventing the worsening of spinal curves, they are sometimes difficult for patients to accept and adhere to. Some studies have indicated that brace treatment may elevate levels of anxiety, stress, and depression in patients, as well as diminish self-esteem and overall quality of life in adolescents [8]. However, other studies have found that brace treatment does not significantly impact the quality of life and mental health of adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis [9, 10]. These discrepancies in research findings may be attributed to factors such as cultural differences, the type of brace used, and gender [11, 12].

Recent studies on the factors influencing brace compliance have produced a wide range of results, often marked by contradictions [13, 14]. The key reasons for accepting brace treatment should be explored within the individual’s mindset, social circumstances, personal relationships, and family characteristics. Understanding these factors is not straightforward and should come from direct feedback from the patients themselves. It’s possible that factors not initially considered by researchers play a significant role in compliance.

Understanding the experiences and perspectives of adolescents throughout the brace treatment period is an important step in exploring effective factors for brace compliance. However, most previous studies on factors affecting brace acceptance have used questionnaires and quantitative methods. Therefore, in order to investigate in depth and better understand the experiences and conditions of adolescents wearing braces and to extract undiscovered factors affecting brace compliance based on the insights gained by the adolescents, a qualitative approach was used. This study aimed to explore adolescents’ experiences of living with spinal deformities, with a focus on facilitators and barriers to brace adherence.

Method

Study design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design within an interpretive structure. Data collection involved conducting in-depth, semi-structured interviews in person [15], and phone conversations. The gathered data were then analyzed through content analysis. The research protocol received approval from the Iran University of Medical Sciences ethics committee (NO.IR.IUMS.REC.1399.428).

Research team

Prior to conducting the interviews, the study authors and experts in health-related research convened three face-to-face meetings. These meetings aimed to elucidate the theoretical framework of the study and refine the interview questions. The research team developed a set of guide questions for the interviews (Table 1). The first author (F.G.), who has five years of experience treating adolescents with spinal deformities, conducted the interviews. She has also participated in several workshops on qualitative research and interviewing techniques.

Recruitment

Participants were selected from three orthotic and prosthetic centers in Tehran, Iran that produced spinal braces. Selection was based on predefined criteria, and eligible individuals were invited to participate. Informed written consent was obtained from the parents, and verbal assent was obtained from the adolescents.

The inclusion criteria for the study consist of adolescents who (1) had AIS or SK, (2) were between 10 and 14 years old at the time of deformity diagnosis and the start of brace treatment, (3) had been under brace treatment for more than three months, and (4) had no background of spinal surgery or mental disorders. Additionally, parents were interviewed about their children’s experiences with spinal deformities from the time of diagnosis. To provide triangulation, interviews were also held with therapists experienced in treating spinal deformities, including orthotists, physiotherapists, and orthopedic surgeons. The study initially employed convenience sampling, followed by purposeful sampling to gather more detailed information from participants at various stages of treatment.

Data collection

Data collection occurred from September 2020 to May 2021, with interview analysis conducted simultaneously. Interviews continued until data saturation was reached, meaning no new information or concepts emerged. Participants were informed that they could end the conversation at any time. During face-to-face sessions, the interviewer introduced herself to the child by providing her name, occupation, and place of work, and explained the study’s purpose in terms understandable to the child. The primary focus of the interviews was on brace compliance.

To encourage openness, follow-up questions were added based on the participants’ responses. Participants and their parents were invited to participate in a phone interview if they had more information to share.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed the Lundman and Graneheim method [16]. The recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim, including non-verbal gestures and body movements. The transcripts were read multiple times to gain a comprehensive understanding and immerse in the data. Content analysis involved generating codes from the most descriptive material, refining these codes, and organizing them into categories [17]. Initial coding and the inductive development of main categories from these codes were conducted.

The interviewer began by inductively coding two interviews and comparing notes, then proceeded to code two more interviews to establish a coding structure that was agreed upon by the other authors. This process led to the creation of groups of significant codes, points, or materials, allowing for the development of titles that characterized the participants’ experiences. Semantic units were identified for each interview, and key phrases were extracted. Initial codes were closely aligned with the research data [18]. Concepts were generated and summarized based on the study questions, with appropriate codes assigned to each item. Subsequently, the remaining interviews were coded, and these codes were organized into categories. The original codes were summarized based on their conceptual similarities into categories through ongoing data analysis and comparison. These categories were refined as the interviews progressed, using data codes and category charts. Research team sessions were then held to combine the analysis results and represent the participants’ experiences. The study followed the 32-item checklist of consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative studies (COREQ) [19] (Appendix).

To enhance the rigor of our findings, we employed collaborative coding, adhered to established research methodologies [20], and applied Lincoln and Guba’s criteria for trustworthiness [21]. Regular team meetings refined data categories, and participant validation ensured study integrity [22]. Results

Forty families were invited to join the research, but eight parents declined for various reasons: three were dissatisfied, two lacked free time, and three were unwilling to participate. The study interviewed seventy-four participants, including 32 adolescents with braces and their parents (27 mothers and five fathers), six orthotists, two physiotherapists, and two spine surgeons. The Participants’ mean age and mean brace-wearing time were 14.2 ± 3.5 years and 8.5 ± 1.4 months, respectively. The average Cobb angles for AIS and SK cases were 37.20° ± 11.46° and 71.8° ± 5.9°, respectively. 20 participants used the Milwaukee brace and 6 used a thoracolumbosacral orthosis. The interviews, conducted in Persian, lasted between 28 and 56 min.

The analysis identified four main categories, 14 categories, and 94 subcategories across 1521 related codes (Fig. 1). The sequences of the main categories outline the compliance process from the initial encounter with the problem. Time passage significantly influenced the creation of milestones and changes in the adolescents’ conditions and compliance rates.

Accepting the diagnosis

This category combines three main categories (There is a problem, seeking a cause, and finding the diagnosis). Each stage has distinct facilitator and barrier factors, which are detailed.

Barriers

Lack of media information and the spread of incorrect information are seen as obstacles to this stage. Access to scientific resources is crucial for obtaining reliable information about spinal deviations and their treatments. The main factors preventing this stage were confusion, lack of awareness, lack of association of spinal deviations, and incorrect sources of information. Participants cited parental denial, feelings of guilt, lack of awareness about disease complications, and concerns about their child being stigmatized as barriers to accepting the diagnosis.

Orthotist interaction

This category emerged from three main categories, including referring to an orthotist, first visit with the orthotic, and orthotist’s communication.

Barriers

Financial issues, insufficient disease explanation, lack of awareness about treatment consequences, and the influence of non-specialists suggesting alternatives were noted as barriers. High brace costs and lack of insurance coverage were cited as major obstacles to visiting orthotic centers. The unpleasant office environment and inappropriate initial interactions with clinic staff during the first visit were cited as inhibitory factors at orthotic centers. During the adolescent’s first meeting with the orthotist, anxiety, fear, confusion, and shock are common for both the adolescent and their family. Poor communication skills, incorrect information, unanswered parent questions, concerns about financial motives, and the orthotist’s lack of connection with adolescents exacerbate these feelings.

Treatment acceptance

The following three main categories emerged from this theme including casting trauma, brace wearing, and brace bitter moon.

Barriers

Issues such as inadequate ventilation in the casting room, inappropriate behavior from the orthotist, and prolonged molding processes make the experience less pleasant for adolescents. Obstacles to successful transitioning through the brace-wearing stage include orthotists not addressing brace issues, parents’ inappropriate feedback and distress, neglecting adolescent complaints, poor communication with the orthotist, insufficient orthotic skill and experience, and inadequate time for fitting and adjustments. Challenges in the initial treatment month include adolescent sensitivity, inappropriate feedback from relatives, strict parental behavior, and the father’s resistance to brace use.

Facilitators

Interview analysis indicated that certain practices, such as having the orthotist of the same gender perform the casting, respecting the adolescent’s privacy, and reassuring them about the treatment’s effectiveness, can reduce psychological stress for adolescents. Adolescents and their families initially fear the long-term use of braces. Orthotists emphasize the need for precise fitting, continual adjustment, morale support, parental involvement, and motivation to ease the brace-wearing process. They mentioned the specialized expertise required for constructing spinal braces. Based on interviews, facilitating the challenging transition through the first month involves providing clear instructions, seeking counselor guidance, family support, and attentiveness to adolescent sensitivities.

Living with a brace

The final category includes five main categories of coping with activities of daily life, social life adaptation, admission/ coping, inner feelings, and compliance.

Barriers

Adolescents face several challenges in adjusting to daily activities with their spinal brace, including difficulty in sitting and standing, time-consuming tasks, shortness of breath, frustration, knee pain, and pressure from the brace itself. The brace’s restrictions on movement caused discomfort in lower limb joints or neck and occasionally led to breathing difficulties. Tasks, including homework, also took longer, contributing to feelings of frustration and fatigue during adjustment. Fear of judgment, lack of explanation, feeling different, not being understood, negative feedback, and being labeled were obstacles to adolescents’ social adaptation mentioned in the interviews. Interview analysis identified brace discomfort, adolescent stubbornness, lack of support, and sleep difficulties as significant factors hindering the admission phase. During treatment, adolescents experience internal emotions like fear, loneliness, sadness, depression, embarrassment, and guilt, often without expressing these feelings to their parents. Fatigue from treatment duration, movement restrictions, clothing limitations, social discomfort with the brace, and dissatisfaction with reminders are key negative factors affecting brace acceptance and adherence. Overall, there was no significant difference in the statements about the challenges and experiences of adolescents wearing the Milwaukee brace and the thoracolumbosacral orthosis (TLSO). Only issues such as greater limitation of movement and a more pronounced cervical ring were noted with the Milwaukee brace compared to the TLSO.

Facilitators

Independence in daily activities is a determining factor for maintaining an adolescent’s morale and self-confidence. Factors such as preparing the home, family support, regular exercise, time passing, and prior training in daily tasks facilitate their adjustment. According to interviews, support from friends, guidance from the mother, openness to feedback, engaging explanations, and time passage helps adolescents adapt socially. Prioritizing the adolescent’s acceptance and adaptation to wearing the brace, familiarity with a brace in close relatives, and motivation to complete treatment were identified as facilitators for accepting the new situation. The adolescent’s acceptance is more crucial than the family’s; the quicker the adolescent adapts, the sooner the family moves out of the crisis stage. Radiographic assessments after wearing the brace for periods and showing curvature reduction, improve the adolescent’s appearance and stature. Furthermore, receiving encouragement and motivation from parents and relatives strengthens the adolescents’ positive feelings towards themselves. This fosters satisfaction with treatment, self-importance, a positive perception of the brace, and enhanced self-image. Key factors for accelerating brace adherence include consistent treatment team presence, peer support, understanding treatment importance, having a knowledgeable and experienced team, and receiving their encouragement.

Discussion

This qualitative study constitutes one component of a larger research project that primarily aimed to understand the brace compliance process for adolescents with spinal deformities through a qualitative approach. The foundational findings concerning the compliance process emerged from interviews have been published previously [23]. That earlier work identified four principal categories constituting the sequential stages of brace compliance. The present study aims to identify the factors influencing brace compliance at each the stages. Throughout the compliance process, facilitator and barrier factors have been delineated and are explained separately. Faster and more successful progress through these stages is thought to improve overall brace compliance. These factors can be used by healthcare providers to improve brace compliance in these patients.

Our analysis shows that brace compliance for spinal deformities goes hand-in-hand with accepting the diagnosis itself. Thus, early diagnosis and treatment by specialists are key for addressing spinal problems. Specialists using scientific methods can diagnose musculoskeletal problems faster, leading to smoother brace compliance and potentially avoiding more invasive procedures. Parents and orthotists believe clear explanations and unbiased specialist evaluations are crucial for both accepting the diagnosis and following the treatment plan, including wearing a brace.

Diagnosis of spinal deformity is a shock for families [24]. Parents reported confusion due to the lack of information and seeing many physicians with different opinions before getting a final diagnosis. Since most families don’t know much about spinal problems, any wrong information can disturb the treatment.

We found that clear explanations from the orthopedic surgeon and providing comprehensive information during the diagnosis session for the family were crucial. When physicians explained the condition, including treatment options, the optimal timing for various interventions, the correct course of treatment, and ultimately the consequences of not receiving treatment, acceptance of the treatment plan and parental confidence in the treatment team were established at primary stages. Otherwise, the patient could lead to abandoning the brace due to a lack of understanding about its importance.

The spinal surgeon can use evidence-based explanations to build hope for treatment success. Wilson et al. reported that, at the time of diagnosis, the primary need for parents was to get information from the physician. Additionally, 25% of patients didn’t have a confirmed diagnosis of scoliosis until their first visit to the physician [25]. On the other hand, parental resistance to accepting the presence of spinal deformity and the need for prompt treatment can significantly cause a delay in the brace compliance process. This can cause the adolescent to miss the valuable window of opportunity for benefiting from non-surgical interventions.

The present study found that a majority of both adolescents and their parents reported having no prior familiarity with spinal deformities, rendering the diagnosis a novel and concerning discovery. Therefore, it is essential to recognize that adolescents diagnosed with spinal deformities and their parents require comprehensive support to cope with the diagnosis and navigate the array of available treatment options. Although spinal deformities and their treatments profoundly affect body image, self-perception, and peer comparisons—factors of heightened significance during adolescence—physicians often overlook the psychosocial needs of these patients. It is recommended that clinicians provide the adolescent’s family with a realistic explanation of the condition, avoiding undue alarm, while referencing successful outcomes from comparable cases. This approach can alleviate the initial shock, fear, and anxiety experienced by the adolescent following diagnosis. Concurrently, timely psychological intervention is critical to help patients process this initial distress and subsequently adhere more readily to prescribed treatments, such as bracing. A supporting study [26] identified several key components for effective support: therapist empathy, access to social workers at the point of diagnosis or brace prescription, formal psychological assessments, and the integration of therapeutic sessions into the standard treatment protocol.

Our findings showed that it is essential for the orthotist to be able to establish a balanced relationship with the adolescents; in this way, they feel friendly with their therapist and also seriously accept their recommendations. The orthotist should be friendly, address the adolescents’ concerns, and consider their specific circumstances. Strong communication skills and a genuine interest in adolescents are crucial for orthotists. According to parents participating in the Wilson et al.’s study, orthotists played a significant role in supporting them [25]. Building trust and communication with adolescents is key to the successful treatment of spinal deformities, and this takes time and effort. Clear explanations from the orthotist that confirm the physician’s plan can build trust early on. A calm and positive environment at treatment centers also helps families feel confident. Providing general information about the treatment journey, including stages, duration, and factors affecting success, can reduce anxiety and uncertainty for them. One of the things that the orthotists explained to the adolescents was that “You are not the only one this has happened to, and you should not feel bad about the situation”. In the previous study, spinal specialists supported the idea that a trusting relationship between therapist and patient motivates adherence to treatment plans. Also, therapists can play a significant and beneficial role in educating and providing accurate insights into the brace treatment process. Provost et al. [26] reported that it is essential to clarify online information, provide accurate information, and interpret medical terminology by orthopedists and orthotists.

Adolescents undergoing spinal casting can feel uncomfortable, so orthotists and caregivers must respect ethical principles and privacy, ensuring a dedicated space for exams and casting. Parental anxiety can hinder adherence to spinal braces. Comprehensive fitting sessions are crucial, allowing orthotists to address adjustments and maximize comfort, enhancing treatment success. Poor brace construction can worsen issues, highlighting the need for proper construction and comfort adjustments [27].

The interviews revealed that fathers often opposed brace treatment, doubting its necessity and effectiveness, and expressed disapproval throughout the process. During the first month, the adolescent is uncertain about the potential for spinal improvement while facing conflicting parental views. It is recommended that the family reach a consensus and calmly explain the reasons for wearing the brace, providing incentives and motivation for better adherence to the treatment program.

Adolescents in this study raised concerns about their dependence on their mothers for daily tasks, especially during the early stages of brace treatment. To ease the adaptation, it’s suggested that home modifications be made: using appropriately sized furniture, providing a comfortable mattress, and adjusting the desk for doing their homework. Family help in the early stages can reduce the difficulty of daily activities. Regular exercise strengthens muscles and increases physical endurance. Over time, the adolescent will adapt and find optimal ways to perform activities. Previous research has also shown that a good mother-child relationship positively impacts brace treatment; support from siblings and therapists is also recommended [28].

Accepting brace treatment is challenging due to the noticeable changes it brings to adolescent’s appearance. Interviews revealed that fear of peer judgment is a major barrier to wearing braces. Adolescents worry about reactions from others, potential ridicule, and how to respond to peers’ questions. Previous studies found that anxiety about others’ perceptions, peer pressure, social relationships, and low family support contribute to stress and reduced adherence to brace treatment. Additionally, low mood, low expectations, anxiety about treatment failure, and poor self-image impact compliance behavior [29]. Participants expressed discomfort at being teased by peers, which led to embarrassment in social settings and reduced their compliance. However, many adolescents reported receiving emotional support from close friends and empathetic responses regarding their situation. Previous studies have indicated that some adolescents with spinal deformities conceal their feelings from family and friends [30], while others find support from family, friends, and classmates when they openly discuss their emotions [31]. Addressing adolescents’ concerns and preparing them for social interactions with braces before their first outing can significantly reduce anxiety and worries, thereby improving treatment compliance. Parents and therapists can help by explaining the temporary nature of brace treatment and reassuring adolescents that wearing a brace does not indicate a flaw or difference. These explanations can boost morale and maintain self-confidence. The support of friends as a motivating factor for brace compliance has also been emphasized [26]. A study found that 64% of adolescents with braces were bothered by negative peer attitudes and critical comments about their body shape during the day and preferred to wear their braces at night [32].

Analysis of interviews with adolescents and their parents uncovered intense emotional fluctuations among adolescents undergoing brace treatment. Therapists emphasized in our study that focusing on the positive impacts of braces, such as improved posture and appearance, can help maintain their morale and resilience during treatment. Also, they noted that adolescents often experience crises after periodic radiographs reveal worsened deformity angles, leading to frustration, depression, and stress about their treatment. At this stage, counseling support is essential to help adolescents regain the hope and motivation needed to continue adhering to their treatment.

During interviews, nearly all adolescents noted the challenges of wearing braces, including discomfort from heat, restricted movements—especially bending—and limitations in clothing choices. Therapists highlighted that the type of brace prescribed significantly impacts compliance. Adolescents are less likely to accept braces that are more restrictive and noticeable under clothing. Previous research has similarly highlighted these issues, noting that patients often stop wearing braces and discontinue treatment due to the daily activity limitations they impose [33].

We found that adolescents experienced increased hope, positivity, and self-esteem when wearing braces and engaging in exercises that helped reduce spinal curvature. Regular follow-up appointments and therapy sessions aimed at informing adolescents about the effectiveness of their treatment and fostering hope for their progress were found to be crucial for maintaining morale. Carroll et al. [34] suggested interventions such as compliance counseling and monitoring daily brace wear hours, which could potentially enhance brace compliance. Some adolescents reported noticing positive changes in their appearance after wearing braces for a few months, such as increased height, improved spinal alignment, a flatter stomach, and a better waistline. These changes satisfied them, and they mentioned becoming more attentive to their bodies since starting treatment. It appears that, in these cases, adolescents’ body image improved. However, this improvement should be evaluated further through questionnaires in future studies.

Orthotists in the present study suggested interacting with the adolescent while constructing the brace and asking their opinion on the color and design of the braces. Adolescents’ opinions on brace aesthetics varied, highlighting the importance of patient involvement in brace appearance. Previous studies suggested that patient-centered, custom-designed braces give adolescents a sense of ownership, and thus, involving adolescents in design decisions will enhance compliance [14, 35]. As well as, factors such as the therapist-patient relationship, the design and aesthetics of the brace [14], the visibility of the brace, and the patient’s quality of life [36] can affect the acceptance of the brace.

Responding appropriately to patients’ questions and addressing their needs are effective communication strategies between therapists and patients. According to Tavernauro et al. [37], effective collaboration among therapists (orthopedist, orthotist, and physiotherapist) leads to greater patient compliance. Scheduling regular appointments is useful for encouraging ongoing communication between the orthotist and the adolescent, and for adjusting the pressure of the brace pads. Additionally, regular and targeted monitoring of brace-wearing habits, combined with preventive strategies and psychosocial support, can effectively promote compliance [14, 31]. Miller et al. found that informing adolescents about sensors that measure and record brace-wearing hours can increase compliance [38].

Limitation

It should be declared that during this study, the authors did not have any intervention or authority to prescribe and suggest the type of brace treatment, and only participants from the three orthotic and prosthetic centers were invited. According to recent studies and the effectiveness of asymmetric design braces, it is suggested that in the future, a study with a qualitative approach should be conducted on the experience of wearing asymmetric design braces of adolescents with spinal deformities. The majority of the individuals were female due to the higher percentage of the female adolescents referred to orthotic and prosthetic centers than males. Although more females are diagnosed with AIS, it is important that males are included, as their experience may be qualitatively different.

Conclusion

The analysis of interviews indicates that various factors influence the compliance process of adolescents with spinal deformities when wearing a brace. Each factor’s impact varies for each individual, necessitating personalized solutions to overcome barriers and enhance compliance. Therefore, it is necessary to consider an effective solution and action to solve the barriers and increase the compliance rate of braces according to the specific conditions and characteristics of each person. With the consensus of the team members, develop a specific program for each adolescent to facilitate and speed up the compliance process formation. Considering that the path to braces adherence in adolescents follows the same pattern with minor differences, therefore passing through some conditions is inevitable. Promoting a positive mindset about the treatment’s significance for health and appearance can aid in proper compliance. The study identifies barriers and facilitators that can be managed to ease the compliance process. A structured follow-up program, therapeutic incentives, educational videos, and periodic workshops showcasing the progress of other adolescents can also be beneficial.
