1.
Introduction

The initial value problem (IVP) for ordinary
differential equations leads to challenging
computational problems. A chief reason is that
approximation errors typically grow exponentially
large with time. So simulating an IVP over a long
time span will break down. Other difficulties include
the presence of singularities as well as the
phenomenon of stiffness (regions where the solution
changes quickly). Although numerical algorithms based
on machine precision are very successful in
practice, they are often qualitatively wrong.
So there remains a need for certifiable or

validated algorithms

studied from the beginning of interval analysis

The computational problems associated with

(

The notions of partially correct algorithms

Partial correctness is useful since we can now
split correctness into separate concerns:
halting and correct output.
Promise problems
(
goldreich:promise-problems:06
)

are also natural in numerical computation,
where typical promises assert non-singularity
or good conditioning.
Algorithms for promise problems may assume
the promised condition without checking it.

Ultimately, validated algorithms require
arbitrary-precision numerics
(e.g., big-number packages such as
gmp
).
Although this is our goal, we avoid a direct
discussion of this aspect in order to focus on
the main algorithmic development.
Following standard practice, we describe an

abstract algorithm

A
that operates on exact
mathematical objects. But
such abstract descriptions must ultimately be
replaced by implementable operations.
Following
(
xu-yap:real-zero-system:19
)

this is done in two steps:
first,

is transformed into its

interval version

(

and
f(U)
are homeomorphic.

3.2.
Reduction of End Cover to Boundary Cover

Theorem
3
justifies the following

Boundary Algorithm
for computing
an
\varepsilon
-box cover of
\mbox{End}_{\bm{f}}(B,h)
:
if
B\in{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$l$}}\!\;}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we compute
an
\varepsilon
-box cover
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,2n
)
for each of the
2n
faces of
B
. Then

\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{2n}{\mathcal{C}}_{i}

is an
\varepsilon
-box cover for the boundary

\partial(\mbox{End}_{\bm{f}}(B,h))
.
Next, we compute a
filler cover

{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
to
cover the “interior” of
\bigcup\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
so that

\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{0}

forms an
\varepsilon
-box cover of
\mbox{End}_{\bm{f}}(B,h)
.

Thus, the Boundary Algorithm reduces an

n
-dimensional cover problem to several

(n-1)
-dimensional ones, modulo
the
Fill Problem
of computing
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
;
the header for this problem is thus

\mbox{{{Filler}}}(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}},\varepsilon)\mathop{\,\rightarrow\,}{\mathcal{C}}_{0}

Unfortunately, this appears to be an ill-formed problem
unless
\varepsilon
is small enough.

To understand the difficulty, note that
\bigcup\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}

is a connected set. The boundary of
\bigcup\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}

is the union of finitely many components,

S_{0}\cup S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{k}
where each
S_{j}

is topologically an
(n-1)
-sphere.
Moreover,
S_{0}
is the unique exterior component such that
the other
S_{i}
’s (
i=1,\ldots,k
) lies in the interior of
S_{0}
.
Also the
S_{i}
’s are not nested (if
S_{i}
is contained
in some
S_{j}
then
j=0
). We want the filler set

{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
to lie inside
S_{0}
, and to cover up

some
of these
S_{i}
’s. But identifying the
S_{i}
’s to
cover is a non-trivial problem especially in higher dimensions.
Let us say that
\varepsilon
is
small enough
if
k=1
.
If
\varepsilon
is small enough, then the boundary

\partial(\bigcup\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})
has two components
S_{0},S_{1}

with
S_{1}
inside
S_{0}
. The boxes of
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}

just have to cover the interior of
S_{1}
but stay within
S_{0}
.
This is a relatively simpler
problem of computational geometry, with no need
for any IVP solving.
In our examples (
n=2
), we can visually
see that our
\varepsilon
is small enough.
In our experimental section, we will
compare this Boundary Algorithm with

\mbox{EndCover}_{\bm{f}}(B_{0},\varepsilon)
.

5.2.

The standard iterative IVP algorithms (e.g.,
\tt StepB_{Direct}
)
suffers from the wrapping effect, and must use Lohner’s
matrix transformation. However, our

and
BoundaryCover
reduces the wrapping effect by tracking
the boundary with smaller boxes.
This leads to improved numerical stability.
Furthermore, our
Refine
algorithm
prevents the intermediate enclosures from becoming
excessively large; thus we avoid unnecessary step-size
reductions leading to loss of precision.
When
T
is small, the
BoundaryCover
method is slower than
the
EndCover
method.
But when
T
is large, the Boundary method becomes faster
since it only needs to handle the boundary; see Eg1, Eg2, Eg4, Eg5 in Table
2
.

6.
Conclusion

We have designed a complete validated
algorithm for the reachability form of the IVP problem, called
the End Cover Problem. We also offer
an alternative technique based on covering the boundary
of the End Cover as a lower-dimensional problem
(and thus possibly faster).
Preliminary implementations show that our algorithms
are practical, robust, and outperforms state-of-the-art
validated algorithms (
CAPD
and
VNODE-LP
).

Theorem
3

Let
\Phi:[0,h]\times B\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a trajectory
bundle where
B\in{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$l$}}\!\;}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

\mbox{End}_{\Phi}

25
)

B

\Phi|^{h}
.

Proof.
The map (
25
) has two properties:

(a)

It is continuous
(this follows from the continuity of
\Phi
).

(b)

It is injective
(this is the stratification property).

If
U
is the interior of
B
, then Brouwer’s
Invariance of Domain implies
U
is homeomorphic
to
\mbox{End}_{\Phi}(U)
.
But the closure of
\mbox{End}_{\Phi}(U)
is the end-slice
\Phi|^{h}
.
Part(b) above says that

\mbox{End}_{\Phi}:B\to\Phi|^{h}

Lemma
1

The number of stages in

EndEncl
(E_{0},{\bm{\varepsilon}};{\bm{p}},H)
is at most

1+\left\lfloor{H/{\overline{h}}}\right\rfloor
where

{\overline{h}}={\overline{h}}({\overline{E}},H,{\bm{\varepsilon}})
, see (
27
)
and

{\overline{E}}=\mbox{{image}}(\mbox{{{{IVP}}}}(E_{0},H))+Box({\bm{\varepsilon}}).

Proof.
Since
\mbox{{{{IVP}}}}(B_{0},H)
is valid,
{\overline{E}}
is bounded. The set

{\overline{B}}={\overline{B}}({\overline{E}},H,{\bm{\varepsilon}})
is also bounded as it is constructed from
bounded sets using continuous
operations. Since
{\bm{f}}^{[k]}
is continuous on the compact set

{\overline{B}}
, each
{\overline{M}}_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,n
)
in the definition (
28
) of

{\overline{h}}={\overline{h}}({\overline{E}},H,{\bm{\varepsilon}})
is finite. Thus,

0<{\overline{h}}\leq\min_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\varepsilon_{i}/{\overline{M}}_{i}\Big)^{1/k}.

Supppose the algorithm terminates after
m
stages.
Then the time sequence of the final scaffold
has the form
{\bm{t}}=(t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})
where
(t_{0},t_{m})=(0,H)
.
The
(j-1)
th stage was extended to the
j
th
stage by calling
{\mbox{\tt StepA}}(E_{j-1},H-t_{j-1},{\bm{\varepsilon}})\to(h_{j},F_{j})
.
Clearly,
h_{j}=t_{j}-t_{j-1}
.
By (
27
),

h_{j}\geq\min\left\{H-t_{j-1},\min_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\tfrac{\varepsilon_{i}}{M_{i}}\Big)^{1/k}\right\}.

If
h_{j}=H-t_{j-1}
, then
j
must be the final stage (
j=m
).
Therefore, for every
j<m
,

h_{j}\;\geq\;\min_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\tfrac{\varepsilon_{i}}{M_{i}}\Bigr)^{1/k}.

We claim that for all
j=1,\dots,m-1
, one has
h_{j}\geq{\overline{h}}
.
This immediately yields

(m-1)\,{\overline{h}}\leq H,\,\text{hence}\,m\leq 1+\lfloor H/{\overline{h}}\rfloor.

To prove the claim, fix any
j<m
. Then

\begin{array}[]{llllll}h_{j}&\geq&\min_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\varepsilon_{i}/M_{i}\Big)^{1/k}&\text{(since $j<m$)}\\
&\geq&\min_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\varepsilon_{i}/{\overline{M}}_{i}\Big)^{1/k}&\text{(since $M_{i}\leq{\overline{M}}_{i}$)}\\
&\geq&{\overline{h}}&\text{(by definition of ${\overline{h}}$)}\\
\end{array}

The above inequality
M_{i}\leq{\overline{M}}_{i}
comes from the
inclusion

{\overline{B}}(E_{j-1},H-t_{j-1},{\bm{\varepsilon}})\subseteq{\overline{B}}({\overline{E}},H,{\bm{\varepsilon}}),

and the definition of

M_{i},{\overline{M}}_{i}
in terms of these two sets.
This establishes
m-1\leq H/{\overline{h}}
as required.

Q.E.D.

Lemma
2

(Extend)

Given a scaffold
{{\mathcal{S}}}
with
m
stages and parameters

{\bm{\varepsilon}}>0
and
H>0
, the subroutine
StepA
executed within

{{\mathcal{S}}}.\mbox{{{Extend}}}({\bm{\varepsilon}},H)

\left\lceil\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{H}{{\overline{h}}}\right)\right\rceil

)

The
Extend
subroutine will call

StepA
which
performs a binary search by halving the
step size
H
until an admissible pair
(h>H/2,F)
is found.
The number of halvings required is
O(\log_{2}(H/{\overline{h}}))
,
as

below by
{\overline{h}}>0
.

Q.E.D.

Lemma
3

(Refine)

Let
{{\mathcal{S}}}
be an
m
-stage scaffold, and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
.
For each stage
i
write

\Delta t_{i}=t_{i+1}({{\mathcal{S}}})-t_{i}({{\mathcal{S}}})
and

G_{i}({{\mathcal{S}}})=((\pi_{i},{\bm{g}}_{i}),(\ell_{i},{\bm{E}}_{i},{\bm{F}}_{i}),(\overline{{\bm{\mu}}}^{1}_{i},\overline{{\bm{\mu}}}^{2}_{i}),(\delta_{i},\widehat{h}_{i})).

The number of phases in
{{\mathcal{S}}}.\mbox{{{Refine}}}(\varepsilon_{0})
is at most

1+N_{0}
where

•

N_{0}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\max\left\{N_{1},N_{2}\right\}
,

•

N_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\Bigl\lceil\log_{2}\frac{2\,w_{\max}(E_{0}({{\mathcal{S}}}))\,e^{{\overline{\mu}}}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Bigr\rceil
,

•

N_{2}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\#\text{EulerTube}+\#\text{Bisection}

•

\#\text{EulerTube}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\Bigl\lceil\log_{2}\frac{8m\,\Delta_{\max}\,e^{{\overline{\mu}}}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Bigr\rceil

•

\#\text{Bisection}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\Biggl\lceil\displaystyle{\max_{i=1,\dots,m,p\in I_{i}}}\left(\log_{2}\Biggl(\frac{\Delta t_{i}}{\,h^{{}^{\text{euler}}}\Bigl(\Delta t_{i},M_{i},p,\delta_{i}^{\prime}\Bigr)}\Biggr)\right)\Biggr\rceil

•

Moreover,

I_{i}=\big[\min_{{\bm{q}}\in\pi(F_{i}({{\mathcal{S}}}))}(\mu_{2}(J_{{\bm{g}}_{i}}({\bm{q}}))),\displaystyle\max_{i=1,\ldots,m}(max\overline{{\bm{\mu}}}_{i}^{2})\big]
,

•

{\overline{\mu}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\max_{i=1,\ldots,m}(max\overline{{\bm{\mu}}}_{i}^{1})
,

•

\Delta_{\max}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\max_{i=1,\dots,m}\delta_{i}
,

•

M_{i}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\|{\bm{g}}_{i}^{[2]}(\pi(F_{i}({{\mathcal{S}}})))\|_{2}
,

•

\delta_{i}^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}{{\texttt{TransformBound}}}\left(\min\!\left(\delta_{i},\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{16me^{{\overline{\mu}}}}\right),\pi,F_{i}({{\mathcal{S}}})\right).

Proof.

N_{1}
is the number of phases needed to satisfy condition (
18
).
Since in each phase we halve
w_{\max}(E_{0}({{\mathcal{S}}}))
, after

N_{1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\Bigl\lceil\log_{2}\frac{2\,w_{\max}(E_{0}({{\mathcal{S}}}))\,e^{{\overline{\mu}}}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Bigr\rceil

phases, condition (
18
) is satisfied.

Next, we consider the phases required for condition (
19
).
Fix a stage
i
.
Note that in each phase, the value
\delta_{i}
is either halved or left unchanged; once
\delta_{i}
satisfies (
19
) it is never modified again.

By condition (
19
),

\delta_{i}<\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{8me^{{\overline{\mu}}}}
and

\delta_{i}\leq\Delta_{\max},

so the number of phases in which
\delta_{i}
is halved is at most

\#\text{EulerTube}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\Bigl\lceil\log_{2}\frac{8m\,\Delta_{\max}\,e^{{\overline{\mu}}}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Bigr\rceil.

Now we count the phases where
\delta_{i}
stays unchanged.
During
Refine
, if
\delta_{i}
is not changed in a phase, then
Bisect
is called in that phase.
Hence it suffices to count the number of calls to
Bisect
.
A call to
Bisect
occurs exactly when the current mini
-
step size, i.e.,
\dfrac{\Delta t_{i}}{2^{\ell}}
, exceeds
h^{{}^{\text{euler}}}
.
Thus we need a lower bound for
h^{{}^{\text{euler}}}
.

Recall that whenever the logarithmic norm
\mu
is
positive, we transform the system by
\pi
, which
yields a new system with a negative logarithmic norm.
Consequently
(see
[
23
, Eq. (12)]
)

h^{{}^{\text{euler}}}(H,{\overline{M}},\mu,\delta)=\min\!\Bigl\{H,\,\frac{2\mu\delta}{M(e^{\mu H}-1)-\mu^{2}\delta}\Bigr\}.

h^{{}^{\text{euler}}}(H,{\overline{M}},\mu,\delta)
is decreasing in
{\overline{M}}
and increasing in
\delta
.
During refinement the set
F_{i}({{\mathcal{S}}})
shrinks, hence

M_{i}=\|{\bm{g}}_{i}^{[2]}(\pi(F_{i}({{\mathcal{S}}})))\|_{2}

provides an upper bound for the parameter
{\overline{M}}
in stage
i
.
Moreover, condition (
19
) guarantees that

\min\!\bigl(\delta_{i},\;\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{16me^{{\overline{\mu}}}}\bigr)
is a lower bound for
\delta(G_{i}({{\mathcal{S}}}))
.
Therefore
\delta_{i}^{\prime}
is a lower bound for the
\delta
-
parameter in stage
i
.
Finally, by definition of
I_{i}
, the parameter
\mu
in the
i
-
th stage always lies in
I_{i}
.

Consequently, for stage
i
the Euler step size is bounded below by

\displaystyle\min_{p\in I_{i}}h^{{}^{\text{euler}}}\!\Bigl(\Delta t_{i},\;M_{i},\;p,\;\delta_{i}^{\prime}\Bigr)
.
Hence the number of calls to
Bisect
for the
i
-
th stage is at most

\max_{p\in I_{i}}\,\log_{2}\Biggl(\frac{\Delta t_{i}}{\,h^{{}^{\text{euler}}}\bigl(\Delta t_{i},M_{i},p,\delta_{i}^{\prime}\bigr)}\Biggr).

Thus,
N_{2}
is the number of phases needed to satisfy
condition (
19
) for every stage.

Q.E.D.

Theorem
4

Let
{\overline{B}}=\mbox{{image}}(\mbox{{{{IVP}}}}(B_{0},H))+Box(\varepsilon)
and

{\overline{\mu}}=\mu_{2}(J({\bm{f}}({\overline{B}})))
.
Then
\mbox{EndCover}(B_{0},H,\varepsilon)
will call
EndEncl
at most

\Theta\!\left(\left(\frac{2w_{\max}(B_{0})e^{{\overline{\mu}}}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{n}\right)

times.

Proof.
Each call of
EndEncl
returns two boxes
{\underline{B}}_{i}
and
{\overline{B}}_{i}
.
We bound the number of indices
i
for which the collection
\{{\underline{B}}_{i}\}
can cover
B_{0}
.

From (
18
), every box
{\underline{B}}_{i}
satisfies a uniform positive bound on its size:

\frac{\varepsilon}{4e^{{\overline{\mu}}H}}<w_{\max}({\underline{B}})<\frac{\varepsilon}{2e^{{\overline{\mu}}H}}
.
Consequently, starting from a initial box
B
, the number of subdivisions needed to obtain a box whose size is below this lower bound is at most

\Bigl\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4\,w_{\max}(B)\,e^{{\overline{\mu}}H}}{\varepsilon}\Bigr\rceil.

Hence, along each coordinate direction, the number of such subdivisions is bounded by the above expression.
In
n
dimensions, it follows that at most

O\!\left(\Bigl(\frac{2\,w_{\max}(B_{0})\,e^{{\overline{\mu}}H}}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{\!n}\right)

boxes are created which completes the proof.

Q.E.D.
