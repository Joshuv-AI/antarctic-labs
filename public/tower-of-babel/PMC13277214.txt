Ethnomedicinal plants used by Guoshan Yao minority communities in the foothills of Nanling Mountain, Guangdong, China
Authors: Dipak Khadka, Baidong Chen, Yong Xie, Kunchun Shui, Xinyi Zhang, Zhifa Liu, Liyuan Song, Chuhui Zhang, Yuanqiu Li, Tien Ming Lee, Wenbo Liao, Shi Shi, Qiang Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Guoshan Yao, a subgroup of the Yao ethnic minority in China, inhabit the biodiverse Nanling Mountain region. Their traditional knowledge (TK) of medicinal plants is increasingly threatened by socio-economic development, generational knowledge loss, and conservation policies. This study documents the medicinal plant diversity, and usage trends, analyzes TK transmission pathways, assesses influencing factors on the medicinal plant use knowledge and compares use knowledge with other Yao groups.MethodsA total of 86 respondents (aged 20–90) from Guoshan Yao households were interviewed in the villages of Nanling National Nature Reserve, Guangdong, China. The Relative Importance (RI) index of the recorded plant species was calculated, negative binomial regression was used to assess factors influencing medicinal plant use knowledge, and the Jaccard index of similarity (JI) was used for the cross cultural analysis.ResultsA total of 61.6% of respondents relied on medicinal plants for primary healthcare; however, 41.8% of respondents reported declining trend in this practice. We documented 224 medicinal plants from 188 genera and 90 families. The most common therapeutic use was for traumatic injuries and orthopedic conditions (71 species, 31.7%). Among the recorded species, Polygala japonica had the highest RI value (2). The negative binomial regression indicated that medicinal plant use knowledge was significantly associated with the source of knowledge transfer, with vertical and multiple sources being significant. Cross-cultural analysis with the Yao of Guangxi and Hunan showed that knowledge of medicinal plants is quite similar among the Yao of Gongcheng Guangxi (JI = 0.18) .ConclusionThe Guoshan Yao maintain substantial TK of medicinal plants, the conservation of which depends on vertical and multiple sources knowledge transmission systems. To conserve this knowledge, we recommend promoting the dissemination of knowledge beyond familial boundaries through community awareness programs, and inter-generational interaction.Keywords: Culture, Ethnomedicine, Plants, Knowledge transfer, Yao

Introduction

Plants have served as a fundamental source of medicine, and have played a crucial part in shaping human societies for millennia [1]. Despite drastic changes in many aspects of human civilization, the use of medicinal plants remains effective in supporting primary health care worldwide [2], underscoring the importance of documenting such knowledge and the factors associated with it.

In China, traditional medicine has increasingly gained recognition as a formal medical system [3]. Medicinal plant knowledge constitutes an integral part of the traditional medicine system (TCM), which is practiced across diverse regions and communities [4]. TCM knowledge originates in traditional villages and forested areas, where it has been transmitted orally from generation to generation for a long time [5]. Typically, older community members, especially within families, are the primary bearers of such traditional knowledge (TK). However, TK transmission systems are now under threat globally [6], particularly in China, where rapid economic development and shifting lifestyles have reduced reliance on TK. The outmigration of youth for education and employment opportunities further weakens intergenerational interaction, thereby endangering the continuity of TK.

The Yao nationality is one of the oldest and most geographically widespread ethnic minority groups in China [7]. The Yao people resides in the mountainous regions of southwest and southern China, with their origins tracing back over 2000 years to Hunan province [8]. To avoid conflicts with the Han nationality, they migrated to the mountainous areas of southern China [9]. Over time, they have developed into four groups and several subgroups, (https://www.neac.gov.cn/seac/ztzl/201806/1067149.shtml) Among these, the Guoshan Yao subgroup, primarily reside in the rugged terrain of the Nanling Mountains of Guangdong province and surrounding areas. It is known for its historical practice of continuously migrating across mountain areas in search of livelihood. For over a thousand years, the Guoshan Yao never ceased their migratory lifestyle. The name “Guoshan Yao” literally means “Crossing Mountain Yao”, reflecting this migratory tradition [10]. The traditional culture of Guoshan Yao has evolved through adaptation to the natural environment, social conditions, and historical memory. Living in floristically rich mountainous regions, the Guoshan Yao have developed their own unique ethnomedicinal system, utilizing local plants for disease treatment and prevention.

Despite their long-established ethnobotanical practices, research on the Guoshan Yao mainly focuses on theoretical aspects such as the technical inheritance of TK [11]. In the context of the other Yao groups, some research on ethnomedicinal plants has been conducted in Guangxi [12, 13], Hunan [14], and Yunnan [15, 16]. This shows the need for research on ethnomedicinal plants knowledge documentation among the Yao of the Guangdong province, China.

Many Yao communities still depend on traditional livelihoods such as farming [17]. Socioeconomic changes and expanding external opportunities have led younger generations to gradually disengage from TK practices. Recent evidence indicates that traditional cultural practices are not being adequately transmitted [18], posing tangible threats to TK preservation among the Yao [19].

This intergenerational transmission gap is worrisome and needs to be addressed promptly to preserve cultural diversity. Research on the use of medicinal plants is limited, and the transmission pathways of the medicinal plants’ knowledge have yet to be assessed among the Yao, particularly in Guoshan Yao. Therefore, our study aims to document the ethnomedicinal plant knowledge of the Guoshan Yao, assess factors supporting this knowledge, and evaluate their knowledge transfer mechanisms. This research provides fundamental data for the development and application of local ethnic medicine, thereby promoting the protection and inheritance of traditional medicinal knowledge. The objectives of our study are: (1) to document the TK of ethnomedicinal plants used, and map the pathways of TK transmission within the Guoshan Yao communities in Guangdong, China, (2) to assess the factors associated with the TK of ethnomedicinal plants used knowledge among the Guoshan Yao, by modeling the relationship between the number of plants reported by each respondents, and predictors age, gender, family size, occupation, primary diseases ailment treatment mode, and knowledge transfer mode using a regression model, (3) to conduct a cross cultural analysis of the ethnomedicinal plants used among the Guoshan Yao Guangdong, Hainan Yao, Gongcheng Guangxi Yao, and Baiku Yao Guangxi China, and (4) to understand the threats to conservation of TK of ethnomedicinal plants which support in the sustainable utilization and conservation of the TK of ethnomedicinal plants among the Guoshan Yao in Guangdong China.

Materials and methods

Study area

We conducted ethnobotanical surveys and specimen collections in five Guoshan Yao settlement sites within the Nanling National Nature Reserve in Guangdong, Southern China. This reserve is critically important for preserving the biodiversity of the Nanling Mountain range, which historically served as a “cultural passway” connecting central and Southern China, underscoring the region’s significance in cultural and ecological studies. The area was designated as a protected zone in 1956. Its climate is subtropical, located at the boundary between southern and northern tropical climates. The region features diverse topographic conditions, including mountains, hills, and basins, that support rich ecological communities with over 2,000 plant species, many of which are medicinal or rare plants. Based on interviews with park staff and a literature review, we selected five Guoshan Yao villages: Luokeng, Luxi, Guikeng, Lianshan, and Taipingdong (Fig. 1).

Map of study area

The study area encompasses 1,160 Guoshan Yao households (HHs), with a total population of 4,895. The elevation of the area ranges from 300 to 1100 m (Table 1). Shao, Huang, Zhao, and Pan are the major local ethnic groups inside the Guoshan Yao. The Guoshan Yao speak the Mienic language which is part of the Hmong-Mien language family (https://glottolog.org/). For the Guoshan Yao people, plants constitute essential livelihood resources, widely used in medicine, food, and crafts. Local economies are primarily driven by agriculture, such as tea cultivation, and ecotourism. However, high levels of youth out-migration have resulted in village populations dominated by elderly residents, thereby intensifying the risk of loss of the intergenerational transmission of TK related to medicinal plants, food, and other resources.

Data collection

We conducted field surveys from January 2024 to October 2025 to document the medicinal plants used. We divided the study sites into five strata based on the Guoshan Yao HHs. First, we performed an observational and free-listing social survey at various locations and made inquiries about medicinal plants in the area, to make a list of different plants, and get ideas on the issues of medicinal plants and their knowledge in the selected villages. Later, to understand the use trend, knowledge transfer, and factors associated with the TK related to medicinal plant use , we used a random sampling method: five villages were chosen as sampling strata based on Guoshan Yao population data, and respondents were randomly selected within each village. Before the survey, each respondent provided consent, and we carefully followed the ethical guidelines of the International Society of Ethnobiology [20]. The interview was conducted with one person per household to prevent peer influence and ensure individual responses.

We interviewed a total of 86 respondents from different HHs, consisting of 64 (74.42%) men and 22 (25.58%) women, aged 20–90 years from the total Guoshan Yao HHs (1160), that accounts to about 7.4% of the total number of Guoshan HHs in the study area, which is above 5% that is acceptable for the ethnobotanical research [21].

In each village, 10–23 respondents were selected from the household data at each site. Most respondents had a primary education (49, 56.98%), and the majority were engaged in agriculture (55, 63.95%) as their primary occupation. We also consulted five key informants who have long worked with medicinal plants in the Yao community to validate the field information. The key informants included local healers, Yao medicinal shopkeepers, the national park authority, and a researcher knowledgeable about the Yao community. Each respondent was asked about their demographic information, the status of medicinal plants used, parts used, modes of use, diseases treated, sources of knowledge, willingness to pass on knowledge, threats to the conservation of traditional knowledge of medicinal plants, and possible ways to preserve this knowledge (Table 2).

Plant identification and voucher collection

After the field interview, voucher specimens were collected from nearby forests and local herbal markets. For specimens that were difficult to identify, follow-up surveys were conducted to ensure correct identification and recording. All collected specimens were identified by Professor Fan Qiang and cross-checked with the valid voucher specimen records in the herbarium of Sun Yat-Sen University, and virtually in iPlant (https://www.plantplus.cn/cn). We also used the eFlora of China (http://www.efloras.org/), the Chinese Virtual Herbarium (https://www.cvh.ac.cn/), World Flora Online (https://www.worldfloraonline.org/), and Plants of the world (https://powo.science.kew.org/) to verify the scientific names and identification of the species. We use Angiosperm Phylogeny Group IV system (APG IV) for the presenting the scientific name in the tables of plant documented. The collected specimen was submitted to the Sun Yat-Sen University herbarium (SYS) for preservation.

Data analysis

The description of diversity, family, species number, and preparation method was documented. The local name (Yao name) of the plnt species recorded in the site are shown on the table using, the international phonetic alphabet (IPA style) following online translator (https://www.internationalphoneticalphabet.org/). For plant use TK transmission, we divided the transmission vector into vertical, oblique, and horizontal, following [22]. The horizontal vectors represent the same generation, downward vertical vectors represent the father and mother, and oblique vectors represent the senior generation from the same family. The knowledge sources are divided into two categories: familial kin and social (non-familial) bonds. The knowledge transfer route for the respondents, and their willingness to transfer, were shown in a diagram using the R program [23].

For the consistency in use of medicinal plants in diseases ailment treated we followed the International Classification of Primary Care (ICPC-2) and categorized the diseases ailment into 13 which include digestive, skin and facial, traumatic injury and orthopedics, rheumatic, gynecological disorders, urinary system, nervous system, pediatric, circulatory system, respiratory system, Immune system, Cancer, and others following [13] with bit modification. The habit of medicinal plants recorded, and their parts used, were shown in an alluvial diagram using Raw Graphs 2.0 [24].

For the recorded medicinal plants, the ethnobotanical indices, the relative importance index (RI) was calculated for all the plants recorded, following [25].

Results

Medicinal plants used status and trend in primary health care

Among the 86 respondents, 53 (61.6%) reported using medicinal plants as their primary method for disease treatment, while 18 (20.9%) relied predominantly on western medicine, and 15 (17.4%) adopted a combination of herbal and western medicine.

Regarding the perceived trends in traditional medicinal plant use, 36 respondents (41.9%) perceived that usage levels have remained consistent, 29 (33.7%) observed a decline, 14 (16.3%) noted an increase, and the remaining 7 (8.1%) reported no noticeable changes.

Medicinal plants recorded

We recorded a total of 224 species belonging to 188 genera and 90 families. Among these, Asteraceae contained the highest number of species (15) followed by Fabaceae (11), Lamiaceae (10) while the genus wise Ardisia was the most species-rich, with (4) species recorded followed by Dioscorea, Ficus, Persicaria, Zanthoxylum (3 each) (Table 3). These medicinal plants are mostly crushed, then mixed with water and boiled or soaked in water for external application, such as topical application, used in washing or bathing, or made into soup or wine for internal administration.

Knowledge transfer mode

Vertical transmission within families was the most prevalent method of knowledge transfer reported by 39 respondents (45.4%) followed by oblique transmission (20, 23.3%), horizontal transmission (18, 20.9%), and multiple transmission (9, 10.5%). The multiple transmission category included combinations of oblique and vertical (5, 5.8%), horizontal, oblique, and vertical (2, 2.3%), as well as horizontal and vertical, horizontal and oblique (1, 1.2%) each. Overall, familial kin knowledge transfer accounted for 75.6% of transmission modes. Father and unspecified elderly family members were the primary sources of knowledge transmission to the younger generation, followed by villagers and grandfather (Fig. 5).

Willingness to transmit traditional medicinal plants knowledge

The majority of respondents (54, 62.8%) expressed willingness to pass their share their medicinal plant knowledge broadly, while a smaller subset (11, 12.8%) preferred to transmit knowledge exclusively to their sons. Threats to the conservation of traditional medicinal plant knowledge

We identified five broad threats to the conservation of TK on medicinal plants: sociocultural, institutional, demographic, economic, and environmental (Fig. 8). Among these, sociocultural threats were most prominent. A significant proportion of youth displayed limited interest in learning TK on medicinal plants, 28 respondents (32.6%) reported that their children lacked motivation to acquire or use such knowledge. Furthermore, 29 respondents (33.7%) reported a decline in the use of medicinal plants within the community. Knowledge transmission at the household level was also limited, with 33 respondents (38.37%) noting infrequent sharing of medicinal plant knowledge within families.

Threats in conservation of traditional knowledge prepared based on the responses of respondents and field observation

Notably, traditional healers and individuals possessing extensive plant knowledge generally refrained from disseminating their expertise beyond family or village boundaries. They regarded such knowledge as a cultural heritage to be safeguarded within kinship networks, expressing concerns about potential misuse if shared externally. Additionally, the increasing urban orientation of traditional knowledge—where its commercial and professional value is more recognized—has reduced its transmission in rural settings. Compounding this issue, most experienced village doctors have retired without adequate successors. Institutional challenges include restrictions imposed by park management on the collection of rare, endangered, and economically important species, such as orchids, and other endemic species, as well as a systematic lack of proper documentation and preservation of local medicinal plant knowledge.

Demographically, out-migration, driven by employment and educational opportunities, has markedly increased (38.4% of respondents’ HHs, including youth and knowledgeable people).

Environmental threats include the decline of key medicinal plant populations, caused by the spread of invasive species, inadequate fire management, and increasing canopy density that reduces light availability necessary for the growth of understory medicinal herbs. Finally, economic threats include the lack of cultivation of medicinal plants and their linkages to viable income-generating opportunities.

Strategies for conservation of traditional medicinal plant knowledge

The respondents proposed nine strategies to promote the conservation of TK on medicinal plants. The most widely supported approach was the enhancement of intergenerational interaction to facilitate knowledge transmission, followed by the conservation and sustainable use of medicinal plant resources. Other important strategies included the systematic documentation and scientific research of TK, creating economic incentives by linking medicinal plant knowledge to income-generating opportunities, and promoting the cultivation of medicinal species. Additionally, respondents emphasized the need to strengthen public awareness and education, implement policy measures that integrate indigenous stakeholders into conservation initiatives, establish specialized Yao medicine hospitals, and ensure that protected areas prioritize the preservation of medicinal plant knowledge and related practices (Fig. 9).

Discussions

Changing patterns and drivers of medicinal plant use

Although our study area is located in rural Southern China, only 61.6% of respondents rely on medicinal plants for primary health care. This proportion is notably lower than previous reports, which stated that 80% of the rural population worldwide uses medicinal plants [28]. This change may reflect the rapid socioeconomic development occurring in rural China, which has changed people’s lifestyles and preferences [29].

Over 33% of participants reported a decline in medicinal plant utilization, attributing this shift to the increasing accessibility and perceived efficacy of allopathic (Western) medicine, which is often regarded as providing more rapid symptomatic relief. Additional factors include a diminishing number of traditional medicine practitioners in rural communities, disruptions in the inter-generational transfer of ethnomedical knowledge, and governmental restrictions on harvesting particular medicinal plant taxa due to biodiversity conservation initiatives in PA.

In contrast, some participants reported increased use of medicinal plants, particularly for chronic conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, where TCM is considered more effective [30]. Some respondents also described integrating medicinal plant therapies with Western treatments, particularly in cases where conventional pharmacotherapy yields sub-optimal results [31].

Diversity of medicinal plants

The number of medicinal plant species documented in this study is comparatively lower than that reported in some broader regional ethnobotanical surveys [14, 32]. This discrepancy is expected given the focused scope of our research at a local community level, involving a smaller study population and fewer specialist practitioners. Firstly, improved transportation infrastructure in the study area facilitates greater access to modern healthcare facilities, potentially reducing reliance on traditional plant-based remedies for primary care. Secondly, the study sites are situated within a Protected Area (PA), where biodiversity conservation measures may restrict the harvesting of certain species and where local communities may not fully perceive or utilize medicinal plants as tangible economic assets, thereby affecting the visibility and transmission of related knowledge.

Our study recorded diverse medicinal plants, with Asteraceae, and Fabaceae hosting the most number of the plants, related with the use value, and availability as these are the top most used as well as top most species rich family in China [33]. Ardisia genus hosting most species might be related with the use of the species of this genus such as Ardisia gigantifolia, Ardisia crenata are used in treatment of physical diseases such as rheumatism, and various inflammatory diseases and is widely used by Zhuang people in their traditional medicine [34]. The similarity on the use of plants among Yao and Zhuang people might be shaped by their common habitat as both people reside together in Nanling corridor [35]. Habit wise herbs are mostly use this is related with the easy use, their east accessibility, and processing [36]. The most prevalent therapeutic applications in our study were for treating digestive system disorders, skin ailments, and traumatic injuries, a finding consistent with reports from Yao communities in Hunan [14].This similarity is likely from comparable ecological and livelihood contexts. Both regions feature mountainous terrain with dense forests and a humid climate, where local residents frequently engage in forestry and traverse difficult landscapes. Such an environment makes skin diseases, musculoskeletal pain, and fractures common health concerns, directly shaping the local pharmacopoeia. Therefore, plants with the highest Relative Importance (RI) values, such as Heptapleurum heptaphyllum and Polygala japonica which are both specifically used to treat rheumatism and fractures, are well-adapted to this context. Their high cultural importance can thus be attributed to perceived efficacy, local availability, and palatability [37], Heptapleurum heptaphyllum is particularly common in the local forests, with studies having confirmed that it substantial amounts of bioactive compounds such as oleanolic acid, Oleanonic acid, heptoleoside D effective against a variety of diseases [38].

The preparation and administration methods further reflect both environmental adaptations and local cultural practices. A significant proportion of recorded remedies (approximately 63.84%) involve external applications, primarily as medicinal baths, poultices, or liniments made by soak plant materials in alcohol. For example, Euphorbia hirta is decocted for baths to alleviate rheumatism, while crushed Ardisia brevicaulis and A. crenata subsp. crenataare infused in alcohol for topical application on wounds. Asarum cardiophyllumis also used in wine as a mouth rinse to treat toothache. Beyond external use, the preparation of medicinal wines for internal consumption is a prominent practice, closely linked to a local culture where alcohol consumption is common, especially among men. It is widely believed that alcohol extraction enhances the bioavailability of the plants’ active compounds. This is exemplified by species such as Nanhaia speciosa and Melastoma sanguineum, which are commonly steeped in wine and consumed for their believed effect of improving male sexual function (tonify yang). These plants use patterns among the Guoshan Yao, particularly in the processing and treatment of diseases and ailments, demonstrate a direct, pragmatic adaptation to their local environment, highlighting their unique livelihood and culture.

Cross cultural analysis

The medicinal plants reported shares more similarity with the study of Gongcheng, northeastern Guangxi [13], followed by Baiku Yao communities in Guangxi province [12] and Jianghua, Hunan, China [14]. More similarities with Guangxi might be related to the proximity of the people from our study area. These differences might also be influenced by the lifestyles of the two types of Yao from different locations. Furthermore, the similarities in climatic conditions across both areas support the provision of habitat for the same plant species. Nevertheless, the recorded medicinal flora demonstrates notable diversity and a high degree of regional specificity, with 94 unique species used in the area compare to other Yao region. This unique botanical resource uses, sustained by the rich biodiversity of the Nanling Mountain Range hotspot, forms a distinct and valuable component of the local Yao people’s ethnomedicinal heritage.

Overall, there is greater variation in medicinal plants in these four areas. Our study showcases the huge contribution of the Yao people to TCM in China. This study has further widened our understanding of the Yao medicinal plant systems in China.

Transmission and preservation of traditional medicinal knowledge

Our study assessed the dynamics of traditional knowledge (TK) transmission regarding medicinal plant use within the Guoshan Yao community. Similar to patterns reported elsewhere [39], knowledge is primarily transferred within family networks, with transmission pathways being closely tied to specific plants and locales. Notably, male family members particularly fathers and grandfathers were cited as the main sources, indicating a more active role in medicinal plant use among men in this community. Approximately 75% of knowledge transmission occurred through vertical (parent-to-child) or oblique (other elder-to-child) pathways. This pattern is more favorable than a scenario dominated by horizontal (peer-to-peer) transfer, though a significant portion (over 19%) of knowledge was still acquired from fellow villagers.

These findings underscore that vertical and diversified transmission pathways are fundamental to sustaining TK. However, a key concern remains regarding transmission efficacy, as research suggests cultural knowledge among the Yao is not always effectively passed to the younger generation (Ning [18]This highlights the need for knowledge dissemination at multiple levels—from parents and extended family to community networks. The role of parents is especially critical, as they serve as primary role models whose practices are often adopted by their children [40, 41]. Therefore, while familial transmission forms the foundational mechanism, broader and more intentional efforts, including enhanced horizontal knowledge sharing, are necessary to raise community-wide awareness and support the long-term conservation of this medicinal plant knowledge.

Threats and strategies for traditional knowledge conservation

The TK associated with medicinal plants in the study area is facing multifaceted pressures from industrialization, agriculture revolution, migration, urbanization, economic globalization, and other forces of the modernization period [42]. Our results demonstrated two threats: decreasing availability of of common medicinal plant resources [43] and a growing disinterest among the younger generation [44]. Additionally, regulations within the PA that restrict the collection of certain medicinal species have limited local access, pushing communities toward commercial alternatives. The concentration of knowledgeable practitioners is reluctantly sharing their knowledge, and lots of traditional experts, doctors, are city-centered, further exacerbates erosion. The Guoshan Yao community needs to recognized that this knowledge constitutes an integral part of their cultural heritage, the conservation of which is essential for maintaining biocultural diversity in this biodiversity hotspot [45].

To address these challenges, we recommended a series of integrated measures. Regular dialogues between community members, local leaders, and PA authorities should be facilitated to incorporate local knowledge into the conservation of rare and endemic species. Sustainable harvesting techniques and the identification of alternative resources should be promoted. For instance, sustainable harvesting techniques and the identification of alternative resources should be promoted. locals reported that certain protective measures (e.g., canopy tree planting) have inadvertently shaded out medicinal herbs, and bans on traditional weed control have allowed invasive species to spread issues that should be reconsidered in management plans. For rare species, ex situ cultivation could be explored without compromising conservation goals. Knowledgeable practitioners should be encouraged and incentivized to share their expertise. Based on field observation and interviews, we suggest connecting medicinal plants with i local livelihood opportunities, such as integrating them into eco-tourism or cultural tourism activities. Promoting inter-generational knowledge exchange through structured community dialogues and supporting sustainable harvesting practices can help embed this TK within contemporary economic and cultural frameworks, thereby fostering its preservation.

Limitations of the study

We conducted this study in five primary Guoshan Yao villages in the foothills of Nanling Mountain. The sample size might not fully represent the variation in knowledge among the Guoshan Yao people. The information is gathered through recall surveys, observation, and key informant interviews; therefore, some important medicinal plants could be missed during the survey. The medicinal plants identified are from both systematic checklists and free-listing methods; hence, we were unable to calculate the indices based on individual respondents’ ethnobotanical data. We have documented the medicinal plants used, knowledge transfer, threats, and conservation of TK related to plants. Additionally, pharmacological studies, such as phytochemical analysis of these plants, could enhance understanding of these species and potentially lead to new medicine discoveries.

Conclusion

Our study on ethnomedicinal plants use knowledge among the Guoshan Yao revealed that the Guoshan Yao community possesses a rich TK system. This TK is in decreasing trend. TK is dependent on the knowledge transfer pathways. This valuable knowledge should be preserved and validated through various pharmacological studies and trials. It should be integrated into the local curriculum and communicated through the media. The process of transmitting TK should involve multiple pathways and extend beyond family members.
