Sensitivity analysis for causal mediation: bridge score, sharp sensitivity bounds, and calibration
Yuki Ohnishi, Fan Li

Abstract
Causal mediation analysis decomposes the total treatment effect into a portion operating through a hypothesized mediator and a residual direct portion. Identification of natural direct and indirect effects typically rests on the mediator stage of sequential ignorability, which cannot be empirically verified and requires explicit sensitivity analysis. We introduce the bridge score , a low-dimensional vector formed from the two treatment-specific mediator densities at a common mediator value, and show that it is a balancing score for the mediator stage of sequential ignorability. Conditional on the bridge score, we then derive a sharp pointwise envelope on the unidentified mediator-outcome confounding function in terms of two interpretable latent confounding parameters. To make the bound operational for sensitivity analysis, we further introduce two calibration approaches. The first is benchmark calibration against an observed covariate, including a rank-based version that is invariant to monotone re-expressions of the benchmark; the second is residual budget calibration based on residual outcome variation. Finally, we show how the pointwise bound can be operationalized for inference through a scalar functional reduction and a Bayesian g-computation algorithm that propagates all sources of uncertainty into posterior draws of the mediation effect estimates.



Keywords: causal mediation analysis; sensitivity analysis; Bayesian g-computation; bridge score; sharp bound; benchmark calibration

Introduction

Causal mediation analysis decomposes the average (total) treatment effect into the portion that operates through a hypothesized mediator (referred to as the natural indirect effect) and the portion that works around the mediator (referred to as the natural direct effect). Identification of the natural direct and indirect effects typically rests on the
sequential ignorability
assumption
(
8
)
. That is, treatment assignment must be ignorable, and the mediator must be ignorable given treatment and baseline covariates. The second condition is rarely supported by design, and any unmeasured factor jointly driving the mediator and the outcome leads to its violation. Hence, there remains substantial interest in pursuing a calibrated sensitivity analysis for the no mediator-outcome confounding assumption to strengthen the credibility of causal mediation analysis.

An emerging literature has developed sensitivity analysis for violations of sequential ignorability.
8
introduced a parametric
\rho
-based parameter tied to the residual correlation between mediator and outcome errors;
21
developed semiparametric, multiply robust identification with a sensitivity layer through a user-specified additive confounding function;
26
developed bias formulas and effect decompositions, complemented by the sensitivity analysis
(
24
)
and bivariate normal latent confounder analyses
(
10
;
13
)
.
6
, as well as follow-up work
(
18
, e.g.,)
, established risk ratio sharp bounds under interpretable parameters for the strength of confounding; Bayesian treatments include
14
;
4
, which emphasize prior factorization into likelihood and sensitivity.

Despite this progress, three statistical challenges remain for credible, nonparametric sensitivity analysis of mediator ignorability on the additive effect measure scale, which is arguably the most commonly used scale in scientific research. First, existing parameterizations face a tension between interpretability and localization. Scalar sensitivity parameters are easy to report, but they collapse violations that may vary with the treatment arm, mediator value, and covariate profile; fully localized sensitivity functions are more faithful to the structure of mediator-outcome confounding, but are difficult to elicit, estimate, and calibrate when the covariate is moderately high-dimensional. What has not been fully developed is a general low-dimensional reduction of the sensitivity object that preserves the mediator information relevant to the cross-world natural effect functional without requiring a full covariate specification.
Second, although sharp nonparametric bounds on mediator-outcome confounding under interpretable parameters for the strength of confounding exist on the risk ratio scale and are most natural for binary outcomes
(
6
;
18
)
, no analogous sharp additive (mean difference) envelope is available. Additive sensitivity in the existing literature is handled either by parametric bias formulas that require a specific latent confounding structure
(
26
;
10
;
13
)
or by Manski-style bounds that use no sensitivity parameters at all and are likely too wide and hence uninformative
(
19
)
.
Third, operational benchmark calibration for sensitivity analysis on mediator ignorability is fragile. Continuous benchmarks calibrated on their raw scale make the implied envelope depend on the analyst’s units or support width, while the E-value style scale coherent summaries
(
23
)
collapse the full envelope into a single robustness scalar and are generally more natural for binary outcomes.

We address these challenges through several contributions. First, we propose the concept of the
bridge score
, which is a two-dimensional vector formed from both treatment-specific mediator densities at a common mediator value. It places the identified outcome regression and the unidentified mediator-outcome confounding correction on the same low-dimensional scale, while encoding the arm asymmetry in the estimand. In contrast to the density-based methods
(
22
;
7
)
, which collapse the two arm-specific densities into a single inverse weight for natural effect estimation, the bridge score retains them as a vector and uses them as a balancing score on which the unidentified sensitivity function is defined. Second, we derive a sharp pointwise additive envelope for the bridge score sensitivity function, parametrized by a residual selection ratio and an outcome range parameter. The result is the additive scale counterpart of the multiplicative risk ratio bound of
6
, providing, for any outcome type, the sharp sensitivity envelope that the risk ratio bound provides for binary outcomes. Third, we provide a benchmark-based calibration against an observed comparator covariate, with a rank-based procedure that is invariant to monotone re-expressions of the benchmark. As a complement, we also provide an alternative residual budget calibration based on bridge score residual outcome variation.
Finally, we operationalize inference through a scalar functional reduction and a Bayesian g-computation implementation that propagates uncertainty in the bridge score, calibration quantities and sensitivity corrections into posterior draws of natural direct and indirect effect estimates.

1.1
Positioning relative to existing sensitivity analyses

Among the prior work surveyed above, two families are widely used in applied mediation sensitivity analysis and warrant explicit contrast with our framework. One is the parametric analysis based on
\rho
proposed by
8
; the other is the risk ratio sharp bound family of
6
;
18
, together with its scalar E-value reduction
(
20
)
. We position the bridge score framework as a complementary tool rather than a replacement.
The
\rho
-based analysis of
8
operates on the additive scale but requires fully specified parametric models for both the outcome and the mediator, a restrictive assumption that may fail under model misspecification and that constrains the form of mediator-outcome confounding the analyst can encode. Our bound is nonparametric. It imposes no functional-form restriction on the outcome regression or the mediator law, and the sensitivity parameters
(\eta_{a},\gamma_{a})
are interpretable features of an arbitrary latent confounder rather than residual-correlation parameters in a working parametric model. The risk ratio bound of
20
is the natural sharp envelope on the multiplicative scale, especially for binary outcomes, and we supply the missing additive counterpart for any outcome type, equipped with a dimension-reduction property and a scale-coherent calibration that the multiplicative framework cannot naturally accommodate. Given such considerations, our framework has the following three differentiating features:

1.

Scale.
The sharp bound of
6
operates on the risk ratio scale and is most natural for binary outcomes, where the risk ratio is the canonical effect measure; applying it to an outcome requires either dichotomization or a log-linear approximation. Theorem
2
supplies an additive analogue on the mean difference scale, so for any outcome type, the analyst no longer has to choose between scale coherence and access to a sharp envelope.

2.

Dimension reduction.
Existing sensitivity analyses do not use the mediator-density bridge score as the localization scale; in common implementations their sensitivity parameters are global or attached to the full covariate/model scale. Proposition
2
shows that, for the additive bound, calibration on the bridge score can deliver a numerically tighter envelope. The residual selection parameter at the bridge score level is no larger than its worst-case full covariate counterpart within the corresponding stratum, with an analogous outcome range comparison under the additional condition stated in the proposition. The bridge score reduction is therefore not only conceptually cleaner but remains informative quantitatively, and it is what makes the additive bound practical when the baseline covariate vector is moderately high-dimensional.

3.

Scale coherent calibration.
Operational calibration is where benchmark-based methods are most often criticized. Comparing latent confounding to a continuous observed covariate makes the resulting envelope depend on the analyst’s choice of units or transformation, and E-value style summaries trade scale dependence for a single robustness scalar at the cost of the two-parameter structure of the underlying bound. In comparison, the rank-based version of our benchmark calibration (Section
4.1
) is invariant to monotone re-expressions of the benchmark and stable under heavy-tailed benchmarks, while preserving the separately interpretable
\eta_{a}
and
\gamma_{a}
inputs that the E-value collapses. Among sensitivity analyses formulated on an additive scale, neither
8
nor the weighting-based approach of
7
provides scale-coherent benchmark calibration of this form.

Data structure, estimands and assumptions

We consider independent observations
O_{i}=(X_{i},A_{i},M_{i},Y_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, drawn from a common superpopulation. Here
A_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is the treatment assignment,
X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
is a vector of pretreatment covariates,
M_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
is a single post-treatment mediator, and
Y_{i}
is the outcome. We suppress the unit index when no confusion can arise.
Let
\nu
be a
\sigma
-finite dominating measure on the support
\mathcal{M}
of
M
such that, for each
a\in\{0,1\}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the conditional law of
M
given
(A=a,X=x)
has density or mass function
f_{a}(m\mid x)=dF_{M\mid A=a,X=x}(m)/d\nu
for
m\in\mathcal{M}
.
This notation covers both discrete and continuous mediators. For
a\in\{0,1\}
and
m\in\mathcal{M}
, let
M(a)
denote the potential mediator under treatment
a
and
Y(a,m)
the potential outcome under treatment
a
and mediator value
m
. Our primary cross-world estimand is the mediated mean under treatment with the mediator set to its natural value under control,
\theta=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,M(0))\}
.
Let
\delta_{a}=\mathbb{E}\{Y(a,M(a))\}
denote the arm-specific observed world means for
a\in\{0,1\}
.
The natural direct effect (NDE) and indirect effect (NIE) on the additive scale are
\mathrm{NDE}=\theta-\delta_{0}
and
\mathrm{NIE}=\delta_{1}-\theta
,
so that
\mathrm{TE}=\delta_{1}-\delta_{0}=\mathrm{NDE}+\mathrm{NIE}
.

We invoke consistency, positivity, and treatment randomization. Treatment randomization is the first stage of sequential ignorability; the second stage, mediator ignorability, is intentionally not assumed and is replaced below by sensitivity functions.

Assumption 1

(Consistency)
.

For all
a\in\{0,1\}
and
m\in\mathcal{M}
,
A=a\implies M(a)=M
, and
A=a,\ M=m\implies Y(a,m)=Y
.

Assumption 2

(Positivity)
.

For each
x
in the support of
X
,
0<\mathbb{P}(A=1\mid X=x)<1
. For each
a\in\{0,1\}
and each
(x,m)
in the support relevant to
\theta
,
f_{a}(m\mid x)>0
.

Assumption 3

(Treatment randomization)
.

For all
a,a^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
and
m\in\mathcal{M}
,
\{Y(a,m),M(a^{\prime}),X\}\perp\!\!\!\perp A
.

Consistency links observed variables to potential outcomes at the realized treatment and mediator. Assumption
3
is guaranteed by randomized treatment assignment. Standard mediation analysis would add mediator ignorability,
Y(a^{\prime},m)\perp\!\!\!\perp M(a)\mid A=a,X
; the rest of the paper studies departures from that strong cross-world identification condition.

3
Bridge score for mediator ignorability

The cross-world quantity
Y(1,M(0))
depends simultaneously on the control mediator law and on the treated outcome regression. This motivates a two-component score that retains both mediator laws at the same
(m,x)
.

Definition 1

(Bridge score)
.

For
m\in\mathcal{M}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
, define the bridge score vector

B(m,x)=\bigl(B_{0}(m,x),B_{1}(m,x)\bigr)=\bigl(f_{0}(m\mid x),f_{1}(m\mid x)\bigr).

For a fixed mediator value, write
B(m)=B(m,X)
.
When the mediator is observed at its realized value, we write
B(M)=B(M,X)
.

The first component is the control mediator law evaluated at
m
, and the second component is the treated mediator law evaluated at the same
m
. This score is identified from the observed mediator distribution and keeps the two nuisance laws needed for
\theta
on one scale.
The term “bridge” here refers to the role of
B(m,x)
in coupling the two arm-specific mediator laws at the same mediator value
m
, thereby providing the common balancing scale on which both the identified regression and the unidentified sensitivity correction are indexed. It is unrelated to the “bridge function” of proximal causal inference
(
15
)
, which is a nuisance function solving an integral equation in the proxy variable identification problem.

Lemma 1

(Balancing property and score-level ignorability)
.

Fix
a\in\{0,1\}
and
m\in\mathcal{M}
. Then
f(x\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b)=f(x\mid A=a,B(m)=b)
for all
(x,b)
in the support; equivalently, conditional on
(A,B(m))
, the event
M=m
carries no additional information about
X
. Furthermore, if mediator ignorability
Y(a,m)\perp\!\!\!\perp M\mid A=a,X
holds, then
Y(a,m)\perp\!\!\!\perp M\mid A=a,B(m)
.

The proof is provided in Supplementary Material Section
A.1
.
The bridge score plays for the mediator a role analogous to the propensity score for treatment assignment
(
17
)
. The analogy is about balancing, not about treatment assignment. Once
(A,B(m))
is fixed, conditioning on the event
M=m
does not further change the distribution of
X
. Unlike a propensity score,
B(m,x)
is indexed by the mediator value and is generally vector-valued, because the natural effect target combines the control mediator law and treated outcome regression. It also differs from a principal score
(
5
, e.g.,)
. That is, the bridge score is built from identified mediator densities rather than from the unidentified joint law of
(M(0),M(1))
.

Next, the primitive unidentified objects are mediator-outcome confounding functions at the score level.

Definition 2

(bridge score sensitivity function)
.

For
a\in\{0,1\}
and
m\in\mathcal{M}
, define

\Delta_{a}(m,b)=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b\}-\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=a,B(m)=b\}.

(1)

The function
\Delta_{a}(m,b)
measures the residual confounding induced by conditioning on the observed mediator value
M=m
within arm
a
, after conditioning on the bridge score. The subscript
a
indexes the arm in which this mediator selection is evaluated; the treatment for the potential outcome remains fixed at
1
because the target
\theta
uses
Y(1,M(0))
. The analogue indexed by full covariates is

\Delta_{a}^{\ast}(m,x)=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=a,M=m,X=x\}-\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=a,X=x\}.

Both functions vanish under exact mediator ignorability.

Proposition 1

(Projection of the full confounding function onto the bridge score)
.

For fixed
(a,m)
,
\Delta_{a}(m,b)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_{a}^{\ast}(m,X)\mid A=a,B(m)=b\right].

The proof is provided in Supplementary Material Section
A.2
.
By Proposition
1
, the bridge score sensitivity function is the conditional expectation projection of the full confounding structure indexed by covariates onto the score.
Thus, the bridge score sensitivity function is not an ad hoc simplification; rather, it is a principled, low-dimensional summary of the full confounding structure induced by the bridge score.
12
proposed a similar sensitivity analysis based on the propensity score for unmeasured treatment-outcome confounding. In our setting, the bridge score sensitivity function plays the corresponding role for mediator ignorability.
To proceed, we define the observed outcome regression on the treated arm
\mu_{1}(m,b)=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid A=1,M=m,B(m)=b)
, and we obtain the following identification result.

Theorem 1

(Identification under bridge score sensitivity functions)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
3
hold. Then

\displaystyle\theta=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Bigl\{\mu_{1}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)-\Delta_{1}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)+\Delta_{0}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)\Bigr\}f_{0}(m\mid X)\,\nu(dm)\right].

(2)

The proof is provided in Supplementary Material Section
A.3
.
Theorem
1
places the identified component
\mu_{1}
and the unidentified correction
(\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1})
on the same bridge score scale. The two corrections have different roles.
\Delta_{1}
adjusts the treated regression for conditioning on the observed mediator inside the treated arm, whereas
\Delta_{0}
adjusts the corresponding transport under the control mediator law. The bridge score is useful for sensitivity analysis because it reduces the covariate dimension while retaining the information of the mediator law at each treatment arm, which determines where the natural effect functional places weight. Thus, calibration can remain local in
(m,b)
without requiring a sensitivity function indexed by full covariates, making the sensitivity parameters easier to elicit, report, and compare across analyses while still allowing confounding strength to vary over the mediator distribution.

3.1
Sharp sensitivity bounds based on the bridge score

We next derive a sharp bound for the bridge score sensitivity functions. For bounding only, suppose there exists a latent scalar confounder
U
such that the residual dependence of
Y(1,m)
on the mediator within arm
a
is explained by
(B(m),U)
.

Assumption 4

(Latent bridge score representation for bounding)
.

For each
a\in\{0,1\}
and each
m\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists a latent scalar random variable
U
such that
Y(1,m)\perp\!\!\!\perp M\mid A=a,\,B(m),\,U
, with
F_{U\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b}\ll F_{U\mid A=a,B(m)=b}
. For each
a\in\{0,1\}
and each
(m,b,u)
in the relevant support, let

\psi_{a}(u;m,b)=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=a,\,B(m)=b,\,U=u\}

denote the arm-specific regression of the treated potential outcome on the latent confounder within a bridge score stratum.

Assumption
4
is used only to define a sensitivity bound. It does not alter the observed data model, and it does not require
U
to be observed.
For
a\in\{0,1\}
and
(m,b)
, define the arm-specific residual selection parameter

\gamma_{a}(m,b)=\sup_{u}\frac{dF_{U\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b}}{dF_{U\mid A=a,B(m)=b}}(u)\;\geq\;1,

(3)

and the arm-specific outcome range parameter

\eta_{a}(m,b)=\sup_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b)-\inf_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b)\;\geq\;0.

(4)

The parameter
\gamma_{a}(m,b)
measures how much conditioning on the mediator event
M=m
tilts the distribution of the latent confounder
U
within arm
a
, after already conditioning on the bridge score
b
. In this sense, it quantifies the magnitude of the residual selection effect induced by
collider bias
at the mediator level. The parameter
\eta_{a}(m,b)
measures the additive spread of the
U
-specific mean of
Y(1,m)
within the same bridge score stratum, and therefore quantifies how strongly the latent confounder can affect the outcome once
m
and
b
are fixed. Together,
\gamma_{a}(m,b)
and
\eta_{a}(m,b)
capture the two ingredients required for bias, which are residual selection on
U
and outcome relevance of
U
, respectively.

Given these two sensitivity parameters, the following theorem provides the sharp bound for the sensitivity function
\Delta_{a}(m,b)
.

Theorem 2

(Sharp additive bridge score bound)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
3
and
4
hold. Then, for every
a\in\{0,1\}
and every
(m,b)
in the relevant support,

\bigl|\Delta_{a}(m,b)\bigr|\leq\Xi_{a}(m,b):=\eta_{a}(m,b)\,\frac{\gamma_{a}(m,b)-1}{\gamma_{a}(m,b)},

(5)

and the bound is sharp.

The proof is provided in Supplementary Material Section
A.4
.
Theorem
2
presents an additive bound analogue of the risk ratio scale bound of
6
, adapted to the bridge score formulation and to the mean difference scale. The parameters
(\gamma_{a},\eta_{a})
are interpretable quantities about the strength of unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding within bridge score strata. Here, sharpness is important because it pins down the exact admissible range of the primitive sensitivity function
\Delta_{a}(m,b)
at each stratum. For fixed
(\gamma_{a},\eta_{a})
, the envelope cannot be narrowed without adding additional assumptions. In particular, the bound in Theorem
2
is sharp in the sense that, for every admissible pair
(\gamma_{a},\eta_{a})
, there exists a valid latent confounder
U
and a joint law of
(Y,M,U)
consistent with the observed data distribution such that
|\Delta_{a}(m,b)|
exactly equals
\eta(\gamma_{a}-1)/\gamma_{a}
. Finally, no observed data marginal is fixed for this sharpness statement.

3.2
Why bridge score sensitivity parameters can be tighter

Theorem
2
is sharp conditional on the bridge score parameters. A separate question is whether those parameters should be elicited at the full covariate level or after compression to
B(m)
. The bridge score helps because it balances the observed covariates with respect to the mediator event, so residual selection parameters defined after conditioning on
B(m)
can be no larger than worst-case parameters at the covariate level within the corresponding score stratum.

Proposition 2

(bridge score sensitivity parameters are tighter)
.

Fix
a\in\{0,1\}
and
m\in\mathcal{M}
. Define the
sensitivity parameters at the covariate level

\displaystyle\gamma_{a}^{\ast}(m,x)=\sup_{u}\frac{dF(u\mid A{=}a,M{=}m,X{=}x)}{dF(u\mid A{=}a,X{=}x)},\qquad\eta_{a}^{\ast}(m,x)=\sup_{u}\psi_{a}^{\ast}(u;m,x)-\inf_{u}\psi_{a}^{\ast}(u;m,x),

where
\psi_{a}^{\ast}(u;m,x)=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=a,X=x,U=u\}
.
Then

\gamma_{a}(m,b)\leq\sup_{x:\,B(m,x)=b}\gamma_{a}^{\ast}(m,x).

(6)

Additionally, assuming
X\perp\!\!\!\perp U\mid A=a,B(m)=b
,
we have

\eta_{a}(m,b)\leq\sup_{x:\,B(m,x)=b}\eta_{a}^{\ast}(m,x).

(7)

The proof is provided in Supplementary Material Section
A.5
.
When the analyst specifies uniform scalar sensitivity parameters
(\gamma,\eta)
, which is the standard mode of use, doing so at the bridge score level produces a bound that is tighter than the same specification at the full covariate level. The improvement is largest when there is substantial heterogeneity in
\gamma_{a}^{\ast}
or
\eta_{a}^{\ast}
across
x
values that are mapped to the same bridge score, i.e., when the covariates contain information about
M
that is redundant once the mediator densities
(f_{0}(m\mid x),f_{1}(m\mid x))
are known.

Remark 1

(On the assumption
X\perp\!\!\!\perp U\mid A,B(m)
)
.

The inequality (
6
) for
\gamma_{a}
holds unconditionally, as a direct consequence of the balancing property of the bridge score.
The inequality (
7
) for
\eta_{a}
, by contrast, requires the additional assumption
X\perp\!\!\!\perp U\mid A=a,B(m)=b
,
which asserts that observed and unobserved confounders decouple within a bridge score stratum. This assumption is not implied by the balancing property, because the balancing property governs the joint law of
(X,M)
given
(A,B(m))
but says nothing about
U
.
It is plausible when the unmeasured confounder acts through mechanisms orthogonal to the observed covariates—for instance, a latent biological or behavioral trait that is not captured by the baseline
X
—and becomes less plausible when
U
is strongly correlated with components of
X
, even after adjusting for the assignment and the bridge score. In practice, this asymmetry means that
\gamma_{a}
specified by the bridge score is unambiguously tighter than its counterpart at the covariate level, while
\eta_{a}
specified by the bridge score is tighter only under an additional decoupling condition.

4
Calibration of the sharp bound

The parameters in Theorem
2
are interpretable features of latent confounders but unidentifiable. We introduce two complementary calibration procedures for implementing the proposed sensitivity analysis framework. The benchmark approach calibrates the latent confounder against an observed covariate
W
. Specifically, auxiliary outcome regressions and conditional laws of
W
provide observed analogues of the outcome range and residual selection parameters. This makes the sensitivity inputs comparable to a known covariate while preserving the two ingredients of the sharp bound. Another calibration approach, residual budget calibration, instead scales
\eta_{a}
by bridge score residual outcome variation and varies
\gamma_{a}
over a substantive grid for sensitivity analysis.

4.1
Benchmark-based calibration

Under the benchmark-based calibration, we first choose an observed benchmark covariate
W
as a substantive proxy for residual mediator-outcome confounding. As in sensitivity analysis outside the mediation context
(
9
;
3
)
, this is only a calibration device. Auxiliary regressions and conditional laws of
W
provide observed analogues
\widehat{\eta}_{a,W}(m,b)
and
\widehat{\gamma}_{a,W}(m,b)
against which strengths of the latent confounder can be calibrated. The outcome range analogue comes from auxiliary outcome regressions across values of
W
within
(A=a,M=m,B(m)=b)
strata, and the residual selection analogue comes from the tilt in the conditional law of
W
between
(A=a,M=m,B(m)=b)
and
(A=a,B(m)=b)
strata. If the analyst wants to allow the latent confounder to be stronger than
W
, amplification factors
\lambda_{a},\kappa_{a}\geq 1
can be imposed through

\eta_{a}(m,b)\;\leq\;\lambda_{a}\,\widehat{\eta}_{a,W}(m,b),\qquad\gamma_{a}(m,b)\;\leq\;\kappa_{a}\,\widehat{\gamma}_{a,W}(m,b).

(8)

The setting
(\lambda_{a},\kappa_{a})=(1,1)
says that
U
is no stronger than
W
as a residual mediator-outcome confounder; larger values encode stronger latent confounding on the outcome range and residual selection dimensions, respectively.

According to Theorem
2
, the bounds (
8
) imply the bound for the sensitivity function
\Delta_{a}(m,b)
:

\bigl|\Delta_{a}(m,b)\bigr|\leq\Xi_{a}(m,b)\leq\lambda_{a}\,\widehat{\eta}_{a,W}(m,b)\,\frac{\kappa_{a}\,\widehat{\gamma}_{a,W}(m,b)-1}{\kappa_{a}\,\widehat{\gamma}_{a,W}(m,b)},

(9)

Next, for a continuous
W
on its raw support, the finite sample envelope can depend on measurement units and, especially when
W
is heavy-tailed, can be dominated by a few of the largest order statistics. A rank version avoids both issues by replacing
W
with

R_{W,a}(m)=F_{W\mid A=a,B(m)}\{W\mid B(m)\}\in[0,1]

and computing
\widehat{\eta}_{a,W}
and
\widehat{\gamma}_{a,W}
on that scale. Since
R_{T(W),a}(m)=R_{W,a}(m)
for any strictly increasing transformation
T
, the envelope depends on the benchmark’s substantive informativeness, not on its units or support width. The following lemma gives the corresponding unit interval representation for the latent confounder.

Lemma 2

(Canonical unit interval representation of the latent confounder)
.

Fix

4.2
Residual budget calibration

Because
\eta_{a}
is the range of the
U

within a bridge score stratum, it is not identified or bounded by the observed data. As an alternative to benchmark calibration, one can use residual outcome variation on the treated arm as a calibration scale, in the spirit of residual budget analyses

\sigma_{\eta}^{2}(M,B)=V_{1}\bigl(1-R^{2}_{1,MB}\bigr),\quad V_{1}=\mathrm{var}(Y\mid A=1),\quad R^{2}_{1,MB}=1-\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{var}\bigl(Y\mid A=1,M,\,B(M)\bigr)\right]}{V_{1}},

(10)

where
R^{2}_{1,MB}

on the mediator and bridge score. Including

(M,X)

The outcome range parameter can then be calibrated by imposing the residual budget restriction

\eta_{a}(m,b)\leq 2\sigma_{\eta}(m,b)\sqrt{k_{a}},\qquad a\in\{0,1\},

(11)

for a user-specified
k_{a}\in[0,1]

\eta_{a}

\psi_{a}
on a small-mass set of

\sigma_{\eta}^{2}

\mathrm{var}\{\psi_{a}(U;m,b)\mid A=a,B(m)=b\}\leq\eta_{a}(m,b)^{2}/4

(12)

Combining (

4.3
Operationalizing the sensitivity analysis

The bridge score bound and the calibration approaches provide a general foundation for sensitivity analysis in the causal mediation settings, without restricting the inferential paradigm. As a concrete example, this section gives a Bayesian g-computation implementation that propagates uncertainty in nuisance quantities for the observed data and in the calibrated sensitivity envelope
(
11
)
. We first record the scalar reduction that makes the implementation finite-dimensional.

Corollary 1

(scalar functional reduction of the bridge score identification)
.

Define the sequential ignorability anchor

\theta_{\mathrm{SI}}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\mu_{1}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)\,f_{0}(m\mid X)\,\nu(dm)\right],

(14)

and the arm-specific aggregated sensitivity functionals

\bar{\Delta}_{a}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Delta_{a}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)\,f_{0}(m\mid X)\,\nu(dm)\right],\qquad a\in\{0,1\}.

(15)

Then, under Assumptions
1
–
3

\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{SI}}+\bar{\Delta}_{0}-\bar{\Delta}_{1},

(16)

so
\theta
depends on the bridge score sensitivity functions through the arm-specific scalars
(\bar{\Delta}_{0},\bar{\Delta}_{1})
.
If, in addition, Assumption
4
holds and Theorem
2
yields the pointwise envelope
\Xi_{a}(m,b)=\eta_{a}(m,b)\bigl(\gamma_{a}(m,b)-1\bigr)/\gamma_{a}(m,b)

\bigl|\bar{\Delta}_{a}\bigr|\leq\bar{\Xi}_{a}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Xi_{a}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)\,f_{0}(m\mid X)\,\nu(dm)\right],

(17)

and
\bar{\Xi}_{a}\leq\sup_{(m,b)}\Xi_{a}(m,b)
, with equality only when
\Xi_{a}\{m,B(m)\}
attains this supremum
f_{0}
-almost surely on the support induced by
f_{0}(\cdot\mid X)
.

The proof is provided in Supplementary Material Section
A.7
.
Corollary
1
records a complementary scalar reduction. The estimand
\theta
enters the identification formula through the function
\Delta_{a}(\cdot,\cdot)
only via the arm-specific aggregated scalar

For each

5
Illustration with immigration-framing data

We illustrate the calibrated sharp bound in the context of causal mediation analysis, where the risk ratio scale is not the natural reporting scale, and show how inference can be carried out directly from the derived sensitivity bounds. The data are the Brader, Valentino and Suhay immigration framing experiment
(
1

6
Discussion

This paper develops a new sensitivity analysis framework for mediator-outcome ignorability that operates natively on the additive scale. The central object is the bridge score, a two-dimensional vector formed from both treatment-specific mediator densities at a common mediator value, which serves simultaneously as a balancing score for the mediator and as the localization scale on which the unidentified confounding correction is defined. Building on this score, we derive a sharp additive envelope for the confounding function, parametrized by an interpretable residual selection ratio and an outcome range parameter, and show that calibration at the bridge score level is provably no looser than calibration at the full covariate level.
Two complementary calibration routes, benchmark-based and residual budget, then translate these parameters into quantities an analyst can reason about substantively. We then illustrate the operationalization of this framework through a Bayesian g-computation algorithm that propagates all sources of uncertainty into posterior draws of the natural direct and indirect effects.
We formulate the framework under the treatment randomization assumption,
\{Y(a,m),M(a^{\prime}),X\}\perp\!\!\!\perp A
, to emphasize the role of the bridge score both as a balancing device for mediator ignorability and as the scale on which mediator-outcome sensitivity is indexed. The same logic could be adapted to the settings under conditional treatment exchangeability,
\{Y(a,m),M(a^{\prime})\}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid X
, by augmenting the bridge score with a balancing score for treatment assignment, such as the propensity score. We leave this extension implicit to keep the present framework centered on mediator-outcome sensitivity.

The framework is tailored to the cross-world natural effect estimand, where the asymmetry between the two treatment arms is the defining structural feature, but the same logic opens several natural directions for extension. First, causal pathways can operate through multiple mediators simultaneously, whether structured into a causal sequence or acting in parallel without a prespecified ordering
(
25
)
. Extending the bridge score to this setting raises questions about whether a single joint score can preserve the arm asymmetry that the natural effect functional depends on, or whether the score must be defined separately for each mediator. Second, we have primarily focused on independent observations, but in other applied settings (e.g., cluster-randomized experiments) where causal mediation analysis is of interest, outcomes and mediators are measured for units within clusters, and the bridge score sensitivity framework would need to account for within-cluster dependence in both the mediator law and the confounding correction
(
2
)
. Furthermore,
16
have recently developed a Bayesian nonparametric approach for studying the natural indirect effect and the spillover mediation effects in cluster-randomized experiments with multiple unstructured mediators, and it would be of interest to generalize the current framework to similar cluster-correlated data settings.

.
Subtracting
\Delta_{1}^{\ast}(m,x)
,

\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=1,X=x\}=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid A=1,M=m,X=x)-\Delta_{1}^{\ast}(m,x).

Combining the preceding displays,

\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid M(0)=m,X=x\}=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid A{=}1,M{=}m,X{=}x)-\Delta_{1}^{\ast}(m,x)+\Delta_{0}^{\ast}(m,x),

(A1)

and therefore

\theta=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\bigl\{\mathbb{E}(Y\mid A{=}1,M{=}m,X)-\Delta_{1}^{\ast}(m,X)+\Delta_{0}^{\ast}(m,X)\bigr\}\,f_{0}(m\mid X)\,\nu(dm)\right].

Step 2: projection onto the bridge score.

Since
f_{0}(m\mid X)=B_{0}(m,X)
is
\sigma\bigl(B(m)\bigr)
-measurable, the tower property gives, for any function
g(m,X)
and each fixed
m
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(m,X)\,f_{0}(m\mid X)\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[f_{0}(m\mid X)\cdot\mathbb{E}\{g(m,X)\mid B(m)\}\bigr].

We claim that, for any integrable function
g
,
\mathbb{E}\{g(m,X)\mid B(m)\}
equals the corresponding bridge score-level quantity for each of the three terms in (
A1
).

For the outcome regression, note that
h(X):=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid A{=}1,M{=}m,X)
is a function of
X
alone. By Assumption
3
,
X\perp\!\!\!\perp A
, so

\mathbb{E}\{h(X)\mid B(m)\}=\mathbb{E}\{h(X)\mid A{=}1,B(m)\}
.
By Lemma
1
,

\mathbb{E}\{h(X)\mid A{=}1,B(m)\}=\mathbb{E}\{h(X)\mid A{=}1,M{=}m,B(m)\}
.
By iterated expectations, the right-hand side equals
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid A{=}1,M{=}m,B(m))=\mu_{1}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)
.

For the sensitivity functions, Proposition
1
and Assumption
3
give

\mathbb{E}\{\Delta_{a}^{\ast}(m,X)\mid B(m)\}=\mathbb{E}\{\Delta_{a}^{\ast}(m,X)\mid A{=}a,B(m)\}=\Delta_{a}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)
for
a\in\{0,1\}
.

Substituting and applying Fubini’s theorem,

\theta=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Bigl\{\mu_{1}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)-\Delta_{1}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)+\Delta_{0}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)\Bigr\}f_{0}(m\mid X)\,\nu(dm)\right],

which is (
2
).
∎

A.4
Proof of Theorem
2

The proof has four steps. Step 1 represents
\Delta_{a}(m,b)
as a signed integral of the latent outcome regression
\psi_{a}(u;m,b)
against
F_{1}-F_{0}
. Step 2 rescales
\psi_{a}
to a bounded function in
[0,1]
using the outcome range parameter
\eta_{a}(m,b)
. Step 3 bounds the signed integral using the Hahn decomposition and the residual selection parameter
\gamma_{a}(m,b)
, yielding the upper bound
\eta_{a}(\gamma_{a}-1)/\gamma_{a}
. Step 4 exhibits a continuous latent construction satisfying Assumption
4
that attains this bound in both signs, establishing sharpness of the bound on the unidentified sensitivity function
\Delta_{a}(m,b)
.

Proof.

Fix
a\in\{0,1\}
and
(m,b)
throughout. Define the shorthand

F_{1}(du)=F_{U\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b}(du),\qquad F_{0}(du)=F_{U\mid A=a,B(m)=b}(du),

and let
\psi_{a}(u;m,b)=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=a,B(m)=b,U=u\}
as in Assumption
4
. By Lemma
2
, we may work without loss of generality on the canonical latent score scale
U\in[0,1]
under the baseline conditional law, but it is convenient to keep the notation
(F_{0},F_{1},\psi_{a})
throughout the argument.

Step 1: represent
\Delta_{a}
as an integral.

By Assumption
4
and the law of iterated expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b\}

\displaystyle=\int\psi_{a}(u;m,b)\,F_{1}(du),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=a,B(m)=b\}

\displaystyle=\int\psi_{a}(u;m,b)\,F_{0}(du).

Hence

\Delta_{a}(m,b)=\int\psi_{a}(u;m,b)\,\{F_{1}(du)-F_{0}(du)\}.

(A2)

Step 2: reduce to a bounded function in
[0,1]
.

Let

\overline{\psi}=\sup_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b),\qquad\underline{\psi}=\inf_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b),\qquad\eta_{a}(m,b)=\overline{\psi}-\underline{\psi},

consistent with the definition of the outcome range parameter in (
4
).
If
\eta_{a}(m,b)=0
, then
\psi_{a}(\cdot;m,b)
is constant and
\Delta_{a}(m,b)=0
, so the claimed bound is trivial. Suppose henceforth that
\eta_{a}(m,b)>0
, and define

r(u)=\frac{\psi_{a}(u;m,b)-\underline{\psi}}{\eta_{a}(m,b)}.

Then
0\leq r(u)\leq 1
for all
u
, and (
A2
) becomes

\Delta_{a}(m,b)=\eta_{a}(m,b)\int r(u)\,\{F_{1}(du)-F_{0}(du)\},

(A3)

because the constant term
\underline{\psi}
cancels.

Step 3: bound the signed integral.

For any measurable set
A
,

F_{1}(A)\leq\gamma_{a}(m,b)\,F_{0}(A).

Therefore, for any
A
,

F_{1}(A)-F_{0}(A)\leq\gamma_{a}F_{0}(A)-F_{0}(A)=(\gamma_{a}-1)F_{0}(A).

If
F_{0}(A)>1/\gamma_{a}
, then
F_{1}(A)\leq 1
implies

F_{1}(A)-F_{0}(A)\leq 1-F_{0}(A)<1-\frac{1}{\gamma_{a}}=\frac{\gamma_{a}-1}{\gamma_{a}}.

If
F_{0}(A)\leq 1/\gamma_{a}
, then

F_{1}(A)-F_{0}(A)\leq(\gamma_{a}-1)F_{0}(A)\leq\frac{\gamma_{a}-1}{\gamma_{a}}.

Hence

\sup_{A}\{F_{1}(A)-F_{0}(A)\}\leq\frac{\gamma_{a}-1}{\gamma_{a}}.

(A4)

For the reverse set difference, write
dF_{1}=h\,dF_{0}
with
0\leq h\leq\gamma_{a}
and
\int h\,dF_{0}=1
. For any measurable set
A
,

F_{0}(A)-F_{1}(A)=\int_{A}(1-h)\,dF_{0}\leq F_{0}(A).

Also,

1=\int h\,dF_{0}\leq\gamma_{a}F_{0}(A^{c})+\int_{A}h\,dF_{0}=\gamma_{a}\{1-F_{0}(A)\}+F_{1}(A),

so

F_{0}(A)-F_{1}(A)\leq 1-\frac{1}{\gamma_{a}}.

Therefore

\sup_{A}\{F_{0}(A)-F_{1}(A)\}\leq\frac{\gamma_{a}-1}{\gamma_{a}}.

(A5)

Since
0\leq r\leq 1
, the Hahn decomposition characterization of signed measures implies

\left|\int r(u)\,\{F_{1}(du)-F_{0}(du)\}\right|\leq\sup_{A}|F_{1}(A)-F_{0}(A)|\leq\frac{\gamma_{a}-1}{\gamma_{a}}.

Combining this with (
A3
) yields

|\Delta_{a}(m,b)|\leq\eta_{a}(m,b)\frac{\gamma_{a}(m,b)-1}{\gamma_{a}(m,b)},

which proves (
5
).

Step 4: sharpness.

We exhibit a construction attaining the bound with a continuous latent confounder. If
\gamma_{a}=1
, then the bound equals zero, so taking
F_{1}=F_{0}
yields
\Delta_{a}(m,b)=0
and sharpness is trivial. Suppose therefore that
\gamma_{a}>1
. Let
U
have distribution
F_{0}
equal to
\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
, and define

A^{\star}=\left[0,\frac{1}{\gamma_{a}}\right].

Define the conditional law
F_{1}
by

\frac{dF_{1}}{dF_{0}}(u)=\gamma_{a}\,\mathbf{1}\{u\in A^{\star}\}.

Because
F_{0}(A^{\star})=1/\gamma_{a}
, this is a valid probability density with respect to
F_{0}
, and

\sup_{u}\frac{dF_{1}}{dF_{0}}(u)=\gamma_{a}.

Fix any pair of real numbers
\overline{\psi},\underline{\psi}
in the support interval of
Y(1,m)
(that is, with
\inf Y(1,m)\leq\underline{\psi}\leq\overline{\psi}\leq\sup Y(1,m)
) such that
\overline{\psi}-\underline{\psi}=\eta_{a}(m,b)
; for binary or
[0,1]
-supported
Y
, any pair
0\leq\underline{\psi}\leq\overline{\psi}\leq 1
with this difference suffices, and existence of such a pair is immediate whenever
\eta_{a}(m,b)\leq 1
, which is an admissibility condition on the postulated outcome range parameter. Set

\psi_{a}(u;m,b)=\underline{\psi}+(\overline{\psi}-\underline{\psi})\mathbf{1}\{u\in A^{\star}\}.

Then
\sup_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b)-\inf_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b)=\eta_{a}(m,b)
. A direct calculation gives

\displaystyle\int\psi_{a}(u;m,b)\,F_{1}(du)

\displaystyle=\overline{\psi},

\displaystyle\int\psi_{a}(u;m,b)\,F_{0}(du)

\displaystyle=\overline{\psi}\frac{1}{\gamma_{a}}+\underline{\psi}\left(1-\frac{1}{\gamma_{a}}\right),

\displaystyle\Delta_{a}(m,b)

\displaystyle=\int\psi_{a}(u;m,b)\,F_{1}(du)-\int\psi_{a}(u;m,b)\,F_{0}(du)

\displaystyle=(\overline{\psi}-\underline{\psi})\frac{\gamma_{a}-1}{\gamma_{a}}

\displaystyle=\eta_{a}(m,b)\frac{\gamma_{a}(m,b)-1}{\gamma_{a}(m,b)}.

Thus the upper bound is attained by an admissible joint law of
(Y,M,U)
satisfying Assumption
4
. No extra restriction on the marginal for observed data is needed.
To attain the lower bound, keep the same
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
but replace
\psi_{a}(u;m,b)
by

\underline{\psi}+(\overline{\psi}-\underline{\psi})\mathbf{1}\{u\notin A^{\star}\},

which yields

\Delta_{a}(m,b)=-\,\eta_{a}(m,b)\frac{\gamma_{a}(m,b)-1}{\gamma_{a}(m,b)}.

Hence both extrema are attained with a continuous
U
, proving sharpness.
∎

A.5
Proof of Proposition
2

Proof.

Fix
a\in\{0,1\}
,
m\in\mathcal{M}
, and
b
in the support of
B(m)
. Let

\mathcal{X}_{b}=\{x:B(m,x)=b\}

denote the set of covariate values mapping to the bridge score value
b

at mediator
m
.

Step 1: mixture representation of the bridge score
conditional laws.

By the law of total probability,

\displaystyle F(du\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}F(du\mid A=a,M=m,X=x)\,F(dx\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b),

(A6)

\displaystyle F(du\mid A=a,B(m)=b)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}F(du\mid A=a,X=x)\,F(dx\mid A=a,B(m)=b).

(A7)

By Lemma
1
,

F(dx\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b)=F(dx\mid A=a,B(m)=b).

Denote this common mixing measure by
\mu_{a,b}(dx)
. Then

\displaystyle F(du\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}F(du\mid A=a,M=m,X=x)\,\mu_{a,b}(dx),

(A8)

\displaystyle F(du\mid A=a,B(m)=b)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}F(du\mid A=a,X=x)\,\mu_{a,b}(dx).

(A9)

Step 2: proof of (
6
).

Fix
u
in the support of
U
. By (
A8
) and
(
A9
),

\frac{dF(u\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b)}{dF(u\mid A=a,B(m)=b)}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}dF(u\mid A=a,M=m,X=x)\,\mu_{a,b}(dx)}{\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}dF(u\mid A=a,X=x)\,\mu_{a,b}(dx)}.

For each
x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}
, the definition of
\gamma_{a}^{\ast}(m,x)
gives

dF(u\mid A=a,M=m,X=x)\leq\gamma_{a}^{\ast}(m,x)\,dF(u\mid A=a,X=x).

Let

\bar{\gamma}_{a}(m,b)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}}\gamma_{a}^{\ast}(m,x).

Then

dF(u\mid A=a,M=m,X=x)\leq\bar{\gamma}_{a}(m,b)\,dF(u\mid A=a,X=x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}.

Integrating both sides against
\mu_{a,b}(dx)
,

\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}dF(u\mid A=a,M=m,X=x)\,\mu_{a,b}(dx)\leq\bar{\gamma}_{a}(m,b)\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}dF(u\mid A=a,X=x)\,\mu_{a,b}(dx).

Using (
A8
) and (
A9
), this implies

\frac{dF(u\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b)}{dF(u\mid A=a,B(m)=b)}\leq\bar{\gamma}_{a}(m,b).

Taking the supremum over
u
yields

\gamma_{a}(m,b)=\sup_{u}\frac{dF(u\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b)}{dF(u\mid A=a,B(m)=b)}\leq\bar{\gamma}_{a}(m,b)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}}\gamma_{a}^{\ast}(m,x),

which proves (
6
).

Taking the supremum over
b
,

\sup_{b}\gamma_{a}(m,b)\leq\sup_{b}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}}\gamma_{a}^{\ast}(m,x)=\sup_{x}\gamma_{a}^{\ast}(m,x),

because the collection
\{\mathcal{X}_{b}\}_{b}
partitions the support of
X

for fixed
m
.

Step 3: proof of (
7
) under

X\perp\!\!\!\perp U\mid A=a,B(m)=b
.

Under the additional assumption

X\perp\!\!\!\perp U\mid A=a,B(m)=b,

the conditional law of
X
given
(A=a,B(m)=b,U=u)
does not depend on

u
. Denote this common measure by
\mu_{a,b}(dx)
. Then, by iterated
expectation,

\displaystyle\psi_{a}(u;m,b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=a,B(m)=b,U=u\}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}\mathbb{E}\{Y(1,m)\mid A=a,X=x,U=u\}\,\mu_{a,b}(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}\psi_{a}^{\ast}(u;m,x)\,\mu_{a,b}(dx).

Fix
u
and
u^{\prime}
. Then

\displaystyle\psi_{a}(u;m,b)-\psi_{a}(u^{\prime};m,b)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}\{\psi_{a}^{\ast}(u;m,x)-\psi_{a}^{\ast}(u^{\prime};m,x)\}\,\mu_{a,b}(dx).

Therefore,

\displaystyle|\psi_{a}(u;m,b)-\psi_{a}(u^{\prime};m,b)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}_{b}}|\psi_{a}^{\ast}(u;m,x)-\psi_{a}^{\ast}(u^{\prime};m,x)|\,\mu_{a,b}(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}}|\psi_{a}^{\ast}(u;m,x)-\psi_{a}^{\ast}(u^{\prime};m,x)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}}\eta_{a}^{\ast}(m,x).

Taking the supremum over
u
and
u^{\prime}
yields

\eta_{a}(m,b)=\sup_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b)-\inf_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b)=\sup_{u,u^{\prime}}|\psi_{a}(u;m,b)-\psi_{a}(u^{\prime};m,b)|\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}}\eta_{a}^{\ast}(m,x),

which proves (
7
).

Taking the supremum over
b
,

\sup_{b}\eta_{a}(m,b)\leq\sup_{b}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{b}}\eta_{a}^{\ast}(m,x)=\sup_{x}\eta_{a}^{\ast}(m,x),

again because the collection
\{\mathcal{X}_{b}\}_{b}
partitions the support
of
X
for fixed
m
.
∎

A.6
Proof of Lemma
2

Proof.

Fix
a\in\{0,1\}
and
(m,b)
, and write

F_{0}(du)=F_{U\mid A=a,B(m)=b}(du),\qquad F_{1}(du)=F_{U\mid A=a,M=m,B(m)=b}(du).

By Assumption
4
,
F_{1}\ll F_{0}
and

\gamma_{a}(m,b)=\sup_{u}\frac{dF_{1}}{dF_{0}}(u),\qquad\eta_{a}(m,b)=\sup_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b)-\inf_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b).

Throughout this argument, the Radon–Nikodým derivative and the conditional regression are understood as versions on the relevant reduced-law support; changes on
F_{0}
-null sets do not affect the conditional means or the sensitivity parameters. Let

Q_{0}(r)=\inf\{u\in\mathbb{R}:F_{0}((-\infty,u])\geq r\},\qquad r\in[0,1],

be the generalized inverse of
F_{0}
. The quantile transform implies that for every
F_{0}
-integrable measurable function
\varphi
,
\int\varphi(u)\,F_{0}(du)=\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(Q_{0}(r))\,dr
. Define

\psi_{a}^{\dagger}(r;m,b)=\psi_{a}\!\bigl(Q_{0}(r);m,b\bigr),\qquad h_{a}(r;m,b)=\frac{dF_{1}}{dF_{0}}\!\bigl(Q_{0}(r)\bigr),

and let
U^{\dagger}\mid A=a,B(m)=b\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
. Then

\int\psi_{a}(u;m,b)\,F_{0}(du)=\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{a}^{\dagger}(r;m,b)\,dr,\qquad\int\psi_{a}(u;m,b)\,F_{1}(du)=\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{a}^{\dagger}(r;m,b)\,h_{a}(r;m,b)\,dr.

Since
Q_{0}
pushes Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
forward to
F_{0}
, up to the usual null-set convention for Radon–Nikodým derivatives,

\sup_{r}\psi_{a}^{\dagger}(r;m,b)-\inf_{r}\psi_{a}^{\dagger}(r;m,b)=\sup_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b)-\inf_{u}\psi_{a}(u;m,b)=\eta_{a}(m,b),\qquad\sup_{r}h_{a}(r;m,b)=\gamma_{a}(m,b).

This concludes the proof.
∎

A.7
Proof of Corollary
1

Proof.

The proof has three parts: (i) the scalar identification (
16
); (ii) the upper bound
|\bar{\Delta}_{a}|\leq\bar{\Xi}_{a}
; and (iii) the comparison
\bar{\Xi}_{a}\leq\sup_{(m,b)}\Xi_{a}(m,b)
together with its equality condition.

Part (i): scalar identification.

By Theorem
1
,

\theta=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\bigl\{\mu_{1}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)-\Delta_{1}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)+\Delta_{0}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)\bigr\}f_{0}(m\mid X)\,\nu(dm)\right].

The integrand is integrable under the moment conditions of Theorem
1
, so by Fubini-Tonelli the outer expectation and the inner integral may be exchanged, and linearity of expectation yields

\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{SI}}+\bar{\Delta}_{0}-\bar{\Delta}_{1},

with
\theta_{\mathrm{SI}}
,
\bar{\Delta}_{0}
,
\bar{\Delta}_{1}
defined in (
14
) and (
15
). This establishes (
16
). Consequently,
\theta
depends on the pointwise bridge score sensitivity functions
(\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1})
only through the arm-specific scalar functionals
(\bar{\Delta}_{0},\bar{\Delta}_{1})
.

Part (ii): upper bound.

By Theorem
2
, for every
(m,b)
in the support,

\bigl|\Delta_{a}(m,b)\bigr|\leq\Xi_{a}(m,b),\qquad a\in\{0,1\}.

(A10)

Since
f_{0}(\cdot\mid X)\geq 0
, applying the triangle inequality for integrals and (
A10
) gives

\displaystyle\bigl|\bar{\Delta}_{a}\bigr|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Delta_{a}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)\,f_{0}(m\mid X)\,\nu(dm)\right]\right|

(A11)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\bigl|\Delta_{a}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)\bigr|\,f_{0}(m\mid X)\,\nu(dm)\right]

(A12)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Xi_{a}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)\,f_{0}(m\mid X)\,\nu(dm)\right]=\bar{\Xi}_{a}.

(A13)

This establishes (
17
).

Part (iii): comparison with the pointwise supremum.

By monotonicity of the integral,

\bar{\Xi}_{a}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Xi_{a}\bigl(m,B(m)\bigr)\,f_{0}(m\mid X)\,\nu(dm)\right]\leq\sup_{(m,b)}\Xi_{a}(m,b),

with equality if and only if
\Xi_{a}(m,B(m))
equals
\sup_{(m,b)}\Xi_{a}(m,b)
,
f_{0}
-almost surely on the support of
(M,B(m))
induced by
f_{0}(\cdot\mid X)
.
∎
