Executive Summary
Growth of the offshore wind industry in the United States is likely to require growth in the
nation’s manufacturing capacity and additional resources and investments to meet energy project
demands. Such growth has the potential to provide positive economic impacts for local
communities, workers, and entire states.

To realize the benefits of developing a domestic offshore wind supply chain, the states of
Maryland, North Carolina and Virginia are working together under the Southeast and Mid-
Atlantic Regional Transformative Partnership for Offshore Wind Resources (SMART-POWER)
regional collaboration to advance the industry, with Delaware as a supporting state. This report is
part of the SMART-POWER Workforce and Supply Chain Analysis study, funded by the
National Offshore Wind Research and Development Consortium. The study assesses challenges
and opportunities for the four Central Atlantic states to understand key regional strengths and
identify supply chain investments that could effectively leverage their resources and provide
benefits to both the member states and the offshore wind sector as a whole.

This report represents one of two parts of the study, which focuses on the business opportunities
and barriers for investment in major offshore wind turbine component facilities and the related
implications for nearby communities, workforce readiness, small and medium-sized businesses,
and overall deployment of offshore wind energy in the region and along the East Coast. The
second part of the study can be found in a companion report published in parallel, A Regional
Approach to Offshore Wind Energy Manufacturing in the Central Atlantic: Workforce (Pons et
al. 2025), which evaluates in more detail the workforce readiness of the region, states, and
communities to meet the needs of major component manufacturing and evaluates relevant
training programs across the region.

Industry Needs and Regional Strengths
The SMART-POWER region’s historical manufacturing and maritime industry expertise is
evidenced through its competitive ports, existing skilled manufacturing-related workforce,
support of educational and workforce training programs, and Tier 2 and Tier 3 businesses in the
region relevant for Tier 1 manufacturing. 1 We identify three focus areas centered around Tier 1
manufacturing for the SMART-POWER region to meet key industry needs that can leverage the
region’s own strengths and resources:

 • Blade manufacturing. This is the European supply chain’s most constrained Tier 1
 component and will experience significant shortages beginning in 2025. There are no
 presently announced U.S. facilities, although the Portsmouth Marine Terminal in Virginia
 was previously considered by Siemens Gamesa Renewable Energy for a blade facility. 2

1
  Tier 1 components are the large, finished components typically staged at a fabrication port, such as blades, towers,
nacelles or foundations. Tier 2 refers to subassemblies or integrated subcomponents that have a specific function for
a Tier 1 component, such as a pitch system for blades or a service lift for towers. Tier 3 refers to subcomponents
which are generally commonly available items integrated into Tier 2 subassemblies.
2
  The Siemens Gamesa Renewable Energy blade facility at Portsmouth Marine Terminal in Virginia was announced
in late 2021 (Radowitz and Ferris 2021) and canceled in November 2023 (Lee 2023).

 v

 • Tower manufacturing. Towers are another significantly constrained component in the
 European supply chain. There are three announced U.S. facilities (US Wind/Haizea
 facility in Sparrows Point, Maryland, Marmen Welcon 3 in Albany, New York, and a US
 Forged Rings facility at an undisclosed East Coast location), none of which are
 operational or in active development.
 • Monopile manufacturing. Monopiles have similar upcoming bottlenecks in the
 European supply chain, but there is only one operational facility in the United States
 (EEW in Paulsboro, New Jersey), and one announced (US Wind/Haizea in Maryland).
We present three different East Coast project deployment scenarios based on announced offshore
wind projects as of November 30, 2024. The baseline deployment scenario is based on project
development data, including project development schedules, state procurement targets, available
lease areas, macroeconomic factors, and potential infrastructure or supply chain constraints,
resulting in 17.2 GW of installed capacity by 2030 and 81.4 GW by 2040, with an erratic
boom/bust pattern of low and high deployment due to competing factors like state targets. The
low deployment scenario modifies the baseline scenario through macroeconomic and supply
chain limitations, resulting in 13.5 GW installed by 2030 and 51.4 GW by 2040, while the high
deployment scenario models favorable macroeconomic and supply chain factors, resulting in
17.2 GW installed by 2030 and 112.8 GW by 2040.

Key Takeaways
After assessing potential business, workforce, and community impact considerations for Tier 1
investments, we outline six key takeaways:
 1. The existing and announced manufacturing facilities in the United States likely do
 not have sufficient production capacity to support the demand for towers, blades,
 and monopiles from offshore wind projects in the SMART-POWER region (nor for
 the whole East Coast pipeline, as estimated by our deployment scenarios).
 Additionally, there are significant bottlenecks expected in the global supply for these
 components beginning in 2025–2028 and lasting at least until 2030.
 2. We estimate that expanding the East Coast supply chain to include two full blade
 manufacturing facilities and one new tower facility could potentially reduce the risk
 of undersupply from 2027 to 2040 across deployment scenarios. The monopile
 demand in these scenarios can likely be met by the two facilities that have already
 been announced. These estimates are based on our assessment of supply and demand
 across the East Coast. There is continuing uncertainty about the presently announced
 facilities, which pose additional challenges for developers as they work to secure
 components but which may lead to opportunities for additional facilities if any of the
 current announcements are delayed or canceled.
 3. The component demand from the deployment scenarios in the SMART-POWER
 region would likely be sufficient to cover the supply of components from the two

 Supply chain investments could potentially bring benefits to port communities such as

 (Figure ES-4), targeted community engagement and benefits could potentially alleviate
 some of these concerns while distributing more broadly the benefits of new investments.

 percentages of low-income households and higher poverty rates than average

 6. Interstate collaboration could help develop a regional supply chain that could meet
 the component demand from SMART-POWER projects, fill gaps in the overall East
 Coast pipeline, and contribute to a stable order book and greater workforce
 readiness for new supply chain facilities. Publicly announced commercial operation
 dates of projects in the East Coast pipeline indicate a potential for boom/bust cycles in
 deployment timelines. This erratic schedule may create challenges for new supply chain
 facilities in securing stable component orders. Ongoing communication between
 SMART-POWER states and project developers in the region could help identify actions
 to mitigate these risks. Earlier supply chain investments may reduce risk of component
 procurement bottlenecks when the global supply chain is most stressed beginning in

Although there is uncertainty in the market and other challenges with developing manufacturing
resources, there is an opportunity for the Central Atlantic states of Delaware, Maryland, Virginia,
and North Carolina, building off the existing SMART-POWER framework, to establish a
strategic vision and act on it to help meet renewable energy targets. Developing tower, blade, or
monopile manufacturing resources could help projects in the region avoid delays attributed to
anticipated global supply chain bottlenecks with the added benefit of creating jobs and economic
growth. Regional communication and coordination between the states could help to achieve
these benefits by establishing strategic plans for building supply chain assets, training the
workforce, and advancing offshore wind project development.

1 Introduction
Growth of the offshore wind industry in the United States is likely to require expansion of the
nation’s manufacturing and maritime capacity and the addition of thousands of jobs. This growth
may potentially help address historic underinvestment in and around port communities while
contributing to the nation’s electricity grid. As the first commercial-scale offshore wind projects
are being built and put into operation along the East Coast, the industry has faced challenges in
getting projects started and securing supporting investment (McCoy et al. 2024). Challenges
range from macroeconomic shocks such as surging inflation and high interest rates to supply
chain, vessel, and port infrastructure constraints. There is general consensus in the industry
regarding the need to develop a domestic supply chain that meets energy project demands and
promotes economic development and job creation (Shields et al. 2023) while reducing barriers
and uncertainty for all domestic projects and stakeholders.

Many states along the East Coast have begun procurement of offshore wind projects and have
identified investments at the local and state levels in ports, manufacturing, workforce, and related
infrastructure to potentially support the development of a domestic supply chain (McCoy et al.
2024). In addition to individual state initiatives, there are efforts underway for East Coast states
to share information and collaborate or coordinate to support the development of a domestic
supply chain. One example is a Memorandum of Understanding signed in 2022 between key
federal and state agencies to explore regional collaboration opportunities in supply chain
development (U.S. Department of Energy et al. 2023). Another regional collaboration effort is
the Southeast and Mid-Atlantic Regional Transformative Partnership for Offshore Wind
Resources, or SMART-POWER, between Maryland, Virginia, and North Carolina, with
participation from Delaware, focused on strengthening the offshore wind industry with emphasis
on supply chain development in the Central Atlantic region (Porter 2020).

This report is part of the SMART-POWER Workforce and Supply Chain Analysis project, which
assesses the challenges and opportunities for Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, and Delaware
to collaborate regionally to develop a domestic supply chain for the U.S. offshore wind industry
and capture the potential benefits. Funded by the National Offshore Wind Research and
Development Consortium and led by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), the
study aims to understand key regional strengths and identify supply chain investments that can
potentially leverage these resources, as well as provide benefits to both the member states and
the offshore wind sector as a whole.

This report represents one of two parts of the study; it focuses on the business opportunities and
barriers for investment in major component facilities and the related implications for nearby
communities, workforce readiness, small and medium-sized businesses, and overall deployment
of offshore wind energy in the region and along the East Coast. The second part of the study is
published in a companion report, A Regional Approach to Offshore Wind Energy Manufacturing
in the Central Atlantic: Workforce (Pons et al. 2025), which evaluates in more detail the
workforce readiness of the region, states, and communities to meet the needs for major
component manufacturing and evaluates relevant training programs across the region.

1.1 Recent Supply Chain Developments
Since the publication of NREL’s national offshore supply chain road map study (Shields et al.
2023), the domestic offshore wind supply chain has experienced growth while dealing with
numerous challenges. As of November 2024, facility, port, and vessel investment
announcements exceeded $7.1 billion across the country (U.S. Department of Energy 2024),
filling in some of the initial needs to meet project deployment. These investments begin to
address some of the seven key national-scale obstacles identified in the road map: (1) investment
risk, (2) siting and technology challenges, (3) limited supplier networks, (4) insufficient port and
vessel infrastructure, (5) limited domestic workforce, (6) cost competitiveness, and (7) ensuring
shared socioeconomic opportunity and environmental benefits.

However, additional critical barriers in the past 3 years also include macroeconomic shocks from
elevated inflation, supply chain bottlenecks since the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, and project
cancellations and delays tied to important facility orders and investments (McCoy et al. 2024).

Within the SMART-POWER region, the continued development and marshaling of the Coastal
Virginia Offshore Wind (CVOW) project and the announcement of a new cable manufacturing
facility by LS GreenLink in Chesapeake, Virginia (LS GreenLink 2024) may signal continued
investment in the offshore wind industry amid recent challenges. We have developed the supply
chain assessment framework for this report with an understanding of these key drivers and
barriers in order to remain aligned with the identified needs of East Coast states, manufacturers,
developers, and other involved stakeholders.

1.2 Supply Chain Assessment Framework
The following assessment was structured to capture numerous supply chain considerations. As
illustrated in Figure 1, the assessment framework starts from a broad scale of analysis at the
global and national levels, then narrows down to regional and community-scale evaluations, then
further down to the individual facility scale. Section 2. The Big Picture is intended to provide
industry context for developing a domestic supply chain and for how various industry gaps align
with SMART-POWER state strengths and interests. Set into this context, the three Tier 1
components 5 we evaluate for manufacturing in the region are blades, towers, and monopiles (the
justification for focusing on these components is described in Section 2.2).

Sections 3–5 evaluate different opportunities, challenges and potential impacts of supply chain
investments, measured and contrasted with quantitative metrics unique to each section:

 • Section 3. Port Locations and Port Communities examines indicative ports for
 manufacturing across the SMART-POWER region, followed by community impact
 considerations, such as potential health and economic vulnerabilities and opportunities
 for the surrounding communities. The three community impact evaluation metrics are
 “location-specific” (making comparisons between port locations) but “component-
 agnostic” (not influenced by the selection of Tier 1 component for a given facility).

2 The Big Picture
The potential challenges and opportunities for supply chain investment in the SMART-POWER
region are set within the broader global and national context. There is a significant opportunity
for SMART-POWER states to realize the benefits of developing an offshore wind supply chain
by leveraging their regional and individual state strengths and interests to add to the growth of
offshore wind in their own states while contributing to the overall industry’s success.

The following subsections detail the broad offshore wind deployment and supply chain
challenges faced globally and nationally and summarize the relevant SMART-POWER state
resources, which result in three supply chain focus areas in this assessment to meet industry
needs with state strengths.

Additionally, we present three East Coast deployment scenarios that frame our evaluation of
supply chain challenges and opportunities, and we discuss guiding social and economic
considerations. In this assessment, we do not consider West Coast industry needs or resources as
a factor in the development of the offshore wind supply chain on the East Coast.

For the data presented in this assessment, we used a cutoff date of November 30, 2024. As such,
public data on project development, lease areas, state targets, supply chain facility
announcements, and other related deployment and supply chain information is current only as of
the cutoff date.

2.1 Supply Chain Challenges and Industry Needs
The offshore wind industry is growing globally and in the United States, encouraged by efforts to
expand energy generation to meet demand growth, government buy-in and incentives for
renewable energy, and grid diversification for reliability, among other factors.

In 2023, the Global Wind Energy Council forecast more than 2 TW of wind energy capacity
installed by 2030 under contemporary policies across the world, with up to 1.5 TW more in
overall wind energy capacity needed to support various climate targets, of which 494 GW
correspond to offshore wind energy (Global Wind Energy Council 2023). Domestically, NREL’s
Offshore Wind Market Report: 2024 Edition (McCoy et al. 2024) identified offshore wind
procurement mandates from eight states totaling 45.7 GW by 2040 as of May 31, 2024, while the
project development and operational pipeline for offshore wind represented a generating capacity
of 80.5 GW.

While deployment goals may bolster offshore wind energy build-out, global supply chains are
not currently capable of supplying the necessary components, vessels, and port infrastructure in a
timely and cost-effective manner. Some key supply chain challenges and bottlenecks include:

 • Mining for central raw materials (such as zinc, copper, iron, and rare earth metals)
 concentrated in very few countries, primarily China, as well as production of gearboxes,
 converters, castings and generators. Disruptions in imports result in significant
 bottlenecks for key offshore wind components relying on these materials and
 subcomponents.

 4

 • Tier 1 component manufacturing for the United States primarily comes from the
 European supply chain, which is also dependent on imports and is currently insufficient
 to meet European offshore wind goals, let alone deployment abroad.
 • Thin component manufacturing margins, project pipeline uncertainty, and conflicts
 between varying component lead times and timeline uncertainty slow down
 manufacturing capacity expansions.
For project installation, the top five supply chain bottlenecks identified (Global Wind Energy
Council 2023; Wood Mackenzie 2023) are:

 • Wind turbine installation vessels (WTIVs): While WTIVs are less likely to experience
 bottlenecks in Europe until 2028, the Jones Act limits foreign-flagged WTIV use in the
 United States, regardless of availability, making it an existing bottleneck domestically.
 • Blades: European bottlenecks are expected to begin in 2025; blades represent the greatest
 investment deficit to meet global 2030 targets.
 • Towers: European bottlenecks are expected to begin in 2026.
 • Fixed-bottom foundations (primarily monopiles): European bottlenecks are expected
 to begin in 2026.
 • Nacelles: European bottlenecks are expected to begin in 2026; nacelles represent the
 smallest (but still significant) investment deficit to meet global 2030 targets, compared to
 the previously listed components.
European component undersupply identified to begin in 2025–2026 is estimated to last (and in
most cases worsen) at least until 2030 (Global Wind Energy Council 2023; Wood Mackenzie
2023).

Developing a domestic supply chain for offshore wind could potentially support project
installations starting in the late 2020s and help meet domestic energy and emission reduction
targets (Shields et al. 2023). Prioritizing the listed components with critical bottlenecks can
likely de-risk projects and the overall industry. Notwithstanding, addressing these multiple
challenges may require a significant level of investment, complicated by the uncertainty in
project rollout, evolving technologies such as alternative foundations and increasing turbine
sizes, and uncertainty in the time frames and coordination across all the different necessary
investments.

2.2 Focus Areas Based on SMART-POWER Strengths
To address industry challenges and needs, this assessment focuses at each stage on the strengths
and resources that exist within the SMART-POWER region. After an initial review of the
region’s ports, we identified six ports that likely meet the physical requirements for Tier 1
manufacturing, with minimum to moderate investment required for component handling
(detailed in Section 3.1), supported by the region’s dynamic maritime industry. In addition, there
are programs, initiatives, or organizations that may increase socioeconomic benefits for port
communities or that have the potential to do so (Sections 3.3 to 3.5).

Additionally, the region’s historical manufacturing experience, particularly in the steel industry,
is evidenced through its existing skilled manufacturing-related workforce, supporting educational

 5

and workforce training programs, and Tier 2 and Tier 3 businesses in the region relevant for Tier
1 manufacturing (Sections 4.1 and 4.2).

As a result, the three selected focus areas are intended to capture the region’s industry strengths
and existing resources, which can be leveraged with strategic investments to support supply
chain development. The three selected focus areas encompass:

 • Full blade manufacturing, considering this is Europe’s most constrained Tier 1
 component, has no presently announced U.S. facility, and the Portsmouth Marine
 Terminal in Virginia was considered by Siemens Gamesa Renewable Energy (SGRE) as
 a location for a blade finishing facility. 6
 • Tower manufacturing, as there are three announced U.S. facilities (US Wind/Haizea
 facility in Sparrows Point, Maryland; Marmen Welcon 7 in Albany, New York; US
 Forged Rings facility at an undisclosed East Coast location), but none are currently
 operational.
 • Monopile manufacturing, with only one operational facility (EEW in Paulsboro, New
 Jersey), and one announced (US Wind/Haizea in Maryland).
While there are other industry needs and state strengths, the following assessment is framed
around these three focus areas as some of the highest priorities for the regional supply chain
development. Section 6 addresses some additional opportunities based on identified state and
regional strengths; to leverage the existing port infrastructure, maritime experience and
waterways, marshaling ports, and storage ports are explored as sidebar studies, as is alternative
foundation fabrication that can use the steel manufacturing expertise of the region.

2.4 Social and Economic Considerations
The following sections of this report evaluate social and economic considerations with the same
detail as market and business considerations, rooted in an understanding of the importance of all

 8

these different dimensions in successful supply chain investments. Proactive engagement with
different groups from port communities, the workforce, and small and medium businesses can
potentially improve trust and buy-in for major projects like the construction and operation of Tier
1 manufacturing facilities as well as the broader offshore wind energy industry. Additional
benefits may include more efficient regulatory processes at local, state, and national levels and
minimizing potential negative impacts such as community opposition, negative press, and
litigation (EPA 2020).

While potential environmental impacts from Tier 1 manufacturing at ports are not explored in
depth in this report, they are an additional dimension of importance to supply chain development
so that environmental benefits may be achieved with minimum negative impacts across supplier
and manufacturer locations and to promote healthy port communities. A discussion of recent
efforts related to environmental and community impacts can be found in NREL’s national supply
chain road map (Shields et al. 2023).

3 Port Locations and Port Communities
Due to the large dimensions of offshore wind components, Tier 1 manufacturing facilities
generally require massive, specialized equipment, infrastructure and vessels for fabrication,
storage, and transportation via waterways. Across the SMART-POWER region, we identified six
different ports that may be suitable for blade, tower, or monopile manufacturing based on their
physical characteristics and capabilities, with little to moderate additional investment likely
needed. Technical port upgrade assessments were beyond the scope of this study. There may be
other port locations, not identified in this study, also suitable for component manufacturing.

Additionally, port communities, defined herein as the immediate 5-mile radius surrounding a
port, were identified and analyzed for each of the indicative port locations within the SMART-
POWER region. While the 5-mile radius may not entirely capture areas in a city or town
involved or affected by offshore wind supply chain development, it is consistent with previous
NREL research (Shields et al. 2023) focused on households more directly and regularly impacted
by port activity. We evaluate the existing public health and socioeconomic vulnerabilities in port
communities to provide an understanding of issues that may coincide or be exacerbated by
additional port activity, but that could also be improved upon through targeted community
investments. We also present opportunities and tools for community engagement and
involvement in the supply chain investment process. As the United States ramps up its offshore
wind supply chain, it is valuable for manufacturers and developers to be aware of the
disproportionate impacts from their projects that communities may experience, as well as how
those effects may be exacerbated if port communities are not afforded the same advantages from
port operations as other communities in the area, such as jobs and economic growth (EPA 2020).

Community Insights: Portsmouth Marine Terminal
Refer to Appendix C.3 for more information on the qualitative research
supporting the following community insights.
Since Portsmouth Marine Terminal and its port community have overlapping
socioeconomic and environmental considerations, lessons learned from this
region could inform the potential risks and benefits of offshore wind
manufacturing, not only for the SMART-POWER region but across other
American ports. Per the 2017 Portsmouth Community Health survey (Portsmouth
Health District and Virginia Department of Health 2018) and additional
interviews carried out by NREL with community groups, different Portsmouth
residents state they would like for any new developments to take a community-
centric approach. They have identified several community concerns, such as:
• High asthma rates, pollution, environmental impacts, and public health issues,
 including hypertension and cancer mortality
• Dominion Energy supply chain activities for the CVOW project engaging
 more with Portsmouth businesses
• Economic challenges that hinder economic mobility, such as housing
 affordability, sustainable wage jobs, energy burdens, and health disparities
• Ensuring that investments in energy and infrastructure projects provide
 tangible benefits to local communities, especially historically underinvested-in
 areas.
Engaging Port Communities in Decision-Making
Developers and community residents generally agree that developments in the
port region should aim to minimize negative impacts on residents and the
environment, but there are often hurdles during the process. One such hurdle is
the level of community engagement during the decision-making process.
Residents of port communities share that they feel frustrated with the existing
meeting and engagement processes, which they think require little meaningful
community input to “check a box” for developers.
There is strong evidence that the public’s involvement in the decision-making
process can contribute valuable local knowledge, inform policy or other solutions,
and improve the perception of decision outcomes among residents (Carley and
Konisky 2020). To ensure fairness in decision-making, it is critical that these
processes be as receptive and welcoming to community input as possible,
especially given the vulnerabilities of port communities (EPA 2020). Beyond
community input, community buy-in is needed for success, according to
Portsmouth resident and Director of the Center for Sustainable Communities,
Garry Harris: “We really need the communities to take ownership of this … not
just informing them, not just consulting them, not just telling [them] about the
project, but there needs to be some ownership here.”

 17

Addressing Disproportionate Burden
Decades’ worth of accumulating negative environmental and economic impacts is
another source of discontent for port communities. Portsmouth experiences high
air pollution, which contributes to public health problems like high rates of
asthma, hypertension, and cancer mortality (Portsmouth Health District and
Virginia Department of Health 2018).
Health disparities in the region are not shared equally; Black residents experience
poverty and asthma at disproportionate rates. Nationally, Black Americans have
the highest mortality rates from air pollution when compared to other racial
groups (Geldsetzer et al. 2024). Low-income and racial minority residents are
most vulnerable to pollution impacts compounded by other social determinants of
health, leading to significant disparities in health outcomes (Geldsetzer et al.
2024). Additionally, these communities have historically held less political
influence and power to combat polluting industries within their communities.
Portsmouth residents are no strangers to the impact of port pollutants; even small
levels of pollutants may have generational impacts on residents (Partnership for
Working Families and Community Benefits Law Center 2016). Residents have
worked for decades to minimize the resulting environmental disparities. While it
can be important that the harms associated with port development are
appropriately addressed, it may also benefit residents to be aware of how different
energy technologies could benefit port communities and reduce health disparities,
especially in communities located near industrial sites, like Portsmouth (Carley
and Konisky 2020; Geldsetzer et al. 2024).

3.4 Environmental and Public Health Considerations
Day-to-day port operations can cause environmental impacts such as air, water, and land
pollution that negatively affect the health of nearby residents, although this varies by type of
operation. The cumulative impact of constant exposure to pollutants can span multiple
generations (EPA 2020). Due to their proximity to port activities, port communities are
disproportionately burdened by pollutants from industrial manufacturing. Developers and
decision makers should be aware that small environmental impacts can have greater effects in
areas dealing with decades of cumulative impacts than when evaluated in isolation. It is
important to work directly with impacted populations—engaging local communities in planning
and decision-making through processes such as participatory planning, community advisory
committees, and forums/workshops—to better understand their issues and identify feasible
solutions that will provide environmental and health protections (EPA 2020).

Energy or manufacturing infrastructure developments may produce pollutants and other negative
impacts such as construction noise disruptions, unpleasant smells, or increased traffic, which
may be disproportionately borne by the surrounding communities (Carley and Konisky 2020;
Getor et al. 2022). While these drawbacks of new energy technologies for local communities are
important to those experiencing them, they should be weighed against the broader health and
environmental impacts or benefits of implementing new energy technologies, especially when
there is an opportunity to offset disproportionate impacts on low-income or minority
communities (Carley and Konisky 2020; Qiu et al. 2022). By prioritizing local health
considerations for immediately impacted communities and taking steps to offset energy burdens
and negative impacts, energy innovations can not only address historic disparities but set a
precedent of fair distribution of benefits for future energy initiatives.

3.5 Potential Intervention Tools
Positive community engagement can potentially result in improved community/government
relations, increased community decision-making capacity, and reduced long-term negative
impacts. A successful collaborative decision-making process may involve appropriate
stakeholders from port communities and can be addressed with different tools, mechanisms, or
best practices. This section breaks down key information about some commonly considered
tools, with greatest detail provided for Community Benefit Agreements (CBAs). Additional
challenges, best practices, and examples specific to offshore wind supply chain development are
discussed by Shields et al. (2023). Tools can be adopted and adapted based on each location’s
unique set of needs and concerns (EPA 2020).

Resulting community benefits may also take many different forms. Benefits may include
prioritization of contracts for small businesses, income and tax diversification, education and
workforce development investments, community infrastructure investments (such as school
campus investments, broadband improvements or public park investments), lower energy bills
for impacted community members, or other forms of benefits (Gill et al. 2023; Shields et al.
2023).

3.5.1 Community Benefit Agreements
CBAs are formal agreements between developers and local communities to ensure that new
developments bring tangible benefits to communities they may impact (NREL 2023; PolicyLink
n.d.) These agreements outline benefits that the developer will commit to providing, such as job
creation, community infrastructure improvements, and/or environmental protections. The main
purpose of a CBA is to address community concerns and mitigate the potential negative impacts
of development projects by ensuring the residents share in the economic and social gains.

 Wind Energy Community Benefits Database
 Example CBAs, community benefit funds, donations, and other kinds of benefits
 from land-based and offshore wind energy initiatives in the United States from
 2022 to 2024 can be found in the Wind Energy Community Benefits Database
 (https://windexchange.energy.gov/projects/community-benefit-agreements), a
 searchable database produced by NREL.

Benefits of CBAs

Effective CBAs result in measurable improvements for affected communities, particularly
vulnerable residents, such as creating good jobs, affordable housing, and neighborhood services.
When CBAs function effectively, impacted communities can receive tangible economic and
social advantages from this transparent procedure and dependable legal framework (Partnership
for Working Families and Community Benefits Law Center 2016).

 • Mitigating negative impacts: CBAs can be used to mitigate potential negative impacts of
 nearby developments. This can include environmental safeguards, noise and pollution
 control, and strategies to minimize displacing residents.
 • Accountability mechanisms: Community organizations can enforce the conditions of the
 CBA directly against the developers, which provides a degree of assurance that is often
 not included through typical planning procedures (PolicyLink n.d.).
 • Economic benefits and local hiring: CBAs have the potential to create a large positive
 impact with respect to job creation and boosting the local economy. Ensuring that jobs at
 various skill and education levels are easily accessible is essential to developing a well-
 rounded local economy.

 21

Risks and Challenges of CBAs

CBAs can be an effective tool for granting local communities protections, yet they also have the
potential to be misappropriated. Weak CBAs can allow for co-option, and in some cases,
developers and elected officials have misused them to help projects get approved (Partnership for
Working Families and Community Benefits Law Center 2016).

 • Community representation: When the community representatives participating in CBA
 negotiations do not adequately represent the variety of community interests impacted by
 the development, it can undermine the potential positive impacts. This can happen as a
 result of developers (both intentionally and unintentionally) excluding groups/residents
 who are most vulnerable or highly critical of the development’s impacts (PolicyLink
 n.d.).
 • Reaching consensus: A CBA is not a perfect solution; it is used to mitigate—not solve
 for—harms caused by developers and existing community vulnerabilities. Discussions
 between community representatives and developers may become tense; thus, it is
 imperative that CBA negotiations neither overpromise nor allow feasible improvements
 to go excluded.
 • Legal fees and enforcement: Community coalitions will often require legal
 representation; in the event they cannot secure free legal counsel, the community will
 have to pay out of pocket. Should terms of the CBA ever need to be enforced, the
 associated legal fees may be substantial (PolicyLink n.d.).

 CBA Guidance
 The Partnership for Working Families and Community Benefits Law Center’s
 “Common Challenges in Negotiating Community Benefits Agreements—and
 How to Avoid Them” (https://citizensplanninginstitute.org/wp-
 content/uploads/2022/06/Effective-CBAs.pdf) offers guidelines for successful
 CBAs, signs of weak CBAs, case examples of detrimental and ineffective CBAs,
 and an analysis of the components of strong and weak CBAs.

Impact assessment tools evaluate the potential social and environmental impacts of projects or
policies, guiding decision-making processes to ensure informed, robust outcomes. These tools
consider various factors such as environmental, social, economic, and health impacts (EPA
2020). A range of assessment tools are available, each designed to focus on specific aspects of
community and environmental well-being.

Benefits of Impact Assessments

 • Informed decision-making: Tools like Environmental Impact Assessments and Health
 Impact Assessments help identify potential risks and benefits before projects are initiated
       (EPA 2020).

 • Risk mitigation: Human Health Risk Assessments can help predict and mitigate potential

Risks and Challenges of Impact Assessments

 • Community engagement challenges: While tools like Social Impact Assessments promote

3.5.3 Qualified Opportunity Zones
Qualified Opportunity Zones (QOZs) were established under the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act
with the goal of promoting economic development and creating jobs in impoverished
neighborhoods. Eligible taxpayers may contribute to a Qualified Opportunity Fund, which must
invest 90% of its assets in QOZ property, with capital gain taxes postponed for reinvested
investments, and all capital gains exempted for 10-year retention (Department of Housing and
Urban Development n.d.[b]). All six port locations in this assessment have a QOZ within their 5-
mile radius (Department of Housing and Urban Development n.d.[c]).

Benefits of QOZs

 • Economic growth in impoverished areas: QOZs aim to stimulate investment and job
 creation in economically distressed neighborhoods, driving local development and
 revitalization by attracting investments.
Risks and Challenges of QOZs

 • Potential for gentrification: Without adequate planning, QOZ investments could lead to
 rising property values and rents, potentially displacing low-income residents.

While the SMART-POWER region presents numerous workforce and business strengths for the

 1. Improving regional connectivity may depend on information sharing and

components of the assessment; for this reason, the metric is not applicable for comparison across
the United States. The framework of the metric, shown in Figure 7, is made up of three parts: the
in-state commuting score, the regional score, and the state score.

4.1.1 Delaware
While Delaware is not known for having a large industrial presence—it has one of the lowest
levels of manufacturing employment in the United States (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics
2024)—its manufacturing sector is growing, representing a workforce growth opportunity.
Because of its current low level of manufacturing employment and its limited capacity in
manufacturing-related training programs, Delaware ranked relatively low overall in workforce
readiness to produce blades, towers, or monopiles compared to other SMART-POWER states.

However, Delaware has a higher unemployment rate that points to an available workforce to
recruit, train, and hire for production facilities, as well as an increase in manufacturing
employment (based on a 1-year percentage change), representing a growing state manufacturing
industry. These factors indicate that Delaware has a growing manufacturing sector with a
population that could enter training programs to learn skills for manufacturing-related jobs, but
the state would need to expand or develop programs to train the necessary skills or leverage
regional training opportunities. In addition to the state-level assessment, in-state and regional
commuting zones were evaluated for Port of Wilmington on the Delaware River to understand
the workforce readiness for nearby communities to support blade, tower, or monopile
manufacturing.

Port of Wilmington, Delaware

Much of the port workforce comes from local labor unions such as the International
Longshoremen’s Union Local 1694, which supplies around 400–500 workers to unload ships or
upward of 1,000 workers during busy periods. Additional workers from other labor unions work
in warehouse storage (Barrish 2024). While the state of Delaware currently has a lower readiness
level for meeting the workforce demands required by a blade, tower, or monopile manufacturing
facility, the in-state and regional commuting zones surrounding Port of Wilmington offer
available workers and existing education and training programs.

Local workforce readiness for this port is driven by a higher number of available workers for

related industry closures. With respect to training, the local area has an average number of
existing registered apprenticeship programs (RAPs) to train the types of occupations needed,

year degree programs. For such occupations, the broader regional area (defined as out-of-state

counties within commuting distance) has a higher number of programs compared to other ports
but a lower readiness for RAPs. The regional area includes Maryland, which has a higher

4.1.2 Maryland
Through workforce investments and partnerships, Maryland is a rising leader in offshore wind
workforce development, providing residents access to training and employment opportunities.
Maryland ranks third out of the four SMART-POWER states in workforce readiness to support
occupations for the production of blades, towers, and monopiles, based on a snapshot of state
economic and workforce data. Compared to the other SMART-POWER states, Maryland has a
lower unemployment rate and lower manufacturing specialty, which decreases the state’s
workforce readiness because it could be more challenging to recruit workers with related skills
into manufacturing careers. However, Maryland Works for Wind—a regional consortium
managing a $23 million investment—factors strongly in the state’s potential to support offshore
wind manufacturing because of its potential to increase the workforce readiness for the state
through program support. Maryland Works for Wind provides grants across the state to develop
occupational training, fund supportive services to increase access for job training, and facilitates
job placement into sectors such as offshore wind fabrication (Maryland Department of Labor
n.d.). This program’s support supplements a state training landscape that has a high number of
two-year and four-year degree programs that are typically used to train management and
engineering roles. To better support component manufacturing, Maryland may consider
continuing to develop the skilled trades through investments in RAPs and two-year degree
programs.

Sparrows Point, Maryland

Sparrows Point, Maryland, is a potential hub for Tier 1 component manufacturing with the

4.2 Allied Tier 2 and Tier 3 Businesses
In addition to workforce readiness, engagement of Tier 2 and Tier 3 businesses, referred to here
as “allied businesses,” in the supply chain allows for small and medium businesses in the region
to benefit from the growing offshore wind industry while reducing costs and uncertainty for Tier
1 manufacturers. In alignment with common terminology, we define the four different tiers in the
offshore wind supply chain:

 1. Tier 1: Finished components. Finished components are the large components typically
 staged at a fabrication port adjacent to the manufacturing or fabrication plant, or a
 project’s marshaling port, ready to be transported and installed offshore. The supply of
 Tier 1 components is typically divided into major work packages contracted directly
 between the project developer and the Tier 1 supplier. These include the wind turbine kits
 (individual rotor blades, nacelle with rotor-hub, and tower), the foundation elements
 (typically a monopile and transition piece), and grid interconnection components (subsea
 array cables, offshore substation, and subsea export cable). Grid interconnection facilities
 onshore include directionally drilled shore-crossings, underground cable vault, onshore
 substation, and underground or overhead power lines.
 2. Tier 2: Subassemblies. Subassemblies are systems of integrated subcomponents that
 have a specific function for a Tier 1 component, such as a pitch system for blades, a
 service lift for towers, or interior deck and ladder assemblies for transition pieces. Tier 1
 manufacturers may have multiple Tier 2 suppliers who are prequalified to produce a
 specific subassembly and typically enter long-lead contracts to purchase these for a
 manufacturing run.
 3. Tier 3: Subcomponents. Subcomponents are commonly available items that are
 integrated into Tier 2 subassemblies, such as motors, gears, casings or housings, switch
 plates, and fasteners. Tier 3 suppliers are typically vendors who routinely stock these
 items, offering them for sale on the open market rather than entering one-on-one
 contracts.
 4. Tier 4: Raw materials. Raw materials, such as steel, copper, rare earth metals, glass
 fiber, carbon fiber, resins, adhesives, or concrete, are directly processed into Tier 3
 subcomponents or Tier 2 subassemblies.
The availability of allied businesses within the SMART-POWER region was evaluated for four
Tier 1 components: blades, towers, monopiles, and transition pieces. Appendix D contains
supply chain hierarchy maps for each component that outline subassemblies and subcomponents.
Although NREL did not undertake a detailed assessment of transition piece manufacturing,
transition pieces are included in this section because they illustrate how they are different from
monopiles with respect to their required number and diversity of Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers.
Since transition pieces and monopiles are generally coupled foundation components, this affects
the number of allied businesses in a state or region that can potentially support their fabrication.

Additionally, allied business availability for the SMART-POWER region was compared with the
overall allied business availability of the East Coast (geographically from Maine to North
Carolina) to understand relative strengths and gaps.

 33

Allied business availability is measured with data obtained from the Oceantic Network’s Supply
Chain Connect (SCC) database, resulting in an aggregate score called Supply Chain Connect
score (SSCC). 10 Details on the methodology can be found in Appendix B.4. These data were

There are four key takeaways from the state and regional assessment:

 1. Towers and transition pieces: The SMART-POWER region likely has significant allied
 business availability for towers and transition pieces, accounting in our results for more
 than 40% of the total East Coast availability. This includes supply for tower
 subcomponents and subassemblies related to personnel access and equipment, such as
 doors, work platforms, ladders, and other secondary steel components for towers. The
 case is similar for transition pieces because of a comparable supply chain hierarchy.
 2. Monopiles: While overall monopile allied business availability is lower across the East
 Coast than for towers and transition pieces, potential allied business availability in the
 SMART-POWER region represents 36% of the total. However, fewer and less
 component-diverse allied businesses are needed for monopile fabrication than for towers
 or transition pieces (as can be seen by comparing the supply chain hierarchy maps in
 Appendix D). Therefore, there are inherently fewer allied businesses self-reporting their
 capabilities to support a monopile facility than to support fabrication of towers or
 transition pieces.
 3. Blades: Potential allied business support for blade fabrication is quite low compared to
 the other Tier 1 components along the East Coast, with approximately one allied business
 reported for every five tower or transition piece businesses. Additionally, the SMART-
 POWER allied business availability is 16% of total East Coast availability. Considering
 the maritime expertise and number of inland waterways in the SMART-POWER region,
 there may be an opportunity to engage fiberglass boat builders in the offshore wind
 supply chain if their capabilities are transferable to blade subcomponent and material
 supply.
 4. Regional collaboration: No one state has seemingly sufficient allied business support
 for all major components to be manufactured, yet the strengths of individual states can
 likely be leveraged in combination and with coordination to meet Tier 1, 2, and 3 supply
 needs.
The SCC data used for the SSCC entails each participating business self-reporting information in
multiple categories. It is possible that other existing allied businesses are not currently captured
in the results but may already be engaged or could engage in the future. Additionally, self-
reported product categories may be incomplete or misinterpreted by participating businesses.

As such, we present the SSCC as a snapshot of potential business availability but identify
opportunities for more state and industry engagement with Tier 2 and Tier 3 businesses, as well
as transparency by Tier 1 manufacturers in reporting their engaged suppliers.

Numerous small businesses may also have little understanding or information on the offshore
wind industry, including how to participate and how broader risks (like project pipeline or
facility investment uncertainty) may impact them. Visibility into these risks and proactive risk
mitigation can potentially be supported by the states and industry.

5 Individual Facility Evaluation
As illustrated in our baseline, low, and high deployment scenarios (Section 2.3), offshore wind
deployment on the East Coast between 2024 and 2040 may vary significantly depending on
different macroeconomic and industry factors. Across any scenario, our results show that
investment in individual Tier 1 facilities is likely necessary to support the growing demand for
components amidst constrained global supply chains. At the same time, facilities’ success will
depend on securing sufficient up-front funding and component orders year after year.

Based on announced and existing facilities, we model the opportunity for new facilities in the
SMART-POWER region for blades, towers, and monopiles. The following subsections describe
the modeled supply and demand for blades, towers, and monopiles on the East Coast, including
these new facilities, as well as the potential supply chain opportunities and challenges for
investing in each facility type.

5.1 Domestic Tier 1 Component Supply and Demand
New blade, tower, and monopile facility competitiveness is influenced by existing domestic
facilities. Critically, no full blade manufacturing or blade finishing facilities are currently
announced in the United States—despite blades being a significantly constrained component in
the global supply chain—after the cancellation of the Siemens Gamesa finishing facility in
Virginia and the presumed cancellation of the General Electric/LM Wind Power facility in New
York (McCoy et al. 2024).

By the assessment’s data cutoff of November 30, 2024, four manufacturing facilities (within the
three focus areas of blades, towers, and monopiles) had been announced on the East Coast:

 • Monopile facility by EEW in Paulsboro, New Jersey—the only tower or monopile
 facility that has begun production
 • Monopile and tower facility by US Wind and Haizea in Sparrows Point, Maryland
 • Tower facility by Marmen Welcon in Albany, New York 11

5.4 Regional Collaboration for Facility Investments
An assessment of the supply chain across the East Coast presents high levels of uncertainty due
to the complexity of many projects, state deployment targets, procurement schedules, port
development, workforce needs, and many more factors. While these issues still exist at a regional
level, regional collaboration between states, such as the SMART-POWER states, could
potentially address some of the challenges more successfully.

Single states likely will not have a large enough pipeline of projects to fully support a new
supply chain facility, but a regional pipeline could potentially have sufficient buying power to
fill the order books of new factories robustly. In this section, we consider the size of the supply
chain that would be required to meet component demand exclusively in the SMART-POWER
region. In this sensitivity study, we remove existing and announced factories from other regions
(i.e., the EEW monopile factory in New Jersey and the Marmen Welcon tower facility in New
York) and add new manufacturing resources that could potentially be developed to support
projects in SMART-POWER lease areas. We retain the announced US Wind facility that is
expected to produce towers and monopiles at Sparrows Point, Maryland. As a result, supply and
demand become more balanced as the market is less prone to oversupply and undersupply.

The localized pipeline of projects (illustrated in Appendix A for the SMART-POWER region) is
mostly met in our analysis by the supply modeled in the region for all components (Figure 15).
The boom/bust cycle of projects is still present in the baseline deployment scenario, also driven
by the lack of projects installed in some years in the region, but the impact of oversupply and
undersupply is reduced. Moderate oversupply for facilities may be more easily addressed by
supplying components to East Coast projects outside the SMART-POWER region, and similarly,
moderate undersupply for projects may be more readily addressed by sourcing components from
other East Coast or foreign facilities.

Nonetheless, there could likely be an increase in oversupply and undersupply risk in a high
deployment scenario, where the increased production capacity of all three components is not

adequately met in years without project installations, and greater capacity installed from new
projects or increased capacity density is not met as closely by the region’s supply on peak
deployment years. This scenario highlights the potential for greater regional collaboration across
the East Coast to reduce these risks.

5.6 Implications for the SMART-POWER States
The opportunities for new facility investments in the SMART-POWER region will likely require
involvement from all participating states to realize their full benefits and reduce uncertainty for
manufacturers and developers. We outline below four main implications for the SMART-
POWER states:

 1. Additional manufacturing capacity is likely needed for the offshore wind sector as a
 whole and for the SMART-POWER states to meet target deployment goals. With global
 supply chain constraints, domestic manufacturing for blades, towers, and monopiles is
 likely insufficient for projects to be constructed in the 2020s and early 2030s. The
 assessment’s results indicate potential opportunity for at least one or two blade facilities
 and one tower facility, assuming all currently announced facilities come online as
 expected. Additional investment opportunities may exist for any of the three components
 if announced facilities are canceled or delayed.
 2. Collaborative strategies from the SMART-POWER states could potentially de-risk
 manufacturing investments by enabling or encouraging a more consistent pipeline and
 robust order books for manufacturers within the SMART-POWER region. This would
 likely provide additional stability to the region’s projects by potentially getting priority
 access to components. Additional collaboration with other East Coast states may be
 beneficial to address potential periods of oversupply and undersupply.
 3. There is a funding gap for new facilities that could potentially be bridged with tax credits,
 state incentives or other mechanisms, as evidenced by the impact of modeled tax credits
 such as the AMPTC. Earlier supply chain investments may also reduce risk of component
 procurement bottlenecks when the global supply chain is most stressed. New facilities for
 towers or monopiles face increased competition from announced facilities and thus may
 also benefit from a first-mover advantage.
 4. Grants and incentives are not necessarily the only way to attract investments; the
 SMART-POWER states have demonstrated through recent legislation, targets, and
 policies that they are “open for business” and that their positive business proposition can
 be competitive with other East Coast states. Under CNBC’s 2024 annual state
 competitiveness for business rankings, Virginia and North Carolina were ranked as first
 and second for highest overall competitiveness (CNBC 2024). Reducing risk for
 facilities, mentioned above, may also attract manufacturers to their region.
As detailed in previous sections, there are several SMART-POWER ports with the physical
requirements and workforce readiness for Tier 1 manufacturing, as well as supporting businesses
across the entire region. While regional collaboration is likely essential to realize the benefits
offered by a U.S. offshore wind industry, each state has different strengths to leverage the
business opportunities and overcome the challenges discussed.

Delaware, while likely not pursuing a Tier 1 facility, has demonstrated support for the offshore
wind industry, most recently with the passing of the Delaware Energy Solutions Act to allow up
to 1,200 MW of offshore wind procurement (Jeanfreau 2024). Delaware is well placed to
complement regional players through its workforce and its lower-tier suppliers as well as tap into
its business, research, and development expertise at hubs like the Science, Technology and
Advanced Research Campus at the University of Delaware. Its location between Maryland and

 46

New Jersey may enable Delaware to leverage partnerships and regional benefits both north and
south of the state. While Tier 1 investment decisions from other states may influence lower-tier
businesses in Delaware, proactive strategies to support small business diversification and
adaptability have the potential to reduce dependency on external investment decisions.

Additionally, there is potential for a Delaware port, such as Port of Wilmington, to serve as a
marshaling port rather than a manufacturing or staging port (see Sections 6.1 and 6.2 for
additional opportunities for marshaling and component storage at SMART-POWER ports).

Maryland has a demonstrated interest and capacity to participate in the offshore wind supply
chain through its manufacturing and port facilities at Sparrows Point and through secondary steel
production by Crystal Steel Fabricators at Federalsburg, an announced Ørsted operations and
maintenance hub in Ocean City and another by US Wind in West Ocean City, and a new
Hellenic Cables manufacturing facility in Baltimore. The state can continue to build on these
facilities as a hub for offshore wind (McCoy et al. 2024; Hooper 2024). The Hellenic Cables
facility is also an example of an offshore wind energy manufacturer receiving a Qualifying
Advanced Energy Project (48C) tax credit from the Inflation Reduction Act (Cenergy Holdings
2024). The state’s historic resources in the maritime and manufacturing industries can potentially
be further revitalized and leveraged for Tier 1 manufacturing and supporting business.

Virginia counts on the experience and expanded capacities at Portsmouth Marine Terminal to
support Tier 1 manufacturing, needing little additional investment to accommodate
manufacturing of blades, and existing capacity for monopiles. High staging and manufacturing
readiness at Portsmouth Marine Terminal are complementary to additional availability at
Fairwinds Landing for offshore wind supply chain investments. A new cable manufacturing
facility by LS GreenLink was also announced to be built in Chesapeake (Office of the Governor
of Virginia 2024). The state’s significant shipbuilding, steel rolling, and welding expertise
extend opportunities for Virginia beyond Tier 1 manufacturing.

North Carolina has Port of Morehead City and Port of Wilmington as two considerably
different but capable ports for Tier 1 manufacturing, with the likely ability to support more than
one Tier 1 facility. There are significant manufacturing strengths and legacies statewide,
particularly for steel manufacturing, and significant inter/intra-state collaboration working
toward renewable energy targets. To prepare for potential in-state manufacturing facility
investments, the North Carolina Taskforce for Offshore Wind Economic Resource Strategies, or
NC TOWERS, was established in 2021 under Executive Order 218, titled Advancing North
Carolina’s Economic and Clean Energy Future with Offshore Wind (North Carolina Department
of Commerce 2024). The order also established the state’s offshore wind targets and created the
position for Assistant Secretary of Clean Energy Economic Development in the Department of
Commerce to find economic and workforce development opportunities, particularly in the
offshore wind industry.

6.1 Marshaling Ports
6.1.1 Asset Description and Regional Demand
A marshaling port is a coastal facility where a fixed-bottom offshore wind project stages major
components and transports them to an installation vessel (Shields et al. 2023). The capabilities of
the port are critical for enabling an efficient installation rate for an offshore wind project. The
size and weight of offshore wind components require design characteristics beyond most existing
ports in the United States. Particular design drivers include having sufficient laydown area,
quayside length, berth depth, channel depth and width, quayside and upland bearing capacity,
and air draft (Shields et al. 2022). The design requirements may change based on whether the
port will interact with feeder barges or larger WTIVs, which could require deeper drafts and
higher air drafts. A basic list of marshaling port requirements is provided in Table 9. See
Parkison and Kempton (2022) for a more detailed description of the activities at a marshaling
port.

6.1.3 Considerations for Investment
A marshaling port could cost on the order of $300–$400 million to construct (Shields et al.
2023), requiring careful consideration of the investment. Committing to such an investment
would require a reasonable degree of confidence that the port would have a consistent series of
contracts, likely for at least the first 10 years of operation; however, the certainty in this pipeline
has been a challenge for the U.S. offshore wind industry in the due to permitting delays and
project terminations (Shields et al. 2023). For the Central Atlantic region to invest in a new
marshaling port, additional study would be required to understand the degree to which the
deployment of the regional pipeline is constrained by existing ports (i.e., Sparrows Point and
Portsmouth Marine Terminal). Such a study could potentially consider the anticipated
deployment schedule of the projects, proximity of the ports to offshore wind projects, likely
vessel spreads for each project, state procurement schedules, and the capabilities of each port.

6.2 Tier 1 Component Storage at Ports
6.2.1 Asset Description and Regional Demand
The establishment of one or more Tier 1 component storage ports within the SMART-POWER
region could potentially enable more continuous production by a new Tier 1 fabrication facility,
while still enabling finished Tier 1 components to be readily available for marshaling and
deployment. This is particularly important to reduce the risk of oversupply and undersupply of
components that may result from a boom/bust demand-supply cycle. As shown in Figure 17
(with blades as an example), Tier 1 component demand in the baseline deployment scenario
exhibits significant variation of this sort, driven by multiple projects having a required COD in
the same year. This coincidence of CODs is generally driven by the alignment of various state
offshore wind procurement targets.

 Figure 17. Peaks in blade demand and supply for blades in a baseline deployment scenario.
 Blades are assumed to be procured from suppliers 2 years before a project’s COD. Each blade plant produces 225
 blades/year.

States are likely to have limited or perhaps no flexibility or mechanism to coordinate their
offshore wind procurement CODs. Storage ports therefore may provide a practical means to
buffer potentially erratic demand for Tier 1 components, enabling Tier 1 fabrication facilities to
schedule stable production, year-on-year, while reducing the risk of supply chain bottlenecks for
developers.

To better understand factors that may impact a component storage strategy, we conducted a high-
level analysis of blade demand variation from 2024 to 2040 by evaluating three interacting input
parameters. Storage of other Tier 1 components such as towers or monopiles may differ in
storage time requirements, or spatial requirements at ports, but are expected to follow similar
patterns. For the analysis, we assume a set number of blades are stored for a given duration
before procurement, if the expected demand in a given year surpasses a “triggering threshold,”
described by three parameters:

 51

 • Storage duration: Are blades to be stored for 1 or 2 years before being procured by a
 developer for project deployment? In other words, is blade demand shifted 1 or 2 years
 before project demand?
 • Triggering threshold: What is the forecast demand threshold (in number of blades/year),
 above which the forecast demand triggers the need for storage? In years where forecast
 blade demand is less than the threshold, no storage would be needed for that year; when
 the forecast demand exceeds the threshold, a certain percentage of that year’s total
 demand will be shifted earlier by using storage to reduce the excess peak demand. 16
 • Percentage shifted: What percentage of a given year's forecast blade demand requires
 storage, thereby shifting production to an earlier year?

The resulting metric quantifies variability of annual blade production, as measured by its
standard deviation. As a reference point, the standard deviation of the original baseline (no
storage), is 538 blades/year. Figure 18 presents the results of the parametric analysis as a heat
map, with greener shades indicating that the standard deviation of year-on-year blade production
is less than the standard deviation of the baseline scenario (i.e., better matched to consistent
production), whereas redder shades indicate that the standard deviation is more than the standard
deviation of the baseline scenario (more erratic).

 Figure 18. Standard deviation of year-on-year blade production for different storage parameters.
The storage case with lowest standard deviation, framed by a black rectangle, corresponds to 2 years of storage with
 a 1,200 blade/year threshold and 25% shift, detailed in Figure 19.

16
  Our parametric analysis is not iterative; therefore, if blade demand is shifted to prior years to the extent where the
earlier year now surpasses the triggering threshold, demand stays fixed at that point rather than being further shifted.
This can result in new demand peaks and is one of the main reasons why some storage parameter combinations
worsen blade demand variability.

The best-case scenario (Figure 19) triggers storage when forecast blade demand exceeds 1,200
blades/year, and stores 25% of that year’s forecast total demand over the prior two years. In this

6.2.2 Potential Resources in the Central Atlantic Region
The area required to store these quantities of blades can be estimated from recent investments in
the Port of Hull, which is located on the north bank of the Humber Estuary on the North Sea
coast of England. Hull has the United Kingdom’s largest blade fabrication plant to date,
developed and owned by turbine original equipment manufacturer SGRE, with its first 75-m-
long blades produced in 2016 for its 6-MW and 7-MW offshore wind turbines (Russell 2016). In
2021–2022, the SGRE blade plant in Hull was expanded to produce 108-m blades for its new 14-
MW to 15-MW offshore wind turbines, with 60 acres available to maneuver and store blades
(Snieckus 2023).
We estimate that at the SGRE facility, a minimum of 2.3 acres are needed immediately adjacent
to any one of the fabrication or finishing halls, in order to have the circular space needed to
rotate a single blade or blade mold for maneuvering into and out of a building. This would leave
57.7 acres for the reported storage capacity of 240 blades at Alexandra Dock (Maslin 2022). This
implies a packing density of 25 blades per 6 acres, or a packing density of 4.2 blades per acre
at a blade storage port. Based on this packing density, a blade storage port optimized for the
best-case demand-shifting scenario described above (400 blades) should have an upland area of
at least 95–100 acres. A smaller area, approximately 60 acres (250 blades), would meet less-
demanding scenarios for blade storage.

 53

Since blades are transported horizontally, storage ports can potentially be sited upriver from
bridges, power lines, and other river crossings. Due to their upriver location, the real estate for
these properties is generally less costly to lease or purchase than for industrial sites that are
located closer to open-water shipping lanes and which have no overhead obstructions along their
routing to open water.

6.2.3 Considerations for Investment
The cost to develop a waterfront industrial site into a storage port for blades or monopiles is at
least an order of magnitude less than the cost to develop a marshaling port as estimated in
Section 6.1. The Port of Argentia in Newfoundland, Canada, is a good example of an industrial
site that has been developed as a storage port for monopiles and blades for U.S. East Coast
offshore wind projects.

In August 2022, the Port of Argentia secured its first monopile storage contract, and a second
contract was signed in March 2023. The required infrastructure improvements included widening
roads, burying utility lines, and creating 3 acres of new laydown area adjacent to the docking
berth, for which the Port of Argentia invested CAD 10 million (USD 7.3 million) over the course
of 10 months (Memija 2023), making the site ready to receive its first monopile shipment.

The port has dedicated over 70 hectares (173 acres) of paved runways to create the upland
storage yard, which has a total storage capacity of 120 monopiles (The Maritime Executive
2024). This implies a packing density of 0.7 monopiles per acre.

Overall, Tier 1 component storage ports present the following opportunities and challenges:

 • Based on ongoing commercial experience, storage ports can potentially be cost-effective
 over a range of upland areas, ranging from 6 acres (as the SGRE blade storage yard in the
 Port of Hull) to nearly 180 acres (as at the monopile storage yard at the Port of Argentia).
 • Ten acres of industrial waterfront upland area can accommodate approximately seven
 monopiles or 42 blades. Thus, 30 acres could likely accommodate 14 monopiles and the
 blade sets for 14 15-MW turbines, corresponding to 210 MW of future offshore wind
 generation capacity per 30 acres of aggregated storage capacity.
 • Based on the Port of Argentia, a storage port investment of USD 10 million could be
 sufficient to develop 180 acres of upland storage and associated quayside improvements,
 corresponding to 1,260 MW of future offshore wind generation capacity. This could store
 approximately 84 monopiles and 252 blades.
 • On the urbanized U.S. East Coast, no such single, large property exists that is not already
 being used as a commercial shipping port or being developed as an offshore wind
 marshaling port. To realize the same storage potential, multiple sites would likely have to
 be developed, and this may introduce logistics complexity.

6.3 Alternative Foundation Fabrication
6.3.1 Asset Description and Regional Demand
All U.S. East Coast offshore wind energy projects under construction or in environmental review
as of November 2024 were planning to install extra-extra-large (XXL) monopiles, having a
bottom diameter of 8.5‒11 m. To build out all active East Coast commercial wind energy leases
in this way would likely require an average supply of 210, 300, and 350 monopiles per year in
low, baseline, and high deployment scenarios, respectively, from 2024 through 2033. As
highlighted in Section 2.1, Europe currently has surplus monopile production capacity that can
supplement the U.S. monopile supply, which at present is quite limited (Global Wind Energy
Council 2023; Wood Mackenzie 2023). However, Europe’s surplus capacity is expected to be
fully absorbed by European demand beyond 2026 (Rystad Energy 2023). Additionally, a
monopile fabrication facility is estimated to cost $300–$350 million with a lead time of 3 years
or longer (Rystad Energy 2023).

By comparison, the U.S. domestic supply chain for monopiles is limited. The monopile finishing
plant in Paulsboro, New Jersey, is expected to be able to fully fabricate 100 XXL monopiles per
year by 2026, processing 0.15 million metric tons (mt)/year of steel (Magyar 2023). The second
announced U.S. monopile fabrication plant at Sparrows Point, Maryland, is expected to have an
annual production capacity of ~67 XXL monopiles, processing 0.10 mt/year of steel (US Wind
n.d.). As a point of comparison, Dominion’s 2.6-GW CVOW project is expected to install 176
XXL monopiles in 2024−2025, having a total weight of 0.27 mt. CVOW’s monopiles are
imported from Europe, but this reference point suggests that just one future project of this size
would consume nearly all the currently announced U.S. monopile production capacity.

Construction of a monopile fabrication plant takes, on average, 3 years of lead time (Rystad
Energy 2023), which may be insufficient to establish a new facility in the United States to
address a monopile supply shortfall in the late 2020’s. However, establishing outdoor fabrication
facilities for gravity base foundations (GBFs) or suction bucket jackets (SBJs; see an example
SBJ in Figure 20) could be established in 12–18 months.

Producing either or both of these alternative foundation types in the SMART-POWER region
could diversify the domestic supply chain for fixed-bottom offshore wind foundations.
Moreover, both these alternative foundation types can be designed to have mass and buoyancy
distributions that enable them to be self-floating, capable of being deployed by simple harbor
tugs.

 55

 Figure 20. Example three-legged suction bucket jacket designed to support a 10-MW reference
turbine (178-m rotor diameter) in 30-m depth (adapted from Kempton et al. 2017). The SBJ design
 has 1,234 t mass, 3 x 12 m diameter suction caissons, and 3.6 m towing draft.
Thus, addressing the monopile supply gap with self-floating alternative foundation types could
potentially avoid the need for heavy lift foundation installation vessels. The looming global
heavy lift floating foundation installation vessel (FFIV) shortage will likely have a negative
impact on nations meeting their offshore wind procurement targets (Spinergie 2024). In terms of
total annual vessel-years, the global market demand (excluding China) for FFIVs is 9 years of
vessel time as of 2023, which was comfortably met by a supply of 12 vessel-years. By 2029,
however, FFIV demand is forecast to be 29 years, but the global FFIV fleet will be able to supply
only 15 years.

In addition to overcoming forecast global shortfalls in monopile supply and FFIV availability,
self-floating fixed-bottom foundations may avoid the environmental effect of impact pile driving
and added cost of noise mitigation, such as bubble curtains. Since monopile driving is an intense
sound source, albeit over limited periods of time relative to a project’s construction phase, U.S.
East Coast pile driving for offshore wind is confined to the 6-month period from May to
October, when the endangered North Atlantic right whale is not migrating, and then only during
daylight hours, due to the need for visual detection of dolphins, whales, or sea turtles by
protected species observers (EDF Energy 2019).

By eliminating impact pile driving, self-floating foundations can potentially be installed year-
round, likely improving a project’s delivery timeline while being more environmentally benign.
As illustrated in Figure 21, this flexibility would more than double the available offshore
installation hours within a calendar year. Even during stormier winter months, there can be calm
periods of several days to a few weeks, which would likely be sufficient to mobilize readily
available harbor tugs to take advantage of even a short weather window to deploy several self-
floating foundations offshore before the next storm develops.

6.3.2 Potential Resources in the Central Atlantic Region
GBFs and SBJs have been fabricated in the United Kingdom using floodable graving docks, but
this infrastructure is not readily available in the United States, where floating drydocks are more
common. Floating drydocks, however, are designed for ships and typically do not have sufficient
width for a GBF or SBJ that can support a large offshore wind turbine. A solution could be to
identify an ocean deck barge with suitable deck area and strength, which can be temporarily
outfitted with proven ballasting boxes to create a semisubmersible work platform. This enables
dry fabrication of a gravity base slab and perimeter wall, after which the barge deck is then
submerged to float off the base to allow finishing alongside the quay.

SBJs for a given turbine size are significantly lighter than GBFs, and SBJ fabrication can be
done on any quay already capable of supporting monopiles because SBJs also typically weigh
less than monopiles. Once the SBJ has been fully fabricated, complete with secondary steel,
coatings, and sacrificial anode installation, self-propelled mobile transporters can be used to

 57

move the SBJ onto a semisubmersible barge platform, which is then submerged to float off the
finished SBJ for towing offshore.

As determined by prior NREL research, a dedicated facility to fabricate self-floating GBFs or
SBJs with a throughput of 50 structures per year would likely need to meet the following
requirements:

 • A quayside section length of 300 m and a width of 100 m
 • The above plan dimensions have a surface area of 30,000 m2 (or 7.4 acres)
 • The quayside area should have an average bearing strength of at least 6 t/m2
 • The quay alongside charted depth, which corresponds to mean lower low water must be
 greater than 7.8 m, and the access channel to open-water sea routes should be deeper than
 10 m at mean lower low water
 • No overhead restrictions (e.g., unlimited air draft).

that between them could potentially cover the entire U.S. East Coast at foundation towing speeds
of 2 to 4 knots and which have over a kilometer of berthing space, enabling a GBF or SBJ
fabrication operation to comfortably coexist with offshore wind project marshaling activities.

6.3.3 Considerations for Investment
While the monopile supply chain has been well-industrialized in Europe, existing and announced
European monopile fabrication plants will not be able to meet European offshore wind demand
and thus will not have any surplus capacity to supply U.S. projects after 2026 or 2027. At that
point, U.S. monopile production capacity will be under 200 XXL monopiles per year,

 58

insufficient to support more than 3 GW/year of offshore wind capacity. By comparison, as
shown in the baseline deployment scenario (Figure 2), the East Coast supply chain may
potentially experience annual fixed-bottom offshore wind project deployment ranging from 4 to
8 GW per year from 2030 through 2035.

The lead time and capital investment required for a new monopile plant is substantial (3 years or
more), and this barrier is compounded by the relatively short build-out period for fixed-bottom
foundations before the U.S. East Coast offshore wind project pipeline is expected to transition to
mostly floating wind projects. By comparison, concrete GBF and steel SBJ outdoor fabrication
facilities can likely be established within 12–18 months for a much lower capital investment
(estimated $25–$30 million) (Rystad Energy 2023). This means that the capital investment can
be recovered more quickly and may be a better fit to the relatively short fixed-bottom project
build-out from 2030 through 2035.

The greatest barrier to adoption of self-floating alternative foundations may be the lack of any
existing U.S. East Coast fabrication facilities, which is perceived as a supply chain risk. For
SBJs, there are existing jacket fabrication facilities that supply production platforms to Gulf and
Caribbean offshore oil and gas industry, and a lower-risk first step would be for these to fabricate
jacket frames, that could be shipped at “flat packs” to SBJ assembly yards on the East Coast.
This approach was successfully undertaken by ScottishPower Renewables for the three-legged,
pin-pile jackets at its East Anglia One project.

The SMART-POWER region is well positioned with assets that could accommodate the
fabrication of alternative foundations to monopiles, including but not limited to concrete GBFs
and steel SBJs. This could benefit the U.S. offshore wind industry be alleviating shortfalls in
monopile supply from European and domestic monopile fabrication plants as well as a looming
global shortage of heavy lift foundation installation vessels. Fabrication facilities of these
foundation types can be established with relatively low capital investment ($25 to $30 million)
and short lead times of 12 to 18 months. Since offshore oil and gas jacket fabrication yards
already exist in the Gulf and Caribbean Sea, they potentially offer a relatively low-risk pathway
by which they could ship “flat packs” of jacket frames and other components to assembly ports
in the SMART-POWER region.

7 Conclusions and Future Work
This report has provided an initial analysis of how the SMART-POWER region could leverage
existing strengths in manufacturing capabilities, workforce readiness, and port assets to develop
a regional offshore wind supply chain. We have introduced a methodology that evaluates supply
chain investment based on key metrics for manufacturing investments, workforce, and
community impact, and have been able to use this methodology to identify potential
opportunities that could benefit both the region and the offshore wind industry. In particular, we
find that developing tower, blade, or monopile manufacturing resources could help projects to
avoid delays attributed to anticipated global supply chain bottlenecks with the added potential
benefit of creating jobs and economic growth. Regional communication and coordination
between the Central Atlantic states of Delaware, Maryland, Virginia, and North Carolina, which
builds off of the existing SMART-POWER framework, could help achieve these benefits by
establishing strategic plans for building supply chain assets, training the workforce, and
advancing offshore wind project development.

7.1 Key Takeaways
Takeaway #1: The existing and announced manufacturing facilities in the United States
likely do not have sufficient production capacity to support the demand for towers, blades,
and monopiles from offshore wind projects in the SMART-POWER region (or for the
whole East Coast pipeline, as estimated by our deployment scenarios).

 • The list of existing and announced factories includes tower facilities in New York
 (Marmen Welcon), Maryland (US Wind/Haizea), and an as-yet-undisclosed location (US
 Forged Rings), and a monopile facility in New Jersey (EEW) as well as monopile
 manufacturing at the same Maryland facility (US Wind/Haizea).
 • Global supply expects to see significant bottlenecks for these components beginning in
 2025–2028 and lasting at least until 2030, which corresponds to potential increase in
 component demand to meet U.S. pipeline needs (starting in the early 2030s).
Takeaway #2: We estimate that expanding the supply chain to include two full blade
manufacturing facilities and one new tower facility could potentially reduce the risk of
undersupply from 2027 to 2040 for the East Coast deployment scenarios considered in this
report. Investing in additional monopile facilities beyond the two that have already been
announced would likely require a higher deployment scenario.

 • These estimates are based on our assessment of supply and demand across the East Coast,
 and an evaluation of the business case metrics for each component across the three
 deployment scenarios.
 • The opportunity for new facilities is greatest for full blade manufacturing due to the
 critically insufficient expected supply, with blades likely in high demand on the East
 Coast across all deployment scenarios. Our results indicate a potentially high business
 opportunity for at least one facility in a baseline or low deployment scenario, and at least
 two facilities in a high deployment scenario.
 • Towers also represent a significant opportunity, although likely for just one more facility
 on the East Coast to avoid redundancy and oversupply under a baseline or high

 60

 deployment scenario; a low deployment scenario results in a lower business case for a
 new tower facility.
 • Monopiles represent a greater risk of insufficient demand to justify an additional facility
 across scenarios, particularly due to decreased monopile demand after 2030 with an
 increase in floating wind projects. Nonetheless, projects in the 2020s and early 2030s
 may face monopile shortages that may prompt other solutions such as facility expansions
 or greater investment in alternative foundations.
 • There is continuing uncertainty and challenges for the presently announced facilities,
 especially in light of investment cancellations over the past 2 years. While this poses an
 additional challenge for developers as they work to secure components, it may also lead
 to opportunities for additional facilities if any of the current announcements are delayed
 or canceled.
Takeaway #3: The component demand from the pipeline of SMART-POWER projects
would likely be sufficient to cover the supply of components from these two blade facilities
and one tower facility, although new facilities will likely need additional funding to make
projects financially viable.

 • Across the three deployment scenarios, the SMART-POWER states account for 24%–
 37% of overall East Coast deployment by 2040. The resulting market is likely sufficient
 to cover the majority of orders needed by new blade and tower facilities in the region, in
 addition to the announced US Wind/Haizea tower and monopile facility.
 • Even with sufficient component demand, any new facility is likely to experience funding
 gaps that may limit their financial viability. Potential approaches to bridge funding gaps
 may include tax credits like the AMPTC, state incentives, or other mechanisms.
 • Collaboration within the SMART-POWER region could potentially provide the region’s
 projects first access to much-needed Tier 1 components, reducing the risk of facing
 supply chain bottlenecks at a national or global level.
 • Collaboration with the broader East Coast supply chain and project pipeline can reduce
 uncertainty for SMART-POWER projects and manufacturers by matching over or
 undersupply of components with the broader East Coast demand and supply.
Takeaway #4: The SMART-POWER states are well suited to host new Tier 1 facilities
because they have at least six potential ports capable of hosting manufacturing facilities
(one to two per state), with a highly capable regional workforce and supporting business
network.

 • All six identified ports meet the minimum physical characteristics needed for Tier 1
 manufacturing or can reach them with additional investment.
 • There is high workforce readiness at Port of Wilmington in Delaware, Sparrows Point in
 Maryland, the Hampton Roads area in Virginia (including Fairwinds Landing and
 Portsmouth Marine Terminal), and Port of Wilmington in North Carolina. Portsmouth
 Marine Terminal and Port of Morehead City, North Carolina, could potentially increase
 workforce readiness with improvements to commuting access and regional connectivity
 between jobs, workers, and training or educational programs.
 • While North Carolina and Maryland have historic manufacturing strengths in the steel
 industry, their existing skilled manufacturing workers have transferable skills in

 61

 occupations needed for blade manufacturing as well. As such, high workforce readiness
 for steel manufacturing indicates a similarly elevated workforce readiness level for blade
 manufacturing.
 • There is a strong network of Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers in the region, with dozens of
 businesses indicating an availability and interest to support blade, tower, or monopile
 manufacturing. This includes key offshore wind manufacturers established in the region
 such as secondary steel production by Crystal Steel Fabricators at Federalsburg,
 Maryland, a new Hellenic Cables manufacturing facility in Baltimore, Maryland, and a
 new cable LS GreenLink manufacturing facility in Chesapeake, Virginia.
 • Suppliers in the region that are currently active in other industries, such as fiberglass boat
 fabrication, may be well suited to engage in component and subcomponent
 manufacturing. A deeper dive is needed to understand how businesses in the offshore
 wind industry and adjacent industries could effectively integrate into a robust offshore
 wind supply chain.
Takeaway #5: Port communities around at least four ports have a high potential for
positive investment impact, which could be enhanced with targeted engagement between
industry and community members to improve the potential for positive outcomes.

 • In addition to job opportunities and business for small and medium enterprises, supply
 chain investments could potentially bring benefits to port communities such as
 investments in education, training, or infrastructure, among other forms of benefits, as
 well as important mitigation of potential negative impacts like noise and pollution from
 construction or manufacturing activity. Port communities with high historic
 underinvestment or unemployment are likely to benefit the most from community
 investments and impact mitigation strategies, including Port of Wilmington, Delaware,
 Fairwinds Landing and Portsmouth Marine Terminal in Virginia, and Port of
 Wilmington, North Carolina.
 • Radio Island development in the Port of Morehead City would be more distant from
 densely populated areas, resulting in a lower investment impact potential but also likely
 minimum negative community impacts.
 • Key existing socioeconomic issues across SMART-POWER ports include high
 percentages of low-income households and higher poverty rates than average
 (disproportionately impacting people of color), and adverse health outcomes from port
 activity such as high adult asthma rates. Although potential negative impacts of offshore
 wind port activity could coincide with existing environmental vulnerabilities and barriers
 to economic mobility, targeted community engagement and benefits could alleviate some
 of these concerns, while distributing more broadly the benefits of renewable energy
 investments.
 • There have been challenges with past and current community engagement efforts, but
 community members and organizations are generally eager for and receptive to
 community engagement that is more representative of multiple community perspectives,
 recognizes different voices and includes them in decision-making processes, and is more
 impactful by leading to tangible community benefits and protections.
Takeaway #6: Interstate collaboration could potentially help develop a regional supply
chain that could meet the component demand from SMART-POWER projects, fill gaps in

 62

the overall East Coast pipeline, and contribute to a stable order book and greater
workforce readiness for new supply chain facilities.

 • Publicly announced CODs of projects in the East Coast pipeline indicate a potential for
 boom/bust cycles in deployment timelines. This erratic schedule may create challenges
 for new supply chain facilities in securing stable order books. Ongoing communication
 between SMART-POWER states and project developers in the region could help
 elucidate what actions could mitigate these risks, such as how state procurement
 schedules could affect CODs or what deployment rates are possible given available local
 supply chain or port resources.
 • Early investments may reduce the risk of component procurement bottlenecks when the
 global supply chain is most stressed beginning in 2025, impacting the numerous projects
 with CODs in 2030–2032. Unlike blade facility investments, new tower or monopile
 facilities will likely face increased competition from announced facilities and thus may
 also benefit from a first-mover advantage.
 • Ports with lower workforce readiness at the local or broader state level could increase
 their readiness through regional coordination within the SMART-POWER region, by
 connecting local and regional workers with local port job opportunities and regional
 programs.
 • The focus of this report has been on collaboration within the SMART-POWER states; as
 a result, Maryland and Virginia show the highest opportunity for regional benefits
 because they each border two states within the region. North Carolina and Delaware can
 also benefit from partnerships within the SMART-POWER region but could also explore
 opportunities with existing supply chains in South Carolina and New Jersey, respectively.

7.2 Future Work
The methodologies and results in this report could be expanded upon to develop a greater
understanding of the challenges and opportunities in the SMART-POWER region or elsewhere
in the U.S. Potential future studies and activities could include:

 • Implementing the methodology from this report in other regions, such as the Northeast,
 the West Coast, or the Gulf, to help to identify the opportunity space for supply chain
 development. Ultimately, results could be compared between different regions.
 • Expanding and refining the database of existing small- and medium-sized businesses in
 the SMART-POWER region that could support the offshore wind industry. These
 findings could then be used to better understand the locations with the strongest
 supporting network for various Tier 1 components.
 • Conducting further analysis to understand how supply chain and deployment activities in
 neighboring regions (particularly in New York and New Jersey) impact the opportunity
 space for the SMART-POWER region.
 • Complementing the desktop studies and evaluated metrics provided in this report with
 additional outreach to communities and small businesses in potential supply chain
 communities.
 • Evaluating the financing needs for new supply chain assets and identifying where actions
 by states could help to de-risk investment.

• Extending the analysis on additional opportunities for the SMART-POWER region on
 marshaling ports, component storage and alternative foundation fabrication to
 characterize the size, benefits and challenges of each opportunity.

Appendix B. Supply Chain Evaluation Methodologies
The methodology for each supply chain evaluation section presented in this assessment is
detailed below. Unless specified, all figures, maps, and graphics were developed by the authors.
Most analyses used a combination of evaluation metrics in order to provide results that can be
compared across ports, deployment scenarios, and components consistently.

B.1 Quantitative Community Impact Analysis
The community impact analysis was composed of two quantitative parts. The first part of the
analysis consisted of developing and calculating the three community impact metrics. As
presented in Section 3.2, the metrics are defined as:

 • Environmental vulnerability: To what extent are environmental challenges experienced
 by port communities?
 • Barriers to economic mobility: To what extent are there barriers present to accessing jobs
 in a port community?
 • Investment impact potential: To what extent could an investment have a positive
 economic impact in a port community?
The level of vulnerabilities, barriers, or impact potential are not an indication of a better or worse
investment opportunity but are socioeconomic cost/benefit trade-offs that can shape the proposal
and development of a port facility. All metrics are weighted equally to allow states and other
stakeholders to compare against their own priorities. Each metric was calculated by port:

 • The census tracts within a 5-mile radius of a port were identified.
 • Community vulnerability data were collected from the first version of the U.S. Council
 on Environmental Quality’s Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool (CEJST,
 Council on Environmental Quality 2022), for the criteria outlined in B-1.
 • A tract was counted as “impacted” if it met the criteria (Table B-1).
 • The proportion of “impacted” tracts out of the total port community tracts was converted
 to a percentage and weighted against the regional average in order to classify it.

B.2 Community Engagement and Qualitative Methods
A literature review was done to analyze the intersections of port communities, environmental

B.3 Workforce Readiness Metric
The companion Workforce report (Pons et al. 2025) contains a more detailed methodology
breakdown for the research and insights in the workforce readiness section (4.1). Multiple
indicators are leveraged to inform the three scores in the workforce readiness metric. The
indicators, their sources, and their assumptions are provided in Table B-3. Once the indicators
were collected, a z-score and associated percentile for each indicator were separately calculated

C.1 Community Impact Metric Breakdown
The six factors included in the environmental vulnerability metric (Figure 6) present similar
trends across ports. Figure C-1 below shows the percentage of the total score each factor
contributes to. Across the board, Superfund site proximity and adult asthma incidence appear to
be the greatest contributors to environmental vulnerability at port communities, although
wastewater discharge is a third major contributor at Port of Wilmington, Delaware, Sparrows
Point, Maryland, and Port of Wilmington, North Carolina. The port community within the 5-mile
radius of Radio Island at the Port of Morehead City has an environmental vulnerability score of
zero due to low incidence of all factors.

The SMART-POWER states as a whole exhibit similar trends, with overall higher relative
incidence of adult asthma than Superfund proximity.

C.3 Community Engagement Interviews
As part of the port community research and the Portsmouth Marine Terminal community
insights, we carried out interviews with port community stakeholders. The following points
summarize the interview that was the most pertinent to issues relating to community engagement
and community impacts or benefits at Portsmouth Marine Terminal; these points are a
complement to the information presented in Section 3.3.

Interviewed person and organization: Garry Harris, President, Center for Sustainable
Communities

Environmental Vulnerability

 • Concern: High asthma rates, pollution, environmental impacts, and public health issues,
 including hypertension and cancer mortality, are significant problems in Portsmouth.
 • Goal to address concern: Mitigate environmental and public health issues through a
 community benefits agreement and improved environmental practices.
 • Current Community Initiatives: A consortium of Hampton Roads groups—including
 urban leagues, community colleges, unions, military, and renewable energy advocacy
 groups—is being formed to address these issues and ensure no externalities are associated
 with the project.
 • Exemplary Programs to Possibly Emulate or Adopt: The Regional Energy Democracy
 Initiative in Texas and Louisiana focuses on energy democracy.
Barriers to Economic Mobility

 • Concern: Dominion supply chain engagement for CVOW is not reaching Portsmouth
 businesses.

 89

 • Goal to address concern: Inform local businesses of Dominion event(s) with enough lead
 time and preparation that local businesses can meaningfully participate.
 • Current community initiatives: Development of a community-based app for renewable
 energy workforce development, involvement of the University of Michigan and Georgia
 Tech in project support, and the creation of community benefits plans and advisory
 boards to guide project integration and impact.
 • Concern: Economic challenges include housing affordability, sustainable wage jobs,
 energy burdens, and health disparities, which hinder economic mobility.
 • Goal to address concern: Enhance economic opportunities through job creation,
 workforce development, and supportive economic policies tailored to the community’s
 needs.
 • Current community initiatives: The creation of jobs and small businesses through
 renewable energy initiatives and workforce development programs; engagement with
 local institutions like Centura College for training and education.
Investment Impact Potential

 • Concern: There is a need to ensure that investments in renewable energy and
 infrastructure projects provide tangible benefits to local communities, especially
 historically underinvested-in areas.
 • Goal to address concern: Maximize the positive economic impact of investments by
 ensuring that they contribute to regional prosperity, job creation, and the development of
 local industries.
 • Current community initiatives: Community ownership over projects/project impacts.
 Pivoting toward a more community-centric approach overall, including tools such as
 Community Benefits Agreements, an advisory board, outreach efforts, and forums.
