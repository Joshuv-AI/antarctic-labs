Why Ethereum Needs Fairness Mechanisms that Do Not Depend on Participant Altruism ISBN: 978-1-4503-XXXX-X/2018/06
Patrick Spiesberger, Nils Henrik Beyer, Hannes Hartenstein

Abstract
. Ethereum’s ideals of decentralization and censorship resistance are undermined in practice, motivating ongoing efforts to reestablish these properties. Existing proposals for fairness mechanisms depend on the assumption that a sufficient fraction of block proposers adhere to Ethereum’s protocols as intended. We refer to such proposers as altruistic, as this behavior may come at the cost of reduced revenue. Prior analyses indicate that a consistent share of 91 % 91\text{\,}\mathrm{\%} of proposers delegate block construction to centralized services, effectively signing externally constructed blocks blindly, and are thus not considered altruistic. To assess whether the remaining 9 % 9\text{\,}\mathrm{\%} of proposers genuinely exhibit altruistic behavior, we conducted an empirical analysis and found that an additional 6.1 % 6.1\text{\,}\mathrm{\%} also interact with such external services. Further, we found that less than 1.4 % 1.4\text{\,}\mathrm{\%} of proposers consistently acted in accordance with Ethereum’s decentralization and censorship resistance objectives. These findings suggest that relying solely on the mere presence of altruistic proposers is insufficient to ensure that proposed fairness mechanisms reestablish Ethereum’s ideals, highlighting the need for additional incentive- or penalty-based mechanisms.

2.
Defining Non-Altruistic Behavior

The term “altruism” is ambiguous and difficult to ground empirically, as moral motivations are unobservable on-chain.
As our objective is not to judge validators, but to infer whether they would deviate from a fairness mechanism once higher revenues become attainable, we adopt a strictly behavior-based definition.
Altruistic behavior is understood as proposer behavior that adheres to the Ethereum protocols without deviation.
Deviations from altruistic behavior manifest along two observable dimensions: (the delegation of) Maxi-
mum Extractable Value (MEV) extraction and shared account governance, which frequently occur concurrently in practice.
In the following sections, we refine our interpretation of (non-)altruism.

2.1.
Extracting Maximum Extractable Value

MEV refers to the additional profit a proposer can capture by strategically ordering, including, or excluding transactions within a block
(
15
)
.
By observing the mempool – a local storage for pending transactions – proposers or delegated entities can exploit publicly broadcast transactions through techniques such as front running or sandwich attacks
(
16
;
7
)
, profiting from user-induced price movements
(
16
)
.
In addition, MEV-related transaction exclusion can also occur, for example by accepting bribes to censor transactions or by preventing the liquidation of their own assets.
These strategies distort execution outcomes and impose additional costs on users, as investigated in prior work
(
7
)
.
We define MEV extraction as non-altruistic behavior when profit-maximizing strategies depend on transaction contents rather than on protocol-defined attributes, such as transaction priority fees voluntarily paid by users
(
5
)
.

In principle, a proposer could extract MEV directly; however, in practice, outsourcing this task to specialized entities, called builders, is often the financially preferable choice.
While this reduces the operational burden on proposers and leads to higher revenue, it also removes the proposers’ abilities to control the block contents, allowing actions like front running or temporary censorship by the delegated party.
Flashbots’ MEV-Boost
(
12
)
exemplifies such delegation by acting as a trusted middleware, with its relays mediating block contents between proposers and builders, thereby implementing Proposer-Builder Separation (PBS).
2
2

2

At the time of this work,
EIP-7732
(PBS enshrined at the consensus layer (ePBS)) was not part of the network. We discuss the impact of ePBS on our analysis in
Section

5
.

In this setup, the builder constructs the block with a focus on maximizing profit, while the proposer merely signs the block selected by a centralized MEV-Boost relay, without any visibility into its contents.
Upon receiving the proposer’s signature, the relay propagates the block.
As a result, the proposer cannot inspect or modify the block’s transactions prior to committing, effectively ceding control over the block’s content to the builder.
This setup centralizes decision-making and incentivizes builders to act in ways that may disadvantage users by constructing blocks that are competitive, often through extensive MEV extraction.
Empirically, MEV-Boost dominates Ethereum today.
Between Q3 2023 and Q2 2025,
88.1\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
–
93.7\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of daily blocks were mediated via MEV-Boost relays
(
17
;
29
)
.
Furthermore, the two largest builders construct over
80\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of all blocks
(
17
)
, illustrating the high centralization inherent in the system.
Consequently, the implementation and dynamics of PBS via MEV-Boost conflict with the decentralization ideal of Ethereum and with the ideal that the network state should be determined by as many independent participants as possible.
We therefore consider proposers who delegate their responsibilities, in addition to engaging in MEV extraction itself, as exhibiting non-altruistic behavior.
We argue that proposers acting in this way offer limited support for fairness mechanisms, as they tend to choose the more profitable strategy whenever circumventing such mechanisms yields higher returns.
If such proposers serve on a committee within a fairness mechanism, and opportunities for non-altruistic, rational behavior arise (e.g., through bribes), we infer that they – or the entity to whom they have delegated their tasks – would take advantage of them.

2.2.
Sharing Account Governance

We refer to the entity that provides the financial resources for a validator as the stakeholder.
3
3

3

A validator account deposits at least 32 Ether (over $
115\,000
as of January 2025).

Operational control over a validator follows exclusively from possession of the validator’s private key, which, under Ethereum’s ideal, is held by the stakeholder.
However, running a validator requires both highly available infrastructure to perform frequent duties and regular maintenance to remain compatible with protocol upgrades.
Due to these requirements, stakeholders typically share infrastructure
(
17
)
that entails possession of multiple validators’ private keys and exercises operational control over those validators.
If a stakeholder grants an infrastructure provider shared or exclusive access to the stakeholder’s validator private key, the stakeholder must either place full trust in that infrastructure provider, operate the infrastructure themselves, or rely on an externally managed custodial arrangement.
Given the substantial capital locked in validator accounts
3
, trust is implausible.
This leaves two prevalent configurations.
First, stakeholders can operate their own infrastructure and manage their validators themselves, implying that a single infrastructure setup holds the private keys for all associated validators.
Second, stakeholders may outsource validator operation to external infrastructures with external custodial arrangements, which retain custody of the private keys for validators of multiple stakeholders.
Such entities are commonly referred to as Staking-as-a-Service (STaaS) providers
(
10
)
, including staking pools.
We therefore move away from the concept that each validator is independent of others, and instead consider setups where operational and strategic control resides with a single entity, such as a STaaS provider.
In both self-managed and externally managed infrastructure setups, we refer to the party that holds operational control as the managing party, and to this party together with the validators it manages as the governance structure
(
21
)
.
Accordingly, we define shared governance as any arrangement in which a managing party controls the keys to at least two validators, regardless of who the stakeholders are.
We argue that once a managing party demonstrates non-altruistic behavior, observable through the actions of a single validator, it is reasonable to expect the same behavior across all validators within its governance structure.
Accordingly, we do not rely solely on block-level evidence for non-altruistic behavior, as is done in related work.
The following two examples demonstrate why proposers within the same governance structure may behave differently, highlighting that a definition based solely on
Section

2.1
is insufficient.
First, proposers can set instructions for the MEV-Boost relay, specifying that blocks from a builder are only accepted if the builder’s offer exceeds a predefined threshold compared to the rewards for constructing the blocks themselves.
4
4

4

In
Lighthouse
(consensus client), this setting is called
builder-boost-factor
.

When a block enabled only limited MEV extraction opportunities, such instructions may result in a block that is indistinguishable from one produced by an altruistic proposer.
In this case, the proposers attempted to behave non-altruistically, but the resulting unsuccessful outcome was influenced by circumstances beyond the proposer’s control, and should therefore not be considered as altruistic.
Second, decentralized STaaS networks such as Lido
(
18
)
exist, which operate with a rotating set of managing parties rather than a single fixed operator.
As a result, operational control over a validator is time-dependent and may alternate among multiple managing parties.
Since stakeholders have no influence over which party controls their validator at any given time, participation in such a network implicitly entails acceptance of rational, non-altruistic behavior whenever at least one managing party acts non-altruistically.
Consequently, observing altruistic behavior for a validator at a specific point in time is insufficient to classify that validator as altruistic if it is known that the same validator may, at other times and without the possibility of stakeholder intervention, be controlled by a non-altruistic managing party.
We therefore argue that, in shared-governance setups with at least one rational managing party, it is prudent to assume that any proposer may deviate from a fairness protocol at an unpredictable point in time.

3.
Methodology

To obtain an upper bound of the actual share of altruistic proposers, we conducted an empirical study spanning the full four-month period from January to April 2025.
From Ethereum’s execution layer, accessed via a self-operated node, we extracted the transactions included in each block.
For each transaction, we recorded the fees paid by the user for prioritized inclusion (priority fees) as well as the
coinbase address
, which identifies the account receiving these fees.
From the Beacon Chain (consensus layer), accessed through the public API of
Beaconcha.in
, we identified the protocol-assigned proposer
(
9
)
via its unique proposer index.
Each execution block references its corresponding Beacon Chain block, enabling cross-layer linkage.
MEV-Boost usage was determined from the public endpoints of well-known relays,

Aestus, Agnostic, bloXroute (Reg.; Max-Profit), Flashbots, Ultrasound, and Titan.
which publish information about their mediated blocks
(
11
;
17
;
28
)
.
Additionally, we incorporated network-layer data

(
31
)
to identify non-broadcast transactions.

3.1.
Analyzing Relay Announcements

As first classifier
I

This exclusion is justified because such behavior reflects an external profit-maximizing intent along with its associated drawbacks, in line with

Method

To identify

5

This procedure allows us to compute both the fraction of mediated blocks among all observed blocks and the fraction of proposers who used MEV

3.2.
Analyzing Shared Account Governance

After excluding proposers who rely on external builders, we subsequently exclude proposers for which we have evidence of participation in a shared governance structure.
Building on our rationale for altruism in

If a cluster contains at least one non-altruistic proposer, all proposers in this cluster are excluded.

Method

This procedure establishes a mapping from

We consider the probability that a proposer specifies a randomly chosen

We proceed methodically as follows: Consider two proposers,

as non-altruistic due to shared governance with

If

\tilde{B}^{P_{B}}

We attribute the missing relay announcement of

P_{B}
.
4

If
P_{A}

P_{A}

As classifier
III
, we differentiate between account types and exclude Contract Account (CA) clusters, retaining only Externally Owned Accounts (EOAs).
EOAs are standard user accounts that, unlike CAs, contain no executable code.
Thus, when EOAs are used as a

CA clusters are excluded because manual inspection indicates that all identified CA clusters belong to STaaS setups with non-altruistic managing parties, where contract code handles the distribution of proposer rewards.
Unassigned CAs, e.g., those with obfuscated code, represent less than
0.01\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of the remaining proposers and are thus negligible for our results.

Method
III
:

To distinguish between account types, we queried our self-operated node for each address using
eth_getCode

We then decompiled the bytecode of contract accounts and manually examined the resulting output to determine their functionality.

In a next refinement step, using classifier
IV

29

to the

of the remaining clusters.
These payments most likely correspond to funds that builders pay proposers for the right to construct blocks.
Since builders are unlikely to send payments to arbitrary addresses, we assume that proposers in these clusters used the builder’s block-building service.
According to our rationale in

2.1
and
2.2

This behavior is particularly prevalent among MEV-extracting STaaS providers, supporting the classification of their proposers as non-altruistic.

Method
IV
:

In order to identify these payments, we query the MEV-Boost relay endpoints
5

The relay response includes a field named

If an MEV-Boost relay returns a recipient matching an unexcluded

3.3.
Examining Block Construction

In
we examined profit-maximizing behavior outsourced to external, centralized services.
With the following classifications, we consider MEV extraction performed directly by proposers.
We analyze two MEV degrees of freedom identified in prior work
(
15
)
: the inclusion of non-publicly propagated transactions (Exclusive Order Flow, XOF transactions) and the ordering of transactions within a block.

As classifier
V
, we distinguish blocks by the presence of XOF transactions and exclude any proposer whose block exhibits them.
Builders and proposers leverage XOF transactions to extract MEV from users by strategically positioning their own transactions around users’ public transactions that are expected to move asset prices
(
7
)
.
We exclude proposers making use of XOF following the rationale in
Section

2.1
.
Based on our manual inspection of observed XOF transactions, we consider the likelihood that a proposer includes their own XOF transaction without profit motives as negligible.

Method
V
:

To detect XOF, we examined the transactions included by the remaining proposers and checked whether they appear in the dataset of
(
3
)
.
This dataset contains all propagated transactions observed by at least one of four monitoring nodes.
Transactions can therefore be divided into those present in the dataset and those absent, the latter most likely corresponding to XOF.
Although it is unlikely that a proposer received a transaction that is unseen by all monitoring nodes, we still observe minor discrepancies when cross-checking our classification with an additional monitor (
Etherscan
).
To avoid substantially underestimating the share of altruistic proposers, we exclude only the identified proposer rather than the entire cluster.
Without this underestimation, misclassifying a single altruistic proposer would lead to the exclusion of all altruistic proposers within the same cluster.

Classifier
VI
distinguishes whether a block’s transaction ordering is verifiably content-independent (i.e., independent of execution data) and excludes proposers with blocks whose ordering cannot be explained by public transaction attributes.
We argue that, in the absence of XOF transactions and under strictly content-independent ordering, proposers cannot reliably extract MEV.
However, an exclusion caused by classifier
VI
does not imply non-altruism, as non-verifiable schemes, such as ordering by internal timestamps, may remain content-independent.
Consequently, we exclude only proposers, not clusters, and treat all remaining proposers excluded by this classifier as potentially altruistic, even when their preference ordering is not transparent.

Method
VI
:

We investigate whether transactions within blocks are ordered according to publicly observable transaction attributes.
Our analysis shows that, when blocks exhibit an ordering, higher-fee transactions generally appear earlier.
Specifically, blocks are typically arranged in an approximately descending sequence of
maxPriorityFeePerGas

(
5
)
.
Such an ordering aims to maximize proposer profits and reflects a simple heuristic implemented by most consensus clients.
Although this ordering is profit-maximizing, we classify the corresponding proposer as altruistic, since the heuristic depends solely on protocol-defined attributes.
Beyond strictly ordered blocks, we identify proposers who deviate from this ordering – i.e., those placing at least one higher-fee transaction after a lower-fee transaction – and classify them as potentially altruistic.
Such deviations can occur even among altruistic proposers, for example when a transaction requires inclusion after a lower-fee transaction, making a strict ordering infeasible.
To quantify ordering beyond a binary distinction, we compute the Spearman rank correlation coefficient (
\sigma_{rs}
)
(
32
)
between transaction priority fees and their positions within the block.
Values of
\sigma_{rs}=1-\epsilon
generally indicate strictly ordered blocks, with
\epsilon>0
accounting for ties in transaction fees.
Following common interpretations of correlation strength
(
22
)
, we consider
\sigma_{rs}>0.7
as strong evidence of altruistic behavior.

For the remaining
0.71\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}

42.1\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of them exhibit a Spearman coefficient of
\sigma_{rs}~>~0.99

99.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}

4.4.
On Potentially Altruistic Proposers

After applying classifiers
I
–
V
, we identified
8155
proposers as potentially altruistic (without definitive evidence).
Of these,
3814
show no indication of violating Ethereum’s intended behavior.
These
8155
proposers correspond to
1.36\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of the total proposer set.
Further, cluster analysis revealed that potentially altruistic proposers are typically organized into small shared governance structures (average 3.95 proposers per cluster).
With a median of one proposer per cluster, some clusters still contain up to
300
proposers.
For ethical reasons, we do not attempt to de-anonymize these larger clusters.
However, we report an empirical observation that provides further context for interpreting the group of proposers we classify as (potentially) altruistic.

A significant portion,
50.01\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
, of the potentially altruistic proposers registered before Ethereum’s transition to Proof-of-Stake (“The Merge”)
(
8
)
. Within the subset of altruistic proposers, the same share amounts to
72.98\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
.
These proposers therefore participated in the early (test) phases of the network, before persistent MEV extraction opportunities existed.

This is especially visible in the validator index range [

Taken together, these findings suggest that proposers from periods without economic incentives are overrepresented in the set of (potentially) altruistic behavior, and their behavior indeed reflects incentive-agnostic participation.

5.
Discussion

Validity under ePBS

With the introduction of Enshrined Proposer-Builder Separation (
ePBS
) in
Ethereum’s Glamsterdam upgrade
, outsourcing block construction to a builder is incorporated into the consensus protocol.
We still regard ePBS as a deviation from Ethereum’s original intent; however, defining non-altruism based on deviations from the protocol is no longer possible.
With ePBS, MEV-Boost relays are no longer required, yet proposers still cannot influence block contents prior to propagation.
Decisions over censorship remain under the builder’s control, while extensive MEV extraction continues.
The findings of this study remain relevant under ePBS, as proposers’ rationality properties are unchanged, since these proposers have already chosen this rational behavior in the past instead of altruism.
If deviating from a fairness mechanism constitutes the financially rational decision, the builder would adopt this deviation to remain competitive, and no intervention by the proposer would be expected.
An intervention (e.g., refraining from outsourcing block production) by a proposer that has previously ‘unofficially’ outsourced block production appears implausible.

On Fairness Mechanisms.

Both Inclusion Lists (ILs)
(
24
)
and Multiple Concurrent Proposer (MCP) setups
(
14
;
19
)
assign a committee to determine which transactions should be included, thereby constructing parts of the block.
These mechanisms enhance censorship resistance because committee members typically have heterogeneous objectives and governance constraints, including censorship resulting from state intervention
(
27
)
.
To remain resilient against bribes, a proposer must either act altruistically and refuse the bribes, or deviations from the protocol must be financially unprofitable under the offered incentives.
In the context of MEV mitigation, Wadhwa et al.
(
26
)
highlight a fundamental limitation: any content-independent ordering policy admits profitable deviations by a coalition of rational actors
(corresponding to non-altruistic proposers in our model).
As a result, content-independent ordering inherently relies on the presence of honest, altruistic participants who follow the protocol even at the cost of foregone revenue.
Under current network conditions, we show that altruistic proposers do exist, but constitute at most a
1.36\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
share.
For committee-based fairness mechanisms, this implies that committees must be sufficiently large, thereby reducing the influence of individual members on the resulting block.
Concretely, a committee size of at least 128 members yields a probability of
1-0.986^{128}\approx$83.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}$
that at least one member can counteract unfair behavior by others, raising concerns about mechanisms that depend solely on altruistic participation.
To incentivize fairness as the rational strategy, Fox et al.
(
13
)
propose a mechanism that rewards proposers for including transactions, with higher rewards assigned to transactions endorsed by only a small number of committee members.
Wadhwa et al. introduce
AUCIL

(
25
)
, an IL design with a perfect Nash equilibrium, in which rational proposers deviate only if an adversary offers sufficiently large bribes to the IL-constructing committee.

FairFIL

(
23
)
is an alternative IL design that shifts the rationality assumption from the proposers constructing the IL to the block builder.
In this design, the inclusion list is no longer created by a dedicated committee; instead, it is created by the builder of block
B_{N}
for slot
N
.
The builder may censor transactions, but is required to record any such censorship explicitly in the
FairFIL
, ensuring accountability.
Subsequent builders must either include the transactions censored in the previous block in block
B_{N+1}
or refrain from constructing that block, as failing to do so would render their block invalid.
This IL design enables verification of the IL in block
B_{N}
by a substantially larger set of validators compared to the size of a committee constructing the IL, thereby significantly increasing the cost of censorship, as an adversary would need to bribe or coordinate Byzantine behavior across this larger group of validators.
However, compared to related fairness mechanisms
(
14
;
25
)
,
FairFIL
incurs an additional one-slot transaction inclusion latency.

Reproducibility and Ethical Considerations.

All data can be reconstructed from the sources listed in Section
3
; public mempools could further be used to identify XOF transactions.
All analysis scripts are publicly available
(
4
)
to support reproducibility.
This study is purely quantitative, analyzing pseudonymous addresses without attempting de-anonymization.
However, de-anonymization could reveal that clusters we treat as distinct may in fact overlap.
In particular, our current method may overestimate the proportion of altruistic proposers.
Nevertheless, we prioritized ethical considerations without expecting significant overall overestimation.

Restricted Observation Window.

Due to the large data volume, our analysis included only
600\,115
proposers, compared to more than one million
7

Beaconcha.in
reported to
1\,067\,651
active validators as of April 30, 2025.
reported validators.
While analyzing only a subset of proposers may introduce deviations in the relative numbers, we consider the resulting upward deviations to be negligible due to the large sample size and Ethereum’s pseudo-random proposer selection process
(
9
)
.
Extending the observation window to longer time periods could reduce the share of (potentially) altruistic proposers, as clusters that appear distinct in the current sample may coincide (clustering) over longer periods.
This further supports the view that the reported
1.36\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
should be considered an upper bound.

Missing Consideration of Transaction Censorship.

Transaction censorship can arise from financial incentives (MEV) or from state-imposed restrictions, such as the U.S. Treasury’s SDN list
(
27
)
.
Both rationales violate Ethereum’s censorship-resistance ideal
(
6
)
.
Nonetheless, we do not consider the exclusion of transactions from a block in this analysis for two reasons.
First, Ethereum nodes do not maintain fully consistent mempools.
As a result, a transaction may be missing unintentionally rather than due to rational or Byzantine behavior by the proposer, providing no reliable evidence of non-altruism.
Second, during our observation period, the only state-imposed restriction known to us was the SDN list.
Sanctions on interactions with the only active service on this list were lifted during the observation window
(
20
)
, and such interactions were rare even when the sanctions were in effect.
For proposers classified as potentially altruistic, we observed no transactions involving sanctioned addresses in the analyzed mempool data.
Therefore, we cannot draw conclusions about whether these proposers comply with state-imposed sanctions.
For these reasons, transaction exclusion is not considered in our analysis, which may slightly overestimate the share of altruistic proposers.

6.
Conclusion

In this empirical study of proposer behavior, we find that Ethereum’s block production is overwhelmingly driven by profit-maximizing strategies.
In line with Buterin’s remark that
"at least a few percent of proposers are altruistic"
, our results do reveal the presence of altruistic proposers.
However, assuming these correspond to proposers not using Flashbots’ MEV-Boost, their actual share is almost an order of magnitude smaller than anticipated.
We find that roughly
98.64\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of proposers behave non-altruistically, while only
1.36\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
provide no clear evidence of such behavior.
Even this fraction likely overestimates true altruism, as borderline cases cannot be conclusively classified.
The reported share should be interpreted as an upper bound, indicating that solely relying on altruism may fail to achieve the intended objectives of proposed fairness mechanisms.
Additional reward or penalty mechanisms therefore seem necessary to ensure fair behavior is the rational choice for proposers.
If committee-based fairness mechanisms continue to depend on altruism, the current share of altruistic proposers indicates that committees must consist of at least 128 members.
In such large committees, the influence of a single proposer is limited, reducing their ability to counteract unfair behavior by non-altruistic participants.
The practical viability of specific committee-based fairness mechanisms warrants further investigation.
