An acoustically transmissive electrochemical vector hydrophone with horn-driven velocity amplification
Authors: Lintao Hu, Qinghua Liu, Honghao Zhang, Hongmin Jiang, Wenlang Zhao, Chuangyi Zheng, Guangyang Gou, Yulan Lu, Junbo Wang, Deyong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low-frequency acoustic sensing is critical for underwater target detection and sound source localization, yet the sensitivity of conventional vector hydrophones is fundamentally limited by the resonance of suspended co-oscillating structures. This paper presents a transmissive electrochemical vector hydrophone with horn-driven particle velocity amplification. By adopting a fixed transmissive configuration, structural resonance is effectively eliminated, while the horn structure enhances particle velocity to improve low-frequency sensitivity. The particle velocity amplification mechanism and its coupling with the electrochemical transduction process are analyzed. Numerical simulations are performed to investigate the effects of horn geometry and structural parameters on the frequency response. Experimental results demonstrate a peak sensitivity of approximately −180 dB re 1 V/μPa at 10 Hz and an effective bandwidth of 10–300 Hz after circuit compensation. The hydrophone exhibits good linearity, a wide dynamic range, and a figure-eight directivity pattern with a null depth exceeding 40 dB, indicating its suitability for low-frequency underwater acoustic detection, with potential applications in underwater surveillance, ocean environmental monitoring, and marine geophysical exploration.

Introduction

Hydrophones are passive sonar devices that convert underwater acoustic signals, such as acoustic pressure, particle velocity, and acceleration, into electrical signals, and are widely used in underwater target detection and sound source localization for marine exploration and unmanned underwater vehicles1–3. Conventional scalar hydrophones measure only acoustic pressure and therefore lack intrinsic directional sensitivity, requiring array processing to estimate the direction of arrival. In contrast, vector hydrophones directly sense acoustic particle velocity or acceleration and intrinsically provide both the magnitude and direction of incident sound waves. Benefiting from their inherent figure-eight directivity pattern, vector hydrophones enable improved localization accuracy and have been increasingly applied in underwater target detection and sound source localization4–6. With the continuous reduction of radiated noise levels from underwater targets7,8, high-frequency acoustic components become increasingly difficult to detect due to strong attenuation, whereas low-frequency acoustic components experience minimal attenuation, allowing them to propagate over much longer distances. Consequently, vector hydrophones with high sensitivity in the low-frequency band are particularly desirable for long-range underwater detection.

According to the vibration sensing mechanism, existing vector hydrophones can be broadly classified into co-oscillating and acoustic transmissive types. Co-oscillating hydrophones rely on suspended mechanical structures, whose low-frequency performance is fundamentally constrained by the resonance of the suspension system9–11. In contrast, acoustic transmissive hydrophones use a liquid with matching acoustic impedance to seawater as a medium to transmit sound waves directly to the sensitive unit inside the hydrophone. This hydrophone design features a fixed installation that avoids generating resonance frequencies capable of interfering with low-frequency detection, making sound transmission-type hydrophones increasingly popular. Representative acoustic transmissive vector hydrophones include hot-wire velocity-type devices12 and cilia-inspired acceleration-type devices13–19. Although these devices avoid suspension-induced resonance, their low-frequency sensitivities (−207 dB re 1 V/μPa20 and −214.4 dB re 1 V/μPa21 at 20 Hz for typical hot-wire and cilia-based devices, respectively) remain insufficient for practical long-range underwater detection.

Electrochemical sensing mechanisms exhibit intrinsically high sensitivity and low mechanical noise at low frequencies, owing to liquid-phase inertial transduction and ion-transport-based signal conversion22–25. Motivated by these advantages, electrochemical transduction has recently been introduced into vector hydrophones to enable the detection of very-low-frequency underwater acoustic signals. For example, Zhong et al. integrated MEMS-based electrochemical sensors into co-oscillating vector hydrophones and achieved improved low-frequency sensitivity26. However, despite the favorable low-frequency characteristics of electrochemical transduction, the overall performance of existing electrochemical vector hydrophones remains fundamentally constrained by suspension-induced resonance inherent to the co-oscillating configuration, which limits further extension toward ultra-low-frequency underwater acoustic detection.

In this work, we propose a sound-transmissive electrochemical vector hydrophone that integrates suspension-free acoustic transmission with electrochemical sensing to overcome the low-frequency limitations of existing vector hydrophones. A horn structure is incorporated to further enhance particle velocity and improve sensitivity. A theoretical model is established to describe sound transmission in the hydrophone, electrolyte vibration near the electrodes, and the resulting electrochemical transduction, and is validated by numerical simulations. The effects of key structural parameters, including the horn-shaped cavity, membrane properties, electrolyte concentration, and pore size, on sensitivity are systematically investigated. Furthermore, a four-electrode integrated electrochemical sensing chip is fabricated using MEMS technology and assembled in a dedicated underwater package. The performance of the proposed hydrophone is experimentally verified in a standing-wave tube over frequencies above 10 Hz.

Materials and methods

Structure and working principle

As shown in Fig. 1a, the core of the horn-driven acoustically transmissive electrochemical hydrophone comprises acoustic membranes, an electrolyte, sensitive electrodes and a chamber. When longitudinal waves from a sound source reach the hydrophone, the acoustic impedance matching provided by the transmissive membranes ensures minimal reflection and efficient coupling of acoustic energy into the internal chamber. Specifically, in the low-frequency regime, external sound pressure creates an instantaneous pressure gradient across the device. Driven by this pressure difference, the flexible membranes undergo macroscopic deformation (as depicted by the dashed lines). Numerical simulation

The theoretical analysis presented above provides a foundational understanding of the sensing mechanism and explains the experimental trends. However, due to the intricate acoustic impedance matching and the complex boundary conditions of the electrochemical reactions, deriving an exact analytical transfer function for the entire system is challenging. Consequently, to achieve a more accurate prediction of the hydrophone’s sensitivity, Finite Element Method (FEM) simulation was employed. To effectively capture the multiphysics coupling—including sound propagation, fluid dynamics, and transient electrochemical kinetics—while maintaining computational efficiency, a representative two-dimensional (2D) model was adopted in this study.

The investigation presented in Fig. 2b examines the relationship between the horn’s mouth radius and the hydrophone’s performanc. The effect of the membrane radius is intrinsically in the curve because the membrane is fixed at the horn mouth. As the membrane radius decreases, the frequency corresponding to the peak sensitivity shifts to higher frequencies, and the amplitude decreases. This occurs because a smaller membrane radius reduces the displacement generated by the same acoustic energy at low frequencies and diminishes the signal-receiving area of the horn, thereby weakening the amplification effect.

Figure 2c examines the relationship between horn length and performance. A decrease in horn length reduces sensitivity without significantly altering the shape of the amplitude-frequency response. Figure 2d explores the effect of membrane thickness. Increasing the membrane thickness shifts the cutoff frequency to higher frequencies and results in more severe attenuation in the low-frequency band. Figure 2e investigates the influence of the membrane’s Young’s modulus. Increasing the Young’s modulus yields an effect on sensitivity similar to that of increasing membrane thickness. Figure 2f studies the impact of electrolyte concentration, where variations in KI ion concentration change the acoustic impedance of the electrolyte. The simulation results indicate that below 1 kHz, alterations in the characteristic impedance of the electrolyte exert a negligible influence on the hydrophone characteristics. The effect of flow hole aperture size on performance is demonstrated in Fig. 2g. As the aperture size increases, the peak sensitivity rises, but the attenuation at both high and low frequencies becomes more pronounced.

In summary, increases in membrane thickness and Young’s modulus, along with a decrease in membrane radius, exacerbate the low-frequency attenuation of the hydrophone performance. Conversely, increasing the horn mouth radius and length improves overall sensitivity. The flow holes primarily influence the flatness of the amplitude-frequency response. Reducing the aperture size decreases peak sensitivity but results in a flatter frequency band. Therefore, the optimal design should maximize the device size, minimize membrane thickness, and select a moderate aperture size. Considering practical design and application constraints, optimal performance is achieved with the following parameter set: a membrane radius of 25 mm, a horn height of 25 mm, a membrane thickness of 1.5 mm (the minimum achievable in practice), and an aperture size of 60 μm.

Fabrication and assembly

The simulation model revealed that the membrane, the horn, and the electrode chip are the critical structures influencing the performance of the transmissive electrochemical hydrophone. As the core unit for the electrochemical reaction, the electrode chip is pivotal in determining the efficiency of the velocity-to-current conversion. The symmetry of the two electrode pairs, their spatial distribution, and the morphology of the flow holes are key factors affecting the electrochemical reaction. Consequently, the sensing unit employs a single chip integrated with four electrodes30–33. Regarding the flow holes, an excessively large aperture results in accelerated sensitivity decay, while an excessively small aperture diminishes peak sensitivity. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that the depth to which electrodes can be sputtered onto the sidewalls is directly proportional to the aperture size, which significantly affects the effective cathode area. Based on these comprehensive considerations, a flow hole diameter of 60 µm was selected.

The fabrication process of the sensitive electrodes is illustrated in Fig. 3a. The main steps are as follows: First, a hole mask is patterned on the front surface of a 525 µm-thick silicon wafer via photolithography. Second, due to the aspect ratio limitations of the etching process, the holes are etched to half the wafer’s depth using Deep Reactive Ion Etching (DRIE). Third, alignment lithography is performed on the back side of the wafer to form a corresponding hole mask. Fourth, DRIE is applied from the back side to complete the through-holes. Fifth, a 1 µm-thick silicon oxide layer is grown on the wafer surface using Low-Pressure Chemical Vapor Deposition (LPCVD). Sixth, photolithography is applied to the front side to pattern the electrode mask. Seventh, the first pair of electrodes is sputtered onto the front side, followed by photolithography on the back side. The single chip contains two pairs of cathodes and anodes arranged in an ACCA configuration, with flow holes distributed in a 5 mm diameter circular region at the center of the chip. The use of alignment lithography for electrode sputtering significantly improves the geometric uniformity of the two electrode pairs. Figure 3c illustrates the microscopic structure of the flow holes, showing multiple flow holes within a larger macro-hole structure. The electrodes on the sidewalls and at the entrances of the flow holes act as the cathode, while the region between the large holes serves as the anode. As shown in the Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) image in Fig. 3d, the sidewalls of the flow holes are observed. The white line in the middle of the holes indicates the interface between the two etching steps, caused by a slight misalignment during the process. Nevertheless, the sidewalls of the flow holes remain relatively straight, facilitating stable fluid flow.

Figure 3e presents the actual structure and assembly of the hydrophone. Simulations indicated that a lower Young’s modulus and thinner membrane result in less low-frequency attenuation. However, considering the static water pressure at operational depths and environmental durability, a customized polyurethane material with a Young’s modulus of 60 MPa and a characteristic acoustic impedance close to that of water was selected for the membrane. In the horn structure design, although increasing the mouth radius and overall length can enhance sensitivity, the maximum dimensions are constrained by the acoustic wavelength. Stainless steel was selected as the cavity material to ensure that acoustic energy is effectively transmitted internally without outward radiation. The interior features a horn-shaped channel, the surface of which is coated with a 20 µm-thick Parylene layer via vacuum vapor deposition to provide electrical insulation and corrosion resistance. A prototype of the hydrophone is shown in Fig. 3f. The hydrophone has a diameter of 70 mm and a height of 44 mm. The size is acoustically compact for low-frequency applications. Results

The performance of vector hydrophones operating in the low- to mid-frequency range is typically characterized using a standing wave tube. Figure 4a illustrates the operating principle of this system. The excitation signal generated by a signal generator is amplified by a power amplifier and then delivered to a sound source (oscillator) located at the bottom of the tube. Plane waves generated by the source are reflected from the water surface and superimpose with the incident waves to form standing waves. During testing, both a standard reference hydrophone (RHS(A)-30, Hangzhou Institute of Applied Acoustics, China) and the electrochemical hydrophone are positioned at the same depth. The current signal from the electrochemical hydrophone is converted into a voltage signal by its conditioning circuit. This voltage signal, along with the output from the standard hydrophone, is transmitted to a computer via a data acquisition card for analysis. Discussion

The experimental results corroborate the simulation findings, indicating that the membrane parameters are critical determinants of performance at low frequencies. Specifically, acoustic wave attenuation is shown to be directly correlated with increased membrane stiffness and thickness, as well as reduced membrane radius. Conversely, the horn structure effectively amplifies sensitivity; this amplification effect is enhanced by increasing both the mouth-to-throat ratio and the horn length. Although variations in KI concentration slightly alter the acoustic impedance, their opposing effects on flow velocity result in a negligible net impact on sensitivity at low frequencies.

Conclusion

This work presents the design and realization of an acoustically transmissive electrochemical vector hydrophone incorporating an acoustic velocity horn structure. A core innovation of this device lies in its operational principle, which relies on the direct transmission of acoustic waves through a membrane-coupled horn, rather than the co-oscillating mechanism used in conventional designs. This fundamental distinction effectively circumvents the low-frequency mechanical limitations inherent in suspended-structure vector hydrophones. Furthermore, the integration of the horn structure generates significant particle velocity gain, substantially amplifying the device’s sensitivity.

A comprehensive theoretical model was established to elucidate the complete acoustic-electric conversion process, validated by numerical simulations. These investigations systematically decoupled the specific influences of the horn structure, membrane properties, electrolyte concentration, and electrode pore size. It was demonstrated that the membrane’s mechanical properties govern the low-frequency attenuation, while the horn structure significantly amplifies overall sensitivity through geometric gain. Furthermore, the analysis revealed that the electrode pore size modulates the trade-off between peak sensitivity and response flatness, whereas variations in electrolyte concentration exert a negligible net effect on low-frequency performance. Guided by these comprehensive optimization insights, a highly symmetric four-electrode chip was successfully fabricated using MEMS technology and assembled with the designed optimized package to form a hydrophone prototype.

The optimized hydrophone demonstrates exceptional performance in the low-frequency regime. It achieves a peak sensitivity of -180 dB re 1 V/μPa at 10 Hz, with a flat -3 dB bandwidth extending from 10 Hz to 300 Hz following circuit compensation. The device exhibits excellent signal fidelity, characterized by high linearity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}^{2}=0.996$$\end{document}R2=0.996), a low noise floor, and a broadband dynamic range of 66 dB. Moreover, the classic figure-eight directivity pattern with a null depth exceeding 40 dB unequivocally validates its capability for precise vector measurement.

Compared with existing hydrophones of similar principles, the proposed device demonstrates a distinct advantage in low-frequency sensitivity while retaining the compact form factor and high signal-to-noise ratio characteristic of electrochemical sensors. These attributes make it particularly suitable for long-distance, low-frequency underwater detection tasks, offering a promising solution for next-generation vector sensor arrays.
