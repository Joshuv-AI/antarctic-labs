Translating Ecological
Realism into Ecotoxicology:
An Experimental Approach for Assessing Plant Biodiversity

Rosa Kim, Kwang Hyun Hwang, Hi Gyu Moon, Shin Woong Kim

Abstract

Traditional ecotoxicological assessments predominantly
rely on
single-species bioassays, often failing to address the complex interspecific
interactions and structural resilience inherent in natural ecosystems.
In this study, we developed a community-level ecotoxicological approach
using soil seed banks as a representative ecological proxy. By reconstructing
diverse plant assemblages directly from field-sampled soil (26 species,
455 individual plants), we evaluated the impacts of a model chemical
(cadmium, Cd) on multidimensional end points, including plant diversity,
biomass productivity, and co-occurrence networks. Our findings revealed
a discrepancy between individual and community-level sensitivities:
the effective concentration 10% (EC10) for the Shannon–Wiener
index was 76.36 mg kg–1, which is approximately
20-fold higher than the hazardous concentration 5% derived from conventional
species sensitivity distribution (SSD) models. Although these metrics
differ in their conceptual basis, this gap offers critical insights
into the conservative margin of regulatory standards. While total
community biomass significantly declined at high Cd concentrations,
dominant species exhibited robust tolerance, suggesting a selective
filtration of sensitive species rather than a uniform inhibition across
all constituents. Furthermore, co-occurrence network analysis demonstrated
that Cd stress reduced structural complexity while simultaneously
increasing interspecific co-occurrence strength among survivors. These
results demonstrate that plant communities possess a significantly
higher buffering capacity against chemical stress. Our approach provides
an ecologically realistic platform for the next generation of ecological
risk assessments, offering deeper and more direct insights into anthropogenic
impacts on terrestrial biodiversity and ecosystem stability.

Introduction

Ecotoxicology aims to
protect biodiversity and ecosystem services
against the structural and functional damages induced by anthropogenic
chemical stress. As a cornerstone of ecological
risk assessment, the species sensitivity distribution (SSD) model
provides a statistically rigorous framework for quantifying toxicity
variation across multiple trophic levels, thereby facilitating the
derivation of protective thresholds.
,
 However, this
framework is often constrained by its reliance on single-species laboratory
bioassays,
,
 which fundamentally overlook the interspecific
interactions inherent in natural ecosystems. While scaling ecotoxicological perspectives from individual to community
levels enhances ecological realism, the difficulty of data acquisition
commensurately increases due to higher complexity and lower reproducibility. Although mesocosm and microcosm studies offer
valuable insights into ecosystem-level dynamics, these designs are often limited by high costs and logistical
complexity. Furthermore, field-scale experiments
frequently struggle to isolate specific environmental drivers from
observed effects.
,
 Consequently, ecotoxicological
impacts at the community level remain relatively understudied.

This limitation is particularly pronounced in plant ecotoxicology.
Standardized plant toxicity tests, such as ISO or OECD protocols,
typically utilize a restricted set of model species (e.g., Lactuca sativa or Avena sativa).
,
 While these assays are efficient for chemical
hazard screening, they fail to capture critical community-level dynamics. In natural plant communities, species engage
in a myriad of positive and negative interactions, occurring directly
through facilitation and competition, or indirectly via alterations
in abiotic and biotic conditions. However,
characterizing these comprehensive interactions remains a methodological
challenge due to the vast number of potential pairwise combinations
across various time scales. Rather than
addressing the plant community dynamics directly, recent research
has frequently focused on soil microbial communities associated with
specific plant types, such as metal-tolerant species.
,
 Although efforts to predict plant biodiversity changes have employed
ecological modeling with field monitoring (e.g., landscape ecotoxicology),
,
 these predictive tools often rely on static snapshots of standing
vegetation. While field-based observations offer high ecological realism,
they frequently struggle to define clear dose–response relationships
or to establish reliable ecological baselines (e.g., reference site).
Despite the evolution of ecotoxicological methodologies, the gap between
highly controlled laboratory bioassays and the multidimensional reality
of field environments persists.
,

This study presents
an experimental approach for assessing plant
community responses to chemical stress under laboratory conditions.
We employed the concept of the soil seed bank, defined as the natural
reservoir of dormant seeds within the soil profile.
,
 The seed bank represents the regenerative potential and historical
genetic diversity of a site. By utilizing
the soil seed bank as a target plant community, we designed the experiment
to incorporate the natural species pool and soil matrix into a controlled
laboratory setting. Field soil was collected in late autumn from a
local grassland to capture peak seed density and high germination
potential, providing a representative matrix for assessing community
assembly. The soil was subsequently exposed
to cadmium (Cd), selected as a model contaminant due to its environmental
persistence, high bioavailability, and well-documented toxicity. The
activation of dormant seed banks under optimized, spring-like conditions
(controlled thermal and moisture regimes) led to the emergence of
26 species (455 individuals). This approach facilitated the comprehensive
quantification of community-level diversity and species composition.
We hypothesize that (1) our method for seed bank activation yields
an ecologically relevant and laboratory-scale plant community, (2)
such field-derived assemblages exhibit sufficient sensitivity to chemical
stress, and (3) community-level metrics offer more integrated ecological
insights compared to traditional single-species assays.

Materials and Methods

Soil Collection and Treatment

Bulk
soil was collected in November 2025 from a natural grassland at Korea
Institute of Toxicology (Jinju, South Korea). The sampling site is
characterized by a diverse temperate plant community, dominated by
perennial grasses and various forbs that contribute to a robust and
heterogeneous soil seed bank. To capture the maximum potential of
the regenerative species pool, a 3 × 3 m quadrat was randomly
established, and pretreated by removing aboveground vegetation in
preparation for the winter dormancy period. The soil surface was then
carefully leveled to expose the uppermost mineral horizon, and approximately
50 kg of soil was sampled from the topsoil (0–10 cm depth).
The soil was air-dried at low temperatures (5–10 °C) under
dark conditions and subsequently passed through a 4 mm sieve. To ensure
rigorous homogenization, 20 kg of the sieved soil was partitioned
into ten 2 kg subunits, each of which was mixed manually for 5 min.
These subunits were further divided into 200 g aliquots and consolidated
into a single bulk container using a stepwise layering and homogenization
process to achieve uniform spatial distribution. For the experimental
units, 180 g of the homogenized soil (water holding capacity: 70%
w/w; pH: 6.6) was placed into individual plastic pots (75 × 75
× 100 mm) and stored at room temperature in the dark.

To
prepare the loading soil for Cd treatment, a subset of the soil was
sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min. This sterilization step was
conducted to mitigate localized microbial shocks caused by concentrated
chemical exposure. Twenty g of this sterilized
loading soil was placed in a plastic cup, to which 10 mL of CdCl2 (Sigma-Aldrich) solution was added to reach test concentrations.
After the loading soils were air-dried, they were thoroughly homogenized
with 180 g of the base soil in each pot. Finally, distilled water
was added to adjust the soil moisture content to 60% of the water
holding capacity. Soil pH and electrical conductivity (1:5 w/v) were
measured across all concentrations using a multiparameter meter (Multi
9630 IDS, WTW, Weilheim, Germany). Total and bioavailable Cd concentrations
were quantified using inductively coupled plasma-optical emission
spectrometry (ICP-OES; 5800, Agilent, USA). Total concentrations were
quantified after microwave-assisted acid digestion (HNO3/H2O2), whereas bioavailable Cd was extracted
using 0.01 M CaCl2 solution (1:10 w/v, shaken for 2 h). Across all Cd treatment levels, soil pH remained
stable between 6.50 and 6.62, and EC values remained consistently
low, ranging from 0.08 to 0.16 dS m–1. The total
nominal (measured) Cd concentrations were 0 (not detected, n = 18), 1 (2.37), 5 (7.84), 10 (24.17), 50 (76.07), and
100 (140.65) (n = 10 per treatment) mg Cd kg–1 dry soil, and 0.01 M CaCl2-extractable
(bioavailable) Cd concentrations ranged from 0.004 to 0.706 mg L–1 (Table S1).

Plant Community Toxicity Tests

This
study employs an experimental approach to investigate the toxicological
effects on the plant community, encompassing diversity, species composition,
and network structures (). To achieve this, each pot was incubated in a growth room
(22 °C, 60% relative humidity, 16 h photoperiod), with soil moisture
maintained at 60% of the water-holding capacity throughout the experimental
period. These conditions simulated a spring-like environment to facilitate
the germination and activation of the indigenous soil seed bank. After
an incubation period of 49 days, all 455 individual plants were manually
harvested. The fresh biomass of shoots and roots was measured for
each individual, after which every specimen was identified to the
species level and enumerated to assess community-level responses across
all treatments.

Identification of Plant Species

Following
the harvest, we initially performed a visual sorting of the 455 individual
plants into 30 putative morphospecies. To ensure taxonomic accuracy,
we subsequently conducted DNA barcoding, which finalized the identification
of 26 distinct species (Table S2). All
voucher specimens have been deposited at the Korea Institute of Toxicology
(Jinju, South Korea) for future reference. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was
extracted from 10–80 mg of plant tissue using the HiGene Genomic
DNA Prep Kit for Plant Tissue (BioFACT, South Korea). Samples were
ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen and lysed in a lysis
buffer containing Proteinase K (20 mg mL–1) and
RNase A (4 mg mL–1) at 65 °C for 10 min. Following
centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was mixed
with binding buffer, and the resulting mixture was purified via spin-column
chromatography followed by washing with 80% ethanol and washing buffer.
The gDNA was eluted in 50 μL of DNA hydration solution and stored
at −20 °C.

For species identification, the internal
transcribed spacer (ITS) and rbcL regions were amplified using universal primers.
,
 PCR was performed in a 25 μL reaction volume containing 2
μL of template gDNA, 12.5 μL of BioFACT Lamp Taq Master
mixture (DNA polymerase, BioFACT, South Korea), 1 μL each of
forward and reverse primers (10 μM), and distilled water up
to the final volume. The amplification was carried out using the following
thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for
2 min; 32 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, annealing
at 56 °C (ITS) or 53 °C (rbcL) for 40 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min; final extension
at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were purified using the HiGene
Gel & PCR Purification System (BioFACT, South Korea) and visualized
via agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm quality and concentration.
The amplicon library was sequenced using the ABI BigDye Terminator
v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The acquired
nucleotide sequences were processed through the ABI 3730XL DNA Analyzer
(Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) to generate consensus sequences. We
subjected the resulting sequences to BLASTn searches of the NCBI GenBank
database and assigned taxonomies based on a minimum identity of 99%
(Table S2). The morphological traits were
cross-referenced.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical
analyses and visualizations were performed in R (version 4.5.1). Normality
and homogeneity of univariate parameters (including number of plants,
richness, diversity indices, and biomass data) were verified using
the Shapiro–Wilk and Bartlett’s tests, respectively.
,
 To identify significant differences between the control and each
treatment group, Dunnett’s posthoc test was employed using
the multcomp package. When the assumptions for parametric analysis (Shapiro–Wilk
and Bartlett’s tests) were not met (p <
0.05), the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, followed
by Dunn’s posthoc test with Holm’s adjustment for comparisons
against the control group (rstatix package, Table S3). Statistical significance was defined
at p < 0.05, with levels of significance denoted
as follows: * (p < 0.05), ** (p < 0.01), and *** (p < 0.001). All plots were
generated using the ggplot2 package.

To evaluate shifts in species composition across
treatments, community data (shoot biomass per species) were square-root
transformed (Hellinger transformation) to reduce the influence of
highly dominant species. Nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS)
based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was performed using the vegan package to visualize differences in community structure.
Significant differences in community composition among treatment groups
were assessed using Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance
(PERMANOVA) with 9999 permutations via the adonis2 function, and pseudo-F statistic, associated degrees of freedom,
the coefficients of determination (R2), and p value were determined.

The relationships between alpha-diversity
indices (Shannon–Wiener
index, Simpson index) and total shoot biomass were evaluated using
Pearson correlation and linear regression analyses. Analysis of Covariance
(ANCOVA) was subsequently performed to compare regression slopes across
treatment groups, ensuring the assumption of homogeneity of regression
slopes was met. Regression models were fitted for both the overall
community and specific target species within individual treatment
groups to determine the coefficients of determination (R
2) and p value. All linear modeling and
covariate analyses were conducted using the stats package.

To characterize the interspecific co-occurrence and
community stability,
network analysis was performed across each treatment. The network
was constructed and visualized using a circular layout via the tidygraph and ggraph packages. Interspecific interaction strengths were quantified
using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (r
s) calculated from the shoot biomass of each species.
Co-occurrence networks were constructed using a dual-filtering strategy.
Edges were retained only if they satisfied both a minimum correlation
magnitude threshold (|r
s| ≥ 0.3)
and statistical significance (p < 0.1).
,
 This approach preserves fundamental system topology while balancing
statistical power under small sample sizes inherent to pot experiments.
,
 Additionally, self-correlations, loops, and multiple edges were
systematically removed. The topological properties were characterized
by calculating edge density, mean edge weight (interspecific association
strength), average strength (overall node connectedness), and modularity
using the igraph package. Furthermore, to control for potential network density biases induced
by variations in the nodes across treatments, the minimum observed
number of nodes was selected as the baseline target node size. For
each treatment group, 12 species were randomly subsampled without
replacement from the species pool, and a subnetwork was reconstructed
to compute its network density. This subsampling procedure was repeated
iteratively (1000 permutations) using the purrr and dplyr packages to generate a standardized empirical density
distribution for each treatment.

Results and Discussion

Effects on Plant Community Diversity and Structure

The number of individuals per pot significantly decreased (A) at 140.65 mg kg–1 of Cd concentrations. Concurrently, species richness
(number of species) and the Shannon–Wiener index significantly
declined at the highest concentration, whereas the Simpson index increased
at this concentration (B–D). The plant community was dominated by eight species: Setaria viridis (22%), Oxalis stricta (22%), Erigeron philadelphicus (13%), Artemisia stolonifera (11%), Veronica
persica (5%), Vicia hirsuta (4%), Ipomoea hederacea (3%), Corchoropsis tomentosa (2%), with 18 additional species
comprising the remaining 18% (E; Table S4 and S5). Native
and invasive species accounted for 67% and 32% of the total, respectively
(F). Despite
the shifts in alpha-diversity, NMDS ordination indicated that overall
community composition remained relatively resilient across the Cd
treatments (F
1,66 = 1.577, R
2 = 0.023, p = 0.119) (G).

Cd is a nonessential element for plants, and its
toxicity is well-known
to induce growth abnormalities and inhibition in many agricultural
species, such as Cicer arietinum, Lens culinaris, Triticum aestivum, and Zea mays.
−

 However, its
impacts on community structure and diversity have seldom been prioritized
as toxicological end points. Field surveys have revealed that metal-contaminated
sites exhibit reduced plant species richness and diversity compared
to uncontaminated areas, often selecting for metal-tolerant taxa.
−

 While these studies suggest that plant community diversity is primarily
driven by soil metal contents or that
high plant diversity mitigates soil contamination, it remains challenging to isolate specific environmental
drivers from observed ecological effects. In the present study, we
isolated the effect of a single variable (Cd addition) under controlled
experimental conditions, including a robust reference set (control
group). The effective concentration 10% (EC10) of Cd for
species richness and the Shannon–Wiener index was calculated
as 2.02 and 76.36 mg kg–1, respectively (Table S6), and these values were comparable to
or up to approximately 20-fold higher than the hazardous concentration
5% (HC5, 0.59–3.57 mg kg–1) derived from
conventional SSD models. These metrics
differ in their conceptual basis and should not be directly compared.
HC5 represents a conservative benchmark designed to protect 95% of
various species including soil invertebrates (e.g., earthworms and
springtails), based on conventional single-species toxicity data (EC10 or NOEC value).
,
 Conversely, our community-level
EC10 reflects an empirical threshold incorporating interspecific
interactions among plant species. Nevertheless, the observed gap between
HC5 and community-level EC10 offers critical insights into
the conservative margin of current regulatory standards.

Although
the toxicity thresholds in our study showed wide confidence
interval ratio (ranging from 8 to 112), which typically indicates
low statistical precision, such variation
is often unavoidable in community-level assessments, due to the wide
spectrum of multispecies interactions between highly vulnerable and
tolerant taxa. Regulatory guidance often
recommends retaining only data that meet stringent statistical precision
criteria.
,
 While such conservative filtering minimizes
analytical noise in laboratory bioassays, applying these rigid cutoffs
to community-level assessments may discard ecologically meaningful
data. In community-level assessments, flexible approaches are strongly
recommended to capture heterogeneous sensitivity patterns while maintaining
protective benchmarks in ecological risk assessment. We emphasize that the community-level responses in our
study reflect more realistic toxicological profiles than those provided
by single-species assays.

Biomass Responses of the Plant Community

The growth of the plant community was markedly inhibited by Cd
exposure, and a trend was clearly discernible upon visual examination
(A). Total
shoot biomass (per pot) was significantly suppressed at the highest
Cd concentration (140.65 mg kg–1) (B). Notably, the top three
dominant species exhibited no significant growth reduction across
all treatments. The dominant species S. viridis showed a compensatory increase in shoot biomass at intermediate
Cd concentration, and the shoot and root growth of O. stricta were significantly stimulated at intermediate
levels (B and
C). The relationship between total shoot biomass (per pot) and alpha-diversity
was slightly modulated by Cd concentration (). At the lowest (2.37 mg kg–1) and the highest (140.65 mg kg–1) Cd levels, the
Shannon–Wiener diversity exhibited a significant positive correlation
with total shoot biomass (R
2 = 0.451 and
0.746, respectively; p < 0.05) (A). Conversely, the Simpson
index showed a significant negative correlation with biomass at the
same concentrations (R
2 = 0.466 and 0.658,
respectively; p < 0.05) (B).

Despite a significant reduction in total shoot
biomass (EC50: 117.87 mg kg–1) (Table S6), the dominant species showed no significant
changes
even at the highest Cd concentrations. Given that EC50 for
shoot growth in agricultural species typically ranges from 61 to 268
mg kg–1, the dominant
species in our study (S. viridis, O. stricta, and E. philadelphicus) exhibited robust tolerance to Cd toxicity.
,
 This suggests that the effects on the community level were driven
by the selective loss of Cd-sensitive species rather than a uniform
inhibition across all constituents. The correlation analysis between
biomass and alpha-diversity further demonstrated that community productivity
(biomass) is maximized when diversity (Shannon–Wiener) and
dominance (Simpson) is high. While these correlations weakened under
intermediate levels, they became most pronounced in the highest Cd
concentration. This trend implies that as the community simplified,
the surviving species maintained strong functional interactions. This hypothesis is supported by our observation
that root biomass remained stable despite a significant decline in
species richness. Although Cd stress typically inhibits root growth
by reducing cell wall elasticity, the
surviving species in our system appeared to be resistant to these
stressors, potentially benefiting from competitive release and interspecific
interactions. For instance, O. stricta is well-known as a noncompetitive species
and easily suppressed by dense ground cover. However, it also possesses a remarkable tolerance to metal toxicity, and in our study, it manifested significant
root stimulation at the highest Cd concentration. This result suggests
that for certain tolerant species, the ecological benefits gained
from the competitive release following the loss of vulnerable neighbors
may outweigh the effects of metal stress.

Co-Occurrence Network Analysis

In
the control group, the plant community formed a highly interconnected
network consisting of 21 species with 16 active interactions (edges).
However, under Cd exposure, the number of active nodes (species with
valid interactions) decreased to 12–14, with the number of
edges ranging from 3 to 6 ( and Table S7). To control
for potential bias by variations in node count, a rarefaction analysis
was performed by subsampling 12 nodes (minimum number of nodes) over
1000 iterations. Specifically, the rarefied average strength and modularity
of the control group dropped from 0.880 to 0.479 and from 0.477 to
0.339, respectively (Table S7). These corrections
demonstrate that the network strength and modularity initially observed
in the control data were largely affected by species richness. Conversely,
the mean edge weight remained consistently higher across all treatments
(0.635–0.668) relative to the control network (0.580). This
persistent elevation indicates that while toxic stress reduced overall
species richness (node loss), the interspecific co-occurrence strength
among surviving species became intensified. These metrics collectively
indicate a transition toward a more condensed and tightly coupled
network architecture under chemical stress.

Our results reveal that Cd stress acts as a stringent
environmental
filter, forcing the plant community into a more simplified network
(node loss) alongside strong interspecific interactions (higher mean
edge weight). These trends indicate that environmental stress clusters
surviving species into a synchronized response profile. Under severe toxicity, the specialized interactions
that define distinct modules are superseded by a universal stress
factor, leading to a structurally homogenized network where niche
differentiation is eroded. Overall, our
findings suggest that environmental stress erodes the structural complexity
of plant communities, driving resilient species into patterns of elevated
network strength.

Potentials and Limitations

The primary
objective of this study was to validate an experimental approach using
soil seed banks as a robust ecotoxicological tool for assessing community-level
responses. Our findings demonstrate the efficacy of this methodology
through three advancements: (1) we successfully transitioned from
single-species bioassays to a rapid and holistic assessment of field-derived
plant communities; (2) we established the utility of community-level
parameters as informative toxicological end points; and (3) we ensured
high ecological realism while maintaining laboratory-scale reproducibility
and repeatability, effectively bridging the gap between controlled
laboratory conditions and complex field dynamics. Despite these advancements,
several limitations remain to be addressed in subsequent research:
(1) while Cd served as an effective model contaminant, a broader spectrum
of chemical stressors (e.g., organic pollutants, microplastics, or
pesticides) is necessary; (2) although structural changes in the community
were evident, biochemical and molecular profiling (e.g., metabolomics)
would further elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying interspecific
toxicological responses; and (3) future models should incorporate
perennial species and other trophic levels, such as microorganisms
or soil fauna, to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of ecosystem
functions.

Ecosystems are not merely an assembly of individual
species; they maintain stability against external stressors through
intricate interspecific networks. The biodiversity and reciprocal
networks found in nature serve as a robust survival strategy and a
cornerstone for evolutionary adaptation. In this study, we addressed
a major hurdle in ecotoxicology by successfully bridging the gap between
field-scale ecological realism and controlled laboratory experimentation.
Our approach provides direct evidence of toxicological impacts at
the community level. We believe the fundamental utility and reliability
of this seed bank-mediated approach was firmly validated. To overcome
the limitations of this study, future research should incorporate
diverse substances and broader field sampling to enhance reproducibility
and applicability. We anticipate that the continued refinement of
this method will catalyze the development of next-generation ecological
risk assessments, facilitating a deeper understanding of anthropogenic
impacts on terrestrial ecosystems.
