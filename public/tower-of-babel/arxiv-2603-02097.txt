ClinConsensus: A Consensus-Based Benchmark for Evaluating Chinese Medical LLMs across Difficulty Levels
Xiang Zheng, Han Li, Wenjie Luo, Weiqi Zhai, Yiyuan Li, Chuanmiao Yan, Tianyi Tang, Yubo Ma, Kexin Yang, Dayiheng Liu, Hu Wei, Bing Zhao

Abstract
. Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly applied to health management, showing promise across disease prevention, clinical decision-making, and long-term care. However, existing medical benchmarks remain largely static and task-isolated, failing to capture the openness, longitudinal structure, and safety-critical complexity of real-world clinical workflows. We introduce ClinConsensus , a Chinese medical benchmark curated, validated, and quality-controlled by clinical experts. ClinConsensus comprises 2,500 open-ended cases spanning the full continuum of care—from prevention and intervention to long-term follow-up—covering 36 medical specialties, 12 common clinical task types, and progressively increasing levels of complexity. To enable reliable evaluation of such complex scenarios, we adopt a rubric-based grading protocol and propose the Clinically Applicable Consistency Score (CACS@ k k ), a threshold-calibrated metric that emphasizes consistent generation of clinically usable responses rather than average correctness. We further introduce a dual-judge evaluation framework, combining a high-capability LLM-as-judge with a distilled, locally deployable judge model trained via supervised fine-tuning, enabling scalable and reproducible evaluation aligned with physician judgment. Using ClinConsensus, we conduct a comprehensive assessment of 15 leading LLMs and reveal substantial heterogeneity across task themes, care stages, and medical specialties. While top-performing models achieve comparable overall scores, they differ markedly in reasoning, evidence use, and longitudinal follow-up capabilities, and clinically actionable treatment planning remains a key bottleneck. These findings highlight that strong language modeling performance does not directly translate to safe and usable clinical behavior. We release ClinConsensus as an extensible benchmark to support the development and evaluation of medical LLMs that are robust, clinically grounded, and ready for real-world deployment.

Related Work

Large language models have been increasingly applied to healthcare tasks such as patient education, diagnostic support, clinical documentation, and chronic disease management. However, recent reviews consistently show that strong performance on knowledge-centric tasks does not necessarily translate into clinical reliability, especially in the presence of hallucination, bias, safety risks, and failures under complex contextual reasoning
(
4
;
6
;
32
;
19

Most early evaluations of medical LLMs rely on static and decontextualized benchmarks, such as MedQA, MedMCQA, and MMLU-style examinations
(
14
;
20
;
12
)
. While these benchmarks are useful for measuring factual recall and broad medical knowledge, they only weakly approximate real clinical practice. Prior work has highlighted a clear knowledge–practice gap: models that perform well on test-style questions often degrade on more realistic diagnosis and management tasks, particularly when success depends on context sensitivity, sequential reasoning, or guideline-consistent decision making
(
10
)
.

Recent benchmarks move toward more realistic and structured evaluation. HealthBench, LLMEval-Med, CSEDB, and MedThink-Bench incorporate open-ended responses, rubric- or checklist-based scoring, physician validation, and finer-grained reasoning assessment
(
2
;
33
;
29
;
34
)
. In parallel, interactive and agentic benchmarks such as AIE/SAPS, AgentClinic, ClinicalLab, and MATRIX evaluate models in multi-turn or simulated clinical environments, revealing additional weaknesses in context tracking, information gathering, and sequential decision-making that are often hidden in static QA settings
(
15
;
22
;
31
;
16
)
. Chinese medical benchmarks, including MedBench, LLMEval-Med, MLB, and MedBench v4, further improve localization by incorporating Chinese clinical resources, local guidelines, and broader specialty coverage
(
13
;
33
;
11
;
7
)
. Nevertheless, existing Chinese benchmarks still mainly emphasize isolated questions, short-horizon encounters, or broad coverage, with limited attention to realistic case settings spanning prevention, treatment, and long-term management.

ClinConsensus is designed to address these limitations. It provides a Chinese clinical benchmark built from expert-curated real-world cases, organized along the continuum of prevention, treatment, and long-term management, and stratified by difficulty. By combining diverse case forms with structured case-specific rubrics, ClinConsensus enables more systematic evaluation of clinical applicability, safety, and consistency in localized real-world care contexts.

Approach

This section outlines the methodologies utilized for curating the ClinConsensus datasets, as well as the approaches used for fine-tuning and evaluating LLMs using ClinConsensus. Figure

3.1.
Data Collection and Processing

We invited a multidisciplinary team of clinical experts in China, leveraging their accumulated professional expertise and daily practice, to manually construct the initial version of the Clinconsensus dataset. During case development, experts could either create original clinical scenarios based on their medical knowledge or transform de-identified real-world cases into natural language narratives, ensuring the authenticity and credibility of contexts while supporting open-ended and complex interactions. The dataset exhibits high textual diversity, encompassing both concise, everyday conversational exchanges and complex, lengthy clinical narratives. The generated cases cover the entire continuum of health management—from prevention and treatment to long-term care—spanning 36 major clinical specialties and 12 categories of common medical tasks (see Figure
2
).

3.2.
Quality Control Process

Case creation and annotation were jointly completed by at least two licensed physicians.
To ensure both sufficient challenge and clinical validity, we adopt a two-stage quality control pipeline.

Stage 1: Difficulty-based filtering.

In the first stage, experts consulted responses generated by three leading LLMs
(DeepSeek-v3, GPT-5, and Gemini-2.5 Pro) and manually graded each case against the
30 evaluation criteria.
Cases for which the aggregated rubric score reached or exceeded 50% were
discarded.
This step removes overly easy cases and ensures that the benchmark focuses on
clinically non-trivial scenarios that cannot be solved by superficial reasoning.

Stage 2: Expert auditing and consistency review.

In the second stage, senior clinicians conducted random audits on approximately
20% of the remaining cases.
They examined case descriptions, rubric definitions, and reference answers for
clinical correctness, necessity, internal consistency, and compliance with both
Chinese and international clinical guidelines.
Cases failing this review were revised or removed.
This stage ensures medical soundness, rubric validity, and evaluation reliability
prior to final inclusion in the dataset.

4.
Judge Framework and Metrics

ClinConsensus adopts a
rubric-based
evaluation protocol in which each model response is assessed against a fixed set of
N{=}30
binary clinical criteria.
For every case
i
and rubric item
r_{j}
, a judge outputs a structured decision

\texttt{criteria\_met}\in\{\texttt{true},\texttt{false}\}
together with a brief evidence-based explanation.
This criterion-level supervision enables fine-grained and clinically interpretable evaluation while allowing scalable benchmarking across large numbers of models and cases.

To balance grading fidelity and scalability, we provide two compatible judging implementations that share the same input–output contract:

•

LLM-as-Judge
: a high-capability proprietary model (e.g., GPT-4.1) that evaluates each rubric criterion independently.

•

Trained Judge
: a lightweight open model distilled to approximate high-quality rubric decisions at substantially lower cost.

Unless otherwise specified, all benchmark results reported in this work are computed using the LLM-as-judge grader.

4.1.
LLM-as-Judge

Given the full conversation context, a candidate model response, and a single rubric criterion
r_{j}
,
we prompt a high-capability LLM to serve as a rubric grader and produce a structured JSON decision
containing the fields
explanation
and
criteria_met
.
This grading procedure is applied independently to each rubric item

j=1,\ldots,N
, producing a set of criterion-level judgments
(
rubric_judgments
) for every response.

Schema-constrained prompt.

We employ a fixed instruction template that provides the conversation context,
the candidate response, and a single rubric item, and instructs the grader to
evaluate only the last assistant turn and return a JSON object containing

explanation
and
criteria_met
.
The complete prompt template is provided in Appendix
C
.

4.2.
Trained Judge

Training data construction.

We construct rubric-level supervision from two sources derived from HealthBench.
First, we use the
oss_meta_eval
split, which contains dual-physician annotations.
Only instances where both physicians agree on the rubric decision are retained as ground-truth labels.
Second, we use a curated
hard
subset in which model-generated responses are graded by an LLM-as-judge to increase coverage of challenging cases.
For training, we use only the binary rubric decision (
criteria_met
) and do not utilize scalar rubric scores.

After filtering and aggregation, the final training set contains approximately 33.7k rubric-level instances, with a held-out validation set of 3.8k instances.

Learning objective.

We formulate rubric grading as a schema-constrained conditional generation task.
Given serialized input

x=\text{Serialize}(\text{context},\text{response},r_{j})
,
the model generates a structured JSON output containing

explanation
and
criteria_met
.
We fine-tune a decoder-only transformer (8B scale) via supervised learning:

(1)

\max_{\theta}\sum_{(x,y)}\log p_{\theta}(y\mid x),

where the cross-entropy loss is computed only on output tokens.
During inference, generation is constrained to the predefined JSON schema to ensure well-formed and deterministic outputs.

4.3.
Clinically Applicable Consistency Score (CACS@
k
)

4.3.1.
Definition

In high-stakes domains such as medicine, average rubric accuracy can be misleading: responses with moderate rubric coverage may still be clinically unusable, whereas improvements above a usability threshold can yield substantially greater practical value. We therefore introduce
CACS@k
, a metric that (i) enforces a clinically meaningful usability threshold and (ii) concentrates reward in the high-score region. An illustrative motivating example is shown in Figure
5
and discussed in Appendix
A
.

Rubric-hit score.

For each case
i
, the rubric grader produces binary decisions
y_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
for each of the
N
rubric criteria. The rubric-hit score is defined as

s_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_{i,j}\in\{0,1,\dots,N\},

where
N=30
in our benchmark.

.

Truncated reverse-ECDF aggregation.

Let
\widehat{P}(s\geq t)
denote the empirical survival function (i.e., the complement of the empirical cumulative distribution function) of rubric-hit scores over a set of evaluated responses
\mathcal{D}
, where
t\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}
.

We define
CACS@
k
as the truncated area under the reverse-ECDF curve from a clinical threshold
k
to the maximum score
N
:

(2)

\mathrm{CACS@}k=\frac{100}{N-k+1}\sum_{t=k}^{N}\widehat{P}(s\geq t).

By summing only over
t\geq k
, CACS@
k
assigns no additional credit to responses that do not reach
k
rubric hits, thereby emphasizing consistency in producing clinically usable outputs. The threshold
k
is not interpreted as a passing score out of
N
, but rather as a truncation point within a cumulative survival aggregation; performance above
k
continues to receive graded credit. Equivalently, CACS@
k
can be viewed as a normalized expected excess of rubric hits above
k{-}1
, increasing with both (i) the proportion of responses meeting the threshold and (ii) how far above the threshold those responses score.

Efficient equivalent form.

Equation (
2
) admits an equivalent sample-wise formulation:

(3)

\mathrm{CACS@}k=\frac{100}{|\mathcal{D}|(N-k+1)}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}}\max(0,\,s_{i}-k+1).

In practice, we compute CACS@
k
using Eq. (
3
), which enables efficient sample-wise aggregation without explicitly constructing the survival curve.

4.3.2.
Calibrating the clinical threshold
k

Rationale.

The threshold
k
operationalizes the minimum rubric coverage required for a response to be considered
clinically usable
under our rubric design. Rather than selecting
k
heuristically, we calibrate it empirically using expert-written reference responses.

Expert calibration protocol.

We randomly sample 250 cases from the full benchmark of 2,500 cases.
Each sampled case contains
N=30
rubric criteria designed to cover clinically relevant considerations.
The rubric criteria were verified by clinical experts to be clinically sound and nonredundant.

We invite attending physicians from top-tier (Class A tertiary) hospitals to author responses following standard clinical writing conventions.
To ensure consistency with the primary evaluation pipeline, these expert responses are graded using the same rubric grader employed in our experiments (GPT-5.1-based), producing rubric-level binary decisions and corresponding rubric-hit scores
s_{i}
.

Setting
k
from expert performance.

Let
\{s_{i}\}
denote the rubric-hit scores of expert-written responses on the 250-case calibration set.
We set the clinical threshold
k
to the empirical mean rubric-hit score of expert responses, yielding
k=7
.
We adopt this value throughout the paper and report
CACS@7
unless otherwise specified.

Justification for the
k=7
threshold.

Although 7 out of 30 may appear modest, this value reflects the empirically observed average number of explicitly articulated high-priority clinical considerations in expert-authored responses under natural, non-exhaustive documentation practices.
In real-world settings, attending physicians typically prioritize the most critical and actionable items rather than exhaustively enumerating all cross-specialty considerations encoded in the rubric.
Thus,
k=7
does not represent a low passing bar nor an upper bound on expert performance, but instead captures the critical mass of salient clinical information that experts consistently articulate in practice.
CACS@7 therefore measures consistency above this usability-aligned truncation point rather than completeness relative to the full rubric space.
Equivalently, CACS@7 can be interpreted as a normalized expected excess of rubric hits above the threshold
k{-}1
, rewarding both the proportion of responses that exceed the usability threshold and the extent to which they surpass it.

5.1.
Main Results

Overall Performance and the Usability Gap.

Figure
12
presents the aggregate performance of 22 models on the 2,500-case ClinConsensus benchmark. Under our strictly calibrated
CACS@7
metric—which penalizes responses that fail to clear the clinical usability threshold—
GPT-5.2
establishes the frontier at 34.7%, followed closely by
Qwen3-Max
(33.3%) and
Qwen-Plus

Crucially, the dumbbell plot exposes a pervasive “usability gap” between average correctness and clinical actionability. Across all models, standard Rubric Accuracy consistently overestimates performance by 12.5 to 15.5 absolute percentage points. For instance, while
GPT-5.2
achieves a Rubric Accuracy of 47.2%, its CACS@7 score of 34.7% reveals that only about one-third of its responses are sufficiently comprehensive and safe for real-world clinical deployment. This systemic discrepancy validates our core premise: isolated factual correctness does not naturally translate into reliable clinical utility, underscoring the necessity of threshold-calibrated metrics.

Task-Level Capability Dichotomy.

Deconstructing the performance across 12 clinical task themes (Figure

This stepwise degradation is closely mirrored by Qwen3-Max (42.5%, 37.9%, 31.5%) and Qwen3.5-Plus (41.0%, 35.8%, 29.4%). This trend indicates that while LLMs are relatively adept at providing general health education and lifestyle guidance, their reliability falters when navigating the high reasoning complexity and rigorous safety constraints required for active clinical diagnosis and intervention.

Performance Across 15 Fine-Grained Clinical Scenarios.

To pinpoint capability bottlenecks within specific operational workflows, we analyzed performance across 15 clinical scenarios (Table
1
, Figure
10
). The results expose a clear operational hierarchy:

•

Strengths in Education and Triage:
Models excel at tasks relying on knowledge retrieval and protocol matching. The highest CACS@7 scores concentrate in
Critical care – recognition
(GPT-5.2: 48.4%),
Health education
(46.1%), and
Clinical teaching
(45.8%), demonstrating reliable textbook alignment.

•

Failures in Documentation and Image/Pathology:
Conversely, performance drops precipitously on structured information processing. Tasks like
Clinical documentation
(extraction/summarization) and
Test interpretation – imaging/pathology
yield universally poor results (mostly
<20\%
), exposing severe limitations in synthesizing dense, multi-modal or unstructured clinical narratives.

•

Bottlenecks in Core Clinical Reasoning:
For the most critical tasks—
Differential diagnosis
(25.16% of cases) and
Personalized treatment planning
(7.68%)—frontier models plateau in the low-to-mid 30s. While capable of outlining generic pathways, they struggle to resolve multi-system constraints into clinically actionable, personalized plans, highlighting the final gap between static QA proficiency and autonomous clinical utility.

6.
Trustworthiness of Automated Grading

ClinConsensus relies on automated rubric grading to scale evaluation across thousands of open-ended clinical cases.
We therefore validate that the automated graders (i) closely match physician judgments and (ii) produce stable and robust
aggregate scores under different graders and repeated evaluations.

6.1.
Physician-aligned meta-evaluation

Meta-evaluation set.

We curate a physician-labeled set of 285 cases stratified by difficulty (low/medium/high: 115/100/70).
For each case, we collect responses from 15 evaluated models and grade each response against
30 binary rubric criteria, yielding a total of

285\times 15\times 30=128{,}250
rubric-level labels per physician grading pass.

Automated graders.

We compare three automated graders under the same input–output contract:
(i) an LLM-as-judge based on GPT-5.1,
(ii) an additional LLM-as-judge baseline based on GPT-4.1, and
(iii) a locally deployable SFT-trained judge model that produces the same structured JSON output
(
criteria_met
and a brief
explanation
).

Metric.

We formulate rubric grading as binary classification and report Macro-F1 (MF1),
which averages the F1 scores of the
met
and
not_met
classes
to reduce sensitivity to class imbalance.

Conservative handling of failures.

If a grader output is missing or cannot be parsed into a valid boolean
criteria_met
,
we count it as an incorrect prediction rather than discarding the instance,
making the reported MF1 a conservative lower bound.

Results.

Figure
6
shows model–physician agreement
across the 12 task themes.
Averaged across themes, the GPT-4.1 judge achieves the highest mean MF1 of
0.815
,
followed by the GPT-5.1 judge at
0.775
and the distilled SFT-8B judge at
0.765
.
Across graders, agreement is consistently lower on
Medical NER
and higher on

Patient education
and
Test / lab interpretation
, indicating stable
theme-wise patterns of grading difficulty.

Cross-grader robustness of CACS@7.

Beyond rubric-level agreement, we examine whether benchmark conclusions depend on the choice of automated grader.
Table

reports theme-wise CACS@7 under both the GPT-4.1 judge
and the distilled SFT-8B judge.
Across all 12 task themes, the two graders yield highly consistent model rankings with only modest absolute
score shifts, indicating that the observed theme-level performance patterns are robust to grader choice
rather than artifacts of a specific evaluator.

7.
Conclusion

In this study, we present
ClinConsensus
, a clinically grounded benchmark designed to evaluate whether medical
LLMs can produce responses that are not only knowledgeable, but also usable within real-world clinical
workflows. By pairing diverse, practice-oriented cases with rubric-based evaluation and a
clinically calibrated threshold (
CACS@
k), ClinConsensus moves beyond exam-style QA and
provides fine-grained insight into safety-critical decision points, failure modes, and practical
readiness.

Our results reveal substantial gaps between strong language modeling performance and consistent
clinical usability, particularly across task themes, care stages, and specialties. These findings
underscore the need for benchmarks that emphasize interpretability, actionability, and alignment
with real clinical practice.

ClinConsensus is released as an extensible evaluation framework. Future work will expand prevention
and public health scenarios, enrich representations of real-world constraints, and explore
interactive and multimodal evaluation settings. We hope this benchmark will serve as a foundation
for developing and assessing medical LLMs that are truly ready for deployment in real healthcare
contexts.

Appendix A
An Additional Case Study for Clinically Applicable Consistency Score

To intuitively understand this objective, we consider the case study in Figure
5
.
The low-scoring response (left) captures some general medical context, achieving a raw accuracy of 6/30.
However, it fails to provide the specific risk stratification and time-critical interventions required for safe clinical decision-making.
Under a standard average accuracy metric, these 6 points would positively contribute to the model’s score, potentially masking its inability to handle the case safely.
In contrast, CACS introduces a
clinical usability threshold
(e.g.,
k=7
) to filter out such “near-miss” responses.
By assigning zero marginal credit to responses below this threshold, CACS ensures that the metric reflects the rate of
usable
clinical output rather than partial correctness on unsafe responses.

Appendix B
Additional Details for Automated Grading Trustworthiness

This appendix provides implementation details for the physician-aligned meta-evaluation and score-stability
analysis in Section
6
.

B.1.
Physician Meta-evaluation Protocol

Rubric-level meta-examples.

We evaluate grader fidelity at the rubric level. A
meta-example
corresponds to a single rubric decision:

(\text{case }i,\ \text{model response }\hat{r}_{i,m},\ \text{criterion }c_{i,j},\ y^{\text{phys}}_{i,m,j}),

where
y^{\text{phys}}_{i,m,j}\in\{0,1\}
denotes the physician label (
met
/
not_met
)
for model
m
’s response on case
i
under criterion
c_{i,j}
.
With 285 cases, 15 models, and
N{=}30
criteria per case, this yields

285\times 15\times 30=128{,}250
rubric-level labels per physician grading pass.

Automated graders.

Both graders consume the same input triple
(\text{context},\hat{r},c_{i,j})
and produce a binary rubric
decision
\hat{y}_{i,m,j}\in\{0,1\}
under a strict JSON schema:

{explanation: string, criteria_met: boolean}
.
We evaluate:
(i) an LLM-as-judge grader (GPT-5.1), and
(ii) an SFT-trained judge model distilled to mimic high-quality rubric decisions.

Theme-wise grouping.

Each case is annotated with one (or multiple) task themes.
For theme
t
, we collect all rubric-level meta-examples whose case belongs to
t
, and compute an
agreement score
\mathrm{MF1}_{m,t}
for each evaluated model
m
.
We report per-theme means across models, as well as the per-model distributions shown in
Figure
6
.

Conservative parsing and failure handling.

We validate all grader outputs against the required JSON schema.
If a grader output is missing, malformed, or cannot be parsed into a valid boolean
criteria_met
,
we conservatively treat the rubric as
not_met
(i.e.,
\hat{y}=0
).
When computing MF1 against physician labels, such invalid outputs are counted as
incorrect
predictions
(i.e., they contribute to
FP
or
FN
depending on the physician label), rather than being dropped.
This prevents inflated agreement due to silent filtering and makes reported MF1 a conservative lower bound.

B.2.
Macro-F1 (MF1) for Rubric-level Agreement

We treat rubric grading as binary classification with
met
as the positive class.
Let
TP,TN,FP,FN
be counts over a set of rubric-level meta-examples.
We compute class-wise F1:

F1_{\texttt{met}}=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN},\qquad F1_{\texttt{not\_met}}=\frac{2TN}{2TN+FP+FN}.

We then report Macro-F1 (MF1) as:

\mathrm{MF1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(F1_{\texttt{met}}+F1_{\texttt{not\_met}}\right).

MF1 assigns equal weight to both classes and is therefore less sensitive to potential imbalance between

met
and
not_met
labels.
