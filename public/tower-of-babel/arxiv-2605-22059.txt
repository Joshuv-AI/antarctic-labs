1.
Introduction: Closed geodesics vs primes in short intervals

We investigate the distribution of closed geodesics on hyperbolic surfaces in short intervals, with the aim of comparing their statistics to those of prime numbers. The analogy between primes and geodesics, embodied in the prime geodesic theorem, suggests that questions about primes in short intervals should have geometric counterparts. We approach this problem from a probabilistic perspective, viewing the surface as random in moduli space with respect to the Weil–Petersson measure. Our goal is to understand the typical behavior, for large genus, of the appropriately weighted number of closed geodesics with lengths in short intervals, and to relate it to conjectural predictions from analytic number theory arising from random matrix statistics of zeros of L-functions.

1.1.
Primes

Recall that the Prime Number Theorem is equivalent to the assertion that
\psi(x)\sim x
as
x\to\infty
, where

\psi(x):=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)

and
\Lambda(n)
is the von Mangoldt function, equal to
\log p
if
n=p^{k}
is a power of a prime
p
, and equal to zero otherwise.

A key objective is to understand the distribution of primes in short intervals, and to do so one studies

\psi(x;H):=\psi(x+H)-\psi(x)=\sum_{x<n\leq x+H}\Lambda(n)

which helps count the number of primes in short intervals. The Riemann Hypothesis (RH) guarantees an asymptotic formula

\psi(X;H)\sim H
as long as
H>X^{\frac{1}{2}+o(1)}
,
H=o(X)
, see
[
13
]
for the currently best unconditional result towards this, and the asymptotic is expected to hold for all intervals if
X^{\varepsilon}<H<X^{1-\varepsilon}
,
\forall\varepsilon>0
(but not for shorter intervals
[
25
]
).
Selberg
[
36
]
showed that the asymptotic formula holds for
almost all
intervals of length
H=o(X)
in
[1,X]
,
H/(\log X)^{2}\to\infty
, assuming RH (unconditionally for
H\gg X^{19/77}
; the exponent
19/77
has been improved, to the current value of
2/15

[
13
]
).

Goldston and Montgomery

[
12
]
studied the variance of

and conjectured

Conjecture 1
.

For every fixed

,

\operatorname{Var}(\psi)(X;H):=\frac{1}{X}\int_{1}^{X}\left|\psi(x;H)-H\right|^{2}dx\sim H\log\frac{X}{H}

holds uniformly for
1\leq H<X^{1-\delta}
.

They showed this to hold, assuming the Riemann Hypothesis and the (“strong”) pair correlation conjecture for the zeros of the Riemann zeta function. See
[
19
,
14
]
for a proof in a function field context.

1.3.
Short intervals

We want to count geodesics in “short” intervals, so define for
H=o(X)

\Psi_{M}(x;H):=\Psi_{M}(x+H)-\Psi_{M}(x)

which counts oriented geodesics of norm

\Psi_{M}(x;H)\sim H,\quad x\to\infty.

1.4.
Short interval variance for higher-degree L-functions

Let
L(s,\pi)
be a primitive automorphic L-function associated
to a cuspidal automorphic representation of
\operatorname{GL}(d)
over the
rationals (see §
5.1
). Its logarithmic derivative has the Dirichlet
series expansion

-\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\pi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Lambda(n)a_{\pi}(n)n^{-s},\qquad\operatorname{Re}(s)>1.

We define the associated Chebyshev function

\psi_{\pi}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)a_{\pi}(n),

for which one has

\psi_{\pi}(x)=O\!\left(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})\right)

for
d>1
, see
[
21
, Theorem 2.3]
.

Consider the variance

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(X;H):=\frac{1}{X}\int_{1}^{X}\bigl(\psi_{\pi}(x+H)-\psi_{\pi}(x)-m_{\pi}H\bigr)^{2}dx,

where
m_{\pi}=1
for the Riemann zeta function, and otherwise

m_{\pi}=0
.

Bui, Keating and Smith
[
8
]
conjectured that for degree
d>1
, there is a phase transition in the
variance of short interval sums at the scale
H=X^{1-1/d}
: for
“very short” intervals,

X^{\varepsilon}<H<X^{1-1/d-\varepsilon},

one should have

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(X;H)\sim H\log X,

while for larger intervals,

X^{1-1/d+\varepsilon}<H<X^{1-\varepsilon},

the variance should instead satisfy

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(X;H)\sim dH\log\frac{X}{H}.

Their argument, following Goldston and Montgomery
[
12
]
,
relates these asymptotics to the form factor governing pair
correlation of zeros:
let

F(X,T)=\sum_{0\leq\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leq T}X^{i(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime})}w(\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}),\qquad w(u)=\frac{4}{4+u^{2}},

where the sum runs over nontrivial zeros
\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}+i\gamma
of
L(s,\pi)
.
Writing

X=T^{d\alpha},

we define the normalized form factor by

K(\alpha):=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{F(T^{d\alpha},T)}{N(T)},

assuming the limit exists.
The pair correlation conjecture predicts that

K(\alpha)=K_{\mathrm{GUE}}(\alpha)=\min(\alpha,1).

We show that in the very short interval regime, the relevant form
factor asymptotics already follow from the Riemann Hypothesis alone.
Thus, unlike the case of the Riemann zeta function (
d=1
),
RH alone already determines the early-time form factor.
Consequently, the very short interval variance regime also follows from RH alone.
Our result is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
L(s,\pi)
be a primitive automorphic
L
-function of degree

d>1
, and assume RH for
L(s,\pi)
. Then the normalized form
factor satisfies

K(\alpha)=\alpha,\qquad 0<\alpha<\frac{1}{d},

recovering the GUE form factor in the early-time regime.

This result helps clarify the appearance of the variance
H\log X

in Theorem
1.1
. For
L
-functions of degree
d>1
, the very short interval regime

H<X^{1-1/d}

corresponds precisely to the early-time range

\alpha<1/d,

where the form factor is already determined by RH. By contrast,
for the Riemann zeta function (
d=1
), there is no corresponding very short interval regime, and Goldston–Montgomery
[
12
]
need the conjecture that the form factor
K(\alpha)
coincides with the GUE form factor
K_{\operatorname{GUE}}(\alpha)=\min(\alpha,1)
for
\alpha>1
. For higher-degree

L
-functions, however, the very short interval regime is already
determined by the early-time form factor. Since the spectrum of the
Laplacian on a hyperbolic surface has much higher density than the
zeros of any finite-degree
L
-function, random hyperbolic surfaces
may be viewed heuristically as an “infinite-degree” limit in
which only this early-time regime survives.
This explains the appearance of the factor
\log X
in
Theorem
1.1
.

To explain the factor of
2
in Theorem
1.1
, we replace the GUE form factor by the GOE form factor expected for
generic hyperbolic surfaces.

Thus Theorem
1.1
may be viewed as the GOE and infinite-density
analogue of the early-time variance regime for higher-degree

L
-functions.

The expected value

Proposition 3.1
.

As

Proof.

We use the simple formula

\Psi(x)=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\\
{\rm primitive}\\
{\rm unoriented}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\

4.
The variance

Our goal is to show that

Theorem 4.1
.

As
x\to\infty
,
H=o(x)
,

\lim_{g\to\infty}\operatorname{Var}_{g}\left(\Psi(x;H)\right)\sim 2\cdot H\log x.

4.1.
Plan of proof

Recall

\psi(x;H)=\sum_{\gamma}f(\ell_{\gamma})

where the sum is over primitive unoriented geodesics, with

f(\ell)=2\ell\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbf{1}_{[\frac{A}{n},\frac{B}{n}]}(\ell)\quad A=\log x,\quad B=\log(x+H).

We now compute the variance

\operatorname{Var}_{g}(\Psi)=\mathbb{E}^{\rm WP}_{g}(\Psi^{2})-(\mathbb{E}^{\rm WP}_{g}(\Psi))^{2}.

We expand
\Psi^{2}
as a sum over pairs
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
of geodesics

\Psi^{2}=\sum_{\gamma}\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}}f(\ell(\gamma))f(\ell(\gamma^{\prime})).

We separate out the diagonal pairs
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}
from the rest

\mathbb{E}^{\rm WP}_{g}(\Psi)=\operatorname{DIAG}+\operatorname{OFF}

and compute each term separately. We will show that

\lim_{g\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\rm WP}_{g}(\operatorname{OFF})=(\lim_{g\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\rm WP}_{g}(\Psi))^{2}

is exactly cancelled out by the squared expected value, so that

\lim_{g\to\infty}\operatorname{Var}_{g}(\Psi)=\lim_{g\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\rm WP}_{g}(\operatorname{DIAG})

and will then compute the diagonal term.

4.2.
The off-diagonal

We decompose

\operatorname{OFF}=\operatorname{OFF}_{SNS}+\operatorname{OFF}_{\operatorname{rest}}

where

5.
Comparison with the theory of L-functions

5.1.
Background on L-functions

In order to explain the comparison between Theorem
1.1
and the conjecture of Goldston and Montgomery, we recall some of the theory of L-functions.

We shall deal with L-functions associated to cuspidal automorphic representations
\pi
of
\operatorname{GL}(d)
over the rationals (when
d=1
then these are either the Riemann zeta function or Dirichlet L-functions), see
[
33
]
. The integer
d
is called the degree of the L-function.
They have an Euler product of the form

L(s,\pi)=\prod_{p}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(1-\alpha_{\pi}(p,j)p^{-s}\right)^{-1},\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)>1

where
d=d_{\pi}
is the degree of the L-function.
The Ramanujan-Petersson conjecture states that
|\alpha_{\pi}(p,j)|\leq 1
(there are many known examples).
These L-functions are primitive, i.e. cannot be written as a product of other L-functions.

Such an L-function admits an analytic continuation to all of the complex plane, save for a possible pole at
s=1
, which can only occur if the degree is
d=1
and
L(s,\pi)
is the Riemann zeta function, since we assume that our L-functions are primitive. We denote by
m_{\pi}=0,1
the order of the pole at
s=1
;
m_{\pi}=0
unless
L(s,\pi)=\zeta(s)
. There is a functional equation of the form

\Phi(s,\pi):=Q_{\pi}^{s/2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s+\mu_{F}(j))L(s,F)=w\,\overline{\Phi(1-\bar{s},\pi)},

with
Q_{\pi}>0
a positive integer,
\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s):=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)
and
|w|=1
.

The zeros
\{\rho_{\pi}\}
of
\Phi(s,\pi)
are called the nontrivial zeros of
L(s,\pi)
.
The Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) asserts that all nontrivial zeros lie on the line
\operatorname{Re}(s)=1/2
.

The analogue of the Riemann von-Mangoldt theorem is

(5.1)

N_{\pi}(T):=\#\{\rho_{\pi}:0\leq\operatorname{Im}(\rho_{\pi})\leq T\}\sim d\frac{T\log T}{2\pi},\quad T\to\infty.

In particular, the larger the degree, the greater the density of zeros.

The logarithmic derivative has an expansion

-\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\pi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Lambda(n)a_{\pi}(n)n^{-s},\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)>1

where for
p
prime,

a_{\pi}(p^{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{\pi}(p,j)^{k}.

6.
Explaining the factor of
2

We now explain heuristically the origin of the factor of
2
in
Theorem
1.1
. The point is that the variance of short interval sums
is determined by pair correlation of the relevant spectrum through the form factor.
For zeros of L-functions one expects GUE statistics,
while for generic hyperbolic surfaces one expects GOE statistics.
The difference between these two symmetry classes produces the
factor of
2
.

We briefly recall the relevant picture. In the work of Goldston and
Montgomery
[
12
]
, and its extension to higher-degree
L-functions by Bui, Keating and Smith
[
8
]
, the variance of
short interval sums is related, via the explicit formula, to the form
factor
K(\alpha)
,
which governs pair correlation of zeros. In the classical setting of
zeros of L-functions, one expects GUE statistics, corresponding to
the GUE form factor

K_{\mathrm{GUE}}(\alpha)=\min(\alpha,1).

For generic hyperbolic surfaces, however, some
evidence suggests GOE statistics for the Laplace eigenvalues
[
7
,
3
,
32
]
.
The corresponding GOE form factor satisfies

K_{\mathrm{GOE}}(\alpha)\sim 2\alpha,\qquad\alpha\to 0,

whereas

K_{\mathrm{GUE}}(\alpha)\sim\alpha,\qquad\alpha\to 0.

Thus the GOE form factor is asymptotically twice as large as the GUE
form factor at early times.

For higher-degree L-functions, the relevant regime for very short
intervals is precisely the early-time range

\alpha<\frac{1}{d}.

As explained in §
1.4
, random hyperbolic surfaces should be viewed
heuristically as an “infinite-degree” limit, since the spectrum of
the Laplacian has much greater density than the zeros of any
finite-degree L-function. Consequently, only the early-time
behavior of the form factor contributes to the variance.

In the Goldston–Montgomery argument,
the variance is determined by the form factor through an integral
formula. Consequently, replacing the GUE form factor by the GOE
form factor multiplies the resulting variance by
2
. Thus the expected GOE statistics for the
Laplace spectrum of generic hyperbolic surfaces lead naturally to
expect

\operatorname{Var}(\Psi_{M}(X;H))\sim 2H\log X.

Accordingly, Theorem
1.1
may be viewed as the GOE analogue of the
early-time variance regime for higher-degree L-functions.
