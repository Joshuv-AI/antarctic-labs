NAIMA: Semantics Aware RGB Guided Depth Super-Resolution
Tayyab Nasir, Daochang Liu, Ajmal Mian

Abstract
Guided depth super-resolution (GDSR) is a multi-modal approach for depth map super-resolution that relies on a low-resolution depth map and a high-resolution RGB image to restore finer structural details. However, the misleading color and texture cues indicating depth discontinuities in RGB images often lead to artifacts and blurred depth boundaries in the generated depth map. We propose a solution that introduces global contextual semantic priors, generated from pretrained vision transformer token embeddings. Our approach to distilling semantic knowledge from pretrained token embeddings is motivated by their demonstrated effectiveness in related monocular depth estimation tasks. We introduce a Guided Token Attention (GTA) module, which iteratively aligns encoded RGB spatial features with depth encodings, using cross-attention for selectively injecting global semantic context extracted from different layers of a pretrained vision transformer. Additionally, we present an architecture called Neural Attention for Implicit Multi-token Alignment (NAIMA) 1 1 1 Code available at: https://github.com/tayyabnasir22/NAIMA-GDSR , which integrates DINOv2 with GTA blocks for a semantics-aware GDSR. Our proposed architecture, with its ability to distill semantic knowledge, achieves significant improvements over existing methods across multiple scaling factors and datasets.

Introduction

Depth maps are crucial for numerous computer vision tasks, including 3D scene reconstruction, object detection, and semantic segmentation
[
24
,
29
,
35
]
. They are widely utilized in real-world applications such as autonomous driving and industrial manufacturing systems
[
35
,
28
,
23
]
. Compared to RGB sensors, depth sensors still lag in terms of resolution, accuracy, and cost-effectiveness. Hence, RGB-guided depth map super-resolution has emerged as a practical solution to enhance depth resolution without the need for expensive hardware.

RGB-guided depth super-resolution is motivated by the observation that RGB images and depth maps provide complementary geometric representations of the same scene
[
35
,
3
,
4
]
. Under this assumption, the objective is to integrate low-frequency structural information from the blurred low-resolution depth map with high-frequency features of the corresponding high-resolution RGB image to reconstruct a high-resolution depth map. Several studies have highlighted a fundamental challenge inherent to this multi-modal setting, where the abundant color and texture information contained in the RGB image is not always aligned with true geometric discontinuities in the depth domain
[
24
,
22
,
11
,
12
]
. Hence, in the absence of semantic guidance, a direct naive fusion of the spatial RGB features with LR depth features can lead to structural inconsistencies, blurred object boundaries, or misaligned geometric structures.

Multi-task learning, which aims to enhance the performance of a primary task by jointly optimizing it with related auxiliary tasks, has recently attracted significant attention in guided depth super-resolution (GDSR). Several prior studies have reported improved GDSR performance by incorporating auxiliary objectives such as monocular depth estimation, gradient prediction, and self-supervised degradation modeling
[
22
,
24
,
23
]
.

Monocular depth estimation, which predicts relative depth from a single RGB image, faces the same challenge of RGB feature noise, where prior works have addressed this issue using multi-task learning, typically using semantic segmentation as an auxiliary task
[
2
,
6
,
10
]
. DepthAnything introduced a unified approach by replacing the auxiliary task with pretrained DINO patch tokens, which provide rich semantic priors absent in standard RGB encoders
[
26
]
. Their work demonstrated that these DINO
[
16
]
embeddings can perform well for semantic tasks, such as semantic segmentation, even without fine-tuning, and can be leveraged to distill strong semantic knowledge to guide the monocular depth estimation.

Motivated by the aforementioned limitations and ideas, we introduce our unified architecture NAIMA (Neural Attention with Implicit Multi-Token Alignment). The core idea is to integrate semantically rich priors into encoded depth features to enable semantics-aware depth super-resolution. To this end, we propose a Guided Token Alignment (GTA) module, which employs cross-attention to query relevant RGB semantic tokens using depth encodings, infusing additional semantic information into the depth representations, instead of depending solely on spatial RGB features.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose a solution to cross-modal misalignment in guided depth super-resolution (GDSR).

•

We propose a semantics-driven framework that provides explicit semantic guidance through pretrained token representations, drawn from a vision transformer.

•

We introduce NAIMA, a semantics-aware GDSR framework that employs a cross-attention-based GTA module to align depth features with pretrained semantic tokens for enhanced reconstruction.

Extensive experimental evaluations show that our approach consistently outperforms existing GDSR methods across multiple datasets and scaling factors. We evaluate NAIMA on the Middlebury
[
17
]
, Lu
[
13
]
, NYU_v2
[
19
]
, and RGBDD
[
7
]
datasets against 11 benchmark methods, using root mean squared error (RMSE) in
cm^{2}
as the evaluation metric. For a 16x scaling factor, NAIMA achieves RMSE improvements of 3.35%, 3.14%, 6.78%, and 5.92% over the next best-performing method on the NYU_v2, RGBDD, Middlebury, and Lu datasets, respectively.

Related Work

Obtaining high-quality depth data is costly and often constrained by hardware limitations
[
1
]
. To address this limitation, prior work has largely focused on multi-modal approaches, where a high-resolution RGB image serves as a guiding modality to recover missing high-frequency details. Despite the recent advancements, the development of new methods in guided depth super-resolution remains comparatively limited when contrasted with the rapid progress observed in RGB image super-resolution.

In recent years, various techniques have been proposed for both standard and RGB-guided depth super-resolution, introducing end-to-end deep learning architectures to reconstruct high-resolution depth maps from low-resolution inputs. Early works such as
[
36
,
9
,
8
,
5
]
were among the first to employ deep neural networks for guided depth super-resolution. These methods typically apply convolutional neural networks to fused features extracted from the low-resolution depth map and the high-resolution RGB guide to reconstruct a single high-resolution depth output.

However, a key challenge in RGB-guided depth super-resolution remains the presence of noise in RGB images, such as misleading color and texture patterns that do not necessarily correspond to actual geometric shapes. This, in turn, can cause blurred boundaries and artifacts in the resultant high-resolution depth map. This issue has been widely recognized in prior work, with many novel architectures proposing mechanisms such as attention-based fusion, auxiliary learning, and constrained feature integration to mitigate noise from RGB guidance
[
11
,
24
,
23
,
22
]
. These approaches aim to selectively filter irrelevant RGB information and focus on strongly correlated features to improve reconstruction quality.

Prior works have used attention for learned feature fusion mechanisms to better model the correlation between RGB and depth modalities
[
33
,
27
,
20
]
. By leveraging attention, these approaches aim to suppress irrelevant texture artifacts, selecting only relevant features, thereby reducing blurred boundaries and reconstruction errors caused by noisy RGB guidance.

In addition, many studies adopt multi-task learning strategies to further regulate the interaction between RGB and depth features. For example, monocular depth estimation (MDE) has been used as an auxiliary task to enhance shared feature representations, improving overall GDSR performance
[
22
]
. Methods such as SGNet and DORNet introduce gradient–frequency estimation and self-supervised degradation modeling, respectively, improving robustness under real-world degraded depth conditions. Similarly, another such study
[
25
]
incorporates depth completion as an auxiliary task, arguing that such supervision helps capture contextual dependencies and better control feature interactions between RGB and depth modalities.

The challenge of noisy texture and color information in RGB has also been widely studied in monocular depth estimation (MDE). Many approaches adopt multi-task learning frameworks, using semantic segmentation as an auxiliary task to mitigate noisy feature correlations and enforce global semantic consistency from RGB images
[
2
,
6
,
10
]
. These methods argue that incorporating high-level semantic supervision helps reduce the impact of misleading texture cues. Recent architectures such as DepthAnything further extend this idea by leveraging pretrained vision transformer embeddings trained on large-scale RGB datasets as an alternative to explicit auxiliary tasks, demonstrating that pretrained semantic representations can effectively guide depth estimation.
However, the integration of such semantic token representations has not yet been thoroughly explored in guided depth super-resolution (GDSR), despite their proven effectiveness in MDE.

Methodology

Figure
2
presents a high-level overview of the
NAIMA
architecture. The input to the model is a high-resolution RGB image
I\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times H\times W}
and a low-resolution depth map
D_{lr}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times h\times w}
, where
h=\frac{H}{s}
,
w=\frac{W}{s}
, and
s
denotes the upscaling factor.

Figure 2:
Overview of Neural Attention for Implicit Multi-token Alignment (NAIMA) architecture. The semantic encoder is based on DINOv2 and extracts high-level semantic representations from the RGB input. GTA is our proposed Guided Token Attention module, which uses cross-attention to inject relevant semantic features into the depth feature maps, along with integration of spatial RGB information. The Upsampler consists of a series of convolutional, deconvolutional, and residual channel attention layers that progressively reconstruct and spatially upscale the final depth features.

Semantic information is extracted from
I
, by passing it through a pretrained
DINOv2
, obtaining intermediate patch-level tokens
\left\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3},\tau_{4}\right\}
. Each token embedding is extracted from a different transformer block and encodes progressively higher-level semantics. Depth features are iteratively encoded using the depth encoder
\mathcal{E}_{D}
, built upon residual channel attention blocks
[
30
]
, which have been widely adopted in the literature for spatial feature representation. Let
E_{i}
denote the enriched depth feature map at level
i\in\left\{1,2,3,4\right\}
, with
D_{0}=D_{lr}
. The iterative encoding is defined as:

E_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{D}(D_{i-1}).

(1)

3.1
Guided Token Alignment:

Figure
3
presents the details of a single
GTA
block. The core idea behind our
GTA
module is to progressively distill higher-level semantic information from the token embeddings into depth feature maps, generating context-aware refined depth embeddings at different levels. For this, we employ cross-attention to compute the correlation between the encoded depth features
E_{i}
(Query) and the RGB tokens
\tau_{i}
(Key and Value). The semantic tokens are first reshaped into their corresponding 2D spatial grid and then undergo convolutional projections to introduce local spatial interactions:

S_{i}=\mathcal{P}(\tau_{i}),

(2)

where
S_{i}
represents the projected semantic feature map.

Figure 3:
Overview of the Guided Token Attention (GTA) module. This module encodes spatial, depth, and semantic features, and merges them using cross attention, to produce a semantically enriched depth feature map while preserving geometric details from both low-resolution depth and RGB inputs.

The projected semantics map
S_{i}
is then passed through a pixel shuffle operation
\mathcal{U}
, which converts channel-wise sub-pixel encodings into a dense, high-resolution spatial representation, ensuring that the semantic features have the same spatial dimensions as the encoded depth map
E_{i}
.

F_{i}=\mathcal{U}(S_{i}),

(3)

Next, the encoded depth feature map
E_{i}
queries the projected semantic features
F_{i}
using standard scaled dot-product cross-attention. This information is infused in the input depth features, producing a semantics-infused feature embedding:

D^{*}_{i}=E_{i}+\alpha*Softmax\left(\frac{E_{i}F_{i}^{T}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)F_{i},

(4)

where
d_{k}
is the scaling factor representing the dimension of the key vectors, and
\alpha
is a learnable parameter that controls the contribution of the distilled semantic features.

At this stage, the depth feature map
D^{*}_{i}
has been enriched with low-frequency details from the low-resolution depth map and semantic information from the RGB tokens. To incorporate high-frequency details from the high-resolution RGB image
I
, we pass it through a simple feature encoder composed of residual blocks:

R^{*}_{i}=\mathcal{E}_{R}(I).

(5)

These spatial RGB features
R^{*}_{i}
are then infused with
D^{*}_{i}
, using an existing feature fusion block
[
18
]
, producing the refined depth feature map at the corresponding level:

D_{i+1}=\delta(D^{*}_{i},R^{*}_{i}),

(6)

where
D_{i+1}
serves as the input for the subsequent
GTA
block. This process is repeated iteratively across all levels. The final refined depth feature map is passed through an upsampling network
\phi
, and the bicubic upsampled input low-resolution depth map is added to it to generate the high-resolution depth map
D_{hr}
.

D_{hr}=\phi(D_{4})+D_{lr}^{\uparrow},

(7)

3.2
Loss Function

We adopt a gradient-aware pixel loss that consists of a pixel-wise
\mathcal{L}_{1}
reconstruction term between the predicted and ground-truth depth maps, along with an additional
\mathcal{L}_{1}
loss computed between their spatial gradients
[
15
]
.

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{1}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{grad},

(8)

where

\mathcal{L}_{1}=\parallel\hat{D}_{sr}-D_{gt}\parallel_{1},

(9)

and

\mathcal{L}_{grad}=\parallel\nabla_{x}\hat{D}_{sr}-\nabla_{x}D_{gt}\parallel_{1}+\parallel\nabla_{y}\hat{D}_{sr}-\nabla_{y}D_{gt}\parallel_{1},

(10)

where
lambda
is the gradient control term set to 0.05
[
15
]
.

4
Experiments

4.1
Datasets

We follow the same training and evaluation protocols as adopted in prior work
[
9
,
14
,
24
]
. Our model is trained on the NYU_v2 benchmark using scaling factors of 4x, 8x, and 16x, where low-resolution depth maps are synthetically generated through bicubic downsampling, following the standard train-test split. For cross-dataset validation, the same models trained exclusively on the NYU_v2 dataset are used without any fine-tuning. Evaluation is conducted on RGBD-D, Middlebury, and LU datasets, using root mean squared error (RMSE) in centimeters for measuring performance.

4.2
Implementation

For NAIMA, the DINOv2 backbone remains frozen to retain the pretrained semantic priors. Consistent with prior work, all models are trained on bicubic downsampled inputs for each scaling factor and optimized for 200 epochs. We use the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.0001, and employ a multi-step learning rate decay scheduler. All experiments are conducted with a batch size of 1 on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

Ablation

Our ablation consists of validating the importance of semantic attention injection into depth features, and verifying the effectiveness of the L1-gradient loss function. To evaluate the role of semantic alignment using cross-attention-based semantics injection into the encoded depth map, we simply remove the cross-attention component and replace it with a simple addition operation, modifying the equation
4
as follows:

D^{*}_{i}=E_{i}+F_{i}.

(11)

For the next ablation, we simply replace the loss function with L1 loss for training the NAIMA architecture. For both cases, we train NAIMA using the NYU_v2 dataset employing the same training configurations as discussed before. 6
Conclusion

We introduce a novel architecture for guided depth super-resolution (GDSR) that incorporates additional semantic priors derived from pretrained vision transformer embeddings. Unlike existing approaches, our framework leverages semantically rich token representations as an explicit modality for guided depth super-resolution, enabling more faithful utilization of RGB information. Our proposed architecture achieves state-of-the-art performance across different scaling factors and datasets. Furthermore, we propose a novel Guided Token Alignment (GTA) module that selectively transfers relevant semantic information across multiple levels of token abstraction into the depth representation. By progressively aligning depth features with semantic tokens of varying complexity, the model learns to perform more reliable GDSR, thereby reducing the adverse effects of RGB texture and color noise, which often lead to erroneous depth discontinuities.

A.4
Additional Implementation Choices

Next, we discuss additional configurations, including patch size selection and training protocols.

Patch Size:

We use a patch size of 420 for 4x scaling factor, and 448 for 8x and 16x scaling factors. These values are selected to ensure compatibility with the DINOv2 semantics encoder, which requires the input image size to be divisible by 14 for its patch tokenization. Additionally, a smaller patch size was chosen for the 4x scaling factor due to GPU memory limitations.

Training Protocols:
We employ a step-based learning rate decay scheduler, using 0.3 as the decay factor, reducing the learning rate after every 50 epochs. All RGB images are standardized using ImageNet normalization statistics. For reproducibility, the complete data processing pipeline and training code are provided in the code repository

2

Code available at:
https://github.com/tayyabnasir22/NAIMA-GDSR
. Additionally, data preparation code is also available publicly

Inference Time Padding:

As aforementioned, our model required input images having a size divisible by 14 for the DINOv2 encoder. During inference, the input images whose spatial dimensions are not divisible by 14 are padded with additional zeros at the bottom right corner before being processed by the model. After the inference, the predicted depth map is cropped back to the original resolution, prior to being evaluated using RMSE. This step is necessary to satisfy the architectural constraints of DINOv2, although it may introduce a minor bias in the reported RMSE due to padding artifacts, slightly adding to the reported error.
