Adaptive Metrics for Norm-Minimization-Based Outer Approximation in Convex Vector Optimization
Mohammed Alshahrani

Abstract
We develop an adaptive-metric framework for norm-minimization-based outer approximation algorithms in bounded convex vector optimization. The key idea is to let the scalarization metric vary across iterations while measuring approximation error in a fixed Euclidean norm. This enables the algorithm to exploit problem geometry dynamically. Our approach rests on two theoretical foundations. First, we prove that the improved Euclidean convergence rate O ⁡ ( k 2 / ( 1 − q ) ) O(k^{2/(1-q)}) —previously known only for the standard ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -norm—extends to all fixed inner-product norms. Second, we establish a dispersion theorem showing that the cut-normals generated by the algorithm naturally spread across all directions when the upper image has a strictly convex boundary with bounded curvature. This geometric condition guarantees that the adaptive metric remains well-conditioned throughout execution. Building on these results, we derive explicit convergence bounds that quantify how metric conditioning influences the Hausdorff error estimates. Numerical experiments on three test problems validate the theoretical convergence rate; on the problems whose Pareto fronts have sufficient curvature, the adaptive metric additionally reduces the iteration count relative to the fixed Euclidean norm. Our results provide a rigorous foundation for adaptive metric selection in convex vector optimization.

Introduction

Vector optimization provides a unifying framework for multiobjective decision-making under partial orders induced by convex cones rather than simple componentwise dominance, for more details, see
[
1
]
. In the convex setting, one is interested in approximating the
upper image

P:=\Gamma(X)+C,

where
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
is a closed, solid, pointed cone,
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a compact convex feasible set, and
\Gamma:X\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
is continuous and
C
–convex. Efficient solution methods for such convex vector optimization problems (CVOPs) are central in applications from finance and risk to control and machine learning; see, for instance,
[
2
,
15
]
and the references therein.

Among objective-space methods,
outer approximation
algorithms have emerged as particularly interesting. Originating from Benson’s algorithm for linear multiobjective optimization and its generalizations, these methods iteratively refine a polyhedral outer approximation of
P
by solving scalarization problems and adding supporting halfspaces, until the approximation is sufficiently accurate in a suitable metric. For CVOPs that are
bounded
—in the sense that the upper image satisfies
P\subseteq\{y\}+C
for some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
—and have polyhedral ordering cones, several such algorithms have been developed based on Pascoletti–Serafini scalarizations, norm-minimizing scalarizations, and various vertex and direction selection rules; see, for instance,
[
2
,
15
]
.

A recent line of work by Ararat et al.
[
2
,
3
]
introduced and analyzed an outer approximation algorithm driven by a
norm-minimizing
scalarization. At each iteration, a reference point
v
(typically a vertex of the current outer approximation) is chosen and a scalar convex program is solved that computes the distance from
v
to the upper image
P
with respect to a fixed norm. The resulting primal–dual optimal pair yields, respectively, a new weak minimizer of the original CVOP and a supporting halfspace of a compact slice of the upper image. This algorithm has two key theoretical properties: (i) it is the first CVOP algorithm for which
finiteness
is proved under mild assumptions, and (ii) it admits a nontrivial
convergence-rate analysis
based on the Hausdorff distance.

In
[
3
]
, the authors establish that, for an arbitrary norm on the objective space, the Hausdorff distance between the outer approximation and the upper image decays on the order of
O(k^{1/(1-q)})
after
k
iterations, where
q
is the dimension of the objective space and
q\geq 2
. This result is obtained by relating the sequence of approximating polytopes to an
H(r,A)
–sequence of cutting, see Definition
5
, in the sense of Kamenev and Lotov et al.
[
13
,
21
]
, and then invoking general approximation results for convex bodies by polyhedra. In addition,
[
3
]
contains a refined analysis, in case of Euclidean-norm-based scalarizations, by exploiting the special geometry of the
\ell_{2}
-norm, the authors prove an improved convergence rate

\delta_{H}(P_{k},P)=O\bigl(k^{2/(1-q)}\bigr),

which is known to be the best possible in the classical multiobjective setting
[
21
]
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first convergence-rate result of this sharp order for a general CVOP algorithm.

The improved convergence rate based on the Euclidean norm raises the following natural question. To what extent is this enhancement tied to the standard Euclidean structure, and to what extent does it reflect more general Hilbertian geometry? While the generic convergence rate of order
O(k^{1/(1-q)})
in
[
3
]
holds for arbitrary norms, the proof of the rate of order
O(k^{2/(1-q)})
uses several arguments that appear to be specific to the
\ell_{2}
-norm (e.g., Pythagorean identities and Euclidean packing estimates). It is not immediately clear whether similar arguments can be carried out for other norms, nor how the geometry of the norm (e.g., asphericity, uniform convexity) influences the achievable exponent.

The aim of this paper is to close this gap at least for the important class of
inner-product norms
on
\mathbb{R}^{q}
. Any such norm can be written in the form

\|y\|_{M}:=\sqrt{y^{\top}My},\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{q},

for some symmetric positive definite matrix
M
. The key observation is that the linear isomorphism
T:=M^{1/2}
is an isometry between
(\mathbb{R}^{q},\|\cdot\|_{M})
and the Euclidean space
(\mathbb{R}^{q},\|\cdot\|_{2})
, and that the norm-minimization–based algorithm of
[
3
]
is invariant under such transformations, provided one simultaneously transforms the ordering cone, the upper image and the compact slice in the objective space; we make this precise in Section
3
(see Proposition
4
).

The approximation of Pareto frontiers and upper images in multiobjective optimization has a rich history spanning several decades. We briefly survey the main threads of this literature, focusing on outer approximation methods, scalarization techniques, convergence rate theory, and alternative approximation paradigms.

The systematic study of objective-space methods for multiobjective optimization began with Benson’s seminal outer approximation algorithm for multiple objective linear programming
[
4
]
. Benson’s algorithm iteratively refines a polyhedral outer approximation of the efficient set by solving weighted-sum scalarizations and adding cutting hyperplanes, providing the first practical method capable of generating all efficient extreme points in the outcome set. The extension of Benson’s approach to convex multiobjective problems was achieved by Ehrgott et al.
[
6
]
, who proved weak
\varepsilon
-nondominance guarantees and gave a construction of separating hyperplanes in the nonsmooth setting. Löhne et al.
[
18
]
introduced primal and dual approximation algorithms that operate simultaneously on the upper and lower images. Their framework allows solid pointed polyhedral ordering cones and provides an
\varepsilon
-solution concept with rigorous approximation guarantees. The comprehensive treatment of vector optimization from a set-optimization perspective, including the theoretical foundations for infimum and supremum concepts, can be found in Löhne’s monograph
[
20
]
and the references therein. Recent work by Keskin and Ulus
[
15
]
provides a systematic comparison of vertex selection rules and direction choices within the Pascoletti–Serafini framework, while Wagner et al.
[
25
]
extend outer approximation methods to unbounded problems. For general background on vector and multicriteria optimization, we refer to Jahn
[
12
]
. Moreover, extensions to nonconvex settings have been developed using branch-and-bound techniques
[
22
,
23
]
.

The choice of scalarization has significant implications for both the theoretical properties and computational efficiency of outer approximation algorithms. The Pascoletti–Serafini scalarization, which finds the minimum distance from a reference point to the upper image along a prescribed direction, has been widely adopted due to its flexibility and geometric interpretability; see
[
15
]
for an extensive computational study comparing different parameter choices. However, this scalarization introduces direction-biasedness: the quality of the approximation can depend strongly on the choice of search directions. To address this limitation, Ararat et al.
[
2
]
introduced a norm-minimizing scalarization that computes the distance from a reference point to the upper image with respect to a fixed norm, without requiring a direction parameter. This approach yields an algorithm that is direction-unbiased in the sense of Klamroth et al.
[
16
]
, who first identified direction-biasedness as a potential source of inefficiency in multiobjective approximation schemes. The norm-minimizing approach also enabled the first finiteness proof for a convex vector optimization algorithm under mild assumptions
[
2
]
. The interplay between scalarization choice and computational tractability in constrained settings is further explored in
[
17
]
.

The study of convergence rates for polyhedral approximation of convex bodies has deep roots in computational geometry and convex analysis. Kamenev
[
13
,
14
]
developed a general theory of adaptive algorithms for approximating convex bodies by polyhedra, introducing the concept of
H(r,A)
-sequences—sequences of cutting halfspaces that remove at least a fraction
r
of the current approximation error at each step. For such sequences, the Hausdorff approximation error decreases at the rate
O(k^{1/(1-q)})
. The improved rate
O(k^{2/(1-q)})
for Euclidean approximation is a classical result in convex geometry, originating with Gruber and Kenderov
[
11
]
, who proved that it is the best possible rate for smooth convex bodies. Lotov et al.
[
21
]
provided a comprehensive treatment of these ideas in the context of interactive decision maps and Pareto frontier visualization; we refer to this monograph and the references therein for the extensive classical literature on polyhedral approximation of convex bodies. The improvement in the Euclidean case stems from packing estimates for spherical caps. In Euclidean geometry, the number of nearly disjoint caps of radius
\varepsilon
on the unit sphere grows as
\varepsilon^{1-q}
. For general norms, the corresponding exponent may be worse, which limits the achievable approximation rate. Ararat et al.
[
3
]
were the first to establish these convergence rates for a specific CVOP algorithm, proving that their norm-minimizing algorithm generates an
H(r,A)
-sequence and thereby inherits the corresponding rate guarantees; see also the references therein for connections to approximation theory and convex geometry. Their analysis leaves open the question of whether the improved Euclidean exponent can be achieved for other norms—a question we resolve affirmatively for inner-product norms in the present work.

While polyhedral outer approximation has been the dominant approach, alternative paradigms have emerged. Eichfelder and Warnow
[
8
]
introduced a box-coverage method that approximates the nondominated set using a finite collection of boxes respecting the natural ordering, providing a simpler structure than polyhedra for subsequent analysis. The concept of local upper bounds, which plays a key role in branch-and-bound methods for nonconvex multiobjective optimization, has recently been extended to general polyhedral ordering cones by Eichfelder and Ulus
[
7
]
. Mixed-integer extensions of convex multiobjective optimization have been developed by De Santis et al.
[
5
]
, combining outer approximation with integer programming techniques.

The present paper extends the convergence rate theory of
[
3
]
in two directions. First, we show that the improved convergence rate based on the Euclidean norm is not special to the
\ell_{2}
-norm but extends to all inner-product norms via a linear isometry argument. Second, we introduce and analyze adaptive inner-product metrics that vary across iterations, and we establish convergence under a dispersion condition (Assumption
2
) on the generated cut-normals. This bridges a gap between the fixed-norm theory and the practical observation that adapting the metric to the geometry of the problem can accelerate convergence. Our dispersion theorem (Theorem
16
) provides sufficient geometric conditions—specifically, strict convexity with bounded curvature of the efficient frontier—under which the adaptive metrics remain well-conditioned. This provides the first rigorous justification for adaptive metric selection in norm-minimization-based vector optimization. In summary, any fixed inner-product norm attains the improved exponent
2/(1-q)
(Theorem
5
), whereas the adaptive-metric variant is guaranteed the generic exponent
1/(1-q)
(Theorem
18
), with a multiplicative constant that improves as the cut-normals disperse (Assumption
2
).

The contributions of this paper are fivefold.

(i)

(Section
3
, Theorem
5
) We show that the improved Hausdorff convergence rate
O(k^{2/(1-q)})
, previously established only for Euclidean norm-based scalarizations, extends to all inner-product norms on the objective space. By exploiting the linear isometric structure induced by positive definite matrices, we prove that the norm-minimization–based outer approximation algorithm is invariant under appropriate coordinate transformations, allowing the Euclidean convergence analysis to be transferred verbatim to arbitrary inner-product geometries.

(ii)

(Section
3
, Theorem
5
) We derive explicit convergence bounds in the original coordinates, in which the constants depend on the spectral properties—the extreme eigenvalues
\lambda_{\min}(M),\lambda_{\max}(M)
, equivalently the condition number
\kappa(M)
—of the underlying inner product and on geometric characteristics of the transformed upper image. This clarifies the precise role played by the choice of norm in the convergence behavior of the algorithm.

(iii)

(Section
5
, Theorems
9
and
11
) We analyze an adaptive-metric variant in which the inner product used in the scalarization varies across iterations while the approximation error is evaluated in a fixed Euclidean norm. Under uniform spectral bounds on the adaptive metrics, we establish correctness and convergence to the slice in the fixed Euclidean norm, and obtain explicit bounds showing how improved metric conditioning leads to sharper convergence constants.

(iv)

(Section
5
, Theorem
16
) We establish a
dispersion theorem
providing sufficient geometric conditions under which the adaptive metrics remain well-conditioned throughout the algorithm. Specifically, we prove that when the image set has a strictly convex boundary with bounded curvature—a condition satisfied by problems with ball or ellipsoidal feasible sets and linear objectives—the cut-normals naturally spread across all directions, ensuring that the minimum eigenvalue of the empirical covariance remains bounded away from zero.

(v)

(Section
6
) We present numerical experiments on three test problems from the literature. The experiments validate the theoretical rate
O(k^{2/(1-q)})
for the Euclidean norm (Theorem
5
); on the two problems whose Pareto fronts have sufficient curvature, the adaptive metric also reduces the iteration count. The dispersion condition—positivity of the smallest eigenvalue of the empirical second-moment matrix
\Sigma_{k}
of the cut-normals—is verified empirically in all cases. For high-dimensional problems (
q\geq 4
), we compare vertex-finding strategies and provide practical recommendations.

Together, these results provide a rigorous foundation for the use of geometry-aware and adaptive inner-product metrics in norm-minimization–based convex vector optimization.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
provides the notation and problem setting. In Section
3
, we extend the improved convergence rate from the Euclidean norm to all inner-product norms. The adaptive-metric framework, in which the scalarization norm varies across iterations, is introduced in Section
4
. Section
5
develops the convergence analysis of this framework and proves the dispersion theorem. Numerical experiments on three test problems are reported in Section
6
. Finally, Section
7
concludes with open questions.

3
Inner-Product Norm Invariance and Improved Convergence Rate

The convergence theory of
[
3
]
is built on the Euclidean norm, whose improved rate
O(k^{2/(1-q)})
exploits its special geometry. The Euclidean norm, however, may not reflect the geometry of a given problem—for instance when the objectives have very different scales—so one would like to measure distances in a problem-adapted way. The inner-product norms
\|\cdot\|_{M}
(
M\succ 0
) provide this flexibility, and they are the metrics that the adaptive-metric framework of Section
4
later tunes. This section shows that the flexibility is free. Every inner-product norm achieves the same rate as the Euclidean one.

Recall from Section
2
the compact slice
A:=P\cap S(\gamma)
and its transform
\tilde{A}:=T(A)
. For a convex compact set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
, denote by

the radius of the smallest Euclidean ball containing
B
, by
r(B)
the radius of the largest Euclidean ball contained in
B
, and by

\omega(B):=\frac{R(B)}{r(B)}

its asphericity. Let
\pi_{q}
be the volume of the Euclidean unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, and define

\bar{\lambda}(B):=16\,R(B)\left(\frac{q\,\pi_{q}}{\pi_{q-1}}\right)^{\frac{2}{q-1}}.

The following is a direct consequence of the Euclidean convergence result in
[
3
, Thm. 7.2]
and Propositions
3
–
4
.

Theorem 5

(Improved convergence rate under inner-product norms)
.

Assume Assumption
1
, and let
\|\cdot\|_{M}
be an inner-product norm induced by
M\succ 0
. Let
(P_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence of outer approximations generated by the norm-minimization–based algorithm of
[
3
]
, where the scalarizations and Hausdorff distance are based on

.

Then the following statements hold.

(i)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists

such that for all

,

\delta_{H}(P_{k},P;\|\cdot\|_{M})\;\leq\;(1+\varepsilon)\,\bar{\lambda}(\tilde{A})\,k^{\frac{2}{1-q}},

where
\tilde{A}=T(A)=TP\cap T(S(\gamma))
.

(ii)

In particular,

\delta_{H}(P_{k},P;\|\cdot\|_{M})=O\bigl(k^{\frac{2}{1-q}}\bigr)\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Proof.

By Proposition
4
,
(\tilde{P}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
with
\tilde{P}_{k}:=TP_{k}
is the sequence of outer approximations generated by the Euclidean version of the algorithm applied to the transformed problem (

P
T

) using the Euclidean scalarization (

⁢

~

(

~

). By

, for every

there exists

Remark 1

(Role of the metric in convergence bounds)
.

Theorem
5
shows that the improved exponent

is not tied to the standard Euclidean structure but to the Hilbertian geometry of the norm. The choice of

enters only through the multiplicative constant: the theorem gives
\delta_{H}(P_{k},P;\|\cdot\|_{M})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\,\bar{\lambda}(\tilde{A})\,k^{2/(1-q)}
, where the constant

is unchanged.

4
Adaptive-Metric Framework

While Section
3
establishes that any fixed inner-product norm achieves the optimal convergence exponent, a natural question arises: can iteration-dependent metrics improve the
constant
in the convergence bound—the factor multiplying
k^{1/(1-q)}
(the constant
\lambda(r,A)
of Theorem
18
), rather than the exponent itself? In this section, we introduce an adaptive-metric framework in which the scalarization norm varies across iterations while the approximation error is measured in a fixed Euclidean norm.

4.1
Framework Design: Fixed Euclidean Accuracy

Throughout this section, the approximation quality is measured in the fixed Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{q}
. The algorithm employs an iteration-dependent inner product norm
\|\cdot\|_{M_{k}}
only inside the scalarization subproblems used to generate supporting halfspaces.

Let

be a closed convex set, and let

be a polyhedral outer approximation that has at least one vertex and the same recession cone,
\mathrm{rec}(P_{k})=\mathrm{rec}(P)
; this equal-recession-cone condition guarantees that
\delta_{H}(P_{k},P;\|\cdot\|_{2})
is finite and attained at a vertex of
P_{k}
. In the convex vector optimization application,
P=\Gamma(X)+C
is the (unbounded) upper image, with
\mathrm{rec}(P)=C
; equivalently, and as used in Theorems
11
and
18
, one may work with the compact slice

and its outer approximations

(for which
\mathrm{rec}=\{0\}
). The error analysis below is identical in both formulations.
We define the Euclidean outer Hausdorff error by

\mathrm{err}_{2}(P_{k},P):=\delta_{H}(P_{k},P;\|\cdot\|_{2})=\max_{v\in V(P_{k})}d_{2}(v,P),\qquad d_{2}(v,P):=\inf_{y\in P}\|v-y\|_{2},

where

denotes the vertex set of
P_{k}
. Assume that each iteration produces a valid supporting halfspace

H_{i}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{q}:\;w_{i}^{\top}y\geq\alpha_{i}\},\qquad w_{i}\in C^{+}\setminus\{0\},

that contains
P
(i.e.,

); here the offset
\alpha_{i}:=h_{P}(w_{i})=\inf_{y\in P}w_{i}^{\top}y
is the support value of

in direction
w_{i}
, attained at the boundary point
y_{i}
generated at iteration
i
(so that
H_{i}
supports
P
at
y_{i}
). Define the normalized cut-normals

u_{i}:=\frac{w_{i}}{\|w_{i}\|_{2}}.

Fix a regularization parameter
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and define, for

M_{k}:=\varepsilon_{0}I\;+\;\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top},\qquad\|d\|_{M_{k}}:=\sqrt{d^{\top}M_{k}d}.

Lemma 6

(Uniform spectral bounds and uniform norm equivalence)
.

For all
k\geq 1
,

\varepsilon_{0}I\preceq M_{k}\preceq(\varepsilon_{0}+1)I.

Consequently, for all
d\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and all
k\geq 1
,

\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}\,\|d\|_{2}\leq\|d\|_{M_{k}}\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}+1}\,\|d\|_{2},

and

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}+1}}\,\|d\|_{2}\leq\|d\|_{M_{k}^{-1}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}}\,\|d\|_{2}.

Proof.

Since
\|u_{i}\|_{2}=1
, we have
0\preceq u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}\preceq I
for all
i
.
Averaging preserves the Loewner order, hence

0\preceq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}\preceq I.

Adding
\varepsilon_{0}I
yields
\varepsilon_{0}I\preceq M_{k}\preceq(\varepsilon_{0}+1)I
.
The norm inequalities follow from the Rayleigh quotient bounds:

\lambda_{\min}(M_{k})\|d\|_{2}^{2}\leq d^{\top}M_{k}d\leq\lambda_{\max}(M_{k})\|d\|_{2}^{2}

and the corresponding inequalities for
M_{k}^{-1}
.
∎

Corollary 7

(Bridge from adaptive metric to Euclidean distance)
.

For any nonempty set

, any

, and any

d_{2}(v,S)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}}\,d_{M_{k}}(v,S),\qquad d_{M_{k}}(v,S):=\inf_{y\in S}\|v-y\|_{M_{k}}.

.

Proof.

By Lemma

,
\|v-y\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}}\|v-y\|_{M_{k}}
for all

.
Taking the infimum over

establishes the claim.
∎

(\mathrm{P}_{M_{k}}(v))
and
\|z\|_{M_{k}}=\|y-v\|_{M_{k}}
.
Optimality of

∎

5
Convergence Analysis

This section establishes the convergence results for the adaptive-metric algorithm. We first prove baseline correctness (Theorem
9

O(k^{1/(1-q)})

Theorem 9

(Baseline correctness (fixed Euclidean accuracy on the slice))

Assume Assumption

(iii)

(Sufficient computable stopping test via the adaptive metric)
Assume additionally that the adaptive metrics satisfy the uniform spectral bounds

(i) By assumption,

∎

(The generated cut removes the selected vertex)

Fix

Theorem 11

(Euclidean convergence under adaptive metrics)

Let

Assume each iteration

Assume further that the scalarizations
(\mathrm{P}_{M_{k}}(v))
are solved exactly—each returning a displacement

mI\preceq M_{k}\preceq MI\qquad\forall k\geq 0

(4)

for some constants

At each iteration, select

where

Then

\delta_{H}(\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{\mathrm{out}},A;\|\cdot\|_{2})\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Equivalently,

\max_{v\in\text{ext}(\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{\mathrm{out}})}d_{2}(v,A)\to 0.

Proof.

Throughout this proof,
\|\cdot\|
(without subscript) denotes the Euclidean norm

and

, and the sequence is nested and decreasing. For any vertex

Let

over vertices,

When

. By Lemma

5.1
Dispersion and Metric Quality

We now introduce a condition quantifying how well the accumulated cut-normals spread across all directions, and show how it improves the quality of the resulting vertex selection—made precise below by the metric-quality parameter
\theta_{k}
(Definition
2
).

Definition 1

(Dispersion)
.

Let
(u_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, let
\Sigma_{k}:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}
denote their empirical second-moment matrix, and let
\eta\in(0,1]
. We say that the sequence
exhibits dispersion with constant
\eta
if there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{k})\geq\eta
for all
k\geq k_{0}
.

Dispersion captures the idea that the unit vectors
u_{i}
do not concentrate in any lower-dimensional subspace. Indeed, since
d^{\top}\Sigma_{k}d=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle u_{i},d\rangle^{2}
, the condition
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{k})\geq\eta
means that every direction
d\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}
receives at least an
\eta
-share of the accumulated cut-normals. As a degenerate counterexample, if all
u_{i}
lay in a single hyperplane
\{d^{\star}\}^{\perp}
, then
\Sigma_{k}d^{\star}=0
and
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{k})=0
, so dispersion would fail.

Assumption 2

(Dispersion of normal vectors)
.

The sequence
(u_{i})_{i\geq 1}
of normalized cut-normals generated by the algorithm exhibits dispersion with some constant
\eta\in(0,1]
, in the sense of Definition
1
.

Definition 2

(Metric quality parameter)
.

For
M_{k}\succ 0
, define the
metric quality parameter

\theta_{k}:=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\min}(M_{k})}{\lambda_{\max}(M_{k})}}\in(0,1].

Note that
\theta_{k}=1/\kappa(M_{k})^{1/2}
, where
\kappa(M_{k})
is the condition number of
M_{k}
. Thus
\theta_{k}\in(0,1]
quantifies the conditioning of the metric: values close to
1
indicate a well-conditioned, near-Euclidean
M_{k}
, while small
\theta_{k}
reflects a large condition number and hence a larger constant in the convergence bounds (as for the fixed-norm case discussed after Theorem
5
).

Proposition 12

(Dispersion yields an explicit constant improvement)
.

Let
M_{k}=\varepsilon_{0}I+\Sigma_{k}
with
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. If Assumption
2
holds, then for all
k\geq k_{0}
,

(\varepsilon_{0}+\eta)I\preceq M_{k}\preceq(\varepsilon_{0}+1)I,\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad\theta_{k}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\eta}{\varepsilon_{0}+1}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
6
,
\varepsilon_{0}I\preceq M_{k}\preceq(\varepsilon_{0}+1)I
. By Assumption
2
,
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{k})\geq\eta
for all
k\geq k_{0}
, so
M_{k}=\varepsilon_{0}I+\Sigma_{k}\succeq(\varepsilon_{0}+\eta)I
. The bound on
\theta_{k}
then follows from its definition.
∎

Lemma 13

(Selection quality in Euclidean distance)
.

Let
A
be compact convex and let
M_{k}\succ 0
. For any

,

\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(M_{k})}\,d_{2}(v,A)\ \leq\ d_{M_{k}}(v,A)\ \leq\ \sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(M_{k})}\,d_{2}(v,A).

If
v^{k}\in\arg\max_{v}d_{M_{k}}(v,A)

,

The first inequalities follow from Rayleigh quotient bounds:
\lambda_{\min}(M_{k})\|d\|_{2}^{2}\leq d^{\top}M_{k}d\leq\lambda_{\max}(M_{k})\|d\|_{2}^{2}

Taking infimum over

maximizes

Assumption
2
postulates that
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{k})\geq\eta>0
, which by Definition

is precisely the statement that the cut-normals are dispersed. A natural question arises:
under what geometric conditions on the problem does this dispersion hold?
In this subsection, we establish, in Theorem

, that dispersion is guaranteed whenever the image set has a strictly convex boundary with bounded curvature, as is the case, for example, for ball or ellipsoidal feasible sets with linear objectives.

We work with the compact slice
A:=P\cap S(\gamma)
of the upper image. The part of the boundary relevant to the algorithm is the efficient frontier
\partial_{\mathrm{eff}}A
introduced in Section

that is not dominated by other points in

Assumption 3

(Strictly convex image set with bounded curvature)
.

The efficient frontier
\partial_{\mathrm{eff}}A
of the slice

satisfies:

(i)

\partial_{\mathrm{eff}}A

;

(ii)

All principal curvatures

(iii)

The

\kappa_{\mathrm{ratio}}\leq(a_{q}/a_{1})^{2}

We now introduce the geometric notions used in the proof of Theorem

Definition 3

For

Definition 4

(Spherical geometry)

(See, e.g.,

(i)

The following lemma is a spherical covering estimate. Given finitely many directions on the sphere, it guarantees the existence of a direction that is angularly far from all of them.

Lemma 14

(Spherical cap covering)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{q-1}
be a measurable region with positive surface measure
\sigma(\mathcal{R})>0

w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}

d^{*}\in\mathcal{R}
such that

\min_{i=1,\ldots,n}\angle(d^{*},w_{i})\geq c_{q}\left(\frac{\sigma(\mathcal{R})}{n}\right)^{1/(q-1)},

where
c_{q}>0
is a constant depending only on
q
.

Proof.

For any
\theta>0
, since
\sin\varphi\leq\varphi
for
\varphi\geq 0
, the cap measure satisfies

\sigma(\mathrm{Cap}(w,\theta))=\omega_{q-2}\!\int_{0}^{\theta}\!\sin^{q-2}\!\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\varphi\;\leq\;\omega_{q-2}\!\int_{0}^{\theta}\!\varphi^{q-2}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi=\frac{\omega_{q-2}}{q-1}\,\theta^{q-1}=:c^{\prime}_{q}\,\theta^{q-1}.

Now let
\theta>0
and suppose every

satisfies
\min_{i}\angle(d,w_{i})<\theta
. Then
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Cap}(w_{i},\theta)
, so by subadditivity,

\sigma(\mathcal{R})\leq n\,c^{\prime}_{q}\,\theta^{q-1},\qquad\text{i.e.,}\qquad\theta\geq\bigl(\sigma(\mathcal{R})/(n\,c^{\prime}_{q})\bigr)^{1/(q-1)}.

By contrapositive, for any
\theta<\bigl(\sigma(\mathcal{R})/(n\,c^{\prime}_{q})\bigr)^{1/(q-1)}
there exists
d_{\theta}\in\mathcal{R}
with
\min_{i}\angle(d_{\theta},w_{i})\geq\theta
. Since the
w_{i}
are finitely many, the set
\{d\in\mathcal{R}:\min_{i}\angle(d,w_{i})\geq\theta\}
is closed in
\mathbb{S}^{q-1}
and nonempty for every such
\theta
; these sets are nested and contained in the compact sphere, so their intersection is nonempty. Hence there exists
d^{*}\in\mathcal{R}
with
\min_{i}\angle(d^{*},w_{i})\geq c_{q}\,(\sigma(\mathcal{R})/n)^{1/(q-1)}
, where
c_{q}:=(c^{\prime}_{q})^{-1/(q-1)}
.
∎

The next lemma provides a quantitative lower bound on the extent
\xi_{k}(d)
(the support-function gap defined above) in directions
d
that are angularly separated from all cut-normals. The key observation is that, under Assumption
3
, the slice
A
contains an
osculating ball
—the ball of radius
1/\kappa_{\max}
internally tangent to
\partial_{\mathrm{eff}}A
—at each boundary point, and this osculating-ball geometry controls how effectively a cut can reduce the extent.

Lemma 15

(Extent lower bound)
.

Under Assumption
3
, let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
be halfspaces containing
A
, with outer normals
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}
. For any
d\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}
and
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
with
\min_{i=1,\ldots,k}\angle(d,w_{i})\geq\theta
, it holds that

h^{+}_{\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}}(d)\geq h^{+}_{A}(d)+\frac{1}{\kappa_{\max}}\left(\frac{1}{\cos\theta}-1\right).

In particular, for
\theta\leq\pi/4
we have
1/\cos\theta-1\geq\theta^{2}/2
, giving
h^{+}_{\bigcap H_{i}}(d)\geq h^{+}_{A}(d)+\theta^{2}/(2\kappa_{\max})
.

Proof.

Let
p(d)\in\partial_{\mathrm{eff}}A
denote the point of the efficient frontier with outer unit normal
d
. A maximizer of
\langle d,\cdot\rangle
over
A
lies on
\partial_{\mathrm{eff}}A
(rather than on the slice cap
\operatorname{bd}S(\gamma)
) when
d
belongs to the normal cone of the efficient frontier; in particular,
d\in-\operatorname{int}C^{+}
suffices, since then the maximizer is a weakly
C
-minimal point. For such
d
, the strict convexity of
\partial_{\mathrm{eff}}A
(Assumption
3
) makes
p(d)
unique. Since all principal curvatures at
p(d)
are at most
\kappa_{\max}
by Assumption
3
(iii), the minimum principal radius of curvature at
p(d)
is at least
1/\kappa_{\max}
. Consequently,
A
contains the osculating ball

B_{d}:=\overline{B}\!\left(p(d)-\frac{d}{\kappa_{\max}},\;\frac{1}{\kappa_{\max}}\right)\subseteq A.

Write
H_{i}=\{y:\langle w_{i},y\rangle\leq b_{i}\}
with
b_{i}\geq h^{+}_{A}(w_{i})
(since
A\subseteq H_{i}
). In particular,
B_{d}\subseteq A\subseteq H_{i}
. Writing
\alpha_{i}:=\angle(w_{i},d)
, the support function of
B_{d}
gives

b_{i}\geq h^{+}_{A}(w_{i})\geq h^{+}_{B_{d}}(w_{i})=\left\langle w_{i},\,p(d)-\frac{d}{\kappa_{\max}}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{\kappa_{\max}}=\langle w_{i},p(d)\rangle+\frac{1-\cos\alpha_{i}}{\kappa_{\max}}.

(5)

Consider the ray
\{p(d)+td:t\geq 0\}
. For
t>0
, the point
p(d)+td
lies in
H_{i}
if and only if

t\cos\alpha_{i}\leq b_{i}-\langle w_{i},p(d)\rangle.

When
\cos\alpha_{i}\leq 0
, this holds for all
t\geq 0
. When
\cos\alpha_{i}>0
, the constraint becomes
t\leq(b_{i}-\langle w_{i},p(d)\rangle)/\cos\alpha_{i}
, and inequality (
5
) gives

\frac{b_{i}-\langle w_{i},p(d)\rangle}{\cos\alpha_{i}}\geq\frac{1-\cos\alpha_{i}}{\kappa_{\max}\cos\alpha_{i}}=\frac{1}{\kappa_{\max}}\!\left(\frac{1}{\cos\alpha_{i}}-1\right).

Since
\alpha\mapsto 1/\cos\alpha-1
is increasing on
[0,\pi/2)
and
\alpha_{i}\geq\theta
, each bound is at least
(1/\cos\theta-1)/\kappa_{\max}
. Thus
p(d)+td\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}
for all
t\leq(1/\cos\theta-1)/\kappa_{\max}
, giving

h^{+}_{\bigcap H_{i}}(d)\geq\langle d,\,p(d)+td\rangle=h^{+}_{A}(d)+t\geq h^{+}_{A}(d)+\frac{1}{\kappa_{\max}}\!\left(\frac{1}{\cos\theta}-1\right).\qed

Theorem 16

(Dispersion under strict convexity)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
3
, suppose the CVOP algorithm achieves the Hausdorff convergence rate

h_{k}:=\delta_{H}(\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{\mathrm{out}},A)\leq Ck^{-2/(q-1)}

(6)

for some constant
C>0
. Then the cut-normals satisfy

\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{k})\geq\eta>0\quad\text{for all }k\geq k_{0},

where
\eta=\eta(C,\kappa_{\max},q)>0
and
k_{0}
depends only on the problem data. Consequently, Assumption
2
holds with this
\eta
.

Proof.

Let
L:=\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{k})
and let
d_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{q-1}
be a corresponding minimum eigenvector, so that
C_{k}(d_{k})=L
. We derive a lower bound
L\geq\eta>0
.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle w_{i},d_{k}\rangle^{2}=kL
and each cut with
\angle(w_{i},d_{k})\leq\pi/4
contributes at least
\cos^{2}(\pi/4)=1/2
to the sum, the number of such cuts satisfies
N_{A}\leq 2kL
(Markov’s inequality). We refer to these as
Group A
; the remaining cuts, with
\angle(w_{i},d_{k})>\pi/4
, form
Group B
.

The outer approximation is
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}=\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}
, where
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
has
m
defining halfspaces (a fixed number depending only on the initialization). All
m+k
halfspaces contain
A
. We apply a covering argument to this full set. Let
n_{1},\ldots,n_{m}
denote the outer normals of the initial halfspaces. Consider the spherical cap
\mathcal{C}:=\mathrm{Cap}(d_{k},\pi/8)
, which has surface measure
\sigma(\mathcal{C})=\Omega_{q}>0
depending only on
q
. For any
d^{*}\in\mathcal{C}
and any cut
w_{i}
in Group B, the geodesic triangle inequality gives

\angle(d^{*},w_{i})\geq\angle(w_{i},d_{k})-\angle(d^{*},d_{k})>\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{8}=\frac{\pi}{8}.

Thus every cut in Group B is at angle greater than
\pi/8
from every point in
\mathcal{C}
. Applying Lemma
14
to
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{C}
with the
N_{A}+m
normals from Group A and
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{0}^{\mathrm{out}}
both, there exists
d^{*}\in\mathcal{C}
such that

\min\!\Big(\min_{w_{i}\in\text{Gp.\,A}}\angle(d^{*},w_{i}),\;\min_{j=1,\ldots,m}\angle(d^{*},n_{j})\Big)\geq c_{q}\left(\frac{\Omega_{q}}{2kL+m}\right)^{\!1/(q-1)}=:\theta^{*}.

For
k
sufficiently large,
\theta^{*}<\pi/8
, so the Group B bound
\pi/8
is not binding and
\min_{\text{all normals}}\angle(d^{*},\cdot)\geq\theta^{*}
.

Since all
m+k
halfspaces defining
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}
contain
A
and every normal is at angle
\geq\theta^{*}
from
d^{*}
, Lemma
15
applies directly to
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}
:

\xi_{k}(d^{*})=h^{+}_{\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{k}^{\mathrm{out}}}(d^{*})-h^{+}_{A}(d^{*})\geq\frac{(\theta^{*})^{2}}{2\kappa_{\max}}.

Since
h_{k}\geq\xi_{k}(d^{*})
and
2kL+m\leq 3kL
for
k\geq m/L
, we obtain

h_{k}\geq\frac{c_{q}^{2}}{2\kappa_{\max}}\left(\frac{\Omega_{q}}{3kL}\right)^{\!2/(q-1)}=c^{\prime}(kL)^{-2/(q-1)}

for a constant
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(\kappa_{\max},q)>0
. Combining with the upper bound (
6
):

c^{\prime}(kL)^{-2/(q-1)}\leq Ck^{-2/(q-1)},

which simplifies to
c^{\prime}L^{-2/(q-1)}\leq C
, giving
L\geq(c^{\prime}/C)^{(q-1)/2}=:\eta>0
. Since this holds for all sufficiently large
k
, we conclude
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{k})\geq\eta
for
k\geq k_{0}
.
∎

Corollary 17

(Metric quality under strict convexity)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
16
, the adaptive metric
M_{k}=\varepsilon_{0}I+\Sigma_{k}
satisfies

\theta_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\min}(M_{k})}{\lambda_{\max}(M_{k})}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\eta}{\varepsilon_{0}+1}}=:\theta_{*}>0.

Remark 5

(Special cases)
.

For a ball
A=\overline{B}(e,R)
, we have
\kappa_{\max}=1/R
and the bound gives
\eta=\eta(R,q,C)>0
. More generally, for an ellipsoid with semi-axes
a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{q}
, one has
\kappa_{\max}=a_{q}/a_{1}^{2}
and the theorem applies directly. More generally, the argument extends to any body whose efficient boundary is piecewise
C^{2}
with strictly positive principal curvatures, by applying Lemma
15
on each
C^{2}
piece with its local curvature bound.

Assumption
3
holds for several structured classes of problems, as the following examples illustrate.

Example 1

(Ball feasible set with linear objective)
.

Let
X=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|x-x_{0}\|_{2}\leq r\}
be a ball and
\Gamma(x)=Mx+b
be affine. Then:

(i)

If
M=I
(identity), then
A=X
is a ball with
\kappa_{\mathrm{ratio}}=1
.

(ii)

If
M
is invertible, then
A=M(X)+b
is an ellipsoid with
\kappa_{\mathrm{ratio}}\leq\kappa(M^{\top}M)
.

In both cases, Assumption
3
holds.

Example 2

(Strongly convex feasible set)
.

Let
X=\{x:g(x)\leq 0\}
where
g
is strongly convex with
\nabla^{2}g(x)\succeq\mu I
for all
x
. If
\Gamma(x)=x
, then
\partial_{\mathrm{eff}}A=\partial_{\mathrm{eff}}X
has principal curvatures bounded below by
\mu
, so Assumption
3
holds.

The dispersion condition can be verified empirically by tracking
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{k})
during algorithm execution; see Section
6
for numerical validation on test problems from the literature.

5.2
Convergence Rate

We now establish a convergence rate by connecting the adaptive-metric algorithm to the
H(r,A)
-sequence framework. We first recall the relevant definition from
[
13
,
21
]
.

Definition 5

(
H(r,A)
-sequence of cutting)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
be a nonempty convex compact set and let
r>0
. A sequence
(A_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
is called an
H(r,A)
-sequence of cutting
if:

(i)

A_{0}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{I}\mathcal{H}(\omega_{i},A)
for some finite collection
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\{0\}
;

(ii)

A_{k}\supseteq A
for all
k\geq 0
;

(iii)

A_{k+1}=A_{k}\cap\mathcal{H}(w_{k},A)
for some
w_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\{0\}
;

(iv)

\delta_{H}(A_{k},A_{k+1})\geq r\cdot\delta_{H}(A_{k},A)
for all
k\geq 0
.

The first condition ensures that
A_{0}
is a polytope containing
A
, while the fourth condition ensures that each cut removes at least a fraction
r
of the current approximation error. For such sequences, the general rate theorem
[
13
,
21
]
establishes that
\delta_{H}(A_{k},A)=O(k^{1/(1-q)})
.

Theorem 18

(Convergence rate of the adaptive-metric algorithm)
.

Let
A:=P\cap S(\gamma)\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
be nonempty, convex, and compact.
Assume that:

(i)

A\subseteq A_{k+1}\subseteq A_{k}
, where
A_{k+1}=A_{k}\cap H_{k}
and each
A_{k}
is a polytope;

(ii)

the adaptive metrics satisfy
M_{k}=\varepsilon_{0}I+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}
with
mI\preceq M_{k}\preceq MI
for some constants
0<m\leq M<\infty
(by Lemma
6
one may take
m=\varepsilon_{0}
and
M=\varepsilon_{0}+1
, so this holds automatically; see Remark
6
);

(iii)

scalarizations are solved exactly and return certificates
\|z^{v}\|_{M_{k}}=d_{M_{k}}(v,A)
;

(iv)

the vertex selection is
v^{k}\in\arg\max_{v\in\operatorname{ext}(A_{k})}\|z^{v}\|_{M_{k}}
;

(v)

the cut halfspace
H_{k}
satisfies
A\subseteq H_{k}
and
v^{k}\notin H_{k}
whenever
v^{k}\notin A
.

Define the Euclidean Hausdorff error
E_{k}:=\delta_{H}(A_{k},A;\|\cdot\|_{2})
and let

\bar{\theta}:=\sup_{k}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(M_{k})}{\lambda_{\min}(M_{k})}}=\frac{1}{\inf_{k}\theta_{k}}<\infty.

Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exist
K\in\mathbb{N}
and a constant
\lambda(r,A)
, depending only on
A
and
r=1/\bar{\theta}^{3}
, such that for all
k\geq K
,

E_{k}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\,\lambda\!\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\theta}^{3}},A\right)\,k^{\frac{1}{1-q}}.

Proof.

Throughout this proof,
\|\cdot\|
(without subscript) denotes the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. From the proof of Theorem
11
, the selection rule ensures

d_{2}(v^{k},A)\geq\theta_{k}E_{k}\geq\frac{1}{\bar{\theta}}E_{k}.

When
E_{k}>0
, Lemma
10
gives
v^{k}\notin H_{k}
while
A\subseteq H_{k}
, where
H_{k}=\{y:(g^{k})^{\top}y\geq(g^{k})^{\top}y^{k}\}
with
g^{k}=M_{k}z^{k}/\|z^{k}\|_{M_{k}}
. Since
A_{k+1}=A_{k}\cap H_{k}\subseteq H_{k}
, the signed distance from
v^{k}
to
H_{k}
gives

d_{2}(v^{k},A_{k+1})\;\geq\;d_{2}(v^{k},H_{k})=\frac{(g^{k})^{\top}z^{k}}{\|g^{k}\|}=\frac{\|z^{k}\|_{M_{k}}^{2}}{\|M_{k}z^{k}\|}.

The spectral bounds (Lemma
6
)
\|z^{k}\|_{M_{k}}^{2}\geq\lambda_{\min}(M_{k})\,\|z^{k}\|^{2}
and
\|M_{k}z^{k}\|\leq\lambda_{\max}(M_{k})\,\|z^{k}\|
yield

\frac{\|z^{k}\|_{M_{k}}^{2}}{\|M_{k}z^{k}\|}\;\geq\;\frac{\lambda_{\min}(M_{k})}{\lambda_{\max}(M_{k})}\,\|z^{k}\|=\theta_{k}^{2}\,\|z^{k}\|.

Since
y^{k}=v^{k}+z^{k}\in A
, we have
\|z^{k}\|\geq d_{2}(v^{k},A)\geq(1/\bar{\theta})\,E_{k}
. Combining,

\delta_{H}(A_{k},A_{k+1})\;\geq\;d_{2}(v^{k},A_{k+1})\;\geq\;\frac{1}{\bar{\theta}^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\bar{\theta}}\,E_{k}=\frac{1}{\bar{\theta}^{3}}\,E_{k}.

Thus
(A_{k})
is an
H(r,A)
-sequence of cutting with
r=1/\bar{\theta}^{3}>0
.
By the general rate theorem for
H(r,A)
-sequences
[
13
,
21
]
, the stated bound on
\delta_{H}(A_{k},A)=E_{k}
holds for all sufficiently large
k
.
∎

Remark 6

(Positivity of the cutting ratio and interpretation of the rate)
.

The condition
\bar{\theta}<\infty
in Theorem
18
is automatically satisfied by the
\varepsilon_{0}
-regularized metric and need not be assumed separately. By Lemma
6
,
\varepsilon_{0}I\preceq M_{k}\preceq(\varepsilon_{0}+1)I
for all
k
, so
\theta_{k}\geq\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}/(\varepsilon_{0}+1)}
and

\bar{\theta}=\frac{1}{\inf_{k}\theta_{k}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+1}{\varepsilon_{0}}}<\infty,\qquad r=\frac{1}{\bar{\theta}^{3}}\ \geq\ \Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon_{0}+1}\Bigr)^{3/2}>0,

for any
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and with no further hypotheses; this settles the positivity of the cutting ratio. Dispersion does not enlarge this worst-case ratio—which is governed by the early, possibly ill-conditioned iterates—but it sharpens the
asymptotic
conditioning that controls the constant. Under Assumption
3
, Theorem
16
and Corollary
17
give
\theta_{k}\geq\theta_{*}=\sqrt{(\varepsilon_{0}+\eta)/(\varepsilon_{0}+1)}
for all
k\geq k_{0}
; the tail
(A_{k})_{k\geq k_{0}}
is then an
H(r^{\prime},A)
-sequence with
r^{\prime}\geq\bigl((\varepsilon_{0}+\eta)/(\varepsilon_{0}+1)\bigr)^{3/2}
, strictly larger than the unconditional bound
(\varepsilon_{0}/(\varepsilon_{0}+1))^{3/2}
. Hence a larger dispersion constant
\eta
yields a better asymptotic rate constant
\lambda(r^{\prime},A)
. The improved Euclidean exponent
2/(1-q)
of
[
3
]
requires additional geometric conditions beyond the
H(r,A)
-sequence framework and is not implied here.

\delta_{H}^{k}=Ck^{2/(1-q)}
only for
k
sufficiently large (depending on the constant
C
and the geometry of
A
), this observation is fully compatible with the theoretical guarantee. To confirm this interpretation, we re-tested Example 2 with
\varepsilon=0.005
(four times smaller), obtaining 338 Euclidean iterations with a fitted slope of
-1.32
—now exceeding the theoretical
-1.0
, consistent with the pre-asymptotic explanation.

For the adaptive metric, only the weaker rate
O(k^{1/(1-q)})
is established by Theorem
18
. Whether adaptive metrics achieve the improved exponent
2/(1-q)
remains an open question (see Section
7
). The empirical slopes are variable: in some problems the adaptive slope is steeper than the Euclidean slope (Example 1 with
q=3
and Example 3), while in others it is shallower (Example 1 with
q=2
and Example 2). This variability, combined with the pre-asymptotic effects described above, means that the fitted slopes do not provide conclusive evidence for or against the improved adaptive exponent. In contrast, the iteration counts are a robust and unambiguous metric: adaptive consistently converges in fewer iterations whenever the problem affords sufficient curvature.

Finally, we note that the Hausdorff error
\delta_{H}^{k}
need not decrease monotonically in
k
: cutting the current farthest vertex can create new vertices of the outer approximation that are temporarily farther from
A
, so the per-iteration maximum vertex distance may rise before the error resumes its decay. This accounts for the small upward spikes visible in Figures
1
and
3
; only convergence to zero is guaranteed (Theorem
11
).

6.5
Vertex-Finding Strategies for High-Dimensional Problems

The outer approximation algorithm requires enumerating all vertices of
A_{k}
at each iteration to select the farthest vertex
v_{k}
. This vertex enumeration has complexity
O(m^{\lfloor q/2\rfloor})
where
m
is the number of halfspace constraints, which grows prohibitively for
q\geq 4
. We develop two approximate strategies that bypass full enumeration.

The LP probe strategy finds candidate vertices by solving
\max\{w_{i}^{\top}y:By\leq b\}
—where
By\leq b
is the halfspace representation of the current outer approximation
A_{k}
—for a set of probe directions
\mathcal{W}
, initialized with
\{\pm e_{j},\pm\bar{w}\}
and augmented by each cut-normal
w_{k}
. This avoids enumeration entirely but may miss vertices that are not maximizers in any probe direction. The hybrid strategy combines LP probes with periodic full enumeration every
N
iterations (we use
N=50
), balancing efficiency with completeness: LP probe iterations are fast, while periodic enumeration discovers any missed vertices.

Convergence is preserved under mild conditions: whenever
E_{k}>0
, at least one vertex
v\in\operatorname{ext}(A_{k})\setminus A
must be found, and the selected vertex must satisfy
d_{2}(v_{k},A)\geq c\cdot E_{k}
for some
c>0
(for full enumeration,
c=1/\bar{\theta}
as in the proof of Theorem
18
; for the probe-based strategies
c
is smaller and depends on the probe set
\mathcal{W}
). This yields an
H(c,A)
-sequence, preserving the
O(k^{1/(1-q)})
rate from Theorem
18
.

6.6
Practical Recommendations

Based on the experiments, we offer the following comments. Adaptive metrics are recommended for problems with curved Pareto fronts: on Examples 1 and 2 they reduced the iteration count by
31
–
33\%
relative to the fixed Euclidean norm. Since the per-iteration cost of updating
M_{k}
is negligible relative to solving the scalarization subproblem, such iteration savings translate directly into wall-clock savings. The exception is problems converging in very few iterations (Example 3, with
27
iterations), where insufficient data limits the adaptive metric’s effectiveness.

For vertex-finding, full enumeration is tractable and should be preferred when
q\leq 3
. For
q\geq 4
, the hybrid strategy with periodic enumeration (every
50
iterations) offers a good balance between speed and vertex detection. Pure LP probing is viable when enumeration is infeasible, though it may miss vertices in early iterations.

The parameter
\theta_{k}
serves as a useful real-time diagnostic of metric conditioning. In all experiments,
\theta_{k}
stabilized in
[0.4,0.6]
. If
\theta_{k}
drops below
0.3
, a larger regularization parameter
\varepsilon_{0}
improves stability at the cost of slower adaptation. Our default
\varepsilon_{0}=0.1
performed well across all problems.

7
Conclusion

We have established that the improved
O(k^{2/(1-q)})
convergence rate for norm-minimization–based outer approximation extends from the Euclidean norm to all inner-product norms via a linear isometry. The adaptive-metric framework, in which the scalarization norm varies across iterations, achieves convergence in the fixed Euclidean norm under uniform spectral bounds, with the constant
\lambda(r,A)
of the
O(k^{1/(1-q)})
bound depending on the dispersion of the cut-normals. The dispersion theorem provides sufficient geometric conditions—a strictly convex boundary with bounded curvature—under which the adaptive metrics remain well-conditioned throughout execution. Numerical experiments confirm the theoretical rate and show that, on the two problems whose Pareto fronts have sufficient curvature, adaptive metrics reduce the iteration count. From a conceptual standpoint, our results clarify the geometric nature of the improved exponent
2/(1-q)
, which is not tied to the standard Euclidean structure but to the Hilbertian geometry of the norm; the role of the specific metric is confined to the constant
\bar{\lambda}(\tilde{A})
of the
O(k^{2/(1-q)})
bound, which reflects the condition number
\kappa(M)
and the circumradius of the transformed slice
\tilde{A}
. The dispersion theorem (Theorem
16
) links problem geometry to algorithm behavior: the curvature of the efficient frontier determines whether the adaptive metric stays well-conditioned. The underlying mechanism is self-limiting—if the cut-normals left some direction
d
poorly covered, then, by the osculating-ball bound of Lemma
15
, the outer approximation would retain a large extent in the direction
d
, contradicting the established convergence rate; this is what forces the eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{k}
to stay bounded away from zero.

7.1
Open Questions

Several directions for further research remain open. The major open problem is whether the Euclidean exponent
2/(1-q)
can be recovered under adaptive metrics. Theorem
18
establishes only the generic
H(r,A)
-sequence rate
O(k^{1/(1-q)})
, and our experiments—while showing a consistent reduction in iteration count—do not provide conclusive evidence for the improved exponent, owing to pre-asymptotic effects. Resolving this would require new packing estimates for varying ellipsoids, since the isometry argument fails when
M_{k}
changes at each iteration.

More broadly, it would be natural to extend the analysis beyond inner-product norms. The uniform convexity of the
\ell_{p}
-norms for
1<p<\infty
suggests that exponents strictly larger than
1/(1-q)
might be attainable, with constants depending on
p
through the modulus of convexity.

Another direction concerns the dispersion bound itself. Theorem
16
establishes
\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{k})\geq\eta>0
without characterizing
\eta
explicitly; our experiments suggest
\eta\approx 0.15
across dimensions
q\in\{2,3,4\}
, pointing towards possible dimension-independent behavior. A deeper analysis of the equilibrium distribution of cut-normals could yield explicit formulas in terms of problem geometry.

Finally, the strict convexity assumption (Assumption
3
) and the reliance on full vertex enumeration both limit the scope of the current theory. Extending the dispersion analysis to non-smooth boundaries, and establishing precise convergence rates for the approximate vertex-finding strategies of Section
6.5
, would broaden the practical applicability of the framework.
