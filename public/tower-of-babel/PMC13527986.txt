When managing the boss at a cost: perceived threat to hierarchy and supervisor ostracism
Authors: Xinyi Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Managing boss, a proactive behavior in which employees align their actions with supervisors’ priorities, has been widely recognized for its positive effects on employee performance and supervisor-subordinate relationships. However, little is known about its potential negative interpersonal consequences from the supervisor’s perspective. Drawing on social information processing theory and status-based perspectives on hierarchical threat, this study examines the conditional process through which managing boss (MB) relates to supervisor ostracism (SO). Using a three-wave, time-lagged survey of 452 supervisor-subordinate dyads in Chinese organizational contexts, the results indicated that MB was positively associated with supervisors’ perceived threat to hierarchy (PTH). However, the simple mediating effect of PTH was not supported. Instead, a second-stage moderated mediation effect emerged, such that the indirect effect of MB on SO via PTH was significant only when supervisors exhibited high power distance (PD). Specifically, PD moderated the relationship between PTH and SO, such that the positive association was stronger under high PD conditions. These findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the potential interpersonal costs of proactive employee behaviors by showing that managing boss is not uniformly interpreted as threatening, but may evoke defensive supervisory responses under hierarchy-sensitive conditions. The study also extends research on hierarchical dynamics, status threat, and supervisor-subordinate interactions, while offering practical implications for managing proactive employee behaviors in culturally and hierarchically sensitive organizational contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05051-x.

Introduction

In contemporary organizations, hierarchical authority structures continue to shape managerial practices and employee behaviors, particularly in contexts where supervisors exert substantial control over critical resources and career opportunities [1]. Employees’ career advancement is therefore closely tied to their supervisors, making it essential for employees to accurately understand and respond to supervisory expectations [2]. This dependence is further intensified in dynamic and uncertain work environments, where supervisors’ goals and demands frequently shift [3]. To remain effective, employees must continuously interpret these changing expectations and adapt their behaviors accordingly.

Such adaptive efforts are captured by the concept of managing boss, which refers to employees’ proactive attempts to understand supervisors’ goals, preferences, and working styles and to adjust their own behaviors to secure support and resources [4]. As a bottom-up and interactive behavior, managing boss has been shown to facilitate constructive supervisor-subordinate relationships and enhance individual effectiveness. Practitioners have long emphasized this practice, highlighting strategies such as understanding supervisors’ communication styles and behavioral patterns [5, 6], accurately identifying task priorities and work emphases [5, 7], and adopting supervisors’ perspectives when addressing work-related issues [7].

Although managing boss is defined as a subordinate-initiated, supervisor-focused relational behavior, it is conceptually distinct from several related constructs, including upward influence, leader-member exchange (LMX), impression management, ingratiation, and proactive followership. Unlike upward influence, which often involves deliberate attempts to shape supervisors’ decisions [8], managing boss emphasizes behavioral alignment with supervisors’ preferences and expectations. Furthermore, LMX reflects the quality of an ongoing dyadic relationship shaped by mutual exchanges [9], whereas managing boss highlights employees’ proactive efforts to understand and adapt to supervisors. In addition, impression management and ingratiation primarily aim to enhance personal image or gain favor [10, 11], while managing boss is more task-focused and oriented toward improving work alignment and effectiveness. Although proactive followership emphasizes employees’ initiative in supporting leaders and organizational goals [12], managing boss specifically underscores upward adaptation and strategic alignment with supervisors’ working styles and priorities. These distinctions suggest that managing boss represents a unique form of proactive relational behavior directed toward supervisors. Unlike upward voice, which involves expressing suggestions intended to influence supervisors’ decisions [13, 14], managing boss centers on adaptive alignment with supervisors’ priorities, work styles, and expectations rather than attempting to shape supervisory decisions.

Despite its practical relevance, academic research on managing boss remains limited [15]. Existing studies have primarily focused on its processes and positive outcomes for employees. For example, Welsh et al. (2019) [15] adopted a qualitative research approach and developed a process model of employees’ managing boss from the perspective of subordinates, while Gajendran et al. (2024) [4] demonstrated that managing boss enhances supervisors’ trust, support, and assistance, thereby improving employee performance. However, by emphasizing employee benefits, prior research has largely overlooked how managing boss affects supervisors themselves. This imbalance restricts a comprehensive understanding of managing boss as a relational phenomenon embedded within hierarchical workplace dynamics.

This omission is particularly consequential in light of growing evidence on supervisor ostracism. Supervisor ostracism - defined as supervisors’ intentional or unintentional neglect, exclusion, or rejection of subordinates - has emerged as a pervasive and damaging form of workplace mistreatment [16, 17]. Compared with peer exclusion, ostracism enacted by supervisors tends to exert more profound and enduring effects on subordinates due to supervisors’ formal authority and control over resources [18]. Survey evidence suggests that more than 75% of workplace mistreatment incidents originate from superiors, and workforce reports identify supervisory exclusion as a key driver of employee turnover. Unlike overt forms of destructive leadership, supervisor ostracism represents a subtle and covert form of interpersonal mistreatment characterized by invisibility and concealment, such as ignoring subordinates’ presence, dismissing their contributions, or withholding acknowledgment [16, 19, 20].

Although managing boss is generally associated with higher task performance and career success [4], supervisors’ reactions to such proactive behavior may be mixed rather than uniformly positive. On the one hand, managing boss can facilitate coordination, build trust, and improve supervisor-subordinate relationships [4]. On the other hand, because such behavior may increase subordinates’ visibility, signal close alignment with supervisory priorities, or create perceptions of informal influence, some supervisors may interpret these cues as indicating potential shifts in relational positioning or status boundaries [21]. Importantly, the present study does not assume that managing boss inherently threatens supervisors’ authority. Rather, consistent with social information processing theory, the argument is that supervisors may construct different interpretations from the same behavior depending on how they process these social cues. For individuals occupying high-status positions, preserving existing status often outweighs the pursuit of additional power, making them particularly attentive to cues associated with possible status erosion [22]. Thus, managing boss may generate both positive and negative relational consequences depending on supervisors’ interpretations of these proactive behaviors.

Importantly, supervisors’ initial reactions to being managed by subordinates are often psychological rather than behavioral. When subordinates’ proactive behaviors are interpreted as signaling closer proximity to supervisory authority or as potentially blurring established hierarchical distinctions, supervisors may experience a perceived threat to hierarchy - a cognitive appraisal that their authority or status could be challenged [23]. This perception represents a potential psychological mechanism that may motivate defensive responses, including ostracism. However, whether such perceived hierarchical threat translates into supervisor ostracism may depend on important contextual conditions and supervisors’ value orientations.

To address these gaps, this study examines the interpersonal consequences of managing boss from the supervisor’s perspective. Drawing on social information processing theory, this study investigates the relationship between managing boss and perceived threat to hierarchy and further examines whether perceived threat to hierarchy functions as a conditional indirect mechanism linking managing boss to supervisor ostracism. Furthermore, power distance is proposed as a key boundary condition shaping supervisors’ responses to perceived hierarchical threat. By integrating these elements, this study advances a more balanced and relational understanding of managing boss, elucidating when and why a behavior often viewed as constructive may also elicit adverse supervisory reactions.

Managing boss and perceived threat to hierarchy

Managing boss (MB) refers to employees’ proactive efforts to identify their supervisors’ priorities and working styles and to adjust their own tasks and behaviors accordingly [4]. This behavior is both future-oriented and adaptive, as it involves anticipating supervisors’ needs and strategically aligning work priorities and communication patterns with supervisory expectations. Prior research suggests that managing boss can strengthen supervisor-subordinate relationships and enhance subordinates’ performance [4].

However, emerging scholarship also indicates that managing boss may carry unintended interpersonal consequences. Importantly, managing boss is not inherently threatening and does not uniformly elicit negative supervisory reactions. Instead, it represents a multifaceted behavior that may generate both positive and negative interpretations depending on how supervisors process the cues embedded in such behavior.

Drawing on social information processing theory, supervisors rely on behavioral cues from subordinates to construct interpretations of relational and hierarchical dynamics. Managing boss behaviors - such as close alignment with supervisory priorities, anticipatory actions, and increased interaction frequency - may increase subordinates’ visibility. These behaviors may also signal greater proximity to supervisory decision processes and authority structures. Although such signals can be interpreted as supportive and cooperative, they may also, under certain conditions, be construed as indicating informal influence, privileged standing, or blurred hierarchical boundaries.

Perceived threat to hierarchy (PTH) refers to a superior’s concern that a subordinate’s behavior or attributes may undermine established status distinctions and weaken the foundations of vertical authority [26]. This concept is consistent with perspectives on hierarchy maintenance, which suggest that individuals occupying high-status positions are motivated to preserve clear status boundaries and protect their relative standing [27, 28]. When lower-status actors are perceived as encroaching upon these boundaries, such perceptions may activate concerns about status erosion and hierarchical instability.

Integrating these perspectives, this study does not argue that managing boss inherently produces threat perceptions. Rather, it proposes a probabilistic relationship: to the extent that supervisors interpret managing boss behaviors as signaling boundary blurring or status encroachment, such behaviors may increase perceived threat to hierarchy. Accordingly, managing boss is expected, on average, to be positively associated with perceived threat to hierarchy, while acknowledging that this relationship depends on supervisors’ interpretations of subordinate behavior. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 1

Managing boss is expected, on average, to be positively associated with perceived threat to hierarchy, contingent on supervisors’ interpretations of subordinate behavior.

Perceived threat to hierarchy and supervisor ostracism

Within organizational contexts, status represents a visible expression of power. As Khan et al. (2018) [26] noted, supervisor-subordinate relationships are inherently unequal. Supervisors possess greater power and control over resources, whereas subordinates hold less power and are more dependent on their supervisors. Positioned at higher hierarchical levels, supervisors not only control and allocate critical resources but also influence others through the use of rewards and sanctions [29]. Aquino and Douglas (2003) [30] revealed that supervisors occupying higher hierarchical positions are more sensitive to perceptions of threat to their status at work. This heightened sensitivity may stem from supervisors’ greater power and resources, as well as their increased attention to subordinate behaviors that could be perceived as challenging or encroaching upon their status.

Supervisor ostracism refers to a form of negative behavior directed toward subordinates who are perceived as threatening [31]. Prior research suggests that such behavior may emerge through at least two related mechanisms. First, in interpersonal interactions, supervisors may express negative attitudes by ignoring or excluding subordinates, thereby inducing subordinates’ feelings of marginalization and psychological distress. These behaviors reduce subordinates’ perceived status and value within the organization, fostering helplessness and burnout, which in turn diminish their work engagement and proactive behaviors, including managing boss [32]. Second, supervisor ostracism may extend beyond interpersonal neglect and involve restricting subordinates’ participation in core tasks or limiting their access to critical social resources, such as senior leaders and key clients [33]. Such exclusion can gradually undermine subordinates’ authority and standing within the team.

Compared with more overt forms of destructive leadership, including abusive supervision and workplace bullying, supervisor ostracism represents a subtler form of negative behavior characterized by high concealment [19]. This covert nature allows supervisors to maintain an image of legitimacy and authority while simultaneously distancing themselves from subordinates perceived as potentially challenging existing hierarchical distinctions. When supervisors perceive their hierarchical position or authority to be threatened, ostracism may therefore function as a defensive response aimed at preserving existing status boundaries and relational control. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 2

Perceived threat to hierarchy is positively related to supervisor ostracism.

The mediating role of perceived threat to hierarchy

Prior research indicates that high-status individuals and groups are particularly sensitive to behaviors by lower-status actors that blur hierarchical distinctions, and such behaviors are consistently interpreted as threatening to their established status [34]. In response, dominant parties are far more likely to enact defensive or exclusionary behaviors to safeguard their hierarchical position [34, 35]. Empirical evidence further corroborates that perceived threat to hierarchy frequently elicit derogation and social exclusion targeted at the perceived source of that threat [26, 34, 35].

In the organizational context of this study, hierarchical threat may emerge when supervisors interpret managing boss behaviors as signaling increased informal influence, privileged alignment, or blurred authority boundaries. As discussed earlier, managing boss behaviors can increase supervisors’ perceptions of threat to hierarchy to the extent that such behaviors are construed as symbolic status encroachment rather than purely supportive cooperation. In turn, supervisors who perceive heightened hierarchical threat may adopt ostracism as a subtle and defensible strategy to maintain status distinctions and reassert hierarchical stability [36]. Accordingly, perceived threat to hierarchy may serve as a potential psychological mechanism linking managing boss to supervisor ostracism. On this basis, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 3

Perceived threat to hierarchy may mediate the relationship between managing boss and supervisor ostracism.

The moderating role of power distance

Power distance (PD) refers to the extent to which supervisors accept and endorse unequal distributions of power within organizations [37]. Supervisors who differ in power distance orientation also differ in how they perceive authority relations at work. Those with high power distance tend to view hierarchical distinctions as legitimate and necessary for maintaining organizational order, whereas supervisors with low power distance favor more egalitarian relationships with subordinates [38]. Given these divergent orientations, supervisors’ power distance is expected to shape how they interpret and respond to perceived threat to hierarchy.

Supervisors with high power distance are more likely to perceive hierarchical threat as meaningful and may adopt defensive behaviors, such as ostracism, to maintain status distinctions and reinforce authority. In contrast, supervisors with low power distance are less sensitive to hierarchical cues and less likely to respond with ostracism even when they perceive potential threat. Therefore, the strength of the relationship between perceived threat to hierarchy and supervisor ostracism is likely to vary as a function of supervisors’ power distance orientation, with stronger associations expected under high PD conditions. The following hypothesis is hereby put forward:

Hypothesis 4

Power distance moderates the relationship between perceived threat to hierarchy and supervisor ostracism, such that the positive association is stronger when supervisors have high power distance than when they have low power distance.

The second-stage moderated mediation effect of power distance

Building on the proposed role of perceived threat to hierarchy, this study further argues that power distance conditions the strength of the indirect relationship between managing boss and supervisor ostracism. Specifically, power distance is theorized to moderate the second stage of the mediation pathway - that is, the relationship between perceived threat to hierarchy and supervisor ostracism.

Power distance reflects the degree to which individuals accept and endorse unequal power distributions and hierarchical structures [37]. Supervisors with different levels of power distance may therefore respond differently to perceived hierarchical threat. For supervisors with high power distance, maintaining clear hierarchical boundaries and supervisory authority is a central concern. Such supervisors are more likely to regard hierarchical distinctions as legitimate and necessary for preserving organizational order. Consequently, when they perceive threat to the existing hierarchy, they are more likely to interpret it as a meaningful challenge to their authority and respond defensively in order to restore hierarchical stability.

Supervisor ostracism represents a relatively subtle and low-visibility form of defensive response that enables supervisors to maintain authority while avoiding overt interpersonal confrontation [19]. Accordingly, supervisors with high power distance may be more inclined to engage in ostracism when they perceive heightened threat to hierarchy. In contrast, supervisors with low power distance tend to value more egalitarian and collaborative relationships with subordinates and are less concerned with maintaining rigid hierarchical distinctions. Even when such supervisors perceive potential threat to hierarchy, they are less likely to respond through ostracism because they are generally less sensitive to status boundary maintenance.

Importantly, power distance is not theorized to moderate the relationship between managing boss and perceived threat to hierarchy in the present model, because the formation of threat perceptions is primarily driven by supervisors’ interpretations of social information cues. Instead, power distance is expected to shape supervisors’ behavioral responses once perceived threat to hierarchy has emerged.

Accordingly, power distance is expected to moderate the second stage of the indirect effect, such that the indirect relationship between managing boss and supervisor ostracism via perceived threat to hierarchy is stronger under conditions of high power distance than under conditions of low power distance. Consequently, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 5

Power distance moderates the indirect effect of managing boss on supervisor ostracism via perceived threat to hierarchy, such that the indirect effect is stronger for supervisors with high power distance than for those with low power distance.

The theoretical model proposed in this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. Drawing on social information processing theory, the model positions managing boss as the independent variable and supervisor ostracism as the outcome variable. The relationship between managing boss and perceived threat to hierarchy is expected to be positive on average, contingent on supervisors’ interpretations of subordinate behavior. Perceived threat to hierarchy is conceptualized as a conditional mediator, which may transmit the effect of managing boss on supervisor ostracism under specific boundary conditions. In particular, power distance is introduced as a moderating variable that influences the effect of perceived threat to hierarchy on supervisor ostracism, thereby shaping the strength of the indirect relationship between managing boss and supervisor ostracism. Together, the model constitutes a moderated mediation framework that integrates the hypothesized direct, conditional mediating, and moderating effects examined in this study.

Methods

Sample and data collection

This study adopted paired data collected from 12 medium- and large-sized Chinese enterprises spanning the manufacturing, retail, food service, and real estate industries. These enterprises were voluntarily recruited through the author’s professional networks, and all participating firms provided consent for employee participation in the study. Information regarding firm ownership and firm size was obtained through a brief organizational background survey completed by administrative staff members of each participating enterprise to ensure the accuracy of organizational background data.

All data were collected through on-site surveys administered during regular working hours to ensure high response quality. A strict supervisor-subordinate matched dyadic design was implemented. Specifically, each participating supervisor was matched with and evaluated only one subordinate employee, thereby forming independent one-to-one supervisor-subordinate dyads rather than a nested structure involving multiple subordinates under the same supervisor. This matching procedure was established prior to data collection and strictly maintained throughout all survey waves. A dyad was included in the final sample only if both the direct supervisor and the subordinate independently consented to participate and completed all required survey items across the three waves of data collection.

To ensure confidentiality and facilitate accurate cross-wave matching, a unique anonymous identification code was assigned to each supervisor-subordinate dyad, and no personally identifiable information was collected. All participants were explicitly informed that their responses would remain strictly confidential and would be used solely for academic research purposes. To encourage careful participation and questionnaire completion, each respondent received a small monetary incentive of 10 RMB after completing the survey.

A three-wave, time-lagged research design was employed to reduce common method bias and mitigate concerns regarding reverse causality [39]. Data collection was conducted from June to October, 2025.

At Time 1 (June-July 2025), 600 subordinate employees completed measures of managing boss and provided demographic information, including gender, age, educational attainment, and tenure with their direct supervisors. A total of 523 valid subordinate questionnaires were obtained.

At Time 2 (August-September 2025), the 523 supervisors whose matched subordinates had provided valid responses in Time 1 survey completed measures of perceived threat to hierarchy and power distance, along with their own demographic information (gender, age, and educational attainment). This stage yielded 487 valid supervisor questionnaires.

At Time 3 (October 2025), the 487 subordinates whose matched supervisors had validly completed the Time 2 survey responded to the supervisor ostracism measure. After excluding incomplete or unmatched questionnaires, the final sample consisted of 452 valid supervisor-subordinate dyads distributed across 12 firms. The number of dyads per firm ranged from 8 to 115 (see Appendix Table A for details). The final matched sample represented an effective response rate of 75.33%.

Because the study employed independent one-to-one supervisor-subordinate dyads, the data structure did not involve multiple subordinate observations nested within the same supervisor. Therefore, the primary analyses were conducted using OLS regression and PROCESS conditional process analyses. Nevertheless, to further examine the robustness of the findings and account for potential firm-level clustering, supplementary multilevel random-intercept analyses at the firm level were additionally conducted, and the results remained substantively consistent.

The participating organizations represented diverse ownership structures, including state-owned enterprises (24.8%), privately owned enterprises (36.5%), collectively owned enterprises (3.1%), foreign-invested or joint-venture enterprises (4.0%), and mixed-ownership enterprises (31.6%). Such diversity enhances the external generalizability of the findings across different organizational contexts.

Regarding demographic characteristics, 69.5% of supervisors were male, with ages ranging from 29 to 56 years. In terms of educational attainment, 46.9% of supervisors held a college degree and 32.1% held a master’s degree or above. Among subordinate participants, 65.3% were male, with a mean age of 29.3 years and an average tenure with their direct supervisors of 3.16 years. Subordinates also demonstrated relatively high educational attainment, with 42.9% holding a college diploma and 15.1% holding a master’s degree or higher.

Measurement

All measurement scales were adopted from established prior studies with demonstrated validity and reliability. All items were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”).

Managing boss

Managing boss was measured by a 10-item scale developed and validated by Gajendran et al. (2024) [4]. A sample item is: “I proactively attempt to get a better understanding of my supervisor’s priorities.” The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.914.

Perceived threat to hierarchy

Perceived threat to hierarchy (PTH) was measured using the 3-item scale developed by Khan et al. (2018) [26]. This scale captures supervisors’ perceptions that subordinates’ behaviors or attributes may challenge established hierarchical distinctions and authority structures. To ensure theoretical relevance for the current study, the items were framed to capture supervisors’ perceptions of hierarchical threat in general, independent of subordinates’ performance or specific task outcomes. A sample item is: “I feel that this subordinate posed a threat to the existing hierarchy.” The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.852.

Power distance

Power distance (PD) was measured using the 6-item scale developed by Dorfman and Howell (1988) [40]. A sample item is: “It is frequently necessary for a supervisor to exercise authority and power when dealing with subordinates.” The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.908.

Supervisor ostracism

Supervisor ostracism (SO) was measured using the 5-item workplace ostracism scale employed and validated by Hitlan and Noel (2009) [41]. Following prior supervisor-subordinate research, item wording was adapted to explicitly refer to supervisors as the source of ostracism, ensuring construct relevance. A sample item is: “I feel like I’m being ostracized by my supervisor.” The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.893.

Based on prior research on managing boss behaviors [4] and supervisor ostracism [41], this study controlled for firm ownership, firm size, demographic differences between supervisors and subordinates (gender, age, and educational attainment), and tenure of working together.

Results analysis

Measurement model assessment

This study adopted the Fornell and Larcker (1981) [42] criterion to assess the convergent and discriminant validity of the measurement model. Convergent validity was evaluated using composite reliability (CR), standardized factor loadings, and average variance extracted (AVE), while discriminant validity was examined following the same criterion. As presented in Table 1, all CR values exceeded 0.850 and all AVE values were above 0.519. In terms of convergent validity, the standardized factor loadings of all reflective items were greater than 0.600, all meeting the recommended threshold values for good convergent validity. Furthermore, the satisfactory factor loadings and reliability estimates provide additional support for the construct validity and contextual appropriateness of the adapted measurement scales used in this study. To further assess discriminant validity, the Fornell-Larcker criterion was examined. Descriptive analysis

This study conducted descriptive analyses, including means, standard deviations, and correlations, to examine the relationships among the focal variables. As shown in Table 3, MB was significantly correlated with PD (r = 0.626), PTH (r = 0.626), and SO (r = 0.351), all p < 0.01. In addition, PD was significantly correlated with SO (r = 0.330; p < 0.01). These findings provide preliminary empirical support for the hypothesized relationships.

To assess potential multicollinearity, variance inflation factor (VIF) diagnostics were conducted using the full regression model predicting supervisor ostracism, including all control variables, focal predictors, and the interaction term (see Appendix Table D). The VIF values ranged from 1.062 to 3.558, all below the commonly recommended threshold of 5, indicating that multicollinearity was not a serious concern [43].

Because firm ownership and firm size were operationalized using dummy coding procedures in the regression analyses, these categorical control variables were not included in the correlation matrix for the sake of parsimony and interpretability. Nevertheless, all dummy-coded control variables were included in the subsequent regression and robustness analyses.

As an additional diagnostic concerning the nested structure of the data, ICC(1) values were estimated for the primary study variables. As reported in Appendix Table B, ICC(1) values ranged from 0.02 to 0.08, suggesting relatively limited, though non-zero, firm-level clustering effects. Given this non-zero clustering, supplementary multilevel random-intercept robustness analyses were also conducted (see Appendix Table C). The results remained substantively consistent with the primary OLS and PROCESS analyses.

Discussion

Drawing on social information processing theory, this study examined a moderated mediation model linking managing boss to supervisor ostracism via perceived threat to hierarchy, with power distance as a second-stage moderator. The findings indicated that MB was positively associated with PTH. In addition, PTH was positively related to SO. However, the simple mediating effect of PTH was not supported in the overall sample, indicating that PTH functions as a conditional rather than universal mediator. Instead, the results revealed a significant conditional indirect effect, such that the indirect effect of MB on SO via PTH emerged only when supervisors had high levels of power distance. Specifically, PD strengthened the relationship between PTH and SO, whereas this relationship was not significant under low PD.These findings suggest that the role of PTH is conditional rather than universal. That is, although managing boss may elicit perceptions of hierarchical threat, such perceptions do not necessarily translate into ostracism unless supervisors hold stronger hierarchical value orientations. All regression analyses controlled for firm ownership and firm size (entered as dummy-coded variables), along with demographic differences and tenure.Although prior research has extensively examined the consequences of managing boss for employees, its impact on supervisors has received limited attention. The present study contributes by showing that managing boss may be associated with negative supervisory reactions under certain conditions. Importantly, this does not imply that managing boss is inherently threatening. Rather, consistent with social information processing theory, managing boss provides social cues that supervisors may interpret in different ways. In some cases, supervisors may view such behavior as supportive and beneficial; in other cases - particularly when hierarchical boundaries are salient - they may interpret it as signaling increased subordinate visibility or potential status encroachment, thereby eliciting perceptions of hierarchical threat.Moreover, perceived threat to hierarchy was found to be positively related to supervisor ostracism. This finding is consistent with theoretical perspectives suggesting that individuals may respond defensively when their status or authority is perceived to be challenged. In such cases, ostracism may function as a relatively low-cost and indirect strategy to maintain hierarchical boundaries and reinforce social distance, compared with more overt forms of confrontation.Furthermore, power distance plays a critical boundary role in shaping whether perceived threat translates into behavioral responses. Supervisors with high power distance tend to place greater value on hierarchical differentiation and authority, making them more sensitive to potential status threat. As a result, when they perceive hierarchical challenges, they are more likely to engage in ostracism. In contrast, supervisors with low power distance emphasize more egalitarian relationships and are less likely to respond defensively, thereby weakening the association between PTH and SO.Finally, consistent with a second-stage moderated mediation model, the indirect effect of MB on SO via PTH was contingent on power distance. Specifically, the indirect effect was significant only at high levels of PD, but not at low levels. This finding underscores that PTH does not function as a general mediating mechanism, but rather as a context-dependent pathway that operates under specific value orientations. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of considering both perceptual processes and individual value differences when examining the interpersonal consequences of managing boss.

Furthermore, power distance plays a critical boundary role in shaping whether perceived threat translates into behavioral responses. Supervisors with high power distance tend to place greater value on hierarchical differentiation and authority, making them more sensitive to potential status threat. As a result, when they perceive hierarchical challenges, they are more likely to engage in ostracism. In contrast, supervisors with low power distance emphasize more egalitarian relationships and are less likely to respond defensively, thereby weakening the association between PTH and SO.

Finally, consistent with a second-stage moderated mediation model, the indirect effect of MB on SO via PTH was contingent on power distance. Specifically, the indirect effect was significant only at high levels of PD, but not at low levels. This finding underscores that PTH does not function as a general mediating mechanism, but rather as a context-dependent pathway that operates under specific value orientations. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of considering both perceptual processes and individual value differences when examining the interpersonal consequences of managing boss.

Theoretical implications

This study makes several contributions to the literature on managing boss, hierarchical dynamics, and workplace ostracism.

First, this study extends the managing boss literature by examining its potential interpersonal costs for supervisors. While prior research has predominantly emphasized the positive outcomes of managing boss - such as improved performance and relationship quality - this study highlights that such proactive behavior may also be associated with less favorable supervisory reactions under certain conditions. Importantly, the findings do not suggest that managing boss is inherently detrimental. Rather, they indicate that its interpersonal consequences depend on how supervisors interpret such behavior, thereby offering a more balanced and context-sensitive understanding of managing boss.

Second, this study contributes to the literature on hierarchical dynamics by introducing perceived threat to hierarchy as a conditional explanatory mechanism. The findings suggest that managing boss may be associated with higher levels of perceived hierarchical threat; however, this perception does not uniformly translate into behavioral outcomes. Instead, the effect of PTH on supervisor ostracism depends on supervisors’ power distance orientations. This extends prior research by showing that hierarchical threat perceptions alone are insufficient to predict outcomes unless combined with relevant value-based boundary conditions.

Third, this study advances research on conditional process models by demonstrating a pattern in which moderated mediation exists in the absence of a significant overall mediation effect. Specifically, the mediating role of PTH was not supported at the aggregate level, but emerged only under high power distance. This finding contributes to a more nuanced understanding of mediation processes by highlighting that underlying mechanisms may operate only under specific contextual conditions, rather than universally across all cases.

Finally, this study contributes to the workplace ostracism and broader mistreatment literature by identifying a novel, context-dependent antecedent of supervisor-initiated ostracism. Prior research has largely focused on negative subordinate characteristics or adverse organizational conditions as drivers of ostracism. By contrast, this study suggests that even constructive and well-intentioned subordinate behaviors may, under certain interpretive conditions, be associated with exclusionary responses. This perspective shifts the focus from purely dispositional explanations toward a more relational and perception-based understanding of workplace mistreatment.

Practical implications

This study offers several practical implications for organizations seeking to improve supervisor-subordinate interactions and reduce unintended negative consequences associated with managing boss behaviors.

First, organizations should provide targeted training to help employees engage in managing boss behaviors more effectively when communicating with supervisors. Rather than discouraging such proactive behavior, organizations should help employees develop communication skills that make managing boss less likely to trigger defensive supervisory reactions. For example, organizations may design training interventions that help employees: (a) assess supervisors’ power distance orientations and adapt communication styles accordingly; (b) frame proactive input and requests as supporting supervisors’ goals rather than as challenging authority; (c) use perspective-taking and timing strategies to raise issues in ways that preserve hierarchical sensitivity; (d) seek informal feedback to better understand whether their proactive communication is perceived as supportive or intrusive. Such training can help employees practice managing boss in ways that strengthen coordination while reducing the risk of unintentionally provoking hierarchical threat.

Second, organizations should help supervisors manage perceived hierarchical threat more constructively. Because perceived threat to hierarchy may trigger supervisor ostracism, organizations should provide leadership training aimed at helping supervisors recognize defensive reactions, regulate status-based concerns, and interpret proactive subordinate behavior less as threat and more as support. Clarifying role expectations and fostering transparent communication may further reduce unnecessary threat perceptions.

Third, organizations may benefit from tailoring management interventions to supervisors’ power distance orientations. For supervisors with high power distance, leadership development programs emphasizing inclusive leadership, cognitive reframing, and non-defensive responses to proactive employees may be particularly valuable. For supervisors with low power distance orientations, organizations can leverage their egalitarian tendencies to promote open communication and collaborative supervisor-subordinate exchanges.

Finally, organizations should establish mechanisms for identifying and addressing subtle forms of supervisor ostracism. For example, anonymous upward feedback systems, climate assessments, or coaching interventions may help detect exclusionary supervisory behaviors early and provide opportunities for behavioral correction before such patterns become entrenched.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study suggest directions for future research.

First, although this study accounted for potential nesting effects through supplementary multilevel robustness analyses, the relatively modest number of firms (12) may limit firm-level inference and the application of more complex multilevel modeling approaches. Although ICC(1) results suggested relatively limited, though non-zero, firm-level clustering, future research with a larger number of organizations is needed to further validate these findings using fully multilevel designs.

Second, the data were collected from Chinese enterprises, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Given that power distance may vary across cultural contexts, future studies could examine whether the proposed relationships hold in lower power distance settings or in cross-cultural contexts.

Third, although a time-lagged design was employed to mitigate concerns regarding common method bias and temporal ambiguity, causal inferences should still be interpreted cautiously. Future research could adopt longitudinal or experimental designs to strengthen causal conclusions.

Fourth, future research could explore additional boundary conditions that may weaken the relationship between perceived threat to hierarchy and supervisor ostracism, such as leader humility, psychological safety, or organizational justice, thereby offering a more nuanced understanding of when perceived hierarchical threat is less likely to trigger defensive supervisory responses.
