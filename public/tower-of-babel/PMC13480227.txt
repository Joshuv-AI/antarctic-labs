Background

Pericarditis is defined as inflammation of the pericardial sac and is most commonly idiopathic or viral in origin.1 Purulent bacterial pericarditis is far less common but carries high morbidity and mortality as clinical deterioration from tamponade and sepsis can occur rapidly without urgent drainage and culture-directed antimicrobial therapy.2 In patients with known malignancy, a large pericardial effusion is frequently assumed to be malignant in etiology; however, tumor-associated erosion into adjacent thoracic structures may create a conduit for contiguous inoculation of oral/gastrointestinal (GI) flora into the pericardial space, fundamentally changing management. For that reason, pericardial fluid should be systematically assessed for infectious, inflammatory, and malignant etiologies (cell count/differential, chemistries, cytology, and cultures), rather than presuming malignant effusion by default.2Take-Home Message•In cancer patients with pericardial effusion, oral/gastrointestinal flora in pericardial cultures should trigger evaluation for an anatomic breach (eg, tumor erosion) and early source control with drainage, culture-directed antibiotics, and surgical escalation when tamponade continues to persist.

Streptococcus intermedius (a member of the Streptococcus anginosus group) is part of the normal oral flora and is associated with abscess forming infections, with rare reports of purulent pericarditis.3,4 Lactobacillus spp are commensal GI organisms and rare causes of invasive infection, but case reports have documented endovascular involvement, and in more severe cases, bacteremia and infective endocarditis.5 Recovery of these organisms from pericardial fluid should prompt evaluation for a mucosal breach or contiguous source of infection rather than hematogenous seeding.

Case Presentation

A 67-year-old male with a past medical history notable for known esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and progressive dysphagia requiring jejunostomy tube placement presented as a transfer from an outside hospital for management of a pericardial effusion with early stages of tamponade. The patient originally presented to the outside hospital for management of his esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, which included planned chemoradiation and potential esophagectomy when he suddenly began to endorse worsening pleuritic chest pain with associated fatigue and headaches. Electrocardiogram demonstrated diffuse ST-segment elevations, which raised concerns for acute pericarditis (Figure 1). Cardiology was consulted, and a transthoracic echocardiogram was performed, revealing a circumferential pericardial effusion measuring approximately 2.5 cm in end-diastole with fibrinous strands and mild right ventricular diastolic collapse, consistent with early tamponade physiology (Figure 2). Computed tomography angiography of the chest demonstrated a large obstructing esophageal mass with erosion into the left main bronchus, supporting the presence of a potential anatomic route for mediastinal/pericardial contamination (Figure 3). Upon arrival to our facility, concern for purulent pericarditis with tamponade physiology prompted initiation of empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics (vancomycin plus piperacillin-tazobactam), and the patient was taken to the operating room to undergo emergent pericardiocentesis on hospital day 1. Approximately 800 mL of yellow, cloudy fluid was removed, and a pericardial drain was left in place. Pericardial fluid analysis showed nucleated cells 49,710/μL, glucose 2 mg/dL, lactate dehydrogenase 7,327 U/L, and pH 7.5. Cytology demonstrated marked neutrophilia compatible with acute inflammation/acute pericarditis and was negative for malignant cells. Blood cultures also remained negative. Pericardial fluid cultures grew S intermedius and Lactobacillus spp., and antibiotics were de-escalated to intravenous (IV) ceftriaxone for a 3-week course. Empiric vancomycin plus piperacillin-tazobactam were initially selected to provide broad-spectrum coverage for gram-positive organisms (including Staphylococcus aureus), gram-negative rods, and anaerobes given the concern for purulent pericarditis in the setting of esophageal/airway erosion. Once cultures identified S intermedius and Lactobacillus spp and blood cultures/cytology were found negative, the decision was made to narrow antibiotic coverage to IV ceftriaxone after a multidisciplinary discussion with the infectious disease team. A planned 3-week course was chosen to treat a deep-seated intrathoracic infection after adequate drainage and clinical improvement, consistent with prolonged IV therapy commonly used for purulent pericardial infections.

Despite pericardiocentesis and drainage, follow-up echocardiogram demonstrated persistent effusion with ongoing features of tamponade, which prompted placement of a surgical pericardial window on hospital day 4. The patient's symptoms and hemodynamics improved prior to discharge. Repeat echocardiogram on hospital day 14 showed resolution of right ventricular collapse with improved effusion; however, moderate pericardial thickening with mild calcification was appreciated, raising concerns for potential constrictive physiology (Figure 4). Although the presence of pericardial thickening with mild calcification raised concerns for possible evolving constrictive physiology, definitive echocardiographic hemodynamic criteria for constriction (eg, Doppler respiratory variation in filling or hepatic vein flow reversal) were not demonstrated or available in the follow-up study. Therefore, interval clinical assessment and repeat echocardiography were recommended to monitor the development of constrictive physiology. The patient improved without complication and was discharged to an inpatient rehabilitation facility for further management. Discussion

Purulent pericarditis is rare in the modern antibiotic era, accounting for <1% of pericarditis cases in developed countries, yet it remains highly lethal without rapid diagnosis and source control.6 Untreated, purulent pericarditis is fatal, with a reported mortality rate approaching 85%.6 In treated populations, the reported mortality rate remains substantial at 40%, with death mostly occurring due to cardiac tamponade, systemic toxicity, cardiac decompensation, and constriction.6 Historically, cases have described high mortality when diagnosis and drainage are delayed, further reinforcing the need to treat suspected purulence as a cardiopulmonary emergency.2 This case highlights a diagnostic pitfall in cardio-oncology: avoid anchoring on the diagnosis of malignant pericardial effusion. Even in the setting of known malignancy, pericardial fluid should be systematically evaluated for infectious, inflammatory, and malignant etiologies (cell count/differential, chemistries including glucose/LDH, cytology, Gram stain, and cultures). In our patient, multiple findings argued against malignant effusion as the primary driver: diffuse ST-segment elevations, fibrinous echocardiographic strands, and a profoundly inflammatory pericardial fluid profile with marked neutrophilia, extremely elevated LDH, very low glucose, and negative malignant cytology, which all support purulent pericarditis rather than malignant effusion.6,7 The unique microbiology found in the fluid studies further reinforced the mechanism. S intermedius (S anginosus group) is associated with deep abscess–forming infection and is a rare cause of purulent pericarditis.3,4 Co-isolation of Lactobacillus spp, an uncommon invasive pathogen described in endovascular infections, suggested an oral/GI flora signature and should prompt evaluation for an anatomic breach rather than assuming hematogenous seeding.5 In this case, computed tomography angiography demonstrated esophageal tumor erosion into the left main bronchus, providing a plausible contiguous route for contamination of the thoracic compartment and pericardial space.

Management principles for purulent pericarditis center on urgent drainage for tamponade relief and source control, comprehensive fluid evaluation (including cytology and cultures) to differentiate malignant from infectious etiologies, and prompt transition to culture-directed antibiotics once organisms are identified. When features of tamponade continue to persist despite prior drainage, or when effusions are thick, loculated, or incompletely evaluated, early escalation to surgical drainage is warranted. Surgical drainage typically involves placement of a pericardial window that allows for ongoing drainage and, depending on intraoperative findings, may include irrigation/washout of purulent material to achieve definitive surgical source control.2 Due to persistent tamponade physiology despite pericardiocentesis, our patient underwent pericardial window placement on hospital day 4. Taken together, this case emphasizes that clinicians should not automatically presume malignant effusion by default in cancer patients; instead, they should pursue a complete pericardial fluid workup and escalate drainage when physiology demands it because delays in diagnosing purulent pericarditis carry disproportionately high mortality rates.2

Finally, follow-up echocardiography demonstrated pericardial thickening with mild calcification, raising a concern for possible evolving constrictive physiology. Definitive Doppler/hemodynamic criteria for constriction were not available or not demonstrated, so the appropriate next step is close outpatient surveillance with repeat echocardiography and symptom monitoring, with escalation to advanced imaging or invasive hemodynamic assessment if the clinical features of constriction emerge.1,2

Conclusions

In patients with esophageal malignancy, a large pericardial effusion should not be presumed malignant by default. Diffuse ST-segment elevations, fibrinous echocardiographic features, and a profoundly inflammatory pericardial fluid profile should prompt urgent evaluation for purulent pericarditis and immediate drainage with cultures. Recovery of oral/GI flora such as S intermedius and Lactobacillus spp should trigger investigation for an anatomic breach enabling contiguous inoculation, including tumor-associated erosion into adjacent thoracic structures. When tamponade features persist despite pericardiocentesis, escalation to surgical drainage is essential, and interval pericardial thickening/calcification warrants follow-up for potential constrictive physiology.
