Accuracy of the rapid‐response electroencephalography's Automated Seizure Burden Estimator: A follow‐up validation study of version 8 (AccuRASE II)
Authors: Zubeda B Sheikh, Michael W K Fong, Monica B Dhakar, Wei Fang, Neishay Ayub, Janine Molino, Hiba A Haider, Brandon Foreman, Emily J Gilmore, Moshe Mizrahi, Ioannis Karakis, Sarah Schmitt, Gamaleldin Osman, Ji Yeoun Yoo, Lawrence J Hirsch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCeribell Inc.'s point‐of‐care electroencephalographic (EEG) system and artificial intelligence‐based Automated Seizure Burden Estimator (ASBE; ClarityPro) have US Food and Drug Administration clearance for diagnosing electrographic status epilepticus (ESE). The AccuRASE study using ASBE version 6 (V6) showed high negative predictive value (NPV) but limited sensitivity and positive predictive value (PPV) at certain thresholds. Version 8 (V8) is the updated algorithm trained with additional EEG samples.MethodsWe tested V8 on the previously used rapid‐response EEG test dataset (not used to train V8). Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were compared against blinded expert annotations. ESE, ESE and possible ESE (ESE/pESE), electrographic seizures (ESz), and Esz with highly epileptiform patterns (Esz/HEPs) at burden thresholds of >1%, >10%, >20%, >50%, and >90% were analyzed. Additionally, we evaluated the built‐in ESE and ESE/pESE alerts (≥90% over 5 min, ≥20% over 1 h, or ≥10 continuous minutes of seizure).ResultsV8 showed 100% sensitivity at lower thresholds (>10%–20%) for ESE (V6 .86), without a specificity loss (V8 .9 vs. V6 .85), and retained NPV (V8 1.0 vs. V6 .99). Sensitivity at 50% threshold was .86 for ESE (V6 .71), and specificity was .94 (V6 .91); PPV for ESE was 30% for ESE and 80% for ESE/pESE. The specificity for ESE remained high for 90% burden (V8 .96, V6 .97) but the sensitivity dropped (.29 vs. V6 .43). Sensitivity was lower for ESz/HEPs (.54 → .44 at >10% threshold), although the algorithm is not designed to detect HEPs. Built‐in alert analysis showed significantly improved sensitivity for ESE (.57 → .86); specificity and NPV remained high (>.94).SignificanceASBE V8 showed meaningful improvements in sensitivity, continued excellent NPV, and improved built‐in alert accuracy for ESE and ESE/pESE. The best seizure burden threshold for ruling out ESE now is 20% (vs. 10% with V6) and that for ruling in ESE/pESE with reasonable certainty is 50% (vs. 90% with V6).Keywords: artificial intelligence, automated, point of care (POC) EEG, rapid response EEG, seizure detection

INTRODUCTION

Twenty percent to 30% of critically ill patients have electrographic seizures (ESz) and electrographic status epilepticus (ESE). 1 , 2 , 3 Prolonged untreated seizures contribute to neurologic decline, worse functional outcomes, and later epilepsy, and timely detection is crucial. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

Continuous electroencephalography (EEG) is essential for detection, but its access is limited due to inadequate technologist and neurophysiologist availability. Rapid‐response EEG (rr‐EEG) systems, also known as “point‐of‐care” EEG systems, have emerged as a potential option, especially in resource‐limited settings. These range from full montage to limited‐montage systems and allow for cloud‐based processing. 9 , 10 , 11 The associated automated seizure detection algorithms accelerate interpretation where on‐call expertise is lacking or delayed. 12 , 13

The Automated Seizure Burden Estimator (ASBE) was designed to quantify seizure burden as a percentage of time occupied by seizures within rolling 5‐min windows. 12 The AccuRASE study (assessing Clarity version 6 [V6], Ceribell Inc.) showed that the use of ASBE allowed ESE to be ruled out in many patients due to its high negative predictive value (NPV), concluding that the study provided “class II evidence that ASBE software can reliably exclude ESE (98% negative predictive value using a <10% burden cutoff) without expert review in most patients requiring rapid response EEG.” 13 However, modest sensitivity at higher thresholds and relatively low positive predictive value (PPV) were the limitations with V6. As ASBE is a machine learning algorithm that continues to learn from additional large training data, updated versions likely perform better. Version 8 (V8) was developed following additional training to address the gaps that the AccuRASE study showed with V6. In this follow‐up study, we compare V8 to V6 and analyze the burden threshold performance, the built‐in real‐time alert accuracy, and V8′s agreement with blinded expert burden classification.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Yale University Institutional Review Board (CR00010848) approved the study and a waiver of informed consent. The STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) and STARD (Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies) reporting guidelines were followed. 13

We reanalyzed the same rr‐EEG dataset evaluated in the AccuRASE study. 13 These EEGs were performed at three Yale–New Haven Health System hospitals: a small community hospital (rr‐EEGs placed by nurses in the emergency department or intensive care units [ICUs]), a medium–large inner city regional hospital (also placed by nurses in emergency departments or ICUs), and one tertiary care center where residents, fellows, or (less commonly) technologists applied the rr‐EEG band in any location, most commonly the emergency department. Recordings were obtained with a 10‐electrode headband system (Ceribell Inc.), processed using ASBE V8 to compare to V6.

Twelve experts from 10 medical centers across the United States and Australia volunteered. These were members of the Critical Care EEG Monitoring Research Consortium (CCEMRC) 14 ; they had at least 1 year of EEG fellowship training (8/12 had 2 years of training) and had passed the self‐assessment examination on American Clinical Neurophysiology Society (ACNS) Critical Care EEG Terminology. 14 Seven of 12 experts were in practice for more than 5 years past fellowship training, and all were board certified in American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology (ABPN) clinical neurophysiology, ABPN epilepsy, or American Board of Clinical Neurophysiology critical care EEG or by an international EEG/clinical neurophysiology board. All experts were from a university‐affiliated tertiary or quaternary care hospital (Table S2). The ASBE function was disabled on these studies, to have an expert EEG review blinded to clinical features, clinical interpretation, and the results of ASBE. Agreement between at least two of three independent expert neurophysiologists blinded to ASBE outputs established the gold standard. Performance was evaluated at five seizure burden thresholds (>1%, >10%, >20%, >50%, >90%) across the following ACNS Critical Care EEG Terminology‐defined categories: ESE, ESE and possible ESE (ESE/pESE), definite seizures (ESz), and ESz and highly epileptiform patterns (Esz/HEPs). 15

Seizure and pattern definitions

Highly epileptiform patterns

HEPs included any pattern qualifying as one of the three options for main term 2 in the ACNS terminology (i.e., spike–wave, periodic discharges, or rhythmic delta activity) other than generalized rhythmic delta activity; any pattern on the ictal–interictal continuum (IIC); brief potentially ictal rhythmic discharges; highly epileptiform bursts; and possible (but not probable or definite) seizures.

Electrographic seizures

ESz were defined based on the ACNS 2021 standardized Critical Care EEG Terminology as:

Epileptiform discharges averaging >2.5 Hz for ≥10 s (>25 discharges in 10 s) OR

Epileptiform discharges averaging >2.5 Hz for ≥10 s (>25 discharges in 10 s) OR

Any pattern with definite evolution (at least two unequivocal, sequential changes in frequency, morphology, or location) lasting ≥10 s.

Any pattern with definite evolution (at least two unequivocal, sequential changes in frequency, morphology, or location) lasting ≥10 s.

Electrographic status epilepticus

ESE was defined as a seizure lasting for 10 or more continuous minutes or a total duration of 20% or more of any 60 min of recording.

Possible electrographic seizures

Possible electrographic seizures were defined for the study's purposes as follows:

Patterns on the IIC (based on ACNS 2021 terminology).

Patterns on the IIC (based on ACNS 2021 terminology).

Continuous high‐amplitude rhythmic background when it seemed concerning for an ictal pattern, not otherwise qualifying for definite or probable ESz or HEPs.

Continuous high‐amplitude rhythmic background when it seemed concerning for an ictal pattern, not otherwise qualifying for definite or probable ESz or HEPs.

Concern for seizures other than above in a technically limited study that did not allow for more certain determination.

Concern for seizures other than above in a technically limited study that did not allow for more certain determination.

Possible ESE

pESE was defined by a rhythmic or periodic pattern that qualifies for the IIC or possible ESz (as defined above) that was present for 10 or more continuous minutes or a total duration of 20% or more of any 60 min of recording.

Possible HEPs

Possible HEPs were defined by patterns concerning for HEPs but uncertain due to technical limitations.

Regardless of the burden, sporadic (nonperiodic) epileptiform discharges were not considered HEPs.

To mimic real‐world situations, the response options for the presence of seizures and epileptiform abnormalities included definite and possible abnormalities for both seizures and HEPs. But for analysis purposes, when analyzing the accuracy of the ASBE for detecting seizures, only the presence of definite ESz was taken as a “yes,” whereas “maybe” was counted as a “no.” In contrast, while testing the accuracy for detecting any highly epileptiform abnormality, definite HEPs and possible (“maybe”) seizures and HEPs were considered a “yes.” This helped us assess the algorithm's performance not only against definite ESz and definite electrographic status, but also for patterns that are borderline and may fall on the IIC (per ACNS ICU EEG terminology) or may just be classified as HEPs as defined above. Thus, we analyzed four different pattern categories: definite electrographic status, definite and possible electrographic status, definite seizures, and seizures and HEPs.

Agreement between two of three experts was considered the reference standard. The experts were also asked to assess the highest 5‐min ESz/HEP burden in the initial 10‐min segment of each EEG. The assessment of burden was limited to the worst 5‐min segment to mimic the performance of ASBE, which assesses the burden in the past 5 min on a rolling basis every 10 s.

In addition, we assessed ASBE's built‐in alert feature, which generates automated notifications when any of the following conditions are met: ≥90% seizure burden within 5 min, ≥20% seizure burden sustained over 1 h, or ≥10 min of continuous seizure activity. Built‐in alert for V6 only included ≥90% seizure burden within a 5‐min trigger in the clinical setting. The latter two cutoffs were added to V8 to match the ACNS definition of ESE. 15 However, to ensure that comparison of V8 against V6 for this function is meaningful, V6 was reanalyzed using all three logics (for the purposes of this study).

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.5.2 (R Core Team), with the “epiR” (version 2.0.88), and the “psych” (version 2.5.6) packages. For confidence intervals, exact binomial confidence limits were calculated. 16 In addition, the highest 5‐min pattern burden in the first 10 min was reported in both EEG expert review and ASBE; we examined weighted kappa statistics to evaluate overall agreement between ASBE burden classifications and expert ratings. 17

Please refer to our previous publication for additional methodological details regarding patient selection, expert selection, EEG pattern definitions and ASBE (ClarityPro V6) functionality (Figure S1 and Supplement A) 13

Data

Data not provided in the article because of space limitations may be shared (anonymized) at the request of a qualified investigator to replicate procedures and results (for 1 year after publication).

RESULTS

The experts reviewed 235 consecutive studies. The median study duration was 3.6 h (.2–16.1 h). Sixty‐nine studies became uninterpretable before the EEG system was intentionally disconnected. In those 69, the median time to the start of the uninterpretable portion was 2 h 23 min (0:02–11:00 h), and 62 of 69 had at least 20 min of interpretable data.

Performance of various seizure burden thresholds

For ESE, V8 had a consistently higher sensitivity, reaching 1.0 at lower seizure burden thresholds (>1%, >10%, and >20%) versus .86 with V6 at these thresholds (Figure 1A, Table 1). The specificity was also higher at >10% and >20% (.90 vs. .85 at >20%), leading to a higher overall accuracy of >20% threshold (.9 vs. .85 in V6). Similarly, 50% threshold showed both higher sensitivity (V8 .86 vs. V6 .71) and higher specificity (V8 .94 vs. V6 .91). The specificity at >90% threshold was comparable to V6 (V8 .96 vs. V6 .97), but the sensitivity dropped to .29 (from V6 .43).

DISCUSSION

ASBE V8 had higher accuracy over V6 across seizure burden thresholds but mainly at >10%, >20% and >50% for ESE, ESE/pESE, and ESz. The built‐in alerts had higher sensitivity, and there was better agreement with expert classification. The sensitivity improvements reduced missed ESE and ESE/pESE, whereas specificity was either the same or higher. PPV increased modestly, and NPV remained excellent for ESE, ESE/pESE, and ESz. In ESz/HEPs, sensitivity dropped from that of V6; the algorithm is not designed to detect HEPs.

V8 had a markedly higher sensitivity for the system's built‐in alerts for ESE (Figure 2). The V6 built‐in alert trigger was a 5‐min burden of 90% or more. An hourly burden of 20% or more and continuous seizure activity of 10 min or more were added as additional triggers for V8 built‐in alerts in alignment with the ACNS definitions of ESE. For comparing V8 against V6 in this study, V6 was rerun with all three trigger functions that V8 now has.

Our previous recommendations based on the V6 results were to use a >10% ASBE burden cutoff to “rule out” ESE and ESE/pESE, because the sensitivity was the highest at this threshold (.86–.88; Table 1). The sensitivity of V8 at >10% threshold for ESE/pESE was improved at .92, with high NPV (.98; Figure 1A/B, Table 1) and good specificity (.92), and this is still the optimum screening threshold for ESE/pESE. However, a >20% burden cutoff achieves perfect sensitivity and NPV of 1.0 with a modestly higher specificity (.9) for ESE (Figure 1A,B, Table 1); thus, 20% is now a better screening cutoff for ESE.

When used in decision‐making for treatment without expert review, a high specificity would be desirable to avoid inappropriate overtreatment, and cutoffs of >50% or >90% may be considered. An ASBE burden cutoff of >90% showed a high specificity (99%) for the group classified by experts as ESE/pESE with a PPV of 80%. (Figure 1B, Table 1) The specificity (and, therefore, PPV) was slightly lower for ESE alone (specificity .96, PPV .2; Figure 1A, Table 1). Thus, in settings where ASBE is used as a decision‐making tool for treatment/transfers, a >90% burden cutoff will minimize false positive results, and this can be used as a confirmation threshold for ESE/pESE. However, this cutoff only detects 29% of ESE and 33% of ESE/pESE; thus, owing to a low sensitivity, it is not helpful for screening (Figure 1A/B, Table 1). Using a 50% ASBE burden cutoff does not significantly compromise the specificity or PPV for detecting ESE but does improve the sensitivity significantly for ESE, as shown in Results (Figure 1B, Table 1). Thus, 50% is a better ESE cutoff for making treatment decisions (treat if >50%) if there is no EEG expert immediately available to confirm. V8 is aligned with V6 in its better performance with detection of patterns classified as ESE and ESE/pESE than those classified as ESz and ESz/HEPs.

Version 8's improved screening capacity (high NPV at low thresholds) allows clinicians higher confidence in excluding ESE (Figure 1, Table 1). Built‐in alerts are more sensitive, but we recommend these alerts be used as a confirmation tool (94%–98% specificity for ESE/pESE) rather than a screening tool. The sensitivity is modest at 86% for ESE and low at 62% for ESE/pESE (Figure 3). Weighted kappa improvements show that ASBE V8 better mirrored expert interpretation of seizure burden and reduced misclassifications. (Table 2). In alignment with V6 performance, V8 performs less well for discrete shorter seizures and patterns that do not meet ESE or pESE definitions. It is limited mainly in its sensitivity of detecting these patterns, which is low to modest (44%–67%) even at >10% burden cutoff.

The AccuRASE study validated V6 primarily as a high‐NPV screening tool. V8 performed better than V6 as a screening tool without a compromise of specificity (rather with improved specificity at some thresholds) and with enhanced built‐in alert performance. The ASBE algorithm is designed for iterations over time, as each version learns from larger and diverse datasets. Future versions have the potential to further enhance sensitivity, specificity, and clinical utility, moving closer to expert‐level performance.

Strengths and limitations

The study was conducted within a single health system, and patients were included from three different hospital settings (community hospital, regional hospital, and a quaternary referral center), allowing diverse patient populations. However, a single health system and the lack of demographic data and clinical indications for rr‐EEG may limit generalizability. As stated in Materials and Methods, the EEG experts came from 12 different centers within and outside the United States, demonstrating the diversity of experts. Additionally, all experts are members of the CCEMRC and had passed the official ICU EEG nomenclature self‐assessment test. 14 However, each study was reviewed by only three experts, and the interrater agreement of experts was moderate for seizures. Future work with a larger number of experts per EEG would add to the study rigor.

As stated in Results, 69 studies became uninterpretable before removal of the device. The median time to start of uninterpretable portion in 69 affected studies was 2 h 23 min. The results from the initial 10 min and initial 2‐h segments (segments retaining study quality) of each record were not significantly different from the analysis of entire recordings (which included uninterpretable segments; Table 1). However, the effects of poor study quality/uninterpretability on the results of this study were not systematically tested. This study does not address electroclinical or purely clinical seizures, as the rr‐EEG used here lacks video. This study only compares expert‐identified seizures on a reduced montage EEG to the algorithm; it is not a comparison with conventional full montage EEG, which will detect the occasional seizure that is not evident in the temporal chains and thus cannot be detected by this device. The comparison between full montage and this limited montage (bitemporal chains in longitudinal bipolar only) has been previously assessed. 18 Please refer to our original publication for additional strengths and limitations, as the same apply to this work. 13

CONCLUSIONS

ASBE V8 showed a meaningful improvement in its screening ability and built‐in alerting for ESE and ESE/pESE, its primary purpose and design. The sensitivity for ESz (not SE/pESE) was only modest, and for the ESz/HEPs group it was low; however, the algorithm was not designed for detection of HEPs. For screening of ESE (i.e., to exclude ESE), V8 at 20% threshold had near‐perfect sensitivity and NPV. For confirmation (i.e., for ruling in ESE or ESE/pESE, and likely initiating treatment), the 50% threshold in V8 achieved the best sensitivity/specificity balance. The 90% threshold remains a highly specific but insensitive threshold, suited only for confirming/ruling in a high seizure burden. These findings support using ASBE V8 as an adjunct to expert EEG interpretation, particularly in settings where continuous EEG and experts are not available.
