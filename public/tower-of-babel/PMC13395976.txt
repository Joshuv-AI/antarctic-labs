Eliciting health state utilities for open wounds in dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa
Authors: Damian Lewis, Adam B. Smith, Marjolijn van Keep, Anna E. Martinez, Gabriela Petrof, Sagair Hussain, Sophie Jones, Sharmila Nikapota, Andria Joseph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeQualitative accounts and studies employing disease-specific outcome measures have demonstrated that people with dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (DEB) display impaired health-related quality of life (HRQoL) due to wound-related challenges. There is a paucity of utility data necessary to inform the health economic evaluation of emerging treatments for cutaneous open wounds.MethodsThe objective was to implement a vignette study with a representative sample of the United Kingdom (UK) general population (N = 1139) and people with DEB (N = 44) to estimate utility values. Vignettes were defined by various attributes, including the number and area of wounds, pain, itch and hours spent managing wounds. Participants valued the vignettes from the perspective of a patient or a caregiver proxy using the EQ-5D-3L or EQ-5D-Y-3L, respectively.ResultsThe mean utilities elicited from the patient perspective ranged from 0.491 to − 0.448 and 0.457 to − 0.462 for the general population (N = 627) and DEB (N = 31) sample, respectively. The utilities elicited from the proxy perspective ranged from 0.443 to − 0.543 and 0.079 to − 0.550 in the general population (N = 512) and DEB (N = 13) sample, respectively.ConclusionThe findings join a limited evidence base indicating that the symptoms associated with the wounds experienced by people with DEB have a considerable detrimental effect on their HRQoL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-026-04322-9.

Introduction

Dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (DEB) accounts for approximately 25 to 30% of epidermolysis bullosa (EB) cases and is defined by blistering below the lamina densa within the upper papillary dermis [1, 2]. Analysis of national databases in England and Wales in 2021 yielded a prevalence of 34.8 per million for all EB subtypes and 10.7 million for DEB [2]. More recent estimates from a retrospective cohort study in England between 1997 and 2024 yielded values of 51.7 per million for all EB subtypes and 8.9 per million for DEB [3]. DEB can be subdivided into dominant (DDEB) or recessive (RDEB) forms. Generally, people with DDEB display milder phenotypes with localised skin blistering on the hands, elbows and feet [4, 5]. RDEB displays phenotypic overlap with DDEB in its localised and intermediate forms; however, it is oftentimes more severe, with widespread blistering, fibrosis, joint contractures and systemic comorbidities, such as anaemia and osteoporosis [4]. DEB comprises a defect much deeper in the dermo-epidermal junction than other EB subtypes, leading to contractures and pseudosyndactyly, limiting their motor function, and aggressive cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, which is often fatal [6, 7].

Wound burden is a fundamental challenge for people with DEB. Recurrent wounds are those that re-blister within one to three weeks, and tend to be characterised as small (< 2.3 cm) or medium (2.5 to 7.5 cm) sized [8]. Chronic wounds are those that remain non-healing for 8 weeks or longer and are larger and remain in an inflammatory state [8–10]. Pain and itch associated with wounds are the most common symptoms and present a persistent challenge as scratching can lead to further trauma, pain, insomnia and depression [8, 11]. Wounds also pose a risk of infection, and management is a complex and time-consuming burden for the patient and their caregivers [12–14]. Accordingly, DEB has a considerable humanistic burden, with those affected reporting impacts on their daily activities (e.g. bathing independently [15–17]) and independence (e.g. ability to move around at home [15, 18]), limited social interactions with friends and family [15, 19, 20] and participation in sports [15, 16], as well as anxiety or depression [15, 16, 20].

There are currently no curative therapies for DEB. The standard of care is limited to multidisciplinary management of wounds and comorbidities [13, 14]. Emerging therapies for treating open wounds, such as birch triterpenes and beremagene geperpavec (B-VEC), have displayed promising outcomes in clinical trials [21, 22]. Despite the impact of DEB on the HRQoL of those affected, there is a paucity of health utility data. Health utility values are necessary for the cost-utility analyses informing the health technology assessment (HTA) of novel interventions. They represent an individual’s strength of preference for a health state, using a 0 (dead) to 1 (full health) scale summarising an outcome’s desirability [23]. Negative values are possible in some scoring algorithms, representing a state that is less preferable than death. Utility values enable the calculation of quality-adjusted life years (QALY), the measure of health benefit preferred by many HTA agencies, including the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [24]. Therefore, the study objective was to conduct a vignette study with a representative sample of the United Kingdom (UK) general population and people with DEB in order elicit health state utilities for DEB.

Materials and methods

Sample and recruitment

Two vignette studies were conducted, targeting a sample of the UK general population (‘general population sample’) and a sample of adults and adolescents with DEB, as well as caregivers of children with DEB (‘DEB sample’). The general population sample was recruited directly via a third-party platform (Qualtrics). Quotas were implemented to ensure the sample was representative of the UK general population in terms of sex, region and age distribution. All participants recruited were adults (≥ 18 years) and resided in England, Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland. The DEB sample was identified and engaged via DEBRA UK and Cure EB (UK-based EB charity, medical research and patient support organisations). Adult and paediatric populations were considered as the intended participants, with recruitment targeting the caregivers of children with DEB (children aged < 6 years and 6 to 12 years), adolescents with DEB (children aged 13 to 17 years) and adults with DEB (≥ 18 years).

Health state definitions

Four health states (very mild, mild, moderate and severe) were defined according to the proportion of body surface area (BSA%) affected by wounds (recurrent and chronic). These health states were developed in consultation with clinical stakeholders. Vignette development

The vignettes were developed to align with the health states defined in Fig. 1. The most prevalent disease-specific complications associated with open wounds identified in published literature were considered. Initial drafts were developed following a narrative review of varied evidence types. This included clinical guidelines, qualitative patient and caregiver accounts and quantitative patient-reported outcome studies [25–32]. The vignettes were drafted with consideration of the disease characteristics (‘attributes’). These included the number and area of wounds (chronic and recurrent), global pain over four weeks, pruritus (itch) over four weeks, hours spent per day managing wounds and pain during wound management. More information about attribute and level selection is presented in Online Resource 1.

Two sets of vignettes were developed from the perspective of (1) the individual experiencing the health state (‘patient perspective’) (i.e. self-rated) and (2) the paediatric patient’s caregiver (‘proxy perspective’). The perspective adopted was sample dependent. The general population sample was equally distributed to either the patient or proxy perspective using Qualtrics’ randomiser function. These participants were asked to imagine a hypothetical child from the proxy perspective. Caregivers of children with DEB (children aged < 6 years and 6 to 12 years) were assigned to the proxy perspective and asked to imagine their child was experiencing the described state. The adolescent (13 to 17 years) and adult (≥ 18 years) samples were old enough to self-rate their health and, as such, were asked to rate vignettes from the patient (i.e. their own) perspective. Alongside the text describing the health state, other aides were utilised to represent wound size and pain. The size of chronic and recurrent wounds was illustrated to participants using fruit-based imagery, based on the methods employed by Solis et al. [33]. Fruit-based images were used as these items represent tangible and recognisable objects, aiding conceptualisation. The (assumed) average diameter (cm) and corresponding area (cm2) of a grape (diameter = 3 cm; area = 7.1 cm2), orange (diameter = 6 cm; area = 28.3 cm2) or melon (diameter = 10 cm; area = 78.5 cm2) were calculated and mapped to the corresponding area per wound reported in each state, reported in the Online Resource 1. Where the area per wound exceeded ± 20% of the area per fruit, a range was provided (e.g. ‘…two wounds… about the size of one to two oranges…’). The vignette development went through a number of revision stages. A focus group, including patients and patient representatives affiliated with DEBRA UK and Cure EB, was conducted to collate feedback on the proposed methods and vignette design. Minor alterations were made, including, for instance, providing more information about the tasks undertaken during wound care (e.g. popping blisters, removing dead or damaged skin and soaking wounds in warm water). The approach and contents were otherwise deemed valid by the patients and patient representatives.

Valuation method

Participants rated the vignettes using the EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-Y-3L [38, 39]. Both instruments are generic measures used to assess health status and health-related quality of life, comprising five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression. The EQ-5D-3L includes three response options describing the levels of problems experienced (no, moderate, extreme) [38]. The three-level version was selected to align with the NICE methods requirements at the time of data collection (August 2023) [24]. The EQ-5D-Y-3L retains the same dimensions with age-appropriate phrasing [39]. For example, the self-care dimension is referred to as ‘looking after myself’ and more relevant activities are provided for the usual activities dimension (e.g. school, sports, playing). In the patient perspective tasks, participants rated the EQ-5D-3L dimensions based on the health state described in the vignette as if it were their own. In the proxy perspective tasks, participants used the EQ-5D-Y-3L to rate the child’s health, responding as they believed the child would (Proxy Version 2) [39].

Vignettes were presented in random order to mitigate ordering effects. The survey was soft launched with a small group of the general population sample (N = 53). The results were analysed to ensure the data collection strategy was methodologically sound and likely to generate valid results. This analysis revealed a short mean time-to-completion (2.34 min). Therefore, a three-minute exclusion rule was implemented to ensure adequate participant engagement, where responses were automatically excluded pre-analysis (not recorded) if the participant completed the survey in under three minutes.

Data analysis

Data were collected in MS Excel and exported to R (version 4.3.0) for analysis. The responses were collated and descriptive statistics were generated. Continuous variables were summarised using mean, standard deviation, median, quartiles, range and 95% confidence intervals. Categorical variables were summarised using frequency counts and percentages. The EQ-5D-3L responses were converted to a utility index score for each participant using the published UK value set [40]. The EQ-5D-3L value set was applied for the proxy-elicited values, as there is currently no published value set for the EQ-5D-Y-3L in the UK. Responses were excluded based on two rules, including (1) if ‘straight-lining’ (identical health profiles for every state) or (2) preference incongruence (severe ≥ mild; severe > moderate; moderate > mild) was observed. Post-hoc Mann–Whitney U tests were conducted to assess whether the utility values differed between perspectives in the general population sample and between samples for the patient perspective. Wilcoxon effect size (r = Z / √ N) was also calculated, as defined by Rosenthal, where Z represents the number of standard deviations the observed U-statistic (ranked difference between groups) differs from its expected value and N represents the total sample size [41]. Effect sizes were defined as small (r > 0.10), medium (r > 0.30), or large (r > 0.50).

Discussion

This study sought to elucidate the HRQoL impact of open cutaneous wounds characteristic of DEB and address a paucity of health preference data. There was a negative association between disease severity and mean health state utility value, consistent with previous work displaying an association between BSA involvement, skin disease severity and HRQoL outcomes [8, 27]. The impact was pronounced in health states defined by a higher BSA% affected by wounds, with negative mean values reported for the ‘moderate’ and ‘severe’ states. Notably, mean utility was also reduced in the ‘very mild’ state relative to the UK general population norms (approximately 0.820 for adults ≥ 18 years), indicating that the challenges associated with the open wounds have a considerable effect on HRQoL, even in cases of mild disease [46].

The utility values elicited from the patient perspective were comparable: mean utility ranged from 0.491 to − 0.448 and 0.457 to − 0.462 for the general population and DEB samples, respectively. Non-significant differences were observed across all health states, with effect size estimates suggesting limited or no practical difference in utility between samples. The increased relative difference in mean utility between the mild and moderate states may reflect the non-linear change in BSA% affected by wounds (recurrent = 3.0% to 8.0%; chronic = 0.5% to 5.5%). Accordingly, the intensity of pain and itch increased from 4 to 8 between the mild and moderate states and from 2 to 4 between the very mild and mild states.

The utility values elicited from the proxy perspective were comparatively lower in both samples. Values for the general population sample ranged from 0.443 to − 0.543. The mean values reported by the DEB sample were notably lower (ranging from 0.079 to − 0.550), with the very mild state reporting a mean value close to zero. Post hoc analyses confirmed that the difference between perspectives was statistically significant (p < 0.05), with participants valuing the health states portrayed in the proxy vignettes as less preferable, on average, than those valuing the patient vignettes. The low relative mean utility observed in the DEB sample may reflect the small number of participants with children aged 6 to 12 years who have DEB (N = 13) included in the analyses.

The results of the current study are consistent with previous findings reporting considerable impairment relative to population norms. For instance, Angelis et al., in two studies of adult DEB patients in eight European countries, noted overall mean EQ-5D-3L-elicited utilities ranging between 0.456 and 0.579, corresponding to the ‘very mild’ state [25, 47]. Similarly, a study conducted by DEBRA UK reported mean EQ-5D-5L-elicited utilities of 0.601 and 0.141 for DEB patients rating their current and worst health, respectively [48]. None of the cited studies reported negative values; however, the samples were not stratified according to disease severity or EB subtype, limiting assessment of the impact of severe disease presentations.

Mean state utilities elicited using the EQ-5D-5L were included in the health economic evidence supporting the HTA of birch triterpenes by NICE [49]. States were defined by BSA% affected by partial thickness wounds, ranging from 0 to 4% in the least severe state to > 25% in the most severe state. While consistent with the current findings in milder disease states, no negative mean values were reported, ranging from 0.56 to 0.08. However, data were collected during the open-label phase (N = 106), with all patients having received treatment. Most patients occupied health states 1 (BSA% < 4%) or 2 (BSA% 5% to 7%), with only 17 patients distributed between the more severe states (BSA% > 11%) [49]. Thus, the impact of open cutaneous wounds on HRQoL in states with higher BSA involvement is uncertain.

Limitations

Negative mean utility values were observed for the moderate and severe health states across samples and perspectives. As noted, the existing literature has not reported negative utility values for EB, which may largely be due to the absence of disease severity categorisation, yet may nevertheless reflect a potential limitation of the current study.

The vignettes did not include generic attributes aligning with the EQ-5D descriptive system. Recommendations from the Decision Support Unit specify including descriptions of generic domains of HRQoL, for example, using the EQ-5D dimensions and response levels [50]. This is intended to reduce focusing effects where the participants may fixate on the disease-specific content (e.g. symptoms and treatment-related adverse events) rather than considering the wider HRQoL context. In this case, the participants may have had insufficient information to determine the impact of the attributes on their overall well-being and assumed a ‘worst-case’ scenario for some aspects of the EQ-5D descriptive system (e.g. mobility, usual activities and anxiety/depression).

The negative values may also reflect the general population’s unfamiliarity with DEB, where participants assumed a ‘worst-case’ scenario relative to their personal health that ignores the influence of patient adaptation. Given EB typically arises from birth and is a chronic condition, patients adopt coping strategies, which may offer a protective factor for their HRQoL [27, 51]. That said, these data were corroborated by the DEB sample valuations of the patient perspective vignettes, with post hoc tests revealing a non-significant difference in utility values between samples.

There are also potential methods-related limitations that must be acknowledged. Applying the adult EQ-5D-3L value set to the EQ-5D-Y-3L responses was a pragmatic decision. At the time of publication, the EuroQol Research Foundation is conducting valuation studies for the youth versions of the EQ-5D. However, there are currently no EQ-5D-Y-3L tariffs available in the UK. The valuation studies informing the tariffs applied to the EQ-5D-Y-3L in other regions have produced higher utility values (with a lower proportion of possible states worse than death; a reduced scale length) than the EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L valuation studies within the same country or region [52]. Authors have suggested this may be due to differences in the valuation protocol, descriptive system used, valuation perspective adopted and special considerations for paediatric survival in the time trade-off tasks included in these studies [52, 53]. Consequently, applying the EQ-5D-3L UK value set may have produced underestimations in the proxy perspective tasks.

Another limitation concerns the vignette content, as important disease-specific characteristics may have been omitted. As the economic model informing the study design evaluates a therapy intended to treat cutaneous open wounds, extra-cutaneous disease manifestations (e.g. oral blistering, ocular symptoms) were not included. However, these manifestations are frequent and impact patient HRQoL [7, 15]. Similarly, the wider HRQoL context, such as the impact of DEB on social and mental well-being, was not considered in the vignettes. For example, the influence of scratching due to pain and itch is not acknowledged as a discrete attribute level in the vignettes, which can lead to further trauma, pain and psychological burden, potentially compounding the HRQoL impact [8, 11]. Further patient preference studies should seek to explore the effect of these attributes.

Strengths

The materials were validated and deemed representative by patients and patient representatives affiliated with DEBRA UK and Cure EB. Moreover—aside from implementing generic attributes within the vignettes—best practices recommended by the Decision Support Unit were followed, including, for instance, employing the EQ-5D-3L to value the vignettes, avoiding probabilistic statements and maintaining a clear and consistent format [50]. Moreover, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to implement a ‘very mild’ health state representing < 2.0% of BSA affected by open cutaneous wounds, which may be a possible outcome for people receiving novel disease-modifying treatment.

Conclusions

The findings join a limited evidence base of generic preference-based data indicating that the symptoms associated with the wounds experienced by people with DEB have a considerable effect on their HRQoL, even in cases of mild disease. Further data are required to examine the effect of severe skin involvement (BSA% > 13%) and extra-cutaneous manifestations.
