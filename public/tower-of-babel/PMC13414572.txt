mRNA-based influenza vaccine expands the B cell response breadth in humans
Authors: Hanover C Matz, Tae-Geun Yu, Kritika Dixit, Caroline Kikawa, Julian Q Zhou, Garazi Pena Alzua, Lowrey Peyton, Anders Madsen, Fangjie Han, Ariana Ghez Farrell, Robert Hoelzl, Aaron J Schmitz, Stephen C Horvath, Hunter K Keplinger, Benjamin S Strnad, Mark J Hoegger, William D Middleton, Michael K Klebert, Nina H Lin, Raffael Nachbagauer, Florian Krammer, Robert Paris, Jesse D Bloom, Jackson S Turner, Rachel M Presti, Jiwon Lee, Ali H Ellebedy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional influenza virus vaccines induce antibody responses of limited breadth. Whether mRNA-based influenza virus vaccines can induce a superior germinal center (GC) response in humans remains unclear. Here we assessed B cell responses in an observational study of cohorts of healthy young adults receiving a licensed, split-virion or investigative mRNA-based quadrivalent seasonal influenza virus vaccine over two consecutive seasons. mRNA-based vaccines consistently elicited higher antibody titers and frequencies of memory B cells. In the draining lymph nodes, mRNA vaccination stimulated sustained GC reactions that persisted for at least 26 weeks after vaccination in 5 of 13 participants across the two seasons. Proteomic analysis of serum IgG repertoire showed that mRNA vaccination increased the number of vaccine-elicited serum IgG clonotypes and promoted intraclonal expansion within pre-existing clonotypes. B cell lineage analyses further indicated that expanded serum clonotypes map to GC B cell-associated sub-branches, consistent with ongoing GC-driven evolution underlying intraclonal expansion. This repertoire remodeling was accompanied by increased binding breadth against antigenically divergent influenza viruses. These findings reveal a key role for persistent GC responses in broadening the repertoire of vaccine-induced antibodies.Subject terms: RNA vaccines, Germinal centres

Main

Vaccines have reduced or eradicated the burden of many infectious diseases1. However, for rapidly evolving pathogens such as the seasonal influenza virus, designing fully efficacious vaccines that induce broadly neutralizing, durable antibody responses remains challenging2. One component of the adaptive immune response to target for improved vaccine efficacy is the germinal center (GC) reaction. GCs are microanatomical structures that form in secondary lymphoid organs after engagement of antigen and cognate B cells and T cells3, facilitate the Darwinian selection of high-affinity antigen-specific B cell clones and ultimately enhance antibody responses4,5. Additionally, GCs contribute to long-term protection by producing bone marrow plasma cells, which continuously secrete antibodies6,7, and memory B cells (MBCs), which rapidly differentiate into antibody-secreting plasmablasts (PBs) after antigen re-exposure8. Understanding how to design vaccines that effectively engage the GC reaction to promote both broadly neutralizing antibodies and prolonged immune memory would greatly advance our ability to combat antigenically variable pathogens.

The influenza virus is a significant public health problem, causing 290,000–650,000 annual global deaths9. Seasonal influenza virus vaccines remain the most effective method for reducing disease burden, with vaccines typically targeting the glycoprotein hemagglutinin (HA) used for viral entry into host cells10. However, due to antigenic drift11,12, vaccines must be reformulated yearly13. Additionally, antigenic imprinting contributes to the generation of recall antibody responses that can be less effective against circulating viral strains14,15. These drawbacks underscore the need to determine how different vaccine platforms influence the GC response to influenza virus vaccination. The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), demonstrated that lipid nanoparticle-encapsidated mRNA-based vaccination is an effective alternative to conventional protein-based and inactivated virus-based vaccines in a primary immune response16,17. However, influenza virus vaccination induces secondary recall responses, and it is not known whether mRNA-based vaccination in humans against influenza virus produces a superior GC response compared to conventional vaccines. In mice, potent GCs are induced by mRNA vaccines to influenza virus18,19. In the context of SARS-CoV-2, mRNA vaccines have been found to induce strong GC responses in mice and humans16,20. Memory responses play a critical role in SARS-CoV-2 booster immunizations21,22, resulting in GCs that can broaden the cross-reactivity of antigen-specific clones23. Furthermore, previous research has shown that both mRNA-based vaccines (to SARS-CoV-2) and inactivated virus-based vaccines (to influenza virus) can produce persistent GCs in humans, in some cases up to 6 months after vaccination16,17,24, but how these persistent GCs reshape the antigen-specific secreted IgG repertoire remains to be fully determined.

We sought to characterize human B cell responses to an investigational mRNA-based quadrivalent seasonal influenza virus vaccine (mRNA-1010)25. Ultrasound-guided fine needle aspirations (FNAs) were used to directly sample the GC compartment in draining axillary lymph nodes. Our results demonstrated that mRNA-based vaccination induces robust recall MBC responses and sustained GCs compared to conventional split-virion vaccination. Proteomic analysis of serum IgG clonotypes (Ig-seq) revealed increased diversity of the serological IgG repertoires following mRNA-1010 vaccination, driven by both induction of new clonotypes and intraclonal expansion within pre-existing clonotypes. This was accompanied by increased binding breadth of circulating antibodies against influenza virus variants.

Results

mRNA-based seasonal influenza virus vaccination induces a robust humoral response

We conducted an observational study (WU397) of 75 healthy adults (ages 20–50, median age 30) over the 2022–2024 Northern Hemisphere influenza virus seasons, with 38 participants receiving mRNA-1010 encoding the HA glycoproteins and 37 participants receiving split-virion quadrivalent influenza virus vaccine (Fluarix) corresponding to the recommended virus strains (Supplementary Table 1). Note that these vaccines include slightly different HAs, as the World Health Organization recommends different strains for egg-produced vaccines like Fluarix than vaccines that do not require viral production in eggs like mRNA-1010 (refs. 26,27). Vaccine formulations for the 2023–2024 Northern Hemisphere season were the same as for the 2022–2023 season, except for an update in the A(H1N1)pdm09 virus component (A/Wisconsin/67/2022 for cell culture- and recombinant-based vaccines and A/Victoria/4897/2022 for egg-based vaccines)28. Blood samples were collected at baseline and at 1, 2, 4, 8, 17 and 26 weeks after vaccination. A subset of these participants (mRNA-1010, n = 13; Fluarix, n = 15) enrolled for FNA specimens, collected at baseline and at 2, 8, 17 and 26 weeks after vaccination (Fig. 1a). HA-specific PB responses in the blood were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot (ELISpot) assay against all four vaccine HA proteins for participants in the 2022–2023 cohort. Frequencies of mean HA-specific IgG+ and IgA+ PBs per million peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) peaked in both vaccine cohorts at 1 week after vaccination (Fig. 1b). At peak, participants receiving mRNA-1010 displayed significantly higher frequencies of IgG+ and IgA+ A/H1-specific and A/H3-specific PBs than participants who received Fluarix (Fig. 1c). IgM+ PB responses were greater in participants receiving Fluarix (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). No significant difference was observed in frequencies of influenza B IgG+ and IgA+ HA-specific PBs between the cohorts (Fig. 1c). Correspondingly, we observed significantly higher fold changes in IgG plasma antibody binding titers to A/H1 and A/H3 as measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) at 4 weeks after vaccination in participants receiving mRNA-1010 than in participants receiving Fluarix in the 2022–2024 influenza season (Fig. 1d). These significantly higher titers persisted up to the final time point collected (week 17 or 26). Significantly higher fold changes in IgG binding titers were observed to the influenza B virus HAs in the mRNA-1010 cohort at 4 weeks after vaccination, but not the final time point (Fig. 1d). In the 2022–2023 cohort, hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) titers against A/H1N1 and A/H3N2 increased relative to baseline at 4 weeks after vaccination, but no significant difference was observed in fold change of HAI titers at 4 weeks or 17/26 weeks after vaccination over baseline between mRNA-1010 and Fluarix recipients (Extended Data Fig. 1c). We detected significantly higher fold changes in circulating HA-specific MBCs by flow cytometric analysis (the gating strategy is provided in Extended Data Fig. 1d) in the 2022–2024 mRNA-1010 cohort at 4 and 17/26 weeks after vaccination over baseline (Fig. 1e). Overall, mRNA-1010 induced more robust peak antibody responses than split-virion vaccination.

Sustained GC response to mRNA vaccination

We next characterized the dynamics of GC responses in participants receiving mRNA-1010 or Fluarix. Both types of vaccine are injected into the deltoid muscle, which drains primarily to the lateral axillary lymph nodes. We used ultrasonography to identify and guide FNAs of accessible axillary lymph nodes on the side of immunization. The same lymph node was sampled in each participant at each time point. Flow cytometric analysis detected high proportions of HA-specific GC B cells in participants with active GC responses after vaccination with mRNA-1010 (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2a). Across the 2022–2023 and 2023–2024 vaccination seasons, only in participants who received mRNA-1010 (5/13) did we detect HA-specific GC responses at 26 weeks (approximately 6 months) after vaccination (Fig. 2b). In neither season were sustained GC responses observed in participants who received Fluarix. This was remarkably different from previous studies24,29 in which participants had not received a seasonal influenza virus vaccine within the last 3 years, yet influenza virus-specific GCs were detected after administration of Flucelvax up to 13 weeks after vaccination (2/7, ~28% of participants). Participants in the current study were not excluded based on prior seasonal influenza virus vaccination history, suggesting that the high frequency of persistent GCs detected in participants receiving mRNA-1010 (~38%) was not impeded by prior responses, in contrast to the lack of persistent GCs in participants receiving Fluarix.

Clonally distinct BCRs from the PB, GC B cell and lymph node plasma cell (LNPC) compartments were expressed as monoclonal antibodies (mAbs; n = 2,989) and tested for HA specificity by ELISA (Supplementary Table 8). HA-specific B cell clones were visualized by overlaying on the B cell subtypes, with most HA-specific BCRs derived from week 1 PBs (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 3e).

Assignment of HA specificity to the scRNA-seq BCR data allowed us to analyze the clonal overlap between the GC and PB compartments in participants receiving mRNA-1010 and Fluarix. As the response to influenza virus vaccination is primarily a recall of HA-specific MBCs29,30, the week 1 PB compartment predominantly represents clones with prior antigen experience. B cell clones detected in both the GC (all time points) and week 1 PB compartment are thus most likely derived from MBCs. Most HA-specific GC B cell clones in all of the participants receiving mRNA-1010 (median = 87%) and one participant receiving Fluarix (median = 20%) overlapped with the week 1 PB compartment (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 9). Although most (>50%) GC clones in all of the participants who received mRNA-1010 overlapped with the week 1 PB compartment, some GC clones were not identified as part of the recall response. Although it is possible that recalled clones were simply not detected at our sequencing depth, it is also possible these clones represent naive primary responses being recruited into GCs and providing further antibody repertoire diversification.

To determine if the persistent GCs identified in participants receiving mRNA-1010 altered somatic hypermutation (SHM) and affinity maturation in these HA-specific overlapping clones, we identified 21 pairs of clonally related BCRs present as week 1 PBs and week 26 GC B cells across three of the four participants receiving mRNA-1010 in the scRNA-seq data (no such paired clones were detected in participants who received Fluarix due to the absence of detectable HA-specific week 26 GC B cells in this cohort). In these 21 paired clones, we observed significantly increased immunoglobulin heavy chain variable gene (IGHV) and light chain variable gene (IGLV) nucleotide mutation frequencies in the week 26 GC B cell compartment compared to the week 1 PB compartment, indicating that these clones had undergone further SHM in GCs (Fig. 3b). However, we did not detect significant increases in their binding affinities by biolayer interferometry (BLI) or ELISA (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b).

We further examined the flow cytometry data for evidence that low-frequency MBCs may be engaged in mRNA-1010 vaccine-induced GC responses. Among HA-specific GC B cells in FNA samples, we observed detectable frequencies of both IgG+ and IgA+ cells in participants who received mRNA-1010, whereas the corresponding populations in participants who received Fluarix were predominantly IgG+ (Fig. 3c). Analysis of the kinetics of GC responses demonstrated that IgA+ HA-specific GC B cells were detectable in participants who received mRNA-1010 in the 2022–2023 cohort up to 26 weeks after vaccination (Fig. 3d). Consistent with this observation, IgA+ GC B cells were detected by scRNA-seq in participants receiving mRNA-1010 but not in participants receiving Fluarix (Fig. 3e). Phylogenetic analysis of the IgA clones suggested they potentially arose from both IgA+ MBCs and IgG+ MBCs that had class switched to IgA (Extended Data Fig. 4c). As the ELISpot PB data demonstrated that IgA+ HA-specific PBs displayed, on average, a fivefold lower frequency than IgG+ HA-specific PBs (Fig. 1c), and other studies suggest that IgA+ influenza virus-specific MBCs are lower frequency than IgG+ MBCs29,31, these results collectively suggest that mRNA-1010 engages low-frequency MBCs in GC responses.

Higher number of vaccine-elicited clonotypes in serum following mRNA vaccination

Given the sustained GC responses and engagement of low-frequency MBCs elicited by mRNA-1010, we next examined how these cellular dynamics reshape the secreted IgG repertoire at the clonotypic level using Ig-seq integrated with high-throughput sequencing of BCRs (BCR-seq32; Fig. 4a). For in-depth analysis, we focused on the serological IgG repertoire specific to the A/H3 component of the vaccine (A/Darwin/9/2021) as A/H3N2 is typically the most antigenically variable vaccine component year to year, and we observed a pronounced difference in A/H3-specific plasma binding titers between the two vaccine cohorts (Fig. 1d). We analyzed 16 participants by Ig-seq, including 8 who received mRNA-1010 (06, 14, 17, 21, 23, 28, 29 and 31) and 8 who received Fluarix (05, 09, 19, 20, 22, 24, 27 and 32) from the 2022–2023 cohort. A/H3-specific serum IgG was affinity purified from sera collected at weeks 0, 4 and 17/26 and analyzed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Spectra were assigned by matching CDRH3 peptides to participant-specific BCR databases generated by BCR-seq.

We subsequently categorized individual IgG clonotypes as ‘pre-existing’ or ‘vaccine-elicited’ based on their presence or absence in prevaccination serum (week 0), respectively (Fig. 4b). We note that ‘vaccine-elicited’ does not imply arising from naive responses but rather denotes clonotypes that became newly detectable in serum after vaccination, likely reflecting activation of previously established MBCs differentiating into antibody-secreting cells in the context of recall responses8. Pre-existing clonotypes comprised most of the week 4 A/H3-specific serum IgG repertoire in both cohorts (83.9% in mRNA-1010 and 86.2% in Fluarix; Extended Data Fig. 5a), consistent with recall-dominated seasonal influenza vaccination responses8,29,33.

Across participants, we identified between 67 and 186 total A/H3-specific serum IgG clonotypes per individual, with the mRNA-1010 cohort exhibiting greater clonotypic diversity at weeks 4 and 17/26 than the Fluarix cohort (Fig. 4c). Despite similar contributions of pre-existing clonotypes in terms of abundance, mRNA-1010 induced a significantly greater number of vaccine-elicited clonotypes at week 4 (Fig. 4d), whereas the number of pre-existing clonotypes was comparable between cohorts (Extended Data Fig. 5b). Although this increase diminished over time, it remained modestly higher at week 17/26 (Extended Data Fig. 5c). Notably, vaccine-elicited clonotypes showed stronger evidence of antigen-driven selection, with increased positive selection in CDRs and no corresponding increase in framework regions, as assessed by BASELINe34 (Fig. 4e). Together, these data indicate that mRNA-1010 increases the clonotypic diversity of the secreted IgG repertoire, consistent with enhanced recruitment and selection of diverse MBC-derived lineages in the context of sustained GC responses.

Intraclonal expansion of pre-existing serum IgG clonotypes after mRNA vaccination

Beyond changes in the number of clonotypes, we next asked whether vaccination altered diversification within clonotypes. To quantify this diversification, we enumerated, for each pre-existing serum IgG clonotype, the number of unique CDRH3 peptide variants identified by Ig-seq (intraclonal CDRH3 diversity). Notably, among highly abundant individual pre-existing clonotypes identified in serum after vaccination, many clonotypes from participants who received mRNA-1010 were drastically expanded with increased diversity in the CDRH3 peptides within single IgG clonotypes (Fig. 4f,g). By contrast, pre-existing clonotypes identified in participants who received Fluarix largely remained unchanged in intraclonal CDRH3 diversity at week 4. Several of these newly detected CDRH3 peptide variants could also be identified in the week 1 PB compartment of our scRNA-seq data. Given that week 0 CDRH3 peptides of pre-existing clonotypes represent constitutively secreted IgG from bone marrow plasma cells elicited by prior exposure, this intraclonal CDRH3 expansion could arise from reactivation of clonally related MBCs and, potentially, continued diversification through GC re-entry and selection24,29,35.

When examining CDRH3 composition within each serum IgG clonotype, vaccine-elicited clonotypes exhibited comparable intraclonal CDRH3 diversity between cohorts (Extended Data Fig. 5d), whereas pre-existing IgG clonotypes from participants who received mRNA-1010 displayed higher intraclonal CDRH3 diversity after vaccination (Extended Data Fig. 5e). The frequency of pre-existing clonotypes exhibiting intraclonal expansion (defined by a higher number of unique CDRH3 peptides at week 4 than at week 0) was substantially higher in recipients who received mRNA-1010, with an approximately threefold higher frequency, on average, than Fluarix (Fig. 4h). Moreover, pre-existing clonotypes with expanded CDRH3 peptides contributed more prominently to the postvaccination serum repertoire in participants who received mRNA-1010 than participants who received Fluarix, and this pattern was maintained through the week 17/26 time point (Fig. 4i). The serum IgG repertoire from participants who received mRNA-1010 showed a marked increase in the CDRH3 diversity index, measured as the effective number of species (exponential of Shannon entropy), incorporating both the number and abundance of unique CDRH3 peptides at each time point36–38 (Extended Data Fig. 5f). Together, these data indicate that mRNA-1010 not only increases the number of serum clonotypes but also updates pre-existing IgG clonotypes by eliciting a broader spectrum of intraclonal variants that constitute a major fraction of the postvaccination serum IgG response.

GC-linked lineage structure underlies intraclonal expansion

To further investigate whether intraclonal expansion detected in serum reflected GC-associated evolution, we performed B cell clonal lineage analysis on A/H3-specific serum IgG clonotypes exhibiting expanding CDRH3 diversity and clonally related to GC B cells identified in the scRNA-seq data. Using these criteria, we identified three IgG clonotypes from participants who received mRNA-1010 and none from participants who received Fluarix, likely reflecting the rarity of such clonotypes39,40 together with the low frequency of IgG+ HA-specific GC B cells in Fluarix (Fig. 3d). For these clonotypes, we inferred clonal lineage trees by integrating BCR-seq data with scRNA-seq data and mapped CDRH3 peptides detected in serum by Ig-seq onto lineage members (Fig. 4j). These pre-existing clonotype lineages contained discrete branches corresponding to CDRH3 peptide detected before vaccination (week 0) and expanded CDRH3 peptide variants detected after vaccination. Importantly, GC B cell sequences identified by scRNA-seq (red circles), spanning weeks 8 to 26 after vaccination, localized to sub-branches corresponding to postvaccination CDRH3 peptide variants, providing a direct connection between sustained GC responses after mRNA-1010 vaccination and the emergence of diversified, clonally related IgG variants in the circulating serological repertoire.

Increased antibody binding and neutralization breadth as a result of mRNA vaccination

The fraction of newly detected CDRH3 peptide variants after vaccination, reflecting the combined contribution of vaccine-elicited clonotypes and intraclonal diversification, positively correlated with the change in anti-A/H3 serum titer at week 4 relative to week 0 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.694; Fig. 5a).

Discussion

We observed that mRNA-based seasonal influenza vaccination is an effective alternative to a licensed split-virion vaccine. In participants receiving mRNA-1010, vaccination resulted in higher peak and final time point antibody titers to A/H1N1 and A/H3N2 seasonal influenza viruses over two vaccination seasons. We observed significantly higher peak antibody titers for influenza A and B virus HAs across the 2022–2024 vaccination seasons in participants that received mRNA-1010 than in participants that received a currently licensed standard-dose vaccine, Fluarix. These results agree with the findings of phase III randomized clinical trials for mRNA-1010. Initial trials demonstrated noninferiority for influenza A virus strains but not influenza B virus strains50, but optimizing the mRNA-1010 formulation improved immune responses to influenza B virus strains51. Subsequently, in a phase III trial, mRNA-1010 met prespecified noninferiority success criteria for immunogenicity endpoints versus licensed standard-dose and high-dose vaccines51. Additionally, we observed that mRNA-1010 induced persistent GCs up to 26 weeks after vaccination, whereas no persistent GCs were detected in individuals who received Fluarix. Previous studies from our group demonstrated sustained GCs 13 weeks after vaccination with Flucelvax, but these participants had not received an influenza virus vaccine within 3 years (ref. 24). The higher frequency of persistent GCs in participants who received mRNA-1010 than in participants who received Fluarix suggests that the response generated by mRNA-1010 is not impeded by pre-existing immunity. This is a potential benefit of mRNA-based seasonal influenza virus vaccination, as pre-existing antibody titers have long been suggested to limit the effectiveness of annual vaccination52.

Although the antibody titers observed in this study did not result in significant differences in HAI between 2022–2023 cohorts, our data suggest that mRNA-1010 better engages a diverse pool of low-frequency MBCs, leading to remodeling of the secreted, HA-specific serum IgG repertoire. We observed increased clonotypic representation after mRNA-1010 vaccination, including a higher number of vaccine-elicited serum IgG clonotypes, together with pronounced intraclonal CDRH3 expansion within pre-existing clonotypes. Lineage analyses further linked expanded serum members to GC B cell-associated sub-branches, supporting the notion that sustained GC activity contributes to the emergence of further diversified IgG variants in circulation. Collectively, these processes result in greater breadth of the influenza virus HA-specific serum IgG response. We observed greater binding breadth of mRNA-1010-induced antibodies at both a polyclonal plasma level and across individual mAbs, with evidence of increased SHM contributing to diversification within the repertoire. Increased binding breadth has been observed for individual antibodies specific to SARS-CoV-2 in the context of mRNA vaccine booster immunizations23 and the plasma antibodies of individuals who received mRNA vaccines compared to SARS-CoV-2 infection53. Combined with the results of our study, these data broadly point to the value of mRNA-based vaccines in stimulating broad binding immunity to pathogen variants. Furthermore, additional studies using a multivalent mRNA vaccine against 20 known influenza virus subtypes in animal models54 and sequential immunization of mRNA lipid nanoparticle vaccines in mice55 demonstrate the capacity of mRNA vaccines for generating optimal cross-protection. In our study, boosting of antibody titers to future strains of influenza virus A/H1N1 and A/H3N2 by mRNA-1010 was observed, suggesting that mRNA-based vaccines have the potential to overcome the constant problem of influenza virus antigenic drift10. Although increased neutralization breadth was not statistically significant for antigenically variant A/H3N2 strains, likely due to sample size limitations, other effector functions beyond neutralization are possible for circulating antibodies, including opsonization and phagocytosis, complement activation and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity56. Significant improvements in binding breadth could expand these protective functions across variant influenza virus strains, providing additional mechanisms to combat antigenic drift.

The mechanisms underlying the robust humoral response and persistent GCs induced by mRNA-1010 are still not fully elucidated. It is possible that mRNA vaccines deliver greater amounts of antigen to the draining lymph nodes, resulting in both the stimulation of low-frequency MBCs and prolonged antigen duration to drive GC persistence. It is also possible that the self-adjuvanting properties of lipid nanoparticle mRNA vaccines57,58 robustly activate either professional antigen-presenting cells and/or B cells for a more potent immune response. The form of antigen presented after mRNA-1010 vaccination (that is, membrane-bound) may also contribute to increased antigen valency and thus stimulation of low-frequency and/or low-affinity MBCs. As an mRNA-based vaccine produces HA antigens directly in human cells after administration, it is also possible that the antigens more accurately match the glycosylation patterns of HA antigens produced by influenza virus infection. This would be an advantage over chicken egg-based vaccines such as Fluarix and other cell-based vaccines, which rely on non-human cells for antigen production and may contribute to the improved binding breadth of the antibodies induced by mRNA-1010. Differences in N-linked glycosylation can affect the immune response to the influenza virus59, and different vaccine formulations can alter the structure of HA antigens60.

Broadly, our results demonstrate that although there is a biological ceiling to antibody affinity with repeat antigen exposure, MBCs may re-engage in GCs for further SHM to diversify the antibody repertoire. Sustained GCs stimulated by mRNA-1010 vaccination potentially enhance this expansion of the MBC repertoire by increasing the opportunity for clones to undergo repeated rounds of mutation. This diversification may provide anticipatory mutations to combat rapidly evolving pathogens such as influenza virus61,62. Increased mutation frequencies in antibodies specific to SARS-CoV-2 have been observed to improve resistance to antigenic mutation in the SARS-CoV-2 RBD63 as well as increasing neutralization potency64. Increased mutation can also improve the neutralization breadth of antibodies specific to human immunodeficiency virus65. The functional benefit of MBC lineages diversified by SHM is apparent based on our Ig-seq and serological binding breadth analyses. The presence of pre-existing clonotypes with expanded CDRH3 diversity and abundant newly detectable secreted vaccine-elicited clonotypes in the serological repertoire of participants who received mRNA-1010 but not in those that received Fluarix demonstrates that the diversification of the available pool of secreted antibodies likely contributes to the improved binding breadth observed in the responses of the mRNA-1010 cohort. Thus, it is possible that repeat doses over multiple influenza seasons of a vaccine such as mRNA-1010 may broaden the influenza virus-specific MBC repertoire via SHM in persistent GCs, in contrast to conventional inactivated vaccines, which, lacking broad MBC stimulation or sustained GCs, may narrow the antigenic landscape of recall responses. Although much of our data support the role of broader recall of MBCs after mRNA-1010 vaccination, our GC sequencing and Ig-seq data suggest that there is the potential for recruitment of primary responses to additionally diversify the antibody repertoire. Clones not identified in the recall response but present in the GC or as vaccine-elicited clones in serum can potentially contribute to further increases in antibody binding breadth. As a vaccine that elicits both strong protection against antigenically drifting seasonal influenza virus strains and broad binding against divergent strains is recommended for developing a universal influenza virus vaccine66, the results suggest that mRNA-based vaccines would greatly contribute to advancing this goal.

Methods

Experimental model and study participant details

Study WU397 was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Washington University in St. Louis (2208058), and written consent was obtained from all participants. Seventy-five participants were enrolled. Participants were aged 20–50 years old. Forty-one participants were women, and 34 participants were men. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Investigators were not blinded to experiments and outcome assessments. Prior vaccination against the influenza virus in previous seasons was not included as an exclusion criterion. Participants had not received a seasonal influenza virus vaccine within the last 6 months and had not received any vaccines within the last 28 days. Blood samples were obtained by standard phlebotomy. PBMCs were isolated using Vacutainer CPT tubes (BD); the remaining red blood cells were lysed with ammonium chloride lysis buffer (Lonza). Cells were immediately used or cryopreserved in 10% DMSO in fetal bovine serum (FBS). Three attending radiologists with expertise in ultrasound performed the ultrasound-guided FNA of axillary lymph nodes. Ultrasound of the axilla was performed with a high-frequency (11- to 14-MHz) linear transducer to identify the most accessible lateral axillary node closest to the central axillary vessels. Characteristic features and dimensions of the selected baseline node were documented in annotated images and cine clips, including size, vascularity, distance to central axillary vessels and distance to skin, to facilitate identification and FNA of the same lymph node at follow-up. Six passes, each consisting of approximately 60 needle throws, were made using 25-gauge needles, which were flushed with 3 ml of RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U ml−1 penicillin/streptomycin, followed by three 1-ml rinses. Red blood cells were lysed with ammonium chloride lysis buffer (Lonza). Cells were washed twice with PBS supplemented with 2% FBS and 2 mM EDTA and immediately used or cryopreserved in 10% DMSO in FBS.

ELISAs

ELISAs were performed in MaxiSorp 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Wells were coated with 1 µg ml−1 recombinant HA antigens or bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS (100 µl) and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Plates were blocked with 0.05% Tween-20 and 10% FBS in PBS (blocking buffer). Plasma samples were tested at a 1:30 starting dilution in blocking buffer, followed by seven additional threefold serial dilutions. Recombinant mAbs were diluted to 10 or 2 μg ml−1 in blocking buffer and added to the plates. Plates were incubated for 90 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS. Goat anti-human IgG horseradish peroxidase (Jackson ImmunoResearch) was diluted 1:2,500 in blocking buffer and added to the plates. Plates were incubated for 90 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS and three washes in PBS. Peroxidase substrate (SigmaFAST o-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to develop the plates. Reactions were stopped by the addition of 1 M HCl. Optical density measurements were taken at 490 nm. The half-maximal binding dilution for plasma was calculated using nonlinear regression (GraphPad Prism v10). The threshold of positivity for recombinant mAbs was set as three times the optical density of background binding to BSA. Recombinant mAbs that demonstrated low cross-reactivity at 10 μg ml−1 were further tested at 2 μg ml−1. For testing the avidity of mAbs, ELISAs were performed as described earlier, but wells were coated with 0.1 µg ml−1 recombinant HA antigens (100 µl). Recombinant mAbs were diluted to 30 µg ml−1 in blocking buffer, followed by seven additional threefold serial dilutions. Area under the curve was calculated using GraphPad Prism v10.

Processing of 10x Genomics single-cell BCR reads

Demultiplexed pair-end FASTQ reads were preprocessed using Cell Ranger v.6.0.1 as previously described17 (Supplementary Tables 2–5). Initial germline V(D)J gene annotation was performed on the preprocessed BCRs using IgBLAST v.1.18.0 (ref. 68) with the deduplicated version of IMGT/V-QUEST reference directory release 202150-3 (ref. 69). Isotype annotation was pulled from the ‘c_call’ column in the ‘filtered_contig_annotations.csv’ files outputted by Cell Ranger. Further sequence-level and cell-level quality controls were performed as previously described17. Checks against potential cross-sample contamination were performed by examining the presence of any pairwise overlap between samples in terms of BCRs with both identical unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and identical V(D)J nucleotide sequences. For each group of cells that originated from different samples but whose BCRs had identical UMI and V(D)J sequences, there tended to be only one cell that also had corresponding transcriptomic data. As such, for each group, when there was exactly one cell with transcriptomics-based annotation, only that cell was kept; when there was more than one cell, none of the cells was kept. Together, 112 cells were removed from the BCR data. Individualized genotypes were inferred based on sequences that passed all quality controls using TIgGER v.1.0.0 (ref. 70) and used to finalize V(D)J annotations. Sequences annotated as non-productively rearranged by IgBLAST were removed from further analysis.

Clonal lineage inference for single-cell BCR data

B cell clonal lineages were inferred on a by-individual basis based on productively rearranged sequences as previously described17. Briefly, paired heavy and light chains were first partitioned based on common V and J gene annotations and CDR3 lengths. Within each partition, pairs whose heavy chain CDR3 nucleotide sequences were within 0.15 normalized Hamming distance from each other were clustered as clones. Following clonal inference, full-length clonal consensus germline sequences were reconstructed using Change-O v.1.2.0 (ref. 71).

Single-cell BCR analysis

A B cell clone was considered HA specific if it contained any sequence corresponding to a recombinant mAb that was synthesized based on the single-cell BCRs and that tested positive for binding via ELISA. Clonal overlap between B cell compartments was visualized using circlize v.0.4.13 (ref. 72). SHM frequency was calculated for each heavy chain sequence using SHazaM v.1.1.0 (ref. 71) by counting the number of nucleotide mismatches from the germline sequence in the variable segment leading up to CDR3. Phylogenetic trees for HA-specific B cell clones containing IgA GC B cells were constructed with heavy chains on a by-participant basis using IgPhyML v1.1.3 (ref. 73) and the HLP19 model74. Trees were visualized using ggtree v3.10.1 (ref. 75).

Processing of 10x Genomics single-cell 5′ gene expression data

Demultiplexed pair-end FASTQ reads were first preprocessed on a by-sample basis, and samples involved in a given analysis were subsequently subsampled to the same effective sequencing length and aggregated using Cell Ranger v.6.0.1 as previously described17. Quality control was performed on the aggregate gene expression matrix consisting of 681,188 cells and 36,601 features using SCANPY v.1.8.2 (ref. 76). Briefly, to remove presumably lysed cells, cells with mitochondrial content greater than 30% of all transcripts were removed. To remove likely doublets, cells with more than 8,000 features or 80,000 total UMIs were removed. To remove cells with no detectable expression of common endogenous genes, cells with no transcript for any of a list of 34 housekeeping genes17 were removed. The feature matrix was subset, based on their biotypes, to protein-coding, immunoglobulin and T cell antigen receptor genes that were expressed in at least 0.05% of the cells in any sample. The resultant feature matrix contained 16,539 genes. Finally, cells with detectable expression of fewer than 200 genes were removed. The same quality control criteria were applied when samples were analyzed on a by-participant basis for selection of B cells for expression as mAbs. All expressed cells were included in the analysis combining all participants, regardless of their quality control metrics in the combined iteration. After quality control, there were a total of 621,877 cells from 109 single-cell samples (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7).

Single-cell gene expression analysis

Transcriptomic data were analyzed using SCANPY v.1.8.2 (ref. 76) as previously described17 with minor adjustments suitable for the current datasets. Briefly, overall clusters were first identified using Leiden graph clustering with a resolution of 0.12 (Extended Data Fig. 3a). UMAPs were faceted by participant and inspected for convergence to assess whether there was a need for integration. Cluster identities were assigned by examining the expression of a set of marker genes29 for different cell types (Extended Data Fig. 3b). To remove potential contamination by platelets, 383 cells with a log-normalized expression value of >2.5 for PPBP were removed. Cells from the overall B cell cluster were further clustered to identify B cell subsets using a Leiden graph clustering resolution of 0.95 (Fig. 2c, Extended Data Fig. 3c and Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). Cluster identities were assigned by examining the expression of a set of marker genes29 for different B cell subsets (Extended Data Fig. 3d) along with the availability of BCRs. Clusters 6 and 22 were further clustered at a resolution of 0.10 to differentiate naive cells, MBCs and PBs/LNPCs. Cells found in the PB/LNPC clusters that came from blood samples were labeled PBs, whereas those that came from FNA samples were labeled LNPCs. Cells found in the GC B cell clusters but that came from blood samples and that had a PB-like expression profile were labeled PBs. Although clusters 10 and 17 clustered with B cells during overall clustering, they were labeled ‘B and T’ as these cells tended to have both BCRs and relatively high expression levels of CD2 and CD3E. Cluster 23 showed no marked expression level of any marker gene and was labeled ‘unassigned’. The ‘B and T’ and ‘unassigned’ clusters were subsequently excluded from the final B cell clustering. Heavy chain SHM frequency and isotype usage of the B cell subsets were inspected for consistency with expected values to further confirm their assigned identities.

Selection of single-cell BCRs for expression

Single-cell gene expression analysis was first performed on a by-participant basis. The number of B cell clones to be expressed was determined by balancing cost and maximizing coverage. For clones found in the week 1 PB compartment but not in the GC B cell or LNPC compartment at any time point, one week 1 PB per clone was selected from every such clone with a clone size of at least four cells. For clones found in the GC B cell or LNPC compartment at any time point but not in the week 1 PB compartment, one GC B cell or LNPC per clone was selected from every such clone with a clone size of at least three cells from all participants except participant 397-06 and from every such clone from participant 397-06. Additionally, for every such clone from participants 397-17 and 397-29 that persisted through week 26 in the GC B cell or LNPC compartment to be expressed, one week 26 GC B cell or LNPC per clone was selected from every such clone from participants 397-17 and 397-29 that had a clone size of one or two cells and that persisted through week 26 in the GC B cell or LNPC compartment. For clones found in both the week 1 PB compartment and the GC B cell or LNPC compartment at any time point, one week 1 PB or one GC B cell or LNPC per clone was selected from every such clone from all participants except participant 397-05 and from every such clone with a clone size of at least three cells from participant 397-05. Last, for every HA-specific clone from participants 397-06, 397-17 and 397-29 that was found in both the week 1 PB and the week 26 GC B cell compartments, if not already expressed, one week 1 PB and/or week 26 GC B cell was selected so that every such clone would have one week 1 PB and one week 26 GC B cell expressed.

For selection, where there were multiple choices in terms of compartments and/or time points, a compartment and/or time point was first randomly selected. Among cells with the matching compartment and/or time point, the cell with the highest heavy chain UMI count was then selected, breaking ties based on IGHV SHM frequency. In all selected cells, native pairing was preserved. The selected BCRs were curated as previously described17 before synthesis.

Recombinant monoclonal antibodies and fragment antigen binding production

Selected pairs of heavy and light chain sequences were synthesized by GenScript and sequentially cloned into IgG1, immunoglobulin κ-chain/immunoglobulin λ-chain and fragment antigen binding (Fab) expression vectors. Heavy and light chain plasmids were co-transfected into Expi293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for recombinant mAb production, followed by purification with protein A agarose resin (GoldBio). Expi293F cells were cultured in Expi293 Expression Medium (Gibco) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

HAI assay

HAI assays were performed using the 2022–2023 cell culture-based vaccine virus strains (A/Wisconsin/588/2019, A/Darwin/6/2021, B/Phuket/3073/2013 and B/Austria/1359417/2021). One volume of serum was treated with four volumes of receptor-destroying enzyme (Seiken) at 37 °C overnight before inactivation at 56 °C for 1 h. In a 96-well U-bottom plate (Greiner Bio-One), serial twofold dilutions of 25 µl of receptor-destroying enzyme-treated sera from a 1:2 dilution in PBS (resulting in a 1:10 dilution of sera) were incubated with 25 µl (4 hemagglutinating units) of influenza virus in duplicate wells for 1 h. Subsequently, 50 µl of 1.0% (vol/vol) turkey erythrocytes (Lampire Biological Laboratories) was added to each well. After a 30-min incubation, individual HAI titers were read as the reciprocal of the highest dilution at which 100% hemagglutination was inhibited. The geometric mean HAI titer was calculated for each participant, and titers of <10 were assigned a value of 5 for calculation purposes. All serum samples were tested for nonspecific binding to turkey erythrocytes before performing the assay.

In vitro microneutralization assay

To test neutralization of A/Thailand/8/2022 and A/Darwin/9/2021 (A/H3N2 reassortant vaccine strains), mAbs were threefold serially diluted at a starting concentration of 10 µg ml−1 in infection medium (1× MEM with tosyl phenylalanyl chloromethyl ketone-treated trypsin). They were then mixed with 100 times 1.64 × 105 and 3.5 × 105, 50% tissue culture infectious dose of the respective viruses and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in a total volume of 100 µl, testing each antibody in duplicate. Virus-only controls were prepared by mixing viruses with infection medium. Virus–antibody mixes, virus-only mixes and medium only were then added to MDCK cells seeded at 25,000 cells per well in 96-well plates (Corning) the day prior. Following a 1-h incubation, cells were washed with PBS, and 100 µl of infection medium with serially diluted antibodies was added. After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, an HA assay with guinea pig blood was performed to detect the presence of the virus.

Sequencing-based neutralization assays

Sequencing-based neutralization assays were performed as described in prior work48,49 with a new combined subtype library containing viruses expressing HAs from both (pdm09)A/H1N1 and A/H3N2 subtypes. Briefly, barcoded influenza viruses were generated using reverse genetics methods and plasmids encoding non-HA segments (PB1, PB2, PA, NA, M, NP and NS) from A/WSN/1933 and a barcoded HA from a recently circulating influenza virus. These strains were then equally balanced as a virus library pool and used to assay human sera neutralization titers. To prevent potentially confounding interactions with sialic acids present in human sera, serum samples were treated with a 1:4 dilution of receptor-destroying enzyme and heat inactivation. For the neutralization assay, sera were used at an initial 1:40 dilution and serially diluted 2.7-fold across seven rows of a 96-well plate. The final row of wells was used as the no-serum controls. The virus library was then diluted, added to each well and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1 h. After incubation, 1.5 × 105 MDCK cells overexpressing 2,6-sialyltransferase were added to each well and incubated under the same conditions for ~16 h before the cells were lysed, and barcodes were deep sequenced. Further details on the sequencing-based neutralization assay protocol are available at 10.17504/protocols.io.ewov1962klr2/v1. To process the sequencing data to neutralization titers, we used the modular analysis pipeline seqneut-pipeline and custom code available at https://github.com/jbloomlab/flu_seqneut_with_Ellebedy_lab.

BLI

Kinetic binding studies were performed on an Octet-R8 (Sartorius) instrument. His tags were removed from Fabs using the thrombin cleavage site preceding the tag. Fabs were treated with biotin-tagged thrombin protease (Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by removal of remaining His-tagged Fabs with HisPur Ni-NTA resin (Thermo Scientific). The thrombin protease was removed via Streptavidin Sepharose High Performance affinity resin (Cytiva). Anti-Penta-His (HIS1K) sensor tips (Sartorius) were pre-equilibrated in HEPES-buffered saline (0.15 M sodium chloride, 10 mM HEPES and 3 mM EDTA, pH 7.6) with 0.05% Tween-20 and 1% BSA (kinetic buffer), followed by loading of HA proteins (10 µg ml−1) to 0.5 nm. Thrombin-cleaved Fabs diluted in kinetic buffer (1,000 nM, six twofold serial dilutions) were loaded for 120 s and dissociated for 500 s in kinetic buffer. A HIS1K sensor dipped in kinetic buffer was used as a reference sensor. Kinetic parameters of reference-subtracted kinetic traces were calculated with Octet BLI analysis software v12.1 using a global fit 1:1 binding model.

A/H3 binding titer for Ig-seq analysis

Half-maximal effective concentration values from the ELISAs were used to determine the A/H3 A/Darwin/9/2021-specific serum binding titers. First, Costar 96-well ELISA plates (Corning, 07-200-721) were coated overnight at 4 °C with 4 µg ml−1 recombinant H3 (SinoBiological, 40859-V08H1) and blocked with a blocking solution containing 1% BSA and 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS (pH 7.4). After blocking, serially diluted serum samples were bound to the plates for 2 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with 1:5,000-diluted anti-human Fab secondary antibody–horseradish peroxidase (Sigma, A0293) for 1 h. The plate was rinsed with a washing solution containing 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS three times between every step. For detection, 50 μl of TMB substrate (Thermo Scientific) was added before quenching with 50 μl of 1 M H2SO4. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Half-maximal effective concentration values were derived from a curve fitting function in GraphPad Prism.

High-throughput sequencing for Ig-seq analysis

VH amplicon was prepared as previously described32,77. Briefly, total RNA from bulk PBMCs (1 × 107 cells) was prepared from week 1 postvaccination samples and reverse transcribed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using SuperScript IV enzyme (Invitrogen) and oligo(dT) primer (Invitrogen). VH transcripts were subsequently amplified using the FastStart High Fidelity PCR System (Roche) with gene-specific primers78.

Paired VH:VL sequencing of B cells from each participant was conducted as previously described79,80. PBMCs were isolated as single cells and emulsified with poly(dT)-conjugated magnetic beads to capture heavy and light chain mRNA. These magnetic beads underwent emulsion overlap extension PCR with reverse transcription. All sequences obtained from heavy chain amplicons and paired VH:VL amplicons were merged and annotated using MiXCR 2.1.5 (ref. 81) and used to construct participant-specific search databases as previously established80,82. High-throughput sequences of VH were included for all donors analyzed by Ig-seq. For donors with FNA-derived data (05, 06, 09, 17, 22, 28 and 29), BCR sequence databases were further supplemented with paired VH:VL sequences obtained from both BCR-seq and scRNA-seq. For one additional participant treated with Fluarix (20), paired VH:VL sequences were supplemented from BCR-seq.

Purification of total IgG from serum and subsequent digestion into F(ab′)2

Each serum sample was first passed through a 3-ml Protein G agarose (Thermo Fisher, 20397) affinity column in gravity mode. Flow-through was collected and passed through the column three times. The column was washed with 20 ml of PBS before elution with 5 ml of 100 mM glycine-HCl (pH 2.7). The eluted solution, containing total IgG, was immediately neutralized with 0.75 ml of 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). Purified IgG was digested into F(ab′)2 with 25 μg of IdeS per 1 mg of IgG for 5 h on a rotator at 37 °C and incubated with Strep-Tactin agarose (IBA-Lifesciences, 2-1206-025) for 1 h to remove IdeS.

Antigen enrichment of F(ab′)2 and MS sample preparation

Recombinant A/H3 (SinoBiological, 40859-V08H1) was immobilized on NHS-activated agarose resin (Thermo Fisher, 26197) by overnight rotation at 4 °C. The coupled agarose resins were washed with PBS, and unreacted NHS groups were blocked with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) for 30 min at room temperature. The resins were further washed with PBS and packed into a 0.8-ml centrifuge column (Thermo Fisher, 89868). For each sample, F(ab′)2 was incubated with the individual antigen affinity columns for 2 h on a rotator at room temperature. Flow-through was collected, and the column was washed with 5 ml of PBS. H3-specific F(ab′)2 was eluted with 1% (vol/vol) formic acid in 0.5-ml fractions. Elution fractions were pooled and concentrated under vacuum to a volume of ~10 μl and neutralized using 2 M NaOH.

The neutralized elution samples and flow-through samples were denatured with 50 μl of 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol and 5 μl of 100 mM dithiothreitol at 55 °C for 1 h and alkylated by incubation with 3 μl of 550 mM iodoacetamide for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Alkylation was quenched with 892 μl of 40 mM Tris-HCl, and protein was digested with trypsin (1:30 (wt/wt) trypsin/protein) for 16 h at 37 °C. Formic acid was added to 1% (vol/vol) to quench the digestion, and the sample volume was concentrated to 150 μl under vacuum. Peptides were then purified using C18 spin columns (Thermo Scientific, 89870), washed three times with 0.1% formic acid and eluted with a 60% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid solution. C18 eluate was concentrated under vacuum centrifugation and resuspended in 50 μl in 5% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid.

LC–MS/MS analysis

Samples were analyzed by LC–MS/MS on an Easy-nLC 1200 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid (Thermo Scientific). Peptides were first loaded onto an Acclaim PepMap RSLC NanoTrap column (Dionex, Thermo Scientific) before separation on a 75 μm × 15 cm Acclaim PepMap RSLC C18 column (Dionex, Thermo Scientific) using a 1.6–76% (vol/vol) acetonitrile gradient over 90 min at 300 nl min−1. Eluting peptides were injected directly into the mass spectrometer using an EASY-Spray source (Thermo Scientific). The instrument was operated in data-dependent mode with parent ion scans (MS1) collected at 120,000 resolution. Monoisotopic precursor selection and charge state screening were enabled. Ions with a charge of ≥+2 were selected for collision-induced dissociation fragmentation spectrum acquisition (MS2) in the ion trap, with a maximum of 20 MS2 scans per MS1. Dynamic exclusion was active with a 15-s exclusion time for ions selected more than twice in a 30-s window. Each sample was run three times to generate technical replicate datasets.

MS/MS data analysis

Participant-specific search databases for MS data acquisition were generated using all VH sequences obtained through BCR-seq and scRNA-seq from each participant involved in this study. VH sequences were grouped into clonotypes on the basis of single-linkage hierarchical clustering, with cluster membership requiring ≥90% identity across the CDRH3 amino acid sequence as measured by edit distance as previously described83. These clustered VH sequences were then combined with a database of background proteins, which included a consensus human protein database (Ensembl 73, longest sequence/gene), decoy VL sequences for individual participants and a list of common protein contaminants (MaxQuant) to construct the peptide search database.

Peptide spectra were searched against the database using SEQUEST (Proteome Discoverer 2.4; Thermo Scientific). Searches considered fully tryptic peptides only, allowing up to two missed cleavages. A precursor mass tolerance of 5 ppm and fragment mass tolerance of 0.5 Da were used. Modifications of carbamidomethyl cysteine (static), oxidized methionine and formylated lysine, serine or threonine (dynamic) were selected. High-confidence peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) were filtered at a false discovery rate of <1% as calculated by Percolator (q < 0.01, Proteome Discoverer 2.4; Thermo Scientific). Isoleucine/leucine sequence variants were collapsed into single peptide groups. For each scan, PSMs were ranked first by posterior error probability, then q value and finally XCorr. Only unambiguous top-ranked PSMs were kept; scans with multiple top-ranked PSMs (equivalent posterior error probability, q value and XCorr) were designated ambiguous identifications and removed. The average mass deviation for each peptide was calculated as previously described84. Peptides with an average mass deviation of >1.7 ppm were removed. Peptide abundance was calculated from the extracted-ion chromatogram (XIC) peak area, as previously described83. For each peptide, a total XIC area was calculated as the sum of all unique peptide XIC areas of associated precursor ions. The average XIC area across replicate injections was calculated for each sample. For each dataset, the eluate and flow-through abundances were compared, and peptides with greater than or equal to fivefold higher signal in the elution sample were considered to be antigen specific.

Clonotype indexing and peptide-to-clonotype mapping

High-confidence peptides identified through MS/MS analysis were mapped to clonotype clusters. Peptides that uniquely mapped to a single clonotype were considered ‘informative’, and clonotypes that detected two or more PSMs were kept as high-confidence identifications. The abundance of each antibody clonotype was calculated by summing the XIC areas of the informative peptides mapping to four or more amino acids of the CDRH3 region. The relative amount in serum of each clonotype was calculated by multiplying its relative abundance by the serum titer for that sample.

Estimation of selection pressure

Selection pressure was estimated with BASELINe as previously described34,85. For each IgG clonotype, a representative matched B cell clone was selected from clones corresponding to detected serum CDRH3 peptides. To ensure high-confidence analysis, the clone with the greatest number of available clonal sequences was chosen, prioritizing clones with scRNA-seq data when present. To maintain comparability, analyses were restricted to participants with both bulk BCR-seq and FNA-derived scRNA-seq data. Heavy chain sequences from the selected clone were collapsed into a representative sequence86. Observed and expected mutations were computed using the HH_S5F targeting model, and posterior probability density functions were computed and visualized with calcBaseline and groupBaseline functions with the focused test used as the selection score87. Statistical significance was assessed with the testBaseline function.

Diversity index

Representative CDRH3s matched to peptide sequences identified by MS/MS analysis were used to measure the diversity of CDRH3 within the repertoire. To compare the diversity quantitatively, we calculated the effective number of species (exponential of Shannon entropy, 1D) as previously suggested37,38: 1D=exp(−∑i=1Spiln(pi)), where pi is the frequency of the ith clone, and S is total number of clones in the repertoire.

Phylogenetic analysis of serum IgG clonotypes

High-throughput BCR sequences obtained for Ig-seq were processed with the Immcantation toolkit suite in R v4.4.0. Paired-end single-chain reads were first merged with PEAR v0.9.6 (ref. 88). Low-quality reads were removed, primer regions were masked, and duplicate reads were collapsed using scripts adapted from pRESTO v0.7.2 (ref. 89). Only sequences with at least two reads for VH amplicons were kept and merged with paired VH:VL reads and scRNA-seq reads. Non-productive sequences were filtered using the Change-O package v1.3.0 and were annotated using IgBLAST68. The Immcantation suite pipeline was used to reconstruct the lineage trees71. Novel V genes were detected with TIgGER70. Using SHazaM v1.3.0 (ref. 71), clonal threshold was determined using length-normalized Hamming distance. Clonal assignment was performed using single-linkage hierarchical clustering with SCOPer v1.3.0 (ref. 90). The lineage trees were inferred using IgPhyML v2.0.0 with Dowser v2.3.1 (ref. 91), implementing partitioned maximum likelihood for mixed datasets containing single-chain and paired-chain BCR sequences92. For representative lineages in Fig. 4j, up to 50 unique sequences from non-GC B cells were randomly downsampled to improve visual clarity.

Multiplex fluorescence bead assay

Recombinant HA proteins, SARS-CoV-2 RBD and BSA were incubated overnight at 4 °C with beads of different fluorescence intensity peaks of the 7K and 8K Blue Particle Array Kit (Spherotech) at 40 µg ml−1, with the exception of recombinant HA A/Perth/16/2009, which was used at 10 µg ml−1. Beads were washed twice with 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS, resuspended in plasma samples diluted 1:200 or mAbs diluted to 10 µg ml−1 in 0.05% Tween-20 and 10% FBS in PBS and incubated for 30 min. Beads were washed twice with 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS, stained with IgG–KIRAVIA Blue 520 (M1310G05, BioLegend, 1:100), incubated for 30 min, washed twice with 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS, resuspended in 2% FBS and 2 mM EDTA in PBS and acquired on an Aurora using SpectroFlo v3.3 (Cytek). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.1 (Treestar). Fold change in MFI was calculated for each week 4 or week 17/26 sample by dividing its MFI by the MFI of the corresponding baseline sample. Fold change in mAb MFI binding to specific HAs was calculated relative to the corresponding MFI of that mAb for SARS-CoV-2 RBD.
