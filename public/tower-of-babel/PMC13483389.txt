Ultrasound reliance and landmark-based proficiency in vascular access training: a multicenter cross-sectional survey of anesthesia trainees and teaching faculty in Eastern China
Authors: Enci Liu, Junjie Qin, Xiaoyu Li, Zhanghuan Chi, Xiujun An, Junping Chen, Jingyan Jiang, Bo Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUltrasound guidance has substantially changed vascular access practice, but increasing reliance on ultrasound may be associated with reduced emphasis on landmark-based skills, particularly in resource-limited settings. If reliance on ultrasound is not matched by maintained landmark-based competence, trainees may be inadequately prepared for clinical situations in which ultrasound is unavailable and timely vascular access is critical. We evaluated reliance on ultrasound among anesthesia trainees and examined whether initial training exposure was associated with current technique preference.MethodsIn this multicenter cross-sectional survey, anesthesia trainees and teaching faculty in Eastern China completed questionnaires assessing confidence, technique preference, and training attitudes regarding internal jugular vein (IJV) and radial artery (RA) cannulation. Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors independently associated with technique preference. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies.ResultsA total of 682 valid responses were analyzed, including 454 trainees and 228 faculty members. Trainees reported greater confidence with ultrasound-guided than with landmark-based techniques (P < 0.001). Initial learning method was the factor most strongly associated with current technique preference: trainees who first learned vascular access using ultrasound were more likely to prefer ultrasound guidance for both IJV cannulation (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = 6.83; 95% confidence interval (CI), 3.85–12.11) and RA cannulation (aOR = 5.54; 95% CI, 3.13–9.79). This pattern persisted despite 90.53% of trainees acknowledging the importance of landmark-based skills. In addition, 43.17% reported anxiety about performing vascular access without ultrasound, and 73.25% of faculty perceived declining landmark-based competence among recent graduates. Principal component analysis supported distinct landmark-based confidence constructs for IJV and RA cannulation, whereas ultrasound-guided confidence appeared more generalizable across access sites.ConclusionsAnesthesia trainees showed substantial reliance on ultrasound guidance for vascular access, and initial training exposure was strongly associated with current technique preference. Training programs should maintain structured teaching and assessment in both ultrasound-guided and landmark-based techniques to improve preparedness across varied clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09702-w.

Introduction

Vascular access is a core procedural skill in anesthesiology and is essential for hemodynamic monitoring, drug administration, and resuscitation in critically ill patients. Over the past two decades, bedside ultrasound has substantially changed vascular access practice, particularly for central venous cannulation and difficult arterial access [1–3]. Multiple studies have shown that ultrasound guidance improves first-attempt success and reduces immediate complications compared with landmark-based techniques [4–8]. Accordingly, current international guidelines recommend ultrasound as the preferred approach for central venous access whenever feasible [9–11].

Although these advances have improved procedural safety and efficiency, they have also introduced a training challenge. Ultrasound may facilitate skill acquisition for novice operators and increase procedural confidence [3, 11, 12], but increasing reliance on real-time imaging may reduce exposure to landmark-based techniques during training. Landmark-based proficiency may still be important when ultrasound is unavailable, delayed, or impractical, including in resource-limited settings, emergency situations, equipment failure, or other high-demand clinical environments [13, 14]. If reliance on ultrasound is not matched by maintained landmark-based competence, trainees may be inadequately prepared for clinical situations in which ultrasound is unavailable and timely vascular access is critical. In this context, vascular access training in anesthesiology must balance proficiency in ultrasound-guided techniques with the ability to perform these procedures across a range of clinical conditions.

Despite the clinical relevance of this issue, empirical data on ultrasound reliance during anesthesia training remain limited. Prior studies have focused mainly on procedural outcomes rather than on how training patterns relate to technique preference, operator confidence, and perceived preparedness for practice without ultrasound. In particular, little is known about whether the initial method of instruction is associated with subsequent procedural preference. Clarifying this relationship may help refine vascular access curricula by identifying whether early training exposure is linked to how trainees later choose to perform these procedures.

China provides a relevant setting in which to examine this issue because ultrasound availability and standardized residency training have expanded rapidly in recent years. We therefore conducted a multicenter survey of anesthesia trainees and teaching faculty in Eastern China. The aims of this study were to characterize current vascular access training patterns, assess trainee confidence and reliance on ultrasound, evaluate faculty perceptions of landmark-based competence, and identify factors independently associated with current technique preference.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter cross-sectional survey was conducted in Eastern China between October 14 and November 20, 2025. Two respondent groups were included: trainees, defined as current anesthesia residents or physicians who had completed residency training within the previous 3 years, and teaching faculty, defined as attending physicians or senior consultants actively involved in resident teaching.

Participants provided electronic informed consent before accessing the questionnaire. Trainees were eligible if they were currently enrolled in anesthesiology residency training or had completed training within the previous 3 years. Teaching faculty were eligible if they were currently involved in resident teaching.

Responses were excluded if consent was not provided, questionnaire completion time was implausibly short, major logical inconsistencies were identified, key outcome variables were missing, or duplicate submissions were detected. Implausibly short completion time was defined a priori as less than one-third of the median completion time observed during pilot testing. Potential duplicate submissions were screened by IP address, and only the first entry was retained after review.

Questionnaire development and content

Separate electronic questionnaires were developed for trainees and teaching faculty based on a review of the relevant literature and current practice in vascular access training (Supplementary questionnaire). Draft questionnaires were reviewed by five experts in anesthesiology education for content relevance, clarity, and face validity, and were pilot-tested in 20 residents and 10 faculty members who were not included in the final analysis. Minor wording revisions were made after pilot testing to improve clarity and usability.

The trainee questionnaire assessed demographic characteristics, training exposure, equipment availability, technique preference for internal jugular vein (IJV) and radial artery (RA) cannulation, and confidence in landmark-based and ultrasound-guided techniques. Additional items addressed the number of attempts typically required for cannulation, frequency of supervisor assistance, perceived reliance on ultrasound, and attitudes toward vascular access training.

The teaching faculty questionnaire assessed demographic characteristics, training resources, teaching practices, perceptions of trainee competence and training gaps, and views on assessment and curriculum design. Both questionnaires included single-choice, multiple-choice, and 5-point Likert-scale items.

Anxiety about performing vascular access without ultrasound was assessed using a single Likert five‑level item: “Are you worried that you may not be able to perform vascular access competently when working in a hospital without ultrasound equipment?” Options: very worried, worried, neutral, not worried, not worried at all. No validated scale (e.g., State‑Trait Anxiety Inventory) was used. In results, “very worried” and “worried” were combined as “reported anxiety”.

Data collection

The survey was administered through Wenjuanxing, a secure online survey platform. Because a complete probability-based sampling frame was not available, a pragmatic nonprobability convenience sampling strategy was adopted to facilitate broad geographic dissemination. Invitation links were sent to program directors at 60 hospitals in Eastern China, who were asked to distribute the survey to eligible trainees and teaching faculty through departmental WeChat groups. This approach enabled recruitment across multiple institutions but did not allow calculation of a conventional response rate because the denominator of eligible recipients was unknown. To assess potential nonresponse bias, we compared early and late respondents (using median submission date as a proxy).

To enhance data quality, the platform was configured to restrict repeated submissions from the same IP address or device. Prespecified exclusion criteria were applied during data cleaning, and all responses were anonymized before analysis.

Variable definitions and data processing

For the primary analyses, technique preference for IJV and RA cannulation was defined by the respondent’s self-reported preferred method and dichotomized as ultrasound-guided or landmark-based. Initial learning method was defined by asking: “When you first learned to perform IJV cannulation (and separately for RA), which technique was primarily taught and practiced?” Response options were “ultrasound‑guided” or “landmark‑based.” No “both equally” option was provided. The question referred to the technique systematically taught and practiced during the early learning phase, not necessarily the first technique ever attempted or successfully performed. Frequency of supervisor assistance was dichotomized as needs assistance for responses of often or sometimes and essentially independent for responses of rarely or never. Non-normally distributed continuous and ordinal variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges.

Statistical analysis

The trainee sample size was estimated a priori because trainee-reported technique preference and confidence were the primary outcomes. Using G*Power 3.1, a two-proportion z-test indicated that at least 350 trainees were required to provide 80% power to detect a 15-percentage-point difference in technique preference at a two-sided α level of 0.05. Recruitment continued until this threshold was exceeded.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0 and SPSS version 27.0.1. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, median [interquartile range], or number (percentage), as appropriate. Paired ordinal comparisons were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, paired binary outcomes using McNemar’s test, and between-group comparisons using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For within-participant comparisons of landmark-based confidence across clinical scenarios, the Friedman test followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests was used.

Internal consistency of the landmark-based confidence scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Exploratory analysis of confidence items was performed using principal component analysis with Promax rotation and Kaiser normalization. Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors independently associated with preference for ultrasound-guided rather than landmark-based cannulation for IJV and RA access. Prespecified covariates were age, sex, possession of a standardized training certificate, and initial learning method. Stepwise selection was not used; covariates were prespecified based on clinical relevance and prior literature.

Missing data for key variables (age, sex, standardized training certificate, initial learning method, technique preference for IJV and RA) were minimal (< 5% for each variable). Therefore, complete‑case analysis was performed, and no imputation methods were used. Responses with missing key outcome variables were excluded during data cleaning as described in "Study design and participants "section.

Further details of the statistical analyses are provided (Supplementary methods). Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies (Supplementary Table S1).

Results

Survey participation and respondent characteristics

A total of 731 questionnaires were submitted, including 490 from trainees and 241 from faculty. After application of the prespecified exclusion criteria, 682 responses met quality criteria and were included in the final analysis, comprising 454 trainees and 228 faculty members. Respondents represented 7 provinces and municipalities in Eastern China. Demographic and professional characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Reliability and validity of the trainee questionnaire

The 3-item landmark-based internal jugular vein (IJV) confidence scale and the 3-item landmark-based radial artery (RA) confidence scale showed good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.905 and 0.867, respectively. The 2 single-item measures of ultrasound-guided confidence for IJV and RA cannulation were moderately correlated with each other (Spearman’s r = 0.58, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Principal component analysis with Promax rotation and Kaiser normalization supported a 3‑component structure explaining 84.48% of the variance. Trainee perspectives, confidence, and technique preference

Most trainees reported that landmark-based cannulation remains necessary in current anesthesia practice (90.53%), and 85.24% supported strengthening landmark-based training during residency (Table 3). For IJV cannulation, 68.50% preferred ultrasound guidance, whereas for RA cannulation, 59.69% preferred the landmark-based technique (Table 3; Fig. 1B).

Among trainees, 43.17% (selecting ‘very worried’ or ‘worried’) reported anxiety about performing vascular access in hospitals without ultrasound equipment, and 45.59% reported insufficient ultrasound machine availability during peak clinical periods. Among the 236 trainees with experience in primary-level hospitals, the most commonly reported challenges were lack of ultrasound equipment (50.85%), limited landmark-based skills (39.41%), and insufficient ultrasound proficiency (35.17%).

Trainees reported greater confidence with ultrasound-guided than with landmark-based cannulation for both IJV and RA access (Table 4; Fig. 2). The proportion reporting frequent supervisor assistance (often or sometimes) was lower with ultrasound-guided than with landmark-based cannulation for both IJV (40.22% vs. 63.96%) and RA (36.26% vs. 63.30%) access (all P < 0.001; Fig. 3). Factors independently associated with technique preference among trainees

In multivariable logistic regression, initial learning method was the factor most strongly associated with current technique preference (Table 6). Trainees who first learned vascular access using ultrasound were more likely to prefer ultrasound guidance for IJV cannulation (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = 6.83; 95% confidence interval (CI), 3.85–12.11; P < 0.001) and RA cannulation (aOR = 5.54; 95% CI, 3.13–9.79; P < 0.001) than those who first learned landmark-based techniques. Older age was associated with a lower likelihood of preferring ultrasound guidance for both IJV (aOR = 0.92; 95% CI, 0.84–0.99; P = 0.034) and RA cannulation (aOR = 0.91; 95% CI, 0.84–0.98; P = 0.010). Female sex was associated with ultrasound preference for RA cannulation (aOR = 1.65; 95% CI, 1.10–2.46; P = 0.015) but not for IJV cannulation (aOR = 1.26; 95% CI, 0.82–1.94; P = 0.286). Possession of a standardized training certificate was not significantly associated with technique preference.

Faculty perspectives and training environment

Faculty responses indicated limited ultrasound equipment availability in many departments. Only 5.26% reported a machine-to-operating-room ratio of 1:1 or 1:2–1:3, whereas 94.74% reported a ratio of 1:4 or higher (Table 7). Teaching patterns were consistent with trainee-reported procedural preferences. Ultrasound guidance was the preferred teaching method for IJV cannulation (64.91%), whereas the landmark-based technique was more commonly emphasized for RA cannulation (60.53%) (Fig. 1A). Dedicated departmental courses were reported more often for ultrasound-guided than for landmark-based techniques for both IJV and RA access. A total of 77.63% of faculty supported establishment of dedicated landmark-based training courses.

Faculty rated trainee ability higher for ultrasound-guided than for landmark-based cannulation at both vascular sites (all P < 0.001) (Table 4). Most faculty (73.25%) perceived a decline in landmark-based skills among recent graduates over the preceding 5 years, whereas 2.19% perceived improvement (Table 7). The most frequently cited reasons for inadequate landmark-based training were reliance on ultrasound equipment (71.05%), lack of targeted simulation training (61.40%), and insufficient training time (40.79%). In addition, 46.05% of faculty reported psychological dependence on ultrasound for IJV cannulation (defined as self-perceived difficulty performing the procedure confidently without ultrasound), and 34.65% reported such dependence for RA cannulation (Fig. 5). Most faculty considered landmark-based proficiency necessary or very necessary (89.47%), and 55.70% reported concern regarding trainee preparedness in ultrasound-scarce settings (Table 7). Although landmark-based cannulation was reported to be included routinely in standardized residency assessment, formal departmental evaluation of ultrasound-guided vascular access skills was reported by approximately two-thirds of respondents (IJV: 67.54%; RA: 63.16%). A total of 82.46% supported incorporation of a dedicated ultrasound-guided vascular access component into the final certification examination.

Discussion

In this multicenter survey of anesthesia trainees and faculty in Eastern China, trainees reported greater confidence, fewer attempts, and less frequent need for supervisor assistance with ultrasound-guided than with landmark-based vascular access, consistent with prior studies demonstrating the procedural advantages of ultrasound guidance [1, 15, 16]. To our knowledge, this is the first multicenter study in Eastern China to provide quantified estimates of the adjusted association between initial training exposure and later technique preference for vascular access, and to examine confidence across different clinical scenarios. Specifically, this study quantifies the association between initial training method and later preference (aOR = 6.83 for IJV and 5.54 for RA), shows that landmark‑based confidence declines specifically in emergency and obese‑patient scenarios, and documents a mismatch between ultrasound reliance and limited equipment availability (94.74% of departments have a machine‑to‑operating‑room ratio of 1:4 or higher). These insights highlight the need for balanced curricula and provide baseline data for future interventional studies. The finding that most trainees still regard landmark-based techniques as necessary, despite strong reliance on ultrasound, underscores the perceived gap in landmark competence.

Notably, 43.17% of trainees reported anxiety about performing vascular access without ultrasound. Although anxiety was assessed using a single non‑validated item, it is possible that such anxiety could affect fine motor skills, prolong procedure time, or increase complication risk; chronic anxiety might also contribute to burnout. These broader implications should remain speculative given the measurement approach. The high proportion nonetheless suggests that many trainees feel unprepared for ultrasound‑scarce environments, which may support the consideration of structured landmark‑based teaching and simulation. Future studies should use validated psychological instruments, such as the State‑Trait Anxiety Inventory, to more rigorously assess anxiety and its potential effects.

Trainees who first learned vascular access using ultrasound were substantially more likely to prefer ultrasound guidance for both internal jugular vein and radial artery cannulation. Although causality cannot be inferred from this cross-sectional design, the magnitude and consistency of these associations suggest that training sequence may be a relevant and potentially underrecognized factor in vascular access education.

An important strength of this study is that similar patterns were observed from both trainee and faculty perspectives. Trainee-reported reliance on ultrasound was consistent with faculty perceptions of declining landmark-based competence. Faculty also reported that dedicated teaching opportunities were more commonly available for ultrasound-guided than for landmark-based techniques, and some also reported psychological dependence on ultrasound in their own practice. These findings suggest that trainee preferences may reflect not only individual learning experience, but also a broader learning environment in which ultrasound-guided practice is preferentially taught and reinforced.

Our findings should not be misinterpreted as opposing optimization of ultrasound use or advocating for replacing ultrasound with landmark techniques. In hospitals with genuinely insufficient ultrasound availability, maintaining landmark as a fallback skill is justified. In tertiary hospitals with relatively accessible ultrasound, we consider landmark and ultrasound techniques equally important, not substitutes. Many trainees will work in primary‑level hospitals after graduation where ultrasound is often unavailable. Therefore, maintaining landmark skills as a core competency during residency training precisely addresses this future reality. Landmark skills serve as a rescue competency when ultrasound is unavailable, not as a routine alternative. Where ultrasound is accessible, optimizing equipment sharing and accessibility remains the priority.

The pattern of confidence across clinical scenarios further supports this perspective. Landmark-based confidence declined in emergency and obese-patient scenarios for both internal jugular vein and radial artery cannulation. This is clinically relevant because these situations may be more technically challenging and time-sensitive. Although we did not assess procedural complications or patient outcomes, the combination of lower landmark-based confidence in more difficult settings, anxiety about practicing without ultrasound, and limited equipment availability suggests a potential gap in preparedness for ultrasound-scarce environments.

These PCA results suggest that confidence in landmark‑based IJV cannulation does not automatically transfer to landmark‑based RA cannulation, possibly due to differences in anatomical complexity. In clinical practice, landmark‑based skills may require site‑specific teaching, whereas ultrasound‑guided skills may transfer more readily across different access sites.

These findings may offer exploratory considerations for curriculum design, though caution is warranted given the self‑reported data. Some educators might consider approaches such as teaching both techniques with structured simulation, exploring a minimum number of landmark‑based procedures before or alongside ultrasound training, assessing competency separately for each technique, or including ultrasound‑scarce scenarios in simulation. More specifically, we propose a hypothesis that could be tested in future interventional studies: a training sequence in which landmark‑based techniques are taught before ultrasound guidance. It is conceivable that learning landmark first could help build an equipment‑independent competency, provide a backup when ultrasound is unavailable, and potentially reduce ultrasound dependence. We do not suggest replacing ultrasound with landmark where ultrasound is accessible. However, it remains unknown whether establishing landmark competence before ultrasound training begins would lead to better long-term outcomes. These possibilities are speculative and require experimental validation.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross‑sectional design identifies associations but cannot establish causality. The strong association could be influenced by unmeasured confounders, including institutional culture, equipment availability, supervision practices, and trainee aptitude. Reverse causality is also possible: trainees who already favored ultrasound might have sought ultrasound‑first training, or they may have rationalized their current preference when reporting initial learning method. Selection bias may be present if trainees with different backgrounds were included differently. Therefore, the observed association should be interpreted as hypothesis‑generating rather than causal. Second, self‑reported data are subject to recall and social desirability bias, especially for trainees who completed initial training years ago. Third, self‑reported confidence may not reflect objective competence; future studies should include simulation‑based testing, directly observed skills (e.g., OSATS), first‑pass success rates, and complication rates. Fourth, the nonprobability convenience sampling precluded calculation of a conventional response rate. The late‑respondent analysis showed that late respondents were older and had higher training levels, suggesting nonresponse bias may be present for these variables. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution regarding age and training level. Fifth, residual confounding (e.g., training culture, equipment allocation, case mix, center clustering) remains possible. Sixth, faculty perceptions of declining landmark competence were subjective, based on recalled observations; future studies should link findings to objective metrics. Seventh, this study was conducted only in Eastern China. These findings may not generalize to other healthcare systems. In settings where ultrasound is universally available (e.g., some high‑resource Western centers), the relevance of maintaining landmark competence may differ. In low‑resource settings where ultrasound is often unavailable, the emphasis on landmark skills would be even greater. Readers should assess applicability to their local context. Finally, we did not include objective measures of procedural performance, so the relationship between self‑reported confidence, preference, and actual competence remains uncertain.

Conclusion

This multicenter survey suggests that anesthesia trainees show substantial reliance on ultrasound guidance for vascular access and that initial training exposure is strongly associated with current technique preference. These findings support training strategies that maintain structured teaching and assessment in both ultrasound-guided and landmark-based techniques to improve preparedness across varied clinical settings. Future prospective studies should evaluate which curricular sequences are most effective for maintaining competence in both techniques.
