Viral Detection and Clinical Disease Features in Pediatric Chronic Rhinosinusitis
Authors: Mary C. Wilding, Amber D. Shaffer, Glenn J. Rapsinski, Monika Johnson, Yasmin Hilliam, John V. Williams, Jennifer M. Bomberger, Amanda L. Stapleton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesChronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) results from complex host‐environment interactions with microbiome dysbiosis and viral infections postulated to drive inflammation and anatomic remodeling. This study investigates the impact of viral presence on pediatric sinonasal disease and clinical outcomes.MethodsA prospective, case–control study with retrospective data collection was conducted at a single‐institution tertiary children's hospital. Pediatric patients undergoing sinus surgery for CRS (cystic fibrosis [CF] and non‐CF CRS groups) and a control group undergoing structural septoplasty were enrolled from 2018–2022. Sinus swabs were collected intraoperatively and during up to 3 years of follow‐up. A 14‐virus panel was run. 16S and custom amplicon sequencing and quantitative PCR assessed microbial profiles. Clinical and endoscopic data were recorded.ResultsSinonasal swabs were collected from 15 CF‐CRS, 21 non‐CF CRS, and 32 control patients during initial sinus surgery. At least one virus was detected in 30.9% of samples (n = 21/68): 18.8% (n = 6/32) in controls, 33.3% (n = 7/21) in non‐CF, and 53.3% (n = 8/15) in CF. Human rhinovirus (HRV) was most common, comprising 45.8% (n = 11/24) of viral detections. Across study duration, viral‐positive CRS individuals were 3.49 times more likely to report nasal drainage (95% CI: 1.59–9.25, p = 0.005) and 4.23 times more likely to exhibit discharge on endoscopy (95% CI: 1.40–12.81, p = 0.011) than viral‐negative individuals. HRV‐positive samples had decreased Corynebacterium prevalence (p = 0.025), increased Haemophilus prevalence (p = 0.052), and increased Pseudomonas relative abundance (p = 0.076) versus viral‐negative samples.ConclusionViral infections can exacerbate pediatric CRS by increasing nasal drainage and endoscopic discharge while promoting chronic inflammation and persistent disease.Level of Evidence4

Introduction

Pediatric rhinosinusitis is one of the most common conditions in the United States, reducing school attendance, limiting physical activity, disrupting sleep quality, and adding medical expenses. Yet the initial triggers of chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) remain unclear [1, 2]. Its development is postulated to result from complex host‐environmental interactions, with previous research in adults suggesting that sinonasal microbiome dysbiosis may perpetuate infection and inflammation [3, 4, 5, 6]. Additionally, viral infections may also contribute to CRS pathogenesis through sustained inflammation, leading to increased mucous production, impaired mucociliary clearance, compromised sinonasal epithelial barrier, and enhanced bacterial adhesion to sinus mucosa [4, 7, 8, 9, 10].

In the adult population, research on viral infections in patients with CRS has yielded conflicting results. Some studies report no significant difference in rates of viral infection of sinus mucosa between CRS and control patients [11, 12, 13, 14]. Conversely, others have identified significantly higher rates of viral infection in patients with CRS compared to controls, as detected by nasal lavage or mucosal sampling [15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

Patients with cystic fibrosis (CF) represent a relevant subgroup, exhibiting extensive sinus opacification by school age, driven by viscous mucus and defective mucociliary clearance from cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) protein dysfunction [20, 21, 22]. Given their impaired mucosal defenses, patients with CF are particularly susceptible to viral pathogens. Despite significant radiographic disease, quality‐of‐life impact is often mild, typically limited to nasal obstruction with few reports of activity or emotional impact [23].

In light of the mixed evidence on viral infection rates in CRS and limited pediatric data, we investigated the role of viruses in the cycle of chronic inflammation. Using viral panels collected during endoscopic sinus surgery and follow‐up evaluations of patients with CRS, both with and without CF, we assessed whether the viruses in the sinonasal mucosa correlated with clinical characteristics and microbiome changes and evaluated the impact of recurrent viral infections over time.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

A prospective, case‐controlled, single‐institution study of patients aged 0–25 years seen at UPMC Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh from 2018–2022 was performed with subsequent retrospective chart review. This age range reflects the clinical population managed at this pediatric institution, where patients with chronic conditions, such as CF‐CRS, are often followed into young adulthood prior to transitioning care. The University of Pittsburgh provided Institutional Review Board approval under study number STUDY19030146.

Cohort Enrollment

Fifteen patients with CRS with CF (CF‐CRS), 21 patients with CRS without CF (non‐CF CRS), and 32 control patients were enrolled from September 2018 through April 2022. All patients, identified from surgical schedules, were evaluated by a single pediatric otolaryngologist at UPMC Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh. CRS diagnoses met established diagnostic criteria [3]; controls had no history of CF or CRS. CF and non‐CF CRS patients underwent endoscopic sinus surgery; control patients underwent structural endoscopic septoplasty. Informed consent was obtained from parents for patients aged 7–17 years or directly from patients aged 18–25 years, with assent from minors when appropriate. Sinonasal Outcome Test (SNOT‐22) scores were completed with guardian assistance as needed.

Viral and Microbiome Swab Collection

After induction of general anesthesia and prior to sinonasal instrumentation, a 0‐degree rigid endoscope was used to visualize the middle meatus of the sinonasal cavity. Under endoscopic guidance, the middle meatus mucosa at the maxillary sinus ostium was swabbed twice at the time of initial surgery for all three groups. One swab was frozen and stored at −80°C for 16S sequencing; the other was placed in universal transport medium. In CRS groups, endoscopic guided swabs were repeated at all routinely scheduled visits, dependent on patient age and disease severity, for up to 3 years. Sinus swabs were processed to assess the presence of viral pathogens by real‐time reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) using previously published primers and methodology [24]. Viral RNA was extracted from the swab in universal transport medium and stored at −80°C until use. Single‐plex RT‐PCR reactions were carried out for all viruses in the panel, and suitable negative and positive controls were included in each assay. Fourteen viruses were included in the panel: respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza A and B, human metapneumovirus (HMPV), human rhinovirus (HRV), parainfluenza 1, 2, 3, and 4 (PIV1, PIV2, PIV3, PIV4), adenovirus, and human coronaviruses Netherlands (NL‐63), OC43, 229E, and SARS‐CoV‐2 [24].

DNA Extraction and 16S Amplicon Sequencing

DNA was extracted from the other swab, sorted by participant, and extracted in lots to reduce intra‐participant batch effects. Each batch included an extraction control—an unused sterile flocked swab processed through the entire extraction and purification process. The protocol “Benzonase 2” was performed as described in Nelson et al. with a shortened hypotonic lysis step (incubation at room temperature for 15 min) [25]. Purified DNA was processed for amplification, library preparation, and sequencing at DNA Services at the University of Illinois at Urbana‐Champaign, IL. 16S V4 variable region primers (515f‐806r) were used to generate amplicons which were quantitated by qPCR and sequencing on a NovaSeq flowcell (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Sequence files were imported into QIIME2 v2021.11 [26] and rarefied to an optimal read depth of 11,500 before classifying taxa using the SILVA 138.1 rRNA database [27]. All further analysis was carried out in R v4.2.1. Prevalence and relative abundance data were compared between HRV‐positive and virus‐negative patients using linear mixed‐effect modeling (R package lme4) controlling for participant ID as a random effect with significance defined as p < 0.10 [28]. Similar linear mixed‐effect models were applied to genera with mean relative abundance > 1 across CF and non‐CF CRS samples to assess associations with endoscopic nasal crusting, with ANOVA and Tukey post hoc test identifying differential relationships.

Data Collection

Chart review was conducted to gather demographics, encounter dates, follow‐up length, endoscopic findings, clinical symptoms, and medication usage—including recent oral antibiotics, nasal irrigations, and nasal sprays. SNOT‐22 scores and clinic notes from day of visit detailing current disease state were used to determine whether a viral‐positive result reflected a potentially pathogenic process or an incidental finding.

Data Analysis

Average age at initial operative visit was compared among CF‐CRS, non‐CF CRS, and control groups using one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's HSD. Exact logistic regression was used to compare the prevalence of viral infection between groups at initial operative visit and to evaluate associations between viral status and categorical variables—including endoscopic findings, clinical symptoms, and medication usage—at initial operative visit. Median SNOT‐22 scores were compared between viral‐positive and viral‐negative groups using Mann–Whitney U test.

Exact logistic regression was also used to compare viral infection rates between CF‐CRS and non‐CF CRS groups across all visits, including operative and follow‐up visits. Repeated infection was defined as multiple viral‐positive visits, regardless of operative or follow‐up visit. Additionally, logistic regression models were used to analyze categorical outcomes across all visits for CF‐CRS and non‐CF CRS groups. Mixed‐effects logistic regression models accounted for repeated measures of clinical symptoms and endoscopic findings within patients, with CF/non‐CF status and viral positivity as fixed effects. A random intercept for patient ID was included to adjust for baseline differences in outcome likelihood across individuals. When appropriate, a random slope for follow‐up visit number captured individual variability over time.

Likelihood‐ratio tests were used to compare the fit of standard logistic regression models with mixed‐effects models including a random intercept and further used to determine whether adding a random slope improved fit. The most appropriate model was selected based on these tests. The continuous variable SNOT‐22 was best analyzed via a linear regression model. Statistical analyses of clinical symptom and endoscopic data were performed with Stata version 18.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX), with a p value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

A total of 15 patients with CF‐CRS, 21 patients with non‐CF CRS, and 32 control patients had sinonasal swabs collected at the time of initial sinus surgery. The CF‐CRS group was 53.3% male, the non‐CF CRS group was 52.4% male, and the control group was 59.4% male. One‐way ANOVA indicated a significant age difference at initial operative visit across groups, (F(2,65) = 5.44, p = 0.007), with the CF‐CRS group significantly younger than the control group (p = 0.008). Viral Infection at Initial Operative Evaluation and Associated Findings

At the time of operative visit, 30.9% of samples (n = 21/68) were positive for at least one virus, varying by group: 6/32 (18.8%) control, 7/21 (33.3%) non‐CF, and 8/15 (53.3%) CF patients. A total of 24 viruses were detected (3 samples were positive for > 1 virus). HRV was the most prevalent virus and accounted for 45.8% of all viruses detected (n = 11/24). Compared to the control group, the CF group had a significantly higher proportion of operative visits with at least one virus detected (OR: 4.76, 95% CI: 1.06–23.52, p = 0.022) and greater frequency of HRV detection (OR: 7.12, 95% CI: 0.98–85.91, p = 0.026) (Table 1).

Endoscopic findings showed no significant differences in nasal polyps, discharge, edema, scarring, or crusting between viral‐positive and viral‐negative patients in any group. Subgroup analysis showed that nasal crusting was more prevalent in HRV‐positive compared to HRV‐negative patients within the non‐CF CRS group (95% CI: 0.85–∞, p = 0.035), though the estimate was limited by small sample size. No significant differences on endoscopy were observed between HRV‐positive and HRV‐negative patients in the CF‐CRS or control groups.

Clinical symptoms, SNOT‐22 scores, and medication usage—including nasal irrigations, nasal sprays, or oral antibiotics within 2 weeks of surgery—were not significantly different between viral‐positive and viral‐negative patients across any of the three groups.

Longitudinal Analysis of CF‐CRS and Non‐CF CRS Groups

The CF‐CRS group included 15 patients assessed across 95 total encounters, with 80 as follow‐up visits from 14 patients after their initial enrollment. In comparison, the non‐CF CRS group included 21 patients with 54 total encounters, 33 of which were follow‐up visits from 10 patients post initial enrollment. The CF‐CRS group had a significantly longer median evaluation period of 22.92 months (range: 1.52–36.71) from initial operative date versus 2.13 months (Range: 0.58–33.77) in the non‐CF CRS group (p = 0.018, Table 2). At least one virus was detected in swabs from the operative visit and/or clinic follow up in 32.6% of CF‐CRS visits (n = 31) and 24.1% of non‐CF CRS visits (n = 13), with HRV remaining the predominant virus in both the CF group (n = 24) and the non‐CF CRS group (n = 6) over the duration of the study (Table 2).

Patients with repeated viral infections were 35.67 times more likely to exhibit edema on endoscopic examination compared to those with zero or one infection (p = 0.014). Effect of Human Rhinovirus Infection on Sinus Microbiota

Finally, analysis assessed whether viral infections changed the sinus microbiota across the cohort, focusing on HRV infection as this was the most common virus detected. There were changes to the prevalence and abundance of important bacterial genera in HRV‐positive samples, including decreased prevalence of Corynebacterium, a common commensal organism, and increased prevalence of Haemophilus (Figure 2). H. influenzae and H. parainfluenzae commonly colonize children with CF [29, 30, 31] and are associated with pulmonary exacerbations [32]. Despite this, there was no significant difference in relative abundance of either Corynebacterium (5.7% decrease; p = 0.47) or Haemophilus (3.6% increase; p = 0.72) in HRV‐positive samples. Relative abundance of Pseudomonas was significantly greater in HRV‐positive samples compared to viral negative samples (Figure 3), with the mean abundance increasing from 4.9% to 13.1% (p = 0.076) when controlling for patient disease state.

Discussion

Pathogenesis of chronic rhinosinusitis is a multifactorial process influenced by host microbial and environmental factors, leading to persistent inflammation [3, 4]. Viral infections commonly trigger acute symptom exacerbations with worsening inflammation, nasal congestion, and facial pain [4, 7]. Although viruses have been demonstrated to disrupt homeostasis in the sinus mucosa, the prevalence of these viral infections in patients with CRS compared to controls has been unclear [4, 7, 8, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Despite viral infections being more common in children compared to adults [33, 34], studies in pediatric CRS are less abundant. Our study corroborates the uncertainty seen in the adult literature, with viral infections being significantly more common in the CF‐CRS population compared to controls, but less distinct differences between control and non‐CF CRS populations.

Variability in reported CRS viral infection rates across studies may reflect variations in anatomic sampling site, influencing both detection sensitivity and representation of the local inflammatory environment of CRS. Liao et al. proposed that the inferior turbinate, commonly used in previous studies, may not accurately represent the viral burden relevant to CRS pathology since inflammation in CRS primarily occurs in the middle meatus and sinuses [11]. Furthermore, nasal lavage sampling can overestimate positivity by capturing airborne viruses [15]. Our study addressed these confounders by sampling the middle meatus, with care to avoid contamination from the anterior nasal cavity.

Longitudinal follow‐up of non‐CF CRS and CF‐CRS populations showed continued respiratory virus presence, suggesting a conducive environment for infection and inflammation. Despite shorter follow‐up in the non‐CF CRS group, viral infection over time rather than at a single instance was still assessable in both groups. Accounting for CF‐status and individual patient variability strengthened our ability to attribute observed changes to viral status.

Prior research has not identified a strong association between presenting symptoms and concurrent viral infection, indicating symptoms alone may not reliably distinguish viral positivity [11, 17, 35]. While we included symptom assessments to contextualize viral PCR results, the possibility of residual viral nucleic acids or incidental detection cannot be fully excluded, as PCR‐based assays may remain positive beyond the period of active infection. Nonetheless, despite similar SNOT‐22 scores in viral positive and negative individuals, longitudinal follow‐up of the CRS cohorts revealed viral detection was linked to increased symptomatic drainage, indicating the presence of clinically relevant infections. While previous literature reports mixed effects of viral infection on endoscopic disease severity [11, 16, 17], we found increased visible endoscopic discharge during viral positivity, correlating viral presence with objective symptom burden during active disease.

Cho et al. suggested that the absent symptom increase may reflect a pre‐symptomatic or asymptomatic viral infection—both common in respiratory viruses [15, 36, 37, 38]. As highlighted above, the lack of symptom or endoscopic differences beyond nasal discharge does not negate the clinical relevance of viral presence in CRS. Previous studies demonstrated that epithelial barrier dysfunction in CRS may facilitate viral entry and provoke aberrant immune responses and impaired mucociliary clearance, sustaining the inflammatory milieu of CRS [4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 39, 40].

Beyond changes to CRS symptoms, viral infections can shape the respiratory microbiota composition [41]. We observed viral infection associated with increases in prevalence and relative abundance for Haemophilus and Pseudomonas, respectively. In the context of CF, viral infections have been associated with increased detection of common pathogens, including Pseudomonas, Haemophilus, Moraxella, and Streptococcus pneumoniae [42, 43], which are linked to heightened airway inflammation and pulmonary function decline [44].

Moreover, research in otherwise healthy children shows that symptomatic and asymptomatic viral infections are associated with denser colonization by these respiratory pathogens [45]. Decreased prevalence of commensal organisms, specifically Corynebacterium, has been associated with respiratory viral infections and a subsequent rise in pathogenic bacteria [46]. Our findings of decreased Corynebacterium prevalence in HRV‐positive samples, along with reduced Corynebacterium relative abundance in non‐CF CRS patients with nasal crusting, align with the idea that viral disruption of the native microbiome may promote pathogenic growth and worsen airway disease.

By longitudinally following our patient cohort, our study offers novel insights into the impact of recurrent viral infections in pediatric CRS. Increased endoscopic edema and greater intranasal steroid use among patients with recurrent viral infections suggest repeated viral exposure perpetuates sinonasal inflammation. Rhinoviruses, in particular, can disrupt the integrity of the nasal epithelium and modulate immune response via cytokines, likely contributing to sustained immune activation and prolonged inflammation [7, 8, 47]. Longer‐term studies examining the impact of repeated viral exposures on symptom burden are warranted to better understand these effects and to support the development of targeted therapies in managing these patients.

One limitation of our study is the significantly shorter follow‐up duration in non‐CF CRS patients compared to CF‐CRS patients, impeding a representative assessment of viral infection rates over time. Follow‐up analysis lacked a control group, limiting comparisons of the three groups to the single swabs taken at surgery. Additionally, the viral panel used targeted only common respiratory viruses; therefore, less common viruses may have gone undetected. Small cohort numbers of control and non‐CF CRS participants led to insufficient statistical power to draw conclusions about the effect of disease state on the impact of HRV co‐infections on the sinus microbiota. Because respiratory viral infections can alter pathogen burden in healthy children and those with CF, future studies should include a larger cohort of non‐CF participants alongside those with CF to clarify the role of disease in dysbiosis.

Conclusion

In the populations examined, the prevalence of viral infection differed across groups, with viral presence being more common in CF‐CRS patients compared to controls. Viral infections contribute to disease exacerbations, marked by increased symptomatic nasal drainage and endoscopic discharge. While the precise role of viral infections in the pathogenesis and progression of CRS remains to be fully elucidated, recurrent infections may promote chronic inflammation and mucosal remodeling, potentially contributing to the refractory nature of the disease.
