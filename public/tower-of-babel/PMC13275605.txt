Effect of dairy calf rearing systems on affective states
Authors: Malina Suchon, Daniel M. Weary, Marina A. G. von Keyserlingk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dairy calves are typically reared in environments that differ greatly from naturalistic conditions: separated from their dam after birth, housed without peer contact, and unable to graze or have outdoor access. An improved understanding of the effect of the farming environment on calves’ affective states may help refine management practices to improve dairy calf welfare. In this narrative review, we examine how characteristics of dairy calf rearing systems relate to both short- and long-term affective states. Affective states are commonly inferred from behavioral proxy measures (e.g., changes in time budgets, play, etc.) or conditioned responses to affective-state paradigms. We categorized environmental elements through physical, social, and temporal components and discuss the current knowledge on their effects on indicators of affective states. Consistent evidence supports that physical features such as adequate milk allowance, different sources of solid food (e.g., concentrate and forage), soft and dry lying surfaces; and a social environment allowing full contact with conspecifics appear important for promoting positive affective states and reducing negative ones in dairy calves. Although the temporal characteristics of environmental features remain comparatively underexplored, available evidence suggests factors such as timing, predictability, and control can modulate how physical and social components are experienced. Overall, knowledge regarding longer-lasting mood states in dairy calves remains limited. Addressing these gaps through future research could help refine rearing practices that support not only the reduction of negative states, but also the promotion of positive welfare in dairy calves.

Introduction

Under naturalistic conditions, calves are able to engage socially with conspecifics and navigate environments that vary in biotic and abiotic elements [1]. In contrast, on most farms, dairy calves are separated from their dam immediately after birth, housed without peer contact, and are unable to graze or have outdoor access [2–4]. Growing evidence indicates that conventional calf housing used on farms provides limiting opportunities for animals to express motivated natural behaviours and can induce negative affective states [5].

Affective states (i.e., emotions and other feelings experienced as pleasant or unpleasant [6]) can be described through their valence (i.e., positive or negative) and arousal (i.e., relative to the intensity of the affective experience; [7]). Two categories of affective states are commonly distinguished: short-term states, sometimes referred to as emotions, and longer-term mood states [8]. Short-term affective states in dairy cattle are commonly inferred using spontaneous physiological (e.g., cortisol, heart rates variability, eye/nose temperature) or behavioral responses, including stimulus-directed behaviors (e.g., escape, withdrawal) and changes in daily behavioral patterns (e.g., vocalizations, facial expressions, inactivity, play; see review [9]). However, the performance of such behaviors can be influenced by multiple factors, including contextual elements that should be accounted for when using those measures to draw inferences regarding the arousal or valence of animals' affective experiences. In addition, several experimental paradigms based on learned responses have been developed to enable inferences on the relative valence of experiences, such as preference tests (e.g. [10]), approach/avoidance tasks (e.g. [11]), and motivation tests assessing willingness to ‘work’ to avoid or access an experience [12]. In contrast, mood states are thought to result from the accumulation of positive and negative emotional experiences [13], and are known to affect the appraisal and expectation of stimuli and events (i.e., cognitive bias; [14]). A range of paradigms has been developed to assess mood states in cattle by measuring differences in sensitivity to reward gain and loss (e.g. [15]), attention allocation (i.e., attention bias tests, e.g. [16]), or appraisal of ambiguous cues (i.e., judgement bias test, e.g. [17]), .

To our knowledge, no attempt has been made to summarize the available scientific information on how calf rearing environments influence affective states. The aim of this narrative review was to synthesize current knowledge on how characteristics of dairy calf rearing systems relate to affective states in calves.

In the farm animal literature, environments providing only those resources needed to maintain biological functioning are often described as ‘conventional’, whereas additional resources are referred to as ‘complex’ or ‘enriched’ [18]. However, describing environments along a gradient from less to more “complex” can be misleading, as environments may be heterogeneous in some dimensions but homogeneous in others. Conceptual frameworks have therefore emerged to classify environmental features across multiple domains (e.g., based on animal welfare outcomes [19]). We have elected to use a three-dimensional framework characterizing environmental elements through physical, social, and temporal components [20]. The physical component encompasses biotic and abiotic features of the environment; the social component includes interactions with conspecifics and humans; and the temporal component captures characteristics of exposure to stimuli, such as timing, frequency, predictability, and control.

This narrative review synthesizes peer-reviewed literature on how characteristics of dairy calf rearing environments relate to affective states. Relevant studies were identified through targeted searches of scientific databases (specifically PubMed and Google Scholar) and screening of reference lists of publications deemed highly relevant to the research question. The three-component framework was defined a priori and used to organize the literature. Evidence from studies using behavioral, physiological, and cognitive indicators of affective states was synthesized qualitatively, focusing on how specific environmental features and characteristics of exposure may influence both short-term affective states and longer-term mood states in dairy calves. As a narrative review, this synthesis did not include a formal systematic search protocol or structured quality appraisal of included studies.

Effect of the physical component of the environment

Nutritive environment

Milk feeding

While calves fed milk ad libitum have been reported to consume up to 16 L/day [21], many farms offer less than 6 L/day [22–24]. In comparison to calves fed more milk, those fed 6 L of milk/day or less vocalize more frequently (5 vs. 8 L/day [25]), perform less locomotory play (6 vs. 12 L/day [26]; 5 vs. 9 L/day [27]) and show a higher frequency of unrewarded milk-feeder visits (believed to reflect motivation to access milk; 4 vs. 12 L/day vs. ad libitum [21]; 6 vs. 8 vs. 10 vs. 12 L/day [28]). Similar responses are reported when milk allowance is reduced over weaning, i.e., the transition from a milk to a solid feed diet [29]. Collectively, these findings show that feeding dairy calves restricted milk rations is associated with behavioral responses often interpreted as being consistent with short-term negative affective states associated with hunger [30].

Although rarely studied, the characteristics of the milk delivery, such as the outlet type or frequency, may also impact affective states. For instance, bucket feeding, which limits opportunities to suckle, has been associated with increased non-nutritive oral behaviors (NNOB; e.g., cross-sucking [31]) in comparison to teat feeding. Abnormal behaviors are thought to be developed in response to adverse environments [32], suggesting that providing milk in ways that do not allow for suckling may lead to thwarted sucking motivation and potentially negative affective states, independent of nutrient intake. Milk feeding frequency appears to impact milk intake, with calves offered the same volume of milk per day but in fewer meals showing lower milk intakes during the first weeks of life (1 vs. 2 feedings/day [33]), more frequent unrewarded visits to the milk feeder (2 vs. 4 feedings/day [34]), and reduced play behaviour (1 vs. 2 feedings/day [35]); these responses are commonly interpreted as consistent with hunger-related negative affect, although sensitive to context-dependent factors. Overall, milk delivery characteristics appear to influence calf affect through multiple, and potentially overlapping, mechanisms, including sucking motivation and nutrient intake. Other feeding characteristics, such as milk volume per meal and feeding duration, may further modulate these responses.

Automatic milk feeders provide access to a teat, and the consumption of the daily milk allowance can be apportioned over multiple daily visits. However, once the allocated milk has been consumed, visits become unrewarded (non-nutritive) until the next allocation; the unexpected inability to feed may elicit frustration, as evidenced by acute behavioural and physiological responses such as elevated heart rate, increased self-grooming, head shaking, tongue rolling, and reduced play [36]. Whether these short-term responses translate into broader or longer-term affective outcomes associated with automatic milk feeding systems remains unclear.

Solid feed

Attributes of solid feed, including taste and physical characteristics, are known to elicit affective reactions in animals in the form of hedonic or aversive responses [37]. Young cattle display food preferences for specific feed components (e.g., high protein [38]) and physical characteristics, favoring texturized concentrate over pellet or meal forms [39] and working harder to access long hay than chopped hay [40].

On farms, dairy calves are almost always provided concentrate grain, but availability of roughage varies [3, 41, 42]. Although heifers and milk-fed calves typically consume grain first or in larger quantities than hay or other roughage (e.g. [40, 43]), they will eat multiple feed types when given the opportunity (e.g. [44, 45]), and fluctuate the food most eaten within meals and from day to day [46]. Calves and weaned heifers are willing to work for hay access even when concentrate or milk was available (dairy calves [40]; beef heifers [47]), and offering roughage to individually housed milk-fed calves is associated with reduced NNOBs, likely due to the availability of more opportunities to chew and ruminate (e.g. [48]). Heifers vary in their preference for dietary variety, with some showing greater acceptance of changing forages or feed flavors [45].

Collectively, this evidence highlights the importance of offering different sources of solid food to young cattle, as reflected in measures of preference and feeding motivation associated with positive short-term affective states. However, welfare implications are not solely linked to limited feed diversity and feed restriction. For example, excessive feeding with unbalanced diets has been associated with altered biological functioning in weaned dairy heifers (e.g. [49]), and is also likely to induce negative affective states.

Rearing substrate

On farms, dairy calves can be housed on various substrates (i.e., surfaces and bedding), ranging from concrete, stones, and dirt, with or without the provision of bedding such as straw or sawdust [4, 50, 51]. Calves demonstrate preferences for certain substrates; for example, Worth et al. [10] found that calves spent more time lying and standing on sawdust than on other substrates (i.e., rubber, sand, and stones) and rearing calves on harder substrates (sawdust vs. rubber, sand, and stones) decreased play [10, 52]. Although this reduction in play may be consistent with negative affect, it may also reflect the increased difficulty of engaging in play on these surfaces.

Rearing substrates influence lying behaviour, with hard or humid surfaces reducing lying, while greater and more frequent straw provision increases this behaviour [52–54]. Calves may lie for up to 18 h/d [55] and lying deprivation is associated with stress and rebound effects in adult cattle [56, 57]. Disruption of lying behaviour may be associated with negative affective states. However, we recognize that this predictive relationship is based on indirect evidence and has not been specifically tested. Overall, providing soft, dry lying surfaces such as straw or sawdust appears important for calf welfare.

Space allowance

Housing calves in restricted spaces, including hutches or indoor pens, can limit their ability to perform behaviors like locomotory play; a series of studies have shown that smaller pens are associated with reduced time playing and fewer play behavior elements (0.9 m x 1.5 m vs. 1.8 × 3.0 m [58]; 1 × 1.5 m vs. 1.5 × 1.5 m vs. 2.0 × 2.0 m [59]). Similarly, calves housed in smaller environments ran longer when given access to a larger arena, demonstrating a rebound effect [52]. Longer confinement in individual pens (i.e., 0.9 m x 1.7 m) produced an even stronger rebound effect [60], likely indicating that such home pens restricted opportunities for active behaviors. The shape of the space also influences locomotory play; Mintline et al. [61] reported that calves spent more time running when given access to long arenas compared to wide ones. Overall, these findings suggest that dairy calves are motivated to perform locomotory play and that housing calves in small spaces reduces their ability to engage in these behaviors, potentially causing them to experience short-term negative affective states. However, locomotory play is also influenced by factors such as flooring ([52]), social context [58], age and health [62]; these factors should be considered when interpreting variation in play behavior.

Additional physical items

Providing cattle with brushes has been associated with short-term positive affective states, as evidenced by high motivation to access the brush [63] and rebound use after deprivation [64]. Brushes are used by young cattle for grooming and oral manipulation [65], and brush access is associated with reduced abnormal oral behaviours [66] and increased play [67, 68]. Calves appear to favour mechanical over stationary brushes [69] and have preferences for specific brush locations [64].

The provision of several physical items simultaneously can increase play in both individual (i.e., individual hutches containing artificial teats, a stationary brush, a rubber chain, and a PVC pipe [70]) and group housing (i.e., cross-bred dairy calves housed with a ball, a tire, a hanging chain, and a scratcher [71]) and also reduce NNOB (i.e., stationary brushes, plastic chain, rubber teat, and a hay net filled with scented hay [72]; stationary brushes, ropes or springs, dry teat, and strawberry-scented hay [73]). Access to these devices also appears to affect mood. Neave et al. [74] found that pair-housed calves reared in basic pens (i.e., 2 m²/calf with river stone substrate) showed greater sensitivity to both reward and loss of reward than calves housed in well-resourced pens (i.e., 5 m²/calf, with woodchip bedding and access to an automated brush, manila rope, and straw). Reward sensitivity is expected to be modulated by mood, such that animals in less positive states show greater motivation to access rewards [75] and stronger responses to reward loss [76]. However, this relationship is not necessarily linear; experiencing severe negative affective states can also lead to anhedonia (i.e., reduced ability to enjoy rewards [77]). Findings from Neave et al. [74] suggest that calves in basic pens showed a pattern more consistent with a moderate negative mood state than anhedonia. Overall, providing multiple physical items, beyond that needed to eat and drink, may promote short and long-term positive affect in calves, though more research is needed on mood states. However, because most studies include multiple concurrent changes in housing conditions (e.g., space allowance, bedding type, and availability of additional items), it is difficult to isolate the contribution of individual elements to these affective differences.

Pasture access

Although pasture systems for dairy calves are common in some regions (e.g., New Zealand [78], Ireland [79] or Uruguay [80]), most research has focused on adult dairy cattle. Therefore the majority of evidence discussed in this subsection is derived from studies using adult cattle rather than calves.

Adult dairy cattle are motivated to access pasture [81], and prefer pasture over smaller or more restricted outdoor areas [82, 83], with use influenced by distance from the barn, time of day, and weather conditions [84–86]. While these findings primarily reflect short-term affective responses, some evidence also suggests potential longer-term effects on mood. For example, although no differences were observed in responses to ambiguous stimuli in a judgement bias, adult cows with overnight pasture access showed lower reward sensitivity [87], consistent with more positive mood states.

To our knowledge, only one study has explored dairy calf preference for outdoor access, reporting increasing use during the evening and with age, and declining use in rain [88]. No work has assessed the effect of outdoor access on affective states in calves, but work on this topic is warranted given that outdoor access can provide opportunities for grazing, oral manipulation and locomotion, larger space, and a soft substrate, features discussed earlier as important for dairy calves.

Effect of the social component of the environment

Access to conspecifics

Access to similar-age partners

Most dairy calves are separated from the dam within 24 h of birth and then reared individually or in groups that vary in size from two to dozens of calves [3, 22, 89]. Evidence suggests that social housing elicits positive short term affective states; for example, individually housed calves show motivation for full social contact with peers of similar age [90] and reduced motivation for partial contact [91]. Social housing has been linked to increased play in some studies (individual vs. pair housing [72, 92]; individual vs. groups of 4 calves [93]) but not others (individual vs. pair [27]; individual vs. group of 4 [58]), but these responses may also, at least in part, be influenced by other factors affecting play, such as increased space allowance needed to accommodate groups, discussed earlier in this review. Evidence suggests that the presence of a peer can downregulate the effect of stressors such as weaning (individual vs. pair housing: [95]) and exposure to novel environments [96, 97]. Additionally, pair-housed calves display more optimistic responses in a judgment bias task than individually housed calves [94], which is typically interpreted as reflecting a more positive mood state. Overall, these findings suggest that full social contact promotes positive affective states (short and long-term) and mitigates stress compared to partial or no contact. However, the effects of different group sizes on affective states remain poorly understood.

Calf social behavior includes both affiliative and agonistic interactions. When allowed full physical contact, calves can form preferential relationships with familiar individuals [98, 99], the presence of which can mitigate responses to stressors (e.g., social buffering during separation from the group [96]), suggesting a role in promoting more positive affective states. In contrast, agonistic interactions between calves are observed when resources are limited (e.g., ratio of calves to number of teat buckets increases [100]), or following mixing of new calves into the group [101], although the responses have been reported to be lower than in adult cattle [102]. Group composition further influences agonistic interactions, with older individuals performing more displacements [103] and group mixing increasing displacements towards unfamiliar calves [101]. Stress arising from social novelty and more frequent agonistic interactions has been linked to anhedonia in weaned heifers (indicative of a negative mood state; [104]).

Previous social experiences can also influence calves’ social interactions and the resulting affective states: socially reared calves show stronger preferences for social contact in various contexts than those isolated from birth (novel arena [98], social novelty [105], social feeding [106]). Pair-housed calves performed better when exposed to feeding competition than individually housed calves [107, 108], suggesting the former are less affected by social stress in competitive contexts. Such effects can be long lasting, as lack of early social contact is associated with lower social rank later in life [109, 110], while pair-housed calves are less affected by social competition after regrouping at an adult age than those reared individually [111]. Collectively, these findings indicate that early social environment shapes later social interactions and social competence, which is likely to influence affective responses to social stress.

Access to older individuals and to the dam

Although the early separation of the calf and the dam is common in dairy systems, there is now increased interest in cow-calf contact rearing [112, 113]. When kept together, the dam and her offspring form a preferential relationship characterized by close proximity and affiliative behaviors [114–116], which can persist over time [117]. Separation from the cow is associated with short-term negative affective states, as indicated by increased vocalizations [118–120] and heart rate [121]. Abrupt separation has been shown to induce a negative judgment bias (consistent with negative mood states) in calves at least for 3 days post-separation [17]. Greater acute stress responses have been reported when separation occurred after longer dam contact (e.g., 4 days [122]; 14 days [123]); whereas, gradual separation through fenceline (i.e., allowing limited contact between cow and calf) appears to reduce distress responses in calves [124, 125]. Weaning stress, in terms of transition to solid feed, and/or change in milk source (i.e., from dam to automatic or manual feeder) can intersect with separation stress, as shown when calves with higher supplemental milk intake during gradual separation vocalized less than those with lower milk intakes [126].

Contact with the dam also affects calves’ social interactions with other individuals. Dam-reared calves initiated social play more frequently when meeting an unfamiliar calf [127] and displayed avoidance and submissive behaviors to threatening behavior when confronting an unfamiliar cow [128], which may indicate more effective social behaviors than calves reared in groups of similar-age peers and may influence the affective experience resulting from social interactions.

Overall, rearing calves in contact with the dam involves several overlapping factors, including social interactions, maternal care, nutrition, and management practices (e.g., daily separation for milking), all of which influence affective states, making it difficult to disentangle the role of individual factors.

Human-animal interactions

Despite being reliant on humans for their care, most calves commonly exhibit a fear response to human approach [129]. The use of handling techniques appear to modulate this short-term affective response, with calves positively handled (i.e., soft voice, slow movements, stroking/patting) approaching faster and interacting more with humans (e.g. [130]), while negative handling (i.e., harsh voice, fast movements, or hits with the hand or an object such as a plastic pipe) leads to longer approach latencies and greater flight distance [131]. Similarly, calves fed in ways that involve visual contact with humans have been reported to interact more quickly with a human than those fed without visual contact [132]. Calves exposed to multiple handlers with different handling methods (i.e., positive, aversive, and neutral) initially developed a general avoidance of all humans before adapting their response to the specific handler (e.g., more contact with the positive handler [11]). However, variation in routine human exposure across experimental rearing systems (e.g., during feeding, cleaning, or husbandry) may also affect fear responses and contribute to variability in outcomes across studies.

Calves given the choice preferred being brushed by a human over entering an empty compartment, suggesting that human contact was perceived as positive [133]. In contrast, some husbandry procedures, such as disbudding, injections, or being restrained in a chute, are known to be aversive [134–136]. Painful husbandry procedures, such as disbudding, have been associated with indicators of negative mood states, including more pessimistic responses in judgment bias tasks, anhedonia [140], and increased sensitivity to loss of reward [15]. A growing body of evidence involving more mature cattle indicates that operant conditioning, in particular positive reinforcement, can mitigate, at least to some degree, the negative emotional states arising from otherwise aversive procedures (e.g. [137–139]), .

Collectively, these findings suggest that human–animal interactions can influence short-term affective states in calves; however, to our knowledge, no studies have directly linked the quality of human–animal relationships with mood states in dairy calves.

Effects of the temporal component

Temporal characteristics of exposure should not be viewed as independent from the physical and social features discussed above, but rather as factors that modulate how these stimuli are experienced. The affective experience of a resource or event may depend not only on its nature (e.g., food, space, social contact), but also on when, how long, how often, and under what degree of predictability or control it is experienced. Thus, temporal factors may amplify, attenuate, or qualitatively alter the affective experiences of physical and social environmental features.

Timing of exposure

To date, research on the timing of exposure to stimuli in dairy calves has mainly focused on social relationships and painful procedures. In cattle, the formation of preferential relationships is influenced by both the timing of exposure and the duration of contact; however, most studies investigating these relationships have used designs in which these factors are confounded, making the separate effects of these factors difficult to disentangle.

As little as 5 min of post-partum contact with the calf induced vocalizations upon separation [141], and stronger responses were reported with increased duration of post-partum contact between dam and calf (1 vs. 14 days [118, 123]; 1 vs. 100 days [142]). These findings suggest that separating the dam from the calf after they develop a bond may elicit more negative short-term affective states than separation right after birth. Calves introduced to same-age peers at 0.5 or 3.5 months develop stronger and longer-lasting preferences than those introduced at 5 months [99]. In cases where calves had been housed together since they were about 2 weeks of age, preferences for known conspecifics were still evident when the calves were 18 months old [99]. These results indicate that the age at which calves are introduced to others and the duration of contact both play a role in the formation of preferential relationships.

Age at which calves are introduced to social housing also affects social rank later in life; heifers socially isolated for the first 8 months of life had lower social rank at 8 and 20 months than those socially housed throughout the experimental period [109]. Veissier et al. [110] reported higher social ranks for heifers socially housed from birth to 19 weeks of age compared to heifers reared individually, but found no difference between individually reared heifers and those socially isolated for 14 weeks and subsequently grouped. Although other studies have compared the effect of grouping calves at different ages (e.g., on social buffering during weaning [143]), age is often confounded with the time spent together, making it difficult to draw conclusions regarding the effect of age per se.

Preliminary work suggests that age at the time of painful procedures may affect later pain sensitivity. For example, heifers disbudded at 3 days of age showed greater rump sensitivity between 28 and 56 days post-disbudding than those disbudded at 35 days [144]. Furthermore, heifers disbudded at either 3 or 35 days had a heightened cardiac, but not behavioral responses to vaccine injection at 11 months of age compared to heifers disbudded at 56 days [145]. However, later work found no differences in nociceptive thresholds between heifers disbudded at 9 or 40 days of age [146]. Overall, these findings provide inconsistent evidence regarding the long-term effects of age at disbudding on pain sensitivity, highlighting the need for further research.

Restricted/temporary exposure

Some researchers have attempted to increase the environmental complexity by providing animals with ‘restricted’ or ‘temporary’ access to one or several resources. Evidence from calves suggests that providing resources even for a limited period can elicit positive mood states, with calves provided daily access to a well-resourced pen for 90 min/d displaying lower reward sensitivity than calves that did not have this access [147]. While work in calves remains limited, evidence from adult cattle support this hypothesis with individuals given overnight pasture access showing lower reward sensitivity than those housed indoors full-time, although no difference was found in a judgment bias test [87].

The effects of different durations and frequencies of access to environmental components in calves remain underexplored. It might be assumed that more access would be more beneficial, but evidence from adult cattle managed in cow-calf contact systems challenges this assumption. Neave et al. [148] found that cows with part-time calf contact showed a more pessimistic bias than did those with full-time or no calf contact (i.e., separated from the calf at 48 h after birth), suggesting that repeated separations elicited a more negative affective state. Providing conceptual support for this pattern, similar effects were observed in mice; Ratuski et al. [149] reported a higher rate of stereotypic behaviors in mice that had access to a playpen 3 times per week for 30 min, compared to mice housed in standard shoebox cages, possibly reflecting a negative contrast of returning to an environment of lower complexity. We posit that when offering restricted access to a resource/environment, the characteristics of the home environment may play a role in how access and removals are experienced.

Within calves, available evidence further supports the idea that the home environment modulates responses to temporary access. Calves in barren housing showed stronger anticipatory behavior for temporary access to a complex pen than did calves housed in complex conditions, suggesting that such access was more rewarding when animals were given features otherwise absent in their home pen [74]. Individually housed calves learned a T-maze task rewarded with access to a social partner faster than pair-housed calves, indicating that this experience was more valuable to them [150]. Calves kept with the dam half of the day (i.e., 8–10 h of separation) showed more play behaviors when their dam was present than did calves separated from the dam only during milkings (i.e., kept with the dam 22 h/d [151]).

In summary, providing temporary access to features (i.e., physical or conspecific) that are absent from the daily environment can elicit both positive and negative affective states. Future research should explore how timing and duration of access influence calf responses, how the value of the environmental element provided modulates affective reactions to their provision and removal, and how even temporary access affects longer-term mood states.

Occurrences of exposure

The first introduction to a stimulus commonly induces fear, a negative short-term affective state in calves, but responses (both physiological and behavioral) vary considerably among individuals [152, 153]. Once the fear response to novelty wanes, animals often increase interactions with the stimulus; for instance, when subjected to a spontaneous object recognition test, calves spent more time exploring novel objects compared to familiar items [154, 155]. Explorative behaviors allow animals to acquire new information, which has been proposed by some authors to be rewarding [156]. Although work on this relationship remains scarce in dairy calves, evidence from other species is supportive. For example, piglets preferred to enter a pen containing a novel object compared to a pen where a familiar object was hidden, and explored and spent more time in contact with the novel object [157]. Similarly, rats tested in a radial maze demonstrated preference for novel arms despite knowing which arms led to a food reward, suggesting that exploration itself was valuable [158].

Prolonged exposure can lead to a progressive decrease in response to the stimulus (habituation), as has been observed in calves in response to a variety physical items [73]. That said, frequency and duration of interactions may not reliably indicate an item’s value and its impact on affective states (see review [159]).

Overall, these findings suggest that repeated exposure can shape affective responses over time, with novel stimuli often initially eliciting negative short-term affective states, but these effects may wane following repeated encounters.

Predictability of the exposure

When events occur repeatedly, they can become predictable to animals, either through the temporal characteristics of the event or through associated cues [160]. In calves, responses to delayed feedings [161] and anticipatory behaviors prior to access to a different environment signaled by a visual cue [74], highlight their ability to expect the occurrence of an event. As the majority of evidence linking predictability to affective states comes from other species, often within paradigms involving aversive events, we caution readers when extrapolating these findings to dairy calves.

Animals often prefer predictable over unpredictable events. Work on other species (i.e., rats, fish, and birds) suggest that when offered a choice between predictable and unpredictable electric shocks, animals generally choose the shock with high predictability (see review [162]). Unpredictability has been associated with more negative short-term affective states: an unpredictable, sudden event (i.e., movement of a panel) during feeding elicited tachycardia in sheep, but such a response was mitigated when the event was signaled or occurred regularly [163]. Rats subjected to unpredictable aversive events (e.g., unfamiliar/tilted cage, damp bedding, reversed light/dark cycle) perceived ambiguous cues more negatively than those in predictable housing [164]. Similarly, lambs exposed to unpredictable and stressful farm routine management showed pessimistic biases [165] and a learning deficit [166], suggesting more negative mood states. However, results from the last three studies should be interpreted with caution, as control groups were not exposed to aversive events; it remains unclear whether unpredictability itself or event aversiveness drove these effects. The role of predictability in calves’ experiences has yet to be explored.

Loss of predictability, in terms of unmet expectations, can induce a frustration-like short-term response, as highlighted by the growing body of work using the successive negative contrast paradigm, where animals face an unexpected decrease in reward, either in quantity or quality. For example, calves trained to approach a milk reward showed longer latencies, increased vocalizations, and increased pressure applied to the bottle when the reward was suddenly reduced [167]. Whether such responses are driven primarily by unmet expectations or would also occur when reductions are anticipated remains unclear.

Although these studies collectively suggest a positive effect of predictability on animals’ affective states, some raise concerns that highly predictable environments of animals kept under human care could lead to boredom [168]. Predictable environments have been associated with general inactivity (e.g., bears [169]), high rates of abnormal repetitive behaviors (e.g., leopards [170]), and increased ‘interest’ toward diverse stimuli (e.g., mink [171, 172]). However, environmental predictability remains under-researched in farm animals, with most studies presenting confounding factors such as the type of stimulus (e.g., aversive or positive) and animals’ control over the experience, making it difficult to reach a consensus on the optimal level of predictability [168].

Control over the exposure

Choosing is defined as the act of selecting from more than one alternative [173]. When individuals are allowed to choose between predictable outcomes, they are given the opportunity to have control over their environment [173]. Although the concepts of choice and control are recognized as important for animal welfare (e.g. [174, 175]), empirical work examining their effects on affective states remains limited in calves. Thus, this section draws largely on conceptual arguments and evidence from other species to consider how opportunities for choice and control may shape calves’ experiences.

Evidence suggests that opportunities for choice and control promote more positive (or less negative) short-term affective states (see review [176]). For example, several species are reported to favour having two options available rather than one, even when it does not provide more reward (rats [177]; pigeons [178]; monkeys [179]). Lambs trained to interrupt an air-blast to receive a food reward exhibited a lower stress response than those exposed to the air-blast for a similar duration before receiving the reward [180].

When offered alternatives, animals are likely to choose options that are preferred and beneficial for them and to avoid less preferred or more aversive experiences. Preferences can change over time and can be impacted by many external and internal factors. For instance, although much evidence indicate that pasture access is valued by adult cattle housed indoors [81, 84], external factors such as weather [85], time of day [81], and social environment [181] can alter the value of outdoor access. Additionally, calves vary in their preferences for environmental features (e.g. [45, 88]), partly reflecting personality differences that influence how animals perceive and engage with their environment. The same event may elicit different affective experiences in different calves (e.g., dairy calves facing transportation: [182], or in response to novelty [153]). Likewise, when facing a stressor, cattle may display different behavioral patterns, with some individuals tending toward more proactive or reactive responses to challenges (e.g., beef cows under highly competitive conditions [183]; primiparous dairy cows’ adaptation to milking parlour [184]; regrouping of dairy heifers [185]). Consequently, there is likely no single optimal condition of exposure or interaction with a given stimulus for all calves. Allowing animals control over access to environmental elements may enable them to match their preferred conditions. Such control is likely to promote positive affective states by allowing animals to maximize positive experiences while minimizing aversive ones.

Excessive choice and control have also been associated with negative affective states, such as indecisiveness or feeling overwhelmed in humans [186], but opportunities for control remain limited in most captive environments, making such negative effects unlikely [187]. The capacity for choice and control may vary among individuals living within the same environment. As associating actions with outcomes is fundamental to exercising choice/control, internal factors such as differences in cognitive ability (e.g. [188]), may modulate this ability. External factors, including social dynamics, can also restrict choice; for instance, dominant animals often monopolize limited resources (e.g. [189]); thereby, constraining opportunities for submissive individuals.

Conclusions

A growing body of literature indicates that specific elements of the environment can influence affective states in dairy calves. Providing calves with adequate milk allowances, a variety of feeds (e.g., concentrate and forage), soft and dry lying surfaces, and full contact with conspecifics all appear important for promoting positive short-term affective states and reducing negative ones. However, we recognize that knowledge remains limited regarding longer-lasting effects on mood states. More broadly, these findings suggest that calf rearing environments should provide features beyond those needed to meet basic biological functioning in order to elicit positive affective states.

The temporal characteristics of environmental features and their impact on calf affective experiences remain largely underexplored, yet available evidence suggests that these factors can modify how physical and social components are experienced. Addressing these gaps in future research could help refine rearing practices to improve calf welfare.
