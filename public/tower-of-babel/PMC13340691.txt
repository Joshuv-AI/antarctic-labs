An unusual presentation of abdominopelvic inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor in an adolescent male: a rare case report
Authors: Nazila Heidari, Helya Bolouki Azari, Ramin Kafshgari, Amirhossein Heidari, Hossein Negahban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm with intermediate malignant potential, most commonly affecting the lungs and the abdominal and pelvic soft tissues. Pediatric abdominopelvic IMTs are exceptionally rare and often mimic malignant tumors.Case presentation:A 14-year-old Iranian male presented with 3 weeks of fever, abdominal and pelvic pain, nausea, constipation, and weight loss. Physical examination revealed a firm hypogastric mass. Laboratory tests showed elevated inflammatory markers but normal tumor markers (carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 19-9, and alpha-fetoprotein). Imaging demonstrated a lobulated abdominopelvic mass between the bladder and rectum with internal necrosis and mild ascites. The patient underwent midline laparotomy under general anesthesia at a pediatric surgery center, during which a 10 × 8 cm firm mass adherent to the omentum was excised entirely with minimal blood loss (≈20 mL). Histopathology revealed a spindle-cell lesion with storiform architecture, chronic inflammatory infiltrate, focal necrosis, and microscopic adipose invasion, consistent with IMT. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient remained asymptomatic with no recurrence at a 6-month follow-up.Clinical discussion:Abdominopelvic IMTs are diagnostically challenging due to nonspecific presentation and imaging overlap with malignancy. Complete surgical excision is the treatment of choice, and long-term surveillance is essential due to the risk of recurrence, particularly in large or epithelioid variants.Conclusion:This report describes an unusual abdominopelvic IMT in an adolescent male and emphasizes the importance of considering IMT in the differential diagnosis of pediatric abdominopelvic masses.

Introduction

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT), also known as pseudosarcoma and inflammatory pseudotumor, is a rare and distinct neoplasm characterized by a proliferation of myofibroblastic spindle cells accompanied by a prominent lymphoplasmacytic inflammatory infiltrate[1]. IMTs are extremely rare, exhibiting no significant sex predilection. Notably, approximately 50–60% of reported cases occur in children and adolescents or young adults[2]. These tumors mainly develop in the lung, pelvic, and retroperitoneal soft tissues, and to a lesser extent, in the reproductive system[3]. In most cases, IMTs present as solitary lesions, whereas multiple tumors are uncommon[4]. In addition, extrapulmonary tumors carry a 25% risk of local recurrence, with less than 5% risk of metastasis[5]. Clinical manifestations of IMTs vary depending on their anatomical location, with approximately 54% of cases being detected incidentally[3]. Herein, we report a rare case of abdominopelvic IMT in an adolescent boy.

Case presentation

This case report has been prepared in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[6]. A 14-year-old Iranian male was referred to the Pediatric Surgical Clinic at a referral hospital in Tehran, Iran, with a history of prolonged fever lasting 3 weeks. The patient also reported periumbilical, epigastric, and pelvic pain, nausea and vomiting, constipation, anorexia, and an unintentional weight loss of approximately 3 kg over the past month. The abdominal and pelvic pain was dull and intermittent, without a specific diurnal pattern or aggravating/relieving factors, and had no prior medical or surgical history. Family history was also noncontributory, and there was no known hereditary or congenital disorder in close relatives. He was a nonsmoker, had no history of alcohol or substance use, and belonged to a low-to-middle-income household. He was a school student and had no recent travel or exposure to infectious individuals. The abdomen appeared flat on physical examination, with a mild protuberance in the hypogastric region. There were no cutaneous abnormalities. Moreover, a firm, mildly tender mass was palpable, extending from the umbilical area to the hypogastrium and laterally toward the right lumbar region. The mass was nonmobile with respiration, and no bruit was detected on auscultation. Bowel sounds were normal and audible. Physical and systemic examinations were otherwise unremarkable.

Investigations

Initial laboratory work-up revealed a complete blood count within normal limits, with no eosinophilia. Inflammatory markers were elevated, with a C-reactive protein level of 100 mg/L (normal range: <6 mg/L). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was elevated at 4810 U/L (normal range: <200 U/L). Liver function tests, renal function tests, serum electrolytes, and urinalysis were all within normal reference ranges. Furthermore, tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 19-9, and alpha-fetoprotein, were all within normal limits. The indirect hemagglutination test for echinococcosis was also negative.

Further, an abdominopelvic ultrasound was performed, revealing a hypoechoic, solid-cystic lesion with irregular margins, measuring 66 × 60 × 58 mm, with an estimated volume of approximately 118 mL. The lesion was located in the abdominopelvic cavity, superior to the urinary bladder, adjacent to bowel loops, and was surrounded by fat stranding. Based on sonographic findings, diverticulitis and an intra-abdominal collection were considered initial differential diagnoses, prompting further evaluation with contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen.

The CT scan demonstrated an avidly enhancing, lobulated mass with well-defined borders, measuring approximately 68 × 62 × 60 mm, situated between the bladder and rectum. The lesion contained hypodense areas, suggestive of internal necrosis, and was associated with mild fat stranding and a small volume of pelvic ascites. Notably, there was nodular infiltration at the right anterolateral border of the lesion, extending into the adjacent peritoneal fat, along with enlargement of regional lymph nodes. These radiologic findings were also suggestive of a desmoplastic reaction or locally aggressive behavior. Additional findings included thickening of the first portion of the duodenum and a hypodense lesion with geographic margins, measuring 15 × 7 mm, adjacent to the falciform ligament in the left hepatic lobe, interpreted as a pseudolesion. Multiple lymph nodes, up to 6 mm in short-axis diameter, were also observed in the region of the mesenteric root.

Considering the patient’s constitutional symptoms, elevated LDH levels, and regional lymphadenopathy, lymphoma was considered a differential diagnosis. However, several radiologic features favored a primary mesenchymal neoplasm over a lymphoproliferative process, including the relatively localized and well-circumscribed nature of the lesion, avid heterogeneous contrast enhancement, areas of internal necrosis, and its close relationship to adjacent bowel loops with an apparent exophytic growth pattern. Accordingly, gastrointestinal stromal tumor was considered the leading preoperative diagnosis. Other mesenchymal tumors, including leiomyoma, leiomyosarcoma, and undifferentiated sarcoma, were also considered in the differential diagnosis. Hypervascular neoplasms such as neuroendocrine tumors and, less commonly, paraganglioma were ruled out because of the lesion’s prominent enhancement pattern. In contrast, infectious and inflammatory etiologies became less likely owing to the absence of significant leukocytosis, negative echinococcosis serology, and imaging findings suggestive of locally invasive tumor behavior rather than a simple inflammatory or infectious collection. Outcome

The patient underwent a midline laparotomy resection of the abdominopelvic mass under general anesthesia at a pediatric surgery center. An open approach was selected over laparoscopy due to the relatively large tumor size, its close proximity and partial adhesion to the omentum, and the need to ensure complete resection with minimal risk of tumor rupture. Additionally, the patient’s smaller body size and the complexity of the mass’s anatomical relations made laparoscopic resection technically challenging and potentially unsafe.

Intraoperatively, a 10 × 8 cm firm, well-defined mass with solid consistency was identified in the abdominopelvic cavity between the bladder and rectum, with adhesion to the greater omentum, without evidence of direct invasion into adjacent organs. Careful, sharp, and blunt dissection was performed along the omental margin to achieve complete mobilization of the mass while preserving surrounding structures. As demonstrated in Fig. 1, the tumor remained attached to a portion of the omental tissue at the time of extraction, reflecting the area of omental adhesion and the surgical dissection plane. The mass was subsequently excised en bloc with the adherent omental margin (Figs 1–2, Supplemental Digital Content Figure S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A65), with an estimated intraoperative blood loss of approximately 20 mL. The resected specimen was submitted in two separate containers for pathological evaluation. The first container, labeled as an omental mass, contained a spherical tumor measuring approximately 8 cm in diameter, partially attached to a segment of omentum measuring 10 × 8 × 10 cm. On gross examination, the cut surface appeared creamy and relatively homogeneous. The second container, labeled as peritoneal fluid for cytological analysis, contained 10 mL of reddish fluid.

Histopathological analysis of the tumor revealed a neoplastic lesion composed predominantly of epithelioid and spindle-shaped cells arranged in interlacing fascicles with a storiform growth pattern. The tumor cells had abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, vesicular nuclei, and inconspicuous nucleoli. Mitoses were rare, estimated at fewer than 5 per 50 high-power fields. A delicate fibrous capsule surrounded the lesion, and the subcapsular region showed vascular congestion with a mixed chronic inflammatory infiltrate composed mainly of lymphocytes and plasma cells, scattered throughout the collagenous stroma. Focal microscopic invasion into adjacent adipose tissue was also observed, and areas of stromal hyalinization and central coagulative necrosis were present (Fig. 3). Immunohistochemical (IHC) evaluation demonstrated strong anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) positivity in neoplastic spindle cells and smooth muscle actin (SMA) positivity, confirming myofibroblastic differentiation. P16 showed partial positivity, while h-caldesmon, CD117, DOG1, and CD34 were negative, thereby excluding smooth muscle and gastrointestinal stromal tumors. The IHC profile is summarized in Table 1. Cytological examination of the peritoneal fluid revealed numerous red blood cells, scattered leukocytes, and occasional mesothelial cells without atypia. The histopathologic and IHC findings correlated well with the preoperative imaging features of a well-defined, enhancing abdominopelvic mass with internal necrosis and mild desmoplastic reaction, confirming the diagnosis of a localized, ALK-positive IMT rather than a malignant soft-tissue neoplasm.

Following a multidisciplinary discussion, the oncology team concluded that no adjuvant therapy was necessary. The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery, was discharged in stable condition 3 days after surgery, and returned for follow-up 1 week later, at which time sutures were removed, and the wound was healing well. The patient was subsequently followed 6 months later, during which time he remained asymptomatic, with no signs of recurrence or postoperative complications in follow-up ultrasound sonography. He resumed normal daily activities and returned to school in good health.

Discussion

IMT is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm classified by the World Health Organization as a tumor with intermediate malignant potential and a low risk of metastasis, but a significant risk of local recurrence[7,8]. It most commonly arises in the lung and soft-tissue viscera (abdomen, pelvis, retroperitoneum, head, and neck). Approximately 50–60% of cases occur in children and adolescents, with no clear sex predominance[1,7,9]. Pelvic IMTs occur within the broader group of abdominopelvic IMTs and are particularly rare[4,10]. Most pelvic IMTs have been reported in adults, and pediatric cases are exceptional[7,9]. Our case adds to the small body of literature by describing a large abdominopelvic IMT in a 14-year-old boy with constitutional symptoms, radiologic features mimicking a malignant neoplasm, and histopathology consistent with an epithelioid variant.

Systemic manifestations such as fever, weight loss, and elevated inflammatory markers, as seen in our patient, reflect the inflammatory component described in some IMTs[4,7]. Local mass-effect symptoms depend on the tumor site; abdominal and pelvic IMTs, in particular, often present with nonspecific symptoms, pain, nausea, vomiting, altered bowel habits, or occasionally obstruction or intussusception. Pelvic masses may also compress urinary or reproductive organs, presenting gynecologic or urologic symptoms (pelvic discomfort, a palpable mass, menstrual irregularities, abnormal bleeding, dyspareunia, dysuria, or hematuria). Large lesions may mimic an acute surgical abdomen or peritoneal carcinomatosis, and because of their proximity to vital structures, complete resection often requires multidisciplinary surgical planning to achieve negative margins and minimize recurrence risk[9–11].

Imaging findings of our patient were nonspecific, raising a differential diagnosis of mesenchymal tumors, neuroendocrine tumors, and paragangliomas. The imaging differential diagnosis of abdominal and pelvic IMT is broad and modality-dependent. On ultrasound, IMTs usually appear as well-defined solid or mixed-echogenic masses with internal vascularity that resemble other solid mesenteric or adnexal tumors, such as desmoid fibromatosis, inflammatory fibroid polyp, or GIST[7,11]. Contrast-enhanced CT generally shows a homogeneous or heterogeneous low-density mass with mild to moderate and often delayed enhancement reflecting fibrosis. CT is helpful in detecting calcifications and assessing involvement of adjacent bowel, mesentery, or bone; findings that may instead suggest entities such as calcifying fibrous tumor, liposarcoma, or metastatic disease[7,11]. Magnetic Resonance Imaging or Positron Emission Tomography-CT can assist in defining local extension and staging; however, histopathologic evaluation with IHC and molecular testing (e.g., ALK, ROS proto-oncogene 1, receptor tyrosine kinase [ROS1], neurotrophic tropomyosin receptor kinase [NTRK]) remains essential for diagnosis[7,11].

Histology in our case demonstrated epithelioid cells in a storiform pattern, features seen in epithelioid inflammatory myofibroblastic sarcoma, but the low mitotic index and absence of marked atypia excluded frank malignancy[7,12]. Microscopic adipose invasion was consistent with the locally infiltrative behavior of IMT and correlated with a higher risk of recurrence, especially in large or abdominopelvic tumors[4,10]. Molecular testing was unavailable in our setting, which limited further classification and potential therapeutic stratification.

Complete surgical excision with negative (R0) margins is the principal curative approach for extrapulmonary IMTs[7,11]. Our patient underwent en bloc resection with an uneventful recovery. Recurrence rates for extrapulmonary IMTs can be quite high at 25%, especially in surgically challenging sites. There appear to be higher recurrence rates when margins are positive, growth is infiltrative, focal necrosis is present, or epithelioid morphology is observed[7,9,11]. Given the presence of these risk factors in our case, careful long-term follow-up is warranted despite the favorable short-term outcome.

When R0 resection is not feasible, multimodal therapy may be indicated, including radiotherapy for unresectable or margin-positive disease and systemic therapies[11]. Targeted tyrosine-kinase inhibitors have revolutionized management in fusion-positive tumors: ALK inhibitors (e.g., crizotinib) yield high response rates in ALK-rearranged IMTs, while ROS1 and NTRK inhibitors are active in corresponding fusion-positive cases[1,7,11]. Conventional chemotherapy or anti-inflammatory regimens (steroids or NSAIDs) may be reserved for unresectable or refractory tumors, though evidence remains limited and primarily from case reports[1,10]. Molecular testing to guide such targeted therapy is therefore strongly recommended[7,11].

Although most IMTs behave indolently, metastasis occurs in <5% of cases[7]. Adverse prognostic factors include large tumor size (>6.5–11 cm), multinodular or surgically challenging location (intra-abdominal, retroperitoneal, pelvis)[7,11], infiltrative margins or incomplete resection[10], microscopic invasion, high cellularity, increased mitotic activity, necrosis, cytologic atypia[4,7], epithelioid morphology[7,12], ALK-negative status[7,11,12], and a high Ki-67 proliferation index[4,7]. Our patient had several poor prognostic features; however, low mitotic activity and the absence of gross invasion suggest a favorable short-term prognosis. The absence of molecular profiling limits prognostic analysis; therefore, further consideration should be given to the routine incorporation of molecular diagnostics into IMT work-up.

Several knowledge gaps remain. First, there is no standardized diagnostic algorithm for suspected IMT, and preoperative diagnosis is difficult due to nonspecific symptoms and imaging, particularly where molecular testing is unavailable[7,9]. Second, data on long-term outcomes (recurrence and survival) in pediatric abdominopelvic IMTs are limited[12], and most evidence for systemic or targeted treatment comes from small series and case reports[1,11]. Finally, the lack of a standardized staging or prognostic system limits risk stratification[4,7,11].

Future research should focus on establishing multicenter registries to better characterize pediatric and extrapulmonary IMTs. Prospective research is also needed to clarify prognostic histopathologic and inflammatory markers. Additionally, pediatric trials of targeted therapies are essential to establish optimal dosing, sequencing, and resistance management. Finally, standardized diagnostic algorithms that integrate histology, IHC, and molecular testing would enhance diagnostic accuracy and treatment equity across healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This case report emphasizes the importance of considering IMT in the differential diagnosis of abdominopelvic masses in adolescents. Complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice, but careful long-term follow-up is required, particularly for tumors with poor prognostic features. Incorporating molecular testing into clinical practice can enhance diagnostic accuracy, guide prognosis, and expand access to targeted therapies. Our case report adds to the limited data on pediatric abdominopelvic IMT and highlights ongoing knowledge gaps that warrant further investigation.
