Diagnostic yield of electroencephalographyin the emergency department: protocol for the EMINENCE-M multicentre retrospective observational study
Authors: Maenia Scarpino, Benedetta Piccardi, Andrea Nencioni, Peiman Nazerian, Federica Lettieri, Enrico Grassi, Ester Marra, Andrea Mannini, Antonio Maiorelli, Piergiuseppe Liuzzi, Antonello Grippo, The Eminence Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionEmergency EEG (emEEG) is increasingly used in the emergency department (ED), but its diagnostic yield remains uncertain. This protocol describes a multicentre observational study aiming to evaluate emEEG findings and their relationship with diagnostic pathways and therapeutic management of patients admitted to the ED.Methods and analysisThis multicentre retrospective study will analyse emEEGs performed on patients admitted to the ED of some Italian teaching and community hospitals over a 1-year period with a target sample size of 3850 patients. The diagnostic yield of emEEG will be evaluated by assessing abnormal and epileptiform findings and the relationship between emEEG findings and subsequent clinical decisions, including confirmation or revision of the initial diagnostic suspicion, decisions regarding home discharge or hospitalisation and medication changes. EEG will be classified according to the terminology of the American Clinical Neurophysiology Society. Clinical and instrumental data will be respectively reviewed by emergency physicians and neurologists/neurophysiologists. In particular, via traditional biostatistics and interpretable machine learning models, the study will evaluate the diagnostic yield of emEEG and its association with subsequent clinical management across defined clinical scenarios in the ED.Ethics and disseminationThis first large-scale multicentre protocol will provide valuable insights for emergency department (ED) clinicians in selecting appropriate candidates for an emergency EEG (emEEG), supporting ethically sound, proportionate use of this resource in a time- and risk-critical setting. By clarifying diagnostic yield and its relationship with subsequent clinical decisions, the study is expected to generate robust evidence to guide emEEG ordering, reduce unnecessary testing and delays, and promote safer, more equitable decision-making (including appropriate home discharge) while minimising potential harms from misdiagnosis or overtreatment. The study has been approved by the Ethics Committee Regione Toscana - Area Vasta Centro (n. 27241). Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and engagement with relevant clinical societies to inform international recommendations and facilitate translation into ED practice. Furthermore, developed models will be made openly available for external and public validation.Keywords: Emergent EEG, Emergency Department, Diagnostic-therapeutic pathway

Introduction

Electroencephalography (EEG) has been a cornerstone for interrogating human brain function for nearly seven decades.1 2 Despite transformative advances in anatomical and functional neuroimaging, EEG retains a unique and indispensable role in the assessment of acute neurological pathology, particularly in patients with altered consciousness and coma.3 4 Its capacity to capture cortical dynamics with millisecond resolution enables real-time physiological appraisal at the bedside—something that static or haemodynamically lagged imaging modalities cannot provide.

In routine practice, EEG’s value is well established in the outpatient management of epilepsy5; however, recent years have witnessed a marked expansion of EEG use in emergency settings.2 6 7 Emergency EEG (emEEG) is rapid, non-invasive, portable and cost-effective; crucially, when neuroimaging is unrevealing, emEEG often supplies decisive functional evidence that helps neurologists and emergency physicians adjudicate competing diagnoses in time-critical situations, where early, targeted interventions can substantially alter outcomes.

Beyond its logistic advantages, emEEG complements neuroimaging by offering a direct readout of neuronal population activity. Whereas CT and MRI characterise structural substrates and, in selected sequences, vascular or metabolic correlates; emEEG discloses patterns of continuity, reactivity, symmetry, organisation and epileptiform activity that refine etiological hypotheses and guide immediate care. This functional window is particularly valuable across a spectrum of emergency presentations, including suspected seizures and status epilepticus—both convulsive and non-convulsive—undifferentiated confusional states and other forms of altered mental status.8

Yet, despite its growing adoption and evident clinical utility, there remains a conspicuous gap in international guidance: there are no widely endorsed, data-driven and diagnostic-personalised recommendations that specify (i) when the performed emEEG recording is expected to be abnormal and/or with epileptiform activity according to clinical admission features (and with which likelihood), (ii) what diagnostic yield should be expected in defined clinical scenarios and (iii) how emEEG findings should be integrated with admission information to optimise the clinical pathway.

The EMINENCE study was conceived to help close this gap by retrospectively evaluating, within a large single-centre cohort, the contribution of EEG to diagnosis, therapeutic decision-making and patient management in the emergency department (ED).9 10 By systematically evaluating emEEG patterns and their relationship to admission phenotypes, EMINENCE aimed to seed evidence-based recommendations and to catalyse the development of standardised pathways for emEEG integration into emergency medicine.10

Building on these foundations, the present multicentre protocol operationalises a next step: the preregistered protocol, the external validation and the diagnostic yield of emEEG in ED in a large multicentric study. By harmonising acquisition and annotation across diverse hospitals and systematically analysing the outcomes associated with emEEG, this study's principal aims to help the generation of evidence-based recommendations that can enhance the integration of emEEG into emergency practice. In doing so, it responds directly to the unmet need for standardised, transparent tools that can inform emEEG ordering, set realistic expectations about diagnostic yield and support time-sensitive ED decision-making. Lastly, diagnostic yield and associations with subsequent clinical decisions will be quantified with interpretability techniques and decision-curve analysis across realistic probability thresholds and to provide transparent, clinician-facing explanations of model predictions.

Material and methods

Study design

This is a retrospective, observational, multicentre study with consecutive enrolment of ED patients receiving clinically indicated emEEG at a number of teaching and community hospitals within the Eminence Study Group (online supplemental material 1). The protocol is preregistered in a Registered Report–style format to promote transparency and analytic rigour prior to data collection.

Study setting and dates

The study will recruit from six EDs (detailed list in online supplemental table S1), spanning teaching and community hospitals, with the option to expand to additional Italian centres if throughput or heterogeneity targets require it. The planned study period will run from 1 January 2026 to 30 June 2027. The planned data retrieval window runs from 1 January 2024 to 31 December 2024 for all the centres, for a total of 12 months. Follow-up extends through the ED stay until discharge in order to complete diagnostic adjudication.

Eligibility criteria

Adults aged 18 years or older who presented to the ED and for whom emEEG (at least 19 channels with the 10–20 International System) was ordered as part of routine clinical care during the same ED episode will be eligible for the study. Patients with the emEEG performed either during regular hospital hours or outside normal operating hours will be included. Further inclusion criteria target the availability of the minimum admission dataset (detailed in online supplemental materials 2).

Patient pathway and data collection

Data will be retrospectively extracted by trained investigators from ED electronic health records (EHRs) of eligible patients. Only variables referring to the first 24 hours from ED admission were considered for inclusion. Source documents included ED triage notes, physician assessment records, prehospital documentation when available and results of investigations performed prior to emEEG. In particular, investigators will record demographics and clinical variables including sex, age, neurological symptoms, vital signs, comorbidities, prior events or surgeries and history of medications (including anti-seizure). Additional variables, including electrolyte or metabolic imbalances, fever, septic state and a history of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia or cardiac and thyroid disorders, will be documented. Furthermore, also the request for a neurological, neurosurgical and/or resuscitator consultation was included in the dataset. A detailed codebook of the RedCap database, which will be used for data collection, is attached in online supplemental table S2).

The initial ED diagnostic suspicion will be grouped, with the possibility for inserting multiple choices in the dataset, in the following categories: (i) transient loss of consciousness, (ii) absence-like episode, (iii) fall of unknown dynamics, (iv) transient ischaemic attack, (v) head trauma, (vi) epileptic seizure, (vii) status epilepticus, (viii) transient global amnesia, (ix) altered consciousness, (x) stroke, (xi) no specific diagnostic suspicion and (xii) others. Others will be used when a clear suspicion can be produced in the ED department but does not fall within any of the aforementioned categories. The use of REDCap for data capture will include a case-report form platform with a central data dictionary, site-specific logs and standardised training modules for all personnel involved in data entry, ensuring standardised data collection across centres.

EEG recording and classification

For each participating centre, technical parameters of the performed emEEG recording will be communicated and compared between centres to evaluate any deviations from the standard emEEG diagnostic items.11 For each enrolled patient, the EEG recording will be retrieved and evaluated by at least a junior and a senior (>10 years’ experience) neurologist/neurophysiologist.

Standard 30-min EEG recordings will be performed using a digital system and an EEG cap with 19 electrodes (Fp1, Fp2, F7, F8, F3, F4, C3, C4, T3, T4, P3, P4, T5, T6, O1, O2, Fz, Cz and Pz), positioned according to the 10–20 International Standard System. Recordings will be acquired at a sampling rate of 128 Hz. During review, digital filters (low-pass filter=30–70 Hz; time constant=0.1 or 0.3 s; notch filter=50 Hz) and sensitivity gain (2–10 V/mm, standard gain=7 V/mm) will be adjusted to meet interpretative needs.12 13

Sedation status will be retrieved, if available. The clinical reading is prioritised for ED requests and interpreted by certified neurologists/neurophysiologists. The classification of EEGs will follow the terminology set by the American Clinical Neurophysiology Society (ACNS).11 Descriptors will include continuity, voltage, the organisation of the anterior-posterior gradient, reactivity, spontaneous variability of the background activity, frequency, symmetry, epileptiform discharges, slow waves, periodic patterns, triphasic morphology and the presence of sleep-related patterns. Seizure patterns, whether motor or electrical, as well as motor status epilepticus (SE) or non-convulsive SE (NCSE), will also be reported. For further details, refer to Hirsch et al.11

Outcomes

The primary outcome is (i) abnormal emEEG, defined as the presence versus absence of any clinically relevant abnormality on qualitative emEEG interpretation according to ACNS-based categories. A second outcome is (ii) epileptiform emEEG, defined as the presence versus absence of epileptiform activity, including spikes or sharp waves, periodic discharges and electrographic seizures or status epilepticus. Four additional outcomes describe the diagnostic yield of emEEG and its relationship with subsequent clinical decisions. These include (iii) confirmation of the initial diagnostic suspicion, defined when the admission diagnostic hypothesis remains the final diagnosis and emEEG provides key supportive evidence; (iv) emEEG-related revision of the initial diagnostic suspicion, defined when the final diagnosis differs from the initial suspicion and the change is specifically attributable to emEEG findings; (v) medication adjustment, defined as any modification in pharmacological management following emEEG (including changes in anti-seizure medications or other therapies) and (vi) patient disposition, defined as discharge vs hospital admission following ED evaluation.

Sample size

Sample size estimation was informed by empirical prevalences derived from the monocentric EMINENCE dataset, which included 1018 consecutive patients undergoing emEEG. In that cohort, 691 recordings were classified as abnormal, corresponding to a prevalence of 67.9% (95% CI 62.8 to 70.6%) with normal EEG in 32.1%. Epileptiform activity was observed in 192 patients, yielding an event fraction of 18.9% (95% CI 13.1 to 19.2%). With respect to diagnostic contribution, emEEG confirmed the initial ED suspicion in 188 patients (18.5%) and contributed to revision in 514 patients (50.5%). These four proportions were used as priors for the multicentre protocol. To ensure adequate precision in discrimination and calibration, the study was powered to secure at least 200 events and 200 non-events per outcome in the pooled development set. This criterion, widely adopted in prediction modelling research, typically yields a 95% CI half-width around the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve of approximately 0.05 for models with expected discrimination in the range of 0.70 to 0.80 and permits reliable estimation of calibration curves.14 Using the observed prevalence from the monocentre study, the required total sample size was calculated as 200 divided by the rarer class fraction for each outcome. For abnormal emEEG, where the rarer class is the normal recording (32.1%), the minimum pooled total required is 603 patients. For epileptiform activity, where the rarer class is the presence of epileptiform discharges (18.9%), the minimum total is 1258 patients. For confirmation and revision of the ED suspicion (18.5% and 50.5%, respectively), the minimums are 1242 and 445 patients. The most conservative requirement is therefore governed by the two rarer outcomes, epileptiform activity and confirmation, both requiring approximately 1250 patients in total.

Accounting for centre clustering, the required total sample size was calculated as N=N0 x DE, where N0=1258 modified to include 10% unusable data, leading to N0=1384. Considering the design effect for unequal centre sizes as DE=1+((CV2+1)×m--1)×ICC, where m- is the mean cluster size (m-~500), CV is the coefficient of variation in cluster sizes (CV<0.25, enforcing balanced recruitment) and the intra-class correlation (ICC~0.002 for binary outcomes in previous data15,17), DE results equal (smaller) to 3.06. The derived sample size of N=1.258 x 3.06=3850 is providing~256–262 events for the rarer outcomes (prevalence 18.5–18.9%) and comfortably maintaining≥200 events/non-events. For an anticipated Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.70–0.80, this delivers a 95% CI half-width of ≈0.03 to 0.04 using standard AUC variance approximations. Leave-one-centre-out internal-external cross-validation will be performed together with pre-planned, blinded re-estimation of ICC and CV mid-study. If the observed ICC exceeds 0.0025 with a CV>0.25 other available centres will be enrolled in the study to match the required sample size.

Statistical analysis plan

The study will retrospectively analyse patient data from the ED hospitals participating in the study to evaluate the role of emEEG in diagnostic definition, therapy adjustment and patient management.

Personal data will be fully anonymised prior to statistical analysis. Before analysis, standard data cleaning procedures will be performed to identify duplicate, incomplete, inconsistent or implausible records. Descriptive statistics will be employed to define the study population, with categorical variables expressed as numbers and percentages and continuous variables as medians (IQR: 25th–75th percentile) with systematic profiling of missingness by variable and by site. Group comparisons (between outcome groups) will be performed using χ2 tests for categorical data and Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables. To avoid overestimating associations, Cramer’s V test will be applied to assess categorical correlations, with values between 0.30 and 0.49 indicating a moderate association and values above 0.50 signifying a strong correlation. All statistical analyses will be conducted using IBM SPSS v27, Jamovi (V.2.6.2), Wizard for Mac (Evan Miller) and Python custom code. Two-tailed tests will be applied, and p values<0.05 will be considered statistically significant. The Bonferroni correction will be used to adjust for multiple comparisons.

ML models deployment and internal validation

Separate supervised classifiers will be trained for each outcome, with logistic regression with elastic-net regularisation serving as a transparent baseline and with random forests and gradient-boosted trees via Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) serving as strong non-linear comparators. Preprocessing will be confined to the training folds and will include standardisation for continuous features, k-nearest neighbour imputation (multiple imputation by chained equations) for continuous missing data, mode imputation for categorical variables and one-hot encoding for multi-category variables. Assumptions of missing at random (MAR) distributions will be evaluated before performing imputation processes. Model development will use NxM nested cross-validation, with an outer N-fold loop for performance estimation and an inner M-fold loop for hyperparameter selection. Primary metrics will include the area under the ROC curve, balanced accuracy, sensitivity and specificity, together with calibration assessments using reliability curves and the Brier score. Subgroup and interaction analyses will be performed on ML model outputs when clinically relevant and supported by sufficient sample size. Decision-curve analysis will quantify net clinical benefit across clinically plausible probability thresholds to inform potential deployment. Model interpretability will be assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values at both the global and local levels, with features grouped into clinically meaningful domains to support human-factor-friendly explanations. Machine learning analyses will be conducted using Python custom code using open-source libraries.

External validation

Following completion of model development using the nested cross-validation framework mixing all subjects across sites, we will undertake a stability analysis of feature importance and predictive performance in a cross-centre design. Specifically, after training and optimising hyperparameters within the NxM nested cross-validation loop, models will be retrained on the combined dataset of all but one participating centre and subsequently evaluated on the left-out centre. The leave-one-centre-out strategy will enable assessment of generalisability across heterogeneous healthcare environments and robustness across EEG recording parameters and admission cohorts. Performance metrics including discrimination, calibration and decision-curve analyses will be calculated for each held-out centre. Stability and robustness of predictive features will be evaluated by comparing feature importance profiles across iterations. When appropriate, heterogeneity in model performance across centres will be synthesised using mixed-effects meta-analytic techniques, allowing quantification of between-site variability and identification of contextual factors influencing generalisation.

Ethics and dissemination

Emergency denotes life-threatening conditions requiring immediate treatment. Many neurological causes of altered consciousness—including NCSE, metabolic or septic encephalopathy and encephalitis—are detectable with emEEG. Its usefulness in practice is well established, particularly within the first 24–48 hours from symptom onset, where it informs the management of epileptic seizures and motor SE/NCSE, antiseizure medication adjustments and the evaluation of loss of consciousness, acute altered mental status or confusion and ischaemic lesions—even when neuroimaging findings are normal.1 6 7 18 19 Despite guidance for NCSE and motor SE,219,21 universally accepted recommendations for broader emEEG use in the ED are lacking.

In the ED, where rapid diagnostic and therapeutic decisions often precede MRI availability22,24 or specialty consultation,25 26 emEEG can both support confirmation and revision of the initial diagnosis, shaping safe discharge versus admission pathways. Ethically, this argues for timely, equitable access to emEEG while guarding against over-prescription driven by convenience or individual habit, which can waste scarce resources, reduce specificity and expose patients to unnecessary treatment changes. Clear indications, standardised workflows and appropriate training are essential—particularly in first- and second-level hospitals with limited personnel—to balance beneficence, non-maleficence, fairness in resource allocation and stewardship of neurophysiology services. The multicentre EMINENCE study is designed to clarify when emEEG changes management beyond traditional seizure detection, promote integration with clinical and neuroimaging data and generate evidence that can underpin international, pragmatic guidance for emergency care.

The study is observational, and emEEG is ordered according to routine clinical care. Risks are therefore minimal and relate primarily to data privacy. The requirement of requesting informed consent has been waived from the competent ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study. The retrospective analysis of the patient database has been approved by the Ethics Committee Regione Toscana—Area Vasta Centro (n. 27241). Each additional site will obtain approval from the relevant ethics committee and will follow local requirements for consent in emergency research. All records will be de-identified before transfer, processed in compliance with General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and stored on secure, role-based access servers. The study complies with ethical principles and good clinical practice guidelines. Due to logistical constraints, informed consent will not be retrievable for all patients.

Dissemination and open science

The protocol and analysis plan will be preregistered in a Stage 1 Registered Report format. On completion, the investigators will submit the stage 2 manuscript to an open-access venue that supports registered reports and will release, subject to local permissions, a de-identified dataset, analysis code and model cards that document scope, assumptions, calibration and fairness checks to facilitate reproducibility and responsible reuse.

Timeline and milestones

The project begins with a 3-month start-up phase devoted to site onboarding, training and finalisation of the case-report forms. Data entry then proceeds for 12 months with interim monthly data freezes and rolling quality control throughout. The final 3 months will be devoted to data cleaning, analysis, external validation and manuscript preparation, after which dissemination activities will begin.

Strengths and limitations

Prior literature is heterogeneous in sample selection, setting and even for what concerns the definition of emEEG, impeding comparison and generalisation: some studies counted all EEGs performed outside routine hours,27 others any urgent EEG to exclude NCSE with 24/7 availability after specialist approval,28 and others any EEG performed within 1 hour of the ED request.19 All included studies had moderated cohorts,719 27,29 statistically limiting robustness as well as the possibility of performing analysis conditioned to the initial diagnosis (or the ED triage-related variables). Lastly, several studies, taking place in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) or in long-term inpatient wards, tackle different clinical requests (eg, brain-death assessment or post-injury prognostication12 30), not reflecting the ED’s time-critical differential diagnosis.13 31 Only a few studies confined recruitment to the ED and often focused narrowly on epileptic disorders.9 29 32 Within our previous monocentric study,9 10 enrolling more than 1000 consecutive ED patients undergoing emEEG, we provided preliminary evidence concerning the dependence between admission diagnoses and emEEG yield. However, being single-centre and performed in a highly specialised tertiary hospital, where multiple dedicated technicians and expert neurophysiologists are available, limits generalisability to resource-constrained settings and may over-represent complex cases. Therefore, the EMINENCE-M study addresses these gaps with a rigorous protocol and adequately powered ED-focused recruitment not restricted to seizure presentations, aiming to deliver the first comprehensive and statistically robust evaluation of EEG’s role in emergency medicine, also in less represented cohorts (eg, SE, global transient amnesia). By standardising definitions and contexts, it seeks to improve external validity and inform scalable recommendations; nonetheless, differences in existing local (regional) workflows, staffing, EEG availability and referral pathways, including centre-specific criteria for requesting emEEG, may remain a residual source of variability across participating hospitals that future implementation studies will need to address. Centre-specific practices will therefore be described and considered when interpreting between-centre heterogeneity in recruitment, case mix and diagnostic yield.
