Bearing capacity mechanism of vibro-replacement stone columns in coral sand with particle breakage
Authors: Xiangji Ye, Zongyuan Pan, Feng Liu, Hongxiang Tang, Zezhou Ji, Wei Zhao, Xin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoral sand presents significant challenges to ground improvement design due to its high void ratio, high compressibility, and particle breakage. Traditional bearing capacity theories for vibro-replacement stone columns are typically based on the assumption of soil shear dilation or constant volume. However, these assumptions fail to account for the particle breakage-induced energy dissipation and volume contraction characteristic of coral sand under high stress, leading to significant discrepancies in calculation results. Based on an analysis of the physical and mechanical properties and the breakage mechanism of coral sand, this paper proposes a novel three-step method for in-situ porosity measurement. Furthermore, a dual-mechanism coupling model, incorporating both energy-equivalent additional confining pressure and breakage-friction coupling, is proposed. By introducing the particle breakage energy dissipation coefficient and the energy-confining pressure conversion efficiency , a modified limit equilibrium model for bulging failure is established to dynamically couple the void ratio evolution and the mobilized internal friction angle. Validation through an airport runway project in coral sand geological conditions demonstrates that the proposed method accurately captures the lateral restraint enhancement of coral sand particle breakage. The calculated lateral ultimate stress (166.3 kPa) is consistent with the field measured range of 158.0–175.0 kPa from nine parallel plate load tests. Considering the full range of measured data, the relative error of the prediction ranges from − 5.3% to + 5.0%. This approach yields significantly higher accuracy compared to traditional standard methods, providing a reliable theoretical basis for engineering design under the conditions of coral sand geology.Keywords: Coral sand, Particle breakage, Energy dissipation, Bearing capacity, In-situ porositySubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Coral sand constitutes the primary seabed sediment in extensive offshore and oceanic regions. Globally, these deposits cover an estimated area of 284,300 km² across tropical and subtropical seas1, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Driven by the rapid advancement of marine engineering, construction activities within coral sand environments have become increasingly prevalent. Coral sand originates from the weathering and degradation of coral reef limestone; its porous biogenic skeleton makes the particles highly crushable during high-energy vibro-replacement, which directly challenges conventional stone column bearing capacity theories2. Critical infrastructure projects in these domains include pile foundations for conventional and renewable energy systems, land reclamation initiatives, and the construction of offshore airports. In the stabilization of these highly compressible foundations, vibro-replacement stone column technology is widely employed due to its high construction efficiency, strong resistance to liquefaction, and significant deep reinforcement effects.

Global distribution map of coral sand3.

Extensive geomechanical studies have characterized the unique biogenic microstructure of coral sand. Unlike terrigenous quartz sand, coral sand particles possess highly irregular shapes and retain numerous intra-particle voids inherited from their original biological skeletons4. While this distinct microstructure contributes to a high initial void ratio and a substantial internal friction angle, it renders the material highly susceptible to breakage. Wang et al.5 confirmed that this porous structure induces skeleton reorganization during early loading stages, resulting in a compression index significantly higher than that of quartz sand. Within the framework of critical state soil mechanics, Xiao et al.6 demonstrated that coral sand undergoes significant particle breakage during shearing, causing a continuous evolution of the particle size distribution (PSD). This breakage behavior involves significant energy dissipation, which fundamentally modifies the soil’s dilatancy characteristics. Consequently, the critical state line (CSL) is non-unique, manifesting instead as a family of rotating curves dependent on the degree of breakage in the plane.

The volumetric contraction induced by particle breakage has profound implications for foundation engineering. Traditional soil mechanics principles posit that pile installation induces shear dilation, creating a positive compaction effect and increasing lateral stress. However, empirical derived from studies on coral sand contradicts this assertion. Pioneering work by Poulos7 and Nauroy et al.8 highlighted that the energy input during pile driving causes severe particle breakage and stress relaxation, resulting in shaft resistance reaching only 20%–30% of that in quartz sand. Further quantification analyses conducted by Shan9 and Jiang10 have elucidated that with the augmentation of relative density, there is a corresponding increase in the proportion of energy dissipation attributable to particle breakage. This phenomenon, in turn, undermines the efficacy of conventional design approaches predicated solely on relative density.

Despite the understanding of driven piles, the design theory for vibro-replacement stone columns in coral sand remains underdeveloped. Currently, bearing capacity calculations primarily follow the cavity expansion theory (CET) proposed by Aleksandar11 or the bulging failure theory by Hughes and Withers12. These classical theories, predicated on the Mohr-Coulomb criterion for terrestrial soils, assume that the soil volume remains constant or undergoes shear dilation during expansion. They fail to account for the energy dissipation mechanism and volumetric contraction caused by the high-frequency vibration and intense compaction of vibro-replacement construction in breakage soils13. Consequently, the direct application of these theories often leads to significant calculation errors, resulting in either excessive conservatism or safety risks due to the overestimation of lateral restraint forces14. The distinct reaming response between terrigenous quartz sand and coral sand directly causes calculation deviation of lateral restraint, as schematically shown in Fig. 2.

Diagram of the reaming mechanism of terrigenous sand and coral sand.

To address these deficiencies, this study aims to transcend the framework of traditional terrestrial soil mechanics by establishing a revised calculation theory that explicitly incorporates the energy dissipation of particle breakage. First, the physical and mechanical properties of coral sand are analyzed, and a three-step method is proposed for accurate in-situ porosity measurement. Second, based on the principle of energy conservation, a particle breakage energy dissipation coefficient is introduced to quantify the energy consumed by breakage during vibro-replacement construction. Third, a modified bulging limit equilibrium model is constructed. This model couples the void ratio evolution with stress levels, theoretically filling the gap in the applicability of classical CET to breakage soils. Finally, the proposed method is validated against in-situ plate load tests from a high-precision coral sand geological airport project, demonstrating its accuracy and engineering value.

Physical and mechanical properties of coral sand

Basic physical indicators and parameter determination of coral sand

As a specialized biogenic geomaterial, coral sand exhibits unique engineering mechanical behaviors governed directly by its material composition and microstructure. Distinct from terrestrial siliceous sand, coral sand is predominantly composed of calcium carbonate, with a content typically exceeding 90%15. X-ray diffraction analysis identifies the primary mineral phases as aragonite and high-magnesium calcite. This mineralogical composition results in low particle hardness, rendering the material highly susceptible to cleavage and breakage under high stress. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations reveal that coral sand retains the skeletal microstructure of original biological organisms, such as reef-building corals, foraminifera, and mollusks. Consequently, the particles exhibit extremely irregular morphologies, characterized primarily by dendritic, rod-like, blocky, and flaky shapes. A defining feature of coral sand is the abundance of intra-particle voids. Research indicates that these intra-particle voids can constitute approximately 10% of the total porosity. This porous structure not only yields a large specific surface area and high water absorption capacity but also serves as the primary structural factor facilitating particle breakage during the vibro-replacement construction compaction process.

Driven by its unique biogenic origin, the physical indices of coral sand differ significantly from those of conventional quartz sand. Attributable to the high mineral density of aragonite, the particle density of coral sand typically ranges from 2.70 to 2.85, which is notably higher than that of standard quartz sand (approximately 2.65). For the accurate determination of specific gravity, the pycnometer method utilizing kerosene as the medium is recommended to ensure complete de-airing of the intra-particle pores. Furthermore, coral sand exhibits an exceptionally high initial void ratio, varying widely between 0.54 and 2.97. This elevated void ratio stems from a dual-porosity system comprising both inter-particle packing voids and intra-particle biological voids, thereby endowing the soil with significant compressibility and sensitivity to collapse upon saturation.

Accurate determination of the in-situ porosity (a core parameter in the bearing capacity calculation model) is a prerequisite for ensuring the design precision of vibro-replacement stone columns. However, due to the high fragility, irregular particle shape, abundant intra-particle voids of coral sand, conventional drilling and sampling methods are prone to cause particle disturbance and pore structure damage, leading to distorted test data. Coral sand particle breakage characteristics and its influence mechanism on stone column bearing capacity

Compared to terrigenous siliceous sand, coral sand has highly irregular particle morphology and abundant intra-particle pores, which makes it significantly more susceptible to particle breakage under stress16,17. The degree of particle breakage in coral sand is jointly affected by consolidation confining pressure, relative density, particle shape, gradation and loading rate, and this breakage behavior will fundamentally change the core mechanical parameters of the soil.Existing studies have confirmed that particle breakage during shearing will directly alter the dilatancy, internal friction angle and consolidation characteristics of coral sand18–21. To quantitatively describe the degree of fragmentation, Hardin22 proposed the classic concept of relative fragmentation potential , which quantifies the degree of fragmentation through the area ratio of fragmentation potential to total fragmentation rate. Unlike terrigenous quartz sand, which exhibits shear dilation under high stress, coral sand mainly shows volumetric contraction due to particle breakage, which means the classical CET based on shear dilation assumption is no longer applicable. However, current studies on coral sand particle breakage mostly focus on the mechanical response in laboratory element tests, and there are still obvious research gaps:

Theoretical derivation of bearing capacity of vibro-replacement stone column considering energy consumption due to breakage

Analysis and theoretical correction approach of particle breakage effect

The estimation of bearing capacity for vibro-replacement stone columns is predominantly based on the CET proposed by Vesic11. Predicated on the assumption of an elastic-plastic medium, this theory postulates that the plastic zone undergoes either constant volume deformation or shear dilation during expansion, thereby inducing a positive compaction effect and enhancing lateral stress. However, field observations from coral sand foundations indicate that the direct application of this theory often yields substantial discrepancies between calculated predictions and measured values. This divergence is primarily attributed to the theory’s failure to account for particle breakage and the associated volumetric contraction characteristic of biogenic carbonate sands under high stress.

Before introducing the modified model, it is necessary to first analyze the distinctly different mechanisms of action of driven piles and vibro-replacement stone columns in coral sand.

Shear-relaxation mechanism of driven piles

Shear-relaxation mechanism of driven piles

For solid driven piles or static pressure piles, the intrusion of the pile body primarily relies on forcibly shearing and displacing the soil. In fragile coral sand, the immense shear force leads to the breakage of particle edges and corners around the pile. Due to the absence of high-frequency vibration assistance, the fine particles after breakage cannot effectively fill the pores, but instead cause local volume contraction due to the destruction of the skeleton. This contraction manifests macroscopically as the release of effective stress around the pile, which is the stress relaxation phenomenon described by Poulos7, leading to a significant reduction in lateral friction resistance.

(2)The breakage-filling mechanism of vibro-replacement stone columns during construction

The breakage-filling mechanism of vibro-replacement stone columns during construction

Unlike the forced shear displacement of driven piles, vibro-replacement construction is a high-energy, high-frequency vibration compaction process. The high-frequency horizontal vibration of the vibroprobe instantly liquefies the saturated coral sand around the pile, eliminating the inter-particle interlocking resistance temporarily. In this specific construction state, the fine debris generated by particle breakage will not cause skeleton collapse and stress relaxation. On the contrary, under the action of the vibrational force field, the crushed fine particles are rapidly filled into the original inter-particle and intra-particle large pores, which promotes the forced skeleton reorganization of the soil around the pile, and the void ratio decreases sharply from the natural deposition stat to the dense state close to the minimum void ratio . It should be emphasized that particle breakage of coral sand has a significant dual effect on its mechanical properties: during the vibro-replacement construction stage, the positive effect of particle breakage, namely breakage-filling-densification, is absolutely dominant. The breakage is no longer a destructive factor leading to stress relaxation, but a necessary way to realize the transition of coral sand from loose state to dense state, and finally provides the stone column with lateral restraint stress much higher than that of natural foundation. However, under the action of long-term static load or overlying overload after construction, the continuous particle breakage of coral sand will lead to the wear of particle edges and corners, the weakening of inter-particle interlocking, and then cause the attenuation of internal friction angle, the increase of compressibility and the decrease of long-term bearing capacity, which is an adverse effect that cannot be ignored in engineering design.

Based on the above analysis, the energy term used to overcome shear dilation in classical theory does not exist in coral sand, and is replaced by the energy consumed in the breakage-filling process. According to the principle of energy conservation, the mechanical work output by the vibro-replacement device does not disappear, but is transformed into an increase in soil compactness through energy consumption in breakage. This paper proposes a modified bulging limit equilibrium model that considers the energy consumption due to particle breakage. The core idea of this model is to map the energy density consumed in particle breakage during the vibro-replacement and reaming process to the increment of effective lateral confining stress through a dimensionless energy consumption coefficient . This not only corrects the defect of traditional theory that ignores volume contraction, but also quantifies the special engineering gain characteristic of coral sand, which is more prone to breakage and compaction. This study focuses on the calculation of the ultimate bearing capacity of vibro-replacement stone columns in coral sand after construction completion. The proposed modified model is based on the core premise that the particle breakage of coral sand has been fully completed during the vibro-compaction construction stage, and the soil around the pile has reached a stable dense state. Within the range of design ultimate load, the secondary particle breakage of the compacted coral sand is not significant, and the strength enhancement effect caused by construction-stage breakage is dominant. For the long-term bearing capacity attenuation caused by continuous particle breakage under long-term load, it needs to be further corrected by combining with the creep characteristics of coral sand, which is not included in the scope of this study.

This study posits that this breakage-induced compaction process is not merely a simple volumetric contraction; rather, it triggers a dual nonlinear mechanical reinforcement mechanism: an energy-equivalent additional confining pressure and a breakage-friction coupling effect.

General modified formula for lateral ultimate stress considering dual-mechanism coupling

Synthesizing the physical evolution processes of the two aforementioned reinforcement mechanisms, the ultimate lateral stress of vibro-replacement stone columns in coral sand foundations is no longer the passive earth pressure under a singular assumption. By adopting the dual mechanisms of energy-equivalent additional confining pressure and breakage-friction coupling, the isolated static equilibrium term and the additional deformation term in traditional models can achieve profound physical integration.

Under the limit equilibrium state, the total effective confining pressure at the calculation depth is jointly composed of the natural soil self-weight stress and the additional confining pressure converted from breakage energy dissipation. The first term realizes the enhancement of total confining pressure: the additional term in the parentheses abandons the traditional empirical estimation based on relative density, directly quantifies the conversion law of construction mechanical energy into effective constraining lateral pressure, and reflects the contribution of passive densification under high-energy vibro-replacement construction. The second term achieves the nonlinear amplification of shear resistance: driven by fines filling and skeleton reorganization, the soil’s shear resistance is strengthened, which is macroscopically manifested as an increase in the internal friction angle and the nonlinear amplification of the passive earth pressure multiplier. Moreover, the product of these two terms forms a superposition effect of the dual mechanisms, which transcends the limitations of single-factor modification and embodies the physical essence of the multiplicative, synergistic growth of lateral ultimate stress under dual-mechanism coupling. This mathematical degeneration not only bridges the theoretical gap of the classical CET in the application to crushable carbonate soils but also fundamentally substantiates the universality and rigor of the proposed modified model within the framework of classical geotechnical mechanics.

Engineering case verification and comparative analysis

Engineering background and parameter determination

To verify the accuracy and engineering applicability of the proposed general modified formula considering the breakage-friction coupling dual mechanism, this study conducts an empirical calculation based on a vibro-replacement stone column ground improvement project for an airport runway test section in coral sand geological conditions. The site is primarily composed of hydraulically filled biogenic coral sand, exhibiting typical characteristics of high porosity, high compressibility and high particle crushability. The runway structure adopts a strip foundation (B = 2 m), and the foundation is treated with high-power vibro-replacement stone columns, with crushed stone as the filling material for the pile body. The results indicate that if the traditional Terzaghi theory without breakage modification or the empirical formula considering only a single additional confining pressure term is continuously used, the design estimates will be heavily conservative. This is because they completely neglect the stress loss caused by the extremely high compressibility of coral sand, as well as the bidirectional amplification triggered by particle breakage and skeleton reorganization. This leads to an error approaching or exceeding 50% compared to the in-situ measurements.

Conversely, the calculated using the dual-mechanism coupling formula proposed in this study falls perfectly within the envelope of in-situ measurements, and the relative error of the prediction ranges from − 5.3% to + 5.0%.

From the quantitative analysis of the contribution proportions, the energy-equivalent additional confining pressure increased the equivalent confining pressure term by 109%, while the increase in the internal friction angle caused by enhanced skeleton interlocking amplified the resistance coefficient by 7.7%. The nonlinear coupling and superposition effect of these two major mechanisms reaches up to 135%, accurately and successfully capturing the true physical essence of the macroscopic lateral pressure response in breakage coral sand foundations. This theory significantly reduces the traditional calculation error from 50% to < 5%, greatly improving the accuracy of rapid fill design and settlement control for coral sands.

Applicability and limitations of the proposed model

The proposed model is mainly applicable to saturated coral sand foundations improved by vibro-replacement stone columns under construction energy levels, drainage conditions, and column spacing comparable to those in the present engineering case. Its accuracy depends on the representativeness of the measured void ratio, the calibration of , , and , and the assumption that construction-stage particle breakage dominates the post-treatment mechanical response. For sites with strong soil heterogeneity, poor drainage, significantly different column spacing, or continued particle breakage and creep under long-term loading, additional calibration or correction may be required before direct application.

Conclusion

To tackle the pivotal engineering challenges marked by substantial calculation discrepancies and inadequate design foundations pertaining to the bearing capacity of vibro-replacement stone columns embedded in coral sand, this study establishes a modified bulging limit equilibrium theory. This theory explicitly incorporates the energy dissipation and dual coupling mechanisms of particle breakage and has been rigorously verified through in-situ testing and engineering measurements. The primary conclusions drawn from this study are outlined as follows:

Elucidation of the dual effect and breakage-friction coupling mechanism of coral sand particle breakage. During the high-frequency vibration compaction stage of vibro-replacement construction, coral sand exhibits significant particle breakage and volumetric contraction. Research demonstrates that this breakage has a dual effect: the dominant positive effect is that the crushed fine particles fill the inter-particle voids, enhance the interlocking action of the reorganized skeleton, and improve the lateral restraint and shear strength of the soil; the adverse effect is that particle edge wear will weaken the inter-particle interlocking, which is not significant under the design load after construction. Failure to account for the dual enhancements (lateral constraint increment and internal friction angle elevation) induced by this breakage-compaction process is the root cause of distortions in traditional methods.
