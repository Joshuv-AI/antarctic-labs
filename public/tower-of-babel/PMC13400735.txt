Ultralight infrared-controlled wireless neuromodulation systems for freely behaving mice
Authors: Hyeonggyeong Cha, Yeonghwa Hong, Giheon Kim, Haeyun Lee, Minseok Kim, Se Hwan Park, Seungjun Lee, Namsun Chou, Hyogeun Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuromodulation techniques including transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and deep brain stimulation (DBS) have been widely investigated for their therapeutic potential in a range of neurological and psychiatric disorders. Basic studies using freely behaving animal models are critical for elucidating the underlying mechanisms of these neuromodulation techniques. However, conventional neuromodulation systems typically rely on wired connections or wireless systems incorporating communication and control modules, which increase device weight and volume, restrict natural behavior, and may introduce confounding factors in behavioral experiments. Here, we present an ultra-lightweight wireless neuromodulation system that can be externally controlled using infrared (IR) light, eliminating the need for complex wireless communication modules. The system incorporates wavelength-selective phototransistors (810 and 950 nm) to enable independent control of tDCS and DBS. The complete tDCS device weighs less than 1.5 g, while the DBS device weighs less than 0.5 g. Characterization of the IR LED array demonstrated uniform light distribution and high thermal stability, with no detectable temperature changes in the experimental environment during prolonged illumination. Open-field behavioral testing confirmed that neither device attachment nor IR illumination affected spontaneous locomotor activity in mice. In stimulation experiments targeting the secondary motor cortex (M2), both tDCS and DBS induced robust circling behavior in freely behaving mice, demonstrating effective modulation of motor-related neural circuits. These results indicate that the proposed systems enable reliable and selective neuromodulation without constraining natural behavior, providing a versatile platform for future behavioral and mechanistic studies of neuromodulation.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Optical sensors

Introduction

Electrical neuromodulation techniques, including transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and deep brain stimulation (DBS), have been extensively investigated for their therapeutic potential in a broad range of neurological and psychiatric disorders, such as depression, anxiety disorders, Parkinson’s disease, and epilepsy1–6. These stimulation modalities modulate brain function primarily by altering the excitability and activity patterns of neural circuits. As clinical interest in neuromodulation continues to expand, there is an increasing emphasis on fundamental studies aimed at elucidating the underlying mechanisms and functional effects of electrical stimulation with high spatial and temporal precision. Rigorous investigation of the mechanisms underlying therapeutic neuromodulation requires experiments in freely behaving animal models, in which behavioral artifacts and confounding factors unrelated to stimulation can be minimized7,8.

In conventional neuromodulation systems, modification of stimulation parameters such as intensity and frequency typically requires wired connections9,10 or, in wireless implementations11–14, relatively bulky architectures that incorporate transmitters, receivers, and control circuitry. These design constraints can interfere with natural behavior in freely moving mice and introduce potential confounding variables in behavioral experiments. Moreover, many existing wireless systems rely on auxiliary mechanical supports, such as crown-type structures, to ensure stable attachment to the animal’s head10,13–15. These additional components further increase overall system weight and volume, potentially inducing stress during long-term or repeated experiments and ultimately compromising data reliability. As summarized in Table S1, most existing systems rely on microcontroller-based control architectures and comparatively bulky configurations, which increase system complexity and may interfere with natural behavior in freely moving animals. In addition, a previously developed light-controlled system is constrained to single-site stimulation and single-modality operation.

In this study, we introduce a phototransistor-based infrared (IR) control strategy as an alternative wireless neuromodulation approach that eliminates the need for communication and control modules (Fig. 1). By directly driving phototransistors using externally applied IR light, we achieved a structurally simple and ultra-lightweight wireless neuromodulation system. Furthermore, by employing phototransistors selectively responsive to distinct wavelength bands (810 and 950 nm) in combination with optical filtering, we developed a system capable of independently controlling two stimulation modalities or sites. The fabricated tDCS device weighs 1.22 g including the battery and 1.38 g including fixation screws and nuts, making it well suited for use in mouse models. The DBS system was further miniaturized, with a device weight of 0.4 g and an electrode weight of ~5 mg. In addition, we implemented a novel mounting strategy that enables stable fixation without bulky crown-type structures, thereby substantially reducing the overall physical burden on the animal.

Behavioral validation in freely behaving mice demonstrated that neither device attachment nor IR illumination produced detectable changes in spontaneous locomotor activity, confirming minimal interference with natural behavior. Subsequent stimulation experiments targeting the M2 region reliably induced turning behavior, indicating effective and targeted stimulation of the intended brain region. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the proposed ultra-lightweight, IR-controlled wireless tDCS and DBS system enables reliable neuromodulation while minimizing physical and behavioral confounds in freely behaving animal models. This platform is expected to facilitate more accurate mechanistic studies of neuromodulation and advance behavior-based neuroscience research.

Materials and methods

Overall design of the infrared-controlled wireless neuromodulation system

We developed an ultra-lightweight, infrared–controlled wireless neuromodulation system for experiments in freely behaving mice. The system operates without conventional control electronics, such as RF communication modules, Wi-Fi components, or microcontrollers. Instead, phototransistors are directly driven by externally applied IR light. This approach substantially simplifies the system architecture while minimizing overall weight and volume, without compromising the stability or reliability of stimulation delivery.

To enable selective control of multiple stimulation channels while minimizing inter-channel crosstalk, two phototransistors with distinct wavelength sensitivities were employed: one responsive to the 800 nm wavelength range (SCM-014TB, ROHM) and another responsive to the 940 nm wavelength range (VEMT3700F, Vishay). Each phototransistor was configured to be independently activated by IR illumination at its corresponding wavelength.

Because the 800 nm phototransistor exhibits partial sensitivity to longer wavelengths, including 940 nm, additional measures were required to ensure robust wavelength selectivity. To suppress undesired activation, a two-step dip-coating process was employed on the light-receiving surface of the 800 nm phototransistor. First, the device was dip-coated with Loctite 401 adhesive at a withdrawal speed of 5 mm/s. Immediately afterward, a second dip-coating step was performed at the same withdrawal speed using an infrared optical filter solution prepared by dissolving 39.8 mg of an IR-absorbing filter material (Epolight 1125, Epolin) in 265 µL of cyclohexanone (C102180, Sigma-Aldrich). The coated devices were then dried under ambient conditions. The resulting coating effectively attenuated its response to 940 nm illumination, thereby enhancing wavelength selectivity and reducing inter-channel crosstalk.

The complete wireless neuromodulation system comprises a power supply, phototransistor-based optical switching circuitry, stimulation electrodes, and an external IR LED array. Electrical stimulation is delivered only when the corresponding phototransistor is activated by IR light of a specific wavelength emitted from the LED array. This configuration enables fully wireless neuromodulation in freely behaving mice without physical tethering. Moreover, the system architecture provides a scalable framework for independently controlling multiple stimulation sites while maintaining minimal crosstalk during simultaneous or sequential stimulation.

Circuit design of the tDCS and DBS systems

To deliver effective tDCS to the mouse cerebral cortex, stimulation currents on the order of ~100 μA are required. Given the electrical resistance of the mouse skull and brain tissue, an output voltage of approximately 10 V is necessary to achieve stable current delivery16,17. To meet this requirement, four coin-type batteries (MS621FE-FL11E, Seiko Instruments), each with a nominal voltage of 3.1–3.3 V, were connected in series to form the power supply, providing a stable output voltage exceeding 11 V at the anode electrode.

In the tDCS circuit, a wavelength-selective switching mechanism was implemented using a p-channel MOSFET (621-DMP510DL-7, Diodes Inc.) to ensure precise stimulation control. The tDCS circuit incorporated 10 kΩ collector resistors (Rc1 and Rc2; 1005 metric size, R1608G103J1) to determine the gate switching voltage, alongside 10 kΩ emitter resistors (1005 metric size; R1608G103J1) configured to limit the current flow against conductivity changes. The voltage node derived from the junction between the collector resistor and phototransistor was coupled to the gate terminal of the MOSFET. This design enables precise on–off control of stimulation using external optical input alone, eliminating the need for wired connections or active electronic control circuitry.

In contrast, the DBS system was designed to generate pulsed electrical stimulation rather than continuous direct current. The DBS circuit incorporated a 100 kΩ resistor (1005 metric size; R1005G104J1, PSA) connected in series with a phototransistor. The voltage node between the resistor and phototransistor was coupled to the gate terminal of a dual-channel p-channel MOSFET (DMP68D1LVQ, Diodes), forming an optically controlled switching architecture.

In this configuration, IR illumination at the appropriate wavelength modulated phototransistor conductivity, thereby altering the voltage at the resistor–phototransistor junction. This voltage modulation controlled the gate voltage of the p-channel MOSFET, opening or closing the current path to generate pulsed electrical stimulation. Consequently, DBS pulse waveforms were produced solely through optical control, without digital timing circuits, microcontrollers, or waveform generators.

Both the tDCS and DBS circuits exploit the wavelength-selective activation of phototransistors to enable independent optical control. By applying IR light at different wavelengths, each stimulation modality can be selectively and reliably activated. This circuit design minimizes system complexity while enabling ultra-lightweight, wireless neuromodulation suitable for experiments in freely behaving mouse models.

Design of the infrared LED array and optical illumination environment

IR LED arrays with distinct wavelength bands were designed as external light sources for operating the phototransistor-based wireless neuromodulation system18. LEDs with peak emission wavelengths of 810 nm (MTE1081C, Marktech) and 950 nm (SFH 4545, ams-OSRAM) were used to selectively activate the phototransistors in the tDCS and DBS circuits, respectively.

For each wavelength, a printed circuit board (PCB) was fabricated by connecting nine LEDs in series. Four identical PCBs were then connected in parallel to form a single LED array. This configuration provided sufficient optical output while reducing variability in illumination caused by differences among individual LEDs.

The LED array was designed to provide highly uniform illumination across the floor of the experimental arena. To achieve this, individual LED modules were arranged with 5 cm spacing, and the entire array was mounted at a height of 25 cm above the arena floor. This spatial configuration reduces illumination variability caused by changes in the animal’s position and helps maintain stable optical stimulation conditions throughout the experiment.

The optical illumination environment was optimized specifically for freely behaving animal experiments. The LED array was oriented to emit light downward toward the arena floor, enabling the phototransistors to receive consistent optical input regardless of the animal’s movement or location.

Overall, the design of the IR LED array and illumination environment provides the external conditions necessary for stable operation of the IR-controlled wireless neuromodulation system and represents a key element for implementing multi-wavelength–based stimulation control in freely behaving mouse models.

Electrode design and fabrication

The design and fabrication of the tDCS electrodes followed the procedures established in our previous study13. These electrodes were designed to conform stably to the scalp surface of mice, enabling reliable delivery of direct current stimulation. The electrode structure and materials were directly adopted from the previously validated design.

For DBS, a bipolar electrode configuration was employed to achieve more spatially localized stimulation compared with a monopolar configuration19. The DBS electrode array consisted of two shanks with an inter-shank spacing of 1.2 mm. Each shank incorporated circular electrode sites with a diameter of 50 μm, arranged along the vertical axis with a spacing of 150 μm. This geometry was selected to enable spatially confined and selective electrical stimulation within the target brain region.

The DBS electrodes were fabricated using a flexible PCB (fPCB)–based process. The fabrication followed the same tin (Sn) electroplating–based method reported in our previous work20. In the present study, both the line width and interline spacing were designed to be 70 μm, ensuring adequate mechanical robustness and electrical reliability of the electrode patterns.

An FPC/FFC connector (503480-0400, Molex) was used to interface the electrodes with the system circuitry. To improve mechanical stability under repeated connections and external mechanical loading, a 0.3 mm–thick reinforcement film was applied at the connector junction. In addition, the junction region was designed to be ~twice the connector insertion depth (1.9 mm), allowing it to function as a structural support element within the system. This design minimizes the risk of mechanical damage during handling or behavioral experiments.

Overall, the fabricated tDCS and DBS electrodes were optimized for their respective stimulation modalities and designed for stable integration with the ultra-lightweight wireless neuromodulation system.

Simulation and electrochemical characterization of DBS electrodes

To quantitatively evaluate the stimulation range and current distribution of the DBS electrodes, electric field simulations were performed using Sim4Life Lite (ZMT, Switzerland). A spatial resolution with a minimum voxel size of 1 μm was used. Brain tissue was modeled as gray matter, with electrical properties obtained from the IT’IS LF 5.0 database. The electrical conductivity of gray matter was set to 0.419 S·m−1.

The electrode shanks were modeled as being fully embedded in gray matter, and a Dirichlet boundary condition of 3 V was applied to the anode electrode surface. Simulation results were analyzed in the mediolateral–anteroposterior (ML–AP) plane by examining the current density distribution. The stimulation range was defined as the distance from the electrode center at which the current density decreased to 1% of maximum value, enabling quantitative assessment of the spatial confinement of DBS stimulation.

To ensure stable and reliable DBS signal delivery, the electrode surfaces were electroplated with black platinum (black Pt)21. Electrodeposition was carried out under constant-current conditions using a NanoZ system (White Matter LLC, USA). The plating solution consisted of 3 wt% chloroplatinic acid (H2PtCl6; 262587, Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.03 wt% lead(II) acetate trihydrate (Pb(CH3COO)2; 32307, Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in deionized water.

Electroplating was performed in a two-electrode configuration, with the Sn electrode serving as the WE and a Pt wire as the CE. A constant current of −4 μA (vs CE) was applied for 32 s. Following electroplating, the electrodes were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and dried at room temperature.

The electrochemical properties of the black Pt–coated DBS electrodes were characterized in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV). Measurements were conducted in a three-electrode configuration, with the fPCB-based DBS electrode as the working electrode, an Ag/AgCl wire as the reference electrode, and a Pt wire as the counter electrode.

EIS measurements were performed over a frequency range of 100 Hz to 104 Hz using a 10 mVrms sinusoidal excitation. CV measurements were conducted over a potential window from −0.6 V to +0.8 V (vs Ag/AgCl) at a scan rate of 50 mV·s−1.

Characterization of tDCS and DBS systems

The performance of the optical filter coating was first characterized by evaluating its thickness and wavelength-dependent transmission properties. For thickness measurements, coated phototransistors were sectioned using a razor blade, and cross-sectional images were obtained using a benchtop scanning electron microscope (EM-30, COXEM, Korea). The coating thickness was measured from the acquired SEM images. To evaluate the optical transmission characteristics of the filter layer, the filter solution was coated onto a glass slide and analyzed using a UV–Vis–NIR spectrophotometer (SolidSpec-3700i, SHIMADZU, Japan). Transmission spectra were measured over the wavelength range of 400–1200 nm at 1 nm intervals.

Electrical characterization was performed to verify that the fabricated tDCS and DBS systems exhibited stable and intended output characteristics in response to external IR illumination, and to evaluate battery lifetime. Output voltages were measured using an oscilloscope (Analog Discovery 3, Digilent, USA), and the anode node voltage of each system was monitored in real time.

During measurements, Channel 1 of the oscilloscope was connected to the node activated by 810 nm IR illumination, while Channel 2 was connected to the node activated by 950 nm IR illumination, allowing simultaneous comparison of output characteristics under the two wavelength conditions.

To analyze the wavelength-dependent response characteristics of the phototransistors, an LED array integrating multiple visible (RGB) and near-infrared (NIR) light sources was prepared. Red (5R3UC2-06), green (5BG4UC00), and blue (5BB4SC00) LEDs (DAWANG, China) were used as visible light sources. To ensure consistent experimental conditions, the optical power density of each visible LED was adjusted to 1.1 mW/cm², corresponding to the maximum output power among the visible light sources. The optical power of all light sources was measured using an optical power meter (PM100D, Thorlabs, USA). In addition, a custom black enclosure with a height of 5 cm was fabricated to block external light interference and support the PCB during measurements. Under each wavelength-specific illumination condition, the test circuit was configured and the output current generated by the phototransistor was measured.

To emulate in vivo stimulation conditions, equivalent resistive loads were connected between the anode and cathode terminals. A 110 kΩ resistor was used for the tDCS system, and a 22 kΩ resistor was used for the DBS system. These resistance values were selected to approximate the effective electrical load presented by mouse tissue during stimulation.

Physical configuration during electrical testing was designed to replicate the behavioral experimental setup. The stimulation circuits were positioned 5 cm above the floor, while the external IR LED array was mounted 25 cm above the floor, matching the optical irradiation conditions used in behavioral experiments.

Battery lifetime was evaluated under the same IR illumination conditions used during stimulation experiments. The voltage and current supplied to the LED array were identical to those used in tDCS and DBS experiments, and system output voltages were continuously monitored for 15 h under each condition. This procedure enabled assessment of long-term electrical stability and battery-powered operational performance.

Overall, these measurements validated the output characteristics, IR responsiveness, and prolonged operational stability of the tDCS and DBS systems.

Optical irradiation uniformity and thermal stability

To verify that the IR LED array provided uniform optical irradiation in a freely moving behavioral environment and to assess potential thermal effects during prolonged illumination, validation experiments were conducted. An acrylic chamber with an internal volume of 29 × 25 × 25 cm3 was fabricated to replicate the behavioral testing arena. The chamber dimensions allowed unrestricted mouse movement and closely matched the conditions used in behavioral experiments.

To evaluate heat generation during LED operation, the internal temperature of the chamber was monitored for 15 min while each LED array was continuously illuminated. Temperature measurements were obtained using an infrared thermal camera, and changes within the chamber were recorded before, during, and after IR illumination. This assessment determined whether LED operation induced any measurable temperature increase in the experimental environment.

To quantify the optical power delivered to the arena floor, optical output was measured using an optical power meter (1936-R, Newport, USA). Measurements were performed at floor level across the entire region accessible to the animals during behavioral testing.

To minimize optical crosstalk between stimulation channels, different input power conditions were applied to the 810 nm LED array depending on the stimulation paradigm. For tDCS experiments, the array was driven at 15.7 V and 330 mA (total power, 5.18 W), whereas for DBS experiments, an input of 14.7 V and 200 mA (total power, 2.94 W) was used. The 950 nm LED array was operated at 11.18 V with a total power of 0.98 W for all experiments.

These power settings enabled selective activation of wavelength-specific phototransistors while effectively suppressing optical crosstalk between channels. Together, these validation experiments confirmed that the LED array provided uniform optical irradiation and a thermally stable environment suitable for freely behaving behavioral studies.

In vivo surgical procedures

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Korea Brain Research Institute (KBRI). Adult male C57BL/6J mice ( ≥ 8 weeks old) were used throughout the study.

Surgical procedures were performed under isoflurane inhalation anesthesia. Anesthesia was induced with 4% isoflurane and maintained at 1.5–2% during surgery. Mice were secured in an automated stereotaxic frame (71000 Automated Stereotaxic Instrument, RWD Life Science, USA), and the scalp was incised to expose the skull.

Electrode implantation sites were determined using the Paxinos and Franklin mouse brain atlas22. The M2 was targeted for both tDCS and DBS stimulation. Depending on the experimental condition, electrodes were implanted in either the left or right M2 hemisphere. For DBS implantation, stereotaxic coordinates were set to mediolateral (ML) ± 0.6 mm, anteroposterior (AP) 1.1 mm, and dorsoventral (DV) 1.25 mm. For tDCS, surface electrodes were attached over the M2 region at mediolateral (ML) ± 0.6 mm and anteroposterior (AP) 1.1 mm.

Small craniotomies for electrode insertion and additional holes for fixation screws were drilled using a micro-drill. Electrodes were slowly lowered to the target depth and secured with stainless steel anchor screws and dental cement (Vertex Self-Curing, Vertex Dental, Netherlands).

Following electrode implantation, the system circuitry was connected, and the device was stably mounted on the skull using the proposed fixation method without a crown-type support structure. The surgical site was then sealed with dental cement to prevent external exposure.

After surgery, mice were placed on a heating pad until recovery of body temperature and then returned to individual cages. A minimum recovery period of one week was allowed before behavioral and neuromodulation experiments. During recovery, animals were monitored daily for general health and implant integrity.

Evaluation of system-induced effects using the open-field test

To evaluate the effects of the infrared-controlled wireless neuromodulation system and IR illumination on locomotor activity in freely behaving mice, an open-field test (OFT) was performed23–25. The experiments were designed to independently assess the influence of system attachment and external IR exposure on spontaneous behavior.

To examine the effect of system attachment, OFT was conducted in four mice one week after electrode implantation and fixation screw surgery. Tests were performed in an open-top acrylic arena (40 × 40 × 40 cm3), and behavior was recorded for 20 min using an overhead camera. Four days later, the same animals were tested again in the identical arena after removal of the system, allowing within-subject comparison of locomotor behavior with and without the implanted device.

To assess the effect of IR illumination, OFT was performed in a separate cohort of four mice. These experiments were conducted in an open-top acrylic arena (35 × 25 × 25 cm3) under three conditions: no LED illumination, 810 nm IR illumination, and 950 nm IR illumination. Mouse behavior under each condition was recorded using the same overhead camera setup.

Recorded video data were processed using idTracker to extract positional coordinates, and locomotor trajectories were analyzed in MATLAB (MathWorks, USA)26. Quantitative measures included total travel distance, mean locomotion speed, and spatial distribution of movement trajectories. These metrics enabled objective assessment of changes in spontaneous locomotor activity associated with system attachment and IR illumination.

Circling behavior test

To assess the functional efficacy of the infrared-controlled wireless neuromodulation system, a circling behavior test was conducted. We selected the M2 region as the stimulation target, as it is known to elicit rotational behavior upon stimulation13. Depending on the experimental condition, stimulation was delivered to either the left or right M2.

All behavioral experiments were conducted in an acrylic arena measuring 29 × 25 × 25 cm3. Each trial lasted 5 min, consisting of a 2.5 min baseline period without stimulation followed immediately by a 2.5 min stimulation period.

For tDCS experiments, continuous direct current (DC) stimulation was applied throughout the stimulation phase. A 950 nm IR LED was used to activate the tDCS circuit targeting the right M2, producing an anode voltage exceeding 11 V during stimulation.

For DBS experiments, pulsed electrical stimulation was delivered in a burst pattern. Each burst consisted of 10 pulses at 250 Hz with a 20% duty cycle, and bursts were repeated at 10 Hz with a 50% duty cycle. Selective activation of stimulation sites was achieved using wavelength-specific IR illumination: an 810 nm LED for left M2 stimulation and a 950 nm LED for right M2 stimulation.

To better visualize circling behavior, we calculated the cumulative rotation angle based on the frame-by-frame displacement of the animal trajectory (Note S1). The heading angle was obtained using the atan2 function, and angle unwrapping was applied to ensure continuity. The cumulative sum of angular changes was used to determine directional bias over time (positive for counter-clockwise, negative for clockwise rotation).

Circling behavior was defined as a rotation of 270° or greater around the animal’s own axis and was counted as a single event. Rotation direction (clockwise or counter-clockwise) was analyzed according to the stimulation site. Stimulation-induced behavioral effects were evaluated by comparing circling behavior between baseline and stimulation periods for each condition.

Results and discussion

Concept and operating principle of the infrared-controlled wireless neuromodulation system

We developed an ultra-lightweight, wireless neuromodulation system that is externally controlled by IR illumination, enabling neural stimulation in freely behaving mice (Fig. 1a). The central operating principle of this system is the wavelength-selective activation of phototransistors, which enables neural stimulation without reliance on wireless communication modules or complex electronic control circuitry.

The system supports two neuromodulation modalities: tDCS and DBS. The tDCS module delivers continuous direct current to the cerebral cortex through skull-mounted electrodes and has a total system mass of less than 1.5 g, including the battery (Fig. 1a). In contrast, the DBS module provides localized, pulsed electrical stimulation through a multichannel electrode implanted within brain tissue, with a total system mass of less than 0.5 g (Fig. 1a).

In both configurations, externally applied IR light serves as the control input. Incident IR illumination activates the phototransistor integrated into the circuit, thereby triggering electrical stimulation through the corresponding electrodes. Continuous IR illumination produces sustained direct current stimulation in the tDCS system, whereas temporally modulated optical input generates pulsed electrical stimulation in the DBS system.

Selective stimulation of distinct neural targets is achieved using IR light at different wavelengths (Fig. 1b). Specifically, LED arrays operating at 810 nm and 950 nm enable independent activation of the left and right secondary motor cortex (M2) regions. The LED arrays were mounted above the behavioral arena and engineered to provide spatially uniform illumination, ensuring consistent optical activation regardless of the animal’s position or movement.

By eliminating receiving antennas and wireless communication electronics, this infrared-based control strategy substantially simplifies system architecture and enables marked reductions in device size and weight. Consequently, the proposed platform offers a lightweight and scalable approach for wireless neuromodulation in freely behaving animal models, while allowing external control over stimulation modality, target location, and temporal patterning.

Design and implementation of wireless tDCS and DBS systems

To realize the IR-controlled wireless neuromodulation platform, we designed and fabricated two distinct ultralight circuit architectures optimized for tDCS and DBS, respectively (Fig. 2). In both systems, phototransistors serve as the primary optical switching elements, enabling wavelength-selective activation in response to external IR illumination.

The tDCS system was designed to deliver continuous direct current while providing a sufficiently high output voltage for effective cortical stimulation. Four coin-cell batteries with nominal voltages of 3.1–3.3 V were connected in series to achieve this requirement (Fig. 2a). A wavelength-selective phototransistor was placed to selectively connect the battery output and the anode electrode, such that the anode voltage increased only upon exposure to IR light at the designated wavelength. This configuration ensured that stimulation was applied exclusively during optical illumination and completely suppressed in its absence.

The fabricated tDCS circuit was integrated onto a compact PCB and mounted on the skull together with the electrodes, batteries, fixation screws, and nuts (Fig. 2b). The overall footprint was comparable to that of a coin, and the ultralight design minimized mechanical burden and interference with natural locomotion in freely behaving mice.

In contrast, the DBS system employed a distinct circuit architecture to generate temporally precise pulsed stimulation (Fig. 2c). A phototransistor and a 100 kΩ resistor were connected in series, and the voltage at their junction was applied to the gate of a dual-channel p-channel MOSFET. This arrangement formed an optically controlled switching circuit in which IR-induced changes in phototransistor conductivity modulated the MOSFET gate voltage, thereby enabling pulsed electrical stimulation in response to external optical input.

The DBS system was integrated onto an ultraminiaturized PCB and fabricated as a monolithic structure directly connected to the implanted DBS electrode (Fig. 2d). The system dimensions were maintained at millimeter-scale dimensions, and the total mass, including the battery and electrode, was kept below 0.5 g.

External optical control was provided by two infrared LED arrays operating at distinct wavelengths (Fig. 2e-f). LEDs emitting at 810 and 950 nm were arranged in series strings of nine devices, with four such strings connected in parallel to form each array. The inter-LED spacing was set to 50 mm to ensure spatially uniform illumination across the behavioral arena floor. The completed arrays were positioned above the experimental arena, providing consistent optical input independent of the animal’s position.

Collectively, the wireless tDCS and DBS systems and their corresponding LED arrays fulfill the requirements of wavelength-selective control, ultra-lightweight construction, and simplified circuit design, establishing a robust hardware platform for wireless neuromodulation experiments in freely behaving mice.

Design, and electrical, and electrochemical characterization of the DBS electrode

To enable spatially confined and precise deep-brain stimulation, we designed a bipolar DBS electrode and systematically characterized its electrical and electrochemical properties (Fig. 3). The electrode consists of two parallel shanks, each incorporating circular electrode sites with a diameter of 50 μm. The sites are arranged along the shank axis with an inter-site spacing of 150 μm, while the inter-shank distance was set to 1.2 mm. This bipolar configuration was intended to achieve a more localized stimulation profile than that of conventional monopolar electrodes (Fig. 3a).

Electric field simulations were performed to quantitatively evaluate the spatial distribution of stimulation produced by the electrode geometry (Fig. 3b). The simulated current density decreased rapidly with increasing distance from the electrode surface. The lateral distance at which the current density dropped to 1% of its maximum value was ~0.11 mm, indicating that the electrode generated highly localized stimulation.

The DBS electrodes were fabricated using Sn as the base material and subsequently electroplated with black Pt to improve electrical stability and stimulation efficiency at the electrode–tissue interface (Fig. 3c, d). Optical microscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images confirmed that the black Pt layer was uniformly deposited and exhibited a porous, rough morphology characteristic of high-surface-area platinum coatings (Fig. 3d).

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was used to further examine surface topography at the nanoscale (Fig. 3e). While the bare Sn electrodes displayed relatively smooth surfaces, the black Pt–coated electrodes exhibited pronounced nanoscale roughness on the order of several hundred nanometers, reflecting a substantial increase in effective surface area.

The functional impact of this surface modification was assessed through electrochemical characterization. EIS revealed that black Pt–coated electrodes exhibited markedly lower impedance across the measured frequency range compared with bare Sn electrodes. Notably, at 1 kHz—a frequency relevant to neural stimulation—the impedance was reduced by approximately one to two orders of magnitude (Fig. 3f).

CV measurements further demonstrated that the black Pt electrodes exhibited stable current responses over a wide potential window and a significantly increased charge storage capacity (Fig. 3g), indicating enhanced electrochemical performance and improved current delivery stability20.

Collectively, the bipolar DBS electrode developed in this study combines a highly localized stimulation profile with robust electrochemical characteristics, making it well suited for integration into the proposed ultralight, infrared-controlled wireless DBS system.

To enhance wavelength selectivity, an infrared-absorbing coating was applied to the phototransistors (Fig. 4a). This coating was designed to suppress unintended activation by off-target infrared wavelengths. Cross-sectional SEM imaging confirmed the formation of a uniform coating layer with a thickness of 44.4 ± 2.5 µm (Fig. S1a, b). In addition, transmission measurements demonstrated that the coated filter effectively attenuated light in the 950 nm wavelength region while maintaining relatively higher transmission near the 800 nm range, thereby improving wavelength selectivity and reducing inter-channel crosstalk (Fig. S1c). Experimental measurements showed that, under 950 nm LED illumination, phototransistors without the coating produced high output voltages, whereas coated devices exhibited substantially reduced output voltages (Fig. 4b). To further evaluate wavelength-dependent phototransistor selectivity, an LED array integrating multiple visible and near-infrared light sources with different emission wavelengths was constructed (Fig. S2). The output current responses of the bare and coated 800 nm phototransistors, as well as the 940 nm phototransistors, were measured under illumination from LEDs with different wavelengths (Fig. S2a, b). Before coating, the 800 nm phototransistor exhibited substantial responses not only to the target 810 nm illumination but also under 950 nm illumination (Fig. S2c). After application of the infrared-absorbing coating, the coated 800 nm phototransistor showed markedly suppressed responses across the visible wavelength range and under 950 nm illumination, while maintaining selective responsiveness near the target 810 nm wavelength region (Fig. S2c). In addition, the 940 nm phototransistor exhibited minimal responses to LEDs with wavelengths below 810 nm and selectively responded to 950 nm illumination (Fig. S2d).

These results demonstrate effective suppression of cross-wavelength activation and enhancement of wavelength selectivity, which was consistently observed in both the tDCS and DBS systems.

The output characteristics of the tDCS system were assessed by monitoring anode voltage responses under wavelength-specific illumination (Fig. 4c). Illumination with an 810 nm LED selectively increased the anode voltage of tDCS channel 1, while channel 2 remained inactive. Conversely, 950 nm illumination selectively activated channel 2 without affecting channel 1. These results confirm that multiple stimulation channels can be independently and reliably controlled using infrared light of different wavelengths.

Long-term output stability of the tDCS system was evaluated by monitoring battery voltage over time (Fig. 4d). Following initial activation, the output voltage remained stable, with no abrupt degradation observed over several tens of hours, indicating suitability for prolonged behavioral experiments.

For the DBS system, pulsed stimulation output was characterized by analyzing anode voltage waveforms (Fig. 4e). In response to infrared illumination, burst-type pulse stimulation was generated independently in DBS channels 1 and 2, with the predefined burst structure (10 pulses per burst) delivered reproducibly and with high temporal stability. This confirms that phototransistor-based optical switching enables precise optical control of pulsed electrical stimulation.

Finally, battery endurance of the DBS system was assessed during extended operation (Fig. 4f). The battery voltage remained stable throughout prolonged stimulation sessions, demonstrating that reliable repetitive DBS stimulation can be achieved using an ultralight coin-cell power supply.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the proposed wireless tDCS and DBS systems provide robust wavelength-selective control, stable stimulation outputs, and long-term operational reliability, establishing a dependable platform for infrared-controlled neuromodulation in freely behaving mouse models.

Thermal stability and irradiation uniformity of the infrared LED array

Stable operation of the wireless neuromodulation system requires that the IR LED array maintain thermal stability during prolonged use while providing uniform optical illumination across the experimental arena. To verify these conditions, we quantitatively assessed both temperature variation and irradiation uniformity.

The optical environment was established using an acrylic enclosure measuring 29 × 25 × 25 cm3 (Fig. 5a). The LED array was mounted at the top of the enclosure and operated under the same voltage and current conditions as used in behavioral experiments. Temperature changes during LED operation were monitored for 15 minutes using an infrared thermal imaging camera while continuously illuminating either the 810 nm or 950 nm LEDs. Results showed no significant temperature increase over time for either wavelength, and the internal enclosure temperature remained nearly constant before, during, and after illumination (Fig. 5b). These findings confirm that the LED array exhibits high thermal stability during prolonged operation.

Thermal stability and illumination uniformity of the infrared LED array for wireless neuromodulation.

a Schematic of the experimental setup used to evaluate the infrared illumination environment. An acrylic enclosure (29 × 25 × 25 cm3) housed an overhead LED array PCB. A PCB support with a central opening allowed unobstructed optical access to the behavioral arena. b Temporal profile of enclosure temperature during continuous illumination with 810 nm and 950 nm LED arrays over 15 min. c Spatial distribution of optical power density measured at the bottom of the enclosure under different operating conditions: 810 nm LED for tDCS stimulation (15.7 V, 330 mA), 810 nm LED for DBS stimulation (14.7 V, 200 mA), and 950 nm LED for both tDCS and DBS stimulation (11.18 V, 88 mA)

Irradiation uniformity was evaluated by measuring the spatial distribution of optical power across the bottom surface of the enclosure using an optical power meter. For tDCS experiments, the 810 nm LED array was operated at 15.7 V and 330 mA, while for DBS experiments it was operated at 14.7 V and 200 mA. The 950 nm array was operated at 11.18 V and 88 mA for both conditions. Measurements indicated that optical intensity was distributed relatively uniformly across the entire enclosure, with minimal variation between maximum and minimum values (Fig. 5c).

Overall, these results demonstrate that the IR LED array provides a thermally stable and spatially uniform optical environment, supporting reliable, wavelength-selective phototransistor activation in freely behaving animal experiments.

Effects of system attachment and infrared irradiation on free-behavioral activity

To determine whether the wireless neuromodulation system affects basal locomotor activity, OFTs were conducted under conditions with and without system attachment, as well as under different IR illumination conditions.

First, the tDCS system was surgically implanted and mounted on the mouse skull, including electrode placement and system fixation (Fig. S3 and Movie S1). An identical procedure was applied for the DBS system (Fig. S4). All surgeries were performed stepwise to ensure secure skull fixation and stable device attachment, and the systems remained firmly in place post-surgery.

OFTs were then conducted in mice with the tDCS system to evaluate the effect of system attachment on spontaneous behavior. Comparisons of locomotor trajectories before and after system attachment revealed no notable differences in movement patterns or velocity distributions (Fig. S5 and Movie S2). Quantitative analyses confirmed that neither total travel distance nor average speed differed significantly between pre- and post-attachment conditions (Fig. 6a).

To assess the effect of IR illumination alone, OFTs were conducted in naïve mice without any implants. Three conditions were tested: no LED irradiation, 810 nm LED, and 950 nm LED. Locomotor trajectories and velocity distributions were comparable across all conditions, with no discernible behavioral changes attributable to IR exposure or wavelength (Fig. S6). Quantitative metrics of total travel distance and average speed similarly showed no statistically significant differences (Fig. 6b).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that neither the physical attachment of the ultralight wireless tDCS/DBS system nor IR-based optical control interferes with spontaneous locomotor behavior in freely moving mice. These findings confirm that any behavioral effects observed in subsequent experiments are attributable to targeted neural stimulation rather than mechanical burden or optical artifacts.

Direction-specific turning behavior induced by M2 stimulation

To evaluate the functional efficacy of the proposed system, we assessed motor responses elicited by targeted neural stimulation. The M2, which reliably induces turning behavior upon activation, was selected as the target region to generate clear, quantifiable behavioral outcomes. Stimulation of M2 produced distinct, direction-specific turning behaviors that depended on both the stimulation modality and the targeted hemisphere.

For tDCS, selective stimulation of the right M2 region induced a significant increase in counter-clockwise turning compared with the pre-stimulation baseline (Fig. 7 and Movies S3-S4). These results are consistent with prior studies showing that focal cortical stimulation, either via tDCS or optogenetic activation, can induce directional motor behaviors27.

A significant increase in turning behavior was observed during stimulation (t(3) = 4.456, p = 0.0210). Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 4 mice). Statistical analysis was performed using two-tailed paired t-test. *p < 0.05

In contrast, DBS elicited turning behaviors opposite to those observed under tDCS, with the direction determined by the stimulation site. Burst-type DBS applied to the left or right M2 region produced corresponding left- or right-biased rotations (Fig. 8 and Movie S5). Notably, DBS of the right M2 increased clockwise turning, opposite to the counter-clockwise bias induced by tDCS in the same region.

a Schematic of burst DBS applied to the left and right M2 regions. Left M2 stimulation preferentially induced counter-clockwise turning, whereas right M2 stimulation induced clockwise turning behavior. b Representative three-dimensional locomotion trajectories before and during burst DBS stimulation. Turning events are highlighted in red (counter-clockwise) and blue (clockwise), demonstrating direction-specific modulation depending on the stimulated hemisphere. c Quantitative comparison of mean turning frequency before and during DBS stimulation, showing a significant increase in turning behavior during stimulation (t(3) = 3.755, p = 0.0330). Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 4 mice). Statistical analysis was performed using two-tailed paired t-test. *p < 0.05

These modality-dependent differences likely reflect distinct underlying mechanisms. tDCS broadly modulates neuronal membrane potentials across cortical areas, directly altering local cortical excitability28. High-frequency burst DBS, however, preferentially activates axonal projections and connected neural circuits rather than somata, inducing behavioral changes indirectly through network modulation and neurotransmitter release29,30. As a result, DBS-induced turning behavior reflects coordinated activity within motor-related networks functionally connected to M2 rather than solely focal cortical activation.

Overall, these results confirm that the ultralight wireless tDCS and DBS systems can effectively stimulate targeted brain regions in freely behaving mice. Moreover, they demonstrate that stimulation modality–dependent neural circuit activation produces distinguishable behavioral outputs, highlighting the utility of the platform for investigating mechanistic and functional differences between neuromodulation approaches.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed an infrared-controlled, ultralight wireless neuromodulation system optimized for use in freely behaving mice and systematically validated its structural and functional performance. The system enables wireless control of both transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and deep brain stimulation (DBS) by leveraging the wavelength-selective responses of phototransistors to external infrared illumination, eliminating the need for complex RF communication modules or microcontrollers. This approach substantially simplifies the system architecture while minimizing overall weight and volume, enhancing suitability for experiments in freely moving animals.

The tDCS system achieved a total weight of less than 1.5 g, including the battery, whereas the DBS system was fabricated as an ultralight structure weighing under 0.5 g. Additionally, a novel fixation strategy was implemented that avoids the use of bulky crown structures commonly employed in previous studies, thereby reducing the physical burden on the animals. Open-field tests confirmed that neither device implantation nor infrared illumination significantly affected spontaneous locomotor activity.

Functional validation targeting the secondary motor cortex (M2) demonstrated that neural stimulation produced direction-specific turning behaviors that depended on both the stimulation modality and the targeted hemisphere. Right M2 tDCS increased counter-clockwise turning, whereas DBS elicited opposite rotations depending on whether the left or right M2 was stimulated. These results highlight that tDCS and DBS engage distinct neural circuit mechanisms and produce differentiable behavioral outputs even when applied to the same anatomical region, confirming that the proposed system enables precise assessment of stimulation modality–dependent effects under freely behaving conditions.

Overall, this infrared-controlled ultralight wireless neuromodulation platform provides a versatile and reliable tool for delivering targeted neural stimulation in freely moving animal models. It offers broad applicability for investigating neuromodulation mechanisms, performing behavior-based neuroscience experiments, and analyzing brain function under diverse stimulation parameters. This study used only male mice; future work including both sexes will be needed to assess generalizability. Future developments toward multichannel stimulation, long-term repeated paradigms, and closed-loop control may further expand its utility for precise, advanced studies of neural circuit dynamics.
