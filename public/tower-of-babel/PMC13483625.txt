Clinical benefit analysis of no tourniquet use in modified single-plane high tibial osteotomy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zhipeng Xue, Xiaomin Li, Ye Huang, Dafu Zhou, Juejue Wu, Xiangdong Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundModified single-plane osteotomy is a modified technique of medial opening-wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO). This study aimed to investigate the effects of tourniquet use versus non-use during modified single-plane high tibial osteotomy (SPHTO) on perioperative recovery and clinical outcomes in patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on the clinical data of 73 patients with medial compartment knee osteoarthritis (KOA) who underwent unilateral SPHTO at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Beijing University of Chinese Medicine from January 2024 to January 2025. Patients were divided into the observation group (without tourniquet use, n = 35) and the control group (with tourniquet use, n = 38) based on whether a tourniquet was applied intraoperatively. All surgeries were conducted by the same team of surgeons, and all patients received standardized perioperative management. Preoperative baseline data, intraoperative indicators, perioperative recovery parameters, postoperative pain and functional scores, as well as the incidence of complications were compared between the two groups.ResultsThere were no statistically significant differences in preoperative baseline data between the two groups, including age, gender distribution, body mass index (BMI), preoperative hemoglobin (Hb), and knee range of motion (ROM) (all P-value > 0.05). Intraoperatively, the intraoperative blood loss in the control group was significantly less than that in the observation group (P-value < 0.05), while no statistically significant differences were observed in operation time, osteotomy distraction angle, and other indicators between the two groups (all P-value > 0.05). The hidden blood loss was (292.98 ± 45.72) mL in the control group and (206.95 ± 42.36) mL in the observation group, with the hidden blood loss in the control group being significantly higher than that in the observation group. During the perioperative period, there were no statistically significant differences in total blood loss, postoperative Hb decrease value, and length of hospital stay between the two groups (all P-value > 0.05), and no blood transfusion was required in either group. Regarding postoperative pain and functional recovery, the visual analog scale (VAS) scores at 1, 3, and 5 days after surgery in the observation group were significantly lower than those in the control group (all P-value < 0.05); the ROM at 3 and 5 days after surgery was significantly greater in the observation group than in the control group (all P-value < 0.05); the Lysholm knee function scores at 1 and 3 months after surgery were significantly higher in the observation group than in the control group (all P-value < 0.05). In terms of complications, the incidence of complications in the observation group was significantly lower than that in the control group (P-value < 0.05).ConclusionThe use of a tourniquet during SPHTO does not reduce perioperative total blood loss or the risk of complications. However, non-use of a tourniquet can achieve faster pain relief, increase knee range of motion, reduce tourniquet-related complications, and promote accelerated postoperative recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10055-0.Keywords: Modified single-plane high tibial osteotomy, Tourniquet, Perioperative recovery, Knee osteoarthritis, Retrospective cohort study

Introduction

High tibial osteotomy (HTO) is a classic knee-preserving surgical procedure for the treatment of medial compartment knee osteoarthritis (KOA). Its core principle is to reshape the lower limb alignment through osteotomy and orthopedics, transferring the load from the degenerated medial compartment to the relatively normal lateral compartment, thereby relieving pain and improving knee joint function [1, 2]. Medial opening-wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) has become one of the clinically preferred HTO procedures due to its advantages of simple operation, precise orthopedics, and fewer complications [3, 4]. Modified single-plane high tibial osteotomy (SPHTO) is an optimized technique of OWHTO. By adjusting the osteotomy position to the distal 1/3 of the tibial tuberosity, it can minimize the impact on patellar height and the mechanical environment of the patellofemoral joint, further improving surgical safety [5].

Intraoperative tourniquet use is a routine practice in orthopedic lower limb surgeries, with the core purpose of reducing intraoperative bleeding and clarifying the surgical field to shorten the operation time [6, 7]. However, numerous studies in recent years have shown that tourniquet use may cause a series of complications such as ischemia-reperfusion injury, nerve palsy, muscle necrosis, and venous thrombosis. Especially in total knee arthroplasty (TKA), non-use of a tourniquet has been proven to improve the efficiency of patients’ postoperative recovery [8]. With the in-depth promotion of the enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) concept in the field of orthopedics, reducing unnecessary invasive operations during surgery and lowering the risk of complications have become the core goals of optimizing perioperative management. As an important knee-preserving surgical procedure, the refinement of perioperative management of HTO directly affects the patient’s recovery process and long-term prognosis, and whether to use a tourniquet is one of the key decision points in perioperative management [9]. At present, there are a few studies on the application value of tourniquets in traditional OWHTO, but the conclusions are inconsistent. Some studies believe that tourniquets can improve the clarity of the surgical field, while others point out that they will increase the risk of postoperative pain. More importantly, as a modified OWHTO procedure, SPHTO differs from the traditional procedure in terms of osteotomy position and operation process, and its intraoperative bleeding characteristics may be different. However, research on the application value of tourniquets in this procedure has not yet been carried out, and there is still a lack of clear evidence-based medical evidence in clinical practice to guide the tourniquet use strategy for this procedure [10]. Based on this, this study compared the clinical effects of using and not using a tourniquet during SPHTO through a retrospective cohort study design, aiming to provide a reference for optimizing the perioperative management plan of this procedure and practicing the ERAS concept.

Materials and methods

Study population

This was a retrospective cohort study that included 73 patients with medial compartment KOA who underwent unilateral modified single-plane high tibial osteotomy (SPHTO) at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Beijing University of Chinese Medicine from January 2024 to January 2025. Inclusion criteria: ① Imaging confirmed medial unicompartmental KOA with knee varus deformity >5°; ② Knee flexion range of motion >100° and medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) <85°; ③ Intact knee ligament function and good joint stability; ④ Failure of conservative treatment for more than 6 months and voluntary acceptance of surgical treatment. Exclusion criteria: ① History of previous knee surgery; ② Complicated with hematological diseases or coagulation disorders; ③ Complicated with severe underlying diseases such as cardio-cerebrovascular diseases and diabetic complications; ④ Incomplete follow-up data or loss to follow-up. Patients were divided into the observation group (without tourniquet use) and the control group (with tourniquet use) according to whether a tourniquet was used during surgery, including 35 cases in the observation group and 38 cases in the control group. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (Ethics Approval No.: BZYSY-2023KYKTPJ-07). All patients gave informed consent and signed the informed consent form before surgery.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated prior to the study based on the primary outcome of postoperative visual analog scale (VAS) scores. According to preliminary data from our center and published literature, the expected mean VAS score at 1 day after surgery was 4.5 in the non-tourniquet group and 5.5 in the tourniquet group, with a pooled standard deviation of 1.0. Using an independent samples t-test with a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 1-β = 0.80, a minimum of 34 patients per group was required to detect this clinically meaningful difference. Considering a potential dropout rate of approximately 10%, the target sample size was set at 38 patients per group. Ultimately, 35 patients in the observation group and 38 patients in the control group were included, meeting the minimum sample size requirement and ensuring adequate statistical power for the primary outcome analysis.

To minimize selection bias in this retrospective cohort study, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was subsequently performed using age, gender, BMI, and Kellgren-Lawrence grading as covariates, resulting in 32 matched pairs (n = 32 per group). Although the matched sample size was marginally below the original estimate of 34 per group, a post-hoc power analysis based on the observed effect size (mean difference in VAS score at 1 day: 1.44 points, pooled SD: 1.0) confirmed that the study maintained adequate statistical power (1-β = 0.78) at α = 0.05 for the primary outcome. This slight reduction in sample size was an intentional trade-off to enhance internal validity and baseline comparability between groups. All primary outcome analyses were conducted on the PSM-matched cohort, while the full cohort was used for sensitivity analysis.

Surgical methods

All surgeries were performed by the same team of senior orthopedic surgeons under continuous epidural anesthesia. In the control group, an electric pneumatic tourniquet (Model HXY‑D04, Hongsheng Medical Devices Co., Ltd.) was placed at the thigh root. The relative inflation pressure was set to 100 mmHg. The mean tourniquet duration was 84.2 ± 11.5 min in the control group. A standardized tourniquet protocol was applied: if the operation time exceeded 90 min, the tourniquet was deflated for 10 min to restore blood perfusion before re-inflation to avoid prolonged ischemia injury. The tourniquet was applied continuously throughout the operation and released before incision suturing. In the observation group, the tourniquet was only placed without inflation. The surgical procedure was consistent in both groups: first, arthroscopic exploration and debridement were performed to clarify the degree of cartilage and meniscal injury and complete the debridement; then, the osteotomy line was marked at the line connecting the lower 1/3 of the tibial tuberosity and 0.5 cm below the superior tibiofibular joint. After positioning under C-arm fluoroscopy, a oscillating saw was used to perform modified single-plane osteotomy, retaining 1 cm of bone on the opposite side as a hinge. The osteotomy gap was gradually distracted to the preset alignment, filled with allogeneic bone (Jiangsu Shuangyang Instruments Co., Ltd.), and fixed with a double-column locking plate; finally, a drainage tube was placed, and the incision was sutured layer by layer (Fig. 1).

Postoperative management

Both groups received a standardized perioperative management plan: ① Pain management: Intravenous infusion of flurbiprofen axetil injection (100 mg, q12h) after surgery, and oral oxycodone and acetaminophen tablets (1 tablet, prn); ② Thrombus prevention: Intravenous infusion of nadroparin calcium injection (4100 IU, qd) was started 12 h after surgery, continuing for 7 days; ③ Rehabilitation training: Isometric contraction of quadriceps and ankle flexion-extension training were started on the 2nd day after surgery; knee flexion-extension training and ambulation with walker assistance were started on the 3rd day after surgery; transition to partial weight-bearing was made 1 week after surgery, and full weight-bearing was gradually restored 8–10 weeks after surgery according to bone healing status. Neither tranexamic acid nor any other systemic or topical hemostatic drugs were used in either group during the entire operation.

Observation indicators

Preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative follow-up data of patients in both groups were collected: ① Baseline data: Age, gender, height, weight, BMI, surgical side, preoperative Hb, preoperative ROM, preoperative knee joint VAS score [10], preoperative Lysholm score, MPTA; ② Intraoperative indicators: Operation time, intraoperative blood loss, osteotomy distraction angle, number of radiation exposures; ③ Perioperative indicators: Total blood loss (calculated using the Gross equation), postoperative Hb decrease value, hidden blood loss, length of hospital stay, blood transfusion status; ④ Postoperative function and pain: Postoperative knee joint VAS score and ROM at 1, 3, and 5 days after surgery, knee joint VAS score and Lysholm score [11] at 1 and 3 months after surgery; ⑤ Imaging indicators: MPTA, femoral-tibial angle (FTA), and posterior tibial slope angle 3 months after surgery; ⑥ Complications: Tourniquet-related pain, incision exudation, lower limb swelling, deep venous thrombosis, etc.

Statistical analysis

SPSS Statistics 26.0 software was used for data processing. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s). Repeated‑measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for multi‑time point observation data (VAS, ROM, and Lysholm scores). The model included two within‑subject factors (time: preoperative, postoperative 1 day, 3 days, 5 days, 1 month, and 3 months for VAS and ROM; preoperative, 1 month, and 3 months for Lysholm) and one between‑subject factor (group: with vs. without tourniquet). Mauchly’s test of sphericity was performed to assess the sphericity assumption; when the assumption was violated (P-value < 0.05), the Greenhouse‑Geisser correction was applied. The primary focus was on the group × time interaction effect, which examines whether the patterns of change over time differed significantly between the two groups. Significant interaction effects were followed by simple effects analysis with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For data that conformed to normal distribution and homogeneous variance, independent samples t-test was used; non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Count data were expressed as the number of cases (percentage) [n(%)], and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for intergroup comparisons. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Medial compartment KOA is a common osteoarticular disease in middle-aged and elderly populations, and its pathogenesis is closely related to excessive load on the medial compartment caused by abnormal lower limb alignment [11, 12]. As a modified OWHTO procedure, SPHTO reduces the impact on the patellofemoral joint by optimizing the osteotomy position and has shown good safety in clinical application [13]. However, there is still a lack of unified standards regarding the tourniquet use strategy in this procedure. By comparing and analyzing the clinical effects of using and not using a tourniquet during surgery, this study found that non-use of a tourniquet can significantly improve the efficiency of patients’ postoperative recovery without increasing perioperative safety risks.

The core advantage of intraoperative tourniquet use is to reduce intraoperative bleeding and clarify the surgical field. However, this study found that although the intraoperative blood loss in the control group was significantly less than that in the observation group (P-value < 0.05), there was no statistically significant difference in the total perioperative blood loss between the two groups (P-value > 0.05). This result indicates that the lower limb tissues are in an ischemic state during tourniquet inflation, and reperfusion bleeding occurs after release, leading to an increase in hidden blood loss, which ultimately offsets the reduced intraoperative blood loss [14]. In addition, no blood transfusion was required in either group, suggesting that the total perioperative blood loss of SPHTO is generally controllable, and there is no need to rely on tourniquets to reduce the risk of blood transfusion. At the same time, there were no differences in operation time, osteotomy distraction angle and other indicators between the two groups, indicating that non-use of a tourniquet did not affect the surgeon’s operation accuracy and surgical efficiency. This may be related to the surgeons’ rich surgical experience and the relatively simple operation of modified single-plane osteotomy.

The hidden blood loss in the control group was significantly higher than that in the observation group (P-value < 0.001), which is one of the core findings of this study. The mechanism underlying this difference is primarily related to ischemia-reperfusion injury induced by tourniquet use. During tourniquet inflation, the lower limb tissues are in a state of complete ischemia and hypoxia, leading to endothelial cell damage, capillary leakage, and increased vascular permeability. Upon tourniquet release, the sudden restoration of blood flow triggers a massive reperfusion response: oxygen free radicals are generated in large quantities, activating the complement system and promoting the release of inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-8 (IL-8). These inflammatory factors further damage the vascular endothelium, increase capillary permeability, and cause plasma and blood components to extravasate into the interstitial space, resulting in substantial hidden blood loss that is not visible in the surgical field [15]. In contrast, the observation group maintained continuous blood perfusion throughout the operation without experiencing ischemia-reperfusion injury; although intraoperative visible blood loss was higher, the hidden blood loss was significantly reduced, ultimately leading to no significant difference in total perioperative blood loss between the two groups.

Clinically, hidden blood loss is often underestimated and easily overlooked, yet it constitutes a major component of total blood loss in orthopedic surgery. Excessive hidden blood loss can lead to postoperative hemoglobin decline, tissue edema, delayed wound healing, and increased risk of infection. In this study, although there was no statistically significant difference in postoperative hemoglobin decrease between the two groups, the hidden blood loss in the control group was approximately 41.6% higher than that in the observation group, suggesting that tourniquet use shifts blood loss from the “visible” intraoperative phase to the “invisible” postoperative phase without reducing the total blood loss [16]. This finding challenges the traditional belief that tourniquets effectively reduce overall blood loss and provides an important evidence-based basis for reconsidering tourniquet use strategies in SPHTO. Furthermore, the increase in hidden blood loss is closely associated with postoperative pain and swelling: extravasated blood components in the tissue interstitium stimulate nociceptors and increase local tissue tension, thereby exacerbating postoperative pain and limiting early functional exercise. Therefore, reducing hidden blood loss by avoiding tourniquet use not only optimizes blood management but also creates favorable conditions for early pain control and functional recovery, which is of significant clinical guiding value for promoting the ERAS concept in HTO surgery.

Postoperative pain and functional recovery are key indicators for evaluating surgical efficacy, and also core factors affecting patients’ postoperative quality of life and rehabilitation compliance. The results of this study showed that the visual analog scale scores at 1, 3, 5 days and 1, 3 months after surgery in the observation group were significantly lower than those in the control group, and the knee range of motion and long-term Lysholm knee function scores were significantly better than those in the control group from 3 days after surgery. This difference suggests that non-use of a tourniquet has a clear promoting effect on patients’ postoperative pain control and functional recovery [17]. From the perspective of pain mechanism, ischemia-reperfusion injury caused by tourniquet use is the core inducement. During tourniquet inflation compressing the lower limb blood vessels, local tissues are in a state of ischemia and hypoxia, mitochondrial function is impaired, and a large number of oxygen free radicals are released after release, triggering oxidative stress response. At the same time, it activates neutrophil infiltration and releases inflammatory factors such as tumor necrosis factor α and interleukin 6. These inflammatory mediators can stimulate nerve endings and enhance local pain perception [18, 19]. In contrast, non-use of a tourniquet can maintain continuous blood supply to lower limb tissues, avoid the occurrence of ischemia-reperfusion injury, reduce the production of pain mediators from the source, and thus effectively reduce the degree of postoperative pain [20].

The reduction of pain further creates favorable conditions for early functional rehabilitation [21]. Postoperative pain can cause patients to have a braking avoidance response and be unwilling to take the initiative to perform rehabilitation training such as knee flexion and extension and quadriceps contraction. Delayed rehabilitation training will increase the risk of joint adhesion and muscle atrophy, thereby delaying the functional recovery process [22]. Due to milder postoperative pain, the observation group had significantly improved compliance with rehabilitation training, and could complete a wider range of knee flexion and extension movements 3 days after surgery, with significantly increased knee range of motion compared with the control group; with the continuous advancement of rehabilitation training, quadriceps strength and knee joint stability gradually improved, which was ultimately reflected in the significant advantage of Lysholm knee function scores at 1 and 3 months after surgery. In addition, continuous blood supply guarantee is also conducive to the repair of soft tissues in the surgical area, reducing local edema and adhesion, and further helping functional recovery [23]. Studies have also confirmed that non-use of a tourniquet can accelerate patients’ postoperative functional recovery by relieving pain and improving the local microenvironment. For patients with medial compartment knee osteoarthritis, rapid postoperative pain relief and functional recovery can not only improve their medical experience, but also help them return to normal life and work as soon as possible, which has important clinical significance [24].

The occurrence of one case of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in the control group, although the between-group difference did not reach statistical significance, warrants in-depth pathophysiological analysis in conjunction with Virchow’s triad. Virchow’s triad comprises three fundamental elements in thrombogenesis: vascular endothelial injury, blood flow stasis, and hypercoagulability. Tourniquet use during lower limb surgery simultaneously affects all three elements, creating a prothrombotic microenvironment. First, regarding vascular endothelial injury, tourniquet inflation generates direct mechanical compression on the vascular endothelium, causing structural damage and functional impairment; upon release, the sudden reperfusion generates a burst of oxygen free radicals, triggering oxidative stress that further exacerbates endothelial dysfunction and exposes subendothelial collagen, thereby activating the intrinsic coagulation pathway. Second, concerning blood flow stasis, tourniquet inflation completely obstructs venous return, causing blood pooling in the distal limb; even after release, the transient hypoperfusion and post-ischemic vasodilation impair venous hemodynamics, significantly reducing flow velocity and prolonging the residence time of activated coagulation factors and platelets in the local circulation. Third, with respect to hypercoagulability, ischemia-reperfusion injury stimulates the release of tissue factor from damaged endothelial cells and activates the extrinsic coagulation cascade; simultaneously, inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6 promote platelet activation and aggregation, while fibrinolytic activity is suppressed during the ischemic period, tilting the hemostatic balance toward a hypercoagulable state. In this study, the DVT case in the control group occurred on the 5th postoperative day, temporally consistent with the delayed manifestation of tourniquet-induced prothrombotic effects [25]. Although both groups received standardized nadroparin calcium prophylaxis, the additional thrombotic risk conferred by tourniquet use may have overwhelmed pharmacological protection in this particular patient, who potentially had unrecognized individual susceptibility factors.

In terms of complications, the incidence rate in the observation group was significantly lower than that in the control group, including 3 cases of tourniquet-related pain in the control group, which further confirmed that tourniquet use would increase the risk of specific complications. In addition, 1 case of deep venous thrombosis occurred in the control group. Although there was no statistically significant difference between the groups, it suggested that tourniquet use may increase the risk of thrombosis. Blood stasis, hypercoagulable state and endothelial function injury caused by tourniquet are important inducements for thrombosis. In this study, both groups received standardized anticoagulant therapy, which may have reduced the incidence of thrombosis, resulting in no significant difference between the groups. However, the potential impact of tourniquet on thrombosis risk still needs to be vigilant. Non-use of a tourniquet can significantly improve patients’ postoperative pain and functional recovery without increasing perioperative safety risks, indicating that routine use of tourniquet in SPHTO may not be necessary, and its clinical benefits are greater [26]. In terms of rehabilitation, the strategy of non-use of tourniquet during SPHTO proposed in this study can be directly incorporated into the ERAS management pathway of this procedure. By reducing tourniquet-related injuries, shortening the duration of postoperative pain, accelerating the functional recovery process, it can further reduce hospitalization costs and improve patients’ medical experience [27]. At the same time, this strategy does not require additional medical equipment or changes to the core surgical process, and has high clinical feasibility and promotion value.

This study has certain limitations: ① The retrospective study design has selection bias, and the sample size is relatively small; ② The follow-up time is short (3 months), and the long-term efficacy and long-term complications have not been evaluated; ③ Stratified analysis of variables such as tourniquet pressure and use time has not been performed. In the future, large-sample, prospective randomized controlled studies need to be carried out to further verify the long-term safety and effectiveness of non-use of tourniquet in SPHTO, and explore individualized tourniquet use strategies for different patient groups.

In conclusion, the use of a tourniquet during SPHTO can only reduce intraoperative blood loss, but cannot reduce total perioperative blood loss. It will also aggravate postoperative pain, delay functional recovery and increase the risk of complications. However, non-use of a tourniquet can achieve faster pain relief, increase knee range of motion, reduce tourniquet-related complications, and promote accelerated postoperative recovery without affecting surgical safety and orthopedic effect. This protocol is more aligned with the enhanced recovery after surgery concept and can be considered as one of the perioperative management options for SPHTO.
