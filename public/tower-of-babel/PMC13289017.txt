ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInappropriate mechanical ventilation plays a pivotal role in the development of ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) and subsequent postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs), thus individualised lung protective ventilation became a hot topic of research during the past decades. Despite promising results from early trials focusing on separated ventilatory parameters, the incidence of PPCs has not decreased significantly, hence a new conceptual direction emerged in the last few years by evaluating the potential role of mechanical power of ventilation (MP), since MP encompasses all mechanical ventilation parameters that can be responsible for lung injury and recent research confirmed correlation between high MP values and the severity of VILI. Reducing MP by optimising positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) is rational, and electrical impedance tomography (EIT) appears to be a promising tool in achieving this goal, as it has the potential to distinguish alveolar recruitment from overdistension, thus providing a more accurate picture of the lungs thereby being superior to other approaches. Therefore, it is worth testing this hypothesis in a clinical trial.Methods and analysisThis multicentric, single-blinded, interventional, superiority, randomised controlled trial will include 200 patients aged 18 years and older undergoing laparoscopic gynaecological surgeries. The trial will be conducted in three Hungarian centres (two non-university teaching hospitals and one university medical centre). Participants will be allocated in a 1:1 ratio to two groups. Patients in the PEEP-EIT group will receive an EIT-guided PEEP titration, while in the PEEP-dynamic pulmonary compliance (Cdyn) group a dynamic pulmonary compliance-guided PEEP titration will be performed in order to determine the optimal level of PEEP. We aim to evaluate the superiority of using EIT-guided PEEP titration versus the Cdyn-guided approach by comparing their effects on MP, as the primary endpoint, measured immediately after the titration procedure and at the end of surgery. Secondary endpoints are oxygenation, respiratory mechanics parameters, global inhomogeneity index, overdistension/collapse and PPCs. Participants will be followed up until the 28th postoperative day for mortality.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the Hungarian Scientific and Medical Research Council Ethics Committee (Registration number: NNGYK/21074-6/2025) on 17 June 2025. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants (or their legally authorised representatives) prior to enrolment in the study. Introduction

Background and rationale

Despite both surgical and anaesthesia-related risk factors of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) are well known, PPCs occur frequently with an incidence of 5% to 33%, resulting in a 30-day mortality of about 20% in the affected patient population.1,3

Mechanical ventilation with inappropriate ventilatory parameters plays a pivotal role in the development of ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) and subsequent PPCs accounting for substantial morbidity and mortality, thus individualised lung protective ventilation (LPV) became a hot topic of research during the past decades.4

Despite promising results from early trials aiming the benefits of several components of LPV in order to reduce the risk of VILI, the incidence of PPCs has not decreased significantly, suggesting that focusing on separated ventilatory parameters may not lead to favourable clinical outcomes.5,11

Applying low tidal volumes (TV, 4–6 mL/kg ideal body weight (IBW)) is a useful tool to prevent volutrauma but may result in increased dead space, atelectasis and hypercapnia. Optimal positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEPopt) may prevent atelectasis and cyclic lung collapse; however, higher PEEP levels may lead to barotrauma, ventilation-perfusion mismatch and haemodynamic compromise.12 13 Driving pressure (dP<15 cmH2O) seems to be an important safety parameter to determine appropriate PEEP and TV, and may decrease the incidence of PPCs as compared with conventional LPV.14 Bedside visualisation of the lungs by ultrasound (US) requires appropriate practice and experience for proper assessment, while it only provides intermittent evaluation, and performing US imaging during surgery may be cumbersome. Electrical impedance tomography (EIT) provides a non-invasive, radiation-free, real-time, continuous monitoring that identifies changes in lung impedance which corresponds to lung volume and gives the opportunity to optimise PEEP by visualising the distribution of ventilation and to distinguish alveolar recruitment from overdistension.15,19 Furthermore, EIT-guided LPV may more effectively counterbalance the harmful effects of both mechanical ventilation and the laparoscopic procedure.20

Alveolar recruitment manoeuvres (ARMs) may improve oxygenation and reverse atelectasis by opening collapsed alveoli but they can cause severe haemodynamic instability especially in hypovolaemic patients.13

Focusing on the amount of energy transferred to the lung parenchyma per unit of time (J/min) during mechanical ventilation became a new direction of research in the past few years.21 22 This energy is called mechanical power of ventilation (MP) and recent trials found a correlation between high MP values (> 12 J/min) and the severity of VILI both in critically ill patients with ARDS and patients with non-injured lungs as well.23,27 MP integrates the major components of positive pressure ventilation that can easily be monitored during surgery (RR, TV and dP), hence calculating MP gives the opportunity to balance the effects of each respiratory parameter.28 Therefore, theoretically, reducing MP below the safety threshold may become the main goal of the LPV strategy. However, in spite of the robust pathophysiological rationale of implementing MP as a goal of LPV, validation of its use in the surgical population is still lacking.29

Despite dynamic pulmonary compliance (Cdyn) guided PEEP titration being extensively evaluated and proven effective in terms of reducing MP,30 31 theoretically, applying EIT in order to optimise PEEP has the potential to achieve a more accurate picture of the lungs, which may further reduce MP and contribute to a more protective mechanical ventilation resulting in improved patient outcomes, thereby being superior to other approaches. Therefore, it is worth testing this hypothesis in a clinical trial, and we aim to validate and evaluate the superiority of the use of the EIT-guided PEEP titration method versus the Cdyn directed approach in patients with non-injured lungs undergoing laparoscopic gynaecological surgery.

These anticipated advantages may improve our knowledge about individualised LPV, enhance postoperative recovery, shorten in-hospital stay and reduce healthcare-related costs in general.

The primary outcome of this study is the effect of two different PEEP titrating methods on MP, while secondary outcomes include oxygenation, respiratory mechanics parameters and PPCs in the investigated patient population.

Methods and analysis

Trial design

The ElecTrical Impedance TomogRaphy vs dynamic compliANce guided positive end-expiratory pressure Titration trial is designed as a multicentric, two-arm parallel-group, single-blinded (subject), interventional, superiority, randomised trial. Patients will be allocated to (1) EIT-guided PEEP titration and (2) Cdyn-guided PEEP titration interventional groups.

Eligibility criteria for participants

Inclusion criteria

All female patients aged ≥18 years who are scheduled for laparoscopic gynaecological surgery (hysterectomy, salpingectomy, adnexectomy, myomectomy) and who have provided written informed consent to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria are age below 18 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status IV, history of severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease grades III or IV), uncontrolled bronchial asthma, history of severe restrictive pulmonary disease, pulmonary metastases, history of any thoracic surgery, need for thoracic drainage before surgery, congestive heart failure (New York Heart Association Functional Classification grades III or IV), extreme obesity (body mass index, BMI > 35 kg/m2) and lack of patient’s consent.

Withdrawal criteria

The withdrawal criteria will be the following: (1) patients who could not comply with the protocol requirements and (2) patients who do not wish to continue in the trial.

Intervention and comparator

Preoperative assessment and admission

A total number of 200 patients scheduled for laparoscopic gynaecological surgery (hysterectomy, salpingectomy, adnexectomy, myomectomy) will be enrolled in this study. An equal number of patients will be randomised into the PEEP-EIT and PEEP-Cdyn groups.

During standard institutional preoperative assessment patient’s eligibility for laparoscopic gynaecological surgery will be evaluated. Medical history, laboratory and chest X-ray or CT scan, 12-lead ECG, ASA physical status, BMI, postoperative Respiratory Failure Risk Index assessment (Assess Respiratory Risk in Surgical Patients in Catalonia), nutritional risk screening (2002 tool) and if required (in case of history of smoking or coronary artery disease) results of spirometry, echocardiography and ergometry will be recorded. Participants fulfilling the inclusion criteria will be asked for their signed informed consent. Patient will be randomised into one of the study groups before induction of anaesthesia.

Standard intraoperative care

Before induction of anaesthesia an arterial cannula will be inserted for invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring and blood gas sampling while an EIT belt (Dräger PulmoVista 500) will be placed between the fourth and sixth intercostal space according to the manufacturer’s recommendation.

Prior to induction of anaesthesia, pre-oxygenation will be performed according to the institutional standards: 50% oxygen will be administered via a tight-fitting face mask for at least 3 to 5 min or until peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) ≥96%.

Immediately after induction of anaesthesia and orotracheal intubation, once a steady state has been reached (table 1), all patients will be submitted to an ARM using the sustained airway pressure by the continuos positive airway pressure method, applying 30 cmH2O PEEP for 30 s. After ARM, PEEP will be set to 6 cmH2O in both groups and LPV (TV=6 mL/kg IBW, fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2)=0.4) will be performed.

After pneumoperitoneum (established by using carbon dioxide; maximal intra-abdominal pressure=12 cmH2O, monitored continuously by the insufflator) and Trendelenburg position (35°) optimal PEEP (PEEP-EIT) will be determined during a decremental PEEP titration procedure.

During surgery ARM will be repeated and arterial blood gas samples (ABGs) will be evaluated every 30 min. In case of decreasing SpO2 (<92%) a rescue ARM will be performed using FiO2 of 1.0. In the event of increased end-tidal carbon dioxide tension (EtCO₂) or arterial hypercapnia, ventilation will be adjusted primarily by increasing respiratory rate (RR) while maintaining LPV principles. TV and PEEP will be kept constant unless clinically indicated.

To maintain anaesthesia, in both groups a target-controlled infusion regimen (propofol–remifentanil) will be performed during surgery. Depth of anaesthesia will also be monitored. Neuromuscular blockade will be achieved using rocuronium administered as an intravenous bolus for induction (0.6 mg/kg), followed by additional boluses to maintain deep block as indicated by quantitative neuromuscular monitoring (train-of-four relaxometry, TOF) to ensure the absence of spontaneous breathing activity during the PEEP titration procedure and subsequent respiratory measurements. Deep neuromuscular blockade will be defined as TOF count 0.

Arterial blood pressure, heart rate (HR) and EtCO2 will be monitored continuously. Cdyn, core temperature and TOF data will be recorded every 15 min.

If hypotension develops vasopressor treatment (ephedrine, phenylephrine, norepinephrine) will be started to maintain mean arterial pressure above 65 mm Hg. For intraoperative fluid management patients will receive a 2 mL/kg/h of balanced crystalloid solution until the end of surgery. In cases of bleeding crystalloid substitution will be given. Packed red blood cell transfusion will be given whenever the attending anaesthetist renders it necessary.

PEEP-EIT group

Patients in the PEEP-EIT group will undergo a decremental PEEP titration procedure directed by EIT PEEP Trial of the PulmoVista 500 device. During the PEEP titration procedure, PEEP will be decreased from 18 cmH2O by 2 cmH2O every 20 ventilatory cycles until a final PEEP of 6 cmH2O. On each level of PEEP Cdyn values will also be recorded. Optimal PEEP is considered as the intersect between the lower percentage of overdistension and collapse, based on the diagnostic tool of the EIT device. After PEEP titration procedure, a lung protective mechanical ventilation will be performed using PEEPopt (PEEP-EIT) and low TV. ARMs will be performed every 30 min. MP, dP, Cdyn, mid-dorsal and dorsal regions of interest (TVROI3, TVROI4) and end-expiratory lung volume (EELV) will be recorded immediately after the PEEP titration trial and every 15 min during surgery. Oxygenation and dead space fraction indicated by PaO2/FiO2 and (a-Et)PCO2, respectively, will be recorded every 30 min during surgery.

GI and overdistension/collapse ratio (ODCL) will be evaluated retrospectively after surgery using the diagnostic software for the EIT device used.

PEEP-Cdyn group

Patients in the PEEP-Cdyn group will receive a decremental PEEP titration directed by Cdyn values. During the PEEP titration procedure, PEEP will be decreased from 18 cmH2O by 2 cmH2O every 20 ventilatory cycles, until a final PEEP of 6 cmH2O. On each level of PEEP, EIT measurements will also be recorded. Optimal PEEP is considered as the PEEP value resulting in the highest possible Cdyn measured by the ventilator. After PEEP titration procedure, a lung protective mechanical ventilation will be performed using optimal PEEP (PEEP-Cdyn) and low TV. ARMs will be performed every 30 min. The same parameters at the same timepoints will be recorded during and after surgery as in the PEEP-EIT group. The detailed intervention and assessment protocol is provided in figure 3.

Postoperative care

After extubation, patients will be admitted to the Post-Anaesthesia Care Unit. ABGs will be collected for PaO2/FiO2 measurements 30 min, 6 and 12 hours after surgery. The arterial cannula is then removed and oxygenation will be assessed non-invasively with SpO2/FiO2 at 24 and 48 hours after surgery. At the same timepoints, EIT measurements including TVROI3, TVROI4 and EELV will also be performed and recorded. On the first postoperative day (POD) (24 hours after surgery), a chest X-ray will be performed and repeated on the following days if developing pulmonary complications are suspected. Chest X-ray will be evaluated by an independent trained radiologist who will not be involved in the study. Postoperative analgesia will be based on institutional protocol. Patients’ clinical progress will be monitored by daily Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores, laboratory and physical examinations.

Follow-up (POD 3–28)

During follow-up period, secondary endpoints, in-hospital stay, 28-days and in-hospital mortality will also be evaluated.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated intervention or comparator

The session will be stopped immediately in case of: (1) severe or prolonged hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg or MAP <65 mm Hg persisting for more than 5 min despite appropriate clinical management, or requiring escalation of vasoactive support); (2) significant cardiac arrhythmias; (3) severe barotrauma leading to pneumothorax; (4) any unanticipated, severe EIT device-associated adverse effect (eg, burns, electrocution); (5) excess of all-cause serious adverse events (SAEs) and (6) or any other event judged clinically significant and related to the PEEP titration procedure. The event will be documented and reported according to the Harms procedures.

In case of respiratory deterioration, ventilation will be adjusted according to standard clinical practice, and continuation of the study protocol will be reassessed. Respiratory deterioration will be defined as any of the following: (1) oxygenation impairment: (SpO² <92% despite optimisation of ventilator settings, rescue ARM and increasing FiO2); (2) hypercapnia: EtCO² > 50–55 mm Hg or clinically significant respiratory acidosis and (3) elevated Pplat (>30 cmH²O) or peak airway pressure exceeding safety limits.

Harms

Participants will receive daily visits from intensive care therapist and gynaecologist in charge from POD 1 until leaving the hospital. The study nurse will be responsible for collecting blood samples and recording relevant required data onto electronic Case Report Forms (eCRFs). During the out-of-hospital follow-up period (until POD 28), patients’ progress, respectively deterioration, will be checked by daily phone call visits.

Investigators will monitor the patients for any AEs, which are defined as severe or prolonged hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg or MAP <65 mm Hg persisting for more than 5 min despite appropriate clinical management or requiring escalation of vasoactive support) and significant cardiac arrhythmias associated with the PEEP titration procedure. AEs will be documented on the eCRF and the principal investigator will be informed.

SAEs are defined as severe barotrauma leading to pneumothorax, significant prolongation of hospitalisation, persistent or significant disability or incapacity, and severe deterioration (life-threatening state or even death) associated with the PEEP titration procedure. All treatment-related SAEs will be recorded and reported to the Hungarian Scientific and Medical Research Council Ethics Committee and the local ethics committees. If any SAEs occur, the trial will be interrupted and an investigation will be performed.

Sample size

The sample size is estimated for the secondary endpoint, the Cdyn. In a previous study conducted by Shu et al, a difference of 4.5% was found regarding the Cdyn (66.3±10.66 mL/cmH2O vs 61.8±11.03 mL/cmH2O) between the PEEP-Cdyn and PEEP-EIT groups.32 A sample size of 200 patients (100 patients in each arm) will be needed to observe this difference, assuming α=0.05 and power of 80% and considering a data loss of 10%.

The sample size calculation was based on Cdyn rather than the primary outcome, MP, because robust prior data for the primary outcome were lacking at the time of trial design, particularly regarding variance and clinically meaningful differences. Furthermore, MP is a derived composite variable influenced by several ventilatory parameters, whereas Cdyn is a well-established and well-tested physiologic measure with more available prior data and clearer interpretability. Therefore, Cdyn was selected as the most reliable and feasible parameter for sample size estimation, while MP remained the prespecified primary outcome of interest as a key exploratory endpoint.

Recruitment

During standard institutional preoperative assessment, pre-screened patients who meet the inclusion criteria will be fully informed about the study, both verbally and written, using a brief and plain language patient information leaflet, and will be invited to consider participation. They will be asked to review the study information leaflet at home carefully. Potential participants will be assured that any questions they may have will be answered. Participants will also be assured that withdrawal of consent may be initiated by them at any time during the trial without consequences. The day before the surgery, the attending anaesthesiologist will invite the patient to discuss any remaining questions and sign an informed consent form.

Randomisation and blinding

The randomisation sequence will be conducted by a blocked randomisation list generator tool (Sealed Envelope, London, UK) by an independent researcher.

Participants will be randomised to two interventional groups in a ratio of 1:1. Randomisation will be carried out by a computer-generated blocked randomisation list with 20 blocks of 10 patients per block. Participants will be included and allocated in numerical order.33

The allocation sequence will be stored in sealed opaque and numbered envelopes, which are accessible only to the designated research staff members. Once a patient is enrolled, the clinical investigator (attending anaesthesiologist) will receive the randomisation number, allocation and intervention information.

Participants and gynaecologists will remain blinded, while the attending anaesthesiologist and study nurse are unblinded due to the nature of the interventions and the need for data collection and administration.

Patient data, intraoperative and postoperative measurements, respiratory parameters, laboratory results and clinical status (SOFA Score) will be collected onto eCRF. Neither eCRF nor patient evaluation charts will be assessed in front of the patient.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up

During recruitment, participants receive comprehensive, all-encompassing information about study procedures, interventions and requirements, while the importance of follow-up will be emphasised.

Statistical methods

Data will be analysed by the research team in collaboration with a medically versed biostatistician after completion of the trial. There will be no interim analysis. Statistical analysis will be conducted on an intention-to-treat basis.

It is expected that the majority of source data will be recorded onto ECRFs; nonetheless, before starting the data analysis, the missing-data mechanism and pattern will be evaluated and these findings will be used to determine whether they have impact on statistical analysis and results and how they can be managed.

Data distribution will be tested by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov analysis. Normally distributed data will be presented as mean and SD and skewed data as median (IQR). Comparing related samples, the paired and unpaired Student’s t-test will be used for normally distributed data and the Wilcoxon signed rank test and Mann-Whitney U-test for skewed data. Differences in proportions will be evaluated using the Fisher’s exact test and risk ratio with associated 95% CI.

Two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance will be used to compare the groups’ MP levels. The relationship between MP levels and PPCs will be evaluated using the Pearson correlation. Statistical analysis of SOFA scores, in-hospital stay, readmission rate, in-hospital and 28-day mortality data of groups will be implemented by the χ2 test. A p value of less than 0.05 will be considered significant.

The study was approved by the Hungarian Scientific and Medical Research Council Ethics Committee (chairperson: Dr Nóra Hosszúfalusi, registration number: NNGYK/21074-6/2025) on 17 June 2025. Data collection and management

Study staff members will receive role-specific training prior to participant enrolment and will be certified by the primary investigator. Training will be recorded and documented in assigning logs. Protocol adherence will be monitored through periodic audits and team discussions.

Patient data are collected onto eCRF. Data are protected in a secured institutional server. Informed consent forms, paper copies of source data (laboratory and radiology results, patient evaluation charts and anaesthesia protocols) and EIT recordings will be stored securely in folders. AEs and SAEs will be recorded into eCRFs. At the end of the study, all data, including patient information and logs will be archived for at least 25 years to ensure long-term accessibility and compliance.

Confidentiality

Participants will be assigned to a unique study identifier. All participant data will be de-identified and stored in an anonymised form.

The coordinating site centre team at Flór Ferenc Hospital Kistarcsa consists of the coordinating principal investigator, subinvestigators and study nurses. These members meet and discuss the progress of the trial weekly. The investigator teams of the other sites consist of the local primary investigators, subinvestigators and study nurses. The teams of the three investigation sites hold monthly online meetings to discuss the progress and share relevant information about the trial.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure

There will not be a Data Monitoring Committee in this trial. Data monitoring will be performed centrally for quality control purposes by an external, independent doctor (Research Supervisor), who will not be involved in the study. Monitoring will evaluate the progress of the study and verify the accuracy and completeness of data recording (eCRF, source data, informed consent forms and outcome variables).

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct

The clinical trial is monitored by annual reviews by the aforementioned research supervisor.

Protocol amendments

All significant protocol changes will be communicated to the Hungarian Scientific and Medical Research Council of the National Centre for Public Health and Pharmacy, research supervisor and IRBs, while clinical trial registry information will be updated. Changes that affect participants significantly will be communicated immediately.

Dissemination policy

All results—including both positive and negative—will be published in open-access, peer-reviewed journals.

Discussion

This investigator-initiated, multicentric, single-blinded, interventional, superiority, randomised controlled trial will assess the validity and possible superiority of an EIT-guided over a Cdyn-directed MP-optimisation strategy during laparoscopic gynaecological surgery in order to reduce PPCs.

Mechanical ventilation as a major risk factor plays a pivotal role in the development of PPCs; however, it is mandatory during laparoscopic surgeries under general anaesthesia. Mechanical ventilation alone alters the excursion of the diaphragm resulting in ventral redistribution of ventilation; additionally, the increased intra-abdominal pressure due to the pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg position during laparoscopy leads to a cephalad shift of the diaphragm decreasing the functional residual capacity and elevating the PTP. Moreover, evidence exists that inappropriate ventilatory parameters may lead to physical (volutrauma, barotrauma and atelectrauma) and biological damage of the lungs (summarised as VILI) causing PPCs, accounting for substantial morbidity and mortality.4 Recent research has suggested the importance of individual LPV applying low TV, PEEPopt, ARMs and limited plateau (Pplat <25 cmH2O), dP (< 15 cmH2O) and transpulmonary (PTP<20 cmH2O) pressures in order to reduce the risk of VILI, even in patients with healthy lungs. However, the results remain controversial, and the incidence of PPCs has not decreased significantly.5,11

Respiratory system is complex, its reactions and tolerance to the effects of positive pressure mechanical ventilation are extremely individual, therefore, focusing on separated ventilatory parameters may not lead to favourable clinical improvement and outcomes.

Low TV (4–6 mL/kg IBW) is a useful tool to prevent volutrauma but may result in increased dead space, atelectasis and hypercapnia. In this scenario, increasing RR can reduce PaCO2, but too short expiratory time may increase intrinsic PEEP resulting in air trapping, and hyperinflation of the lungs may occur. However, optimising TV or RR is not particularly difficult by measuring dP or EtCO2, respectively, and these parameters are regularly appropriate for routine use in patients with non-injured lungs. In case of pulmonary pathologies or morbid obesity, estimating the end-inspiratory PTP by measuring oesophageal pressure, thus determining individually appropriate TV can be useful, however it is too complicated and expensive for everyday use.

Applying PEEPopt may prevent atelectasis and cyclic collapse of the alveoli. On the other hand, high PEEP may lead to barotrauma, ventilation-perfusion mismatch and haemodynamic compromise can develop due to a high intrathoracic pressure.12 13 PEEP can be titrated applying respiratory mechanics parameters (Cstat / Cdyn; dead space fraction; end-expiratory PTP; dP) or bedside visualisation of the lungs using US or EIT can be another option. Targeting the highest possible Cstat or Cdyn is simple and may be useful, however the main concerns about the use of these basic respiratory mechanics parameters are that neither overdistension of the lungs nor distribution of ventilation can be measured or even estimated. However, dP—calculated as the difference between Pplat and PEEP (dP=Pplat PEEP)—seems to be an important safety parameter to determine appropriate PEEP and TV, and may contribute to reducing the incidence of PPCs.14

Accurate visualisation of the lungs using US requires proper practice and experience, moreover it only provides intermittent evaluation, while it may be cumbersome in surgical settings.

EIT provides a non-invasive, radiation-free, real-time, continuous monitoring, that identifies changes in lung impedance which corresponds to lung volume. Besides these advantages, EIT imaging gives the opportunity to optimise PEEP by visualising the distribution of ventilation, and to distinguish alveolar recruitment from overdistension. The global inhomogeneity index (GI) quantifies the homogeneity of the TV distribution. It correlates with lung recruitability and may guide ventilatory settings in order to optimise alveolar homogeneity.15 16 During an EIT-guided titration procedure, regional overdistension (OD) and alveolar collapse (CL) can be quantified (ODCL), and PEEPopt is considered as the intersection between the lower percentage of overdistension and collapse.17,19 Therefore, optimising PEEP using EIT to counterbalance the effects of laparoscopic procedures has some rational as indicated in a previous trial.20

In conclusion, there are several methods to determine PEEPopt, each with its own advantages and disadvantages, however there is no recommendation on superiority and best practice.

Recognising these issues, a new conceptual direction emerged in the last few years by evaluating the potential role of the amount of energy transferred to the lung parenchyma per unit of time (J/min) during mechanical ventilation.21 22 This energy is called MP of ventilation, and recent trials found correlation between high MP values (MP >12 J/min) and the severity of VILI both in critically ill patients with ARDS and patients with non-injured lungs as well.23,27 MP integrates the major components of positive pressure ventilation that drive VILI (static, dynamic and resistive, related to PEEP, dP, flow and airway resistance respectively; and RR), thus gives the opportunity to balance the effects of each respiratory parameter. Theoretically, reducing MP below the safety threshold may be the main goal of the LPV strategy. Due to the original equation being very sophisticated and difficult to calculate, a surrogate formula was established including RR, TV, peak inspiratory pressure (Ppeak), PEEP and Pplat: MP=0.098 × RR × TV (L) × [Ppeak – ½ × (Pplat – PEEP)]. These parameters can be acquired from the ventilators of all anaesthesia machines, thus MP can be easily calculated during surgery.28 It is important to notice, that TV is a predominant parameter in the formula, while PEEP is a double-edged sword. Inappropriate PEEP may increase MP, whereas PEEPopt can decrease it by improving pulmonary compliance. Neglecting the role of RR is common in terms of the genesis of VILI, however, it is obvious that it has a linear correlation with MP, that should be taken into account. Despite the robust pathophysiological rationale of implementing MP as a goal of LPV, some limits must be emphasised. First, validation of its use in the surgical population is still lacking. Second, due to the complexity of the interaction between the respiratory parameters and inhomogeneities in the distribution of ventilation (atelectasis and hyperinflation may be present at the same time in different lung areas), local lung tissue injury may still develop.29

Although Cdyn-directed LPV has several proven advantages, EIT-guided LPV may more effectively counterbalance the harmful effects of both mechanical ventilation and the laparoscopic procedure. Theoretically, applying EIT in order to optimise PEEP has the potential to achieve a more accurate picture of the lungs, which may further reduce MP and contribute to a more protective mechanical ventilation resulting in improved patient outcomes, thereby being superior to other approaches. Therefore, it is worth testing this hypothesis in a clinical trial, and we aim to validate and evaluate the superiority of the use of the EIT-guided PEEP titration method by comparing its effects on MP, oxygenation, respiratory mechanics parameters, GI, ODCL and PPCs to the Cdyn-guided approach in patients with non-injured lungs undergoing laparoscopic gynaecological surgery.

This study has some limitations. First, it is not powered for either the MP as the primary outcome or a patient-centred outcome such as the PPCs, which may limit the ability to detect differences in robust endpoints. However, the comparator strategy based on Cdyn reflects current clinical practice, which may increase the clinical relevance of the findings. Second, MP, although physiologically meaningful, is a surrogate outcome and it is a derived composite measure including TV, airway pressures, RR, flow and resistance-related components, thus its complexity may carry the potential for pitfalls and difficulties, while its direct relationship with patient-centred outcomes may be limited. Third, the study population is restricted to patients undergoing laparoscopic gynaecological surgery, which may limit generalisability to other surgical populations or even males. On the other hand, the fact that this study exclusively includes a female population raises another important aspect. Despite sex-related differences in respiratory physiology having been described, gender-associated differences in pulmonary protective ventilation remained under-represented in the literature, moreover the vast majority of trials are conducted on male patients. Furthermore, there is increasing evidence that during intraoperative ventilation females receive higher TV (often >8 mL/kg) compared with males, and thus less often receive low TV ventilation. Recent trials suggested that the gender inequity in ventilator management is due to females’ shorter height and greater error when body height is visually estimated.34,38 Our study provides data in a more homogeneous female cohort which may lead to some limitations discussed above, thus should be considered when interpreting the results. However, our results may draw attention to pay more and special care to women during mechanical ventilation. Fourth, the requirement for an EIT device and the expertise needed to use and evaluate it may limit the broader applicability of such an intervention in routine clinical practice. Finally, the single-blinded design may introduce performance bias, as treating clinicians cannot be blinded to the intervention.

The potential implications of our results can further improve our knowledge about individualised protective ventilation, enhance postoperative recovery, shorten in-hospital stay and reduce healthcare-related costs, and in the case of positive results, these anticipated advantages may presumably apply to all patients undergoing various types of laparoscopic surgery.
