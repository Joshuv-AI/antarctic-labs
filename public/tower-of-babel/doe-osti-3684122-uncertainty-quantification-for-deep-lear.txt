Abstract
Deep neural networks predict protein-ligand binding affinity accurately enough to guide virtual screening,
but a bare point prediction gives no basis for deciding which predictions to act on. This report documents
a comparative evaluation of six uncertainty-quantification approaches applied to a three-dimensional convo-
lutional neural network (3D-CNN) for binding-affinity prediction: a deep ensemble, a bootstrap ensemble,
Monte Carlo dropout, a convolution-fed Gaussian process, residual estimation with an input/output kernel
(RIO), and an in-series residual network. All six were compared against a deterministic 3D-CNN baseline
on PDBbind 2016, using 3390 refined-set complexes for training, 377 for validation and the 290-complex
core set for testing, with a 19 × 48 × 48 × 48 voxelised atomic representation. Methods were assessed for
point-prediction accuracy, calibration, sharpness, dispersion, and prediction-interval coverage and width.
 No configuration produced uncertainty estimates that were simultaneously well calibrated, sharp and dis-
persed, and every configuration was overconfident, covering between 15 and 53 percentage points below
the level implied by its own interval construction. The cause is structural: every method reports the spread
of point predictions from models trained under a squared-error objective, so the estimated uncertainty is
epistemic only and omits measurement noise in the affinity labels entirely. Calibration and sharpness traded
off strongly against each other across method families, with a rank correlation of −0.79 between sharpness
and miscalibration area, so that every apparent calibration gain was obtained by widening the predictive dis-
tribution; dispersion, by contrast, was uncorrelated with calibration and therefore constitutes an independent
axis rather than part of the same trade-off. The ensemble methods gave the best point accuracy, reducing
root-mean-square error from 1.72 to 1.53 pK units, although the accuracy improvement was not universal:
the in-series network left predictions unchanged by construction, the Gaussian process at one kernel setting
degraded accuracy, and Monte Carlo dropout reduced error magnitudes while reducing correlation. Both
examined hyperparameters, the dropout rate and the Matérn smoothness parameter, changed the results
substantially.
 Taken together, the results support a narrow recommendation. Where the computational budget permits
thirty training runs, an ensemble is the reasonable default, since it gave the best point accuracy and varied
its uncertainty enough to differentiate complexes, although its estimates should be recalibrated on held-out
data before deployment. The frozen-network methods are far cheaper but come with a caveat: RIO, the
best of them on point accuracy, reported an almost constant uncertainty with a coefficient of variation of
0.017, which makes it close to useless as a ranking signal for uncertainty-based acquisition. Three limita-
tions bound these conclusions: each configuration was run once, with no repetitions or statistical tests; the
relationship between predicted uncertainty and realised error was not measured, so the study characterises
average properties of the intervals rather than the per-complex behaviour that matters in practice; and the
conventional PDBbind protocol places closely related proteins and ligands on both sides of the train and test
split. The last of these makes the overconfidence result conservative, since a test set resembling the training
set is close to the easiest case for uncertainty estimation. Future work will re-evaluate under a leakage-aware
partition, add a rank correlation between predicted uncertainty and absolute error to the metric suite, and
evaluate methods that model the aleatoric component explicitly.

Keywords: uncertainty quantification, deep learning, calibration, protein-ligand binding affinity, 3D convo-
lutional neural networks, PDBbind, Gaussian processes, deep ensembles, Monte Carlo dropout.

Executive summary
Deep learning models that predict how tightly a small molecule binds to a protein target are now accurate
enough to guide drug-discovery campaigns, but they report a single number for every complex and give no
indication of when that number should be trusted. Uncertainty quantification supplies the missing signal by
attaching a predictive distribution to each prediction. We evaluated six ways of adding such a signal to a
three-dimensional convolutional neural network (3D-CNN) for binding-affinity prediction, and this report
documents that evaluation.
 The methods evaluated were a deep ensemble of thirty independently initialised networks; a bootstrap
ensemble of thirty networks trained on resampled data; Monte Carlo dropout, which leaves dropout active at
prediction time and averages repeated stochastic passes; a convolution-fed Gaussian process (CFGP) fitted
on the internal representation of a frozen network; RIO, a Gaussian process fitted to the frozen network’s
prediction errors; and an in-series network trained to predict those errors directly. All six were compared
against a single deterministic 3D-CNN on the PDBbind 2016 benchmark, training on 3390 complexes,
validating on 377 and testing on 290.
 Each method was judged on five properties. Accuracy asks how close the predictions are to measured
affinities. Calibration asks whether a stated confidence interval contains the true value as often as it claims
to. Sharpness asks how narrow the intervals are, since an interval wide enough to contain everything is
useless. Dispersion asks whether the method varies its uncertainty from one complex to another, since a
method that reports the same uncertainty everywhere cannot flag unfamiliar molecules. Coverage and width
summarise the intervals directly.
 Four findings emerged.

1. No method was good on all counts. Every configuration failed on at least one of calibration, sharpness
 and dispersion, and the failures were of different kinds. The ensembles were the most balanced overall.
2. Every method was overconfident, by a wide margin. All of them reported intervals narrower than the
 observed errors warranted, and their intervals contained the true value between 15 and 53 percentage
 points less often than their own construction implied they should. The reason is structural: none of the
 methods modelled the experimental noise present in measured binding affinities. They measured only
 how much different versions of the model disagreed with one another, which is a different and smaller
 quantity.
3. Calibration and sharpness trade off against each other. Across all methods, better calibration came
 with wider intervals. Every apparent calibration success in the study was bought by widening the pre-
 dictive distribution rather than by estimating uncertainty better. Dispersion, in contrast, moved indepen-
 dently of calibration, so the two can be improved separately.
4. Ensembling improved accuracy, but not everything did. Averaging thirty networks reduced root-
 mean-square error from 1.72 to 1.53 affinity units. Three configurations did not improve accuracy: the
 in-series network cannot change the prediction by construction, CFGP with one kernel setting made
 accuracy substantially worse, and Monte Carlo dropout reduced error magnitudes while weakening the
 correlation with measured values.

 Three limitations bound what these results can support. First, each configuration was run once, with no
repetitions or statistical tests, so small differences between methods are not established. Second, the study
never measured whether a method’s uncertainty is larger on the complexes it actually predicts badly, which
is the property that matters most in practice; it measured only average properties of the intervals. Third, and
most important for interpreting the accuracy figures, the standard PDBbind protocol used here places closely
related proteins and molecules on both sides of the train and test split, so the results describe performance

 5

on chemistry the model has largely seen before rather than generalisation to genuinely new targets. Later
work has built partitions specifically to avoid this, and re-running the comparison under one of them is the
first item of recommended future work.
 The practical recommendation from the study is narrow. If the computational budget allows thirty training
runs, use an ensemble, and recalibrate its uncertainties on held-out data before deployment. If it does not,
the cheap alternatives are available but come with a caveat: the cheapest well-performing option, RIO,
reported an almost identical uncertainty for every complex, which makes it close to useless for deciding
which predictions to trust.
 One caveat attaches to the coverage figures specifically. The prediction intervals were built as the smallest-
to-largest range of a set of repeated predictions, and the number of repetitions differed between methods, so
those figures are comparable within groups of methods rather than across all six. This does not change any
conclusion above, but it should be repaired before the coverage numbers are reused.

1 Introduction
Deep neural networks trained on three-dimensional protein-ligand complexes now produce binding-affinity
predictions that are competitive with, and substantially cheaper than, physics-based scoring functions [1–
3]. What these models do not produce is any indication of when a prediction should be trusted. A three-
dimensional convolutional neural network (3D-CNN) trained on the PDBbind refined set returns a single
number for every complex it is shown, whether that complex resembles its training data or not, and the
number carries no measure of confidence. In a virtual-screening or active-learning pipeline, where the
model output determines which compounds are advanced to expensive downstream evaluation, this omission
is consequential: a screening campaign that cannot distinguish confident predictions from guesses cannot
allocate its experimental budget rationally, and an active-learning loop has no principled query strategy
without an uncertainty signal.
 Uncertainty quantification (UQ) supplies that missing signal by pairing each point prediction with a
description of the predictive distribution around it. The available methodology ranges from full Bayesian
treatment of network weights [4, 5] through sampling-based posterior approximation [6] to inexpensive
approximations that reuse an already-trained network [7, 8]. These methods differ by orders of magnitude
in computational cost, in how much they perturb the training pipeline, and in what component of predictive
uncertainty they actually capture. Which of them is practical for a large 3D-CNN operating on voxelised
molecular complexes, and what quality of estimate each delivers in that setting, was not established when
this work was carried out.
 In this report we document a comparative evaluation of six uncertainty-quantification approaches applied
to a single 3D-CNN architecture for protein-ligand binding-affinity prediction. The evaluated methods are
a deep ensemble, a bootstrap ensemble, Monte Carlo (MC) dropout, a convolution-fed Gaussian process
(CFGP), residual estimation with an input/output kernel (RIO), and an in-series residual network. All six
are compared against a single deterministic 3D-CNN on the same data partition, using the same trained archi-
tecture, and are assessed along five axes: point-prediction accuracy, calibration, sharpness, dispersion, and
prediction-interval coverage and width. We report which conclusions the resulting measurements support,
and which they do not.

Scope. This report describes each method as implemented, defines every evaluation metric mathematically,
and states which conclusions the measurements support and which they do not. It introduces no new method
and makes no claim of novelty for the methods evaluated. The remainder is organised as follows. Section 2
defines the sources of predictive uncertainty and the families of methods for deep networks; Section 3
places the study in context; Section 4 describes the data, representation, architecture and training; Section 5

2 Background
2.1 Protein-ligand binding-affinity prediction
Binding affinity measures the strength of the non-covalent association between a small molecule and a
protein target, and it is among the primary quantities that computer-aided drug discovery estimates before
committing to experimental assays. Structure-based machine-learning approaches treat the problem as su-
pervised regression: given a three-dimensional representation of a protein-ligand complex, predict a scalar
affinity, usually on the pK scale as − log10 of the dissociation or inhibition constant. Voxel-based models
discretise the binding pocket onto a regular grid and apply convolutional networks to the resulting tensor
[1, 2], whereas graph-based models represent atoms as nodes and interatomic distances as edges. The Deep
Fusion work of Jones et al. [3] compared and combined both families, and its 3D-CNN component is the
model on which the present study builds.
 Structure-based affinity models are almost always trained and evaluated on PDBbind [9, 10], a curated col-
lection of protein-ligand complexes with experimentally determined structures and binding measurements.
PDBbind is organised into a large general set, a higher-quality refined subset filtered on structural resolution
and measurement type, and a small core subset selected by clustering to serve as a held-out benchmark [11].
The conventional protocol, and the one followed here, trains on the refined set and tests on the core set. The
limitations of that protocol are discussed in Section 9.

2.2 Sources of predictive uncertainty
We distinguish three quantities that are often conflated. Throughout, x denotes an input complex, y its
measured affinity, ŷ a model point prediction, and σ a predicted standard deviation.
 Point-prediction accuracy concerns only the discrepancy between ŷ and y, aggregated over a test set. It
says nothing about whether the model knows when it is wrong. A model can be accurate on average and
yet assign the same confidence to a well-characterised kinase inhibitor and to a chemotype it has never
encountered.
 Epistemic uncertainty, also called model or systematic uncertainty, arises from the fact that the train-
ing data do not determine a unique model. Different initialisations, different training subsets and different
architectures can all fit the observed data comparably well while disagreeing elsewhere in input space. Epis-
temic uncertainty is in principle reducible: it shrinks as more informative data are collected, and it is the
component that should grow for inputs far from the training distribution.
 Aleatoric uncertainty, also called statistical or data uncertainty, arises from variability inherent in the data-
generating process. In binding-affinity prediction it includes measurement error in the reported affinities,
variation across assay conditions and laboratories, and error in the deposited structures. It is irreducible by
additional sampling from the same process.
 Predictive uncertainty is the total uncertainty attached to a prediction at a specific input, and it combines
both components. The distinction matters for this report because most of the methods evaluated here esti-
mate only one part of the total. Methods that measure the spread of predictions across an ensemble of models,
or across stochastic forward passes through one model, are measuring disagreement among plausible mod-
els, which is an epistemic quantity. They do not observe measurement noise at all, and unless the training
objective is modified to predict a noise term, they cannot recover it. This is a well-known property of the
original deep-ensemble formulation, which pairs ensembling with a heteroscedastic negative-log-likelihood
loss precisely so that the aleatoric part is modelled explicitly [12]. Section 5 states, for each method evalu-

ated here, exactly what variation the reported σ measures. No claim is made anywhere in this report that a
method separates epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty, because none of the implementations documented in
the project record supports such a claim.

2.3 Uncertainty quantification in deep neural networks
Approaches to UQ for deep networks can be grouped by the inference machinery they use and, orthogonally,
by how much they disturb an existing training pipeline. Both groupings are useful for reading the results.
 By inference machinery, the presentation distinguished four families. Frequentist and ensemble methods
construct a population of models, by varying initialisation or by resampling the training data, and read
uncertainty off the spread of their predictions [12–14]; related frequentist constructions estimate the two
uncertainty components separately [15]. Hybrid sampling methods draw samples from the weight posterior
using Hamiltonian or Langevin dynamics [6], which is asymptotically faithful but expensive at the scale of
a 3D-CNN. Variational inference replaces the intractable posterior with a tractable family and optimises a
variational bound [4, 5]. Perturbation and approximation methods inject noise into an otherwise standard
network and interpret the induced output variation as an approximation to posterior uncertainty [7, 16, 17].
This study draws on the first and fourth families, together with two Gaussian-process constructions built on
a frozen network.
 By pipeline disturbance, methods split into those that require retraining and those that do not. Deep
ensembles, bootstrap ensembles and MC dropout all require either multiple training runs or a modified ar-
chitecture, and are correspondingly expensive; a thirty-member ensemble of 3D-CNNs costs thirty training
runs. CFGP, RIO and the in-series network are built on top of a single pre-trained network whose weights
are frozen, which makes them cheap but makes their uncertainty estimates entirely dependent on the repre-
sentation that one network happened to learn. This trade-off between cost and dependence on a single fitted
model recurs throughout the results.
 A separate difficulty, and one that motivates the evaluation methodology of Section 6, is that there is
no ground truth for uncertainty. For a point prediction the target y is observed and the error is directly
measurable, but no observation of “the correct σ ” exists for a single complex. Evaluation therefore proceeds
indirectly, by checking distributional properties that a well-behaved predictive distribution should satisfy
over a test set. Reference posteriors obtained from Hamiltonian Monte Carlo or from an exact Gaussian
process are sometimes used as a proxy ground truth [18], but neither is computationally feasible for a
network of this size, and neither was computed in this study.

3 Related work
Empirical comparisons of UQ methods. The methodological premise of this study, that UQ methods
must be compared empirically because their theoretical guarantees do not predict their behaviour on real
models, is well established. Yao et al. [18] compared ten approximate inference schemes for Bayesian neu-
ral networks against Hamiltonian Monte Carlo reference posteriors and found that commonly used aggregate
metrics such as test log-likelihood can be misleading, and that inference schemes designed to capture pos-
terior structure do not reliably produce better posterior approximations. In materials science, Tran et al.
[19] assembled the suite of accuracy, calibration, sharpness and dispersion diagnostics that the present study
adopts, applied it to six UQ methods for adsorption-energy prediction, and reported that a convolution-fed
Gaussian process performed best among the methods they examined. That paper is the direct methodological
ancestor of this work: it supplies both the evaluation protocol used in Section 6 and the CFGP construction
described in Section 5.5.

UQ in molecular property prediction. Within chemistry, Scalia et al. [20] and Hirschfeld et al. [21]
independently benchmarked scalable UQ methods for molecular property prediction and reached broadly
consistent conclusions: ensembles are strong but expensive baselines, MC dropout is cheaper but often
poorly calibrated, and no single method dominates across datasets and metrics. Both studies worked with
ligand-only property prediction rather than protein-ligand complexes, and both used graph or fingerprint rep-
resentations rather than voxel grids, so neither addressed the question of what happens when the underlying
model is a large 3D-CNN operating on a sparse volumetric input.

Broader context. Several strands of the wider literature bear on how these results should be read. Surveys
by Abdar et al. [22] and Gawlikowski et al. [23] have organised the field, and Yu et al. [24] reviewed UQ
for drug discovery specifically. Evidential regression [25] is a single-pass alternative that was not evaluated
here, and benchmarking infrastructure for uncertainty comparisons in chemistry has since matured through
the Uncertainty Toolbox [26] and the UNIQUE framework [27]. A leakage-aware comparison of uncertainty
methods for this same prediction problem has also been reported [28].

Data leakage in PDBbind evaluation. A parallel line of work questions whether the conventional PDB-
bind protocol measures generalisation at all. Section ?? reviews it in the context of the present results.

4 Dataset and experimental setup
4.1 PDBbind 2016 and data partitions
All experiments use the 2016 release of PDBbind [9, 10] and follow the partitioning protocol of the Deep
Fusion study [3]. The core set is held out as the test set. The remaining refined-set complexes, that is the
refined set with the core set removed, supply the training and validation data: 3390 complexes for training
and 377 for validation, against 290 test complexes (Table 1). The 2016 refined set contains 4057 complexes
and the core set 290, so the training and validation partitions together exhaust the refined set outside the
core, and the validation partition is 10% of that remainder. A single fixed partition is used for every method
compared in this report, so all differences between methods are differences in method rather than in data.
 Unlike Jones et al. [3], this study did not use the PDBbind general set for any purpose, and it evaluated
only crystal structures. The docking-pose evaluation reported in the Deep Fusion work was not repeated.

4.2 Atomic input features and voxelisation
Each complex is converted into a dense volumetric tensor. Only heavy atoms and heteroatoms are retained,
and every retained atom carries the property vector listed in Table 2: a one-hot element encoding, hy-
bridisation state, heavy valence, hetero valence, five structural flags, partial charge, a protein-versus-ligand
indicator and van der Waals radius. This property set is taken unchanged from the Deep Fusion 3D-CNN
[3], which in turn follows Stepniewska-Dziubinska et al. [2].
 Atomic coordinates are centred on the ligand and discretised onto a cubic grid whose extent is approx-
imately 48 Å in each dimension with a voxel edge of 1 Å, which is sufficient to cover the binding pocket
while limiting collisions between atoms mapped to the same voxel. A Gaussian blur with σ = 1 is then
applied along each spatial axis, spreading each atom’s features into neighbouring voxels and avoiding an
excessively sparse representation. The resulting input tensor has shape 19 × 48 × 48 × 48.

4.3 3D-CNN architecture
The predictive model is a three-dimensional convolutional network closely related to the Deep Fusion 3D-
CNN [3]; larger and structurally different architectures were explored without clear improvement. Table 3
gives the layer sequence. Five convolutional layers, each followed by batch normalisation and a rectified
linear activation, are arranged with two residual short-cut connections and two max-pooling stages. Flat-
tening is followed by three fully connected layers, the first producing a 100-dimensional representation and
the last producing the scalar affinity prediction. Dropout is available after each convolutional block, after
flattening, and after the first two fully connected layers; it is active at inference only for the Monte Carlo
dropout experiments of Section 5.4.
 The 100-dimensional representation produced by the first fully connected layer plays a second role in
this study. It is the feature vector consumed by the two Gaussian-process methods, the convolution-fed
Gaussian process (Section 5.5) and RIO (Section 5.6), both of which treat the trained network as a fixed
feature extractor. References to the “latent representation” of the 3D-CNN throughout this report denote
that vector.

5.1 Deterministic baseline
The baseline is a single 3D-CNN trained as described in Section 4.4 and evaluated by a single forward pass.

5.2 Deep ensemble
Thirty networks were trained independently from different random initialisations, and their predictions were
combined. The point prediction is the mean over members and the uncertainty is the standard deviation over
members.
 This is the ensembling component of the method proposed by Lakshminarayanan et al. [12], without
the two additional components that paper specifies. Each member here is the plain scalar regressor of Ta-
ble 3, trained under squared error, rather than a mean-and-variance head trained under a heteroscedastic
negative-log-likelihood objective, and no adversarial-training term was used. The consequence is important
for interpreting the results: with no member modelling observation noise, the reported σ measures disagree-
ment among thirty fitted models and nothing else. It is a purely epistemic quantity and omits the aleatoric
component of predictive uncertainty entirely. Sections 7.4 and 8 return to this point.

5.3 Bootstrap ensemble
Thirty networks were trained on resampled training sets, each drawn with replacement from the training
partition at 60% of its size. As with the deep ensemble, the point prediction is the member mean and the
uncertainty is the member standard deviation.
 Drawing m = 0.6n rather than m = n examples makes this an m-out-of-n bootstrap [13]. Smaller replicates
are individually less well fitted and more variable, so this choice increases between-member disagreement
relative to the classical construction. No separate noise-variance term was estimated and added, as Heskes
[31] prescribe for bootstrap prediction intervals, so this method also measures model disagreement alone.

5.4 Monte Carlo dropout
Dropout was left active at inference and each complex was passed through the network N = 100 times,
following Gal and Ghahramani [7]. The point prediction is the mean over passes and the uncertainty is
the standard deviation over passes. Three dropout rates were evaluated, r ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.2}, with dropout
applied at the positions marked in Table 3.
 Two properties of this configuration bear on the results. First, one hundred stochastic passes against
thirty ensemble members means the two families draw different numbers of samples from their predictive
distributions. That is immaterial for the variance-based metrics, which converge in the number of samples,
but it is not immaterial for the interval metrics, which are built as sample envelopes; Section 6.5 takes this up.
Second, stochastic inference is performed with the network in training mode, so batch normalisation also
uses inference-batch statistics rather than the running statistics accumulated during training. This introduces
a second source of variation, one that depends on batch composition and that perturbs the point predictions
as well as their spread. It is a plausible contributor to the correlation degradation reported in Section 7.1,
where Monte Carlo dropout is the only method whose correlation falls below the baseline at every setting.

5.5 Convolution-fed Gaussian process
A Gaussian process was fitted on top of the frozen network, taking the 100-dimensional latent representation
z as its input and the affinity as its target. Features were standardised using statistics computed on the
training partition, and the Gaussian process was fitted on the training partition. The kernel is a scaled
Matérn kernel [32] under a Gaussian likelihood, with hyperparameters obtained by maximising the exact
marginal likelihood. Three values of the Matérn smoothness parameter were evaluated, ν ∈ {0.5, 1.5, 2.5}.
Because the Gaussian process supplies its own posterior mean, this method changes the point prediction
relative to the frozen network.

 13

 The construction follows Tran et al. [19], who introduced the convolution-fed Gaussian process and
reported it as the strongest of the six methods they compared for adsorption-energy prediction. One element
differs. In the original formulation the Gaussian process consumes pooled outputs of the convolutional
layers, whereas here it consumes a post-fully-connected representation. The difference is consequential
in principle: pooled convolutional features retain spatial structure that the fully connected layers discard,
whereas a representation two layers from the scalar output is close to a low-dimensional summary of the
prediction itself. A Gaussian process on such a representation has limited scope to distinguish inputs that
the frozen network maps to similar predictions, which bears on the low dispersion this method exhibits at
ν ∈ {0.5, 1.5} (Section 7.3).

5.6 RIO
RIO [8] fits a Gaussian process to the residuals of a pre-trained network using a composite kernel over both
the network’s input and its output, and adds the fitted residual mean back to the network prediction. Here the
composite kernel is the sum of two scaled radial basis function kernels, one over the latent representation
z and one over the base prediction ŷ, under a Gaussian likelihood with hyperparameters obtained by exact
marginal-likelihood maximisation. Writing ε for the residual,

                               ε = y − ŷ,     ŷRIO = ŷ + µε ,   σRIO = σε ,                              (1)

where µε and σε are the Gaussian-process posterior mean and standard deviation of the residual at the test
input.
 Two aspects of this implementation differ from the original method and affect how the results should be
read. First, the input component of the composite kernel operates on the latent representation rather than
the raw input. This is a necessary adaptation, since the raw input is a tensor of roughly 2 × 105 voxels
and a kernel over that space is not practical, but it is still a departure: an uncertainty estimate computed
over a learned representation can only distinguish inputs that the frozen network itself distinguishes, which
limits the method’s ability to flag inputs the network maps confidently but wrongly. Second, the residual
Gaussian process was fitted on training-partition residuals. Residuals computed on data the frozen network
was fitted to are systematically smaller than residuals on unseen data, so a residual model fitted on them
learns a downward-biased picture of the error scale and reports uncertainties that are too small at test time.

5.7 In-series neural network
A second 3D-CNN, architecturally identical to the first and initialised from its weights, was trained to
predict the residual of the frozen first network. The frozen network supplies the point prediction, which the
construction leaves unchanged, and the second network supplies the uncertainty as the absolute value of its
output. Residual targets were computed on the validation partition, and the second network was trained on
that partition.
 Two features of the construction shape its behaviour. First, because the predicted residual is reported
as σ rather than added to ŷ, the point predictions are identical to the baseline by construction, and Table 4
confirms it. Second, and less obviously, the quantity the second network estimates is not a scale. Training

6 Evaluation methodology
All metrics are computed on the 290 complexes of the PDBbind 2016 core set. We write N = 290 for the
number of test complexes, yn for the measured affinity of complex n, ŷn for the point prediction, σn for the
predicted standard deviation, and Fn for the predictive distribution at complex n. All quantities are in pK
units unless stated otherwise. We write 1[ · ] for the indicator function, equal to 1 when its argument is true
and 0 otherwise.

6.1 Point-prediction accuracy
Point-prediction accuracy is assessed with four standard regression metrics, computed while ignoring the
uncertainty estimate entirely: root-mean-square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), median abso-
lute error (MDAE), and the Pearson correlation coefficient between predictions and targets (CORR). RMSE
and MAE are in pK units and are sensitive to large errors and to all errors respectively; MDAE is robust
to outliers; CORR measures linear association and is invariant to affine rescaling, so it can move indepen-
dently of the error magnitudes. All four are reported because a method can reduce error magnitudes while
degrading association, and Section 7.1 shows a case where exactly that happens.

into a plateau in between, indicates predictive distributions that are too narrow: observations fall into the
extreme tails more often than the stated quantiles allow, which is overconfidence. The opposite pattern
indicates predictive distributions that are too wide, which is underconfidence. The more pronounced the
plateau, the more severe the overconfidence.

property of the method.
 A further caveat applies to the three methods whose intervals are built from Gaussian samples. For those,
the sampling step and the sharpness and calibration computations use the stored uncertainty on different
scale conventions, differing by a square root. Their coverage and width figures are therefore not commensu-
rate with their own sharpness and calibration figures, and should be compared within each block rather than
across the two. The direction of the coverage conclusion in Section 7.4 is not affected, because reconciling
the conventions would narrow the affected intervals and lower their coverage further.

6.6 Out-of-distribution behaviour
Out-of-distribution behaviour refers to how a model’s uncertainty responds to inputs unlike its training
data. A useful method should assign visibly larger uncertainty to a complex whose protein family or ligand
chemotype is absent from training. No out-of-distribution experiment was performed in this study: every
reported number comes from the core set, drawn from the same curation process and release as the training

 17

data. Dispersion is used as a proxy, indicating whether a method could respond to distributional shift, not
whether it does.

7 Results
All results are single-run measurements on the 290-complex PDBbind 2016 core set. No configuration was
repeated, no variability estimates were computed, and no statistical tests were performed. Every difference
discussed below is therefore a difference between two individual runs, and small differences should not be
treated as established.

7.1 Point-prediction performance
Table 4 reports the four accuracy metrics. Three observations follow directly from it.
 First, the ensemble methods give the largest error reductions relative to the deterministic baseline. The
deep ensemble reduces RMSE from 1.72 to 1.53 and MDAE from 1.18 to 0.97, and the bootstrap ensemble
performs comparably. This is consistent with previous observations that averaging independently trained
networks reduces prediction variance [12, 20], and it is a property of the averaging rather than of uncertainty
quantification as such: the same reduction would be obtained by ensembling without ever computing a
variance.
 Second, the improvement is not universal. The in-series network reproduces the baseline on all four
metrics, 1.72, 1.37, 1.18 and 0.71, because its second network predicts a residual that is reported as σ and
never added to ŷ (Section 5.7). CFGP at ν = 2.5 raises RMSE to 2.18, which is materially worse than the
baseline. MC dropout lowers RMSE, MAE and MDAE at all three dropout rates but lowers the correlation as
well, from 0.71 at baseline to 0.69 at r = 0.1 and 0.68 at r = 0.05 and r = 0.2. Two mechanisms are available.
Averaging over stochastic forward passes shrinks predictions towards the mean, which reduces squared error
while weakening the linear association with the target. In addition, stochastic inference was performed with
the whole network in training mode, so batch normalisation normalised by inference-batch statistics rather
than by the running statistics accumulated during training (Section 5.4); this perturbs the point predictions
themselves in a way that depends on batch composition. The evidence here does not separate the two.
 Third, the deterministic baseline evaluated here is weaker than the published Deep Fusion 3D-CNN
trained on the refined set, which reports RMSE 1.501 and MAE 1.164 on the same core set [3]; those
two values were read from the corresponding table of the preprint [29] and should be checked against
the published table before release. The most likely explanation is the difference in optimisation regime
described in Section 4.4, since the present study used stochastic gradient descent at 1 × 10−4 in PyTorch
rather than Adam at 1 × 10−3 in TensorFlow. Whatever the cause, the consequence for reading this report is
that comparisons should be made within Table 4 and not against externally published 3D-CNN numbers.
 Taken together, the accuracy results support a narrower statement than a blanket improvement. The two
ensemble methods, RIO, and CFGP at ν ∈ {0.5, 1.5} reduced RMSE, MAE and MDAE relative to the
baseline in a single run. MC dropout reduced the error metrics while reducing correlation. The in-series
network left accuracy unchanged by construction, and CFGP at ν = 2.5 degraded it.

Coverage and width move together almost perfectly. Ordering the ten configurations by MPIW and by PICP
produces nearly the same ranking, with a Spearman rank correlation of 0.95 over the values in Table 5. The
narrowest intervals belong to CFGP at ν = 0.5, with MPIW 1.57 and coverage 40.3%, and the widest to
CFGP at ν = 2.5, with MPIW 5.49 and coverage 74.8%; MC dropout at r = 0.2 attains the highest coverage,

83.4%, with MPIW 4.95. Figure 5 displays the relationship. Part of that near-perfect association is built in:
both quantities are computed from the same sample envelope, so a method that produces a wider envelope
necessarily covers more.
 Read against the implied nominal levels of Equation 9, the coverage results are severe. Every configu-
ration falls far below the level its own interval construction implies. The six configurations built from 30
samples imply 93.5% coverage and achieve between 40.3% and 74.8%, a shortfall of 19 to 53 percentage
points. The three MC dropout configurations imply 98.0% and achieve between 50.3% and 83.4%, a short-
fall of 15 to 48 points. The smallest shortfall in the study, 14.6 points, belongs to MC dropout at r = 0.2,
which is also the configuration with the widest intervals of any method other than CFGP at ν = 2.5.
 This converts the calibration finding of Section 7.2 from a qualitative reading of curve shapes into a
quantified one, and it does so through an independent route: the calibration curves and the coverage counts
are computed from different constructions, and both say that the predictive distributions are far too narrow.
 Two caveats attach to the cross-method comparison. First, the implied level differs between the two
sample-count groups, so MC dropout’s coverage is not directly comparable with the rest; its intervals are
inflated by roughly 23% for the same underlying scale purely because 100 samples were drawn rather than
30 (Section 6.5). Second, for CFGP, RIO and the in-series network the sampling step and the sharpness
computation use the stored uncertainty on scale conventions that differ by a square root, so their coverage
and width figures are not on the same footing as their sharpness and calibration figures (Section 6.5). Both
caveats push in the same direction for the headline conclusion: reconciling either would narrow the intervals
for the affected methods and lower their coverage further.

7.5 Hyperparameter sensitivity
Two hyperparameters were varied, and both change the results substantially. Appendix A reproduces the
corresponding calibration and sharpness panels.
 For MC dropout, increasing the dropout rate from 0.05 to 0.1 to 0.2 monotonically improves calibration,
with miscalibration area falling from 0.158 to 0.120 to 0.077, and monotonically worsens sharpness, with
values rising from 0.668 to 0.830 to 1.077. Coverage rises from 50.3% to 66.6% to 83.4% and MPIW from
3.26 to 3.76 to 4.95. The dropout rate therefore acts approximately as a width-scaling knob: larger rates
inflate the predictive distribution, which relieves the overconfidence common to all methods at the cost of
less informative intervals. Accuracy does not move monotonically, with RMSE of 1.672, 1.609 and 1.640
across the three rates.
 For CFGP, the Matérn smoothness parameter produces a qualitative change rather than a graded one. The
settings ν = 0.5 and ν = 1.5 behave almost identically, both extremely sharp and both very poorly calibrated.
The setting ν = 2.5 behaves like a different method: it has the best calibration of any configuration in the
study, the worst sharpness, a coefficient of variation indistinguishable from zero, and the worst RMSE of
any configuration at 2.18. This pattern is consistent with the Gaussian process at ν = 2.5 fitting a large,
nearly homoscedastic noise variance together with a comparatively poor posterior mean.
 Where a single setting of each method is shown alongside the other methods, in Figures 2 and 3 and in
the upper block of Table 4, that setting is r = 0.1 for MC dropout and ν = 1.5 for CFGP. Neither is the

using metrics computed on the test set, which Section 9 discusses.

8 Discussion
8.1 What the experiments establish
Adding uncertainty quantification to a large 3D-CNN is not a matter of selecting a method and applying it.
Every configuration failed on at least one of the three properties a useful uncertainty estimate must have,
and the failures were of different kinds. CFGP at small ν was tight and badly miscalibrated; at ν = 2.5
it was well calibrated but constant, and had the worst point accuracy in the study. RIO was moderately
calibrated but barely varied across complexes. The in-series network varied strongly, was calibrated poorly,
and left the point prediction untouched. The ensembles and MC dropout were the most balanced and were
still overconfident. No configuration was simultaneously well calibrated, sharp and dispersed.
 The trade-off between calibration and sharpness is the clearest signal in the data (Section 7.6). It is not
an artefact of one method family, and it has a straightforward reading: with predictive distributions that
are uniformly too narrow, calibration can be bought by widening them, and widening them costs sharpness.

they happen to produce, and the metric that would distinguish those two things, the relationship between
predicted uncertainty and realised error, was not measured.
 The universality of the overconfidence is the second clear signal, and it is quantified twice over: through
the calibration curves, and independently through the coverage shortfall of 15 to 53 percentage points against
the levels the interval construction implies (Section 7.4). Its cause is structural. Every method here reports
the spread of point predictions produced by models trained under a squared-error objective, and none esti-
mates measurement noise in the affinity labels. The reported uncertainty is therefore epistemic only. PDB-
bind affinities are aggregated from heterogeneous assays and carry substantial experimental variability, so
a predictive distribution that omits that component should be expected to be too narrow. This suggests that
the most promising direction for this model family is not a different posterior approximation but an explicit
noise model, whether through a heteroscedastic output head [12] or through post-hoc recalibration.

8.2 Practical implications
For a screening or active-learning deployment, the results support a narrow recommendation. If the computa-
tional budget permits repeated training, an ensemble is the reasonable default: it gave the best point accuracy
in the study, mid-range calibration, and a dispersion sufficient to differentiate complexes. The cost is thirty
training runs of a 3D-CNN, which is substantial. If the budget does not permit retraining, the frozen-network
methods are all cheap, and among them RIO gave the best point accuracy, but its near-constant uncertainty
(Cv = 0.017) makes it close to useless as a ranking signal for uncertainty-based acquisition, which is the
main reason one would want uncertainty quantification in an active-learning loop [37]. Two of the three
frozen-network methods also carry defects identified in Section 5 that are cheap to remove: RIO’s residual
Gaussian process was fitted on training-partition residuals, which biases its scale estimate downwards, and
the in-series network’s uncertainty estimates a conditional-mean residual rather than a scale. Neither method
should be redeployed in its present form.
 None of these recommendations should be applied without recalibration. Since every method was over-
confident, and since post-hoc recalibration on a held-out split is inexpensive and well understood for regres-
sion [33], recalibrating before deployment is a straightforward improvement that this study did not attempt.

9 Limitations
The study followed the conventional PDBbind 2016 protocol used by the associated Deep Fusion work [3]:
train on the refined set with the core set removed, test on the core set. That protocol removes exact complex
overlap between the partitions but does not control for similarity. The core set is constructed by clustering the
refined set and selecting representatives [11], so core complexes are drawn from the same protein families
and chemical series as the training complexes by construction. The Deep Fusion study itself reports that of
the 269 core-set compounds for which SMILES strings could be constructed, 51 also occur in the refined
training set [29].
 Work since 2017 has documented the consequences. Li and Yang [38] showed that structural and se-
quence similarity between splits inflates apparent scoring power, Su et al. [39] traced the dependence of
measured performance on training-set composition, and Volkov et al. [40] argued that networks trained on
such splits may learn protein and ligand identity rather than interaction physics. Li et al. [41] constructed the
Leak-Proof PDBbind partition specifically to control protein and ligand similarity across splits, and Durant
et al. [42] and Graber et al. [43] have extended the analysis.
 The implication for this report is specific and worth stating precisely. The accuracy figures in Table 4 are
most defensible as measurements of interpolation within a chemical and structural neighbourhood the model

 25

has already seen, not as measurements of generalisation to dissimilar proteins and ligands. The consequence
for the UQ results is sharper still: a test set that resembles the training set is close to the easiest possible
case for uncertainty estimation, because the domain shift that uncertainty is supposed to detect is largely
absent. That every method was nonetheless overconfident is therefore a conservative result, and there is no
basis in these data for expecting the methods to behave better under a leakage-aware split. A leakage-aware
re-evaluation would give a more stringent measure of both accuracy and uncertainty quality, and Section 10
lists it as the first item of future work.

10 Conclusions and future work
We compared six uncertainty-quantification approaches applied to a 3D-CNN for protein-ligand binding-
affinity prediction, evaluated on the PDBbind 2016 core set against a deterministic baseline. The following
conclusions are supported by the reported measurements.

1. No evaluated configuration produced uncertainty estimates that were simultaneously well calibrated,
 sharp and dispersed. Each was deficient on at least one axis, and the deficiencies differed in kind across
 methods.
2. Every configuration was overconfident, and severely so. All calibration curves showed predictive distri-
 butions that were too narrow, and none showed the opposite pattern. Independently, every configuration
 covered between 15 and 53 percentage points below the level implied by its own interval construction.
 The cause is structural: every method reports the spread of point predictions from models trained under
 a squared-error objective, so the estimated uncertainty is epistemic only and omits measurement noise in
 the affinity labels.
3. Calibration and sharpness trade off against each other across method families, with a rank correlation
 of −0.79 between sharpness and miscalibration area. Every apparent calibration gain in this study was
 obtained by widening the predictive distribution.
4. Dispersion does not participate in that trade-off. Its rank correlation with miscalibration area is 0.07. The
 two properties can be improved independently.
5. Coverage and interval width track each other almost exactly, with a rank correlation of 0.95. This is partly
 built in, since both are computed from the same sample envelope, and the comparison across methods is
 further shaped by the number of samples drawn, which was 100 for MC dropout and 30 for every other
 method.
6. The ensemble methods gave the best point accuracy, reducing RMSE from 1.72 to 1.53, consistent with
 the variance reduction expected from averaging independently trained networks. The improvement was
 not universal: the in-series network left accuracy unchanged by construction, CFGP at ν = 2.5 degraded
 it, and MC dropout reduced error magnitudes while reducing correlation.
7. Both examined hyperparameters changed the results substantially. The dropout rate acted approximately
 as a width-scaling control, and the Matérn smoothness parameter produced a qualitative change in CFGP
 behaviour rather than a graded one.

Future work. Six directions follow from the limitations, in rough order of value per unit of effort. First,
re-evaluate under a leakage-aware partition such as Leak-Proof PDBbind [41], with external test sets, which
would establish whether these conclusions survive a stringent split. Second, add a rank correlation between
predicted uncertainty and absolute error to the metric suite, so that the question the study was really asking

 26

can be answered. Third, repeat each configuration several times and report means and standard deviations.
Fourth, apply post-hoc isotonic recalibration on a held-out split [33] and report calibrated alongside uncal-
ibrated metrics; given that every method here was overconfident, this is the change most likely to yield an
immediate improvement. Fifth, evaluate methods that model the predictive distribution explicitly, includ-
ing heteroscedastic ensembles under a proper scoring rule [12], evidential regression [25] and Bayes by
backprop [4], all of which supply the aleatoric component that the methods evaluated here omit. Sixth,
record training and inference cost so that the accuracy and calibration results can be weighed against the
computational budget an active-learning campaign would actually have.
