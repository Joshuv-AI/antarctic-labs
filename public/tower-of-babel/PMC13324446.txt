A comprehensive survey of genetic variants in neuroblastoma
Authors: Wei Wang, Ling Zhang, Jianbin Wang, Yucan Lin, Xunbin Yu, Bao yan Zheng, Xi Zhuang, Zhongli Hu, Lizhi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuroblastoma (NB) is the most common extracranial solid tumor in children and is characterized by marked clinical and molecular heterogeneity. Genomic alterations play a critical role in NB pathogenesis; however, population-specific mutational features remain insufficiently characterized, particularly among Chinese patients.MethodsWhole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on tumor, para-tumor, and matched peripheral blood samples from nine pathologically confirmed Chinese patients with NB. Somatic variant profiles were compared with four publicly available NB datasets from cBioPortal, published in 2012, 2013, 2015, and 2023. Mutational patterns, recurrently altered genes, and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment were analyzed using R version 4.3.2 and clusterProfiler version 4.10.0.ResultsA total of 77 missense variants were identified in our cohort. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) represented the predominant variant type, and C > T substitutions were the most frequent nucleotide change. MAP1A variants, comprising two missense variants in one patient, and RBM33 variants, comprising two distinct variants in two patients, were detected in our cohort and, to the best of our knowledge, have not been previously reported in NB, although their frequencies were low. No MYCN amplification or variants in ALK, ATRX, or DAXX were detected. Comparative analysis with the cBioPortal datasets revealed no somatic variants universally shared across all cohorts. In addition, high-risk patients exhibited distinct mutational patterns, with enrichment of the Gene Ontology term “collagen-containing extracellular matrix.”ConclusionsThese findings highlight the molecular diversity of NB and suggest the presence of potential population-specific genetic features in Chinese patients. The low-frequency MAP1A and RBM33 variants identified in this cohort warrant further validation in larger, independent cohorts. Moreover, the enrichment of extracellular matrix–related pathways in high-risk NB supports further investigation of tumor–microenvironment interactions as potential therapeutic targets.Keywords: Neuroblastoma, Whole-exome sequencing, Somatic variants, cBioPortal, Population-specific variants, Extracellular matrix, MAP1A, RBM33

Introduction

Neuroblastoma (NB) is the most common extracranial solid tumor in children, predominantly affecting infants and young children1. It is characterized by marked biological and clinical heterogeneity2. NB originates from neural crest-derived precursor cells of the sympathetic nervous system and most commonly arises in the adrenal glands and paraspinal sympathetic ganglia3. It accounts for approximately 8–10% of all pediatric malignancies, representing a major challenge in pediatric oncology1, 4. The incidence of NB varies across geographic regions. In high-incidence regions, including India, Malta, and Georgia, the incidence rate may reach 1.35 cases per 100,000 children5, whereas in the United States, it ranges from 0.5 to 0.85 cases per 100,000 children6. In China, the reported incidence is approximately 7.7 cases per million children, equivalent to 0.77 cases per 100,000 children7. Clinically, NB presents with diverse manifestations depending on the primary tumor location and the extent of metastatic dissemination, including abdominal masses, bone pain, and systemic symptoms8.

Despite advances in early diagnosis and multimodal treatment strategies, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, stem cell transplantation, and immunotherapy, NB remains difficult to treat, particularly in patients with high-risk disease. Diagnosis is typically established based on imaging findings, histopathological examination, and molecular assessment9. Prognosis is strongly influenced by age at diagnosis, tumor stage, and molecular characteristics. High-risk NB is frequently associated with metastatic disease, therapeutic resistance, and unfavorable clinical outcomes, with a five-year event-free survival (EFS) rate remaining below 50%10, 11. Consequently, NB remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality in children. Therefore, a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying NB heterogeneity is essential for improving risk stratification and developing more effective therapeutic strategies.

Genomic alterations play a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of NB. The genetic landscape of NB is highly complex, encompassing both recurrent driver events and heterogeneous low-frequency variants. Among the best-characterized genomic alterations, amplification of MYCN, located on chromosome 2p24, occurs in approximately 20–30% of NB cases and is strongly associated with aggressive disease and poor prognosis12, 13. ALK, located on chromosome 2p23, encodes a receptor tyrosine kinase, and activating variants in ALK have been identified in 6–17% of NB cases, contributing to aberrant signaling pathways that promote tumor cell proliferation and survival14, 15, 16. In addition to these established alterations, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and large-scale sequencing analyses have identified multiple NB susceptibility genes, including CASC15, BARD1, LIN28B, and ATRX17, 18. Notably, ATRX alterations have been associated with alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT) and unfavorable prognosis in a subset of patients12, 17. Collectively, these findings underscore the genetic complexity and heterogeneity of NB, encompassing both inherited susceptibility loci and tumor-associated genomic alterations.

Beyond MYCN amplification and ALK activation, NB is further characterized by recurrent segmental chromosomal alterations and abnormalities in telomere maintenance, including 11q loss, 17q gain, TERT rearrangements, and ATRX alterations12, 14, 15, 16. Germline predisposition is relatively uncommon but clinically important, particularly in familial NB cases involving ALK or PHOX2B. In parallel, common susceptibility loci identified through GWAS support a polygenic contribution to NB risk17, 18. Together, these genomic events influence tumor differentiation, genomic instability, telomere biology, and clinical behavior, thereby contributing to the biological diversity and clinical heterogeneity of NB.

MAP1A is located on chromosome 15q13 and encodes a neuron-associated microtubule protein involved in neuronal development, cytoskeletal organization, and axonal growth19, 20. In addition, MAP1A has been implicated in autophagy and immune regulation in several malignancies19, 20, 24. RBM33, located on chromosome 7q34, encodes an RNA-binding protein involved in RNA processing and post-transcriptional regulation, including mRNA export and N6-methyladenosine (m6A)-related RNA modification. Recent studies have suggested that RBM33 may regulate RNA stability and gene expression through RNA metabolic pathways21, 22, 23, 24. Although MAP1A and RBM33 have been associated with neuronal development and RNA regulatory processes, respectively, their potential roles in NB remain unclear and require further investigation. Large-scale genomic studies and public cancer databases, such as cBioPortal, have enabled comprehensive analyses of NB-associated variants25, 26, providing valuable resources for comparing variant profiles across cohorts and identifying potential driver events. To further elucidate the molecular basis of NB and its potential population-specific characteristics, we analyzed the variant profiles of nine Chinese patients with pathologically confirmed NB treated at our institution and compared them with publicly available cBioPortal datasets released in 2012, 2013, 2015, and 2023. The inclusion of our institutional cohort enabled us to explore potential genetic features that may be underrepresented in global databases and to investigate differences in variant profiles between Chinese patients and international cohorts. By analyzing somatic variants, their frequencies, and potentially affected functional pathways, this study aimed to improve the understanding of NB genomics and its implications for tumor progression and risk stratification. These findings may provide new insights for future research and the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Patient sample collection

Between January 2019 and December 2021, tumor tissue, para-tumor tissue, and peripheral blood samples were collected from nine patients with pathologically confirmed NB at Fujian Provincial Hospital, Affiliated to Fuzhou University. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had a histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of NB, available tumor tissue and matched peripheral blood samples for sequencing, and complete basic clinical and follow-up information. Patients were excluded if the DNA quality was insufficient for sequencing, matched peripheral blood samples were unavailable, or key clinical data were incomplete. All enrolled patients were of Chinese ethnicity. The inclusion of our institutional cohort was intended to explore potential population-specific genetic variants that may be underrepresented in global databases and to enable direct comparison with publicly available datasets from cBioPortal. Matched peripheral blood samples were used as controls for sequencing analysis; however, the present study primarily focused on characterizing the overall variant landscape of the cohort rather than performing a dedicated comparison between germline and somatic variants.

Tumor stage was determined according to the International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INSS). For variant landscape comparison and to increase the statistical contrast between groups, a simplified risk grouping strategy was applied in this analysis: patients with INSS stage I–II disease were assigned to the low-risk group, whereas those with INSS stage IIa–IV disease were assigned to the high-risk group. This grouping strategy was used solely for genomic analysis in the present study and does not replace the formal INRG risk classification used in clinical practice. Clinical information, including age at diagnosis, tumor location, MYCN amplification status, recurrence, and survival outcome, was recorded. MYCN amplification status was determined by pathological evaluation at diagnosis using standard clinical laboratory methods, such as fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians, as appropriate. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital, Affiliated to Fuzhou University (approval number: K2023–11-017).

DNA extraction, library construction, and whole-exome sequencing

Tissue samples were ground in liquid nitrogen using a prechilled mortar and pestle. Genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (Tiangen, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol and stored at −20 °C until further use. Sequencing libraries were constructed, followed by exome capture. Qualified libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina platform using a paired-end 150-bp sequencing strategy at Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), according to the required data output and library concentration.

Bioinformatic analysis

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control before downstream analysis. Reads containing adapter contamination, excessive low-quality bases, or a high proportion of unknown bases (N) were removed. Specifically, read pairs were discarded if either read contained adapter contamination, defined as more than 10 nucleotides aligned to the adapter sequence, more than 10% uncertain bases, or more than 50% low-quality bases with a Phred score < 5. The remaining high-quality reads, referred to as clean data, were used for subsequent alignment and variant analysis. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using BWA. The aligned reads were subsequently sorted, and duplicate reads were marked using Sambamba.

Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (indels) were identified using SAMtools and annotated with ANNOVAR. Copy number variants (CNVs) were detected using CoNIFER.

MYCN amplification status was determined from pathological diagnostic reports based on standard clinical laboratory methods, such as FISH, performed at the time of diagnosis. ATRX and DAXX variants were examined using the WES data, and no pathogenic variants in these genes were identified in this cohort.

Four NB studies available in cBioPortal were used for descriptive external comparison: nbl_amc_2012, which included whole-genome sequencing data from 87 primary NB tumors with matched normal samples; nbl_broad_2013, which included whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing data from 240 high-risk tumor–normal pairs; nbl_ucologne_2015, which included whole-genome sequencing data from 56 tumor–normal pairs; and nbl_msk_2023, which included MSK-IMPACT targeted sequencing data from 223 pediatric tumor–normal pairs. Because these cohorts differed substantially in sequencing coverage, platform, case composition, and analytical workflows, cross-cohort comparisons in the present study were interpreted descriptively rather than as direct estimates of mutation burden or variant frequency.

Functional enrichment analysis

To investigate the potential biological relevance of the identified variant-associated genes, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using R version 4.3.2 and the clusterProfiler package version 4.10.0. The analysis was based on the org.Hs.eg.db annotation database version 3.17.0 and the GO database updated in 2023. Genes derived from pairwise intersections between our cohort and the cBioPortal datasets, as well as genes shared within the high-risk subgroup, were used as input for enrichment analysis. GO terms with an adjusted P value <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Variant filtering and interpretation

Variants were filtered to retain high-confidence candidate variants. Common variants were excluded based on population allele frequency, defined as a minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥0.01, using public databases including dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, ExAC, and gnomAD. Candidate variants were subsequently annotated and interpreted according to genomic location and predicted functional impact. No additional Sanger sequencing validation was performed for the variants identified in this study.

The variant identification and interpretation strategy followed commonly used approaches in next-generation sequencing studies27, 28, 29. A schematic overview of the analytical workflow is shown in Fig. 1.

Software and databases used

The principal software tools used in this study included BWA for sequence alignment, Sambamba for duplicate marking and BAM processing, SAMtools for SNV and insertion/deletion (indel) detection, CoNIFER for CNV detection, and ANNOVAR for variant annotation. Downstream statistical and functional analyses were performed using R software. Public databases and resources used for annotation and comparative analyses included cBioPortal, dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, ExAC, gnomAD, COSMIC, OMIM, and the GWAS Catalog.

Statistical analysis and visualization

All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R software version 4.3.2. Summary statistics were calculated, and waterfall plots, Venn diagrams, and GO enrichment plots were generated using the ggplot2, ComplexHeatmap, and VennDiagram packages. GO enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package version 4.10.0 with the org.Hs.eg.db annotation database. Enrichment analysis was conducted using genes with q < 0.05, and the database used for analysis was updated in January 2023. For GO analysis of the high-risk group, eight genes carrying shared variants were included; however, we acknowledge that the limited number of genes may reduce statistical power.

Results

Demographic characteristics and clinical features of the patients

A total of nine patients with pathologically confirmed NB were included in this study, all of whom were diagnosed between January 2019 and December 2021. The cohort comprised five males and four females, with a median age of 36 months at diagnosis (range, 3–84 months). The primary tumor sites included the adrenal gland (n = 4) and mediastinum (n = 5), both of which are common anatomical locations for NB.

According to the INSS, three patients were classified as stage I, one as stage IIA, one as stage II, one as stage IIB, one as stage III, and two as stage IV (Table 1). For genomic comparison, patients with INSS stage I–II disease were assigned to the low-risk group (n = 4), whereas those with stage IIa–IV disease were assigned to the high-risk group (n = 5).

No MYCN amplification was detected in any patient. During follow-up, recurrence occurred in two high-risk patients, including one patient who died, whereas no recurrence was observed in the low-risk group, and all low-risk patients were alive at the last follow-up. Overall, these clinical characteristics reflect the heterogeneity of NB and provide important clinical context for interpreting the genomic findings in this cohort.

Variant landscape and potential significance in the NB cohort

We analyzed somatic variants in paired tumor–normal samples from our cohort and compared the results with publicly available cBioPortal datasets (Fig. 2). Overall, NB exhibited a relatively low mutational burden, with heterogeneous variant patterns across cohorts.

Somatic variant landscape of neuroblastoma in the Fujian cohort. (A) Summary of variant classification, variant type, SNV class, variants per sample, variant classification distribution, and the top 10 recurrently altered genes identified by WES in nine patients. In our cohort, missense variants were identified in 77 genes, with SNPs representing the predominant variant type. The median number of variants per sample was 10. The most frequent base substitution was C > T. The most recurrently altered genes included MAP1A, with two missense variants detected in a single patient; RBM33, detected in two patients with different variant types; and ATP2A1, ASAP2, ARHGAP6, AQP12A, ACTG1, ACIN1, ABCC3, and AASDH.

In the four cBioPortal datasets released in 2012, 2013, 2015, and 2023, SNPs were also the predominant variant type. However, substitution patterns differed across cohorts. C > A was the most common SNV class in the 2012, 2013, and 2023 datasets, whereas C > T and C > A substitutions occurred at comparable frequencies in the 2015 dataset. The median numbers of variants per sample were 6, 14, 12, and 2 in the 2012, 2013, 2015, and 2023 datasets, respectively, indicating inter-study variability in mutational burden. The most recurrently altered genes in each dataset are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1, and additional details regarding variant classification and mutational spectra are provided in the supplementary figures.

Functional enrichment analysis of NB-related variant-associated genes

To investigate the potential biological relevance of the identified variant-associated genes, GO enrichment analysis was performed. For the 33 genes derived from pairwise intersections between our cohort and the cBioPortal datasets, enrichment analysis showed that these genes were primarily associated with chromatin organization- and cytoskeleton-related terms, including the Swr1 complex, nucleosome, INO80-type complex, stress fiber, actomyosin, and contractile actin filament bundle. In addition, these genes were enriched in DNA-related molecular functions, including helicase activity, catalytic activity acting on DNA, ATP hydrolysis activity, and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeler activity (Fig. 6A, B). For the eight genes shared within the high-risk subgroup, GO analysis identified enrichment of the “collagen-containing extracellular matrix” term (Fig. 6C). Overall, these findings suggest that the shared variant-associated genes may be functionally related to chromatin organization, cytoskeletal architecture, and extracellular matrix-associated processes. However, these results should be interpreted with caution because of the limited number of genes included in the enrichment analysis.

Discussion

NB is a biologically and clinically heterogeneous pediatric tumor, and this heterogeneity remains a major challenge for prognostic assessment and therapeutic management30, 31.In this study, we characterized the somatic variant landscape of nine Chinese patients with NB and compared the findings with four publicly available NB cohorts from cBioPortal. Overall, our results revealed substantial variability across patients and datasets at both the clinical and molecular levels.

Clinically, our cohort exhibited heterogeneity in age at diagnosis, primary tumor site, stage distribution, and clinical outcome. The median age at diagnosis was 36 months, which is older than the classically reported median age for NB but remains within the recognized pediatric age range for this disease30, 31. Primary tumors arose in the adrenal gland and mediastinum, both of which are common anatomical sites of NB30. In addition, the cohort included patients with both localized and metastatic disease, reflecting the broad clinical spectrum of NB. From a prognostic perspective, recurrence and death occurred only in the high-risk group, whereas no recurrence was observed in the low-risk group during follow-up. This pattern is consistent with current risk-stratified treatment frameworks, in which tumor stage, age at diagnosis, and molecular features collectively influence prognosis30, 32, 33. Although the cohort size was limited, the observed differences in recurrence and survival between the two groups were directionally consistent with established NB risk stratification systems, supporting the clinical relevance of incorporating disease stage and outcome information when interpreting genomic findings.

At the molecular level, our data demonstrated substantial variability in somatic variant profiles. We identified 77 missense variants, with SNPs representing the predominant variant type. A predominance of single-nucleotide substitutions has also been reported in previous genomic studies of NB, although specific mutational spectra and recurrently altered genes vary across cohorts31, 34. Notably, no somatic variant was shared across all five datasets, even after stratification by risk group. This limited overlap is consistent with the recognized genomic heterogeneity of NB, in which relatively few recurrent somatic alterations are observed beyond established drivers such as MYCN amplification and ALK activation31, 35, 36. In addition, no shared somatic variant was identified across all high-risk samples in our cohort, further suggesting that high-risk NB may arise through diverse genomic routes rather than through a single uniform mutational pattern.

Among the best-characterized genomic alterations in NB, MYCN amplification and ALK activation remain the most clinically relevant17, 37, 38. MYCN amplification has been reported in approximately 20–30% of NB cases and is strongly associated with rapid tumor progression and poor prognosis 12, 13, 38, whereas ALK variants occur in a subset of sporadic and familial cases and may represent actionable therapeutic targets 14, 15, 16. A recent multicenter study in China suggested that MYCN polymorphisms may have only a modest association with NB susceptibility 39.However, neither MYCN amplification nor ALK variants were detected in our cohort. This finding may be partly attributable to the limited sample size and risk-group distribution of our cohort and is also consistent with the known molecular heterogeneity of NB.

In addition to these established alterations, variants in MAP1A and RBM33 were identified in our cohort and therefore warrant further consideration. MAP1A encodes a neuron-associated cytoskeletal protein involved in microtubule stability, axonal development, and neuronal differentiation40, 41. To the best of our knowledge, MAP1A variants have not previously been reported in NB. However, MAP1A has been associated with immune regulation and autophagy in other malignancies, including bladder cancer, lung cancer, and pancreatic cancer42, 43, 44. RBM33 is an RNA-binding protein implicated in RNA processing and post-transcriptional regulation21. To date, the biological function of RBM33 remains incompletely characterized22. Recent evidence suggests that RBM33 preferentially binds intronless RNA and facilitates its nuclear export, while other studies have indicated that RBM33 may also be involved in mRNA methylation21, 23. At present, variants in RBM33 have not been reported in NB. The presence of MAP1A and RBM33 variants in our cohort may reflect rare or population-associated genomic events in NB. Nevertheless, these observations should be interpreted with caution because of the limited sample size and the absence of functional validation.

When our cohort was compared with the cBioPortal datasets, the overlap in altered genes was limited, even within the same risk categories. This discrepancy may reflect differences in cohort composition, sequencing strategies, and analytical pipelines, and may also be influenced by ancestry-related genetic background30, 35, 45. Recent studies have suggested that NB may differ across populations in terms of epidemiology, clinical presentation, and genetic susceptibility30, 45. In particular, studies of Chinese cohorts have begun to evaluate susceptibility loci and polygenic risk models in NB, suggesting that population-specific genetic architecture may contribute to disease risk46. Although our study was not designed to formally assess population-specific variation, the limited overlap between our cohort and international datasets underscores the importance of incorporating underrepresented local populations into NB genomic research.

Functional enrichment analysis provided additional insight into the potential biological relevance of the observed variant-associated genes. Genes identified from pairwise intersections were mainly enriched in DNA repair and chromatin remodeling pathways, both of which have been implicated in tumor progression and genomic instability in NB and other malignancies31, 47, 48, 49. In the high-risk subgroup, overlapping genes were enriched for the “collagen-containing extracellular matrix” term, which may be relevant to tumor invasion and metastatic behavior 50, 51. Although these findings are biologically plausible, they should be interpreted cautiously because the enrichment analysis was based on a limited number of genes.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, which limits the generalizability of the findings and reduces the likelihood of detecting recurrent alterations. Second, the identified variants were not independently validated by Sanger sequencing. Third, although matched peripheral blood samples were included in the sequencing workflow to support variant calling, we did not conduct a dedicated comparative analysis of germline and somatic variant patterns. Future studies involving larger Chinese and multiethnic cohorts, combined with functional validation of candidate genes and integrated genomic and clinical analyses, are needed to further clarify the biological and clinical significance of these findings.

Conclusion

This study provides a comparative analysis of somatic variants in a Chinese NB cohort and publicly available international datasets, revealing substantial molecular heterogeneity and identifying MAP1A and RBM33 variants in our cohort. High-risk patients in our cohort exhibited poorer clinical outcomes and variant-associated genes enriched in extracellular matrix remodeling, suggesting a potential association with more aggressive tumor behavior. Although this study was limited by its small sample size, our findings underscore the value of incorporating population-specific data into NB genomic research. Future studies involving larger Chinese and multiethnic cohorts, combined with functional validation of candidate genes, are warranted to clarify the biological relevance of these alterations in NB.
