Novel genomic regions associated with adult—plant resistance to multiple fungal pathogens in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) revealed by DArT marker sequencing
Authors: Elżbieta Czembor, Łukasz Stępień, Jerzy H. Czembor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wheat is among the top three most important cereal crops globally and serves as a staple food for approximately 40% of the world’s population. Fungal leaf diseases such as yellow and leaf rusts (YR, LR), septoria nodorum blotch (SNB), septoria tritici blotch (STB), and powdery mildew (PM) have a major effect on yield loss in wheat, and resistance breeding is so far the most effective strategy to minimize those losses. Adult plant resistance (APR) is a crucial component of durable disease resistance; it reduces the pathogen’s infection rate, keeping disease levels below the damage threshold, even in the absence of complete immunity. Therefore, this study aimed to identify sources of resistance in a collection of 411 accessions from diverse global origins. These accessions were phenotyped across 2018–2019. DArTseq technology and Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) analysis were conducted to identify single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with APR for evaluated pathogens. DArT analysis showed that wheat chromosome 2B contains genomic regions associated with resistance to SNB, and that SNPs on chromosome 3B are associated with resistance to YR. On chromosome 6 A, there is a strong potential to explore, as a shared resistance locus for YR and SNB was found. SNPs: 3,937,236, 1,056,817 were consistent in both years, meaning their association with disease resistance is reliable and repeatable. Chromosome 7D is a strong region for SNPs significantly associated with both LR and SNB resistance. While multiple disease resistance genes are present on 7D, the 610 Mb LR locus is distinct from known LR, PM, and SNB loci, making it a strong candidate for functional validation. These findings highlight the value of historical resistance sources and uncover novel genomic regions for breeding a broad-spectrum APR-based resistance. Dual-trait loci, especially those effective against both biotrophic and necrotrophic pathogens, represent a promising material for achieving durable resistance in elite wheat cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13353-026-01077-4.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the oldest cultivated plants in the world and has long been among the most important crops for many countries. It ranks among the top three cereal crops globally and serves as a staple food for approximately 40% of the world’s population. Wheat grain has high nutritional value, consisting of approximately 70% starch and 14% protein, along with high-quality amino acids, unsaturated fatty acids, fibre, vitamins, and minerals. (Alomari et al. 2023; Saleem et al. 2022). Fungal pathogens causing leaf diseases such as Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Pst) causing yellow rust (YR), Puccinia triticina (Pt) causing leaf rust (LR), Parastagonospora (syn Stagonospora; Phaeosphaeria, Septoria nodorum) causing septoria nodorum blotch (SNB), Zymoseptoria tritici (formerly Mycosphaerella graminicola) causing septoria tritici blotch (STB) and Blumeria graminis f.s tritici (Bgt), causing powdery mildew (PM) have a big effect on yield loss. Wheat production must increase by an estimated 60% to meet future food demands (Delabre et al. 2021; Rizzo et al. 2021; Varshney et al. 2021).

Gene banks play a dual role: preserving plant genetic resources and serving as reservoirs of genetic diversity essential for breeding programs (Chawla et al. 2023; Thudi et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2018). In a study on gene banks, 529,577 wheat accessions were identified globally, with 83,377 (15.7%) classified as landraces (De Carvalho et al. 2013). Breeding for disease resistance is a key focus, with gene banks—such as the Polish Gene Bank—playing a vital role by preserving landraces rich in genetic diversity. However, without proper documentation and curation, the full potential of these genetic resources remains untapped (Bapela et al. 2023; Parra-Quijano et al. 2012; van Bemmelen et al. 2021; Weise et al. 2020).

Enhancing the sustainable use of historical cultivars or landraces through advanced molecular tools, better documentation, and improved knowledge-sharing is vital (Belmain et al. 2022; Jaureguiberry et al. 2022; Knapp and Sciarretta 2023; Williamson and Leonelli 2022 Kertho et al. 2015; Kankwatsa et al. 2017; Pasam et al. 2017). In this context, it is crucial to extensively investigate historical wheat cultivars and landraces—especially those from European regions—for valuable genetic traits (Adhikari et al. 2022; Gangurde et al. 2022; Mapuranga et al. 2022b; Nguyen and Norton 2020; Raggi et al. 2021). Over the past two decades, most studies aiming to identify genetic resistance to the most important foliar diseases, such as SNB, STB, LR, YR, and PM, have relied on bi-parental mapping populations (Alemu et al. 2021; Cheng et al. 2022; Czembor and Arseniuk 2019; Tong et al. 2024). QTLs identified through traditional bi-parental mapping often span large genomic regions encompassing many genes, complicating the identification of causal genes (Crossa et al. 2017; Mu et al. 2019). Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) using GWAS have accelerated this work, enabling the development of SNP markers and high-resolution QTL mapping for disease resistance traits (Abdullah et al. 2023; Adhikari et al. 2022; Ajilogba and Walker 2023; Atsbeha et al. 2023; Kilian and Graner 2012; Plant et al. 2022). The use of resistant cultivars remains the most effective strategy to control fungal diseases (Jeon et al. 2023; Mapuranga et al. 2022a, b; Wang et al. 2023).

Plant disease resistance is generally classified into two main categories: seedling resistance, also known as all-stage resistance (ASR) or as a pathogen race-specific resistance, and adult plant resistance (APR), which has been postulated as a sustainable way of prolonging the durability of disease resistance (Bolton et al. 83 2008; Sinha et al. 2022). The term “multi-stage resistance” (MSR) describes QTLs that are effective at more than one plant growth stage (Bolton et al. 2008; Sinha et al. 2022). APR differs from classic R genes, which are typically quantitative, can be polygenic, and are expressed mainly at later growth stages, rather than being conferred by single, race-specific immune receptor genes that act from the seedling stage. Unlike major genes, which often encode NLR-type receptors detectable through approaches such as MutRenSeq, APRs and are not normally restricted to the immune receptor class. Consequently, GWAS are particularly useful for identifying SNPs and QTLs underlying these non-NLR forms of resistance. (Baranwal 2022; Lagudah 2011). Patterns of host resistance differ markedly between Z. tritici or S. nodorum and other pathogens in wheat (Czembor and Czembor 2022; Downie et al. 2021; Faris et al. 2010; Friesen and Faris 2021; Gyawali et al. 2018; Phan et al. 2018; Spyroglou et al. 2022). The major ASR genes of wheat against rust pathogens (Puccinia spp.) provide near immunity at the seedling stage and continue to give high levels of protection at later plant growth stages (Chen 2005; Ellis et al. 2014; Wellings et al. 2012). SNB and STB development depends on the interaction between pathogen necrotrophic effectors (NEs) and host susceptibility genes (Downie et al. 2021; Faris et al. 2010; Friesen and Faris 2021; Gyawali et al. 2018; Phan et al. 2018; Spyroglou et al. 2022). Quantitative resistance can be combined with qualitative genes to improve resistance against SRB and SNB. However, for these diseases, the translation of seedling race-specific resistance providing very high protection against infection at the adult plant stage is rarely observed. In addition, equivalent loci to the rust APR genes have yet to be definitively identified for Septoria pathogens. Therefore, the search and combining of different ASR and APR genes has long been postulated as a sustainable way of prolonging the durability of disease resistance against Z. tritici or S. nodorum (Brown 2015).

QTLs for YR, LR, and stem rust (SR) resistance have been identified across Europe, Asia, and the Americas using GWAS and traditional mapping methods (Bariana et al. 2016; Godoy et al. 2018; Hovmøller et al. 2023; Kaur et al. 2023a; Lagudah 2011; Liu et al. 2017; Long et al. 2019; Mehrabi et al. 2022; Norman et al. 2023; Lakkakula et al. 2025; Qureshi et al. 2018; Tibbs Cortes et al. 2021; Tyrka et al. 2021; Yao et al. 2019, 2020; Zegeye et al. 2014). As an example, over 80 LR resistance genes (Lr), 67 YR (Yr1 to Yr67) R genes and 42 loci were identified (Bariana et al. 2016; Beddow et al. 2015; Cloutier et al. 2022; Esmail et al. 2021; Hovmøller et al. 2023; Li et al. 2020; McIntosh 2024; Mushtaq et al. 2024; Norman et al. 2024; Ren et al. 2023; Rodríguez-Vázquez et al. 2023; Shahinnia et al. 2023; Sotiropoulos et al. 2022; Thach et al. 2016).

In Poland, climate variability determines whether YR or LR predominates (Shahinnia et al. 2022). Across Europe and Asia, multiple loci for YR resistance have been identified, including 12 SNPs linked to APR and up to 30 QTLs detected by GWAS (with both ASR and APR effects) (Kokhmetova et al. 2023; Kumar et al. 2020; Kuraparthy et al. 2007; Norman et al. 2023; Li et al. 2021; Saleem et al. 2022; Shi et al. 2023; Wamalwa et al. 2019; Yao et al. 2019;.

Major PM resistance genes are located mainly on the “A” and “B” subgenomes, and numerous loci (e.g., 113 ATLs) have been mapped under field conditions (Kaur et al. 2023b; Mapuranga et al. 2022a; Simeone et al. 2020). For SNB and STB, 89 genomic regions have been identified (27 ASR, 48 APR), with additional QTLs reported in GWAS and global collections (e.g., CIMMYT, Vavilov, Nordic). The most durable APR QTLs occur on chromosomes such as 2B, 2D, 4 A, 4B, 5 A, 6B, 7 A, and 7B (Alemu et al. 2021; Arraiano and Brown 2017; Atsbeha et al. 2023; Brown et al. 2015; Francki et al. 2020; Goudemand et al. 2013; Gurung et al. 2014; Kidane et al. 2017; Langlands-Perry et al. 2022; Lin et al. 2022; Louriki et al. 2021; Mahboubi et al. 2022a, b; Muqaddasi et al. 2019, 2021; Riaz et al. 2020; Ruud 2019; Tabib Ghaffary et al. 2018; Vagndorf et al. 2017; Würschum et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2018, 2022; Yates et al. 2019).

The most durable APR QTLs were mapped to chromosomes 2B, 2D, 4 A, 4B, 5 A, 6B, 7 A, and 7B, with 3 A and 6 A significant at both stages. GWAS identified 24 MTAs, grouped into 20 QTLs for STB resistance. (Atsbeha et al. 2023; Gurung et al. 2014). In total, 25 genomic regions were associated with resistance to at least two rusts, including seven regions on chromosomes 1B, 6B, and 2B that conferred resistance to all three. Altogether, 16 QTLs were identified for YR, 18 for LR, and 27 for SR (Dinglasan et al. 2018, 2019; Phan et al. 2018). Some loci, notably on 2D, 3 A, and 5B (linked to Tsn1), confer dual resistance to SNB and YR (Kuraparthy et al. 2007; Liu et al. 2020). ASR and APR loci have been reported for Septoria diseases, but the well-known pleiotropic multi-pathogen APR genes such as Lr34/Yr18/Sr57/Pm38, Lr46/Yr29/Sr58/Pm39, and Lr67/Yr46/Sr55/Pm46 found on wheat chromosome 7D, which are very useful against rusts and PM, do not give consistent, reliable protection against SNB and STB. Evidence is mixed — some studies show little effect or even increased susceptibility in some backgrounds. At the same time, other papers identify distinct STB or SNB QTLs and novel broad-spectrum resistance loci specific to Septoria pathogens. Breeding for durable resistance to STB and SNB should focus on disease-specific loci (e.g., Stb or SNB QTLs) and validated Septoria-targeted APR genes, rather than the broad-spectrum rust APRs.

This study aimed to characterize a diverse collection of 411 common wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) accessions, preserved in the Polish Gene Bank, including both modern, elite cultivars and historical cultivars sourced from Poland, other European countries, North and South America, Asia, and Africa. DArTseq and GWAS analysis were conducted to: (1) identify SNP markers associated with resistance to major wheat leaf diseases, including YR, LR, PM, STB, and SNB, and (2) uncover genetic variations and alleles of potential value for deployment in future wheat breeding programs aimed at improving disease resistance.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

For genotypic studies, 411 accessions held at the Polish Gene Bank (National Centre for Plant Genetic Resources: NCPGR) were included. All of them were also phenotyped in 2019. In 2018, 399 of those accessions were evaluated. The collection was divided into twelve sub-collections, labelled Sub1 to Sub12. The number of evaluated accessions originating from Central, Northern, and Southern European countries, with the exclusion of 114 accessions from Poland, was presented in Figure S1.1 and Table S2.

The Polish sub-collection Sub1 included cultivars cultivated before 1985 (74 genotypes), classified as historical cultivars, while Sub2 included cultivars cultivated after 1985, classified as elite cultivars (39 genotypes). The remaining accessions from Central Europe include AUT (Austria), CHE (Switzerland), SVK (Slovakia), DEU (Germany), CZE (Czech Republic), and HUN (Hungary) (Sub3: 60 accessions). Sub4 (33 accessions) represents Northern European countries, including DNK (Denmark), FIN (Finland), NOR (Norway), SWE (Sweden), NLD (Netherlands), BEL (Belgium), GBR (United Kingdom), and IRL (Ireland). Southern Europe (Sub5: 34 accessions) includes ROU (Romania), GRC (Greece), YUG (Yugoslavia), HRV (Croatia), BGR (Bulgaria), ESP (Spain), FRA (France), ITA (Italy), and PRT (Portugal).

Figure S1.2 shows the number of evaluated accessions originating from North America, South America, South Asia, East Asia, the Middle East, South and East Africa, North Africa, and Australasia.

Accessions originated from North America (Sub7: 41 accessions), which include CAN (Canada), USA (United States), and MEX (Mexico); South America (Sub8: 28 accessions) includes ARG (Argentina), BRA (Brazil), CHL (Chile), COL (Colombia), ECU (Ecuador), URY (Uruguay), and PER (Peru); Asia (Sub9: 31 accessions) is divided into South Asia, which includes AFG (Afghanistan), IND (India), NPL (Nepal), and PAK (Pakistan), and East Asia, which includes CHN (China) and JPN (Japan), as well as the Middle East, which includes IRT (Iran), IRQ (Iraq), ISR (Israel), JOR (Jordan), and SYR (Syria); Africa is divided into South & East Africa (Sub6: 21 accessions), which includes KEN (Kenya), NGA (Nigeria), and ZAF (South Africa), and North Africa (Sub5: 35 accessions), which includes DZA (Algeria), EGY (Egypt), MAR (Morocco), and TUN (Tunisia); and Australasia (Sub10: 9 accessions) includes AUS (Australia) and NZL (New Zealand). Additionally, accessions were collected from the former Soviet Union (Sub11, 10 accessions), and 30 accessions with unknown country of origin (Sub12) were also evaluated.

Passport data, including Institute code (INSTCODE)/Institute name, accession number (ACCENUMB), OTHERNUMB/number in AGROBANK project within which the research was conducted, accession name (ACCENAME), acquisition date (ACQDATE), country of origin (ORIGCTY) (Table S2).

Phenotypic evaluation of accessions for GWAS analysis

Field experiments were conducted in 2018 and 2019 at the experimental fields of the Plant Breeding and Acclimatization Institute–National Research Institute (PBAI–NRI) in Radzikow, near Warsaw, Poland. No specific permissions were required, and no endangered or protected species were involved. The trials followed a randomized complete block design. In 2018, seeds were sown in three replications (blocks), and in 2019, in two replications. Each plot consisted of two rows, with a row length of 2.0 m, plant spacing of 4.0 cm, and row spacing of 20.0 cm, with 100 kg/ha of N and 75 kg/ha of P fertilization. Weeding was performed manually.

Phenotypic evaluation of the accessions was conducted under natural infection conditions, with nearly the entire list of descriptors recommended by the International Plant Genetic Resources Institute (IPGRI), which is widely accepted across most European countries and respected globally ([https://old.vurv.cz/Ewdb/asp/IPGRI_descr_1985.pdf, accessed on 29 October 2021]. Plant susceptibility to LR, YR, PM, STB, and SNB was scored on a 1–9 scale, where 1 indicates no disease symptoms (an immune reaction). In 2018, trait measurements were conducted at the early milky-waxy maturity plant growth stage according to Zadoks et al (1974) (PGS Z 7.5). In 2019, scoring was performed at two growth stages: first, at the heading PGS Z 5.5, when 50% of the plants in a plot had half of their heads emerged, and again two weeks later (PGS Z 7.5).

Weather conditions

Temperature and precipitation conditions in the fields where the experiment was conducted were recorded during the critical plant development stages from plant heading to physiological maturity in both 2018 and 2019 and reported by Czembor et al. (2022). In 2018, the period from heading to physiological maturity experienced a mean temperature of 20.4 °C, with maximum daily temperatures reaching up to 35.0°. Total precipitation during this stage was 178.4 mm. In 2019, the same developmental period was characterized by a mean temperature of 20.1 °C, a maximum temperature of 37.5 °C, and a total of 103.0 mm of precipitation in June and July.

Statistical analysis of phenotypes

A statistical analysis of the traits was performed using Statistica software (version 13.3, 1984–2017, TIBCO Software Inc.) and Microsoft Excel 2019. Each trait was described using the following parameters: average value (Avg.), minimum observed value (Min.), maximum observed value (Max.), standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV; calculated as SD/mean × 100%), and standard error (SE) to estimate the variation in the sub-collection means.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA; α ≤ 0.05) was conducted to determine significant differences among accessions for LR, YR, PM, STB, and SNB (p-values less than 0.001 may be expressed as p < 0.001, or as exponentials in studies of genetic associations). The Student’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) t-test assesses whether differences between accessions are statistically significant. For large samples (as in this study), their results are approximately those of a Z-test as the t-distribution approaches normality.

Molecular data analysis using DArTseq and GWAS

Approximately 1 g of young leaf tissue from the 3rd to 4th node of each seedling was collected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method with chloroform and isoamyl alcohol (Doyle and Doyle 1987). DNA quality was assessed via 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis and standardized to 50 ng/µL for DArTseq genotyping.

The DArTseq data were processed using the dartR v1.1.11 package in the R programming language (Gruber et al. 2018). DArTseq markers were scored with DArTsoft14 software, implemented in the KDCompute plug-in system developed by Diversity Arrays Technology (2017) (http://www.kddart.org/kdcompute.html), based on alignment with the Chinese Spring Wheat reference genome (RefSeq v1.0) [International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium (IWGSC), 2018], obtained from the IWGSC database (https://urgi.versailles.inra.fr/download/iwgsc/).

SNPs and genotypes were removed if SNP markers contained > 5% missing data and genotypes contained > 10% missing data, respectively. SNPs with a reproducibility score (RepAvg) below 100%, monomorphic SNPs, and rare SNPs (MAF < 1%) were removed. When multiple SNPs originated from the same fragment, one was retained at random. After filtering, 428 and 1,405 high-quality SNPs were retained for analysis. GWAS was performed using the GAPIT R package (v2018.08.18) with the BLINK C model (Paradis 2010; Huang et al. 2018; Alqudah et al. 2020; Wang and Zhang 2021). Only markers mapped to chromosomes with no missing data were included. Significant marker–trait associations were identified using Bonferroni and FDR (≤ 0.05) corrections at p = 0.01. Manhattan plots visualized –log10(p) values across the 21 wheat chromosomes (A, B, and D genomes), with solid lines indicating Bonferroni thresholds and dashed green lines indicating the FDR threshold (Wang et al. 2012). Copula-based Joint Analysis of Multiple Phenotypes was explained according to Konigorski et al. (2019). R2 UnAdj explains how much the SNP explains the phenotypic variance (%) in the collection.

Results

A collection of 411 spring wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) accessions, including both historical and modern elite cultivars, was tested for resistance to multiple fungal diseases—YR, LR, PM, and SNB—in adult plants under field conditions. The phenotypic data for GWAS analysis were presented in Table S1, and the summarized results of ANOVA, LSD tests assessing the variation in collection, and 12 sub-collections are presented in Table S2. Statistically significant difference in disease severity among different accessions and sub-collections indicates the ANOVA F-value is large enough to reject the null hypothesis of no difference. The F-value is a ratio of between-group variance to within-group variance, so a high value suggests that the variation in disease severity between the different groups is much greater than the variation within each group, indicating distinct genotypes. Polish sub-collections represent historical cultivars (Sub1) and elite cultivars (Sub2). Sub-collections originating from Europe (Central: Sub3, North: Sub4, South: Sub5), Africa (Sub6), North and South America (Sub7, Sub8, respectively), Asia (Sub9), Australasia (Sub10), the Soviet Union (Sub11), unknown country of origin (Sub12).

SNB and STB phenotypic data and GWAS analysis

The frequency distribution of Polish historical and elite common wheat accessions based on STB and SNB resistance, scored at the Z 7.5 PGS in 2018 and in 2019, is presented in Table S4 and Figures S3.4, S3.5. Table S4 and Figure S3.6 present the frequency distributions of wheat accessions collected across Europe in 2018 and 2019. Sub3 (Central Europe) showed significant variation in 2019 but was weaker in 2018. STB scores from 2018 to 2019 were dominated by accessions classified as moderately resistant, with scores in the 2.0–2.9 range (approximately 50–55%), and a smaller proportion rated as resistant (1.0–1.9) (Table S4, Figure S3.6). The moderately susceptible group (3.0–3.9) was relatively small. In contrast, SNB scores for 2018 showed a more balanced distribution across groups. Accessions in Sub4 were primarily scored as moderately resistant and moderately susceptible. Sub5 (South Europe) had strong significance in 2019. STB scores from both 2018 and 2019 were concentrated in the 2.0–2.9 and 3.0–3.9 ranges. However, a susceptible group was also present in 2019, with scores in the 5.0–5.9 range. Notably, for SNB in 2018, a large proportion of accessions (around 45% DS) were scored in the 4.0–4.9 range (genotypes susceptible), and a significant number also fell into the 5.0–5.9 range.

Figure S3.7 visualizes the distribution of historical spring wheat accessions by STB and SNB caused in Sub6, Sub7, Sub8, and Sub9 originating from Africa, North America, South America, and Asia, respectively. For SNB Sub6 (North Africa), and Sub7 (North America) showed some significance in 2018, while Sub8 (South America) and Sub9 (Asia) had no significant variation across years.

Discussion

The study was undertaken to characterize a collection of wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) accessions, including both historical and elite cultivars sourced from Poland, other European countries, North and South America, Asia, and Africa. Utilizing the DArTseq Genotyping-by-Sequencing (GBS) approach, the primary objectives of this study were: (1) to evaluate molecular and gene-based diversity within the wheat collection, with a particular emphasis on traits related to adaptation and disease resistance; and (2) to identify genomic regions associated with adult-plant resistance to fungal pathogens causing leaf diseases Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Pst), Puccinia triticina (Pt), Blumeria graminis f. sp. tritici (Bgt) and Zymoseptoria tritici through GWAS analysis.

This study represents the first GWAS to investigate resistance to multiple fungal diseases—YR, LR, PM, and SNB—at the APR stage under field conditions, using accessions originating from Poland and various countries, all preserved in the Polish Gene Bank. Future deployment of resistance genes in wheat breeding hinges on improved collaboration between genetic researchers and plant breeders (Hazard et al. 2018). Linked molecular markers now offer critical tools for marker-assisted selection (MAS), significantly enhancing the precision and efficiency of breeding programs. Understanding the genetic diversity, origin, and chromosomal distribution of these resistance genes is essential for developing robust cultivars with durable resistance.

Old cultivar relatives continue to serve as vital reservoirs of novel disease resistance alleles. Techniques such as speed breeding and fine-mapping strategies offer solutions to overcome these traditional bottlenecks, accelerating the transfer of resistance traits into elite cultivars. Rapid transfer of QTLs into breeder-friendly markers, such as KASP assays. Allele-Specific Polymorphism or Competitive Allele-Specific PCR), is vital for their widespread use in wheat improvement pipelines (Abdullah et al. 2023; Kaur et al. 2023a; Yadav et al. 2025). Pathogen resistance is orchestrated through complex biochemical and cellular networks. As an example, over 920 QTLs or resistance genes have now been mapped across wheat chromosomes, with at least 26 regions showing pleiotropic effects across multiple diseases (Tong et al. 2024). These clusters present strategic targets for gene pyramiding, enabling the stacking of multiple resistances into a single cultivar to enhance durability.

Resistance genes can lose their effectiveness over time due to changes in the pathogen population (Ali et al. 2014; Peters Haugrud et al. 2023). Early resistance responses, often triggered by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), are essential for mounting an effective defence during these early developmental stages. Resistance expressed during later stages, such as the milky-waxy grain filling (PGS Z 7.5) is also critical and may involve resistance proteins with greater durability (Bai et al. 2022; Dinglasan et al. 2022; Lamichhane et al. 2018; Ren et al. 2023; Zelba et al. 2024). QTLs identified for APR may not necessarily correlate with ASR, and only a few resistance loci have shown effects in field studies (Czembor et al. 2025; Francki et al. 2020; Zatybekov et al. 2022). Numerous studies across Europe, Asia, and the Americas have identified QTLs for YR, LR, and SR resistance using GWAS and traditional mapping methods (e.g. Bariana et al. 2016; Delfan et al. 2023; Genievskaya et al. 2022; Godoy et al. 2018; Kaur et al. 2023a; Kumar et al. 2020; Kuraparthy et al. 2007; Lagudah 2011; Lakkakula et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2017; Long et al. 2019; Mehrabi et al. 2022; Norman et al. 2023; Qureshi et al. 2018; Riaz et al. 2018; Zegeye et al. 2014). In our results, GWAS identified 15 SNPs at Z 7.5 PGS, one each on chromosomes 2B, 4B, 6 A, and 7D, and two SNPs each on chromosomes 1D, 3B, 5D, 6D, and 7 A. Some novel loci were indicate that contribute to Septoria and LR variation scored at 7.5 PGS 2018, with major loci on 3B (YR, 512 Mb, h² = 2.03), 5D (SNB, 440 Mb, h² = 1.68), 6 A (YR, 182 Mb, h² = 1.14), and LR scored at 5.5 PGS 2019 in 7D (610 Mb, h² = 2.2), as well as moderate loci in 1D at 7.5 PGS 2018 (SNB, 374 Mb, h² = 0.57) and 4D (YR, 40 Mb, h² = 0.54) represent regions not previously associated with STB/SNB or LR resistance.

Chromosome 2B contains genetic regions (e.g., SNPs like 5331638) strongly associated with resistance to SNB resistance. This suggests that one region of the genome might help to protect wheat against multiple fungal diseases.

Some SNPs located on chromosome 3B (1030977, 1087976, 1076333, 1091132, and 4990122) consistently showed association with YR resistance and the repeatability across seasons and trials, suggesting they are stable markers likely flanking a pleiotropic QTL influencing multiple disease resistance traits. These SNPs likely flank or sit within a pleiotropic QTL — a region of DNA that affects multiple disease resistance traits, as two SNB-associated SNPs overlapped with this region.

Chromosome 6 A has a shared resistance locus across YR and SNB. SNPs: 3,937,236, 1,056,817 are consistent across both years for YR and associated with SNB. Another SNP on chromosome 6 A mapped to the same genomic region, suggesting a shared locus or tightly linked genes affecting both YR and SNB. The 6 A region may harbour a pleiotropic QTL — one locus affecting multiple diseases or a cluster of tightly linked genes, each resisting a different pathogen. Targeting this region in breeding programs may yield wheat cultivars with broader, more durable resistance profiles.

We confirmed that chromosome 7D is a strong dual-resistance region, containing LR resistance SNPs (3950426 and 5970324). These two SNPs are located only 4,167 base pairs apart, indicating strong LD, likely tagging the same resistance locus or regulatory region. The LR locus scored in 2019, located at 610.9 Mb displayed a particularly high heritability (h² = 2.2), and together these SNPs explained 4.4% of the phenotypic variance, making this a high-confidence QTL region. This locus is well beyond the positions of known LR resistance genes such as Lr19 (~ 35 Mb), Lr29 (~ 45 Mb), Lr34/Pm38 (~ 50 Mb), and Lr68 (~ 120 Mb) (McIntosh et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2021), suggesting a novel LR resistance QTL.

Although multiple disease resistances exist on 7D, the novel 610 Mb LR locus appears independent of known resistance genes, making it a strong candidate for functional validation, including annotation of SNB-LR or receptor-like kinase genes. Another SNP (3949012) on 7D was significantly associated with YR resistance, while SNP 3,941,811 was associated with SNB resistance, suggesting additional linked resistance regions on this chromosome. In contrast, the SNB locus at ~ 182 Mb (7.5 PGS 2018) and YR loci at ~ 42 Mb and ~ 545 Mb are positioned nearer to previously characterized resistance genes, possibly representing minor or pleiotropic QTLs conferring partial resistance. Notably, 7D harbors broad-spectrum resistance genes such as Lr34/Pm38/Yr18, which provide adult resistance to multiple fungal pathogens. However, studies (e.g., Alemu et al. 2021; Tomkowiak et al. 2019–2021; Kokhmetova et al. 2023; Gupta et al 2023; Raman 2012) have shown that classical APR genes (Lr34, Lr46, Lr67) display variable and background-dependent effects against Zymoseptoria tritici and other hemibiotrophic pathogens.

For instance, Bansal (2014) demonstrated that in several near-isogenic lines (NILs), Lr34 and Lr46 increased susceptibility to STB in certain genetic backgrounds, highlighting that these APR genes do not consistently confer protection against necrotrophic or hemibiotrophic pathogens. This observation aligns with the distinct infection biology of Septoria species and supports the notion that disease-specific QTLs and Stb genes are more reliable for SNB/STB resistance breeding.

In contrast, the novel loci identified in the current study are independent of these classic APR genes and appear to contribute directly to STB/SNB resistance in the evaluated germplasm, highlighting new targets for breeding programs. One additional SNP on 7D was identified at Z 7.5 PGS as significantly associated with SNB resistance. Although this SNB-associated SNP is not specified by ID, the overlapping chromosome and timing suggest a nearby or linked region to the LR SNPs. This co-localization makes it a prime candidate for pleiotropic or tightly linked resistance loci. Dual-trait QTLs like this are valuable for breeders, as they reduce the need to introgress multiple regions, enabling stacking resistance to both biotrophic (e.g. rust) and necrotrophic (e.g. SNB) pathogens.

Conclusion

Gene banks hold extensive collections of historical wheat cultivars. For these genetic resources to be effectively utilized by breeders, they must be thoroughly characterized using both phenotypic and molecular methods. Particularly valuable are genotypes that carry resistance genes effective against multiple pathogens simultaneously. DArTseq is an efficient molecular characterization that enables the identification of resistance genes responsible for combating various wheat diseases. The novel loci identified in this study are independent of known APR genes and contribute directly to SNB and LR resistance, providing new targets for wheat breeding programs aimed at durable multi-disease resistance. Future work integrating fine-mapping, comparative genomics, and gene annotation will be essential to validate these novel loci and elucidate their potential contribution to durable, broad-spectrum disease resistance in wheat.
