Budget-Constrained Causal Bandits: Bridging Uplift Modeling and Sequential Decision-Making
Abhirami Pillai

Abstract
Treatment allocation under budget constraints is a central challenge in digital advertising: advertisers must decide which users to show ads to while spending a limited budget wisely. The standard approach follows a two-stage offline pipeline - first collect historical data to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects (HTE), then solve a constrained optimization to allocate the budget. This works well with abundant data, but fails in cold-start settings such as new campaigns, new markets, or new customer segments where little historical data exists. We propose Budget-Constrained Causal Bandits (BCCB), an online framework that learns which users respond to ads while simultaneously spending the budget, making treatment decisions one user at a time. BCCB unifies three components into a single sequential process: learning individual-level ad effectiveness, exploring users whose response is uncertain, and pacing the budget over time. We evaluated on the Criteo Uplift dataset, a large-scale advertising dataset from a real randomized controlled trial. Our key finding is a data-efficiency crossover: offline methods require approximately 10,000 historical observations to produce reliable results, while BCCB operates effectively from the very first user. Furthermore, BCCB exhibits 3-5x lower performance variance between runs, making it more practical for real campaign planning. Among purely online methods, BCCB consistently outperforms standard Thompson Sampling, budgeted Thompson Sampling, and greedy HTE estimation across all budget levels tested.



1
Introduction

Digital advertising is a multi-billion dollar industry where advertisers must allocate limited budgets across large user populations. The fundamental challenge is that not all users respond equally to advertising - some users are strongly influenced by ads, others would have converted regardless, and some are entirely unaffected. This variation in individual-level response, known as heterogeneous treatment effects (HTE)
[
4
,
27
]
, means that treating all users equally wastes budget on users who would not benefit from the ad.

A substantial body of work addresses this problem through uplift modeling: estimating the causal effect of showing an ad to each individual user, then targeting those with the highest predicted response
[
12
,
8
]
. When combined with budget constraints, this becomes a resource allocation problem:select the set of users to treat that maximizes total conversions without exceeding the budget. The dominant approach follows a two-stage pipeline: first train an uplift model on historical randomized trial data, then solve a constrained optimization typically formulated as a Multi-Choice Knapsack Problem - to determine which users to target
[
33
,
3
,
34
]
.

However, this two-stage pipeline suffers from well-documented limitations. The prediction and optimization stages are trained with misaligned objectives: the machine learning model minimizes statistical error globally, while the optimizer requires accuracy specifically at the budget-induced decision boundary. Errors from the prediction stage cascade into the optimization stage and are amplified by the deterministic solver. Furthermore, unconstrained predictive models often produce non-monotonic uplift curves that violate basic economic assumptions about consumer behavior.

Recent work has made significant progress in addressing these limitations through end-to-end Decision-Focused Learning (DFL), which unifies prediction and optimization into a single differentiable architecture. The End-to-End Cost-Effective Incentive Recommendation model (E3IR) integrates Integer Linear Programming directly into the neural network as a differentiable layer, enforcing monotonicity and smoothness through Lipschitz regularization
[
9
]
. Bi-Level Decision-Focused Causal Learning (Bi-DFCL) resolves the counterfactual problem by using scarce randomized trial data to correct biased observational models through implicit differentiation
[
24
]
. Direct Heterogeneous Causal Learning (DHCL) prevents error cascading by training models to directly predict a composite decision factor rather than raw uplift estimates
[
2
]
. Large-Scale Budget-Constrained Causal Forests (LBCF) integrate budget constraints directly into tree-splitting criteria for distributed computing environments
[
32
]
.

Despite this progress, all existing approaches, whether two-stage or end-to-end, share a fundamental assumption: the availability of sufficient historical data for offline model training. This assumption frequently breaks down in practice. New advertising campaigns have no historical data. Expansion into new markets or customer segments means historical data may not be representative. Seasonal shifts, product launches, and changing user behavior can render historical models outdated. In all these scenarios, the offline paradigm - regardless of its architectural sophistication - either fails entirely or produces unreliable allocations.

An alternative paradigm exists in the multi-armed bandit literature, where decisions are made sequentially and the algorithm learns from each observation in real time
[
26
,
22
]
. Budgeted bandits extend this framework to settings where each decision has a cost and total spending is constrained
[
30
,
29
]
. However, existing budgeted bandit methods do not explicitly model heterogeneous treatment effects - they treat the problem as choosing between generic arms rather than estimating individual-level causal effects that vary with user characteristics.

In this paper, we bridge these paradigms. We propose Budget-Constrained Causal Bandits (BCCB), an online framework that combines HTE learning with Thompson Sampling exploration and adaptive budget pacing. Unlike all offline methods -whether two-stage or end-to-end - BCCB does not require pre-collected historical data. It learns and allocates simultaneously, making treatment decisions one user at a time. Unlike standard bandit methods, BCCB explicitly models heterogeneous treatment effects and incorporates per-user cost variation into its decision rule.

Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We propose BCCB, a unified online framework for budget-constrained treatment allocation that combines HTE estimation, exploration via Thompson Sampling, and adaptive budget pacing into a single sequential decision process.

2.

We provide an empirical characterization of the data-efficiency tradeoff between offline and online approaches using the Criteo Uplift dataset
[
8
]
. We identify a crossover point: offline methods require approximately 10,000 historical observations to produce reliable results, while BCCB operates effectively from the first observation.

3.

We demonstrate that BCCB exhibits 3–5
\times
lower performance variance than offline methods across repeated runs, offering more predictable outcomes for campaign planning.

4.

Among purely online methods, we show that BCCB consistently outperforms standard Thompson Sampling, budgeted Thompson Sampling, and greedy HTE estimation across all budget levels, with the largest gains at high budgets.

2
Related Work

Our work draws on three areas of research: uplift modeling and budget-constrained treatment allocation, bandit algorithms under resource constraints, and the emerging study of offline-to-online transfer in sequential decision-making. We review each in turn and position our contribution relative to the existing literature.

2.1
Uplift Modeling and Budget-Constrained Treatment Allocation

Uplift modeling estimates the causal effect of a treatment on an individual’s outcome, distinguishing users who are genuinely influenced by an intervention from those who would behave the same regardless
[
12
,
7
]
. The foundational framework relies on the Neyman-Rubin potential
outcomes model, where the individual treatment effect is defined as the difference between treated and untreated potential outcomes
[
21
]
. Because both outcomes cannot be observed for the same individual, a range of estimation strategies have been developed, including meta-learners such as the T-learner, S-learner, and X-learner
[
15
]
, as well as tree-based methods such as Causal Forests
[
4
,
27
]
.

When treatment allocation is subject to a budget constraint, the problem is typically formulated as a variant of the Multi-Choice Knapsack Problem (MCKP), where the goal is to select a subset of users to treat that maximizes total uplift without exceeding the available budget
[
33
]
. This gives rise to a two-stage pipeline: first estimate individual-level treatment effects
using a causal inference model, then solve the constrained allocation problem using operations research techniques such as Lagrangian relaxation
[
3
,
34
]
. The dominant industrial framework for this two-stage approach is exemplified by
11
, whose offline pipeline serves as the baseline our online bandit directly complements.

Several recent works have identified fundamental limitations of this decoupled approach. The prediction and optimization stages optimize different objectives, leading to misalignment at the decision boundary
[
10
]
. Errors from the prediction stage are amplified rather than absorbed by the deterministic optimizer. To address these issues, end-to-end Decision-Focused Learning (DFL) frameworks have been proposed. The End-to-End Cost-Effective Incentive Recommendation model (E3IR) integrates Integer Linear Programming as a differentiable layer within the neural network, enforcing monotonicity through Lipschitz regularization
[
9
]
. Bi-Level Decision-Focused Causal Learning (Bi-DFCL) uses implicit differentiation to combine scarce randomized trial data with large-scale observational data, achieving an automatic bias-variance tradeoff
[
24
]
. Direct Heterogeneous Causal Learning (DHCL) reduces error cascading by training the model to predict a composite decision factor directly
[
2
]
. Large-Scale Budget-Constrained Causal Forests (LBCF) embed budget awareness into the tree-splitting criteria of causal forests for distributed computing
environments
[
32
]
. Recent benchmarking efforts have systematically compared 13 deep uplift models across multiple datasets, finding that newer methods offer more limited improvement over traditional approaches than previously believed
[
19
]
.

Despite the architectural diversity of these methods, they all require sufficient historical data for offline model training. Sawant et al.
[
23
]
first proposed combining Thompson Sampling with CATE estimation for marketing but without a formal budget constraint. The most closely related concurrent work is DISCO
[
31
]
, which applies Thompson Sampling with an integer program for personalized discount allocation, though it does not estimate CATE in the formal HTE sense and does not study the offline-vs-online tradeoff. Berrevoets et al.
[
6
]
bridge CATE and contextual bandits explicitly, noting budget constraints as future work; a gap our paper directly addresses. Our work addresses the
complementary setting where sufficient historical data is unavailable.

2.2
Bandit Algorithms Under Budget Constraints

The multi-armed bandit framework provides a principled approach to sequential decision-making under uncertainty, balancing exploration of uncertain options with exploitation of known good options
[
26
,
16
]
. Thompson Sampling, which selects actions by sampling from a posterior distribution over reward parameters, has emerged as a practical and
theoretically grounded exploration strategy
[
22
]
.

Budgeted variants of the bandit problem introduce a resource constraint: each arm pull incurs a cost, and the total expenditure must not exceed a fixed budget.
30
study Thompson Sampling under knapsack-style budget constraints.
20
extend budgeted bandits to settings with stochastic costs. More recently,
29
improve budgeted Thompson Sampling through information relaxation techniques that incorporate remaining budget information into the sampling procedure. Contextual bandits generalize the framework by conditioning decisions on observed features
[
17
,
1
]
, enabling personalization. Hsieh et al.
[
14
]
use the term “uplift” in a structural decomposition sense distinct from the
Neyman–Rubin CATE framework we adopt here.

However, existing budgeted bandit methods model the problem as a choice among generic arms. They do not explicitly estimate heterogeneous treatment effects - that is, they do not learn how the
causal
impact of treatment varies across individuals as a function of their characteristics. Our work combines the sequential, budget-aware decision-making of budgeted bandits with the individual-level causal effect estimation of uplift modeling.

2.3
Offline-to-Online Transfer and Cold-Start Learning

A growing body of work studies how offline data can accelerate online learning.

18
and
25
propose frameworks that extract information
from logged data to warm-start bandit algorithms, mitigating the cold-start
problem.
13
study latent bandits where offline datasets with
unobserved heterogeneity are used to learn complex models for each latent
state, enabling faster online adaptation. In the advertising domain
specifically,
5
address the cold-start problem for new
ad products using a UCB-based algorithm tailored to pay-per-click auction
systems. The DARA framework uses reinforcement-learning-finetuned large
language models for few-shot budget allocation in data-scarce advertising
scenarios
[
28
]
.

These works demonstrate the value of bridging offline and online paradigms,
but none empirically characterize the conditions under which online learning
outperforms offline methods for treatment allocation. Our primary empirical
contribution is precisely this characterization: we identify the amount of
historical data at which offline methods become viable, and show that below
this threshold, online approaches that learn and allocate simultaneously offer
superior and more stable performance.

3
Problem Formulation

We consider a sequential treatment allocation setting motivated by online advertising. Users arrive one at a time over a horizon of
T
rounds. At each round
t
, the decision-maker observes a user with feature vector
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an associated cost
c_{t}>0
representing the price of serving an ad to that user. The decision-maker must then choose an action
a_{t}\in\{0,1\}
, where
a_{t}=1
denotes showing the ad (treatment) and
a_{t}=0
denotes not showing the ad (control).

Following the Neyman-Rubin potential outcomes framework
[
21
]
, each user has two potential outcomes:
Y_{t}(1)
if treated and
Y_{t}(0)
if not treated. Only the outcome corresponding to the chosen action is observed. The individual treatment effect for user
t
is defined as:

\tau(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}(1)-Y_{t}(0)\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}]

(1)

which represents the causal effect of showing the ad to a user with features
\mathbf{x}_{t}
. This quantity varies across users - some users have large positive
\tau(\mathbf{x}_{t})
(responsive to ads), some have
\tau(\mathbf{x}_{t})\approx 0
(unaffected), and some may have negative
\tau(\mathbf{x}_{t})
(ad-fatigued).

The decision-maker operates under a total budget constraint
B>0
. Each treatment incurs cost
c_{t}
, and the cumulative spending must not exceed the budget:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}a_{t}\cdot c_{t}\leq B

(2)

The objective is to maximize the total number of conversions from treated users over the horizon:

\max_{\{a_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}a_{t}\cdot Y_{t}(1)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_{t=1}^{T}a_{t}\cdot c_{t}\leq B

(3)

This problem is challenging for three reasons that distinguish it from standard settings in the literature:

Unknown treatment effects.
Unlike offline uplift modeling, the decision-maker does not have access to a pre-trained model of
\tau(\mathbf{x}_{t})
. Treatment effects must be learned online from sequentially observed outcomes, creating an exploration-exploitation tradeoff: the decision-maker must occasionally treat users with uncertain effects to improve its estimates, even though this exploration consumes budget.

Heterogeneous costs.
Unlike standard multi-armed bandits, each treatment decision has a different cost
c_{t}
that depends on the user. A user with a high treatment effect but also a high cost may be less valuable than a user with a moderate treatment effect and a low cost. The decision-maker must therefore reason about cost-effectiveness, not just treatment effectiveness.

Budget depletion.
Unlike unconstrained bandit problems, the decision-maker has a finite and non-replenishable budget. Spending too aggressively early in the horizon leaves no budget for potentially high-value users arriving later. Spending too conservatively wastes budget that could have generated conversions. The algorithm must pace its spending across the horizon.

An oracle with perfect knowledge of
\tau(\mathbf{x}_{t})
for all users and full foresight of future arrivals could solve this as an offline optimization problem. In the online setting, however, the decision-maker must simultaneously learn treatment effects, manage uncertainty through exploration, and pace a limited budget - three objectives that interact in non-trivial ways. Our proposed method, Budget-Constrained Causal Bandits (BCCB), addresses all three within a unified framework, as described in the following section.

4
Method

We present Budget-Constrained Causal Bandits (BCCB), an online framework that unifies three components - heterogeneous treatment effect estimation, Thompson Sampling exploration, and adaptive budget pacing - into a single sequential decision process. We describe each component and then present the combined decision rule.

4.1
Online HTE Estimation

BCCB estimates individual-level treatment effects online using a two-model approach. We maintain two separate classifiers: one that estimates
\mathbb{P}(Y=1\mid\mathbf{x},T=1)
, the probability of conversion for treated users, and another that estimates
\mathbb{P}(Y=1\mid\mathbf{x},T=0)
, the probability of conversion for control users. Both models are trained incrementally using stochastic gradient descent with a logistic loss, updating their parameters after each observation via
partial_fit
.

The predicted treatment effect for a user with features
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is:

\hat{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\hat{\mathbb{P}}(Y=1\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},T=1)-\hat{\mathbb{P}}(Y=1\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},T=0)

(4)

Before sufficient observations have been collected (fewer than 50 treated and 50 control observations), we use an optimistic prior of
\hat{\tau}=0.002
, reflecting a small positive expected treatment effect. This encourages early exploration by defaulting to treatment when the model has not yet learned to distinguish responsive from non-responsive users.

4.2
Thompson Sampling Exploration

To balance exploration and exploitation, BCCB maintains a Beta posterior distribution over the global treatment success rate. Let
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
denote the parameters of the Beta distribution for the treatment arm, updated after each treated observation:

\alpha_{t+1}=\alpha_{t}+Y_{t}\cdot\mathbb{1}[a_{t}=1],\quad\beta_{t+1}=\beta_{t}+(1-Y_{t})\cdot\mathbb{1}[a_{t}=1]

(5)

with
\alpha_{0}=\beta_{0}=1
as a uniform prior. An analogous posterior is maintained for the control arm.

At each round, we draw samples
\theta_{t}^{(1)}\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})
and
\theta_{c}^{(1)}\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha_{t}^{(c)},\beta_{t}^{(c)})
and compute an exploration bonus:

\text{exploration\_bonus}_{t}=(\theta_{t}^{(1)}-\theta_{c}^{(1)})\cdot\eta

(6)

where
\eta>0
is an exploration weight hyperparameter. This bonus is positive when the Thompson sample favors treatment, encouraging the algorithm to explore uncertain users even when the HTE estimate is ambiguous.

4.3
Adaptive Budget Pacing

To prevent premature budget exhaustion, BCCB employs an adaptive pacing mechanism. At each round
t
, we compute a budget pressure ratio:

\text{pace}_{t}=\frac{B_{t}/B_{0}}{(T-t)/T}

(7)

where
B_{t}
is the remaining budget at round
t
,
B_{0}
is the initial budget, and
T
is the total horizon. When
\text{pace}_{t}>1
, the remaining budget is proportionally larger than the remaining horizon, and the algorithm spends more freely. When
\text{pace}_{t}<1
, the budget is being consumed too quickly, and the algorithm becomes more selective.

The pacing ratio directly modulates the treatment threshold: a lower pace raises the bar for treatment, ensuring that only high-value, cost-effective users are treated when the budget is scarce.

4.4
Combined Decision Rule

At each round
t
, BCCB combines all three components into a single decision rule. Given a user with features
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and cost
c_{t}
, the algorithm first checks the hard budget constraint: if
c_{t}>B_{t}
, the user is not treated. Otherwise, it computes:

\text{score}_{t}=\hat{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\text{exploration\_bonus}_{t}

(8)

\text{value\_per\_dollar}_{t}=\frac{\text{score}_{t}}{c_{t}}

(9)

\text{threshold}_{t}=\frac{\lambda}{\max(\text{pace}_{t},\epsilon)}

(10)

where
\lambda
is a base threshold hyperparameter and
\epsilon
is a small constant to prevent division by zero. The treatment decision is:

a_{t}=\mathbb{1}\left[\text{value\_per\_dollar}_{t}>\text{threshold}_{t}\right]

(11)

The decision rule can be interpreted as follows: a user is treated when the expected causal benefit per unit cost, augmented by an exploration bonus, exceeds a budget-aware threshold. When the budget is plentiful (
\text{pace}_{t}
is large), the threshold is low, and more users are treated. When the budget is scarce (
\text{pace}_{t}
is small), the threshold rises, and only users with high predicted treatment effects and low costs are treated. The exploration bonus ensures that uncertain users are occasionally treated even under tight budgets, preventing the algorithm from converging prematurely to a suboptimal policy.

4.5
Baseline Methods

We compare BCCB against four baselines, each of which lacks one or more of the three components:

Standard Thompson Sampling.
Maintains Beta posteriors over treatment and control conversion rates. Treats when the treatment sample exceeds the control sample. Does not model heterogeneous treatment effects, does not consider costs, and does not manage budget. This baseline isolates the value of personalization and budget awareness.

Budgeted Thompson Sampling.
Extends Standard Thompson Sampling with a hard budget constraint and a pacing mechanism that raises the treatment threshold when spending is too aggressive. Does not model heterogeneous treatment effects. This baseline isolates the value of HTE learning.

HTE Greedy.
Learns heterogeneous treatment effects online using the same two-model approach as BCCB, but always treats when the predicted effect is positive. No exploration via Thompson Sampling and no budget pacing. This baseline isolates the value of exploration and budget management.

Offline Uplift.
Trains a logistic regression uplift model on a separate historical dataset before deployment, then applies it greedily with a cost-effectiveness threshold. Does not learn online or explore. This baseline represents the standard two-stage offline pipeline
[
33
,
9
]
and enables our central comparison of online versus offline paradigms.

5
Experimental Setup

5.1
Dataset

We evaluate on the Criteo Uplift Prediction dataset
[
8
]
, a large-scale benchmark derived from a real randomized controlled trial in online advertising. The dataset contains approximately 14 million observations, each representing a user who was randomly assigned to either a treatment group (shown an advertisement) or a control group (not shown an advertisement). Each user is described by 12 anonymized features (
f_{0}
through
f_{11}
) and two binary outcome variables indicating whether the user visited or converted. We use the 10% subsample provided by the
scikit-uplift
library, yielding 1,397,960 observations.

The dataset exhibits an 85/15 treatment-control split, with a treatment group conversion rate of 0.31% and a control group conversion rate of 0.19%, yielding an average treatment effect (ATE) of 0.12%. While this average effect is small, we observe substantial heterogeneity across user segments: splitting users by feature medians reveals ATE ratios ranging from 12
\times
(feature
f_{0}
) to 65
\times
(feature
f_{8}
) between high and low subgroups. This heterogeneity is precisely what motivates personalized treatment allocation.

5.2
Cost Simulation

The Criteo dataset does not include per-user serving costs. Following standard practice in the budget-constrained allocation literature, we generate synthetic costs using a log-normal distribution:
c_{i}\sim\text{LogNormal}(\mu=-0.5,\sigma=0.7)
, clipped to the range
[\$0.05,\$5.00]
. This produces a right-skewed cost distribution with a mean of $0.77, reflecting realistic ad auction dynamics where most impressions are inexpensive but a subset of high-value users command significantly higher prices. Costs are generated with a fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility across all experiments.

5.3
Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate all methods using the
replay method
for offline bandit evaluation. Users are streamed sequentially in a randomized order. When a bandit algorithm’s treatment decision matches the user’s actual treatment assignment in the original randomized trial, we observe the user’s real outcome and update the algorithm. When the decision does not match, the user is skipped. This approach provides unbiased evaluation using only observational data from the randomized trial, without requiring counterfactual outcome imputation.

For the online methods comparison, we use a subsample of 100,000 users with budgets ranging from $1,000 to $8,000. For the scalability analysis, we test at 100,000, 500,000, and 1,397,960 users with a fixed budget of $5,000. For the data-efficiency crossover experiment, we vary the offline training set size from 1,000 to 50,000 observations and evaluate on a held-out set of 100,000 users. All experiments are repeated with 3 random seeds (42, 123, 456) and we report means and standard deviations.

We measure four metrics: total conversions from treated users (primary), total cost incurred, treatment rate (fraction of users treated), and cost per conversion.

7
Discussion and Limitations

Our results demonstrate that BCCB provides a practical solution for budget-constrained treatment allocation in data-scarce settings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged.

Offline methods dominate with sufficient data.
When 50,000 or more historical observations are available, offline uplift methods significantly outperform BCCB. Practitioners with access to rich historical data from the same population should prefer offline approaches. BCCB is most valuable in cold-start scenarios where such data does not exist.

Synthetic costs.
The Criteo Uplift dataset does not include per-user serving costs. We generate synthetic costs using a log-normal distribution calibrated to realistic ad auction price ranges. While this is standard practice, real auction costs may exhibit more complex structure. Validation on datasets with real cost information would strengthen our conclusions.

Linear HTE models.
BCCB currently uses linear classifiers for online HTE estimation. More expressive models such as online random forests or neural networks with incremental updates could improve treatment effect predictions. We leave the integration of more sophisticated online learners as future work.

8
Conclusion

We proposed Budget-Constrained Causal Bandits (BCCB), an online framework for treatment allocation that unifies heterogeneous treatment effect learning, Thompson Sampling exploration, and adaptive budget pacing into a single sequential decision process. Unlike existing offline approaches whether two-stage pipelines or end-to-end decision-focused architectures BCCB does not require historical data and operates effectively from the first user.

Our central empirical finding is a data-efficiency crossover between offline and online paradigms. Offline uplift methods require approximately 10,000 historical observations to produce reliable results and exhibit 3–5
\times
higher performance variance than BCCB. Below this threshold, offline methods either fail entirely or produce highly unreliable allocations. BCCB provides stable, predictable performance regardless of historical data availability, making it particularly suitable for new campaigns, new markets, and other cold-start scenarios common in digital advertising.

Several directions for future work emerge from this study. First, integrating more expressive online HTE models could improve treatment effect predictions. Second, a hybrid approach that warm-starts BCCB with limited offline data could combine the strengths of both paradigms. Third, theoretical regret bounds for the budget-constrained HTE learning setting would complement our empirical findings. Finally, deployment in a live advertising system would provide the most compelling validation of BCCB’s practical value.
