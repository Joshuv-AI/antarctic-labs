Rivaroxaban for the prevention of atherothrombotic events in advanced CKD: are we off TRACK?
Authors: Bjorn K I Meijers, Thomas A Mavrakanas
Published: 2026

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), including apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, and dabigatran, have revolutionized the prevention and treatment of thrombo-embolic disease. Strong evidence supports their use in (non)valvular atrial, venous thromboembolism, cancer-associated thrombosis, and postoperative thromboprophylaxis [1].

In addition to the standard therapeutic and prophylactic anticoagulant regimens used for the treatment and prevention of thromboembolic disease, a third dosing strategy has emerged for rivaroxaban from three clinical trials in patients at high risk for cardiovascular events. This approach, established in the ATLAS ACS-2 TIMI 51, COMPASS, and VOYAGER trials, demonstrated that rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily (BID) plus aspirin significantly reduced the risk of major adverse cardiovascular and/or limb events in patients with coronary artery disease or peripheral artery disease compared with aspirin monotherapy [2–4].

Patients with kidney failure are at very high risk of cardiovascular events, but they were excluded from these three landmark clinical trials. While in COMPASS and VOYAGER a subgroup analysis suggested benefit from rivaroxaban in patients with a low estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), the median eGFR at baseline was >60 ml/min/1.73 m2 and individuals with stage 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD) were excluded from both trials. This leaves uncertainty as to whether low-dose rivaroxaban is beneficial in high-risk patients with kidney failure [4, 5].

The TRACK study (Treatment of cardiovascular disease with low dose Rivaroxaban in Advanced Chronic Kidney disease) was designed to examine this evidence gap [6]. This multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial randomized adults with stage 4 or 5 CKD, corresponding to an eGFR of <29 ml/min/1.73 m2 or treated with maintenance hemodialysis, and considered to have elevated cardiovascular risk, to receive rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily (BID) or matching placebo. The primary endpoint was a composite of nonfatal myocardial infarction, stroke, a peripheral vascular event, or cardiovascular death, whichever occurred first. The primary safety outcome was major bleeding. A priori power calculations indicated that 1900 participants and 515 events were needed over 3 years to detect a hazard ratio of 0.75 at 90% power. TRACK was terminated preterm due to futility, after randomization of 1458 patients. The primary endpoint occurred in 164 (22.6%) patients in the low-dose rivaroxaban group and 151 (20.7%) in the placebo group (P = .46). Moreover, low-dose rivaroxaban significantly increased the risk for major bleeding with a hazard ratio of 1.51 [confidence interval (CI) 1.02–2.22; P = .04].

This could lead us to conclude that, while low-dose rivaroxaban reduces cardiovascular risk in individuals at high risk for atherothrombotic events and relatively preserved kidney function, no such benefit exists in individuals affected by advanced CKD or kidney failure. But does TRACK answer this hypothesis? Was low-dose rivaroxaban studied in patients with kidney failure at high risk of thrombotic events?

First, it is worthwhile to compare inclusion criteria between the three landmark clinical trials with low-dose rivaroxaban and TRACK [2–4, 6]. In COMPASS, patients were eligible to be included if they met the criteria for coronary artery disease (90.6%), peripheral arterial disease (27.2%), or both. Patients with coronary artery disease who were younger than 65 years of age were also required to have documentation of atherosclerosis involving at least two vascular beds or to have at least two additional risk factors (current smoking, diabetes mellitus, an eGFR <60 ml per minute, heart failure, or nonlacunar ischemic stroke ≥1 month earlier). In ATLAS ACS-2 TIMI 51 and VOYAGER, study participants were recruited after a recent acute coronary syndrome or after revascularization for peripheral artery disease. In short, they were all individuals with proven cardiovascular disease receiving study treatment in secondary prevention.

In contrast, TRACK included patients with advanced CKD or kidney failure (eGFR at or below 29 ml/min/1.73 m2) at increased risk of cardiovascular disease, with at least one risk factor, including age 65 or above, diabetes mellitus requiring pharmacotherapy, past history of ischemic stroke (6.5%), peripheral artery disease (6.6%), or coronary artery disease (17.7%). These numbers alone indicate that the patient population included in TRACK, with ∼70% of patients treated in primary prevention, is not comparable to those included in the other three clinical trials, all receiving rivaroxaban for secondary prevention (30.8% vs. 100%).

Even with careful patient selection, the observed benefit was relatively modest in the three landmark trials with low-dose rivaroxaban, with numbers needed to treat ranging from 38 to 77. Unfortunately, a subgroup analysis according to the presence or absence of established cardiovascular disease at baseline was not reported. Therefore, inclusion of patients without established coronary or peripheral vascular disease might have biased the results of the TRACK trial toward the null.

Second, the primary composite endpoint in TRACK is driven to a large extent by sudden cardiac death and did not differ between rivaroxaban or placebo. Rivaroxaban exerts its antithrombotic effect by selectively inhibiting Factor Xa, which directly attenuates the propagation phase of the coagulation cascade and suppresses the generation of thrombin. In the general population, cardiovascular events are driven primarily through lipid-driven, intimal plaque buildup and subsequent rupture, driving thrombosis. Kidney failure, however, accelerates atypical cardiovascular disease via mineral and bone disorder, uremic toxicity, malnutrition and inflammation, electrolyte disturbances, and hypervolemia. TRACK clearly shows that the mode of action of rivaroxaban does not significantly alter the risk of kidney failure associated sudden death. The number of actual atherothrombotic events (stroke, myocardial infarction) is too low to draw definite conclusions on whether rivaroxaban has a beneficial effect. A relevant signal was seen, however, in peripheral arterial disease events. Occurrence was numerically lower in those receiving rivaroxaban compared with placebo, with a hazard ratio of 0.69. Also here, the number of events was too low to draw any definite conclusions, with wide CIs (0.36−1.34; P = .6).

Third, the risk of major bleeding was significantly increased in the low-dose rivaroxaban group. The finding that rivaroxaban increases the bleeding risk is not a new finding. In the ATLAS ACS-2 TIMI-51 study, studying low-dose rivaroxaban after an acute coronary syndrome, the risk of minor (P = .02) and fatal bleeding events (P = .04) was significantly higher with rivaroxaban 5 mg vs. rivaroxaban 2.5 mg BID, without clear benefit of the higher dosing regimen [7]. While 2.5 mg is the lowest dose studied in patients with normal kidney function, the question is whether this should be truly considered very low dose in advanced CKD. Single-dose pharmacokinetic (PK) studies of higher dose rivaroxaban demonstrated that a 10-mg dose of rivaroxaban in hemodialysis patients without residual kidney function results in drug exposure comparable to 20 mg in healthy volunteers [8]. Additional studies found that changes in PK in chronic dialysis patients were comparable to changes observed in patients with moderate-to-severe renal impairment not on dialysis [9]. While we do not have high-quality PK or pharmacodynamic (PD) data of low-dose rivaroxaban, it is highly likely that the dose studied in TRACK, i.e. 2.5 mg BID, has resulted in an anticoagulant effect comparable to 5 mg BID in the general population, a dose that was shown to increase the bleeding risk without clear benefit over the 2.5-mg dosing.

Fourth, the study showed harm (higher bleeding risk and absence of benefit) among participants >65 years of age. Is it possible though that many of these individuals were primarily enrolled because of age, while the condition that rivaroxaban is intended to address (coronary or peripheral vascular disease) was largely absent in this subgroup?

The main risk of TRACK is the implicit conclusion potentially drawn by physicians and pharmaceutical companies alike that yet another medical intervention in kidney failure has been proven futile. Such a conclusion would further strengthen renalism, i.e. a therapeutic nihilism driven by concerns about side effects, so often found in other specialties when confronted with individuals having kidney failure [10]. It is therefore of utmost importance to understand what TRACK does and does not tell us. The message of TRACK is clear and unequivocal. There is no clinical benefit of rivaroxaban 2.5 mg BID in (mainly) primary prevention in patients with kidney failure, and this intervention results in increased risk of bleeding. TRACK, however, does not provide a definite answer to the question whether in kidney failure PK/PD-optimized truly low-dosed rivaroxaban in secondary prevention is beneficial.
