Prehabilitation Prior to Colorectal Cancer Surgery: Impact and Implementation
Authors: Elize W. Lockhorst, Theresia A. M. Backhuijs, Teun M. E. Kerkhoff, Robert R. J. Coebergh van den Braak, Ninos Ayez, Cornelis Verhoef, Paul D. Gobardhan, Jennifer M. J. Schreinemakers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectivesMultimodal prehabilitation aims to improve surgical outcomes and preoperative fitness in patients undergoing surgery for colorectal cancer (CRC). This study evaluated implementation of a standardised prehabilitation programme for elective CRC surgery, assess its impact, and identify pitfalls and challenges during implementation.MethodsRetrospective single‐centre study, all CRC patients scheduled for resection between January 2022 and April 2024 were included. Since May 2023, standardised multimodal prehabilitation was introduced. Before, patients received physiotherapy and a dietician if indicated, although this was not routinely offered. Patients were divided into a prehabilitation and non‐prehabilitation group. Clinical data, prehabilitation details, and physiotherapy results were collected from patient charts.ResultsAmong 401 patients, 198 (49%) underwent prehabilitation. Participation increased after standardisation (40% vs. 59%, p < 0.001). Strength and physical fitness improved significantly (leg press 100 vs. 135 kg; steep ramp 160 vs. 213 W; p < 0.001). Patients in the non‐standardised programme experienced a significantly longer postoperative hospital stay than those in standardised programme (4 vs. 3 days, p = 0.010). Despite more comorbidities in prehabilitated patients, complication rates were similar between patients without and with prehabilitation (28% vs. 24%, p = 0.45).ConclusionStandardised prehabilitation appears beneficial and safe, improving participation, referral justification and outcomes, particularly in high‐risk colorectal cancer patients.

Introduction

In Europe, colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second most common form of cancer and cause of cancer‐related deaths [1]. Primary treatment for non‐metastasised CRC consists of radical en bloc resection of the primary tumour along with local lymph node dissection with or without (neo)adjuvant therapy. Up to 44% of patients undergoing major abdominal oncological surgery experience postoperative complications, with an incidence varying depending on the type of surgery [2, 3]. Higher levels of physical activity along with low levels of inactivity, defined as sitting time, are found to be positive predictors for postoperative functional recovery in patients who undergo surgical resection for primary CRC [4, 5]. In addition, reduced cardiorespiratory fitness prior to surgery indicates a reduced physiological reserve, which is associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications and mortality [6]. Additionally, frailty is associated with significantly higher rates of postoperative complications, mortality, readmission, reoperation and length of stay [7, 8, 9, 10]. By screening patients who might benefit from prehabilitation, surgical risks can be determined and adjustable factors can be optimised perioperatively.

Prehabilitation is defined as a process from diagnosis to surgery consisting of interventions (exercise, nutrition, psychological strategies and respiratory training) aimed to enhance functional capacity and physiological reserve to allow patients to withstand surgical stressors, improve postoperative outcomes and facilitate recovery [11]. Multiple trials suggested that prehabilitation may lead to clinically meaningful improvements in the patient's preoperative functional exercise capacity and surgical outcomes [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. In clinical practice, many hospitals are providing multimodal prehabilitation programmes [20]. However, although some (interview)studies have assessed the requisites for implementing a prehabilitation programme, literature regarding the pitfalls and challenges remain limited [21, 22, 23, 24]. Moreover, little is known about patient participation. The aim of this study was to evaluate the implementation of a standardised prehabilitation programme for patients undergoing elective colorectal cancer surgery, to assess its effects and benefits, and to identify pitfalls and challenges encountered during the implementation process.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective single‐centre study was conducted at the Amphia Hospital, a large teaching hospital in the Netherlands, between January 2022 and April 2024. All patients diagnosed with primary CRC scheduled for elective primary tumour resection were included, excluding those with primary appendiceal cancer, obstructive CRC, or under 18 years of age.

The study evaluated effects and implementation of a standardised prehabilitation programme, along with patient participation. Non‐standardised prehabilitation (January 2022–April 2023) was compared to the standardised programme (May 2023–April 2024), along with patients’ baseline characteristics and postoperative outcomes. Additionally, outcomes were compared between patients who underwent prehabilitation and those who did not, focusing on complications, hospital stay, and mortality.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee (W24.262).

Prehabilitation

Between January 2022 and April 2023, patients were referred for prehabilitation without a standardised protocol at the discretion of the surgeon. Since May 2023, a standardised prehabilitation programme was implemented in the surgical department. In order to embed prehabilitation in daily practice, the standardised programme was developed to assess and optimise patients’ physical status, diet and negative factors (e.g. smoking). This multimodal approach is coordinated by a team consisting of a sports medicine specialist, physiotherapists, dieticians, and trained nurses. Regular team evaluations are conducted with the coordinating team and a network of local physiotherapists. From May 2023, the intention was that all patients diagnosed with primary CRC scheduled for elective tumour resection were reviewed and subsequently offered this standardised prehabilitation programme. This assessment and decision regarding prehabilitation referral were made by the leading surgeon in consultation with the patient's case manager. The decision to refer patients for prehabilitation was based on the clinical assessment of the treating surgeon. Factors to be considered were daily physical activity, nutritional status and physical condition assessed at the outpatient clinic. Once a patient was referred, a baseline screening was conducted by a trained nurse, which consisted of the following questionnaires and physical tests. The FitMáx (developed by Maxima Medical Centre, the Netherlands) questionnaire was used to estimate a patient's VO2max [25]. Patients with an estimated VO2max below 18 mL/min/kg were referred to the sports medicine specialist. Patients underwent a CardioPulmonary Exercise Test (CPET) conducted by the sports medicine specialist to precisely measure the VO2max and to evaluate cardiopulmonary and muscle function during exercise. Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) in this study measured by VO2max is a well‐established predictor of morbidity and all‐cause mortality. A poor preoperative CRF, as measured by a maximal cardiopulmonary exercise test, indicates a reduced physiological reserve, which increases the risk for a complicated postoperative time course including morbidity and mortality. Patients with an increased preoperative level of CRF may have a greater physiological reserve that allows them to better cope with surgical stress [6]. This risk assessment was included in the CPET report and communicated to the treating surgeon. In case of a very low VO2max below 15 mL/min/kg, the sports medicine specialist and the treating surgeon would discuss the possibility to postpone surgery for a few weeks, allowing the patient more time to optimise their health. The handgrip strength test and Timed Chair Stand Test were utilised to assess physical fitness levels [26, 27]. The latter was also used to assess the patient's risk of a fall. Every patient was advised to undergo supervised training with a physiotherapist nearby the patient's home, adhering to the training regimen outlined in the international PREHAB‐trial [12]. In short, the training regimen included 1‐h sessions of high intensity interval training (HIIT) and strength exercises, 3 times a week. HIIT consisted of four 2‐min‐long intervals levelled at 85%–90% of their peak power measured by a steep ramp test (SRT) at the first training with a physiotherapist, alternated with 4‐min intervals at 30% of peak power as indicated in the training regimen. The main goal of HIIT was to increase cardiorespiratory fitness in the short training time of 3 weeks. Strength training consisted of 2 sets of 10 repetitions for major muscle groups at 65%–70% of the baseline one‐repetition maximum (1RM) [12]. The goal of the strength exercises was to increase the 1RM by 10% within 3 weeks of training. All patients with a haemoglobin level below 7.5 mmol/L for women and 8.5 mmol/L for men were referred for a ferric carboxymaltose (Ferinject) injection according to Dutch guidelines [28].

All patients received a leaflet containing information on a healthy diet. Patients were referred to a dietician when indicated by >10% weight loss over a time span of 6 months prior to admission and a body mass index (BMI) < 18.5 kg/m2. Furthermore, if the patient scored more than 1 point in the Patient‐Generated Subjective Global Assessment Short Form (PG‐SGA SF) [29]. Or when the patient scored more than 16 points on the Rate‐a‐Plate questionnaire [30].

All patients over the age of 70 underwent a Geriatric 8 (G8) and 6 item Cognitive Impairment Assessment (6CIT). Those who scored below 15 points on the G8 and more than 7 points on the 6CIT, were referred to the geriatrician. Patients who were not referred to the geriatrician were screened for polypharmacy. Patients taking five or more medications daily were referred to the lifestyle pharmacist. In case of smoking, patients were referred to a smoking cessation coach. This trajectory consists of multiple face‐to‐face or digital interactions and, if necessary, integrated with pharmacotherapy. During the baseline screening, the nurse conducted a mental resilience screening. This included questions aimed to identify signs of anxiety related to previous medical treatments. Findings were documented in the patient's medical record, along with the patient's preference regarding consultation with a medical psychologist. If the patient opted for a consultation with the psychologist, a referral was arranged accordingly. In case of signs of depression or anxiety related to life events, patients were referred to a social worker. Based on the literature, a prehabilitation period of 3 to 6 weeks was considered optimal for improving preoperative fitness and reducing the risk of postoperative complications [31]. Therefore the prehabilitation period needed to last at least 3 weeks.

Outcome Parameters and Data Analysis

Demographic, clinical, tumour‐ and surgery‐related data up to 90 days postoperatively were collected from patient charts, along with details of the prehabilitation programme and physiotherapy assessments. Reasons for non‐participation in the prehabilitation programme were extracted.

Continuous baseline covariates were compared using a T‐test and reported as means with standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed. If non‐normal, the Mann‐Whitney U test was utilised, with results presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical covariates were compared using Chi‐square test and Fisher's exact were appropriate. Baseline and final physiotherapy measurements were compared using the related‐samples Wilcoxon Signed Rank test.

All analyses were conducted using SAS v9, with p‐values below 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Between January 2022 and April 2024, 401 patients underwent elective surgery for colorectal cancer and were included in this study.

Non‐Standardised Versus Standardised Prehabilitation and the Implementation

Significantly fewer patients participated in the non‐standardised prehabilitation programme before May 2023, compared to the increased participation seen after implementation of the standardised programme (n = 84 (40%) vs. n = 114 (59%), p < 0.001). Furthermore, data indicated a significant increase in the reporting of the decision to abstain from prehabilitation after implementation of the standardised programme (44% vs. 67%, p < 0.001) (Table 1). Reasons to abstain from prehabilitation included: surgery scheduled within 3 weeks of diagnosis, adequate levels of physical activity, clinical signs of (partial) bowel obstruction, patient refusal, or inability to enrol in the programme due to cognitive or physical impairment (Table 1).

Patient characteristics did not significantly differ between the non‐standardised and standardised programme. Haemoglobin levels were higher in the standardised group, however, this difference was statistically significant only in women (p = 0.01). Overall, the implementation of the standardised programme after May 2023 decreased the median postoperative hospital stay from 4 (IQR 2.8–6.3) to 3 days (IQR 3–6) (p = 0.01) (Table 2). No significant differences were observed between the groups in the proportion of patients experiencing complications (Clavien Dindo ≥2), with rates of 24% in the non‐standardised group and 25% in the standardised group (p = 0.90). Similarly, 90‐day mortality rates did not differ significantly (2% vs. 1%, p = 0.58).

Questionnaires and Paramedics

Compared to the non‐standardised programme, standardised prehabilitation showed higher adherence to completing FitMáx questionnaires (35% vs. 90%, p < 0.001). Although more patients had a VO2max ≤ 18 (28% vs. 36%, p < 0.001), less patients were referred to the sports medicine specialist (63% vs. 50%, p = 0.70). Reasons for abstaining from referral included low haemoglobin levels (32%), physical complaints (32%), rescheduling of surgery to an earlier date (9%) or unknown reasons (27%). In contrast, compared to the non‐standardised programme, more patients were referred to the physiotherapist after the implementation of standardised prehabilitation (86% vs. 96%, p = 0.014). Regarding dietary consultations, the standardised group demonstrated a lower referral rate to dietitians (100% vs. 68%, p = 0.55).

In the non‐standardised group 88% of the patients over 70 years old were screened via the G8 tool and 6CIT, compared to all patients in the standardised group (p = 0.10), resulting in a comparable number of referrals to the geriatrician (90% vs. 85% respectively, p = 0.23). Nine patients with a G8 score of 18 or lower were not referred to a geriatrician due to the treating surgeon's assessment deeming it unnecessary because of adequate fitness (44%), patient refusal (11%), or for unknown reasons (44%). Finally, compared to non‐standardised prehabilitation, an overall increase in referrals to paramedical specialties was observed following standardised prehabilitation (smoking cessation coach: 0% vs. 28% of all smoking patients, p = 0.07; medical psychologist: 0% vs. 2%, p = 0.51).

Physiotherapy Results

Among patients receiving physical therapy, 94% received five or more sessions, and over half (52%) received the recommended nine or more sessions, with an average of nine sessions (min 1–max 17). Except for the steep ramp test (SRT) borg score, all physiotherapy measurements improved significantly including the SRT Wmax (160 vs. 213 W, p < 0.001) and 1RM leg press weight (100 vs. 135 kg, p < 0.001) (Table 3). A significant correlation was observed between the number of physiotherapy appointments and the SRT heartrate (Pearson correlation 0.37, p = 0.04). No significant associations were observed between the number of appointments and the other five physiotherapy outcome parameters. The aimed improvement of ≥10% for the 1RM leg press was achieved in 82% of patients for whom initial and final measurements were available.

No Prehabilitation Versus Prehabilitation

Of the 401 patients who underwent colorectal surgery, 198 patients (49%) underwent prehabilitation. Gender distribution was comparable, 45% versus 49% females in the no‐prehabilitation and prehabilitation group, respectively (p = 0.46). Patients not enroled in the prehabilitation programme were significantly younger (65 vs. 73 years, p < 0.001), had lower ASA scores (ASA I–II 76% vs. 57%, ASA III 23% vs. 40%, ASA IV–V 1% vs. 3%, p < 0.001), were less likely to be active smokers (8% vs. 15%, p = 0.03) and had higher haemoglobin levels (8.3 vs. 7.9 mmol/L, p = 0.004) compared to those enroled for prehabilitation (Table 4).

Physically Fit Patients

Interestingly, a subgroup analysis showed that patients who did not undergo prehabilitation because they were deemed physically fit had a significantly lower incidence of complications compared to those who received prehabilitation and those who did not, for reasons unrelated to physical fitness (4% vs. 24% and 31%, respectively, p = 0.01). Patients classified as (physically) fit had significantly more often an ASA score of 1 or 2 (p = 0.003), higher preoperative haemoglobin level (p = 0.01) and were younger (p < 0.001) compared to both the other non‐prehabilitated patients prehabilitated patients.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that a standardised prehabilitation programme increased patient participation and improved the justification for specialist referrals. Patients in the standardised programme experienced a significantly shorter postoperative hospital stay than those in non‐standardised programme. While prehabilitation patients had worse baseline characteristics, postoperative complications and mortality did not differ significantly from those who did not underwent prehabilitation.

Participation rates improved notably with the standardised approach (40% vs. 59%), likely due to increased awareness and the structured referral protocols. Consistent with previous studies, the timing of elective tumour resection frequently influenced prehabilitation eligibility (22%–37% in this study, 23%–42% in literature) [22, 32, 33, 34, 35]. Another common reason for omitting prehabilitation was surgeons’ assessment of patient physical fitness. Previous studies showed that subjectively assessed functional capacity by clinicians is not reliable enough in predicting physical fitness or frailty [36, 37]. Contradictory, in our study patients who were deemed physically fit by the treating surgeon experienced significantly less complications. This suggests that experienced surgeons can reliably assess fitness in regards to colorectal surgery and make appropriate decisions regarding the need for prehabilitation. In line with literature, a small minority of patients were ineligible due to bowel obstruction, refused participation or were not able to participate [38].

The median postoperative stay in patients in the non‐standardised programme was 1 day longer compared to patients in the standardised programme (4 vs. 3 days, p = 0.01), which resonates with the 1 to 2 day reduction reported in literature [17, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44]. Interestingly, a similar reduction in hospital stay was observed among patients who did not undergo prehabilitation after May 2023. Similar to this observation, Ripollés‐Melchor et al showed a decrease in postoperative complications with an increase in adherence to the ERAS protocol [45]. These observations highlight the positive impact of adherence to standardised perioperative protocols by patients and all caregivers involved in order to optimise modifiable factors, and thereby improve postoperative outcomes.

Physiotherapy referral rates significantly increased from 86% to 96% following the implementation of the standardised programme, exceeding most literature reports (17%–97%) [33, 41, 44, 46, 47, 48]. In the group of patients in the standardised programme, just over half of the patients attended at the goal of nine sessions, resulting in a significant improvement in all but one outcome parameters. Although both the number of attended sessions and the observed improvement are consistent with previous studies, further measurable improvement may be achieved by improving adherence to the training regimen [16, 34, 39, 47, 48, 49]. The current study highlights physiotherapy as a critical component of prehabilitation. In addition to enhancing physical fitness, selected patients were referred to the sports medicine specialist for assessment and optimisation of perioperative morbidity and mortality risk. Counterintuitively, fewer patients were referred to the sports medicine specialist following the implementation of the standardised prehabilitation programme. This decline in referrals can be attributed to the introduction of specific criteria—such as low haemoglobin levels or physical and cognitive impairments—that identified patients unlikely to benefit from a sports medicine consultation. Unlike other studies in which all patients underwent CPET testing [12, 50, 51], in our cohort only patients with FitMáx VO2max < 18 mL/min/kg were referred, emphasising a more targeted and resource‐efficient approach.

Malnutrition significantly increases surgical risk, hospital stays, and costs [52]. Previous studies reported varying malnutrition rates among colorectal cancer patients [12, 41, 47, 53]. Although preoperative dietary interventions improved nutritional parameters, changes were often not statistically significant [12, 14, 44, 47, 53]. Our protocol included dietary screening with clear criteria, resulting in fewer dietary referrals post‐implementation (100% vs. 68%, p = 0.547). Similar to referrals to the sports medicine specialist, this reduction is likely attributed to more clearly defined referral criteria. Smoking cessation support was provided selectively to motivated patients, resulting in lower referral rates (28%) compared to other studies reporting universal referral (100%) [14]. A recent meta‐analysis showed that intensive behavioural support as offered in our prehabilitation programme (multiple face‐to‐face interactions combined with pharmacotherapy) increases the chance of quitting before and continuous smoking cessation after surgery, and reduces postoperative complications while low‐intensity behavioural support did not [54]. Psychological distress is associated with increased analgesic needs and complication rates [55]. Additionally, psychological support can improve surgical preparedness [38]. In this study, patients exhibiting signs of anxiety or depression who expressed a desire to consult a medical psychologist were referred. Although the true rate of patients that can benefit from preoperative psychological consultation is probably higher than the 2% observed in this study, we believe that not all patients will benefit, and that selective referral is more efficient and cost effective than standard referral for all patients as observed in other studies [44].

Older patients [56], smokers [57], patients with lower haemoglobin levels [58] and those with higher ASA classifications [59, 60] have been observed to be at a greater risk of experiencing postoperative complications after colorectal surgery. Previous studies recommend discussing these risks with older patients who are inherently at risk, with the treating physician and geriatricians playing a key role in the thoroughly evaluating frailty and geriatric syndromes [61]. Therefore, all patients ≥70 years were screened using the G8 tool and 6CIT and if indicated referred to the geriatrician with a high referral rate of 85% compared to 40%–55% in literature [33, 53, 62, 63].

In our study, compared to the no‐prehabilitation group, patients in the prehabilitation group were older, were more likely to be active smokers, had a median lower haemoglobin level and a higher ASA classification. While poorer postoperative outcomes would be expected given the patient characteristics, this was not observed, indicating a potential beneficial effect of prehabilitation. These findings are in line with van der Hulst et al. who presented similar postoperative complication rates in frail but prehabilitated patients compared to no‐prehabilitation [63]. Nevertheless, literature remains inconclusive, with some studies showing significant reductions in complications while others report no differences, likely due to heterogeneity in study designs and patient populations [13, 17, 19, 31, 33, 39, 41, 42, 43, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71].

Implementing a standardised prehabilitation programme for colorectal surgery patients presents several challenges. The involvement of a multidisciplinary team and the introduction of a structured protocol led to increased referral rates, likely due to greater awareness and vigilance among healthcare providers. By establishing clear referral criteria, the appropriateness of referrals to paramedical specialists improved, resulting in fewer unnecessary consultations. Furthermore, surgeons demonstrated the ability to reliably assess a patient's fitness, ensuring that only those likely to benefit from prehabilitation were included. Based on objective parameters such as ASA score, lower age, and higher haemoglobin levels these patients were significantly fitter, and experienced less postoperative complications.

Despite these strengths, pitfalls were identified. Although patient refusal rates for participation in prehabilitation were low, engagement with additional support services such as smoking cessation coaching and psychological counselling was limited. This suggests that while physical optimisation is well‐integrated into prehabilitation pathways, greater emphasis and active encouragement may be necessary to enhance participation in psychosocial interventions, which are equally critical for overall surgical outcomes. Another ongoing challenge is the need for thorough and consistent documentation. Clear documentation of the clinical rationale behind decisions not to offer prehabilitation is crucial, to ensure that patient‐centred care remains the priority over surgical scheduling pressures. As a future plan, recording consultations between the surgeon, case manager, and patients could provide valuable insights into the decision‐making process regarding prehabilitation, including potential referral biases. The retrospective design of this study may have led to underreporting of reasons for not initiating prehabilitation and limits detailed evaluation of referral decisions. Moreover, as referral was based on surgeon judgement, this may have introduced selection bias and variability in patient inclusion. Although our findings suggest that surgeons in this cohort were able to adequately assess patient fitness, this may not be generalisable to all settings. Therefore, more standardised referral pathways, such as universal screening followed by objective triage, may help to reduce potential bias and improve consistency in future practice. Despite the retrospective nature, the strength of our study is that we evaluated a prehabilitation programme in a representative real‐life population.

Conclusion

This study provided an overview of the implementation of a standardised prehabilitation programme and its challenges in a real‐life clinical population, highlighting increased participation and improved referral justification. Surgeons appeared able to assess physical fitness preoperatively. Patients in the standardised programme had shorter postoperative hospital stays compared to those in the non‐standardised group. Despite worse baseline characteristics, prehabilitation patients had no significant differences in postoperative complications or mortality. These findings suggest that prehabilitation is safe and may benefit higher‐risk patients. However, results should be interpreted with caution due to the retrospective design and potential selection bias. Prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings and further clarify the role of standardised prehabilitation in routine clinical practice.
