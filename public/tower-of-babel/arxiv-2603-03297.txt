TTSR: Test-Time Self-Reflection for Continual Reasoning Improvement Thanks: Work in progress.
Haoyang He, Zihua Rong, Liangjie Zhao, Yunjia Zhao, Lan Yang, Honggang Zhang

Abstract
Test-time Training enables model adaptation using only test questions and offers a promising paradigm for improving the reasoning ability of large language models (LLMs). However, it faces two major challenges: test questions are often highly difficult, making self-generated pseudo-labels unreliable, and existing methods lack effective mechanisms to adapt to a model’s specific reasoning weaknesses, leading to inefficient learning. To address these issues, we propose TTSR , a self-reflective test-time self-evolving training framework. TTSR employs a single pretrained language model that alternates between the roles of a Student and a Teacher at test time. The Student focuses on solving problems and learning from synthesized variant questions, while the Teacher analyzes the Student’s failed reasoning trajectories, summarizes recurring reasoning weaknesses, and synthesizes targeted variant questions accordingly. This process guides the model to improve within a learnable regime through a continual self-evolving loop. Experimental results on multiple challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks show that TTSR consistently improves reasoning performance and generalizes well across different model backbones and general-domain reasoning tasks. These findings suggest that teacher-mediated self-reflection provides an effective pathway for stable and continual reasoning improvement at test time.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong potential for multi-step reasoning, yet their performance on challenging mathematical and logical tasks remains brittle: a single incorrect deduction, missing case analysis, or premature commitment can derail the entire solution. A natural direction is to move beyond static inference and enable
inference-time improvement
, where the model adapts using only the test inputs it encounters. This direction is complementary to prompt-only
test-time strategies
that increase inference computation via sampling, voting, and explicit search/planning (e.g., self-consistency and tree-based deliberation)
21
;
30
;
31
.

Test-time Training
(TTT) instantiates this idea by updating model parameters during inference using self-supervision from unlabeled test data
1
. Recent work further frames TTT as test-time reinforcement learning, where learning signals are derived from multiple sampled solutions, self-consistency, or verifiable rewards
37
;
33
. These approaches suggest that LLMs can refine their reasoning behavior online without access to ground-truth annotations. However, in difficult reasoning regimes, test questions are often near the boundary of the model’s competence. As a result, pseudo-labels or reward signals produced by the model itself become noisy and unstable, leading to inefficient or even degenerative updates
1
;
37
;
13
. A pragmatic workaround is to inject stronger external supervision at test time (e.g., teacher-generated data or guidance), but this reliance weakens the goal of fully autonomous self-evolution and limits applicability in settings where stronger teachers are unavailable or undesirable
14
;
20
;
6
.

In parallel,
self-play
and related self-evolving paradigms have emerged as a general recipe for autonomous model improvement without additional human data
12
;
23
. To mitigate instability in recursive learning, later work adopts role specialization and co-evolutionary designs, improving robustness and scalability
17
;
3
;
7
;
11
. Nevertheless, many self-play systems prioritize
task-level
diversity or difficulty scaling, while paying limited attention to the
fine-grained capability deficiencies
that manifest within the model’s reasoning traces on individual instances.

This paper proposes that stable test-time self-evolution requires two properties: (i) learning signals must remain informative even when the original test instance is too hard for reliable self-labeling, and (ii) adaptation must be
instance-aware
, explicitly diagnosing and addressing recurring reasoning failures rather than treating all errors as interchangeable noise. To this end, we introduce
TTSR
(
T
est-
T
ime
S
elf-
R
eflection), a self-reflective test-time self-evolving training framework that uses a
single
pretrained model alternating between two functional roles: a
Student
and a
Teacher
.

At a high level, the Student performs standard inference by sampling a group of reasoning trajectories for each test question, and it updates its policy online (built upon GRPO) using self-supervised signals. The Teacher does not solve the test question directly; instead, it observes the Student’s trajectories, identifies failures relative to a pseudo-target obtained via self-consistency, and performs lightweight reflection to summarize recurring weaknesses exhibited in the failed traces. Crucially, the Teacher then
synthesizes targeted variant questions
designed to expose and correct these weaknesses. The Student learns not only from the original test input but also from these targeted variants, forming a continual self-evolving loop that nudges learning toward a more
learnable regime
while remaining fully autonomous (no external teacher model or ground-truth labels).

We evaluate TTSR on multiple challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks and find that it yields consistent improvements at test time, generalizes across different model backbones, and transfers to broader general-domain reasoning tasks. Collectively, these results support the hypothesis that
teacher-mediated self-reflection
can provide a practical and stable pathway for continual reasoning improvement during inference.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are:

•

We introduce a
trace-level
perspective on test-time training: by leveraging a Teacher role to distill
reflection signals
from the Student’s failed reasoning trajectories, we shift test-time adaptation from
task-level
optimization to
trace-level
optimization, enabling instance-aware correction beyond difficulty scaling or generic self-consistency.

•

We propose
TTSR
, a fully autonomous test-time self-evolving framework in which a single model alternates between
Student
and
Teacher
roles; the Teacher reflects on failed reasoning traces and synthesizes targeted variants to drive stable online improvement.

•

We demonstrate that TTSR consistently improves performance on challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks and generalizes across model backbones and general-domain reasoning tasks, suggesting a scalable mechanism for continual test-time reasoning enhancement.

Related Work

The most relevant prior work to our study can be broadly categorized into two research directions:

Self-Play for LLMs.

Self-play has become a foundational paradigm for autonomous model improvement, inspiring early efforts toward self-evolving reasoning in LLMs without additional human supervision
12
;
22
.
Early studies primarily explored iterative self-play as a practical framework for self-improvement, often emphasizing alignment-oriented objectives (e.g., SPIN)
4
. To address degenerative feedback and instability in purely recursive learning, subsequent work introduced role-specialized and co-evolutionary designs, improving training stability, scalability, and effectiveness in low-data regimes
17
;
3
;
7
. More recent research shifts the focus from framework-level design toward capability-oriented self-evolution, including adversarial game-based learning
5
, task-specific self-play
27
, long-context evolution
29
, and data-free self-play paradigms
15
. At a broader conceptual level, dynamic self-challenging and unsupervised post-training strategies
36
;
26
signal a transition from imitation of external supervision
32
toward autonomous self-correction driven by intrinsic verifiability, enabling increasingly data-free self-evolving LLM systems
11
;
35
;
9
. However, despite these advances, existing approaches largely emphasize task-level diversity or difficulty scaling, while paying limited attention to fine-grained capability deficiencies exposed during reasoning on individual instances. This gap limits the efficient correction of concrete reasoning weaknesses, motivating more instance-aware self-evolving strategies.

Test-Time Training (TTT).

Test-time training adapts model parameters during inference using self-supervision from unlabeled test data
2
. A representative line of work formulates TTT as test-time reinforcement learning, where learning signals are derived from multiple sampled predictions or verifiable rewards, enabling models to iteratively refine their solutions on individual test instances
37
;
33
. These approaches demonstrate that large language models can continue improving their reasoning behavior at inference time without access to ground-truth annotations. However, in practice, test questions encountered during test-time training are often highly challenging, which makes pseudo-labels or reward signals derived from the model itself noisy and insufficient to provide stable and effective learning signals
2
;
37
. Moreover, some recent TTT approaches rely on data or supervision synthesized by stronger teacher models to guide adaptation, particularly on difficult reasoning tasks
14
;
20
;
6
. Such reliance limits the applicability of test-time training to fully autonomous self-evolving settings and constrains the model’s ability to improve purely through self-generated learning signals.To address these limitations, we introduce TTSR, a test-time self-evolving training framework designed to enable stable model evolution during inference.

3
Preliminary

This section briefly reviews the optimization and adaptation techniques underlying our framework, including Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
19
)
for reward-based learning and Test-Time Training (TTT)
37
for label-free model adaptation at inference time.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
19
)
is a policy-gradient algorithm for large language models that removes the need for an explicit value function. Instead of estimating advantages via a learned critic, GRPO derives relative advantages by normalizing outcome-level rewards across a group of sampled responses, which enables stable optimization under sparse or binary supervision.

We model the language model as a stochastic policy
\pi_{\theta}
that generates a response
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{|y|})
conditioned on an input question
q
. For each question
q
, a behavior policy
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}
samples a group of
G
candidate responses
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
, each receiving a sequence-level reward
R_{i}
from a verifier.

To derive learning signals without a value function, GRPO normalizes rewards within the sampled group and defines a relative advantage for each response:

\tilde{A}_{i}=\frac{R_{i}-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{R})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{R})+\delta},\qquad\mathbf{R}=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{G}\},

(1)

where
\delta
is a small constant for numerical stability. The same advantage
\tilde{A}_{i}
is shared across all token positions within response
y_{i}
.

GRPO updates the policy parameters by maximizing a PPO-style clipped surrogate objective with an explicit KL regularization term:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle{}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D}_{q},\,\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}

(2)

\displaystyle\Biggl[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{i}|}\Bigl(\min\!\bigl(r_{i,t}(\theta)\,\tilde{A}_{i},\,\mathrm{clip}(r_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,\tilde{A}_{i}\bigr)-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\parallel\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}\right)\Bigr)\Biggr],

where

r_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid q,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid q,y_{i,<t})},\qquad\tilde{A}_{i}=\frac{R_{i}-\mathrm{mean}(\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G})}{\mathrm{std}(\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G})+\delta}.

(3)

Here,
\epsilon
controls the clipping range, and
\beta
balances the KL regularization term, which constrains policy updates to remain close to the behavior policy and improves optimization stability.

Test-Time Training (TTT).

Test-Time Training enables a pretrained policy to adapt its parameters during inference using only unlabeled test inputs. Let
\mathcal{X}_{\text{test}}=\{x^{(t)}\}
denote the set of test queries.

Starting from an initial policy
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
, TTT seeks adapted parameters by optimizing a self-supervised objective:

\theta^{\dagger}=\arg\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x^{(t)})}\big[\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{TTT}}(y,\tilde{y})\big],

(4)

where
\tilde{y}
denotes a pseudo-target constructed from the model’s own predictions, typically obtained via self-consistency or consensus-based aggregation. The reward function
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{TTT}}
measures agreement between a candidate response and the pseudo-target, providing a learning signal for inference-time adaptation.

4
TTSR: Test-Time Self-Reflection

We present
TTSR
, a teacher-reflective test-time self-evolving training framework built upon Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and Test-Time Training (TTT).
TTSR employs a single pretrained language model that alternates between two functional roles at test time: a
Student
and a
Teacher
.
The Student focuses on solving test questions and learning from synthesized variants, while the Teacher observes the Student’s reasoning failures, performs lightweight reflection to summarize recurring weaknesses, and synthesizes targeted variant questions accordingly.
Through this role alternation, the model continuously improves its reasoning ability during inference without external supervision.Let
\pi_{\theta}
denote the pretrained language model.
At test time,
\pi_{\theta}
alternates between two roles with shared parameters but distinct functional behaviors.

Student Role.

Given a test question
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{test}}
, the Student samples a group of
G
reasoning trajectories

\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
,
each representing a complete chain-of-thought reasoning process followed by a final answer.
These trajectories are used both for inference and for constructing self-supervised learning signals during test-time adaptation.

Teacher Role.

The Teacher does not directly solve the test question.
Instead, it observes the Student’s reasoning trajectories and identifies failures relative to a pseudo-target obtained via self-consistency.
Based on these failures, the Teacher performs lightweight reflection to summarize recurring reasoning weaknesses and synthesizes variant questions designed to expose and correct such weaknesses.

4.1
Student: Solving Problems and Test-Time Adaptation

During test-time adaptation, the Student serves as an online solver that continuously refines its reasoning behavior through self-supervised learning.
Rather than treating inference as a static process, the Student iteratively updates its policy by learning from both the original test question and the variant questions synthesized by the Teacher.
This online self-evolving procedure enables the Student to progressively improve its reasoning capability using only unlabeled test-time data.

Test-Time Training Set Construction.

At test-time iteration
t
, the Student updates its policy
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
using a dynamically constructed training set.
Specifically, we define the training dataset at iteration
t
as

\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{X}_{\text{test}}\;\cup\;\mathcal{X}^{(t-1)}_{\text{var}},

(5)

where
\mathcal{X}_{\text{test}}
denotes the original test set, and
\mathcal{X}^{(t-1)}_{\text{var}}
represents the set of variant questions synthesized by the Teacher using the previous policy
\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}
.
The Student samples training questions from
\mathcal{D}_{t}
and performs test-time adaptation via reward-based optimization.

Majority Voting Reward.

For each training question
x\in\mathcal{D}_{t}
, the Student samples a group of
G
reasoning trajectories using the current policy:

\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot\mid x).

(6)

Following the Test-Time Reinforcement Learning paradigm, we aggregate the sampled trajectories via majority voting to obtain a consensus outcome
\hat{y}(x)
, which serves as a pseudo-correct reference.
An outcome-level pseudo-correctness reward is then assigned to each trajectory according to its agreement with the consensus:

R_{S}(y_{i}\mid x)=\mathbb{I}\!\left[y_{i}=\hat{y}(x)\right].

(7)

Based on these rewards, we define the empirical pseudo-correctness score for question
x
as

s_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}R_{S}(y_{i}\mid x),

(8)

which measures the stability of the Student’s predictions under stochastic sampling at iteration
t
.
The Student is optimized using GRPO with these pseudo-correctness rewards, enabling stable and label-free test-time adaptation.

4.2
Teacher: Reflection-Guided Curriculum Synthesis

Reflection on Reasoning Steps.

At test-time iteration
t
, the Teacher performs reflection based on the Student’s failed reasoning behaviors observed in the previous iteration.
Specifically, using the policy
\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}
, we collect a set of reasoning trajectories whose pseudo-correctness rewards are zero.
From these trajectories, we randomly sample up to
M
failed instances; if fewer than
m
such instances are available, all failed instances are used.

Each sampled instance consists of a tuple
(x,y,\hat{y})
, where
x
denotes the question,
y
the Student’s reasoning trajectory, and
\hat{y}
the corresponding pseudo-correct reference obtained via majority voting.
Conditioned on this set of failed tuples, the Teacher with policy
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
analyzes the reasoning steps in
y
and produces a reflection summary that characterizes both the incorrect reasoning patterns and the missing or insufficient steps leading to failure.
This reflection focuses on the process-level deficiencies of the Student’s reasoning rather than the final answer correctness.

Reflection-Guided Question Synthesis.

Given the reflection summaries derived from failed reasoning trajectories, the Teacher synthesizes auxiliary training questions to form an adaptive curriculum for the Student. Let
\mathcal{F}^{(t-1)}=\{(x_{k},y_{k},\hat{y}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
denote a set of failed instances sampled from the previous iteration, where each tuple consists of a question, the Student’s reasoning trajectory, and its pseudo-correct reference.We construct a natural-language synthesis prompt
p_{t}
based on the failed set
\mathcal{F}^{(t-1)}
, which summarizes the reasoning errors and missing steps exhibited by the Student.

\mathcal{X}^{(t)}_{\text{var}}=\left\{x^{\prime}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}\!\left(\cdot\;\middle|\;p_{t},\,\mathcal{F}^{(t-1)}\right).

(9)

These synthesized questions are designed to preserve the core reasoning structure of the original test question while selectively modifying conditions or constraints to expose the reflected reasoning weaknesses.
Group sampling is employed to encourage diversity among the generated variants, ensuring that the resulting curriculum covers multiple manifestations of the same underlying reasoning deficiency.

Difficulty Reward.

We evaluate the difficulty of a variant question
x^{\prime}
using the Student’s pseudo-correctness score at iteration
t
.
Concretely, the Student samples
G
trajectories under
\pi_{\theta_{t}}
and computes the empirical pseudo-correctness score
s_{t}(x^{\prime})\in[0,1]
as defined in Section
4.1
.
We then define an entropy-based capability-frontier reward:

R_{\text{diff}}(x^{\prime})=\frac{H\!\left(\mathrm{Bern}\!\left(s_{t}(x^{\prime})\right)\right)}{\log 2}=-\frac{s_{t}(x^{\prime})\log s_{t}(x^{\prime})+(1-s_{t}(x^{\prime}))\log(1-s_{t}(x^{\prime}))}{\log 2}.

(10)

which is maximized when
s_{t}(x^{\prime})\approx 0.5
, i.e., when the Student exhibits maximal uncertainty on
x^{\prime}
.

Similarity Penalty Reward.

To encourage exploration and reduce redundancy in the synthesized curriculum, we introduce a similarity penalty reward that discourages near-duplicate question generation.
The penalty is applied at the group level, allowing the Teacher to reuse shared reasoning structures while avoiding trivial paraphrases.Let
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}=\{x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{M}\}
denote the set of variant questions generated in the current iteration, and let
x
be the original test question.
We treat the reference question as part of the generation batch and define the extended set

\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\cup\{x\}.

We then compute a pairwise similarity score
\mathrm{sim}(z_{i},z_{j})\in[0,1]
for all
z_{i},z_{j}\in\mathcal{Z}
, where the definition of
\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
is provided in Appendix
B.1
.

The group-level similarity penalty is defined as

R_{\text{sim}}(x^{\prime}_{i},x)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Z}|-1}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}\setminus\{x^{\prime}_{i}\}}\max\!\left(0,\ \mathrm{sim}(x^{\prime}_{i},z)-\tau\right),

(11)

where
\tau
controls the tolerance for textual overlap. This penalty is incorporated into the Teacher’s reward to promote diverse yet structurally relevant question synthesis.

Teacher Reward.

The Teacher is optimized using a reward that balances question difficulty and diversity.
For each synthesized question
x^{\prime}_{i}
, the overall reward is defined as

R_{T}(x^{\prime}_{i})=\max\!\left(0,\;R_{\text{diff}}(x^{\prime}_{i})-\lambda\,R_{\text{sim}}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},x)\right),

(12)

where
R_{\text{diff}}(x^{\prime}_{i})
denotes the difficulty reward that encourages questions near the Student’s capability frontier, and
R_{\text{sim}}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},x)
is a group-level similarity penalty that discourages redundant generations within the batch and with respect to the original question.

In addition, a strict format constraint is enforced during synthesis: only questions correctly enclosed within the predefined
<question>
tags are considered in the reward computation, while outputs violating the format requirement are discarded and do not contribute to the Teacher’s optimization.

Main Results

Main Results.

Table

Overall, TTSR consistently outperforms all baselines on all three base models, with clear improvements observed across both domains.

Mathematical Reasoning.

On mathematical benchmarks, TTSR achieves substantial gains over test-time reinforcement learning baselines.
For example, on
Qwen3-4B-Base

Qwen3-8B-Base

General Reasoning.

In addition to mathematics, TTSR also improves performance on general reasoning benchmarks. Across all models, TTSR achieves consistent gains on
GPQA-Diamond
and
MMLU-Pro
, with improvements typically ranging from 3 to 7 points over the strongest baseline. These results suggest that the benefits of reflection-guided adaptation extend beyond mathematical reasoning, improving overall reasoning robustness without sacrificing generalization.

Effect of Model Architecture.

The largest relative improvements are observed on
OctoThinker-8B-Hybrid-Base
, where TTSR improves average performance by more than 15 points over the base model.
This indicates that models with explicit reasoning-oriented inductive biases can particularly benefit from structured reflection and targeted test-time training.
Across all settings, TTSR maintains consistent gains over baselines, demonstrating its effectiveness as a general test-time self-evolving framework.

6
Conclusion

We propose
TTSR
, a self-reflective and self-evolving test-time training framework for improving the reasoning ability of large language models.
TTSR addresses key challenges in test-time adaptation, including unreliable pseudo-labels on highly difficult test questions and the lack of effective mechanisms for adapting to model-specific reasoning weaknesses.
By explicitly reflecting on failed reasoning trajectories and synthesizing targeted variant questions that remain within a learnable regime, TTSR enables stable and effective reasoning improvement during inference.
Experimental results on a range of challenging mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that TTSR consistently improves performance across different model backbones.
Overall, this work shows that self-reflection–driven self-evolution provides a promising pathway for advancing test-time training.

Appendix B
Method Details

B.1
Similarity Metric Definition

To quantify the similarity between two questions in the similarity penalty, we adopt a sequence-based similarity measure that captures contiguous overlapping spans between two token sequences.
This metric is designed to penalize near-duplicate generations while remaining robust to minor surface variations.

Sequence Representation.

Given two questions represented as token sequences
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, we define their total length as

T=|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|,

(13)

where
|\cdot|
denotes the number of tokens in the sequence.

Matching Blocks.

We identify a set of non-overlapping matching blocks between
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
.
Each matching block is defined as a contiguous subsequence that appears in both sequences.
The matching process first finds the longest contiguous matching subsequence and then recursively searches for additional matches in the remaining left and right segments.
Let the resulting set of matching blocks be represented as

\mathcal{B}=\{(i,j,n)\},

where
(i,j,n)
denotes a matching block of length
n
starting at position
i
in
S_{1}
and position
j
in
S_{2}
.
The total number of matched tokens is then defined as

M=\sum_{(i,j,n)\in\mathcal{B}}n.

(14)

Similarity Ratio.

Using the above definitions, we compute the normalized similarity ratio between
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
as

\mathrm{sim}(S_{1},S_{2})=\frac{2M}{T},

(15)

which takes values in the range
[0,1]
.
A higher similarity ratio indicates greater overlap between the two sequences, while a lower value reflects more substantial differences in surface form. This similarity measure is used to compute the pairwise similarity scores required by the similarity penalty described in Section
4.2
.
By focusing on contiguous matching spans rather than isolated token overlap, the metric effectively discourages trivial paraphrasing while allowing moderate overlap that preserves shared reasoning structure.

Explicitly state how the new question will be designed to more reliably trigger the given reasoning weakness, while remaining solvable and fair.
