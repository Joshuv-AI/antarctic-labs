 ABSTRACT

Molten salts such as LiCl/KCl mixtures act as a liquid electrolyte and solvent in pyroprocessing enabling
high-temperature separation of uranium, transuranics, and fission products from spent nuclear fuel. Molten
salts are also used in a wide range of applications including electrodeposition, electrorefining, and as
fuel/coolant salts in molten salt reactors. The chloride activity (αCl-), which plays a critical role in the
complex chemical phenomena that occur in these media, can be described quantitively in terms of model
acid-base equilibria. Chloride ion activity influences not only the electrochemical and corrosive behavior
of salt but also its conductivity and redox characteristics. Because electrochemical measurements are
instrumental in studying the properties of molten salts, having a reliable reference electrode that can endure
high temperatures and extended service life typical of pyroprocessing molten salt environments is essential.
In this work, we aim to develop a reliable and convenient method for measuring chloride ion activity over
a wide range of temperatures and salt compositions in actinide salt mixtures. Specifically, the chloride
reference electrode is tested in a typical pyroprocessing salt mixture, LiCl-KCl-UCl3, with the goal of
measuring the chloride activity as a function of actinide chloride concentration and temperature. During FY
2026 the method was applied to a zirconium chloride surrogate melt and to both uranium trichloride and
uranium tetrachloride in the LiCl-KCl eutectic. There were challenges associated with the measurements.
The surrogate measurement returned negative cell potentials above 550 °C, corresponding to a chloride
activity greater than that of the fully dissociated alkali chloride reference; uranium trichloride was oxidized
to uranium tetrachloride on contact with the chlorine gas required by the electrode; and the uranium
tetrachloride measurement was lost when a pressure imbalance forced the melt through the separating frit.
A separate leak test of an alumina-coated frit prepared by atomic layer deposition showed that the coated
separator does not hold a composition difference between the two half-cells for the duration of a
measurement. This report documents each of these outcomes, identifies the responsible failure mode, and
defines the corrective actions that will be carried into the next performance period.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Molten chloride salts have garnered considerable attention because of their application in pyrochemical
processing, electrorefining, and as coolant/fuel salts in molten salt reactors. [1-4] Chloride activity (αCl-)
plays a crucial role in determining the properties of these salts in pyroprocessing and can be described
quantitively in terms of acid-base equilibrium models. Chloride ion activity influences not only the
electrochemical and corrosive behavior of salt, but also its conductivity and redox characteristics. Because
electrochemical measurements are essential for investigating molten salt properties and evaluating
corrosion processes, the availability of a reliable reference electrode capable of withstanding the high
temperatures and extended operating time typical of molten salt environments is essential. This need is
particularly evident in processes such as electrorefining, where the electrochemical separation and
extraction of actinides require a stable reference electrode.

Various reference electrodes have been investigated for use in molten salt applications, including
metal/metal ion electrodes (such as Ag/AgCl and Pb/PbCl2) and metal pseudo-reference electrodes such as
Pt and W rods. [6, 7] However, each approach has significant limitations. Metal/metal ion electrodes
require the precise fabrication of a conductive membrane to isolate the electrode from the salt, making long-
term stability difficult to achieve. In contrast, metal pseudo-electrodes are prone to potential drift over
time, which limits their long-term usefulness. We have focused on developing an electrochemical technique
for the direct quantification of α(Cl⁻) by measuring the potential difference across molten salt systems using
a Cl₂/Cl⁻ reference electrode. This method offers a practical means for continuous, in situ assessment of

 1

chloride activity in a variety of molten metal chloride environments, providing a valuable diagnostic tool
for industrial and research applications. [8]

In this work, we will continue the development of a chlorine sensitive reference electrode for the in-situ
measurement of chloride activity (pCl-) in high-temperature molten salts. We have tested the reference
electrode in LiCl-KCl-MgCl2 and demonstrated a reduction of chloride ion activity with increasing MgCl2
concentration. We have extended to focus on salts relevant to pyrochemical processing. Initial tests have
included a LiCl-KCl eutectic base salt with the LiCl-KCl-UCl3 ternary mixture where the goal is to measure
the chloride activity as a function of UCl3 concentration and temperature using the chloride reference
electrode.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL

2.1 DESCRIPTION OF CHLORIDE REFERENCE ELECTRODE

We have been exploring the use of the standard chlorine electrode for measuring the (αCl -) throughout this
project using a dual-chamber reference electrode. One chamber contains the working solution with
dissolved analyte while the other side contains the reference melt with alkali halide mixture assumed to be
fully dissociated. The two chambers are separated by a fine frit and both sides are exposed to equal pressure
chlorine gas until saturated. A carbon electrode placed in either solution measures the open circuit potential
between the two chambers. The voltage difference between the two electrodes allows for direct
measurement of αCl-. Figure 1 shows a schematic depiction of the dual-chamber reference electrode that
we have been using in this project and has been shown in previous reports.

2.3 SALT PREPARATION AND CELL OPERATION

Salts were purified, handled, and characterized following the procedures established for this project [13].
The LiCl-KCl eutectic used as the reference melt and as the solvent for the working melt was taken from a
single batch so that any residual difference in purity between the two half-cells was minimized. All loading
operations were performed in an inert atmosphere glovebox. The working half-cell was charged with the
analyte-bearing eutectic and the reference half-cell with the pure eutectic from the same batch.
The assembled cell was heated under dynamic high vacuum until the salt on both sides of the separating
frit was visibly molten and the melt level in each chamber contacted the frit, which is required for ionic
transport between the half-cells. The cell was then backfilled with flowing argon, and the graphite
electrodes were lowered into the working and reference melts under argon. Only after the electrodes were
in place was the gas supply switched to chlorine, which was flowed through both chambers until the melts
were saturated. Open circuit potential was then recorded between the two graphite electrodes with a Gamry
potentiostat. Temperature was changed stepwise during a run, and the potential was allowed to stabilize at
each setpoint before the value was recorded.

 3. RESULTS

Four experimental campaigns were completed during FY 2026. The first used ZrCl 4 as a non-radioactive
surrogate for a tetravalent actinide cation, and the second and third applied the method to UCl 3 and UCl4
dissolved in the LiCl-KCl eutectic. The fourth was a leak test of a separator prepared by atomic layer
deposition, undertaken to address the salt diffusion between half-cells identified in the previous
performance period [12]. Each campaign is described below, together with the failure mode it exposed and
the corrective action it defines.

3.1 ZIRCONIUM CHLORIDE SURROGATE MEASUREMENTS

ZrCl4 was selected as a surrogate for tetravalent uranium because it is non-radioactive, it presents the same
formal cation charge as U4+, and it is a strong chloride acceptor that forms hexachlorozirconate complexes
in alkali chloride melts. A working melt of 5.0 wt% ZrCl4 in the LiCl-KCl eutectic was measured against a
pure LiCl-KCl eutectic reference using graphite electrodes, with chlorine flowing through both chambers.
The full open circuit potential trace is shown in Figure 2. Temperature was stepped through the sequence
550, 600, 450, 400, 600, 650, and 700 °C. The order was chosen after the initial ramp to 600 °C failed to
reach a stable reading, at which point the remaining low-temperature setpoints were taken before the high-
temperature setpoints.

At 400, 450, and 550 °C the potential is positive, between 14 and 40 mV, which by Equation 7 corresponds
to α(Cl-) below unity and is the expected behavior for a melt containing a Lewis-acidic tetravalent cation.

 5

At and above 600 °C the potential is negative, reaching -87.1 mV at 700 °C. A negative potential implies
α(Cl-) greater than unity, that is, a working melt containing more free chloride than the fully dissociated
alkali chloride reference, which is not a physically meaningful result for a ZrCl4-bearing melt. The offset is
not attributable to the baseline of the cell: pure LiCl-KCl eutectic measured against itself in this apparatus
returns approximately 0.1 mV [8].
The temperature dependence is also inverted with respect to expectation. In the LiCl-KCl-MgCl2 system,
α(Cl-) rises toward unity as temperature increases because the chloride coordination complexes are
entropically destabilized, so ΔE should decrease in magnitude and approach zero at high temperature rather
than cross zero and grow in the negative direction. Two further observations constrain the interpretation.
First, the two measurements at 600 °C differ by 15.8 mV, so the deviation is not reproducible within a
single run even at a fixed setpoint. Second, the first of those two measurements was taken during a
temperature ramp before the potential had settled, but the second was taken under settled conditions and is
still negative, so incomplete thermal equilibration alone does not account for the sign.
Several explanations are consistent with the data and cannot be separated without further measurement.
ZrCl4 is volatile and sublimes near 331 °C, so at the higher setpoints a significant fraction of the solute may
leave the working melt and redistribute through the shared gas path into the reference chamber,
progressively reducing and then reversing the composition difference the cell is intended to measure. ZrCl4
is also reactive toward fused silica, and formation of zirconium oxychloride species at the expense of the
cell body would release chloride into the working melt and raise the free chloride content there in a way
that is chemically real but is not the quantity the measurement is intended to report. A thermal gradient
between the two graphite electrodes would additionally contribute a thermoelectric potential that grows
with furnace setpoint and is independent of chloride activity. Because the priority for the performance
period was to advance to uranium-bearing melts, this experiment was not repeated, and the anomaly remains
open. Resolving it is a prerequisite for interpreting any subsequent measurement on a tetravalent system,
and the planned diagnostic is a matched-composition run in which both half-cells are charged with the same
ZrCl4-bearing melt, which should return zero potential at every temperature if the cell itself is sound.

3.2 URANIUM TRICHLORIDE IN LiCl-KCl EUTECTIC

A pre-fused stock of 5.0 wt% UCl3 in LiCl-KCl eutectic was loaded into the working side of the cell inside
the glovebox and diluted with pure LiCl-KCl eutectic to a target concentration of 2.0 wt% UCl 3. The
reference side was charged with pure LiCl-KCl eutectic. The cell was heated to 400 °C under dynamic high
vacuum until all salt was visibly molten. The charged volume was smaller than intended because of an error
in the volume calculation, but the melt still contacted the frit on both sides, so ionic transport between the
chambers remained possible and the experiment was continued. The cell was backfilled with flowing argon,
the graphite electrodes were placed into the working and reference melts, and the gas was then switched to
pure chlorine.
The melt changed color immediately on initiation of chlorine flow, from the dark purple characteristic of
U(III) to the light green of dilute U(IV), as shown in Figure 3. The reaction is given in Equation 8.

When looking at the thermodynamics of this process, this outcome is expected since the Cl2/Cl- couple is
substantially more oxidizing than the U(IV)/U(III) couple in the LiCl-KCl eutectic [14], so any U(III) in
contact with a chlorine-saturated melt will be oxidized quantitatively. The consequence is a limitation of
the technique rather than a fault in its execution: the Cl2/Cl- electrode cannot be used to measure the chloride
activity of a U(III)-bearing melt, because the gas that defines the reference potential destroys the analyte.
Measurement of α(Cl-) in LiCl-KCl-UCl3 therefore requires an electrode that does not impose a chlorine
potential on the working melt, such as the membrane-type electrode with an internal reference that is under
development in this project [12], or a solid-state reference electrode of the type recently reported for high-
temperature chloride melts [7].
The open circuit potential was recorded after the conversion in order to characterize the behavior of the
cell, and the trace is shown in Figure 4, however, the generated signal was not usable. The excursions in
the first 1,000 s were traced to a poor electrode connection, which was corrected once identified. The regular
square excursions between the potentiostat limits that dominate the record above 600 °C are attributed to
gas bubbles forming on the graphite surface and intermittently breaking contact between the electrode and
the melt. The small charged volume aggravated this behavior, because a shallow melt column across the
frit raises the solution resistance between the half-cells and makes the measured potential far more sensitive
to momentary loss of electrode wetting.

3.3 URANIUM TETRACHLORIDE IN LiCl-KCl EUTECTIC

Because UCl3 converts to UCl4 under the conditions imposed by the electrode, the logical next step was to
begin with the tetravalent species. A working melt of 2.0 wt% UCl4 in LiCl-KCl eutectic was loaded into
the working side of the cell inside the glovebox, with pure LiCl-KCl eutectic in the reference side.
During assembly it was found that the newly fabricated cell had been made without the central connecting
tube between the two chambers. That crossover equalizes the pressure between the chambers while gas is
flowed through both sides, and it is present on the cell used for the earlier measurements. On the new cell
the corresponding member was solid rather than hollow. The salt was melted under vacuum with both sides
of the cell connected to the manifold, and the experiment was continued by connecting the two head spaces
externally through the stoppers with tubing.
The external connection proved insufficient. When chlorine flow was started to saturate the melt, a slight
pressure mismatch between the two chambers forced the salt through the fine frit and combined the working
and reference solutions before any measurement could be started, as shown in Figure 5. No open circuit
potential data were obtained.

 8

Figure 5. Electrochemical cell during chlorine saturation of the 2.0 wt% UCl4 working melt (left) and detail
of the separating frit (right), showing the working melt driven through the frit into the reference chamber.

The sensitivity of the configuration to pressure imbalance is easily quantified. Taking the density of the
molten LiCl-KCl eutectic as approximately 1.6 g cm-3, a pressure difference of only about 0.16 kPa, or 1.6
mbar, between the two chambers is sufficient to displace the melt by 1 cm across the frit. An external tubing
connection has finite flow impedance, so a transient imbalance of this size develops readily at the moment
gas flow is initiated even when the head spaces are nominally joined. The fine frit offers very little resistance
to bulk liquid flow driven by such a differential, in contrast to its useful resistance to diffusive mixing.

3.4 EVALUATION OF THE ALUMINA-COATED SEPARATOR

The measurements described above, and those reported in the previous performance period, are all limited
by diffusion of salt between the two chambers through the fine frit, which sets an upper bound on the
duration of any single measurement [12]. Atomic layer deposition was applied to reduce the effective pore
size of the separator. A fine quartz frit was coated with Al 2O3 using trimethylaluminum and water as the
two reactive precursors, with 250 deposition cycles completed. At the growth rate measured previously for
this process, that corresponds to a layer of approximately 25 nm at the frit surface, with a decreasing
thickness into the interior of the pores. The coated frit was sealed into a quartz tube (Figure 6)and assembled
into the two-chamber cell shown in Figure 7, and the assembly was tested at 500 °C.

The reference chamber was charged with pure LiCl-KCl eutectic and the working chamber with LiCl-KCl
eutectic containing 5.0 wt% MgCl2, a composition measured during the previous performance period to
give a stable potential corresponding to α(Cl-) = 0.970 [8, 12]. A separator that holds the composition
difference should therefore return a small, stable, positive potential of a few millivolts. The result is shown
in Figure 8.

Figure 8. Open circuit potential measured across the alumina-coated frit between a pure LiCl-KCl eutectic
reference and a LiCl-KCl eutectic working melt containing 5.0 wt% MgCl2 at 500 °C. (Left) Full record;
the salt melted after approximately 800 s. (Right) Detail of the record after 1,500 s, showing the potential

The salt melted after approximately 800 s, after which the potential settled to about -450 mV and then rose
monotonically, reaching approximately -95 mV after a further 4,500 s and continuing to flatten toward zero.
Neither the magnitude nor the sign of the initial value is consistent with the known chloride activity of the
working composition, which indicates that a stable and well-defined liquid junction was never established.
More importantly, the direction of the drift is diagnostic. A potential that decays toward zero is the signature

 10

of the two half-cells equilibrating in composition, that is, of chloride exchange through the separator
proceeding until no activity difference remains between the chambers. The alumina coating therefore did
not reduce leakage sufficiently to maintain a composition gradient over the time required for a
measurement.
The test does not distinguish between the possible leak paths. The deposited layer is thinnest in the interior
of the pores, where the precursors penetrate least, so the coating may simply be incomplete where it matters
most. The seal between the frit and the quartz tube is an alternative path that would produce the same
signature. It is also possible that the alumina layer is attacked by the melt at 500 °C over the duration of the
test. This result shows that a fused-silica frit coated using these ALD conditions, 250 coating cycles with
short duration exposure, and sealed in this manner, is unsuitable for measurements of the required duration.
The next steps are to increase the number of deposition cycles and to characterize the coated frit by gas
permeation before it is committed to a salt experiment, so that separators are screened without consuming
a full measurement campaign, and in parallel to continue development of the lanthanide oxychloride
membrane approach reported previously [12].

Two differences from the magnesium case are worth noting in advance. The coordination number is six

 4. SUMMARY

The objective for this performance period was to extend the Cl2/Cl- reference electrode from the LiCl-KCl-
MgCl2 system, where it has been demonstrated and published [8], to salts relevant to pyrochemical
processing of spent nuclear fuel. Four campaigns were completed, and each identified a specific and distinct
obstacle.
The ZrCl4 surrogate measurement returned potentials of a reasonable magnitude but of the wrong sign
above 550 °C, corresponding to a chloride activity greater than that of the fully dissociated alkali chloride
reference. The volatility of ZrCl4, its reactivity toward the fused silica cell body, and a possible

 11

thermoelectric contribution between electrodes at different temperatures are all consistent with the
observation, and the experiment was not repeated because the priority was to advance to uranium-bearing
melts. The UCl3 measurement established a fundamental limitation of the technique: U(III) is oxidized
quantitatively to U(IV) on contact with the chlorine gas that defines the reference potential, so a chlorine
electrode cannot report the chloride activity of a U(III)-bearing melt. The UCl4 measurement was lost before
it began when a cell fabricated without the internal pressure-equalization crossover allowed a small pressure
imbalance to force the working melt through the separating frit. The leak test of the alumina-coated frit
showed the measured potential drifting steadily toward zero against a known expected value of 1.89 mV,
demonstrating that a separator coated by 250 atomic layer deposition cycles does not hold a composition
difference between the half-cells for the time a measurement requires.
Taken together, these results sharpen the requirements on the apparatus and on the technique. Two of the
four obstacles are engineering issues with direct remedies that will be implemented before the next
campaign: restoration of the internal pressure crossover with bubbler-referenced venting of both chambers,
and verification of salt volume, electrode connection, and separator permeability before a cell is committed
to a measurement. The remaining two are more consequential. The separator remains the limiting
component of the dual-chamber design, and work will continue on both thicker deposited coatings, screened
by gas permeation before use, and the lanthanide oxychloride membrane approach reported previously [12].
The incompatibility of chlorine gas with U(III) means that the trivalent actinide chemistry central to
electrorefining is out of reach of the chlorine electrode in its present form, and the membrane-type electrode
with an internal reference solution, which does not impose a chlorine potential on the working melt,
becomes the primary route to that measurement.
