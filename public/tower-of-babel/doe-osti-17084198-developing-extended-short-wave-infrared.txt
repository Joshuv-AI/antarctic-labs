LDRD PROJECT NUMBER: 240596

LDRD PROJECT TITLE:
Developing extended short-wave infrared detectors with improved radiation
hardness for space-based surveillance applications

PROJECT TEAM MEMBERS:
Aaron Muhowski (5266), Evan Anderson (5229), Lilian Casias (6751)

ABSTRACT:

Space-based strategic applications rely on sensors that operate in the short-wave infrared regime
for detection and surveillance. Materials used in space are subjected to a harsh radiation
environment holding high-energy particles, and consequently space-based infrared detectors suffer
from radiation-induced damage. In state-of-the-art infrared detectors based on inorganic
semiconductors, the high-energy particles interact with the atomic structure of the material and
result in degradation of device performance and lifetime. This project is focused on developing a
detector configuration for the short-wave and extended short-wave infrared regions that
demonstrates enhanced radiation hardness, without negatively impacting other device parameters.

Our approach is centered on one key element: use of quantum-confined nanostructures embedded
in the detectors to improve radiation hardness of infrared detectors. We use bulk semiconductor
materials as an exemplar system, and it is shown that detectors integrated with quantum dashes
perform ~5x better in radiation environments when compared to detectors without the
nanostructures. The structural parameters (thickness, density, size etc.) of the quantum dashes are
found to have a significant impact on the performance of these detectors. Due to the inevitably low
detection volume (few tens of nanometers) of nanostructures, the responsivity and efficiency of
the detectors are sub-par, and we offer future directions/preliminary theoretical considerations to
mitigate this limitation. This project also had discovery research impact by providing clarification
on the dimensionality of quantum dashes based on radiation hardness.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS:

State-of-the-art detectors in the shortwave infrared (SWIR) and extended shortwave infrared (e-
SWIR) regimes are based on two material systems1,2: HgCdTe and III-V semiconductors. While
both technologies have positive aspects, neither is well-positioned to meet mission needs in this
area. HgCdTe exhibits superior device metrics, but is plagued by issues with non-uniformity, high
cost, and cooling requirements2. On the other hand, while III-V detectors don’t have
manufacturability issues, most are complex superlattices/heterostructures3 or are prone to high
radiation damage.4

The problem statement/key technology gap to be addressed in the R&D is as follows: current
choices for SWIR/e-SWIR detectors are not ideal and are limited by radiation performance and/or
manufacturability issues. Our proposed solution for the problem above: opt for III-V detectors
(no manufacturability issues) and use quantum-confined nanostructures as the absorbing medium
to enhance radiation hardness. While working towards this big-picture objective, we seek to
answer the following questions:

 1. Do the nanostructures used for this target wavelength (SWIR/e-SWIR) exhibit any
 radiation hardness at the material level?
 2. Can the radiation-hardness of SWIR/e-SWIR detectors be enhanced by using 3-
 dimensional (3D) quantum-confined nanostructures as the absorbing medium?
 3. Are we trading off other device metrics such as quantum efficiency/responsivity for
 improved radiation tolerance?

Summary of results:

 1. InAs quantum dashes (QDashes) grown on InP substrates were identified as the
 nanostructures used for this R&D based on the target wavelength (additional details in the
 Discussions section). While InAs/InP QDashes have demonstrated efficient
 emission/absorption in the SWIR/e-SWIR regime and have been used in various device
 platforms, the radiation performance of these nanostructures has not been quantified at a
 material level. We performed a straightforward pre- and post-radiation (neutron)
 photoluminescence (PL) study to confirm their radiation hardness. Based on this study,
 QDashes emitting around 2µm were found to exhibit ~5x better radiation performance
 compared to quantum wells (QWs) in a similar wavelength range. This also helps us draw
 rudimentary conclusions about the dimensionality of QDashes. Based on other reports5, the
 demonstration of radiation hardness on a material level implies a certain degree of carrier
 localization.

 2. Detector structures with and without integrated quantum dashes were designed, epitaxially
 grown, fabricated, and tested. The results showed typical device parameters for SWIR
 detectors including dark current densities and responsivities.
 3. We also performed a pre- and post-radiation device testing study of the detector structures.
 The dark current density levels are compared pre- and post-radiation on detectors with and
 without integrated QDashes in the detection volume. This showed similar results as the PL
 study – detectors with integrated quantum dashes performed ~5x better under radiation
 compared to detectors without QDashes.

The above-mentioned results are summarized in the “Technical Insights” section of the Addendum
slides (slide 5-8).

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY:
The R&D methodology used in this project can be split into (4) separate but complementary
thrusts:

 1. Design: Due to the scoping nature of this project, the design of experiments was
 straightforward. With the big-picture goal of quantifying radiation resistance of detectors
 integrated with nanostructures, (2) experiments were designed and executed: PL-based rad
 hardness and device-level rad hardness of QDashes.

 For the PL study, a InP-based structure containing 5 stacks of InAs QDashes embedded in
 an AlInGaAs (50/50 InGaAs/AlInAs) matrix was used. The reference sample for this study
 was a 5-stack QW structure with similar emission wavelength (1.8-2µm).

 For studying the device-level rad hardness, a simple p-i-n AlInGaAs (50/50
 InGaAs/AlInAs) structure was used. The p- and n-doped regions (doping density: 1e18cm-
 3
 ) were 200nm thick and the intrinsic region was 1µm thick. The InAs QDash layers were
 embedded in the middle of the intrinsic region, and the number of QDash layers was varied
       between 0-40.

 Both PL and device structures were streamlined to deduce radiation hardness of QDashes
 without other factors leading to inconsistencies.

 2. Epitaxial growth: All structures described in this study were epitaxially grown using
 molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) on InP substrates. The native oxide on the InP substrates
 was first thermally desorbed at 540℃ for 5 minutes. The substrate was then cooled to

 490℃ for the growth of the layer structure. A V/III beam equivalent pressure (BEP) of 13-
 15 is maintained throughout the growth. On this MBE system, the substrate temperature is
 measured using a pyrometer and the growth rates are calibrated using reflection high
 energy electron diffraction (RHEED).

3. Device fabrication: For device fabrication, conventional lithography was performed to
 fabricate all the devices. Citric acid (40 g)/H3PO4 (10 ml)/H2O2 (10 ml)/H2O (240 ml) was
 used to wet-etch the mesas. The contact metal (12 nm Ti/150 nm Au) was deposited by
 electron-beam evaporation as the top and bottom contact layers. For surface passivation,
 SU-8 was used to conformally cover the mesa sidewalls.

 Mask: The circular mesa devices are designed in different sizes for variable-area diode
   analysis (VADA), with diameters of 100, 150, 200, 250, 350, and 500 µm. There is a
 pattern for the transfer length method (TLM) on the right side of the die. This pattern is
 dedicated to measuring the contact resistance between the top and bottom contact layers
 and the metal.
 For a second set, devices were fabricated through common cleanroom photolithographic
 processes at MESA. Devices were reticulated through a mesa etch to the heavily n-doped
 InP substrate. The wet etch was nonselective to the epitaxial device layers, consisting of
 1:4:45 H3PO4: H2O2:H2O. The ohmic contacts to the highly doped n-type layer were a
 metal stack of Ge/Au/Ni/Au followed by a 30 s rapid thermal anneal at 380 °C under
 vacuum with an Ar purge gas flowing. The ohmic contacts to the highly doped p-type
 contact layer at the top of each device mesa consisted of a metal stack of Ti/Pt/Au. An etch
 of 1:10 HCl:H2O to remove the native oxide was performed immediately before each
 metallization step with metal deposited through electron beam evaporation.

4. Testing: Dark current–voltage (I–V) characterization was performed on devices at room
 temperature using a probe station connected to an Agilent 4156C semiconductor parameter
 analyzer. Measurements were conducted on devices of various diameters to investigate the
 dominant dark-current mechanisms. In particular, the dependence of dark current on device
 size was analyzed to distinguish between bulk-generated current, which scales with device
 area, and surface-related current, which scales with device perimeter.

 To measure the spectral response, the IR source of a Fourier transform infrared (FTIR)
 spectrometer is focused onto the sample with an off-axis parabolic mirror. A preamplifier
 is used to amplify the signal from the sample. The raw spectrum from the sample is divided
 by the response from a spectrally flat detector to account for the FTIR’s optics.

presented in the Introduction section.

For a material level assessment on radiation hardness of QDashes, a pre- and post-radiation
(neutron) PL study was conducted with a QW reference. Figure 1 (left) below shows the pre-
radiation PL spectra from both the QDash and QW samples at room temperature. As expected, the
QDash PL profile shows inhomogeneous broadening attributed to the size variation of the
QDashes. The wavelength of emission is comparable between the two structures (~1.9µm).

 Figure 1

The comparison between QWs and QDashes pre- and post-radiation PL results is shown in figure
1(right). Integrated PL (area under the curve) is plotted at different measurement temperatures (4-
90K). This shows a stark difference between radiation behavior of the two active regions: the
degradation in integrated PL for QW-containing structures is ~5x (4.78) higher compared to
QDash containing structures with the QDashes showing minimal degradation. This advantage with
3-dimensional nanostructures (lower PL degradation) has previously been observed with quantum
dots. It is attributed to a high degree of carrier localization in these nanostructures that confines
the damage and alleviates any long-range degradation.

With further analysis of this data, the difference between detector structures with and without
QDashes becomes apparent. Figure 7 summarizes these findings – At -1V bias, the increase in
dark current level for a p-i-n detector structure (without QDashes) is ~9.25x, while the device with
QDashes (10x stacks) only experienced a 1.95x increase. The contrast between the two devices
gets more distinct at -5V, where the QDash-containing device only recorded a modest 2.26x
increase in dark current and the level in the device without QDashes increased by ~26x. This
clearly demonstrates a higher radiation tolerance of detector structures that contain QDashes – the
improvement is ~4x at -1V and >10x at -5V.

  Figure 7: Pre and post-radiation IV curves for detector structures with and without QDashes

The enhanced radiation hardness of detectors containing QDashes can be attributed to the potential
of interfaces to act as defect sinks6-9. The integration of 3-dimensional nanostructures introduces
a large volume fraction of interfaces which have shown higher resistance to multiple forms of
radiation degradation. The strongest physical hypothesis is strain-assisted, interface-mediated
atomic-defect recombination, enhanced by short diffusion distances. QD-based PDs, which could
reduce the buildup of electrically active generation centers and therefore suppress the radiation-
induced increase in dark current. The coherent-precipitate discussion in Ref. 6, Section 5.8 [1], is
the most relevant foundation, while the PRB publication establishes why interface efficiency must
accompany high interface density. In addition, the property of QDashes showing enhanced
radiation hardness may also imply a certain level of carrier localization, which has been a point of
contention in the community.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS:
The natural next step for this exploratory effort is to pursue a longer-term LDRD project that
addresses other performance metrics of the detectors (in addition to radiation hardness). We have
shown that the use of 3-dimensional nanostructures in the detection volume increases radiation
performance, but this also negatively affects responsivity/efficiency of these devices (due to the
extremely low thickness of nanostructures). In order to compensate for lower
responsivity/efficiency, we propose an avalanche photodiode (APD) configuration in these
devices. In addition, the integration of a photonic crystal on top of the detector could lead to
higher light retention/absorption. This device configuration is illustrated in figure 8 below with

CONCLUSION: The results presented in this report support the hypothesis of enhanced
radiation hardness in detector structures that contain 3-dimensional nanostructures. Based on pre-
and post-radiation PL/device characterization, we come to the conclusion that SWIR/e-SWIR
detectors that use QDashes in the detection volume show ~4-10x (depending on the bias voltage)
improved radiation behavior compared to devices without QDashes. The material-level
assessment is based on decrease in PL (after radiation) from QDashes compared to QWs and the
device-level assessment is made based on increase in dark current of detector structure with and
without QDashes. We attribute the improved radiation hardness of QDash-containing devices to
an increased volume fraction of interfaces. This also provides some preliminary understanding of
dimensionalty in QDashes (which has been a long-standing point of contention in the
community) since the demonstration of radiation hardness could imply a certain level of carrier
localization.
