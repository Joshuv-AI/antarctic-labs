A biocatalytic platform for the stereoselective synthesis of diverse atropisomeric compounds using engineered metalloenzymes
Authors: Xiaochen Cai, Dong Cui, Yuchen Chu, Yanan Rui, Xinyu Duan, Yongzhen Peng, Zhiguo Wang, Jian Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atropisomeric compounds are widely distributed in natural products and pharmaceuticals and serve as key scaffolds in asymmetric catalysis. Despite their remarkable structural diversity, current synthetic efforts have primarily focused on C–C biaryl atropisomers, while the synthesis of other classes of atropisomeric compounds has been much less explored. Biocatalysis offers precise control over the stereoselectivity of reactions, however, its application in the synthesis of atropisomeric compounds remains at an early stage. In this work, we report a versatile biocatalytic platform that integrates metalloenzyme-catalyzed reduction reactions to access a broad array of structurally diverse atropisomeric compounds, including C–N atropisomers, diaryl ethers, and styrene derivatives, with high yields and excellent enantioselectivities. Molecular dynamics simulations provided mechanistic insights into the origin of the high stereoselectivity, and this study expands the repertoire of metalloenzyme-catalyzed, new-to-nature transformations for the synthesis of valuable molecules.

INTRODUCTION

Atropisomerism, defined by restricted bond rotation that gives rise to molecular chirality, is widespread in natural products and pharmaceuticals (Fig. 1A) (1–4). In addition to their biological relevance, atropisomeric compounds play crucial roles as catalysts and ligands in asymmetric catalysis (5). Atropisomeric compounds display structural diversity, with C–C biaryl atropisomers being the most extensively studied owing to the wide range of synthetic methods available for their construction (6, 7). In comparison, the synthesis of other classes of atropisomers, such as C–N atropisomers, axially chiral diaryl ethers, and axially chiral styrenes, remains relatively underexplored (8, 9). This is largely attributable to their enhanced rotational flexibility and lower stereochemical barriers, which render the control and preservation of axial chirality considerably more challenging. Given the significance of these structures, there is a growing demand for more versatile and efficient synthetic strategies capable of accessing structurally diverse atropisomers.

Enzyme catalysis has garnered significant attention in both academia and industry due to its exceptional stereoselectivity (10–12). While considerable progress has been made in the enzymatic synthesis of centrally chiral compounds, the development of biocatalytic strategies for atropisomer construction remains limited (13, 14). Natural products constitute a structurally rich reservoir of diverse atropisomers, providing a basis for discovering enzymes capable of catalyzing their synthesis (15). However, the inherent complexity of these molecules often necessitates enzymes with high catalytic specificity, limiting their broader application in synthetic methodologies (16). To date, only a limited number of biocatalytic methods for the synthesis of atropisomers have been developed. Representative examples include P450 catalyzed radical reactions (17–19), halogenases (20), lipases (21–23), ketoreductases (24–26), alcohol dehydrogenases (27–29), imine reductases (30–34), and ene-reductases (35) catalyzed the functionalization of prestereogenic or racemic atropisomeric precursors. In addition, artificial metalloenzymes have been engineered to catalyze non-natural atroposelective transformations, such as the Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling (36) and olefin metathesis (37). To date, the biocatalytic synthesis of atropisomers still relies mainly on a limited number of enzyme classes, and current strategies are typically confined to the transformation of a single type of atropisomeric substrate. Given the structural diversity of atropisomers, it is therefore of great significance to develop efficient and general biocatalytic methods to expand the toolbox for atropisomer synthesis.

The incorporation of reactive intermediates, characteristic of traditional chemical processes, into enzyme active sites to enable enzyme-catalyzed new-to-nature reactions has significantly broadened the scope of biocatalysis (38–40). Among these, enzyme-catalyzed transformations of carbene and radical intermediates have seen remarkable progress (18, 41–54). Metal hydride species, as one of the most important intermediates in traditional organic chemistry, play a crucial role in hydrogenation reactions. However, enzymatic transfer hydrogenation involving metal hydride intermediates remains rare, with reported examples limited to ketone reductions by the Hartwig (55, 56) and Ward groups (57, 58) and olefin reductions by the Ji group (59). Our group has also employed this intermediate to achieve asymmetric transfer hydrogenation of imines (60). Recently, the Ward group and Athavale group independently utilized this intermediate to realize enzyme-catalyzed metal hydride atom transfer (MHAT) reactions (61, 62). However, to date, all reported examples have focused on the synthesis of centrally chiral compounds, while the construction of axially chiral atropisomers remains unreported. Harnessing the structural diversity of natural metalloenzyme libraries, we aim to develop a broadly applicable method for synthesizing diverse atropisomers, thus offering a robust strategy for atropisomer synthesis (Fig. 1B).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

We initially selected 1-(2-(dimethylamino)phenyl)-1H-indole-2-carbaldehyde (1a) as a model substrate to assess a panel of representative metalloenzymes, employing phenylsilane as the reductant. Because this substrate can racemize via a transient Lewis pair interaction between the NMe2 and the aldehyde groups, its combination with a highly enantioselective reduction offers a practical platform for dynamic kinetic resolution (26, 63). In line with this design, most of the tested enzymes exhibited measurable activity and stereoselectivity, thereby validating the feasibility of the transformation (Fig. 2). Notably, human carbonic anhydrase (hCAII) from Homo sapiens, containing a zinc-centered active site, afforded (R)-2a in 66% yield with 96% ee (Fig. 2, Entry 5). To further probe the system, a range of reducing agents was examined. Reaction efficiency decreased markedly with increasing steric hindrance of the silanes, and the use of NADPH as a reductant completely abolished product formation, demonstrating pronounced orthogonality relative to conventional ketoreductase-catalyzed reductions (table S4). Interestingly, the use of HBpin as the hydrogen source still resulted in detectable product formation, representing a deviation from previous reports. Furthermore, additional experiments confirmed that the reaction does not proceed in the absence of either a reducing agent or an enzyme (Fig. 2, Entries 7, 8).

To further enhance the catalytic efficiency, hCAII was subjected to protein engineering. Considering that residues surrounding the active site exert the greatest influence on reactivity, eight residues in this region were selected as hotspots for mutagenesis. Given the significant role of steric effects in governing substrate binding within the enzyme active site, alanine (A), leucine (L), and phenylalanine (F) were selected as representative residues for site-directed mutagenesis (table S2). Several variants exhibited significant improvements in both yield and selectivity. For instance, E106L, W208L, and W208A consistently increased the yield to approximately 90% while maintaining excellent stereoselectivity (98% ee). The optimal variant, V121A, achieved an even higher yield of 94% with near-perfect stereoselectivity (99% ee) (Fig. 2, Entry 6), and further saturation mutagenesis at position V121 did not afford improved results (table S3). The turnover number (TON) of the reaction was determined to be approximately 840, which is comparable in magnitude to those reported for alcohol dehydrogenases (27).

Having established the optimal reaction conditions, we proceeded to investigate the substrate scope of the atropisomer synthesis (Fig. 3). Initially, a series of C–N atropisomer-containing precursors was subjected to evaluation (Fig. 3A). Halogenated derivatives, including chloro- and fluoro-substituted substrates 1b-1g, were smoothly transformed into the corresponding (R)-alcohols by V121A-hCAII in high yields and with excellent optical purities. Methyl- and methoxy- substituted substrates 1h-1l were also well tolerated by V121A-hCAII, yielding the corresponding (R)-alcohols with moderate efficiency and good stereoselectivity. Replacing the dimethylamino group on the benzene ring with methyl or ethyl groups rendered the substrates configurationally stable (1m and 1n). Kinetic resolution of these substrates afforded the desired products (R)-2m in 14% yield with 75% ee (E = 7.9), and (R)-2n in 30% yield with 74% ee (E = 9.1). By contrast, introduction of a tert-butyl group completely suppressed the reaction, likely owing to its larger steric demand (fig. S3). To assess the scalability of the reaction, the model transformation was performed on a 0.1 mmol scale. The reaction was conducted using cell lysate under ambient conditions at 20°C for 4 hours. Following a simple workup, (R)-2a was obtained in 83% isolated yield with 99% ee, underscoring the efficiency of the reaction. To further demonstrate the synthetic utility of this method, alcohol 2a was derivatized to furnish bifunctional thiourea (R)-8 in 60% overall yield and 99% ee, providing a potentially useful scaffold for asymmetric organocatalysis (26).

To further assess the generality of this strategy, we focused on different classes of atropisomers. Among these, axially chiral styrenes constitute an important subclass, being prevalent in both pharmaceutical and materials contexts. We therefore sought to extend our methodology to the enantioselective construction of axially chiral styrene derivatives (Fig. 3B). The use of (E)-2-(2-hydroxynaphthalen-1-yl)-3-phenylacrylaldehyde (3a) as the substrate led to good catalytic performance, affording (S)-4a with V121A-hCAII. Then, different substituents on the phenyl ring of the substrate were investigated. It was observed that substrates bearing various halogens (F, Cl, Br) on the phenyl ring were efficiently converted, affording moderate to good yields (40–90% yield) with near-complete selectivity ((S)-4b-4f). A strongly electron-withdrawing nitro group on the phenyl ring was also well tolerated, affording the product in 41% yield with an 84% ee ((S)-4g). Moreover, substrates bearing electron-donating substituents such as methyl or methoxy groups were efficiently transformed into the corresponding alcohols with excellent enantioselectivity ((S)-4h-4i). In addition, the heteroaryl substrate 3j delivered the desired product in excellent yield with 99% ee. Furthermore, naphthyl substrates bearing either electron-donating or electron-withdrawing substituents were also compatible, consistently providing the products with 99% ee ((S)-4k-4n). We next extended this strategy to axially chiral diaryl ethers (Fig. 3C). Although 2-(2-(tert-butyl)-6-methylphenoxy)isophthalaldehyde (5a) underwent smooth desymmetrization to afford (R)-6a in 60% yield and 96% ee, both the yield and enantioselectivity diminished in subsequent substrate-scope studies (fig. S2). We therefore selected 5a as the model substrate for further mutant screening. As shown in the Supporting Information (table S7), mutant W208A improved the reaction substantially, delivering (R)-6a in 91% yield with >99% ee. Introduction of substituents (5b-5d) onto the phenyl ring was also tolerated, and afforded moderate to good yields with satisfactory stereoselectivity.

To investigate the reaction mechanism, we first performed isotope labeling experiments. Under the model reaction conditions, when PhSiD3 was used as the reducing agent, deuterium incorporation reached 98%, whereas incorporation was less than 1% when a deuterated buffer was employed (Fig. 4A). These results indicate that the reduction proceeds via the silane. We further employed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to rationalize the origin of the reaction’s stereoselectivity. The much more convergent RMSD profiles of the (R)-form substrate 1a signified its enhanced binding stability relative to the (S)-form (figs. S7 and S8). Accordingly, the distance between the hydride (atom H1) of Zn–H and the C1 atom of substrate aldehyde group (denoted as dZn–H@H1···1a@C1), a key index of the hydride transfer efficiency, converged to a value of ~3.30 Å in all three MD simulation replicas of the V121A-hCAII–(R)-1a (fig. S7), which is proper for an efficient hydride transfer. While it demonstrated a spike and finally converged to be ~5.21 Å in the replica-1 MD simulation of V121A-hCAII–(S)-1a, and in replica-2 and replica-3 the final converged distances were ~8.94 Å (fig. S8). These excessive distances of dZn–H@H1···1a@C1 in all three MD replicas indicate that (S)-1a binds to V121A-hCAII in a non-reactive conformation. In addition, by forming parallel π-π stacking interactions with H94 and electrostatic interactions with Zn–H through the aldehyde group, the (R)-form 1a showed higher binding affinity than the (S)-form as indicated by the binding free energies derived from molecular mechanics-generalized Born surface area (MM/GBSA) calculations (−14.71 ± 0.70 vs −12.16 ± 0.19 kcal/mol, table S8). Notably, the axial chirality of the (R)-/(S)-form substrate which was indicated by the dihedral ∠C4-C3-N1-C2 remained unchanged during MD, suggesting an atroposelective dynamic kinetic resolution (DKR) process catalyzed by V121A-hCAII.

In conclusion, we have established a biocatalytic platform for the stereoselective synthesis of a broad spectrum of atropisomeric compounds, including C–N atropisomers, diaryl ethers, and styrenes, enabled by engineered metalloenzyme-catalyzed transfer hydrogenation reactions. This reaction system exhibits broad substrate scope and potential for scale-up. MD simulations provided insight into the origin of the complementary stereoselectivities observed for the atropisomeric products catalyzed by metal enzyme. By addressing the limited availability of enzymatic tools for atropisomer synthesis, this work demonstrates that the integration of reactive chemical intermediates with enzyme catalysis can unlock new-to-nature reactivity, thereby overcoming critical synthetic challenges and further expanding the biocatalytic synthetic repertoire.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Unless otherwise noted, all chemicals, salts and solvents were obtained from commercial suppliers, and used without further purification. The 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded with a Bruker AVANCE NEO 400 MHz spectrometer with TMS as an internal standard. NMR data are reported as follows: chemical shift, multiplicity (s = singlet, d = doublet, t = triplet, q = quartet, m = multiplet, dd = doublet of doublet, dt = doublet of triplet, br = broad), coupling constants (Hz), and integration. The high resolution mass spectrum (HRMS) was obtained on Waters XEVO G2-S TOF. Enantiomeric ratio of the reaction products was determined with a Daicel Chiralpak IA, IC, ID, IG-3, AD-H, AS-H, OD-H column (250 × 4.6 mm) on either a Hitachi Primaide or an Agilent 1260 Infinity III using n-hexanes/isopropanol as the mobile phase.

General procedure

Substrate (5 mM), hydrogen source (15–30 mM), enzyme (about 0.025 mM), solvent (1 mL, 900 μl 50 mM PBS buffer (pH 8.0) and 100 μl DMSO), shaking at 20°C, 800 rpm, then extracted with EtOAc for three times. Yields and enantiomeric excess (ee) of Substrate were determined by HPLC.
