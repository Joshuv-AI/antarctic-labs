Workshop Overview
The 2026 Nondestructive Evaluation for Advanced Manufacturing (NDE for AM) Workshop was a two-day
workshop held at the ORNL Manufacturing Demonstration Facility (MDF) in Oak Ridge, Tennessee, on May 19–20,
2026. The workshop brought together researchers, federal program managers, end users, OEMs, standards
bodies, and regulatory agencies to assess the state of the art in NDE for AM parts, identify technology gaps, and
establish R&D priorities.
The workshop was structured around seven technical sessions, a keynote address, a working lunch talk, an evening
poster session, and a total of sixteen breakout groups distributed across Day 1 and Day 2 topics. This report
provides summaries of each presentation, panel discussion, and breakout session, followed by a cross-cutting
synthesis of themes, challenges, gaps, and priorities identified across the workshop.

Executive Summary
The 2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop convened more than sixty researchers, program managers,
regulators, OEM representatives, standards body participants, and end users at the ORNL Manufacturing
Demonstration Facility on May 19–20, 2026. The workshop assessed the state of the art in NDE for AM across
seven technical sessions and six structured breakout groups, producing the findings summarized in this report.
The following paragraphs highlight the most significant conclusions for readers who require a concise overview.
The NDE for AM field has matured with more structural, not technical, problems remaining. Porosity detection,
AI-accelerated CT reconstruction, in-situ monitoring POD analysis, and multimodal process characterization have
all advanced to the point of demonstration. SIMURGH (ORNL, R&D 100 Award 2025) delivers more than 12× faster
CT with more than 4× improved detectability. Peregrine’s in-situ POD study showed 95% detection of lack-of-
fusion flaws above 260 μm. Interrupted tensile testing with CT registration has identified the exact causative flaw
in IN-625 specimens — tracking it from 33 μm to 254 μm. The problems that dominate the workshop discussion
are not whether these tools work; rather, they are how to qualify these tools, how to share the data that they
generate, and how to build the workforce to operate them.
The process–structure–property–performance chain is a central unresolved problem. Effect-of-defect data —
quantitative relationships between AM flaw type, geometry, and distribution and resulting mechanical
performance knockdowns — is absent for most material-process-application combinations. Without it, NDE
acceptance criteria cannot be technically justified, in-situ monitoring data cannot be credited for qualification,
and rapid qualification targets cannot be validated. Every session and every breakout identified this gap.
Addressing it requires a coordinated, multi-institution, publicly funded data program anchored at facilities like
ORNL’s MDF that can generate and share pedigree-quality datasets across material systems.
Qualification frameworks lag the technology. CT hardware, in-situ monitoring tools, and AI algorithms are
advancing faster than the regulatory and standards frameworks needed to credit them. In-situ monitoring has no
IQ/OQ/PQ analog; AI NDE, as yet, has no regulatory qualification pathway; ASTM still publishes reference
radiographs on film. The NDE for AM field is now in a phase where deliberate engagement with standards bodies
and regulatory agencies — the NRC, FAA, DOW, ASTM E07/F42, SAE AMS-AM, and ANSI — is the critical rate-
limiting activity, not further technology development.
Distinct needs for initial qualification and in-service inspection. While priorities, challenges, and needs for NDE
in initial AM part qualification and in-service inspection share commonalities, key differences require separate
attention. For instance, acceptance criteria must be developed independently for each inspection context, as
technical requirements and operational risks vary between initial qualification and ongoing service. Maintaining
consistent communication and awareness across both contexts is also essential, since advances or data from
one can influence the other. For example, an in-situ inspection record collected during manufacturing may
inform subsequent in-service inspections of the same part, improving reliability and supporting lifecycle
management. Coordinated information sharing ensures that both initial qualification and in-service inspection
benefit from evolving best practices and technical insights.

Seven priorities for community investment. The cross-cutting synthesis at the end of this report maps the top
challenges to their associated gaps and derives seven community priorities:

 (1) a cross-sector effect-of-defect data program;

 (2) a tiered IQ/OQ/PQ qualification framework for in-situ monitoring tools;

 (3) a cross-agency qualification standards alignment working group;

 (4) a national AM NDE data commons with mandated FAIR data delivery;

 (5) design-for-inspectability guidelines and integration of NDE simulation tools;

                                                                                    ORNL | May 19–20, 2026 | Page 12

 (6) a regulatory-accepted AI qualification pathway modeled on performance demonstration testing; and

 (7) an NDE 4.0 workforce development program with new certification pathways and subsidized
 standards participation.

These priorities are not independent — progress on any one accelerates the others — and all seven are within
reach with coordinated federal investment and multi-sector collaboration of the kind this workshop was
designed to catalyze.

Introduction
The 2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop was held at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory
Manufacturing Demonstration Facility on May 19–20, 2026. This event was structured to facilitate focused
technical exchange and collaborative problem-solving in the rapidly evolving domain of nondestructive
evaluation (NDE) for advanced manufacturing. Over two days, the workshop featured seven technical sessions,
six structured breakout topics, a keynote address, and an evening poster session. These formats were designed
to encourage both formal presentations and interactive discussions, ensuring that participants could engage
with emerging research, industry challenges, and perspectives of end users and regulators.

Attendance comprised more than sixty individuals representing a broad spectrum of expertise and
organizational roles. The participant pool included researchers, program managers, regulatory authorities,
original equipment manufacturers (OEMs), standards body participants, and end users. This diversity of
backgrounds was instrumental in fostering a multidisciplinary perspective, with attendees bringing insights from
academic research, government oversight, industrial production, and standards development. Such a mix
enabled the workshop to address not only technical advancements but also the structural and regulatory
challenges facing the field.

The technical sessions addressed seven key areas central to NDE for additive manufacturing: (1) DOE/NRC
program perspectives, (2) AI/ML applications, (3) ex-situ NDE, (4) qualification and certification, (5) in-situ
monitoring and NDE, (6) digital infrastructure, and (7) rapid qualification frameworks. Breakout groups explored
these topics further. The collaborative nature of the workshop is reflected in the synthesis of findings, which
maps current challenges to priorities for future community investment and federal coordination.

Overall, the workshop provided a comprehensive assessment of the state of the art in NDE for advanced
manufacturing, with discussions reflecting both the maturity of technical solutions and the need for structural
progress in qualification, data sharing, and workforce development. The broad engagement across sectors and
institutions underscores the importance of coordinated, multi-disciplinary approaches to advancing the field and
ensuring that emerging technologies can be effectively qualified, standardized, and deployed at scale.

Cross-Cutting Synthesis — Observations, Common Themes, and
Priority Recommendations
The 2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop brought together more than sixty participants across seven
technical sessions, six breakout groups, a keynote, and an evening poster session over two days. Reviewing the
full body of presentations, panel discussions, and breakout outputs, several themes emerge with striking
consistency across topics, sectors, and institutional perspectives. This synthesis identifies those common threads,
maps the top challenges to their associated gaps, and derives the community’s highest-priority action items.

Cross-Cutting Themes
Theme 1: The Field Has Matured — and the Hard Problems Are Now Visible
A recurring observation across sessions and breakouts was that the NDE for AM field has made genuine,
measurable progress. Five years ago, industry’s primary concern was whether AM could produce parts without
unacceptable porosity. That question is now largely answered — manageable porosity is achievable with known
process controls, and the detection tools to find it have advanced substantially. CT hardware speed is no longer
the bottleneck. In-situ monitoring platforms now exist and have been demonstrated with rigorous POD studies.
AI-assisted CT reconstruction and analysis (e.g., flaw detection) have achieved commercial adoption. The
problems that remain are harder and more structural: connecting NDE signals to structural performance,
establishing qualification frameworks that regulators will accept, building the data infrastructure to sustain all of
this at scale, and developing the workforce to operate it. The workshop’s discussions reflected this shift — from
“can we detect flaws?” to “how do we qualify detection, and what do we do with the answer?”
Theme 2: The Process–Structure–Property–Performance Chain Is Broken in the Middle
Every session, from DOE/NRC program perspectives (Session 1) through in-situ monitoring (Session 5) and
qualification (Session 4), converged on the same unresolved connection: the chain from AM process parameters
through in-situ sensor signals, through detected flaws, through microstructure, through post-processing effects,
to final mechanical performance is not closed. Effect-of-defect data — quantitative relationships between specific
AM flaw types (lack of fusion, keyhole pores, spatter-induced inclusions, delamination), their geometry and
distribution, and the resulting structural performance knockdowns — is broadly absent for most material-process-
application combinations. Without it, NDE acceptance criteria are either borrowed from conventional
manufacturing (where the defect morphology is fundamentally different) or determined through expensive, case-
by-case fracture mechanics analyses that take six months or more. The Q&C breakout called this the hardest
unresolved standards need; the In-Situ breakout identified it as the most important engineering problem; and
Session 7 demonstrated that the tools to begin closing this gap — high-throughput CT with SIMURGH, interrupted
testing with automated registration — are now available and producing results.
Theme 3: Qualification Frameworks Are the Binding Constraint
Multiple presenters made the same observation from different angles: the technology is often ahead of the
qualification framework. CT hardware is fast enough; the bottleneck is qualified acceptance criteria. In-situ
monitoring tools produce useful data; the bottleneck is the absence of an IQ/OQ/PQ analog for monitoring
systems and the undefined “further showing” threshold from regulators. AI algorithms perform at application-
relevant accuracy levels; the bottleneck is the absence of regulatory-accepted AI qualification pathways. This
pattern — technology outpacing qualification — appeared in Sessions 1, 3, 4, 5, and 7 and in every breakout group.
The solution is not more technical work alone; it requires deliberate, sustained engagement between technical
developers (national laboratories, industry, universities) and qualification authorities (NRC, FAA, DOW, NAVSEA,
ASME, ASTM). The workshop itself was an important step in this direction.
Theme 4: Multimodal, Multi-Scale, and Multisensor Approaches Are Necessary — Not Optional

No single NDE modality is sufficient for AM parts. This observation was made in every technical session and every
breakout. UT provides rapid volumetric screening but struggles with as-built surfaces and inhomogeneous
microstructure. CT provides definitive volumetric characterization but is costly, time-consuming, and inapplicable
in-service. Neutron imaging accesses residual stress and elemental contrast inaccessible to X-rays. In-situ optical
and thermal monitoring captures the build layer-by-layer but cannot replace post-build NDE without additional
qualification. The LLNL study cited in Session 7 quantified this directly: a single sensor modality reaches
approximately 75% detection accuracy, while multi-sensor fusion reaches above 95%. In the Ex-Situ breakout, the
standard practice workflow was described as UT first, CT for confirmation, with neutrons for residual stress when
needed. In the Q&C breakout, the consensus was that multimodal in-situ fusion is necessary for volumetric
acceptance evidence. The community is converging on a tiered, multimodal inspection paradigm that uses each
method for what it uniquely provides, but the frameworks to integrate data across modalities — in both the
technical and the qualification sense — are immature.
Theme 5: Data Infrastructure Is the Invisible Prerequisite
Every technical advance described in this workshop — AI-assisted CT reconstruction, in-situ monitoring POD
analysis, VPPM flaw statistics prediction, interrupted testing flaw tracking, build signature qualification, Bragg-
edge strain mapping — requires large, well-curated, pedigree-quality datasets to train, validate, and deploy. Yet
across the breakouts, the most consistent community failure mode was described as data fragmentation:
proprietary datasets locked within individual organizations, insufficient metadata making data non-reusable,
inconsistent file formats preventing inter-organization exchange, and no shared repositories providing the
community-scale datasets that AI requires. Session 6 and its breakout addressed this directly — through DICONDE,
ONDE, Damara Tern, and modular automation architectures — but adoption of all these tools remains limited.
The AI/ML breakout double-starred data infrastructure investment as the highest-priority gap. Until the data
infrastructure problem is solved, the technical advances in NDE cannot scale to deployment.
Theme 6: Fragmentation Across Sectors, Organizations, and Standards Bodies Is a Significant Structural
Challenge
The Q&C breakout identified institutional siloing as a dominant challenge — and this observation recurred in
almost every other session. NASA, DOW, NAVSEA, FAA, NRC, ASME, ASTM, SAE, and individual OEMs each
maintain their own qualification procedures, data formats, and acceptance criteria, with minimal inter-agency
alignment. Research conducted on machines with uncertain provenance or inadequate maintenance at under-
resourced universities generates data that is not reliable for qualification purposes. Small and medium-sized
businesses and universities are excluded from standards body participation by cost and bandwidth. The Digital
Infrastructure breakout observed that NDE is falling behind the engineering disciplines it serves because it cannot
deliver data in the formats those disciplines need. No single organization can solve these coordination problems
unilaterally. The repeated calls from multiple sessions for America Makes, ANSI, and JAMWG to play stronger
orchestrating roles reflect the genuine need for a neutral, trusted coordinating body that can align these
fragmented efforts and hold stakeholders accountable for cross-sector data sharing, standards alignment, and
workforce development.
Theme 7: Workforce Development Is a Significant Need
Workforce gaps appeared in every breakout session and in multiple technical sessions. The NDE community faces
converging shortfalls: no trained Level 2 technicians certified for in-situ process monitoring data review; NDE
certification frameworks (NAS-410, SNT-TC-1A) that do not cover CT analysis for complex AM parts; new graduates
who have never heard of qualification and certification requirements; NDE Level 3s who are now also expected to
manage IT infrastructure, digital data pipelines, and AI validation without budget for any of it; and standards
bodies dominated by large primes, leaving SMEs and universities without voice. These are not problems that more
research will solve. They require deliberate investment in curriculum development, new certification structures,
incentive-based training access, and mechanisms to fund participation in standards development by organizations
that cannot afford to do it voluntarily.

Top Challenges, Associated Gaps, and Derived Priorities
The following maps the top challenges identified across all sessions and breakouts to their associated gaps and
the resulting priorities. Items are ordered by the frequency and urgency with which they were raised across the
workshop.
1. Challenge: No Quantitative Link Between AM Flaws and Structural Performance
Acceptance criteria for AM parts are borrowed from welding and/or casting workmanship standards not
analytically tied to AM flaw morphology or structural significance. Defining a tolerable flaw size for a specific AM
geometry and loading condition takes six months or more, or is simply not done.
Gap: No community-scale effect-of-defect database for AM flaw types (lack of fusion, keyhole porosity,
delamination, spatter inclusions) linking flaw geometry, distribution, and orientation to fatigue life, fracture
toughness, creep, and tensile knockdowns across material-process combinations. AMSC Gap 8 (acceptance classes)
and the four new AMSC gaps from v3.0 partially address this but remain open.
Priority: Establish a collaborative, cross-sector effect-of-defect initiative that builds upon existing activities and
leverages available platforms and technologies. Prioritize the material-process combinations most needed by
DOE-NE, NAVSEA, and NAVAIR. Make resulting datasets publicly available as a community resource.
2. Challenge: No Qualification Framework for In-Situ Process Monitoring Tools
In-situ monitoring tools can make unsubstantiated detection claims because no framework defines what evidence
is required to qualify them. Post-build processing (HIP, heat treatment, machining) invalidates in-situ data in ways
that are not yet characterized. Current industry practice — fabrication technicians reviewing their own in-situ data
— violates the fabrication/inspection separation that standards require.
Gap: No IQ/OQ/PQ analog for in-situ monitoring installations; no traceable calibration infrastructure or AM-
specific calibration blocks; no POD data for in-situ systems; no personnel certification pathway for data reviewers;
undefined “further showing” threshold from FAA, NRC, and DOW; no procedure for returning a degraded
monitoring system to a qualified state.
Priority: Develop a tiered IQ/OQ/PQ framework for in-situ monitoring tools aligned with ARP7065’s three-tier
taxonomy. Convene FAA, NRC, and DOW alongside ASTM F42 and SAE AMS-AM to define minimum evidence
thresholds for each tier. Commission AM-specific calibration reference artifacts. Establish a pilot program at ORNL
MDF correlating in-situ data to post-processing outcomes and ex-situ NDE across multiple material systems.
3. Challenge: Fragmented, Siloed, and Misaligned Qualification Standards
NASA, DOW, NAVSEA, FAA, ASME, ASTM, SAE, and individual OEMs maintain incompatible qualification
requirements. There is no common hierarchy, no agreed-upon terminology, and no mechanism to prevent
duplicated or contradictory standards work. The 28 America Makes/ANSI gaps remain largely unaddressed at the
implementation level.
Gap: No AI qualification framework for NDE (covering AI validation, UQ, explainability, and acceptance criteria);
no standardized XCT ground-truth generation protocol; no qualified NDE procedures for AM pressure boundary
components under ASME BPVC; no process-agnostic rapid qualification framework with measurable targets;
incomplete cross-SDO coordination between ASTM E07 and F42.
Priority: Charge America Makes/ANSI with convening a cross-agency NDE qualification alignment working group
with representatives from NASA, DOW, NAVSEA, NAVAIR, FAA, and NRC. Define three measurable rapid
qualification targets (time, cost, coupon count) and a technology-agnostic pathway to meet them. Assign the
ASTM E07–F42 cross-committee liaison as a standing function, not an ad hoc effort.
4. Challenge: Data Is Not FAIR — Fragmented, Proprietary, and Non-Interoperable
NDE and AM process data is fragmented across proprietary file formats, locked in institutional databases, and
inadequately metadata-tagged. AI models cannot be trained, qualified, or deployed without community-scale

datasets. Spatial registration across modalities, long-term temporal linkage for life prediction, and integration with
structural engineering digital twins are all limited by data infrastructure restrictions.
Gap: Limited community-curated, publicly accessible NDE+AM datasets (especially for UT and AE waveforms);
limited digital CT reference volumes for cross-OEM benchmarking; no North American equivalent to European data
ownership regulations mandating user data access; no shared algorithm vocabulary across vendors; limited open-

NDE file format reader development through DOE or NSF instrument funding.
5. Challenge: Inspectability Is Not Considered at Design Time
AM parts are routinely designed to be manufactured — not to be inspected. Legacy casting and forging drawings
are adapted to AM without redesign for inspection access, forcing either overbuild or uninspectable final
geometries. Surface conditions on as-built AM surfaces are not defined or standardized, preventing reliable UT
coupling before final machining. A breakout session noted that determining when a surface is inspectable enough
to proceed — before committing to expensive final machining — is an important operational decision that
currently lacks supporting standards or tools.
Gap: No integration of NDE simulation at the design stage; no standardized surface condition definitions and
tolerances for AM; no AM-specific reference radiographs; no formal designer/NDE engineer collaboration
frameworks; no cost-comparison tools for design-for-inspectability tradeoffs.
Priority: Develop accessible design-for-inspectability guidelines derived from NDE simulation studies and publish
as a free ASNT/ASTM handbook. Create AM-specific reference radiograph sets for common discontinuity
morphologies. Standardize surface condition definitions (roughness + waviness) and tolerance bands for UT, PT,
and RT applicability. Include an NDE engineer in AM design reviews as a standard practice. Design iteration
demonstrations informed by NDE simulations and part lifecycle requirements.
6. Challenge: AI/ML for NDE Has No Regulatory Qualification Pathway
AI models for NDE image analysis, CT reconstruction, and in-situ anomaly detection are technically capable but
currently lack regulatory-accepted qualification pathways, explainability requirements, uncertainty quantification
standards, or liability frameworks. AI ground truth depends on expert-labeled data that is itself expensive and
scarce. Models trained on one system may fail on another, and long-term deployed model drift is unmonitored.
Gap: No community benchmarks for acceptable AI detection performance per application; no standards for AI
validation (UQ, explainability, acceptance criteria) in NDE; no AI qualification framework accepted by NRC, FAA, or
DOW; Sweden’s Technical Justification AI-assisted nuclear NDE qualification exists as a reference with no US
equivalent; hallucination mitigation for AI NDE report generation is unaddressed; long-term AI system
performance monitoring is absent.
Priority: Establish a DOE/NRC-co-sponsored working group to develop AI qualification criteria for NDE, modeled
on the performance demonstration testing (PDT) framework used for human inspector qualification. Define
application-specific benchmark performance targets (e.g., ≥95% detection, ≤20% false call rate for ex-situ UT).
Require long-term model performance monitoring as a condition of regulatory approval.
7. Challenge: Workforce Is Unprepared for the Current and Future NDE Landscape
NDE certifications were designed for conventional modalities and do not cover CT data analysis for AM, in-situ
monitoring data review, or AI-assisted interpretation. New graduates can be unaware that qualification
requirements exist. Standards body participation may be inaccessible to small organizations. Digital NDE requires
IT expertise that Level 3 engineers do not necessarily have and usually do not have budget for.
Gap: No certification pathway for in-situ monitoring data reviewers; existing NAS-410/SNT-TC-1A frameworks do
not cover CT for AM; no cost-effective training access for SMEs and universities; ASTM/SAE standards participation

Organizer Observations and Suggested Next Steps
Several observations from the organizers emerge from reviewing the full breadth of workshop content.
There are several national organizations helping advance AM, including DOE and the national laboratories, various
organizations in the DOW, and SDOs. As this workshop was held at the ORNL MDF, we had the opportunity to
discuss its distinct capabilities firsthand. ORNL’s Manufacturing Demonstration Facility is at the intersection of
several challenges identified in this report: it houses commercially representative AM machines across multiple
processes, operates SIMURGH, Peregrine, Pelican, and Damara Tern as deployed tools, has access to ORNL’s
neutron imaging (MARS, VENUS), and has demonstrated the full pipeline from AM process to in-situ monitoring
to ex-situ CT to mechanical testing in a single facility. Several of the highest-priority actions identified in this
synthesis — the effect-of-defect data program, the in-situ monitoring correlation study, the AI qualification pilot
— could be anchored at MDF with appropriate inter-agency support and coordination.
The rapid qualification milestone is within reach. The combination of SIMURGH-based high-throughput CT, in-situ
monitoring with formal POD analysis, OT-based build signature qualification, and AI-assisted anomaly detection
has moved the six-month/$180K/400-coupon baseline from a fixed constraint to a starting point that can be
systematically reduced. The 2026 workshop heard credible evidence that a six-fold reduction is achievable — not
just aspirationally, but through specific technical pathways that participants could describe in detail. What is
missing is the qualification framework that allows that evidence to be credited. That framework gap should be the
near-term focus of federally coordinated effort.
Geopolitical and supply-chain risks require policy attention. Two sessions raised concerns that were not purely
technical: acquisition of AM equipment for qualification research from sources that may present challenges to
national security or compliance requirements (including machines manufactured outside the domestic market in
federally funded programs), and geopolitical pressures on ISO standards bodies that threaten global
harmonization. These risks compound the technical challenges by potentially directing national qualification
investments toward equipment that cannot be used in controlled programs, and by fragmenting international
standards alignment. Policy-level attention to TAA compliance in research procurement, and proactive
engagement with standards bodies, would reduce these risks at relatively low cost.
A follow-on workshop should track progress. The 2026 workshop identified specific, actionable gaps. A 2028
workshop — or a standing annual working session — organized around the seven priority areas above would allow
the community to measure progress, update priorities as the technology evolves, and maintain the cross-sector
connections formed here.
The organizers recommend that the next convening explicitly track: (1) effect-of-defect datasets published since
2026; (2) in-situ monitoring qualification frameworks advanced; (3) ASTM digital reference standards progress;
(4) AI NDE qualification pilots completed; (5) workforce development programs launched; (6) design for
inspectability progress; and (7) digital infrastructure progress. These metrics would give the community a shared
scorecard and a mechanism to hold itself accountable.

NDE at its Extremes: Applications for Unique Cases
Shant Kenderian · The Aerospace Corporation
Overview
Shant Kenderian presented three case studies from The Aerospace Corporation illustrating how custom NDE
solutions must be engineered from first principles when conventional methods fail — a circumstance increasingly
common in advanced manufacturing contexts. The Aerospace Corporation operates as a nonprofit FFRDAC
supporting the US Space Force, with a mandate to provide independent, technically rigorous assessments without
commercial incentive to over-report problems.
Case Study Highlighted: Kissing Unbonds Under Artemis Heat Shield Tiles
The primary case study detailed the development of a single-sided ultrasonic inspection system for detecting
kissing unbonds beneath 1.25"–3" thick heat shield blocks on the Artemis crew capsule. Standard techniques —
X-ray, terahertz, backscatter X-ray — could image the tile and bond line geometrically but could not distinguish a
bonded interface from a kissing unbond (where surfaces are in contact but not adhered). Mechanical wave
propagation was required, but the highly inhomogeneous, scattering, and absorptive character of the ablative tile
material severely degraded ultrasonic signal quality.
The team developed a seven-step signal processing workflow to overcome these challenges: (1) selection of a low-
frequency, high-damping transducer to allow sound penetration while preserving echo separation; (2) use of an
alcohol-based couplant that wicked under the tile surface to create a gradual acoustic impedance transition; (3)
time-gated amplitude mapping of the front-wall and back-wall echoes; (4) amplitude ratio normalization of the
two echoes to cancel material inhomogeneity effects; (5) phase analysis exploiting the sign flip of the reflection
coefficient at an air gap versus a bonded interface; (6) amplitude-weighted phase mapping to suppress low-
confidence phase readings; and (7) synthetic aperture focusing (SAFT) to recover spatial resolution lost to the low-
frequency transducer. The resulting binary map reliably identified kissing unbonds above the critical flaw size and
passed a Probability of Detection (POD) study at 93–95%, surpassing the required 95th-percentile threshold.
Kenderian emphasized that each NASA design iteration of the heat shield required a new inspection solution,
underscoring a broader lesson: NDE for novel or AM materials is typically a custom development problem, not a
product selection exercise. Every application demands understanding of wave physics, material interaction, and
signal processing in combination.
Supporting Case Studies (briefly noted)
Two additional cases were introduced. The first involved using ultrasonic attenuation as a quantitative metric for
cavitation damage progression in rubber components, developing a Comparative Scan Index (CSI) and a power-
law growth model to predict degradation trajectory from prior test states. The second addressed detection of
fiber dimples in a composite sandwich structure beneath bonded cork insulation — another instance where signal
normalization and phase exploitation proved essential.
Key Messages for the Workshop
 • NDE for AM and novel materials is rarely a plug-and-play exercise. Each material-geometry-defect
 combination may require a purpose-built technique.

Session 1.1 — DOE Office of Nuclear Energy AMMT Program: Overview and
Perspective on NDE of AM Nuclear Components
Meimei Li · DOE AMMT / Argonne National Laboratory
Program Overview
Meimei Li presented the mission and structure of the DOE Office of Nuclear Energy Advanced Materials and
Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program. The program's goal is to accelerate the development,
qualification, and deployment of advanced materials and manufacturing technologies across the full spectrum of
advanced reactor concepts — Sodium-cooled Fast Reactors, Molten Salt Reactors, Gas-cooled Reactors, and
advanced Light Water Reactors.
AMMT is organized into four technical areas: (1) Advanced Materials and Manufacturing, focused on metallic
materials and innovative fabrication methods including integration with traditional manufacturing; (2)
Environmental Effects, addressing how materials perform under irradiation, corrosion at high temperatures, and
mechanical loading in reactor coolants; (3) Rapid Qualification, centered on data-driven qualification frameworks
for high-temperature and AM materials in nuclear environments; and (4) Technology Maturation, covering
component-scale demonstrations, codes and standards engagement, and scalable manufacturing pathways.
Scientific Foundation: Processing–Structure–Property–Performance
Li framed the program's R&D around the PSPP (Processing–Structure–Property–Performance) relationship.
Understanding both how initial processing establishes microstructure and baseline properties, and how reactor
service conditions (irradiation, temperature, stress, coolant) subsequently evolve that microstructure and
degrade properties, is foundational. Computational tools — processing simulations, microstructure models,
property predictions — run in parallel with physical experiments to extend understanding beyond what can be
directly measured.
Digital Qualification: Multi-Dimensional Data Correlation (MDDC)
The central programmatic vehicle for accelerating qualification is the MDDC framework — a digital thread that
fuses data from feedstock, process parameters, in-situ monitoring, post-build NDE, destructive testing, and
material characterization into a comprehensive component record. An AI/ML and physics-based model layer over
this data enables pattern discovery, defect prediction, and performance forecasting. Over a 3–10 year horizon,
AMMT envisions progressing from component demonstrations to digitally aided qualification to full AI-based
design.
NDE Across the AM Nuclear Component Lifecycle
Li emphasized that NDE for nuclear AM is not a single-step activity. It is required at every phase: design and
qualification (defect distribution characterization, acceptance criteria), build process (in-situ monitoring, post-
build NDE), post-processing (confirm defect reduction, dimensional verification), certification and installation
(final acceptance, baseline mapping), in-service operation (flaw growth monitoring, degradation assessment), and
repair/failure analysis (inspection of parent material and repairs, root-cause feedback). Nuclear applications
impose uniquely demanding requirements: high safety significance, limited post-installation inspectability due to
radiation and access constraints, 40–80+ year design lives, and harsh multi-stressor environments.

Research Highlights
AMMT has explored X-ray computed tomography (including support for SIMURGH for AI-accelerated
reconstruction), dual-energy X-ray imaging, Bragg-edge neutron imaging for phase mapping and strain
measurement, advanced ultrasonic methods, and neutron diffraction for residual stress characterization. A case
study on a DED T-valve demonstrated integrated qualification using simultaneous Digital Image Correlation (DIC)
and infrared thermography during the build, followed by post-build XCT — with the combination providing
stronger qualification evidence than either approach alone. A separate study tracked defect evolution from pre-
creep to post-creep XCT in LPBF 316H specimens, beginning to establish quantitative defect–performance
relationships.
NDE Qualification Pathway Gaps
Li identified five unresolved qualification challenges: absence of a mature AM-specific nuclear NDE qualification
standard; limited defect acceptance criteria tailored to AM defect populations (existing criteria designed for
wrought/forged cannot be directly applied); insufficient defect-performance datasets linking AM flaws to fracture,
fatigue, creep, corrosion, and irradiation performance; undefined regulatory pathway for in-situ monitoring data
to substitute for or supplement qualified post-build inspection; and an immature overall framework for AM NDE
qualification aligned with ASME Code and NRC requirements.
Key Takeaway
Successful deployment of AM nuclear components will require integrated qualification linking manufacturing
data, multimodal NDE, performance testing, and digital tools within a code- and regulatory-aligned framework.
NDE must evolve from a verification step into a continuous, lifecycle-integrated qualification tool.

Session 1.3 — Optimizing Nondestructive Examinations of Components Made Using
Advanced Manufacturing Technologies
Stephen Cumblidge · US Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC)
Regulatory Context
Stephen Cumblidge presented the NRC's developing guidance framework for NDE of components manufactured
using advanced manufacturing technologies (AMT), driven by Section 401 (Action DD5) of the ADVANCE Act of
2024, which directed the NRC to develop guidance on NDE of new and novel materials with an initial focus on
AMT components. A key challenge recognized upfront is that the landscape of AMT adoption in nuclear is a moving
target — it is difficult to predict which specific processes and components industry will bring to nuclear
applications over the next 5–10 years. Accordingly, the NRC chose to develop general, technology-agnostic
guidance rather than process-specific rules.
The Importance of Purposeful NDE Programs
Cumblidge articulated a core principle: NDE programs must begin with clearly defined goals and pre-established
acceptance criteria. NDE performed without a defined purpose or acceptance standard is counterproductive and
can create regulatory complications that are difficult to resolve. Every NDE program should answer two
foundational questions: What performance characteristic is being assured? What constitutes an acceptable versus
unacceptable result?
Framework: Mitigating Fabrication Flaws
During the development phase for addressing fabrication defects, practitioners must: define the important
parameters governing component construction (materials, process parameters, post-processing); characterize the
expected flaw population for the material/method combination; establish acceptance criteria for those flaws
(whether workmanship-based or fracture-mechanics-based); identify or develop an NDE method capable of
reliably detecting and sizing the relevant flaws to the required sensitivity; and identify alternative approaches
(e.g., destructive testing of production samples) when NDE is not feasible. During the implementation phase, this
requires developing certifications/qualifications for both the manufacturing process and the NDE itself,
documented in a technical basis document.
Framework: Mitigating Service-Induced Degradation
For in-service degradation, the development phase requires characterizing expected degradation mechanisms
under reactor operating conditions, calculating critical flaw sizes that would threaten reliability, and identifying
NDE or online monitoring methods capable of detecting degradation before it reaches critical levels. For

                                                                                    ORNL | May 19–20, 2026 | Page 33

2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop — Report

geometries or materials resistant to NDE, alternative programmatic controls must be developed. The
implementation phase requires inspection plans for components with known active degradation mechanisms
(timed to catch degradation before unacceptable thresholds), statistically based sampling plans for components
with no known degradation mechanism, and formal certification of personnel, equipment, and procedures.
Graded Approach
The rigor of the NDE program should be scaled to the safety significance of the component. Qualitative measures
(does it contain primary coolant? is it needed for shutdown cooling?) and quantitative measures (formal PRA
evaluation, failure modes and effects analysis) both inform the appropriate level of programmatic investment.
This is directly analogous to the framework embedded in ASME Code Section XI Division 2, the Reliability and
Integrity Management (RIM) program, which the NRC identified as the most complete existing template for
structuring these programs.
Artificial Intelligence in NDE
Cumblidge acknowledged that AI for NDE data analysis is advancing but not yet generally at the level of a trained,
qualified inspector. AI is seen as most immediately promising for online monitoring applications. The NRC is
actively tracking AI developments and expects that performance demonstration testing frameworks — analogous
to those used for qualifying human inspectors — will be the basis for qualifying AI-based NDE systems for nuclear
applications.
Key Messages
 • Engage the NRC early and provide well-documented technical bases — this dramatically reduces review
 time and back-and-forth.
 • Existing experience from the current fleet (codes, qualification programs, hard-won lessons about
 degradation mechanisms) should inform new AMT applications wherever possible.
 • The NRC's focus is on ensuring that what is submitted is technically complete, not on being prescriptive
 about which specific methods are used.

SESSION 1 PANEL DISCUSSION — NDE for AM: Current Challenges and State of the Art

Panelists: Meimei Li (DOE AMMT/ANL), James Kemp (DOE AMMTO), Stephen Cumblidge (NRC), Craig Gerardi (Kairos
Power), Bruce Greer (EPRI)
Moderator: Ahmed Hassen, ORNL

Opening Theme: Are Current Qualification Approaches Keeping Pace with AM?
The panel opened with an assessment from Craig Gerardi of Kairos Power, a vertically integrated advanced reactor
developer currently constructing the Hermes demonstration reactor at ORNL. Kairos is beginning to incorporate
AM components, starting with non-safety-critical parts as a stepping stone toward safety-significant applications.
Gerardi identified the gap between novel inspection techniques demonstrated in laboratory settings and reliable,
field-deployable methods that can be executed by technicians with consistent and interpretable results. The
technical maturity exists, but the translation to repeatable qualified methods — both during fabrication and in-
service — remains the central challenge.
Scope of Gaps: Technical and Organizational
Bruce Greer of EPRI, drawing on extensive experience in developing NDE capabilities for the current and future
fleet, emphasized that the gap space is vast and heterogeneous. Key technical uncertainties include understanding
how AM-specific features — anisotropic grain structure, surface roughness from as-built surfaces, complex
internal geometries, residual stress distributions — affect the capability and reliability of NDE methods developed
and qualified for conventional wrought/forged materials. Surface roughness, for example, directly limits the
sensitivity of ultrasonic testing in ways that must be characterized and either corrected for or controlled through
manufacturing specifications. Greer expressed the need for the NDE community to develop structured roadmaps
identifying which gaps each stakeholder should prioritize, to avoid duplication and ensure coverage.
In-Situ Monitoring: Value and Limitations
A recurring theme was the promise and current limitations of in-situ process monitoring. Kenderian (from the
audience) noted that even for techniques that work well ex-situ, the in-situ environment introduces compounding
challenges: limited access, curved and changing surfaces, highly variable and evolving microstructure through the
build, temperature gradients, and the need to synchronize data spatially with the build process in real time. The
ultimate goal — providing real-time feedback to pause and correct a build before defects propagate — is
technically demanding but represents a meaningful shift in how qualification can be approached.
Li noted that in-situ monitoring data, while rich, must be correlated with post-build NDE before it can stand alone
as qualification evidence. The hybrid approach — in-situ plus ex-situ — is the near-term reality, with the longer-
term aspiration of in-situ data providing sufficient assurance to reduce the post-build NDE burden.
An attendee from Eldor Harris (laser powder bed fusion manufacturer of heat exchangers) described practical
challenges in deploying in-situ monitoring across a production fleet of 7–10 machines: inconsistent machine-to-
machine calibration, varying lighting conditions, and the absence of defined acceptance criteria for what
monitoring systems flag. Current practice is to use monitoring for process control rather than part qualification.
Post-Build Heat Treatment as a Show-Stopper for In-Situ Qualification
Craig Sheppard of NAVSEA raised an important point: for many Navy AM applications, post-build heat treatment
is required to achieve the necessary microstructure and mechanical properties. Any in-situ monitoring data
collected during the build reflects a microstructure that will be fundamentally altered by heat treatment, making
direct correlation between in-situ signals and final component quality significantly more complex. This was
identified as an upstream qualification challenge that must be resolved before in-situ monitoring can be credited
for acceptance in high-reliability applications.
AI and Data: Opportunities and Standardization Needs

                                                                                   ORNL | May 19–20, 2026 | Page 35

2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop — Report

Willie Kemp noted DOE's strong interest in using AI and ML to analyze manufacturing and NDE data at scale, but
acknowledged that data is neither uniform across material systems nor sufficient in volume for all application
spaces. Cumblidge added that the NRC's existing experience with performance demonstration testing (PDT)
frameworks — where both human inspectors and, increasingly, AI systems are evaluated against large specimen
sets containing planted flaws of known type and size — provides a model for how AI-based NDE could be qualified.
For components with the legacy inspection record needed to assemble training datasets, the pathway is relatively
clear; for novel AM components, the data scarcity problem is significant and will require deliberate investment in
reference specimens and characterized flaw sets.
Emily Currier of NAVAIR raised the data governance dimension: standardized formats for storing inspection data,
traceability through software version changes, data integrity assurance over long lifetimes, and how data-format-
dependent algorithms should be re-qualified when software is updated. These operational data management
issues compound the purely technical challenges.
Scaling from Coupon to Component
Li acknowledged that most AMMT NDE work to date has been conducted on small coupons and that the
translation to component-scale inspection — particularly for large AM components with complex geometries —
remains poorly understood. It is not guaranteed that a technique effective on a coupon will be effective on a full
component with the same material but different geometry and build history. Component-scale demonstration
programs were identified as a near-term priority.
What Industry Needs from DOE and the NRC
The panel converged on several actionable asks. From AMMTO's perspective, the most valuable industry
contribution is identifying the specific material-process-application combinations of highest near-term relevance,
so that federal R&D resources can be directed efficiently. From the NRC's perspective, Cumblidge emphasized that
early, proactive engagement — submitting well-documented technical bases before applications are finalized —
is the single most powerful lever for accelerating the licensing timeline. The NRC is not looking for perfection
upfront; it is looking for a clear, technically defensible story.
Greer and Gerardi both emphasized that end users need NDE methods and qualification frameworks to be simple
enough for nuclear organizations to implement. A technically excellent approach that requires specialized
expertise unavailable in a standard utility organization will not be adopted regardless of its merits. Usability and
standardization are as important as technical performance.
Key Panel Conclusions

Session 2.1 — Unsupervised Learning-Enabled Pulsed Infrared Thermographic
Microscopy of Subsurface Defects in Stainless Steel
Alexander Heifetz · Argonne National Laboratory
Motivation and Context
Alexander Heifetz presented Argonne National Laboratory's work on pulsed infrared thermography enhanced by
unsupervised machine learning to detect subsurface pores in LPBF stainless steel. The NRC requires pre-service
and in-service inspection of safety-related nuclear components, and LPBF defects — principally pores exceeding
roughly 20 µm or positioned near the surface — can initiate cracks in service.
Technique Principle
In pulsed infrared thermography (PIT), which is based on thermal-light transport physics, a short burst of thermal
energy is deposited by a flash lamp on a specimen surface. As heat is diffusing into the material bulk, surface
temperature transient is recorded via blackbody radiation sensing with a high-speed MWIR camera (FLIR x8501sc,
1280×1024 full frame at 181 fps, and higher fps at lower pixel frames). Subsurface defects impede heat diffusion
and appear as localized surface temperature hot spots in the transient data. The technique is non-contact,
compact, rapid (roughly a minute per cell-phone-sized area), and one-sided, providing practical advantages over
X-ray CT and contact ultrasound for in-line or in-service deployment. The practical detection limit without
processing is well above the theoretical diffraction-limited 5 µm resolution; machine learning is used to recover
resolution lost to thermal blurring and detector noise.
STSCS Algorithm
The Spatial-Temporal Separation-Based Clustering Segmentation (STSCS) inverse imaging algorithm processes the
thermogram data cube through four stages: wavelet-based spatial de-noising; PCA temporal decomposition;
neural learning-based independent component analysis (ICA) blind source separation to separate defect-related
thermal signals from background (adapted from radar signal processing); and K-means clustering-based
thresholding segmentation with morphological refinement to produce a binary defect map. The approach is fully
unsupervised — no labeled training data is required — a practical advantage for novel materials with sparse
ground-truth flaw datasets.
Validation Results
Progressive inverse imaging algorithms and calibration specimen refinement produced a steady reduction in the
minimum detectable defect size. For LPBF-imprinted hemisphere voids (250 µm to 1 mm) in Westinghouse-
fabricated SS316 plates, detection was demonstrated down to 750 µm. For EDM-drilled flat-bottom hole (FBH)
specimens in 3 mm SS316 plates, the minimum detectable size reached 200 µm. With the latest STSCS algorithm
applied to FBH specimens (203 µm to 127 µm), the smallest detected defect was 127 µm at 254 µm depth. In a
finer specimen set (152 µm to 76 µm), the smallest detected was 101 µm at 127 µm depth. These results were
published in Scientific Reports (2024) and highlighted in Nuclear Newswire (February 2025).
Future Directions
 • Push detection below 100 µm in SS316 specimens.
 • Transition to realistic AM-generated defect morphologies beyond FBH calibration specimens.

Session 2.2 — SIMURGH: AI-Powered Fast and Accurate X-Ray CT Reconstruction and
Super-Resolution for High-Throughput NDE in Advanced Manufacturing
Obaid Rahman · Oak Ridge National Laboratory
Overview and Motivation
Obaid Rahman presented SIMURGH, ORNL's AI-powered X-ray CT reconstruction and super-resolution framework.
Conventional high-quality CT of dense materials requires scan times on the order of hundreds of minutes, creating
a bottleneck for high-throughput AM process parameter optimization, mechanical specimen characterization, and
component-level qualification. SIMURGH recovers image quality from fast, sparse-view, or low-dose scans using
deep learning.
Framework Architecture
Training data generation pairs high-quality iterative reconstructions from dense-view reference scans against
standard reconstructions from the same raw data. Where reference scans are unavailable, physics-based GANs
synthesize realistic training pairs. The deployed model uses a fast 2.5D deep learning architecture that processes
groups of consecutive slices efficiently. During deployment, fast sparse-view scans are fed through the trained
network to produce clean, artifact-free reconstructions.
High-Throughput Process Parameter Optimization
Using PAR-AM sample holders (up to ten samples scanned in parallel), SIMURGH enabled characterization of 390
LPBF 316H stainless steel coupons — a dataset normally requiring roughly 30 days — in approximately 5 days.
After reconstruction and segmentation, metrics including porosity, flaw morphology, dimensional accuracy, and
defect distribution are mapped back to process parameters, enabling rapid identification of optimal processing
windows and geometry-dependent defect formation patterns.
Dense Materials and Component-Scale Performance
For a dense nickel alloy specimen, SIMURGH reduced scan time from over 2 hours to approximately 10 minutes
while delivering approximately 4× better flaw detectability and sizing performance validated against optical
micrographs. For large complex components where X-ray scatter is a major challenge, SIMURGH was shown to
recover the image quality of a 300-minute scatter-corrected scan from a 20-minute scan, and to provide useful
reconstructions even from a 5-minute scan without scatter correction hardware.
Super-Resolution Reconstruction
When field-of-view constraints or detector binning sacrifice spatial resolution, SIMURGH's super-resolution
module computationally enhances the result. Applied to dense 316L stainless steel, it enabled approximately 8×
faster scanning with approximately 4× effective super-resolution enhancement, outperforming a GAN-based
reference on flaw detectability for small defect diameters. Preliminary results on a large Raytheon/ORNL
component showed consistent improvement up to approximately 4× super-resolution across multiple
enhancement factors.

SIMURGH began as an ORNL research project in 2019, progressed through generative AI data augmentation,
commercial release of a SIMURGH-based PAR-AM tool through ZEISS, and applications with RTX, INL, and DOE-NE

Session 2.3 (Lightning Talk) — Machine Learning Approach for 3D Acoustic Emission
Source Localization in Additively Manufactured Metals
Jinying Zhu & Yuhan Bao · University of Nebraska–Lincoln
Motivation
Accurate 3D localization of acoustic emission (AE) sources during AM builds would enable real-time spatial
feedback on defect-generating events. Most prior AE work focused on classification rather than localization.
Complex wave propagation through the build plate and growing printed geometry makes traditional TDOA-based
localization unreliable.
Hybrid System and Physics-Informed GPR
An integrated system combined four contact AE sensors on the build plate bottom with two airborne microphones
above the build. Electrical sparks simulated AE events. A physics-informed Gaussian Process Regression (GPR)
model incorporated two physics priors: a TDOA-based mean function constraining predicted distances to be
consistent with wave velocity and time differences, and CAD-geometry nearest-neighbor matching to assign
predictions to physically valid part locations. Training on a bare build plate (domain shift) generalized to a printed
geometry without retraining.
The physics-informed GPR reduced 2D localization RMSE from 0.936 cm (TDOA-only) to 0.609 cm and improved
nearest-neighbor matching accuracy from 83.75% to 98.75%. Combining GPR (x–y) with airborne TDOA (z)
achieved full 3D RMSE of 0.636 cm with a relative error of approximately 4.3%.
Future Work
Next steps include validation under real LPBF printing conditions with actual AE events (not spark simulations) and
extension to more complex and continuous 3D geometries.

SESSION 2 PANEL DISCUSSION — AI/ML Applications

Panelists: Alexander Heifetz (ANL), Obaid Rahman (ORNL), Jinying Zhu (UNL), Andrew Dougherty (RDI Technologies).
Moderator: Ryan Meyer, ORNL.

Model Generalizability Across Materials and Processes
The panel addressed whether AI/ML models trained for one material system transfer to others. Rahman explained
that SIMURGH models depend primarily on imaging resolution — the noise and artifact character — rather than
on material intrinsic properties. A model trained for one material typically transfers well to another scanned at
the same resolution, but retraining is required when resolution changes substantially. ORNL has built a library of
model weights covering aluminum, steel, nickel, and refractory materials at various resolutions. For high-reliability
POD-critical applications, material-specific benchmarking against ground truth (optical micrographs, serial
sectioning) remains necessary. Zhu added that incorporating physical priors into the model provides implicit
regularization that improves generalization across geometries, as demonstrated by the domain-shift result in the
AE localization work. Heifetz added that a foundation model trained on a large volume of thermography data can
be used for data analysis across different materials and length scales.
Validation Protocols and Confidence
Rahman described ORNL's standard validation protocol: a high-quality dense-view scan serves as ground truth;
SIMURGH is applied to a sparse-view scan of the same sample; the two are registered and compared. For flaw
detection validation, this is supplemented by registration to optical micrographs and serial sectioning. The Air
Force is currently conducting an independent validation of a SIMURGH application using 3D serial sectioning
against CT data — representing the kind of rigorous third-party benchmarking nuclear and defense communities
will ultimately require before crediting AI-NDE results in qualification decisions. Heifetz described an ANL
approach where material flaws detected with rapid infrared thermography imaging are validated with CT data.
The Data Gap for Non-Imaging Modalities
Zhu raised a critical observation: AI advances in image-based NDE have benefited from mature computer vision
architectures and open benchmark datasets. For waveform-based NDE modalities — ultrasound, acoustic
emission, eddy current — public datasets are far more limited and the AI toolchain correspondingly less mature.
The panel identified creation of community-curated, publicly available NDE datasets (particularly for ultrasonic
waveforms with known defect ground truth) as a high-impact community action item that could catalyze rapid AI
progress in these domains, analogous to what ImageNet did for computer vision. Heifetz added that an alternative
approach is to convert eddy current data into images, which enables applying computer vision techniques to this
NDE modality.
Motion Amplification as Process Health Monitoring
Discussion of the RDI motion amplification technology explored whether it could detect subsurface volumetric
defects from surface vibration. Dougherty clarified that the primary application is machine health and structural
vibration monitoring. However, for AM applications, continuous monitoring of machine vibration state provides a
first-stage process health indicator: exceedance of threshold vibration levels can flag process deviations for closer
part inspection, complementing but not replacing direct part NDE.

 qualification still requires material-specific benchmarking.
 • Physics-informed ML approaches reduce training data requirements and improve generalization across

Session 3.1 — NDE Approaches for Inspecting Components Fabricated Using Solid
Phase Processing
Richard Jacob · Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL)
Solid Phase Processing Overview
Richard Jacob introduced Solid Phase Processing (SPP) — a family of manufacturing methods that produce high-
performance metallic microstructures through severe plastic deformation without bulk melting. PNNL's SPP
portfolio includes Friction Stir Processes (welding, processing), Shear Assisted Processing and Extrusion (ShAPE™),
and Ultra-High Vacuum Cold Spray (UHV Cold Spray). The ability to nondestructively inspect materials coming off
SPP lines is critical to ensuring structural integrity, particularly as SPP generates defect populations and
microstructural features distinct from those of conventional cast/wrought or fusion-based AM materials.
PNNL NDE Capability Suite
PNNL's NDE capabilities include volumetric methods — conventional pulse-echo UT, acoustic microscopy,
advanced UT (phased array, full matrix capture, laser UT), and X-ray radiography/CT — as well as surface and near-
surface methods including IR thermography, eddy current, and liquid penetrant. The combination of volumetric
and surface methods is important for SPP materials where defects may be planar, located at layer interfaces, or
manifest as partially consolidated feedstock regions.
ShAPE™ Case Studies
ShAPE (Shear Assisted Processing and Extrusion) uses combined linear and rotational shear to extrude near-net-
shape solid or hollow profiles from feedstock that may include machining chips and scrap — enabling recycling of
secondary materials without prior homogenization. The process produces extremely fine, uniform grain
structures.
Bismuth Telluride (BiTe) rods (2.5 mm diameter): Radial UT imaging of transverse cross-sections revealed a
characteristic swirl pattern consistent with the rotational deformation path — a finding confirmed by microscopy.
Longitudinal UT also captured the spiral crack pattern. Radiography was judged unlikely to detect these tight
planar indications.
Zirconium pucks (5-layer and 2-layer, from Zr chips feedstock): 20 MHz axial UT on the 5-layer puck identified lack-
of-fusion defects at layer interfaces (center and outer edge), and a characteristic swirl pattern of possible
porosities when the probe was focused precisely at the first interface. Radiography (275 kVp, 5 µm spot) identified
small porosities below 0.5 mm diameter that UT missed, but did not detect the planar indications visible with UT
— an important demonstration of complementarity. Shadowing, edge effects, focal spot limitations, and
attenuation all complicated UT of this geometry. On the 2-layer puck, the rough as-processed bottom surface
precluded UT entirely; thermography of that surface identified chips that appeared poorly bonded or at risk of
fracture — a case where thermography provided information inaccessible to UT.
Grain Size Measurement via UT Backscattering
In friction stir processed 316L stainless steel, PNNL demonstrated quantitative grain size measurement using UT
backscattering noise analysis. The stir zone produces extremely fine grains (~1 µm, versus ~30 µm in the base
material). By fitting the backscattering signal to a theoretical model relating scattering intensity to grain size

(power-law dependence), grain sizes were extracted and validated against EBSD measurements, with results
published in the Journal of Nondestructive Evaluation (2025). This illustrates NDE's potential role not just in
detecting defects but in characterizing microstructural state — particularly valuable for SPP materials where the
quality of the process is reflected in grain refinement.
Cold Spray NDE
Three cold spray coatings on stainless steel substrates (316 SS, Inconel, CrC-NiCr) were inspected with 10 MHz

within the cold spray layer appeared as elevated noise. The UT successfully resolved the coating-substrate

of the cold spray porosity remains challenging.
NDE Gaps for SPP and AM Components
Jacob closed with a comprehensive gap list applicable to SPP and AM NDE broadly: surface roughness limiting
coupling and resolution for UT; anisotropic microstructure and mechanical behavior affecting wave propagation;
geometric complexities (internal channels, layer interfaces, near-net shapes); ambiguity about which regions
require inspection (weld zone vs. entire part, volumetric vs. surface); unique imperfection morphologies not
covered by existing acceptance standards; undefined critical flaw type, location, and size criteria; and no
operational experience with in-service degradation modes for SPP components in service. Destructive testing and
materials testing remain essential for NDE validation and for determining what constitutes a critical defect in each
application. Key references: Jacob et al. 2020 (PNNL-30759, NRC TLR-RES/DE/CIB-2020-12) and the America

Session 3.2 — Advances in Computed Tomography for Additively Manufactured
Components
Shane Sunkin · VisiConsult X-ray Systems & Solutions
Why CT is the Gold Standard for AM Inspection
Shane Sunkin (ASNT NDT Level III) presented a broad survey of recent and emerging advances in industrial CT for
AM. He opened by arguing that CT is the gold standard for metallic AM component inspection because it is
uniquely capable of revealing internal features that cannot be accessed by any other method: porosity, powder
entrapment, internal channel geometry, lack-of-fusion at build interruptions, pre- and post-HIP comparisons, and
internal wall thickness measurements. Crucially, Sunkin emphasized that NDT engineers must be involved in
design reviews before parts are finalized — the freedom AM gives designers to create complex geometries can
render parts uninspectable if inspection constraints are not considered during design.
Linear Accelerators for Dense Components
For thick, dense components where conventional X-ray tubes lack penetrating power, linear accelerators (LINACs)
provide MeV-energy X-rays. Sunkin described the LINAC principle as analogous to a straight roller coaster
accelerating electrons via timed RF waves until they hit a target at MeV energies. Practical considerations include
pulsed output, narrow spectrum, and typical integration times around 500 ms to synchronize with the pulse train.
Example applications shown included a dense fuel injector where LINAC imaging provided sharp boundary
definition — including internal lattice structures and enclosed cavities — that was unachievable with conventional
tubes.
Photon-Counting Detectors
Photon-counting detectors (PCDs) represent a significant departure from conventional indirect-conversion
detectors. Rather than converting X-rays to light via a scintillator and then to charge, PCDs use a direct-conversion
semiconductor (typically cadmium telluride) to count each photon individually. Key consequences: electronic

                                                                                     ORNL | May 19–20, 2026 | Page 44

2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop — Report

noise below a threshold can be rejected, enabling lower-dose acquisition without contrast loss; and spectral
information is captured per photon, enabling single-scan material discrimination without dual-energy acquisition.
Current limitations include small active areas, pulse pile-up at high flux, charge sharing, thermal management
requirements, and cost. However, PCDs are increasingly entering industrial production environments.
Metal Jet X-Ray Sources
Liquid-metal jet (MetalJet) sources from Excillum replace a solid metal anode with a continuously refreshed
stream of molten metal (typically an indium-gallium alloy), removing the thermal limit on focal spot power. This
enables approximately 1,000 W at a 30 µm spot — roughly an order of magnitude more flux than a comparable
solid-anode source — and the newer F1016 model reaches 1,600 W at the same spot size. At low keV energies,
flux gains of up to 100× are achievable. This is transformative for thin-section AM components where high
resolution and rapid acquisition are both required.
Advanced Scan Trajectories
Beyond simple rotational CT, emerging trajectories include helical CT (combined rotation and vertical translation,
better for tall parts), tilted CT or laminography (for flat parts or limited-rotation geometries), limited-angle scans,
spherical and multi-spherical scans, and part-specific optimized trajectories. These trajectories reduce beam-
hardening and cone-beam artifacts, particularly for complex AM geometries. Sunkin noted an open question
about whether non-perpendicular acquisition angles could cause parallax effects on fusion-line-type defects.
Reconstruction Algorithm Advances
Traditional FBP/FDK algorithms are fast but limited for sparse data, beam hardening, and multi-material parts.
Model-based iterative reconstruction (MBIR) uses physical system models to improve accuracy. AI-driven
reconstruction methods (as demonstrated by SIMURGH and others) clean up artifacts and reduce scan time
requirements substantially. Sunkin showed a multi-material part comparison where a novel reconstruction
dramatically reduced streaking artifacts around dense features relative to standard reconstruction.
Scatter Mitigation and Multiline Detectors
Flat-panel detectors are fast but capture scatter from the full cone beam. Line detectors with collimation eliminate
scatter but reduce throughput. The multiline approach — using many detector lines with automatic collimation
matched to the field of view — balances scatter rejection with throughput, representing a practical advance for
dense AM components.
Robotic CT Systems
Robotic CT enables unique scan trajectories and automated part handling beyond what traditional rotary stages
allow. Robotic systems support multiple scans on the same part without manual intervention, custom grippers for
varied part shapes, and overnight batch inspection pipelines with consistent repeatability. For high-volume AM
production, this is where throughput gains are most practically realized.
Integrated High-Speed CT Systems
Combining MetalJet sources, photon-counting detectors, helical acquisition, and GPU-cluster reconstruction,
integrated systems can now inspect a full part in under 7 seconds (demonstrated for 4680 battery cells, with
potential applicability to AM parts). Sunkin's key message: hardware speed is no longer the primary bottleneck.
The bottleneck has shifted to qualification frameworks, data infrastructure, AI validation, and standards — exactly
the open questions this workshop was convened to address.

Session 3.3 — Neutron Imaging for NDE at ORNL: HFIR/MARS and SNS/VENUS
Beamlines
Jean Bilheux (presenting for Hassina Bilheux) · Oak Ridge National Laboratory
Overview
Jean Bilheux presented on behalf of Hassina Bilheux (SNS VENUS Scientist), describing ORNL’s neutron imaging
capabilities at two complementary facilities: the MARS beamline at the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR), which
provides cold white-beam imaging optimized for high spatial resolution; and the VENUS beamline at the Spallation
Neutron Source (SNS), a new time-of-flight hyperspectral imaging facility and the first of its kind in the world to
integrate AI/ML from the ground up. VENUS is one of only three equivalent hyperspectral neutron imaging
facilities globally, alongside analogous instruments in the U.K. and Japan.
Neutron Imaging Advantages for NDE
Neutrons offer capabilities complementary to X-ray CT: sensitivity to light elements (H, O, N) invisible to X-rays;
access to magnetic information; ability to differentiate atoms of similar atomic number (e.g., Zn and Cu);
element/isotope sensitivity; and suitability for in-situ and in-operando sample environments. The primary NDE-
relevant techniques are imaging (defect detection), stress/strain analysis via Bragg-edge methods, and diffraction
for phase and microstructure characterization.
MARS Beamline at HFIR
MARS is optimized for high spatial resolution (below 20 µm) 2D and 3D NDE. Its detector suite spans five
configurations ranging from a high-resolution mode (3.8 µm pixel, 36×24 mm² FOV, 10–15 µm resolution) to a
large-FOV mode (16 µm pixel, 90×90 mm², ~50 µm resolution), with acquisition times from under 30 seconds to
15 minutes per radiograph depending on configuration. A demonstrated application used MARS 3D CT to reveal
composition gradients in a graded IN718/R41 superalloy AM component, with neutron attenuation contrast
distinguishing the elemental transition zone across build layers — information inaccessible to X-ray CT due to
similar X-ray attenuation of the two alloys (Huang et al., Frontiers in Metals and Alloys, 2022). A neutron
radiograph of additively manufactured Al6061 friction stir weld specimens revealed intense dark regions at each
layer interface, published in Metals (2023).
VENUS Beamline at SNS — Hyperspectral Time-of-Flight Capabilities
VENUS uses the pulsed SNS proton beam to generate neutron pulses whose wavelength is encoded in time-of-
flight. Each pixel of the detector simultaneously records a full transmission spectrum as a function of neutron
wavelength, enabling Bragg-edge radiography and tomography. By tracking how transmission changes across
crystallographic Bragg edges, VENUS maps phase fractions, grain orientation, texture, and residual strain across
an entire 2D field of view simultaneously — fundamentally different from point-by-point diffractometer strain
measurements. Its largest detector configuration (ANDOR CCD, 20×20 cm² FOV) accommodates components of
substantial size, with the experimental table rated to 500 kg and 1 m³ of space. The VENUS cave is equipped with
a 2-ton crane and a removable roof for loading oversized components.
A key VENUS application demonstrated was 2D residual strain mapping under applied load, where the shift of
Bragg-edge positions as a function of position encodes the local strain state. A Bragg-edge radiography study of
binder-jet AM Si–SiC ceramics before and after silicon melt infiltration (published in International Journal of
Applied Ceramic Technology, 2025) showed that X-ray transmission provided uniform contrast for both samples,
while neutron Bragg-edge imaging at 5 Å clearly distinguished the pre- and post-infiltrated phases — enabling
phase-sensitive NDE of ceramics opaque to X-ray contrast. In-situ Bragg-edge measurements tracked residual
strain relaxation in AM Inconel 625 across 250–865°C with 10-minute acquisitions producing full 2D strain maps
matching simulation results (Additive Manufacturing and Nuclear Instruments and Methods, 2021).
AI Integration at VENUS

Overview and Motivation
Dayakar Penumadu (Fred N. Peebles Professor and IAMM Chair, UTK) presented UTK’s high-energy X-ray CT and
multimodal characterization capabilities for materials that are extremely difficult to image with conventional X-
ray systems due to high density or attenuation — including tungsten, rhenium, tantalum, hafnium, zirconium, and
titanium. The UTK Nuclear Engineering facility houses a Linatron M9A (9 MVp, 30 Gy/min at 1 m) and a Comet

facility also operates a Fast Neutron Source (16x70 ft shielded hall), a Genie 16 DT neutron generator (3e8 n/s at
14.1 MeV), and an Adelphi DD109 source (1e9 n/s at 2.45 MeV), enabling combined X-ray and neutron multimodal
characterization. At 9 MeV, the mass attenuation coefficient for most dense metals drops to tractable levels, but
every acquisition remains a research-level effort requiring careful interpretation of beam hardening, scatter, and
resolution tradeoffs.
Calibration Methodology for Dense Materials
The team developed a systematic calibration methodology using tungsten as the primary test material. 2D
radiography of a 38.1×101.6 mm tungsten block at 9 MeV demonstrated that path-length-dependent contrast
variations could be quantified from gray-value line profiles, establishing baseline performance expectations. For
3D phantom calibration, tungsten cubes (7 mm) with EDM-drilled holes ranging from 0.25 mm to 5 mm in diameter
(eight blocks, A through H) were arranged in a 3D-printed holder and scanned using the 9 MeV LINAC with a rotary
stage. Reconstructed slices showed that 0.5 mm holes were detectable with reduced contrast while 0.25 mm holes

Penumadu presented combined high-energy X-ray and neutron imaging as the multimodal endpoint of the UTK
capability. Because photon and neutron attenuation coefficients follow entirely different elemental trends, the
two modalities provide contrast for different material features. For example, very thin hydrocarbon layers —
invisible to high-energy X-rays — are highly attenuating to neutrons and are clearly visible in neutron images.

CT vs. Ultrasound: Workflow Sequencing
A recurring theme was the appropriate sequencing of CT with other modalities. Multiple panelists described CT
as typically the last step in an inspection workflow rather than the first. The typical sequence begins with
ultrasound — including phase-contrast enhanced UT using phased array gantry systems — which provides rapid
volumetric screening at lower cost. CT is then applied for definitive characterization of flagged indications, or for
geometry-dependent cases where UT is inadequate. This sequencing is driven by the cost and time associated
with CT, even as scan times decrease.
Added context from the nuclear industry: pre-service inspections use moderate radiography, but in-service
inspections in nuclear plants are almost entirely UT and eddy current (EC) testing — CT is simply not feasible in a
radiation environment. This reinforces the importance of CT as a manufacturing-time qualification tool rather than
an in-service monitoring tool.
Detectability of Tight/Kissing Defects
A question from the floor about detecting tightly closed cracks (kissing bonds, collapsed pores post-HIP) prompted
detailed discussion. Penumadu explained that conventional CT is fundamentally limited to defect sizes that span
multiple pixels in the reconstructed volume — sub-pixel features cannot be resolved. The practical lower limit for
current lab-scale CT is on the order of 2,000–4,000 pixels across the field of view. Grating-based interferometry
(X-ray phase contrast and dark-field CT) was identified as the most promising path to sub-pixel feature detection,
providing absorption, phase, and dark-field contrast simultaneously from a single acquisition. However, this
technique is currently limited to dog-bone-scale specimens in laboratory settings; scaling to industrial component
sizes requires significant development. The consensus was that multi-modal fusion — high-energy CT for bulk
characterization combined with lab-scale interferometry on extracted coupons, tied together by feature
extraction algorithms — is the near-term realistic path.
Neutron Imaging for Kissing Bonds and Residual Stress
The panel discussed neutron-specific capabilities relevant to the session themes. Neutron grating interferometry
at MARS has demonstrated sensitivity at the nanoscale — potentially capable of detecting kissing bonds that are
invisible to conventional CT. An international round-robin study using neutron sources (including reactor and
pulsed sources) to characterize kissing bond analogs in AM specimens was mentioned as ongoing. For residual
stress, neutron diffraction and Bragg-edge strain mapping were identified as uniquely capable of providing non-
destructive bulk stress measurements — a critical need given that residual stress in AM parts degrades fatigue
performance and can confound NDE signal interpretation.
Cost of Inspection Relative to Part Criticality
An attendee from GE Aerospace raised the cost-of-ownership question: given the wide range of inspection
modalities from simple radiography to multi-hour high-energy CT, what is the appropriate cost basis for inspecting
AM parts? The panel consensus, echoed by the NAVAIR representative, was that inspection investment is
appropriately scaled to part criticality and consequences of failure. For single-load-path, safety-critical
components, cost is essentially no constraint — all stops are pulled. For non-critical balance-of-plant components,
a simple radiograph or UT scan may be entirely sufficient. The key is having a defensible criticality classification
system and having defined acceptance criteria before selecting the inspection method.
How to Choose the Right NDE Modality
A broader question from the moderator asked how the community could help practitioners navigate the
increasingly complex landscape of available NDE methods. Panelists offered several perspectives. Jacob noted
that ASNT produces quick-reference decision guides (porous vs. non-porous part, surface vs. volumetric defect,

etc.) as a starting filter, followed by expert consultation at the Level 3 or research scientist level. Bilheux cited

 • CT is the gold standard for ex-situ characterization of AM components but is typically the last step in a
 multimodal inspection sequence, preceded by UT and followed by targeted destructive validation.
 • Detecting tight/kissing defects and sub-pixel features requires X-ray phase-contrast (grating

 • Neutron imaging — particularly Bragg-edge strain mapping and neutron interferometry — provides

Standards Ecosystem: Historical Context
Jess Waller (New Mexico State University) provided a comprehensive survey of NDE standards development for
metal AM parts, tracing the field from its origins at a 2013 Penn State technology exchange through its current
state in 2026. The foundational document was NASA/TM-2014-218560, a state-of-the-discipline report co-
authored by Waller that catalogued AM NDE efforts across NASA, identified top-level challenges (deeply
embedded flaws, planar high-aspect-ratio defects perpendicular to the build direction, rough as-built surfaces

launched the standards development effort. Subsequent NASA documents include MSFC-SPEC-3716/3717 (2017,
process control and qualification for LPBF at Marshall), NASA-STD-6030 and NASA-STD-6033 (2021, agency-wide

a 90/95 POD requirement (NASA-STD-5009) for all Class A fracture-critical parts, with a defined inspection
sequence: pre-HIP volumetric NDE → HIP → machining → proof test → post-proof surface and volumetric NDE.
The handbook also provides a comprehensive flaw-type-to-NDE-method applicability matrix covering lack of
fusion, build layer voids, cross-layer voids, porosity, inclusions, cracking, surface anomalies, and dimensional

AMSC NDE Working Group: Eight Original Gaps and Four New Additions
The America Makes/ANSI Additive Manufacturing Standardization Collaborative (AMSC) identified 95 standards
gaps across all AM domains in 2018, of which 8 were NDE-specific. Waller walked through the current status of

ISO/ASTM TR 52906 (2022). Gap 3 (NDE of AM part guides, HIGH PRIORITY) — two standards published: ASTM
E3166 (2020) and ISO/ASTM TR 52905 (2023). Gap 6 (NDE of polymers and non-metallic AM, elevated from LOW
to MEDIUM PRIORITY) — one standard in progress (ASTM WK85121); the slide noted that not all polymeric parts
are Class C (do-no-harm), citing NASA examples including CubeSat structures, TPS heat shields, and thruster
manifolds where CT has been applied. Gap 8 (acceptance classes for critical parts, HIGH PRIORITY) — a work item
(ASTM WK75329, Practice for NDT Quality and Acceptability Levels of Additively Manufactured L-PBF Aerospace
Components) is now in ASTM F42.07 subcommittee ballot. Four new NDE gaps were added in AMSC Version 3.0
(2023): a 3D Image Quality Indicator (IQI) standard for CT sensitivity (HIGH PRIORITY); reference radiographic
images for AM anomalies (HIGH PRIORITY); effect-of-defect to define critical flaw type/size/distribution (MEDIUM

Session 4.2 (Lightning Talk) — NDT for Additive Manufacturing: NAVSEA Perspective

Session 4.4 (Lightning Talk) — ARP7065: Standardizing In Situ Process Monitoring
Taxonomy for Future Qualification Frameworks
Tyler LeBrun · The Barnes Global Advisors / University of Texas / SAE International (AMS-AM Committee Chair)
Tyler LeBrun (Chair of the SAE AMS-AM parent committee) presented ARP7065, an SAE Aerospace Recommended
Practice under development to establish a common taxonomy and organizational framework for in situ process
monitoring (ISPM) of AM. The core motivation is terminological fragmentation: “in situ process monitoring,” “in
situ assessment,” “in-process monitoring,” “on-machine inspection,” and related terms are used inconsistently
across producers, users, and regulators, impeding meaningful technical communication. Standards Development
Organizations (SDOs) should focus on outcomes of ISPM rather than prescribing specific methods — remaining
performance-based and path-independent to avoid picking technological winners, stifling innovation, or
prematurely foreclosing superior future approaches. This outcome-based framing is designed to dovetail with
existing SAE process control standards AMS7003 (LPBF process controls) and AMS7032 (machine qualification).
ARP7065 organizes ISPM into a category-tier-type framework. Two categories are distinguished: passive systems
(sensor evaluation — record only) and active systems (sensor control — directly influence the process). Three tiers
reflect increasing proximity to the part: Tier 1 (Machine Sensor Evaluation/Control, supporting machine
acceptance); Tier 2 (Process Execution Evaluation/Control, supporting process acceptance); and Tier 3 (Part
Attribute Evaluation/Control, supporting part acceptance). Tier 3 is further subdivided by type: A (Flaw
Evaluation/Control), B (Material Evaluation/Control), and C (Geometry Evaluation/Control). The Tier 3B active —
Material Control — and Tier 3C active — Geometry Control — categories represent near-future or aspirational
capabilities (real-time microstructure tailoring and in-flight geometry correction). ARP7065 is intended as a parent
document, with child documents to follow for each block, creating qualification frameworks for individual ISPM
modalities. The taxonomy has received positive engagement from Johns Hopkins University, NFC, machine OEMs,
and industry users.

Session 5.1 — Recent Advances on the Use of In Situ Monitoring as an NDE Tool for
Laser Powder Bed Fusion
Zackary Snow · Oak Ridge National Laboratory
Motivation and Framework
Snow opened by framing the purpose of in-situ process monitoring around four complementary goals: record
keeping, process understanding, anomaly detection, and bolstering traditional ex-situ NDE. The talk centered
specifically on laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) and the challenge of scaling computed tomography inspection to
radiographically dense, complex AM components. CT artifacts, noise, and the resolution-scan time-field-of-view
trade-off limit what conventional ex-situ methods can resolve — motivating in-situ approaches that generate
layer-wise information throughout the build. Over the past several years, ORNL has developed the Peregrine
software platform, which collects multimodal sensor data in real time, performs layer-wise image processing, runs
AI-based anomaly detection, and constructs a full 3D digital record of each component.
Case Study 1: Direct In-Situ Flaw Detection and POD
The first case study, conducted in collaboration with RTX Technologies Research Center, investigated the spatial
correlation between spatter particles observed in-situ and lack-of-fusion flaws confirmed by ex-situ XCT. The build
was designed on an EOS M290 in Inconel 718: dedicated “spatter generators” were printed upstream with
elevated energy density (370 W, 500 mm/s, 84 J/mm³) while downstream specimens used nominal parameters
(285 W, 960 mm/s, 34 J/mm³) at a reduced argon flow rate. Peregrine collected visible camera and EOSTATE OT
thermal emission imagery at 83 μm/pixel and 9 fps respectively. All resulting XCT volumes were registered to the
in-situ data stack, enabling systematic comparison between in-situ signals and volumetric flaw populations.
A deep-learning neural network was trained to directly detect lack-of-fusion flaw locations from the multimodal
in-situ image stack. Probability-of-detection (POD) analysis was performed following MIL-HDBK-1823A using
ORNL’s Pterodactyl software. The network detected 95% of the 342 XCT flaws with spherical equivalent diameters
(SED) of approximately 260 μm or greater, and detected all 145 flaws with SEDs of approximately 325 μm or
greater (maximum SED 590 μm). The resulting a₉₀/ₕ₅ metric demonstrated that in-situ POD analysis is applicable
to L-PBF and, in principle, extensible to other AM processes.
Case Study 2: Voxelized Property Prediction Model (VPPM)
The second case study introduced the Voxelized Property Prediction Model (VPPM) framework as a
complementary approach to direct flaw detection. Rather than predicting individual flaw locations from in-situ
data, VPPMs predict XCT-derived flaw statistics within approximately 1 mm³ super-voxels. Inputs include
multimodal in-situ anomaly detections (swelling, overheating, recoater disturbances, spatter), off-axis and on-axis
sensor data, scan path features, part geometry metadata, and build log data. XCT training targets include flaw

count density, percent porosity, and maximum flaw size per super-voxel. The VPPM architecture is a fully
connected neural network bridging the in-situ and CT domains.
Demonstration experiments were performed on a Colibrium Additive M2 in SS316H (100 μm layer thickness) with
visible reflected and thermal emissions cameras, yielding a fully public dataset of 64 registered CT scans. The build
design produced a multimodal, spatially non-uniform flaw population by subjecting nominal specimens to
upstream spatter generator contamination. VPPM predictions of percent porosity and maximum flaw size agreed

sizing of rogue stochastic lack-of-fusion flaws in specimens that were withheld from training. The framework
subsequently generalized to an EOS M290 in Inconel 625, reproducing spatially localized delamination features

Session 5.2 — Advanced Manufacturing: In-Situ NDE During Additive Manufacturing

Current Practices, Gaps, and Strategic Context
Panetta opened with a strategic overview of where NDE fits within the AM qualification ecosystem. Current
commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) sensors monitor machine condition and melt pool dynamics but provide no
measurement of deposited microstructure or defect generation. The Aerospace Corporation’s program positions

detectability encompasses microstructure, contamination, and subsurface defects. Most in-situ monitoring sensor
technologies remain at TRL 4, with optical at TRL 8/9, accelerometers at TRL 8, and thermal imaging at TRL 6/7.
Key gaps to advancing TRL include signal-to-known-defect correlation, lack of baseline and OEM data, absence of
standards, undefined critical flaw sizes, inability to generate representative natural flaws, sensor and data fusion

Session 5.3 — Electrical Tomography Methods for Non-Invasive Monitoring and NDE
of Manufacturing Processes
Tyler N. Tallman · Purdue University (presented remotely)
Self-Sensing Materials and Electrical Impedance Tomography
Tallman’s talk surveyed electrical tomography as a class of radiation-free, low-cost, high-temporal-resolution NDE
and process monitoring modalities that exploit the stimulus-responsive electrical properties of many engineering
materials. Many materials — including carbon fiber composites, conductive nanocomposites, and cement —
exhibit coupling between their permittivity and conductivity and mechanical, thermal, or damage state; such
materials are described as self-sensing. Paired with electrical tomography, full-field spatial maps of these
properties can be reconstructed non-invasively from boundary electrode measurements.
Electrical impedance tomography (EIT) estimates the spatially varying conductivity distribution within a domain
from voltage-current measurements at its boundary. The reconstruction is cast as an ill-posed inverse optimization
problem and requires regularization to constrain the solution space. Standard regularization strategies include
Tikhonov (L2 identity), smoothness (discrete Laplace), and edge-preserving total variation operators. Tallman’s
group developed a mixed regularization combining a smoothness term and a focal term (sensitive to large
statistical deviations in the data), which is specifically suited to detecting small, highly localized damage such as
fiber ruptures and impact damage. Applications demonstrated include impact damage detection in carbon black-
modified glass fiber tubes, impact damage in a NACA airfoil carbon fiber laminate, notch and impact damage in
an annulus-stiffened carbon fiber tube, and indentation damage in carbon fiber under bending loads. A physics-
informed neural network (PINN) variant was shown to improve localization for complex AM geometries such as a
printed carbon fiber truss structure.
Manufacturing Application and ECT Concept
In a manufacturing context, EIT was applied to monitor the in-process condition of a thin film used during
semiconductor fabrication (sponsored by Samsung Electronics). Methods and hardware were validated on a
surrogate carbon nanofiber (CNF)-modified polyurethane film (150 mm × 120 mm × 0.71 mm), successfully
detecting ruptures, pinholes smaller than 1 mm, and thinned regions. Mixed regularization outperformed
standard smoothness methods for localized defects, while smoothness regularization was better suited for
detecting gradual thinning. Samsung is currently integrating this approach into their manufacturing process.
Tallman introduced electrical capacitance tomography (ECT) as a non-contact variant that maps permittivity rather
than conductivity. Unlike EIT, ECT does not require physical contact with the specimen, making it more amenable
to in-process monitoring. The electric field is not constrained to the specimen (unlike EIT current), which reduces
spatial resolution but shares similar inverse-problem mathematics. Prior work by Gupta et al. demonstrated planar
ECT (PECT) for NDE of carbon fiber composite delamination. Tallman proposed a “smart film” concept: process
films (used in FDM AM, composite lamination, or roll-to-roll electronics) lined with electrodes on one side and
bounded by a conductive mesh acting as a Faraday cage to contain the electric field. This concept remains at the
conceptual stage and was presented as an emerging research direction actively seeking demonstration.

Session 5.4 — Embedded Sensing and Thermographic Process Monitoring in DED
Eric MacDonald · University of Texas at El Paso
Embedded Sensors and 3D Printed Electronics
MacDonald presented two complementary approaches to instrumenting AM processes by placing sensors where
conventional access is impossible. The first demonstrated binder jetting of sand molds with embedded Bluetooth
sensor packages: sensors measuring pressure, temperature, motion, rotation, and magnetic field were suspended

The second approach demonstrated screen-printed sensors embedded within DED tensile bar builds: a sensor

MacDonald noted the sensor data serves dual purposes — monitoring the deposition process and enabling end-

MacDonald described an ongoing effort using a Mitsui Seiki CNC-integrated hot-wire laser DED head to monitor
each deposited layer as it cools. Thermal gradient imagery acquired between layers showed spatially non-uniform

thermal anomalies and internal porosity. MacDonald acknowledged the result was initially misinterpreted (surface

CT rather than a replacement, with the distinct advantage that layer-by-layer detection could enable in-process
correction — machining off a defective layer before resuming the build.

Session 6.1 — Management of Digital NDT Records with DICONDE
Gregory Mohr · Serco / Submarine Industrial Base Program Office
Context: NAVSEA and the Submarine Industrial Base
Mohr opened the session by framing DICONDE within the context of the Submarine Industrial Base (SIB) program,
which is working to accelerate uptake of digital NDT methods across the shipbuilding sector. Multiple NAVSEA
documents now require DICONDE wherever it is available, including T9074-AS-GIB-010/271 (Rev. 2) requirements
for nondestructive testing methods, the CR/DX allowance letter (9074 Ser 05P/089, 14 Apr 26), welding standard
250-1500-1 (Revision 19), and information notes document 392-0775. All of these require a robust data
management plan for digital NDT records. Mohr credited Craig Sheppard (NAVSEA) for the careful implementation
work that produced the allowance letters enabling the transition from film radiography to computed radiography
(CR) and digital radiography (DX/DDA), including associated checklists covering processes and procedures,
technique parameters, and data management requirements.
What DICONDE Is — and Why It Matters
DICONDE — Digital Imaging and Communication in Nondestructive Evaluation — is not merely a file format. It is
a full ecosystem for managing digital NDT data, built by extending the DICOM standard (Digital Imaging and
Communications in Medicine) that has been universal for medical, veterinary, and dental digital imaging since
Version 3 in 1993. DICOM is managed by NEMA and is jointly standardized with ISO 12052. DICONDE extensions
are managed by the ASTM E07.11 subcommittee, which renames fields and adds NDE-specific information objects
without altering the underlying data structures, preserving full interoperability with the DICOM software
ecosystem. Because value representations, required/optional attributes, and network protocols remain identical,
all DICOM-compatible tools remain usable with DICONDE data.
DICOM’s network architecture uses a client-server model with Service Class Provider (SCP) and Service Class User
(SCU) roles. Machine-to-machine communications rely on DIMSE (DICOM Message Service Element) level-6
protocols running over TCP/IP, ensuring transactional integrity — data is confirmed received before a transfer
concludes. Web protocols enable machine-user interactions via standard browsers. Encryption and PKI-based
security are defined in Part 15, requiring no reinvention. Data objects are self-contained and include all necessary
metadata in a defined hierarchy: Component (analogous to “patient”), Study (examination/inspection), Series,
and Objects. This self-contained structure enables plug-and-play exchange across vendors. The DICOM standard
is freely available in multiple formats at dicomstandard.org/current; implementers need only read a vendor’s
conformance statement (Part 2) to understand what the system supports.
DICONDE Standards Hierarchy and Practical Implementation
The ASTM DICONDE standards family is organized in a hierarchy rooted in NEMA PS3 DICOM. At the top level,
ASTM E3169 provides a tutorial guide to the family. ASTM E2339 defines the core industrial extension and
component labeling. Modality-specific information object definitions cover computed radiography (E2738),
ultrasound (E2663, updated 2025), digital radiography/DDA (E2669), computed tomography (E2767), eddy

Session 6.2 — Designed to Evolve: The Path to Obsolescence-Resistant NDE
Automation
Abhishek Shukla · UTEX Scientific Instruments
The Obsolescence Problem in NDE Automation
Shukla opened with a frank assessment of a problem familiar to anyone who operates long-lived inspection
systems: NDE automation technology obsolesces on a timeline of years, while aircraft and submarine programs
operate on timescales of decades. He illustrated this with a Google Ngram analysis of the words “mechanization”
and “automation,” noting that the frequency of “automation” in published text has peaked and troughed
repeatedly in alignment with each major enabling technology wave — PLCs in the 1960s, personal computers in
the 1990s, and AI/ML in the 2020s. Each wave causes organizations to rebuild automation systems rather than
evolve them. The central question he posed is: when the next wave arrives, can your system survive it? Specifically,
can you replace the instrument, the robot or gantry, the inspection software, and the data file format — and still
have a working system?
Modular Architecture: A Case Study in Multi-Part Automated Inspection
Shukla described two deployed automated inspection systems as concrete examples. The first was a next-
generation replacement for a 15-year-old system at an aerospace site, designed to inspect over 2,000 part types
(approximately 6,000 instances per aircraft) with an expandable part library. The system achieved multi-inspection
coverage using linear and radius phased array transducers, and was built to be multi-instrument: the current
ultrasonic pulser-receiver (a MicroPulse LPA from Peak NDT, UK) can be swapped out for an Evident (Olympus)
instrument, a TPAC, or others without any change to the data collection software, robot programming, or analysis
configuration. Requalification is still required, but the software infrastructure does not need to be rebuilt. The
second example was a fully automated robotic inspection cell in which the robot reads QR codes on parts and
shelves, selects the appropriate end-effector gripper from three options, and executes the scan. Because part
pickup position varies slightly, the system uses the acquired UT amplitude data across the array, normalizes by
time-of-flight, adjusts the robot scan path in real time, and delivers correctly analyzed results. This live path
correction removes the need for perfect gripper repeatability.
Separation of Concerns: Automation, Data Collection, and Analysis
The architectural principle underlying both systems is modularization via strict separation of concerns. The
automation layer (robot/gantry/PLC) communicates with the data collection software through a defined machine
interface that exchanges metadata; neither side needs to understand the other’s internals. The data collection
software is responsible only for talking to the instrument and producing data files; it is replaceable without
touching the automation. The analysis software (IW Evaluate or any alternative) operates on the data files
independently. This means the robot can be replaced without changing the software, and the software can be
replaced without changing the robot. Shukla noted that inter-organizational collaboration is a prerequisite — the

Session 6.3 — Accommodating Inconsistent Geometries for Mapping NDE Data in the
Digital Thread
Stephen D. Holland, Lijie Liu, Adarsh Krishnamurthy, Qing Li · Center for Nondestructive Evaluation (CNDE), Iowa State
University
The Problem: Geometric Inconsistency Breaks NDE Data Projection
Holland opened with a concrete failure case: a flash thermography dataset acquired on a composite panel could
not be correctly projected onto the component’s CAD model because the specimen had warped out of shape
when removed from its autoclave mold, producing a major misalignment between the thermal image and the CAD
geometry. As-built geometry never perfectly matches the design — the question is only how different it is.
Geometric inconsistencies are pervasive in aerospace manufacturing: unstiffened composite structures distort
due to residual thermal stresses from curing; aluminum skins are floppy until stiffeners are attached; large-scale
structures such as aircraft wings deflect substantially depending on fuel load; and AM parts routinely exhibit mm-
order deviations from the CAD model that are consequential when NDE is being used to localize flaws to mm
precision.
Existing reverse-engineering approaches (Tang, 2010) create independent models from measurements without
preserving CAD topology, making it impossible to map NDE data back to the design domain. Non-rigid registration
methods (Deng et al., 2022) are intrinsically non-unique and fail for large deformations. Holland’s objective was
to enable a bidirectional, topology-preserving mapping between as-designed and as-built representations so that
any NDE measurement on the physical part can be accurately projected onto the design CAD model, enabling
cross-serial-number data fusion, life prediction, and design feedback.
MeshMap: Eigenmode-Based Mesh Fitting
The first component, MeshMap, fits the nominal CAD mesh to a measured point cloud using finite element
vibrational eigenmodes as a deformation basis. FE modal analysis of the design mesh generates a set of mode
shapes; an optimized linear combination of these modes is then found that minimizes the distance between the
deformed mesh and the measured point cloud. Because physical deformations of real structures tend to be
smooth and low-frequency in character, a small number of modes captures the dominant shape. In the primary
case study — a metal bar simultaneously twisted and bent — five eigenmodes captured the gross deformation,
and the superposition of all modes gave a near-exact fit. Because the deformed mesh retains the same vertex
topology as the original CAD mesh, every point on the fitted mesh has a well-defined correspondence back to the
nominal design coordinates.

2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop — Report

CADMap: Topology-Preserving NURBS Surface Fitting
The second component, CADMap, extends the fitting from the polygonal mesh to the actual NURBS (Non-Uniform
Rational B-Spline) surfaces of the CAD model. The MeshMap result is used to segment the point cloud by CAD
surface patch. Each NURBS patch is then independently fit to its segment. Topology-preserving constraints are
enforced across surface boundaries: matched pairs of points on adjacent trim curves in parametric space are
forced to the same geometric location, ensuring water-tight continuity with negligible gaps between adjoining
surfaces. The loss function is a weighted combination of Chamfer distance (average of minimum point-to-surface
distances) and Hausdorff distance (maximum of minimum distances), optimized via projected gradient descent.
The resulting deformed CAD model retains the original patch topology, enabling bidirectional point
correspondence between the as-built surface and the design coordinates. Quantitative validation across three
case studies yielded normalized Chamfer distances of 0.33–0.51% and Hausdorff distances of 1.95–4.00%, with
negligible average gaps between adjacent surfaces, and the framework performed consistently across different
scanner types. Holland framed the remaining unmet need as “reversing the digital thread”: sending NDE
measurement information back into the design domain to drive process optimization and digital twin updates
awaits CAD software functionality that does not yet exist. The codebase is being prepared for open-source release
under NSF funding.

Session 6.4 — Damara Tern: A Data Management Platform for Additive Manufacturing
at ORNL MDF
Stephanie Cooper · Oak Ridge National Laboratory
Motivation: AM as a Reproducible Experiment
Cooper framed each AM build as a scientific experiment that must be reproducible, understandable, and
optimizable. To achieve this, data must be captured at every stage of the manufacturing workflow: materials
selection and feedstock, CAD design, iterative process development, the AM build itself (with in-situ process
monitoring), post-processing (heat treatment, finishing), component evaluation and testing, and mechanical
characterization with coupons. The aggregate of these records constitutes a manufacturing data package
representing a single component. Repeated across many builds, these packages form a manufacturing science
library from which insights can be extracted and fed back into future production — closing the optimization loop.
The challenge is making this data collection structured, standardized, and trustworthy enough to be shared across
ORNL, industry partners, academia, and federal agencies.
The Operation-Trackable Data Model
Damara Tern organizes all data around two core abstractions. An Operation is any action performed by a machine
or a human (for example, an L-PBF build, a heat treatment cycle, a CT scan, or a tensile test). A Trackable is any
physical or digital object that can be the subject of an operation (for example, a build plate, a metal powder lot, a
printed build, or a tensile specimen). Operations consume input trackables and produce output trackables; by
recording these relationships, the platform can reconstruct the complete digital thread for any component —
tracing a tensile specimen back through sectioning, heat treatment, the AM build, and procurement of raw
materials. This model is intentionally generic: the same framework applies regardless of AM process, material, or
downstream test type.
Platform Architecture and Interface
Damara Tern is implemented as a Django (Python) web application organized around modular “apps”: core apps
for home, operations, trackables, database connectivity (mdf_db), and a REST API; optional apps for visualization,
LLM integration, report generation, synchronization, and process-specific extensions (Peregrine integration,
tensile testing, XCT, etc.). The platform stores only metadata and pointers to data files — it functions as a library
catalog, not a data warehouse. The relational database contains information about operations, trackables,

machines, people, projects, locations, and file pointers; the actual data files reside in external storage. Operation
entry forms are intentionally generic but extensible: machine-specific fields (shielding gas, build plate material,

allowing new machines and processes to be onboarded rapidly. The interface provides operation pages as “catalog

What Metadata Matters — and the Problem of the “Why”
The moderator opened the panel with three linked questions: what metadata is essential for FAIR (Findable,
Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) NDE data; how do AI/ML applications change or extend those requirements;
and how does one justify the cost of collecting and maintaining richer metadata? Holland led off by observing that
storing inspection parameters and recovering them later are obvious requirements — the harder challenge is
capturing the “why”: why was this test performed, why was this step included in the manufacturing process?
Those contextual motivations are the easiest metadata to lose and the hardest to reconstruct later. Mohr
reinforced this from an implementation perspective: interoperability is the real key because there is no all-in-one
solution — NDT data must always be cross-referenced against ERP systems, well-tracking databases, or other
enterprise records, and the ability for one system to talk to another is more valuable than any individual data field.
Cooper noted that domain experts typically have a clear intuitive understanding of what metadata matters — it
is what they write in their notebooks — but the harder challenge is harmonizing terminology across facilities and
teams. The same physical quantity is called different things at different organizations, and existing standards have
not fully solved this. She argued for building a shared dictionary or controlled vocabulary associating equivalent
terms across organizations, as inconsistent naming is a greater practical barrier than missing data fields. Shukla
reframed the core metadata requirements as who, what, when, and why — agreeing with Holland that “why” is
the hardest — and added that the unit problem (millimeters vs. inches, or even just inconsistent unit labeling
across instrument vendors) remains unresolved industry-wide. He called for lookup tables or translation layers

Session 7.1 — Rapid Qualification Using Multimodal In-Situ Monitoring and the Digital
Safe Zone
Shuchi Khurana · Addiguru
The Qualification Cost Problem
Khurana opened by framing the qualification cost problem with publicly available numbers from Eaton Aerospace,
presented at AMXM the previous year: approximately six months, $180,000, and 400 test coupons to qualify a
single L-PBF process at a single facility. She noted that delta qualification — required even when moving a machine
from one side of a room to the other during facility expansion — restarts the full clock. These costs negate many
of the production advantages that AM offers in the first place. Her target was a six-fold reduction: roughly one
month, $30,000, and 30–50 coupons. The enabling question is what in-situ monitoring data, combined with what
analysis approach, is sufficient to compress the qualification evidence base to that level while maintaining
confidence in part quality.
Sensing Modalities and What Each Contributes
Khurana organized the sensing landscape around the electromagnetic spectrum from 400 nm to 12,000+ nm.
Visible-range cameras (400–700 nm) capture layer-by-layer images and, with AI-based analysis, detect recoater
disturbances, super-elevation, spatter, soot, and powder spreading anomalies — automating what was previously
a human visual inspection task. Near-infrared (NIR) imaging enables hotspot detection and identifies lack-of-fusion
events and gas flow variation. Optical tomography (OT, near-IR wavelength) generates per-layer composite images
that reveal spatially non-uniform intensity patterns correlated with subsurface porosity and melt pool instability.
Longwave infrared (LWIR) cameras provide interlayer temperature measurements — capturing surface
temperature in the 0–250 °C range immediately before the next laser scan — and enable heat accumulation
mapping. Acoustic sensors at early TRL showed promising results for additional anomaly detection.

                                                                                   ORNL | May 19–20, 2026 | Page 68

2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop — Report

A key use case Khurana emphasized was stopping a failing build: when the system detects anomalies of sufficient
severity or frequency, the operator can halt the build before incurring post-processing and inspection costs on a
part that will ultimately be rejected. A second use case is saving a build: the LWIR-based distortion prediction
algorithm, trained on hundreds of builds, can alert the operator to imminent distortion 50–100 layers in advance,
allowing intervention — such as switching off the affected part or modifying process parameters — before the
failure materializes. Khurana showed a case in which heat accumulation patterns visible at layer 3–4 correctly
predicted distortion that appeared at layer 404, with corroborating evidence from NIR data.
Anomaly Characterization, Defect Probability, and the Digital Safe Zone
Addiguru’s platform classifies detected anomalies (recoater hopping, streaking, swelling, spatter, soot, clumps) by
defect probability: low, medium, or high. Anomalies that appear in one or two layers and do not repeat are
generally benign; anomalies that recur over multiple layers correlate with higher defect probability. The
classification model was trained on data from ten-plus machines across multiple builds and is designed to operate
out-of-the-box without retraining by a domain expert, enabling use by technicians without AI expertise. The build
report produced at the end of each run summarizes anomaly locations, brightness variation, interlayer
temperature trends, and defect probability assessments. The overarching qualification concept Khurana
presented is the “digital safe zone”: a multimodal process signature envelope defined by nominal builds that in-
situ data from subsequent builds must fall within for the build to be considered qualified. Khurana argued that
this approach, combined with the AI-assisted stop/save capabilities, could reduce the coupon burden by providing
continuous process evidence across every layer of every build — rather than relying entirely on destructive testing
after the fact. Addiguru won an award at the ASTM Americas monitoring challenge among 15 participating
companies.

Session 7.2 — Optical Tomography-Based Build Signatures for Expedited L-PBF
Qualification
Yash Parikh · EOS North America, Inc.
The Build Signature Concept
Parikh framed his talk around the analogy of a handwritten signature: just as one can immediately recognize a
forgery because it deviates from the established signature pattern, a build process can be “signed” by running
three to five identical nominal build jobs and extracting the resulting optical tomography (OT) gray value signature.
Any subsequent production build can then be compared against this signature — with tolerance limits set around
it — to determine whether the build process is in control. He presented this as a mechanism for meeting
qualification requirements with fewer coupons by replacing statistical sampling of destructive test specimens with
continuous per-layer process evidence from every build. EOS is the world’s largest L-PBF manufacturer with
approximately 6,000 systems sold globally; Parikh noted that the MDF at ORNL houses roughly 50 of these metal
systems within walking distance.
Closed-Loop OT Control and Cross-Section Sensitivity
The experimental platform used an EOS M290 with 142 coupon specimens arranged on a 250×250 mm build plate
— a geometry with three sequential cross-sectional changes across 300 layers (rectangular base, cylindrical
middle, conical top) — specifically designed to test whether OT sensors detect changes in cross-sectional area.
The OT output is a dimensionless gray value (8-bit or 16-bit) that captures melt pool emission intensity. Parikh
demonstrated that the gas flow direction (back to front on the M290) produces a spatially non-uniform OT
distribution across the plate, and that the 67° scan rotation pattern imposes additional systematic variation. The
cross-sectional transitions were clearly visible as step changes in per-layer gray value profiles, confirming that the
OT signature encodes geometric information as well as process state.

                                                                                     ORNL | May 19–20, 2026 | Page 69

2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop — Report

EOS’s closed-loop control system uses a PID controller — with a user-defined correction factor (e.g., 5% or 10%
gray value reduction) — to actively compensate the laser power in real time based on deviation from a target gray
value. This is not a black box: the correction factor is user-specified and the controller’s action is deterministic and
interpretable. Parikh showed side-by-side builds with and without closed-loop control: without control, individual
specimens at different plate positions had mean gray values of approximately 41,000–49,000 (varying with gas
flow proximity); with closed-loop control, all specimens converged to approximately 41,000 — substantially
reducing position-dependent variation. Ti-6Al-4V coupons extracted from closed-loop and open-loop builds
showed similar microstructures (α/β ratio ~98/2%) but measurably different hardness distributions, confirming
that the gray value control has real metallurgical consequences.
Location-Specific Qualification and Reduced Coupon Counts
A key finding was that combining all specimens into a single aggregate signature and applying a tolerance band
does not work reliably: specimens near the gas flow inlet have characteristically different (and noisier) signatures
than specimens far from it. Parikh proposed location-specific signatures as the solution — establishing a master
signature and tolerance band for each distinct build plate location by running two or three qualification builds at
that position, rather than pooling all positions together. He applied this approach to specimens from a complex
mixed build (54 different part types spanning tensile bars, density cubes, fatigue specimens, and functional
components) and demonstrated that vertical tensile bars and fatigue specimens could each be qualified against
their own location-specific signatures. He proposed that the AMS 7032 qualification standard — which currently
requires a full layout of 41 specimens — could be addressed with as few as 23 specimens if closed-loop control
and OT-based signature qualification are used together, and that the methodology is being proposed to the
relevant standards bodies for adoption.
Cross-machine transferability was explored in collaboration with Ohio State University: baseline OT signatures
from the EOS M290 at EOS were used to predict qualification outcomes on an OSU machine of the same type.
Because each machine has its own inherent OT distribution — reflecting differences in optics, gas flow installation,
and build chamber conditioning — the absolute gray values differ between machines, but the relative trends
within a given machine are consistent. Parikh showed that specimens in identical plate positions on the two
machines showed opposite OT rank orderings (EOS sample #5 higher than #22; OSU sample #22 higher than #5),
confirming that machine-specific baseline signatures are required before cross-machine qualification can be
applied. The data were collected at approximately 400 GB per build using 10-second OT capture intervals, with a
Mira prototype thermal camera generating approximately 2 GB for the same build.

Session 7.3 — AI-Assisted CT Characterization and Interrupted-Test Registration for
Flaw Evolution Analysis
Rahul Franklin · Oak Ridge National Laboratory
High-Throughput CT Characterization with SIMURGH
Franklin presented a high-throughput CT characterization and flaw evolution tracking workflow applied to L-PBF
tensile specimens printed across a range of energy densities. Hundreds of specimens printed with varying process
parameters were CT-scanned using SIMURGH — ORNL’s AI-enabled CT reconstruction framework — which
accelerates reconstruction and improves artifact correction through model-based iterative reconstruction (MBIR)
with deep learning-based beam hardening compensation. SIMURGH’s MBIR approach corrects beam hardening
artifacts before segmentation, significantly simplifying downstream flaw identification without altering the
underlying projection data. Initial characterization identified a low-porosity process window between 52 and 135
J/mm³ energy density, and three parameters were selected for detailed study: 51, 71, and 135 J/mm³.
Hybrid Specimens and Interrupted Testing

To isolate the effect of each parameter on mechanical failure, Franklin’s group printed hybrid tensile specimens
with three distinct gage-length segments printed at 51, 71, and 135 J/mm³ respectively, each color-coded in post-
processing. CT scanning confirmed that the 135 J/mm³ segment had the highest porosity, 71 J/mm³ the lowest,
and 51 J/mm³ intermediate. Specimens were subjected to interrupted tensile testing: CT scans were acquired at

fracture. For 316L stainless steel specimens, fracture consistently occurred in the 135 J/mm³ segment as expected.
Flaw population evolution was tracked by comparing registered CT volumes across the three time steps: sphericity
of flaws (initially ~0.9, indicating near-spherical gas porosity) decreased significantly at the interrupted stage,
indicating that flaws were elongating and coalescing. Equivalent diameter distributions shifted toward larger sizes
between interrupted and post-fracture stages, consistent with void growth and crack initiation. Interestingly,
considerable deformation was also observed in regions with very low porosity, suggesting that grain structure and
crystallographic orientation play roles in determining local failure behavior independent of porosity — an avenue
identified for further microstructural characterization.

For IN-625 Inconel specimens, the interrupted-test registration workflow succeeded in identifying the specific pre-
existing flaw that caused final fracture. The causative flaw, initially measured at a sphericity of 0.87 and an
equivalent diameter of approximately 33 μm, had elongated dramatically by the interrupted stage to a sphericity
of 0.37 and an equivalent diameter of approximately 254 μm — a transformation that was unambiguously
traceable across registered CT datasets. Post-fracture visualization confirmed the flaw growing into a crack,
providing a direct experimental link between initial flaw morphology and failure mechanism. A third specimen set,
printed far from the argon gas flow, exhibited extensive spatter-induced internal porosity; the workflow
successfully identified the failure-initiating flaw in this case as well.
The initial registration workflow was manual: fiducial features within each specimen were identified by hand and
used to compute the rigid-body transformation aligning scans from different time steps. This was effective but
time-prohibitive for the hundreds of specimens in the study. Franklin described an automated registration
approach under development that uses unique surface features at the upper and lower grip sections as fiducials,
extracts the transformation automatically, and registers the gage region with no manual intervention in a matter
of seconds. The first two datasets tested with automated registration showed no perceptible loss of accuracy
compared to the manual process. Once validated on additional specimens, the automated workflow will be
presented at a conference. The full pipeline — SIMURGH-based CT reconstruction, AI-assisted segmentation, and
automated registration for time-series flaw tracking — constitutes a scalable high-throughput characterization
capability for linking manufacturing process parameters to mechanical performance through quantitative flaw
evolution data.

Progress, Gaps, and Where the Field Should Focus Next
An audience comment prompted a broader discussion about whether federal investment in AM NDE has made
sufficient progress. Khurana offered a candid assessment: five years ago, industry’s primary pain point was

SIMURGH’s MBIR and the Artifact Correction Question
An audience question asked whether SIMURGH’s model-based iterative reconstruction — which reconstructs CT
volumes from sparse projection angles using a physics-informed forward model — risked removing real features
(such as flaws) along with beam hardening artifacts. Franklin clarified that MBIR corrects beam hardening artifacts
by operating on a physics-based forward model of the X-ray propagation, not by post-processing the
reconstructed volume. Because the correction is applied in projection space (or equivalently through the iterative
reconstruction loop) rather than to the already-reconstructed image, it does not have the same risk of confusing
real material heterogeneity with streak artifacts. If a flaw is present and does not match the CAD model, the
mismatch is preserved and visible in the output. The artifact correction is for training purposes — making the
reconstructed volumes cleaner and more consistent so that downstream AI-based segmentation produces reliable
results — and does not alter the projection data itself.

Breakout Session: AI/ML Applications in NDE for Advanced Manufacturing (Day 1)

The group’s near-term priorities centered on using AI to accelerate inspection throughput — particularly through
AI-assisted large-area scanning (UT, ET, and similar modalities) — and on achieving industrially acceptable
detection performance. A widely cited target was greater than 95% detection accuracy, matched against the
performance of the best human expert systems. Nuclear industry participants added a specific qualification
perspective: the NRC’s current expectation is 100% detection of flaws above a defined size threshold before AI
can be accepted as a qualified technique. False call rates are treated as a separate concern: participants reported
that nuclear industry sponsors could tolerate flagging rates in the range of 15–20% (areas flagged for secondary
human review) provided that no true positives are missed. TVA participants described a demonstration on reactor
upper-head penetration data in which an AI system flagged approximately 7–7.5% of total scan line length for
review — a rate considered acceptable — with separate industry experience suggesting rates as low as 2% in some
configurations.
A second priority cluster addressed accelerating the qualification and acceptance of AM parts. Participants agreed
that AI should play a role in compressing qualification timelines but that problem definition must precede model
development: clarity about what the AI is being asked to detect or decide is foundational. The group flagged a
strategic question about where AI should and should not be applied — with the pragmatic recommendation to
identify “low-hanging fruit” in non-fracture-critical, lower-risk applications first, building operational experience
and regulatory trust before moving to safety-critical components. Human-assisted AI (AI as a decision-support
tool rather than a decision-maker) was broadly endorsed as the appropriate near-term deployment posture.
Additional priorities included developing generalizable models and secure data workflows, building trust in AI
algorithms through transparent validation, requiring training datasets to include both positive (flaw) and negative
(clean) examples, and integrating physical reasoning into model architectures.

Data availability and quality dominated the challenges discussion. Labeled training data is scarce in NDE/AM,
proprietary and closed datasets restrict community access, and non-digital legacy NDE records require digitization
before they can contribute to model development. Inconsistent metadata and data conditioning standards, data
leakage risks during training, and noise that can mask genuine defect signals were all raised. In-situ monitoring
data presents a particular complication: flaws detected during a build may not persist to the final part because
subsequent layers can re-fuse them, and post-build HIP treatments can close pores entirely — making it difficult
to establish ground truth that connects in-situ observations to final-part condition.
Model robustness was a second major challenge: AI models trained for one material, geometry, machine, or
process parameter set frequently fail to generalize to others. Explainability of model outputs — including the
ability to provide confidence metrics acceptable to regulators — was identified as a persistent barrier to
deployment. The knowledge gap between NDE practitioners and AI/ML specialists was flagged as a systemic

2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop — Report

obstacle: NDE engineers need to be able to understand, use, and critically evaluate AI tools without becoming
computer scientists, and the shop-floor deployment environment often has infrastructure constraints (hardware,
connectivity, real-time compute) that laboratory-developed models do not account for. A pointed question was
raised about whether AI/ML is actually needed in all cases, or whether rule-based approaches — or hybrid
combinations of rules and learned models — are more appropriate for the reliability demands of safety-critical
NDE.
Gaps
The group identified several specific gaps requiring community investment. Infrastructure for data management
— shared repositories, standardized metadata schemas, and interoperable formats — was double-starred as a
high-priority need. Benchmarks and performance criteria for AI in NDE are absent: there is no community-agreed
standard for what constitutes acceptable AI detection performance in a given application context. Workforce
development and AI literacy across the NDE practitioner community were flagged as structural gaps. Technical
gaps included methods to compress or dimensionality-reduce large scan datasets using AI/ML, approaches for
training reliable models from limited data, and adequate computational infrastructure for real-time analysis of
large datasets.
A specifically called-out gap was the absence of a clear definition of what AI-based qualification means relative to
traditional qualification frameworks: when an AI model is used as part of an NDE process, what evidence is
required, what standards apply, and who has the final authority? Hallucination mitigation — preventing AI systems
from generating plausible but incorrect outputs, particularly in report generation — was raised as an under-
addressed technical gap. Governance questions including ownership of AI outputs, liability for missed calls, and
regulatory acceptance pathways were identified as gaps requiring engagement from standards bodies, operators,
and regulators jointly. The Swedish nuclear qualification experience was cited as a reference case: a Swedish
organization has successfully qualified an AI-assisted program at TJ in which AI analysis of clean areas is accepted
as primary analysis, with flagged areas reviewed by a qualified examiner — but participants noted that the
demonstration process required to achieve a comparable qualification in the US context does not yet exist.
Cross-Cutting Themes
Human-in-the-loop design was broadly supported across all participant groups as an essential architectural
principle — not as a temporary concession but as a durable design requirement for safety-critical NDE. The debate
centered on the precise role boundary: should AI filter and flag for human review, or should it be permitted to
make autonomous accept/reject decisions in bounded, well-characterized contexts? Industry/laboratory
collaboration was double-starred by multiple groups as a high-priority enabler — national laboratory
infrastructure (access to diverse machines, materials, and process conditions independent of commercial
pressure) was seen as uniquely positioned to generate the ground-truth data and application-agnostic
benchmarks that industry and regulators need. Transferable, application-agnostic technology — models and
frameworks that work across materials, geometries, and machines rather than being retrained for each new
configuration — was identified as a key long-term R&D goal, with digital twin capability as a needed enabler for
synthetic data generation. The group also raised long-term performance monitoring of deployed AI systems as an
under-addressed need: once an AI tool is in service, what processes exist to detect model drift, performance
degradation, or out-of-distribution operation?

A second priority was accelerating qualification timelines in a quantifiable way. The group emphasized that “rapid

six months, $180,000, and 400 coupons was referenced as a baseline; a six-fold reduction was identified as the
practical near-term goal. In-situ monitoring, closed-loop process control, and AI/ML-assisted analysis were all
cited as enabling tools for this reduction, provided they are integrated into a qualification framework that
regulators and design authorities will accept. Participants also prioritized developing accepted standards and
guidance specifically for AI-enabled NDE qualification, connecting multimodal sensor data to defect
characterization and final performance, and reducing dependence on expensive post-build NDE by leveraging in-

A third priority cluster addressed workforce and standards participation. Participants from multiple organizations
noted that new graduates are often unaware that qualification and certification requirements exist at all —
universities emphasize the capability of AM without adequately preparing students for the regulatory and quality
environment they will enter. The group proposed incentive-based models (analogous to Autodesk’s free university
licensing) to embed qualification literacy early. Standards body participation was flagged as skewed toward large
OEMs, leaving SMEs, universities, and mom-and-pop shops without representation or voice — a structural
challenge that perpetuates standards that are inaccessible or irrelevant to much of the community. Making three
tiers of standards (for universities, SMEs, and large regulated industries) that are harmonized but appropriately
written for each audience was proposed as a practical approach.

Fragmentation was the dominant challenge theme across both days. There is no common hierarchy of agreed-
upon qualification requirements: NASA operates on a performance-based model assuming competent execution;
DOW follows its own approval path with multiple levels of oversight; individual OEMs treat their qualification
procedures as proprietary and will not share them; and none of these paths are currently aligned.
A second major challenge is connecting in-situ process data to final-part acceptance criteria. For most AM
processes, the chain from process parameters through in-situ sensing, microstructure, post-processing (heat
treatment, HIP), and final mechanical performance remains broken or incomplete. The discussion highlighted a
specific example: for wire DED of high-strength steels, hydrogen dispersion during deposition and its positional
encoding in the part would be the physically relevant signal for predicting hydrogen-induced cracking that can
manifest 30 days post-build — but no current in-situ monitoring system captures this. More broadly, the
acceptance criteria in existing standards were developed for conventionally manufactured parts and do not
transfer cleanly to AM: for example, F42 documents list a broad menu of mechanical tests without guidance on
which tests apply to which application, forcing individual programs to determine this independently at
considerable cost. The challenge of defining minor versus major process changes — and determining whether
each requires requalification — was discussed at length, with no current consensus across agencies.
Technical challenges for in-situ volumetric monitoring were also discussed. Participants agreed that ultrasound is
currently the most promising modality for in-process volumetric assessment during L-PBF, but it faces significant
obstacles: complex geometry assumptions, multiple backscattering from the powder bed, acoustic velocity
gradients driven by thermal gradients within the part, and the fundamental coupling problem in a process
environment. The consensus was that no single sensor is sufficient for complex geometries; multimodal and
multisensor fusion — combining UT with IR, optical tomography, eddy current, and acoustic emission — is
necessary, and the signal processing to fuse these coherently into a qualification decision is not mature. Standards
for in-situ monitoring methods (ASTM E3353 was cited) need to be updated more frequently given the pace of
development.

Gaps
America Makes has identified 28 qualification and certification gaps in its published roadmap; several were
specifically called out by participants. Gap 23 addresses production incorporation of AM that facilitates diverse
challenges including surface finish — relevant when a part must be examined every 10 years in service, not just
accepted once. Gap 25 covers nuclear industry use of AI and machine learning in NDE, described by participants
as already a present gap rather than a future one: nothing has been formally qualified in the US nuclear context.
The group noted that the existing 28 gaps need to be translated into actionable community-wide priorities rather
than remaining as a list in a report. Participants called out a need to add gap items specifically for in-service AM
part performance (not just pre-service acceptance), pedigree replication for AM vs. conventional parts, and
qualification of NDE procedures for AM-produced pressure boundary components (ASME Boiler and Pressure
Vessel Code context — currently no qualified examination procedures exist for additively manufactured pressure
components).
Standards gaps include the absence of standardized AI qualification frameworks for NDE — covering AI validation,
uncertainty quantification, explainability, and acceptance criteria — and insufficient guidance for incorporating AI
outputs into qualification and certification pathways. Data gaps include lack of interoperable, well-curated
datasets connecting process parameters, in-situ signals, defect populations, microstructure, heat treatment
history, and final mechanical performance across multiple material systems. Ground-truth standardization is
absent: XCT is used as the reference method for most current AM qualification research but there is no formal
standard governing how XCT-based ground truth is generated, validated, or reported. The correlation between
process-structure-property-performance for specific material-process combinations (particularly nickel
superalloys, titanium alloys, and aluminum alloys under different AM processes) remains incomplete across the
conditions needed for certification. Material- and application-specific qualification specifications for sectors with
the most demanding requirements are largely absent.
At the ecosystem level, the group noted that standards body participation is effectively voluntary and unfunded
for most organizations: standards development work is done on personal time by employed professionals, and no
mechanism ensures that SMEs, universities, or under-resourced organizations can participate. The lack of cost-
effective training pathways to bring new practitioners up to the existing standards baseline was called out as a
critical gap — one that compounds over time as each new graduate cohort enters industry without awareness of
the qualification framework they are operating within. Finally, geopolitical risk to global standards harmonization
was raised.
Cross-Cutting Themes
The group converged on several cross-cutting themes. First, trust in AM is significantly different from trust in
conventional manufacturing: conventional processes have decades of service history, established pedigree
documentation, and known defect populations. AM makes the material and the shape simultaneously, introducing
uncertainty at a level that compels more comprehensive NDE — which in turn drives up cost and time and risks
negating AM’s advantages. Building trust in AM will require not just better NDE but a long-term track record of in-
service performance, and the field has not yet accumulated that record. Second, the challenge of incremental
qualification change management — defining what constitutes a minor versus major change to a qualified system,
and what requalification is triggered — was repeatedly raised as an under-addressed problem that affects
machine updates, software changes, facility moves, and supplier substitutions. Third, the group identified a need
for a more mature connection between NDE and final in-service performance, particularly for parts going into
service for 20–60 year reactor or pressure vessel lifetimes where creep, fatigue, and environmental degradation
are the relevant failure modes rather than the as-built defect populations that current NDE can characterize.
Finally, there was strong agreement that financial incentives — analogous to EV tax rebates — could accelerate
compliance if structured around AM qualification standard adoption or buy-American equipment requirements,
and that this represents a policy lever not currently being used.

Breakout Session: Digital Infrastructure for NDE Automation (Day 2)

The group’s highest-priority need was interoperability and infinite data flexibility — the ability to form any
relationship between datasets, support any modality combination, allow any authorized stakeholder to access the
data they need, and remain compatible across decades of system upgrades without losing information. The group
argued that the NDE community either needs to converge on a small set of open formats (as it has effectively
done with PDF for reports) or ensure that all formats in use are interoperable with each other, so that an inspector
does not need twelve different software packages to review data from twelve different instruments. The ONDE
file format — developed collaboratively with instrument vendors and industry stakeholders and approaching
version 1.0 — was introduced as a community effort specifically designed for this goal: more accessible than
DICONDE for organizations without dedicated data standards experts, while targeting full interoperability with
the DICONDE ecosystem. The group agreed that ONDE and DICONDE are not competing — if DICONDE can be
made to do what ONDE is trying to do, the problem is equally solved. What matters is accessibility and
interoperability, not which format wins.
A second priority was synchronizing NDE digital infrastructure with the digital infrastructure of component owners
and structural analysts. The group observed that NDE is falling behind: structural and fatigue engineers now work
in fully digital environments and want NDE data they can ingest directly — not a picture or a PDF, but queryable,
mesh-registered data they can feed into FEA, CFD, and life prediction models. A naval aviation participant noted
that the Navy’s digital twin program for aircraft wants to know whether a part should be repaired, when it can fly
again, and for how many additional cycles — not the raw A-scan waveform. NDE organizations that cannot deliver
data in this form will be left behind as automation accelerates in manufacturing. The group called this the “in
sync” problem: NDE must be at least as digital as the engineering disciplines it serves, or it becomes the bottleneck.
A third priority was formalizing requirements for open data formats in contracts and statements of work. The
group noted that market incentives for digital infrastructure adoption will not self-generate: someone must take
the first step by making open formats a contractual deliverable requirement, which then creates demand for
trained personnel, consultants, and competitive inspection services. NAVSEA participants described current
practice: statements of work now include language requiring that all delivered digital NDE data either use an
accessible open format or be accompanied by the software (with license) needed to read it; scanning to PDF or
carbon copy scans is explicitly prohibited where digital collection methods are used. The group identified this as
the lever that will move the market — once the requirement exists, training companies, consultants, and job-shop
inspectors will build the capability to meet it.

Spatial registration across modalities was identified as the first major technical challenge: registering data from
multiple NDE methods (UT, ET, radiography, CT, penetrant, thermography) to each other and to a physical part or
CAD model in a way that allows meaningful joint interpretation. Even within a single modality, data from two
different instruments of the same type may not register cleanly without significant effort. The challenge is
compounded by the fact that many NDE methods are not natively digital — converting penetrant, magnetic
particle, and film radiography records into a form that can be registered with digital data requires additional steps
(digital photography, optical character recognition for handwritten reports) that are not standardized.
Long-term data linkage and temporal compatibility posed a second challenge. NDE programs and part inspection
histories span decades: ships, aircraft, and nuclear plants are inspected repeatedly over 20–60-year service lives,
and the ability to trend data across inspection cycles depends on all datasets being in comparable and accessible
formats. The group noted the classic failure mode: a file that existed at a path no longer does after a database
upgrade; a pointer that worked five years ago now points to nothing. Well-designed database systems store data
in internal locations that users cannot accidentally disturb, but inter-system linkage — connecting an NDE
database to a structural analysis database, a maintenance management system, or a digital twin platform —
requires data models that are fundamentally compatible, or information is lost on every round-trip. The group

                                                                                     ORNL | May 19–20, 2026 | Page 77

2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop — Report

emphasized forward and backward compatibility as a non-negotiable requirement for any digital infrastructure
intended for long-lived assets.
Unanticipated overhead and cybersecurity barriers constitute a third challenge. Digital infrastructure requires IT
support — server maintenance, patch management, security monitoring, access control — that most NDE
organizations have no budget for and no dedicated staff to provide. The joke in the room was that a Level 3 NDE
engineer does not have an IT budget; the reality is that the people being asked to implement digital NDE workflows
are often the same people performing the inspections, and the IT burden falls on them by default. Cybersecurity
requirements in the defense and nuclear sectors can further restrict what devices are allowed on networks, what
formats are permitted for data transfer, and what cloud services are available — sometimes reducing functional
capability below what a small commercial shop can achieve. Participants noted that COVID-era IT policy changes
forced some DOW digital adoption that had previously been blocked, but that new security patches can still
neutralize a robot or monitoring system if its software is flagged by automated security tools as suspicious.
Proprietary data ownership and misaligned incentives represent a fourth challenge. Data is a revenue generator
for instrument manufacturers and inspection service providers. Historically, manufacturers retained ownership of
data generated on their instruments; European data ownership regulations (effective as of late 2025) now require
instrument manufacturers to provide users with access to their own data at a quality equivalent to what the
manufacturer holds — a shift that is driving adoption of open formats by major vendors. This regulatory
framework does not yet have a North American equivalent, and the group argued that similar requirements would
accelerate the transition here. The structured-versus-semi-structured data tension — database systems assume
you know what you want to store before you store it, but NDE research often does not know what data will matter
until later identified as a persistent design challenge for any shared infrastructure.
Gaps
Digital reference standards were the most discussed gap. ASTM and other standards bodies still publish reference
radiographs on film; moving them to digital and extending them to CT reference volumes has been discussed for
years but lacks momentum. The group identified a need for reference volumes for CT that enable benchmarking
of data quality across different OEM systems — so that an inspection performed on a Nikon CT, a Northstar CT,
and a Waygate CT can be shown to be equivalent. Without this, program managers cannot confidently farm out
inspections to competing vendors or job shops, limiting flexibility in the manufacturing and inspection supply
chain. The group called for a round-robin reference volume exercise within ASTM, and noted that as a volunteer
organization, ASTM will only do this if practitioners show up and drive it. A shared vocabulary for digital image
processing algorithms — edge filters, sharpening filters, deblur filters — was also identified as a gap, because each
vendor uses proprietary names for what are functionally the same operations, making it impossible to specify or
compare processing pipelines across systems.
Training, education, and consultant availability were identified as an interconnected gap: the analog-to-digital
transition for NDE practitioners requires hands-on applied guidance, not just awareness of the possibility.
European oil and gas sectors have benefited from government-industry cost-sharing pilot programs that
embedded digital NDE consultants in sites and paid for the transition; the group called for similar programs in
North America. ASNT’s existing push into K–12 and university NDE education was identified as a platform to
leverage for digital NDE content, and the NDE.org website was proposed as a venue for sharing non-commercial
educational materials. The group also identified a gap in usability of NDE data analysis software — tools like
volume graphics packages require significant training even for experienced Level 3 NDE engineers, and if front-
line Level 1 and Level 2 technicians cannot use the tools intuitively, the digital infrastructure will not translate into
operational capability. Open-source code for reading common NDE file formats was identified as a necessary
enabler: without it, even a well-documented open format effectively remains proprietary because the barrier to
entry for writing a reader is too high for most organizations.

Breakout Sessions: In-Situ NDE / Monitoring (Day 2)

The overarching priority identified across both days was establishing a qualification framework for in-situ process
monitoring tools — a framework analogous to the IQ/OQ/PQ (Installation Qualification / Operational Qualification
/ Performance Qualification) structure that already exists for AM machines but extended to cover the monitoring
systems themselves. Currently, no such framework exists: machine OEMs and third-party sensor developers can
make claims about detection capability without a standardized body of evidence required to substantiate those
claims, and no procedure exists for returning a system to a qualified state after sensor degradation or
recalibration. The group, led by Rahman, proposed that such a framework should be outcomes-based and
categorical — explicitly stratifying the different things in-situ monitoring can and cannot claim to do. A three-tier
hierarchy was articulated: (1) machine-state monitoring (confirming process inputs such as laser power and
oxygen threshold are within specification); (2) process acceptance (detecting build-to-build process variability and
triggering early build rejection before post-build NDE investment); and (3) product acceptance (detecting and
characterizing specific flaw populations to a defined size, type, and confidence level). Most commercially available
tools currently operate at tiers 1 and 2; tier 3 — the level required to reduce or eliminate post-build ex-situ NDE
— remains largely undemonstrated at the level of evidence regulators require.
A second priority was linking in-situ monitoring to ex-situ NDE and final performance through shared databases
and aligned datasets. The group repeatedly emphasized that in-situ data alone is not interpretable without
correlating it to ex-situ characterization ground truth and long-term mechanical performance outcomes. Priorities
in this area include building shared repositories connecting process history, in-situ sensor signals, ex-situ NDE
results, microstructure, and mechanical properties; developing community datasets that are pedigree-quality
(spatially and temporally registered, with complete metadata) even if proprietary; and establishing effect-of-
defect relationships that allow in-situ anomaly observations to be mapped to structural consequences. The goal
of eventually operating without post-build NDE — “the shining city on the hill” as one participant described it —
requires this correlation chain to be unambiguously established for each material-process-part-criticality
combination. High-temperature UT transducers and ruggedized acoustic emission sensors capable of surviving the
AM process environment were identified as hardware priorities that directly enable this correlation work.
Performance-based standards and standardized calibration approaches were identified as the standards
development priorities, with a strong preference for technology-agnostic frameworks that will remain relevant as
the state of the art evolves.

The most technically fundamental challenge is that post-build processes invalidate in-situ monitoring data in ways
that are not yet characterized. Heat treatment changes microstructure; removal from the build plate can
introduce cracking in certain materials; HIP closes pores — but some kissing or partially fused defects can re-open
under operating temperature; machining exposes subsurface porosity that was not accessible to surface or near-
surface inspection during the build. In each case, the condition of the part at the time of in-situ monitoring may
not reflect the condition of the part in service. Until boundaries can be drawn identifying which combinations of
post-processing operations and part criticality categories allow in-situ data to stand as the primary acceptance
evidence, in-situ monitoring will remain supplementary to rather than a replacement for post-build NDE. This
boundary-identification problem was framed as the most important engineering problem the field needs to solve
to enable tier-3 in-situ qualification.
Machine-to-machine repeatability of in-situ monitoring systems is a challenge distinct from and in addition to the
challenge of qualifying the monitoring capability itself. Just as each installed AM machine must go through IQ and
OQ individually, each installation of an in-situ monitoring system must also be individually qualified to
demonstrate that its measurements are consistent with the claimed performance. This creates a two-tier
qualification burden: demonstrating the fundamental detection capability of the monitoring system design, and
then demonstrating that each serial installation of that system meets the same specification. The traceable
calibration infrastructure to support either tier does not currently exist: there are no AM-specific calibration blocks

                                                                                      ORNL | May 19–20, 2026 | Page 79

2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop — Report

or reference artifacts analogous to those used in conventional UT and radiography NDE, and calibration methods
for in-situ sensors are not standardized across systems or vendors. A related issue is sensor longevity and
degradation: most in-situ monitoring systems described at the workshop have only 18–24 months of operational
data, and no service-life characterization or recalibration-to-qualified-state procedure has been established. A
monitoring system that silently degrades while continuing to report pass/go decisions is a qualitatively different
failure mode from one that fails obviously — and the first scenario is more dangerous.
Additional challenges include: acceptance criteria that were not designed for AM (the group noted a specific
example — fracture mechanics acceptance calculations being applied to cracks at 3 μm scale while digital
radiography pixel sizes are ~100 μm, creating an inherent disconnect between the structural significance of a flaw
and the resolution of the inspection method used to accept or reject it); the pace of AM technology development
exceeding the pace of NDE and monitoring method development and standardization; workforce gaps (no trained
Level 2 NDE technicians certified for in-situ process monitoring data review; current industry practice is that
fabrication technicians review their own in-situ data, which violates the separation between fabrication and
inspection that existing standards require); standards that currently do not accommodate in-situ data as qualifying
evidence; and the scalability challenge of analyzing and storing hundreds of gigabytes of per-build in-situ data at
industrial production rates.
Gaps
The highest-priority gap identified was the definition of what constitutes “further showing” from regulators’ and
design authorities’ perspectives when in-situ process monitoring is proposed as the primary or sole qualification
evidence. The FAA and other regulators have added language to MMPDS (the material properties handbook)
flagging AM entries as potentially requiring “further showing” — but without defining what further showing
means, program managers and tool developers cannot know what body of evidence will be sufficient to achieve
acceptance. This ambiguity, combined with regulators’ reluctance to be prescriptive, creates a situation where
industry cannot commit to the data collection investments required because the finish line keeps moving. The
group called for explicit engagement between regulators (FAA, NRC, DOW) and the standards community to define
minimum evidence thresholds for each tier of in-situ monitoring capability.
Closely related gaps include: lack of standardized AM calibration blocks and reference artifacts for in-situ sensor
validation; absence of a traceable calibration chain for in-situ monitoring systems; no IQ/OQ analog for monitoring
system installations; no procedure for returning a monitoring system to a qualified state after recalibration or
component replacement; absence of POD data for in-situ monitoring systems characterizing detection confidence
as a function of flaw size, type, and location; and no formal cross-training or certification pathway for personnel
who review in-situ process monitoring data. The group also identified a gap in standards committee
representation: machine OEMs, small and medium-sized businesses, and contract manufacturers are
underrepresented in the ASTM F42 and SAE committees writing the standards that govern their operations,
creating an echo chamber dominated by large aerospace primes. Proprietary data retention was identified as a
structural gap: companies sitting on large AM build and inspection datasets will not share them even for neutral
third-party reduction to allowable tables, slowing the development of industry-wide property specifications.
Finally, the group raised the question of whether current in-situ monitoring research will be relevant to emerging
AM technologies — beam shaping, ultrafine processing at 10 μm beam diameters, area printing — and called for
technology-agnostic qualification frameworks that can accommodate these developments without requiring full
re-qualification programs for each new process variant.

Breakout Sessions: Ex-Situ NDE (Day 1)

Design for inspectability was the dominant priority across sessions. Groups argued that NDE for AM is
systematically hampered by the fact that parts are designed to be manufactured — not to be inspected — and

                                                                                   ORNL | May 19–20, 2026 | Page 80

2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop — Report

that this design heritage mostly traces to castings, forgings, and subtractive processes for which radiographic or
surface NDE was sufficient. The group’s position was that the industry should be asking whether parts will be
inspectable, not just manufacturable and dimensionally conformant, and that the geometric freedom of AM
should be actively leveraged to improve inspection access rather than replicated from legacy casting drawings.
Priorities flowing from this include developing designer guidelines for inspectability, building simulation and
modeling tools that predict inspection feasibility from a near-net-shape CAD model, and creating feedback loops
from NDE engineers to designers during the design phase rather than after the first failed inspection attempt.
A second priority was establishing technically grounded acceptance criteria linked to structural requirements. The
groups repeatedly observed that current acceptance criteria for AM components are often borrowed from
welding standards (T9074-AS-GIB-010/271, ASTM criteria adapted from casting), and that the connection
between these workmanship-based criteria and the actual structural performance requirements of the part is
typically not formally established. The criteria exist because they are “service-proven” in a conventional
manufacturing context, not because they have been analytically derived for AM. When program offices are asked
to specify a tolerable flaw size for a given geometry and loading condition, the process to obtain that answer
commonly takes six months or more — a timeline that defeats AM’s production advantages. Fitness-for-service
frameworks that explicitly connect NDE indication characterization to structural significance were identified as a
high-value research and standardization target.
Additional priorities included rapid screening methods that can triage parts before committing to costly XCT or
full radiography campaigns; high-throughput XCT capabilities integrated close to the AM machine; leveraging
VENUS-type multispectral and hyperspectral neutron/X-ray capabilities as non-destructive ground-truth tools for
characterization beyond conventional density-contrast imaging; integration of in-situ process monitoring records
with ex-situ inspection to reduce uncertainty and improve qualification confidence; and phase/microstructure
characterization through grain mapping and tomographic approaches. The group also called for a broader cultural
and incentive shift: the current structure rewards minimum-compliance inspection, and the community needs
mechanisms to reward quality-focused NDE investment.

Surface conditions were the most-discussed technical challenge. AM surfaces — particularly L-PBF down-skin
surfaces, DED outer layers, and any as-built surface that has not been finish-machined — present a fundamentally
different challenge to UT coupling than conventionally machined surfaces. Undulating waviness, micron-to-tens-
of-microns-scale roughness, and the combination of high-frequency roughness and low-frequency waviness
require different solutions and different vocabulary. The group debated terminology, settling on “surface
conditions” as a more appropriate term than “surface roughness” because the latter implies only the high-pass
spatial frequency component and misses the waviness that most affects beam path integrity during UT. Adaptive
wedges and bladder-style couplant systems have been demonstrated for casting inspection, but their capability
relative to AM surface severity is uncertain. Air-coupled and laser UT offer non-contact alternatives but at
significantly reduced sensitivity for the dense metal parts typical in aerospace and defense AM. The group noted
that determining when a surface is inspectable enough to proceed — before committing to expensive final
machining — is an important operational decision that currently lacks supporting standards or tools.
Inhomogeneous microstructure was the second major technical challenge. AM parts — especially large DED
components — develop spatially varying grain structure, texture, and thermal history across their volume. A large
component with a bulky core and thin features extending from it will have experienced fundamentally different
thermal cycling in different regions, producing different grain morphology, different prior grain boundary
distributions, and different local acoustic velocity. This means that a single UT calibration cannot apply uniformly
across the part: sizing will be inaccurate in regions where the acoustic velocity deviates from the calibration block
value, and in the extreme case, some regions may produce backscatter so strong that coverage is lost entirely.
Even after heat treatment designed to homogenize microstructure, prior grain boundaries persist and continue
to influence ultrasonic propagation. The group identified this as a challenge that scales with component
complexity and will not be solvable by materials processing alone — it requires either advanced signal processing
                                                                                     ORNL | May 19–20, 2026 | Page 81

2026 NDE for Advanced Manufacturing Workshop — Report

methods (backscatter characterization, spatially resolved velocity mapping) or design-phase mitigation through
geometry and thermal management.
Complex internal geometries — heat exchangers, gyroid lattices, conformal cooling channels — present a third
category of challenge that is largely unsolved. CT is the only viable volumetric method for many of these
configurations, but large metal AM parts may not fit within available CT systems, and resolution must be matched
to the expected channel surface roughness (which itself must be known in advance to set the resolution
requirement — a chicken-and-egg problem). Conventional ID inspection techniques are not applicable to sealed
internal channels. The group noted that this challenge is not unique to AM but is significantly amplified by AM’s
ability to produce internal geometries that were previously impossible to manufacture. Additional challenges
include cost and logistics of XCT and XCT reconstruction software, the difficulty of building material-specific NDE
databases that correlate indication characteristics to known flaw types, and the risk of applying AI/ML models
trained on one material system to a different one without validation.
Gaps
The most-cited gap was the absence of NDE simulation tools accessible to designers. Simulation-informed
inspection planning — in which a near-net-shape AM model is interrogated computationally before physical
inspection to determine whether a given NDE method can achieve the required coverage — exists at the research
level in specialized codes but is not accessible to organizations without dedicated NDE simulation expertise.
Without these tools, inspectability is discovered empirically during the first attempt, often after significant
manufacturing investment. The group called for both development of accessible simulation platforms and
derivation of designer guidelines from simulation studies, so that organizations without in-house NDE simulation
capability can still make design decisions informed by inspectability principles.
A closely related gap is the lack of collaboration infrastructure between NDE engineers and AM designers. These
two communities have limited shared vocabulary and limited structured interaction during the design phase; NDE
requirements are typically imposed on a finalized design rather than informing it. Establishing formal mechanisms
— standards, process flows, handbooks — that define what NDE engineers need to know from designers
(geometry, surface condition intent, access constraints) and what designers need to know from NDE engineers
(minimum inspectable wall thickness, beam access requirements, surface condition tolerance) would materially
improve the quality and cost-effectiveness of AM inspection. AM-specific reference radiographs and AM-specific
acceptance criteria illustrations (analogous to existing ASTM casting reference radiographs but reflecting the
actual discontinuity morphologies in AM — lack of fusion at infill/perimeter boundaries, gas porosity, keyhole
porosity, delamination) were specifically identified as a missing tool that NAVSEA would find operationally
valuable.
Additional gaps identified include: standardized surface condition definitions and tolerances for AM components
— currently handled inconsistently across programs and lacking formal acceptance values for the waviness
component; adaptive UT hardware (wedges, couplant systems) qualified for the range of surface conditions
encountered in as-built AM; cost-comparison frameworks to help programs evaluate the relative economics of
surface treatment, adaptive wedge development, hybrid manufacturing, and inspection deferral options;
education and awareness of NDE requirements and capabilities across the AM community, especially for early-
career engineers and organizations new to safety-critical AM; and rapid-screening tools that provide triage-level
defect information at low cost and short cycle time before committing to full XCT, enabling more efficient use of
advanced characterization resources.

Likert Style Questions (Responses to Q1 – Q4)
Figure 1 provides a summary of responses to Questions 1 through 4, revealing that the majority of participants
agreed or strongly agreed that resolving in-service NDE challenges is equally important as addressing initial quality
NDE challenges for AM deployment (Questions 1 and 2). Agreement was observed to increase from 54% for non-

uncertain (30% for non-nuclear and 25% for nuclear applications).

participants expressed uncertainty, and one disagreed with this statement.

is sufficiently mature to support widespread production deployment. Conversely, 15% agreed with the statement,
while 30% indicated uncertainty.

Response to Q12 (Open)
Question 12 asked participants how they would define “Rapid Qualification” and presented the question in an
open format inviting a variety of potential responses. These responses cannot be visualized neatly with word
clouds or other graphs or charts, but themes can be extracted and are summarized below in narrative form.

When participants were asked to describe what Rapid Qualification means, their answers reflected a wide variety
of perspectives. Overall, the responses indicate both enthusiasm and reservations regarding the concept of
accelerating qualification in advanced manufacturing.

Rapid Means Significant Reduction in Time
Many respondents associated “rapid” with speed, focusing on making lengthy qualification processes considerably
shorter. Examples included:
 • Completing qualification in minutes or hours (such as “10 minutes” or “Within 1 hour of part
 manufacturing”)
 • Conducting qualification during or immediately after the build (for example, “Qualification at time of
 manufacture,” “During the build,” or “Closed-loop qualification immediately after the build”)
 • Reducing qualification timelines from months or even years (for instance, “1 month,” “Going from
 decades to a couple of years,” or “In ASME code base in under 5 years”)
This viewpoint emphasizes the importance of timing and proximity to the manufacturing event itself.

Modern, Data-Based, or Automated Approaches
Other participants emphasized that rapid qualification involves not only speed but also the methods used to
achieve it. Key concepts mentioned included:
 • Utilizing artificial intelligence or model-based techniques (such as “Let AI do it,” “Model based,”
 “Modeling”)
 • Relying on existing or in-situ data (such as “Leveraging data driven approaches,” “Qualification done using
 historical data and in-situ monitoring data”)
 • Implementing automation or real-time assessment (for example, “Real time qualification,” “Closed loop
 qualification immediately after the build”)
 • Reducing the use of physical artifacts or inspection steps (such as “Reduce tested material,” “Reduce
 physical testing,” “Reducing inspection requirements”)
For these respondents, rapid qualification is achieved through analytics, automation, and leveraging available
data, thereby minimizing the reliance on expensive and time-consuming physical testing.

Context Influences Interpretation of “Rapid”
Some participants noted that the term “rapid” can vary depending on several factors, including:
 • Industry expectations
 • Type of materials or components
 • Specifics of the machine or process used
 • Definitions of what constitutes an acceptable timeframe
This indicates that rapid qualifications are highly dependent on context, and any definition should align with the
standards and requirements of the specific industry or application.

Caution, and Awareness of Risks
Some individuals expressed reservations or concerns about potential drawbacks.
 • Comments regarding risk (such as “Risky,” “Accepting risk,” “Low risk”)
 • Concerns about omitting important steps (for example, “Skipping process qualification”)
 • Questions about whether rapid qualification is attainable (such as “No such thing,” “Good luck?”)
 • Observations that accelerating processes can sometimes result in delays (for example, “Expedite/Rapid”
 usually results in “delays”)
These comments highlight the importance of risk management, thorough procedures, and well-defined standards
as critical safeguards when seeking to accelerate qualification processes.
