Can an active outdoor living initiative in kindergartens in two disadvantaged neighbourhoods increase physical activity among children? Findings from a within-subject design of the Happy Healthy Active Children initiative
Authors: Alberte Laura Oest Müllertz, Anna Stage, Paul Bloch, Peter Elsborg, Tina Termansen, Maria Stougård
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate whether the Happy Healthy Active Children (HHAC) initiative increased children’s time spent in different physical activity (PA) intensities in kindergartens located in disadvantaged neighbourhoods.DesignA within-subject design, nested within the HHAC initiative, with children acting as their own controls.SettingSeven kindergartens in the adjacent disadvantaged neighbourhoods of Tingbjerg and Husum located in Copenhagen, Denmark.ParticipantsChildren aged 3–5 years attending participating kindergartens. A total of n=51 children provided valid accelerometer data from at least one HHAC activity day and one regular kindergarten day.InterventionHHAC is a partnership-based initiative promoting active outdoor living through co-created activities centred around play, nature and cooking. In the kindergarten setting, activities were implemented one day per week over 10 weeks from the second half of 2023 to the first half of 2024.Outcome measuresPA was measured using thigh-worn Axivity AX3 accelerometers over two 10-day periods during the initiative. Time spent in moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA), light PA and sedentary behaviour (SB) was compared between HHAC activity days and regular kindergarten days.ResultsCompared with regular days, children spent significantly more time in MVPA (8.6 min, p=0.002) and significantly less time in SB (9.2 min, p=0.007) on HHAC activity days. Play activity days showed the largest effects, with increased time spent in MVPA (13.2 min, p=0.002) and significantly less time spent in SB (19.2 min, p<0.001). No significant differences were observed for nature or cooking activities.ConclusionThe HHAC initiative, particularly its play-based activities, increased MVPA and reduced SB among children in kindergartens located in disadvantaged neighbourhoods.

Introduction

Physical activity (PA) is fundamental to the health and well-being of children, influencing not only their physical health but also their cognitive development, social interactions, and emotional well-being.1 2 Despite its recognised importance, a concerning trend of decreasing PA levels among children has been seen in recent years, attributed in part to the rise of sedentary behaviours (SB) facilitated by modern lifestyles.1 In Denmark, the national health authority recommends that children should engage in at least 60 min of moderate PA each day, and at least 3 days a week, this should be vigorous PA, while the SB should be limited. However, studies indicate that many children do not meet these recommendations.3 This poses a significant public health challenge, as it is associated with an increased risk of obesity, cardiovascular disease, and other chronic health conditions.4 Further, socioeconomic inequalities in PA persist, with children from socially vulnerable families—characterised by low education, low income, unemployment or non-Danish ethnic backgrounds—being particularly at risk.57 A systematic review by Gautam et al. found that children from low socioeconomic households face an elevated risk of low PA compared with their peers from higher socioeconomic households.5

Early childhood represents a critical window of opportunity for establishing lifelong health behaviours, underscoring the importance of initiatives aimed at promoting PA during this formative stage.8 Early-childhood education and care institutions, such as kindergartens, where children spend a substantial amount of time, emerge as pivotal settings for implementing such initiatives.9 Previous PA initiatives in institutions have shown mixed and often modest effects on children’s PA levels,1012 argued to be due to challenges related to implementation and insufficient anchoring within the institutional setting. Studies highlight the need for initiatives that bring careful considerations of various factors such as staff training and motivation, available resources, environmental design, curriculum integration, and cultural relevance.1315

Previous initiatives often lack the active involvement of educators, parents, and local stakeholders and thus overlook the importance of co-creating interventions with the possible consequence that interventions may not be fully integrated into the daily routines. Moreover, literature has a limited focus on disadvantaged neighbourhoods, despite evidence suggesting that children in these settings may face greater barriers to engaging in PA.14 15 While some studies acknowledge the greater barriers to PA faced by children living in these areas, few studies have specifically targeted such areas, and the studies that have often lack taking cultural and social contexts of the population they aim to serve into consideration.14 15 These knowledge gaps point to the need for culturally sensitive initiatives that can ensure long-term sustainability within kindergarten settings and align with the diverse needs of disadvantaged neighbourhoods.

The Happy Healthy Active Children (HHAC) initiative addressed these gaps by applying a supersetting approach, emphasising a strategic and partnership-based initiative with coordinated actions across settings, including kindergartens, schools, after-school programmes, leisure groups, and families.16 The HHAC initiative aimed to promote active outdoor living through structured activities in three complementary themes: play, nature, and cooking. While HHAC worked holistically across multiple settings, the current study focused specifically on the activities carried out in the kindergarten setting (comprising children aged 3–5 years) and consequently does not focus on HHAC in its entirety. Specifically, the current study aims to compare time spent in different PA intensities on days with HHAC activities compared with regular days (without HHAC activities). Regular kindergarten days were defined as standard kindergarten days without planned HHAC activities, reflecting the institutions’ usual routines and pedagogical practices, typically consisting of a mix of free play, structured group activities, outdoor play, meals, and rest periods. Additionally, the study explores which of the HHAC activity themes (play, nature, and cooking) had the greatest impact on time spent in the different PA intensities by comparing days with each specific activity to regular days as well as to each other.

Methods

Study design

The HHAC study applied a realist evaluation approach divided into a qualitative and a quantitative component. The current study employed a within-subject design, with the children acting as their own controls. The study compared time spent in different PA intensities on days with HHAC activities compared with regular days, estimating the average within-child difference in minutes associated with HHAC days. The study was reported with inspiration from the TREND (Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomised Designs) guidelines.17 Due to the nature of the current study not all checklist items are relevant.

Study setting

HHAC was implemented in the adjacent disadvantaged neighbourhoods of Tingbjerg and Husum located in Copenhagen, Denmark, from 2023 to 2025. The neighbourhoods are home to residents from a wide range of ethnic and social backgrounds.18 The area is diverse, with 74.3% of its population having immigrant roots, in contrast to 10.1% in the general Danish population.19 This diversity enriches the community with a variety of cultures, languages, and traditions. However, the neighbourhood is also characterised by indicators of social vulnerability, including a higher proportion of residents not in employment (26.4% compared with the national average of 14.1%) and lower educational attainment.20 21 Educational attainment is defined as the highest completed level of formal education (ie, primary, secondary or tertiary education). In this population, approximately 40% of residents had primary school as their highest completed level of education, compared with approximately 25% nationally.21 The educational data were derived from the Danish Educational Register.21 This includes only education completed at Danish institutions, which may result in underestimation of the educational attainment of some individuals, particularly migrants. Additionally, the area experiences significantly higher rates of diabetes and other chronic illnesses compared with other regions in Copenhagen.22

The HHAC initiative

The HHAC initiative was a settings-based initiative that aimed to enhance health and well-being in the two disadvantaged neighbourhoods Tingbjerg and Husum in Copenhagen, Denmark. HHAC was carried out within the long-term strategic initiative Tingbjerg Changing Diabetes (TCD).23 TCD is a community-based health promotion initiative addressing social determinants of health and health inequities in disadvantaged neighbourhoods. TCD is based on the supersetting approach which is a strategic framework that ‘strives to attain synergistic effects through coordinated engagement of multiple stakeholders in multiple settings to mobilize local resources and strengthen social networks for collective community action23 24’.

The HHAC initiative worked with the supersetting framework across settings in collaborative efforts to create and execute activities focusing on play, nature, and cooking. Through these activities, children and parents were encouraged to (re)discover the joy of playing together, exploring nature and socially interacting over food and meals.

The HHAC partnership consisted of multiple community stakeholders, activity experts, and research partners. A description of the partners and their roles in HHAC is shown in online supplemental table 1.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement was embedded throughout the development and implementation of the HHAC initiative. In line with the supersetting approach,24 children, parents, kindergarten staff, and other local community stakeholders contributed to the design and adaptation of activities. Activities were developed in partnership with these groups to ensure local ownership and sensitivity to the social, cultural, and institutional context. In the kindergarten setting, involvement included pre-meetings with kindergarten staff, observations of institutional contexts, and continuous dialogue during implementation. Educators contributed to the adaptation and refinement of activities to ensure feasibility and relevance within everyday kindergarten practice. Ongoing sparring and dialogue sessions between activity experts and kindergarten staff further supported activity development, competence-building, and local anchoring of the initiative.

The HHAC initiative in the kindergarten setting

The current study focuses on the activities carried out in the kindergarten setting, hence only the activities here are described. The overall HHAC initiative is described in a study by Stougaard et al.16 The initiative was implemented over 10 weeks in each kindergarten, with one day of HHAC activities per week from late October 2023 to June 2024. An activity day consisted of a regular kindergarten day combined with two to three hours of HHAC activities followed by a brief sparring session between activity experts and kindergarten staff to promote competence-building and anchoring of activities in the institutions. Kindergartens were offered economic compensation and training sessions but declined as they preferred keeping staff in the institutions and having the training take place there during normal working hours. The 10 weeks were divided into the three themes: three weeks of play, three weeks of nature activities, and three weeks of cooking activities, with the order of themes varying between kindergartens. The final week included a parent event that integrated all three components. Each activity day was facilitated by an activity expert with support from kindergarten staff, and activities were coordinated to ensure consistency across all participating kindergartens.

Recruitment

Kindergartens were recruited prior to project initiation and participated as institutional partners in the HHAC initiative. All seven kindergartens within the two neighbourhoods were invited to participate in the HHAC initiative, to avoid selective enrolment of individual institutions and minimise potential recruitment bias.

Within these kindergartens, all children (approximately n=350) were invited to participate in the project activities and in the PA measurements. Recruitment for the measurement component was voluntary and independent of everyday pedagogical activities, ensuring that participation did not affect the children’s daily kindergarten experience. Based on previous experience related to obtaining parental consent among residents of the two neighbourhoods and the importance of communicating clearly about the purpose of the study and the measurements, we applied a two-step process for obtaining parental consent: (1) parents or legal guardians received study information and consent forms via hand-outs on Aula, the official digital communication platform used in Danish early childhood education, and (2) non-responders from the initial step were contacted directly by pedagogical staff. Further, researchers conducted informal pop-up meetings at the kindergartens, allowing parents to ask questions directly. Study materials and consent forms were provided in five languages (Danish, Arabic, Turkish, Urdu and English), selected based on the most commonly spoken languages in the area. While complete data on language coverage among all eligible participants were not available, another local study using the same translations found that approximately 16% of participants reported language backgrounds outside these groups, indicating some residual linguistic diversity beyond the provided translations.31 Nevertheless, the selected languages were considered to cover the majority of the target population in the area.

Data collection procedures

Data on PA was collected twice for 10 consecutive days during the initiative period, obtaining data from two out of the three themes: play, nature, or cooking, in each kindergarten. The order of the activity themes was rotated across kindergartens, ensuring that, while individual children did not provide data for all three themes, the study sample as a whole provided data from each activity component. This design was deliberately chosen to avoid overburdening the children while still allowing comparisons between themes and covering the full range of HHAC activities.

PA was measured with Axivity AX3 accelerometers (Axivity, Newcastle, UK), with a sampling frequency of 50 Hz. Validity and reliability of the use of AX3 accelerometers for children has previously been described in a study by Hedayatrad et al.32 AX3 accelerometers were attached to the skin on the left thigh with self-adhesive plasters. The thigh was chosen as the placement site because thigh-worn accelerometers provide valid estimates compared with wrist placement.33 In addition, attaching the device to the thigh using adhesive tape reduces the likelihood that the children would remove the device during the monitoring period, which can be a challenge with wrist-worn devices.33 A researcher (ALOM) attached all accelerometers in the kindergarten with help from institutional staff. The institutional staff and parents were given information and extra material in case the accelerometer should fall off within the given period and instructed to put the accelerometer back on using extra tape. The accelerometers were attached on the day before an activity, worn continuously for 10 consecutive days and removed by a researcher (ALOM) the day after an activity. The 10-day period was selected for both practical and methodological reasons. First, the HHAC activities were conducted on a fixed weekday; therefore, a 10-day wear period ensured that two activity days could be captured within the same measurement period, while also including regular days before and after the activities. Second, longer monitoring periods improve the reliability of accelerometer-derived estimates of habitual PA in children by accounting for day-to-day variability in activity patterns.

Based on prior experience collecting data in study settings such as Tingbjerg and Husum, requesting additional personal information from parents—such as socioeconomic status, parental education or home environment factors—was considered likely to reduce response rates and introduce selection bias. To minimise barriers to recruitment and ensure sufficient participation among families, these data were therefore not collected.

Data processing and statistical analyses

Accelerometer data were processed in R with ActiGraph counts. PA intensities were assigned using Pate et al’s cut points, which have been recommended to estimate physical intensities among children aged 3–5 years.34 Weekend days were excluded from analysis. Valid participants were defined as having at least one valid HHAC activity day and one valid regular kindergarten day. Valid days captured the entire kindergarten day, 09:00–15:00 hours (360 min), hence if a child was not wearing the device throughout the full day, the day was removed from analysis. Non-wear time was defined as 60 min of continuous zeros.

PA outcomes were analysed using linear mixed-effects models at the day level, with repeated measurements nested within children and children clustered within kindergartens. The models were used to assess differences in time spent in different PA intensities on days with HHAC activities compared with regular days, for each of the three outcomes: moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA), light PA (LPA) and SB. The kindergarten day type (HHAC vs regular day) was included as a fixed effect. Random intercepts for child and kindergarten were included to account for repeated measurements within children and clustering within kindergartens. All models were adjusted for age and gender. Statistical analyses were performed using the lme4 package, with p values calculated using the lmerTest package in R V.2024.04.2. The function ‘confint’ was used to estimate 95% CIs. All tests were two-tailed and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive results

A total of 81 children (23% of the approximately 350 eligible children) provided consent to participate in the measurements across the seven kindergartens included. Of these, 51 children (63%) had valid PA data from ≥1 day with HHAC activities and ≥1 regular day and were included in the final analysis. The remaining children were excluded because they did not contribute valid data from both HHAC activity days and regular kindergarten days. Across the 51 included children, a total of 565 valid observation days were included in the analysis, comprising 410 regular kindergarten days and 155 days with HHAC activities. Each child contributed an average of 6.1 (SD=2.1) days, comprising 4.5 (SD=1.8) regular kindergarten days and 1.7 (SD=0.6) days with HHAC activities. The primary reasons for exclusion of days were attributable to child absence during measurement days or incomplete accelerometer recordings, for example, due to removal of the accelerometer during the measurement period or if the accelerometer wear time during the kindergarten period (09:00–15:00 hours) was incomplete (<360 min), or if the accelerometer was lost. Among the days with HHAC activities, data were collected primarily on days with nature activities (n=80), followed by play (n=56), and cooking activities (n=19). Although efforts were made to capture an approximately equal number of days for each activity type, the logistical circumstances resulted in an uneven distribution. The sample consisted of 59% girls and had a mean age of 4.5 years (SD=0.5). Across all observed days, children accumulated an average of 104.7 min (SD=33.0) of MVPA during the kindergarten day, 118.4 min (SD=22.6) of LPA, and 137.0 min (SD=40.3) of SB. Discussion

The present study has investigated the impact of the HHAC initiative on children’s time spent in different PA levels in kindergartens in two disadvantaged neighbourhoods in Copenhagen, Denmark. The study found that children spent 8.6 min more time in MVPA and 9.2 min less in SB on days with HHAC activities compared with regular days, while LPA remained unchanged. The increase of 8.6 min in MVPA can be considered of clinical significance, as even modest increases in MVPA are associated with beneficial effects on children’s physical health, motor development, and overall well-being.35 However, it is worth noting that PA levels on regular kindergarten days already exceeded the national health authority’s recommendation of engaging in at least 60 min of moderate PA each day, and at least 3 days a week, this should be vigorous PA. Our descriptive results indicate that, on average, children engaged in 102.5 (SD=33.0) minutes of MVPA on regular kindergarten days, well above the recommended minimum. These findings suggest that the kindergarten environment itself fosters an active lifestyle, with HHAC activities serving as an enhancement rather than a compensatory measure for insufficient activity.

Further, the present study found that among the different HHAC activity components, only days with play activities were associated with increased MVPA and reduced SB. The findings suggest that while the HHAC activities generally were associated with more time spent in MVPA, certain components (namely play activities) proved more associated than others with higher PA intensities and reducing SB. This aligns with existing research emphasising the role of active play in enhancing PA intensities in children. Notably, Engelen et al. found that providing children with more opportunities for free play—particularly through playground modifications—led to an increase in MVPA and a decrease in SB.36 Similarly, Goldfield et al. demonstrated that structured and unstructured active play in childcare settings resulted in significant increases in PA levels.37 Additionally, the significant less time spent in SB observed in this study supports the notion that active play not only encourages movement but also discourages prolonged periods of inactivity. This is particularly relevant given the well-documented concerns about excessive sedentary time among young children.1 2 As research suggests, embedding active play into early childhood education and care can provide a sustainable way to enhance PA levels, while also supporting the development of fundamental movement skills.38 39

The nature and cooking activities did not have a significant impact on PA intensities compared with regular kindergarten days. However, it is worth noting that PA levels on nature and cooking activity days were not significantly lower than on regular kindergarten days, during which children already exceeded the national health authority’s recommendations. This suggests that nature and cooking activities maintained an already high level of PA, rather than reducing it. In this context, the lack of additional PA on nature and cooking activity days may simply reflect a ceiling effect, where PA levels were already high. Further, when interpreting these findings, it is important to consider that the activities were diverse and not intended primarily to increase PA. For example, activities related to cooking were largely sedentary, whereas other components encouraged substantial physical engagement. PA was measured as one outcome of interest because it represents a key aspect of children’s health and well-being; however, the intervention’s overarching aim was to foster active outdoor living. From this perspective, variations in PA across themes are expected and do not necessarily reflect the overall success of the HHAC initiative. Rather, these differences highlight the multistranded and systemic nature of the HHAC initiative, in which complementary activities across pedagogical, social, and environmental domains collectively contribute to longer-term outcomes, including, but not limited, to PA. The findings suggest that evaluating complex interventions requires a nuanced approach, considering both the diversity of activities and their broader educational and developmental objectives. For nature, previous studies, such as Barton et al., indicate that while nature-based activities offer opportunities for PA, they are generally less effective at increasing MVPA when compared with structured play.40 This, further, aligns with findings from Johnstone et al., who reviewed nature-based early childhood education settings and found mixed evidence regarding their impact on PA.41 Some studies indicated increased MVPA, others highlighted improvements in motor competence, reduced SB and enhanced balance. These benefits suggest that while nature-based activities may not always elicit the highest intensity PA, they contribute to overall physical development and active engagement. Similarly, Mygind et al. emphasised the broader benefits of immersive nature experiences beyond PA.42 Their systematic review found conditional support for improvements in self-esteem, cognitive performance, and social skills through nature-based activities.

Further, for the cooking-based activities, the lack of impact could be attributed to the fact that cooking activities were not as directly linked to physical exertion as the play-based activities. Cooking activities among young children have been found to support other developmental outcomes, such as fostering an interest in healthy eating and increasing knowledge of fruits and vegetables.43 Therefore, while cooking-related activities may not directly increase PA intensities, they play a crucial role in promoting positive nutrition behaviours and establishing healthy eating habits.44 Given the broader benefits of such initiatives, integrating cooking-based activities alongside physically engaging play and nature-based activities may contribute to a more holistic approach to promote childhood health and well-being.

Limitations

The current study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the relatively small sample size, combined with the multilevel structure of the data, likely resulted in limited statistical power and wide 95% CIs for several estimates. This reduces the precision of the estimates and implies that, although some associations reached statistical significance, the magnitude of these effects should be interpreted with caution. Similarly, non-significant findings may reflect insufficient statistical power rather than the absence of true associations. The limited number of observations within specific HHAC activity subgroups further contributes to this uncertainty and reduces the robustness of these stratified analyses. In addition, attrition in the study sample may have introduced selection bias if children with valid measurements differed systematically from those excluded; however, no additional demographic differences beyond age and sex could be assessed, limiting the ability to fully evaluate potential selection effects. Second, the study did not explicitly account for temporal variation in children’s PA patterns. This includes potential systematic differences across weekdays, weeks, or broader scheduling patterns within kindergarten settings, as well as environmental factors such as weather conditions that may influence outdoor activity opportunities and intensity levels. Such time-related and context-dependent factors were not modelled in the present analyses and may therefore represent unmeasured sources of variation in PA outcomes. Future studies should consider explicitly incorporating temporal structure (eg, day-level clustering, seasonal variation and weather data) in both study design and statistical modelling to improve precision and reduce potential bias. Additionally, the kindergartens included were not randomly selected, which may limit the representativeness of the sample and affect the generalisability of the results to the broader socially disadvantaged population. Another limitation is that the study only collected data on age and gender, which restricts our ability to analyse other potential confounders, such as socioeconomic status, parental education, or home environment factors, that may influence children’s PA and the effects of the HHAC initiative. It was decided not to collect this information from the parents to reduce barriers for recruitment. However, it does limit this study’s ability to examine potential socioeconomic gradients or other contextual influences on outcomes. Furthermore, although activity day logs were completed by activity experts immediately after each HHAC activity day to document the sequence of events, any deviations from the planned activities, and fidelity to the HHAC protocol, these data were not systematically analysed in the present study. Consequently, we cannot fully assess the level of intervention fidelity or how variations in implementation may have influenced the observed outcomes. Lastly, since this study used a within-subject design, it cannot provide evidence of causality.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the HHAC initiative was associated with more time spent in MVPA and less time in SB, particularly through play-based activities, highlighting the value of active play in fostering healthier PA behaviours in young children. While nature and cooking activities did not show significant effects on PA, they may still contribute to other aspects of child development, such as cognitive and social growth. By focusing on the most effective components, such as play, and considering the cultural and contextual relevance of activities, initiatives like HHAC can help address the public health challenge of increasing PA among children in socially vulnerable populations. Future research should further investigate how different components of multistranded initiatives like HHAC can be effectively implemented and anchored in everyday kindergarten practices, while also examining their broader impacts on children’s development beyond PA.
