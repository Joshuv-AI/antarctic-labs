 1. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND

The U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) places the health and well-being of our nation’s veterans
as its top priority. VA is dedicated to offering timely access to high-quality, evidence-based mental health
care that meets the needs of veterans and supports their reintegration into society. One of our core
missions is to prevent suicide among veterans through innovative approaches and resources.

With funding from the VA Office of Mental Health and Suicide Prevention (OMHSP), the Community
Determinants of Health (EDH) Data project has developed innovative datasets associated with specific
health outcomes, a methodology for transforming spatiotemporal data from one spatial reference (e.g., a
1km grid) to another (e.g., US Census Tracts), and capabilities for modeling health outcomes. These
datasets represent an enhancement of the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ),
addressing key gaps by introducing finer spatial resolution (Census Tract) and additional geographical
covariates into existing data.

The curation and standardization of these datasets is a complex task since they often originate from
various sources and are measured at different spatial and temporal resolutions. For example, US Census
data products typically use census blocks, block groups, or counties, while data like weather data are
available on 1km grids. Some economic data may only be available at the zip code level. In this context,
‘standardized’ means that all datasets share the same spatial extent (e.g., US Census Tract and/or County),
and ‘curated’ implies a repeatable process with data provenance and the use of appropriate methodologies
for covariate conversion.

The Community Determinants of Health datasets draw from multiple sources, resulting in variables with
varying degrees of availability, patterns of missing data, and methodological considerations across
different sources, geographies, and years.

 3. CONTENTS AND STRUCTURE

3.1 DATASET CURATION DOCUMENTATION STANDARD FORMAT

Each data source description adheres to a standardized format with the following fields:

1) Source: The name of the organization that provided the raw data (e.g., Health Resources and Services
 Administration [HRSA] for the Area Health Resources Files [AHRF]). Note: Prior to the FY23Q4
 release, we referred to the source organization as the “sponsor.”
2) Description: A brief, general description of the data.
 – Inclusion in the datasets: Lists the determinants of health domains to which the data
 source has contributed variables. Includes additional information relevant to the dataset.
3) Resources: Links to original data source documentation, data download sites, and other pertinent
 information.
4) Update Frequency: Indicates how often each dataset will be updated.
5) Variable Definitions and Specifications (in tabular format):
 – Variable name (column name)
 – Variable label (optional, if different from the variable or column name)
 – Source table (optional, if multiple data tables were available from the original data
 source)
 – Numerator (for derived variables; optional)
 – Denominator (for derived variables) or original variable (when renamed for the EDH
 dataset; optional)
 – Total_rows: Indicates the number of rows in each column within each dataset (Starting in
 FY23Q2).
 – Null_rows: Specifies the count of null rows for each column in each dataset (Starting in
 FY23Q2).
6) Variable Availability Across Years (in tabular format):
 – Variable name (column name)
 – Data year availability (e.g., 2009 to 2018)

This standardized format ensures consistency and ease of reference in the curation documentation for
each data source.

3.2 DATASET CONVENTIONS

3.2.1 Overview of Variable Derivation

The variables within the Determinants of Health datasets are derived from various data sources through
one of two methods:

- Direct extraction from the original data source: When the data was readily available from the source,
we renamed the original variables to ensure clarity and consistency across years, aligning them with the
naming conventions of the Determinants of Health data files.

- Derivation using data from the original data source: In certain cases, we calculated percentages or
rates for inclusion in the data files. We provide numerators and denominators for these variables, along
with their respective sources, in the data source descriptions.

 3

3.2.2 Standardization of Table Names

Since FY24Q1, all datasets have followed the naming convention:
<dataset_name>_<spatial_resolution>_<year(s)>.

Example: hidta_county_2018_2021.

Beginning in FY25Q4, we were requested to also include the cmty_ prefix in table names.
Example: [OMHSP_FY25Q4].[cmty_parks_access_county].

3.2.3 Variable Assignment to Annual Datasets

Variables appear in the annual datasets corresponding to:

1. The single year represented by the original data source
(e.g., US Area Deprivation Index 2020), or

2. The final year in a period represented by the data
(e.g., American Community Survey 2012–2016 is included in the 2016 dataset).

When possible, we also provide the unioned data along with a year column for each row.

3.2.4 Variable Availability

Variable availability varies across data years. Following each data source description in this report, you
will find a table outlining the availability of each variable in the annual datasets.
If a variable is not available, it is marked as NA (not available) or a null value.

3.2.5 Variable Naming Conventions

With the exception of geographic identifiers (typically named FIPS), variable names use descriptive titles,
generally retained from the original source column names.

3.2.6 Handling of Missing Values

In the datasets, missing values are represented by a blank, with one exception: Provider ratio variables
from the County Health Rankings (CHR) data use negative values when the number of providers is
zero. This detail is further explained in the CHR data description.

For comprehensive information about each data source, please refer to the subsequent sections of this
report.

3.3 DATASET VERSIONING

In terms of dataset versioning, we utilize the Microsoft SQL Server database system to provide these
datasets to be consistent with the VA’s CDW work environment. Each dataset is stored in a dedicated
table within a schema in the database. The quarterly releases are organized under distinct schema names
within the database, such as OMHSP_FY22Q1, OMHSP_FY22Q2, OMHSP_FY22Q3,
OMHSP_FY22Q4, OMHSP_FY23Q1, and so forth. These schema names facilitate distinguishing
between releases when we deliver the same dataset, albeit updated, from one release to the next. Our
approach to naming schemas with fiscal year and quarter identifiers (e.g., FY25Q1) was designed to

ensure precise tracking of datasets over time. It provides an unambiguous record of when datasets were
delivered, which is critical for project management and accountability.

3.5 FIPS AS GEOGRAPHIC IDENTIFIERS AND PRIMARY KEYS

At ORNL, we utilize the Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS) as geographic identifiers and
primary keys in each dataset or table for this project. FIPS codes are publicly recognized standards
developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) for computer systems and non-
military applications, particularly for standardizing codes of geographical areas. FIPS specifications
encompass various geographical areas:
 • FIPS 10-4 for country and region codes
 • FIPS 5-2 for state codes
 • FIPS 6-4 for county codes
These codes are unique within their respective geographic entities. For example, FIPS state codes are
unique within a country, and FIPS county codes are unique within a state. Since counties nest within
states, a complete county FIPS code combines the state and county identifiers. For instance, if multiple
counties end with “001,” the state FIPS code is added to make each county FIPS code distinct (e.g.,
01001, 02001, 04001), where the first two digits indicate the state, and the last three digits represent the
county.
Although NIST initiated the replacement of FIPS with the Geographical Name Information System
(GNIS) Feature ID in 2002, many federal organizations in the United States, including the US Census
Bureau, continued to use FIPS due to its broader coverage and precision in identifying geographic
entities, especially smaller areas with uncertain natural boundaries. The US Census Bureau maintains a
comprehensive hierarchy of census geographic entities for reference.
As the primary key in all datasets for this project, we consistently use the column “FIPS” to ensure unique
data identification, regardless of the source FIPS granularity. We specify the FIPS granularity, such as
region, state, county, census division, tracks, group blocks, etc., in the metadata table and reports’
descriptions. Users are presumed to be familiar with joining datasets using FIPS columns at different
geographic levels.

These exceptions are the following:

3.6 MAPPING ZIP CODES TO FIPS CODES FOR COUNTIES: OUR METHODS

When realigning spatial data to different boundaries that do not perfectly match or nest within the original
spatial units, some data loss is inevitable. This occurs because the spatial distribution of data at higher
resolutions than the native unit is often unknown. For example, certain zip code boundaries overlap with

 10

multiple county boundaries. When attempting to map zip code-level data to counties, there are situations
where data must be reassigned to two or more counties with limited knowledge of how to allocate it
accurately. Various methods exist to mitigate the degree of data loss, each with its strengths and
weaknesses based on the data’s nature. For social data, one effective approach is to allocate data based on
population distribution or addresses within those boundaries to reduce misallocation.

 4. CONNECTICUT COUNTY TO PLANNING REGION CROSSWALK

4.1 DATA SOURCE

Connecticut Office of Policy and Management (OPM)

4.2 DESCRIPTION

Summary:

Two crosswalk tables are provided to show the proportional relationships (“multipliers”) between
Connecticut’s legacy county boundaries and the state’s new Planning Regions adopted as county-
equivalents for federal statistical purposes. These tables enable users to convert values from counties to
planning regions and vice versa. Together, they support consistent analysis across changing geographic
definitions.

Dataset Creator:

Connecticut Office of Policy and Management (OPM), based on population and geographic alignment
estimates published in their Planning Region adoption documentation. The multiplier column was
generated by the ORNL team.

Applications:

  • Reallocating historical county-based datasets to the modern Planning Region geography.

  • Aligning public health, demographic, or environmental indicators with the new county-equivalent
 units.

  • Enabling longitudinal studies that require consistency across the 2022 county-equivalent
 transition.

  • Supporting aggregation, harmonization, and trend analyses for federal, state, or research
 reporting.

Advanced information:

Background

We developed these tables in response to a request from the VA, documented in the Master Data Issue
Tracker (Issue No. 102), which noted that Connecticut updated its FIPS county structure in 2022.
Beginning that year, the state transitioned from traditional county boundaries to planning region (Council
of Governments) boundaries for federal statistical reporting. To support VA analyses that span years
before and after this geographic change, the VA team requested a crosswalk table that accurately maps
legacy county boundaries FIPS codes to the new planning region FIPS codes. Two separate tables are
provided to support Connecticut’s county–to–planning region conversions. The
[OMHSP_FY26Q1].[cmty_fipsctxwalkco2pr_county] table contains multiplier values used to convert
measurements from the pre-2022 county boundaries to the 2022 planning region boundaries. Conversely,

 14

the [OMHSP_FY26Q1].[cmty_fipsctxwalkpr2co_county] table provides the corresponding multipliers for
converting values from planning regions back to counties. Together, these tables enable consistent and
reproducible transformations between the two geographic frameworks.

Connecticut transitioned from traditional counties to nine state-defined Planning Regions, which were
approved by the U.S. Census Bureau in 2022 as county-equivalent units for statistical reporting. This
dataset provides a structured crosswalk (via multipliers) that allows analysts to convert county-based
population measures to planning regions and vice versa.

Multiplier Column

Data provided by the American Community Survey (ACS) for the state of Connecticut includes a
crosswalk from the former County FIPS codes to the new Planning Region FIPS codes at the town level,
along with population estimates for every town in the state. In this dataset, the town is the smallest
geographic unit available and serves as the building block for both Counties and Planning Regions. Each
town is associated with a legacy county boundaries and a newly created Planning Region.

The multipliers represent the proportion of each county’s population (or other relevant count-based
measure) that falls within each Planning Region. They allow the redistribution of county-level values to
Planning Regions and the allocation of Planning Region values back to counties.

To explain how the multiplier (proportion) is calculated, it is helpful to consider the conversions between
Planning Regions and Counties. The same logic is applied whether calculating multipliers for Planning
Region to County or County to Planning Region. The example below describes the process used for
calculating the Planning Region to County multipliers.

  1. First, the total population of each Planning Region is calculated by summing the populations of
 all towns assigned to that Planning Region.

  2. Next, because a Planning Region may include towns from one or more legacy county boundaries,
 the total population contributed by each of those Counties is calculated.

  3. The multiplier is defined as the proportion of the Planning Region’s total population that comes
 from each contributing County. This proportion is the multiplier used when converting
 population-based measures from the Planning Region level to the legacy county boundaries level.

Example: Capitol Planning Region

The Capitol Planning Region contains towns from two Counties: Hartford and Tolland. The 2022
populations of the towns in these counties were:

Hartford County: 825618

Tolland County: 149973

The total population of the Capitol Planning Region is:

825618 + 149973 = 975591

 15

The multipliers are calculated as follows:

Hartford County multiplier (proportion):

825618 / 975591 = 0.84627472

Tolland County multiplier (proportion):

149973 / 975591 = 0.15372528

Limitations

  • Multipliers assume that proportional relationships (e.g., population shares) are an adequate proxy
 for redistributing data. This approach works best for county-based measures (population, case
 counts, etc.).

  • Measures that are not population-proportional (e.g., rates, ratios, facility-based measures) may
 require more advanced modeling.

  • Historical data before 2022 may not reflect boundary changes, so redistributions are estimates,
 not exact conversions.

Instructions How to Use This Table

As stated above, this table provides multipliers to convert values between Connecticut’s county
boundaries and the 2022 Planning Region boundaries. To estimate a Planning Region–level value from a
county-level measurement:

  • Multiply the county value by the multiplier associated with the county–planning region pair in the
 table.

  • This gives an approximation of what the measurement would be under the 2022 Planning Region
 boundaries.

Method for Identifying the Nearest Facility:

 7. POVERTY ESTIMATES

7.1 DATA SOURCE

US Census Bureau – American Community Survey (ACS), Table S1701

7.2 DESCRIPTION

Summary:

This dataset provides annual county-level estimates of the number of individuals and families living
below the poverty threshold, derived from the U.S. Census Bureau’s American Community Survey
(ACS) Table S1701 for years 20122023.

Dataset Creator:

U.S. Census Bureau. American Community Survey (ACS) 5-Year Estimates: Table S1701 – Poverty
Status in the Past 12 Months. Retrieved from
https://data.census.gov/table/ACSST5Y2021.S1701?q=S1701

Applications:

  • Monitoring socioeconomic disparities at the county level

  • Supporting public health evaluations, including analyses of social determinants of health

  • Informing program planning, resource allocation, and grant applications

  • Longitudinal tracking of poverty trends across more than a decade

  • Integrating poverty indicators into environmental, demographic, or health risk models

Advanced information:

The ACS poverty measure compares an individual’s or family’s total income over the previous 12 months
to a federally defined poverty threshold that varies by household size and composition. Individuals living
within the same family share the same poverty determination because the measure is based on combined
family income. Households composed of unrelated individuals are assessed individually.

Under ACS methodology:

  a) If total family income is below the applicable poverty threshold, all members of that family are
 classified as living in poverty.

  b) If total family income is equal to or above the threshold, the family is not considered to be in
 poverty.

 33

The dataset is created by extracting, organizing, and standardizing data from the U.S. Census Bureau’s
ACS 5-year estimates (Table S1701: Poverty Status in the Past 12 Months). Estimates are originally
produced by the U.S. Census Bureau through nationwide household sampling and statistical modeling.

Limitations

  • ACS estimates are subject to sampling variability, especially in small or rural counties.

  • Poverty thresholds do not vary geographically (except for Alaska and Hawaii), which may not
 fully capture differences in local cost of living.

  • Poverty measures reflect income only, without consideration of assets, debt, or temporary
 hardship.

  • Families of unrelated adults do not exist in ACS definitions, which can affect classification of
 shared households.

  • ACS values represent multiyear pooled estimates (5-year), not point-in-time poverty levels.

  • Note: It appears that no data is available for FIPS 35039 County for the year 2018.

Source Citation

  • U.S. Census Bureau. (2025). American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates: Table S1701
 – Poverty status in the past 12 months. Retrieved December 2025, from
        https://data.census.gov/table/ACSST5Y2021.S1701?q=S1701

Specification

  • Variable universe: Population for whom poverty status is determined

  • Measurement units: Counts of individuals and families below poverty; total population

  • Source table: ACS S1701 (Poverty Status in the Past 12 Months)

Publications Related to the Dataset’s Subject Area

  • London, A. S., Landes, S. D., & Wilmoth, J. M. (2022). Service-connected disability and poverty
 among US veterans. The Oxford handbook of the sociology of disability, 441-467.

  • Pooler, J. A., Srinivasan, M., Miller, Z., & Mian, P. (2021). Prevalence and risk factors for food
 insecurity among low-income US military veterans. Public Health Reports, 136(5), 618-625.

  • Tsai, J., & Hooshyar, D. (2022). Prevalence of eviction, home foreclosure, and homelessness

        study. Public health, 213, 181-188.
