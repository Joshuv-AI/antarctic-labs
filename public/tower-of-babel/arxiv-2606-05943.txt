Introduction

The theory of bounded or unbounded linear operators on Hilbert space is well developed and there exists a huge quantity of research papers and books on linear operators. In constrast, conjugate-linear operators, or equivalently, antilinear operators (that is, operators
T
satisfying

T(\alpha x+\beta y)=\overline{\alpha}\,T(x)+\overline{\beta}\,T(y)\quad\textrm{for}\quad\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C},\,x,y\in{\mathcal{D}}(T))

are rarely investigated in the literature.

Antilinear operators occur in the study of unbounded
C
-symmetric operators and
C
-self-adjoint operators.
A densely defined linear operator
T
on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
is called
C
-symmetric (resp.
C
-self-adjoint) with respect to a conjugation
C
on
{\mathcal{H}}
if
CTC\subseteq T^{*}
(resp.
CTC=T^{*}).
For such operators, the
antilinear
operators
TC
and
T^{*}C
play an important role (see
[
10
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
1
]
). In the finite dimensional case,
C
-self-adjoint operators can be described by complex symmetric matrices, a well-studied class of matrices (see e.g.
[
3
]
,
[
5
, Chapter 11]
).

Antilinear unitary operators appear in quantum physics since the work of E. Wigner. By Wigner’s theorem
[
16
]
, each symmetry of the set of pure states of a quantum mechanical system is given by a unitary or by an antiunitary operator. Wigner
[
17
]
also classified the antiunitary operators
A
for which the unitary operator
A^{2}
has a complete set of eigenvectors. Further, the
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
- symmetry operator in quantum mechanics is an antilinear operator (see e.g.
[
15
]
).

The subject of the present paper are densely defined closed antilinear unbounded operators on general complex Hilbert spaces. Our particular emphasize is on the structure of unbounded antilinear normal operators.

Let us describe the contents of this paper. In Section

2
, we develop basic definitions on antilinear operators. For a densely defined antilinear operator
T
its adjoint
T^{\dagger}
is defined by

\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle T^{\dagger}y,x\rangle\quad\textrm{for}\quad x\in{\mathcal{D}}(T),y\in{\mathcal{D}}(T^{\dagger}).

This is Wigner’s classical definition
[
17
]
. We show that for each densely defined closed antilinear operator
T
the linear operator
T^{\dagger}T
is self-adjoint and positive, so the definition
|T|:=\sqrt{T^{\dagger}T}
of the absolute value of
T
makes sense.

In Section
3
, the polar decompositon of densely defined closed antilinear operators is obtained (Theorem
3
) and we introduce some natural classes of antilinear operators.
As in the linear case, we define that
T
is self-adjoint if
T=T^{\dagger}
and
T
is normal if
T^{\dagger}T=TT^{\dagger}
. Antilinear normal operators are closely related to
C
-normal operators as defined in
[
11
]
, see Remark
2
. A densely defined closed antilinear operator

if self-adjoint if and only if
T=C|T|=|T|C
for some conjugation
C
(Theorem
4
).

In Section
4

T
is normal if and only if there exists a conjugation
C
such the product
TC
is a linear normal operator (Proposition
8
and Theorem
9
). Further, a densely defined closed antilinear operator
T
is normal if and only if there exists an antilinear unitary operator
V
such that
T=V|T|=|T|V
and
T^{\dagger}=V^{\dagger}|T|=|T|V^{\dagger}
(Theorem
11
).

Section
5
is devoted to
antilinear normal operators on the two-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. This simple case illustrates the general case and it delimits possible results.

In Section
6

N_{1}
and

be normal linear operators on Hilbert spaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
, respectively, and define a normal linear operator
{\mathcal{N}}
on

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}N_{1}&~0\\
~0&N_{2}\end{array}\right).

Further,
let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a conjugation of the Hilbert space

of the form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}~0&C_{2}\\
C_{1}&~0\end{array}\right)

such that
{\mathcal{N}}{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{N}}

\displaystyle T:={\mathcal{N}}\,{\mathcal{C}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}~~~0&N_{1}C_{2}\\
N_{2}C_{1}&~~~0\end{array}\right).

defines an antilinear normal operator
T

The main result of this paper is Theorem

In the finite-dimensional case, antilinear normal operators were studied and their structure was classified by F. Herbut and M. Vujicic
[
7

adapting Wigner’s description of antiunitaries. As note above,
the present paper is about antilinear operators on general Hilbert spaces including in particular unbounded operators.

Let us fix some notational conventions that will be used throughout this paper.
All Hilbert spaces are complex and their scalar products are linear in the first variables and conjugate-linear in the second. The symbols

,

The spectral measure of a normal or self-adjoint linear operator

For an antilinear operator

As in the linear case, the spectrum

For a subset

2
Antilinear Operators

Definition 1
.

Let
T
be mapping of a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
into a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
defined on a linear subspace
{\mathcal{D}}(T)
of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, the
domain
of
T
. Then
T
is called a
conjugate-linear operator
, or an
anilinear operator
, if

\displaystyle T(\alpha x+\beta y)=\overline{\alpha}\,T(x)+\overline{\beta}\,T(y)\quad\textrm{for}~~~\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C},~x,y\in{\mathcal{D}}(T).

In this paper, we prefer to speak about
antilinear
operators rather than
conjugate-linear
operators.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a Hilbert space with scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. We define another Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}
as follows. The sets of
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}
are the same and also the additions of vectors. The complex multiple
\lambda\circ x
of
x\in{\mathcal{H}}
in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}
is equal to the multiple
\overline{\lambda}\,x
in
{\mathcal{H}}
and the scalar product of
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle x,y\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}}=\langle y,x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\quad x,y\in{\mathcal{H}}.

To avoid confusion, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
x\in{\mathcal{H}}
, the symbol
\lambda\circ x
means the complex multiple of
x
in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}
and
\lambda x
denotes the complex multiple
of
x
in
{\mathcal{H}}
.

We verify (for instance) linearity and conjugate-linearity of the new scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}}
: For
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
and
x,y\in{\mathcal{H}}
, we derive

\displaystyle\langle\lambda\circ x,\mu\circ y\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}}=\langle\overline{\lambda}\,x,\overline{\mu}\,y\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}}=\langle\overline{\mu}\,y,\overline{\lambda}\,x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\overline{\mu}\,\lambda\,\langle y,x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\lambda\,\overline{\mu}\langle x,y\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}}.

Note that
({\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}})^{\mathrm{anti}}
is just the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
.
The embedding of
x\in{\mathcal{H}}
into
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}
is denoted by
\iota(x)
. In particular, for
y\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}
,
\iota(y)
denotes the embedding of
y
into
{\mathcal{H}}=({\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}})^{\mathrm{anti}}
.

Let
T
be a mapping of a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
into a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
. Clearly,
T:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
is an antilinear operator if and only if
T:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
is a linear operator, or equivalently,
T:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}^{\mathrm{anti}}
is a linear operator. This enables us to study antilinear operators by means of linear operators.

For mappings
T:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
and
S:{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
we define mappings
j(T):{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
and
j(S):{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
by

\displaystyle j(T)(y):

\displaystyle=T(\iota(y)),\quad y\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}},

\displaystyle j(S)(x):

\displaystyle=S(\iota(x)),\quad x\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}.

Notions such as closedness, boundedness or density of domains are the same for
T
and
j(T)
and for
S
and
j(S)
, because
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}
have the same norms.

Let us recall the definition of the adjoint of a densely defined linear operator
S:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
. The domain of
S^{*}
is

{\mathcal{D}}(S^{\ast}){=}\{y\in{\mathcal{H}}_{2}:\text{There exists $u\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}$ such that $\langle Sx,y\rangle_{2}{=}\langle x,u\rangle_{1}$ for $x\in{\mathcal{D}}(S)$}\}.

Since
{\mathcal{D}}(S)
is dense in
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
, then the vector
u\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
is uniquely determined by
y
. Setting
S^{\ast}y=u
,
S^{*}
becomes a linear operator
S^{\ast}:{\mathcal{H}}_{2}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\langle Sx,y\rangle_{2}=\langle x,S^{\ast}y\rangle_{1}\quad\text{for}~~x\in{\mathcal{D}}(S),~~y\in{\mathcal{D}}(S^{\ast}).

(7)

Now suppose that
T
is a densely defined
antilinear
operator of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
into
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
. Then
j(T):{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
is a
linear
operator with dense domain in
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}
, so its adjoint
j(T)^{*}:{\mathcal{H}}_{2}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}
is a linear operator which is defined by the preceding. We define the adjoint
T^{\dagger}
of
T
by

T^{\dagger}y:=\iota(j(T)^{*}y),\quad y\in{\mathcal{H}}_{2}.

This means that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}(T^{\dagger}){=}\{y\in{\mathcal{H}}_{2}:

\displaystyle\text{There exists $u\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}$ such that $\langle Tx,y\rangle_{2}{=}\langle u,x\rangle_{1}$ for $x\in{\mathcal{D}}(T)$}\},

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad T^{\dagger}y=u,\quad y\in{\mathcal{D}}(T^{\dagger}),

Since
j(T)^{*}:{\mathcal{H}}_{2}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}
is linear,
T^{\dagger}=\iota\circ j(T)^{*}:{\mathcal{H}}_{2}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
is an antilinear operator and equation (
7
) reads as

\displaystyle\langle Tx,y\rangle_{2}=\langle T^{\dagger}y,x\rangle_{1}\quad\text{for}~~x\in{\mathcal{D}}(T),~~y\in{\mathcal{D}}(T^{\dagger}).

(8)

Definition 2
.

The antilinear operator
T^{\dagger}
defined above is called the adjoint operator of the antilinear operator
T
.

Note that (
8
) coincides with the definition of the adjoint of an antilinear operator according to Wigner
[
17
]
. The preceding approach to the definition of
T^{\dagger}
can be considered as a mathematical justification of Wigner’s definition.

As usual, the adjoint of a linear operator
S
is denoted by
S^{*}
.
Note that for an antilinear operator
T
and a linear operator
S
we have
(cT)^{\dagger}=c\,T^{\dagger}
and
(cS)^{*}=\overline{c}\,S^{*}
for
c\in\mathbb{C}
,
c\neq 0.

If
A
is a linear operator and
S
and
T
antilinear operators, then
AT
,
TA
are antilinear,
ST
is linear and

\displaystyle T^{\dagger}A^{*}\subseteq(AT)^{\dagger},~~~A^{*}T^{\dagger}\subseteq(TA)^{\dagger},~~~S^{\dagger}T^{\dagger}\subseteq(TS)^{*}.

Let
A:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
be a densely defined antilinear operator. Then

\displaystyle j(A)^{*}j(A):{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}},\quad j(A)j(A)^{*}:{\mathcal{H}}_{2}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}.

From the corresponding definitions we obtain

\displaystyle A^{\dagger}A=\iota\circ(j(A)^{*}j(A))\circ\iota

(9)

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
A
is a densely defined closed antilinear operator of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
into
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
. Then
A^{\dagger}A
and
AA^{\dagger}
are positive self-adjoint operators on
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
, respectively. Moreover,
{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\dagger}A)
is a core for the operator
A
.

Proof.

We carry out the proof for
A^{\dagger}A
; the case of
AA^{\dagger}
is similar.

For
x,y\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\dagger}A)
, using (
8
) twice we compute

\langle A^{\dagger}Ax,y\rangle=\langle Ay,Ax\rangle=\langle x,A^{\dagger}Ay\rangle.

Hence
A^{\dagger}A
is symmetric. Since
\langle A^{\dagger}Ax,x\rangle=\langle Ax,Ax\rangle\geq 0
,
A^{\dagger}A
is positive.

Since
\|Ax\|=\|j(A)\iota(x)\|
for
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A)
and
A
is closed by assumption, the linear operator
j(A)
of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}
into
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
is closed. Hence
j(A)^{*}j(A)
is positve self-adjoint linear operator on the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}
(see e.g.
[
14
, Proposition 3.18(ii)]
). In particular it follows therefore from (
9
) that
A^{\dagger}A
is closed.

Since
A^{\dagger}A
is a closed positive symmetric operator, to prove its self-adjointness it suffices to show that
{\mathcal{R}}(A^{\dagger}A+\lambda I)
is dense in
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
for
\lambda>0
. Assume that
y\in{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
y\perp{\mathcal{R}}(A^{\dagger}A+\lambda I)
. Then, for
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\dagger}A)
,

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\langle y,(A^{\dagger}A+\lambda)x\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}=\langle y,\iota\circ j(A)^{*}j(A)\iota(x)\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}+\langle y,\lambda x\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\langle j(A)^{*}j(A)\iota(x),\iota(y)\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}}+\langle\iota(\lambda x),\iota(y)\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}}

\displaystyle=\langle(j(A)^{*}j(A)+\lambda)\iota(x),\iota(y)\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}}.

As noted above,
j(A)^{*}j(A)
is positive self-adjoint operator on
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}
. Therefore, the preceding implies that
\iota(y)=0
. Thus
y=0
. Hence
{\mathcal{R}}(A^{\dagger}A+\lambda I)
is dense and
A^{\dagger}A
is self-adjoint.

For the linear operator
j(A):{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
it is well-known that
{\mathcal{D}}(j(A)^{*}j(A))
is a core for
j(A)
, see e.e.
[
14
, Proposition 3.18]
. Since the norms of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}^{\mathrm{anti}}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
are the same, it follows from (
9
) that
{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\dagger}A)
is a core for
A
.
∎

One could try to study antilinear operators by passing to linear operators on the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}
as done in the proof of Proposition
1
. This has the disadvantage that an antilinear operator
T:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
becomes a linear operator of
{\mathcal{H}}
into another Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathrm{anti}}
.
We will use another way and try to express an antilinear operator as a product of some appropriate linear operator and some conjugation.

3
Polar Decomposition and Classes of Operators

For a densely defined closed linear operator
T
its absolute value
|T|
is the unique positive square root
\sqrt{T^{*}T}
of the positive self-adjoint operator
T^{*}T
.

Definition 3
.

Let
A
be a densely defined closed antilinear operator from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
into
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
. The
absolute value

|A|
of
A
is the positive square root of the linear positive self-adjoint operator
A^{\dagger}A
(by Proposition
1
):

\displaystyle|A|:=\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}.

As in the linear case we have the following fact.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
A
is a densely defined closed antilinear operator. Then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}(A)={\mathcal{D}}(|A|)\quad\text{and}\quad\|Ax\|=\|\,|A|\,x\|~~~\text{for}~~~x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A).

(10)

Proof.

The proofs follows verbatim the same pattern as in the linear case (
[
14
, Lemma 7.1]
). For

x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\dagger}A)={\mathcal{D}}(|A|^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\|Ax\|^{2}=\langle Ax,Ax\rangle=\langle A^{\dagger}Ax,x\rangle=\langle|A|^{2}x,x\rangle=\|\,|A|\,x\|^{2}.

(11)

By Proposition
1
,
{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\dagger}A)
is a core for
A
. Also,
{\mathcal{D}}(|A|^{2})
is core for the self-adjoint operator
|A|
. Equation (
11
) shows that the graph norms of
A
and
|A|
coincide on
{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\dagger}A)={\mathcal{D}}(|A|^{2})
. Taking the closures in (
11
) with respect to the graph norms gives (
10
).
∎

Definition 4
.

An antilinear bounded operator
U
defined on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
is called an
antilinear partial isometry
if
\|Ux\|=\|x\|
for
x\in{\mathcal{N}}(U)^{\bot}
.

That is,
U
is an antilinear isometric map of the closed subspace
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}:={\mathcal{N}}(U)^{\bot}
, the initial space of
U
, on the closed subspace
{\mathcal{R}}(U)
, the final space of
U
, and
U
is zero on the complement of
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
.

The following formula (
12
) is the polar decomposition for antilinear operators.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
A:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
is a densely defined closed antilinear operator. Then there exists a unique antilinear partial isometry
U_{A}
with initial space
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(|A|)}
and final space
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(A)}
, called the
phase operator
of
A
, such that

\displaystyle A=U_{A}|A|.

(12)

Moreover,

\displaystyle(U_{A})^{\dagger}=U_{A^{\dagger}}.

(13)

Proof.

The proof proceeds as in the linear case; for the reader’s convenience we sketch the main reasoning. From (
11
) it follows that
U_{A}(|A|x)=Ax
for
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A)={\mathcal{D}}(|A|)
defines an isometric map of
{\mathcal{R}}(|A|)
on
{\mathcal{R}}(A)
. Since
A
is antilinear and
|A|
is linear,
U_{A}
is antilinear. By continuity,
U_{A}
extends to an isometric map of
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(|A|)}
on
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(A)}
. Setting
U_{A}y=0
for

y\in{\mathcal{R}}(|A|)^{\bot}
,
U_{A}
becomes an antilinear partial isometry. Clearly, the antilinear partial isometry
U_{A}
with initial space
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(|A|)}
and final space
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(A)}
is uniquely determined by (
12
).

We prove (
13
).
Set
T:=U_{A}|A|U_{A}^{\dagger}
. Using that
U_{A}^{\dagger}U_{A}x=x
for
x\in\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(|A|)}
and

A^{\dagger}=(U_{A}|A|)^{\dagger}=|A|U_{A}^{\dagger}
, we derive

T^{2}=(U_{A}|A|U_{A}^{\dagger})(U_{A}|A|U_{A}^{\dagger}))=U_{A}|A|^{2}U_{A}^{\dagger}=AA^{\dagger}.

The linear operator
T
is antiunitarily equivalent (via
U_{A}
) to the positive self-adjoint operator
|A|
. Hence
T
is also positive and self-adjoint, so
T=|A^{\dagger}|
by the uniqueness of the positive square root. Then

U_{A}^{\dagger}|A^{\dagger}|=U_{A}^{\dagger}\,T=U_{A}^{\dagger}\,U_{A}|A|U_{A}^{\dagger}=|A|\,U_{A}^{\dagger}=(U_{A}|A|)^{\dagger}=A^{\dagger}=U_{A^{\dagger}}|A^{\dagger}|,

so
(U_{A})^{\dagger}=U_{A^{\dagger}}
by the uniqueness of the phase operator.
∎

Many standard notions for linear operators carry over almost verbatim to antilinear operators. It is very easy to “guess” the following definitions.

Definition 5
.

A densely defined antilinear operator
T
on
{\mathcal{H}}
is called

\bullet

symmetric
if
T\subseteq T^{\dagger}
,

\bullet

self-adjoint
if
T=T^{\dagger}
,

\bullet

formally normal
if
T^{\dagger}T\subseteq TT^{\dagger}
,

\bullet

normal
if
T^{\dagger}T=TT^{\dagger}
,

\bullet

antiunitary
if
T
is a bijection of
{\mathcal{H}}
and
\|Tx\|=\|x\|
for
x\in{\mathcal{H}}
.

Note that an antilinear densely defined operator
T
is formally normal if and only if
{\mathcal{D}}(T)\subseteq{\mathcal{D}}(T^{\dagger})
and
\|Tx\|=\|T^{\dagger}x\|
for all
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(T)
; likewise
T
is normal if and only if
T
is formally normal and
{\mathcal{D}}(T)={\mathcal{D}}(T^{\dagger})
.

The following theorem clarifies the structure of antilinear self-adjoint operators. For bounded operators, this result was stated as
[
13
, Proposition 3.1]
. The unbounded case requires additional arguments.

Theorem 4
.

A densely defined closed antilinear operator
T
on
{\mathcal{H}}
is self-adjoint if and only there is a conjugation
C
on
{\mathcal{H}}
such that

4
Antilinear Normal Operators

As mentioned in the introduction, our main emphasize in this paper is on antilinear normal operators.

A simple characterization of normality is given in the following lemma.

Lemma 7
.

Let
T
be a densely defined closed antilinear operator. Then
T
is normal if and only if
|T|=|T^{\dagger}|
.

Proof.

If
T
is normal, then
T^{\dagger}T=TT^{\dagger}\geq 0
. Taking the positive square root of the positive self-adjoint operators (by Proposition
1
) on both sides of this equality we obtain
|T|=|T^{\dagger}|
. Conversely, squaring
|T|=|T^{\dagger}|
gives
T^{\dagger}T=TT^{\dagger}
.
∎

A general method for the study of antilinear normals is to write them as products of linear normal operators with conjugations.

Proposition 8
.

Let
N
be a linear normal operator. Then there is a conjugation
C
on
{\mathcal{H}}
such that
T:=NC
is an antilinear normal operator and
T^{\dagger}T=N^{*}N
.

Proof.

Since the normal operator
N
is closed,
N^{*}N
is a self-adjoint operator. By the spectral representation theorem, the operator
N^{*}N
is up to unitary equivalence a multiplication operator by some real function on a measure space. Let
C
denote the standard conjugation on this measure space defined by
(Cf)(t)=\overline{f(t)}
. Then
CN^{*}NC=N^{*}N
.
Clearly,
T=NC
is a densely defined antilinear operator. Using that
N
is normal we derive

\displaystyle T^{\dagger}T=(NC)^{\dagger}NC=CN^{*}NC=N^{*}N=NN^{*}=(NC)(NC)^{\dagger}=TT^{\dagger}.

(15)

Thus,
T
is an antilinear normal operator.
∎

Theorem 9
.

Suppose that
T
is an antilinear normal operator. Then there exists a conjugation
C
such
N:=TC
is a linear normal operator and
T=NC
,

\displaystyle|N|=|T|\quad\textrm{and}~~~C|T|C=|T|.

(16)

If
E_{|T|}
denotes the spectral measure of
|T|
, then
CE_{|T|}(M)C=E_{|T|}(M)
for all Borel sets
M
of
[0,+\infty)
.

Proof.

Again we use the spectral representation theorem and represent the self-adjoint operator
|T|
as multiplication operator by some real function. Then the standard conjugation
C
on this function space satisfies
C|T|=|T|C
and

C|T|C=|T|.
Then

CT^{\dagger}TC=C|T|^{2}C=(C|T|C)(C|T|C)=|T|^{2}=T^{\dagger}T.

Therefore, setting
N:=TC
and using that
T^{\dagger}T=TT^{\dagger}
by Lemma

we compute

\displaystyle N^{*}N

\displaystyle=(TC)^{*}(TC)=CT^{\dagger}TC=T^{\dagger}T=TT^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=(TC)CT^{\dagger}=(TC)(TC)^{*}=NN^{*}.

(17)

Thus,
N
is linear normal operator. Moreover,
N^{*}N=T^{\dagger}T

implies
|N|=|T|
.

From
[
14
, Proposition 5.15]
it follows that

is equivalent
to

and so to
CE_{|T|}(\cdot)C=E_{|T|}(\cdot)
.
∎

Remark 1
.

The preceding proof shows that for each conjugation

is highly non-unique in general. For instance, if

is antiunitary, then

, so each conjugation

on

can be taken. An antilinear operator

on

is antiunitary if and only if

for some linear unitary operator

and some conjugation

on

.

.

Antilinear normal operators are closely related to the

and

For each normal linear operator

\displaystyle K:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0<\arg z\leq\pi/2\}~~\textrm{and}~~K_{\mathbb{T}}:=K\cap\mathbb{T}.

(68)

According to Definition
6
a normal operator
N
is called
K
-supported
if

E_{N}(\mathbb{C}\backslash K)=0.

The following theorem is the main result of this paper. By an
antinormal operator
we mean an antilinear normal operator.

Theorem 22
.

Suppose that
T
is a densely defined closed antilinear operator on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

T
is antinormal.

(ii)

T
is of the form

\displaystyle T=T_{\mathrm{sa}}\oplus T_{\mathrm{nsa}}~~~\textrm{on}~~~{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sa}}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{nsa}},~~\textrm{where}

(69)

\bullet

T_{\mathrm{sa}}=SC_{0}=C_{0}S
for some positive self-adjoint linear operator
S
and some conjugation
C_{0}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sa}}
and

\bullet

T_{\mathrm{nsa}}={\mathcal{N}}{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{N}}

for some normal operator
{\mathcal{N}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}N_{1}&~0\\
~0&N_{2}\end{array}\right)
, with normal linear operators
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
, respectively, such that
N_{1}
is
K
-supported, and some conjugation
{\mathcal{C}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}~0&C_{2}\\
C_{1}&~0\end{array}\right)
on

{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{nsa}}={\mathcal{H}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{2}.

Theorem
22
will be proved in Section
8
. Here we
discuss this result, derive some consequences, and collect a number of useful facts and formulas for an antilinear normal operator
T
. For this we retain the notation of Theorem
22
.

is supported by the set

is completely non-self-adjoint according to Proposition

which explaines our notation

\displaystyle N_{2}=C_{1}N_{1}C_{2}\quad\textrm{and}\quad N_{1}=C_{2}N_{2}C_{1}.

Next, by Proposition

we have

{\mathcal{N}}(T_{\mathrm{nsa}}-\lambda I)=\{0\}
for all

Even more, under the stronger condition
\sigma(N_{1})\subseteq K
the resolvent set
\rho(T_{\mathrm{nsa}})

is whole complex plane
\mathbb{C}
and the resolvent of
T_{\mathrm{nsa}}

Finally,

and Proposition

is irreducible.

Next we state some useful formulas.
On the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sa}}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{nsa}}\equiv{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{nsa}}\oplus({\mathcal{H}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{2})

\displaystyle(T_{\mathrm{nsa}})^{\dagger}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}~~~0&N_{1}^{*}C_{2}\\

Since

of

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}E_{\mathcal{N}}(M){\mathcal{C}}=E_{\mathcal{N}}(M^{c}).

(81)

Obviously,

Finally, we characterize the unitary equivalence of antinormal operators.

Proposition 25
.

Let
T
and

be two antinormal operators with corresponding operators

\displaystyle u_{0}Su_{0}^{-1}=S^{\prime},\,u_{1}N_{1}u_{0}^{-1}=N_{1}^{\prime},\,u_{2}N_{2}u_{2}^{-1}=N_{2}^{\prime},u_{0}C_{0}u_{0}^{-1}=C_{0}^{\prime},u_{2}=C_{1}^{\prime}u_{1}C_{2}.

Clearly,

and

The following auxiliary lemma is used in the proof of Theorem

It is a slight generalization of a lemma in

Lemma 26
.

Let
C
be a conjugation or an skew-conjugation on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{G}}
and
S
a bounded self-adjoint operator on
{\mathcal{G}}
. Suppose that
CS=SC
and

\displaystyle\langle CS^{m}x,x\rangle=0~~~\textrm{for all}~~x\in{\mathcal{G}},~m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(82)

Then there exists a closed linear subspace
{\mathcal{R}}
of
{\mathcal{G}}
such that
S{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
and

{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{R}}\oplus C{\mathcal{R}}.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be the collection of closed subspaces
{\mathcal{R}}
of
{\mathcal{G}}
such that
{\mathcal{R}}\bot C{\mathcal{R}}
and
S{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}
. A straightforward application of Zorn’s lemma shows that
{\mathcal{S}}
has a maximal element, say
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
, with respect to the inclusion. Let
{\mathcal{G}}_{0}:={\mathcal{R}}_{0}\oplus C{\mathcal{R}}_{0}.
It suffices to show that
{\mathcal{G}}_{0}={\mathcal{G}}
.

Suppose that
x\in{\mathcal{G}}_{0}^{\bot}.
Let
{\mathcal{R}}_{x}
denote the smallest
S
-invariant linear subspace which contains
x
and let
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
be the closure of
{\mathcal{R}}_{x}+{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
. Clearly,
{\mathcal{R}}_{x}
and
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
are invariant under
S
. We show that
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\bot C{\mathcal{R}}_{1}
. For this it suffices to prove that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{x}+{\mathcal{R}}_{0}\,\bot\,(C{\mathcal{R}}_{x}+C{\mathcal{R}}_{0}).

(83)

Clearly,
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}\bot C{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
, because
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}\in{\mathcal{S}}
. Since
SC=CS
and
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
is
S
-invariant,
C{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
is
S
-invariant as well. Thus,
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
is
S
-invariant. Since
S
is self-adjoint and bounded,
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}^{\bot}
is also
S
-invariant. Hence, since
x\in{\mathcal{G}}_{0}^{\bot}
,
{\mathcal{R}}_{x}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}_{0}^{\bot}
, so that
{\mathcal{R}}_{x}\bot{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{R}}_{x}\bot C{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
. Therefore, since
C
is a conjugation or an anto-conjugation,
C{\mathcal{R}}_{x}\bot{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
.

It remains to prove that
C{\mathcal{R}}_{x}\bot{\mathcal{R}}_{x}
. Using the assumptions on
S
, especially (
82
), we derive

\langle CS^{k}x,S^{n}x\rangle=\langle S^{n}CS^{k}x,x\rangle=\langle CS^{k+n}x,x\rangle=0,

so that
CS^{k}x\bot S^{n}x
for
k,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
This implies that
C{\mathcal{R}}_{x}\bot{\mathcal{R}}_{x}
.

In the preceding two paragraphs we have proved that (
83
) holds. Therefore,
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\in{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}_{1}.
From the maximality of
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
it follows that
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}={\mathcal{R}}_{1}
. Hence
{\mathcal{G}}_{0}^{\bot}=\{0\}
, that is,
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}\oplus C{\mathcal{R}}_{0}\equiv{\mathcal{G}}_{0}={\mathcal{G}}
.
∎

in the case of a general antinormal operator
T
. Recall from Theorem
11
that the positive self-adjoint operator

|T|
commutes with
V_{T}
and so with
V_{T}^{2}
, because
T
is antinormal. Hence
|T|
commutes with the spectral projections of
V_{T}^{2}
, that is,

E_{V_{T}^{2}}(M)\,|T|\subseteq|T|E_{V_{T}^{2}}(M)

for each Borel subset
M
of
\mathbb{T}
. Therefore, the closed subspaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sa}}=E_{V_{T}^{2}}(\{1\}){\mathcal{H}}
,

{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{nsa}}=E_{V_{T}^{2}}(\mathbb{T}\backslash\{1\}){\mathcal{H}}
,
E_{V_{T}^{2}}(C_{+}){\mathcal{H}}
,  and
E_{V_{T}^{2}}(C_{-}){\mathcal{H}}
reduce the self-adjoint operator
|T|
.

Also, the closed subspace
{\mathcal{G}}:=E_{V_{T}^{2}}(\{-1\}){\mathcal{H}}
reduces the operator
|T|
. Let
S
denote the inverse of the operator
(I+|T|)\lceil{\mathcal{G}}
acting on the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{G}}
.
Since
V_{T}
commutes with
|T|
,
J:=V_{T}\lceil{\mathcal{G}}
commutes with the bounded self-adjoint operator
S
on
{\mathcal{G}}
. Therefore, Lemma
26
applies and the correponding subspace
{\mathcal{R}}
is invariant under
S
. Hence the closed subspaces
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
of
{\mathcal{G}}
defined by (
88
) are invariant under
S
. This implies that
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
reduce the operator
|T|
. Hence
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}=E_{V_{T}^{2}}(C_{+}){\mathcal{H}}\oplus{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}=E_{V_{T}^{2}}(C_{-}){\mathcal{H}}\oplus{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
reduce
|T|
. From the preceding we conclude that

|T|\lceil{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{nsa}}=|T|\lceil{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\oplus|T|\lceil{\mathcal{H}}_{2}~~~\textrm{on}~~{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{nsa}}={\mathcal{H}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{2}.

Let us abbreviate
|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}:=|T|\lceil{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{nsa}},|T|_{1}:=|T|\lceil{\mathcal{H}}_{1},|T|_{2}:=|T|\lceil{\mathcal{H}}_{2}.
Then

\displaystyle|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}|T|_{1}&~0\\
~0&|T|_{2}\end{array}\right).

Since
|T|
commutes with the spectral projections
E_{V_{T}^{2}}(\cdot)
, it also commutes with the spectral integrals
U_{1}^{\prime}
and
U_{2}^{\prime}
. Obviously,
|T|
commutes with
{\rm i}\,I_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}
and
-{\rm i}\,I_{{\mathcal{K}}_{2}}
, so it commutes with the operators
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
. Therefore, the block matrix
|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}
commutes with the unitary operator block matrix
U
defined by (
8
), that is,
U|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}\subseteq|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}U.

Hence
U|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}U^{*}\subseteq|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}.
Since
U|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}U^{*}
is self-adjoint, we get

U|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}U^{*}=|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}
. Then
|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}=U^{*}|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}U
. Thus,

\displaystyle U|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}=|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}U~~\textrm{and}~~U^{*}|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}=|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}U^{*}.

(107)

Define
N_{1}:=U_{1}|T|_{1}
,
N_{2}:=U_{2}|T|_{2}
, and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}N_{1}&~0\\
~0&N_{2}\end{array}\right)=U|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}=|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}U,

where the last equality follows from (
107
). Since
T=V_{T}|T|=|T|V_{T}
and
V_{\mathrm{nsa}}=U{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}U
by (
104
), we conclude that

T_{\mathrm{nsa}}=|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}V_{\mathrm{nsa}}=|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}U{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{N}}{\mathcal{C}}
and
T_{\mathrm{nsa}}=V_{\mathrm{nsa}}|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}={\mathcal{C}}U|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}={\mathcal{C}}{\mathcal{N}}.

Now we show that the linear operators
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are normal. Using (
107
) we derive

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}^{*}{\mathcal{N}}=|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}U^{*}U|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}=|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}^{2},~~{\mathcal{N}}{\mathcal{N}}^{*}=|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}UU^{*}|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}=|T|_{\mathrm{nsa}}^{2}.

(110)

Hence the diagonal operator block matrix
{\mathcal{N}}
is normal and so are their diagonal entries
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
.

Next we prove that
N_{1}
is supported by the set
K
. Since
N_{1}=U_{1}|T|_{1}
and the unitary operator
U_{1}
and the positive self-adjoint operator
|T|_{1}
commute, using the product property of the spectral measure
E_{N_{1}}
of the normal operator
N_{1}
we obtain

E_{N_{1}}(\mathbb{C}\backslash K)=E_{N_{1}}((\mathbb{T}\backslash K_{\mathbb{T}})\times[0,+\infty))=E_{U_{1}}(\mathbb{T}\backslash K_{\mathbb{T}})E_{|T|_{1}}([0,+\infty)).

Since
U_{1}
is supported by
K_{\mathbb{T}}
as shown above,
E_{U_{1}}(\mathbb{T}\backslash K_{\mathbb{T}})=0
and hence
E_{N_{1}}(\mathbb{C}\backslash K)=0
, that is,
N_{1}
is supported by
K
.

The formulas (
7
) and (
7
) follow by straightforward computations. Note that (
110
) is just the first formula of (
7
).
All assertions about
T_{\mathrm{nsa}}
are proved.

Now we turn to the case of
T_{\mathrm{sa}}
which is much simpler. Recall that
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sa}}=E_{V_{T}^{2}}(\{1\}){\mathcal{H}}
by (
89
). From (
87
) it follows that
V_{T}
leaves
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sa}}
invariant. Since
V_{T}^{2}x=x
for
x\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sa}}
and
V_{T}
is antiunitary,
C_{0}:=V_{T}\lceil{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sa}}
is a conjugation and
S:=|T|\lceil{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sa}}
is a positive self-adjoint operator on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{sa}}
. Since
T=V_{T}|T|=|T|V_{T}
, we have
T_{\mathrm{sa}}=SC_{0}=C_{0}S,
see also Theorem
4
.

This completes the proof of Theorem
22
.
