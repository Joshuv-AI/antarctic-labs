Generative Augmented Inference
Cheng Lu, Mengxin Wang, Dennis J. Zhang, Heng Zhang

Abstract
Data-driven operations management often relies on parameters estimated from costly human-generated labels, such as purchase decisions, expert annotations, or survey responses. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) and other AI systems offer inexpensive auxiliary data, but introduce a new challenge: AI outputs are not direct observations of the target outcomes, but could involve high-dimensional representations with complex and unknown relationships to human labels. Conventional methods leverage AI predictions as direct proxies for true labels, which can be inefficient or unreliable when this relationship is weak or misspecified. We propose Generative Augmented Inference (GAI), a general framework that incorporates AI-generated outputs as informative features for estimating models of human-labeled outcomes. GAI uses an orthogonal moment construction that enables consistent estimation and valid inference with flexible, nonparametric relationship between LLM-generated outputs and human labels. We establish asymptotic normality and show a “safe default” property: relative to human-data-only estimators, GAI weakly improves estimation efficiency under arbitrary auxiliary signals and yields strict gains whenever the auxiliary information is predictive. Empirically, GAI performs strongly across diverse settings, outperforming benchmark estimators. In conjoint analysis with weak auxiliary signals, GAI reduces estimation error by approximately 50% and lowers human labeling requirements by more than 75%. In retail pricing, where all methods have access to the same auxiliary inputs, GAI consistently outperforms alternative estimators, highlighting the value of its construction rather than differences in information. In health insurance choice, it cuts labeling requirements by over 90% while maintaining decision accuracy. Across applications, GAI improves confidence interval coverage without inflating width. Overall, GAI provides a principled and scalable approach to integrating AI-generated information into decision-making pipelines.

Introduction

Operations management research across virtually every subfield relies on human-generated data that is expensive, time-consuming, or difficult to collect at scale. Estimating consumer preferences requires conjoint surveys costing $5–20 per respondent
(
23
)
; calibrating demand and choice models depends on transaction records that capture only realized—not latent—demand
(
52
,
29
)
; learning heterogeneous price sensitivities requires costly price experiments that sacrifice revenue during exploration
(
9
)
; and measuring treatment effects demands carefully designed field experiments
(
22
)
. Beyond these core areas, supply chain management requires on-site audits by trained inspectors to verify supplier compliance with social and environmental standards
(
57
,
26
)
; healthcare operations depend on clinical trial endpoints that cost tens of thousands of dollars per patient to collect
(
2
)
or inherently limited observational data, as in organ allocation
(
1
)
; and platform operations rely on expert human judgment to identify fraudulent reviews and content
(
35
)
. In each case, the cost of obtaining reliable human-generated labels fundamentally constrains sample sizes and, consequently, the precision of parameter estimates that inform operational decisions.

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) and AI-based generation systems offers a potential solution to this data scarcity problem
(
14
,
40
)
. AI systems can generate annotations, simulate survey responses, and produce predictions at costs orders of magnitude lower than human labeling—often $0.01–0.10 per observation. The “digital twin” paradigm, which uses LLM-based simulations of individual decision-makers to generate synthetic behavioral data, exemplifies this opportunity
(
49
)
.
25
develop ORLM that trains open-source LLMs to automate optimization modeling and solving with a data synthesis framework, demonstrating the potential for AI systems to augment traditional operations research methodologies. If AI-generated data could reliably augment scarce human labels, researchers could achieve the statistical precision of large samples while collecting only a fraction of the human-labeled observations.

However, realizing these efficiency gains while maintaining valid statistical inference presents a fundamental challenge for two reasons. First, when AI outputs are intended as noisy substitutes of human labels, they can differ systematically from human judgments in complex, context-dependent ways. In our retail pricing application, LLM-based digital twins
(
49
)
predict purchases at a 30% rate when the true rate is 44%—a substantial and systematic bias. In our vaccine conjoint study, LLM-generated choice predictions agree with human responses only 54% of the time, barely better than random guessing. Naively pooling such AI-generated data with human labels would introduce bias that could overwhelm any efficiency gains, leading to misleading parameter estimates and invalid confidence intervals. Second, and more importantly, AI outputs can often be structured auxiliary representations rather than just noisy labels—they may include reasoning traces, confidence scores, persona descriptions, or high-dimensional embeddings that are categorically different from the outcome of interest. This distinction fundamentally changes the statistical formulation of the problem: rather than a bias-correction problem (correcting inaccurate predictions), it becomes a problem of efficiently leveraging auxiliary information for inference.

Therefore, the core methodological question is:
How can AI-generated signals be incorporated into statistical estimation without requiring them to serve as accurate surrogates for the outcome?
Existing approaches to AI-augmented inference, most notably Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI;
4
,
5
), utilizes AI predicted labels as noisy proxies of the true outcome. Unlike the “proxy” view, modern generative AI systems can produce outputs that are categorically different from the outcomes researchers seek to estimate. This highlight the need for an inferential framework that does not require AI outputs to function as surrogate labels.

We propose
Generative Augmented Inference
(GAI), a framework that resolves this tension through a conceptual shift: rather than treating AI outputs as proxies for outcomes, we treat them as
informative features
that help predict outcomes (
Figure

1
). This distinction is subtle but consequential. Under the proxy view, AI outputs must approximate human labels to be useful; under the feature view, AI outputs need only be
informative
of human labels to contribute information.

Figure 1
:
GAI incorporates AI outputs as auxiliary features rather than surrogate labels. In contrast to PPI, which applies corrections at the loss-function level and relies on AI predictions approximating the outcome, GAI embeds auxiliary signals within a Neyman-orthogonal score function. This construction allows the estimator to leverage auxiliary data for bias correction and efficiency gains, even when AI representations are biased, or weakly informative.

To illustrate, consider an LLM asked to predict whether a consumer will purchase a product. Under the proxy view, the LLM’s binary yes/no prediction would serve as a stand-in for the true purchase outcome. If the LLM systematically underpredicts purchases (e.g., 30% predicted vs. 44% actual), this bias must be corrected. Under the feature view, we instead ask: does the LLM’s representation—including prediction and any additional outputs such as its reasoning, confidence scores, or persona characteristics—help predict the actual purchase?
Even a biased prediction can be highly informative: knowing that the LLM predicts “no” may shift the expected purchase probability downwards, while “yes” shifts it upwards. GAI exploits the representations as features rather than requiring the predictions to be accurate proxies of human labels.

Concretely, consider estimating the parameters of a generalized linear model (GLM) from a dataset where human labels are observed for only a subset of observations, but AI-generated auxiliary information is available for all observations. The auxiliary information may be an LLM’s binary purchase prediction, a vector of persona characteristics, a high-dimensional embedding, unstructured texts, or any other AI-generated signal—its structure is left deliberately unrestricted. GAI proceeds in two steps:

1.

Estimate nuisance functions.
Using the labeled subset, we estimate two functions: (i) the
outcome prediction function
—the best prediction of human labels given both covariates and AI outputs, capturing whatever predictive information the AI signal contains about the outcome; and (ii) the
propensity score
—the probability that an observation receives a human label, which may depend on covariates and AI outputs. These nuisance functions can be estimated using flexible machine learning methods such as random forests, neural networks, or gradient boosting.

2.

Construct a bias-corrected estimator.
We combine the estimated nuisance functions into a Neyman-orthogonal score function that integrates information from both labeled and unlabeled observations. The Neyman orthogonality property
(
19
)
ensures that estimation error in the nuisance functions affects the final parameter estimates only at second order. This robustness allows researchers to use flexible, data-adaptive methods for nuisance estimation without compromising the validity of inference on the target parameters.

1.1
Contribution

This paper makes three main contributions to the operations management literature:

1.

Problem Formulation and Methodology.

We identify that AI-assisted labeling creates a fundamentally new statistical regime: AI outputs are not noisy surrogates for human labels, but structured auxiliary representations that can be leveraged as informative features. This distinction leads to a different problem formulation from prediction-replacement approaches, in which AI outputs augment the covariate space rather than substitute for missing outcomes.

Building on this insight, we develop GAI, a unified framework for AI-augmented estimation in GLMs, where we allow model misspecification. The method exploits a natural orthogonal moment structure, yielding a principled estimator that supports valid statistical inference while accommodating the full diversity of modern AI outputs, including discrete predictions, continuous scores, and high-dimensional or unstructured representations such as text embeddings. In this way, GAI provides a general statistical framework for integrating AI-generated signals into classical estimation problems.

2.

Theoretical Guarantees.

We establish that the GAI estimator is asymptotically normal,
\sqrt{n}
-consistent, and admits a closed-form variance expression (Theorem
5.1
). Beyond these standard asymptotic guarantees, we prove a key “safe default” property: under random selection into labeling, where the probability of receiving a human label does not depend on covariates or AI outputs, GAI is guaranteed to weakly improve upon primary-only estimation in asymptotic efficiency, and strictly improves whenever the AI outputs are informative (Corollary
5.2
). Thus, under random labeling, incorporating AI outputs can never worsen performance relative to ignoring auxiliary AI data entirely, regardless of how biased or inaccurate those outputs may be.

We further show that the efficiency gains from GAI admit an interpretable variance decomposition (Corollary
5.3
). Specifically, variance reduction can arise from three distinct sources: (i)
sample expansion
, where auxiliary observations effectively enlarge the sample available for estimation; (ii)
representational power of AI
, where AI outputs provide richer transformations of the covariates and help approximate complex conditional expectations even when they contain no information beyond
\mathbf{X}
; and (iii)
extra predictive information from AI
, where AI outputs contain signal about human labels beyond the observed covariates. This decomposition provides a unified explanation for when and why AI augmentation improves statistical efficiency.

3.

Empirical Evidence Across Three Auxiliary Data Regimes.

We evaluate GAI across three real-world business applications that progressively shift the landscape in favor of existing methods, yet GAI delivers strong and consistent gains throughout. Across all settings, GAI substantially improves point estimation accuracy, maintains confidence interval coverage at or above the nominal level without inflating interval width, and reduces required human labels by 67–90%. The consistency of these results across diverse auxiliary data formats, information structures, and prediction quality levels demonstrates the broad applicability of the framework.

Our lead application,
vaccine conjoint analysis
, presents the most demanding test. We estimate a multinomial logit model using two types of auxiliary information derived from LLM chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning: high-dimensional text embeddings (
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{3072}
) and discrete choice labels (
z\in\{-1,0,1\}
). The LLM’s discrete predictions achieve only 54% accuracy—barely better than a coin flip—and the LLM observes only the same vaccine attributes as the outcome model, so the AI output contains no additional predictive information beyond features in the linear model. This is precisely the type of auxiliary data that PPI-based methods struggle to utilize effectively. Nevertheless, both GAI variants reduce MAPE from 19–32% to 16–17% by extracting signal from the AI’s outputs, whereas PPI-based methods fail to improve over primary-only estimation. GAI with just 50 labels outperforms primary-only estimation with 200 labels—more than a 75% reduction in required human annotations—and lowers decision error rates from 6.9% to 2.6%. Comparing the two representations, embeddings achieve smaller MAPE and better coverage while labels produce narrower confidence intervals, demonstrating that both forms of AI output can be effectively leveraged.

Our second application,
retail pricing with digital twins

(
49
)
, places all methods on a level playing field by restricting every estimator to the same binary AI prediction. The digital twin’s purchase predictions are systematically biased (30% vs. 44% actual), but carry extra predictive information from persona features beyond the price covariate. Even with identical auxiliary inputs, GAI reduces MAPE from 10–22% obtained from PPI++, the most competent benchmark, to 7–12% and achieves valid CI coverage (96–100%) with near-zero decision errors. GAI with 100 labels matches the precision that primary-only estimation requires 300 labels to attain—a 67% reduction. This controlled comparison isolates the source of GAI’s advantage: it is the feature-based methodology, not access to richer data.

Our final application,
health insurance coverage analysis
using U.S. Census data, tests GAI on PPI’s home turf: well-calibrated ML predictions with 85% accuracy trained on features beyond the income covariate, using the dataset and AI representations constructed by the PPI authors themselves. Even in this favorable regime, GAI achieves MAPE of 140–160% versus 290–980% for primary-only estimation, and GAI with 100 labels surpasses primary-only estimation with 1,000 labels—a more than 90% reduction. GAI attains 99–100% CI coverage and zero decision errors, whereas PPI++ undercovers (83–93%) and incurs 4.2% decision errors.

The operations management (OM) literature has increasingly embraced data-driven and AI-based methods.

Our work contributes to this literature by providing a principled framework for combining scarce human-labeled data with abundant AI-generated representations while remaining robust to systematic AI errors.

Transfer Learning.
Classical semi-supervised methods

,

,

)
use unlabeled features to improve prediction, while transfer learning
(

,

)
addresses distribution shift between source and target domains, such as leveraging data from existing operations to improve decisions in new settings
(
17
)
or learning policies robust to environment shifts between training and deployment
(
46
)
.
Similar challenges arise in operations: simulation and optimization models are widely used for inventory management, demand forecasting, and workforce incentive design, but calibrating these models requires real-world data that may be scarce, expensive, censored, or missing
(
11
,
12
,
32
,
28
,

,

,

)
. The AI-augmented estimator (AAE) framework
(
54
)
addresses the analogous challenge of combining AI and human data through parametric debiasing. Our setting is distinct: we have AI-generated
representations
(not just labels) for auxiliary data, and utilize them as features in semi-parametric estimations. GAI can be viewed as addressing the sim-to-real problem in inference: how to leverage abundant simulated or AI-generated data flexibly while maintaining valid inference grounded in limited real observations.

Prediction-Powered Inference.

4
,
5
introduced PPI and PPI++ for constructing valid confidence intervals by leveraging ML predictions on unlabeled data. Our approach differs from PPI-based methods in two fundamental ways: PPI uses labeled data to estimate and correct for the bias in predictions, yielding valid inference when predictions are reasonably accurate proxies for outcomes.

First, PPI requires AI outputs as surrogate labels, so it cannot handle settings where AI outputs differ fundamentally from the true outcomes—precisely the setting that motivates our framework, as discussed in the introduction. Second, PPI’s confidence-interval construction and statistical guarantees require AI outputs to be a deterministic function based solely on observable features. However, AI-generated data differs in two key ways: (i) AI outputs may be inherently stochastic due to the internal randomization of AI models; and
more fundamentally
,(ii) AI outputs may depend on predictive information beyond observable features, such as in digital twin-based data generations(see Example
3.1
for details). In such cases, the PPI-based methods do not apply and might only inflate variance relative to using human labels alone (Appendix
A
), whereas GAI, by Corollary
5.2
, is guaranteed to weakly dominate the human data-only estimator by capturing the extra unobservable information. More broadly, GAI highlights the distinction between “AI-based generation” and “machine learning-based prediction”: the representational power of AI models creates the possibility of recovering unobservable information that govern the true DGP. Readers can refer to Section
5.1
for a more in-depth comparison.

Neyman Orthogonality and Doubly Robust Estimation.

The AI-assisted labeling problem induces a specific data structure: outcomes are partially missing, but for all observations we observe structured AI-generated representations that may be high-dimensional, unstructured, or categorically different from the outcome. This structure is richer than the classical missing-data setting
(
45
,
33
)
, where the researcher observes only covariates for units with missing outcomes. In our setting, the auxiliary signal encodes predictive information about the latent response that goes beyond what standard covariates provide.

This problem structure naturally admits an orthogonal moment representation. Semiparametric methods developed for missing outcomes provide useful conceptual tools. In particular, augmented inverse probability weighted (AIPW) estimators
(
43
,
44
)
combine inverse probability weighting with an outcome model to obtain doubly robust estimators for linear regression problems, and later work studied their efficiency properties
(
24
,
10
)
.
While the resulting GAI score function shares the algebraic form of AIPW estimators, the role of each component differs substantively.
First, the augmentation term leverages AI-generated representations—which may include reasoning traces, embeddings, or persona descriptions—that encode high-dimensional predictive information about the latent response, rather than correcting for missing outcomes using observed covariates alone.
Second, our analysis is developed within the double machine learning framework
(
19
)
that has been adopted and extended in recent econometrics and ML research
(
27
)
. We provide a clean set of conditions—most notably the explicit construction of Neyman orthogonality and rate requirements for nuisance parameter estimation—together with practical implementation tools such as cross-fitting—that allow our estimator to achieve valid inference across a broad class of generalized linear models. Our contribution is thus not a new semiparametric principle, but rather the recognition that AI-generated representations create a new estimation regime that is well-suited to orthogonal moment methods, together with the formal analysis of the resulting estimator’s properties in this context.

An earlier, preliminary version of this work was submitted to a machine learning conference
(
6
)
. The present manuscript substantially extends that version by developing a variance-decomposition result that captures the sources of efficiency gains, strengthening the comparison with baseline approaches, providing additional implementation guidance for model selection in nuisance estimation, and adding a new application that extends the experiment to richer AI data regimes.

Problem Formulation

In this section, we formulate the problem within a

misspecified GLM
framework and describe the corresponding data-generating and data-collection processes.

3.1
A Misspecified Generalized Linear Model Framework and Applications

We consider the following estimation framework:
We let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
denote a human label of interest, associated with a random feature matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
.
\mathbf{y}\,|\,\mathbf{X}
follows an
unknown
ground truth distribution. We aim to recover a target parameter
\bm{\beta}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which solves:

\bm{\beta}^{*}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y};\bm{\beta})\right],

(1)

where
\ell(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y};\bm{\beta}):=b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta})-\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}
denote a loss function and
b(\cdot):\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
is a known twice-continuously differentiable convex function on an open convex
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
We assume that
\mathcal{B}
is an open convex set such that, for some compact set
\breve{\mathcal{X}}
with
\mathcal{X}\subset\breve{\mathcal{X}}
, it holds that
\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}\in\Theta
whenever
\mathbf{X}\in\breve{\mathcal{X}}
and
\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}
.
Under these conditions, one can verify that

\bm{\beta}^{*}
satisfies the first-order condition:

\text{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y};\bm{\beta}^{*})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{X}^{\top}\left(\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbf{y}\right)\right]=0\,.

(2)

An important special case of our framework is the class of canonical GLMs, which impose a structured functional form on the conditional distribution of
\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}
. In particular, canonical GLMs assume that
\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}
belongs to the exponential family, with density
f(\mathbf{y}\,|\,\mathbf{X};b,\bm{\beta}^{*})\propto\exp\left\{b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*}\right\}
. Under this specification, (
1
) corresponds to the expected log-likelihood minimization estimator and
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\left(\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbf{y}\right)
in (
2
) is the canonical score function. Consequently, our framework covers canonical GLMs that are widely used in applied modeling pipelines across industry and scientific research, including:

•

Linear regression:

k=1
,
b(\theta)=\tfrac{1}{2}\theta^{2}
.
This corresponds to squared-loss estimation for continuous outcomes and is the workhorse model in empirical studies, including treatment-effect estimation, forecasting, and policy evaluation.
Note that linear regression, viewed as a special case of GLMs, requires normal densities conditional on
\mathbf{X}
.

•

Logistic Regression and MNL:
for
k
non-baseline classes,

b(\bm{\theta})=\log\!\big(1+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\exp(\theta_{j})\big)
.
This is the standard model for discrete choice among multiple alternatives, which is widely used for multiclass classification applications, such as text classification, image recognition, and recommendation systems, where predicted utilities determine selection probabilities among competing options. When
k=1
, this reduces to logistic regression.

•

Poisson regression:

k=1
,
b(\theta)=\exp(\theta)
.
This models count outcomes and event rates, with applications such as demand incidence, click/conversion counts, and arrival processes in computer systems. Poisson GLMs are standard for modeling event rates in systems monitoring, reliability, and online experimentation.

While canonical GLMs posit a specific exponential-family form for the conditional distribution of
\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}
, real-world data-generating processes rarely conform exactly to these assumptions. Our framework explicitly accommodates such deviations by relaxing the exponential-family requirement. In particular, we impose no restrictions on the true conditional distribution of
\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}
and allow for an arbitrary, unknown ground-truth relationship. As a result, our approach permits model misspecification and interprets the target parameter as the best approximation within a selected GLM class: With a
b(\cdot)
function from GLM family, (
1
) seeks a GLM density closest to the ground truth density in the conditional Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, i.e.,

\bm{\beta}^{*}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}}\left[\log\left(\frac{f(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X};b,\bm{\beta}^{*})}{f^{*}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X})}\right)\right]\right],

where
f^{*}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X})
corresponds to the true, unknown conditional density.
Therefore, we broadly view (
1
) as a
best-in-class
estimator, which can be regarded as the best generalized linear representation of the relationship between
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{X}
.

3.2
Data Generation and Collection

To estimate the best-in-class parameter
\bm{\beta}^{*}
, we rely on observed data and an appropriate estimation procedure. In this section, we describe the available data, the underlying data-generating process, and the mechanism by which observations are collected.

Consider a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{\Xi_{i}=(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i},w_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with
partially observable
human labels. Each data sample is i.i.d. In each sample,
\mathbf{X}_{i}
represent the feature matrix,
\mathbf{y}_{i}
denote the human label of interest. Due to the substantial cost of human labeling, not all human labels are observed. We let
w_{i}\in\{0,1\}
indicate whether the human label
\mathbf{y}_{i}
is observed. While obtaining human annotations is expensive, AI-generated labels are comparatively low-cost. Therefore, for each sample we additionally observe
\mathbf{z}_{i}
, which denotes AI-generated auxiliary information, whose structure is left deliberately unrestricted.
\mathbf{z}
may be structured (e.g., categorical labels, probability scores, or continuous values), high-dimensional vectors (e.g., embeddings), or unstructured objects (e.g., texts, images). Given
\mathcal{D}
, we define the
primary
(human-labeled) and
auxiliary
(AI-only) subsamples

\mathcal{D}^{\text{P}}=\{\Xi_{i}\in\mathcal{D}:w_{i}=1\},~\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}=\{\Xi_{i}\in\mathcal{D}:w_{i}=0\}

and let
n_{\text{P}}=|\mathcal{D}^{\text{P}}|
,
n_{\text{A}}=|\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}|
.

3.2.1
Human Annotation Mechanism

We assume human labels are unobserved at random conditional on features and AI representations, i.e.,
w\perp\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}
. This assumption is natural when primary data collection is carefully designed (e.g., random sampling of human annotations). We define
e^{*}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})=\mathbb{E}[w\,|\,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]>\kappa
for some
\kappa>0
. In particular,
e^{*}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
represents the probability that the actual human label is observed given
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{z}
. Such a data collection mechanism is common in practice, as the decision to collect real human labels can be based on observed feature information and AI-generated signals. For example, practitioners may prioritize human annotation for samples with specific covariate profiles, for cases where AI-generated predictions exhibit high uncertainty.

3.2.2
AI Generation Mechanism

The core idea of our method is simple: we utilize
\mathbf{z}
to extrapolate
\mathbf{y}
on

\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}
using a principled approach.
Given AI-generated auxiliary information
\mathbf{z}
, we define
\mathbf{g}^{*}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\,|\,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]
, which serves as a key object that we estimate using machine learning methods in an intermediate step of our method (see Section
4.2
).
The benefit of introducing
\mathbf{z}
is twofold. First, the AI signal
\mathbf{z}
may contain predictive information about
\mathbf{y}
beyond what is captured by
\mathbf{X}
. This can occur when AI outputs are generated using auxiliary information not included in
\mathbf{X}
, making
\mathbf{z}
informative about
\mathbf{y}
even after conditioning on
\mathbf{X}
. Second, even when
\mathbf{y}\perp\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
, querying a powerful AI system can produce representations
\mathbf{z}
that summarize complex patterns in
\mathbf{X}
relevant for predicting
\mathbf{y}
.
Learning
\mathbf{g}^{*}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
using machine learning methods is often easier compared with learning
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]
in practice. For these two reasons, we can use the estimate of
\mathbf{g}^{*}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
to effectively extrapolate
\mathbf{y}
on
\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}
and
the convergence requirement in Assumption
5.1
is likely to be satisfied.
Then, instead of using only a small data sample

\mathcal{D}^{\text{P}}
, we can utilize the entire sample
\mathcal{D}
to estimate the GLM with provably strong empirical performance. We illustrate with concrete examples below.

Example 3.1

(
\mathbf{y}\,\not\perp\,\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
: Digital Twin Generation)

We tailor this example to the retail pricing experiment in
Section

6.2
. Each observation corresponds to an individual–product–price query. For simplicity, we let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}
denote the primary covariates used in the demand model (an intercept and price).
In practice, there can be other features included in
\mathbf{X}
.
Let
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denote a vector of individual-level persona and demographic features (e.g., psychometrics and demographics). In the digital-twin setting, the agent is trained on each individual’s persona information, so the (unobserved) purchase outcome depends on both price and
\mathbf{U}
. Concretely, suppose the ground-truth data-generating process is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y=1\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{U})~=~\sigma\!\left(\eta_{0}^{*}+\eta_{1}^{*}\,\mathrm{price}+h(\mathbf{U})\right),

(3)

where
\sigma(t)=1/(1+e^{-t})
and
h(\mathbf{U})
is an unknown (possibly high-dimensional) function capturing heterogeneous baseline propensity to purchase across persona types. The parameter of interest for the pricing study is the
population
effect of
\mathbf{X}
in a model that conditions only on
\mathbf{X}
(rather than on

4.1
The Challenges

Here we formalize the challenges and discuss alternative methods.
The canonical score
\mathbf{X}^{\top}(\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta})-\mathbf{y})

2

\mathbf{y}

w=0

\mathcal{D}^{P}=\{\Xi_{i}=(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i},w_{i}):w_{i}=1\}

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\,P}
is the solution to the empirical score equation based on the canonical GLM score:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{P}}\sum_{i:w_{i}=1}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\!\left\{\nabla b\!\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\bm{\beta}\right)-\mathbf{y}_{i}\right\}~=~0\,.

(5)

We denote by
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\,P}
the primary-only estimator. By construction, this estimator relies exclusively on the primary sample and is therefore fundamentally constrained by the primary sample size, which is often small due to the high cost of human data collection.
The primary difficulty with this approach is that it ignores the auxiliary sample completely.
We also remark that when
e(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
is not a constant and the model is indeed misspecified, it is not difficult to construct examples where this estimator does not have the desired statistical properties, such as consistency.

An alternative approach that utilizes the entire dataset is to naïvely pool the primary (human-labeled) and auxiliary (AI-generated) observations and directly plug them into the empirical score equation as if all labels were equally reliable. Specifically, the naive estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\sf Naive}
is the solution to the following empirical score equation:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{P}+n_{A}}\left\{\sum_{i:w_{i}=1}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\!\left\{\nabla b\!\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\bm{\beta}\right)-\mathbf{y}_{i}\right\}+\sum_{i:w_{i}=0}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\!\left\{\nabla b\!\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\bm{\beta}\right)-\mathbf{z}_{i}\right\}\right\}~=~0\,.

(6)

While this procedure leverages a larger effective sample size, it generally yields biased estimates when the AI-generated labels do not coincide with the true human outcomes. Additionally, when the auxiliary signal
\mathbf{z}
takes forms beyond a direct label surrogate, the naive pooling formulation is no longer applicable.

4.2
Generative Augmented Inference

To address these challenges, we propose the following score function:

\bm{\psi}(\Xi;e,\mathbf{g};\bm{\beta}):=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\left[\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta})-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})+\frac{w}{e(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})}(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{y})\right]\,.

(7)

\mathbf{y}

4.3
Hyperparameter Selection for Nuisance Estimation

Because GAI’s score function satisfies Neyman orthogonality, first-order errors in estimating the nuisance functions
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
and
e(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
do not affect the asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}

(
19
)
. Model selection for the nuisance estimators is therefore not central to the method’s validity—a reasonable fixed specification (e.g., ridge-regularized logistic regression) is sufficient in most applications.

When abundant data are available, however, practitioners may still wish to tune hyperparameters to improve finite-sample performance. We propose a nested cross-validation procedure that preserves the independence structure required for valid inference: within each outer fold
k
of the cross-fitting procedure, an inner cross-validation loop selects the best model configuration using only data from the remaining
K-1
folds, evaluating prediction loss on primary observations in the inner validation set. This ensures that model selection does not contaminate the out-of-fold predictions used for final inference. We demonstrate this procedure in the retail pricing experiment (Section
6.2
), searching over 8 configurations spanning three model classes.

5.1
Asymptotic Normality and Strong Dominance

The theoretical properties we discuss here are built upon the following assumptions:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{g}^{*}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})\|_{P,2}

In typical applications where human labels are collected via random sampling—such as randomly selecting respondents for annotation, randomly assigning observations to human review, or randomly subsampling from a larger dataset—we have

Corollary 5.2

(Dominance over Primary-Only)

Let
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{{P}}}
be estimator obtained through using only human data only, i.e., a solution to

If
e(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})=\rho
and

(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})
, it holds that
\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{\text{{P}}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\right)\leadsto N\left(\bm{0},\,\bm{\Sigma}^{\text{P}}\right)
, where
\bm{\Sigma}^{\text{P}}=\frac{1}{\rho}\mathbf{J}^{-1}\text{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y};\bm{\beta}^{*})\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y};\bm{\beta}^{*})^{\top}\right]\mathbf{J}^{-1}
and
\bm{\Sigma}^{\text{P}}\succeq\bm{\Sigma}^{\text{GAI}}
.
Also,
\bm{\Sigma}^{\text{P}}\succ\bm{\Sigma}^{\text{GAI}}
as long as

and

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbf{X}^{\top}\left(\nabla b\big(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*}\big)-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\,|\,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]\right)\cdot\left(\nabla b\big(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*}\big)-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\,|\,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]\right)^{\top}\mathbf{X}\bigg]\succ 0.

It is well-known that estimators based on Neyman-orthogonal score functions are semi-parametric efficient in many settings, for example, when the loss function model is correctly specified as the log-likelihood
(
51
,
19
)
.
However, this is not necessarily true in our setting due to model misspecification, so the dominance result given above is remarkable.
Corollary
5.2
establishes a “safe default” property: under random labeling, adopting GAI can never degrade performance relative to ignoring the AI data entirely, and will strictly improve performance whenever the AI outputs carry any predictive information. This guarantee is operationally valuable because it eliminates the risk of “doing worse by trying to use AI data,” which is a real concern with methods whose performance depends on prediction quality.

The random selection condition (
e(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})=\rho
constant) is essential for the dominance guarantee. In Online Appendix
D
, we provide a constructive example showing that dominance can fail when the labeling probability
e(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
depends non-trivially on covariates or AI outputs. Intuitively, if human labeling is strategically directed toward observations where the outcome is more predictable (e.g., labeling “easy” cases), the unlabeled observations may contribute noise rather than signal. When labeling is non-random, the unlabeled set may be systematically harder to predict, and the augmentation term can amplify noise rather than reduce it. The fundamental issue is that strategic labeling creates a selection effect that can make the labeled sample more informative per observation than the unlabeled sample, potentially reversing the efficiency ordering. In practice, the random selection condition is satisfied when human labels are collected via random sampling, stratified sampling based only on observed covariates, or other designs where labeling probability does not depend on outcomes or AI predictions conditional on covariates. We discuss extensions to non-random labeling designs as a direction for future work in Section
7
.

It is also instructive to compare our approach with PPI
(
4
,
5
)
, which further highlights the generality of GAI for AI-data augmentation. The comparison reveals two key differences.
First, PPI treats AI outputs as surrogate labels for
\mathbf{y}
and therefore cannot be directly applied when
\mathbf{z}
is categorically different from
\mathbf{y}
—precisely the setting that motivates our framework, as discussed in the introduction.

Second, PPI’s confidence-interval construction and statistical guarantees require
\mathbf{z}
to be a deterministic function based solely on
\mathbf{X}
. However, AI-generated data differs in two key ways: (i)
\mathbf{z}
may be inherently stochastic due to the internal randomization of AI models; and
more fundamentally
,(ii)
\mathbf{z}
may depend on predictive information beyond
\mathbf{X}
, as in our retail pricing experiment with digital twins (see Example
3.1
for details).

In such cases, the PPI-based methods do not apply and might only inflate variance relative to using human labels alone, whereas GAI, by Corollary
5.2
, is guaranteed to weakly dominate the primary-only estimator. In Appendix
A
, we construct an example where PPI increases variance, and PPI++ ignores all AI data (thus becomes equivalent to the human data-only estimator), while GAI substantially reduces variance. Fundamentally, PPI imposes
\mathbf{y}\perp\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
by construction as it builds on
\mathbf{z}=f(\mathbf{X})
. In contract, GAI allows
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
, which is becoming more representative of common AI data generation regimes, such as the digital twins. More broadly, GAI highlights the distinction between “AI-based generation” and “machine learning-based prediction”: the representational power of AI models creates the possibility of recovering unobservable information that govern the true DGP, beyond what is available from
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
alone.

5.2
Sources of Variance Reduction

The proof of
Corollary

5.2
(Online Appendix
C.2
) also reveals that the variance reduction arises from a specific structure in the score function. To provide deeper insight into when and why GAI achieves variance reduction, we formalize this decomposition in the following corollary.

Corollary 5.3

(Variance Decomposition)

Under the conditions of Corollary
5.2
, the variance gain of GAI over primary-only estimation decomposes as:

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{\text{P}}-\bm{\Sigma}^{\text{GAI}}

\displaystyle\propto\underbrace{(\frac{1}{\rho}-1)}_{\text{(I) Gain from sample expansion}}\cdot\Bigg\{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbf{X}^{\top}\Big(\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]\Big)\Big(\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]\Big)^{\top}\mathbf{X}\bigg]}_{\text{(II) {Potential gain from representational power of $\mathbf{z}$}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbf{X}^{\top}\Big(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]\Big)\Big(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]\Big)^{\top}\mathbf{X}\bigg]}_{\text{(III) Gain from extra information from }\mathbf{z}\text{\ given }\mathbf{X}}\Bigg\},

(11)

where
\rho=n_{P}/n\in(0,1)
is the fraction of labeled observations.

Both terms (II) and (III) in Equation
11
are positive semidefinite. The decomposition is additive because the cross-product between (II) and (III) vanishes by the law of iterated expectations (See Online Appendix
C.6
for detailed proof). This decomposition identifies three distinct sources of variance reduction:

Source (I): Sample Expansion.
The factor
1/\rho-1
captures the variance reduction from expanding the effective sample size. Primary-only estimation uses only the
n_{P}
labeled observations, whereas GAI leverages both labeled and auxiliary observations. If either term (II) or term (III) is positive definite, as the auxiliary sample grows relative to the primary sample (i.e.,
\rho\to 0
), this factor increases, amplifying the variance reduction.

Source (II): Representational Power of
\mathbf{z}
.
This term captures the representational power of
\mathbf{z}
when the ground truth model is mis-specified, meaning
\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})\neq\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]
.
GAI could achieve variance reduction even if
\mathbf{z}
contains no information beyond
\mathbf{X}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{y}\perp\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
.
This occurs because the true conditional expectation function
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]
is unknown and may be highly complex, falling outside the canonical GLM family. Model misspecification ensures the term (II) to be positive definite and allows GAI to realize variance reduction through sample expansion.
Beyond this asymptotic improvement, GAI also yields a gain in finite-sample performance when
\mathbf{z}
, leveraging its pre-trained knowledge, provides a better representation of
\mathbf{X}
. Specifically, if
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]
is highly complex, estimating it using only the limited primary sample is challenging. The auxiliary representation
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
leverages
\mathbf{z}
to capture part of this complexity, thereby facilitating learning in small samples. For example, in
Example

B.1
, directly estimating
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X})
from primary data alone is challenging, whereas incorporating
\mathbf{z}
as an additional feature allows even a simple logistic regression model to recover
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X})
effectively. This illustrates one source of variance reduction achieved by GAI.

Source (III): Extra Information in
\mathbf{z}
Given
\mathbf{X}
.
This term captures the additional predictive power of
\mathbf{z}
for
\mathbf{y}
beyond what
\mathbf{X}
alone provides. It is strictly positive definite whenever
\mathbf{y}\not\perp\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
, i.e., whenever knowing
\mathbf{z}
helps predict
\mathbf{y}
even after conditioning on
\mathbf{X}
. This reveals another source of gain: if the auxiliary information provides additional signal about the outcome, GAI can exploit this through the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]
.

Experiments

We evaluate GAI across three empirical applications that span materially different auxiliary data regimes. We deliberately order these applications so that the conditions become progressively more favorable for PPI-based methods, making GAI’s sustained advantage increasingly difficult to attribute to any single feature of the data.

First, a

(Section

) uses high-dimensional LLM embeddings derived from near-random predictions (54% accuracy) with no extra information (

). This is the most demanding setting for any augmentation method and illustrates GAI’s unique ability to extract signal from unstructured AI outputs that PPI-based methods cannot even accept as input. Second, a
retail pricing study
(Section
6.2
) places all methods on a level playing field: every estimator receives the same binary AI prediction. Since the digital twin is trained on persona features beyond price,
\mathbf{z}
provides extra predictive information (
y\not\perp z\mid\mathbf{X}
), yet GAI’s advantage stems from its methodological approach, not from a data advantage. We also illustrate the hyperparameter selection procedure (Section
4.3
). Third, a
health insurance census analysis
(Section
6.3
) evaluates GAI on PPI’s home turf: well-calibrated ML predictions with 85% accuracy and extra information (

), using the dataset and AI representations constructed by the PPI authors themselves.

Evaluation Metrics.

Across all three applications, we evaluate each method along two complementary dimensions:
point estimation
and
inference quality
.

1.

Point estimation
, measured by mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) relative to ground-truth parameters
\bm{\beta}^{*}
:

\text{MAPE}=\frac{100}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{|\widehat{\beta}_{j}-\beta^{*}_{j}|}{|\beta^{*}_{j}|+c},

(12)

where
d
is the number of parameters. Following prior work
(
54
)
, we set
c=1
in the conjoint and pricing studies to stabilize ratios when
|\beta^{*}_{j}|
is close to zero. To be consistent with
4
, we set
c=0
in the census study. MAPE summarizes the average percentage deviation of estimated coefficients from their true values, with lower values indicating more accurate estimation.

2.

Inference quality
, measured by three complementary metrics: (a)
confidence interval coverage
—the empirical frequency with which 95% confidence intervals contain the true parameter value, where valid inference requires coverage near the nominal 95% level; (b)
confidence interval width
—the average width of 95% confidence intervals, where narrower intervals indicate more precise inference but only if coverage remains valid; and (c)
decision errors
---cases where inference leads to incorrect managerial decisions
1
1

1

.

Benchmark Methods.

We compare GAI against four benchmark approaches that represent the main alternatives available to practitioners:

1.

Primary
: Maximum likelihood estimation using only human-labeled data, ignoring all AI-generated information. This approach provides valid inference but may lack precision when human labels are scarce.

2.

Naive
: Maximum likelihood estimation that pools human and AI labels without correction, treating AI outputs as if they were human labels. This approach is simple but can introduce substantial bias when AI predictions differ systematically from human judgments.

3.

PPI
: The prediction-powered inference approach of
4
, which treats AI outputs as noisy proxies for the outcome and constructs confidence intervals that adapt to prediction quality.

4.

PPI++
: A computationally efficient variant of PPI
(
5
)
that tunes a shrinkage parameter to optimize interval width.

Before presenting the detailed results, we highlight the main empirical findings that emerge consistently across all three applications. GAI delivers substantial improvements in point estimation accuracy, reducing MAPE by 50–75% relative to the strongest benchmark in each setting. These gains translate into dramatic label savings: in most applications, GAI with the smallest primary sample outperforms Primary with the largest. Regarding inference, GAI’s confidence intervals attain coverage at or above the nominal 95% level in nearly every configuration, whereas PPI++ frequently undercovers (as low as 83% in the census study). Despite this higher coverage, GAI’s intervals are always better or at least comparable in width to those of PPI++. Decision quality follows the same pattern—GAI achieves the lowest decision error rates across all studies, often by a wide margin. In other words, GAI achieves substantially better coverage and decision quality without meaningful sacrifice in interval precision. The consistency of these findings across three diverse applications—spanning different auxiliary data formats, information structures, and prediction accuracy levels—demonstrates the broad applicability of our approach.

6.1
Vaccine Conjoint Analysis

This application presents the most demanding test for AI-augmented estimation: the LLM’s discrete predictions are barely better than a coin flip (54% accuracy), and the AI observes no information beyond the covariates already in the model (
y\perp\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
). Any gains must therefore arise from GAI’s ability to extract useful structure from the AI’s outputs—either discrete labels or high-dimensional embeddings of the reasoning process. This setting showcases GAI’s capability to leverage AI outputs in multiple forms, even when PPI-based methods struggle to utilize them effectively.

Conjoint analysis is a standard tool in marketing for recovering consumer preference parameters from discrete choice data
(
23
,
34
)
. Respondents repeatedly choose among product alternatives described by attribute bundles, and researchers infer the underlying part-worth utilities governing these choices.

Data.
Following
54
, we utilize a vaccine conjoint experiment dataset with 1,971 respondents from
30
. Each task presents two hypothetical vaccines with 11 attributes and lets respondents select their preferred option. Each respondent was asked to take five tasks in the survey. We estimate the benchmark “ground truth” parameters
\bm{\beta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{11}
by fitting the same logistic choice model to the full human dataset. We exclude respondents who never select any vaccine option, as many public LLMs are restricted from choosing an opt-out option when generating auxiliary labels; this exclusion is applied consistently both when constructing
\bm{\beta}^{*}
and when forming experimental subsamples.

AI Representations.
For each choice task,
54
prompts LLMs using CoT reasoning to generate detailed deliberation about the vaccine choice before making a prediction, recording the decision as labels
z\in\{-1,0,1\}
(abstain, option 1, or option 2).
2
2

2

See

We evaluate multiple auxiliary generators that vary in the underlying model. Across these configurations, we obtain consistent, qualitatively similar results; for brevity, we report GPT-4o in the main text and defer the full set of robustness results to Online Appendix
E.1.3
. We consider two forms of auxiliary representation: (1)
Labels
: the discrete choice prediction
z\in\{-1,0,1\}
, which can be used by both GAI and PPI-based methods; and (2)
Embeddings
: the full LLM response text converted into a vector representation using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large model, yielding
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{3072}
that capture the semantic content of the LLM’s reasoning. The embedding encodes not just the predicted choice, but also the underlying reasoning patterns, attribute considerations, and decision heuristics that the LLM employs.

As explained, GPT-4o achieves low accuracy in this setting, barely better than random guessing in a binary choice task, and the LLM observes only the same vaccine attributes that comprise
\mathbf{X}
, which implies
y\perp\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
. Any efficiency gains must therefore arise from GAI’s sample expansion and representational power (Source (I) and (II) in Corollary
5.3
) rather than from extra information. PPI-based methods struggle in this setting: while they can use the discrete labels, the low prediction accuracy limits their effectiveness, and they cannot directly utilize the high-dimensional embeddings.

Experimental Design.
We construct repeated experiments by drawing a primary sample of size
n_{P}\in\{50,100,150,200\}
and an auxiliary sample of size
n_{A}=1000
from the available task pool.
3
3

3

We randomly sample
n_{P}/5
respondents and
n_{A}/5
respondents and use all their five choices to compose the primary set and auxiliary set.
For each
(n_{P},n_{A})
configuration, we run 50 independent trials. Within each trial, the underlying random draw of observations is held fixed across methods to ensure fair comparison. All reported statistics are averaged over the 50 trials.

GAI Implementation.
We implement GAI with both auxiliary representations using 5-fold cross-fitting. For GAI (Embeddings), given the high dimensionality of the embedding space (
d_{z}=3{,}072
), we use strong
\ell_{2}
regularization: logistic regression with ridge penalty (regularization strength
C=0.01
, maximum 2,000 iterations). The embeddings are standardized to zero mean and unit variance before model training. For GAI (Labels), we use
\ell_{2}
-regularized logistic regression with
C=0.05
.
In each fold, we fit
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
on four-fifths of the primary data and generate out-of-sample predictions for the held-out primary fold as well as for all auxiliary observations. This procedure is repeated across the five folds, and the resulting predictions for each auxiliary observation
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
are averaged. Because auxiliary data are never used in training, all predictions for auxiliary observations are strictly out of sample. Predictions for primary observations are also out of sample by construction due to cross-fitting.
Since primary and auxiliary observations are randomly sampled from the same underlying population, we set
\widehat{e}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
to the constant
n_{P}/(n_{P}+n_{A})
.

Under the MNL model, we then estimate
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
by solving a single optimization problem that minimizes the GAI-adjusted cross-entropy loss:

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=\arg\min_{\bm{\beta}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\widehat{\tau}_{ij}\log p_{ij}(\bm{\beta})\right],

where

\widehat{\tau}_{ij}=\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i})\!\left(1-\frac{1}{\widehat{e}}\right)+\frac{w_{i}y_{ij}}{\widehat{e}},\quad\text{ and }\quad p_{ij}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{\top}\bm{\beta})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}\exp(\mathbf{X}_{i\ell}^{\top}\bm{\beta})}.

This estimator coincides with the general procedure described in Algorithm
1
. Finally, we compute standard errors according to Theorem
5.1
.

6.2
Retail Pricing Study

Having established that GAI can extract signal from unstructured, near-random AI outputs, we now ask a sharper question: when every method receives the
same
auxiliary information, does GAI’s feature-based approach still outperform the surrogate-label strategy of PPI? This application provides a controlled comparison on a level playing field.

We consider the problem of estimating a logistic demand model that relates purchase decisions to price. The AI purchase predictions exhibit systematic bias (30% vs. 44% actual purchase rate), and we restrict all methods to the same binary AI prediction to ensure a fair comparison.

Data.
We use the data and pricing experiment from
49
, which contains 2,058 participants who completed over 500 questions across four survey waves spanning diverse domains including demographics, personality traits, values, and behavioral preferences. Each participant was presented with 40 products at randomized prices and asked to indicate whether they would purchase each item. We evaluate our methods on all products and observe consistent results. For conciseness, we report the results of the household cleaning product in the main text and show the comprehensive performance across products in Online Appendix
E.2.3
. The outcome model uses only price as the covariate:
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{1},\text{price}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}

d=2

\bm{\beta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

AI Representations.
The AI purchase labels
z\in\{0,1\}

49

The AI prediction rate (30.07%) substantially underestimates the true purchase rate (44.10%), indicating systematic bias. 7
Conclusion

This paper introduces GAI, a framework that enables operations researchers and practitioners to use AI-generated data for statistical estimation while preserving inferential validity. The central insight is conceptual: AI outputs should be treated as
informative features
for predicting human-generated outcomes, rather than as
surrogate labels
that replace them. Formalized through Neyman orthogonality, this perspective supports valid inference across a wide range of AI output quality, from systematically biased LLM predictions or reasoning texts to well-calibrated forecasts.

Our theory shows that GAI is asymptotically normal with a closed-form variance expression, allowing standard confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. More importantly, under random selection into labeling, GAI
strictly dominates
primary-only estimation in asymptotic efficiency whenever AI outputs contain predictive information about outcomes, regardless of whether those outputs are biased or inaccurate. These gains arise through three channels: sample expansion, representational power of AI, and extra predictive information from AI.
Our empirical results across three applications provide strong evidence to support these predictions.

GAI helps resolve a core tension in data-driven operations management: precise estimation requires large labeled samples, but human-generated ground truth is costly. By combining a limited number of human labels with abundant AI-generated data, GAI reduces this burden while maintaining statistical rigor. Across all applications, GAI consistently matches the precision of substantially larger human-labeled samples, often reducing labeling needs by more than half. Such gains can translate into substantial cost savings in settings such as conjoint analysis, demand estimation, and field experimentation. Although our applications focus on consumer choice and demand estimation, the framework applies more broadly to operations settings where human labels are scarce and AI-generated signals are available, including healthcare operations, supply chain forecasting, service operations, and quality management. In each case, GAI provides a principled way to combine expensive human judgments with inexpensive AI-generated information.

Several limitations suggest promising directions for future research. First, our dominance guarantees rely on random selection into labeling; when labeling is strategically targeted, these guarantees may fail. Extending the property to non-random labeling settings is therefore an important next step. Second, integrating GAI into real-time decision systems raises computational challenges, making online versions of the framework a valuable direction for future work.

More broadly, AI outputs create a statistical setting that is neither standard missing data nor noisy labeling, but one with auxiliary representations available for all observations. This shifts the inferential challenge from bias correction toward efficient information extraction. Human labels remain the gold standard, but AI-generated signals can substantially reduce the amount of human input needed to achieve a desired level of precision. As AI capabilities continue to advance, frameworks with rigorous guarantees for human-AI data integration will become increasingly important for credible empirical research in operations management and beyond.

Appendix A
Example illustrating distinction between GAI and PPI

We construct the following example illustrating the key distinction between GAI and PPI. Let
X
be a binary random variable such that
\mathbb{P}(X=1)=\mathbb{P}(X=-1)=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Let
U
be a hidden random variable with
\mathbb{E}[U]=0
and
\mathrm{Var}(U)=\sigma^{2}
.
X
and
U
are independent. We assume that
Y
is governed by both
X
and the hidden random variable as
Y=XU
. We have
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[U]=0
and
\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)\mathrm{Var}(U)=\sigma^{2}
.
Suppose that the AI model fails to correctly specify the rule on
X
, but captures the hidden variable
U
, outputting
Z=U
. We observe two independent datasets:

•

\mathcal{D}^{\text{P}}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{P}}
i.i.d.,

•

\mathcal{D}^{\text{A}}=\{(X_{j},Z_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n_{A}}
i.i.d..

Suppose our goal is to estimate the mean of
Y
. Let
r=n_{P}/n_{A}
. Following
5
, given a fixed constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the PPI mean estimator can be written as

\widehat{\theta}^{\sf PPI}_{n,N}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}+\lambda\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\right).

As
n\to\infty
, it is easy to verify that

\sqrt{n}\,(\widehat{\theta}^{\sf PPI}(\lambda)-0)\;\xrightarrow{d}\;\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\;\sigma^{2}\big(1+\lambda^{2}(1+r)\big)\right).

Meanwhile, we know that the human data-only estimator is
\widehat{\theta}^{\mathrm{P}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
, with

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta}^{\mathrm{P}}_{\lambda}-0)\;\xrightarrow{d}\;\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\;\sigma^{2}\right)\,.

Clearly, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma^{2}(1+\lambda^{2}(1+r))\geqslant\sigma^{2}
. The basic PPI corresponds to the case of
\lambda=1
, which inflates the variance from the human data-only estimator. PPI++ optimizes
\lambda
by minimizing the asymptotic variance, yielding
\lambda^{*}=0
(see Example 6.1 of
5
.)
As a result, PPI++ has no improvement over the human data-only estimator, as it is essentially discarding all axillary samples.

Under this setup, the AI-signal correlates weakly with the actual human label because it fails to capture the entire mechanism behind
Y
(missing
X
). However, it provides significant information on inferring actual human label by recovering the hidden variable
U
. PPI fails to utilize this information. In contract, GAI operates in a fundamentally different way. Using GAI, we can easily recover the relationship between
X
and
Y
, recovering
g^{*}(X,Z)=XZ
with the primary set. Then, the GAI estimator is
\widehat{\theta}^{\mathrm{GAI}}=\frac{1}{n+N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}g^{*}(X_{j}Z_{j})\right)=\frac{1}{n+N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}Y_{j}\right)
, where

\sqrt{n+N}(\widehat{\theta}^{\mathrm{GAI}}-\theta^{*})\;\xrightarrow{d}\,\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\;\sigma^{2}\right)\,,

which always reduces the asymptotic variance from the human data-only estimator and efficiently uses all data samples.

While this example is stylized, it is constructed to illustrate the key distinction between GAI and PPI. PPI inherently imposes
\mathbf{y}\perp\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
, as
\mathbf{z}=f(\mathbf{X})
. In contract, GAI allows
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
, the latter being more representative of common AI data generation regimes, such as the digital twin setting in Example
3.1
. More broadly, GAI highlights the distinction between “AI-based generation” and “machine learning-based prediction”: the representational power of AI models creates the possibility of recovering information about unobserved variables
U
that govern the data-generating process, beyond what is available from
(X,Y)
alone.

Appendix B
Auxiliary Example for Section

Example B.1

(
\mathbf{y}\perp\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
: Off-the-Shelf LLM Generation)

This example follows the setting of
54
.
They consider a conjoint analysis framework in which
\mathbf{X}
denotes the feature vectors of a choice set with
K
alternatives,
\mathbf{y}
represents the consumer’s choice, and
\mathbf{z}
is the AI-generated choice.
The model corresponds to

, yielding a standard MNL formulation.
While similar to Example
3.1
,
\mathbf{z}
may in principle be correlated with
\mathbf{y}
conditional on
\mathbf{X}
, for example when the AI system is queried using additional customer persona or behavioral data.
However, in many applications it is practically convenient to use LLMs in an off-the-shelf fashion.
In such cases both
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{z}
are generated solely from
\mathbf{X}
, implying
\mathbf{y}\perp\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
.
Consequently,
\mathbf{z}
does not contain additional information about
\mathbf{y}
beyond what is already present in
\mathbf{X}
.

Despite this conditional independence,
\mathbf{z}
can still be useful for estimation.
If the AI output has strong representational power and provides a highly informative summary of
\mathbf{X}
, then a simple parameterization such as

\displaystyle g_{(j)}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})~=~\frac{\exp\!\left(\check{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{(j)}+\eta[\mathbf{z}=j]\right)}{1+\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{K}}\exp\!\left(\check{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{(\ell)}+\eta[\mathbf{z}=\ell]\right)}~=~g_{j}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z};\bm{\theta}^{*}),\quad\forall j\in\mathcal{K},

as proposed in Online Appendix A of
54
, can approximate
\mathbb{P}(y=j\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})
well.

To see the intuition, suppose
\mathbf{z}=\phi(\mathbf{X})
where
\phi(\cdot)
is a complex nonlinear mapping implicitly learned by the LLM.
Recovering an equivalent representation using
\mathbf{X}
alone would require estimating

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\!\left(\check{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{(j)}+\eta\,[\phi(\mathbf{X})=j]\right)}{1+\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{K}}\exp\!\left(\check{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{(\ell)}+\eta\,[\phi(\mathbf{X})=\ell]\right)},\quad\forall j\in\mathcal{K},

which may be difficult in practice.
One might attempt to approximate
\phi(\cdot)
using flexible methods such as polynomial or spline expansions.
However, with limited human-labeled data this approach may lead to substantial model misspecification or overfitting on the small labeled sample
\mathcal{D}^{\text{P}}
, making it difficult to extrapolate
\mathbf{y}
to the full dataset
\mathcal{D}
.
In such cases the resulting performance may not be substantially better than using
\mathcal{D}^{\text{P}}
alone and ignoring the auxiliary sample.

Although this example is deliberately stylized, the same intuition extends to more general settings—particularly when the AI signal
\mathbf{z}
takes more flexible or high-dimensional forms.
In such cases AI-generated features can act as rich summaries of latent structure in
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]
, improving estimation even when
\mathbf{y}\perp\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{X}
.

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\leqslant~\frac{2\sqrt{n}C}{\kappa^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\text{E}\left[\left|\widehat{e}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})-e^{*}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})\right|\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z})-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}))\right\rVert\,\big|\,I^{c}\right]\Big/\epsilon^{2}+o_{P}(1),

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle a(n)/n^{r_{1}+r_{2}}=o(n^{-1/2}).

Therefore,
{\mathrm{P}}\left(\lVert\text{(III)}\rVert_{\infty}\geqslant\epsilon\,|\,I^{c}\right)
is
o_{P}(1)
and
(III)
is
o_{P}(1)

\beta^{*}

C.6
Proof of Corollary
5.3

This appendix provides the proof of Corollary
5.3
, which decomposes the variance gain of GAI into two interpretable components.

Recall that the primary-only estimator uses only labeled observations, while GAI incorporates auxiliary data through the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]
. To understand the variance gain, we decompose the GLM score
\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y};\bm{\beta}^{*})=\mathbf{X}^{\top}(\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbf{y})
by inserting intermediate quantities.

From the proof of Corollary
5.2
, the asymptotic variance of the primary-only estimator minus that of the GAI estimator is proportional to:

\bm{\Sigma}^{\text{P}}-\bm{\Sigma}^{\text{GAI}}\propto(\frac{1}{\rho}-1)\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbf{X}^{\top}\Big(\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]\Big)\Big(\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]\Big)^{\top}\mathbf{X}\bigg],

(22)

where
\rho=n_{P}/n\in(0,1)
is the fraction of labeled observations. This expression is always positive semidefinite (PSD), confirming that GAI never increases variance relative to primary-only estimation.

The variance reduction in (
22
) is strictly positive definite (i.e., GAI strictly dominates) whenever the inner expectation is positive definite. We decompose the gap
\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]
into interpretable components:

\displaystyle\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Big(\nabla b(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}^{*})-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]\Big)}_{\text{(II) Model misspecification}}+\underbrace{\Big(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]\Big)}_{\text{(III) Extra information in }\mathbf{z}}.

(23)

Since
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X}]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{z}]\,\big|\,\mathbf{X}\big]
by the law of iterated expectations, term (III) has conditional mean zero given
\mathbf{X}
. This implies that the cross-product between terms (II) and (III) vanishes:

\mathbb{E}\Big[\text{(I)}\cdot\text{(II)}^{\top}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\text{(I)}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\text{(II)}^{\top}\mid\mathbf{X}\big]\Big]=0.

Therefore, the variance gain decomposes additively into two non-negative components as stated in Corollary
5.3
.

)
