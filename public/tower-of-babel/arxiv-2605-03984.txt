Flow Sampling: Learning to Sample from Unnormalized Densities via Denoising Conditional Processes
Aaron Havens, Brian Karrer, Neta Shaul

Abstract
Sampling from unnormalized densities is analogous to the generative modeling problem, but the target distribution is defined by a known energy function instead of data samples. Because evaluating the energy function is often costly, a primary challenge is to learn an efficient sampler. We introduce Flow Sampling , a framework built on diffusion models and flow matching for the data-free setting. Our training objective is conditioned on a noise sample and regresses onto a denoising diffusion drift constructed from the energy function. In contrast, diffusion models’ objective is conditioned on a data sample and regresses onto a noising diffusion drift. We utilize the interpolant process to minimize the number of energy function evaluations during training, resulting in an efficient and scalable method for sampling unnormalized densities. Furthermore, our formulation naturally extends to Riemannian manifolds, enabling diffusion-based sampling in geometries beyond Euclidean space. We derive a closed-form formula for the conditional drift on constant curvature manifolds, including hyperspheres and hyperbolic spaces. We evaluate Flow Sampling on synthetic energy benchmarks, small peptides, large-scale amortized molecular conformer generation, and distributions supported on the sphere, demonstrating strong empirical performance.

Introduction

Figure 1
:
Flow Sampling is the first diffusion sampler able to perform sampling from an unnormalized density on Riemannian manifolds. The figure depicts a learned diffusion process using iterative Flow Sampling from a mixture of spherical von Mises–Fisher distributions.

Many problems in the computational sciences require sampling from high-dimensional probability distributions that are specified only up to a normalization constant
(
21
;
50
;
12
)
,

q(x)=\frac{\exp(r(x))}{Z},\quad Z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(r(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}<\infty.

(1)

The target distribution is
q
, and it is defined by the unnormalized log density
r(x)
, which is referred to as the
reward function
in reinforcement learning, or the
negative energy
in physical the sciences, computational chemistry and biology. One can typically evaluate
r(x)
and its gradient
\nabla r(x)
, often at high computational cost, but does not have access to samples from
q
.

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods
(
25
;
49
)
, including Langevin dynamics
(
56
;
55
)
, converge asymptotically to the target distribution but often mix slowly and generate samples sequentially.
This limits their applicability in settings such as materials design
(
12
)
that require fast, reusable samplers across many target instances. This motivates the development of
amortized
sampling methods that replace long sequential simulations with learned sampling dynamics.

In this work, we introduce
Flow Sampling
, a diffusion-based framework for learning amortized samplers directly from unnormalized density functions.
Flow Sampling learns to sample the target distribution using a conditional denoising process constructed from the energy function, such that its marginal distribution matches the target by design.
It utilizes the known interpolant process and a detached state of the model to obtain reusable gradients of the negative energy which reduces considerably the required number of energy function evaluation.
In contrast, existing diffusion-based methods
(
51
;
18
;
3
)
learn to sample the target distribution through Monte Carlo corrections, such as importance weighting, resampling, or auxiliary MCMC. While principled, these approaches rely on stochastic estimation and repeated energy function evaluations, which can significantly increase computational cost.

More recently, diffusion samplers based on stochastic optimal control or Schrödinger bridge problems haved emerged. They learn the diffusion dynamics by optimizing divergences over diffusion path measures
(
65
;
26
;
40
)
. Although theoretically well grounded, this perspective requires characterizing optimal controls or bridges, leading to complex training schemes and auxiliary networks. By building on the diffusion models
(
28
;
60
)
and flow matching
(
38
;
43
;
4
)
formulation, we alleviate these restrictions, resulting in a flexible design space and an easy to implement method. Despite the non-optimal control or coupling, in practice Flow Sampling cuts training cost by at least half on the large-scale amortized molecular conformer generation benchmarks
(
26
)
. Moreover, the Flow Sampling naturally extends to Riemannian manifolds. Similar to Riemannian flow matching
(
17
)
, we replace the affine interpolant with a geodesic interpolant, and furthermore we derive a closed-form formula for the conditional drift on constant curvature manifolds, which includes the hypersphere and hyperbolic spaces.

Our main contributions:

1.

We introduce Flow Sampling, a principled framework for learning
diffusion samplers from unnormalized densities by matching conditional
denoising diffusion drifts.

2.

We derive a simple closed-form regression target that reuses
endpoint energy gradients along the interpolant process, leading to an
efficient replay-buffer training algorithm summarized in
Algorithm

.

3.

We extend diffusion-based samplers to geometries beyond Euclidean spaces. In particular, we derive a closed-form formula for the conditional denoising diffusion drift on constant curvature Riemannian manifolds, including the hypersphere and the hyperbolic spaces.

4.

We demonstrate state-of-the-art performance on synthetic energy
benchmarks and amortized molecular conformer generation, while reducing
simulation training cost by
4
–
8

Preliminaries: Flow Matching

A flow process
\left(X_{t}\right)_{0\leq t\leq 1}
is defined by a
velocity field

v:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[0,1]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and a boundary condition,

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{X_{t}}=v_{t}\left(X_{t}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t},\quad X_{0}\sim p_{0},

(2)

where the
source distribution

p_{0}
is the marginal of the process at time
t=0
. The
probability path

p_{t}
is the marginal of the process at time
0\leq t\leq 1
, and it is given by the
continuity equation
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t}(x)+\nabla\left[p_{t}(x)v_{t}(x)\right]=0.

(3)

Given a dataset of i.i.d. samples from a target distribution
q
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and an easy to sample source distribution
p_{0}
, the goal of flow matching (FM)
(
38
;
43
;
7
)
is to learn a velocity field
v^{\theta}_{t}
such that the marginal of
X_{t}
interpolate between the source and the target, i.e.,

X_{0}\sim p_{0},\quad X_{1}\sim p_{1}\equiv q.

(4)

To learn
v^{\theta}_{t}
, FM uses a supervising flow process conditioned at time
t=1
. Given
X_{1}=x_{1}
, the conditional velocity is,

v_{t|1}(x|x_{1})=\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}\left(x-\alpha_{t}x_{1}\right)+\dot{\alpha}_{t}x_{1}.

(5)

The ODE (
2
) for the conditional flow (
5
) is solved by,

X_{t}=\sigma_{t}X_{0}+\alpha_{t}x_{1},\quad X_{0}\sim p_{0}.

(6)

The marginal of the
interpolant
(
6
)
X_{t}
is the
conditional probability path
that is given by the push forward formula

p_{t|1}(x|x_{1})=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{d}}p_{0}\left(\frac{x-\alpha_{t}x_{1}}{\sigma_{t}}\right).

(7)

Finally, the training objective for the velocity is the FM loss

{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{FM}}\left(\theta\right)=\E\left[\left\lVert v_{t}^{\theta}\left(X_{t}\right)-v_{t|1}\left(X_{t}|X_{1}\right)\right\rVert^{2}\right],

(8)

where
X_{1}\sim q
sample is obtained from the dataset, and
X_{t}\sim p_{t|1}
is sampled using the interpolant (
6
).

Indeed, the generated probability path by the optimal
v^{\theta}_{t}
is

p_{t}(x)=\E\left[p_{t|1}(x|X_{1})\right],

(9)

that equals the source,
p_{0}
and target

q
at
t=0
and
t=1
.

3
Flow Sampling

In contrast to flow matching, we are interested in the data free settings, but our goal is similar. Given a score function of the target
\nabla\log q(x_{1})=\nabla r(x_{1})
, and an easy to sample source distribution
p_{0}
, our goal is to learn a diffusion process
\left(X_{t}\right)_{0\leq t\leq 1}
such that its probability path interpolates between source
p_{0}
and target
p_{1}\equiv q
.

Diffusion process

A diffusion process is defined by a drift
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a diffusion coefficient
g_{t}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and a boundary condition,

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{t}=u_{t}\left(X_{t}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad\ X_{0}\sim p_{0}.

(10)

where
B_{t}
is the Brownian motion. The probability path
p_{t}
of a diffusion process is given by the Fokker–Planck equation,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t}(x)+\nabla\left[p_{t}(x)u_{t}(x)\right]=\frac{g_{t}^{2}}{2}\nabla^{2}p_{t}(x).

(11)

We recall the standard correspondence between deterministic transport and a diffusion with the same probability path.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
v_{t}
be a velocity that defines the flow process (
2
) with a marginal
p_{t}
. Then the drift

u_{t}(x)=v_{t}(x)+\frac{g_{t}^{2}}{2}\nabla\,\log p_{t}(x),

(12)

defines a diffusion process (
10
) with the same marginal
p_{t}
.

Proposition
3.1
is a known result
(
8
)
; we defer the proof to the appendix. This observation makes explicit how score information enters the drift, which is exactly the mechanism we will exploit to inject target information.

Key Idea

Given a data sample
X_{1}\sim q
, the conditional flow (
5
) which is the supervision for the FM loss (
8
), is independent of the target distribution
q
. Thus, all learning signals for the target
q
come through marginalization.

In data-free settings, we don’t have access to data samples
X_{1}\sim q
, hence we cannot marginalize over the target
q
. Our key observation is that we can condition on samples from the source
X_{0}\sim p_{0}
, move all the signal about the target
q
to a supervising denoising diffusion process conditioned at time
t=0
by
X_{0}
, and marginalize over the source
p_{0}
.

We use a conditional probability path defined as a push forward of the target distribution

p_{t|0}\left(x|x_{0}\right)=\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}^{d}}q\left(\frac{x-\sigma_{t}x_{0}}{\alpha_{t}}\right).

(13)

This is in contrast to flow matching and diffusion models
(
28
)
that pushes the source distribution (
7
). Still, in both cases the marginal probability path is the same,

\E\left[p_{t|0}\left(x|X_{0}\right)\right]=p_{t}(x)=\E\left[p_{t|1}\left(x|X_{1}\right)\right].

(14)

The difference between pushing forward the source
p_{0}
or the target
q
is illustrated in Figure
2
. Notably, the former is independent of the target, and the latter is a scale and shift of the target.

Supervising drift
Importantly, we identify two processes that generate the conditional path in (
13
). The first, is defined by a conditional velocity similar to FM (
5
) but conditioned on time
t=0
. That is,

v_{t|0}(x|x_{0})=\frac{\dot{\alpha}_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}\left(x-\sigma_{t}x_{0}\right)+\dot{\sigma}_{t}x_{0},

(15)

which generates the probability path
p_{t|0}
(
13
) since they satisfy the continuity equation (
3
). The second, is defined by the conditional drift

u_{t|0}(x|x_{0}):=v_{t|0}(x|x_{0})+\frac{g_{t}^{2}}{2}\,\nabla\log p_{t|0}\left(x|x_{0}\right),

(16)

which by Proposition (
3.1
), generates the same conditional probability path
p_{t|0}
(
13
).

The two processes are important. The second process, i.e., the drift (
16
) allows to sample
X_{1}\sim q
given
X_{0}=x_{0}
using simulation, and regressing onto the drift
u_{t|0}
suffices to learn a sampler of
q
. The first process, i.e.. the velocity (
15
) is solved in closed form by the interpolant (
6
) and allows efficient reuse of existing samples
X_{1}\sim q
with simulation free sampling of
p_{t|0}
, significantly reducing training cost.

Training objective

Similar to FM, we use the marginalization trick
(
39
)
and train our drift
u^{\theta}
by regressing onto the conditional drift
u_{t|0}
(
16
). This yields a
diffusion matching
(DM) objective

{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{DM}}\left(\theta\right)=\E\left[\left\lVert u^{\theta}(X_{t})-u_{t|0}\left(X_{t}|X_{0}\right)\right\rVert^{2}\right],

(17)

where
X_{0}\sim p_{0}
and
X_{t}\sim p_{t|0}
. The conditional
p_{t|0}
is sampled using the flow process (
2
) defined by
v_{t|0}
(
15
) which can be done efficiently since it is solved by the interpolant,

X_{t}=\sigma_{t}X_{0}+\alpha_{t}X_{1},\quad X_{0}\sim p_{0},\ X_{1}\sim q.

(18)

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X_{0}=x_{0}
, and
v_{t|0}
the conditional velocity (
15
) that generates
p_{t|0}
the conditional probability path (
13
). Then, for every
g_{t}\geq 0
and
X_{1}\sim q
,

\displaystyle u_{t|0}\left(X_{t}|x_{0}\right)=\dot{\alpha}_{t}X_{1}+\dot{\sigma}_{t}x_{0}+\frac{g_{t}^{2}}{2\alpha_{t}}\nabla r\left(X_{1}\right).

(19)

where
X_{t}
is the interpolant (
18
) defined by
v_{t|0}
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
is in Appendix
E
. This proposition is extremely useful, since it implies that for every sample from the target
X_{1}\sim q
, the score
\nabla\,\log q(X_{1})=\nabla\,r(X_{1})
needs to be evaluated only once, to evaluate
u_{t|0}
, the conditional drift (
19
), along
X_{t}
the interpolant (
18
) for all
t\in[0,1]
and
X_{0}\sim p_{0}
. Hence, we can use a
replay buffer

{\mathcal{B}}
to store the pairs
X_{1},\nabla\,r\left(X_{1}\right)
and reuse them during training. This improves training efficiency significantly in cases where the reward is costly to compute.

Linear process

We train our model using the known linear scheduler
(
38
;
42
)
,

\alpha_{t}=t,\quad\sigma_{t}=1-t,\quad g_{t}^{2}=2\gamma t,\quad\gamma>0.

(20)

The squared diffusion coefficient is chosen
g^{2}_{t}\propto\alpha_{t}
to account for the possible singularity at time
t=0
as implied by Proposition (
3.2
). This yields a stable target for the regression, and
\gamma
is a hyper-parameter that is used to further regularize the target during training.

3.1
Diffusion matching with the Flow Sampling loss

Sampling the target
X_{1}\sim q
using simulation with the conditional drift (
16
) requires repeated evaluations of the energy gradient
\nabla r(x)
, which we want to avoid.

Fixed point iteration

To overcome this, we propose a fixed point iteration training algorithm. We defined
X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}
to be samples from the detached current state of the model, i.e.,
\bar{\theta}=\text{stopgrad}(\theta)
, using the Euler–Maruyama algorithm,

X_{t+h}^{\bar{\theta}}=X_{t}^{\bar{\theta}}+hu_{t}^{\bar{\theta}}\left(X_{t}^{\bar{\theta}}\right)+\sqrt{2\gamma th}Z_{t},

(21)

where
Z_{t}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,I\right)
. Then, by minimizing the DM loss (
17
), but sampling
X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}\sim p_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}
instead of
X_{1}\sim q
during training we obtain our flow sampling objective.

Flow Sampling Objective

We train
u^{\theta}_{t}
by regressing onto a closed-form target (
19
), and linear process (
20
). The target distribution is approximated with samples of the current detached state of the model
X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}\sim p_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}
.

{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{FS}}\left(\theta\right)=\E\left[\left\lVert u_{t}^{\theta}(X_{t})-\left(X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}-X_{0}+\gamma\nabla r\large(X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}\large)\right)\right\rVert^{2}\right],

(22)

where
X_{t}
is the linear interpolant process

X_{t}=(1-t)X_{0}+tX_{1}^{\bar{\theta}},\,\,X_{0}\sim p_{0},\,\,X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}\sim p_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}.

(23)

We use the same alternating scheme as Adjoint Sampling
(
26
)
, which consists of two phases:

1.

Exploration phase:
Using the current detached model
u^{\bar{\theta}}
, we draw a batch of samples
X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}\sim p_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}
with the Euler–Maruyama solver, evaluate the score
\nabla r\left(X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}\right)
, and store the pairs in the replay buffer
{\mathcal{B}}\leftarrow{\mathcal{B}}\cup\left\{X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}},\ \nabla r\left(X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}\right)\right\}
.

2.

Optimization phase:
Fixing the state of the replay buffer
{\mathcal{B}}
, we iterate over it
\left\{X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}},\ \nabla r\left(X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}\right)\right\}\sim{\mathcal{B}}
, and perform multiple gradient steps on
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{FS}}\left(\theta\right)
(
22
).

The two phases are repeated until convergence. The pseudo code for our training method is given in Algorithm

, where a Gaussian source
p_{0}={\mathcal{N}}\left(0,I\right)
is assumed. The number of energy gradient
\nabla r(x)
evaluations equals the number of

acquired
during training
(
26
)

4
Extension to Riemannian Manifolds

A strong advantage of our flow sampling formulation for training diffusion samplers
(
2
;
61
;
26
)
is that it naturally extends diffusion samplers to non-Euclidean geometries.

This section describes how to adapt flow sampling to Riemannian Manifolds. Observing our FS loss (
22
) we identify three key requirements: (i) simulation of diffusion process on manifolds
(
30
)
for sampling, (ii) an interpolant process (
18
) on manifolds
(
17
)
, and (iii) a conditional drift (
19
) on manifolds that generate the same marginal as the interpolant.

Setup
Let an ambient space
{\mathcal{A}}=\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
equipped with a metric
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\Sigma}
given by
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
an invertible symmetric matrix. We assume a complete, connected, smooth Riemannian manifold with a constant curvature
\kappa
given by

{\mathcal{M}}=\left\{x\in{\mathcal{A}}\ |\ \left\langle x,x\right\rangle_{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{\kappa}\right\}

(24)

Note, in case
{\mathcal{M}}
has more than one connected component, we treat each one separately. The tangent space
T_{x}{\mathcal{M}}
at a point
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
is defined as

T_{x}{\mathcal{M}}=\left\{w\in{\mathcal{A}}\ |\ \left\langle w,x\right\rangle_{\Sigma}=0\right\}.

(25)

Diffusion on manifolds
As in the Euclidean case, a diffusion process is defined by a drift
u_{t}(x)\in T_{x}{\mathcal{M}}
, and a boundary condition,

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=u_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\gamma t}P^{\perp}_{X_{t}}\circ\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad X_{0}\sim p_{0}.

(26)

where,
B_{t}
is the standard Brownian motion in the ambient space
{\mathcal{A}}
, the diffusion coefficient is
g_{t}:=\sqrt{2\gamma t}
, and
P_{x}^{\perp}
is an orthogonal projection defined for any
x,w\in{\mathcal{A}}
as

P_{x}w=\frac{\left\langle x,w\right\rangle_{\Sigma}}{\left\langle x,x\right\rangle_{\Sigma}}x,\quad P^{\perp}_{x}w=w-P_{x}w.

(27)

Thus,
P^{\perp}_{X_{t}}\circ\mathrm{d}B_{t}\in T_{X_{t}}{\mathcal{M}}
. The marginal of the process
p_{t}
is defined by the Riemannian Fokker-Planck equation

\partial_{t}p_{t}=-\mathrm{div}_{{\mathcal{M}}}(p_{t}u_{t})+\gamma t\Delta_{{\mathcal{M}}}p_{t},

(28)

where
\mathrm{div}_{{\mathcal{M}}}
and
\Delta_{{\mathcal{M}}}
are the divergence and Laplacian operators (resp.) generalized to manifolds.

Lastly, the Euler–Maruyama solver on the manifold is

X_{t+h}=\exp_{X_{t}}\left(hu_{t}(X_{t})+\sqrt{2\gamma th}P^{\perp}_{X_{t}}Z_{t}\right),

(29)

where
Z_{t}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,I\right)
, and
\exp_{x}:T_{x}{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}
is the exponential map of
{\mathcal{M}}
. For our set of constant curvature manifolds (
24
),
the exponential map can be solved in closed form
(
17
)
that is dependent on
\Sigma
and
\kappa
.

Geodesic Interpolant
Given
X_{0}=x_{0}\in{\mathcal{M}}
and
X_{1}\sim q
, similar to Riemannian flow matching (RFM)
(
17
)
, instead of an interpolant (
18
) between
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
, we use the geodesic,

X_{t}=\phi_{t}\left(X_{1}|x_{0}\right)=\exp_{X_{1}}\left[(1-t)\log_{X_{1}}(x_{0})\right],

(30)

where
\log_{x}:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow T_{x}{\mathcal{M}}
is the logarithmic map which can be solved in closed form for the set of manifolds we assumed. The marginal of the geodesic interpolant is the conditional probability path,

p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})=q(\phi_{t|0}^{-1}(x|x_{0}))\,\big|\det\nolimits_{{\mathcal{M}}}D\phi_{t|0}^{-1}(x|x_{0})\big|.

(31)

Conditional drift
Similar to the Euclidean case, we find a conditional drift
u_{t|0}
that generate the conditional path (
31
)
p_{t|0}
. Evaluated at the geodesic interpolant (
30
), the drift is

u_{t|0}(X_{t}|x_{0})=\dot{X}_{t}+\gamma t\nabla_{{\mathcal{M}}}\log p_{t}(X_{t}|x_{0}),

(32)

where
\dot{X}_{t}=\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{t}\left(X_{1}|x_{0}\right)
is the derivative w.r.t. time of the geodesic, and the conditional score decomposes to

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\mathcal{M}}}\log p_{t|0}(X_{t}|x_{0})=

\displaystyle(J_{t}^{-1})^{*}\,\nabla_{{\mathcal{M}}}r(X_{1})

(33)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{{\mathcal{M}}}\log|\det\nolimits_{{\mathcal{M}}}\left(J_{t}\right)|,

(34)

where
J_{t}=D\phi_{t}(X_{1}|x_{0})
is the Jacobian of the geodesic, and
(J_{t}^{-1})^{*}
is its inverse adjoint.

Proposition 4.1
.

For Riemannian manifold
{\mathcal{M}}
defined in (
24
) with a metric
\Sigma
and constant curvature
\kappa
, the Jacobian of the geodesic w.r.t.
X_{1}
is

J_{t}=tT_{X_{1}\rightarrow X_{t}}P_{\dot{X}_{1}}+c_{t}\left(X_{1},x_{0}\right)T_{X_{1}\rightarrow X_{t}}P_{\dot{X}_{1}}^{\perp},

where
T_{X_{1}\rightarrow X_{t}}:T_{X_{1}}{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow T_{X_{t}}{\mathcal{M}}
is the parallel transport,
c_{t}\left(X_{1},x_{0}\right)
is a time dependent scaling,

c_{t}(X_{1},x_{0})=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sin(t\omega_{1}\sqrt{\kappa})}{\sin(\omega_{1}\sqrt{\kappa})}&\text{if }\kappa>0\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{\sinh(t\omega_{1}\sqrt{|\kappa|})}{\sinh(\omega_{1}\sqrt{|\kappa|})}&\text{if }\kappa<0\end{cases},

(35)

and
\omega_{1}=\left\lVert\dot{X}_{1}\right\rVert_{\Sigma}
the geodesic distance of
X_{1}
and
x_{0}
.

Proposition (
4.1
) allows us to compute the conditional drift (
32
) in closed form. As it is composed of rank-1 matrices, it is efficient to evaluate. Its proof is based on results in Jacobi fields
(
37
)
, and given in Appendix
E
.

Riemannian Flow Sampling objective

The model
u_{t}^{\theta}
is trained by regressing onto
u_{t|0}
the conditional drift (
32
) that is given in closed form in Proposition
4.1
.

{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{RFS}}\left(\theta\right)=\E\left[\left\lVert P_{X_{t}}^{\perp}u_{t}^{\theta}\left(X_{t}\right)-u_{t|0}\left(X_{t}|X_{0}\right)\right\rVert^{2}_{\Sigma}\right]

(36)

where
X_{t}
is the geodesic interpolant (
30
), and the target distribution is approximated with
X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}\sim p_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}
samples of the current detached state of the model using the Riemannian Euler-Maruyama solver (
29
). Optimization is done using the adjusted to manifold version of Algorithm
1
.

4.1
Flow sampling on a hyper-sphere

As an example, we consider the case of a hyper-sphere
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathbb{S}}^{d}
given by setting the metric and the curvature to

\Sigma=I,\quad\kappa=1.

(37)

Given
X_{0}=x_{0}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
, and
X_{1}\sim q
, the geodesic distance is

\omega_{1}=\left\lVert\log_{X_{1}}\left(x_{0}\right)\right\rVert=\arccos\left(X_{1}^{\top}x_{0}\right).

(38)

The derivative w.r.t. time of the geodesic interpolant (
30
) at time
t=1
is

\dot{X}_{1}=-\log_{x_{1}}\left(x_{0}\right)=\omega_{1}\frac{\cos{\omega_{1}}X_{1}-x_{0}}{\sin{\omega_{1}}},

(39)

and, the geodesic interpolant (
30
) is the SLERP function,

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=\cos\left({(1-t)\omega_{1}}\right)X_{1}-\sin\left((1-t)\omega_{1}\right)\frac{\dot{X}_{1}}{\omega_{1}},

(40)

and its derivative w.r.t. to time
t
is

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}\sin\left({(1-t)\omega_{1}}\right)X_{1}+\cos\left((1-t)\omega_{1}\right)\dot{X}_{1}.

(41)

The parallel transport from
X_{1}
to
X_{t}
on the hyper-sphere is the Householder reflection about the midpoint
X_{1}+X_{t}
,

T_{X_{1}\rightarrow X_{t}}=I-2P_{X_{1}+X_{t}}.

(42)

Since the parallel transport
T_{X_{1}\rightarrow X_{t}}
is an orthogonal operator, using Proposition
4.1
the inverse Jacobian adjoint is

\left(J_{t}^{-1}\right)^{*}=\frac{1}{t}T_{X_{1}\rightarrow X_{t}}P_{\dot{x}_{1}}+\frac{\sin\left(\omega_{1}\right)}{\sin\left(t\omega_{1}\right)}T_{X_{1}\rightarrow X_{t}}P_{\dot{X}_{1}}^{\perp}.

(43)

This implies the correction to the conditional score is

\nabla_{{\mathcal{M}}}\log\det\left(J_{t}\right)=(d-1)\left(t\cot{t\omega_{1}}-\cot{\omega_{1}}\right)\frac{\dot{X}_{1}}{\omega_{1}}.

(44)

Hence we have all terms in the conditional drift (
32
) in closed form. Importantly, both the projection operator (
27
) and the parallel transport operator (
42
) include only rank 1 operators, thus are computationally efficient.

Related Works

Learning-Augmented Classical Samplers
MCMC and SMC methods provide asymptotically exact samples from unnormalized distributions, but can mix slowly or require many target evaluations in high-dimensional, multimodal settings. Learning-augmented variants
replace hand-designed proposals with normalizing flows or learnable
stochastic dynamics
(
5
;
9
;
23
;
45
;
6
;
29
)
, typically requiring importance weighting or
accept–reject corrections that drive up energy-evaluation cost.

Diffusion Samplers
Diffusion samplers adapt score matching to unnormalized targets via
auxiliary corrections (MCMC, importance weighting, or resampling), each
inflating energy-evaluation cost
(
51
;
18
;
16
)
.
PITA
(
1
)
trains a temperature-annealed ladder of
diffusion samplers, but requires high-temperature MCMC data to
bootstrap. Off-policy variants
(
44
;
54
)
use trajectory-level objectives over a replay buffer
but typically require parameterizing the model in terms of the energy
gradient
(
27
)
. iDEM
(
3
)
instead
estimates the target score by Monte Carlo along the noising path,
incurring many energy evaluations per step to control variance.

Stochastic Optimal Control and Adjoint Sampling
A closely related line of work formulates sampling from unnormalized
distributions as a stochastic optimal control problem, closely
connected to Schrödinger bridges
(
64
;
62
)
, leading to bridge-based diffusion samplers and
transport objectives
(
13
;
54
;
63
;
16
;
26
;
40
)
.
(
47
)
further incorporates molecular inductive bias through well-tempered collective-variable biasing.
Flow Sampling is inspired by this line of work but departs from SOC
formulations by avoiding optimal couplings, yielding a simpler
training objective that supports non-memoryless noise schedules and
arbitrary source distributions without requiring an additional corrector network. In Appendix
D
we show that with a Brownian-bridge
supervising path and matching Gaussian source, the Flow Sampling and
Adjoint Sampling regression targets agree in conditional expectation.

Reward-tilting and Fine-Tuning
RL-style fine-tuning methods such as GRPO
(
59
)

and Flow-GRPO
(
41
)
optimize a pretrained generative
model toward high-reward samples via policy gradients, without
explicitly targeting a particular distribution. A complementary line
instead targets the reward-tilted distribution—the base reweighted by
an exponential reward.
Concurrent to our work, Tilt Matching
(
52
)

regresses a velocity field that anneals from a base toward the tilted
target using only reward evaluations, while Meta Flow
Maps
(
53
)
amortize one-step posterior sampling
across intermediate noise levels to enable steering and off-policy
fine-tuning.

6.4
Von Mises-Fisher Mixtures on
\mathbb{S}^{2}

Directional and rotational data arise in applications such as robotics and graphics, and are naturally represented on spherical domains
(
15
;
14
)
. To validate the Riemannian Flow Sampling framework developed in
Section

4.1
, we consider sampling on the two-dimensional unit sphere

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{2}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\|x\|_{2}=1\}.

We construct a multimodal target distribution given by a mixture of von Mises–Fisher (vMF)
(
11
)
components, the spherical analogue of Gaussians, with density

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{vMF}}(x)\propto\exp(\kappa\,\mu^{\top}x)

parameterized by mean directions
\mu\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and concentration
\kappa\geq 0
.
We then construct a mixture distribution

q(x)\propto\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\,f_{\mathrm{vMF}}(x;\mu_{k},\kappa_{k}),

(45)

where the mixture components are evenly weighted (
w_{k}=1/K
) and the mean directions
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are chosen to lie along the coordinate axes and diagonal directions of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Using the closed-form conditional drift derived in
Section

4.1
, we apply iterative Flow Sampling directly on the manifold in ambient space without reparameterizations. As shown in Figure
1
, the learned diffusion accurately recovers the multimodal target density, demonstrating that Flow Sampling extends naturally beyond Euclidean spaces and can operate directly on curved geometries.

6.5
Discussion on model efficiency

Training computational cost per a single exploration, followed by a training phase
Algorithm

1
is composed of three primary components: i) Number of energy gradient evaluations ii) Number of function evaluations (NFE (Train)) to sample
X_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}\sim p_{1}^{\bar{\theta}}
, and iii) number of optimization steps. Regarding i) i.e. number of energy gradient evaluations, AS
(
26
)
, ASBS
(
40
)
and Flow Sampling are equally efficient. Regarding iii), AS and Flow Sampling are performing 100 gradient updates per optimization stage. However, since ASBS requires fitting an auxiliary corrector network which doubles the cost of optimization and results in about
\times 2
gradient updates per optimization stage. Most importantly, ii) the NFE to acquire a sample from the model using the Euler-Maruyama solver.

Tables
2
and
3
show that Flow Sampling maintains consistent performance across metrics even when substantially reducing NFE (Train).
In particular, reducing NFE from 512 to 64 on conformer generation in
Table

3
potentially cuts the exploration-phase cost by
\times 8
relative to the 512-NFE baseline, which dominates the training cost.
Furthermore, we observe strong degradation in the low-NFE regime for the baselines: ASBS on Ala2 in
Table

2
and AS on conformer generation in
Table

3
.
In contrast, Flow Sampling remains robust in both settings, indicating that the efficiency of the exploration phase significantly improves the scalability of the method for larger models or datasets.

7
Conclusion

We introduce Flow Sampling, a principled method for learning to sample from unnormalized densities by matching conditional denoising diffusion drifts (
19
). We present a simplified formulation for training diffusion-based samplers, and provide a pytorch pseudo-code in Algorithm
1
. We extend diffusion samplers to non-Euclidean geometries, where we provide closed-form formulas for the conditional drift on constant curvature Riemannian manifolds. We validate our Flow Sampling method empirically, observing strong results in the synthetic-energy dataset as shown in Table
1
and on small peptides in
Table

2
. On the amortized conformer generation benchmark reported in Table
3
, our Flow Sampling demonstrates a
4\times
–
8\times
speedup in simulation training, potentially enabling a considerable increase in scale of diffusion-based samplers.

Limitations
Flow Sampling is trained as a fixed-point procedure: since target samples
are unavailable, the ideal conditional diffusion-matching objective is
approximated using samples from the detached current sampler. This is
effective empirically, but we do not yet provide global convergence
guarantees for the resulting replay-buffer dynamics. Finally,
our Riemannian extension relies on closed-form conditional drifts for
constant-curvature manifolds; extending the same efficiency to more general
manifolds and constrained spaces remains an important direction.

Impact Statement

This work is primarily intended for scientific and industrial research applications and does not introduce immediate risks of misuse. Nevertheless, as with advances in generative modeling more broadly, care should be taken to ensure responsible deployment, especially in application domains such as drug discovery and materials science, where modeling biases or inaccuracies could influence downstream decisions.

Flow Sampling has direct relevance to computational chemistry, including molecular modeling and conformer generation, and may contribute to accelerating research in drug development, materials engineering, and biophysical modeling.

Appendix A
Synthetic Energy Experiment Details

A.1
Double Well Potential (DW-4)

We use the same double-well potential as in iDEM
(
3
)
, which was originally proposed in
35
. DW-4 describes a pair-wise distance potential energy for a system of 4 particles
\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\}
, where each particle has 2 spatial dimensions
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

(d=8)
. The potentials analytical form is given by:

E(x)=\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{ij}a(d_{ij}-d_{0})+b(d_{ij}-d_{0})^{2}+c(d_{ij}-d_{0})^{4},\quad d_{ij}=\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert_{2}

(46)

where we set
a=0
,
b=-4
,
c=0.9
and temperature
\tau=1
.

A.2
Lennard-Jones Potential (LJ-13, LJ-55)

Similarly to DW-4, the Lennard-Jones potential is also based pair-wise distances of a system with
n
particles, but each each particle has 3 spatial dimensions. Its analytical form is given by

E^{\text{LJ}}(x)=\frac{\epsilon}{\tau}\sum_{ij}\left(\left(\frac{r_{m}}{d_{ij}}\right)^{6}-\left(\frac{r_{m}}{d_{ij}}\right)^{12}\right),\quad d_{ij}=\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert_{2}

(47)

where
r_{m},\tau,\epsilon
and
c
are physical constants. As in
35
and
3
, we add the additional a harmonic potential:

E^{\text{osc}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\|x_{i}-x_{\text{COM}}\|^{2}

(48)

where
x_{\text{COM}}
refers to the center of mass of the system. Therefore, the final energy is then

Geometric

Because our energy and diffusion process is invariant to rotational and permutations symmetries, we also take these symmetries into account when measuring distance between generated and ground truth point clouds (e.g., from long run MCMC). Note this
\mathcal{W}_{2}
metric is different from what is reported in

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\left(\inf_{\pi}\int\pi(x,y)d(x,y)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(50)

where
\pi
is the transport plan with marginals constrained to
\mu
and
\nu
respectively. Here the distance metric

takes into account all point-cloud symmetries, and is obtained by minimizing the squared Euclidean distance over all possible combinations of rotations, reflections (

d(x_{0},x_{1})\approx\min_{R\in SO(d)}\|x_{0}-(R\otimes\tilde{P})x_{1}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\tilde{P}=\arg\min_{P\in\mathbb{S}(k)}\|x_{0}-Px_{1}\|_{2}^{2}.

(52)

(

\mathcal{W}_{2}(E_{\mu},E_{\nu})=\left(\inf_{\pi}\int\pi(x,y)|x-y|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{y}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

Appendix D
Connection to Adjoint Sampling via Control Variates

In this section we relate the Adjoint Sampling
(
26
)
target to the target obtained
from Flow Sampling when the supervising path is defined using the same
Brownian bridge pair law. The comparison is made at the target endpoint
law. In particular, we take
X_{1}\sim q
, construct the Brownian bridge
interpolant used by Adjoint Sampling, and apply Flow Sampling to the
conditional path induced by this interpolant. The resulting Flow Sampling
target differs from the Adjoint Sampling target by a samplewise control
variate whose conditional expectation given
X_{t}
is zero.

Adjoint Sampling considers the controlled SDE

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\sigma(t)u(X_{t},t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad X_{0}=0.

(60)

The base process is obtained by setting
u\equiv 0
, with marginals

\displaystyle p_{t}^{\mathrm{base}}=\mathcal{N}(0,\nu_{t}I),\qquad\nu_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(61)

For a target
q(x)\propto e^{r(x)}
, the terminal cost used by Adjoint Sampling, corresponding the optimal control formulation, is

\displaystyle g(x)=\log p_{1}^{\mathrm{base}}(x)-r(x).

(62)

In this zero-base-drift sampling setting, the
Adjoint Sampling regression target for the control is

\displaystyle-\sigma(t)\nabla g(X_{1}).

(63)

Supervising Process
We now define the Flow Sampling supervising path using the same Brownian
bridge pair law. Given
X_{1}\sim q
, let

\displaystyle X_{t}=\lambda_{t}X_{1}+s_{t}\varepsilon,\qquad\lambda_{t}:=\frac{\nu_{t}}{\nu_{1}},\qquad s_{t}^{2}:=\frac{\nu_{t}(\nu_{1}-\nu_{t})}{\nu_{1}},\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).

(64)

Conditioned on
\varepsilon
, the map
X_{1}\mapsto X_{t}
is
deterministic and invertible for
t\in(0,1)
, and therefore pushes

q
forward to the conditional path

\displaystyle p_{t|\varepsilon}(x|\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{t}^{d}}q\!\left(\frac{x-s_{t}\varepsilon}{\lambda_{t}}\right).

(65)

Applying Flow Sampling to this conditional path gives a control target

u^{\mathrm{FS}}_{t|\varepsilon}
. The result below shows that this
target has the same conditional regression signal as the Adjoint Sampling
target in (
63
).

Theorem D.1

(Flow Sampling recovers the Adjoint Sampling target)
.

Let
X_{t}=\lambda_{t}X_{1}+s_{t}\varepsilon
be the Brownian bridge
interpolant in (
64
), with
X_{1}\sim q
, and
let
u^{\mathrm{FS}}_{t|\varepsilon}(X_{t}|\varepsilon)
be the Flow
Sampling control target associated with the conditional path
(
65
). Then, for every
t\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\E\left[u^{\mathrm{FS}}_{t|\varepsilon}(X_{t}|\varepsilon)\,\middle|\,X_{t}=x\right]=\E\left[-\sigma(t)\nabla g(X_{1})\,\middle|\,X_{t}=x\right].

(66)

Consequently, the two samplewise targets define the same least-squares
regression target as functions of
X_{t}
.

Proof.

We first compute the Flow Sampling target for the conditional path
(
65
). Along the interpolant,

\displaystyle v_{t|\varepsilon}(X_{t}|\varepsilon)=\dot{\lambda}_{t}X_{1}+\dot{s}_{t}\varepsilon,\qquad\nabla\log p_{t|\varepsilon}(X_{t}|\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{t}}\nabla r(X_{1}).

(67)

Using

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}_{t}=\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}}{\nu_{1}},\qquad\dot{s}_{t}=\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}(1-2\lambda_{t})}{2s_{t}},

(68)

the Flow Sampling physical drift target is

\displaystyle b^{\mathrm{FS}}_{t|\varepsilon}(X_{t}|\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=v_{t|\varepsilon}(X_{t}|\varepsilon)+\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}}{2}\nabla\log p_{t|\varepsilon}(X_{t}|\varepsilon)

(69)

\displaystyle=\sigma(t)^{2}\left[\frac{X_{1}}{\nu_{1}}+\frac{1-2\lambda_{t}}{2s_{t}}\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{t}}\nabla r(X_{1})\right].

(70)

Since the controlled SDE (
60
) is parameterized by
the physical drift
\sigma(t)u(X_{t},t)
, we compare targets at the level
of the control:

\displaystyle u^{\mathrm{FS}}_{t|\varepsilon}(X_{t}|\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sigma(t)}b^{\mathrm{FS}}_{t|\varepsilon}(X_{t}|\varepsilon)

(71)

\displaystyle=\sigma(t)\left[\frac{X_{1}}{\nu_{1}}+\frac{1-2\lambda_{t}}{2s_{t}}\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{t}}\nabla r(X_{1})\right].

(72)

We now compare this with the Adjoint Sampling target. Since

p_{1}^{\mathrm{base}}=\mathcal{N}(0,\nu_{1}I)
, the terminal cost
(
62
) satisfies

\displaystyle-\sigma(t)\nabla g(X_{1})

\displaystyle=-\sigma(t)\nabla\left(\log p_{1}^{\mathrm{base}}(X_{1})-r(X_{1})\right)

(73)

\displaystyle=\sigma(t)\left[\frac{X_{1}}{\nu_{1}}+\nabla r(X_{1})\right].

(74)

Subtracting (
74
) from
(
72
) gives

\displaystyle u^{\mathrm{FS}}_{t|\varepsilon}(X_{t}|\varepsilon)-\bigl(-\sigma(t)\nabla g(X_{1})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma(t)(1-2\lambda_{t})}{2}\left[\frac{\varepsilon}{s_{t}}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{t}}\nabla r(X_{1})\right].

(75)

Thus the two targets differ pointwise by the term on the right-hand side.
We now show that this term vanishes after conditioning on
X_{t}
.

Fix
X_{t}=x
. The conditional density of
X_{1}\sim q
given
X_{t}=x
under the Brownian bridge law is given by,

\displaystyle p(X_{1}=x_{1}\mid X_{t}=x)

\displaystyle\propto q(x_{1})\exp\!\left(-\frac{\left\lVert x-\lambda_{t}x_{1}\right\rVert^{2}}{2s_{t}^{2}}\right).

(76)

Moreover, under this conditional law,

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\frac{x-\lambda_{t}X_{1}}{s_{t}}.

(77)

Applying Stein’s identity
(
31
)
to
(
76
) gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\E\left[\nabla_{X_{1}}\log p(X_{1}\mid X_{t}=x)\,\middle|\,X_{t}=x\right]

(78)

\displaystyle=\E\left[\nabla r(X_{1})+\frac{\lambda_{t}}{s_{t}^{2}}(x-\lambda_{t}X_{1})\,\middle|\,X_{t}=x\right]

(79)

\displaystyle=\E\left[\nabla r(X_{1})+\frac{\lambda_{t}}{s_{t}}\varepsilon\,\middle|\,X_{t}=x\right].

(80)

Dividing by
\lambda_{t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\E\left[\frac{\varepsilon}{s_{t}}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{t}}\nabla r(X_{1})\,\middle|\,X_{t}=x\right]=0.

(81)

Combining (
75
) with
(
81
) proves
(
66
).

Finally, least-squares regression onto functions of
X_{t}
depends only
on the conditional expectation of the samplewise target. Therefore
(
66
) implies that the Flow Sampling target
and the Adjoint Sampling target define the same regression target as
functions of
X_{t}
.
∎

This result is a target-law comparison. In the practical fixed-point
training procedures used by both methods, the endpoint samples are obtained
from the current detached sampler and are reused through a replay buffer.
The calculation above explains why, when the endpoint law has reached the
target distribution, the Brownian bridge Flow Sampling target reduces to
the same conditional regression signal as Adjoint Sampling, with the
remaining sample-wise difference acting as a zero-mean control variate.

for every

. We show that the Jacobian can be decomposed to a tangential field and a normal field. Importantly, the Jacobi equation (
99
) takes a simpler form in case

is a tangential field or

is a normal field. Indeed

\displaystyle\left\langle\gamma^{\prime}(s),\gamma^{\prime}(s)\right\rangle_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\left\langle T_{\gamma(0)\rightarrow\gamma(s)}\gamma^{\prime}(0),T_{\gamma(0)\rightarrow\gamma(s)}\gamma^{\prime}(0)\right\rangle_{\Sigma}

Hence uniqueness implies,

Second we consider the normal directions. Let
