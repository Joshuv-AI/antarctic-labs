Eye Darkening Under Dehydration Stress in the Neotropical Frog Boana punctata (Schneider, 1799)
Authors: Ricardo Reques, Eduardo F Schaefer, Eduardo A Sanabria, Leonardo D Aguiar, Lorena B Quiroga, Miguel Tejedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAmphibians face significant dehydration challenges due to their permeable skin and weak water retention mechanisms, especially under ongoing global warming that predicts increasing land dryness. This makes the identification of physiological stress indicators crucial to assessing hydric risk in amphibian populations. This study examines iris darkening as a response to dehydration in the neotropical tree frog (Boana punctata) under controlled conditions. Twenty‐five adult frogs were collected in the province of Chaco, Argentina, and subjected to sequential dehydration treatments (10, 20, and 30 min) using two methods (constant airflow of 0.2 m/s or desiccant rice), followed by 2‐h rehydration. Linear mixed‑effects models revealed a strong negative relationship between hydration level and iris darkening, increasing about 1.5% in dark area for each 1% loss in hydration level, consistent across dehydration methods and with similar sensitivity across body sizes and individuals, although larger frogs showed lower baseline darkening. Iris darkening showed partial reversal post‐rehydration. This validates iris darkening as a robust, non‐invasive biomarker for hydric stress in wild amphibian populations facing climate‐driven aridity.Keywords: amphibians, Boana punctata, dehydration stress, iris pigmentation, neotropical anurans, non‐invasive physiological marker

Introduction

The highly permeable skin and limited water retention capacity make terrestrial amphibians strongly dependent on external water sources, exposing them to greater risks of dehydration compared to other terrestrial organisms. This vulnerability is especially concerning under global warming scenarios, which predict increased intensity and frequency of droughts, enhancing land dryness and escalating water stress in amphibian populations (Bovo et al. 2018). Dehydration impairs amphibian locomotor performance, thermal tolerance, plasma volume, and endocrine function (e.g., corticosterone) (Hillman 2018; Ontivero et al. 2024; Greenberg and Palen 2021; Narayan 2013), though partially offset by lymphatic mechanisms and behavioral adaptations (Anderson et al. 2017).

Iris darkening represents a relatively unexplored response to hydric stress in amphibians, although it has been reported in fish as an indicator of physiological stress (Freitas et al. 2014). Understanding amphibian iris chromatic variation may therefore provide valuable insights into the health and adaptive capacity of amphibians under environmental changes. The amphibian iris contains chromatophores, primarily melanophores and xanthophores, which store pigments such as melanin, pteridines, and carotenoids (Bagnara et al. 1969). Variation in pigment type, abundance, and distribution influences iris colouration and modulates the amount of light reaching the retina (Nishioka 1977). Melanin dominates anuran iris pigmentation (posterior epithelium; enhances visual acuity by light absorption; Bagnara et al. 1969; Aho 1996), while pteridines/carotenoids respond to hormonal/environmental cues (Bagnara et al. 1969; Freitas et al. 2014). Iris changes occur via rapid physiological or slower morphological mechanisms (Nilsson Sköld et al. 2013).

Here, we provide the first experimental assessment of dehydration‐induced iris darkening in an amphibian species, the Neotropical frog Boana punctata, using two methods (constant airflow or desiccant rice), and its reversibility following rehydration. We further discuss its potential role as a phenotypic adaptation and its application as a non‐invasive biomarker for monitoring amphibian population dehydration stress in the context of climate change and increasing hydric stress.

Material and Methods

Study Species, Animal Collection, and Husbandry

B. punctata is a widely distributed hylid occurring throughout the Amazon basin, extending southward into eastern Paraguay and along the Paraguay‐Paraná river system to Argentina (Figure 1) (IUCN SSC Amphibian Specialist Group 2023), where it is mostly associated with riparian habitats and aquatic environments of the Humid Chaco. The species has a prolonged reproductive season and typically aggregates in the shoreline of large water bodies, most often near rivers, from lowland regions to 1400 m a.s.l. (Brunetti et al. 2015). During December 6, 2024, and January 15, 2026, a total of 25 adult B. punctata were collected from El Moncholo lagoon, Las Palmas, Chaco Province, Argentina (−27.057457° S; −58.694446° W, 56 m a.s.l.; Figure 1). Specimens were transported to the laboratory within 1 h of capture, identified, and kept individually in plastic containers (1 L volume) with a moist paper substrate at a constant room temperature of 20°C and a 12L:12D photoperiod. The Environmental Secretariat and biodiversity of the Province of Chaco granted the permit of collection (Resolution No. 164/23). Experiments follow the Ethical Principles in Animal Research guidelines and were approved by the Consejería de Agricultura, Pesca, Agua y Desarrollo Rural de la JJAA (ref. 4‐01‐2025‐001).

Dehydration Protocol

Individuals were weighed throughout the dehydration process using an OHAUS Scout SKX 123 EU balance (precision 0.001 g). All frogs began in a fully hydrated state (FH). Dehydration was induced using two experimental methods. In the Wind method (N = 13), individuals were placed inside PVC wind tunnels (300 mm length, 100 mm diameter), fitted with 100 mm diameter fans controlled by an Arduino module to maintain constant airflow (0.2 m s−1; ~0.3 m3 s−1 total flow). In the Rice method (N = 12), frogs were placed in PVC containers (25 cm diameter × 12 cm depth) filled with unused commercial dry rice taken from sealed packages, ensuring consistent low‐humidity conditions across trials. Individuals were enclosed within rubber mesh boxes (0.8 mm pore size) to prevent direct contact with the desiccant while allowing exposure to low humidity conditions. Each individual underwent three sequential dehydration intervals of 10, 20, and 30 min (D1, D2, D3), with body mass recorded at the end of each interval. Following dehydration, frogs were rehydrated for 2 h (RH) in their individual containers lined with water‐saturated blotting paper.

Percentage body water loss (BWL) was calculated as:

BWL = [(MFH − Mt)/MFH] × 100,

where MFH is body mass in the fully hydrated state, and Mt is body mass at each dehydration interval.

Iris Imaging and Analysis

Digital images of the right eye were obtained at FH, after each dehydration treatment (D1–D3), and after rehydration (RH), using a Nikon D80 digital SLR camera fitted with a Sigma 50 mm f/2.8 EX Macro lens. Images were captured under standardized conditions: frogs were positioned 10 cm from the camera with a millimetric scale included in the frame. Illumination was provided by a 10 W cold LED lamp diffused through 75 g m−2 A4 paper placed 1 m from the subject to minimize glare and ensure homogeneous lighting. Iris pigmentation was quantified in ImageJ (Schneider et al. 2012). The iris area (excluding the pupil) was manually delineated and converted to 8‐bit grayscale. A uniform intensity threshold for dark tones was applied across all images, and the percentage of dark pixels was calculated for each individual at each dehydration treatment. This threshold was defined in ImageJ using a fixed grayscale cutoff selected from a representative subset of images.

Statistical Analysis

Iris darkening (percentage of dark area) was analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models to account for repeated measurements. Hydration level (percentage body hydration) was treated as a continuous fixed effect, dehydration method (Wind vs. Rice) as a between‐subject fixed factor, and individual identity as a random intercept to account for non‐independence of repeated observations. Because hydration level directly captures the physiological state under investigation, it was treated as the primary continuous predictor rather than the experimental stage (FH, D1–D3, RH), allowing a mechanistic evaluation of the relationship between body water status and pigmentation dynamics independent of categorical treatment structure.

The primary model was specified as:

Area ∼ Hydration × Method + (1∣Individual)

To evaluate potential size‐dependent effects, body mass at each hydration level was incorporated as a covariate. Extended models included:

Area ∼ Hydration × Method + Mass + (1∣Individual),

and

Area ∼ Hydration × Mass + Method + (1∣Individual).

Model selection was based on likelihood ratio tests and Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC). Fixed effects were evaluated using Type III ANOVA with Satterthwaite's approximation of degrees of freedom. Marginal and conditional R 2 values were calculated to quantify variance explained by fixed effects alone and by the full model, respectively. To assess inter‐individual variation in dehydration sensitivity, we fitted a model including random slopes for hydration:

Area ~ Hydration × Method + (Hydration|Individual).

This model was compared to the random‐intercept‐only model using likelihood ratio tests and AIC. Variance associated with random slopes was examined to quantify heterogeneity in individual responses.

Post hoc pairwise comparisons between methods within treatment levels were conducted using estimated marginal means (emmeans package; Lenth et al. 2018). All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2 (R Core Team 2025) using the packages lme4, lmerTest, emmeans, and performance, within the RStudio environment (RStudio Team 2025). Finally, hydration percentages across experimental stages (FH, D1, D2, D3, RH) and methods were summarized using boxplots. Within each method, differences between FH and each subsequent treatment were evaluated using paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests. Data are available in Reques et al. (2026).

Results

Hydration Effects and Methodological Validation

Iris darkening was strongly and negatively associated with hydration level (F 1, 121.6 = 130.71, p < 0.001). The fitted model estimated a 1.44% to 1.51% increase in darkened iris area per 1% loss in hydration, consistent with the progressive changes observed across sequential dehydration stages (Figure 2). These results confirm a robust physiological response of iris pigmentation to body water loss. Dehydration method (Wind vs. Rice) did not significantly affect iris darkening F 1, 123.1 = 1.02, p = 0.314, nor was the Hydration × Method interaction significant (F 1, 121.6 = 0.58, p = 0.450). Estimated slopes were comparable between methods (Wind: −1.51 ± 0.16 SE; Rice: −1.32 ± 0.20 SE), indicating consistency of the hydration–pigmentation relationship across experimental procedures (Figure 3). The model explained a substantial proportion of variance (marginal R 2 = 0.535; conditional R 2 = 0.619), underscoring the strong predictive contribution of hydration level on iris darkening (Table S1).

Lines show fitted relationships from the linear mixed‐effects model, including individual as a random intercept. Iris darkening increased significantly with decreasing hydration (p < 0.001). Slopes did not differ between methods (Hydration × Method interaction: p = 0.45), indicating consistent physiological responses across experimental approaches. Hydration values > 100% correspond to post‐rehydration measurements (RH), when body mass slightly exceeded the fully hydrated reference (FH = 100%).

Body Mass Effects

Body mass differed between cohorts (Wind: 0.70 ± 0.093 g; Rice: 1.32 ± 0.116 g; p < 0.001) and explained inter‐individual variation in darkening (F 1, 35.6 = 11.74, p = 0.0015), with larger frogs showing lower baseline pigmentation (Figure S1; Table S2). Baseline pigmentation refers to the iris darkening measured in fully hydrated frogs (FH) prior to any dehydration treatment. However, mass did not interact with hydration (F 1, 120.3 = 0.65, p = 0.42), indicating size‐independent dehydration sensitivity.

Inter‐Individual Variation in Dehydration Sensitivity and Method × Treatment Effects

Allowing individual‐specific hydration slopes did not improve model fit relative to the random‐intercept model (ΔAIC = +3.6; χ 2 = 0.40, df = 2, p = 0.82), and variance in slopes was negligible (σ 2 = 0.004). These results suggest limited inter‐individual heterogeneity in the hydration–pigmentation relationship. When hydration was treated categorically (Treatment × Method), the interaction was significant (F 4, 96 = 3.45, p = 0.010). Iris darkening was greater in the Wind treatment relative to Rice during D1 (t = 2.93, p = 0.004), D2 (t = 3.26, p = 0.001), D3 (t = 2.76, p = 0.007), and RH (t = 5.96, p < 0.0001), but not under fully hydrated conditions (t = 0.45, p = 0.652; Figure S2). These differences remained after controlling for body mass (extended model marginal R 2 = 0.58).

Hydration Dynamics Across Treatments

Hydration declined progressively across dehydration stages in both methods. In the Wind method (n = 13), hydration decreased from 100.0 ± 0.0% (FH) to 90.8 ± 3.8% at (D1), 83.9 ± 4.1% (D2), and 77.7 ± 3.7% (D3) (Figure S3). In the Rice method (n = 12), declines were less pronounced: 100.0 ± 0.0% (FH), 90.7 ± 6.0% (D1), 87.6 ± 5.6% (D2), and 84.9 ± 5.7% (D3). Rehydration restored hydration to near baseline levels in both groups (Wind: 100.0 ± 5.7% at RH; Rice: 98.9 ± 9.1% at RH). Within‐method paired Wilcoxon tests confirmed significant dehydration effects in the Wind treatment at all stages (FH–D1, FH–D2, FH–D3: all p = 0.0017). In the rice method, differences were significant from D2 onwards (FH–D2 and FH–D3: p = 0.0313), whereas FH‐D1 was marginal (p = 0.0625). Rehydration did not differ significantly from baseline in either method. Overall, Wind induced greater water loss at D2 and D3 relative to Rice, confirming the effectiveness of both procedures while demonstrating differences in desiccation intensity (Figure S3).

Discussion

The strong negative association between hydration state and iris pigmentation in B. punctata demonstrates that dehydration is tightly coupled to ocular melanophore dynamics. In amphibians, color change is mediated by the dermal chromatophore unit—an integrated system of xanthophores, iridophores, and melanophores under endocrine control. Melanophores regulate reflectance via intracellular redistribution of melanosomes, which mask iridophores and reduce light scattering (Bagnara et al. 1969). The linearity and magnitude of the response observed here indicate that iris darkening is consistent with activation of this conserved chromatophore system in response to hydric stress.

Dehydration activates the HPI axis and corticosterone release in amphibians (Barsotti et al. 2018). Pituitary α‐MSH mediates pigment dispersion via melanosome redistribution in melanophores (Smith 1920; Hadley and Bagnara 1969; Nilsson Sköld et al. 2013; Bagnara et al. 1969), providing a plausible mechanistic link between hydric stress and chromatophore activation. The iris darkening observed here is consistent with this endocrine‐mediated redistribution of melanosomes within melanophores. Comparative evidence supports evolutionary conservation of this mechanism. In the fish Oreochromis niloticus, iris darkening increases under confinement and air exposure and functions as a reliable stress indicator (Freitas et al. 2014). The occurrence of a comparable response in anurans is consistent with a shared vertebrate pathway linking stress physiology and ocular pigmentation. Amphibian hydrothermal physiology is strongly constrained by water balance (Hillman 2018; Greenberg and Palen 2021), and dehydration alters fluid composition, water flux (Anderson et al. 2017), and locomotor capacity (Ontivero et al. 2024). Ocular tissues are also sensitive to hydric stress in vertebrates, and measurable evaporative water loss through the eye has been documented in reptiles (e.g., Mautz 1980), underscoring the susceptibility of the visual system to dehydration. However, to our knowledge, no iris darkening has been reported in non‐avian and avian reptiles attributable to dehydration. These vertebrates exhibit sunken eyes, dry eye spectacles in Xanthusiidae lizards (Mautz 1980), or socially mediated eye closure (Lanham and Bull 2004) to lessen water loss through the eyes. Within this integrative physiological context, iris darkening appears as a rapid and quantifiable component of the coordinated dehydration response. Although the mechanistic basis of iris darkening under dehydration appears clear, its ecological or adaptive significance remains unknown. No functional advantage has been demonstrated to date, and the response may simply reflect endocrine activation under hydric stress. At present, its main ecological relevance lies in its potential use as a non‑invasive indicator of dehydration in wild amphibian populations.

Partial reversal post‐rehydration (not fully to baseline in 2 h) indicates dynamic regulation with time‐dependent kinetics, consistent with chromatophore dynamics and sustained endocrine signaling (Freitas et al. 2014; Nilsson Sköld et al. 2013). Body mass explained part of the inter‐individual variation in pigmentation magnitude (Table S2) but did not influence the hydration–pigmentation slope. This distinction indicates that body size influences baseline optical properties—potentially via structural or geometric factors—without modifying physiological sensitivity to dehydration. Thus, the mechanistic coupling between water loss and melanophore activation appears size‐independent.

Method‐specific differences in absolute darkening levels likely reflect variation in dehydration intensity rather than differences in physiological responsiveness. The Wind method induced greater water loss than Rice at later stages, producing correspondingly higher pigmentation levels. However, the slope of the hydration–pigmentation relationship remained consistent across methods, reinforcing the conclusion that iris darkening scales predictably with body water status irrespective of desiccation pathway.

Notably, we detected negligible inter‐individual variation in hydration slopes, indicating a relatively canalized response across individuals. The combination of low slope heterogeneity and high explanatory power strengthens the case for iris darkening as a standardized physiological biomarker that does not require individual calibration. In contrast to endocrine markers such as circulating corticosterone—which often exhibit substantial inter‐individual variability (Narayan 2013)—digital quantification of ocular pigmentation provides a rapid, non‐invasive, and reproducible phenotypic proxy for hydric stress, facilitating field monitoring applications.

Hydration trajectories confirmed that both dehydration protocols generated a clear gradient of water loss while allowing near‐complete recovery after rehydration. The parallel recovery of hydration state and iris pigmentation further supports a physiological link rather than being solely explained by handling or experimental duration. Consistency across desiccation approaches strengthens the causal interpretation that ocular melanophore activation tracks internal water balance.

However, direct endocrine measurements will be necessary to confirm the proximate hormonal mechanisms underlying this response. Nevertheless, the broader implications remain relevant given the hydrothermal sensitivity of amphibians and projected increases in climatic variability (Greenberg and Palen 2021; Bovo et al. 2018). Rapid, non‐invasive indicators of hydric stress are increasingly valuable for both experimental and field‐based assessments. Future studies should directly quantify endocrine mediators (e.g., corticosterone and α‐MSH), examine melanophore redistribution histologically, and test the generality of this response across taxa differing in ecology and water‐balance strategies. Dehydration‐induced iris darkening in B. punctata—rapid, reversible, highly consistent, and linearly tied to hydration with low inter‐individual variance—represents a practical, conserved biomarker of hydric stress for amphibians.
