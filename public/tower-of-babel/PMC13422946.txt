Reaching young and low-income people for sexual healthcare in Zurich, Switzerland: perspectives from healthcare workers – a qualitative study
Authors: Leonie Arns-Glaser, Elea Widrig, Vasileios Nittas, Jan S Fehr, Benjamin Hampel, Andrea Farnham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFree voluntary counselling and testing (VCT) interventions improve access to HIV and sexually transmitted infections prevention, particularly for underserved populations. Financial barriers are widely recognised as a key deterrent to testing uptake in vulnerable populations. In 2023, Zurich (Switzerland) introduced a pilot programme offering free VCT for young people and low-income residents.ObjectivesThis study explores healthcare professionals’ (HCPs) experiences with implementing the programme, with a focus on perceived benefits, barriers and opportunities for improvement.DesignWe conducted a qualitative descriptive study based on semistructured interviews with HCPs.SettingTwo clinics participating in a city-wide pilot programme providing free counselling and testing in Zurich, Switzerland.Participants12 HCPs (60% response rate) from the two participating clinics were interviewed.OutcomesHCPs’ perceived benefits of the programme, barriers to uptake, underserved populations and recommendations for improvement.ResultsHCPs viewed the programme as an effective entry point into sexual healthcare for young people and first-time testers, particularly due to the removal of financial barriers. Counselling was seen as its most important component, promoting knowledge-sharing and early engagement in preventative healthcare. While the programme successfully reached well-informed, highly educated individuals, key high-risk groups, including vocational students, conservative individuals, asylum seekers and those under 18, remained underserved. Reported barriers included stigma, misinformation and long appointment waiting times. HCPs recommended improved outreach to vocational schools, more inclusive educational materials and mobile testing initiatives to improve accessibility.ConclusionsHCPs consider free VCT programmes a critical tool for equitable access to sexual healthcare. However, to maximise their reach and impact, targeted, context-sensitive strategies are needed to engage underserved and high-risk populations more effectively.

Background

Voluntary counselling and testing (VCT) is a key strategy in HIV and sexually transmitted infection (STI) prevention. Sexual health counselling has been linked to positive sexual health outcomes among adolescents and emerging adults, including reductions in risky sexual behaviours and increased HIV awareness.1 Early engagement with such services during adolescence shapes long-term health behaviours,2 highlighting VCT’s role in prevention and behavioural changes.1

While these benefits are well-established, access to VCT remains uneven across many high-income settings. Fees continue to reduce testing uptake in several European countries,3 and even where services are free, concerns about stigma, confidentiality and fear of disclosure persist, especially in marginalised or rural communities.4 5

Healthcare professionals’ (HCPs) insights as frontline implementers are critical in identifying and addressing practical and contextual barriers to care. Their understanding of local populations can guide more responsive and culturally sensitive services.5 6 For VCT to be effective, access must be consistently accessible and sustainably integrated into routine care.6 The insights of HCPs are essential for achieving responsive and sustainable sexual healthcare. To our knowledge, in high-income settings, existing research has mainly evaluated uptake after free VCT was available, while HCPs’ perspectives on implementing free VCT remain underexplored.7

In the city of Zurich, Switzerland, a pilot project offering free VCT for HIV and STIs was launched to improve access for young people and low-income groups. This initiative represents a targeted effort to reduce financial and structural barriers in a high-income setting, with the potential to inform broader integration of equitable sexual health services.7 This study explores the perspective of HCPs implementing the programme, with a focus on perceived impact, implementation barriers and opportunities for improving access to sexual healthcare. Specifically, we ask: (1) How do HCPs view the impact and utility of the project for clients? (2) Which populations remain underserved, and why? (3) What strategies could improve outreach to low-income populations at the highest risk of negative sexual health outcomes? (4) What policy recommendations are voiced by HCPs?

Methods

Study design

We conducted a qualitative descriptive study using semistructured interviews to explore HCPs’ perspectives on the implementation of a free HIV/STI VCT programme in Zurich, Switzerland. This approach was selected to capture the lived experiences of providers and identify practical barriers and facilitators to implementation. Reporting follows the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines (SRQR) (see online supplemental materials S1).8

Study setting and recruitment

This study was conducted as part of a pilot project providing free VCT for HIV, syphilis, chlamydia, gonorrhoea and hepatitis C to Zurich residents under 26 years old and low-income individuals (as determined by eligibility for a social discount programme). The service was delivered beginning in June 2023 at two community-based sexual health clinics: Checkpoint Zurich, which is specialised in care for Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Transgender, and Intersex (LGBTI+) populations, and Test-In, which focuses on the general public. During the first year of the pilot project, the free VCT service attracted a predominantly young clientele, with a median (IQR) age of 24 (22–26) years; 84% of visits were by people aged under 26 years who qualified based on age, while the remaining 16% qualified through low income. Just under half (46%) of attendees were assigned female at birth, and the largest demographic group were women having sex with men (43%). The majority (67%) were born in Switzerland or Liechtenstein. Notably, 39% of visits in the first year were by individuals with no prior HIV testing history.7

We used a total population sampling approach. All HCPs directly involved in the pilot—either administering VCT or conducting client intake—were invited via email and staff meetings. Participation was voluntary and counted as paid working time.

A total of 12 HCPs participated in the study, with eight based at Checkpoint and four at Test-In. Participants’ median age was 34.5 years (range: 22–52). Nine had a medical background and three had a background in social and educational fields. At Checkpoint Zurich, 8 of 16 eligible HCPs (50%) participated, and all 4 HCPs at Test-In (100%) took part, resulting in a sample of 12 participants, reflecting an overall response rate of 60%.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted one-on-one in private rooms between December 2023 and January 2024 by a single interviewer (LA-G), using a semistructured guide (online supplemental materials S2). Interviewer’s characteristics and potential influences are listed in the online supplemental materials S3. On average interviews were 45 min (range: 29–57 min) and were audio-recorded on participants’ consent. Recordings were translated from Swiss German dialects into Standard German and transcribed verbatim using Töggl (töggl.ch), an Automatic Speech Recognition-based transcription tool designed for Swiss German dialects. EW and LA-G verified the transcriptions against the audio recording and made corrections where necessary.

The interview guide was developed based on literature, project update meetings with stakeholders and HCPs, and piloted with an HCP working in the field. It included open-ended questions on the perceived benefits and utility of the pilot programme, population groups reached or under-reached, barriers to access and suggestions for improving implementation. During each interview, participants completed two semiquantitative sorting tasks to rank (1) the population groups most in need of VCT and (2) perceived barriers to VCT uptake (see online supplemental materials S4). At two separate points during the interview, participants were shown (1) a set of cards with predefined population groups based on the literature and (2) a set of predefined barrier categories based on the literature and experience, and asked to order the cards vertically, with higher order indicating greater perceived importance. The sorting task was intended to complement the qualitative interview data by providing a structured overview of which groups and barriers HCPs considered most relevant. Results were summarised descriptively by displaying the distribution of relevance groups across categories and the distribution and mean ranking of selected hurdles. Rankings were documented with photographs on participants’ consent. These photographs were used as the primary record of the completed task outputs: final category placements and rank orders were extracted into a tabular dataset, aggregated across participants and analysed descriptively to generate figure 1A,B.

Public and patient involvement and engagement

Patients were not involved in the design, recruitment or conduct of this study, which focused on HCPs’ perspectives during the implementation of a policy-driven pilot. Findings are being shared with stakeholders involved in the programme.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using thematic analysis following a general inductive approach9 and using MAXQDA24.10 Two researchers (EW and LA-G) conducted the initial open coding of all transcripts independently. VN then led the thematic synthesis, working collaboratively with EW and LA-G to sort and merge these initial codes into broader conceptual categories. This iterative process resulted in a final coding framework that was re-applied to the entire dataset by EW and LA-G. These codes were then grouped into higher-order interpretative themes used to structure the narrative results, while table 1 presents the broader conceptual frameworks. Data frame analysis was carried out by AF, EW and LA-G. Discrepancies in coding and theme interpretation were resolved through discussion. Quotations used in the results section were translated into English and lightly edited to improve readability while preserving the original meaning by LA-G (fluent in both languages).

Results

Table 1 presents coding frameworks and representative quotations underpinning the analysis. For readability, the narrative results are organised under four higher-order themes: perceived benefits and utility, populations reached and gaps in reach, barriers to access and recommendations for improving implementation. The most frequently coded codes were ‘Consultation’ and ‘Recruiting and Outreach’, both under the ‘Implementation’ theme (coded 73 and 55 times, respectively), as well as ‘Entry Point’ (coded 69 times) and ‘Broader Access’ (coded 59 times), both under the Benefits theme.

Perceived benefits and utility of the programme

Entry into sexual healthcare

HCPs viewed the free VCT programme as an effective entry point into sexual healthcare, with many emphasising how cost removal encouraged access among young people who might not otherwise seek testing (quote 1, quote 6).

I think it’s a very useful project. I think we reach many, many people, who otherwise would not have come to get tested.

Quote 1: Test-in, interview 5

For many clients, the visit was their first exposure to in-depth, non-judgemental sexual health education (quote 2).

[…] for the young people who just need a place to come and talk. Sometimes they are at a loss, maybe they were unfaithful, maybe they experience diffuse symptoms. […] They just want to talk to someone. They are overwhelmed, if they have something they can’t figure out and can’t find an answer.

Quote 2: Test-in, interview 6

While testing initially drew clients in, the educational discussions enabled knowledge-sharing that extended beyond the individual (quote 3, quote 5).

[…] the actual sex education really only happens with us. […].

Quote 3: Checkpoint, interview 12

Several HCPs expressed initial scepticism about low-risk clients seeking testing, but most ultimately saw this as a positive opportunity for early engagement and long-term health awareness (quote 4).

A very positive impact of the project is that people have some kind of contact to the healthcare system, to sexual health that was positive for the people. Because, after all, we often find ourselves in stressful or unpleasant situations where we might have to come back to such a place, whether it’s for PEP [HIV post-exposure prophylaxis], sexual violence, or anything else that happens in life. If you already have our clinic as a place linked to a positive experience, it’s less stress-inducing to come to us or also just to know there are other places than the hospital [where one can go].

Quote 4: Checkpoint, interview 3

Peer-to-peer knowledge sharing

Beyond increasing access, HCPs observed that the programme contributed to broader awareness and peer-to-peer information sharing. Word-of-mouth promotion, especially among well-connected groups such as international students and young women, was reported to be a key driver of uptake (quote 5, quote 6, quote 7).

VCT clients are always also multipliers of knowledge. The question is how much of the information they got was internalised after the consultation […] I estimate about 10% sticks with them. I always try to give a go home message that they hopefully spread a bit [among their peers]. This works better with people that are perceived as female than people perceived as male.

Quote 5: Checkpoint, interview 12

I mean, I think a lot of people are now being nudged to do it, people who wouldn’t have thought of it on their own. You know, because it’s plastered all over the place and being advertised everywhere. And then it’s also being talked about among female friends.

Quote 6: Test-in, interview 5

And then suddenly there is a wave of Asian [international] students, where you know word about the free testing pilot got around in that community.

Quote 7: Test-in, interview 6

Clients also often reported discovering the service via Google or social media (quote 8).

Many say they found out about it [free testing pilot] via social media.

Quote 8: Test-in, interview 11

Gendered patterns in uptake and health-seeking behaviour

In both clinics, HCPs noted that many heterosexual men were encouraged by their female partners to get tested (see quote 9 and quote 10), suggesting that young women may act as key nodes in these informal information networks (see quote 5).

In men, it is usually the case that their girlfriend sends them (laughs) or someone else like that.

Quote 9: Checkpoint, interview 2

HCPs noted gendered patterns in health-seeking behaviour. Women were described as more socialised to attend preventative health visits (e.g., gynaecology visits) (quote 15), whereas heterosexual men had lower sexual health literacy and rarely accessed preventative healthcare services (quote 10). The free VCT programme was seen as a low-threshold opportunity to engage this group.

[…] among men, it’s about fifty-fifty, specifically among men without an immigrant background. I’d say roughly 50% are reasonably well-informed or are encouraged by their female partners to get tested. That’s actually the biggest factor […]. They’re really missing even the most basic information, and these are also the people when I ask them, “Do you have a family doctor? Do you get your vaccinations and preventive checkups regularly?”, who always just have blank looks on their faces […]. When it comes to general health awareness, we can’t really make up for all that with a project like this.

Quote 10: Checkpoint, interview 12

Populations reached and gaps in reach

Groups well reached

HCPs reported that the programme effectively reached students, individuals with university education and socially connected youth. However, several groups were perceived as under-represented despite potentially high need.

But if I am in my everyday life and you see the fifth hetero cis woman who only had one boyfriend and never had sex outside her relationship, I get the feeling this is not why I am [working] here. […] Then I ask myself, where are the teenage boys towards whom I am more compassionate, whom I am also more secure in counselling. Where are the 17 years old boys who just have come out. I don’t think they end up here.

Quote 11: Checkpoint, interview 7

Underserved groups

Young people under 18, particularly those identifying as queer, were rarely seen at Checkpoint, despite the clinic’s traditional focus on LGBTQI+ clients. HCPs speculated that existing targeted programmes for men who have sex with men (MSM) and sex workers may explain their lower-than-expected presence.

Yes, they [young men who have sex with men] are somewhat difficult to reach. I find it striking that so few MSM, who are at high risk, actually come. We might reach some of them through our campaigns in May and November, which are specifically targeted at them. However, it is challenging to determine whether these efforts are effectively addressing the gap, perhaps they are.

Quote 12: Checkpoint, interview 4

Individuals with more conservative worldviews, those not in education or training and people in low-wage jobs were described as largely absent from the programme. Vocational school students were particularly difficult to reach, with outreach efforts largely unsuccessful.

There is an incredibly high social barrier to going to a health clinic […] to talk about their sexuality or about risks. These are people you encounter way, way outside the gay bubble, people who haven't been reached by prevention efforts at all.

Quote 13: Checkpoint, interview 12

[…], I wrote to 18 vocational schools […] and I didn't hear back from any of these 18 vocational schools.

Quote 14: Test-in, interview 9

This pattern of under-reached young people and those with lower education also emerged in the sorting task (see figure 1A).

Semiquantitative ranking findings

In the semiquantitative sorting activity, HCPs ranked asylum seekers, individuals from sub-Saharan Africa and those with no formal education as the least reached but highest-need groups (see figure 1A).

Barriers to access

Personal barriers

HCPs noted that many young people had difficulty accurately assessing their sexual health risks. Some avoided testing due to low perceived risk, while others attended despite very limited sexual experience. This mismatch occasionally led to questions about the programme’s utility but was also viewed as an opportunity for early education.

I believe the biggest hurdle, where I still see it in many, is the lack of awareness of their risk. So, I think a lot of people who actually have a very high risk don’t estimate it as high at all, and many people who perhaps have no risk or a very low risk estimate it rather very high. […] People who are generally open about their sexualities, illnesses, bodies are again mostly female read people, as they are trained from a young age to go to the gynaecologist, talk about the pill […]

Quote 15: Checkpoint, interview 12

The semiquantitative sorting exercise showed that lack of sexual education was the second most important barrier to accessing VCT services after financial concerns. This prioritisation was consistent among HCPs at both sites and further supported by the semiquantitative sorting task (see figure 1B), as emphasised by the high importance HCPs gave to the sexual education they provide (see quote 2 and quote 3). This reinforces the finding that, for many clients, meaningful sexual health education ‘really only happens’ during the VCT consultation. Accordingly, lack of risk awareness was discussed as one of the biggest hurdles (see quote 15).

Other highly ranked barriers identified in the semiquantitative sorting activity included embarrassment, fear of a positive result and lack of motivation. Language barriers were also frequently noted as an important omission from the sorting task.

Structural barriers

Structural barriers such as opening hours and long waiting periods were ranked as less critical in the sorting activity, which focused on prepilot conditions (see figure 1B). Nonetheless, several HCPs raised the issue of the current waiting times of up to 2 months as problematic, particularly for people with urgent needs.

[…] if you have a free offer but no available slots at all, that kind of doesn’t make sense…

Quote 16: Checkpoint, interview 7

Living with family was another barrier for younger clients, who feared parents discovering testing through insurance or mail correspondence (quote 17).

The one thing most [of the young clients] ask, is if we send anything to their home. […] Depending a bit on their cultural background, but the fear of the parents finding out and being ashamed is there […]

Quote 17: Checkpoint, interview 10

While universities were receptive to outreach, vocational schools were not. HCPs also mentioned that some individuals not eligible for the social discount programme still faced financial struggles. They observed that individuals qualifying for free testing through the social discount programme were often older students or artists, rather than individuals in low-income employment (see quote 18).

They are often artists who seem to be very open people who have points of contact with the [queer] community. […] Yes, they are most often artists or students.

Quote 18: Checkpoint, interview 8

Recommendations for improving implementation

Outreach and access points

HCPs suggested a range of strategies to improve reach and impact. These included expanding outreach through mobile testing in locations such as schools, bars, clubs and public spaces where MSM meet for anonymous sex, and creating more anonymous, less queer-coded VCT spaces to attract individuals with more traditional mindsets. Schools were viewed as promising venues for deep prevention among youth, especially those from conservative households.

We already do mobile testing at parties or other events. I feel like we should also do this in schools.

Quote 19: Checkpoint, interview 8

They [conservative people] would have to be more educated and open towards [sexual healthcare]. If you reach them in schools, it would be less shameful. At home this topic is not talked about at all.

Quote 20: Checkpoint, interview 10

Communication materials and literacy

Additionally, HCPs recommended revising informational materials to be more inclusive of heteronormative populations and women (see table 1, Implementation, ID 3). MSM-focused materials were seen as potentially overwhelming or off-putting to other groups.

Increase STI awareness in the medical community

Moreover, HCPs noted that general practitioners and gynaecologists often did not address STIs adequately. One recommendation that they had was for gynaecologists to test for chlamydia more extensively (quote 21, table 1, facilitators/motivators, ID 6).

In my opinion, gynaecologists should test more systematically, unless the woman says she is in a long-term monogamous relationship, okay, but with everyone else, why not test for chlamydia every time? They are already swabbing them. […] Or why don’t general practitioners take a swab first and only then prescribing them a course of antibiotics?

Quote 21: heckpoint, interview 4

Capacity and service organisation

Several HCPs expressed a desire for shorter appointments or other process improvements to increase daily capacity without compromising quality of care, noting that reducing the initial appointment times by 5 min already allowed for one additional appointment per day (table 1, implementation, ID 11).

Discussion

This study explored the perspectives of HCPs involved in the implementation of a free HIV/STI VCT programme in Zurich, Switzerland.7 HCPs generally perceived the programme as highly beneficial in increasing access to sexual health services, particularly in young, well-connected populations. Counselling was viewed as a core component, with potential to improve sexual health literacy and encourage peer knowledge-sharing. However, several high-need populations including younger adolescents, individuals from conservative or migrant backgrounds and those in low-income employment were perceived as under-represented. Barriers to access included stigma, limited sexual health education and logistical challenges such as long waiting times.

These findings align with existing literature from other high-income settings, where free STI testing reduces financial barriers but such services struggle to reach marginalised groups.13 Word-of-mouth and social media effectively attracted young people, students and women, while heterosexual men and adolescents under 18 remained harder to engage.4 14–17 The observed gender differences reflect prior studies showing women’s greater engagement with preventative care compared with men, who require targeted outreach.18 19

Consistent with previous implementation research, HCPs reported that the counselling and information-sharing aspects of the intervention were particularly beneficial, not only for individual education but also to diffuse sexual health knowledge through informal social networks.20 A quantitative evaluation of the same pilot supports the importance of the education component, highlighting that people who had a VCT consultation perceived improvements in their sexual health knowledge and valued the non-judgemental discussions about sexuality.7 Additionally, HCPs noted that early positive experiences with sexual healthcare could reduce stress and encourage individuals to return during future crises. This aligns with literature suggesting that familiarity with healthcare providers and the clinical environment fosters increased health-seeking behaviour and consistent service utilisation,1 21–25 including easier discussions around sensitive topics such as HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis and ongoing engagement with sexual healthcare.26 27

The perspectives of the HCPs in this study highlight the critical role of free VCT services in promoting equitable access and reducing stigma around sexual healthcare in order to reach national and international goals of STI reduction and HIV elimination.28 29 However, as HCPs noted, access alone is not sufficient. To improve reach, strategies should include mobile testing, culturally tailored outreach and increased presence in vocational schools and non-academic youth environments.

The findings also suggest the need for broader institutional efforts to normalise sexual healthcare within mainstream primary care and educational settings. HCPs repeatedly noted the absence of sexual health conversations in general practice and gynaecological care, pointing to a system-wide gap in preventative sexual health. Programmes like this VCT programme can bridge the gap, but sustainable impact will require integration into routine care structures, broader community engagement and ongoing efforts to destigmatise STI testing across all social groups.3 30 31 HCPs offered recommendations focused on increasing visibility, addressing stigma and improving sexual health literacy. Strengths and limitations

Key strengths include diverse HCP representation (counselling, administration, outreach) across both pilot sites, providing comprehensive implementation perspectives. Triangulating qualitative interviews with semiquantitative activities enriched the analysis. However, limitations include potential reduced generalisability due to single urban location and two testing centres. Using a single interviewer, while ensuring consistency, may have introduced subjective bias during probing. Additionally, social desirability bias may have influenced responses about the successful implementation of the programme or under-reporting challenges they are facing. The findings represent HCPs’ subjective perspectives, an inherent qualitative study limitation.

Conclusions

Free VCT programmes enhance access to sexual healthcare among young people and first-time testers, introducing many to sexual healthcare services for the first time. However, targeted outreach, structural adaptation and inclusive communication strategies are essential to expand reach and reduce inequities. Future interventions must specifically target young and low-income groups with culturally and contextually appropriate strategies, integrating deep prevention within schools and communities. These insights should inform the refinement and scaling of free testing initiatives in Switzerland and other high-income contexts seeking to meet national and global sexual health goals.
