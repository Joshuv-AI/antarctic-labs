Geometric helices on del Pezzo surfaces from tilting
Pierrick Bousseau

Abstract
. We prove that all geometric helices in the derived category of coherent sheaves on a del Pezzo surface are related by a sequence of elementary operations: rotation, shifting, orthogonal reordering, derived dualization, tensoring by a line bundle, and tilting. As a consequence, any two non-commutative crepant resolutions of the affine cone over a del Pezzo surface are related by mutations. The proof relies on a geometric interpretation of tilting operations as cluster transformations acting on toric models of a log Calabi–Yau surface mirror to the del Pezzo surface.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation and main result

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface over
\mathbb{C}
and
D(Z)
denote its bounded derived category of coherent sheaves. Let
n
be the rank of the Grothendieck group
K(Z)
of
Z
, so that
n=\rho+2
, where
\rho
is the Picard rank of
Z
.
A
geometric helix
on
Z
, in the sense of
[
14
]
, is a sequence of objects
\mathbb{H}=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
in
D(Z)
satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, the corresponding thread
(E_{i+1},\ldots,E_{i+n})
is a full exceptional collection,

(ii)

E_{i+n}=E_{i}\otimes\omega_{Z}^{-1}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(iii)

for all
i<j
,
\operatorname{Hom}^{k}(E_{i},E_{j})=0
unless
k=0
.

A thread
\mathbb{E}=(E_{i+1},\dots,E_{i+n})
in a geometric helix is called a
very strong
exceptional collection.

Geometric helices and very strong exceptional collections are of particular interest because they provide an algebraic description of the derived category
D(X)
of the non-compact Calabi–Yau 3-fold
X
given by the total space of the canonical line bundle of
Z
. Indeed, let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a very strong exceptional collection on
Z
, and let
\pi:X\rightarrow Z
be the natural projection.
Setting
T:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\pi^{\star}E_{i}
, the algebra
B(\mathbb{E}):=\mathrm{End}(T)
is a

CY_{3}
algebra isomorphic to the path algebra of a quiver with potential
(Q,W)
. Moreover, there is an equivalence

\Phi_{\mathbb{E}}:D(B(\mathbb{E}))\xrightarrow{\sim}D(X)\,,

where
D(B(\mathbb{E}))
is the bounded derived category of finitely-generated
B(\mathbb{E})
-modules – see

[
14
, §3.3]
and
[
51
, §4.2]
. Descriptions of
D(X)
in terms of quivers with potential play an important role in both enumerative geometry
[
3
,
2
,
8
,
13
]
and theoretical physics
[
4
,
5
,
16
,
17
,
35
,
34
]
.

Geometric helices on del Pezzo surfaces always exist, but they are far from unique.
Starting from a geometric helix

\mathbb{H}
, one can construct many others by applying standard operations in the derived category, such as rotation, shifting, orthogonal reordering, derived dualization, and tensoring by a line bundle; see §
4.3
for details. These operations do not change the associated algebra
B(\mathbb{E})
or the quiver with potential
(Q,W)
. More interesting are the
tilting operations
introduced by Bridgeland–Stern
[
14
]
, building on earlier work of Herzog
[
35
]
in the theoretical physics literature. Tilting operations act non-trivially on the associated algebra
B(\mathbb{E})
and induce combinatorial mutations of the quiver with potential.
The main result of this paper, stated as Theorem
5.19
in the main body, shows that tilting operations, together with the basic operations above, suffice to connect any two geometric helices. In particular, any two quivers with potential associated to geometric helices on a del Pezzo surface are related by mutations.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface. Any two geometric helices on
Z
are related by a sequence of the following operations:
rotation, shifting, orthogonal reordering, derived dualization, tensoring by a line bundle, and tilting.

1.1

R
-module, such that
\Lambda
is Cohen-Macaulay as

.
For a del Pezzo surface

, consider the Gorenstein ring

. Geometrically,

is a non-commutative crepant resolution of

for every very strong exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}
on

correspond to mutations of non-commutative crepant resolutions. Recently, Nordskova proved

Consequently, Theorem

Corollary 1.2

Let

1.2.
Structure of the proof

Let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a very strong exceptional collection on
Z
. To prove Theorem
1.1
we consider the dual exceptional collection
\mathbb{F}=(F_{n},\dots,F_{1})
and the corresponding basis
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})=\{[F_{i}]\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
of
K(Z)
. Tilting operations on the helix generated by
\mathbb{E}
induce seed mutations on
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
, viewed as a seed in the sense of cluster algebras. We prove that the seeds
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
are of
q
-Painlevé type in the sense of
[
47
]
. In particular,
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
determines a lattice
polygon in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, referred to as a T-polygon
– see §
2.5
.

A deep combinatorial result of Kasprzyk–Nill–Prince
[
43
]
classifies T-polygons up to mutations. A simpler and more conceptual proof of this result was later obtained by Lutz
[
45
]
using a factorization result in the birational geometry of log Calabi–Yau surfaces
[
30
, Proposition 3.27]
–
[
11
, Theorem 1]
. The relevant log Calabi–Yau surfaces are constructed from the seeds
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
via an explicit blow-up construction of toric surfaces, following the work of Gross–Hacking–Keel
[
27
]
on the birational geometry of cluster algebras. These log Calabi–Yau surfaces are classically related to del Pezzo surfaces by mirror symmetry
[
1
,
7
,
18
,
26
,
29
,
45
]
.
The factorization result
[
30
, Proposition 3.27]
–
[
11
, Theorem 1]
shows that different presentations of a log Calabi–Yau surface as blow-ups of toric varieties are related by a sequence of elementary cluster mutations.

Using the classification of T-polygons up to mutations, we reduce the problem to the following question. Let

\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
and
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}=(E_{1}^{\prime},\dots,E_{n}^{\prime})

be two very strong exceptional collections with the same ranks
r(E_{i})=r(E_{i}^{\prime})
and the same anticanonical degrees
d(E_{i})=d(E_{i}^{\prime})
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Then, we must show that
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
are related by a sequence of elementary transformations.

To do this, we show that the seed
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
and
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
are related by an element of the orthogonal group of
Z
, defined as the group of isometries of
K(Z)
with respect to the Euler form that preserve both the rank and the anticanonical degree. In Proposition
3.10
, we show that
O(Z)
admits a semi-direct decomposition
O(Z)=W(Z)\ltimes\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
, where
W(Z)
is the Weyl group generated by the reflections in the
(-2)
-curve classes, and
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
is the group of line bundles orthogonal to the canonical divisor.

To conclude the proof, we establish the existence of an action of the Weyl group
W(Z)
, and more generally of an affine Weyl group
\widehat{W}(Z)
, on very strong exceptional collections by tilting operations and orthogonal reordering. This result is obtained as a special case of a more general result on the geometry of log Calabi–Yau surfaces, Theorem
2.11
, which identifies a combinatorial Weyl group defined in terms of seed mutations with a geometric Weyl group defined in terms of
(-2)
-curve classes on log Calabi–Yau surfaces.
The factorization result
[
30
, Proposition 3.27]
–
[
11
, Theorem 1]
again plays a crucial role in the argument.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
In §
2
, we review the correspondence between seed mutations and the geometry of log Calabi–Yau surfaces, and prove
in Theorem
2.11
the general comparison between combinatorial and geometric Weyl groups.
In §
3
, we study the Weyl group, the affine Weyl group, and the orthogonal group of a del Pezzo surface
Z
, all acting by isometries on
K(Z)
. In particular, we establish the semi-direct decomposition
O(Z)=W(Z)\ltimes\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)

in Proposition
3.10
.
In §
4
, we review geometric helices, very strong exceptional collections, and tilting operations. Finally, in
§
5
, we prove that the seeds
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
associated to very strong exceptional collections are of
q
-Painlevé type, and we conclude the proof of the main result, Theorem
5.19
.

1.3.
Related works

1.3.1.
Mutations of exceptional collections

For historical reasons, the word mutation is used in two distinct contexts: for exceptional collections and for quivers. These two notions are not compatible in general. As discussed above, tilting of very strong exceptional collections on del Pezzo surfaces correspond to quiver mutations. As reviewed in §
4.3
, tilting operations can be interpreted as particular sequences of mutations of exceptional collections that preserve the very strong condition. In contrast, individual mutations of exceptional collections do not generally preserve this property. Kuleshov–Orlov
[
44
, Theorem 7.7]
proved that any two full exceptional collections of sheaves on a del Pezzo surface are related by a sequence of mutations – see also
[
24
, §6]
for an exposition of the proof.
This differs from Theorem
1.1
, which concerns connecting very strong exceptional collections via tilting operations. Moreover, our proof of Theorem
1.1
does not rely on
[
44
, Theorem 7.7]
– see Remark
5.12
.

1.3.2.
Hille–Perling polygons and Hacking bundles

In this paper, we associate to each very strong exceptional collection on a del Pezzo surface a seed of

q
-Painlevé type, and hence a T-polygon in

\mathbb{R}^{2}
. An alternative construction, due to Hill–Perling
[
36
,
49
]
, also assigns a T-polygon to a very strong exceptional collection.
The two constructions are not obviously the same. In particular, the T-polygons arising from the Hille–Perling construction are defined only up to the action of
GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
, whereas for our purposes it is essential that our T-polygon is defined without this ambiguity. Nevertheless, we expect that the two constructions produce T-polygons that agree up to the action of
GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
. Furthermore, it follows from
[
43
,
45
]
that T-polygons modulo

GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
are in one-to-one correspondence with

\mathbb{Q}
-Gorenstein degenerations of del Pezzo surfaces to toric Fano surfaces. This suggests a natural connection between our results and Hacking’s construction of exceptional vector bundles via degenerations to toric surfaces
[
31
,
32
,
52
,
53
]
. Finally, T-polygons and their mutations also play a significant role in the symplectic geometry of del Pezzo surfaces

[
20
,
54
,
55
]
.
We leave the exploration of these questions for future work.

1.3.3.
Seiberg duality in physics

Quivers with potential describing the derived category
D(Z)
of a del Pezzo surface
Z
appear naturally in theoretical physics. For instance, they describe the four-dimensional
\mathcal{N}=1
superconformal field theories living on a stack of D3 branes placed at the tip of the cone over the del Pezzo surface, which are related via the AdS/CFT correspondence to IIB string theory on
\mathrm{AdS}_{5}\times X_{5}
, where
X_{5}
is a five-dimensional Sasaki-Einstein manifold
[
35
,
34
]
. In another direction, these quivers with potential define supersymmetric quantum mechanics that describe BPS particles in the four-dimensional
\mathcal{N}=2
theories obtained from M-theory on
\mathbb{R}^{4}\times S^{1}\times X

[
16
,
17
]
.
Tilting operations on geometric helices, that is, quiver mutations, correspond to Seiberg dualities for the four dimensional
\mathcal{N}=1
theories or for the supersymmetric quantum mechanics. Therefore, Theorem
1.1
can be reformulated in these contexts as stating that any two of these physical theories can be related by a sequence of Seiberg dualities.

2.
Seeds, mutations, and log Calabi–Yau surfaces

In this section, we review the notions of seeds and seed mutations in a finite-rank free abelian group endowed with a skew-symmetric form of rank two. We then review, following
[
27
]
, the geometric interpretation of seed mutations as cluster mutations acting on toric models of log Calabi–Yau surfaces. The main result, Theorem
2.11
, identifies a Weyl group defined combinatorially in terms of seed mutations with a Weyl group defined geometrically using log Calabi–Yau surfaces. Finally, we review the classification of seeds of
q
-Painlevé type following
[
43
,
45
,
47
]
.

2.1.
Seeds, cyclically ordered seeds, and mutations

Throughout §
2
, we fix a free abelian group
N
of finite rank
n
together with a group homomorphism

\psi:N\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,

with finite cokernel. We denote by
K=\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)
its kernel.
Let
\langle-,-\rangle=\det(-,-)
be the standard determinant pairing on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. By slight abuse of notation, we use the same symbol for the induced integral skew-symmetric bilinear form on
N
obtained by pullback along
\psi
.

In this paper, a
seed
in
N
is an unordered basis
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
N
such that
\psi(e_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is a non-zero primitive vector for all
i\in I
. A
cyclic ordering
of a seed
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an ordering
I\simeq\{1,\dots,n\}
such that the vectors
\psi(e_{i})
are counterclockwise cyclically oriented in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
: after making the standard identification
\mathbb{R}^{2}=\mathbb{C}
, there exist determinations
\theta_{i}
of the arguments of
\psi(e_{i})\in\mathbb{C}
such that

(1)

\theta_{1}\leqslant\theta_{2}\leqslant\dots\leqslant\theta_{n-1}\leqslant\theta_{n}\leqslant\theta_{1}+2\pi\,.

A
cyclically ordered
seed
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}=(e_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
consists of a seed together with a choice of cyclic ordering. Given a cyclically ordered seed
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}
, we write
\mathbf{s}
for the underlying seed obtained by forgetting the cyclic ordering.

Let
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a seed in
N
. For every

j\in I
and
\epsilon\in\{\pm\}
, the
seed mutation

\mu_{j}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{s})
of
\mathbf{s}
is the seed
\mu_{j}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{s})=\{e_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i\in I}
given by

(2)

e_{i}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}-e_{j}\,\,\,\,\,\text{if}\,\,i=j\\
e_{i}+[\epsilon\langle{e_{i},e_{j}\rangle}]_{+}e_{j}\,\,\,\ \text{if}\,\,\,i\neq j\,,\end{cases}

where
[a]_{+}:=\max(0,a)
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Two seeds
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
are
mutation equivalent
if there exist sequences
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}
and
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{k}\in\{\pm\}
such that
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}=(\mu_{j_{k}}^{\epsilon_{k}}\circ\cdots\circ\mu_{j_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}})(\mathbf{s})
. For every seed
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in
N
,
j\in I
and
\epsilon\in\{\pm\}
, we have
\mu_{j}^{-\epsilon}(\mu_{j}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{s}))=\mathbf{s}
, and so mutation equivalence defines an equivalence relation on the set of seeds.
For every seed
\mathbf{s}
in
N
,
j\in I
and
\epsilon\in\{\pm\}
, the seed
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}=\{e_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i\in I}:=\mu_{j}^{\epsilon}(\mu_{j}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{s}))
is obtained by applying to
\mathbf{s}
the linear transformation
T_{e_{j}}^{\epsilon}
defined by

(3)

T_{e_{j}}^{\epsilon}(e_{i})=e_{i}+\epsilon\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle e_{j}\,.

In particular, two seeds
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
related by the linear transformation

T_{e_{j}}^{\epsilon}
are mutation equivalent.

Remark 2.1
.

In
[
47
, Definition 2.3]
, two seeds are called mutation equivalent if they are related by a sequence of seed mutations and seed isomorphisms, where a seed isomorphism is a linear automorphism of
N
preserving
\langle-,-\rangle
up to a sign. For the present paper, it is essential that seed isomorphisms are not included in the definition of mutation equivalence. This discrepancy in terminology is taken into account when we refer to results of
[
47
]
in §
2.5
below.

2.2.
Seeds and log Calabi–Yau surfaces

In this section we review the relationship between seed mutations and the birational geometry of log Calabi–Yau surfaces, following
[
27
]
. In particular, we recall how seeds give rise to log Calabi–Yau surfaces via toric models and how seed mutations correspond to cluster birational transformations between these models.

In this paper, a
log Calabi–Yau surface
is a pair
(Y,D)
consisting of a smooth projective surface over
\mathbb{C}
together with a non-empty singular normal crossings anticanonical divisor
D
. We refer to
[
22
,
27
,
28
]
for background on the geometry of log Calabi–Yau surfaces. A morphism
\pi:(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})\rightarrow(Y,D)
between log Calabi–Yau surfaces is called a
corner blow-up
(resp.​ an
interior blow-up
) if
\pi
is the blow-up at a singular (resp.​ smooth) point of
D
, and
\widetilde{D}
is the preimage (resp.​ the strict transform) of
D
.
A
toric model
for
(Y,D)
is a morphism
\pi:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y},\overline{D})
where
\pi
is a composition of interior blow-ups, and
(\overline{Y},\overline{D})
is a smooth projective toric surface with its toric boundary divisor.
The toric model is
framed
if we additionally fix an identification
\overline{Y}\setminus\overline{D}\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{\star})^{2}
.
By
[
28
, Lemma 1.3]
, after a sequence of corner blow-ups any log Calabi–Yau surface admits a toric model.

We next recall the construction of log Calabi–Yau surfaces from seeds in
N
following
[
27
]
.
Let
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a seed in
N
. Choose a smooth complete fan
\Sigma
in

\mathbb{R}^{2}
containing the rays
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\psi(e_{i})
for every
i\in I
. Let
\overline{Y}
be the smooth projective toric surface with fan
\Sigma
, and let
\overline{D}\subset\overline{Y}
be the toric boundary divisor.
Denote by
\overline{D}_{i}
the irreducible toric divisor corresponding to the ray
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\psi(e_{i})
. The inclusion

\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
induces an identification
\overline{Y}\setminus\overline{D}\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{\star})^{2}
.
For each
i\in I
, choose a point
p_{i}\in\overline{D}_{i}
such that
p_{i}
is a smooth point of
\overline{D}
, and assume that
p_{i}\neq p_{j}
if
i\neq j
. Let
Y
be the blow-up of
\overline{Y}
at the points
p_{i}
, and let
D
be the strict transform of
\overline{D}
.
The pair
(Y,D)
is a log Calabi–Yau surface.
The log Calabi–Yau surface
(Y,D)
, together with the blow-up morphism
\pi:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y},\overline{D})
and the identification
\overline{Y}\setminus\overline{D}\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{\star})^{2}
, is called a
framed toric model
for the seed
\mathbf{s}
. Framed toric models for
\mathbf{s}
are unique up to corner blow-ups, corresponding to refinements of
\Sigma
, and deformation equivalence, corresponding to varying the points
p_{i}
on the toric divisors
\overline{D}_{i}
.

By
[
28
, Proposition 4.1]
, if the collection of points
(p_{i})_{i\in I}
is chosen in the complement of the union of countably many proper Zariski closed subsets of
\prod_{i\in I}\overline{D}_{i}
, then the log Calabi–Yau surface is
generic
in the sense that
Y\setminus D
does not contain any smooth rational curve with self-intersection
(-2)
. The framed toric model is called generic in this case.

We now describe the geometric interpretation of seed mutations.
Let
\pi:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y},\overline{D})
be a generic framed toric model for a seed
\mathbf{s}
in
N
. Let
j\in I
and
\epsilon\in\{\pm\}
, and consider the mutated seed
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}:=\mu_{j}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{s})
as in (
2
). By
[
27
, §5]
, after possibly performing corner blow-ups on
(Y,D)
, there exists a generic framed toric model for the seed
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
of the form
\pi^{\prime}:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y}^{\prime},\overline{D}^{\prime})
for the same surface
(Y,D)
,
such that the induced birational map

(\mathbb{C}^{\star})^{2}=\overline{Y}\setminus\overline{D}\dashrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{\star})^{2}=\overline{Y}^{\prime}\setminus\overline{D}^{\prime}

is an elementary cluster transformation – see
[
27
, §5]
for details.
Concretely, after subdividing
\Sigma
if necessary, we may assume that the ray
-\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\psi(e_{j})
also belongs to
\Sigma
. The projection
\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{R}\psi(e_{j})
then induces a toric morphism
\overline{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
which is generically a
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-fibration.
The morphism
\pi^{\prime}:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y}^{\prime},\overline{D}^{\prime})
is obtained by contracting the
(-1)
-curve given by the strict transform in
Y
of the
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-fiber passing through
p_{j}
. We say that the framed
toric models
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
are related by
cluster mutation
.

A fundamental result of
[
30
]
(see also
[
11
]
) shows that any birational transformation arising from a change in the presentation of a log Calabi–Yau surface as a blow-up of a toric surface factors into cluster mutations. Since this statement plays a central role in the present paper, we record it for convenience.

Theorem 2.2
.

(
[
30
, Proposition 3.27]
, see also
[
11
, Theorem 1]
).
Let
\pi:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y},\overline{D})
be a generic framed toric model for a seed
\mathbf{s}
in
N
. Let
\pi^{\prime}:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y}^{\prime},\overline{D}^{\prime})
be a toric model for
(Y,D)
which is the composition of interior blow-ups at distinct points. Then, there exists a seed
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
obtained from
\mathbf{s}
by a sequence
\mu
of seed mutations, and an isomorphism

\overline{Y}^{\prime}\setminus\overline{D}^{\prime}\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{\star})^{2}
making
\pi^{\prime}
into a framed toric model for
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
which is related to
\pi
by a sequence of cluster mutations induced by
\mu
.

2.3.
The intersection form

Let
\mathscr{S}
be a mutation equivalence class of seeds in
N
.
In this section we review the construction of a canonical integral symmetric bilinear form

(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}}

on
K=\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)
called the intersection form.
The construction follows
[
27
, §5]
and
[
47
, §3.1]
and uses the geometry of log Calabi–Yau surfaces.

Let
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}\in\mathscr{S}
and
\pi:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y},\overline{D})
be a framed toric model for
\mathbf{s}
as in §
2.2
, with exceptional curves
E_{i}
indexed by
i\in I
.
Let
NS(Y)
denote the Néron-Severi group of divisors in
Y
, endowed with the intersection form
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\alpha\cdot\beta
.
Consider the subgroup

(4)

\Lambda_{(Y,D)}:=\{\alpha\in NS(Y)\,|\,\alpha\cdot D_{\rho}=0\,\,\text{for every irreducible component}\,\,D_{\rho}\,\,\text{of}\,\,D\}\,

of divisor classes orthogonal to the irreducible components of
D
.

According to
[
27
, Theorem 5.5]
and
[
47
, Proposition 3.8]
, there is an isomorphism

(5)

\iota_{\pi}:K\xrightarrow{\sim}\Lambda_{(Y,D)}\,,

defined as follows.
Let
a\in K
, that is
a=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}e_{i}\in N

such that
\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\psi(e_{i})=0
.
Then,

(6)

\iota_{\pi}(a):=\pi^{\star}C_{a}-\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}E_{i}\,,

where
C_{a}\in NS(\overline{Y})
is the unique curve class on the toric surface
\overline{Y}
such that

C_{a}\cdot\overline{D}_{\rho}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
\overline{D}_{i}=\overline{D}_{\rho}\end{subarray}}a_{i}\,,

for every ray
\rho
of
\Sigma
, where
\overline{D}_{\rho}
denotes the corresponding toric divisor of
\overline{Y}
.
The inverse map
\iota_{\pi}^{-1}
is given by

(7)

\displaystyle\iota_{\pi}^{-1}:\Lambda_{(Y,D)}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow K\subset N

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sum_{i\in I}(\alpha\cdot E_{i})e_{i}\,.

Using the isomorphism
\iota_{\pi}
, we define the
intersection form

(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}}
on
K
as the restriction to
\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
of the intersection form on
NS(Y)
. By
[
27
, Theorem 5.6]
(see also
[
47
, Lemmas 3.10-3.15]
), this bilinear form
(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}}
is independent of the choices of the framed toric model
\pi
and of the seed
\mathbf{s}\in\mathscr{S}
. Hence, it depends only on the mutation equivalence class
\mathscr{S}
.

We now give a purely combinatorial description of this intersection form.
Let
I\simeq\{1,\dots,n\}
be a cyclic ordering of the seed
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, and let
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}=(e_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
as in
(
1
).
Define the bilinear form

\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}:N\times N\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}

by

(8)

\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}(e_{i},e_{j}):=\begin{cases}1\,\,\,\text{if}\,\,\,i=j\\
\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle\,\,\text{if}\,\,\,i>j\\
0\,\,\,\,\text{if}\,\,\,i<j\,.\end{cases}

Since
\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}(e_{i},e_{j})-\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}(e_{j},e_{i})=\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle
,
and
K
is the kernel of
\langle-,-\rangle
, the restriction
\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}|_{K}
is a symmetric bilinear form on
K
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}
be a cyclically ordered seed with underlying seed
\mathbf{s}\in\mathscr{S}
.
Then, for every
\alpha,\beta\in K
, we have

(\alpha,\beta)_{\mathscr{S}}=-\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}(\alpha,\beta)\,.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}=(e_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
, and set
v_{i}:=\psi(e_{i})
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
Since
(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}}
and
\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}|_{K}
are symmetric bilinear forms on
K
, it suffices to show that
(a,a)_{\mathscr{S}}=-\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}(a,a)
for every

a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}e_{i}\in K
, that is, such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}v_{i}=0
.
By (
8
), we have

\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}(a,a)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i>j}a_{i}a_{j}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}-\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}a_{i}a_{j}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\,.

On the other hand, since the exceptional curves
E_{i}
satisfy
E_{i}^{2}=-1
, it follows from (
6
) that

(a,a)_{\mathscr{S}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}+C_{a}\cdot C_{a}\,,

where
C_{a}\cdot C_{a}
is the self-intersection of the curve
C_{a}
on the toric surface
\overline{Y}
.
By standard toric geometry (see
[
23
, §5.3, Corollary, p.111]
),
when
a_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
the self-intersection
C_{a}\cdot C_{a}

equals the area of a polygon whose sides have outer normal vectors
v_{i}

and integral lengths
a_{i}
.
Since the vectors
v_{i}
are cyclically ordered in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

by (
1
), this area is given by
\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}a_{i}a_{j}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle
. Hence, we obtain

C_{a}\cdot C_{a}=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}a_{i}a_{j}\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle\,,

for all
a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}e_{i}\in K
with
a_{i}\geq 0
, and so the general case follows by bilinearity.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

A cyclically ordered seed
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}=(e_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
in
N
determines a quiver with linear ordering, with
n
vertices indexed by
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, and
\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle
arrows from the vertex
i
to the vertex
j
for all
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n
. The matrix of the bilinear form
\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}
is the “unipotent companion matrix” of this quiver with linear ordering, in the sense of
[
21
]
.

2.4.
Weyl groups

The main result of this section, Theorem
2.11
, establishes the equality between two Weyl groups: one defined combinatorially in terms of seed mutations, and the other defined geometrically via the associated log Calabi–Yau surface. Throughout this section, we fix a mutation equivalence class
\mathscr{S}
of seeds in
N
.
For every seed
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}\in\mathscr{S}
, and distinct elements
e_{j}
and
e_{k}
of
\mathbf{s}
such that
\psi(e_{j})=\psi(e_{k})
,
consider the unique linear map

(9)

t_{jk}^{\mathbf{s}}:N\rightarrow N

such that
t_{jk}^{\mathbf{s}}(e_{i})=e_{i}
for all
i\notin\{j,k\}
,
t_{jk}^{\mathbf{s}}(e_{j})=e_{k}
, and
t_{jk}^{\mathbf{s}}(e_{k})=e_{j}
. In other words,
t_{jk}^{\mathbf{s}}
simply permutes the elements
e_{j}
and
e_{k}
of the basis
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
N
.

Definition 2.5
.

The Weyl group of
\mathscr{S}
is the subgroup
W_{\mathscr{S}}
of automorphisms of
N
generated by the linear transformations
t_{jk}^{\mathbf{s}}
for every seed
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}\in\mathscr{S}
, and distinct elements
e_{j}
and
e_{k}
of
\mathbf{s}
such that
\psi(e_{j})=\psi(e_{k})
.

We give in Lemma
2.9

below an alternative description of
W_{\mathscr{S}}
as a group of isometries of
K=\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)

endowed with
the intersection form

(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}}
introduced in §
2.3
.

Definition 2.6
.

A
root
of
\mathscr{S}
is an element
\alpha\in N
for which there exists a seed
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}\in\mathscr{S}
, and two distinct elements
e_{j}
and
e_{k}
of
\mathbf{s}
such that

\psi(e_{j})=\psi(e_{k})
and
\alpha=e_{j}-e_{k}
. We denote by
\Phi_{\mathscr{S}}
the set of roots of
\mathscr{S}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Every root
\alpha\in\Phi_{\mathscr{S}}
satisfies

(\alpha,\alpha)_{\mathscr{S}}=-2
.

Proof.

By Definition
2.6
, there exists a seed
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
distinct elements
e_{j}
and
e_{k}
of
\mathbf{s}
such that
\psi(e_{j})=\psi(e_{k})
and
\alpha=e_{j}-e_{k}
.
To calculate
(\alpha,\alpha)_{\mathscr{S}}
, consider a framed toric model

\pi:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y},\overline{D})
for
\mathbf{s}
as in §
2.2
, with exceptional curves
E_{i}
indexed by
i\in I
, and the isomorphism
\iota_{\pi}:K\xrightarrow{\sim}\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
given in (
5
). Then, we have
\iota_{\pi}(\alpha)=\iota_{\pi}(e_{j}-e_{k})=E_{k}-E_{j}
.
Since
E_{j}^{2}=E_{k}^{2}=-1
and
E_{j}\cdot E_{k}=0
, we have
(E_{j}-E_{k})^{2}=-2
, and so the result follows.
∎

For every
\alpha\in K
with
(\alpha,\alpha)_{\mathscr{S}}=-2
, consider the isometry of
(K,(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}})
given by the reflection with respect to
\alpha
:

\displaystyle s_{\alpha}:K

\displaystyle\longrightarrow K

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\longmapsto\beta+(\beta,\alpha)_{\mathscr{S}}\,\alpha\,.

Lemma 2.8
.

Consider a seed
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}\in\mathscr{S}
, and distinct elements
e_{j}
and
e_{k}
of
\mathbf{s}
such that
\psi(e_{j})=\psi(e_{k})
.
Then, for every cyclic ordering
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}
of
\mathbf{s}
, denoting
\alpha=e_{j}-e_{k}
, we have

t_{jk}^{\mathbf{s}}(\beta)=\beta-\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}(\beta,\alpha)\alpha

for every
\beta\in N
. In particular, the automorphisms
t_{jk}^{\mathbf{s}}
preserve
K\subset N
and
t_{jk}^{\mathbf{s}}|_{K}=s_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

The formula for
t_{jk}^{\mathbf{s}}
follows by a direct computation using the definition of
\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}
given in (
8
).
The statement regarding the restriction to
K
then follows since
\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}(\beta,\alpha)=-(\beta,\alpha)_{\mathscr{S}}
for every
\alpha,\beta\in K
by Lemma
2.3
.
∎

By Lemma
2.8
,
W_{\mathscr{S}}
preserves
K\subset N
, and so we obtain by restriction a group homomorphism

\rho:W_{\mathscr{S}}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(K)

sending the generators
t_{jk}^{\mathbf{s}}

to the reflections
s_{\alpha}
with
\alpha=e_{j}-e_{k}
, where
\mathbf{s}=(e_{i})_{i\in I}
.

Lemma 2.9
.

The restriction map

\rho:W_{\mathscr{S}}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(K)

is injective and defines an isomorphism between
W_{\mathscr{S}}

and the subgroup of isometries of
(K,(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}})
generated by the reflections
s_{\alpha}
with respect to the roots
\alpha\in\Phi_{\mathscr{S}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.8
,
it suffices to show that
\rho
is injective. Let
g\in W_{\mathscr{S}}
be such that
\rho(g)=\mathrm{id}
. Write

g=t_{j_{\ell}k_{\ell}}^{\mathbf{s}_{\ell}}\circ\dots\circ t_{j_{1}k_{1}}^{\mathbf{s}_{1}}

and denote by
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}\in\Phi_{\mathscr{S}}
the corresponding roots. Fix a seed
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}\in\mathscr{S}
, and
let
\pi:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y},\overline{D})
be a framed toric model for
\mathbf{s}
as in §
2.2
, with exceptional curves
E_{i}

indexed by
i\in I
. Consider the identification

\iota_{\pi}:K\xrightarrow{\sim}\Lambda_{(Y,D)}\subset NS(Y)
given by (
6
), and
the linear map

\displaystyle\varphi:NS(Y)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow N

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sum_{i\in I}(\beta\cdot E_{i})e_{i}\,.

Since
\varphi(-E_{i})=e_{i}
for all
i\in I
, the map
\varphi
is surjective.
By Lemma
2.7
, the roots

\alpha_{m}
satisfy
(\alpha_{m},\alpha_{m})_{\mathscr{S}}=-2
for all

1\leqslant m\leqslant\ell
, and so the classes
\iota_{\pi}(\alpha_{m})

in
\Lambda_{(Y,D)}\subset NS(Y)

have self-intersection
-2
. The corresponding reflection
r_{\iota_{\pi}(\alpha_{m})}
acts on
NS(Y)
and fixes the irreducible components of
D
. Moreover, we have

\varphi\circ\iota_{\pi}=\mathrm{id}
by (
7
), and so it follows
from Lemma
2.8
that
\varphi\circ r_{\iota_{\pi}(\alpha_{m})}=t_{j_{m}k_{m}}^{\mathbf{s}_{m}}\circ\varphi
.
Since
\rho(g)=\mathrm{id}
, the composition
\widetilde{g}:=r_{\iota_{\pi}(\alpha_{\ell})}\circ\dots\circ r_{\iota_{\pi}(\alpha_{1})}
acts trivially on
\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
. In addition, the reflections
r_{\iota_{\pi}(\alpha_{m})}
act trivially on the classes of irreducible components of
D
, therefore the same holds for
\widetilde{g}
. Consequently,

\widetilde{g}
acts trivially on the whole of
NS(Y)
.
Using the compatibility

\varphi\circ\widetilde{g}=g\circ\varphi
together with the surjectivity of
\varphi
, we deduce that
g=\mathrm{id}
, and this completes the proof of the injectivity of
\rho
.
∎

Finally, we describe the Weyl group
W_{\mathscr{S}}
in terms of the geometry of log Calabi–Yau surfaces. Fix a seed
\mathbf{s}\in\mathscr{S}
and
\pi:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y},\overline{D})
a framed toric model for
\mathbf{s}
as in §
2.2
, with exceptional curves
E_{i}

indexed by
i\in I
.
Following
[
28
, Definition 1.6]
, a
root
of
(Y,D)
is an element
\alpha\in\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
with
\alpha\cdot\alpha=-2
, which is realized by a smooth rational curve on a deformation equivalent pair
(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})
. We denote by
\Phi
the set of roots of
(Y,D)
.
The
Weyl group

W
of
(Y,D)
is the group of isometries of
NS(Y)
generated by the reflections with respect to the roots
\alpha\in\Phi
. Elements of
W
fix the classes of the irreducible components of
D
, and so are uniquely determined
by their action on the orthogonal subspace
\Lambda_{(Y,D)}\subset NS(Y)
. Hence,
W
can also be viewed as a subgroup of the group of isometries of
\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
.
If
(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})\rightarrow(Y,D)
is a corner blow-up, then there is a canonical identification
\Lambda_{(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})}\simeq\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
, and so a corresponding identification of the sets of roots and of the Weyl groups of
(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})
and
(Y,D)
. Therefore, we consider in the following
(Y,D)
up to corner blow-ups.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
E
and
F
be two disjoint smooth rational curves in
Y
such that
E^{2}=F^{2}=-1
,
E
and
F
are not contained in
D
and intersect the same irreducible component of
D
. Then
E-F
is a root of
(Y,D)
.

Proof.

There exists a morphism
(Y,D)\rightarrow(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})
that contracts
E
and
F
to two distinct points
p
and
q
lying on the same irreducible component
D^{\prime}_{i}
of
D^{\prime}
. Bringing the points
p
and
q
together on
D^{\prime}
and blowing up them successively defines a log Calabi–Yau surface deformation equivalent to
(Y,D)
containing a smooth rational curve of class
E-F
, so
E-F\in\Phi
.
∎

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
\mathscr{S}
be a mutation equivalence class of seeds in
N
, and
\pi:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y},\overline{D})
be a framed toric model for a seed

\mathbf{s}\in\mathscr{S}
.
Under the identification

\iota_{\pi}:K\xrightarrow{\sim}\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
, the roots and the Weyl group of
\mathscr{S}
coincide with the roots and the Weyl group of the log Calabi–Yau surface
(Y,D)
respectively:

\Phi_{\mathscr{S}}=\Phi\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,W_{\mathscr{S}}=W\,.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
\Phi_{\mathscr{S}}=\Phi
. We first show the inclusion
\Phi_{\mathscr{S}}\subset\Phi
. Let
\alpha\in\Phi_{\mathscr{S}}
. By Definition
2.6
, there exists a seed
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}=\{e_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i\in I}\in\mathscr{S}
and distinct elements
e_{j}^{\prime}
and
e_{k}^{\prime}
of

\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
such that
\psi(e_{j}^{\prime})=\psi(e_{k}^{\prime})
and
\alpha=e_{j}^{\prime}-e_{k}^{\prime}
. Since both seeds
\mathbf{s}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
are in
\mathscr{S}
, there exists a sequence of seed mutations that
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}=\mu(\mathbf{s})
.
As reviewed in §
2.2
, it follows that, up to corner blow-ups,
there exists a framed toric model
\pi^{\prime}:(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y}^{\prime},\overline{D}^{\prime})
for
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
related to
\mathbf{s}
by cluster mutations induced by

\mu
.
By construction, there exist exceptional curves
E_{j}^{\prime}
and
E_{k}^{\prime}
for
\pi^{\prime}
, intersecting the same irreducible component of
\overline{D}^{\prime}
, such that
\iota_{\pi^{\prime}}(\alpha)=E^{\prime}_{j}-E^{\prime}_{k}
, and so

\alpha\in\Phi
by Lemma
2.10
.

It remains to show the reverse inclusion
\Phi\subset\Phi_{\mathscr{S}}
. Let
\alpha\in\Phi
. If
I=\emptyset
, there is nothing to show, so assume that
I\neq\emptyset
.
Then
Y
contains
(-1)
-curves and so
Y
is not isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Therefore, by

[
22
, Theorem 6.12]
, there exist two disjoint smooth rational curves
E
and
F
in
Y
such that
E^{2}=F^{2}=-1
,
E
and
F
are not contained in
D
and intersect the same irreducible component of
D
. Let
(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})
be the log Calabi–Yau surface obtained from
(Y,D)
by contracting
E
and
F
. By
[
28
, Lemma 1.3]
, there exists
\pi^{\prime}:(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})\rightarrow(\overline{Y}^{\prime},\overline{D}^{\prime})
which is the blow-up of distinct smooth points on the toric divisor
\overline{D}^{\prime}
of a toric smooth projective surface
\overline{Y}^{\prime}
. Then, by Theorem
2.2

(
[
30
, Proposition 3.27]
, see also
[
11
, Theorem 1]
), there exists an identification

(\mathbb{C}^{\star})^{2}\simeq\overline{Y}^{\prime}\setminus\overline{D}^{\prime}
such that composition
(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y}^{\prime},\overline{D}^{\prime})
is the framed toric model for a seed
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
related to
\mathbf{s}
for a sequence of mutations. Since
E
and
F
are exceptional curves of
(Y,D)\rightarrow(\overline{Y}^{\prime},\overline{D}^{\prime})
intersecting the same irreducible component of
D
, it follows that
\iota_{\pi}^{-1}(\alpha)\in\Phi_{\mathscr{S}}
.
∎

2.5.
Seeds of
q
-Painlevé type and T-polygons

In this section, we review the concept of seeds of

q
-Painlevé type, together with the associated T-polygons, and discuss their classification up to mutation.

Definition 2.12
.

A mutation equivalence class
\mathscr{S}
of seeds in
N
is of
q
-Painlevé type
if the intersection form

(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}}
on
K
is negative semi-definite but not negative definite.
A seed in
N
is of
q
-Painlevé type if its mutation equivalence class is of
q
-Painlevé type.

By
[
47
, Proposition 3.22 (3)]
, if
\mathbf{s}
is a seed of
q
-Painlevé type, then there exists a unique primitive element

(10)

\delta_{\mathscr{S}}=\sum_{i\in I}c_{\mathbf{s},i}e_{i}\in K

such that
c_{\mathbf{s},i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
(\delta_{\mathbf{s}},\delta_{\mathbf{s}})_{\mathscr{S}}=0
. Moreover,
\delta_{\mathscr{S}}
depends only on the mutation equivalence class
\mathscr{S}
of
\mathbf{s}
.

Let
\mathbf{s}
be a seed of
q
-Painlevé type. Following
[
47
, Eq. (3.7)]
, we define a convex polygon
P_{\mathbf{s}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as the intersection of the half-spaces
\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,\langle v,\psi(e_{i})\rangle\geq-c_{\mathbf{s},i}\}
for all
i\in I
. By
[
47
, Proposition 3.30]
,
P_{\mathbf{s}}
is a Fano polygon without remainder, or equivalently a
T-polygon
in the terminology of
[
45
]
:

(i)

P_{\mathbf{s}}
is a bounded convex polygon in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
containing the origin
0
in its interior and each vertex of
P_{\mathbf{s}}
is a primitive element of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,

(ii)

for every edge
e
of
P_{\mathbf{s}}
, the lattice length of
e
is divisible by the lattice distance between
e
and the origin.

Fix a cyclic ordering
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}=(e_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
of
\mathbf{s}
. Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
be the cyclically ordered collection of vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
of the form
\psi(e_{i})
, without repetition. Then, it follows from the proof of
[
47
, Proposition 3.30]
that
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
are exactly the primitive directions of the edges of
P_{\mathbf{s}}
. Moreover, given an edge
e
with primitive direction
v_{j}
, the lattice distance between
e
and the origin is equal to
c_{\mathbf{s},i}
for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
such that
v_{j}=\psi(e_{i})
. Finally, the lattice length of
e
is equal to this lattice distance times the number of
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
such that
\psi(e_{i})=v_{j}
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}=(e_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}=(e_{i}^{\prime})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}

be two cyclically ordered seeds in
N
. Assume that there exists
f\in SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
such that
\psi(e_{i})=f(\psi(e_{i}^{\prime}))
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Then,
c_{\mathbf{s},i}=c_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},i}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.

Proof.

The assumption implies that
P_{\mathbf{s}}=f(P_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}})
with
f\in SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
. The result follows since the lattice distances between the origin and the edges of a lattice polygon are invariant under the action of
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
∎

The following result follows from
[
47
, Corollary 3.33]
and plays a key role in the proof of the main result of this paper, Theorem

Theorem 2.14
.

(
[

3.
Affine Weyl groups and del Pezzo surfaces

In this section, we introduce and examine the relationships between the finite Weyl group
W(Z)
, the affine Weyl group
\widehat{W}(Z)
and the orthogonal group
O(Z)
, all acting on the Grothendieck group
K(Z)
of the derived category of coherent sheaves on a del Pezzo surface
Z
.

3.1.
Del Pezzo surfaces, Euler form, and Weyl groups

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface over
\mathbb{C}
, that is, a smooth projective connected surface over
\mathbb{C}
with ample anticanonical line bundle
\omega_{Z}^{-1}
. Then
Z
is isomorphic either to
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
or to
the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
at
m
points in general position,
for some integer
0\leqslant m\leqslant 8
– see for example
[
19
, §8]
. We write
NS(Z)
for the Néron-Severi group of
Z
, and
\operatorname{Pic}(Z)
for its Picard group. Since
H^{1}(Z,\mathcal{O}_{Z})=0
, the first Chern class induces an isomorphism
c_{1}:\operatorname{Pic}(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}NS(Z)
.
We denote by
K_{Z}=c_{1}(\omega_{Z})
the canonical divisor class and by
\alpha\cdot\beta
the intersection pairing of divisors
\alpha,\beta\in NS(Z)
. One has
K_{Z}^{2}>0
, and by the Hodge index theorem the restriction of the intersection form to the orthogonal complement

K_{Z}^{\perp}:=\{\alpha\in NS(Z)\,|\,\alpha\cdot K_{Z}=0\}

is negative definite.

Let
D(Z)=D^{b}\operatorname{Coh}(Z)
be the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves on
Z
, and
K(Z)=K_{0}(D(Z))
its Grothendieck group. For an object
E
in
D(Z)
, we write
[E]\in K(Z)
for its class in
K(Z)
,
r(E)
for its rank,

d(E)=c_{1}(E)\cdot(-K_{Z})
for its anticanonical degree, and
\chi(E)
for its Euler characteristic. The numerical invariants
r(E)
,
d(E)
, and
\chi(E)
depend only on the class
[E]\in K(Z)
. This gives rise to a linear map

\displaystyle\psi=(r,d):K(Z)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}

\displaystyle[E]

\displaystyle\longmapsto(r(E),d(E))\,.

Let
\delta\in K(Z)
denote the class
[\mathcal{O}_{x}]
of the skyscraper sheaf at any point
x\in Z
. This class is independent of the choice of
x
and satisfies

(r(\delta),d(\delta),\chi(\delta))=(0,0,1)
.
The Grothendieck group
K(Z)
is a free abelian group of rank
3\leqslant n\leqslant 11
, and admits a canonical decomposition

(11)

\displaystyle K(Z)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus NS(Z)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta

\displaystyle[E]

\displaystyle\longmapsto(r(E),c_{1}(E),\chi(E)-r(E))\,.

With respect to this decomposition, the kernel of the degree map is

\mathrm{Ker}(d)=\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta\,,

the kernel of the rank map is

\mathrm{Ker}(r)=NS(Z)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta\,,

and the the kernel of
\psi=(r,d)
is

(12)

\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)=K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta\,.

We denote by
\chi(-,-)
the Euler form on
K(Z)
, defined by

\chi(E,F)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{i}\dim\operatorname{Hom}^{i}(E,F)\,.

By the Riemann-Roch theorem, the Euler form is given explicitly by

(13)

\chi(E,F)=r(E)\chi(F)+r(F)\chi(E)-r(E)r(F)-c_{1}(E)\cdot c_{1}(F)-r(F)d(E)\,.

In particular, we have
\chi(\delta,\delta)=0
, and for
\chi(\delta,E)=\chi(E,\delta)=r(E)
for any object
E
of
D(Z)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface.

(i)

The skew-symmetrization of the Euler form
\chi(-,-)
is given by:

(14)

\chi(E,F)-\chi(F,E)=r(E)d(F)-r(F)d(E)\,.

(ii)

The restriction of the Euler form
\chi(-,-)
to

\mathrm{Ker}(d)
is symmetric: for every
[E],[F]\in\mathrm{Ker}(d)
, one has

(15)

\chi(E,F)=r(E)\chi(F)+r(F)\chi(E)-r(E)r(F)-c_{1}(E)\cdot c_{1}(F)\,.

Equivalently, for every
(r,C,m)\in\mathrm{Ker}(d)=\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta
, one has

(16)

\chi((r,C,m),(r^{\prime},C^{\prime},m^{\prime}))=rm^{\prime}+r^{\prime}m+rr^{\prime}-C\cdot C^{\prime}\,.

(iii)

The restriction of the Euler form
\chi(-,-)
to
\mathrm{Ker}(r)=NS(Z)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta
is given by the negative of the intersection form on
NS(Z)
:
for every
C,C^{\prime}\in NS(Z)
and
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
, one has

\chi((0,C,m),(0,C^{\prime},m^{\prime}))=-C\cdot C^{\prime}\,.

Proof.

The result follows immediately from (
13
).
∎

Finally, we define affine and finite roots and Weyl groups of a del Pezzo surface.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface.
An
affine root
of
Z
is an element
\alpha\in\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)=K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta
such that
\chi(\alpha,\alpha)=2
.
We denote by
\widehat{\Phi}_{Z}
the set of affine roots of
Z
.
For every
\alpha\in\widehat{\Phi}_{Z}
, the reflection of
K(Z)
with respect to
\alpha
is the linear automorphism

(17)

\displaystyle r_{\alpha}:K(Z)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow K(Z)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\longmapsto\beta-\chi(\beta,\alpha)\alpha\,.

The
affine Weyl group

\widehat{W}(Z)
is the group of linear automorphisms of
K(Z)
generated by the reflections with respect to the affine roots
\widehat{\Phi}_{Z}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Every affine root
\alpha\in\widehat{\Phi}_{Z}
belongs to
\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)
by definition, and so
\chi(\alpha,\beta)=\chi(\beta,\alpha)
for every
\beta\in K(Z)
by Lemma
3.1
(i).

Definition 3.4
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface. A
finite root
of
Z
is an affine root contained in
K_{Z}^{\perp}
. We denote by
\Phi_{Z}
the set of finite roots of
Z
.
The
finite Weyl group

W(Z)
is the group of linear automorphisms of
K(Z)
generated by reflections with respect to the finite roots
\alpha\in\Phi_{Z}
.

The group
W(Z)
preserves the decomposition
K(Z)=\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\langle K_{Z}\rangle\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta
, where
\langle K_{Z}\rangle=K(Z)\cap\mathbb{Q}K_{Z}
, and acts trivially on
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus\langle K_{Z}\rangle\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta
. Thus,
W(Z)
is determined by its action by isometries on
K_{Z}^{\perp}
.
By the Hodge index theorem, the intersection form is negative definite on
K_{Z}^{\perp}
,
and so
W(Z)
is a finite group.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface. Then, the finite Weyl group
W(Z)
is isomorphic to the group of isometries of
NS(Z)
fixing
K_{Z}
.

Proof.

When
Z
is isomorphic to the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in
3\leqslant m\leqslant 8
points, this is
[
19
, Corollary 8.2.15]
. The remaining cases are verified directly as follows. If
Z=\mathbb{P}^{2}
, then
K_{Z}^{\perp}=0
, and so the result is trivial. If
Z=\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
, then
K_{Z}^{\perp}=\mathbb{Z}\beta
with
\beta^{2}=-2
, and so the only non-trivial isometry of
K_{Z}^{\perp}
is the reflection with respect to
\beta
. If
Z
is the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in one point, then, as reviewed in Lemma
3.12
below,
K_{Z}^{\perp}=\mathbb{Z}\alpha
with
\alpha^{2}=-8
. The only non-trivial isometry of
K_{Z}^{\perp}
is the reflection with respect to
\alpha
, which does not extend to an isometry of
NS(Z)
fixing
K_{Z}
because
NS(Z)
is unimodular. Similarly, if
Z
is the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in two points, then, as reviewed in Lemma
3.12
below, there exists an orthogonal decomposition
K_{Z}^{\perp}=\mathbb{Z}\beta\oplus\mathbb{Z}\alpha
with
\beta^{2}=-2
and
\alpha^{2}=-14
. An isometry
f
of
K_{Z}^{\perp}
must send
\beta
to
\pm\beta
. Upon composing with the reflection with respect to
\beta
, we may assume that
f(\beta)=\beta
. If
f
is non-trivial, then
f
is the reflection with respect to
\alpha
, which again does not extend to an isometry of
NS(Z)
fixing
K_{Z}
since
NS(Z)
is unimodular.
∎

3.2.
Orthogonal groups and finite Weyl groups

In this section, we introduce the orthogonal group of a del Pezzo surface
Z
as a group of isometries of
K(Z)
, and we describe it explicitly in terms of the finite Weyl group in Proposition
3.10
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface. The
orthogonal group
of
Z
,
denoted by
O(Z)
, is the group of isometries of
(K(Z),\chi(-,-))
preserving the rank and the degree.

The finite and affine Weyl groups
W(Z)
and
\widehat{W}(Z)
are naturally subgroups of
O(Z)
.
Define the subgroup
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
of
\operatorname{Pic}(Z)
by

\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z):=\{L\in\operatorname{Pic}(Z)\,|\,c_{1}(L)\cdot K_{Z}=0\}\,.

Lemma 3.7
.

The action of
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
on
K(Z)
by tensor product realizes
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
as a subgroup of the orthogonal group
O(Z)
.

Proof.

For every
[E],[F]\in K(Z)
, and
L=\mathcal{O}(D)\in\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
with
D\in K_{Z}^{\perp}
, we have
r(E\otimes L)=r(E)
,
c_{1}(E\otimes L)=c_{1}(E)+r(E)D
, and
\chi(E\otimes L,F\otimes L)=\chi(E,F)
.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

For every
L=\mathcal{O}_{Z}(D)\in\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
with
D\in K_{Z}^{\perp}
, the action of
L
on
K(Z)
by tensor product preserves the direct sum decomposition of
K(Z)
into
\langle K_{Z}\rangle
and
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta
, and acts on
the latter as follows:

(18)

\displaystyle t_{L}:\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta

\displaystyle(r,C,m)

\displaystyle\longmapsto(r,C+rD,m+C\cdot D+\frac{r}{2}D^{2})\,.

Proof.

For every
[E]\in K(Z)
, we have
r(E\otimes L)=r(E)
,
c_{1}(E\otimes L)=c_{1}(E)+r(E)D
, and,
by the Riemann–Roch theorem:

\chi(E\otimes L)=\chi(E)+c_{1}(E)\cdot D+\frac{r(E)}{2}(D^{2}-D\cdot K_{Z})=\chi(E)+c_{1}(E)\cdot D+\frac{r(E)}{2}D^{2}\,,

where we used that
D\cdot K_{Z}=0
.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

For every
f\in O(Z)
, there exists
L\in Pic^{0}(Z)
such that
f([\mathcal{O}_{Z}])=[L]
.

Proof.

Since
f
preserves both the rank and the degree, the class
f([\mathcal{O}_{Z}])

is of the form
f([\mathcal{O}_{Z}])=(1,D,m)\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta
.
Because
f
is also an isometry for the Euler form, we obtain from (
16
):

1=\chi(\mathcal{O}_{Z},\mathcal{O}_{Z})=\chi(f([\mathcal{O}_{Z}]),f([\mathcal{O}_{Z}]))=2m+1-D^{2}\,.

It follows that
m=\frac{D^{2}}{2}
. On the other hand, since
D\in K_{Z}^{\perp}
, the Riemann-Roch theorem yields
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{Z}(D))=1+\frac{D^{2}}{2}
. Therefore,
\chi(\mathcal{O}_{Z}(D))-r(\mathcal{O}_{Z}(D))=\frac{D^{2}}{2}=m
, and so the line bundle
L:=\mathcal{O}_{Z}(D)\in\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
satisfies
[L]=f([\mathcal{O}_{Z}])
.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

The orthogonal group
O(Z)
is generated by the finite Weyl group
W(Z)
and the subgroup
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
. Moreover, these subgroups combine as a semi-direct product, so that

O(Z)=W(Z)\ltimes\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)\,.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.9
, for any
f\in O(Z)
, there exists
g\in\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
such that
g\circ f([\mathcal{O}_{Z}])=[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]
. Therefore, to prove the generation of
O(Z)
by
W(Z)
and
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
, it suffices to prove that every
f\in O(Z)
such that
f([\mathcal{O}_{Z}])=[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]
lies in
W(Z)
.

Let
f\in O(Z)
fixing
[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]
.
As
f
preserves the rank, it preserves the decomposition of
K(Z)
into the direct sum of
\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]
and
NS(Z)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta
.
We first analyze the image of
\delta
.
Since
f
preserves rank and degree, we must have
f(\delta)\in K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta
.
Now
\chi(\delta,\delta)=0
, and the Euler form is positive definite on
K_{Z}^{\perp}
by the Hodge index theorem, and zero on
\mathbb{Z}\delta
, so
f(\delta)=\pm\delta
. To determine the sign, observe that, since
f
fixes
[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]
, we have
\chi([\mathcal{O}_{Z}],f(\delta))=\chi(f([\mathcal{O}_{Z}]),f(\delta))=\chi([\mathcal{O}_{Z}],\delta)=1
. This implies that
f(\delta)=\delta
.

Next consider the action of
f
on
NS(Z)
. For any class
C\in NS(Z)
, the image
f(C)
is of the form
(0,C^{\prime},m)\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus NS(Z)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta
.
Since
f
preserves the Euler form and fixes
[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]
, we have

0=\chi((0,C,0))=\chi([\mathcal{O}_{Z}],(0,C,0))=\chi([\mathcal{O}_{Z}],(0,C^{\prime},m))=\chi((0,C^{\prime},m))=m\,,

thus
m=0
. Therefore,
f
preserves the decomposition
NS(Z)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta
and acts trivially on
\mathbb{Z}\delta
. Finally, as
f
preserves the degree and the intersection form is non-degenerate on
NS(Z)
, we necessarily have
f(K_{Z})=K_{Z}
. The restriction of
f
to
NS(Z)
is an isometry fixing
K_{Z}
, and hence
f\in W(Z)
by Proposition

3.5
. This concludes the proof that the orthogonal group
O(Z)
is generated by
W(Z)
and
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
.

To prove that
O(Z)=W(Z)\ltimes\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
, first observe that the intersection of
W(Z)
and
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
is reduced to the identity since
W(Z)
is finite and
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
is torsion free. Therefore, it remains to show that
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
is a normal subgroup of
O(Z)
. Since
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
is abelian, it suffices to show that, for every
L=\mathcal{O}_{Z}(D)\in\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
and
w\in W(Z)
,
we have
wt_{L}w^{-1}\in\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
.
A direct computation from the formula for
t_{L}
given in (
18
), together with the preservation of the intersection form by the action of
W(Z)
on
K_{Z}^{\perp}
, shows that
wt_{L}w^{-1}=t_{L^{\prime}}
with
L^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}_{Z}(wD)\in\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Orthogonal groups and affine Weyl groups

In this section we prove Proposition
3.15
, which describes the relation between the orthogonal group of a del Pezzo surface
Z
and its affine Weyl group. We also explain how this affine Weyl group naturally identifies with those arising in the theory of affine Lie algebras.
These results are not used in the proof of the main theorem in §
5
and are included for their intrinsic interest.

Definition 3.11
.

The
finite root lattice

R(Z)

of a del Pezzo surface
Z
is the subspace of
K_{Z}^{\perp}
generated by the finite roots
\alpha\in\Phi_{Z}
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface. Then, the following holds:

(i)

If
Z
is not isomorphic to the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in one or two points, then

K_{Z}^{\perp}=R(Z)\,.

Moreover,
R(Z)
is the root lattice of a semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{Z}
in the list

A_{0}\,,\,\,\,A_{1}\,,\,\,\,A_{1}+A_{2}\,,\,\,\,A_{4}\,,\,\,\,D_{5}\,,\,\,\,E_{6}\,,\,\,\,E_{7}\,,\,\,\,E_{8}\,.

(ii)

If
Z
is isomorphic to the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in two points, then there exists an orthogonal decomposition

K_{Z}^{\perp}=R(Z)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\alpha

with
\alpha\cdot\alpha=-14
. Moreover,
R(Z)
is the root lattice of the simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{Z}
of type
A_{1}
.

(iii)

If
Z
is isomorphic to the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in one point, then
R(Z)=\emptyset
and

K_{Z}^{\perp}=\mathbb{Z}\alpha

with
\alpha\cdot\alpha=-8
.

Proof.

This is a classical result in the theory of del Pezzo surfaces, following from the description of
Z
as a blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, see for instance
[
46
, Chapter IV]
or

[
19
, Chapter 8]
. Note that in (ii), one can take
\alpha=-2H+3E_{1}+3E_{2}
, and in (iii) one can take
\alpha=H-3E_{1}
, and where
H
is the pullback of the class of a line in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, and
E_{1}
,
E_{2}
are the exceptional curves of the blow-up
Z\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
.
∎

By Lemma
3.12
, the real vector space

R(Z)\otimes\mathbb{R}
admits a canonical identification with the dual of the Cartan subalgebra of a finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{Z}
.
We explain below how enlarging
R(Z)
to

\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus R(Z)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta

naturally gives rise to the dual of the Cartan subalgebra of an affine Lie algebra.
For background on affine Lie algebras, we refer to
[
41
]
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface. Then,
(\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus R(Z)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta)\otimes\mathbb{R}

endowed with the negative of the restriction of the Euler form is isometric to the dual of the Cartan subalgebra of an affine Lie algebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{Z}
. Moreover, the affine roots of
Z
are the real roots of the corresponding affine root system.

Proof.

Using the notation of
[
41
, Chapter 6]
, the dual of the Cartan subalgebra of an affine Lie algebra is of the form

\mathfrak{h}^{\star}=\mathbb{R}\Lambda_{0}\oplus\mathring{\mathfrak{h}}^{\star}\oplus\mathbb{R}\delta\,,

where
\mathring{\mathfrak{h}}^{\star}
is the dual of the Cartan subalgebra of a finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra.
Comparing
[
41
, Eq. (6.2.7)]
with (
11
), and
[
41
, Eq. (6.2.4)]
with (
16
), we obtain, that

\Lambda_{0}\mapsto[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]-\delta\,,\,\,\,\,\mathring{\mathfrak{h}}^{\star}\simeq R(Z)\otimes\mathbb{R}\,,\,\,\,\,\,\delta\mapsto\delta\,,

defines an isometry between
(\mathbb{Z}[\mathcal{O}_{Z}]\oplus R(Z)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta)\otimes\mathbb{R}
and the dual of the Cartan subalgebra of an affine Lie algebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{Z}
.
∎

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface. Then, the following holds:

(i)

If
Z
is not isomorphic to the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in one or two points, then

O(Z)=\widehat{W}(Z)\,.

(ii)

If
Z
is isomorphic to the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in two points, then

O(Z)=\widehat{W}(Z)\ltimes\mathbb{Z}\,.

(iii)

If
Z
is isomorphic to the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in one point, then
\widehat{W}(Z)
is trivial and

O(Z)=\mathbb{Z}\,.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.8
and Proposition
3.10
, both
O(Z)=W(Z)\ltimes\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
and
\widehat{W}(Z)
preserve the decomposition

K(Z)=\langle K_{Z}\rangle\oplus\mathrm{Ker}(d)
, and act trivially on
\langle K_{Z}\rangle
. Therefore, it suffices to prove the result in restriction to
\mathrm{Ker}(d)
.
For (i), assume that

is not isomorphic to the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in one or two points. By Lemma
3.12
(i), we have
K_{Z}^{\perp}=R(Z)
, and so by Proposition
3.13
,
\mathrm{Ker}(d)\otimes\mathbb{R}
is isometric to the dual of the Cartan subalgebra

of an affine Lie algebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{Z}
.
Therefore,
[
41
, Proposition 2.5]
identifies the affine Weyl group
\widehat{W}(Z)
with the semi-direct product

W(Z)\ltimes\mathring{\mathfrak{h}}^{\star}

where
\mathring{\mathfrak{h}}^{\star}=K_{Z}^{\perp}
acts by “translations” given explicitly in
[

, Eq. 6.5.2]
. Using the identification between

and

4.
Geometric helices on del Pezzo surfaces and tilting operations

In this section, we first recall general facts about exceptional collections on del Pezzo surfaces and then review following
[
14
]
the notions of geometric helices and tilting operations.

4.1.
Exceptional collections on del Pezzo surfaces

In this section, we recall several foundational results on exceptional collections on del Pezzo surfaces, mainly due to Gorodentsev
[
25
]
and Kuleshov–Orlov
[
44
]
. We adopt the notation introduced in §
3.1
for the derived categories of del Pezzo surfaces.
We define the slope of a vector bundle
E
on a del Pezzo surface
Z
by
\mu(E)=d(E)/r(E)\in\mathbb{Q}
. We then define slope stability in the usual way. If
E
is a torsion sheaf, we set
\mu(E)=\infty
.

An object
E
in the derived category
D(Z)
is
exceptional
if
\operatorname{Hom}(E,E)=\mathbb{C}
and
\operatorname{Hom}^{k}(E,E)=0
for all
k\neq 0
. By
[
44
, Proposition 2.10]
, every exceptional object in
D(Z)
is isomorphic to a shift of a coherent sheaf.
Moreover, if
E
is an exceptional sheaf, then, by
[
44
, Proposition 2.9]
and
[
25
, 2.4.​ Theorem]
, either

(i)

E
is a slope stable vector bundle, or

(ii)

E=\mathcal{O}_{D}(k)
is the push-forward of a line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(k)
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, from an exceptional curve
D\simeq\mathbb{P}^{1}\subset Z
.

By
[
25
, Corollary 2.5]
, an exceptional sheaf
E
is uniquely determined by its class
[E]\in K(Z)
in the Grothendieck group.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
E
be an exceptional object in
D(Z)
. Then:

(i)

(r(E),d(E))\neq(0,0)
.

(ii)

r(E)
and
d(E)
are coprime.

Proof.

By
[
44
, Proposition 2.10]
, every exceptional object in
D(Z)
is a shift of a sheaf on
Z
, so it suffices to prove the result when
E
is a sheaf. When
E=\mathcal{O}_{D}(k)
for an exceptional curve
D
, we have
(r(E),d(E))=(0,1)
and so the result holds.
When
E
is an exceptional bundle on
Z
, the restriction

E|_{C}
to a smooth anticanonical curve
C\subset Z
is a simple vector bundle by
[
44
, Lemma 3.6]
.
Viewed as a vector bundle on the elliptic curve

C
, it has rank
r(E)
and degree

d(E)
.
By Atiyah’s classification of vector bundles on elliptic curves
[
6
]
, a vector bundle on

C
is simple if and only if its rank and degree are coprime. Hence

r(E)
and
d(E)
are coprime, which proves the claim.
∎

An
exceptional collection

\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{m})
is a sequence of exceptional objects
E_{i}
in
D(Z)
such that
\operatorname{Hom}^{k}(E_{j},E_{i})=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant m
. An exceptional pair is an exceptional collection consisting of two objects. An exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}
is
full
if the smallest full triangulated subcategory of
D(Z)
containing all objects of
\mathbb{E}
is
D(Z)
. If
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
is a full exceptional collection, then
([E_{i}])_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
is a basis of
K(Z)
, and so the length
n
of
\mathbb{E}
has to be equal to the rank of
K(Z)
. Conversely, since
Z
is a del Pezzo surface, any exceptional collection of length
n=\mathrm{rk}\,K(Z)
is full by
[
44
, Theorem 6.11]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
(E_{1},E_{2})
is an exceptional pair in
D(Z)
. Then

(19)

\chi(E_{1},E_{2})=r(E_{1})d(E_{2})-r(E_{2})d(E_{1}).

Moreover, if
(E_{1},E_{2})
is an exceptional pair of sheaves on
Z
, then
\operatorname{Ext}^{2}(E_{1},E_{2})=0
and at most one of the spaces
\operatorname{Ext}^{k}(E_{1},E_{2})
with
k=0,1
is non-zero.

Proof.

Since
(E_{1},E_{2})
is an exceptional pair, we have
\chi(E_{2},E_{1})=0
, and so (
19
) follows from Lemma
3.1
(i).
The second part of Lemma
4.2
is precisely
[
44
, Corollary 2.11]

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(E_{1},E_{2})
be an exceptional pair of sheaves on
Z
, then the following holds:

(i)

\operatorname{Hom}(E_{1},E_{2})\neq 0\iff\mu(E_{1})<\mu(E_{2})
,

(ii)

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(E_{1},E_{2})\neq 0\iff\mu(E_{1})>\mu(E_{2})
,

(iii)

\operatorname{Ext}^{k}(E_{1},E_{2})=0\,\,\,\text{for all}\,\,\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\iff\mu(E_{1})=\mu(E_{2})
.

Proof.

The result follows immediately from Lemma
4.2
.
∎

4.2.
Geometric helices on del Pezzo surfaces

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface, and set
n:=\mathrm{rk}\,K(Z)
.
A
helix
on
Z
is a sequence of objects
\mathbb{H}=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
in
D(Z)
such that

(i)

for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
the corresponding thread
(E_{i+1},\ldots,E_{i+n})
is a full exceptional collection in
D(Z)
,

(ii)

E_{i+n}=E_{i}\otimes\omega_{Z}^{-1}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

By
[
14
, Remark 3.2]
(c), it is in fact sufficient to require that a single thread of

\mathbb{H}
is a full exceptional collection.
The following notion appears under various names in

[
9
,
15
,
14
,
35
,
34
,
51
]
. We adopt the terminology introduced in
[
14
]
.

Definition 4.4
.

A helix
\mathbb{H}=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
on
Z
is
geometric
if for all
i<j

\operatorname{Hom}^{k}(E_{i},E_{j})=0\text{ unless }k=0.

The following exceptional collections are precisely the threads of geometric helices. We follow the terminology introduced in
[
48
]
.

Definition 4.5
.

A
very strong exceptional collection
on
Z
is a full exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
such that

\operatorname{Hom}^{k}(E_{i},E_{j}\otimes\omega_{Z}^{-p})=0,

for all
k\neq 0
when
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
and
p\geq 0
.

Recall that an exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\cdots,E_{n})
is
strong
if
\operatorname{Hom}^{k}(E_{i},E_{j})=0
for all
k\neq 0
and
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
. A
cyclic strong
exceptional collection is a full exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}
such that, denoting by
(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
the corresponding helix, the exceptional collection
(E_{i+1},\dots,E_{i+n})
is strong for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. A very strong exceptional collection is clearly cyclic strong. The following result shows that the converse also holds on del Pezzo surfaces.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface.

(i)

Suppose
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}=(E^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,E^{\prime}_{n})
is a cyclic strong exceptional collection on
Z
. Then there is a unique
p\in\mathbb{Z}
such that all objects
E_{i}=E^{\prime}_{i}[p]
lie in
\operatorname{Coh}(Z)
. Moreover, each
E_{i}
is a vector bundle.

(ii)

If
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\ldots,E_{n})
is a cyclic strong exceptional collection on
Z
consisting of vector bundles then
\mathbb{E}
is very strong precisely if

(20)

\mu(E_{1})\leqslant\mu(E_{2})\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\mu(E_{n})\leqslant\mu(E_{1})+K_{Z}^{2}.

(iii)

A full exceptional collection on
Z
is very strong if and only if it is cyclic strong.

Proof.

To prove the first part of (i), let
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}=(E^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,E^{\prime}_{n})
be a cyclic strong exceptional collection on
Z
. Suppose by contradiction that
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
is not the global shift of a very strong exceptional collection of sheaves. Then, since
D(Z)
is indecomposable by
[
37
, Proposition 3.10]
, there exist
i<j
such that
E_{i}^{\prime}
,
E_{j}^{\prime}
are shifts of sheaves by different integers and
\operatorname{Hom}(E_{i}^{\prime},E_{j}^{\prime})\neq 0
. Up to a applying a global shift to the collection, we may assume that
E_{i}^{\prime}
is a sheaf, and so
E_{j}^{\prime}=E_{j}[k]
where
E_{j}
is a sheaf and
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
\mathrm{Ext}^{k}(E_{i}^{\prime},E_{j})=\operatorname{Hom}(E_{i}^{\prime},E_{j}[k])=\operatorname{Hom}(E_{i}^{\prime},E_{j}^{\prime})\neq 0
. Since
(E_{i}^{\prime},E_{j})
is an exceptional pair of sheaves, Lemma
4.2
implies that
k=1
and Lemma
4.3
(ii) implies that
\mu(E_{i}^{\prime})>\mu(E_{j})
.
Consider now the helix
(E_{\ell}^{\prime})_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}
generated by
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
. By construction, we have
\mu(E_{i+n}^{\prime})=\mu(E_{i}^{\prime}\otimes\omega_{Z}^{-1})=\mu(E_{i}^{\prime})+K_{Z}^{2}>\mu(E_{j})
.
On the other hand, since
j<i+n<j+n
and
(E_{j}^{\prime},\dots,E_{j+n-1}^{\prime})
is an exceptional collection,
(E_{j},E_{i+n}^{\prime})
is an exceptional pair of sheaves.
Applying Lemma
4.3
(i), we deduce that
\operatorname{Hom}(E_{j},E_{i+n}^{\prime})\neq 0
and so

\operatorname{Hom}^{1}(E_{j}^{\prime},E_{i+n}^{\prime})=\operatorname{Hom}^{1}(E_{j}[1],E_{i+n}^{\prime})=\operatorname{Hom}(E_{j},E_{i+n}^{\prime})\neq 0
, in contradiction with the fact that

(E_{j}^{\prime},\dots,E_{j+n-1}^{\prime})
is strong.

To prove the second part of (ii), let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a cyclic strong exceptional collection of sheaves. Assume by contradiction that
\mathbb{E}
contains torsion sheaves. Since
\mathbb{E}

is full, it cannot consist entirely of torsion sheaves. Moreover,
D(Z)
is indecomposable, and so there exists
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
such that
E_{i}
is torsion free,
E_{j}
is torsion and either
\operatorname{Hom}(E_{i},E_{j})\neq 0
or
\operatorname{Hom}(E_{j},E_{i})\neq 0
. Since there are no non-zero maps from a torsion sheaf to a torsion free sheaf, we have
\operatorname{Hom}(E_{j},E_{i})=0
, so
\operatorname{Hom}(E_{i},E_{j})\neq 0
and necessarily
i<j
.
As above, consider now the helix
(E_{\ell}^{\prime})_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}
generated by
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
. Then,
(E_{j},E_{i+n})
is an exceptional pair of sheaves in the exceptional collection
(E_{j},\dots,E_{j+n-1})
, with
E_{i+n}=E_{i}\otimes\omega_{Z}^{-1}
torsion free, and so with
\mu(E_{j})=\infty>\mu(E_{i+n})
.
Then Lemma
4.3
(ii) implies that

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}(E_{j},E_{i+n})\neq 0
, contradicting the assumption that
(E_{j},\dots,E_{j+n-1})
is strong. This concludes the proof of (i).

Statement (ii) follows immediately from Lemma
4.3
using that
\mu(E_{i}\otimes\omega_{Z}^{-1})=\mu(E_{i})+K_{Z}^{2}
, and
K_{Z}^{2}>0
since
Z
is a del Pezzo surface.

Finally, we prove (iii). The implication from a very strong to cyclic strong is immediate.
Conversely, let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a cyclic strong exceptional collection. By (i)-(ii),
we may assume that
E_{i}
’s are vector bundles satisfying

\mu(E_{1})\leqslant\dots\leqslant\mu(E_{n})\leqslant\mu(E_{1})+K_{Z}^{2}
.
To show that
\mathbb{E}
is very strong, let
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
and
p\geq 0
. If
p=0
, then
\operatorname{Hom}^{k}(E_{i},E_{j})=0
for
k\neq 0
since
\mathbb{E}
is strong. If
p>0
, then
\mu(E_{i})\leqslant\mu(E_{1})+K_{Z}^{2}\leqslant\mu(E_{j})+K_{Z}^{2}\leqslant\mu(E_{j})+pK_{Z}^{2}=\mu(E_{j}\otimes\omega_{Z}^{-p})
. Hence, by Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.3
(i), we obtain that
\operatorname{Hom}^{k}(E_{i},E_{j}\otimes\omega_{Z}^{-p})=0
for all
k\neq 0
.
Therefore,
\mathbb{E}
is very strong, completing the proof.
∎

The following result shows that very strong exceptional collections and geometric helices exist on all del Pezzo surfaces.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface. Then,

(i)

there exists a geometric helix, and so very strong exceptional collections, on
Z
,

(ii)

there exists a very strong exceptional collection of line bundles on
Z
if and only if
\mathrm{rk}\,NS(Z)\leqslant 7
, that is, if and only if
Z
is not isomorphic to a blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in seven or eight points.

Proof.

Statement (i) follows from the proof of
[
9
, Proposition 7.3]
, which in turn relies on
[
44
, Claim 6.5]
.
Alternatively, it can be deduced from
[
14
, Example 8.6]
, where explicit examples of very strong exceptional collections are constructed on all del Pezzo surfaces, using the existence of three-block exceptional collections on del Pezzo surfaces which are blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
at
m\geq 3
points
[
42
, Proposition 4.2]
.

Statement (ii) holds for cyclic strong exceptional collections by
[
36
, Theorems 5.13-5.14]
, and so for very strong exceptional collections by Proposition
4.6
(iii).
∎

4.3.
Geometric helices and tilting operations

Let
\mathbb{H}=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a geometric helix on a del Pezzo surface
Z
. Then, one can produce new geometric helices
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}=(E_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
using the following operations:

(i)

Rotation
by
k\in\mathbb{Z}
:
E_{i}^{\prime}=E_{i+k}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(ii)

Shift
in the derived category by

k\in\mathbb{Z}
:
E_{i}^{\prime}=E_{i}[k]
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(iii)

Orthogonal reordering
: there exists
j\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\operatorname{Hom}^{\bullet}(E_{j},E_{j+1})=\operatorname{Hom}^{\bullet}(E_{j+1},E_{j})=0
: then,
E_{i}^{\prime}=E_{i}
for
i\notin\{j,j+1\}
,
E_{j}^{\prime}=E_{j+1}
,
E_{j+1}^{\prime}=E_{j}
.

(iv)

Derived dualization
:
E_{i}^{\prime}=E_{-i}^{\vee}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
E_{-i}^{\vee}
is the derived dual
R\mathscr{H}om(E_{-i},\mathcal{O}_{Z})
.

(v)

Tensor product by a line bundle

L\in\operatorname{Pic}(Z)
:
E_{i}^{\prime}=E_{i}\otimes L
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

In this section, we review the notion of tilting for geometric helices, which is a more interesting operation on the set of geometric helices introduced in
[
14
]
; see also
[
35
, §5.1]
. We begin by recalling some properties of the dual exceptional collection.
By
[
14
, Lemma 2.5]
, for every exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
in
D(Z)
, there exists a unique exceptional collection

\mathbb{F}=(F_{n},\dots,F_{1})
in
D(Z)
, called the
dual exceptional collection
to
\mathbb{E}
, such that
\operatorname{Hom}^{k}(E_{i},F_{j})=\mathbb{C}
if
i=j
and
k=0
, and

\operatorname{Hom}^{k}(E_{i},F_{j})=0
else.
Note that the dual exceptional collection

\mathbb{F}=(F_{n},\dots,F_{1})
to a very strong exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})

is never very strong. Indeed, by
[
14
, Corollary 2.10]
and
[
14
, Lemma 2.5]
, we have
F_{1}=E_{1}
and
F_{n}=E_{n}\otimes\omega_{Z}[2]
. In particular,
\mathbb{F}
does not satisfy Proposition
4.6
(i).

.

Let

(i)

there exists sheaves

(F_{n}[p],\dots,F_{1}[p])=(F_{n}^{\prime}[2],\dots,F_{b}^{\prime}[2],F_{b-1}^{\prime}[1],\dots,F_{c}^{\prime}[1],F_{c-1}^{\prime},\dots,F_{1}^{\prime})\,.

Moreover,
\mu(F_{i}^{\prime})\geq\mu(F_{j}^{\prime})
for every
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
such that either

n\geq i>j\geq b
, or
b-1\geq i>j\geq c
, or
c-1\geq i>j\geq 1
.

(ii)

the vectors
(r(F_{i}),d(F_{i}))\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
are cyclically ordered in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, that is, there exist determinations
\theta_{i}
of the arguments of the complex numbers

such that

\theta_{1}\leqslant\theta_{2}\leqslant\dots\leqslant\theta_{n-1}\leqslant\theta_{n}\leqslant\theta_{1}+2\pi\,.

Proof.

Statement (i) follows from
[

, Lemma 8.3]
, together with

and

to determine the range of shifts. For (ii),
\theta_{1}\leqslant\theta_{2}\leqslant\dots\leqslant\theta_{n-1}\leqslant\theta_{n}
follows from (i). Finally, since
F_{1}=E_{1}
,
F_{n}=E_{n}\otimes\omega_{Z}[2]
, and
\mu(E_{n})\leqslant\mu(E_{1})+K_{Z}^{2}
by Proposition
4.6
(ii), we have

\mu(F_{n})=\mu(E_{n}\otimes\omega_{Z})=\mu(E_{n})-K_{Z}^{2}\leqslant\mu(E_{1})=\mu(F_{1})\,,

and so
\theta_{n}\leqslant\theta_{1}+2\pi
.
∎

considered in Example

.

We first define tilting operations on good very strong exceptional collections defined as follows.

Definition 4.10
.

A very strong exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{j},\dots,E_{n})
on a del Pezzo surface
Z
is called
good

for the object
E_{j}

if the dual exceptional collection
\mathbb{F}=(F_{n},\ldots,F_{j},\ldots F_{1})
has the following property:

k>j\implies\operatorname{Hom}^{p}(F_{k},F_{j})=0\text{ unless }p=1,

k<j\implies\operatorname{Hom}^{p}(F_{j},F_{k})=0\text{ unless }p=1\,.

We denote by
\langle-,-\rangle
the pullback by

\psi=(r,d):K(Z)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}
of

\langle-,-\rangle=\det(-,-)
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Lemma 4.11
.

A very strong exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{j},\dots,E_{n})
on a del Pezzo surface
Z
is good
for the object
E_{j}

if and only if the dual exceptional collection
\mathbb{F}=(F_{n},\ldots,F_{j},\ldots F_{1})
has the following property:

k>j\implies\langle[F_{k}],[F_{j}]\rangle\leqslant 0\,,

k<j\implies\langle[F_{k}],[F_{j}]\rangle\geq 0,.

Proof.

For
k>j
, the pair
(F_{k},F_{j})
is exceptional, and so
\chi(F_{j},F_{k})=0
, and
\chi(F_{k},F_{j})=\langle[F_{k}],[F_{j}]\rangle
by Lemma
4.2
. Moreover, by Lemma
4.2
, at most one of the spaces
\operatorname{Hom}^{p}(F_{k},F_{j})
is non-zero, and so the result follows.
∎

Recall that for any objects
E
,
F
in
D(Z)
, the left and right mutations
L_{F}E
and
R_{F}E
are the unique objects in
D(Z)
up to isomorphism for which there exist exact triangles

(21)

\operatorname{Hom}^{\bullet}(F,E)\otimes F\xrightarrow{ev}E\rightarrow L_{F}E\,,

and

R_{F}E\rightarrow E\xrightarrow{coev}\operatorname{Hom}^{\bullet}(E,F)^{\star}\otimes F\,.

Definition 4.12
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface, and
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{j},\dots,E_{n})
be a very strong exceptional collection on
Z
which is good for
E_{j}
. Then, the left

tilt
of
\mathbb{E}
from
j
to
1
is

(22)

\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{E})=(L_{E_{1}}\dots L_{E_{j-1}}E_{j}[-1],E_{1},\dots,E_{j-1},E_{j+1},\dots,E_{n})

and the right
tilt
of
\mathbb{E}
from
1
to
j
is

(23)

\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{E})=(E_{2},\dots,E_{j},R_{E_{j}}\dots R_{E_{2}}E_{1}[1],E_{j+1},\dots,E_{n})

respectively – see
[
14
, Remark 7.7]
.
By
[
14
, Theorem 7.4]
, both
\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{E})

and
\mu_{j,1}^{-}(\mathbb{E})
are very strong exceptional collections.

By construction,
\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{E})=(E_{1}^{\prime},\dots,E_{n}^{\prime})
is good for
E_{j}^{\prime}=E_{j-1}
, and

\mu_{1,j}^{-}\circ\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{E})=\mathbb{E}\,.

Similarly,
\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{E})=(E_{1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,E_{n}^{\prime\prime})
is good for
E_{j}^{\prime\prime}=R_{E_{j}}\dots R_{E_{2}}E_{1}[1]
, and

\mu_{j,1}^{+}\circ\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{E})=\mathbb{E}\,.

The following result determines the
corresponding dual exceptional collections.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{j},\dots,E_{n})
be a very strong exceptional collection on a del Pezzo surface
Z
which is good for
E_{j}
.
Let
\mathbb{F}=(F_{n},\dots,F_{j},\dots,F_{1})
be the dual exceptional collection of
\mathbb{E}
.
Then, the dual exceptional collection of
\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{E})
is

(F_{n},\dots,F_{j+1},L_{F_{j}[-1]}F_{j-1},\dots,L_{F_{j}[-1]}F_{1},F_{j}[-1])\,,

and the dual exceptional collection of

\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{E})

is

(F_{n},\dots,F_{j+1},F_{1}[1],R_{F_{1}[1]}F_{j},\dots,R_{F_{1}[1]}F_{2})\,.

Proof.

This follows by direct calculation using
[
14
, Lemma 2.5]
which states that the dual exceptional collection
(F_{n},\dots,F_{1})
of an exceptional collection
(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
is given by
F_{j}=L_{E_{1}}\dots L_{E_{j-1}}E_{j}
for all

.
∎

To define tilting operations on geometric helices, we will need to find good threads in helices. To do this, we will use the following result.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a thread with dual collection
\mathbb{F}=(F_{n},\dots,F_{1})
in a helix
\mathbb{H}=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
on a del Pezzo surface
Z
.
Consider the neighboring thread
(E_{0},\dots,E_{n-1})
and let
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}=(F_{n-1}^{\prime},\dots,F_{0}^{\prime})

be its dual collection.
Then the classes in
K(Z)
of the objects of
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}

are given by

[F_{i}^{\prime}]=T_{[F_{n}]}([F_{i}])
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
and
[F_{0}^{\prime}]=T_{[F_{n}]}([F_{n}])=[F_{n}]
,
where, for any
\alpha\in K(Z)
,
the linear map
T_{\alpha}:K(Z)\rightarrow K(Z)
is defined by

(24)

T_{\alpha}(\beta)=\beta+\langle\beta,\alpha\rangle\alpha\,.

Proof.

By
[
14
, Lemma 3.4]
, we have

\mathbb{F}^{\prime}=(L_{F_{n}}F_{n-1},\dots,L_{F_{n}}F_{1},F_{n}[-2])\,,

and so
[F_{0}^{\prime}]=[F_{n}]
, and for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n-1
,

[F_{i}^{\prime}]=[F_{i}]-\chi(F_{n},F_{i})[F_{n}]\,.

For
n>i
, the pair
(F_{n},F_{i})
is exceptional, so
\chi(F_{i},F_{n})=0
, and so

\chi(F_{n},F_{i})=\langle[F_{n}],[F_{i}]\rangle=-\langle[F_{i}],[F_{n}]\rangle

by Lemma
4.2
. Hence, we obtain

[F_{i}^{\prime}]=[F_{i}]+\langle[F_{i}],[F_{n}]\rangle[F_{n}]
, and this
completes the proof.
∎

The proof of the following proposition is a reformulation of the argument given in
[
14
, §8]
– see also
[

, §5.1]
.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
\mathbb{H}=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}

be a geometric helix on a del Pezzo surface
Z
and choose an element
E_{j}
of
\mathbb{H}
.
Then there is a thread
\mathbb{E}=(E_{i+1},\ldots,E_{j},\ldots,E_{i+n})
of
\mathbb{H}
which is good for
E_{j}
.

Proof.

Start with any thread
\mathbb{E}=(E_{i+1},\dots,E_{i+n})
of
\mathbb{H}
containing
E_{j}
. Let
\mathbb{F}=(F_{i+n},\dots,F_{i+1})
be the dual exceptional collection. By Proposition
4.8
(ii), the vectors
(r(F_{i+k}),d(F_{i+k}))_{0\leqslant k\leqslant n}
are cyclically ordered in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
By Lemma
4.14
, rotating
\mathbb{E}
acts on the set of vectors
(r(F_{i+k}),d(F_{i+k}))_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n}
by an element of
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
and by cyclic relabeling.
Therefore, up to replacing
\mathbb{E}
by a different thread in
\mathbb{H}
, one can assume that
k>j
implies
\{F_{k},F_{j}\}\leqslant 0
and that
k<j
implies

\{F_{k},F_{j}\}\geq 0
. Then,
\mathbb{E}
is good for
E_{j}
by Lemma
4.3
.
∎

We can finally define tilting operations on geometric helices.

Definition 4.16
.

Let
\mathbb{H}=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a geometric helix on a del Pezzo surface

. For every

, there exists a thread
\mathbb{E}
of
\mathbb{H}
that is good for

by Proposition

. Then, we define the left and right
tilts

\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{H})

and
\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{H})

of

at

as the helices generated by the very strong exceptional collection
\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{E})
and
\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{E})
respectively defined as in
(

)-(

).
By
[
14
, Theorem 7.4]
, the helices
\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{H})
and
\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{H})
are geometric.
Moreover, for every

, the tilted helices
\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{H})

and
\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{H})

are uniquely determined up to orthogonal reordering of objects of

, and only depend on

modulo

.

Lemma 4.17
.

Let
\mathbb{H}=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a geometric helix on a del Pezzo surface
Z
,
and let
p\in\mathbb{Z}
be as in Proposition
4.6
(i) the unique integer such that all objects
E_{i}[p]
lie in
\operatorname{Coh}(Z)
. Then, for every
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, and for every object
E_{i}^{\prime}
of the tilted helices
\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{H})

and
\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{H})
, the object
E_{i}^{\prime}[p]
lies in
\operatorname{Coh}(Z)
.

Proof.

Since
\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{H})
is a geometric helix, there exists a unique

such that all the objects

of
\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{H})

∎

5.
Classification of geometric helices on del Pezzo surfaces

In this section, we first explain how to associate a seed of
q
-Painlevé type to every very strong exceptional collection on a del Pezzo surface. We then prove that tilting of geometric helices generated by very strong exceptional collections is compatible with seed mutations. Next, we show that the affine Weyl group acts on the set of very strong exceptional collections via sequences of tilting operations and orthogonal reorderings. Finally, we prove the main result of this paper, Theorem
5.19
, referred to as Theorem
1.1
in the Introduction, which shows that all geometric helices on a del Pezzo surface are related to each other by a sequence of elementary operations.

5.1.
Very strong exceptional collections and seeds of
q
-Painlevé type

In this section, we first explain how to construct seeds from very strong exceptional collections on a del Pezzo surface
Z
. We then show in Theorem
5.6
that these seeds are of
q
-Painlevé type in the sense reviewed in §
2.5
.
We will apply the notation and results of
\lx@sectionsign\ref{sec_seeds_mutations}
with
N=K(Z)
and
\psi=(r,d):N=K(Z)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Note that
\psi
has indeed a finite cokernel by the following elementary result.

Lemma 5.1
.

If the del Pezzo surface
Z
is not isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{2}
or
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
, then the map
\psi=(r,d):K(Z)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is surjective.
If
Z=\mathbb{P}^{2}
(resp.​
Z=\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
), then the cokernel of
\psi
is a finite group of order two (resp.​ three).

Proof.

Since
\psi([\mathcal{O}_{Z}])=(1,0)
, the vector
(1,0)
always lies in the image of
K(Z)
. If
Z
is not isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{2}
or
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
, then
Z
contains an exceptional curve
E
. In this case,
\psi([\mathcal{O}_{E}])=(0,1)
, so
(0,1)
is also in the image of
K(Z)
, and
\psi
is surjective.
If
Z=\mathbb{P}^{2}
(resp.
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
), we have
K_{Z}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(-3)
(resp.​
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-2,-2)
) and so the result follows.
∎

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a very strong exceptional collection on a del Pezzo surface
Z
. Let
\mathbb{F}=(F_{n},\dots,F_{1})
be the dual exceptional collection.
The
seed
associated to
\mathbb{E}
is the multiset
\{[F_{i}]\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
of classes in
N=K(Z)
:

\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}):=\{[F_{i}]\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\,.

The
cyclically ordered
seed
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbb{E})
associated to
\mathbb{E}
is the seed
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
together with the cyclic ordering induced by the ordering of
\mathbb{E}
.

Remark 5.3
.

By Lemma
4.1
, the vectors
\psi([F_{i}])
are non-zero and primitive in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Moreover, since
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{F}
are full exceptional collections, the classes
([F_{i}])_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
form a basis of
K(Z)
. Hence,
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
is indeed a seed in the sense of §
2.1
. By Proposition
4.8
, the vectors
\psi([F_{i}])
are cyclically ordered in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Therefore,
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbb{E})
is indeed a cyclically ordered seed in the sense of (
1
) in §
2.1
.

We defined in §
2.3
a bilinear form
\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}(-,-)
on
N
for every cyclically ordered seed – see (
8
). On the other hand, as reviewed in §
3.1
,
K(Z)
carries a natural Euler form
\chi(-,-)
.
The result below shows that these two bilinear form coincide when
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbb{E})
is the cyclically ordered seed associated with a very strong exceptional collection.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a very strong exceptional collection on a del Pezzo surfaces
Z
. Then, the bilinear forms
\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbb{E})}(-,-)
and
\chi(-,-)
on
N=K(Z)
coincide:

\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbb{E})}(-,-)=\chi(-,-)\,.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{F}=(F_{n},\dots,F_{1})
be the dual exceptional collection.
Since
([F_{i}])_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
forms a basis of
K(Z)
, it suffices to prove that
\chi_{\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})}([F_{i}],[F_{j}])=\chi(F_{i},F_{j})
for all
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
. If
i=j
, then
\chi(F_{i},F_{i})=1
because
F_{i}
is an exceptional object.
If
i>j
, the pair
(F_{i},F_{j})
is exceptional and so

\chi(F_{j},F_{i})=0
, and
\chi(F_{i},F_{j})=\langle[F_{i}],[F_{j}]\rangle
by Lemma
4.2
. The result then follows by comparison with the definition of

\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbb{E})}(-,-)
given in (
8
).
∎

As a result, we obtain the following description of the intersection form
(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}
associated by §
2.3
to the mutation equivalence class
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})
of the seed
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a very strong exceptional collection on a del Pezzo surface
Z
. Then, the intersection form
(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}
on
K=\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)
coincides with minus the restriction of the Euler form: for every
\alpha,\beta\in K=\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)
, we have

(\alpha,\beta)_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}=-\chi(\alpha,\beta)\,.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
, we have
(\alpha,\beta)_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}=-\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbb{E})}(\alpha,\beta)
for all
\alpha,\beta\in K
. By Lemma
5.4
, we also have
\chi_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbb{E})}(\alpha,\beta)=\chi(\alpha,\beta)
for all
\alpha
,
\beta\in K(Z)
, and so the result follows.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a very strong exceptional collection on a del Pezzo surface
Z
. Then, the mutation equivalence class of seeds
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})
is of
q
-Painlevé type.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.5
, we have
(K,(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})})\simeq(\mathrm{Ker}(\psi),-\chi(-,-)|_{\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)})
.
On the other hand, we have
\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)=K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta

by (
12
).
By Lemma
3.1
(iii),
\mathbb{Z}\delta
is the kernel of
-\chi(-,-)|_{\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)}
and
-\chi(-,-)|_{K_{Z}^{\perp}}
is the intersection form on
K_{Z}^{\perp}
, which is negative definite by the Hodge index theorem. Thus, the intersection form
(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}
is negative semi-definite but not negative definite.
Hence, the mutation equivalence class of seeds
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})
is of

q
-Painlevé type by Definition
2.12
.
∎

By (
10
) in §
2.5
, for every seed
\mathbf{s}=\{e_{i}\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
whose mutation equivalence class
\mathscr{S}
is of
q
-Painlevé type, there exists a unique primitive element
\delta_{\mathscr{S}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{\mathbf{s},i}e_{i}
with
c_{\mathbf{s},i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
and
(\delta_{\mathscr{S}},\delta_{\mathscr{S}})_{\mathscr{S}}=0
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a very strong exceptional collection of bundles on a del Pezzo surface
Z
. Then, we have
\delta=\delta_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}

and
r(E_{i})=c_{\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}),i}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{F}=(F_{n},\dots,F_{1})
be the dual exceptional collection.
Write
\delta=[\mathcal{O}_{x}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}[F_{i}]
with
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then, applying
\chi(E_{j},-)
and using the duality between
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{F}
, we obtain

r(E_{j})=\chi(E_{j},\mathcal{O}_{x})=\chi(E_{j},\delta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\chi(E_{j},F_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\delta_{ji}=a_{j}\,.

Hence
\delta=\sum_{j=1}^{n}r(E_{j})[F_{j}]
.
Moreover, we have
r(E_{j})\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
since the
E_{j}
’s are bundles by assumption,
and
(\delta,\delta)_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}=-\chi(\delta,\delta)=0
by Lemma
5.5
. Since in addition
\delta
is a primitive element of
K(Z)
, it follows that
\delta=\delta_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}
and
r(E_{j})=c_{\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}),j}
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
by uniqueness of
\delta_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}
and
(c_{\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}),j})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}
.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

As reviewed in §
2.5
, when
\mathscr{S}
is of
q
-Painlevé type, the lattice
(K,(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}})
is isometric to a lattice of the form
\mathbb{Z}\delta_{\mathscr{S}}\oplus Q_{\mathscr{S}}
, where
Q_{\mathscr{S}}
is negative definite. By the proof of Theorem
5.6
, we obtain an isomorphism
Q_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}\simeq K_{Z}^{\perp}
.

5.2.
Tilting operations and seed mutations

In this section, we describe the compatibility between the seed mutations introduced in
§
2.1
and the tilting operations of geometric helices reviewed in §
4.3
. We begin by explaining the compatibility with tilting operations of good very strong exceptional collections in the sense of Definition
4.10
.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a very strong exceptional collection on a del Pezzo surface
Z
. Assume that
\mathbb{E}
is good for the object
E_{j}
for some
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
. Then, tilts of
\mathbb{E}
commute with seed mutations of
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
, that is,

\mathbf{s}(\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{E}))=\mu_{j}^{+}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}))\text{ and }\mathbf{s}(\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{E}))=\mu_{1}^{-}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}))\,.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.13
, the classes

[F_{i}^{\prime}]
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
,
of the objects of the
exceptional collection dual to
\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{E})
are given by
[F_{j}^{\prime}]=-[F_{j}]
, and for every
i\neq j
,
[F_{i}^{\prime}]=[F_{i}]-\chi(F_{j},F_{i})[F_{j}]
. For
j\geq i
, the pair
(F_{j},F_{i})
is exceptional, so
\chi(F_{i},F_{j})=0
and
\chi(F_{j},F_{i})=\langle[F_{j}],[F_{i}]\rangle=-\langle[F_{i}],[F_{j}]\rangle\leqslant 0
by Lemmas
4.2
-
4.3
. Hence, for all
i\neq j
, we obtain

[F_{i}^{\prime}]=[F_{i}]+[\langle[F_{i}],[F_{j}]\rangle]_{+}[F_{j}]\,,

and so the result follows by comparison with the formula (
2
) describing seed mutations. The result for
\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{E})
is obtained analogously.
∎

In the following result, we denote by
\mathrm{Aut}_{K}(K(Z),\langle-,-\rangle)
the group of automorphisms of
K(Z)
preserving the skew-symmetric bilinear form
\langle-,-\rangle
and acting trivially on
K=\mathrm{Ker}(\langle-,-\rangle)
.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\mathbb{H}=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a geometric helix on a del Pezzo surface
Z
. Let
\mathbb{E}
be a thread of
\mathbb{H}
containing the object
E_{j}
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Then, there exist threads
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}_{+}
and
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}_{-}
in the tilted helices
\mu_{j,1}^{+}(\mathbb{H})
and
\mu_{1,j}^{-}(\mathbb{E})
respectively, and
f\in\mathrm{Aut}_{K}(K(Z),\langle-,-\rangle)
such that

\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}^{\prime}_{+})=f(\mu_{j}^{+}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})))\text{ and }\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}^{\prime}_{-})=f(\mu_{1}^{-}(\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})))\,.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.15
, there exists a thread
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}
of
\mathbb{H}
which is good with respect to
E_{j}
. Since
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}
is obtained from
\mathbb{E}
by rotation in
\mathbb{H}
, there exists
f\in\mathrm{Aut}(K(Z))
such that
\mathbf{s}(\widetilde{\mathbb{E}})=f(\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}))
by Lemma
4.14
. More precisely,
f

5.3.
Affine Weyl groups and tilting operations

We introduced in Definitions

3.2
-
3.6

the affine Weyl group
\widehat{W}(Z)
and the orthogonal
O(Z)
of a del Pezzo surface
Z
, which both act by isometries on the Grothendieck group
K(Z)
. In this section, we first show that
O(Z)
admits a natural action on the set of geometric helices. We then prove that, for any very strong exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}
on
Z
, the group
\widehat{W}(Z)
coincides with the Weyl group
W_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}
associated in §
2.4
to the mutation equivalence class
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})
of the seed
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
. As an application, Theorem
5.15
shows that the action of
\widehat{W}(Z)\subset O(Z)
on geometric helices can be described in terms of tilting operations and orthogonal reorderings.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\mathbb{H}=(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a geometric helix on a del Pezzo surface
Z
,
and let
p\in\mathbb{Z}
be as in Proposition
4.6
(i) the unique integer such that all objects

lie in
\operatorname{Coh}(Z)
. For every

, there exists a unique geometric helix

such that
[E_{i}^{\prime}]=w([E_{i}])
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, and all objects
E_{i}^{\prime}[p]
lie in
\operatorname{Coh}(Z)
.

Proof.

Up to a global shift, we may assume that
p=0
, so that
\mathbb{H}
is a geometric helix of sheaves. By
[
25
, Corollary 2.5]
, exceptional sheaves are uniquely determined by their classes in
K(Z)
; this immediately yields the uniqueness of
w\cdot\mathbb{H}
. It therefore remains to establish existence.
By Proposition
3.10
, we have a decomposition
O(Z)=W(Z)\ltimes\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
. Since
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
acts on geometric helices by tensor product, it suffices to construct the action for elements
w\in W(Z)
.
Let
\mathbb{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
be a very strong exceptional collection arising as a thread of
\mathbb{H}
. By Proposition
[
42
, Proposition 5.3]
, there exists a full exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}=(E_{1}^{\prime},\dots,E_{n}^{\prime})
such that
[E_{i}^{\prime}]=w([E_{i}])
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. In particular, we have
r(E_{i}^{\prime})=r(E_{i})
and

, hence
\mu(E_{i}^{\prime})=\mu(E_{i})
for all

.
It then follows from Proposition
4.6
(ii) that

to be the geometric helix generated by
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 5.12
.

The proof of
[
42
, Proposition 5.3]
given by Karpov–Nogin, which establishes the existence of the action of
W(Z)
on the set of full exceptional collections of sheaves on a del Pezzo surface
Z
, relies on the fact that all
full exceptional collections are related by mutations
[
44
, Theorem 7.7]
.
An alternative, more elementary and geometric argument can be obtained by interpreting
W(Z)
as a monodromy group of the moduli space of del Pezzo surfaces, together with the observation that exceptional collections on such surfaces deform in smooth families. Indeed, the reflection with respect to a
(-2)
-curve class
\alpha\in K_{Z}^{\perp}
is exactly the monodromy around a small loop in the moduli space of del Pezzo surfaces encircling the locus where
\alpha
becomes effective.

As reviewed in §
4.3
, given a geometric helix
on a del Pezzo surface, one can produce new geometric helices

in the Introduction, shows conversely that any two geometric helices are related by a sequence of these operations.

Theorem 5.19
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface. Any two geometric helices on

rotation, shifting, orthogonal reordering, derived dualization, tensoring by a line bundle, and tilting.

\mathbb{H}

By Proposition

\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
are geometric helices of bundles.
We prove that
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}

are related by rotation, orthogonal reordering, derived dualization, tensoring by a line bundle, and tilting. These operations preserve geometric helices of bundles: this is immediate for rotation, orthogonal reordering, derived dualization, tensoring by a line bundle, and follows from Lemma
4.17
in the case of tilting.

Let
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
be very strong exceptional collections obtained as threads of
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
respectively.
Consider the associated seeds
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
and
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
as in Definition
5.2
.
By Theorem
5.6
, their mutation

\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})
and
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E}^{\prime})
are both of
q
-Painlevé type.
Moreover, Lemma
5.5
shows that
(K,(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})})
and

(K,(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E}^{\prime})})
are both isometric to

(K_{Z}^{\perp}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\delta,-\chi(-,-))\,.

By Theorem
2.14
(
[
47
, Corollary 3.33]
) together with the correspondence between seed mutations and tilting operations of geometric helices
given in Proposition
5.10
,
there exists a thread
\mu(\mathbb{E})
in a geometric helix obtained from
\mathbb{H}
by a sequence of tilting operations and orthogonal reorderings, and an automorphism

g\in GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
such that

\psi(\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}^{\prime}))=g\circ\psi(\mu(\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})))\,.

Replacing
\mathbb{E}
by
\mu(\mathbb{E})
, we may assume that

(30)

\psi(\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}^{\prime}))=g\circ\psi(\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}))\,.

We next arrange that
g
lies in
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
If
\mathrm{det}(g)=-1
, consider the derived dual collection
\mathbb{E}^{\vee}
. Then,
\psi(\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}))=\rho\circ\psi(\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E}^{\vee}))
, where
\rho\in GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
is the reflection
(r,d)\mapsto(r,-d)
.
Thus, replacing
g
by
g\circ\rho
, and
\mathbb{E}
by
\mathbb{E}^{\vee}
, we may always assume that (
30
) still holds and now with
g\in SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

We then apply Lemma
5.16
and Lemma
5.17
: after replacing
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
by a thread of a helix obtained from
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
by a sequence of
rotations, orthogonal reorderings, and tensorings by a line bundle, we may arrange that

\psi([F_{i}^{\prime}])=\psi([F_{i}])\text{ for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant n\,,

where
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}=(F_{n}^{\prime},\dots,F_{1}^{\prime})
and
\mathbb{F}=(F_{n},\dots,F_{1})
are the exceptional collections dual to
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{E}
respectively.

Lemma
5.18
then shows that there exists an element
g\in O(Z)
of the
orthogonal group of
Z
such that
[E_{i}^{\prime}]=g([E_{i}])
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
By Proposition
3.10
, we have
O(Z)=W(Z)\ltimes\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
, where
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
acts on
K(Z)
by tensor product with line bundles.
Therefore, up to tensoring
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
by a line bundle, we may assume that
g
is an element of the finite Weyl group
W(Z)
, and so in particular of the affine Weyl group
\widehat{W}(Z)
.
By Theorem
5.15
,
there exists a very strong exceptional collection
\mathbb{E}^{\prime\prime}=(E_{1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,E_{n}^{\prime\prime})
such that

[E_{i}^{\prime\prime}]=g([E_{i}])\,\,\,\text{for all}\,\,\,1\leqslant i\leqslant n\,.

Moreover, the geometric helix generated by
\mathbb{E}^{\prime\prime}
is obtained from the geometric
helix generated by
\mathbb{E}
by a sequence of tilting operations and orthogonal reorderings.
Therefore, up to replacing
\mathbb{E}
by
\mathbb{E}^{\prime\prime}
, we may assume that
[E_{i}^{\prime}]=[E_{i}]
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.

Finally, by
[
25
, Corollary 2.5]
, an exceptional sheaf is uniquely determined by its class in
K(Z)
. Therefore, since
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
are very strong exceptional collections of bundles,
[E_{i}^{\prime}]=[E_{i}]
implies
E_{i}^{\prime}=E_{i}
, so
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}
and this completes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.20
.

The group of auto-equivalences
\operatorname{Aut}(D(Z))
of
D(Z)
naturally acts on the set of geometric helices.
Since
\omega_{Z}^{-1}
is ample, it follows from
[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
that

\operatorname{Aut}(D(Z))=\operatorname{Aut}(Z)\rtimes(\operatorname{Pic}(Z)\times\mathbb{Z}[1])
, where
\operatorname{Aut}(Z)
is the group of automorphisms of
Z
.
We now explain why
\operatorname{Aut}(Z)
does not appear in the statement of Theorem
5.19
.
First, the exceptional objects are rigid, and so the connected component
\operatorname{Aut}^{0}(Z)
of the identity acts trivially on exceptional collections. Hence, the action of
\operatorname{Aut}(Z)
factors through an action of the discrete quotient
\operatorname{Aut}(Z)/\operatorname{Aut}^{0}(Z)
.
By
[
19
, Proposition 8.2.39]
, this quotient injects via its action on
K_{Z}^{\perp}
in the finite Weyl group
W(Z)
.
By Theorem
5.15
,
W(Z)
acts on geometric helices via tilting operations and orthogonal reorderings.
Thus, the action of
\operatorname{Aut}(Z)
is already accounted for by these operations.

5.5.
Orthogonal group and admissible isometries

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface and
\mathbb{E}
a very strong exceptional collection on
Z
. In this final section, we give an interpretation of the orthogonal group
O(Z)
of
Z
introduced in Definition
3.6
in terms of the geometry of a framed toric model
(Y,D)
for
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
as in §
2.2
. This result is not used in the proof of the main result in §
5.4
above but is of independent interest.

Given a log Calabi–Yau surface
(Y,D)
, consisting of a smooth projective surface
Y
and a singular reduced anticanonical divisor
D
, the group of
admissible isometries

\Gamma(Y,D)
of
(Y,D)
is the group of isometries of
NS(Y)
fixing the classes of the irreducible components of
D
and preserving the nef cone of a generic deformation of
D
– see
[
22
, Definition 9.1]
and
[
28
, Lemma 4.5]
. By
[
28
, Theorem 5.15]
,
\Gamma(Y,D)
is also the global monodromy group of deformations of
(Y,D)
.

For the following statement, observe that the restriction to
\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
as in (
4
) induces an injection of
\Gamma(Y,D)
in the group of isometries of
\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
since
\Gamma(Y,D)
fixes the classes of the irreducible components of
D
. Moreover, recall from (
5
) that there exists a natural identification
K\simeq\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
.

Proposition 5.21
.

Let
Z
be a del Pezzo surface,
\mathbb{E}
a very strong exceptional collection on
Z
and
(Y,D)
a framed toric model of the seed
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
. Then, the actions of the orthogonal group
O(Z)
and of the group of admissible isometries
\Gamma(Y,D)
on
K\simeq\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
induce an isomorphism

O(Z)\simeq\Gamma(Y,D)\,.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.6
, the seed
\mathbf{s}(\mathbb{E})
is of
q
-Painlevé type. In particular,
the intersection form
(-,-)_{\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{E})}
is negative semi-definite but not negative definite. Hence,
[
22
, Lemma 9.18]
applies: there exists an orthogonal decomposition
\Lambda_{(Y,D)}=\mathbb{Z}[D]\oplus\overline{\Lambda}
, together with an injection
\overline{\Lambda}\rightarrow\Gamma(Y,D)
, inducing a semi-direct product decomposition

\Gamma(Y,D)=G\ltimes\overline{\Lambda}\,,

where
G
is the group of isometries of
\overline{\Lambda}
which extend to isometries of a unimodular lattice
\Lambda_{0}
of type
E_{8}
containing
\overline{\Lambda}
as a saturated sublattice.
On the other hand, by Proposition
3.10
, we have

O(Z)=W(Z)\ltimes\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)\,.

Thus, to prove Proposition
5.21
, it suffices to identify these semi-direct product descriptions of
\Gamma(Y,D)
and
O(Z)
.
Using the identifications
\Lambda_{(Y,D)}=K=\mathrm{Ker}(\psi)=\mathbb{Z}\delta\oplus K_{Z}^{\perp}
, one checks that
\mathbb{Z}[D]=\mathbb{Z}\delta
and
\overline{\Lambda}=K_{Z}^{\perp}
.
Moreover, comparing (
18
) with the explicit description of the injection
\overline{\Lambda}\rightarrow\Gamma(Y,D)
given in the proof of
[
22
, Lemma 9.18]
, one sees that the action by tensor product of
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Z)
on
K_{Z}^{\perp}
coincides with the action of
\overline{\Lambda}
on
\Lambda_{(Y,D)}
.
It remains to identify
G
and
W(Z)
as groups acting by isometries on
\overline{\Lambda}=K_{Z}^{\perp}
. To do this, note that if
L
is a lattice, realized as a saturated sublattice of an unimodular lattice
L_{0}
, then the group of isometries of
L
that extends to isometries of
L_{0}
acting trivially on
L^{\perp}
is independent of
L_{0}
: indeed, it is
precisely the group of isometries of
L
acting trivially on the discriminant group
L^{\star}/L
. By definition,
G
is the group of isometries of
\overline{\Lambda}
that extend to isometries of the unimodular lattice
\Lambda_{0}
acting trivially on
\overline{\Lambda}^{\perp}
. On the other hand, by Proposition
3.5
,
W(Z)
is the precisely the group of isometries of
K_{Z}^{\perp}=\overline{\Lambda}
that extend to isometries of the unimodular lattice
NS(Z)
acting trivially on
\langle K_{Z}\rangle=(K_{Z}^{\perp})^{\perp}
. Hence, we conclude that
G=W(Z)
and this completes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.22
.

Let
\mathbf{s}
be a seed of
q
-Painlevé type and let
(Y,D)
be a framed toric model for
\mathbf{s}
. After contracting any exceptional curve contained in
D
, we may assume that no component of

Then, it follows from
[
47
, Proposition 3.22]
-
[
50
, Proposition 2]
that

in nine points, and that

(-2)
-curves

[
47
,
50
]
, the

The action of

.
While Proposition

admits a similarly natural interpretation in terms of

.
