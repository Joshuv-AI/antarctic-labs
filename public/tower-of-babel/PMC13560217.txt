Optimizing instrument selection during core decompression for osteonecrosis of the femoral head: a 3D analysis
Authors: Reza Bergemann, Ryan Mullan, Alexandra Massey, Steven Tommasini, Daniel Wiznia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCore decompression is the leading surgical treatment for pre-collapse osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH). There are many different instruments that are used during core decompression, ranging from drill bits to expandable reamers, yet there is limited guidance regarding how to select the appropriate instrument for an individual patient’s unique anatomy. We sought to examine how lesion characteristics affect instrument performance. To quantitatively compare instruments’ efficacy at removing necrotic bone while preserving healthy bone, we developed an in-silico metric called the resection fraction, defined as the volume of necrotic bone resected divided by the total volume of bone (healthy and necrotic) resected. We hypothesize the optimal instrument will vary with lesion size and type (ARCO 2021).MethodsCT-based 3D models of 100 femurs with ONFH were created from a retrospective database of patients who underwent 3D guided computer navigated core decompression at our institution. Using these models, core decompression procedures were simulated with six different instruments. 3D volumetric analysis was used to determine the volumes of necrotic and healthy bone resected, and the resection fraction was calculated. Statistical analysis was performed to compare instrument performance across all lesions, as well as by lesion size and type.ResultsA total of 100 hips from 75 unique patients with ONFH were modeled and each hip’s core decompression procedure was simulated with six different instruments. All three expandable reamers had higher resection fractions compared to straight instruments (drill bits). For small volume lesions, the small expandable reamer had the highest resection fractions. The medium expandable reamer had the highest resection fractions for larger lesions. The large expandable reamer had lower resection fractions across all sizes.ConclusionsCompared to straight instruments, expandable reamers had the highest resection fractions. To maximize resection fraction, surgeons should personalize instrument choice to patient anatomy; small expandable reamers should be used for smaller lesions, while medium-sized reamers should be used for larger lesions. The largest expandable reamer performed worse than either the small or medium reamer for almost all lesions. Further work is needed to validate the in-silico resection fraction metric with clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-07057-y.Keywords: Avascular necrosis, Osteonecrosis of the femoral head, Joint preservation, Core decompression

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is caused by impaired microvascular supply to the femoral head leading to bone death and subchondral fracture with potential progression to collapse of the femoral head [1–3]. In the United States, ONFH affects between 20,000 – 30,000 patients every year, with an average age of onset between 20–40 years old [4, 5]. Risk factors include trauma, alcohol use disorders, blood dyscrasias, and corticosteroids [6, 7]. ONFH is a significant contributor to the national arthroplasty burden, accounting for over 10% of all THAs performed in the United States [8]. Unfortunately, patients with ONFH who undergo THA have higher rates of revision surgery, iatrogenic fractures, and periprosthetic infection compared to patients with osteoarthritis undergoing THA [9]. Given worse outcomes, and the relatively young age of patients with ONFH, it is preferable to delay the progression of ONFH and subsequent THA. Core decompression, a surgical technique to resect necrotic bone and promote revascularization, has emerged as the primary treatment to preserve the femoral head, prevent collapse, and the need for THA, with a pooled success rate of approximately 65% [10].

There are a variety of different techniques used to perform core decompression. Conventional core decompression typically uses cannulated drills and trephines between 4-8 mm diameter to resect necrotic bone and promote revascularization [11, 12]. Currently, a 5-mm cannulated drill is used as standard of care in a National Institute of Health funded multi-center randomized control trial investigating core decompression with or without bone marrow aspirate [13]. Multiple small-diameter pins have been proposed as a modification to the conventional core decompression procedure, with the advantage of improved access to multiple regions of the femoral head [14]. Advanced core decompression uses a percutaneous expandable reamer for larger diameter reaming within the femoral head, while limiting the diameter of the drill tract and protecting the lateral cortex [15]. In all cases, adjuncts such as synthetic bone substitute or demineralized bone matrix may be used to fill the surgical defect and promote revascularization and bone growth [15–17]. Though not commonly performed during advanced core decompression, vascularized or non-vascularized cortical bone grafts may also be used [18, 19]. The range of different techniques and instruments used during core decompression may explain the wide range of success rates that have been reported for core decompression [10]; however, there is limited direct comparison between these different approaches.

One factor that has been shown to correlate with the success of core decompression is the percent of osteonecrotic bone resected [20]. The ideal procedure would resect as much necrotic bone as possible while retaining as much healthy bone as possible. Preservation of healthy bone is crucial to avoid complications such as fracture. In addition, resection of lateral cortical bone increases the risk of fracture [21, 22]. To facilitate the evaluation of core decompression surgical plans, we introduce the resection fraction metric, where V is the volume of bone resected:

This function ranges from zero to 1. A theoretically perfect procedure will resect only necrotic bone and thus have a resection fraction of 1. A procedure that resects no necrotic bone will have a resection fraction of zero. Increasing the volume of necrotic bone resected increases the resection fraction, while increasing the volume of healthy bone resected will decrease it.

With the many different instruments available for use during core decompression, and the limited comparisons between them, we sought to evaluate how instrument performance varies across a range of osteonecrotic lesions. Using 3D models of proximal femurs and ONFH derived from intraoperative CT scans of patients with ONFH undergoing 3D computer navigated core decompression, we simulated core decompression with different instruments including 5 mm drill bits, multiple small diameter pins, as well as the newer expandable reamers. We hypothesize that the optimal instrument selection for each lesion will vary based on lesion size and stage.

Methods

We maintain a retrospective database of all patients with osteonecrosis of the femoral head who underwent a 3D navigated core decompression procedure at our institution. Inclusion criteria for the study included: 3D computer navigated core decompression of the femoral head between May 27, 2022 and March 10, 2025, with intraoperative CT imaging prior to the placement of instrumentation within the femoral head. Individuals were excluded if they were under the age of 18 at time of surgery or if intraoperative CT imaging was insufficient for 3D analysis, i.e. imaging did not adequately capture the entire proximal femur and osteonecrotic lesion or had excessive artifacts. Perioperative volumetric imaging, patient demographic and anthropometric data, and risk factors were collected. This study was approved by the institutional review board (protocol #2000039132).

3D modeling

All imaging was acquired using an O-arm Surgical Imaging System (Medtronic, Galway, Ireland), with a 0.415 × 0.415 × 0.833 mm voxel size. Simpleware version V-2024.06-SP1 (Synopsys, Mountain View, CA, USA) image processing software was used to segment the CT scans and generate 3D models of the femur and osteonecrotic lesion. Initial segmentation was performed using threshold values, determined based on each hip’s individual anatomy, followed by manual refinement and Gaussian smoothing. Cystic and/or sclerotic changes secondary to osteonecrosis were included in the model of the osteonecrotic lesion. When available, preoperative MRI imaging was compared against the CT-based 3D models to ensure proper representation of the osteonecrotic lesion. For each patient’s 3D model, the total volume of the femoral head and the total volume of the osteonecrotic lesion were determined. Image segmentation was validated by a fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeon or musculoskeletal radiologist.

For each patient, six core decompression procedures were simulated using six different surgical instruments/approaches: 1—a single 2.8 mm diameter pin, 2—multiple 2.8 mm diameter pins, 3—a single 5.0 diameter drill, as well as three expandable reamers of varying sizes, 4—small (3.3 mm shaft diameter, expandable to 7.0 mm, (Arthrex IOBP Decompression Device)), 5—medium (5.0 mm shaft diameter, expandable to 18.0 mm (Arthrex AVN Expandable Reamer)), and 6—large (9.0 mm shaft diameter, expandable to 21 mm (Stryker X-Ream Percutaneous Expandable Reamer) (Table 1). The pins and drills were modeled as simple cylinders. The expandable reamers were modeled as two cylinders, one representing the volume of the shaft, and the second representing the volume resected by the expandable cutting tip (Fig. 1). The surgical plans were conducted using the following parameters for all instruments, including multiple pins: 1. The lateral entry point should be located on the lateral cortex above the lesser trochanter as entry below the lesser trochanter has been shown to increase fracture risk [23]. 2. The instrument should not disrupt the cortical bone in the femoral neck. 3. The tip of the instrument should pass through the osteonecrotic lesion and end just below subchondral bone, maximally resecting necrotic bone. In the case of multiple pins, a minimum of 2 pins and a maximum of 9 pins were utilized per lesion, with the exact number left to the surgeon’s discretion. A median of 6 pins were utilized (IQR: 4–7). All expandable reamers were deployed to their maximum diameter, as is our current clinical practice. Initial surgical plans were performed by a trained biomedical engineer, and reviewed, modified and approved by an arthroplasty fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeon who oversees a specialized avascular necrosis clinic at their institution. Boolean operations were utilized to calculate the amount of healthy and necrotic bone resected by the different instruments. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed utilizing R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables are reported as counts and/or percentages, while continuous variables are reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Continuous variables were compared using Wilcoxon signed rank test with Benjamin-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons as appropriate. Effect sizes were estimated using Hodges-Lehmann median difference with 95% confidence intervals. Generalized additive models with a shrinkage cubic regression spline basis and smoothing parameters estimated by REML was used to evaluate the relationship between resection fraction and lesion size (in mm3). Generalized additive modeling was performed using R package mgcv version 1.9–4 (Simon Wood, Edinburgh, Scotland).

Results

Patient and osteonecrotic lesion characteristics

A total of 80 patients underwent 3D navigated core decompressions throughout the study period; 4 patients were excluded from this study due to incomplete imaging, and 1 was excluded as they were less than 18yo at time of surgery. In total, 75 patients were included in this study. The median age of the patients was 40 and 46.7% were female. 54.7% of patients had a history of significant corticosteroid use. Fifty patients had bilateral ONFH; of these 25 patients underwent core decompression bilaterally. A total of 100 unique hips with ONFH underwent core decompression and were included in this study. Of the 100 hips, 46% were left-sided. The median volume of the femoral head was 50834.4mm3 (IQR: 43679.1–61479.8). The median volume of the osteonecrotic lesion was 8648.6 mm3. The majority (87%) were ARCO 2019 stage 2, with a minority stage 1 (3%) or stage 3A/B (10%). Using the ARCO 2021 classification, 21% were type 1, 57% type 2 and 22% type 3. Discussion

In this study, we simulated core decompression surgeries on 3D models of 100 unique patient hips with osteonecrosis of the femoral head, using commonly utilized surgical instruments for core decompression. When treating osteonecrosis of the femoral head, the efficacy of core decompression has been found to be related to the amount of necrotic bone remaining post-procedure [20]. However, resecting more necrotic bone frequently comes at the cost of increasing the size of the surgical defect and resecting more healthy bone, which increases the risk of peri-operative fractures. In this study, we were able to compare how different instruments can be used preferentially to resect more necrotic bone and retain healthy bone.

We found, regardless of whether one is trying to maximize the amount of necrotic bone, or minimize the amount of healthy bone resected, any one of the expandable reamers performed superiorly to pins or drill bits. There was not a significant difference between the amount of necrotic bone resected by the small expandable reamer and multiple pins, however the small expandable reamer resected substantially less healthy bone. Conversely, the medium expandable reamer resected as much healthy bone as multiple pins but resected five times as much necrotic bone as the multiple pins. Whether one wishes to maximize necrotic bone resection, or preserve healthy bone, all the expandable reamers had higher resection fractions when compared to straight instruments.

Using the resection fraction metric, we found the optimal expandable reamer varied depending on lesion size and type. For smaller lesions in the first quartile of volume (< 4227mm3), the smaller expandable reamer had higher resection fractions. Larger lesions had higher resection fractions with the medium expandable reamer. Across all ARCO 2021 types, the large expandable reamer performed worse than either the small or medium expandable reamer. For type 2 and 3 lesions, the medium reamer had the highest resection. However, for type 1 lesions, there was not a significant difference between the small and medium reamers; this finding may be due to the fact that there can be significant difference in the size of lesion, even within the same ARCO 2021 type. We would thus suggest surgeons to use lesion size to inform their instrument choice rather than ARCO 2021 type.

The large expandable reamer had a significantly worse resection fraction than either the small or medium size reamers across all lesions, even for the largest lesions in the fourth quartile of volume. This is likely as it has the largest diameter shaft of the expandable reamers, even compared to its expanded head diameter. As such, even though more necrotic tissue is removed with the large reamer, significantly more healthy tissue will be removed along the shaft tract. Development of new expandable reamers with larger maximum expanded diameters, and smaller shafts may better serve the largest ONFH lesions.

Our results are overall in line with current literature; however direct comparison is difficult given the significant variation of techniques and instruments described. A recent study of patients who underwent traditional core decompression found a 100% survival rate of stage I ONFH and 62% survival rate of stage II ONFH [12]. Multiple pin drilling was found to have a success rate of 86% in stage I ONFH and 56% and stage II ONFH [24]. A two-year follow-up of advanced core decompression using the large expandable reamer reported an overall survival rate of 67%: 89% in stage I ONFH, 67% in stage II, and 53% for stage III. Focusing on stage II lesions which makes up the majority of the patient population represented here, the large expandable reamer had the highest success rate, followed by traditional core decompression, and multiple pin drilling with the lowest success rate. This mirrors the median resection fraction scores across all lesions found in this study. This comparison is limited by the lack of comparable data for the other instruments simulated.

In comparison to prior studies performing 3D analysis of core decompression for ONFH, our simulated surgeries left substantially more necrotic bone remaining. Landgraeber et al. simulated core decompression with an instrument of the same dimensions as our large expandable reamer and reported a mean remaining necrotic bone volume of 2184mm3[20], substantially less than our identical device's reported median remaining necrotic bone volume of 6338mm3. The exact reason for this discrepancy is unclear; notably the mean ONFH volume reported by Landgraeber et al. was 4054mm3, compared to the 8648.6 mm3 we report here. This difference may be related to the methodology for creating the 3D models; Landgraeber et al. utilized MRI imaging with a 3 mm slice thickness, while we utilized CT imaging with 0.833 mm slice thickness. In general, CT imaging has smaller voxel sizes and a higher resolution which is optimal for more accurate imaging segmentation, 3D modeling, and volume calculations.

The usage of the resection fraction in this study is novel. To date, there is no objective way of directly comparing different core decompression procedures. The resection fraction is a simple metric that integrates the two objectives of core decompression: to remove necrotic tissue and preserve healthy tissue. Comparing either necrotic or healthy tissue resection alone would not capture this balance; instruments that resect large volumes of necrotic and healthy bone would appear superior, but if it resects a disproportionate amount of healthy bone, the net benefit would be reduced by the risk of fracture. The resection fraction can be further fine-tuned per surgeon objectives. If a surgeon wished to prioritize necrotic bone removal, a multiplier could be added to volume of necrotic bone, effectively minimizing the penalty associated with healthy bone resection. For example, applying a multiplier of two would double the value of removing more necrotic bone compared to preserving healthy bone, thus rewarding instruments that more aggressively remove tissue, such as the larger reamer. Alternative modifications could include increasing the weight of healthy cortical bone resected as cortical bone removal has been found to be associated with risk of fracture [21, 22]. This modification would thus prioritize procedures with lower risks of fracture. Incorporation of a finite-element-analysis into the calculation of the resection fraction would provide more direct evaluation of fracture risk, though at the cost of significantly increased computational complexity. A clinical trial analyzing the resection fraction of actual core decompression procedures, and long-term patient outcomes are needed to validate this metric. Further modification to this metric may be needed to best align it with clinical outcomes.

The lack of clinical correlation of the resection fraction is the most significant limitation of this study. Nonetheless, given the lack of data directly comparing outcomes of instruments, we feel this is a valuable addition to the literature. The other limitations of this study are related to differences between the simulated procedure compared to actual core decompression procedures. Our simulation assumes surgeons can accurately target the optimal trajectory of their Kirshner wire. This is a challenge on two fronts: first, the surgeon must be confident their desired trajectory accurately targets the osteonecrotic lesions, which can be difficult with standard intraoperative fluoroscopy—we thus encourage surgeons to utilize computer navigated technologies to ensure accurate core decompression [25]. A second challenge is that even if the initial trajectory is accurate, the small Kirshner wires that are used to initially target the ONFH lesion may bend and deviate within the femur. This can be minimized with a stiff wire and careful positioning. Nonetheless, deviations may occur during actual core decompression procedures alternating the trajectory and thus actual amounts of bone that will be removed during core decompression. This significantly affects expandable reamers which can resect substantially greater volumes of necrotic bone; this is only a benefit if the instrument is positioned optimally and resects the bone that was intended to be removed. Another limitation that arose from simulation is that the surgical defect created by the instruments were modeled as ideal cylinders. This is a simplification of the complex cutting dynamics of these instruments and may not perfectly reflect the defect created during core decompression. This is particularly relevant for the expandable reamers, as the profile created during expandable blade deployment may deviate from the perfect cylinders modeled. Lastly, image segmentation and surgical simulations were not repeated, thus inter/intra-observer reliability could not be assessed.

Clinical studies comparing instrument selection are needed to validate the findings in this study and to guide surgeons in picking the appropriate instruments for their patients. Future studies should also explore further personalization of the core decompression procedure to patients’ anatomy and more precise targeting of the osteonecrotic lesion. As not all expandable reamers can be partially deployed, in this study, all expandable reamers were deployed to maximal diameter throughout the lesion. For certain instruments, it is possible to incrementally deploy the reamer so that one can more selectively target necrotic bone while preserving healthy tissue. Future studies should investigate how such methods can be used to further enhance the efficacy of core decompression and should develop guidelines to inform reamer deployment techniques. 3D-printed patient-specific instrumentation and 3D navigated technologies have been incorporated into core decompression to improve accuracy [25–28]. These methods should be integrated with optimal instrument selection to make core decompression, as safe, efficient and effective as possible.

In conclusion, expandable reamers generally resected more necrotic bone, while preserving more healthy bone than single or multiple straight drills or pins. The expandable reamers also had significantly greater resection fractions than the straight drill bits. Based on these in-silico results, we thus recommend surgeons use expandable reamers during core decompression; the exact instrument used should be selected based on lesion sizes. The small reamer is most appropriate for small, early-stage lesions, while larger lesions should utilize the medium reamer. The large reamer was not an optimal choice for any lesion size or type; while it removes more necrotic bone than either reamer, it does so at the cost of substantially more healthy tissue. Further work is needed to evaluate differences in these instruments’ performance in clinical practice.
