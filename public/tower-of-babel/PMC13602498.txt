Psychological predictors of professional readiness: modeling the effects of preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills on self-efficacy and professional attitudes
Authors: Hüseyin Serçe, Mevlüt Aydoğmuş, Cihad Şentürk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychological readiness is widely recognized as a fundamental determinant of preservice teachers’ professional growth, instructional effectiveness, and long-term commitment to the teaching profession. Contemporary educational reforms emphasize that teachers must possess not only strong pedagogical and subject-matter knowledge but also advanced 21st-century competencies, including critical thinking, collaboration, problem solving, and technology literacy. These competencies are essential for effective instructional decision-making and adaptability in increasingly complex learning environments. Concurrently, psychological constructs such as teacher self-efficacy and professional attitudes play a central role in shaping preservice teachers’ motivation, persistence, and professional identity formation. However, empirical evidence explaining how 21st-century competencies contribute to preservice teachers’ psychological readiness through self-efficacy and professional attitudes remains limited. Accordingly, exploring how 21st-century skills contribute to the development of teacher self-efficacy has become an emerging research priority in teacher education.MethodsThis cross-sectional correlational study included 401 preservice teachers enrolled in teacher education programs at two public universities in Konya, Türkiye. Data were collected using the Multidimensional 21st Century Skills Scale, the Teacher Self-Efficacy Perception Scale-Short Form, and the Attitudes toward the Teaching Profession Scale. To examine the hypothesized relationships among variables, structural equation modeling (SEM) with maximum likelihood estimation was conducted. Model adequacy was evaluated using standard fit indices. Because the data were collected at a single point in time, the analyses focused on associations among variables rather than causal relationships.ResultsThe SEM results revealed that 21st-century skills significantly and positively predicted both teacher self-efficacy and attitudes toward the teaching profession. Specifically, 21st-century skills showed a strong positive effect on teacher self-efficacy (β = .58, p < .001) and a moderate positive effect on attitudes toward the teaching profession (β = .39, p < .001). The model explained 34% of the variance in teacher self-efficacy and 15% of the variance in professional attitudes, indicating that 21st-century competencies function as meaningful psychological resources for preservice teachers.ConclusionsThe findings underscore the importance of integrating 21st-century skill development into teacher education curricula as a pathway to strengthening preservice teachers’ psychological readiness for the teaching profession. From a theoretical perspective, the study contributes to educational psychology by conceptualizing 21st-century competencies as psychological resources that support the development of teacher self-efficacy and positive professional attitudes. Competency-based training models that foster both cognitive and motivational outcomes may enhance future teachers’ resilience, confidence, and professional engagement. These results offer practical implications for teacher education policy and contribute empirical evidence from a non-Western context to the field of educational psychology.

Introduction

The teaching profession plays a pivotal role in shaping the intellectual, social, emotional, and cultural development of societies. Beyond transmitting academic knowledge, teachers are expected to foster students’ socio-emotional competencies, moral development, and adaptive problem-solving abilities to prepare them for life in complex and rapidly changing environments [2, 43, 148]. From a psychological perspective, effective teaching requires an integration of cognitive competence, emotional regulation, and motivational resilience, and these characteristics influence instructional behavior and classroom effectiveness [70, 136]. Accordingly, teacher education is increasingly recognized as both a professional preparation process and a psychologically formative experience that shapes preservice teachers’ beliefs, identities, and long-term professional commitment [44, 94]. Within this framework, understanding the competencies that preservice teachers must develop, as well as the psychological constructs that support their professional growth, is essential for designing high-quality teacher education programs grounded in educational and psychological theory. These constructs include teacher self-efficacy, professional attitudes toward teaching, and motivational orientations, which together influence teachers’ readiness for the profession [15, 59, 144].

Recent transformations in education have intensified the need for teachers who possess 21st-century competencies that enable them to address the complex demands of contemporary classrooms and contribute to student success in global and knowledge-based societies. These competencies include critical thinking, problem solving, creativity, collaboration, communication and digital literacy, and they are widely recognized as fundamental for promoting meaningful learning and student engagement [48, 152, 156]. Since teachers are responsible for cultivating these competencies in their students, they are expected to internalize and apply them effectively in their own instructional practices [128]. Research in teacher education highlights that 21st-century competencies are not only professional requirements but also determinants of preservice teachers’ preparedness for teaching, professional competence and capacity to manage complex learning environments [28, 36].

Contemporary perspectives in educational psychology suggest that these competencies also shape key psychological dimensions of teaching. Studies grounded in social cognitive theory indicate that preservice teachers with higher levels of 21st-century skills often report stronger beliefs in their professional capabilities and a higher sense of instructional control [15, 143]. These competencies contribute to the development of teacher self-efficacy, which is defined as the belief in one’s ability to plan, organize and carry out the actions necessary for effective teaching [15]. High levels of teacher self-efficacy are associated with increased instructional effectiveness, persistence when faced with challenges and a greater willingness to adopt innovative pedagogical approaches [87, 144]. Positive professional attitudes have also been identified as significant determinants of teachers’ motivation, job satisfaction and long-term commitment to the profession [123, 151]. These findings indicate that 21st-century skills function not only as practical teaching tools but also as psychological resources that support preservice teachers’ professional development and readiness for teaching.

Although 21st-century competencies and psychological constructs have been individually explored in teacher education research, empirical studies that examine how these domains interact remain insufficient. Prior research has largely emphasized the instructional outcomes of 21st-century skills [7, 146] or investigated teacher self-efficacy and professional attitudes as isolated psychological variables [67, 144, 149]. Recent studies have begun to acknowledge the psychological significance of professional competencies [45, 129]; however, there is still limited evidence explaining whether 21st-century skills function as psychological resources that enhance preservice teachers’ sense of capability, motivation and professional commitment. The available findings are also predominantly based on Western or high-income educational systems, which restricts the transferability of results to countries implementing evolving teacher education reforms [94, 139]. There is a growing call for empirical studies that integrate competence-based teacher education with psychological theory to explain how preservice teachers develop readiness for the profession in diverse cultural and educational contexts [69, 135]. Addressing this research gap is essential for advancing a holistic understanding of preservice teacher development and for informing evidence-based teacher education policies.

Although preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills, teacher self-efficacy, and attitudes toward the teaching profession have each received growing attention in teacher education research, these constructs have typically been examined independently rather than within an integrated explanatory framework. Consequently, the ways in which contemporary competencies contribute to preservice teachers’ psychological readiness for the teaching profession remain insufficiently understood. In particular, limited empirical evidence exists regarding whether 21st-century skills function as personal and cognitive resources that strengthen preservice teachers’ beliefs in their teaching capabilities and support the development of more positive professional attitudes. Addressing this issue is important because psychological readiness plays a crucial role in shaping teachers’ motivation, persistence, instructional engagement, and long-term commitment to the profession [15, 162].

Teacher self-efficacy, defined as teachers’ beliefs in their ability to organize and execute the actions required to successfully accomplish teaching tasks, is widely recognized as a key psychological factor influencing teachers’ professional behaviors and attitudes [144]. Despite its importance, relatively few empirical studies have investigated how broader competencies associated with the 21st-century learning paradigm relate simultaneously to the development of teacher self-efficacy and professional attitudes among preservice teachers.

The present study addresses this gap by examining the relationships among preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills, teacher self-efficacy, and attitudes toward the teaching profession within a single explanatory model. Specifically, this study aims to investigate the predictive associations among these constructs using structural equation modeling (SEM).

Conceptual framework

The conceptual framework of this study is grounded in social cognitive theory, which explains how individuals’ beliefs and competencies interact with contextual factors to shape their motivation and behavior [15]. Within this perspective, preservice teachers’ professional readiness is conceptualized as a psychological state that reflects their perceived capability to meet the demands of teaching and their commitment to the profession [3, 121]. Psychological constructs such as teacher self-efficacy and professional attitudes play a central role in this process because they influence how preservice teachers interpret professional challenges, regulate their efforts and sustain long-term engagement in teaching [123, 144]. At the same time, contemporary teacher education highlights the importance of developing 21st-century skills, including critical thinking, collaboration, problem solving and digital literacy, as essential competencies for success in modern educational environments [110, 156]. Recent evidence suggests that these competencies not only enhance instructional effectiveness but also function as psychological resources that strengthen self-beliefs and shape professional identity [118]. Accordingly, this study integrates psychological theory and competence-based perspectives to examine how preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills predict their self-efficacy and professional attitudes as indicators of professional readiness.

Teacher self-efficacy

The concept of self-efficacy, originally introduced by Bandura, has long been recognized as a critical psychological construct that influences human functioning across domains. [16] defined self-efficacy as individuals’ beliefs about their ability to exercise control over behaviors, thought processes, motivation, and emotional states. This definition highlights that individuals with similar abilities, or even the same individual under different circumstances, may demonstrate varying levels of performance depending on the strength of their self-efficacy beliefs. In essence, self-efficacy functions as an internal motivational mechanism that determines what a person perceives they can accomplish in a given situation based on their knowledge and skills [130].

A strong sense of self-efficacy has been shown to enhance motivation, resilience, and perseverance in the face of challenges. Individuals with higher self-efficacy are generally better equipped to cope with stress, remain committed to their organizations, and achieve higher performance outcomes. Conversely, individuals with low self-efficacy may lack motivation, avoid challenges, and experience reduced professional commitment and performance [35, 113]. In the teaching profession specifically, these dynamics are particularly salient, as teachers’ beliefs about their own competencies strongly influence both instructional practices and student outcomes. Although teaching is one of the oldest human occupations, its professionalization is relatively recent, and it is widely accepted today that teachers must possess specific knowledge, skills, and dispositions to be effective. Self-efficacy constitutes one of the foundational dimensions of such professional competencies [116, 124].

Since its introduction, the construct of self-efficacy has been widely investigated in diverse fields such as psychology, science, mathematics, and education. Across these fields, self-efficacy is consistently understood as an individual’s judgment regarding their capability to plan, organize, and execute actions required to achieve a desired outcome [131]. Within education, one of the most critical applications of the construct is teacher self-efficacy, which refers to the belief that teachers can influence student engagement, learning, and achievement [144]. Research has consistently emphasized that teacher self-efficacy is positively related to effective classroom management, student motivation, instructional quality, and overall academic performance [88, 160]. Moreover, recent evidence suggests that teacher candidates' 21st-century skills positively affect their professional competence when controlling for demographic variables [159]. In contrast, low teacher self-efficacy has been associated with teacher burnout, reduced organizational commitment, and lower levels of student achievement [66].

More recent scholarship has reinforced the significance of teacher self-efficacy in contemporary education. For example, [145] highlighted that teachers with higher self-efficacy were more adaptive in adopting innovative pedagogical strategies and integrating technology into teaching. Similarly, [71] demonstrated that teacher self-efficacy not only contributes to improved teaching practices but also mediates the relationship between teacher well-being and student achievement. These findings suggest that self-efficacy is not merely an individual belief but also a mechanism shaping broader educational outcomes [47].

In teacher education, examining preservice teachers’ self-efficacy perceptions has become increasingly important. Preservice teachers’ beliefs about their instructional abilities can influence their preparedness for professional practice and their long-term commitment to the profession. Studies indicate that preservice teachers with higher levels of self-efficacy are more likely to engage in reflective practice, adopt student-centered teaching strategies, and demonstrate resilience when faced with classroom challenges [157]. Furthermore, their self-efficacy perceptions are predictive of later teaching performance, classroom management effectiveness, and student outcomes (Octoria et al., 2022). Contemporary research emphasizes the importance of developing 21st-century skills such as information and technology literacy, critical thinking and problem-solving, entrepreneurship and innovation, social responsibility and leadership, and career consciousness among preservice teachers to enhance their professional competencies [159].

Given the dynamic and complex demands of the 21st-century classroom, developing preservice teachers’ self-efficacy has emerged as a central focus of teacher education programs. Teacher educators are encouraged to design learning environments that provide mastery experiences, constructive feedback, and opportunities for collaborative practice, all of which have been shown to strengthen self-efficacy beliefs [83]. Moreover, in an era of rapid technological change and increasing diversity in classrooms, preservice teachers’ confidence in their ability to integrate digital tools, manage diverse learners, and apply innovative pedagogies has become a pressing issue [8].

Similarly, [155] emphasized that high self-efficacy is one of the fundamental elements of effective teaching and learning. She noted that strong self-efficacy beliefs are closely associated with sustaining motivation, adopting innovations, implementing effective instructional and classroom management strategies, and providing individualized guidance to students. Therefore, it can be inferred that teachers with high levels of self-efficacy are more likely to adopt contemporary teaching–learning approaches rather than relying solely on traditional methods. In conclusion, self-efficacy remains a critical construct for understanding and improving the teaching profession. Preservice teachers’ self-efficacy beliefs are not only predictive of their future professional success but also have a direct impact on student achievement and educational quality. Consequently, identifying and enhancing preservice teachers’ self-efficacy perceptions should be prioritized in teacher education programs and educational policy. This approach will provide a stronger foundation for preparing competent, motivated, and adaptive teachers who can meet the evolving challenges of education in the twenty-first century.

Attitudes toward the teaching profession

Teachers influence not only the academic performance of students but also the overall learning environment through their personalities, values, and dispositions. While knowledge and instructional skills are indispensable, teachers’ attitudes, behaviors, interests, needs, and values constitute the essence of their professional identity and significantly shape the classroom climate [30]. Attitudes, which are widely recognized as the foundation of behavior, emerge as mental, emotional, and behavioral predispositions formed through experience, knowledge, feelings, and motives [140]. In this sense, attitudes are not only reflections of individual experiences but also indicators of teachers’ professional orientation and commitment.

Attitudes toward the teaching profession have been conceptualized as a set of beliefs and learned dispositions that determine teachers’ actual and potential responses to teaching activities, leading them to react positively or negatively in professional contexts [85]. Teachers’ sense of belonging to the profession, their appreciation of its social significance, and their willingness to engage in continuous professional development are all closely linked to their attitudes [86]. From a structural perspective, attitudes consist of three interrelated components: the cognitive, rooted in beliefs; the affective, stemming from emotions associated with those beliefs; and the behavioral, which manifests in observable actions [57]. A positive alignment across these dimensions strengthens teachers’ professional identity, while negative attitudes can hinder both teacher performance and student learning outcomes [126].

Recent scholarship underscores that teacher attitudes play a pivotal role in shaping students’ motivation, self-confidence, learning goals, and achievement [84]. In particular, preservice teachers’ attitudes are of critical importance because these attitudes are still in the process of being formed and are highly malleable during initial teacher education. Studies show that preservice teachers who demonstrate positive attitudes toward the profession are more likely to enter the workforce with stronger professional commitment and resilience, which in turn enhances instructional quality and student outcomes [76, 121].

Teacher education programs thus play a central role in cultivating positive professional attitudes. Curricula that integrate reflective practice, opportunities for meaningful classroom engagement, and exposure to the broader social value of teaching contribute to the development of favorable dispositions toward the profession [18]. Moreover, contemporary teacher education emphasizes the need to align preservice teachers’ professional attitudes with 21st-century educational demands, including inclusivity, adaptability, and lifelong learning [24]. Such alignment not only supports teachers’ self-concept but also ensures that they remain responsive to evolving classroom challenges.

Investigating preservice teachers’ attitudes toward the teaching profession also carries significant implications for policy and practice. Understanding the factors that shape these attitudes, such as curriculum content, pedagogical approaches, mentoring experiences, and institutional culture, can inform the design of teacher education programs that better support positive professional development. Furthermore, longitudinal studies examining how these attitudes evolve during teacher education and early career phases can provide valuable insights into sustaining teacher motivation and preventing professional burnout [52, 64, 160].

In conclusion, preservice teachers’ attitudes toward the teaching profession represent a critical determinant of their future effectiveness and commitment. Positive attitudes not only enhance teacher performance but also foster student learning, motivation, and well-being. Consequently, teacher education programs should place greater emphasis on cultivating favorable professional dispositions through supportive curricula and experiential learning opportunities. Future research in this area can provide deeper insights into the mechanisms through which attitudes are formed and transformed, offering evidence-based guidance for strengthening the teaching profession in contemporary educational contexts.

21st-century skills

In today’s era of rapid scientific and technological advancement, characterized by the fifth industrial revolution, the cultivation of qualified individuals equipped with 21st-century skills has become an urgent priority in education [112]. In this context, the focus has shifted from the mere accumulation of knowledge to the acquisition of competencies that enable learners to apply, adapt, and innovate in diverse contexts. Life skills, career readiness, and project-based approaches are now considered essential for preparing individuals to meet the dynamic challenges of the globalized knowledge economy [31, 153].

The literature emphasizes that 21st-century skills encompass a broad set of competencies, including critical thinking, problem solving, communication, collaboration, creativity, digital literacy, social responsibility, leadership, and self-direction [48, 98, 122, 141]. Beyond cognitive and technical domains, these skills also highlight dispositions such as adaptability, resilience, and innovation, which are necessary for lifelong learning [99]. Importantly, the transition from knowledge acquisition to the development of “learning how to learn” is viewed as a cornerstone of 21st-century education [106].

In the Information Age, characterized by constant transformation, individuals and societies must adapt rapidly to survive and thrive. Keeping pace with technological innovation requires not only technical competencies but also the ability to critically evaluate information, integrate diverse perspectives, and act responsibly in digital and social spaces [138, 152]. Therefore, cultivating these skills is essential for both personal development and societal progress.

For teachers, equipping future generations with these competencies presents a significant challenge, as many current educators were not trained in 21st-century learning environments themselves [46]. This reality necessitates a systematic and dual focus on building these essential competencies during initial teacher preparation programs and through continuous, high-quality professional development [40]. Faculties of education therefore play a pivotal role in ensuring that preservice teachers not only understand these skills conceptually but are also capable of modeling and transferring them effectively to their students.

Research further indicates that preservice teachers’ attitudes and sense of self-efficacy are central to the successful integration of 21st-century skills into their teaching practice. Positive professional attitudes foster greater motivation, job satisfaction, and willingness to engage in innovative practices [127]. Likewise, teachers with higher self-efficacy are more likely to implement student-centered pedagogies, utilize technology effectively, and persist in overcoming instructional challenges [73, 102]. Recent studies also show that preservice teachers’ digital literacy and collaboration skills enhance their readiness to integrate 21st-century learning approaches in diverse classroom contexts [115, 137].

Taken together, these findings highlight that 21st-century skills represent not only a set of competencies to be transmitted to students but also critical capabilities that preservice teachers themselves must develop. Strengthening these competencies through teacher education programs will contribute significantly to the preparation of future educators who are capable of guiding learners toward success in an increasingly complex and interconnected world.

From the perspective of social cognitive theory, individuals’ competencies and prior learning experiences play a crucial role in shaping self-efficacy beliefs [15, 100]. In teacher education contexts, competencies associated with the 21st-century learning paradigm such as critical thinking, collaboration, problem solving, and digital literacy are considered essential capabilities for addressing complex instructional challenges [10]. Developing these competencies during teacher preparation may provide preservice teachers with opportunities to experience mastery in demanding instructional situations. According to social cognitive theory, mastery experiences constitute one of the most influential sources of self-efficacy beliefs because they strengthen individuals’ perceptions of competence in performing specific tasks [17, 117]. As preservice teachers gain confidence in their instructional capabilities, they are more likely to develop stronger self-efficacy beliefs. In turn, higher levels of teacher self-efficacy have been associated with more positive attitudes toward the teaching profession and stronger professional commitment [33, 53]. Accordingly, within the framework of this study, 21st-century skills are conceptualized as competence-based resources that are expected to be positively associated with preservice teachers’ teacher self-efficacy and their attitudes toward the teaching profession.

The aim and significance of the study

Although the number of studies focusing on preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills in Faculties of Education remains limited, existing research provides valuable insights into the development and implications of these competencies. For instance, [91] reported a positive and significant relationship between preservice teachers’ self-efficacy perceptions regarding 21st-century skills and their lifelong learning tendencies. Similarly, [82] found a statistically significant relationship between self-directed learning skills and 21st-century competencies. In another study, [107] demonstrated that despite differences in preservice teachers’ levels of 21st-century skills, teacher education programs could foster the acquisition of these competencies.

Other empirical evidence also highlights the critical role of these skills. [5] concluded that preschool teacher candidates’ attitudes toward STEM education and project-based learning (PBL) were moderately and significantly correlated with their perceptions of 21st-century competencies. [26] found a moderate and positive association between preservice teachers’ emotional intelligence and their 21st-century skills. Furthermore, [80] revealed that these skills significantly improved preservice teachers’ readiness for online learning environments. Experimental research by [11] also indicated that cooperative and flipped learning models enhanced preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills, with posttest scores showing significant differences in favor of the experimental group. Similarly, [4] demonstrated that undergraduate students’ mastery of 21st-century skills increased progressively with grade level.

A growing body of research has further examined preservice teachers’ perceptions of competence in relation to 21st-century skills. [12] explored preservice teachers’ competence perceptions, while [14] investigated the core competencies they should possess. [1], [56] and [133] studied preservice teachers’ metaphorical perceptions of these skills, whereas [58] examined the relationship between educational beliefs and 21st-century competencies. Similarly, studies across various departments of higher education (e.g., [50, 51, 68, 105] confirm the importance of equipping students with these competencies.

Research on in-service teachers also reinforces the relevance of 21st-century skills. [163] examined teachers’ competence perceptions through metaphors, while [75] identified teacher self-efficacy as a mediating factor between students’ perceptions of learning experiences and teachers’ 21st-century skill competence. Other studies investigated teachers’ abilities in using 21st-century skills [41, 42, 61], their relationship with critical thinking [96], their professional development [104, 132] and their links to professional commitment [92]. International scholarship also underscores the significance of these skills for teacher preparation and practice, highlighting their role in enhancing innovation, collaboration, and digital literacy [27, 101, 152].

Although previous studies have provided valuable insights into the development and implications of 21st-century skills, much of the existing literature has examined these competencies in relation to isolated psychological or educational outcomes. Consequently, relatively limited research has investigated how preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills simultaneously relate to key psychological dimensions of professional readiness, particularly teacher self-efficacy and attitudes toward the teaching profession, within an integrated explanatory framework. Understanding these relationships is important because teacher self-efficacy and professional attitudes represent central indicators of teachers’ motivation, instructional engagement, and long-term commitment to the profession [88, 160].

The fact that many of these studies were conducted after 2020 indicates that 21st-century skills have become a major focus in educational research worldwide. Accordingly, the present study aims to investigate the impact of preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills on their self-efficacy perceptions and attitudes toward the teaching profession. Data were collected from two faculties of education in Konya during the 2022–2023 academic year.

The primary aim of the present study was to examine the structural relationships proposed in the conceptual model, specifically the effects of preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills on their teacher self-efficacy and attitudes toward the teaching profession. Accordingly, the analyses were conducted and reported at the overall sample level in order to test the proposed structural model using structural equation modeling. By examining these constructs within a structural equation modeling framework, the study seeks to provide empirical evidence on how contemporary competencies contribute to preservice teachers’ psychological readiness for the teaching profession. In doing so, the study contributes to the literature by integrating 21st-century skills, teacher self-efficacy, and professional attitudes within a single explanatory model of preservice teachers’ professional readiness.

This study is expected to contribute meaningfully to the literature in several ways. First, exploring the relationships among these variables may help identify more effective methods for integrating 21st-century skills into teacher education. Second, findings related to preservice teachers’ self-efficacy perceptions and professional attitudes may inform targeted interventions to enhance their professional development. Finally, the study offers a potential roadmap for teacher educators and policymakers by providing empirical evidence on how strengthening preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills can positively influence their readiness for the teaching profession. Method

Research model

This study employed a correlational research design, which is a quantitative approach used to examine the relationship between two or more variables without intervention, manipulation, or control by the researcher [39]. Correlational designs enable the identification of associations among variables and provide opportunities to predict certain outcomes based on these relationships. Accordingly, this study investigated the predictive relationships between preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills, their teacher self-efficacy perceptions, and their attitudes toward the teaching profession. Descriptive characteristics of the participants

A convenience sampling method was employed due to the large and heterogeneous structure of the target population, as well as the advantages it offers in terms of cost and time efficiency compared to other sampling techniques. Including preservice teachers from multiple teacher education departments provided an opportunity to capture a broader range of perspectives regarding their awareness of 21st-century skills, thereby offering valuable insights into the preparation of future teachers and the acquisition of these competencies. Furthermore, the study’s setting in Konya, one of Turkey’s major educational hubs, increases the potential relevance of the findings. With a population of approximately 2.25 million and five universities as of 2021, Konya serves as a significant center for higher education. Examining preservice teachers from different departments within this context provides a comprehensive perspective on the awareness of 21st-century skills and contributes to a deeper understanding of teacher education in Turkey.

Data collection tools

In this study, three scales were employed to measure preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills, teacher self-efficacy, and attitudes toward the teaching profession. The data collection instruments are briefly described below.

Multidimensional 21st-century skills scale

The Multidimensional 21st-Century Skills Scale was developed by Cevik and Senturk [31] for students aged 15–25 and was employed in this study to measure the 21st-century skills of preservice teachers. The scale consists of five factors and 41 items: Information and technology literacy (15 items), critical thinking and problem-solving (6 items), entrepreneurship and innovation (10 items), social responsibility and leadership (4 items) and career consciousness (6 items). Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating stronger perceptions of 21st-century competencies.

To establish construct validity, Cevik and Senturk [31] conducted both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. Reliability was examined through Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, which demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency across the five subscales. In the present study, further confirmatory factor analysis confirmed the five-factor structure with acceptable fit indices (χ2/df = 2014.17/774; p < 0.01; GFI = 0.90; AGFI = 0.85; RMSEA = 0.05; CFI = 0.95; NFI = 0.91; SRMR = 0.058). The Cronbach’s alpha values ranged between 0.71 and 0.85, indicating adequate to high reliability. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the scale is both valid and reliable for assessing preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills.

Teacher Self-Efficacy Perception Scale-Short Form (TSES-SF)

The Teachers’ Sense of Efficacy Scale–Short Form (TSES-SF) was originally developed by [144] and subsequently adapted into Turkish by Karaoğlu [78]. The scale consists of 12 items grouped into three factors: self-efficacy in student engagement (4 items), self-efficacy in instructional strategies (4 items), and self-efficacy in classroom management (4 items). Items are rated on a nine-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 9 (a great deal), with higher scores indicating stronger perceptions of teacher self-efficacy.

The construct validity of the scale was previously confirmed through confirmatory factor analysis, while internal consistency reliability was supported by Cronbach’s alpha coefficients [78]. In the present study, confirmatory factor analysis further verified the three-factor structure, yielding acceptable model fit indices (χ2/df = 329.12/943, p < 0.01; CFI = 0.97; GFI = 0.95; NFI = 0.97; NNFI = 0.97; IFI = 0.97; TLI = 0.96; RMSEA = 0.077; SRMR = 0.048). Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the subscales ranged from 0.74 to 0.80, indicating satisfactory reliability. These results confirm that the TSES-SF is both a valid and reliable instrument for measuring preservice teachers’ self-efficacy perceptions.

The TSES-SF was selected for this study because teacher self-efficacy is widely recognized as a critical factor influencing professional development and student achievement. Self-efficacy beliefs shape teachers’ practices in relation to student engagement, instructional strategies, and classroom management, all of which are central to effective teaching. Moreover, the TSES-SF is one of the most frequently employed instruments internationally for assessing teacher self-efficacy, and the availability of a validated Turkish version makes it particularly appropriate for investigating preservice teachers’ perceptions within the Turkish educational context.

Attitudes Towards Teaching Profession Scale (ATTPS)

The Attitudes Towards Teaching Profession Scale (ATTPS) was developed by [72] to assess preservice teachers’ attitudes toward the teaching profession. The scale has a unidimensional structure consisting of 12 items. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores reflecting more positive attitudes toward the profession.

The validity of the scale was originally established through construct, face, content, and convergent validity analyses, while reliability was confirmed using Cronbach’s alpha. Analyses conducted with the present study’s dataset also supported the single-factor structure, yielding acceptable model fit indices (χ2/df = 112.59/46, GFI = 0.95, AGFI = 0.91, CFI = 0.98, NFI = 0.96, NNFI = 0.97, IFI = 0.98, RMSEA = 0.065, SRMR = 0.050, PNFI = 0.67, PGFI = 0.56). Internal consistency reliability was found to be high (α = 0.90). These results indicate that the ATTPS is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing attitudes toward the teaching profession among preservice teachers, practicing teachers, and individuals considering entry into the profession. The single-factor structure of the ATTPS also provides advantages in terms of clarity and ease of administration, making it a practical tool for both research and applied settings in teacher education.

Data collection procedure

The data were collected online over a one-month period during the 2022–2023 academic year. To ensure accessibility and ease of use, all instruments were transferred to an online format, allowing participants to complete them via computers, tablets, or mobile phones. On average, it took approximately ten minutes to complete the survey package. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary. Prior to data collection, participants were informed about the purpose of the research, the voluntary nature of their involvement, and their right to withdraw at any stage without penalty. They were also assured that their responses would remain strictly confidential and that anonymity would be preserved throughout the research process.

Given that all variables were measured using self-report instruments at a single point in time, the potential for common method bias was carefully considered. Although the possibility of common method bias cannot be entirely ruled out, several procedural steps were implemented during the data collection process to minimize this risk. Participants were assured of anonymity and confidentiality and were informed that there were no right or wrong answers, which was intended to reduce evaluation apprehension and encourage honest responses. In addition, clear instructions were provided for each scale to facilitate accurate responses and minimize misunderstanding. The instruments included in the survey measured conceptually distinct constructs, which may also help mitigate potential method bias [119]. In addition, the study employed validated measurement scales widely used in the literature, which may further reduce the likelihood of systematic measurement bias.

Data analysis

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to test the hypotheses of the study. SEM is a comprehensive statistical technique that allows researchers to examine relationships among variables and evaluate the fit of theoretical models simultaneously [89]. In this study, SEM was applied to examine the associations between preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills, teacher self-efficacy, and attitudes toward the teaching profession. The use of SEM was particularly appropriate given the study’s aim of testing hypothesized relational patterns and assessing the extent to which one set of variables was associated with the others. SEM provides the advantage of modeling multiple independent and dependent variables concurrently, offering a more nuanced understanding of complex relationships.

Measurement model

Prior to testing the structural relationships, a measurement model was specified and evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the validity of the latent constructs included in the study. In this model, preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills were represented as a multidimensional latent construct consisting of five observed factors: information and technology literacy, critical thinking and problem-solving, entrepreneurship and innovation, social responsibility and leadership, and career consciousness. Teacher self-efficacy was modeled as a latent construct with three observed dimensions: student engagement, instructional strategies, and classroom management. Attitudes toward the teaching profession were specified as a unidimensional latent construct represented by its observed indicators. The CFA results, reported in the Data Collection Tools section, indicated that all measurement models demonstrated acceptable fit indices and satisfactory factor loadings, thereby supporting the construct validity of the scales used in this study.

Structural model

Following the validation of the measurement model, the structural model was specified to examine the hypothesized relationships among the latent variables. In this model, preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills were specified as the exogenous (predictor) variable, whereas teacher self-efficacy and attitudes toward the teaching profession were specified as endogenous (outcome) variables. The structural paths defined in the model correspond directly to the study hypotheses.

In line with the hypotheses of the study, preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills were specified as the predictor variable in the structural model, whereas teacher self-efficacy perceptions and attitudes toward the teaching profession were specified as outcome variables. Hypothesis 1 was tested by examining the structural path between 21st-century skills and teacher self-efficacy, while Hypothesis 2 was tested by examining the structural path between 21st-century skills and attitudes toward the teaching profession. Empirical support for the hypotheses was evaluated based on the significance and direction of the standardized path coefficients obtained from the SEM analysis, together with the overall model fit indices [62, 89]. This analytical strategy enabled a clear correspondence between the study hypotheses, the measured constructs, and the empirical evidence used to interpret the findings.

Prior to conducting SEM, the dataset was examined to ensure compliance with statistical assumptions. Skewness and kurtosis values were calculated to evaluate normality. Values within the range of ± 1.5 are generally considered indicative of normal distribution [142]. In this study, skewness values ranged from −0.98 to −0.56 and kurtosis values from 0.36 to 1.21, confirming that the data were normally distributed. Cook’s distance values were also examined to detect multivariate outliers, with all values below 1, indicating no influential cases. Furthermore, variance inflation factor (VIF) values were computed to test for multicollinearity, with the highest value observed as 2.54. Since VIF values greater than 10 are typically regarded as problematic [158], the findings confirmed that multicollinearity was not an issue in the dataset. All analyses were carried out using the IBM SPSS AMOS 28.0 statistical package.

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the associations between preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills, teacher self-efficacy, and attitudes toward the teaching profession. The findings of the structural equation model indicated that 21st-century skills were significantly associated with both teacher self-efficacy and attitudes toward the teaching profession. In particular, the results revealed a relatively strong association between 21st-century competencies and teacher self-efficacy, whereas the relationship with professional attitudes was more moderate. Taken together, these findings suggest that competencies associated with the 21st-century learning paradigm may be related to psychological resources that support preservice teachers’ professional readiness.

This study was conducted with 401 preservice teachers enrolled in eight departments across two faculties of education in Konya, one of Turkey’s largest cities by population and area. The relationships among these variables were analyzed using structural equation modeling. The results demonstrated that preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills were positively associated with both self-efficacy perceptions and professional attitudes, with the strongest relationship observed for self-efficacy. These findings are consistent with and extend prior research emphasizing the role of 21st-century competencies in fostering teacher development and professional readiness [32, 60, 74, 75, 91, 92, 154]. Consistent with these studies, the hypotheses suggesting that preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills are positively related to their self-efficacy perceptions and attitudes toward the profession were supported.

One of the key findings of this study was that preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills were positively related to their attitudes toward the teaching profession. As [93] note, these skills encompass critical thinking, information literacy, problem solving, decision making, and the ability to generate and share knowledge, all of which are central to professional growth and democratic engagement. They also align closely with the general teaching competencies outlined by the Turkish Ministry of National Education [103], including professional values, student engagement, curriculum knowledge, and collaboration with families and communities. Similarly, recent international research underscores that 21st-century skills such as creativity, innovation, communication, collaboration, and digital literacy are essential for shaping teachers’ professional identity and sustaining motivation in the teaching profession [7, 34, 38, 110]. Moreover, evidence suggests that teachers who possess higher levels of these competencies may be more likely to adapt to diverse classroom contexts and adopt student-centered pedagogies [9, 63, 79]. Thus, fostering preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills could contribute to more positive attitudes toward the teaching profession and support the integration of innovative pedagogical approaches in teacher education programs.

Another important finding of the study was that preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills were strongly associated with their perceptions of teacher self-efficacy. Previous research has shown that individuals with strong self-efficacy beliefs tend to be more effective in classroom management, instructional planning, and promoting student engagement [88, 144]. The present results demonstrated that competencies such as information and technology literacy, critical thinking and problem-solving, entrepreneurship and innovation, social responsibility and leadership, and career awareness were closely related to preservice teachers’ self-efficacy beliefs. These findings are consistent with studies indicating that the development of 21st-century competencies can enhance teachers’ confidence in implementing innovative pedagogical practices and adapting to complex educational environments [58, 114, 150]. These findings also align with prior research emphasizing the role of self-directed learning as a key mechanism linking instructional approaches to lifelong learning tendencies among preservice teachers [81].

The stronger relationship observed between 21st-century skills and teacher self-efficacy may be interpreted through the perspective of social cognitive theory. According to [15], efficacy beliefs are shaped primarily through mastery experiences and the successful application of knowledge and skills in relevant contexts. Many competencies associated with the 21st-century learning paradigm require individuals to engage actively in complex cognitive and social tasks, which can provide opportunities for preservice teachers to develop confidence in their teaching capabilities [49]. When preservice teachers successfully apply skills such as problem solving, collaboration, and digital literacy in instructional contexts, they are likely to develop stronger beliefs about their ability to manage classroom activities and support student learning. Previous research has similarly indicated that teachers’ efficacy beliefs are closely linked to perceptions of competence and successful task performance [6, 87, 143].

While 21st-century skills were also positively associated with attitudes toward the teaching profession, the magnitude of this relationship was more moderate. This difference may be explained by the multidimensional nature of professional attitudes. Attitudes toward teaching are shaped not only by individual competencies but also by broader psychological and contextual factors such as professional identity, career expectations, institutional context, and social perceptions of the teaching profession [24, 65, 134]. Consequently, although 21st-century competencies appear to be associated with more positive orientations toward the profession, they represent only one of several factors influencing preservice teachers’ professional attitudes.

In a study conducted by [13], it was demonstrated that preservice teachers’ social entrepreneurship characteristics had a significant positive impact on their self-efficacy perceptions and attitudes toward the teaching profession. The findings of the present study similarly indicated that 21st-century skills were associated with preservice teachers’ self-efficacy and professional attitudes. When these findings are considered together, it may be suggested that social entrepreneurship could be embedded within the broader framework of 21st-century competencies. These competencies are increasingly recognized as important for effective teaching in rapidly changing educational contexts [21, 77]. Indeed, the proactive and innovative nature of social entrepreneurship appears to align closely with the “soft skills” emphasized in contemporary education, such as resilience and social agency [97], thereby offering a complementary perspective on the role of these competencies in supporting preservice teachers’ professional development. Consequently, integrating both social entrepreneurship and 21st-century competencies into preservice teacher education programs can be considered a potentially beneficial approach for supporting teachers’ readiness for the profession.

At the same time, the results of this study should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Because the research employed a cross-sectional design, the findings reflect associations rather than definitive causal relationships. In addition, the relatively moderate explanatory power observed for attitudes toward the teaching profession suggests that other variables not included in the model may also play an important role. Future research using longitudinal or experimental designs is likely to provide regarding the relationships among these variables.

To conclude, the findings of this study suggest that 21st-century competencies may play a meaningful role in relation to preservice teachers’ professional development. Strengthening these competencies within teacher education programs could contribute to enhancing preservice teachers’ confidence in their teaching abilities and fostering more positive orientations toward the teaching profession; however, such implications should be interpreted in light of the study’s design and limitations.

Implications for teacher education and policy

The findings of this study suggest the potential importance of placing greater emphasis on 21st-century skills in preservice teacher education, as these competencies appear to be meaningfully associated with self-efficacy beliefs and attitudes toward the teaching profession. Developing competencies such as critical thinking, collaboration, problem solving, and technological literacy appears to be related to stronger professional efficacy beliefs and more positive orientations toward teaching. These results suggest that teacher education programs could benefit from more explicit integration of 21st-century skills into their curricula to better prepare preservice teachers for the complex demands of contemporary classrooms.

At the program level, teacher education curricula can incorporate courses and learning experiences that focus on critical and creative thinking, information and technology literacy, social and cultural awareness, digital ethics, and leadership. Complementary activities such as seminars, workshops, and conferences can provide preservice teachers with opportunities for experiential learning and reflection. Increasing awareness of the nature, significance, and application of these competencies appears to be linked to stronger professional self-efficacy and may contribute to more positive orientations toward the teaching profession.

From a policy perspective, the results point to the potential value of embedding a coherent 21st-century skills framework within initial teacher education (ITE), not as peripheral or optional content but as a central component integrated across coursework, practicum experiences, and performance-based assessments [46, 111]. For example, practicum requirements may include structured opportunities for teacher candidates to design, implement, and reflect on technology-enhanced collaborative learning activities assessed through analytic rubrics linked to 21st-century learning competencies [109, 147]. Because teacher self-efficacy is often understood to develop through mastery experiences, teacher education programs are well positioned to emphasize repeated, scaffolded, and coached instructional enactments rather than isolated workshops or purely theoretical instruction [143, 160].

In addition, the positive association observed between 21st-century skills and attitudes toward the teaching profession can be interpreted as suggesting that teacher education policies might also benefit from supporting preservice teachers’ professional identity formation. Program standards could encourage structured opportunities for collaborative inquiry, such as lesson study or design thinking projects, as well as community-responsive initiatives that connect digital tools to authentic educational challenges. Such experiences have been reported to be associated with stronger professional commitment and teacher retention [37, 95]. To ensure that technology use supports pedagogical goals rather than driving them, teacher education curricula could be structured around TPACK-aligned competencies and assessed through capstone portfolios or micro-credentials demonstrating classroom-ready performance [90, 125]. Targeted mentoring and induction programs extending into the early years of teaching may further support early-career efficacy development [120].

Finally, policy frameworks may also need to more explicitly address issues of equity and system capacity. Strengthening school–university partnerships and practice-based teacher residency models can help support access to high-quality, technology-supported instructional environments across different institutional contexts [55, 161]. Similarly, accreditation and accountability mechanisms such as program review processes may incorporate direct evidence of candidates’ enactment of 21st-century skills and development of teacher self-efficacy rather than relying solely on indicators such as course completion [25, 108]. Aligning curriculum, clinical practice, assessment systems, and early-career induction policies is likely to provide a potential pathway for translating the moderate but positive associations identified in this study into sustainable professional capacity within teacher education systems.

Limitations

Despite the significance of the findings, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the participants were selected using a convenience sampling approach from two faculties of education in Konya, Türkiye, where preservice teachers from eight different departments were enrolled. Although this sampling strategy enabled access to a relatively large group of participants, it may limit the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings to broader populations of teacher candidates in different institutional or regional contexts. Future studies should therefore include participants from diverse regions and a wider range of teacher education programs to enhance the generalizability of the results.

Second, the study relied exclusively on self-reported data from preservice teachers, which reflects their perceptions rather than actual teaching performance. In addition, because all variables were measured using self-report instruments administered at a single point in time, the possibility of common method bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Although participants were assured of anonymity and confidentiality and were informed that there were no right or wrong answers in the survey, the use of a single data collection method may still introduce potential method-related bias. Future studies could address this limitation by incorporating multiple data sources, such as observational measures, performance-based assessments, or reports from in-service teachers and students, to provide more comprehensive insights.

Third, the study focused only on selected variables aligned with its objectives. Future research may examine the relationships between 21st-century skills and additional constructs to provide a broader understanding of teacher development processes. Moreover, the data were collected within a single academic period, which limits the ability to capture developmental changes over time. Longitudinal research designs are therefore recommended to better understand how preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills, self-efficacy, and professional attitudes evolve during teacher education.

Finally, the extent to which 21st-century skills are systematically integrated into teacher education curricula remains an open question. Future studies may critically examine how these competencies are embedded within teacher preparation programs and explore strategies for strengthening their integration into curriculum design, practicum experiences, and professional development activities.

Conclusion

This study examined the relationships between preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills, teacher self-efficacy, and attitudes toward the teaching profession. The findings indicated that preservice teachers’ 21st-century competencies were positively associated with both teacher self-efficacy and professional attitudes, with a stronger relationship observed for self-efficacy. These results suggest that competencies such as critical thinking, problem solving, digital literacy, collaboration, and innovation may play an important role in shaping preservice teachers’ perceptions of professional capability and their orientation toward the teaching profession. In particular, the stronger association with teacher self-efficacy indicates that competence-based skills may function as psychological resources that strengthen preservice teachers’ confidence in their ability to perform teaching-related tasks.

From a theoretical perspective, the findings contribute to the literature by linking 21st-century competencies with key psychological constructs related to teacher development within the framework of social cognitive theory. While previous research has often examined these competencies in relation to instructional practices or technological integration, the present study highlights their potential role in shaping preservice teachers’ professional beliefs and attitudes.

From a practical perspective, the results emphasize the importance of integrating 21st-century competencies more systematically into teacher education curricula. Providing preservice teachers with opportunities to develop skills such as critical thinking, collaboration, and digital literacy may help strengthen their sense of professional competence and foster more positive orientations toward the teaching profession. At the same time, the findings should be interpreted with caution because the cross-sectional design of the study does not allow for definitive causal conclusions. Future research employing longitudinal or experimental designs may provide deeper insights into how these competencies contribute to preservice teachers’ professional development over time.

Overall, the study contributes to the literature by integrating preservice teachers’ 21st-century skills with key psychological constructs of teacher development within a single explanatory framework. By demonstrating that these competencies are positively associated with both teacher self-efficacy and professional attitudes, the study provides additional insight into the psychological dimensions of teacher preparation and highlights the potential role of competence-based training in strengthening preservice teachers’ professional readiness.
