Bilibili and TikTok heart failure short video evaluation: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hong Lu, Fang Liu, Yi-lin Guo, Ding-ming Song, Yi-bo He, Deng-ke Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prognosis of heart failure is highly dependent on patient self-management, and short-video platforms have become a key channel for the public to access information about heart failure (HF). This cross-sectional study systematically evaluated HF-related short videos on TikTok and Bilibili platforms. After screening from January 10 to 11, 2026, 190 videos were included (103 from Bilibili and 87 from TikTok). Two cardiologists conducted quality assessments using the Global Quality Score, modified DISCERN scale, and Patient Education Assessment Tool for Audiovisual Materials (PEMAT-A/V). Results showed higher engagement for TikTok videos and longer duration for Bilibili videos (both P < 0.05). Content primarily focused on symptoms (71.1%) and treatment (66.3%), with insufficient coverage of prevention (36.3%). Overall quality was moderately low: median scores were 3 on both the Global Quality Score and modified DISCERN scale; PEMAT understandability score was 69%, and actionability score was 50%. Bilibili videos scored higher on actionability (P = 0.029), and videos uploaded by professional institutions demonstrated the best quality (P = 0.045). Longer video duration and inclusion of symptom-related content were independent predictors of higher GQS scores. Notably, video interactivity showed no positive correlation with content quality, revealing an obvious quality–dissemination decoupling phenomenon. This finding carries important clinical implications, as high-quality professional medical content fails to gain corresponding public dissemination, which hinders standardized popular science and long-term self-management among HF patients. The overall quality of heart failure short videos urgently requires improvement, necessitating enhanced professional content supply and rigorous quality control via multiparty collaborative mechanisms. Additionally, short-video health education should be integrated into routine clinical management and telemedicine systems to optimize patient self-management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58582-z.Keywords: Heart failure, Health education, Social media, Short videos, Quality analysisSubject terms: Cardiology, Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Heart failure (HF), as a complex clinical syndrome, is one of the main causes of death and rehospitalization among patients with cardiovascular diseases worldwide, imposing a heavy public health and socioeconomic burden1,2. According to statistics, the prevalence of HF in North America and Europe is 1% to 2%, among which 80% of new cases occur in people over 65 years old3. The prevalence of heart failure is on the rise due to population aging, improved survival rates after myocardial infarction, and enhanced treatment and survival rates for HF patients. The latest projections in the United States indicate that the prevalence of HF will increase by approximately 46% from 2012 to 2030, with corresponding medical costs rising by about 127%4. There are relatively few studies on the incidence of HF in China. A survey conducted in six provinces of China revealed that the age-standardized prevalence of HF was 1.10%, and the prevalence increased with age5. The effective management of HF is essentially a long-term process, which highly depends on patients’ correct understanding of the disease and their long-term self-management ability6. Therefore, providing patients and the public with high-quality, easily understandable, and practical disease education information is a crucial step in improving clinical outcomes7. In addition to accessible and standardized health education, long-term clinical prognosis and optimal self-management of heart failure rely heavily on structured clinical care pathways, regular long-term follow-up, remote symptom monitoring, and telemedicine-assisted management models. Integrated multidimensional care is essential to reduce readmissions and improve patient outcomes. As highlighted in recent work applying artificial intelligence to organize multi‑dimensional medical knowledge, chronic disease health education should adopt systematic, multi‑perspective content construction rather than scattered information output8.

With the rapid development of digital health dissemination, short-video platforms have become the primary channel for the public to obtain health information9. In China, TikTok and Bilibili, with their concise, visual, and highly interactive content format, have demonstrated unique potential in enhancing the accessibility and public engagement of health education10,11. According to Sensor Tower category ranking data from April 14 to May 13, 2026, the Chinese version of TikTok consistently ranked between 3rd and 6th in the “Entertainment” category on the App Store in mainland China. During the same period, Bilibili also maintained a stable ranking between 4 and 6th in the “Entertainment” category. In contrast, the international version of TikTok dominates Western markets, consistently ranking 1st–3rd and 2nd–5th in the “Entertainment” charts on Google Play in the U.S. and the U.K., respectively, while ranking 1st in the “Social” charts in both markets. Bilibili showed limited international penetration, ranking 83rd–133rd in the “Entertainment” charts in the U.S. and the U.K. In recent years, studies have evaluated short videos in fields such as mitral valve regurgitation11 and coronary heart disease12, and have confirmed that there are widespread problems with the quality of the information. However, previous studies have mostly focused on a single platform or a single quality dimension, and there is still a relative lack of systematic and multi-platform comparative studies for HF, a high-burden chronic disease13. Therefore, conducting a systematic assessment of the educational features, current quality status, and key influencing factors of heart failure-related short videos holds significant academic value and practical significance.

This study employed a cross-sectional design to include HF-related short videos from TikTok and Bilibili. It utilized multi-dimensional standardized tools to evaluate video quality, including the Global Quality Score (GQS), the Modified DISCERN scale (mDISCERN), and the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool for Audiovisual Materials (PEMAT-A/V). Additionally, it used Spearman correlation analysis to explore the intrinsic relationships among interaction data, video sources, content types, and quality ratings. The aim was to provide empirical evidence for optimizing HF health education strategies and improving the quality of public health information.

Methods

Search strategy

This cross-sectional study aims to select the two major mainstream short-video platforms in China (Bilibili and TikTok) as the research subjects. To minimize the influence of personalized algorithms on search results, from January 10th to January 11th, 2026, using the newly registered platform accounts, enter the Chinese keyword “心力衰竭” (corresponding to the English “heart failure”) in the search bars of the two platforms. Based on the default comprehensive sorting of the platform, initially retrieve the top 150 videos from the search results. Inclusion criteria include HF-related videos presented in simplified Chinese, with a duration ranging from 30 s to 1 h. Exclusion criteria include content unrelated to HF, duplicate published/reposted videos, video records of outpatient consultations/ward rounds/surgeries, interview videos, commercial advertisements, non-Chinese videos, and videos whose publishers’ identities cannot be confirmed. A total of 190 videos were finally included (87 from TikTok and 103 from Bilibili). The detailed screening process is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart for searching heart failure videos (January 10–11, 2026).

Data collection

The following data were extracted for each included video and recorded in a standardized spreadsheet: website address, the interactive data of the videos (number of likes, comments, saves, and shares), video duration, video content, and sources of the videos (uploader). Each video was coded using a multi‑label content classification scheme to record the presence or absence of nine distinct heart failure‑related educational domains: definition, symptoms, causes, assessment, treatment, prognosis, complications, risk factors, and prevention. A single video could cover multiple domains simultaneously, with each domain coded as 1 if present and 0 if absent. The sources of the videos (i.e., the identities of the uploaders) are classified into 4 categories: (1) Professional Individuals: Cardiovascular specialists with certifications, whose qualifications are verified and marked by the platform. (2) Non-professional individuals: These include doctors from other disciplines, individuals with a medical background (such as nurses and medical students), and those without any medical background (such as ordinary internet celebrities and patients). (3) Professional institutions: Official accounts of medical institutions, medical associations, etc. (4) Non-professional institutions: Enterprises, self-media teams, etc.

Video assessments

Three validated tools were used to assess the video quality, including GQS, mDISCERN, and PEMAT-A/V14. All the evaluations were independently conducted by two cardiologists with more than 10 years of clinical experience in cardiovascular medicine.

GQS

A 5-point Likert scale, used to conduct a comprehensive evaluation of the overall effectiveness and smoothness of the video14:

Score 1: Poor quality, poor flow of the site, most information missing, not at all useful for patients.

Score 2: Generally poor quality and poor flow, some information listed but many important topics missing, of very limited use to patients.

Score 3: Moderate quality, suboptimal flow, some important information is adequately discussed but others poorly discussed, somewhat useful for patients.

Score 4: Good quality and generally good flow, most of the relevant information is listed, but some topics are not covered, useful for patients.

Score 5: Excellent quality and excellent flow, very useful for patients.

mDISCERN

There are 5 questions included, each to be scored as “yes” (1 point) or “no” (0 points)15. The total score ranges from 0 to 5 points. The assessment items include:

Are the aims clear and achieved?

Are reliable sources of information used?

Is the information presented balanced and unbiased?

Are additional sources of information listed for patient reference?

Are areas of uncertainty mentioned?

PEMAT-A/V

This tool is used to evaluate two different dimensions: understandability (PEMAT-U) and actionability (PEMAT-A). The final score is the percentage of applicable items that meet the criteria16.

The inter-rater reliability of the scores for mDISCERN, GQS and PEMAT was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa (κ) coefficient between the two main raters17. The disagreements among the raters were resolved through discussion. If necessary, they would consult a third senior researcher.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the R software (version 4.3.3). In this study, all continuous variables were non-normally distributed and were described using the median and quartiles [M(P25, P75)]. Categorical variables were described using frequency and percentage (%). The comparisons between the two groups were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank sum test or the chi-square test, while comparisons among three or more groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient was used to analyze the correlations among different video variables as well as the relationships between the variables and the scores. Multivariable linear regression was performed to identify independent predictors of GQS score, including variables with P < 0.10 in univariable analysis; multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor. All statistical tests were conducted using two-tailed tests. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

The content coverage analysis (Fig. 2C) indicates that the video content mainly focuses on clinical symptoms (71.1%) and treatment plans (66.3%). However, information regarding prevention (36.3%) and complications (25.8%) is relatively insufficient. In terms of content, the video interaction data related to “definition” on Bilibili is the highest, while the “evaluation” type videos on TikTok have received relatively higher levels of interaction and attention.

Video assessments

Among the 190 videos, the majority had GQS scores concentrated around 3 points (n = 121, 63.7%), followed by 4 points (n = 35, 18.4%) and 2 points (n = 29, 15.3%). Only 5 videos (2.6%) received a score of 5 (Fig. 3A). The mDISCERN score was mainly 3 points (n = 142, 74.7%), followed by 4 points (n = 30, 15.8%). Only a few videos received 5 points (n = 1, 0.5%) or were below 3 points (n = 17, 8.9%; Fig. 3B). The PEMAT-U score was mainly 69% (n = 52, 27.4%), and 100% was only observed in 2 cases (1.1%; Fig. 3C). The PEMAT-A score had the highest proportion of 50% (n = 71, 37.4%), with only 2 cases scoring 100% (1.1%) and 2 cases scoring 0% (1.1%) (Fig. 3D). There was no statistically significant difference in the GQS score, mDISCERN score, and PEMAT-U score between Bilibili and TikTok (all P > 0.05). The PEMAT-A score of HF short videos on Bilibili was significantly higher than that on TikTok (P = 0.029; Fig. 3E), suggesting that videos on Bilibili are more likely to provide specific action guidance.

The median score of PEMAT-U in non-professional institutions was the highest, but the sample size was small and the representativeness was limited (P = 0.07; Fig. 4C). The median GQS score for each topic was 3 (moderate level), indicating that there is still room for improvement in the overall video quality (Fig. 5A). The quartiles (P75 = 4.00) for the definitions, causes, and treatments topics in the videos were higher than those of most other topics, indicating that some of the related content videos had reached a good quality level. The mDISCERN scores for each topic were generally 3 (3, 3). However, the videos containing preventive content had the lowest reliability lower limit (P5 = 1.50), suggesting that there was a significant risk of misleading or inaccurate information regarding this topic (Fig. 5B). In the PEMAT-U scoring system, the videos that include definitions and etiology content have a relatively high lower limit of comprehensibility (P5 being 62% and 54%, respectively), indicating that these fundamental topics place greater emphasis on public understanding in terms of language clarity and organizational logic (Fig. 5C). The distribution of the PEMAT-A score, which includes content on complications and prevention, was relatively better [75% (50%, 75%)], indicating that these topics in the videos were more inclined to provide specific risk management or health behavior recommendations and were more action-oriented (Fig. 5D).

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationships among interaction data, video duration, content themes, and ratings (Fig. 6). The results showed that there was a significant moderate positive correlation among the four quality assessment indicators (P < 0.001), indicating that the quality evaluations of different dimensions are interrelated. Among them, GQS had the strongest correlation with PEMAT-U (r = 0.594) and PEMAT-A (r = 0.538). The four interaction indicators (likes, comments, saves, and shares) showed a very strong positive correlation (r > 0.80, P < 0.001). This indicates that the popularity of the video is highly consistent across different interaction dimensions. However, the interaction indicators showed no significant correlation or a weak negative correlation with the various scores, indicating that high user interaction does not necessarily equate to high information quality. The duration of the video was weakly positively correlated with GQS (r = 0.247, P < 0.001), PEMAT-U (r = 0.206, P < 0.01), and PEMAT-A (r = 0.245, P < 0.001). This suggests that longer videos may contain more comprehensive, easier-to-understand, and more operational information. The video source was weakly positively correlated with the GQS score (r = 0.170, P < 0.05) and the PEMAT-U score (r = 0.192, P < 0.01), but had no correlation with the mDISCERN score and the PEMAT-A score (P > 0.05). In terms of the content theme, the three topics of complications, risk factors, and prevention are strongly positively correlated with each other (0.530 < r < 0.686, all P < 0.001), forming a closely related “risk management” content cluster. The indicators related to prognosis, complications, risk factors, etc., generally showed a negative correlation with the user interaction metrics (-0.275 < r < -0.163, all P < 0.05), while the definitions were positively correlated with likes, comments, and shares (0.149 < r < 0.216, all P < 0.05). This indicates that users may prefer the explanatory videos about “What is HF”, but it is difficult to trigger widespread user interaction for more complex or serious topics. Furthermore, the defined related videos were positively correlated with the scores of GQS, mDISCERN, and PEMAT-U (0.147 < r < 0.232, all P < 0.05); symptom-related videos were positively correlated with GQS (r = 0.196, P < 0.01); and etiology-related videos were positively correlated with PEMAT-U score (r = 0.189, P < 0.01). The videos related to assessment, treatment, prognosis, complications, risk factors, and prevention were positively correlated with the PEMAT-A score (0.153 < r < 0.335, all P < 0.05). This indicates that the type of content has a limited impact on the quality score. The quality of basic cognitive content is slightly better, while the content related to specific clinical interventions and management tends to provide more explicit action guidance.

Multivariable analysis

Univariable linear regression was first performed, and variables with P < 0.10 were included in the multivariable linear regression model. The final model included video duration, uploader, number of content domains, definition, symptoms, causes and assessment. Variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity, with all VIF values < 3.5 indicating no significant collinearity. Multivariable linear regression analysis showed that longer video duration (β = 0.181, P = 0.029) and inclusion of symptom-related content (β = 0.172, P = 0.048) were independent positive predictors of higher GQS scores. Inclusion of disease definition showed a marginally significant positive association with higher quality (β = 0.143, P = 0.079). The model was statistically significant (F = 3.561, P = 0.001).

Discussion

With the rapid development of digital health communication, short-video platforms have become one of the core channels for the public to obtain health information. TikTok and Bilibili, as two highly influential short-video platforms in China, with their large user bases and efficient dissemination mechanisms, play a significant role in the field of health science popularization18,19. HF, as a chronic cardiovascular disease with high incidence and mortality rates worldwide, its disease management is highly dependent on patients’ correct understanding of the disease and their self-management capabilities20,21. Although international authoritative guidelines have provided clear recommendations on the assessment, treatment, and prevention of HF22,23, there is still a significant lack of awareness of the disease at the public level. In this context, short videos, due to their advantages of presenting information in an intuitive manner, having a fast dissemination speed, and being easy to understand, have shown great potential in enhancing public awareness of complex diseases such as HF and promoting healthy behaviors. However, the quality of health information directly affects the effectiveness of dissemination and patient safety. Poor or misleading information may undermine public trust and even lead to harmful health decisions24. Therefore, this study focuses on the HF-related short videos on TikTok and Bilibili. Through multi-dimensional assessment of their characteristics, quality, and key influencing factors, it provides data support and practical references for optimizing HF health science popularization strategies, improving the quality of popularization, and enhancing the dissemination efficiency.

This study reveals significant differences in the characteristics of HF science popularization short videos between TikTok and Bilibili: The interaction data of TikTok videos is significantly higher than that of Bilibili, which is mainly composed of professional individuals (80.5%). While the duration of Bilibili videos is significantly longer, the sources of the videos are more diverse. This difference essentially stems from the core positioning of the two platforms and the differences in user behavior preference25. The algorithm of TikTok is highly optimized for short, concise, and impactful content, which meets the users’ need to quickly obtain information during their fragmented time. It can quickly capture the audience’s attention and promote interaction. Professional doctors may be more inclined to conduct lightweight scientific explanations on TikTok in order to reach a wider audience. The community culture of Bilibili leans towards knowledge learning. Users have a high tolerance for long content, so the videos are longer in duration and have deeper content. This discovery is consistent with the previous research conclusions of health-related short videos. For instance, Zheng et al.26 found in their study on videos related to liver cancer that the interaction volume of TikTok videos was significantly higher than that of Bilibili videos, while the duration of Bilibili videos was longer. Chen et al.’s27 research on the popular science video about thyroid nodules also observed similar differences in platform characteristics. The bias toward “emphasizing foundational knowledge while downplaying prognostic complications” evident in this research content is fundamentally the result of both user demand and the difficulty of science communication. From the perspective of user needs, the public’s understanding of diseases usually begins with “what it is (definition),” “what are the symptoms,” and “what to do (treatment).” Such content is closely related to daily life and is likely to attract attention. And aspects such as prognosis and complications, which pertain to the long-term outcome of the disease, have lower user awareness and receive relatively less attention. From the perspective of scientific communication difficulty, prognosis assessment and complication management require consideration of individual patient conditions (such as underlying diseases and treatment responses), which is highly specialized and has complex expressions. Ordinary publishers find it difficult to provide accurate scientific explanations, while professional publishers also need to balance professionalism and accessibility, resulting in a relatively low output of such content. This bias is also present in the health education videos about diseases such as myocardial infarction28 and uterine fibroids15, indicating that this is a common problem in health education short videos.

This study indicates that the overall quality of HF short videos is at a moderate level. Although Bilibili videos scored higher on the PEMAT-A scale than TikTok videos, there remains significant room for improvement overall. This finding is consistent with previous cross‑sectional studies evaluating health information quality on Chinese short‑video platforms, which also reported that the overall quality is below average and needs further improvement29,30. This situation highlights the insufficient accuracy and practicality of HF short videos, making them inadequate to meet patients’ professional needs for disease management. For chronic diseases like HF that require long-term self-management, actionable guidance is of crucial clinical significance20. For instance, clearly informing the specific grams of sodium salt intake per day, recommending the types and frequencies of exercises suitable for patients with HF, and instructing on how to monitor weight and symptom changes daily. Notably, such quantified lifestyle and dietary recommendations vary to some extent across regional clinical guidelines and international consensus statements. It is necessary for these videos to remind viewers that the content is not a substitute for professional medical consultation and that individuals should follow personalized guidance from their own healthcare providers.

This study employed the Kruskal–Wallis H test to validate the impact of video source on video quality. Results indicated that only the GQS score exhibited statistically significant differences among different uploaders (P = 0.045), with professional institutions producing the highest video quality. Spearman correlation analysis was further employed to validate the relationship between video sources and GQS scores. This is consistent with the research conclusions of He et al.29 in the educational videos on inflammatory bowel disease and Zuo et al.31 in the educational videos on osteoarthritis, confirming that professional background is a key positive influencing factor for the quality of educational videos. The advantage of professional entities lies in their professional medical knowledge background: (1) Accuracy of information: professional entities are familiar with the HF diagnosis and treatment guidelines and clinical norms and can accurately convey disease-related knowledge, avoiding information deviations. (2) Logical content, professional entities are good at constructing clear knowledge frameworks and presenting complex medical concepts systematically, thereby enhancing the comprehensibility of the content32. It is worth noting that although the quality scores of professional institutions were generally higher, their proportion in this study was only 7.4% (14 cases). This phenomenon may be related to the distribution of their educational resources and the positioning of their communication strategies. Most medical institutions tend to release scientific popularization content through traditional channels (such as their official websites and official accounts), and they pay limited attention and invest relatively little in short-video platforms, which results in their insufficient influence in the field of short-video scientific popularization.

Shockingly, there is no significant positive correlation between the user interaction data of the video and the scores of multiple information quality assessments. Even in some cases, there is a weak negative correlation. This indicates that on the short-video platform, the “spread popularity” of HF information is disconnected from its “information quality.” This discovery is consistent with the results of numerous studies on other health topics, suggesting that this is a common issue in the current field of health science popularization videos33,34. The reasons for this “quality-disconnection between content and dissemination” phenomenon are multifaceted. Firstly, the platform’s recommendation algorithms play a central role. Algorithms usually distribute content based on immediately quantifiable metrics such as completion rate and interaction rate. However, these metrics are more often influenced by the entertainment value of the video, the attractiveness of the title, the background music, or the personal charm of the host, rather than the depth and accuracy of the medical content. This algorithm-based recommendation mechanism is prone to creating an information bubble. Highly interactive videos are continuously pushed to users because they align with their preferences, even if they are of average quality35. Secondly, user behavior patterns are crucial. The majority of ordinary users lack a professional medical knowledge background and are unable to effectively distinguish the authenticity and quality level of the information. Their interactive decisions are more often based on emotional resonance, curiosity about the title, or simple trust in the issuer’s certification mark (such as “doctor”), rather than a critical assessment of the information itself35,36. Finally, the strategies of content creators have also contributed to this phenomenon. Some creators, especially those from non-professional sources, may resort to “clickbait” headlines, exaggerated symptom descriptions, or overly simplistic treatment plans in an attempt to attract attention in the fierce competition for traffic37. This weak correlation conceals significant public health risks. The recommendation mechanism that overly relies on user interaction data may inadvertently expand the dissemination range of low-quality or misleading health information.

Furthermore, this study further explored the independent influencing factors of video quality through multivariable linear regression. The results indicated that longer video duration and coverage of symptom-related content were independent positive predictors of higher GQS score, while other baseline characteristics showed no significant independent association after adjustment. Longer videos tend to contain more comprehensive and systematic knowledge, providing more detailed and standardized health information for the public. Symptom-oriented content is the most concerned entry point for audiences, and targeted interpretation of clinical symptoms also helps improve the overall structural integrity and normative quality of short videos. Future health communication practice can focus on optimizing content structure, enriching systematic symptom interpretation, and guiding reasonably arranged video length, so as to improve the overall quality of heart failure-related short-video popular science.

Although this study strives for rigor, it still has the following limitations. Firstly, in terms of the sample and scope, we only included Chinese short videos on two major Chinese platforms. The sample size is relatively small, and other important platforms such as Redbubble and YouTube were not covered. Therefore, the conclusions should be treated with caution when being generalized to other languages, cultural backgrounds, or platforms. Secondly, in terms of assessment tools, although the GQS, mDISCERN, and PEMAT scales are widely used in health information assessment, their reliability and validity in the rapidly evolving medium of short videos still require further verification and improvement through future research. Furthermore, since the evaluators were all from the same healthcare system, evaluator bias may be present. In addition, there may be significant differences in the knowledge and professional experience among medical staff, which could lead to potential discrepancies between raters. Again, the research design was a cross-sectional study, and video sampling was completed only over two consecutive days, which was insufficient to capture the dynamic changes in the quality of HF short videos. Finally, the influence of the algorithm was difficult to completely separate. Although we used newly registered accounts to reduce the impact of personalized recommendations, the search results were still dominated by the platform’s default sorting algorithm, which might prevent the sample from fully representing the overall picture of all relevant videos on the platform.

In the future, the research scope should be expanded to include more short-video platforms and conduct cross-national comparisons between China and Western countries. Longitudinal monitoring should be performed to dynamically track changes in content quality and dissemination patterns. Meanwhile, hospitals, cardiovascular associations, and professional medical institutions should be encouraged to increase the production and release of standardized, guideline-based heart failure health education content. Targeted popular science materials should be developed according to the knowledge gaps and health literacy levels of different populations, such as elderly patients, high-risk groups, and family caregivers. Furthermore, more practical and validated evaluation tools and quality rating scales specifically designed for short-video health information should be established. Platforms should optimize their recommendation algorithms and content review mechanisms to prioritize high-quality, reliable, and actionable medical information instead of only relying on click-through and interaction rates. Emerging artificial intelligence‑driven knowledge graph techniques have shown great potential in structuring fragmented medical knowledge and supporting clinical decision‑making, which provides new ideas for standardizing health information dissemination on short‑video platforms38. Finally, heart failure education via short videos should be integrated into routine clinical management, telemedicine follow-up, and remote monitoring systems. This integration aims to form a closed-loop management model that connects online science popularization with offline clinical care. Simply improving the quality of online health education content is not enough. Without integration into standardized clinical care pathways and regular follow-up systems, it cannot substantially optimize long-term prognosis in heart failure patients. It is worth noting that telemedicine and remote monitoring have been proven to be effective39. They facilitate continuous patient engagement and early recognition of clinical deterioration, thereby improving heart failure outcomes. Standardized short-video health education can serve as a valuable complement to telemedicine strategies. It helps patients with systematic self-management and prevents them from becoming an isolated, unregulated source of medical information.

Conclusions

This study systematically evaluated the quality and characteristics of HF-related short videos on Bilibili and TikTok. The results showed that the overall quality of the videos was moderate, and there was a scarcity of excellent content; the content distribution was uneven, with more emphasis on symptoms and treatment and less on prevention and complications. Multivariable linear regression analysis further identified that longer video duration and inclusion of symptom-related content were independent positive predictors of higher video quality, while other factors such as uploader type had no significant independent impact after adjustment. The research has confirmed the “quality-disconnection” phenomenon, which means high interaction volume does not necessarily indicate high quality. This highlights the potential biases of platform algorithms in the distribution of health information. Professional sources are the key guarantee of quality, and their videos perform better in terms of reliability and overall evaluation. Furthermore, the video performs reasonably well in terms of comprehensibility, but it lacks specific and actionable instructions, which limits its practical support for patients’ self-management.

In summary, the HF science popularization videos generally have inconsistent quality, an insufficient supply of professional content, and the risk of information distortion due to the influence of traffic. These problems may affect the effectiveness of science popularization and even mislead health decisions. To address these issues and give full play to the role of short videos in HF health education, it is necessary to integrate HF short-video education into routine clinical management, telemedicine follow-up, and remote monitoring systems, forming a closed-loop management model connecting online popularization and offline clinical care. In the future, it will be necessary to promote collaboration among platforms, professional creators, and regulatory authorities to optimize algorithms and review mechanisms, enhance the supply of professional content, and establish standardized evaluation systems. This will better leverage the positive role of short videos in public health education and chronic disease management.
