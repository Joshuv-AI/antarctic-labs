Association between preoperative prognostic nutritional index and postoperative delirium in older adults with hip fractures: a real-world study
Authors: Qian Wu, Yongsheng Tian, Tianyu Zhao, Xiaolan Feng, Wenquan Ding, Huiji Xu, Chuchu Wang, Yahui Tong, Hongxia Zhu, Dinghau Jiang, Lixin Huang, Wu Xu, Shenghao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic nutritional index (PNI), a marker reflecting nutritional and immunological status, has been proposed as a predictor of adverse postoperative outcomes. However, whether its association with postoperative delirium (POD) differs between femoral neck fractures and intertrochanteric fractures remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients aged ≥60 years who underwent hip fracture surgery between 2023 and 2024 PNI was calculated preoperatively, and POD was diagnosed during hospitalization using standardized clinical criteria. Analyses were conducted separately for femoral neck and intertrochanteric fracture groups for comparison. Restricted cubic spline (RCS), cox regression, subgroup analyses, receiver operating characteristic (ROC), propensity score matching (PSM), and Kaplan-Meier analyses were performed.ResultsA total of 339 patients with femoral neck fractures and 260 patients with intertrochanteric fractures were included in this study. A significant association between preoperative PNI and POD was observed in patients with femoral neck fractures. In patients with femoral neck fractures, RCS analysis demonstrated an approximately linear inverse relationship between PNI and POD risk (p for trend < 0.001). In Cox proportional hazards models, each one-unit increase in PNI was associated with a 24% reduction in the risk of POD (HR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.66-0.88, p < 0.001). ROC curve analysis indicated moderate predictive performance(AUC = 0.699).Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the association.ConclusionA lower preoperative PNI was associated with an increased risk of POD among older adults with femoral neck fractures, but not among those with intertrochanteric fractures. PNI may serve as a simple tool for preoperative risk stratification in patients with femoral neck fractures.

Introduction

Hip fractures constitute a substantial public health challenge in the aging population worldwide. The global incidence is projected to increase from 1.26 million cases in 1990 to 4.5 million by 2050, with nearly half of these cases expected to occur in Asia, particularly in China [1]. Among older adults, the one-year mortality rate following hip fracture is as high as 12.70%, largely driven by poor functional recovery and a high burden of postoperative complications [2]. These complications include postoperative delirium (POD), pneumonia, urinary tract infections, deep vein thrombosis, and hemorrhage [3,4]. It is estimated that approximately one-third of elderly patients develop at least one postoperative complication, and 7.2% experience multiple complications [5].

POD is one of the most frequent and clinically significant complications following hip fracture surgery in older adults [6]. This acute postoperative neurocognitive disorder is characterized by the sudden onset of fluctuating disturbances in attention and awareness. Reported incidence rates vary widely, ranging from 4.70% to 74.00%, depending on patient characteristics and the diagnostic criteria applied [7,8]. The pathogenesis of POD is multifactorial, resulting from the complex interplay between predisposing vulnerabilities, such as advanced age, pre-existing cognitive impairment, frailty, and malnutrition, and acute precipitating factors including surgical trauma, anesthesia exposure, postoperative pain, sleep disturbance, and immobilization [9]. At the neurobiological level, accumulating evidence suggests that surgery-induced systemic inflammation, blood-brain barrier dysfunction, oxidative stress, and cholinergic neurotransmission imbalance collectively contribute to the acute cognitive deterioration observed in susceptible individuals [10]. POD is associated with substantial adverse outcomes, including prolonged hospital stay, increased healthcare costs, higher likelihood of discharge to institutional care, accelerated functional decline, and increased short- and long-term mortality [11,12]. Despite ongoing advances in perioperative management, POD remains challenging to predict and prevent, highlighting the urgent need for simple, reliable, and clinically accessible tools to facilitate early risk stratification and targeted preventive strategies in this vulnerable population.

Accumulating evidence indicates that preoperative nutritional status plays a pivotal role in shaping postoperative outcomes, including the risk of POD [13]. Malnutrition can compromise immune function, impair wound healing, increase susceptibility to infection, and adversely affect cerebral physiology, thereby contributing to the development of delirium. The prognostic nutritional index (PNI), calculated as serum albumin (g/L) + 5 × total lymphocyte count (×109/L), is a simple, objective, and cost-effective composite marker reflecting both nutritional reserve and immune competence. Originally proposed to predict surgical outcomes in patients with gastrointestinal malignancies, PNI has since demonstrated prognostic value across a broad spectrum of surgical populations. Recent studies have reported that lower preoperative PNI is associated with an increased risk of POD in older adults with hip fractures [14]. Nevertheless, important gaps remain in literature. Most prior investigations have treated hip fractures as a homogeneous condition, combining femoral neck and intertrochanteric fractures despite their distinct biomechanical characteristics, surgical strategies, and physiological stress responses [15,16]. Direct comparisons of the predictive performance of PNI between these fracture subtypes are limited. Moreover, previous studies have predominantly relied on cross-sectional logistic regression models, which do not account for the time-dependent nature of POD onset and often lack robust methods, such as propensity score matching (PSM), to mitigate confounding.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between preoperative PNI and POD risk separately in femoral neck and intertrochanteric fracture patients. We employed Cox proportional hazards models with progressive covariate adjustment, PSM for sensitivity analysis, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves to comprehensively characterize temporal dynamics and compare whether differences existed between fracture types.

Methods

Study design and cohort selection

This retrospective observational study included patients aged 60 years and older who were diagnosed with hip fractures and underwent surgical treatment at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, a large tertiary teaching hospital, between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2024. A total of 689 patients with hip fractures were initially identified. Patients were subsequently excluded according to the following criteria: (1) a documented history of preoperative delirium or other psychiatric disorders, including depression or schizophrenia; (2) multiple or pathological hip fractures; (3) incomplete key clinical data during the follow-up period; (4) anesthesia techniques other than general anesthesia; and (5) the presence of severe infection or severe dysfunction of the heart (New York Heart Association class III-IV), liver (Child-Pugh class C), or kidneys (chronic kidney disease stage 4–5 or eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2). This retrospective observational study represents Level III evidence.

Endpoint events and follow-up

Consistent with our previous report [17], POD was assessed twice daily-from postoperative day 1 to day 7-at 8:00 am and 6:00 pm by trained nurses and physicians using the Chinese revised version of the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM-CR). All assessment results were prospectively documented in the electronic medical record (EMR) system. The data were subsequently reviewed and analyzed retrospectively by three independent investigators. A CAM-CR total score greater than 22 was considered indicative of delirium [18].

Variables and data quality control

Preoperative nutritional status was assessed using the PNI, calculated as serum albumin (g/L) + 5 × total lymphocyte count (/L) [19]. Consistent with previous studies, to minimize the influence of extreme values and facilitate classification analysis [20,21], PNI was additionally categorized into tertiles: T1 (23.90–41.05), T2 (41.05–44.75), and T3 (44.75–57.40).

Based on existing literature and clinical plausibility, potential confounding variables were identified independently by three clinicians (YT, TZ and QW) through the hospital EMR system and subsequently verified manually. These covariates were categorized into four main domains. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, educational level, and body mass index (BMI). Comorbidities comprised hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and a history of cerebral infarction. Laboratory parameters were obtained from fasting (≥8 h) venous blood samples collected within 24 h prior to surgery and included hemoglobin (Hb), red blood cell count (RBC), white blood cell count (WBC), lymphocyte count (LYMPH), eosinophil count (EOS), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides (TG), glucose (GLU), creatinine (Cr), and serum albumin (ALB). All biochemical analyses were performed in the central laboratory of hospital using standardized automated analyzers under strict internal quality control procedures. Surgery-related variables included duration of anesthesia, the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, and type of surgical procedure, categorized as closed reduction and internal fixation (CRIF), open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), hemiarthroplasty, or total hip arthroplasty (THA).

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the independent samples t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, chi-square test, one-way analysis of variance, or Kruskal-Wallis H test, as appropriate. Cox proportional hazards regression models with progressive covariate adjustment (Models 1–3) were constructed to evaluate the association between PNI and POD in both fracture types. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, and BMI. Model 3 incorporated comprehensive adjustment for demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, surgical variables, and ASA classification. In addition, collinearity analysis was performed for the covariates included in the models to ensure the absence of significant multicollinearity. PNI was analyzed both as a continuous variable and categorized into tertiles to assess dose-response relationships. Since preliminary Cox regression analyses revealed no significant association between PNI and POD in intertrochanteric fracture patients, subsequent analyses were restricted to femoral neck fracture patients. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions with three knots (10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) were used to model the dose-response relationship. Threshold effect analyses identified potential inflection points using segmented Cox regression, with model fit compared by log-likelihood ratio test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed for both fracture types to determine optimal PNI cutoff values using the Youden index, with corresponding sensitivity, specificity, and other diagnostic metrics calculated.

To validate the robustness of the findings and control for residual confounding, several sensitivity analyses were performed, including subgroup analyses, additional covariate adjustment, and PSM. Subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, education level, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, history of cerebral infarction, surgical anesthesia time, and operation type were conducted with interaction tests. In addition, further adjustment was performed for clinically important POD-related factors, including dementia status, perioperative blood transfusion, postoperative ICU admission, opioid exposure, benzodiazepine exposure, and the highest postoperative visual analog scale (VAS) pain score within 48 h after surgery. PSM was also performed separately for both fracture types. Patients were stratified into high-PNI and low-PNI groups based on ROC-derived cutoff values. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm with a caliper width of 0.20 was applied, adjusting for demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, surgical variables, and ASA classification. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD < 0.10 indicating adequate balance). Cox regression (Model 4) was applied to the matched cohorts with full covariate adjustment, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by PNI tertiles were compared using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0). All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.050 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

As shown in Figure 1, 689 patients were initially screened according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, 35 patients with a previous history of delirium or other psychiatric disorders were excluded, as were 17 patients with multiple or pathological hip fractures, 23 patients with incomplete key clinical data, 7 patients who underwent anesthesia techniques other than general anesthesia, and 8 patients with severe infection or severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction. Ultimately, 599 patients were included in the final analysis, comprising 339 patients with femoral neck fractures and 260 patients with intertrochanteric fractures.

Patients screening and study enrollment flowchart.

Baseline characteristics of 260 patients with intertrochanteric fractures and 339 patients with femoral neck fractures stratified by POD status are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Among intertrochanteric fracture patients, those who developed POD were significantly older (85.00 ± 5.00 vs. 79.00 ± 8.00 years, p < 0.001) and had higher ASA classification scores (p = 0.005) compared with non-POD patients. In the femoral neck fracture cohort, POD patients were older (81.00 ± 9.00 vs. 77.00 ± 8.00 years, p = 0.023), had higher rates of hypertension (80.77% vs. 53.99%, p = 0.008) and prior cerebral infarction (26.92% vs. 9.27%, p = 0.012), exhibited lower EOSs (p = 0.029), LYMPHs (p = 0.009), and ALB levels (p = 0.038), and had shorter anesthesia duration (p = 0.028). Notably, preoperative PNI was significantly lower in POD patients across both fracture types (intertrochanteric: p = 0.440; femoral neck: p = 0.004). Additionally, surgical approach differed significantly in the femoral neck fracture group (p = 0.003), with hemiarthroplasty more frequently performed in POD patients (76.92% vs. 42.17%).

To further explore the association between PNI and POD, patients were categorized into PNI tertiles (Table 1). Higher PNI levels were associated with younger age, higher BMI, increased LYMPHs, and higher ALB concentrations (all p < 0.001). Several hematological and metabolic parameters, including RBC, Hb, TC, LDL-C, and TG, increased progressively across tertiles. The distribution of ASA classification and surgical approach differed significantly among tertiles. Notably, a significant difference in POD incidence was observed across PNI tertiles (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed across PNI groups in sex, smoking or drinking status, educational level, diabetes, hypertension, history of cerebral infarction, or anesthesia duration.

Cox regression models with progressive adjustment

Cox proportional hazards regression models with progressive covariate adjustment were constructed to evaluate the association between PNI and POD in both fracture types (Table 2). For femoral neck fractures, Model 1 examined PNI as a continuous variable without covariate adjustment, demonstrating a significant inverse association (HR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.82–0.95, p < 0.001). When PNI was analyzed as tertiles (T1 as reference), both T2 (HR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.07–0.60, p = 0.004) and T3 (HR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.07–0.59, p = 0.004) showed significantly reduced POD risk, with a significant trend across tertiles (p for trend < 0.001). In Model 2, after adjustment for age, gender, and BMI, PNI remained independently associated with POD both as a continuous variable (HR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.83–0.97, p = 0.007) and as tertiles (T2: HR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.07–0.65, p = 0.006; T3: HR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.08–0.83, p = 0.023; p for trend = 0.005). Model 3 incorporated comprehensive adjustment for all baseline covariates, including demographic factors, lifestyle variables, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, surgical factors, and ASA index levels. The protective effect of PNI persisted (continuous: HR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.66–0.88, p < 0.001; T2: HR = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.03–0.45, p = 0.002; T3: HR = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.02–0.52, p = 0.007; P for trend < 0.001). In contrast, among patients with intertrochanteric fractures, no significant association was observed between PNI and POD across all models, whether analyzed as a continuous variable or categorized into tertiles (all p > 0.050). As shown in Supplementary Table 2, all covariates included in the regression analyses exhibited acceptable collinearity statistics, with no evidence of significant multicollinearity in either the femoral neck fracture group or the intertrochanteric fracture group

RCS and threshold effect analysis in patients with femoral neck fractures

To examine the potential dose-response relationship between PNI and the risk of POD, RCS analysis was performed (Figure 2). The RCS curve suggested a largely linear inverse association between PNI and POD risk across the observed PNI range (approximately 30–52), with no evidence of nonlinearity (P for nonlinearity = 0.989). Threshold effect analysis using a two-piece-piecewise logistic regression model identified an inflection point at PNI = 35.82 (Table 3). In the standard logistic regression model assuming linearity, each one-unit increase in PNI was associated with 10% lower odds of POD (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.83–0.98, p = 0.021). In the piecewise model, no significant association was observed below the threshold (PNI < 35.82), whereas a significant inverse association was detected above the threshold (PNI ≥ 35.8; OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.74–0.98, p = 0.030). However, comparison of the piecewise and linear models using the log-likelihood ratio test showed no significant difference in model fit (p = 0.439), indicating that the linear model adequately described the relationship between PNI and POD.

ROC curve analysis

ROC curve analysis was performed to assess the discriminative performance of preoperative PNI for POD in patients with femoral neck fractures (Supplementary Figure 1). PNI demonstrated moderate discriminative ability, with an area under the curve of 0.699. The optimal cutoff value of PNI was identified as 40.65 based on the Youden index. At this threshold, PNI yielded a sensitivity of 69.20% and a specificity of 71.90% for predicting POD. Additional diagnostic performance measures, including positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and overall accuracy, are provided in Supplementary Table 3. The corresponding ROC curve analysis and diagnostic performance metrics for patients with intertrochanteric fractures are presented in Supplementary Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 4.

Sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analysis

The association between PNI and POD across predefined subgroups is illustrated in Figure 3. Overall, higher PNI was significantly associated with lower POD risk (HR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.82–0.95, p = 0.001). This inverse association remained generally consistent across most subgroups stratified by gender, age, BMI, smoking status, educational level, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, history of cerebral infarction, surgical anesthesia time, and operation type. No significant interactions were observed across all subgroups (all p for interaction > 0.050). Notably, the association appeared consistent across different surgical procedures, including internal fixation, hemiarthroplasty, and total hip arthroplasty, suggesting that the protective effect of PNI on POD risk is independent of surgical approach.

Subgroup analyses of the association between PNI and POD in patients with femoral neck fractures.

Additional covariate adjustment

To further assess the robustness and stability of the association between preoperative PNI and POD, additional clinically relevant covariates, including perioperative blood transfusion, postoperative ICU admission, opioid exposure, benzodiazepine exposure, and highest postoperative VAS pain score, were additionally adjusted in Model 1. Model 2 was subsequently constructed by incorporating these variables together with all previously adjusted covariates. As shown in Supplementary Table 5, after further adjustment, the association between preoperative PNI and POD remained statistically significant in patients with femoral neck fractures (p < 0.001), whereas no significant association was observed in patients with intertrochanteric fractures (p > 0.050). These findings were generally consistent with the primary analyses.

Propensity score matching and Kaplan–Meier curves

PSM was performed separately for femoral neck fractures and intertrochanteric fractures based on predefined PNI cutoff values. For femoral neck fractures, 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.20 SDs was applied. Before matching, 235 patients were classified into the high-PNI group and 104 into the low-PNI group. After matching, 75 matched pairs (150 patients) were included in the final cohort (Table 4). All covariates, including demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, surgical factors, and ASA index levels, were adjusted in the matching process. Prior to matching, substantial imbalances were observed between groups, as evidenced by SMDs > 0.10 for multiple variables and statistically significant differences for several baseline characteristics. After matching, SMDs for most variables were reduced to < 0.10, and all P-values exceeded 0.050, indicating excellent covariate balance and comparability between groups. A similar PSM procedure was performed for patients with intertrochanteric fractures, resulting in 59 matched pairs (Supplementary Table 6), with comparable baseline characteristics achieved after matching (all p > 0.050).

As shown in Supplementary Table 7, which was applied to the PSM cohort with full covariate adjustment, the protective association between higher PNI and reduced POD risk remained significant in femoral neck fracture patients (continuous: HR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.38–0.86, p = 0.007; T3 vs T1: HR = 0.06, 95% CI: 0.00–0.77, p = 0.031; P for trend = 0.034). These findings confirmed the robustness of the observed association after accounting for potential confounding through PSM. Conversely, in patients with intertrochanteric fractures, no significant association was observed between PNI and POD across all four models, regardless of whether PNI was analyzed as a continuous variable or categorized into tertiles (all p > 0.050). The trend analysis likewise showed no significant linear relationship in any of the models (Model 1: p = 0.651; Model 2: p = 0.201; Model 3: p = 0.783; Model 4: p = 0.710).

To further visualize the association between PNI and POD risk in the PSM cohort, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis stratified by PNI tertiles was performed. For femoral neck fracture patients, the survival curves demonstrated significant differences across PNI tertiles (Figure 4, p = 0.030). Patients in the highest PNI tertile (T3) exhibited the lowest cumulative incidence of POD, while those in the lowest tertile (T1) showed the highest risk. The survival curves separated early in the postoperative period and remained divergent throughout the 7-day observation period, with the number at risk decreasing from 50 patients in each tertile at baseline to 41, 45, and 45 patients in T1, T2, and T3, respectively, by day 7. These findings provide visual confirmation of the dose-response relationship between preoperative PNI levels and POD risk, reinforcing the results from the Cox regression analyses. In contrast, Kaplan-Meier analysis for intertrochanteric fracture patients revealed no significant differences in POD-free survival across PNI tertiles (Supplementary Figure 3, p = 0.510), consistent with the Cox regression findings.

Discussion

In this cohort study of older adults undergoing hip fracture surgery, we found that lower preoperative PNI was significantly associated with an increased risk of POD in patients with femoral neck fractures, whereas no significant association was observed in those with intertrochanteric fractures. RCS analysis demonstrated an approximately linear inverse relationship between PNI and POD risk in femoral neck fracture patients, and this association remained robust after multivariable adjustment and PSM. Furthermore, the predictive performance of PNI showed moderate discriminative ability. These findings suggest that the prognostic relevance of preoperative nutritional and immunological status may differ according to fracture subtype.

The present findings extend emerging evidence on nutritional status and POD in elderly hip fracture populations. Recently, Karahan et al. [22] incorporated multiple nutritional-inflammatory biomarkers, including PNI and other composite indices, into an integrated prognostic framework for mortality prediction following hip fracture surgery in older adults, highlighting the growing interest in composite immunonutritional risk stratification models. And previous studies established associations between low preoperative PNI and increased POD risk. Mi et al. [23] demonstrated a correlation between preoperative PNI values and POD in elderly patients with hip fractures. Furthermore, Xu et al. [14] demonstrated that high PNI has a protective effect against POD in elderly patients with femoral neck fractures. Hong et al. [13] obtained the same results through systematic review and meta-analysis. However, these studies have some key limitations. First, most studies treated hip fractures as a homogeneous population, combining femoral neck fractures and intertrochanteric fractures in their analyses. Some studies only analyzed patients with femoral neck fractures, neglecting the differences between the two groups in anatomical location, vascular supply, surgical approach, and inflammatory characteristics. Second, most studies used logistic regression, failing to capture the time dependence of POD occurrence and data truncation. Third, few studies used PSM to rigorously control confounding factors. This study addresses some of these shortcomings. We used a Cox proportional hazards model to analyze time-varying changes, controlled for confounding factors with PSM, and made stepwise adjustments in four stratified models, supplemented by Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis. Our systematic comparison of the innovative predictive value of PNI across different fracture types revealed significant heterogeneity: PNI effectively predicted POD in femoral neck fractures but showed no correlation in intertrochanteric fractures. These improvements not only confirm previous findings but also deepen our understanding of fracture mechanisms by more rigorously revealing fracture-specific heterogeneity.

The association between low preoperative PNI and increased POD risk in femoral neck fracture patients is likely underpinned by several interconnected biological mechanisms. First, hypoalbuminemia-reflecting sustained elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α-primes the neuroimmune axis toward heightened reactivity. These peripheral inflammatory mediators access the central nervous system via vagal afferent signaling, circumventricular organ transport, and direct translocation across a structurally compromised blood-brain barrier (BBB), activating resident microglia and initiating neuroinflammatory cascades that ultimately precipitate delirium [10,24]. Second, hypoalbuminemia reduces plasma oncotic pressure while surgical stress downregulates key tight junction proteins-including occludin and claudin-5-increasing BBB permeability and facilitating the entry of neurotoxic substrates and activated immune cells into the brain parenchyma [25,26]. Third, protein-energy malnutrition curtails acetylcholine precursor availability and, in conjunction with lymphopenia-driven inflammatory mediator release, inhibits cholinesterase activity and potentiates central anticholinergic effects-collectively impairing the cholinergic transmission upon which attentional control and arousal regulation critically depend [27–

29]. Fourth, malnutrition depletes endogenous antioxidant reserves while surgical trauma simultaneously drives excessive reactive oxygen species generation, impairing mitochondrial respiratory chain function and reducing ATP synthesis capacity; in elderly patients with already diminished mitochondrial reserve, this renders cerebral tissue particularly vulnerable to perioperative hypoxia and hypoperfusion [30,31]. Fifth, lymphopenia compromises T-cell-mediated pathogen clearance, substantially elevating the risk of nosocomial infections-each an independent precipitant of POD-whose associated systemic inflammatory response further propagates neurological deterioration through neuroinflammation, microcirculatory dysfunction, and metabolic derangement [32,33]. These mechanisms operate in dynamic synergy: low PNI amplifies surgery-induced systemic inflammation, which disrupts BBB integrity, suppresses cholinergic transmission, overwhelms neuronal energy metabolism, and ultimately precipitates delirium [34]. The differential association observed between femoral neck fractures and intertrochanteric fractures may partly relate to differences in anatomical structure, surgical management, inflammatory burden, and patient characteristics between these two fracture types. Previous comparative evidence suggested that arthroplasty procedures may provide advantages over internal fixation techniques in femoral neck fractures with respect to functional outcomes and reoperation risk [35]. In the present study, femoral neck fracture patients more frequently underwent arthroplasty procedures, whereas intertrochanteric fractures were predominantly treated with internal fixation. In addition, intertrochanteric fracture patients in our cohort were generally older and frailer, which may have contributed more substantially to POD risk. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the observed subgroup differences may also reflect residual confounding, treatment heterogeneity, differences in event structure, or limited statistical power rather than true fracture-type-specific biological effects. Further large-scale, multicenter, and prospective studies are still needed to validate these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms.

Our findings may have implications for the perioperative management of elderly patients with femoral neck fractures. As an easily obtainable index derived from routinely available laboratory parameters, PNI may serve as a supplementary marker for identifying patients at increased risk of POD. Although the discriminative performance of PNI was moderate, patients with lower PNI levels may warrant closer perioperative monitoring and more comprehensive delirium prevention strategies, including environmental optimization, cognitive stimulation, early mobilization, optimized sleep hygiene, and cautious use of anticholinergic medications. In addition, multidisciplinary collaboration involving orthopedics, geriatrics, and clinical nutrition may help improve perioperative care in nutritionally vulnerable patients. However, given the moderate predictive performance of PNI observed in this study, it should not currently be considered a standalone risk stratification tool. In addition, sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors may substantially influence nutritional and immunological status in older patients [36], yet these variables were not comprehensively included in the present analysis. Therefore, further prospective multicenter studies are still required to validate its clinical utility and to determine whether combining PNI with other clinical predictors could further improve predictive accuracy.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths that merit consideration. First, we evaluated the association between preoperative PNI and POD using time-to-event analyses based on Cox proportional hazards models, allowing a more appropriate assessment of POD risk within the early postoperative period. Second, POD was systematically assessed using a validated instrument with twice-daily screening by trained personnel, which minimized outcome misclassification. Third, we performed comprehensive adjustment for clinically relevant confounders, supplemented by subgroup analyses, RCS modeling, and PSM, enhancing the robustness and consistency of our findings. Finally, the focus on fracture-specific analyses enabled identification of heterogeneity between femoral neck and intertrochanteric fractures, providing clinically meaningful insights.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design may limit causal inference and generalizability to other populations or healthcare settings. Second, although extensive covariate adjustment was performed, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be completely excluded, including baseline cognitive impairment, visual impairment, hearing impairment, and other sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors. Third, the relatively small number of POD events, particularly in subgroup and matched analyses, may have reduced statistical power and contributed to wide confidence intervals. The observed differences between fracture types should also be interpreted cautiously, as they may partly reflect residual confounding or limited statistical power rather than true biological heterogeneity. Furthermore, the present study did not include comparative analyses between PNI and other inflammatory or immunonutritional biomarkers. Finally, only patients receiving general anesthesia were included in this study; therefore, the generalizability of the findings to other anesthesia modalities may be limited.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a low preoperative PNI is a clinically relevant predictor of POD in elderly patients undergoing surgery for femoral neck fractures. These findings suggest that preoperative nutritional and immunological status may play a role in postoperative neurological vulnerability. Given its simplicity and accessibility, PNI may be a potentially useful supplementary marker for perioperative risk assessment; however, further large-scale prospective studies are needed before its routine clinical application can be recommended.
