Dietary glycyrrhizic acid improves growth performance and modulates upper respiratory microbiota in weaned piglets
Authors: Fang Wang, Yahui Sang, Jiaye Guo, Yin Fu, Mengyao Yang, Fa Shan, Yuancai Chen, Sumei Zhang, Xiaoying Li, Junqiang Li, Longxian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNatural products with dual immunomodulatory and antimicrobial functions offer promising strategies to reduce antibiotic use in livestock. Glycyrrhizic acid (GA), the principal bioactive component of licorice, has demonstrated anti-inflammatory and antiviral properties, yet its translational potential in swine health remains underexplored. This study evaluated the efficacy of GA in weaned piglets under commercial nursery conditions as an antibiotic alternative. A total of 225 weaned piglets were assigned to five groups: negative control (CON, basal diet), farm routine (FA, conventional antibiotics), and three GA-supplemented groups (GLL, 0.65 g/kg; GLM, 1.3 g/kg; GLH, 2.6 g/kg).ResultsThe result showed that dietary GA supplementation (2.6 g/kg) numerically improved growth performance and reduced cough scores, although not statistically significant. GA significantly decreased the diarrhea index and improved skin scores. GA also significantly increased serum IgG and IgM levels in piglets and showed a trend toward higher IgA levels. Furthermore, GA exhibited a trend toward lowering serum IL‑1β levels while upregulating IFN‑γ and IL‑10 levels. Regarding antioxidant parameters, GA significantly upregulated T‑SOD, GSH‑PX, and CAT activities and downregulated LDH activity. Metagenomic analysis revealed that high‑dose glycyrrhizic acid (GA) significantly increased the abundance of Alloprevotella, while decreasing the abundances of Moraxella pluranimalium and 11 other pathogenic species associated with respiratory diseases and lung injury, including Glaesserella parasuis, Mesomycoplasma hyorhinis, Mesomycoplasma hyopneumoniae, Streptococcus suis, among others, thereby reshaping the upper respiratory tract microbiota of pigs.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings support GA as a viable non-antibiotic strategy for improving immune function, antioxidant capacity, and respiratory health in weaned piglets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05622-5.

Background

The porcine industry provides one-third of the global meat consumption [1]. However, global swine production still faces challenges such as maintaining productivity while reducing dependence on antimicrobials. The use of antibiotics for sub-clinical preventive treatment or as growth promoters had been restricted by regulations in the EU’s 2006 ban and China’s 2020 prohibition [2]. Respiratory pathologies now constitute the primary driver of antimicrobial use in modern swine operations [3]. This therapeutic paradox underscores the urgency for alternatives that concurrently address disease management and growth optimization. Phytogenic supplements emerge as scientifically validated candidates, with demonstrated capacities to modulate immune-microbial crosstalk while enhancing production metrics [4–5].

Glycyrrhizic acid (GA) is a natural extract derived from the roots of Glycyrrhiza species owns notable antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, cough relief, antioxidant, and microbiota-modulating properties, as evidenced by clinical studies [6–9]. Previous studies highlight its therapeutic potential in respiratory disorders, since GA exhibits prophylactic effects against chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [10], acute respiratory distress syndrome [11], and it also showed clinical efficacy in managing unexplained chronic cough [12] and post-intubation sore throat and cough [13]. These findings collectively underscore the promising role of GA in the prevention and treatment of respiratory-related pathologies in animal husbandry.

Recently, there has been growing interest in the applications of GA within pig production systems. Glycyrrhizic acid (GA) exerts anti‑porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) activity by inhibiting the viral entry stage [14]. It also suppresses porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) infection and the secretion of pro‑inflammatory cytokines through the HMGB1/TLR4‑MAPK p38 signaling pathway [15]. These lines of evidence reflect its potential for the prevention and control of swine diseases. Moreover, GA has been shown to protect vital organs against endotoxemia-induced damage in pigs by modulating systemic inflammatory responses and improving systemic hemodynamics, highlighting its role in swine health management [16].

However, to date, the effects of GA on porcine production parameters and its interactions with upper respiratory microbiota remain poorly understood. This study aims to investigate the effects of GA on growth performance, immune function, and upper respiratory tract microbiota in weaned piglets under antibiotic-free conditions, thereby providing scientific evidence for the implementation of natural botanical alternatives in swine health management.

Methods

Source of glycyrrhizic acid

Glycyrrhizic acid (3%, batch number 20240401, from Luoyang Lansealy Company) was added to the piglet diet daily as a feed additive. The purity was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) according to the Chinese Veterinary Pharmacopoeia.

Animals and housing

The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Henan Agricultural University (approval No. HNND2023031506). All experiments were conducted in accordance with animal ethics guidelines. A randomized controlled trial using a completely randomized design was conducted on a commercial swine production farm (a Landrace×Yorkshire breeding herd with a capacity of over 30,000 sows). A total of 225 female weaned piglets (Landrace×Yorkshire) from the same weaning batch, 25 days of age with an initial body weight 5.46 ± 0.065 kg (mean ± SEM), were randomly assigned to five groups. Each group consisted of three replicates (n = 15 per replicate). Based on the results of preliminary experiments, three dose levels of glycyrrhizic acid were established. Accordingly, the experimental design consisted of five treatment groups (Fig. 1): (a) control group (CON): piglets in this group received no antibiotics or GA, and only underwent routine vaccination and deworming procedures; (b) farm routine group (FA): piglets in this group followed the routine farm vaccines and medication procedure; (c) high-dose GA group (GLH): Piglets in this group were fed with a diet containing 2.6 g/kg of GA; (d) medium-dose GA group (GLM): Piglets in this group were fed a diet with 1.3 g/kg of GA; (e) low-dose GA group (GLL): piglets in this group were provided a diet having 0.65 g/kg of GA. GA was uniformly mixed into the commercial feed for piglets according to the required dosage. The commercial feed complied with the dietary formulations (Supplementary Table 1), which were based on the NRC (2012) guidelines to meet or exceed the established nutritional requirements, and was provided by Shangqiu Shuanghexin Feed Co., Ltd., China. The farm medication and health program is presented in Supplementary Table 2.

The test group and detection index diagram of this study

The entire trial lasted for 40 days. It included a three-day adaptation feeding period and a subsequent 37-day experimental period. The study was carried out in a commercial pig nursery facility with a capacity of 6,000 piglets. The facility was equipped with slatted floors, automatic feeding and drinking systems, as well as environmental control systems. Environmental parameters were carefully regulated. In the first week, the temperature was maintained at 26 °C to 28 °C, then it decreased by 1 °C per week until it stabilized at 22 °C to 24 °C. The relative humidity was kept within the range of 60% to 70%. Throughout the entire trial period, all piglets had unrestricted, ad libitum access to both feed and water. Moreover, piglets from each group were raised in isolation within separate pens on the farm, ensuring that there was no contact between piglets from different groups.

Growth performance measurement

The individual body weight of the piglets was measured and then recorded on day 0th (as initial weight) and day 37th (as final weight) of the experimental trial. Additionally, the daily feed intake of piglets in each group was monitored and recorded. Subsequently, the average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated for each group.

Health monitoring and clinical scoring

Two certified veterinarians blinded to the study objectives and group assignments were hired to evaluate animal health and conduct the relevant clinical scoring. The cough frequency and fecal consistency of weaned piglets in each group were independently assessed by two veterinarians twice daily at 08:00 and 17:00, with each observation lasting 5 min, following the standardized scoring criteria [17, 18] (Supplementary Table 2). Over the whole experimental period, two veterinarians independently assessed the skin condition and hair color of piglets in each group on a weekly basis, following standardized scoring criteria, which were adapted from previous studies with modifications [19] (supplementary Table 2). A daily record of the number of piglet deaths and eliminations in each group was maintained.

Sample collection

On the 37th day of experimental period, which marked the final day of the experiment, blood samples, nasal and throat swabs were collected from the piglets. All blood and swab sampling procedures complied with internationally recognized animal welfare standards to minimize animal discomfort. Specifically, within each replicate, three piglets with body weights close to the final mean were randomly selected, and blood samples were drawn from their anterior vena cava using a 12-gauge needle. Ten milliliters of blood were collected into coagulation accelerator tubes and centrifuged to obtain serum, which was then used for the assessment of immune and antioxidant parameters. Nasal swabs were obtained from the posterior nasal region, and throat swabs were collected from the pharyngeal/laryngeal regions of the piglets. For each replicate group, the swabs were collected from nine randomly selected pigs. Subsequently, the swab samples placed into cryopreservation tubes, and promptly flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for the ensuing metagenomic testing.

Immune and antioxidant capability measurement

Serum immune parameters, including interferon‑γ (IFN‑γ, H025‑1‑2), immunoglobulin A (IgA, H108‑1‑2), immunoglobulin G (IgG, H106‑1‑1), immunoglobulin M (IgM, H109‑1‑1), tumor necrosis factor‑α (TNF‑α, H052‑1‑1), interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β, H002‑1‑1), interleukin‑6 (IL‑6, H007‑1‑1), and interleukin‑10 (IL‑10, H009‑1‑2), were measured using ELISA kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). The serum activities of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, G1610), total superoxide dismutase (T‑SOD, G4306), malondialdehyde (MDA, G4302), glutathione peroxidase (GSH‑PX, G4310), and catalase (CAT, G4307), were determined using commercial kits (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., China). All assays were performed strictly according to the manufacturers’ instructions, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate, with the mean value used for subsequent analysis.

Metagenomic testing

According to reference [20], swab samples were pooled in equal volumes to form one composite sample per pen prior to DNA extraction. For metagenomic analysis, each pen served as an independent biological replicate (n = 3 per treatment group). Total DNA was extracted from pooled swab samples of piglets using the E.Z.N.A.® Viral DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek). Subsequently, DNA libraries were constructed in accordance with the Illumina TruSeq DNA Sample Prep Guide (v.2), employing high-quality DNA samples (OD260/OD280 = 1.8–2.2, OD260/OD230 ≥ 2.0). Finally, paired-end sequencing (150-bp) was carried out by Shanghai Biozeron Biotechnology Co., Ltd (China). The sequencing depth for each pooled sample was ≥ 10 Gb of raw data. Raw reads were quality-filtered using fastp (version 0.23.4) with the following parameters: Q20 ≥ 95%, Q30 ≥ 90%, and reads with ambiguous bases (N) exceeding 10% were discarded. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases (Phred score < 20) were trimmed from both ends.

Bioinformatic analysis entailed the removal of host sequences (Bowtie 2 v2.4.5) [21], assembly (MEGAHIT v1.2.9) [22], and the prediction of open reading frames (MetaGeneMark v3.38) [23]. Subsequently, gene abundance was computed, and taxonomic classification was carried out with the aid of the NCBI non-redundant database and DIAMOND software (version 2.0.14) [24]. Finally, the Lowest Common Ancestor (LCA) algorithm was employed to gather gene counts and abundance data for each sample across diverse taxonomic levels, namely kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species [25]. All bioinformatics tools were run using default parameters unless otherwise specified, with software versions as indicated in parentheses. The alpha diversity indices, including richness, shannon index, and simpson index, were computed. Subsequently, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) were carried out using Tutools (https://www.cloudtutu.com) and Wekemo Bioincloud (https://bioincloud.tech/task-meta). Finally, random forest analysis was employed to showcase the disparities in species abundance among groups.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (v.26.0.0.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The pen served as the statistical unit for data analysis. One-way ANOVA was applied to data that met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05 and high significance at P < 0.01. A trend was defined as 0.05 ≤ P ≤ 0.10. For variables violating the homogeneity of variance assumption, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was employed. For blood parameters, the three piglets sampled per pen were averaged to obtain a single pen‑level value, and the pen (n = 3 per treatment) was used as the experimental unit. Clinical scores from the two veterinarians were averaged to obtain a daily pen‑level score, which was analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Results are expressed as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). To evaluate dose‑dependent effects, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was calculated between GA dosage and each parameter. A significant or monotonic correlation was interpreted as a dose‑dependent relationship. Graphical presentations were created using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Alpha diversity was evaluated using the Shannon, Simpson, and Invsimpson indices, whereas beta diversity was visualized by principal component analysis (PCOA). Random forest analysis was conducted using the online platform (https://bioincloud.tech/) with 500 decision trees and 10‑fold cross‑validation to identify the microbial taxa that best discriminated among treatment groups.

Results

Growth performance and clinical manifestations

Regarding growth performance, no significant differences in initial, final body weight, and mortality proportion were observed among any of the groups (P > 0.05) (Table 1). Although the GLH group exhibited the highest ADG and the lowest F/G, the improvements were not statistically significant compared with the CON or FA groups (P > 0.05). Specifically, ADG in the GLH group was 5.28% higher than in the CON group and 3.87% higher than in the FA group, while F/G was 2.6% lower than in the CON group and 0.67% lower than in the FA group (Table 1). No significant differences in mortality proportion were found among any of the groups.

Immune capability

The immune capability of piglets in each group was evaluated by measuring the serum immunoglobulin levels and cytokine expression (Fig. 2). Serum IgA levels were highest in the GLH group but did not differ significantly from those in the CON or FA groups, whereas a trend toward higher levels was observed compared with the GLL group (P = 0.090) (Fig. 2a). For serum IgG, the GLH group exhibited a trend toward higher levels compared with the CON group (P = 0.080) and a significant increase compared with the FA group (P < 0.05). Moreover, the GLH group had significantly higher IgG levels than both the GLM and GLL groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2a). Regarding serum IgM, the GLH group showed a significant increase compared with the CON group (P < 0.05) and a trend toward an increase compared with the FA group (P = 0.067). Additionally, the GLH group had significantly higher IgM levels than the GLL group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2a). Of note, for all three immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, and IgM), no significant differences or trends were detected between the FA and CON groups (Fig. 2a). Overall, all three immunoglobulin parameters showed positive correlations with the dosage of glycyrrhizic acid.

The cytokine expression levels are presented in Fig. 2b. The highest IFN-γ level was observed in the GLH group, which did not differ significantly from the CON or FA groups; however, it was significantly higher than in the GLM and GLL groups (P < 0.05). A positive dose-dependent correlation was found for IFN-γ, and no difference or trend was detected between FA and CON. Regarding IL‑1β, all glycyrrhizic acid‑treated groups showed numerical decreases relative to CON, with the GLH group exhibiting a trend toward lower levels (P = 0.076). Although IL‑1β levels in glycyrrhizic acid‑treated groups were lower than in the FA group, no significant differences were observed, and a negative dose‑dependent correlation was noted. For IL-10, the GLH group showed trends toward higher levels compared with both CON (P = 0.071) and FA (P = 0.052), and significantly higher levels than the GLM and GLL groups (P < 0.05); a positive dose-dependent correlation was observed. No significant differences in IL‑6 levels were found among any groups. for all cytokines measured, no significant differences or trends were detected between the FA and CON groups.

Antioxidant capability

We also measured serum antioxidant parameters (Fig. 2c). Glycyrrhizic acid treatment reduced serum LDH levels. Compared with the CON group, LDH was significantly lower in the GLH group (P < 0.05); the GLH group showed a trend toward lower LDH versus the GLM group (P = 0.057) and a significant reduction versus the GLL group (P < 0.05); although LDH levels were lower in the treated groups than in the FA group, no significant differences were detected. MDA levels were measured, and no significant differences were observed among the five groups. T-SOD levels were increased by glycyrrhizic acid treatment, and the GLH group exhibited a trend toward higher T-SOD compared with the CON group, a significant increase compared with the FA group (P < 0.05), a trend toward increase compared with the GLM group (P = 0.10), and a significant increase compared with the GLL group (P < 0.05). For GSH‑PX, no significant differences were found between any treated group and the CON group; however, compared with the FA group, the GLH and GLM groups showed trends toward higher levels (P = 0.082 and P = 0.060, respectively). CAT levels were elevated by glycyrrhizic acid treatment, with the highest level in the GLH group. The GLH group showed a trend toward higher CAT compared with the CON group (P = 0.073). Although CAT level was higher than in the FA group, the difference was not significant. Compared with the GLM and GLL groups, the GLH group exhibited trends toward higher CAT level (P = 0.051, P = 0.051).

Taxonomic composition of microbiome in upper respiratory tract

The compositional changes of the microbiome in the upper respiratory tract of piglets in each group were evaluated through metagenomic sequencing. A total of 565,539,118 high-quality sequences were obtained from all nose swab samples of piglets. These sequences were used to identify 6,663 species, which belonged to 85 phyla and 1,585 genera. The respiratory microbiome was predominantly composed of bacteria (97.34%), with minor contributions from viruses (2.12%), archaea (0.53%), and eukaryotes (0.02%)(Fig. 3a). Among bacterial phyla, Pseudomonadota (45.97%), Bacteroidota (29.16%), and Bacillota (19.76%) were the dominant (Fig. 3b). At the genus level, Moraxella, Streptococcus, and Glaesserella were the most prevalent across all samples (Fig. 3c). The Kruskal‑Wallis test was used to analyze the relative abundance of the top 50 genera. It was found that g_ Alloprevotella and g_Pasteurella were significantly higher in GLH group than other groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3d).

Pathogenic bacteria analysis in upper respiratory tract

The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to analyze the top 20 species in terms of relative abundance at the species level. It was found that Moraxella pluranimalium and uncultured Moraxella sp. were significantly lower in GLH group than other groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5a). Among the identified microbiome bacteria in upper respiratory tract of piglets from each group, a total of 27 representative main pathogenic bacteria for swine were recorded (Additional material: Table S3). Among the 27 selected recorded pathogens, 12 were identified as potential causes of respiratory symptoms and damage to the respiratory tract for swine. Random forest analysis was applied to assess differential abundance of selected pathogenic bacteria across the groups of piglets (Fig. 5b). Among these, the abundances of 11 pathogenic species associated with respiratory diseases were reduced in the GLH group compared with both the CON and FA groups: Glaesserella parasuis, Mesomycoplasma hyorhinis, Mesomycoplasma hyopneumoniae, Trueperella pyogenes, Streptococcus suis, Mannheimia varigena, Actinobacillus porcinus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae, and Actinobacillus suis.

Discussion

Licorice is known to possess well-documented pharmacological properties, such as anti-inflammatory, antitussive, immunomodulatory, and gastroprotective activities [26–29]. Previous studies have confirmed the growth-promoting efficacy of licorice in animal husbandry [14, 30]. In this study, dietary supplementation with glycyrrhizic acid showed numerical benefits for growth performance in weaned piglets, although the differences were not statistically significant. Nevertheless, glycyrrhizic acid demonstrated potential benefits in modulating immune function, enhancing antioxidant capacity, and managing respiratory tract health. Moreover, this study is the first to investigate the modulatory effect of glycyrrhizic acid on the upper respiratory tract microbiota of pigs.

The characteristic flavor of glycyrrhizic acid, which is 150 times sweeter than sucrose, likely enhances the palatability of feed [31]. This might be one of the factors accounting for the numerical increases in average daily feed intake and average daily gain observed in the glycyrrhizic acid-supplemented groups compared with the control group throughout the experimental phases [30]. In pig production, in addition to monitoring feed intake, cough scores, diarrhea index, and skin and hair condition scores are commonly used to monitor the health status of pigs [17–19]. According to traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) theory, the lung governs the skin and hair, and is internally and externally paired with the large intestine [32, 33]. This implies that the health of the respiratory system is directly reflected in the condition of the skin, hair, and the large intestine. Interestingly, the observation that glycyrrhizic acid simultaneously reduced cough scores and diarrhea index while improving skin and hair condition aligns well with the classical “lung–skin–intestine axis” framework in TCM.

The concentrations of serum IgA, IgG, and IgM, which are immunologically active molecules in humoral and cellular immunity, positively correlate with immune status and overall health [34]. Glycyrrhizic acid possesses immunomodulatory properties and has been widely used in the management of autoimmune diseases [35]. In a murine model of allergic rhinitis, glycyrrhizin treatment dose‑dependently reduced serum levels of specific IgE, IL‑4, IL‑5, and IL‑6, while significantly upregulating serum IgA, IgG, IgM, IL‑2, and IL‑12 levels. These results indicate that glycyrrhizin modulates general immunity in allergic rhinitis mice [36]. Furthermore, as an immune adjuvant, glycyrrhizic acid activates innate immune pathways to increase serum IgA, IgG, and IgM levels, and upregulates the expression of co‑stimulatory molecules CD80, CD86, and CD28, which are critically involved in T‑cell and B‑cell activation and proliferation. These effects collectively enhance both innate and adaptive (cellular and humoral) immune responses in pigs [37, 38].

The anti‑inflammatory and antioxidant effects of glycyrrhizic acid can be mediated by multiple signaling pathways, including the NF‑κB, PI3K/Akt, MAPK, JAK/STAT3, and pyroptotic pathways [39]. This regulatory effect is characterized by the simultaneous suppression of pro-inflammatory mediators (COX-2, iNOS, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, HMGB1) and the upregulation of immunoregulatory cytokines (IL-10 and TGF-β), along with the induction of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT [39–41]. Consistent with previous reports, we also observed that glycyrrhizic acid exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects through the regulation of cytokines and antioxidant activities. It is noteworthy that serum IL‑6 levels in the glycyrrhizic acid‑treated groups remained comparable to those in the control group. This cytokine profile modulation suggests glycyrrhizic acid selectively attenuates pathological inflammation without compromising protective immune responses [39, 42].

For the metagenomic sequencing of the microbiome, our findings align with previous studies which identified Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, and Actinomycetota as dominant phyla in porcine upper airways, with Glaesserella, Streptococcus, Mycoplasma, and Bergeyella comprising principal genera [43]. While the taxonomic hierarchy corresponds with previous observations, variations in relative abundances and secondary genera likely reflect methodological differences in antibiotic administration, dietary composition, and environmental exposure [44]. In pigs, the diversity and composition of respiratory microbiota may play a pivotal role in regulating host immune homeostasis and mitigating porcine respiratory disease complex [45]. Consequently, investigating dynamic changes in upper respiratory microbiota could serve as an effective strategy for identifying respiratory disorders [46].

A reduced overall microbial alpha‑diversity is commonly considered an indicator of dysbiosis. The Shannon index, Simpson index, and invsimpson index are important metrics that reflect species richness and evenness, respectively [47]. In the present study, normal alpha‑diversity was observed following glycyrrhizic acid treatment, suggesting that dietary glycyrrhizic acid did not disrupt the overall microbial community structure or induce a state of dysbiosis. Notably, this pattern aligns with a valaciclovir intervention study in COPD patients, which reported no change in α‑diversity but decreased total 16 S bacterial load and H. influenzae in sputum [48]. This parallel suggests that selective suppression of pathogens without compromising overall microbial diversity may be an advantageous feature of certain therapeutic interventions.

Previous studies have reported an association between oral microbiome dysbiosis and chronic respiratory diseases, showing that the abundance of Alloprevotella is significantly decreased in patients with chronic respiratory diseases [49]. Another study on the nasopharyngeal microbiota in South African infants with lower respiratory tract infection revealed that the abundance of Alloprevotella was higher in the control group than in the respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) cases [50]. Furthermore, Alloprevotella has been demonstrated to play an important role in maintaining lung health [51]. In the present study, we observed that high‑dose glycyrrhizic acid treatment significantly increased the abundance of Alloprevotella. This finding provides important evidence for the modulation of the upper respiratory tract microbiota in pigs using glycyrrhizic acid.

Moraxella pluranimalium is a commensal bacterium commonly found in the porcine nasal cavity, but it may exhibit opportunistic pathogenicity and has been shown to stimulate nasal mucosa and induce pro‑inflammatory cytokine production [52]. Previous studies have demonstrated that licorice and its bioactive compounds, including glycyrrhizic acid (GA), possess antimicrobial activity against pathogens [53]. GA effectively inhibits the proliferation of Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG) and significantly alleviates MG-induced tracheal and lung damage [54]. Its antibacterial activity is attributed to alterations in the local chemical environment of the bacterial cell membrane [55] and inhibition of bacterial arginine synthesis [56]. Consistent with these findings, our metagenomic analysis revealed that the abundances of Moraxella pluranimalium, an uncultured Moraxella sp., and 11 other respiratory pathogens were significantly reduced in the high‑dose GA (GLH) group. Together with the observed enhancement of immune, anti‑inflammatory, and antioxidant capacities, GA treatment may exert synergistic effects with direct pathogen suppression to reduce respiratory inflammation and oxidative stress, thus improving overall respiratory health [57].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The number of replicate pens per treatment was only three, which limited statistical power to detect small to moderate effects. Therefore, most observed improvements were numerical and not statistically significant, and the study should be considered exploratory. Nevertheless, although the growth‑promoting effects of glycyrrhizic acid did not reach statistical significance in this study, the dose‑dependent immunomodulation, reduction of pathogenic bacteria in the upper respiratory tract, and trends toward improved clinical symptoms suggest that glycyrrhizic acid holds promise as a potential alternative to antibiotics in weaned piglet production. Further optimization of dosage and larger‑scale trials are warranted to confirm its efficacy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that dietary supplementation with glycyrrhizic acid modulates the upper respiratory tract microbiota, reduces the abundance of pathogenic bacteria, and improves certain immune and antioxidant parameters in weaned piglets. Although growth performance improvements were numerically observed, they did not reach statistical significance. Collectively, these findings support the potential of glycyrrhizic acid as a promising natural alternative to conventional antibiotics in commercial swine production systems, although further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm its efficacy.
