Combined epicardial substrate ablation and pulsed field pulmonary vein isolation in Brugada syndrome
Authors: Luke Byrne, James Mannion, Waleed Sanaullah, Joseph Galvin
Published: 2026

Introduction

Brugada syndrome (BrS) is an established cause of sudden cardiac death (SCD) owing to ventricular fibrillation (VF) in individuals with structurally normal hearts. It is estimated to account for up to 20% of SCD in this population.1

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) are effective in the prevention of SCD in BrS.2 However, ICDs do not prevent VF recurrence, with up to 25% of patients receiving an appropriate ICD shock, which can significantly impair quality of life.3 More recently, epicardial substrate-based ablation has been shown to reduce the burden of VF in patients with BrS.4

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is common in BrS and is associated with a worse prognosis.5 Management of AF in this population is more challenging, given that atrioventricular nodal–blocking agents and many antiarrhythmic drugs are contraindicated.

Despite the coexistence of VF and AF in patients with BrS with more severe phenotypes and the competing risks of appropriate and inappropriate shocks, strategies targeting both arrhythmias remain limited. In this report, we describe a combined ablative strategy targeting epicardial substrate and the pulmonary veins (PVs) in a patient with recurrent VF requiring ICD therapy and symptomatic AF with rapid ventricular rates.

Case report

A 31-year-old man with BrS and a remote history of cardiac arrest was referred to our center after 4 appropriate ICD shocks for VF. He had a spontaneous type 1 Brugada pattern on electrocardiogram (ECG) and paroxysmal AF (pAF) with recurrent symptomatic episodes at rapid rates. He did not carry a pathogenic mutation in the SCN5A gene. He underwent early PV isolation (PVI) in another center 4 years before his current presentation. He had an AF recurrence documented on his ICD and was waitlisted for a redo PVI in our center owing to the limited pharmacologic options available for patients with BrS with AF. He was taking bisoprolol 2.5 mg to minimize the risk of inappropriate shocks secondary to pAF with rapid rates and had a single-chamber ICD in situ. He was placed on quinine 300 mg twice daily after an initial ICD shock, given the limited access to quinidine, with a view to minimizing the risk of further VF. However, shortly after initiating quinine, he developed a VF storm. The case was discussed at our electrophysiology multidisciplinary meeting, and a consensus was reached to proceed with epicardial substrate ablation to minimize the risk of further appropriate shocks for VF and redo PVI to manage symptoms and prevent inappropriate shocks for rapidly conducting AF.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with standard mechanical ventilation. Epicardial access was obtained using a micropuncture needle in a steep left anterior oblique projection, and a 45-cm steerable sheath (Agilis™, Abbott, Abbott Park, IL) was advanced into the pericardial space. The sheath was left on free drainage; however, manual aspiration was performed intermittently throughout the procedure as guided by clinical evidence of tamponade. The indwelling catheter was removed to facilitate this. No frank pericardial bleeding was observed at any stage throughout the procedure.

Mapping was performed in sinus rhythm without sodium channel blockade, given the presence of a spontaneous type 1 Brugada pattern at rest. High-density voltage and late annotation mapping of the epicardium were performed using a CARTO 3™ mapping system and an Octaray catheter (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA). A preprocedural computed tomography coronary angiogram was integrated with the electroanatomic map to delineate coronary anatomy.

The anticipated procedural endpoints for epicardial ablation were elimination of both the fractionated electrograms in the epicardial right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) and the baseline Brugada pattern on the patient’s resting ECG. The procedural endpoint for PVI was the demonstration of entry and exit block in each of the PVs.

Extensive areas of low-voltage and fractionation were identified in the epicardial RVOT and basal right ventricle, extending into the left ventricle. During ablation, a notable attenuation of the coved ST-segment elevation was observed, accompanied by an increase in heart rate. Interestingly, aspiration of pericardial fluid, which had been on continuous free drainage during ablation but drained slowly with the ablation catheter in the sheath, resulted in normalization of heart rate and reappearance of the type 1 Brugada ECG pattern (Figure 2). After completion of the epicardial lesion set, femoral venous access was obtained using ultrasound guidance. Intravenous heparin was administered at a dose of 100 U/kg after vascular access, and transseptal puncture was performed using an SL1 sheath and BRK1 needle. A Faradrive sheath (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA) was then advanced into the left atrium, and an activated clotting time of >300 seconds was targeted. Heparinized saline was infused through the transseptal sheath, and intravenous heparin boluses were administered. A 5F quadripolar catheter was placed in the right ventricular apex to facilitate back pacing. Left atrial mapping demonstrated reconnection of the left-sided PVs. Pulsed field ablation (PFA) was performed using the Farapulse system (Boston Scientific), and isolation of all PVs was demonstrated at the end of the case through demonstration of both entry and exit block by pacing at an output of 20 mA at a pulse width of 2 ms within each PV from the PFA catheter. No significant vagal effects, fluctuations in heart rate, or change in the Brugada ECG pattern was noted during PFA administration. No procedural complications occurred, and the pericardial drain, having been left on free drainage without evidence of pericardial bleeding, was removed uneventfully 4 hours later.

Given the persistence of the Brugada ECG pattern at the end of the procedure, the significant burden of ICD shocks, and symptomatic AF, quinine sulfate was continued and bisoprolol was discontinued to minimize arrhythmic risk.

1 short self-terminating episode of pAF was documented the next night. At 3-month follow-up, the patient remained free from further ventricular or atrial arrhythmias and ICD therapies and was tolerating quinine without any significant side effects.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the feasibility of a combined ablative strategy targeting both ventricular and atrial arrhythmic substrates in a patient with BrS and recurrent ICD therapies. In the BRAVE trial, 83% of patients after a single procedure and 90% with a repeat procedure were free from ICD therapy for VF after substrate-based ablation. VF recurrence was more likely in patients with a concurrent early repolarization pattern or in those with a persistent Brugada ECG pattern at the end of the case. An irrigated-tip, contact force–sensing catheter was also used in this study.4 Furthermore, the BRAVO registry reported good long-term outcomes with a low procedural complication rate of 2.5%.6 Taken together, these data support catheter ablation as an effective treatment strategy in BrS, particularly in patients with recurrent ICD therapies and when pharmacologic options are limited. Despite the persistence of the Brugada ECG pattern at the end of the procedure, the patient remains free from ICD therapies at 3-month follow-up.

The management of AF in BrS can also be challenging, with many medications used in rate and rhythm control contraindicated for patients with BrS. Quinidine may be used to prevent AF recurrence; however, data on its efficacy are limited.5 In addition, quinidine is not available in many countries, limiting its use.7 Quinine, a stereoisomer of quinidine, has been suggested in case reports to reduce VF episodes in BrS and was continued in this patient after multidisciplinary discussion to minimize arrhythmic risk, although robust data supporting its use, particularly in AF, are lacking.8,9 This was due to the presense of extensive arrhythmic substrate and the persistence of the Brugada ECG pattern after the procedure.

PVI is now established as a first-line rhythm control strategy in patients with symptomatic AF and has proven efficacy over antiarrhythmic drugs.10 However, it is less effective in patients with BrS vs those without BrS, with 61.7% of patients with BrS free from atrial tachyarrhythmias on follow-up vs 78.3% in the non-BrS group. Inappropriate ICD shocks owing to AF reduced significantly before and after ablation from 27.6% to 3.4%.11 PFA has emerged as an attractive ablation modality in recent years, offering a safe and effective alternative to radiofrequency and cryoablation. PFA causes irreversible electroporation, targeting atrial myocardiocytes, and has been shown to be noninferior to thermal ablation modalities in terms of AF freedom. Shorter procedure times and a lower risk of damage to surrounding structures are benefits seen with the use of PFA.12,13 However, there are limited data on its utilization in BrS. PVI was achieved rapidly in this case without the need for additional heparin boluses, and no procedural complications were encountered. Vagal episodes during PFA-mediated PVI are well described, particularly when ablating the left-sided PVs.14 Back up pacing is important to mitigate the proarrhythmic impact of vagal hyperactivity and bradycardia in BrS. In this case, no significant variation in heart rate or alteration to the Brugada ECG pattern was seen in response to PFA.

A key observation in this case was the dynamic modulation of the Brugada ECG pattern during the procedure. Attenuation of the ST-segment elevation was observed during periods of increased heart rate, with reemergence of the Brugada pattern after a reduction in heart rate. This is consistent with previous pacing studies demonstrating that Brugada ECG expression is rate dependent. Bradycardia and vagal activity have been shown to worsen ST-segment elevation seen in BrS and may represent a potential trigger for VF. This provides mechanistic insight into the increased risk of SCD during periods of high vagal tone, such as sleep, and the exacerbation of the Brugada phenotype with parasympathomimetic agents.15,16 Increased vagal tone can decrease inward calcium flow, and increase outward potassium flow mediated by the transient outward current channel, with a resultant increase in the transmural voltage gradient in the epicardial RVOT, and the Brugada ECG pattern.17 Bradycardia may potentiate this ionic imbalance by allowing more time for the transient outward current channel to recover. In addition, the patient was taking low-dose bisoprolol to minimize the risk of inappropriate shocks secondary to AF. This was stopped because of the risk of VF associated with beta blockade. Conversely, increasing the heart rate through overdrive pacing or with an isoproterenol infusion attenuates the coved ST-segment elevation pattern and can reduce arrhythmic events by augmenting inward calcium currents.18,19 In this case, the gradual buildup of irrigation fluid within the pericardium resulted in cardiac tamponade physiology. This occurred despite the steerable sheath being on continuous free drainage. It is important to recognize this as a potential pitfall, providing false reassurance of procedural success. Removing the ablation catheter and aspiration of pericardial fluid from the sheath may help circumvent this issue.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the feasibility of a combined epicardial substrate modification and PVI strategy in BrS. The dynamic rate-dependent nature of the Brugada ECG during epicardial ablation and the importance of pericardial fluid management to ensure accurate assessment of procedural endpoints are also illustrated. Further research and longer-term follow-up are required to determine the safety and efficacy of a combined ablative approach in BrS.
