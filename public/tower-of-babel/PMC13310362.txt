Debunking Constipation Myths: What Community Pharmacists Need to Know
Authors: C Díaz López
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConstipation is a highly prevalent gastrointestinal disorder that significantly impacts quality of life and is associated with a substantial healthcare burden. Despite advances in its clinical understanding, its management remains hindered by the persistence of myths and unfounded beliefs that compromise appropriate treatment and obstruct effective healthcare intervention. This study aims to identify, analyze, and clarify some of the most frequent misconceptions surrounding constipation, providing community pharmacists with an evidence-based tool to enhance their clinical and educational practice.A narrative review of the scientific literature published between 2015 and 2025 was conducted, drawing from biomedical databases (PubMed, ScienceDirect, Cochrane) and institutional sources. Selected materials included articles, clinical guidelines, and consensus documents focused on myths related to the pathophysiology, treatment, and prevention of constipation.Among the most prevalent misconceptions are: the necessity of daily bowel movements, the effectiveness of increased fluid intake or olive oil on an empty stomach, the alleged harmfulness or addictive potential of laxatives, the belief that constipation affects only women, and its supposed direct link to colorectal cancer. Misunderstandings regarding the role of dietary fiber, probiotics, and specific foods were also explored.The findings reveal a significant discrepancy between popular beliefs and current scientific knowledge. It is concluded that community pharmacists, as first-line healthcare providers, must play an active role in dispelling misconceptions about constipation through rigorous, evidence-based, and personalized patient education.

INTRODUCTION

Constipation (from the Latin “stringĕre”, meaning “to compress” or “to tighten”) is a gastrointestinal disorder or symptom that affects approximately 15% of the global population and generates substantial healthcare expenditure [1,2]. Given its high prevalence, constipation imposes a significant burden on quality of life, comparable to that associated with other common chronic diseases [3].

Constipation may be defined as an alteration in bowel habits [4] which may involve changes in bowel movement frequency, stool consistency and size, straining during defecation, and post-evacuation satisfaction. The perception of what constitutes a normal bowel habit varies considerably among individuals, making it difficult to establish a universally accepted definition of constipation [5].

The main risk factors identified in the scientific literature include female sex (particularly during pregnancy, menopause, or certain phases of the menstrual cycle, due to hormonal variations affecting intestinal motility), advanced age (being more common in individuals over 65 years of age), unhealthy lifestyle habits (sedentary behaviour and poor dietary patterns), obesity and associated comorbidities (hypertension, dyslipidaemia, depression, polypharmacy), the use of medications that may cause constipation (opioids, tricyclic antidepressants, antihypertensives, or anticholinergics), and chronic suppression of the defecation reflex [6-10].

Experts have highlighted the difficulty of defining and classifying constipation, emphasizing the need for further discussion, debate, and refinement due to the considerable interindividual variability in stool frequency, consistency, and ease of defecation.

In clinical practice, it is essential to identify alarm signs that warrant immediate medical referral. These include sudden onset of constipation, the presence of blood or mucus in the stool, severe abdominal pain, unexplained weight loss, fever, a family history of colorectal cancer, prolonged symptoms that do not respond to treatment, or the occurrence of constipation in children under six years of age without a known cause [5].

Despite the high prevalence of constipation and its impact on quality of life, its management in the community setting continues to be influenced by the persistence of misconceptions that lack scientific support. These beliefs, widely disseminated in popular culture, are often transmitted through informal and highly accessible channels such as family environments, social media, or non-specialized sources of information, contributing to inappropriate or potentially harmful health practices.

Within this complex clinical context, community pharmacists are often the first point of contact between patients and the healthcare system. Consequently, they play a key role in the identification and management of constipation, as well as in providing health education and evidence-based lifestyle and dietary recommendations tailored to the needs of each patient.

With the aim of contributing to evidence-based pharmaceutical care of practical clinical value, a series of statements representing some of the most widespread myths surrounding constipation were selected. To this end, a review of the available scientific literature was conducted, including scientific articles, clinical guidelines, management protocols, and consensus documents.

To identify all relevant and up-to-date information on the subject, electronic search was performed using databases and documentary sources specific to the health sciences:

Databases:

PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Cochrane were searched. The objective was to identify scientific publications from the last ten years (2015–2025) that were freely available in full text, published in English or Spanish, and addressed myths or misconceptions related to constipation and/or its therapeutic management. The search strategy included the keywords “constipation,” “myths,” “digestive disorders,” “prevention,” “treatment,” and “pharmaceutical care.” Separate searches were conducted in each database using these keywords combined with the Boolean operators AND and OR, yielding a substantial number of records for screening. In most cases, reviewing the abstracts was sufficient to determine the relevance of an article to the topic under study, although full-text review was required for some publications.

Public Institutional resources:

World Health Organization (WHO), Spanish Agency of Medicines and Medical Devices (AEMPS), Spanish Ministry of Health, and the Royal Spanish Academy (RAE).

This methodological approach aims to provide pharmacists with a useful tool to identify misconceptions and refute them using evidence-based arguments, thereby promoting informed, safe, and patient-centred healthcare.

MYTH 1. Increased Fluid Intake Improves Constipation

It is true that an association may exist between low water intake and constipation. However, current evidence suggests that increasing fluid intake alone, in individuals who are already adequately hydrated, does not have a positive effect on constipation [8]. Certain factors, such as age, level of physical activity, diet, and hydration status, may influence the effectiveness of adequate fluid intake in constipated individuals [3]. At present, there is insufficient evidence to support increasing fluid intake or physical exercise as stand-alone treatments for constipation [16]. This is because, under physiological conditions, the small intestine processes between 7 and 10 litres of fluid per day, making additional fluid intake of limited relevance. Therefore, consuming more water than the amount recommended by official health organizations for maintaining optimal health does not appear to have clinically significant effects on constipation management [17,18].

MYTH 2. Taking a Tablespoon of Olive Oil on an Empty Stomach Relieves Constipation

Although this popular belief is widespread in Spain, it is much less common in other countries, where people do not routinely consume oil in this manner. The existence of lubricant laxatives formulated with non-edible oils, such as liquid paraffin, which soften stools and facilitate their passage, may explain the origin of this misconception. If lubricant laxatives are effective, why would olive oil not have the same effect?

The key difference lies in absorption. Non-edible oils such as paraffin reach the colon intact and can therefore exert their lubricating action directly on the stool. In contrast, edible oils are absorbed in the small intestine and do not come into contact with the stool. The only circumstance in which edible oils could soften stools would be when consumed in quantities exceeding the absorptive capacity of the intestine. However, the intestine is highly efficient at absorbing fats, meaning that such an effect would require a substantial caloric intake. Olive oil may improve stool consistency only if consumed in amounts greater than those that can be absorbed, but determining this threshold would essentially be a process of trial and error [11]

MYTH 3. It Is Necessary to Have a Bowel Movement Every Day

One of the most widespread beliefs is that daily bowel movements are necessary to maintain normal intestinal function and that failing to do so indicates constipation. However, this assertion is not supported by scientific evidence. One of the main challenges in the clinical management of constipation is its definition, since there is no universally normal bowel movement frequency. Considerable interindividual and intraindividual variability exists.

Several international guidelines and consensus documents agree that a bowel movement frequency ranging from three times per week to three times per day can be considered physiologically normal, provided that additional criteria are met: stools should have an appropriate consistency (types 3–5 on the Bristol Stool Form Scale),[8] excessive straining should not be required, and there should be no sensation of incomplete evacuation or rectal blockage.

As previously mentioned, bowel movement frequency is highly variable among individuals and is influenced by factors such as diet, physical activity, age, ethnicity, emotional status, and individual gastrointestinal physiology. Therefore, the absence of a daily bowel movement should not, by itself, be considered a diagnostic criterion for constipation [8,18].

MYTH 4. Constipation Is Only a Symptom, Not a Disease

There is a common belief that constipation is not a disease in its own right, but merely a symptom. Although this statement is partially true, it requires clarification from both a clinical and pathophysiological perspective.

From a semantic and clinical standpoint, the Royal Spanish Academy defines a symptom as a phenomenon indicative of a disease [19], whereas a disease is defined as an alteration of health affecting a living being or part thereof [20]. In this context, constipation may be considered either a symptom or a disease. It is a symptom when it results from an identifiable cause, such as the use of opioids or the presence of neurological, endocrine, or structural disorders. Conversely, it may be considered a disease when it constitutes an independent functional disorder with no apparent organic cause [9].

The latter situation corresponds to functional constipation, which is characterized by impaired bowel function that cannot be explained by structural, metabolic, or drug-related abnormalities. Mechanisms involved include alterations in colonic motility, rectal sensitivity, and the composition and function of the intestinal microbiota, among others [12,14].

The International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10) recognizes constipation as an independent disorder under code K59.0 (excluding incomplete defecation and faecal impaction) within the chapter on diseases of the digestive system (K00–K93). Likewise, ICD-11, the updated version of the classification, includes constipation under code ME81 within Chapter 17, “Diseases of the Digestive System.” These classifications confirm its official recognition as a disease, at least when no secondary cause is identified [21,22].

From a clinical perspective, constipation should be regarded as a symptom when it forms part of a broader clinical condition and can be attributed to a known cause. However, when it is chronic, isolated, and fulfils specific diagnostic criteria such as the Rome IV criteria, it should be managed as a functional bowel disorder [14].

MYTH 5. Laxatives and Constipation

Misconceptions surrounding laxative use may cause unnecessary concern among patients and lead to inappropriate use. Community pharmacies are key healthcare settings in which pharmacists can identify and dispel misinformation and erroneous beliefs.

5.1 Stimulant Laxatives Damage the Intestine

Stimulant laxatives may produce structural intestinal alterations only when used at supratherapeutic doses, that is, when misused. Moreover, published studies indicate that any potentially harmful effects are reversible and lack clinical relevance. No long-term study has demonstrated morphological changes in intestinal smooth muscle or in the human enteric nervous system when bisacodyl or sodium picosulfate is used to treat constipation. Furthermore, studies suggesting harmful effects are often biased because they fail to account for confounding factors such as age, metabolic disorders, or neurological diseases. [8]

5.2 Laxatives Cause Electrolyte Imbalances

Numerous studies evaluating newer laxatives, such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), have reported unchanged serum electrolyte concentrations throughout clinical trials. Nevertheless, patients who continuously use high doses of laxatives may experience potassium and water loss through the stool. However, electrolyte disturbances have not been demonstrated in patients using laxatives at recommended doses. Particular caution should be exercised when magnesium-based laxatives are used in older adults, as reduced renal function may predispose them to hypermagnesaemia. [9]

5.3 Laxatives Cause Addiction

Most laxatives are not systemically absorbed, and none cross the blood–brain barrier; therefore, there is no pharmacological basis for addiction. Individuals who misuse laxatives often have underlying psychiatric disorders and may mistakenly believe that laxative-induced diarrhoea promotes weight loss [1,6].

5.4 Laxatives Cause Tolerance

There is a common belief that prolonged laxative use may reduce their effectiveness because of tolerance. However, there is no solid evidence supporting this claim in healthy humans.

Available studies indicate that most patients do not develop tolerance to the effects of laxatives when they are used at therapeutic doses and under professional supervision. Some experimental studies conducted in animal models and in patients with slow-transit chronic constipation have suggested the possibility of intestinal adaptive mechanisms. Nevertheless, these findings cannot be extrapolated to the general population. To date, no structural changes or clinically significant loss of efficacy have been observed in long-term studies of laxatives such as bisacodyl or sodium picosulfate.

Therefore, there is no scientific justification for discontinuing an effective laxative because of concerns about tolerance, provided it is used appropriately and under medical supervision [6].

MYTH 6. Constipation Can Always Be Prevented

This statement is inaccurate because the possibility of preventing constipation largely depends on its underlying cause. When constipation is secondary to inappropriate lifestyle habits—such as low-fibre diets, physical inactivity, insufficient fluid intake, or chronic suppression of the defecation reflex—its incidence may be reduced through lifestyle and dietary interventions. Likewise, occasional episodes of constipation associated with changes in routine, travel, or short-term pharmacological treatments may be mitigated through preventive measures tailored to individual patient characteristics.

However, when constipation is associated with underlying organic or functional causes, such as neurological disorders, endocrine diseases, or the chronic use of medications known to cause constipation (e.g., opioids, anticholinergics, or iron supplements), it may not be entirely preventable. In these circumstances, the therapeutic objective focuses on symptom relief and the adoption of healthy lifestyle measures rather than complete eradication of the condition.

Furthermore, there is a subtype known as primary (idiopathic) chronic constipation, whose pathophysiology involves abnormalities in colonic motility, rectal sensitivity, or pelvic floor coordination, and whose precise cause remains incompletely understood. This form of constipation may occur even in individuals with healthy lifestyles, reinforcing the concept that not all cases are preventable or solely attributable to lifestyle factors [6,9].

MYTH 7. Constipation Is a “Women’s Problem”

Although women are indeed more likely to experience constipation, this condition affects both sexes and all age groups. In certain physiological situations, such as pregnancy, the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, or menopause, hormonal fluctuations increase the likelihood of constipation. Moreover, women are generally more likely to report this symptom during consultations in primary care settings or community pharmacies [1,6].

MYTH 8. Foods and Constipation

Fruits

Fruits are foods characterized by their content of fibre, minerals, vitamins, and other compounds such as polyphenols, all of which contribute to maintaining optimal health and a healthy gut microbiota. Available scientific evidence indicates that the consumption of any type of fruit may have beneficial effects on bowel function due to its content of different types of fibre, sugars (fructose and sucrose), and sorbitol. Furthermore, sorbitol is a slowly and incompletely absorbed polyol. Consequently, the unabsorbed fraction remains within the intestinal lumen, increasing the osmotic load and exerting an osmotic laxative effect [6,10,23].

• Apple

Popular belief often classifies apples as an fruit with astringent properties. However, this concept requires some nuance, as the effect depends on how the fruit is consumed. Scientific evidence has shown that the consumption of raw apples with the skin intact may increase stool weight and frequency and improve intestinal transit. These effects are attributed to their dietary fibre content, including both soluble fibre (pectins) and insoluble fibre (cellulose and hemicellulose), which are present in higher concentrations in the peel. Therefore, apples may be a useful food within a balanced diet aimed at the dietary management of functional constipation [24].

• Banana

As with apples, bananas are often believed to worsen constipation. It should be noted that, like any other fruit, bananas contain fibre, although the amount varies considerably according to the degree of ripeness. There is no evidence linking banana consumption to poorer bowel habits. On the contrary, fruit consumption is associated with better overall health and a greater intake of fibre and micronutrients. Nevertheless, choosing less ripe bananas may provide a higher fibre intake [11].

• Prunes and kiwifruit

These fruits are widely recognized for their beneficial effects on constipation. In this case, the belief is supported by scientific evidence. Studies have shown that the daily consumption of two green kiwifruits is associated with improved bowel function and greater gastrointestinal well-being in individuals with constipation. This effect is attributed to the fibre present in the kiwifruit cell walls, which has a high capacity for swelling and water retention, thereby softening stools and increasing stool frequency [25]. Furthermore, as kiwifruit contains high levels of vitamin C (even higher than oranges), it may contribute to improved microbial diversity and increased production of faecal short-chain fatty acids, including butyrate and propionate [23]. Similar benefits have been observed with prunes, which are rich in both fibre and sorbitol, the latter acting as an osmotic laxative [26].

Dairy Products

There is a common belief that cow’s milk protein allergy plays an important role in the development and persistence of functional constipation in children. Similarly, it is often thought that the transition from breastfeeding to formula feeding may cause constipation. However, there are no well-designed, conclusive studies supporting a clear causal relationship between functional constipation in children and Cow’s milk protein allergy. Consequently, scientific societies do not recommend the routine exclusion of cow’s milk from the diet as a treatment for paediatric constipation in the absence of a confirmed diagnosis of allergy or intolerance.

Nevertheless, lactose intolerance may be associated with either diarrhoea or constipation, the latter potentially related to methane production by intestinal bacteria. In otherwise healthy individuals, there is no recommendation to avoid dairy products when experiencing constipation, as no association has been demonstrated between dairy consumption and constipation [18,27].

MYTH 9. Constipation Increases the Risk of Colorectal Cancer

This belief is deeply rooted in the general population and has even been echoed by some healthcare professionals. However, review studies published to date have concluded that individuals with constipation do not have a higher prevalence of colorectal cancer.

There is, nevertheless, a connection between constipation and colorectal cancer: dietary fibre [11]. A high intake of fibre from food sources (excluding fibre supplementation) has been associated with a reduced risk of developing colorectal cancer. Therefore, the apparent relationship between constipation and colorectal cancer may be explained by inadequate fibre intake rather than by constipation itself. Nevertheless, in patients with chronic constipation, any change in bowel habits accompanied by alarm signs or symptoms should prompt medical consultation [8,16].

MYTH 10. Probiotics and Constipation

Although probiotics are widely recommended by the general public for various health conditions, evidence regarding their effectiveness remains inconsistent. Some strains appear to provide beneficial effects, whereas others show little or no benefit [28].

Several studies suggest that the intestinal microbiota may play a role in the pathogenesis of constipation and, conversely, that constipation itself may alter the composition of the gut microbiota. Consequently, intestinal dysbiosis may contribute to constipation in some patients with chronic constipation [1]. When dysbiosis occurs, the microbiota is generally characterized by reduced levels of lactobacilli and bifidobacteria, microorganisms known to promote intestinal motility and thereby support bowel function [28]. The intestine contains a large number of chemoreceptors capable of detecting bacterial metabolites and cellular components of gut microorganisms. Alterations in the intestinal microbiota may therefore trigger activation of the intestinal immune system [29].

In light of these findings, certain synbiotic formulations may be beneficial in chronic constipation. Synbiotics combine probiotics with prebiotic fibres such as inulin or fructooligosaccharides, which serve as substrates for probiotic growth. In addition, they may increase the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which help reduce inflammation and modulate intestinal peristalsis [30].

However, published studies have yielded heterogeneous results [3], and there is currently no robust scientific evidence demonstrating that the probiotic products commonly dispensed in community pharmacies, or those naturally present in foods, are effective for the treatment of constipation [1,16,31], with the exception of specific bacterial strains and indications listed in the World Gastroenterology Organisation (WGO) Global Guidelines on Probiotics and Prebiotics [32].

MYTH 11. Fibre and Constipation

Dietary fibre, whether soluble or insoluble, is generally beneficial for most individuals; however, fibre deficiency does not appear to be a major cause of chronic constipation. If the population followed the recommendations established by health authorities and consumed at least 30 grams of fibre per day, constipation would theoretically not occur [11].

It is risky to assume that all dietary fibres or isolated fibre supplements exert a laxative effect in patients with chronic constipation. This misconception may stem from studies that have linked low dietary fibre intake with the development of constipation [33]. While some patients with constipation experience improvements in bowel habits following increased fibre intake, others respond poorly to fibre supplementation. This is particularly true in certain patients with constipation-predominant irritable bowel syndrome, slow-transit idiopathic constipation, or primary defecatory disorders.

For this reason, treatment recommendations for chronic constipation should be based on evidence-based guidance regarding fibre use and should take into account the individual patient’s clinical history before implementation. Failing to do so risks perpetuating misconceptions about constipation management and may deprive patients of more effective therapeutic options [11,18].

Importantly, the benefits of dietary fibre extend well beyond constipation management. A high fibre intake (25–30 g/day) has been associated with lower overall mortality and a reduced risk of developing cancer, stroke, and type 2 diabetes mellitus [11].
