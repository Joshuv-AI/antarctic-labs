Contribution of Sport Type to the Association Between Handgrip Strength and Morbidity/Mortality: Future Follow-up Study and Possible Mechanisms
Authors: Takashi Abe, Jeremy P. Loenneke, Koya Suzuki, Yoshimitsu Kohmura, Akemi Abe, Shuichi Machida, Hisashi Naito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite significant research efforts to advance the field, the mechanisms underlying the association between handgrip strength (HGS) and morbidity and/or mortality still need to be fully understood. We focused on the debate in previous studies regarding the mechanism of this association and examined the impact of the main covariates utilized in those studies on HGS and morbidity and/or mortality. Physical activity has been extensively investigated in previous studies as a covariate to explain the association between HGS and morbidity and/or mortality, but no consensus has been reached that physical activity is a mediator explaining this association. Assessing people's complete lifetime physical activity history is a challenging task. To help better understand the association between the two, we proposed a research model that focuses on the type of sport played during the developmental period and explores their impact on morbidity and mortality in adulthood. Furthermore, if sport contributes to HGS improvements during development and influences morbidity and mortality, we also proposed mechanisms that could be considered in future studies. Unfortunately, further follow-up is needed before the success of this research model can be confirmed. Future studies would also be necessary to clarify the factors related to the future health benefits of improving HGS during development.

Background

Evidence from numerous clinical and epidemiological studies has shown that handgrip strength (HGS) is favorably associated with short and long-term mortality [1–7] and morbidities such as heart disease [8–10], diabetes [11, 12], cancer [13, 14], dementia [15, 16], and loss of independence [17] or work limitation [18]. This is not limited to middle-aged and older adults but is also used to predict future health and current physical fitness for children and adolescents in many countries [19–21]. The HGS of each individual may be determined by genetic [22, 23] and nongenetic factors [24, 25]. The nongenetic factors include lifestyle choices such as physical and sporting activities [26, 27], nutritional status [28], and alcohol/smoking [29, 30]. Furthermore, results from cross-sectional studies have shown that HGS differs with age, with values being dramatically higher during development, relative stability in early adulthood, and values being lower in late adulthood [31, 32]. In particular, the developmental period is thought to be when significant individual differences in HGS occur [31]. Therefore, if HGS is a valid health biomarker, it is necessary to find the most effective ways to increase it during the developmental period and study whether increasing it confers health benefits and the mechanisms underlying the association between the two [33].

Approximately 60% of American youth between the ages of 6 and 17 have participated in team sports or have taken sports lessons after school or on weekends [34]. Additionally, approximately 60% of US adults (age 18 and older) report participating in one type of sport, exercise, or recreational activity in the previous 30 days [35]. There is no doubt that regular physical activity and sports, along with reducing sedentary time, are important for maintaining and improving health [36–38]. Interestingly, studies reported that mortality risks or life expectancies differ depending on the type of sport. Regular participation in racquet sports (e.g., tennis and badminton) has been associated with favorable long-term health and mortality compared to selected other sports (e.g., soccer, cycling, and jogging) [37, 38]. These findings are supported by the inverse association between HGS and morbidity and HGS and mortality (hereafter referred to as “morbidity/mortality”) [1–16]. However, those studies discussed socio-psychological factors but did not address the physiological mechanisms underlying the effects of different types of sports on long-term health and mortality. Sports played with equipment, including racquet sports, do not appear to slow the decline in HGS in middle-aged and older adults [39] but may improve HGS in children and adolescents during developmental stages [40]. This ultimately underscores the value of achieving high HGS in early adulthood.

As mentioned above, many studies have reported an association between HGS and disease-specific morbidity and mortality or all-cause mortality. Early studies in 1995 [1, 41] reporting the association of low HGS with higher all-cause mortality did not describe the mechanism(s) behind the results. In 2000, Rantanen and colleagues [42] reported the association between HGS and all-cause mortality within different body mass index (BMI) levels in initially healthy men. The authors proposed four factors (i.e., childhood lifestyle, muscle strength in adulthood, skeletal muscle mass, and physical activity) to explain why HGS is a predictor of all-cause mortality. The four proposed factors are divided into two elements: (1) frailty factors associated with aging from middle age onwards (i.e., reduction of strength, loss of muscle mass as an amino acid pool, and low physical activity) and (2) lifestyle factors during growth and development to achieve high HGS (i.e., physical activity and nutrition state) [42]. A quarter century has passed since then, and many new epidemiological follow-up studies have been published. This narrative review aims to do the following. First, we summarize previous studies regarding the mechanistic discussion behind the inverse association between HGS and morbidity and mortality. After that, we briefly summarize the impacts of covariates utilized in previous studies on HGS and morbidity/mortality. Finally, we present current research on the effects of sport type on HGS in children and adolescents.

Findings

Mechanistic Discussions from the Previous Papers

Research to Clarify Mechanisms (Rantanen and Colleagues)

In 2003, Rantanen and colleagues [4] conducted a prospective cohort study to elucidate the underlying mechanisms behind the association between HGS and morbidity/mortality. To explore the mechanisms explaining the association between low HGS and higher cause-specific and total mortality, the authors investigated several potential confounding factors, such as age, race, body mass/height, smoking, physical activity, markers of inflammation (i.e., C-reactive protein and interleukin-6), nutritional status (i.e., serum albumin and unintentional weight loss), depressed mood, and the presence of chronic diseases. The authors repeatedly showed that HGS was associated with cause-specific and total mortality in older disabled women. However, chronic disease, inflammation, poor nutritional status, physical inactivity, and depression did not explain the association, and the reason for the association between HGS and mortality/morbidity remains unclear. Subsequent studies [2, 3, 5, 6, 8–19, 43–66] have also adjusted the association between HGS and morbidity/mortality using several potential confounders but still revealed an inverse association. The potential confounders are mainly divided into three categories: (1) sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, ethnicity, residence, education, household income); (2) lifestyle factors (e.g., body mass index, alcohol consumption, smoking status, dietary intake, physical activity); and (3) self-reported or doctor-diagnosed medical conditions (e.g., chronic diseases, health-related risk factors, mental health), although each study selects relevant factors from them. Furthermore, those potential confounders are generally collected from middle-aged and older adult participants at the start of the study. As pointed out by Rantanen et al. [42], lifestyle factors (physical activity/sports, nutrition, etc.) during development should be considered mechanisms explaining the inverse association between low HGS and mortality, but studies targeting middle-aged and older adults have yet to include those confounders.

Research to Clarify Mechanisms (Newman and Colleagues)

In 2006, Newman and colleagues [6] conducted a study using a sample from the Health, Aging and Body Composition Study cohort (2292 participants, aged 70–79) to examine the association between handgrip and knee extension strength and mortality. The authors analyzed the association between muscle strength and mortality accounting for potential confounding factors, including sociodemographic (age, ethnicity, education), lifestyle (height, smoking status, physical activity), and self-reported medical conditions (inflammatory markers, number of comorbidities, depression scores). Interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and C-reactive protein (CRP) were analyzed as inflammatory markers. Body composition was also estimated using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (lean mass, fat mass) and computed tomography (midthigh muscle cross-sectional area). That study also found that handgrip and knee extension strength were independently associated with mortality in older adults. Indexes of muscle mass (total and limb lean mass and midthigh cross-sectional area) did not attenuate this association. Furthermore, the association remained even after adjusting for the level of physical activity, the number of comorbidities, and inflammatory markers (IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP). The authors point out that these factors may have been insufficiently evaluated but also speculated the possible involvement of muscular strength-related hormones (testosterone, insulin-like growth factor-1). Physical activity was generally assessed at a single time point (baseline), so follow-up studies need longer-term assessments. Previous research has pointed out [42] that the evaluated physical activity may not accurately capture each individual's level. Furthermore, caloric intake and nutritional balance may also need to be accounted for as confounders to adjust the results.

Physical Activity as a Moderator?

Regardless of whether study results were adjusted for physical activity, several studies [5, 10, 42–44, 47, 48, 50–52, 59–63, 65, 66] point to physical activity, especially resistance exercise, as a factor explaining the inverse association between HGS and morbidity/mortality. Some of these studies speculated that exercise releases myokines through muscle contraction [56, 63, 67] or improves endothelial dysfunction and autonomic imbalance [5, 64]. Celis-Morales and colleagues [24] utilized UK Biobank data from approximately half a million participants to investigate the impact of baseline HGS and physical activity level on all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease events. The authors reported that the higher mortality associated with activity was most significant among participants in the lowest HGS tertile and lowest among those in the highest HGS tertile. A similar pattern was also observed for cardiovascular disease events [24]. Previous studies discussed physical activity as a factor explaining the inverse association between HGS and morbidity/mortality, likely because scientific evidence supports the association between physical activity and several disease risk factors [67–69]. However, previous studies have not discussed the impact of physical activity on HGS in middle-aged and older adult participants. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have been published regarding the effects of various types of exercise training (i.e., aerobic resistance) on HGS in middle-aged and older adults. However, the results have not been consistent [70–73].

Other Factors as Moderators

Although almost all studies assessed participants' alcohol consumption and adjusted the study results, only a few studies [5, 9, 13, 15, 51, 52] analyzed dietary contents. Previous studies have examined nutritional characteristics such as fruit/vegetable [9, 52], red meat [13, 15, 51, 52], and processed food [13, 15, 51, 52] intake as confounders. HGS has previously been suggested to be an indicator of nutritional status [74], and some studies [4, 42, 50, 52, 60, 75] have discussed that body protein metabolism, including diet, influences HGS via muscle mass.

In an earlier study [42], Rantanen and colleagues stated that “maximal HGS may indicate a person's vigor and greater HGS may mark general intrinsic vitality and stamina, leading to a longer lifespan. Further, genetic factors strongly influence HGS, indicating that it may relate to the candidate gene for longevity.” Similar arguments have been made in the discussion of some previous studies [3, 4, 6, 49, 65]. Therefore, HGS may be helpful as an intrinsic health indicator, as its association with morbidity and mortality remains even when adjusted for various confounders. However, there is no evidence on whether HGS, a surrogate for vigor, improved through nongenetic factors (e.g., physical activity, sports, and diet) during development, and whether it affects morbidity and mortality in middle-aged and older adults [27, 33].

Impact of How to Determine HGS for Individuals

In sports where the equipment is commonly utilized with the dominant hand, such as racquet sports, HGS in the dominant hand is ~ 20% higher than in the non-dominant hand [76, 77]. Therefore, individual values will vary depending on how HGS is determined, i.e., whether it is the mean of peak value in both hands or the maximum among all measurements in both hands. Three primary evaluation methods for HGS have been used in the previous epidemiological follow-up studies. The technique involves measuring the HGS of the right and left hands 2 or 3 times and using (1) the average of the peak values of the right and left hands [5, 7, 10–13, 15, 45, 48, 50–53, 58, 60, 66] or (2) the highest value among the right and left hands for analysis [4, 9, 16, 19, 42, 49, 61, 65]. Alternatively, a study chooses the dominant or non-dominant hand, and (3) uses the higher value of the selected hand [1, 2, 6, 43, 46, 54, 56, 63, 64]. Therefore, how HGS is determined for each individual may affect the study results.

Impact of Confounders on HGS and Morbidity/Mortality

Previous studies have considered a number of different variables that may confound the relationship between HGS and morbidity/mortality. Below, we have summarized the main covariates used in previous research.

Age

Impact of Age on Disease Risks

Aging is a significant risk factor affecting physiological homeostasis and is closely associated with various human diseases, including cardiovascular diseases [78], diabetes [79], and neurodegeneration [80]. An appreciation of the molecular changes that underlie aging are essential for understanding aging-related diseases. A recent follow-up study has attempted to elucidate whether the molecular changes during aging are linear or nonlinear [81]. The authors of that study revealed a consistent nonlinear pattern in molecular markers of aging, reporting that significant dysregulation occurs at two critical time points, around a chronological age of 40 years (i.e., cardiovascular disease, lipid/alcohol metabolism changes) and at an age of 60 (i.e., shifted immune regulation and carbohydrate metabolism) years. Although these results were obtained from a follow-up study, it is important to note that the average observation period for participants was just under two years, so the results are potentially different from those obtained from individuals over a longer period of time. Further investigation is needed to determine whether these results are consistent across all individuals.

Impact of Age on HGS

Several cross-sectional studies have examined age-related comparisons of HGS across countries, regions, and ethnicities and have reported normative values for each age group separated out by sex [31, 82–85]. However, because each study draws a subset of participants from the population as the study sample, each age group may have sample bias. For example, some cross-sectional research shows no difference in HGS between people in their 20 s and 30 s [82, 83], while others show that HGS is higher in their 30 s than in their 20 s [84]. Furthermore, the age at which lower HGS occurs compared to young and middle-aged groups varies between studies. The sample bias may cause differences in the results of the cross-sectional studies. It may also be an artifact of taking a continuous variable (age) and treating it as categorical. When categories are made, all those within a given age range are assumed to be equal on that variable. In contrast, although longitudinal studies are limited, one study reported that HGS measured in younger adults (18 years old) decreased in middle age (35 years old) [86]. Another longitudinal study [39] compared HGS measured during university (about age 20) with HGS at their current age. Graduates' HGS continued to increase even after they graduated from university and entered their 30 s. However, their HGS decreased from age 50, even though they practiced sports with grasping tools. Longitudinal changes in HGS are expected to vary among individuals and to be influenced by participants' environmental factors (e.g., sports, occupation, nutrition, illness, etc.).

Obesity

Impact of Obesity on Disease Risks

Obesity is a complex multifactorial disease [87], and accumulated excess body fat, especially its distribution [88], has adverse health effects. The World Health Organization has published the leading risks for mortality using selected risk factors [89]. Overweight and obesity are among the top five risk factors for mortality worldwide, including risk for chronic diseases such as heart disease and cancer. The World Health Organization states that these effects occur in countries of all incomes (high, middle, and low income).

Impact of Obesity on HGS

Many studies have used body mass index (BMI) as a surrogate for obesity, and it is still debated whether obesity directly affects HGS. HGS reflects the maximum voluntary strength derived from a combined contraction of extrinsic and intrinsic hand flexor muscles [90, 91], and those muscle groups may be associated with arm lean mass. For example, a study [92] compared the absolute HGS of middle-aged women without obesity (n = 80, BMI 22.0 ± 2.2 kg/m2) and middle-aged women with obesity (n = 173, BMI 37.8 ± 5.3 kg/m2) and found no difference in HGS between the two groups. In both groups, HGS was weakly correlated with total fat-free mass estimated by bioelectrical impedance analysis but not fat mass or BMI. When HGS was adjusted for total fat-free mass, women with obesity had approximately 10% lower HGS than women who were lean. A similar study in older women [93] compared HGS in three BMI groups: lean (n = 598, BMI < 24 kg/m2), normal weight (n = 630, BMI 24–29 kg/m2), and obese (n = 215, BMI > 29 kg/m2). Body composition was estimated using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). HGS was similar among the three groups with and without adjusting for age, height, physical activity, pain, depression, and arm lean tissue mass. Although HGS was not adjusted for body composition, another study of middle-aged and older adults [94] comparing HGS by BMI quartiles found no between-group differences in women but observed that men with a higher BMI had higher HGS. A recent study [95] examined the mediating effect of DXA-estimated body composition (lean mass and fat mass) on the association between HGS and BMI of adolescents (58 boys and 60 girls). A weak positive association was observed between HGS and BMI or lean mass but not fat mass. Lean body mass was the only significant mediator between BMI and HGS association. Although studies on HGS in obese individuals did not report the same results, HGS appears to be influenced by lean mass rather than accumulated excess body fat.

Alcohol

Impact of Alcohol Consumption on Disease Risks

The health effects of alcohol are thought to depend mainly on the amount and pattern of drinking, with moderate consumption often having beneficial effects on health, especially the incidence and mortality of cardiovascular diseases. In contrast, abuse is deleterious to health. Systematic reviews with meta-analyses [96, 97] have revealed that light to moderate alcohol consumption is associated with a reduced risk of the incidence and mortality of cardiovascular and coronary heart diseases. Dose–response analysis showed that the lowest risk of coronary heart disease mortality occurred with 1 ~ 2 drinks a day. It has also been reported that moderate alcohol intake plays a role in reducing the risk of type 2 diabetes incidence [98]. However, another study suggests that the previous research may be overestimating the benefits of moderate consumption of alcohol for cardiovascular disease outcomes [99]. Furthermore, a recent report has shown that there is no safe alcohol intake in terms of to developing cancer [100].

Impact of Alcohol Consumption on HGS

A systematic review with meta-analysis [101] examined the impact of alcohol consumption on the prevalence of sarcopenia in older adults, and the results indicate that alcohol drinking did not contribute to sarcopenia development. Another systematic review with meta-analysis [102] investigated the association between alcohol consumption and the risk of frailty in community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults. The results showed that alcohol intake is associated with a lower incidence of frailty compared with no alcohol consumption. However, the included studies did not adjust for potential confounders. Although HGS is included in the diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia and frailty, the reviews did not examine the direct impact of alcohol consumption on HGS. A recent large-scale study [103] examined the associations between alcohol consumption and HGS using cross-sectional (n = 196,561) and longitudinal (n = 12,298) samples. The cross-sectional data indicated that HGS was higher in those with higher alcohol intake and lowest in non-drinkers after adjusting for several confounders. However, the difference in mean HGS between the two groups was within 2 kg in men and women. The longitudinal data showed no association between alcohol consumption at baseline and HGS at follow-up.

Smoking

Impact of Smoking on Disease Risks

Cigarette smoking is a worldwide public health challenge, and it has been reported as a vital risk factor for cardiovascular disease and non-cardiovascular outcomes, including cancer and lung disease, in a dose-dependent manner [104, 105]. Studies conducted in the mid-twentieth century focused on the link between smoking and lung cancer [104]. However, subsequent research has shown that smoking is strongly associated not only with death from lung cancer but also with death from respiratory and vascular diseases [104, 105]. For example, an earlier study showed that the rate of lung cancer in smokers was ten times greater than in non-smokers in the 1960s and 20 times greater in the 1980s [104]. Furthermore, a study reported that among middle-aged men, overall survival was significantly higher in those who reported not smoking compared with those who reported smoking at baseline, leading to a considerably higher number of years lived free of cardiovascular disease (29 vs. 24 years), indicating a delay in cardiovascular disease onset by about 5 years in non-smokers [105].

Impact of Smoking on HGS

It is reported that smokers had lower muscle protein synthesis and higher myostatin and muscle atrophy F-box expression than non-smokers [106]. These results suggest the possible effect of smoking on skeletal muscle mass and strength. However, few studies have investigated the impact of smoking on HGS, and all of them have been cross-sectional studies [30, 107, 108] with inconsistent results, so the effect still needs to be determined. Future longitudinal studies will be needed to characterize how smoking affects HGS.

Food

Impact of Diet on Disease Risks

A suboptimal diet is a risk factor for poor health and contributes to non-communicable diseases and premature death [109, 110]. While maintaining a healthy weight through a balance between caloric intake and expenditure is essential for disease prevention and improvement, today's obesogenic dietary environment may require a caloric intake that feels moderately restricted. In addition, dietary fat, protein, and carbohydrate types appear to be important determinants of chronic disease and mortality risk [111–113]. Specifically, it is recommended that people eat minimally processed plant-based foods and healthy fats and reduce their intake of red and processed meats and added sugars [109]. A systematic review and meta-analysis have shown that the consumption of ultra-processed foods may be associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, depression, and all-cause mortality [114], although some have expressed doubts [115]. A healthy diet is also enhanced by including various plant-based foods rich in polyphenols and other phytonutrients with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [116].

Impact of Diet on HGS

A systematic review with meta-analysis using randomized controlled trials showed that dairy protein consumption did not improve HGS in middle-aged and older adults, but it significantly increased appendicular lean soft-tissue mass [117]. Another systematic review with meta-analysis investigated whether increasing daily protein ingestion contributes to HGS gain in healthy participants. They reported that the effect of ingesting more protein on HGS is still being determined [118]. However, a study suggests that the quality of ingested amino acids may contribute to the age-related decline in HGS in older adults [119]. Nonetheless, increasing protein and amino acid intake, which stimulates muscle protein metabolism, appears to have a limited effect on HGS in adulthood.

Meanwhile, a recent study used National Health and Nutrition Survey data to examine the association between HGS and composite dietary antioxidant index (a composite score of multiple dietary antioxidants including vitamins A, C, and E, selenium, zinc, and carotenoids) in American adults [120]. As a result, the composite dietary antioxidant index was associated with HGS in men but not women. Furthermore, in male teenagers, one study reported the possible association of higher homemade meal intake with higher HGS and that of high ultra-processed food with lower HGS [121].

Physical Activity

Impact of Physical Activity on Disease Risks

Physical inactivity is a well-established risk factor for premature death and several non-communicable diseases, such as coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes, breast cancer, and colon cancer [122]. To ensure people understand the benefits of exercise and reducing sedentary time, several academic organizations worldwide recommend physical activity guidelines to prevent and improve disease [36, 123–125]. Physical activity has numerous documented health benefits [122–125], the potential to create cost savings and reduce resource utilization, and the ability to be applied as a preventive health intervention for almost every individual.

Impact of Physical Activity on HGS

Cross-sectional studies investigating the impact of physical activity on HGS have found that active individuals have higher HGS than sedentary individuals in young, middle-aged, and older adults [126, 127]. However, the causal association between these results still needs to be clarified and confirmed through longitudinal intervention studies. A systematic review with meta-analysis [70] reported statistically significant but small effects (standardized mean difference: 0.28, p < 0.001) of various exercise intervention types on HGS in healthy community-dwelling older adults. Similarly, another review reported no consensus regarding the association between baseline physical activity level and HGS over time [128]. Regarding resistance training effects, other systematic reviews with meta-analyses have reported limited effects on HGS in older adults [71, 72, 129]. These previous studies have focused on old and very old participants, so it is necessary to confirm the effects of resistance training on HGS in younger and middle-aged individuals [73].

Impact of Type of Sports on HGS

Developmental Period

Before starting our research in this area, we searched for studies comparing the impact of different sports on HGS in children and adolescent athletes but did not find any relevant research. Thus, we attempted to provide some answers to this question using the Juntendo University Faculty of Health and Sports Sciences database. The Juntendo University database [the Juntendo Fitness Plus (J-Fit Plus) Study] annually collects students' anthropometric, physical fitness, and motor ability measurements, including HGS. The test results compiled in the project’s database have been available since 1973. To compare sports types and HGS, we selected two types of male sporting events among first-year university students with matching physiques (i.e., height and body mass); soccer (n = 1127) targets the lower body movement, and kendo (n = 297) and baseball (n = 698) use the lower body simultaneously with upper body movement (including gripping) [130]. Those in the lower- and upper-body sports (kendo and baseball) had about 4 kg higher HGS than those in the lower-body sports (soccer). Comparing each sport, it found that HGS with each sport was different from the other, with kendo > baseball > soccer (between each sport, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the difference between sports has become greater over time, and recently, the difference in HGS between soccer and kendo is estimated to be approximately 9 kg. Similar results were observed between female soccer and kendo athletes [33].

Many of the aforementioned participants were engaged in a single sport from early adolescence to university. It is important to know at what age the difference in HGS starts to appear. Therefore, we targeted young athletes between the ages of 6 and 13 years and investigated the relationship between age and the difference in HGS between soccer and kendo, two sports that were previously found to differ in HGS [40]. The findings showed that kendo athletes who used bamboo swords during practice/play had significantly higher HGS than soccer athletes who did not utilize handgrip implements for their sport. The relationship between sport and HGS depended upon age, with significant differences between the two groups observed in athletes 8.5 years and older. These results suggest that sports involving gripping sports equipment may contribute to increased HGS during development and lead to the acquisition of a higher baseline HGS as a young adult. However, the study’s cross-sectional nature limits the ability to determine whether the sport type could induce differential changes in HGS. Future longitudinal studies may clarify the potential contribution of sports that involve gripping equipment to improve HGS gains.

In Adulthood

Cross-sectional studies have been conducted regarding the age-related comparisons of HGS in men and women. For example, some cross-sectional research shows no difference in HGS from people in their 20s to 40s [77, 83], while others show that HGS is higher in their 30 s than in their 20 s [84, 131]. Because each study draws a subset of participants from the population as the study sample, each age group may have sample bias. Such sample bias may cause differences in the results of the above cross-sectional studies. Therefore, although cross-sectional studies provide approximate reference values for each age group, they cannot clarify the effect of sport type on age-related changes in HGS in young adults.

On the other hand, several studies have reported changes in HGS in adults using follow-up samples, but most of these studies have addressed changes from late middle age to old age [132–135]. In addition, follow-up periods differed between studies, and it is impossible to determine the influence of sport type on changes in HGS.

Recently, we investigated the influence of sport type on age-related changes in HGS in young adults. As mentioned earlier, the J-Fit Plus Study database contains HGS data for each sport type during university years. We were particularly interested in changes in kendo athletes who may have acquired high HGS through sports during their development. Thus, we compared the HGS of kendo club members while they were at university with their current HGS [39] and found that 84% of graduates responded that they continued practicing kendo after graduation (94% in their 20s and 30s). The graduates in their 20s/30s had higher HGS now than when they were at university. Despite having practiced kendo, however, the HGS of graduates decreased after age 50. Furthermore, graduates in their 40s showed HGS similar to their university days [39]. Although it is necessary to compare the changes in HGS in sports events where graduates do not hold equipment with their hands during practice and play to determine the effects, this pattern of HGS change in kendo is similar to the results obtained in previous cross-sectional studies [84, 131]. Taken together, these results suggest that the impact of sport type on HGS during adulthood may be limited and that changes in HGS are most important during development.

Novel Approaches to Uncovering the Impact of Sport Type on HGS

Follow-up Studies for the Future

Databases used to examine the association between HGS and morbidity/mortality often include results from physical activity questionnaires. It is challenging to obtain detailed information on physical activity and sports habits across childhood and adulthood, although information on status at the time of the baseline survey is available. The Juntendo Fitness Plus Study (J-Fit Plus Study) is a project of Juntendo University to explore the students' cumulative data on anthropometric, physical fitness, and motor ability tests from various perspectives, and its findings have been available since 1973 [33, 130]. These data are unique because students usually have a strong sports background since early childhood and are more likely to continue engaging in a single sport from early adolescence to university. In addition, after graduating from university, many students continue as coaches and athletes in the same sports they practiced at university [39]. Therefore, examining the effects of different sports types on HGS and morbidity/mortality in university graduates who primarily engage in one sport may be possible.

As mentioned earlier, we divided sports into two groups based on whether they used sports equipment during play, and we further compared HGS among soccer, kendo, and baseball players, adjusting for height and body mass [130]. These three sports were selected because of the large number of participants in the database; however, there are other comparable sports in the database with smaller participant counts that could serve similar purposes. We attempted to do additional analysis to count the number (percentage) of graduates with low HGS in two sport types. Previous studies have mainly focused on older adults, so cutoff values for low HGS in younger adults are unknown. However, since HGS in middle age is relatively stable, standards for middle-aged people can be used as a reference. We set the cutoff value for low HGS at < 36 kg, based on the cutoff values for middle-aged men in the three follow-up studies (< 36 kg for lower than 56 years [52], < 44 kg for 50–54 years [8], < 38 kg for 40–55 years [24]). In lower-body sports (soccer), 129 graduates had low HGS measured during university (11.4%), while in lower- and upper-body sports (kendo and baseball), 29 graduates had low HGS measured during university (2.9%). The J-Fit Plus Study began in 1973, so the age of graduates who participated in the first survey is now 70. Therefore, the crude all-cause and disease-specific mortality rates for both sports types are currently meager, and further observation is warranted to understand the impact of different sports on the association between HGS and mortality/morbidity. By utilizing this unique database, which includes approximately 15,000 graduates from 18 different major sports and tracks their health status, it may be possible to explore the influence of sport type on HGS during development and morbidity/mortality in adulthood.

Possible Mechanisms and Future Research

Despite significant research efforts to advance the field, the mechanisms for the associations between HGS and morbidity/mortality are not fully understood [5, 45, 57, 62]. Previous studies have investigated the association between the two and explored the mechanism using the measured covariates [2–19, 43–65]. Impacts of Genetics and Family Lifestyle

Studies consistently show that genetic factors influence HGS [22, 23]. For example, heritability estimates for HGS are approximately 60% for adult twins [136, 137]. Interestingly, analyses using only twins who have not lived together in the past 30 years and only have contact about once a month yielded similar estimates [137]. HGS is also correlated between parents and their children [138, 139]. Furthermore, parents can influence their children’s physical activity and sports [140, 141] and often teach their young children the sports they themselves have participated in. In other words, children of parents who have experience playing sports that use equipment (i.e., baseball, kendo, tennis, etc.) may be likely to choose the same type of sport, and children of parents who have experience playing sports that do not use equipment (i.e., soccer, running) are similarly likely to choose the same sport. Furthermore, sports experiences with parents may lead to motivation and knowledge about maintaining physical activity throughout life, potentially resulting in higher participation rates. On the other hand, meals in the home are shared among many family members until the offspring becomes independent. Studies have recently reported that ingested nutrients are related to disease onset via genetic risk factors [142, 143], but the association between these nutrients and HGS remains unclear. Therefore, future studies should investigate how intrafamilial genetic factors, sports choice, and family dietary habits may influence the association between HGS and morbidity/mortality.

Why is the Growth and Developmental Period so Important?

As mentioned above, sports using equipment during the developmental period may improve HGS compared with sports without equipment [40]. Consequently, individuals participating in sports with equipment may attain higher HGS levels during young adulthood [130]. However, while no evidence to date exists linking sport acquired HGS and subsequent disease onset [26, 144], some findings should be considered. For example, a recent study reported that higher levels of low-grade systemic inflammation in mid-childhood at age nine were associated with specific mental and cardiometabolic disorders in young adulthood. However, higher levels at age 17 were not associated with those outcomes [145]. Recently, a systematic review with meta-analysis investigating the cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between HGS and markers of insulin resistance and inflammation in children and adolescents has been reported [146]. The study found that cross-sectional studies using relative HGS per body weight showed an association between HGS and some markers of insulin resistance and inflammation. However, longitudinal studies are limited, and no conclusions can be drawn. Thus, future studies may shed light on associations between changes in HGS and changes in insulin resistance and inflammatory markers in children and adolescents.

On the other hand, a study that examined the association between baseline HGS in older women and tracked changes in their inflammatory markers reported an inverse relationship between the two variables [147]. They speculated that inflammation markers partly explained the association between HGS and mortality. Although the mechanism is unknown, this suggests that acquiring high HGS by adulthood may be able to suppress low-grade systemic inflammatory responses.

Impacts of Muscle Contractions During Different Sport Types

Skeletal muscles produce “myokines” in response to sports and physical activity. Those repeated myokine responses may affect inflammation responses and the prevention/treatment of lifestyle-related diseases such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and dementia through physiological, metabolic, and immunological effects via crosstalk between muscles and other organs (e.g., brain, adipose tissue, bone, liver, pancreas, etc.) and communication within the muscle itself [148]. For instance, interleukin-6 (IL-6) is generally classified as a pro-inflammatory cytokine because it is secreted by T-cells and macrophages and promotes immune system activation and inflammation [149]. However, IL-6 and IL-6-induced acute phase proteins have anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects when originating from skeletal muscle, reducing the immune system's inflammatory response [150]. Physical activity and sports have been shown to induce this differential effect of IL-6. When acute changes in IL-6 following upper- and lower-body resistance exercises (50% of one repetition maximum, five sets to volitional failure, 3 min rest interval between sets) were compared, a greater increase was observed after leg press than after bench press exercise [151]. The authors indicated that this may be related to the amount of muscle the exercise activates.

On the other hand, a study examined the IL-6 release from the arm and leg muscles during whole-body exercise and found that IL-6 release was higher from exercising arms than from legs [152]. Due to the lack of comparative studies, it is currently impossible to determine whether IL-6 release from muscle is related to the amount of active muscle or whether it differs between arm and leg muscles. However, sports involving equipment and both upper and lower limbs require more muscle activation than sports involving only the lower body, which may result in higher IL-6 release from activated overall muscles.

As mentioned above, whole-body exercise with grasping sports tools during development is noteworthy because it releases myokines, including IL-6, and improves HGS. The physiological benefits of these sports activities may also have implications for disease prevention in the future. Exercise-induced IL-6 also promotes lipolysis, improves energy utilization, and may help prevent obesity-related disorders [153]. Further studies are needed to confirm these beneficial effects of exercise on health indicators and morbidity/mortality, depending on the type of sports.

Conclusion

Numerous studies have shown that HGS is favorably associated with morbidity and mortality. Regarding the causal relationship between the two, various factors, such as sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and self-reported or doctor-diagnosed medical conditions, have been examined during both the developmental and adulthood periods. However, despite significant research efforts to advance the field, the mechanisms underlying this association are not fully understood. Assessing causal relationships using observational epidemiological studies may be challenging, and new approaches may need to clarify the mechanistic association between HGS and future health. Physical activity has been extensively investigated in previous studies as a covariate to explain the association between HGS and morbidity/mortality, but no consensus has been reached that physical activity is a mediator explaining this association. Assessing people's physical activity across a lifetime is a challenging task. To help better understand the association between the two, we proposed a research model that focuses on the type of sport played during the developmental period and explores their impact on morbidity and mortality in adulthood. Although we focused on sports because they are readily classified as activity types, a similar effect may also apply to repeated object grasping during active play and exercise in daily life. Furthermore, if sport contributes to HGS improvements during development and influences morbidity and mortality, we also proposed mechanisms that could be considered in future studies. Unfortunately, further follow-up is needed before the success of this research model can be confirmed. It would also be necessary to clarify the factors related to the future health benefits of improving HGS during development.
