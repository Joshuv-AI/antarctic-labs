Synergistic Stabilization and Rheological Regulation of Fluorine-Free Foams Stabilized by Silica Nanoparticles and Siloxane-Hydrocarbon Surfactant Mixtures
Authors: Jiaqi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanoparticle (NP)-stabilized fluorine-free foams, as a sustainable alternative to traditional aqueous film-forming foams (AFFF), rely on foam stability and rheological properties for their practical effectiveness. NP concentration governs microscopic structural evolution and flow behavior, thereby determining foam stability as well as the spreading and coverage performance on fuel surfaces. This study systematically investigates the rheological properties and stabilization mechanisms of fluorine-free foams costabilized by hydrophilic silica NPs and a 648/BS-12 binary surfactant system. The NP-stabilized foams exhibit distinct non-Newtonian shear-thinning behavior. With increasing NP concentration, the system is proposed to undergo three characteristic stages: adsorption mode transition, rheological percolation, and stable jamming. The adsorption mode transition at 1.5 wt % NP is suggested by simultaneous anomalies in surface tension and electrical conductivity. At 1.75 wt %, the system is interpreted as undergoing a fundamental liquid-to-solid transition, marked by a pronounced stress overshoot, and the solid-like features become more pronounced at higher concentrations. At 2.0 wt %, a three-dimensional network constructed by NP-surfactant aggregates is proposed to effectively suppress foam drainage and bubble coarsening, as independently supported by time-resolved optical microscopy. These findings provide a theoretical basis and quantitative design criteria for high-performance fluorine-free firefighting foams based on nanoparticle–binary surfactant systems.

Introduction

Liquid fuel fires have long been a major safety concern in the petrochemical and transportation industries due to their high heat release rates and fast spread. Aqueous film-forming foams (AFFFs) are frequently employed to extinguish this particular kind of fires. This is a consequence of their major ingredients, long-chain (C8–C10) fluorocarbon surfactants, which allow them to spread swiftly on hydrophobic fuel surfaces and extinguish fires more efficiently. However, the extensive usage of traditional AFFF core components has created severe global concerns about PFAS’s environmental persistence, bioaccumulation, and toxicity. , With increasingly rigorous environmental requirements, developing effective and environmentally friendly fluorine-free firefighting foams (F3) has become an urgent priority in fire safety engineering. Short-chain (C4–C6) fluorocarbon surfactants are currently being used as the primary approach to decreasing AFFF toxicity in place of long-chain fluorocarbon surfactants. − Nonetheless, research reveals that shortening the fluorocarbon chain significantly decreases interfacial activity, resulting in a marked decline in foam stability and burn-back resistance, − which fails to meet practical fire suppression requirements. This indicates that relying solely on molecular structure adjustments of surfactants is insufficient to achieve an ideal replacement for long-chain fluorocarbon surfactants. To address this dilemma, current research focuses on understanding the interfacial stabilization mechanisms of multicomponent complex systems, emphasizing the urgent need to reconstruct the interfacial and bulk structures of foam through the application of innovative functional materials. −

Among various fluorine-free alternative strategies, polyether-modified silicone surfactants are regarded as suitable candidates to replace fluorocarbon surfactants due to their flexible siloxane backbones and low surface energy, providing excellent surface activity and wetting properties. − Previous research has found that fluorine-free foams containing silicone surfactants frequently fail to create stable liquid films with high viscoelasticity, resulting in quick drainage and poor resistance to disproportionation at high temperatures or external disturbances. , Therefore, the introduction of suitable foam stabilizers to optimize rheological properties is critical to improving the extinguishing efficiency and practical application of silicone-based firefighting agents. Inorganic nanoparticles (NPs) enhance foam stability by accumulating in foam films and Plateau borders to form a stable network structure, thereby delaying foam drainage and coarsening, , while avoiding the degradation and high flow resistance issues associated with traditional biopolymer thickening. At critical concentrations, the additional NPs can trigger microstructural phase transitions in the dispersion, dramatically altering the foam’s flow behavior. − This concept of reinforcing phase structures is highly consistent with the latest developments in advanced fire-safe materials, where structured microbubbles and dual templates are engineered into robust, superelastic fire-protective aerogels and hydrogels. ,

Changes in the flow behavior of foaming solutions directly determine the spreading ability and liquid film stability of the foam on fuel surfaces, fundamentally altering its fire-extinguishing efficiency. , Because the flow characteristics of foams are primarily determined by their rheological properties, thoroughly exploring the impact of the critical NP addition concentration on the rheological performance of stable foams is of great significance. , However, the existing research lacks a comprehensive mechanistic explication of the evolution rules of rheological structures caused by nanoparticles in surfactant systems, as well as a quantitative regulating mechanism for silicone foam stability.

The vast majority of existing studies on NP-stabilized foams employing a three-stage structural evolution framework have investigated single-surfactant systems, in which one ionic or nonionic surfactant is combined with one NP type. , In these systems, the adsorption behavior, percolation threshold, and jamming concentration are governed primarily by NP–surfactant electrostatic or van der Waals interactions, and the associated concentration thresholds can be estimated from single-component models. In binary surfactant systems, however, the competitive adsorption between two chemically distinct surfactant species at the NP surface introduces concentration-dependent dynamics that have no counterpart in single-component formulations and that fundamentally alter the threshold at which adsorption mode transition, network formation, and jamming occur. This is particularly relevant for silicone/hydrocarbon surfactant combinations, which represent the most practically important class of fluorine-free firefighting foam formulations , but whose three-stage rheological evolution has not been systematically quantified.

In view of this, using silica nanoparticles (NPs), silicone surfactant (648), and hydrocarbon surfactant (BS-12) as raw materials, different types of mixed dispersions were prepared to systematically study the regulation mechanism of NP concentration on the properties of the dispersions and foams. By analyzing the dynamic surface tension, viscosity, conductivity, and rheological properties of the mixed dispersions, the interactions between NPs and surfactants at the gas–liquid interface were systematically investigated. Furthermore, the foamability, foam stability, and foam rheological properties were characterized. This study systematically characterizes and proposes three characteristic rheological evolution stagesadsorption mode transition, rheological percolation, and stable jammingas a mechanistic framework for the 648/BS-12/NPs dispersion system as the NP concentration increases. This framework delineates the proposed continuous evolution path of the system from a viscous fluid to a durable solid-like gel, offering an important theoretical foundation and design strategy for the focused development of high-performance fluorine-free firefighting foams.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The nonionic silicone surfactant, polyether-modified siloxane 648 (100% purity), was purchased from Keying KYC-Guangzhou Yangu Trading Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) as a colorless to slightly yellow liquid. The amphoteric hydrocarbon surfactant BS-12 (99% purity) was obtained from Shandong Yousuo Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China); it is a colorless to light yellow transparent liquid with a 1% aqueous solution pH of 6.0–7.0 at 25 °C. Figure illustrates the molecular structure of BS-12. Hydrophilic fumed silica NPs (purity 99.8%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd., with an average specific surface area of 300 m2 /g.

Mixed Dispersions with Different Components

The preparation process for the mixed dispersions was as follows: First, a beaker was filled with the proper volume of deionized water. Subsequently, the two surfactants (648 and BS-12) were added to the beaker while being stirred magnetically. After that, the NPs were slowly introduced into the surfactant solution and stirred thoroughly. Finally, the beaker was placed in a ScientZ-2400F ultrasonic disperser and treated at 1200 W for 10 min to obtain the uniform mixed dispersions.

The dual-syringe method was used to prepare the foams for this investigation. The apparatus consisted of two 60 mL syringes connected by a short rubber tube with a 5 mm diameter. During preparation, the mixed dispersion and air were loaded into the two syringes at a volume ratio of 1:2. A homogeneous foam sample was then obtained by repeatedly pushing and pulling the syringe plungers 30 times at a constant speed. The dual-syringe method ensures that different foam dispersions have the same initial volume and generates foams with uniform distribution and high reproducibility.

Characterization Methods

Characterization of Mixed Dispersions

A QBZY-3 automatic tensiometer (Shanghai Fangrui Instrument Co., Ltd.) , was used to measure surface tension using the Wilhelmy plate method. During the test, the sample stage was controlled to rise slowly until the platinum plate made contact with the gas–liquid interface of the solution. After a while, the measured surface tension value would steadily rise and reach stability after a period; this steady value was identified as the equilibrium surface tension. Conductivity was measured using an SG23-B multiparameter tester (Mettler-Toledo Instruments Shanghai Co., Ltd.). The fundamental principle involves calculating conductivity by measuring the current generated between electrodes placed in the solution.

Before testing, the instrument was calibrated using a standard solution; subsequently, the electrode was immersed in the mixed solution within a constant-temperature water bath, and the reading was recorded once stabilized. To ensure the reproducibility of the equilibrium surface tension, electrical conductivity and initial foam height measurements, each of these tests was repeated independently at least three times, and the corresponding data are reported as the arithmetic mean with standard deviation indicated by error bars in the relevant figures. The stability and dependability of the aforementioned surface tension and conductivity testing techniques have been extensively confirmed in earlier studies, and they are appropriate for the quantitative assessment of the system in this investigation. ,

Foam Coarsening Observation

The bubble coarsening behavior of the foams was characterized using a Zeiss Stemi 508 stereoscopic microscope equipped with a 5× objective lens. Immediately after generating the foam via the dual-syringe method, a small aliquot of the foam sample was deposited onto a clean glass slide and gently sealed with a standard coverslip to maintain a consistent observation layer thickness. The slide was then transferred to the microscope stage and observed under transmitted-light mode. Sequential micrographs were recorded by the built-in camera at a resolution of 2584 × 1936 pixels, with a fixed acquisition interval of 60 s throughout the coarsening process. The captured images were subsequently imported into ImageJ software for batch processing, from which the equivalent diameter of individual bubbles was extracted. The resulting bubble-size data were further analyzed by frequency-distribution statistics to construct the bubble diameter distribution at different coarsening times, thereby quantitatively reflecting the temporal evolution of the foam microstructure.

Rheological and Foam Performance Testing

The rheological parameters of the mixed dispersions and foams were determined using an Anton Paar MCR302e rheometer, which offers a wide measurement range, high precision, and excellent reproducibility. The apparent viscosity of mixed dispersions was measured using a double-gap coaxial cylinder measurement device (Model DG26.7) by controlling the shear rate within the range of 0.1 s–1 to 1000 s–1. The oscillatory strain amplitude sweep method was employed to investigate the viscoelasticity of the mixed dispersions. Amplitude sweep tests were utilized to determine the viscoelasticity of the foams, with an oscillatory strain range of 0.01% to 100% and a constant angular frequency of 10 rad/s. Thixotropic experiments were carried out in oscillatory mode over three intervals; to minimize the impact of foam decay on the results, all experiments had to conclude within 300 s.

Foamability of the dispersions was characterized using a modified Ross-Miles method. The foamability testing apparatus consisted of a spherical funnel and a cylindrical container. A 200 mL amount of foam dispersion was released from the spherical funnel into a cylindrical container containing 50 mL of the same dispersion, triggering foam formation through the impact of the falling liquid. Once all the dispersion was released and the foam height stabilized, the initial height (h0) was measured to characterize foamability.

Foam stability was assessed by monitoring foam drainage. After preparing the foam via the dual-syringe method, the two syringes were separated, and one syringe with the foam was placed vertically upward to allow for natural drainage under gravity. A CCD camera was used to capture the entire drainage process, and the volume of drained liquid was quantified using the syringe’s scale to assess foam stability. ,

Results and Discussion

Characterizing Mixed Dispersions

Surface Tension and Conductivity of Mixed Dispersions

The surface tension and conductivity of the seven mixed dispersions are depicted in Figure . Surface tensions for the individual BS-12 and 648 solutions are 26.890 and 20.239 mN/m, respectively. In this experiment, the surface tension of the BS-12/648 mixed dispersion without NPs is 20.828 mN/m, which falls between the two individual values, indicating that BS-12 and 648 molecules coexist at the gas–liquid interface. Sample N-0# exhibits the lowest surface tension, indicating that the addition of NPs raises the surface tension of the BS-12/648 system, thereby reducing surface activity. Furthermore, the surface tension shows a gradual upward trend as the NP concentration increases. Notably, a distinct inflection point in surface tension emerges for sample N-3# when the NP concentration reaches 1.5 wt %. This phenomenon suggests intense competitive adsorption within the mixed dispersion; BS-12 and 648 molecules migrate from the gas–liquid interface to the nanoparticle surfaces, resulting in a lower surfactant concentration at the interface, which consequently weakens their ability to lower surface tension. −

Surface tension (mN/m) and conductivity (μS/cm) of mixed dispersions at 25 °C.

In this study, BS-12 is a zwitterionic surfactant, whereas 648 is a nonionic surfactant. The silica NPs themselves are hydrophilic due to the presence of silanol groups (Si–OH) on their surfaces, which can ionize in aqueous solutions to carry negative charges. The nonionic 648 molecules primarily adsorb to the NP surfaces via van der Waals forces to form aggregates. Additionally, as a zwitterionic surfactant, BS-12 molecules can display cationic behavior. Their positively charged headgroups can adsorb onto negatively charged silica NPs through electrostatic interactions, which is further enhanced by hydrogen bonding. ,

After BS-12 molecules adsorb onto the NP surfaces, their hydrophobic tails orient outward, effectively shielding the hydrophilic nature of the silica. As more BS-12 molecules adsorb, the hydrophilic nanoparticles in the system gradually transform into hydrophobic BS-12/648/NP aggregates. The migration of these aggregates to the gas–liquid interface ultimately causes the overall surface tension of the mixed dispersion to rise. The nonmonotonic transition point observed at 1.5 wt % NP concentration (N-3#) occurs because the total specific surface area reaches a critical value, initiating massive adsorption of free surfactant molecules from the solution. This competitive adsorption is likely to drive an abrupt shift in the chemical properties at the gas–liquid interface. The shift in the aggregate adsorption mode may provide the necessary structural basis for bulk percolation and the formation of a continuous network at greater concentrations. ,

The conductivity of the mixed dispersions provides additional evidence for the adsorption interactions between NPs and surfactant molecules. As illustrated in Figure , the introduction of NPs reduces the conductivity of the dispersions, indicating a reduction in the amount of freely moving ions in the solution. To ensure a consistent comparison, the volume ratio of the aqueous phase was kept constant across all samples, and any potential dilution effects from the nanoparticle addition were found to be negligible at the concentrations studied. This phenomenon can be attributed to the BS-12 molecules (exhibiting cationic behavior) adsorbing onto negatively charged NP surfaces, consuming free charges in the solution. − Therefore, the experimental conductivity results provide strong evidence for significant adsorption interactions between the NPs and surfactant molecules. The nonmonotonic transition in electrical properties exhibited by sample N-3# is extremely consistent with the aforementioned surface tension results; at this point, the rearrangement of the interfacial electrical double layer causes transient counterion release. This simultaneous shift in the interfacial properties of the mixed dispersion further suggests that a transition in the adsorption mode of NP composite aggregates may occur at this concentration (1.5 wt %).

The nonmonotonic shift at 1.5 wt % NP is interpreted through a mass-balance coupling between the bulk solution and the gas–liquid interface. The simultaneous decrease in electrical conductivity and increase in surface tension suggests a depletion of free surfactant species from both the aqueous phase and the interface. Specifically, as the total surface area of the nanoparticles reaches a critical threshold at 1.5 wt %, the adsorption of the zwitterionic BS-12 molecules onto the negatively charged silica surfaces is interpreted as undergoing a transition from sparse coverage to a more densely packed configuration. This process is proposed to lead to the immobilization of ionic headgroups, which is reflected in the drop in bulk conductivity. Consequently, to maintain chemical potential equilibrium, surfactant molecules are proposed to migrate from the gas–liquid interface back into the bulk to occupy the newly available sites on the NP surfaces. This interfacial-to-bulk migration is consistent with the reduced surfactant density at the bubble surface inferred from the observed rise in surface tension. This coupled response across independent physical phases offers a self-consistent mechanistic interpretation of the onset of competitive adsorption without relying on a single observable. We note that the present assignment of an adsorption mode transition rests on indirect interfacial and bulk measurements; while the coupled surface-tension and conductivity response makes this interpretation well supported, direct microscopic confirmation of the adsorption configuration would require complementary techniques and is left to future work.

Flow Behavior of Mixed Dispersions

The variation of apparent viscosity with shear rate for the mixed dispersions is shown in Figure a. All mixed dispersions exhibit typical non-Newtonian shear-thinning behavior. Their apparent viscosity diminishes as the shear rate increases and gradually stabilizes at high shear rates. This phenomenon originates from the disruption and rearrangement of the dispersions’ internal structure in the shear direction. The shear-thinning behavior in the N-0# system devoid of nanoparticles is primarily attributed to the disentanglement of associative colloids under shear force, which are formed by the entanglement of 648 and BS-12 surfactant micelles. At this point, the apparent viscosity is relatively the lowest, indicating weak interactions between surfactant micelles formed solely by free dispersion.

Curves of (a) apparent viscosity and (b) shear stress variation with shear rate.

After NPs are introduced into the solution, hybrid aggregates generated by silica NPs and surfactant molecules increase the internal friction of the mixed dispersions. Meanwhile, these aggregates can form cross-linked structures with associative colloids. Both effects increase the flow resistance, resulting in an overall apparent viscosity greater than that of the N-0# system. With rising NP concentration, aggregate concentration rises, and cross-linked structures become denser, resulting in a steady increase in the system’s apparent viscosity.

Figure b presents the variation of shear stress with shear rate in mixed dispersions. The shear stress increases as the shear rate increases. At the same shear rate, mixed dispersions containing NPs require a higher shear stress, which increases with NP concentration.

Viscoelasticity of the Mixed Dispersions

Figure depicts the variation of the viscoelastic moduli of the dispersions with different compositions as a function of shear strain. The G″ of N-0# is greater than the G′ within the test range, with disordered fluctuations, indicating that the system contains solely discrete surfactant micelles and behaves entirely as a liquid. Upon adding a small amount of NPs (N-1#), the mixed dispersion exhibits immediate elasticity at extremely low strains, which may consistent with the onset of a transition in adsorption mode between surfactant molecules and NPs at this concentration, potentially giving rise to an unstable, weak elastic response.

Variation in the viscoelastic modulus of dispersions with different components over shear strain.

As the NP concentration increases further, the viscoelastic behavior of the system undergoes what appears to be a fundamental transformation, generating distinct yield and flow points. With increasing shear strain, the G′ curve displays a stable plateau identified as the Linear Viscoelastic Region (LVR). The G′ values of the six NP-containing foams within the LVR are 0.054, 3.6708, 6.1836, 9.805, 17.236, and 104.56 Pa, respectively. The modulus increases with NP concentration, demonstrating that the addition of NPs enhances the system’s viscoelasticity. The LVR’s terminus is the yield point; once the strain exceeds this point, the structure begins to deform irreversibly.

Foam Properties

Foamability

The foamability of the various mixed dispersions is illustrated in Figure . The stepwise addition of NPs leads to a corresponding decrease in the initial foam height. Sample N-0# exhibits the optimal foamability with an initial height reaching 330 mL, which is attributed to the excellent foaming properties of the 648/BS-12 binary system.

Initial foam height of mixed dispersion liquids with different components.

Foamability shows an overall declining trend as NP concentrations increase. Particularly when the concentration reaches 1.5 wt % (N-3#), the initial foam height shows a marked decreases. This phenomenon is closely related to prior observations of abrupt changes in surface tension and conductivity, as well as a significant increase in viscosity at 1.5 wt %.

These results are consistent with the interpretation that the foaming performance of surfactant solutions is synergistically influenced by multiple factors, including surface tension, conductivity, viscosity, and particle aggregation behavior. − Specifically, previous studies have demonstrated that, while the addition of NPs can promote bubble accumulation by enhancing liquid film rigidity when the concentration reaches 1.5 wt % (N-3#), the substantial increase in viscosity is sufficient to dominate the system’s behavior. This significantly hinders the migration of surfactant molecules and aggregates to the gas–liquid interface, resulting in a reduced effective concentration at the interface. These combined factors ultimately lead to poor foamability in mixed dispersions with high NP concentrations.

Foam Drainage Characteristics

Figure presents the variation of drained liquid volume over time for foams with different NP concentrations. As observed, the drained volume for all samples exhibits an increasing trend over time. Because foam drainage is a gravity-dominated process, the liquid content continuously decreases as drainage proceeds, leading to a corresponding decay in the gravitational driving force. Graphically, this is manifested as a rapid initial increase in drained volume, followed by a gradual leveling off. Furthermore, the experimental results show that the drainage curves of the six NP-containing foams are all located below that of the NP-free sample. The maximum drained volume gradually decreases with increasing NP concentration, indicating that the addition of NPs can effectively delay drainage, thereby promoting foam stability.

Variation of the volume of foam drainage with different components over time.

This stabilizing effect can be explained by the synergy of multiple factors, including changes in surface activity, increased system viscosity, particle aggregation behavior at the liquid films and Plateau borders, alterations in maximum capillary pressure, and variations in interbubble gas diffusion rates. This study elucidates the NP stabilization mechanism primarily by analyzing the apparent viscosity and particle aggregation behavior of the mixed dispersions. On the one hand, the formation of discrete clusters is proposed to significantly increase the internal friction of the system, thereby slowing down the liquid flow velocity within the foam and decelerating the drainage process. On the other hand, electrostatic forces present in the 648/BS-12/NP mixed dispersion drive the mutual adsorption of components and their accumulation among foam channels, liquid films, and Plateau borders. , This physically restricts the thinning of liquid films and flow within the drainage channels. Through the jamming effect, these aggregates form a stable and dense network structure that effectively hinders liquid flow and inhibits bubble coarsening, thereby delaying foam drainage and enhancing stability.

Foam Flow Behavior

Figure a depicts the relationship between the apparent viscosity of the foams and the shear rate. The apparent viscosity of all foams decreases as shear rate increases, indicating that they are non-Newtonian fluids with shear-thinning behavior. This behavior is primarily caused by the disruption of aggregates formed by NPs and surfactant molecules under shear. Specifically, upon adding NPs to the samples, BS-12 and 648 molecules adsorb onto the nanoparticle surfaces via hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces, respectively, and aggregates form stable network aggregates. As the shear rate increases, most bubbles and aggregates generated by foam components deform and gradually coalesce, eventually rupturing in the direction of the shear force. Over time, the foam liquid coatings thin out, correspondingly weakening their strength and elasticity. This contributes to a decline in the overall viscosity of the foam, consistent with the shear-thinning properties of non-Newtonian fluids. , Meanwhile, as the NP concentration increases, the structural integrity of the foam system strengthens, and the critical shear rate required for complete rupture also rises, suggesting greater resistance to external destructive forces. This structural reinforcement is associated with an overall elevation of the apparent viscosity of the BS-12/648 foams.

Curves of (a) apparent viscosity and (b) shear stress variation with shear rate.

Notably, the response of apparent viscosity to concentration occurs earlier than the complete network structure is formed. A substantial rise in viscosity is observed at an NP content of 1.5 wt % (N-3#), whereas samples below this concentration exhibit only marginal differences. Composite aggregates generated at this stage significantly increase the hydrodynamic volume and internal friction of the dispersed phase. Although a physically jammed condition has not yet been reached, it is sufficient to significantly increase the viscous resistance to flow, thereby providing a fundamental rheological barrier for foam stability.

Figure b illustrates the variation of shear stress exerted on the foams as a function of shear rate. The experimental data reveal that at the same shear rate, the shear stress experienced by NP-containing foams increases monotonically with the NP concentration. This suggests the addition of NPs makes the internal structure more robust, significantly enhancing the foam’s ability to withstand deformation. Particularly when the NP concentration reaches 2.0 wt % (N-5#), the shear stress curve exhibits a significant upward leap, consistent with the onset of a high-strength jammed state.

To quantitatively elucidate the dynamic response mechanism of the foams under various shear fields, the Cross model was used to fit the flow curves. This model effectively represents the full-shear-range behavior of complicated multiphase fluids through four fundamental physical parameters. 1 τ=τ0−τ∞1+(kγ)m+τ∞

Among them, the zero-shear stress (τ0) represents the stress value as the shear rate approaches zero, directly reflecting the static strength of the internal structure required to initiate flow. The infinite-shear stress (τ∞) corresponds to lower stress limit at extremely high shear rates once the internal structure is completely disrupted. The characteristic time­(k) defines the dynamic transition from τ0 to τ∞ on a time scale, essentially characterizing the material’s response speed to increasing shear force. The dimensionless flow index (m) determines the slope of the stress response curve in the transition zone, and intuitively reflects the severity of the shear thinning phenomenon that occurs in the foam’s internal structure under shear force. Furthermore, correlation coefficients (R2) for all foams are very close to 1, confirming the applicability of the Cross model.

According to Table , τ0 steadily grows by orders of magnitude from 0.005 Pa (N-0#) to 10.52 Pa (N-6#). This suggests that NPs considerably strengthen the internal network at rest, thereby limiting flow at low shear rates. As the NP concentration increases, τ∞ initially increases slowly, indicating that residual structural elements contribute to shear resistance within a certain concentration range. However, at a critical concentration of 2.0 wt % (N-5#), the rheological parameters are significantly discontinuous. τ∞ drops sharply to 89.07 Pa, the characteristic time k increases to 6.88 × 10–4 s, and the flow index m approaches unity. This fundamental transition in rheological parameters is consistent with the system entering what we interpret as a complete jammed state. − In this proposed jammed state, composite aggregates are inferred to form a dense, rigid network, exhibiting structural brittleness. The high yield stress suggests that the jammed network is exceptionally stable at rest, effectively resisting gravity drainage. However, once the shear force exceeds the critical value, the network is proposed to undergo drastic overall deconstruction rather than progressive disentanglement, leading to near-Newtonian behavior at high shear rates. Overall, this controllable shear-thinning and enhanced structural strength are expected to allow for rapid spreading and stable sealing during firefighting.

Foam Viscoelasticity

The macro-rheological behavior characterized by steady-state shear tests is consistent with the interpretation that the 648/BS-12/NPs system enters a high-strength jammed state at N-5# (2.0 wt %). To further determine the concentration range of the rheological transition from adsorption-dominated to network-dominated and analyze the response mechanism, dynamic oscillatory strain sweep tests were conducted.

Figure a displays the curves of oscillatory stress (σ) versus oscillatory strain (γ). As strain increases, stress increases monotonically, indicating structural changes within the foam. Foam amplitude sweeps: (a) oscillatory stress versus oscillatory strain curves; (b) complex modulus curves.

The variation of these moduli with strain is shown in Figure b. As strain continues to increase, σ–γ relationship deviates from this law, marking the entry into the nonlinear rheological region. A significant rheological boundary exists between samples, providing a basis for viscoelastic analysis.

When the NP concentration is below 1.75 wt % (N-0# to N-4#), the stress transitions smoothly to a steady state with low values. This suggests that even if the apparent viscosity increases at 1.5 wt % (N-3#), the system is interpreted as still consisting of discrete aggregates that have not yet formed a continuous three-dimensional network. However, at 1.75 wt % (N-4#), a discontinuous step-like increase in stress occurs. The turning point of this curve is the yield point, indicating the onset of irreversible deformation. Notably, high-concentration samples exhibit a prominent stress overshoot near the yield point. Stress overshoot is interpreted as occurring in the proposed jammed state because the densely packed particle network creates a strong cage effect and geometric interlocking. Extra stress must be applied to overcome this energy barrier to initiate flow. This characteristic is interpreted as indicating the formation of a continuous network with macro-structural strength at 1.75 wt % (N-4#), consistent with the typical rheological percolation. At 2.0 wt % (N-5#), the percolation network appears to reach mechanical saturation, and strong interactions between aggregates are proposed to drive the system into a stable, high-strength jammed state. Furthermore, the yield point increases with NP concentration, supporting the progressive reinforcement of foam elasticity and stability.

Figure a shows the G′ and G″ curves for samples N-0# to N-3#. When the NP concentration is below 1.75 wt %, the system consistently satisfies G‴> G″, exhibiting liquid-like properties with weak viscoelasticity. Even if a rigid framework is not established, the competitive adsorption between NPs and surfactants disrupts the interfacial arrangement, slightly reducing the macroscopic viscoelastic response.

Figure b shows the G′ and G″ curves for samples N-4# to N-6#. At 1.75 wt % (N-4#) and above, the viscoelastic behavior transforms significantly, forming distinct yield and flow points. In the linear viscoelastic region (LVR), the elastic response is controlled by the network formed by liquid films and NPs. Stable three-dimensional structures penetrate the liquid films and Plateau borders, increasing friction between bubbles and enhancing resistance to deformation and recovery. Beyond the yield point, irreversible deformation begins. The intersection of G′ and G″ marks the flow point, where the foam transitions from gel-like to liquid-like behavior. Near the flow point, G′ shows a brief upward fluctuation before declining. This phenomenon results from internal structural rearrangement; as strain increases, the network undergoes compression and reorganization, creating a transient strain-hardening effect. When the strain exceeds the network capacity, structural failure occurs via bubble sliding, Plateau border drainage, and bubble rupture, leading to a sharp decrease in G′. ,

To rigorously quantify the liquid-to-solid transition at the percolation threshold, several rheological markers were extracted from the oscillatory strain sweep data. In the linear viscoelastic region, the loss tangent values for samples N-0# through N-3# are 1.18, 1.23, 1.81, and 1.70, respectively. The consistent condition where G″ is greater than G′ throughout the entire strain range confirms that these four samples exhibiting liquid-like behavior with no G′/G″ crossover. In contrast, for samples N-4# through N-6#, the tan δ values drop to 0.065, 0.065, and 0.046, providing quantitative evidence that the system transitions to solid-like behavior at 1.75 wt %.

Foam Thixotropy

Viscoelastic tests showed that samples with high NP concentrations (N-4# to N-6#) are interpreted as having formed strong networks and exhibited quantifiable thixotropic recovery. A three-step oscillatory shear test was used to characterize this behavior by analyzing the reference, high-oscillation, and regeneration intervals. In the reference interval (low strain), G′ and G″ remains constant, indicating an intact network. In the high-oscillation interval, the strain exceeds the LVR limit, and G′ and G‴ drop sharply with G‴ > G′. This indicates that the network is disrupted, and the foam is rearranged into a viscous-dominated flow state. In the regeneration interval, after returning to low strain, G′ and G″ increase rapidly but do not return to their initial values, indicating negative thixotropy.

Figure a depicts the G′ and G″ variations for three interval tests in the oscillation mode. For all samples, the recovery rate of G′ was below 50%, showing that the NP network can only be partially rebuilt after disruption. Additionally, higher NP concentrations lead to denser and more complex networks, requiring longer rearrangement times and thus prolonging the thixotropic recovery time.

Figure b depicts the loss factor across the three intervals. The loss factor quantifies the ratio of viscous dissipation to elastic storage. In the reference and regeneration intervals, the loss factor is less than 1, indicating elastic dominance. In the high-oscillation interval, it switches to viscous dominance. These results show that NP-stabilized viscoelastic foams possess good thixotropy when strain is removed, although the degree of recovery is limited. This phenomenon is likely related to the brittleness of the dense network. This partial structural recovery is crucial in firefighting; after being sheared during deployment, the foam maintains the integrity of the extinguishing barrier, which is essential for blocking oxygen and inhibiting fuel vapor volatilization.

Foam Coarsening

The steady shear, oscillatory amplitude sweep, and three-interval thixotropy tests presented above collectively support the three-stage structural evolution framework proposed for the 648/BS-12/NPs system with increasing NP concentration: adsorption mode transition at N-3# (1.5 wt %), rheological percolation at N-4# (1.75 wt %), and stable jamming at N-5# (2.0 wt %). To further examine whether these rheological interpretations are consistent with behavior at the bubble scale, the three samples representing the characteristic stages were selected for optical microscopy observation, with the NP-free sample N-0# as a reference. The coarsening process of the four foams was tracked over 20 min.

The coarsening process of foams with different NP concentrations is shown in Figure a. From the initial moment to 20 min, the bubbles in all foams gradually grow larger and decrease in number. N-0# bubbles change from spherical shape to polyhedral structure with time, with extremely thin liquid films at 20 min. N-3# retains more medium-sized bubbles, but the bubble walls have thinned considerably. N-4# and N-5# retain a large number of small bubbles and thicker liquid films, and the bubble structure of N-5# at 20 min is almost identical to that at 10 min, while N-4# still shows visible bubble enlargement during the same interval. These differences arise from the coupled effects of bubble size, interfacial network strength, and gas transport across liquid films and Plateau borders. In addition, a large number of shadows are observed at the boundary between liquid films and Plateau borders in the coarsening images, which correspond to the aggregation of nanoparticles and surfactants.

The variation of bubble size distribution of different foams is shown in Figure b. During coarsening, the bubble size distribution changes from concentrated to dispersed, and the size range and average value increase continuously. At the same time, the bubbles with NPs are smaller in average diameter and more concentrated in distribution compared with bubbles without NPs. As the NP concentration further increases, the average bubble radius becomes smaller and the distribution becomes more concentrated, indicating that the addition of NPs delays foam coarsening, and this inhibitory effect strengthens monotonically with NP concentration. This is because NPs and surfactants form a stable network structure at the boundary of liquid films and Plateau borders, enhancing the viscoelasticity of the continuous phase and restricting gas transport between bubbles, thereby slowing down the coarsening process of foam and the fundamental change of foam structure. The progressive narrowing of the bubble size distribution with increasing NP concentration further reflects the three-stage structural evolution of the system, in which the network gradually develops from discrete aggregates to a percolating framework and ultimately to a densely jammed state.

The area increase of N-5# foam is the smallest, indicating that it has the best stability, N-4# foam shows an intermediate trend, while the area growth of N-3# foam is the fastest among the NP-stabilized samples and approaches that of N-0# at late times. Obviously, the foam coarsening was decelerated by the presence of NPs, and the deceleration effect strengthens with NP concentration. For N-3#, the adsorption mode transition between surfactants and NPs produces hybrid aggregates that thicken the liquid films, but the aggregates remain discrete and cannot block long-range gas transport between bubbles; the bubble distribution at 20 min therefore approaches that of N-0#. For N-4#, the aggregates connect into a spanning network distributed along the boundaries of liquid films and Plateau borders, which restricts gas diffusion between bubbles and shifts the bubble distribution to noticeably smaller sizes. However, this network has just reached the proposed percolation threshold and is not yet dense enough to fully seal the gas channels, so the bubbles continue to coarsen at a reduced rate. For N-5#, the network is interpreted as reaching the densely jammed state proposed on the basis of the rheological measurements; the gas-transport pathways through liquid films and Plateau borders are inferred to be effectively closed by the rigid NP-surfactant framework, and the bubble structure becomes stable at late times. The divergent late-stage coarsening behavior between N-4# and N-5# therefore provides independent kinetic evidence at the bubble scale for the percolation-to-jamming transition that takes place between 1.75 and 2.0 wt %, in consistent with the rheological results.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the molecular interactions, foam stability, and rheological behavior of silicone/hydrocarbon surfactant blends in the presence of silica NPs. The NP concentration significantly regulates the microscopic structural evolution and macroscopic properties of these fluorine-free systems. The main findings are as follows:

The introduction of NPs reduces the conductivity and foaming capacity of the mixed dispersions but induces a significant increase in apparent viscosity and shear-thinning behavior. Rheological analysis supports a stepwise structural evolution with increasing NP concentration. Three characteristic concentration thresholds were identified, interpreted as corresponding to the adsorption mode transition, the construction of a rheological percolation network, and the establishment of a proposed jammed state. At low concentrations, the system behavior is governed by discrete interactions between NPs and micelles, while at the proposed jamming threshold, the experimental evidence is consistent with the formation of a continuous dense framework.

The improvement in foam stability is highly correlated with this proposed structural evolution. In the proposed jammed state, the dense particle network is inferred to reside at the liquid films and Plateau borders, forming an effective barrier against drainage and coarsening. The addition of NPs greatly enhances the viscoelasticity and thixotropy of the foams, with this strengthening effect increasing monotonically with concentration. Notably, in the high-concentration proposed jammed regime, the foam exhibits excellent structural recovery and shear resistance. This stabilization strategy, consistent with a particle jamming mechanism, suggests that the performance of silicone foams can be effectively tuned by regulating NP phase behavior, providing theoretical support for the design of new fluorine-free firefighting agents.
