Commentary on Comstock et al.: Here we go again—Kratom and the failure to learn from the opioid crisis
Authors: Andrew Kolodny
Published: 2026

Public health crises often begin with familiar patterns that are easy to overlook. The early years of the opioid crisis offered multiple warning signs, yet they were dismissed or misunderstood. Similar patterns are now emerging with kratom.

The North American opioid crisis did not begin with illicitly synthesized fentanyl. As opioid prescribing accelerated in the 1990s and 2000s—driven largely by aggressive and misleading marketing—patients were overexposed through medical treatment, and excess medications circulated in households and communities. The result was a sharp rise in opioid use disorder (OUD) [1].

The epidemic of OUD did not unfold evenly across the country. In states where oversight was weaker, opioid use increased more rapidly, and the resulting harms were greater [2]. The lesson was straightforward, when opioid availability increases, opioid use disorder increases. This lesson is at risk of being overlooked.

As kratom use rises in the United States (US), severe adverse public health effects are rising in parallel. Kratom is marketed as a safe, non‐addictive herbal supplement, yet its pharmacology does not support these claims. In 2018, the US Food and Drug Administration concluded that the principal compounds in kratom products—mitragynine and 7‐hydroxymitragynine—are opioids and warned the public not to use kratom, citing serious risks, including addiction, seizures, liver toxicity, cardiac arrest and death [3].

The policy landscape for kratom varies substantially across states. Some states have enacted bans; others allow unrestricted sales; and a third group has adopted the Kratom Consumer Protection Act (KCPA), a framework developed and promoted by the kratom industry that sets age limits and labeling requirements while preserving broad access [4]. This variation creates a natural experiment to examine the relationship between availability and harm.

Comstock and colleagues [5] offer new evidence on these policy approaches. They found that states adopting KCPAs did not differ from unregulated states in rates of kratom‐related severe health outcomes, whereas states that banned kratom had substantially lower rates of adverse outcomes [5]. Another recent analysis examining the association between state kratom policy status and kratom overdose deaths found a similar pattern [6].

Since 2019, several states have adopted the KCPA, including jurisdictions that had previously banned kratom. Evidence from recent analyses suggests that states shifting from unregulated kratom to the KCPA will not see a public health benefit [5, 6]. Additionally, states that shift from a ban on kratom to the KCPA will likely see an increase in serious adverse events, including deaths.

Recently, the kratom industry has sought to attribute rising harms to semi‐synthetic products with high concentrations of 7‐hydroxymitragynine (7‐OH) and other potent alkaloids, suggesting that adverse public health outcomes are limited to these highly potent, semi‐synthetic kratom products [7]. This framing is not supported by the evidence. Serious kratom‐related harms were increasing long before these products became available in 2023. Policies targeting 7‐OH alone are, therefore, unlikely to address the underlying problem.

The experience with prescription opioids underscores the consequences of failing to act on early warning signs. With kratom, those signs are already present. As an opioid product, kratom should not be widely available for over‐the‐counter sale. Lawmakers should follow the evidence and apply the hard‐earned lessons of the opioid crisis before these harms become more entrenched.
