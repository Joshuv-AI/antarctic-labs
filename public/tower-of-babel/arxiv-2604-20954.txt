SubTr pica


Abstract
We present SubTropica , a Mathematica package that performs symbolic integration of multi-polylogarithmic integrals using recent advances in tropical geometry. It focuses on the class of linearly-reducible Euler integrals , such as Feynman integrals, and expands them using a tropical subtraction scheme. The engine behind it is HyperIntica , a native Mathematica package for hyperlogarithm integration that can be used independently. This paper documents both packages and illustrates their usage on examples from across different physics applications. Additionally, we introduce an AI-driven library of Feynman integrals, which catalogs diagrams discussed in the literature and serves as a database for computed results. Its online version is available at subtropi.ca and features a graphical user interface for diagram input and retrieval of records.

Introduction

A recurring bottleneck in perturbative calculations across high-energy physics is the evaluation of multi-dimensional integrals. Examples include Feynman integrals, phase-space integrals, energy and cosmological correlators, and so on. They belong to a common class known as
Euler integrals
:

\displaystyle I(\varepsilon,{\bf s})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n}\prod_{i}P_{i}(x,{\bf s})^{a_{i}\varepsilon+b_{i}}\,,

(1.1)

where
P_{i}(x,{\bf s})
are polynomials in the integration variables
x
with coefficients collectively denoted by
{\bf s}
, the exponents
a_{i},b_{i}
are fixed constants, and
\varepsilon>0
is a small real parameter (typically the dimensional regulator
\varepsilon=(4-\mathrm{D})/2
in the Feynman integral context).

\displaystyle I(\varepsilon,{\bf s})=\sum_{i=-N}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{i}\,I_{i}({\bf s})\,,

(1.2)

in terms of

. The central difficulty is that, often, the integrand of (

While not all Euler integrals satisfy this condition, a large and practically important class does, including, e.g., many multi-loop Feynman integrals relevant for phenomenology.

Feynman integral conventions.

Throughout this paper and the accompanying package, we adopt the following normalization convention for Feynman integrals:

I_{\vec{\nu}}(\mathbf{s})\equiv\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon L\gamma_{\text{E}}}}{(i\pi^{\mathrm{D}/2})^{L}}\int\frac{\mathcal{N}\,\mathrm{d}^{\mathrm{D}}\ell_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{\mathrm{D}}\ell_{2}\cdots\mathrm{d}^{\mathrm{D}}\ell_{L}}{(q_{1}^{2}-m_{1}^{2})^{\nu_{1}}(q_{2}^{2}-m_{2}^{2})^{\nu_{2}}\cdots(q_{N}^{2}-m_{N}^{2})^{\nu_{N}}}\,,

(1.3)

where
\{\ell_{a}\}_{a=1}^{L}
represents the set of the
L
independent loop momenta, and
q_{e}
and
m_{e}
are the momenta and masses associated with the internal edges. The exponents
\nu_{i}
are the integer powers of the respective propagators or irreducible scalar products, while
\mathcal{N}
is a numerator polynomial in the loop momenta. The vector
\mathbf{s}
collectively refers to the kinematic variables, such as masses and Mandelstam invariants. The quantity
\gamma_{\text{E}}
is a shorthand for the Euler constant
-\Gamma^{\prime}(1)\approx 0.577
. The Feynman
i\epsilon
prescription is left implicit. We use mostly-minus
(+-\ldots-)
conventions.

The resulting Schwinger-parameter representation is

I_{\vec{\nu}}(\mathbf{s})=\Gamma(\omega)\left(\prod_{e=1}^{E}\frac{(-1)^{\nu_{e}}}{\Gamma(\nu_{e})}\right)\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{E}x}{\text{GL(1)}}\prod_{e=1}^{E}x_{e}^{\nu_{e}-1}\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{s})}{\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{D}/2-\omega}[-\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{s})]^{\omega}}\,,

(1.4)

where
E
denotes the number of internal edges (those with
\nu_{e}>0
),
x_{e}
denotes the Schwinger parameter associated with every internal edge
e
,
and

\omega\equiv\sum_{e=1}^{E}\nu_{e}-\frac{L\mathrm{D}}{2}\,,

(1.5)

is the superficial degree of divergence, see, e.g.,
[
47
, App. A]
. The factors
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{F}
are the first and second Symanzik polynomials respectively, while
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}
is the numerator polynomial. Modding out by the gauge redundancy
\text{GL}(1)
can be done by fixing one of the Schwinger parameters, say
x_{E}=1
.

Measure conventions.

In the text, we write
x_{i}
with the standard measure
\prod_{i}\mathrm{d}x_{i}
(as in (
1.4
)), and
\alpha_{i}
with the logarithmic measure
\prod_{i}\mathrm{d}\log\alpha_{i}
(as in (
3.1
)). The variables are the same (
x_{i}=\alpha_{i}
) and the different labels signal which measure is in play. The
\mathrm{d}\log
form is natural when tropicalizing integrands. The
\mathrm{d}x
form is standard in the Feynman-integral literature and is used by the integrator
HyperIntica
. The two measures simply differ by the Jacobian
\prod_{i}x_{i}
.

3
Theory and algorithms

We begin by defining Euler integrals and their
\varepsilon
-expansion, then introduce the Newton polytope machinery that governs the singularity structure. The Nilsson–Passare continuation and tropical subtraction algorithms convert a divergent Euler integral into a sum of locally finite terms, which are then integrated one variable at a time using the hyperlogarithm calculus implemented in
HyperIntica
(and
HyperInt

[
71
]
). We close this section with a discussion of linear reducibility, the key condition that ensures that this sequential integration terminates in closed form.

3.1
An invitation to Euler integrals

Mathematically, an Euler integral can be defined as the Mellin transform of a rational function,

\displaystyle I({\bf s},c)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geqslant 0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}\dots\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{n}}{\alpha_{n}}\times\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}P_{j}(\alpha,{\bf s})^{c_{j}}\right)\,,

(3.1)

where
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
and
P_{j}
are all polynomials in the integration variables
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
with coefficients collectively denoted
{\bf s}
.

An Euler integral is a meromorphic function of the exponent variables
c

[
80
]
. On the other hand, the dependence on the coefficient variables
{\bf s}
is much more complicated, involving sophisticated generalizations of the logarithm, which we discuss later in this section (see Sec.
3.3
).

To streamline the discussion in this section, we will restrict ourselves to the case where the exponent variables depend on a common real parameter
\varepsilon
as
2
2

2

In the examples section below (see Sec.
4.3.2
), we will encounter integrals for which more than one such parameters appear, and show how to deal with them using
SubTropica
.

\displaystyle c_{j}=c_{j}(\varepsilon)=a_{j}+b_{j}\varepsilon\,,

(3.2)

where
a_{j},b_{j}
are fixed complex numbers.
In quantum field theory, the parameter
\varepsilon
is often called the
dimensional regulator
. While the subtraction still applies in the case of general
a_{j}
, the integration discussed below needs integer
a_{j}
.

With this parametrization, the Euler integral becomes a function
I({\bf s},\varepsilon)
of finitely many coefficient variables
{\bf s}
and a single real parameter
\varepsilon
. It turns out that
I({\bf s},\varepsilon)
extends to a meromorphic function of
\varepsilon
. We have an expansion around
\varepsilon=0
,

\displaystyle I({\bf s},\varepsilon)=\sum_{i=-N}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{i}I^{(i)}({\bf s})\,.

(3.3)

In this package, we provide an algorithmic and practical way to find an expansion for the coefficient functions
I^{(i)}({\bf s})
in terms of convergent integrals that can be evaluated with the algorithm implemented in the
Maple
program
HyperInt

[
71
]
and the new
Mathematica
package
HyperIntica
shipped with
SubTropica
.

The strategy we follow is based on the
tropical subtraction formula
presented in
[
80
, Sec. 6]
reviewed below (see also
[
54
,
22
]
). The idea is to add and subtract “counter-terms” from the original integrand, according to a combinatorial pattern that reflects the boundary structure of a polytope attached to the integral. Once the dust settles, we are left with an expansion of the Euler integral,

\displaystyle I({\bf s},\varepsilon)=\sum_{i}\phi_{i}(\varepsilon)I_{i}({\bf s},\varepsilon)\,,

(3.4)

as a combination of
locally finite
integrals
I_{i}({\bf s},\varepsilon)
.
By definition, these are integrals whose
\varepsilon
-expansion can be performed directly at the integrand level, providing the desired integral representation for the coefficients
I^{(i)}({\bf s})
.

The tropical subtraction formula is valid only for integrals that satisfy a certain
geometric property
. Luckily, any integral can be written as a combination of such, using the
Nilsson–Passare
analytical continuation
[
70
]
, or tropical continuation for brevity. In fact, tropical continuation can be used to expand an integral directly in locally finite ones. Therefore it provides an alternative to tropical subtractions
[
73
]
. However this usually results in large complicated expressions, which limits the usefulness of this approach.
Nevertheless, even when expanding a divergent integral in locally finite ones is prohibitive, limiting the tropical continuation to expand in integrals that merely satisfy the geometric property, rather than being locally finite, is often manageable.

In summary, chaining tropical continuation and tropical subtraction gives an efficient algorithm to expand an arbitrary Euler integral in terms of locally finite ones. This is the core functionality of
SubTropica
.

A comment on positivity.

The treatment of Euler integrals simplifies drastically if each polynomial appearing in the integrand is sign-coherent, that is if its coefficients all have the same sign.
3
3

3

This requirement can, in fact, be relaxed to allow polynomials that are
completely non-vanishing

[
18
]
.
In this case the Euler integrand does not vanish in the interior of the domain of integration.
A crucial consequence is that the singularities of the Euler integral (poles in
\varepsilon
) arise only from the
boundary
of the integration domain.
This is where tropical geometry enters the game: the
Newton polytope
of the integrand provides the only systematic way to detect these singular corners.

In the rest of this section, we will discuss two methods to expand an Euler integral in terms of locally finite ones. Both algorithms are rigorously valid only in the case of sign-coherent polynomials. If this property is not satisfied, the Euler integral may have additional poles in
\varepsilon
that are
not
captured by these methods. This is a famous issue that plagues all direct-integration techniques and their implementation in public software such as
asy

[
58
]
,
FIESTA

[
86
]
or
pySecDec

[
49
]
.

Currently, the only safe way to deal with this problem (and detect otherwise “hidden” singularities) is to perform a careful Landau analysis: one has to enumerate the irreducible components of the Landau variety which have positive solutions. Although this can be done algorithmically with publicly available software, such as
Macaulay2

[
43
]
and
Bertini

[
12
]
, it is computationally much more expensive than the tropical analysis discussed here. Therefore, this is to this day no generic practical solution (see, e.g.,
[
48
,
36
]
for related discussions).

Figure 2
:
The crown diagram features a non-sign-definite Symanzik polynomial.

In physical applications, this issue arises only in certain advanced calculations involving highly non-planar massless diagrams, such as the crown diagram in Fig.
2
, which is connected to the problem of “Glauber modes” (singularities that do not fall into the standard soft-collinear classification)
[
36
]
. The current version of
SubTropica
does not handle these divergences and a systematic treatment is left to future work.

3.2
Tropical algorithms

In this section, we describe how to use rudiments from tropical geometry to detect and calculate divergences of Euler integrals.

3.2.1
Divergences

The first step is to understand how divergences originate.

A warming-up example.

Let us consider a simple toy example,

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}\ \mathrm{d}\alpha_{2}\ \alpha_{1}^{1+\varepsilon}\alpha_{2}^{\varepsilon}\left(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\right)^{-(2+\varepsilon)}\,.

(3.5)

The singular behavior of the integrand around the origin suggests that the integral may develop a divergence there.
In order to expose it, we perform the rescaling

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\to\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}\,.

(3.6)

The integral transforms to

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}\ \mathrm{d}\alpha_{2}\ \alpha_{1}^{-1+\varepsilon}\alpha_{2}^{\varepsilon}\left(1+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\right)^{-(2+\varepsilon)}\,.

(3.7)

Note how the singular behavior of the integrand is completely captured by the
monomial

\alpha_{1}^{-1+\varepsilon}
: the polynomials resulting from the rescaling are non-vanishing at the origin.
This allows to neatly extract a pole in
\varepsilon
.
Integrating around
\alpha_{1}<1
(which corresponds to a small neighborhood of the origin in the original coordinates) gives

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}\ \mathrm{d}\alpha_{2}\ \alpha_{1}^{-1+\varepsilon}\alpha_{2}^{\varepsilon}\left(1+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\right)^{-(2+\varepsilon)}=\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{2}\left(\alpha_{1}^{-1+\varepsilon}\alpha_{2}^{\varepsilon}(1+\alpha_{2})^{-(2+\varepsilon)}\right)\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{1})\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{2}^{\varepsilon}(1+\alpha_{2})^{-(2+\varepsilon)}+\dots\end{split}

(3.8)

The crucial step was to consider the rescaling (
3.6
), whose origin is subtler than it seems. For example, consider the similar rescaling
\alpha_{2}\to\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
. As
\alpha_{1}\to 0
, it does the same job as (
3.6
) of “blowing-up” the origin, but now we have

\displaystyle A\to\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{2}\ \alpha_{1}^{\varepsilon}\,\alpha_{2}^{\varepsilon}\,\left(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\right)^{-(2+\varepsilon)}\,,

(3.9)

so that integrating around
\alpha_{1}\sim 0
does not produce a pole in
\varepsilon
.

Tropicalization.

Let us now formulate the problem in more general terms. Consider an Euler integral

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geqslant 0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}\dots\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{n}}{\alpha_{n}}\ \mathcal{I}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\,,

(3.10)

and an arbitrary rescaling

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\to\alpha_{i}\lambda^{-\rho_{i}},\quad\rho=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}\,,

(3.11)

Note that we can always eliminate one of the original variables
\alpha_{i}
in favor of
\lambda
, and thus regard (
3.11
) as a change of coordinates.
With this understanding the limit
\lambda\to 0^{+}
probes a corner of the boundary of the integration space, where the integration variables scale as (
3.11
).
Clearing denominators and factoring polynomials, we can pull out an overall monomial in
\lambda
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\alpha_{i}\lambda^{-\rho_{i}})=\lambda^{-\mathrm{Trop\ \mathcal{I}(\rho)}}\mathcal{J}(\alpha,\lambda)\,.

(3.12)

We use (
3.12
) to define the
tropical integrand
,

\mathrm{Trop\ }\mathcal{I}
, a function that associates to the scaling vector
\rho
minus the exponent of this overall monomial.
Keeping in mind the overall logarithmic differential form, which is invariant under the transformation, we see that

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,\mathrm{Trop\ }\mathcal{I}(\rho)|_{\varepsilon=0}<0\quad\text{for all }\rho\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad A=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)\,.

(3.13)

Due to (
3.2
), the tropical integrand is always at most linear in
\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Trop\ }\mathcal{I}(\rho)=a+b\ \varepsilon\,,

(3.14)

depending on the value of
a
we say that
\rho
is either convergent (
\mathrm{Re}\,a<0
), logarithmically divergent (
\mathrm{Re}\,a=0
) or power divergent (
\mathrm{Re}\,a>0
).

As it stands, the criterion (
3.13
) is not particularly helpful, because it requires checking the sign of the tropical integrand over infinitely many vectors
\rho
.
Luckily, it turns out that one only needs to evaluate the tropical integrand on a finite set of “critical” vectors
[
7
]
.
These are the normals to the facets of a polytope canonically attached to the integrand
\mathcal{I}
.

Newton Polytopes.

The Newton polytope of a polynomial is the convex hull of the exponents of its monomials
[
93
]
. In other words, if

\displaystyle P=\sum_{{\bf m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}s_{\bf m}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{m_{i}}\,,

(3.15)

then the Newton polytope
\mathrm{Newt\ }P
is the convex hull

\displaystyle\mathrm{Newt\ }P\equiv\mathrm{Conv}\{{\bf m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mid s_{\bf m}\neq 0\}\,.

(3.16)

For an Euler integrand,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\alpha)=\prod_{j}P_{j}(\alpha)^{a_{j}+b_{j}\varepsilon}\,,

(3.17)

we define its Newton polytope to be
\mathrm{Newt\ }\mathcal{I}\equiv\mathrm{Newt\ }\prod_{j}P_{j}
.

The convex hull description (
3.16
) is known as the
vertex presentation
of the polytope. Every polytope admits a dual description, or
facet presentation
, as an intersection of half-spaces,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Newt\ }P=\bigcap_{j}\;\{\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\;|\;d_{j}-\rho_{j}\cdot\alpha\geqslant 0\}.

(3.18)

for some constants
d_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}
and vectors
\rho_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
.

Suppose that the locus where one of inequalities of (
3.18
) is saturated is a co-dimension one boundary of
\mathrm{Newt\ }P
, also called a
facet
.
Then the corresponding vector
\rho_{j}
can be interpreted geometrically as the outer normal to the facet.

The collection of all the normals to the facets is a finite set of “critical” vectors that control the singularities of the integral.
If these vectors are convergent (using the terminology introduced below (
3.13
)), then the Euler integral does not have poles in
\varepsilon
.

In the case of Newton polytopes, the vertex description (
3.16
) is immediately available from the knowledge of the polynomial (
3.15
).
The facet description, however, requires some work. The problem involves only linear algebra, but it can be computationally very demanding, depending on the underlying combinatorics. Fortunately, efficient and sophisticated solutions to this problem are implemented in various software. In
SubTropica
we outsource this computational task to
polymake

[
38
,
8
]
.

Example.

Let us illustrate the notions introduced so far by revisiting the example (
3.5
).
The Euler integrand is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\alpha_{1}^{2+\varepsilon}\alpha_{2}^{1+\varepsilon}\left(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\right)^{-(2+\varepsilon)}\,,

(3.19)

it tropicalizes to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Trop\ }\mathcal{I}=(2+\varepsilon)\alpha_{1}+(1+\varepsilon)\alpha_{2}-(2+\varepsilon)\max(2\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\,,

(3.20)

Note the slight abuse of notation, where we keep using the same variables,
\alpha_{i}
, as arguments of both
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathrm{Trop\ }\mathcal{I}
.

\alpha_{1}
\alpha_{2}

Up to translation, the Newton polytope

is the triangle of Fig.

\rho_{1}=(-1,-2)
,
\rho_{2}=(1,1)
, and
\rho_{3}=(0,1)

\rho_{1}

\mathrm{Trop\ }\mathcal{I}(\rho_{1})=\varepsilon

The rescaling (

We have now understood how Newton polytopes encode the singularities of an Euler integral. The poles in the dimensional regulator

3.2.2
Locally finite expansion

We now turn to the expansion of a divergent Euler integral in terms of locally finite integrals, Eq. (
3.4
).
As we will see shortly, the Newton polytope of the Euler integral once again plays a central role: it encodes in its boundary structure the data necessary to construct the expansion.

Tropical continuation.

Consider an Euler integral
I(\varepsilon)
and suppose that
\rho
is a divergent vector.
Consider the shifted Euler integral

\displaystyle I(\lambda;\varepsilon)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geqslant 0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}\dots\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{n}}{\alpha_{n}}\,\mathcal{I}(\alpha_{i}\lambda^{-\rho_{i}};\varepsilon)\,,

(3.22)

which reduces to the original integral
I(\varepsilon)
for
\lambda=1
.
In fact,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}I(\lambda;\varepsilon)=0
, because the dependence on
\lambda
can be eliminated at the integrand level by the rescaling
\alpha\to\alpha\lambda^{\rho}
which leaves invariant both the measure and the integration domain.
4
4

4

In this context,
\lambda
should be thought of as a parameter, not an integration variable.

We have

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geqslant 0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}\dots\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{n}}{\alpha_{n}}\left.\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\mathcal{I}(\alpha_{i}\lambda^{-\rho_{i}},\varepsilon)\right)\right|_{\lambda=1}=-I(\varepsilon)\mathrm{Trop\ \mathcal{I}}(\rho)+\sum_{i}I_{i}(\varepsilon)\,,

or equivalently,

\displaystyle I(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Trop\ \mathcal{I}}(\rho)}\left(\sum_{i}I_{i}(\varepsilon)\right)\,.

(3.23)

Here,
I_{i}(\varepsilon)
are new Euler integrals that arise by the action of
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}
on
\mathcal{J}_{\rho}(\alpha,\lambda)
in (
3.12
).
It turns out that the integrands
\mathcal{I}_{i}
for the integrals
I_{i}
satisfy
\mathrm{Trop\ }\mathcal{I}_{i}\leqslant\mathrm{Trop\ }\mathcal{I}
, with the inequality being strict when both sides are evaluated on the ray
\rho
.
This can be seen by considering how the facet
\rho
of the Newton polytope of
\mathcal{I}(\alpha\lambda^{-\rho};\varepsilon)
is “shaved” by the action of
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}
.

By iterating this procedure sufficiently many times, we can express
I(\varepsilon)
as a linear combination of locally finite Euler integrals multiplied by explicit poles in
\varepsilon
.
This is the tropical continuation due to Nilsson-Passare
[
70
,
14
,
82
]
.
While it provides a systematic approach to compute the
\varepsilon
-expansion of an Euler integral, tropical continuation is computationally very expensive. This can be traced to two main bottlenecks:

1.

Iterating derivatives produces an exponential number of integrals.

2.

The pole part
I(\varepsilon)
is presented in terms of integrals with the same dimension as
I(\varepsilon)
.

We will next describe an alternative strategy which overcomes these limitations.
It uses more elaborate information about the boundary structure of the Newton polytope: not only which are the facets responsible for divergences, but also how they intersect in lower dimensional faces of the polytope.

Geometric property.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a polytope, and suppose that a subset of its facets,
\{\mathcal{F}_{i},\ i=1,\dots,n\}
, share a common vertex. Then we say that these are
compatible
facets.

If divergent facets are compatible, they can conspire to produce higher order poles.
In this sense, the Newton polytope leaves an imprint of itself in the pole structure of an Euler integral.
To capture this pattern we construct the set of all collections of compatible, divergent (normals to the) facets,

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt\Sigma^{\rm div}=\{\sigma|\sigma=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m}),\ \rho_{i}\text{ are divergent, compatible normals}\}\cup\{\varnothing\}.

(3.24)

Note that we conventionally include the empty set, to streamline formulae down the line.

Consider now an Euler integrand
\mathcal{I}
with at most logarithmic divergences.
We say that
\mathcal{I}
satisfies the
geometric property
if for every divergent vector
\rho
we are able to find a vector
w_{\rho}
such that
w_{\rho}\cdot\rho^{\prime}=-\delta_{\rho,\rho^{\prime}}
, for all divergents normals
\rho^{\prime}
that are compatible with
\rho
.

The geometric property has an important consequence.
The set
\Sigma^{\rm div}
is an abstract simplicial complex. Furthermore, if

\sigma=(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m})\in\Sigma^{\rm div}
, then the normals
\rho_{i}
are linearly independent and the corresponding facets meet in a face of codimension
m
,

In general, Euler integrals do not satisfy the geometric property nor have only logarithmic singularities.
However, we can always tropically continue along all vectors
\rho
which are either power-divergent or do not admit an accompanying vector
w_{\rho}
. By iterating the procedure sufficiently many times, one is eventually left with logarithmically divergent integrals that satisfy the geometric property.

Our interest in integrals that satisfy the geometric property is due to the existence of an algorithm that allows to expand them in terms of locally finite integrals, and which is substantially more efficient than tropical continuation. This will be discussed next.

Tropical subtraction.

The main idea is to add and subtract suitable “counter-terms”
\mathcal{I}^{\rm ct}
from a divergent Euler integrand. The counter-terms must be chosen so that they cancel the divergences of
\mathcal{I}
, while being simple enough that they can be integrated as many times as the order of the pole they cancel.
In other words, the goal is to write

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\left[\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{I}^{\rm ct}\right]+\mathcal{I}^{\rm ct}\,,

(3.25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\rm ren}\equiv\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{I}^{\rm ct}\,,

(3.26)

is locally finite, and the integral

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geqslant 0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}\dots\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha_{n}}{\alpha_{n}}\,\mathcal{I}^{\rm ct}\,,

(3.27)

can be partially integrated, dropping one dimension for each pole in
\varepsilon
exposed.

If
\mathcal{I}
is an integrand that satisfies the geometric property, and with at most logarithmic divergences, then we can follow

to construct such counter-terms.
The combinatorics of the subtraction formula (
3.25

\Sigma^{\rm div}
from (

It reflects the intersection pattern of the facets of
\mathrm{Newt\ }\mathcal{I}

\rho

The counter-terms are the product of two factors.

The first is the
restriction

initial form
in the context of tropical and toric geometry
[
30

).
It is the integrand obtained by keeping from each polynomial

\mathrm{Newt\ }P
.
The fact that the integrand and its restriction have similar Newton polytopes, and thus matching singularities, is what makes them natural candidates in an ansatz for the counter-terms.

Unfortunately, restrictions alone cannot be used as counter-terms. The reason is that they introduce singularities in addition to the ones that they cancel.
This can be easily seen already in the simplest case of one-dimensional integrals. Their Newton polytopes are necessarily always segments. Integrands with a segment and its vertices for Newton polytopes cannot be combined in a locally finite combination.

In order to address this issue, we introduce the second factor of the counter-term.
To any vector
\rho
, we associate the function

\displaystyle v_{\rho}=\frac{1}{1+\prod_{i}\alpha_{i}^{w_{i}}}\,.

(3.28)

To an element
(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m})\in\Sigma^{\rm div}
we associate the function

\displaystyle v_{(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m})}=\prod_{i}v_{\rho_{i}}^{1-\mathrm{Trop\ }\mathcal{I}(\rho_{i})}\,.

(3.29)

We can finally construct the counter-terms. For any
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\rm div}
we define the counter-term

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}=v_{\sigma}\times\mathcal{I}|_{\sigma}\,,

(3.30)

where

denotes the restriction on the intersection of the compatible facets with normals
\rho_{1,\dots,m}
.
We also set
\mathcal{I}_{\varnothing}=\mathcal{I}
.

For any counter-term integrand
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}\to\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{ren}}=\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathrm{\Sigma}^{\rm div}|\sigma\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}}(-1)^{|\sigma|-|\sigma^{\prime}|}\mathcal{I}_{\sigma^{\prime}}.

(3.31)

Summary of the main algorithm.

The algorithms described in this section provide a systematic way to obtain the expansion (
3.4

The procedure requires three steps.

1.

Compute the polytope

2.

Apply Nilsson–Passare tropical continuation on power divergent vectors, and on sufficiently many vectors that violate the geometric property.

3.

Compute the tropical subtraction formula for each of the resulting integrals.

Local finiteness ensures that we can expand the integrands in
\varepsilon

These can then be integrated with the algorithm by Brown and Panzer, which will be reviewed in Sec.

3.3
Integration of locally finite integrands

The purpose of this section is to briefly review the by-now well-understood strategy of
[
21
,
73
]
(first applied in
[
26
,
2
,
6
,
5
]
) for evaluating locally finite expanded Euler integrals obtained from the procedure discussed in Sec.
3.2
. We will further restrict ourselves to the cases for which these integrals are of the form

f_{n}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{n-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n}\,,

(3.39)

where
f_{n-1}
is assumed to be expressible as a product of rational functions and hyperlogarithms (defined below in (
3.40
) and discussed further in App.
B
). Other integration domains
[a,b]
can be accommodated through the change of variables
x\mapsto t=\frac{a+bx}{1+x}
.

In cases where the linear reducibility condition discussed later in Sec.
3.4
is satisfied, then the integral
f_{n}
can be evaluated by integrating one variable at a time. One efficient method to achieve this rests on two key structural properties of hyperlogarithms: their simple behavior under differentiation, which systematizes the construction of primitives/antiderivatives, and the fact that they form a shuffle algebra. The latter property allows one to reorganize products of hyperlogarithms into canonical combinations and, in particular, to “shuffle regularize” hyperlogarithms by isolating unambiguously the finite contributions of antiderivatives at (potentially) singular integration boundaries.

We summarize here how these elements combine to yield a complete integration procedure.

3.3.1
Hyperlogarithms and their derivatives

Hyperlogarithms
are defined as iterated integrals

\mathrm{H}(z;\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{w})\equiv\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\differential t_{1}}{t_{1}-\sigma_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\frac{\differential t_{2}}{t_{2}-\sigma_{2}}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{w-1}}\frac{\differential t_{w}}{t_{w}-\sigma_{w}}\,,

(3.40)

with the convention
\mathrm{H}(z;\varnothing)=1
. The sequence
\vec{\sigma}=[\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{w}]
is called a
word
, and the integer
w
is its (transcendental)
weight
.

The entire integration algorithm for the
f_{i}
’s follows from the action of the total differential. Differentiating with respect to the upper limit
z
yields

\frac{\differential}{\differential z}\mathrm{H}(z;\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{w})=\frac{1}{z-\sigma_{1}}\,\mathrm{H}(z;\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{w})\,.

(3.41)

This relation encodes the fundamental point: differentiation peels off the first letter
\sigma_{1}
, while integration prepends a new letter. When the letters
\sigma_{i}
themselves depend on a parameter, the total differential acquires additional contributions from the variation of each letter (see Eq. (3.3.32) in
[
74
]
for the complete formula). However, the core principle remains unchanged: the differential structure of hyperlogarithms is entirely determined by the rational one-forms
\mathrm{d}\log(z - \sigma)
.

3.3.2
The integration strategy

Suppose now that we wish to integrate the variable
x_{n}
from
0
to
\infty
, given an integrand
f_{n-1}
that has already been expressed in terms of hyperlogarithms. The strategy proceeds in three steps.

In the first step, the integrand is written as a linear combination of terms

f_{n-1}=\sum_{\vec{\sigma},\tau,k}\frac{\mathrm{H}(x_{n};\vec{\sigma})}{(x_{n}-\tau)^{k}}\,\lambda_{\vec{\sigma},\tau,k}\,,

(3.42)

where the words
\vec{\sigma}
and poles
\tau
depend on the remaining variables but not on
x_{n}
, while
\lambda_{\vec{\sigma},\tau,k}
are integration constants (which can themselves involve hyperlogarithms, but in the other integration variables and parameters). This representation is achieved through a transformation that exploits the shuffle algebra and path-composition properties of iterated integrals (see, e.g.,
[
91
]
). These give rise to the
shuffle product
, which, for hyperlogarithms, states that

\mathrm{H}(z;u)\times\mathrm{H}(z;v)=\sum_{w\in u\shuffle v}\mathrm{H}(z;w)\,,

(3.43)

where
u\shuffle v
denotes the sum over all “interweavings” of the original sequence (i.e., all possible ways of merging the two ordered sequences of words
u
and
v
into a single sequence while preserving the internal order of
u
and
v
respectively; see e.g., (
B.4
)).
This identity is used repeatedly to separate the
x_{n}
-dependent part of each word from the integration constants.

In the second step, we construct an antiderivative
F
satisfying
\partial_{x_{n}}F=f_{n-1}
by “integrating back” the differential relation (
3.41
).

Step 2: Antiderivative construction.

We construct an antiderivative
F
satisfying
\partial_{x_{n}}F=f_{n-1}
by processing each term in the sum (
3.42
). For a given term, the rational (in
x_{n}
) coefficient
c(x_{n})\equiv\frac{\lambda_{\vec{\sigma},\tau,k}}{(x_{n}-\tau)^{k}}
is first decomposed via partial fractions:

c(x_{n})=p(x_{n})+\sum_{i,j}\frac{c_{ij}}{(x_{n}-a_{i})^{j}}\,,

(3.44)

where
p(x_{n})
is a polynomial in
x_{n}
, the
a_{i}
are the (possibly spurious, meaning it cancels in the sum) pole locations, which may depend on other variables.
Crucially
, the
c_{ij}
are constants in
x_{n}
. This last property is guaranteed by linear reducibility: all polynomial factors in the denominator decompose into linear terms
(x_{n}-a_{i})
, and the standard partial fraction theorem then yields constant numerators. We handle the polynomial and pole parts separately.

For the polynomial part, let
P(x_{n})\equiv\int_{0}^{x_{n}}p(t)\,\differential t
. Integration-by-parts using (
3.41
) gives the antiderivative

\int p(x_{n})\,\mathrm{H}(x_{n};\vec{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}x_{n}=P(x_{n})\,\mathrm{H}(x_{n};\vec{\sigma})-\int\frac{P(x_{n})}{x_{n}-\sigma_{1}}\,\mathrm{H}(x_{n};\sigma_{2},\ldots)\,\mathrm{d}x_{n}\,.

(3.45)

The first term contributes directly to the antiderivative; the correction term has a rational prefactor
P(x_{n})/(x_{n}-\sigma_{1})
which is decomposed again via partial fractions, continuing the recursion.
The recursion terminates when the weight reaches zero, and the hyperlogarithm in (
3.45
) becomes
\mathrm{H}(x_{n};\varnothing)=1
. Indeed, its derivative vanishes

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}\mathrm{H}(x_{n};\varnothing)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}(1)=0\,.

(3.46)

and, consequently, no further integration-by-parts corrections are generated and we are left with rational integrands. Assuming linear reducibility, partial fractions allows to decompose these leftover integrands according to (
3.44
),
which integrates directly since:

\diamond

polynomials integrate to polynomials,

\diamond

higher-order poles (
j>1
) integrate to rational functions, via

\displaystyle\int\frac{c}{(x_{n}-a)^{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{n}=\frac{c}{(1-j)(x_{n}-a)^{j-1}}\,,

(3.47)

\diamond

simple poles (
j=1
) integrate to logarithms, via

\displaystyle\int\frac{c}{x_{n}-a}\,\mathrm{d}x_{n}=c\,\mathrm{H}(x_{n};a)\,.

(3.48)

This is all we have to do for the polynomial contribution to (
3.42
) from (
3.44
).

Now we consider the pole part of (
3.44
). For simple poles (
j=1
), inverting (
3.41
) gives directly

\int\frac{c}{x_{n}-a}\,\mathrm{H}(x_{n};\vec{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}x_{n}=c\,\mathrm{H}(x_{n};a,\vec{\sigma})\,,

(3.49)

and it is clear that the pole location
a
just becomes a new letter prepended to the word. For higher-order poles (
j>1
), direct integration yields a rational function which generates an integration-by-parts correction on the right-hand side:

\begin{split}\int\frac{c}{(x_{n}-\tau)^{j}}&\,\mathrm{H}(x_{n};\vec{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}x_{n}=\frac{c}{(1-j)(x_{n}-\tau)^{j-1}}\,\mathrm{H}(x_{n};\vec{\sigma})\\
&\qquad-\int\frac{c}{(1-j)(x_{n}-\tau)^{j-1}}\,\frac{\mathrm{H}(x_{n};\sigma_{2},\ldots)}{x_{n}-\sigma_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{n}\,.\end{split}

(3.50)

This correction term, after yet another partial fraction, involves poles of order at most
j-1
times a hyperlogarithm of lower weight. The recursion thus terminates in the exact same fashion as it did for the polynomial part and the antiderivative
F
for the original integral is thus obtained in terms of hyperlogarithms.

In the third and final step, we evaluate the antiderivative we found at the integration endpoints. The definite integral is obtained as

f_{n}\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{n}=\lim_{x_{n}\to\infty}F(x_{n})-\lim_{x_{n}\to 0}F(x_{n})\,.

(3.51)

Both limits require careful treatment, as hyperlogarithms generically diverge when letters coincide with integration boundaries, which we discuss next.

3.3.3
Regularization at the endpoints

The prototypical divergence in hyperlogarithms arises from

\mathrm{H}(z;\underbracket{0,\ldots,0}_{k})=\frac{\log(z)^{k}}{k!}\,,

(3.52)

which diverges as
z\to 0
. Shuffle regularization extracts finite values by exploiting (
B.5
) to isolate divergent contributions. For instance, the identity

\mathrm{H}(z;a)\cdot\log(z)=\mathrm{H}(z;a,0)+\mathrm{H}(z;0,a)\,,

(3.53)

expresses the divergent integral
\mathrm{H}(z;a,0)
as the difference of
\mathrm{H}(z;a)\cdot\log(z)
and the convergent term
\mathrm{H}(z;0,a)
. Regularization then discards pure-logarithm divergences while preserving all algebraic relations.

In practice, three regularizations will arise naturally. The regularization
\mathrm{Reg}_{0}
handles trailing zeros in (
3.52
), which produce divergences as the argument approaches the lower integration bound.
The regularization
\mathrm{Reg}_{\sigma}
handles leading letters equal to the upper limit
\sigma
, which produce divergences as the argument approaches that limit. For instance,

\mathrm{H}(z;\sigma)=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\differential t}{t-\sigma}=-\log(\sigma- z)\to+\infty\quad\text{as }z\to\sigma\,.

(3.54)

Finally, for integrals from
0
to
\infty
, the regularization
\mathrm{Reg}_{\infty}
handles constant trailing of
-\lambda<0
. At large
t
, the kernel
\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t-(-\lambda)}\sim\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}
has a pole at infinity, so letters
-\lambda
accumulate logarithmic divergences from the innermost integrations analogously to trailing zeros at the origin:

\mathrm{H}(\infty;\underbracket{-\lambda,\ldots,-\lambda}_{k})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\differential t_{1}}{t_{1}+\lambda}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{k-1}}\frac{\differential t_{k}}{t_{k}+\lambda}\sim\frac{\log(z)^{k}}{k!}\Big|_{z\to\infty}\to+\infty\,.

(3.55)

We can thus define the
regulated periods
as the fully regularized value that combines both boundary contributions:

\mathtt{zeroInfPeriod}(w)\equiv\mathrm{Reg}_{\infty}\,\mathrm{Reg}_{0}\,\mathrm{H}(\infty;w)\,.

(3.56)

A crucial consistency requirement is that logarithmic divergences from the two boundaries must cancel for the integral to be well-defined.

A comment on analytic continuation.

When positive letters
\lambda>0
lie on the integration path
[0,\infty)
, the contour must be deformed around the corresponding singularities. The choice of deformation, i.e., passing above or below each pole, can be tracked by the “delta-symbol”
\delta[\sigma]=\pm 1
, representing the sign of
\mathrm{Im}(\sigma+i\varepsilon)
. Each contour deformation contributes terms proportional to
\pm i\pi
, which combine to produce the correct analytic continuation. See
[
73
]
for more details. As a practical matter, one can fix the correct sign by matching the analytic formula with numerical evaluation.

3.3.4
A worked example

To illustrate the full procedure, consider

f_{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}}{1-x_{1}+x_{1}x_{2}^{2}}\,,

(3.57)

integrated in the order
(x_{1},x_{2})
(the opposite order fails; see Sec.
3.4
). The Möbius map
x_{1}=x_{3}/(1+x_{3})
transforms the
x_{1}
-integral to
[0,\infty)
, giving
1-x_{1}=1/(1+x_{3})
,
x_{1}x_{2}^{2}=x_{3}x_{2}^{2}/(1+x_{3})
, and hence

f_{0}=\frac{1}{(1+x_{3})(1+x_{3}x_{2}^{2})}\,.

(3.58)

Partial fractions in
x_{3}
yield two simple poles:

\frac{1}{(1+x_{3})(1+x_{3}x_{2}^{2})}=\frac{1/(1-x_{2}^{2})}{x_{3}+1}+\frac{1/(x_{2}^{2}-1)}{x_{3}+1/x_{2}^{2}}\,.

(3.59)

Since the integrand is a pure rational function (
\mathrm{H}(x_{3};\varnothing)=1
, weight zero), the simple-pole rule (
3.49
) gives the antiderivative directly:

F(x_{3})=\frac{1}{1-x_{2}^{2}}\,\mathrm{H}(x_{3};-1)\;+\;\frac{1}{x_{2}^{2}-1}\,\mathrm{H}\!\left(x_{3};-\frac{1}{x_{2}^{2}}\right)\,.

(3.60)

We now evaluate at the boundaries. At
x_{3}=0
both hyperlogarithms vanish (no trailing zeros in the words). At
x_{3}\to\infty
, each
\mathrm{H}(x_{3};\sigma)
with
\sigma<0
grows as
\log x_{3}+\log|\sigma|
, but the leading
\log x_{3}
divergences cancel because the two coefficients sum to zero:
\frac{1}{1-x_{2}^{2}}+\frac{1}{x_{2}^{2}-1}=0
. The surviving finite parts give

f_{1}=\frac{\log(x_2^2)}{x_{2}^{2}-1}\,.

(3.61)

For the second variable we integrate
f_{1}
over
x_{2}\in[0,1]
. Writing
\log(x_2^2)=2\,\mathrm{H}(x_{2};0)
and decomposing
\frac{1}{x_{2}^{2}-1}=\frac{1}{2(x_{2}-1)}-\frac{1}{2(x_{2}+1)}
, the simple-pole rule gives the antiderivative

G(x_{2})=\mathrm{H}(x_{2};1,0)-\mathrm{H}(x_{2};-1,0)\,.

(3.62)

At
x_{2}=0
, the trailing zeros produce a
\log(x_2)
divergence. The shuffle identity
\mathrm{H}(x_{2};\sigma)\cdot\mathrm{H}(x_{2};0)=\mathrm{H}(x_{2};\sigma,0)+\mathrm{H}(x_{2};0,\sigma)
gives
\mathrm{H}(x_{2};\sigma,0)=\mathrm{H}(x_{2};\sigma)\cdot\log x_{2}-\mathrm{H}(x_{2};0,\sigma)
. Then the regularization prescription discussed above discards the
\log x_{2}
term, leaving
\mathrm{Reg}_{0}\,\mathrm{H}(x_{2};\sigma,0)=-\mathrm{H}(x_{2};0,\sigma)
for
\sigma=\pm 1
. Since
\mathrm{H}(x_{2};0,\sigma)=-\mathrm{Li}_{2}(x_{2}/\sigma)
and
\mathrm{Li}_{2}(0)=0
, we get
G(0)=0
.

At
x_{2}=1
, we apply
\mathrm{Reg}_{0}
(same trailing-zero identity as above) and then
\mathrm{Reg}_{1}
(which would strip leading letters equal to the upper limit
1
, but neither
[0,1]
nor
[0,-1]
starts with
1
, so
\mathrm{Reg}_{1}
acts trivially). Using
\mathrm{H}(1;0,\sigma)=-\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1/\sigma)
, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{1}\,\mathrm{Reg}_{0}\,\mathrm{H}(1;1,0)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{H}(1;0,1)=\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1)=\zeta(2)\,,

(3.63a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{1}\,\mathrm{Reg}_{0}\,\mathrm{H}(1;-1,0)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{H}(1;0,-1)=\mathrm{Li}_{2}(-1)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}\,.

(3.63b)

Combining these results, we obtain

f_{2}=G(1)-G(0)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\,,

(3.64)

which is correct. We discuss next what would have happened if the integration order was taken be
(x_{2},x_{1})
instead of
(x_{1},x_{2})
.

3.4
Integration order and linear reducibility

The choice of the integration order plays a central role in evaluating hyperlogarithms, as it drastically affects the complexity of intermediate steps, or even whether the procedure explained in Sec.
3.3
terminates at all. For these reasons, the choice of integration order deserves separate attention.

To see this concretely, return to the integral (
3.57
) from Sec.
3.3.4
. In the order
(x_{1},x_{2})
, the polynomial
1-x_{1}+x_{1}x_{2}^{2}
is linear in
x_{1}
. After integrating
x_{1}
, the resulting letter
x_{2}^{2}-1=(x_{2}-1)(x_{2}+1)
factors linearly in
x_{2}
, and the algorithm runs to completion, yielding
\pi^{2}/4
.

In the opposite order
(x_{2},x_{1})
, the polynomial
1-x_{1}+x_{1}x_{2}^{2}
is quadratic in
x_{2}
, and one would find the antiderivative

\frac{1}{1-x_{1}+x_{1}x_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{1}(1-x_{1})}}\arctan\left(\frac{\sqrt{x_{1}}\,x_{2}}{\sqrt{1-x_{1}}}\right)\right]\,.

(3.65)

The
\arctan
function cannot be expressed as a hyperlogarithm with rational arguments, since
\arctan(z)=\frac{i}{2}[\log(1 - i z)-\log(1 + i z)]
and
z=\sqrt{x_{1}}\,x_{2}/\sqrt{1-x_{1}}
involves square roots. The integral is not linearly reducible in this order in the
x_{1},x_{2}
variables.

3.4.1
Fubini reduction

The question of finding linearly-reducible (LR) orders amounts to checking if at all steps of the integration, the integrand polynomials are linear in the current integration variable. The key observation is that one does not need to perform full integration to answer this question. This is because for the purposes of finding linearly-reducible orders, we only care about the singularity structure of the integrand, not the form of the integrand itself.

To this effect, we employ the strategy used in

, which uses the Fubini reduction algorithm
[
23
,
73
]
to analyze singularities of integrals, see
[
29
, Sec. 2.2]
. In the first step, we use dynamical programming to find the set of singularities for every possible integration order. In the second step, we crawl the space of orders discarding those that include at least one non-linear polynomial. At the same time, we score the orders according to the metric described below.

3.4.2
Ordering orders

Once we have determined all the LR orders, we have to order them according to which one is expected to yield the most efficient integration. For this purpose, we assign scores to every LR order, which are meant to be a proxy for how complicated intermediate expressions it is expected to produce.

The intuition is that those LR orders that have large discriminants will also produce large integrands. Hence, we score each LR order by the total size (measured as the number of
Mathematica
expression leaves using
LeafCount
) of its polynomials.
To corroborate this intuition, we collected a representative list of 34 example Feynman integrals. For each individual integrand term, we integrated it according to a randomly chosen LR order. We recorded the score associated with the LR orders and the eventual integration times using
HyperIntica
. The total number of data points is
10\,020
. The results are collected in Fig.
4
. Given that the integration times and scores span multiple orders of magnitude, it is more convenient to talk about logarithms of both quantities.

Figure 4

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log T_{\text{order}}]

\displaystyle\approx\alpha\log S_{\text{order}}+\beta

\displaystyle\!\!\!\text{with}\quad\alpha\approx 1.15\,,\quad\beta\approx-9.45\,,

(3.66a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\log T_{\text{order}}]

\displaystyle\approx\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\text{with}\quad\sigma\approx 2.92\,.

In particular, we find that the variance is approximately constant, which means that
T_{\text{order}}
follows an approximately log-normal distribution.

If we could freely choose an LR order for each integrand term, we would simply pick the one with the smallest score. However, the algorithm described in Sec.
3.2
forces us to pick the same LR order for every term belonging to the same face
F
of the polytope. Hence, we are tasked with a problem: how to choose a single LR order that minimizes the total expected integration time for a number of integrands?

Let us denote with
S_{\text{order}}^{(i)}
and
T_{\text{order}}^{(i)}
, respectively, the score and the timing of the integrand
i\in F
using a fixed order.
We construct the following mean-variance cost function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{F,\text{order}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in F}T_{\text{order}}^{(i)}\right]+\lambda\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\left[\sum_{i\in F}T_{\text{order}}^{(i)}\right]}

(3.67a)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\beta+\sigma^{2}/2}\left[\sum_{i\in F}(S_{\text{order}}^{(i)})^{\alpha}+\lambda\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}}-1}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in F}(S_{\text{order}}^{(i)})^{2\alpha}}\right]\,,

(3.67b)

where we optimistically assumed that integration times are mutually independent. Here,
\lambda
is the risk-aversion factor. We set
\lambda=1.645
to ensure
95\%
certainty.

In our setup, we are dominated by the variance since
\lambda\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{\sigma^{2}}-1}\approx 117
. This should be clear from Fig.
4
, since the wide error bars mean we can at best predict
T_{\text{order}}
within two orders of magnitude. Hence, in practice, we can essentially ignore the first term in (
3.67b
) and instead use

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{F,\text{order}}=\sum_{i\in F}(S_{\text{order}}^{(i)})^{2\alpha}\,,

(3.68)

where we dropped the irrelevant constants and the square root which is strictly monotonic. This is the cost function we use in the code to determine the optimal LR order.
Empirically, we found that it is good at minimizing the computational effort and penalizing outliers that would otherwise stall the integration.

4.3.2
Fourier transforms in small-

We first turn our attention to dimensionally regulated Fourier transforms arising in small-

\mathrm{D}=2-2\varepsilon

[
24

(see also
[

\displaystyle I=\frac{\text{e}^{-2\varepsilon\gamma_{E}}}{\pi^{\mathrm{D}}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{\mathrm{D}}\ell_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{\mathrm{D}}\ell_{2}}{\ell_{1}^{2}(\ell_{1}-\ell_{2})^{2}}\,\log\!\left(\frac{\ell_{1}^{2}}{\ell_{2}^{2}}\right)\,\text{e}^{i(\ell_{1}\cdot X_{1}+\ell_{2}\cdot X_{2})}\,,

Physics aside, the presence of the logarithm in (

could try to evaluate directly. A natural solution is to introduce a new regulator

\displaystyle F_{q}=\frac{\text{e}^{-2\varepsilon\gamma_{E}}}{\pi^{\mathrm{D}}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{\mathrm{D}}\ell_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{\mathrm{D}}\ell_{2}}{(\ell_{2}^{2})^{1-q}(\ell_{1}-\ell_{2})^{2}(\ell_{2}^{2})^{q}}\,\text{e}^{i(\ell_{1}\cdot X_{1}+\ell_{2}\cdot X_{2})}\,,

reproduces the original integral at

\displaystyle F_{q}=\frac{\text{e}^{-2\varepsilon\gamma_{E}}\,\Gamma(\mathrm{D}-2)}{\Gamma(q)\,\Gamma(1-q)}\int

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}x}{\mathrm{GL}(1)}\,x_{1}^{-q}\,x_{3}^{q-1}\,(x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}+x_{2}x_{3})^{\mathrm{D}/2-2}

5
Conclusion

We have presented
SubTropica
, a
Mathematica
package for the automatic evaluation of linearly reducible Euler integrals via tropical subtractions. The package combines the tropical subtraction algorithm of
[
80
]
with
HyperIntica
, a self-contained reimplementation of
[
72
]
, providing an end-to-end workflow entirely within
Mathematica
. As demonstrated through examples ranging from multi-loop Feynman integrals to
phase-space integrals, Fourier transforms in high-energy QCD, and energy correlators, the package achieves a high level of automation while remaining accessible through a graphical interface. We have also released the
SubTropica
library, a repository of Feynman integrals computed in the literature together with their results.

Several directions for improvement and extension are worth mentioning.

Extending the domain of physical applications.

While a large number of physical computations are naturally formulated in terms of Euler integrals already, we expect that the class of applications can be enlarged further. For instance, phase-space integrals have, in some cases, been rewritten in this form, see, e.g.,
[
6
,
52
,
88
]
.
Other examples include correlation functions for large-scale structure in cosmology, which can often be brought into Euler integral form
[
85
]
(see also
[
51
]
).
With or without
SubTropica
, being able to achieve this systematically would unlock a whole new class of computational tools. We leave it as a
challenge to the community.

Improving tropical subtraction schemes.

Recall from Sec.
3.2.2
that one of the current bottlenecks is the need for Nilsson–Passare continuation whenever the integrand does not satisfy the geometric property. Circumventing this limitation would yield enormous speedups for certain classes of integrals, and hence should be explored more thoroughly. As an encouraging example, Hillman’s tropical subtraction formula
[
54
]
achieves this
objective for integrals with UV divergences. A concrete goal would be to extend it to IR divergences as well.

C++
backend.

Another bottleneck, specific to
Mathematica
, is its slow implementation of polynomial operations such as
Series
,
Apart
,
Together
, and
Factor
. The clear path forward is to introduce a
C++
backend implementing these functions. We plan to pursue this in the immediate future.

Parallelization.

For every face of the Newton polytope,
HyperIntica
is launched in parallel on multiple integrals. In practice, we find that a small subset of integrals consumes most of the compute. A better strategy would weight integrals by a predicted-complexity estimate to balance the load. Additionally, it would be desirable to explore possible parallelization of the standalone package
HyperIntica
.

Extending the class of integrals.

The requirement of linear reducibility, while satisfied by a large class of physically relevant integrals, is a genuine limitation. For example, transverse
\mathrm{d}z\wedge\mathrm{d}\overline{z}
integrals in, e.g., high-energy QCD
[
25
]
would require
HyperIntica
to account systematically for holomorphic anomalies. Moreover, although
SubTropica
deals with some integrals involving algebraic (e.g., square-root) letters, automatic detection of such features is not yet systematic. On the other hand, algorithmic methods of rationalization do exist, see, e.g.,
[
15
]
. We have implemented prototype AI-assisted
strategies to address this problem, which will be featured in a future release of
SubTropica
.

Finding linear orders.

Even though we have found simple-to-compute heuristic metrics that correlate reasonably well with the eventual integration time (see Sec.
3.4.2
), the predictor still has an enormous variance, spanning three orders of magnitude. In practice, this means a single integral can occasionally dominate the wall-clock budget, clogging the pipeline. More reliable ways of finding linearly-reducible orders, perhaps AI-assisted, therefore remain a
priority.

Extensions beyond hyperlogarithms?

The tropical subtraction scheme is completely general and it can be applied to any Euler integral, regardless of whether it is multi-polylogarithmic or not. From this point of view, it can be used in conjunction with any future direct-integration software, e.g., for elliptic iterated integrals. We look forward to seeing progress in this direction.

Expansion by regions.

A natural extension of
SubTropica
would be to implement the expansion by regions (see, e.g.,
[
58
]
), which is already formulated in terms of polyhedral geometry. We intend to add this feature in a future release.

Crown problems.

The issue of non-positive integrands mentioned in Sec.
3.1
is not only of practical concern, but also tied to a deep problem of defining perturbative quantum field theory in the first place. It is not entirely clear that in such cases Feynman integrals are analytic, or, more precisely, boundary values of a single analytic function
[
48
]
. It is plausible that one is forced to split the integral into multiple analytic functions, each with a different kinematic
i\epsilon
prescription. Curiously, one of the simplest examples of this problem, the crown diagram (Fig.
2
), belongs to a master-integral family computed in
[
50
,
10
]
, suggesting that differential equations might serve as a guide for understanding the issue of non-positive Feynman integrals.

SubTropica
as an end-to-end tool for scattering amplitudes.

With a simple click-to-integrate approach, we demonstrated that one does not need to be an expert on Feynman integrals to be able to compute them. There is no reason to stop here. In the future, we plan to extend this approach to scattering amplitudes wholesale, streamlining the full pipeline from the Lagrangian to the observable. We imagine a future in which every computation in quantum field theory will be only a single
Shift+Enter
away.

B: Gauge selection and scoring.

SubTropica automatically detects whether the input integrand is homogeneous in the integration variables. When it is (e.g., for integrands arising from the Schwinger representation of a Feynman graph), the code assumes an overall
\mathrm{GL}(1)
redundancy and searches for the gauge choice
x_{i}=1
that yields the simplest integration (according to the scoring metric set by "Heuristic", which is discussed below).

"Gauge" -> Automatic (default) scans all candidate gauges, scores them, and selects the best one, while "Gauge" -> {x3 -> 1} fixes the gauge directly. "IncludeGauges" -> {1,2,5} restricts the automatic search to
x_{1}=1
,
x_{2}=1
,
x_{5}=1
, whereas All (default) considers every Feynman parameter.

Note that if the integrand is
not
homogeneous (e.g., the Lee--Pomeransky representation of a Feynman graph), SubTropica detects this automatically and integrates over all parameters without gauge fixing. When necessary, this behavior can also be forced manually even on homogeneous integrands via "Gauge" -> {}.
V

"Heuristic" controls how candidate linearly reducible integration orders are ranked. The default "LeafCountLinear" minimizes the sum of leaf counts of intermediate polynomials, while alternatives include "ByteCountLinear", "TermCountLinear", "TotalDegreeLinear", and root-mean-square variants.
V

On complicated examples, gauge scoring can be memory-intensive. SubTropica implements options to spread the memory pressure when needed. The option "ScanScoreInterval" -> {m,n} restricts scoring to faces
m
through
n
, reducing cost. "ScoreInParallel" -> k splits candidates into
k
parallel chunks, while All (default) processes all gauges in one batch. "ScoringMemoryFraction" -> f (
0<f\leqslant 1
, default
0.5
) sets the fraction of available RAM budgeted for parallel scoring, controlling the number of gauges scored concurrently.

The option "TimeUpperBound" -> T sets a per-gauge time limit in seconds and candidates exceeding it are deprioritized.

V

"MethodLR" selects the algorithm used to find linearly reducible integration orders. The default "Lungo" uses an optimized Fubini reduction algorithm. The alternative "Espresso" combines it with deduplication via proportional-polynomial intersection, which is typically faster but might lead to sub-optimal integration orders.
V

"MethodPolysAndPairs" controls how the polynomial sets used for the linear reducibility check are extracted from the integrands and counter-terms.
The default "Fast" reads the irreducible polynomial factors directly from the internal monomial-polynomial decomposition (STtoCoeffMonPols) of
each counter-term integrand
before
expanding in
\varepsilon
, which avoids the cost of assembling and re-factoring the summed integrand at each
\varepsilon
-order.
The alternative "Standard" first sums all integrands and counter-terms at each
\varepsilon
-order and then extracts the polynomial set via STpreparePolysAndPairs,
which can detect cancellations between counter-terms that reduce the polynomial set. When "Fast" is selected (default) and no linearly reducible order is found,
STIntegrate automatically falls back to "Standard" and retries, since the reduced polynomial set relevant up to the specified Order may admit a linearly reducible order that the all-orders
"Fast" set does not allow.
V

"FindRoots" -> True (default) attempts to factor univariate polynomials of degree greater than one during the linear-order search by introducing auxiliary root variables (e.g., Wm[i]/Wp[i]), extending the class of linearly reducible integrals at the cost of increased complexity. Setting it to False restricts the search to orders that are linearly reducible without algebraic letters.
V

C: Parallelization and memory.

"KernelsAvailable" -> n sets the number of sub-kernels (default
\$\mathtt{ProcessorCount}-1
). Setting "KernelsAvailable" -> 0 or 1 runs the entire pipeline in serial on the main kernel: no sub-kernels are launched, and all ParallelTable/ParallelMap calls fall back to their serial equivalents automatically.
Sub-kernels are launched on the first STIntegrate call and reused across subsequent calls in the same session; it is advisable to restart kernels after large runs to release accumulated memory.
V

"ClearCachesPerIntegrand" -> False (default) clears all sub-kernel memoization caches only once, after all counter-term integrands for a given diagram have been integrated, maximizing cache reuse. Setting it to True clears caches after each individual integrand, which reduces peak memory at the cost of losing intermediate integrand cache reuse.
V

When FiniteFlow
[
77
]
and SPQR
[
27
]
are loaded, the potentially costly partial-fraction step inside HyperIntica uses finite-field reconstruction via SPQRPolynomialQuotient, avoiding intermediate expression swell. This introduces an independent layer of parallelism (via FiniteFlow’s internal C++ threads) that is non-interfering with
Mathematica

V

D: Output and diagnostics.

"Verbose" -> True enables detailed print output throughout the computation.

"SaveSlowestIntegrand" -> "file.m" records the slowest integrand after the run (default False).

"SetProblemID" -> Automatic generates a unique run identifier used to name intermediate directories, and can be set to any string. "SimplifyOutput" -> f applies f to the final result. Passing Identity skips simplification entirely.

E: Checkpointing and workflow.

For difficult or long-running computations, the pipeline can be paused at intermediate stages and resumed later, and individual tropical faces can be targeted selectively.

"StopAt" interrupts the pipeline and saves an intermediate result to disk as a checkpoint under checkpoints/<ID>.m (where <ID> is the problem identifier set by "SetProblemID") that can be reloaded later via "StartAt". The accepted values are "AfterBuildingIntegrand" (returns the raw parametric integrand {prefactor, integrand, variables, coefficients}), "AfterExpansion" (returns the list of locally finite integrands from the tropical subtraction without launching HyperIntica), and "AfterLinearOrder" (additionally records the linearly reducible integration orders). The default Automatic runs the full pipeline without saving any checkpoint. The lower-level functions invoked at each stage, STExpandIntegral (at "AfterExpansion") and STFasterFubini (at "AfterLinearOrder"), are documented in App.
A.6.3
and
A.7
respectively (see also Fig.
6
). The valid values for "StopAt" and all other options with enumerable values can be queried interactively via the helper function STOptionValues[]. For instance, STOptionValues["StopAt"] returns the list of accepted strings.
V

"StartAt" resumes the pipeline from a checkpoint previously saved by "StopAt". The argument is the checkpoint identifier returned by the earlier call.
V

"SelectFaces" -> All (default) passes all tropical face integrands to HyperIntica. Setting it to a single integer
n

Linear reducibility.

The order of variables (i.e., the order in which we integrate) matters a lot: the algorithm requires each polynomial encountered during integration to factor linearly the current variable. When calling HyperIntica directly, a wrong order can silently return an incomplete (and thus incorrect) result. The two global flags $NoAlgebraicRootsContributions and $HyperWarnZeroed control the response to a non-linearly-factoring polynomial:

\diamond

$NoAlgebraicRootsContributions = True (default): the non-linear factor is set to zero. If $HyperWarnZeroed = True (default), two messages fire: LinearFactors::nonlinear identifies the offending polynomial and LinearFactors::zeroed reminds the user to verify the ordering. Setting $HyperWarnZeroed = False suppresses both reminders. In either case the result is
0
, which is incorrect if the ordering is not linearly reducible.

\diamond

$NoAlgebraicRootsContributions = False: HyperIntica returns $Failed immediately upon encountering the non-linear factor, with message LinearFactorsE::nonlinear. This makes an incorrect ordering immediately visible.

Note that using $NoAlgebraicRootsContributions = True is safe when HyperIntica is called from STIntegrate, which verifies a valid order exists first, but it is in general advisable to verify a chosen order is linearly reducible by setting $NoAlgebraicRootsContributions = False before calling HyperIntica directly as a standalone function.

Divergence checking.

The global flag $HyperInticaCheckDivergences controls whether HyperIntica computes and verifies boundary expansions at each integration step. In HyperIntica.wl the default is True: after finding the primitive in each variable, the code expands the result at
z=0
and
z=\infty
, collects all pole terms, and checks that they cancel. If a divergence is detected and $HyperInticaAbortOnDivergence = True (default), the computation aborts with $Failed. Setting $HyperInticaAbortOnDivergence = False instead stores the divergence (if any) in the association $HyperInticaDivergences and continues.

When the flag is set to False, the boundary expansions themselves are skipped entirely, not just the final cancellation check, so a speedup generally comes from avoiding the computation of the expansions altogether.

When HyperIntica is called from within STIntegrate, the flag is automatically set to False, since the tropical subtraction algorithm guarantees that every counter-term integrand passed to HyperIntica is locally finite. When using HyperIntica as a standalone function on an integral whose finiteness is not known
a priori
, the user should keep $HyperInticaCheckDivergences = True and $HyperInticaAbortOnDivergence = True (the default in standalone mode).

Memoization and cache management.

During integration, HyperIntica accumulates several internal memoization caches. These persist for the duration of the session, which is beneficial when evaluating many related integrals, and can be cleared at any time by calling ForgetAllMemo in the notebook where calculations are done.

Appendix B

Notation used in HyperIntica

This appendix collects the conventions used by HyperIntica, following those of Panzer’s HyperInt
[
73
]
. The material overlaps with the review in Sec.
3.3
but is presented here in self-contained form for reference.

Hyperlogarithms and words.

As introduced in Sec.
3.3
, the hyperlogarithm
\mathrm{H}(z;w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
is the iterated integral

\mathrm{H}(z;w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})=\int_{0<t_{n}<\cdots<t_{1}<z}\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{1}}{t_{1}-w_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{2}}{t_{2}-w_{2}}\cdots\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{n}}{t_{n}-w_{n}}\,,

(B.1)

or equivalently
\mathrm{H}(z;w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t-w_{1}}\,\mathrm{H}(t;w_{2},\ldots,w_{n})
with
\mathrm{H}(z;\emptyset)=1
. The sequence
w=[w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}]
is called a
word
, its entries are the
letters
, and the integer
n=|w|
is the
weight
. For example, the relevant alphabets are
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
for classical multi-zeta values and
\Sigma=\{-1,0,1\}
for alternating Euler sums.

Internally, HyperIntica stores linear combinations of hyperlogarithms as
wordlists
: lists of (coefficient, word) pairs

\mathtt{wordlist}=\bigl\{\{c_{1},w_{1}\},\{c_{2},w_{2}\},\ldots\bigr\}\hskip 9.24994pt\longleftrightarrow\hskip 9.24994pt\sum_{i}c_{i}\cdot\mathrm{H}(z;w_{i})\,.

(B.2)

Shuffle product.

The shuffle product
u\shuffle v
of two words
u=[u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}]
and
v=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}]
is the sum over all interleavings that preserve the relative order within each word (cf. (
3.43
)):

u\shuffle v=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(m,n)}\sigma(u,v)\,,

(B.3)

where
\Sigma(m,n)
is the set of
(m,n)
-shuffles. It satisfies the recursion

[a,u^{\prime}]\shuffle[b,v^{\prime}]=\bigl[a,(u^{\prime}\shuffle[b,v^{\prime}])\bigr]+\bigl[b,([a,u^{\prime}]\shuffle v^{\prime})\bigr]\,,

(B.4)

with
u\shuffle\emptyset=\emptyset\shuffle u=u
, and the key algebraic property is that hyperlogarithms satisfy a shuffle algebra:

\mathrm{H}(z;u)\cdot\mathrm{H}(z;v)=\sum_{w\in u\shuffle v}\mathrm{H}(z;w)\,.

(B.5)

Shuffle regularization.

Iterated integrals diverge when letters coincide with integration boundaries. Shuffle regularization exploits the algebra (
B.5
) to systematically extract finite values. The basic idea is that the shuffle product lets us isolate divergent contributions; for instance, for a word
[a,0]
with one trailing zero,

\mathrm{H}(z;a,0)=\mathrm{H}(z;a)\cdot\log(z)-\mathrm{H}(z;0,a)\,,

(B.6)

where
\mathrm{H}(z;0,a)
is convergent as
z\to 0
and the divergence is carried entirely by
\log(z)
. Regularization then discards the pure-logarithm divergences while preserving all algebraic relations.

As discussed in the main text, HyperIntica uses three regularizations, all following this pattern. They are summarized in the table below:

Reg.

Boundary

Divergent word structure

Divergence

\mathrm{Reg}_{0}

z\to 0

[w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-k},\underbracket{0,\ldots,0}_{k}]

\dfrac{\log(z)^{k}}{k!}

\mathrm{Reg}_{\sigma}

z\to\sigma

[\underbracket{\sigma,\ldots,\sigma}_{k},w_{k+1},\ldots,w_{n}]

\dfrac{(-\log(\sigma-z))^{k}}{k!}

\mathrm{Reg}_{\infty}

z\to\infty

[w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-k},\underbracket{-1,\ldots,-1}_{k}]

\dfrac{\log(z)^{k}}{k!}

Explicitly, for a word with
k
divergent letters at the relevant boundary, the regularization shuffles with the pure-divergent word and applies an alternating sign:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{0}\mathrm{H}(z;[w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-k},0^{k}])

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{u\in[w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-k-1}]\shuffle[0]^{k}}(-1)^{k}\mathrm{H}(z;[u,w_{n-k}])\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{\sigma}\mathrm{H}(\sigma;[\sigma^{k},w_{k+1},\ldots,w_{n}])

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{u\in[\sigma]^{k}\shuffle[w_{k+2},\ldots,w_{n}]}(-1)^{k}\mathrm{H}(\sigma;[w_{k+1},u])\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{\infty}\mathrm{H}(\infty;[w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-k},(-1)^{k}])

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{u\in[w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-k-1}]\shuffle[-1]^{k}}(-1)^{k}\mathrm{H}(\infty;[u,w_{n-k}])\,.

Words consisting entirely of the divergent letter regularize to zero. Note the asymmetry:
\mathrm{Reg}_{\sigma}
acts on
leading
entries (closest to the upper bound
z
) while
\mathrm{Reg}_{0}
acts on
trailing
entries (closest to the lower bound
0
), reflecting the ordering
0<t_{n}<\cdots<t_{1}<z
in the iterated integral. For illustration,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{0}\mathrm{H}(z;[a,0])

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{H}(z;[0,a])\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{1}\mathrm{H}(1;[1,a])

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{H}(1;[a,1])\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{1}\mathrm{H}(1;[1,a,b])

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{H}(1;[a,1,b])-\mathrm{H}(1;[a,b,1])\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{\infty}\mathrm{H}(\infty;[-1])

\displaystyle=0\,.

Periods.

The fully regularized periods combine both boundary regularizations:

\displaystyle\mathtt{zeroOnePeriod}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Reg}_{1}\mathrm{Reg}_{0}\mathrm{H}(1;w)\,,

(B.7a)

\displaystyle\mathtt{zeroInfPeriod}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Reg}_{\infty}\mathrm{Reg}_{0}\mathrm{H}(\infty;w)\,.

(B.7b)

After regularization, the results are finite linear combinations of convergent hyperlogarithms, expressible in terms of multi-zeta values. A crucial property is that shuffle regularization respects the shuffle product
[
74
]
,

\mathrm{Reg}\bigl(\mathrm{H}(z;u)\cdot\mathrm{H}(z;v)\bigr)=\mathrm{Reg}\left(\sum_{w\in u\shuffle v}\mathrm{H}(z;w)\right)\,,

(B.8)

ensuring consistency of the integration algorithm.

Multi-zeta values and classical functions as hyperlogarithms.

The multiple polylogarithm (MPL) with positive integer indices
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
and arguments
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
is

\mathrm{Li}_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=\sum_{m_{1}>m_{2}>\cdots>m_{k}\geqslant 1}\frac{z_{1}^{m_{1}}z_{2}^{m_{2}}\cdots z_{k}^{m_{k}}}{m_{1}^{n_{1}}m_{2}^{n_{2}}\cdots m_{k}^{n_{k}}}\,,

(B.9)

converging for
|z_{1}|\leqslant 1
(with
|z_{1}|<1
or
n_{1}\geqslant 2
at the boundary). For nonzero integers
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
with
|n_{1}|\geqslant 2
, the multiple zeta value (MZV), also called alternating Euler sum when negative indices appear, is the specialization

\begin{split}\zeta(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k})&\equiv\mathrm{Li}_{|n_{1}|,\ldots,|n_{k}|}\!\left(\frac{n_{1}}{|n_{1}|},\ldots,\frac{n_{k}}{|n_{k}|}\right)\\
&=\sum_{m_{1}>\cdots>m_{k}\geqslant 1}\frac{(\mathrm{sgn}n_{1})^{m_{1}}\cdots(\mathrm{sgn}n_{k})^{m_{k}}}{m_{1}^{|n_{1}|}\cdots m_{k}^{|n_{k}|}}\,,\end{split}

(B.10)

where
\mathrm{sgn}n_{i}=n_{i}/|n_{i}|\in\{-1,+1\}
. When all
n_{i}>0
, this is a classical MZV. When at least one index is negative, the alternating signs
(-1)^{m_{i}}
give an alternating Euler sum. The weight is
|n_{1}|+\cdots+|n_{k}|
and the depth is
k
. Some familiar special cases are

\displaystyle\zeta(2)=\pi^{2}/6,\hskip 9.24994pt\zeta(-2)=-\pi^{2}/12,\hskip 9.24994pt\zeta(-1)=-\log 2\,,

\displaystyle\zeta(1,2)=\zeta(3),\hskip 9.24994pt\zeta(-1,2)=-\tfrac{3}{2}\log(2)\,\zeta(2)+\zeta(3)\,.

The connection to hyperlogarithms is that, for positive indices
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
with
n_{1}\geqslant 2

,

\zeta(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})=(-1)^{k}\mathrm{H}\Bigl(1;\underbracket{0,\ldots,0}_{n_{k}-1},1,\ldots,\underbracket{0,\ldots,0}_{n_{1}-1},1\Bigr)\,.

(B.11)

More generally, for
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
the word uses letters in
\{-1,0,1\}
, with each index
n_{i}
contributing
|n_{i}|-1
zeros followed by a letter encoding the sign transitions.

For reference, we collect the standard dictionary. The natural logarithm is
\log(z)=\mathrm{Reg}_{0}\mathrm{H}(z;0)
, while the classical polylogarithm
\mathrm{Li}_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z^{k}/k^{n}
reads

\mathrm{Li}_{n}(z)=-\mathrm{H}\bigl(z;\underbracket{0,\ldots,0}_{n-1},1\bigr)\,,

(B.12)

giving in particular
\mathrm{Li}_{1}(z)=-\log(1-z)=-\mathrm{H}(z;1)
,
\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z)=-\mathrm{H}(z;0,1)
, and
\mathrm{Li}_{3}(z)=-\mathrm{H}(z;0,0,1)
. More generally, the MPL is expressed as

\mathrm{Li}_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})=(-1)^{k}\mathrm{H}\Bigl(1;\underbracket{0,\ldots,0}_{n_{k}-1},\frac{1}{\prod_{j=k}^{k}z_{j}},\ldots,\underbracket{0,\ldots,0}_{n_{1}-1},\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}}\Bigr)\,.
