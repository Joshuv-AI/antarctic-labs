A systematic review of qualitative research exploring resilience in Academia
Authors: Fiona Yu, Ginger Chu, Albara Alomari, Melody Smith, Ritin Fernandez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe high demands of academia place staff under considerable stress, and enhancing resilience has been proposed as a means of coping with stress; however, resilience in academic contexts remains underexplored. This systematic review aimed to investigate resilience in academia from the perspective of academic staff.MethodThis review was registered within PROSPERO (CRD420251018894) and conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. Qualitative studies were identified through searches of CINAHL, MEDLINE, PsycINFO, EMBASE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library, from inception to April 2025. Eligible studies involved academics and focused on resilience.ResultsThirteen qualitative studies were included. Three main themes were identified: factors influencing resilience, barriers to building and sustaining resilience, and strategies to strengthen resilience.ConclusionsFindings highlight the need for targeted resilience interventions and education programmes to enhance well-being in academia.

Introduction

High levels of stress and burnout in academia have resulted in low staff retention (Rahman et al., 2024), and negative impacts on teaching quality and learning outcomes for students (Chong et al., 2025). Resilience may play a key role in supporting academics' ability to cope with their role demands (Yu et al., 2025), ultimately improving workforce sustainability and teaching and learning outcomes. Limited evidence exists regarding academic resilience, and the majority of this evidence is quantitative (Hunsu et al., 2023; Rudd et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2025). Findings show a variety of factors associated with academic resilience; however, these quantitative studies are limited in that they do not reveal factors of importance from the perspectives of the academics themselves.

Understanding how, why, and in what ways academics experience resilience from their own perspectives is essential for guiding effective intervention development and implementation and for providing an important adjunct to the predominantly quantitative evidence base. In this systematic review, we aim to address the evidence gap by posing the following question: How is resilience experienced among academics, and what are its impacts?

Resilience is a multidimensional construct that varies across contexts, is influenced by life experiences and is shaped by dynamic interactions with individuals and environments (Pearson et al., 2025), addressing the socioecological factors affecting a developing person, from the microsystem (e.g., family, school) to the macrosystem (e.g., economy, culture). Therefore, we designed this review following the multi-systems model of resilience (Liu et al., 2017) and Bronfenbrenner’s socioecological model (Bronfenbrenner, 1994). The multi-systems model of resilience comprises three levels: core, internal, and external resilience, which are related to intra-individual, interpersonal, and socio-ecological factors, respectively. Bronfenbrenner’s socioecological model enabled us to better understand how socioecological factors influence resilience in higher education across the microsystem (e.g., peers and colleagues) and macrosystem (e.g., culture, policies, legislation). These two models conceptualise resilience as dynamic rather than static and relational rather than purely individual, aligning with qualitative enquiry that explores how and why, which can capture academics' lived experiences and interactions across individuals, workplaces, and organisations (Robinson & Williams, 2024). Importantly, these two models provide a scaffold for systematically synthesising qualitative evidence, thereby strengthening analytical transparency and theoretical coherence. Therefore, following the model-based framework, this review aimed to examine academic resilience, with particular focus on the factors, barriers, and strategies identified through qualitative research.

Material and methods

This systematic review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (Ref: CRD420251018894) and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al., 2021).

Search criteria and data selection

Studies were included if they involved academics working in universities and other higher education institutions and investigated resilience as a primary outcome, along with its associated factors, using a qualitative approach (Supplementary File 1). Full-text English peer-reviewed qualitative journal articles published between inception and April 2025 were included in this systematic review. Six databases were searched between March 17 and April 4, 2025: CINAHL Ultimate (EBSCO), MEDLINE (Ovid), PsycINFO, EMBASE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. The search keywords and MeSH terms were identified using the format ‘population’, ‘exposure’, and ‘outcomes’, along with Boolean connectors (Supplementary Files 2 & 3).

The search results were imported into EndNote and then exported to Covidence for data selection. Two authors independently reviewed the titles, abstracts, and full texts in accordance with the search eligibility criteria to extract the data. Any discrepancies during study selection were resolved during team discussions.

Data analysis

Qualitative data were synthesised using the JBI meta-aggregation approach. Findings, along with supporting illustrations such as participant quotations, were extracted from each included study and grouped into categories based on semantic similarity, with each category reflecting a shared idea or concept across studies (Porritt et al., 2024). All the team members were involved in this analysis. One of the authors rechecked all the data, and any discrepancies were discussed within the team to reach a consensus.

Quality assessment

The Hawker quality assessment tool (Hawker et al., 2002) was utilised to assess study quality by assigning an overall rating of Good, Fair, Poor, or Very Poor. Studies were included if they were graded as “Fair ” or “Good ”; those ranked as “Poor” or “Very Poor” were excluded, as excluding low-quality studies did not affect the overall results (Ogilvie et al., 2005). Two authors conducted the quality assessment independently, and any disagreements were resolved within the team.

Researcher positionality and reflexivity

The researchers on this team had diverse backgrounds in academia, nursing education, and qualitative research. These backgrounds provided familiarity with academic workplaces, work-related stress, and resilience-related factors, which supported the interpretation of the findings from the included studies. We recognise that our academic roles, professional experiences, and disciplinary positions may have influenced how we interpreted the findings. To reduce the influence of individual assumptions, all the team members reviewed the findings, illustrative quotations, and themes, and refined the interpretations through iterative discussion to ensure that the synthesis remained grounded in the included studies.

Results

After an initial search, we identified 8,507 studies, of which 5,939 were selected for title and abstract screening after removing 2,568 duplicates. After screening the titles and abstracts of the studies, 5,748 articles were excluded, leaving 191 for full-text review. Of 191 studies, 174 were excluded due to wrong sample (n = 26), wrong outcomes (n = 42), wrong study design (n = 94), and no English full text (n = 12). Consequently, 17 studies met the eligibility criteria. A quality assessment was conducted with these 17 studies, 4 of which were evaluated as “Poor” or “Very Poor”, and excluded, thus leaving 13 studies included in this systematic review (Figure 1). Characteristics of included studies.

Study characteristics

Table I shows that 10 of the included studies were conducted in Australia, Mexico, New Zealand, South Africa, and the United States of America. The remaining three were conducted in China, Finland, and the United Arab Emirates. A total of 236 participants were included, holding various academic roles, including professors, associate professors, senior lecturers, lecturers, instructors, assistants, and research assistants.

The included studies utilised diverse qualitative and mixed-methods approaches. Where reported, methodological approaches included phenomenology, autoethnography, qualitative case study, narrative approaches, and mixed-methods designs. Qualitative data were generated through semi-structured interviews, focus groups, narrative frames, reflective narratives, documents, or observations. Analytical approaches included thematic analysis, content analysis, narrative analysis, autoethnographic analysis, and analysis informed by grounded theorising. Some studies were guided by theoretical or conceptual perspectives, including resilience theory, emotional resilience, organisational resilience, or professional identity. These methodological and analytical differences were considered when interpreting the findings to help explain how resilience was understood at the individual, interpersonal, workplace, and organisational levels in academia.

Findings

The final themes were developed through iteratively synthesising findings reported across the included studies, using the JBI meta-aggregation process. The extracted findings were reviewed study by study and then compared between studies to identify shared meanings, patterns, and differences. Similar findings were grouped into categories and refined into broader themes. The multi-systems model of resilience and Bronfenbrenner’s socioecological model were used as sensitising frameworks to support the interpretation and organisation of findings. This process resulted in three main themes: (1) factors influencing resilience, (2) barriers to building and sustaining resilience, and (3) strategies to strengthen resilience. Each theme and its supporting categories are presented in Table III and discussed below.

What helps academics remain resilient

Across studies, academics described resilience as a dynamic, multifaceted capacity supported by both personal and organisational resources, spanning seven subthemes (Table III). The importance of personal capacities was clear, including emotional regulation, self‑efficacy, perseverance, and growth‑focused attitudes. However, variability existed in how these capacities were developed: for some academics, they stemmed from accumulated career experience, whereas for others they were tied to cultural beliefs or identity. Organisational factors, work–life boundaries, education and training, supportive institutional policies and opportunities for learning and professional development contributed to resilience.

A summary of findings with evidence.

Emotional resilience and regulation, as essential to controlling emotions, thus increasing resilience, were reported in two studies (Chan et al., 2021; Dohaney et al., 2020). Academics described how emotional maturity grew through experience rather than avoidance. As one participant reflected, “I think you just grow from maturity… you mellow, and you just learn from feedback” (Chan et al., 2021, p. 453). This suggests that resilience was not a fixed trait, but something refined through repeated exposure to professional challenges. ‘Resilience attributes and behaviours’ were discussed in nine studies (Dohaney et al., 2020; Iheduru-Anderson et al., 2024; Krsmanovic et al., 2024; Matahela & van Rensburg, 2024; Montero-Hernandez et al., 2014; Shaik et al., 2022; Shaya et al., 2023; Siltaloppi et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022). These included speaking up, adopting a growth mindset, persevering, remaining focused, seeking support, being viewed as a leader or mentor, serving as a role model, prioritising duties, establishing social connections, offering guidance on challenging tasks, and upholding their own or religious beliefs. One academic described: “…having an ability to adapt …means that you're going to be in the best position to respond to physical limitations…” (Dohaney et al., 2020, p.2 Supplementary Information 2). These qualities helped academics turn negative emotions into protective resilience to remain focused and self-controlled, establish connections and collaborations, and seek support to maintain professional development and ensure well-being (Krsmanovic et al., 2024; Matahela & van Rensburg, 2024).

“Institutional policy ” was a factor influencing resilience by allowing academic freedom (Dohaney et al., 2020). “Work-life balance and boundaries ” was another resilience-related factor discussed in three studies (Cadete & Ruggunan, 2024; Chan et al., 2021; Dohaney et al., 2020). Academics who were able to manage work-life boundaries appeared to recover better from work demands. One participant expressed: “I just really make sure that I exercise and build in regular time off… I try not to do any work on it and have a complete break” (Chan et al., 2021, p. 454). This suggests that boundary-setting is not merely a personal preference but an important resilience strategy that helps academics conserve energy and maintain well-being. ‘Self-efficacy and empowerment’ was analysed in five studies (Cadete & Ruggunan, 2024; Chan et al., 2021; Dohaney et al., 2020; Iheduru-Anderson et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2022), referring to resilient individuals who possess high levels of self-efficacy and strong inner strength, enabling them to take a big-picture view in complex situations, cope with adversity, regulate their emotions, and adapt to the work environment to achieve academic success. As one participant reflected, “After being thrown to the lions of formal academia, I quickly realised the need to develop different skills to succeed…” (Iheduru-Anderson et al., 2024, p. 28). This suggests that self-efficacy and empowerment are important for academic resilience, building confidence in one's ability to navigate complex academic environments.

“Learning and personal development ” was identified in two studies (Matahela & van Rensburg, 2024; Montero-Hernandez et al., 2014), which found that resilient learners perceived learning as a vital component of their professional development and an enjoyable journey, valued learning opportunities, and committed to their learning tasks. As one participant described, “… As a researcher, one never stops learning. I keep studying, attending seminars, [and] conferences…. [I]n the training… to be open to learn new stuff’ (Montero-Hernandez et al., 2014, p. 344). Participants in these studies recognised that learning was a valuable strategy for personal growth, and active learners defined themselves as resilient doers with persistent interest, enjoying their learning journey, acquiring new knowledge, integrating their learning into practice, and renewing their skills. “Resilience education and training ” was discussed in two studies (Richards et al., 2024; Shaik et al., 2022), which supported the need for resilience content to be embedded in the curriculum through workbooks, self-reflective activities, and training programmes that cater to students’ cultural needs and learning objectives, highlighting the importance of integrating resilience training into students’ learning to improve teaching outcomes.

What makes resilience difficult to sustain

Five subthemes were generated, indicating five barriers to sustaining academic resilience. Through the lens of the multi-systems model of resilience (Liu et al., 2017) and Bronfenbrenner’s socioecological model (Bronfenbrenner, 1994), it was understood that academic resilience was ecologically embedded in the systems rather than individually owned; therefore, the barriers to sustaining resilience did not arise from academics themselves but from the misalignment across ecological structures.

“Inadequate resilience education and integration ”, including insufficient recognition, knowledge sharing, planning, and collaboration, as well as over-relying on teaching assistants, was a significant barrier to enhancing resilience (Dohaney et al., 2020). “Structural barriers ” were among the most damaging threats to resilience, reported across seven studies (Cadete & Ruggunan, 2024; Chan et al., 2021; Dohaney et al., 2020; Iheduru-Anderson et al., 2024; Krsmanovic et al., 2024; Montero-Hernandez et al., 2014; Shaik et al., 2022). These included limited resources, poor recognition, and rigid performance systems. One academic described the direct psychological toll: “performance management system… I was marked as being underperforming… I was spiralling down into this depression” (Cadete & Ruggunan, 2024, p. 12). Another felt deliberately sidelined: 'I feel deskilled… deliberately given assignments requiring very little analytical thinking' (Iheduru-Anderson et al., 2024, p. 29). These accounts reveal how institutional structures, not just individual capacity, determine whether resilience can be sustained. Some lecturers highlighted a lack of organisational support for mental health, particularly for women academicians postnatally. The “structural barriers ” placed academic staff in a situation where they felt like “sinking” or “swimming” (Shaik et al., 2022), highlighting the importance of strengthening managerial support within universities.

“Personal barriers ” such as a lack of time to attend resilience training, suggesting that most academic staff often worked long hours, far above their regular work hours (Dohaney et al., 2020). ‘Workload and job demand’ was identified from five studies (Cadete & Ruggunan, 2024; Chan et al., 2021; Dohaney et al., 2020; Krsmanovic et al., 2024; Montero-Hernandez et al., 2014). When workload was excessive or poorly supported, academics had fewer opportunities to recover and engage in professional development, which weakened their capacity to sustain resilience. As one participant reflected, “I worked weekends and nights… our department should have provided some sort of support… Doing the training programme was definitely lots of extra work” (Montero-Hernandez et al., 2014, p. 343). This suggests that academic workload and job demands should not be understood as individual management issues but as organisational conditions that may undermine academic resilience. ‘Workplace culture’, including loss of trust in their organisations or cultural oppression, led academics to lose confidence in working with others and expressing their opinions, which contributed to feelings of not belonging in the workplace (Iheduru-Anderson et al., 2024). One participant reflected: “I began to feel isolated and confused...I have had to reach out to White and Black faculty from other institutions for support…” (Iheduru-Anderson et al., 2024, p. 29). This suggests that unsupportive workplace cultures may undermine academic resilience; conversely, positive workplace cultures may help academics feel valued and connected, thereby strengthening resilience.

How academics adopt and strengthen resilience

Aligning with the Multi-Systems Model of Resilience (Liu et al., 2017) and Bronfenbrenner’s socioecological model (Bronfenbrenner, 1994), these seven subthemes indicated that strengthening resilience was achieved through interactions among organisational, social, and collegial support rather than through individual-level interventions. Sustainable resilience emerged when support, leadership, relationships, and individuals aligned across systems, positioning resilience as a shared responsibility for providing guidance, ensuring fair workload and resource allocation, and facilitating policy enactment.

“Innovation and creativity ”, to be embedded with resilience, enhanced academic staff’s ability to deal with complex situations and find innovative ways to promote their agendas and leverage initiatives to create new research opportunities (Dohaney et al., 2020; Montero-Hernandez et al., 2014; Richards et al., 2024; Shaik et al., 2022; Shaya et al., 2023; Siltaloppi et al., 2022). Creative approaches allowed academics to adapt institutional demands while maintaining their professional identity and commitment. As one participant described, “So, this new way of doing it … I start[ed] thinking about how … I [could] do it differently so that I [could] involve them more and that was... very successful... for me” (Shaik et al., 2022, p. 177). This suggests that innovation and creativity should be supported as part of resilience-building strategies to help academics better cope with challenges. “Leadership and strategic planning ” as a strategy to strengthen resilience was described in one study (Dohaney et al., 2020), which concluded that resilient leadership involved effective communication with staff members and external stakeholders, strategic planning and preparedness, thereby enabling prompt responses and efficient crisis management.

“Organisational support ” was recognised as an essential strategy to enhance resilience in three studies (Cadete & Ruggunan, 2024; Dohaney et al., 2020; Iheduru-Anderson et al., 2024), which concluded that organisational support enabled faculty members to cope with work-related adversity, balance work and life, and enhance trust and work autonomy, thus improving their mental health and well-being. “Supporting system and collegiality’ was crucial in enhancing resilience, identified in three studies (Clemons, 2024; Dohaney et al., 2020; Matahela & van Rensburg, 2024). Supporting systems and collegial relationships helped reduce isolation, provided emotional and practical support, shared experiences, and increased academic confidence. One participant described: “…they were here, helping me for weeks, bringing me food… I put out an all-call… and they all came” (Clemons, 2024, p. 571). These supports included an ethic of care, commitment to care, and supportive working conditions and resources, which were key to enhancing resilience and fostering a profound sense of purpose, thereby increasing staff sustainability in higher education.

“Technology and digital resilience ” was recognised as a coping mechanism that promotes collaboration and social connections for knowledge sharing in academia (Krsmanovic et al., 2024). “Mentorship and representation ”, particularly with people of a similar cultural identity, could foster better mentorship, enhancing their inner resilience and comfort in adapting to a new environment (Iheduru-Anderson et al., 2024). “Social and collegial support ” was a strategy to strengthen resilience, identified in four studies (Chan et al., 2021; Dohaney et al., 2020; Iheduru-Anderson et al., 2024). This strategy helped academics share challenges, manage stress, seek advice, and maintain motivation, strengthening a sense of belonging within the workplace. One participant reflected: “The faculty of colour in my school support each other… we understand our unique challenges” (Iheduru-Anderson et al., 2024, p. 29). This suggests that social and collegial support may help academics manage negative experiences (e.g., manuscript rejections, criticism), articulate their needs, evaluate their progress, set professional development goals, and establish a platform for early-career researchers to enhance resilience in academia.

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to explore factors, barriers, and strategies influencing resilience in academia, using a qualitative approach and drawing on the multi-systems model of resilience (Liu et al., 2017) and Bronfenbrenner’s socioecological model (Bronfenbrenner, 1994). This review presents novel findings on academic resilience, offering new evidence on influencing factors, barriers and strategies for sustaining resilience. This is the first review to provide academic insights into resilience, offering an in-depth understanding of its attributes and the need to overcome barriers and develop strategies to promote resilience at multiple levels.

Insights into individual resilience

This systematic review found that the concept of individual resilience and its associated factors in academia encompasses personal capacity, growth, and a supportive network, marked by flexibility, capability, and adaptability. These were characterised by self-control, emotional regulation, self-discipline, perseverance, self-efficacy, self-empowerment, open-mindedness, and collaboration, evidenced in the study by Iheduru-Anderson et al. (2024) “…I am fighting the good fight that I hope will support behaviour among all that promotes inclusion, respect, and success for all…” (p. 28). Having these attributes and resilient behaviours enables academic staff to transform negative emotions into protective resilience, remain focused and self-controlled, establish connections and collaborations, and seek support to maintain professional development and ensure their well-being. The finding suggests that detaching from emotions or stepping back from the situation can be an effective strategy to enhance resilience, enabling individuals to approach complex situations rationally. Resilient individuals value their learning opportunities, commit to their tasks, develop new approaches to overcoming adversity, and draw on their cultural beliefs. The finding aligns with the study by Ross et al. (2024), which found that academic resilience involves personality traits, trust, strong relationships, and the capacity to recover from adversity. The finding is also mirrored in the study by Ang et al. (2021), which revealed that resilience was associated with internal resources (e.g., determination, aspirations), a positive outlook, and meaningful relationships. In addition, this finding corroborates the scoping review by Price (2023), which demonstrated that designing resilience is a core practice for increasing cognitive flexibility and adaptability in higher education. Overall, this finding suggests that the multidimensionality of individual resilience and its associated factors in academia is a global construct that warrants further investigation to tailor interventions to academics’ needs, enhancing their self-belief and persistence in professional development and well-being.

This review identified ‘limited time’ as a personal barrier that should be considered in developing resilience, enabling employees to stay focused on academic jobs and balance their work and life. Clear boundaries should be established between personal and work life, and individuals may behave differently to find the best fit in their circumstances. This finding suggests that resilience can serve as a personal resource that buffers the negative impact of work-life conflicts on academics and helps set boundaries to achieve a good balance between professional development and family life. Aligning with Bernuzzi et al. (2022) and the study by Quinn and Hartland-Grant (2024), this finding indicates that resilient academics possess a rich resource that supports them in enriching their work and family relationships, and that a balanced work-life enables them to become more resilient at work.

Embedding resilience content in teaching and learning, identified in this review, suggests that resilience training should be incorporated into higher education to enhance individuals’ flexibility, capability, adaptability, creativity, and emotional regulation. Providing resilience training can enhance individuals’ self-efficacy and empowerment, enabling them to adopt a broader perspective in complex situations and, in turn, become more confident in engaging in learning activities to achieve their academic goals. Consistent with the study by Ross et al. (2024), this finding indicates that building resilience among academics, enabling them to adapt to stress and improve the quality of graduates, has become critical. Corroborating the studies by Ang et al. (2021) and Price (2023), this finding supports the current notion that resilience must be embedded in the curriculum, synergising learning and teaching practices with the emerging concept of resilience to create opportunities and provide pathways for educational innovation. In addition, consistent with studies (Azevedo & Shane, 2019; Cai & Meng, 2025; Steel et al., 2024), this finding emphasises the importance of providing resilience training programmes as practical strategies to improve educators’ innovative teaching behaviours, thereby enhancing students’ learning performance and academic success.

Insights on organisational resilience

We identified that organisational-level resilience encompassed institutional structures, support systems, policies, and strategic leadership planning, aligning with Bronfenbrenner’s macrosystem (broad institutional context) and underscoring how macro-level factors influence individual resilience in academia. This finding highlights a crucial point: resilience is more than merely situated resistance; it can transform detrimental policy and work-culture changes into a moral foundation for advancing professional agendas. This finding emphasises the importance of building resilient leadership and strategic planning, establishing a supportive management system, and fostering collegiality at an organisational level, evidenced in the quote here, “You need to have informed, persuasive leadership that identifies preparedness … and … support staff in the task of figuring out the best ways to be resilient …” (Dohaney et al., 2020, p. 5 Supplementary Information 2). Aligning with the study by Celbis et al. (2025), Dasuki et al. (2025), and Young et al. (2024), this finding reveals that resilient organisations can translate the concept of resilience into operational practice to develop resilient communities, enabling staff members to be prepared, find solutions, and make positive adjustments through reflection in the face of unexpected events, thereby fostering innovation potential, generating more sensible, sustainable, and dynamic institutional structures and supporting systems.

This review also identified organisational-level barriers to enhancing resilience, including inadequate education and integration, structural barriers, excessive workload and job demands, and a negative workplace culture. These barriers could include heavy workload, a lack of skills in digital technologies, insufficient support for women academics and training, and limited resources. They could also be seen as ineffective communication, a lack of recognition of cultural identity and staff achievement, low levels of trust and autonomy, a lack of collaboration, a lack of a cohesive and nurturing institutional community, and a lack of resilience planning. This finding suggests that incorporating customised support, protective mechanisms, and action strategies into organisational resilience plans should minimise these barriers. Aligning with the study by Evenseth et al. (2022) and Mallak and Shank (2021), this finding suggests that enhancing academics’ ability to manage heavy workloads, supporting institutional learning, and increasing resource supply may help them overcome these barriers to enhancing workplace resilience. Corroborating the study by Fietz et al. (2021), this finding indicates that cultural aspects, backgrounds, values, and differences should be considered in management practice to mitigate the adverse effects of these barriers on organisational resilience.

Integrating the multi-systems model of resilience (Liu et al., 2017) and Bronfenbrenner’s socioecological model (Bronfenbrenner, 1994) provides a deeper understanding of resilience in academia. Following the multi-systems model, our findings suggest that academic core resilience includes emotion regulation, resilience attributes, self-efficacy and empowerment. However, academic core resilience interacts with internal and interpersonal systems, including academics' efforts to establish work-life balance, maintain boundaries, engage in professional development, manage their workload, and use creativity. At the external or socioecological level, resilience is further influenced by organisational support, structural conditions, leadership, and workplace culture.

Bronfenbrenner’s socioecological model further explains how these levels interact. At the microsystem level, peers, colleagues, team leaders, mentors, and supervisors can strengthen resilience through support, social cohesion, and a positive workplace culture, but they can also weaken resilience through isolation and limited interpersonal support. At a macro level, institutional policies, higher education systems, and organisational structures influence academics’ capacity to sustain resilience. Therefore, barriers to enhancing resilience, such as a lack of recognition, limited resources, or weak social cohesion, should not be attributed solely to individual coping capacity but also be understood as system-level conditions that may impede academic resilience.

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review provided unique insights into resilience and its associated factors, barriers to enhancing resilience, and relevant strategies from participants’ perspectives at the individual and organisational levels in academia. Focusing on qualitative studies is a strength of this systematic review, providing academics’ insights into the factors influencing resilience, the barriers to sustaining resilience and the need for strategies. The findings were derived from 13 studies conducted across different countries with diverse cultural backgrounds, providing a broad perspective on resilience. However, some academicians may have held executive roles in administration, which we did not consider in this systematic review, and this may have influenced their perceptions regarding resilience. Additionally, the role of academia was not consistently described in some studies, which may have influenced the overall data analysis. Moreover, in our search criteria, we selected full-text English-language qualitative journal articles, which may have excluded relevant non-English research. Finally, it should be noted that this systematic review did not analyse the relationships between individual and organisational resilience. However, the findings have prompted further study of the bidirectional influence of resilience between the microsystem and the macrosystem, in accordance with Bronfenbrenner's (1994) ecological model.

Implications for research and practice

The findings from this review suggest that resilience should be built into human resource practices to develop practical policies that support academic staff (Pantinople et al., 2024). Effective resilience interventions should also be designed as motivational resources to fit individual schedules and conducted within work periods, allowing academic staff to complete their training at work (Montero-Hernandez et al., 2014). Incorporating resilience education into teaching curricula can enhance students’ well-being and learning outcomes, and may also help alleviate faculty workload (Clemons, 2024). Supporting personal resources to enhance individual resilience may be considered at the university, such as providing vouchers for bubble baths, gym memberships, or concert tickets (Shaik et al., 2022). Regular IT training can help update and improve academics’ e-skills, thereby enhancing their digital resilience (Krsmanovic et al., 2024), and a leadership resilience training workshop may help foster a positive workplace culture that promotes resilience (Shaya et al., 2023).

Overall, consistent with the multi-systems model of resilience and Bronfenbrenner’s socioecological model, building resilience in academia should not be limited to strengthening individual core resilience. It also requires workplace and organisational support through practical policies and procedures that address academics’ needs, provide adequate resources, and create conditions to sustain resilience. Further research is needed to explore the interactions between individual resilience within the microsystem and organisational resilience within the macrosystem, providing deeper insights into the multidimensional nature of resilience.

Conclusions

This review provides valuable academic insights into resilience at both individual and organisational levels, which may help enhance flexibility, capability, and adaptability, overcome barriers, and inform institutional strategies to enhance resilience in academia. Resilience interventions or education programmes should be developed to enhance well-being in academia, and further research is needed to examine the interactions between resilience and environmental factors within the microsystem and macrosystem.
