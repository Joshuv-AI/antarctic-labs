MSign: An Optimizer Preventing Training Instability in Large Language Models via Stable Rank Restoration
Lianhai Ren, Yucheng Ding, Xiao Liu, Qianxiao Li, Peng Cheng, Yeyun Gong

Abstract
Training instability remains a critical challenge in large language model (LLM) pretraining, often manifesting as sudden gradient explosions that waste significant computational resources. We study training failures in a 5M-parameter NanoGPT model scaled via μ \mu P, identifying two key phenomena preceding collapse: (1) rapid decline in weight matrix stable rank (ratio of squared Frobenius norm to squared spectral norm), and (2) increasing alignment between adjacent layer Jacobians. We prove theoretically that these two conditions jointly cause exponential gradient norm growth with network depth. To break this instability mechanism, we propose MSign, a new optimizer that periodically applies matrix sign operations to restore stable rank. Experiments on models from 5M to 3B parameters demonstrate that MSign effectively prevents training failures with a computational overhead of less than 7.0%.

Introduction

Modern deep learning optimizers and training techniques – including Adam
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
)
, layer normalization
(
Ba et al. 2016
)
, and learning rate schedules – generally enable reliable training. However, as the model scale increases, pretraining large language models (LLMs) becomes increasingly fragile. Training failures manifest as sudden, unrecoverable gradient explosions and corresponding loss growth, which are difficult to predict and can waste substantial computational resources
(
Chowdhery et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)
.

Empirical Investigation: Identifying the Failure Mechanism.

We systematically study training failures using a 5M-parameter NanoGPT model derived from
\mu
P scaling
(
Yang et al. 2022
)
, which provides a controlled environment for reproducible failure analysis. Through extensive monitoring, we identify two critical phenomena that consistently precede training collapse (Figure
1
):

•

Observation 1: Stable Rank Collapse.
The stable rank of weight matrices – defined as
\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})=\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2}/\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}^{2}
– declines sharply in the steps preceding failure. This indicates that spectral energy becomes concentrated in the top singular directions, reducing the “effective dimensionality” of the weight matrices.

•

Observation 2: Jacobian Alignment Growth.
The alignment between adjacent layer Jacobians increases, meaning the top singular subspaces of consecutive layers become increasingly correlated. It prevents the typical cancellation effects in matrix products.

We prove that these two phenomena jointly cause training instability: low stable rank implies high layer Jacobian spectral norms (since
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}/\sqrt{\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})}
for fixed Frobenius norm), and high alignment ensures these norms multiply constructively across layers. The total Jacobian norm grows as
(aM)^{L}
where
a
is alignment,
M
is layer Jacobian norm, and
L
is depth, leading to exponential gradient explosion when
aM>1
.

Our Solution: The MSign Optimizer.

To break the stable rank collapse condition, we propose
MSign
, an optimizer that periodically applies the matrix sign operation
\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{T}
(where
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^{T}
is the SVD). This operation maximizes stable rank by equalizing all non-zero singular values to 1, while preserving the column and row spaces. We restore the original Frobenius norm after the operation to maintain training dynamics. MSign is applied every
P
steps (typically
P=100
) to projection weights, with computational overhead below 7.0%.

Experimental Validation.

We validate MSign across four model configurations spanning 5M to 3B parameters: NanoGPT-5M

Ablation studies reveal that applying MSign to attention layers (particularly output projections) is sufficient, MLP-only application does not prevent failures.

Literature Review

Training Instability in Large Language Models.

Training instability in large language models has been widely observed across major LLM projects.
Chowdhery et al. 2023
documented loss spikes during PaLM training that required manual intervention and checkpoint rollbacks, while
Zhang et al. 2022
provided detailed logs of OPT-175B training showing dozens of restarts due to hardware failures and gradient explosions.
Zeng et al. 2023
reported similar challenges in GLM-130B training. Several factors have been proposed to explain instability:
Kaplan et al. 2020
studied the sensitivity of training dynamics to learning rate in the context of scaling laws;
Pascanu et al. 2012
analyzed gradient clipping as a remedy for exploding gradients in RNNs;
Dong et al. 2021
identified attention entropy collapse as a failure mode in Vision Transformers. More recently,
Wortsman et al. 2024
showed that small-scale proxy models can predict instabilities at larger scales, and
Moniri et al. 2024
proposed a theoretical framework connecting loss spikes to heavy-tailed gradient noise. However, these explanations typically address symptoms rather than underlying mechanisms.

Low-Rank Structure in Neural Networks.

The prevalence of low-rank structure in neural network weights and gradients has been extensively documented.
Denil et al. 2013
demonstrated that neural network weights exhibit significant redundancy, with up to 95% of parameters predictable from the remaining 5%.
Arora et al. 2019
proved that gradient descent in deep linear networks implicitly biases toward low-rank solutions. In the transformer context,
Hu et al. 2022
exploited low-rank structure for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, while
Zhao et al. 2024
showed that gradients during transformer training maintain consistently low stable rank, motivating memory-efficient optimizers.
Huh et al. 2024
found that representations across different models and modalities converge to similar low-dimensional structures. Our work extends these observations by connecting low-rank gradient structure directly to training instability through the stable rank mechanism.

Jacobian Analysis in Deep Networks.

The role of Jacobians in neural network optimization has received substantial theoretical attention. Classical work by
Glorot & Bengio 2010
established the importance of proper weight initialization for maintaining stable gradient flow.
Saxe et al. 2013
analyzed dynamics in deep linear networks through the lens of Jacobian singular values.
Pennington et al. 2017
and
Yang & Schoenholz 2017
developed mean-field theories for Jacobian evolution in residual networks and general deep networks respectively.
Fort et al. 2019
empirically studied the relationship between Jacobian eigenvalue spectra and model trainability, finding that successful training correlates with specific spectral properties.
Xiao et al. 2018
proposed dynamical isometry as a condition for stable training. Our contribution is identifying inter-layer Jacobian
alignment
as a key mechanism amplifying gradient explosion, distinct from prior work that focused on individual layer Jacobian norms.

Stable Rank and Matrix Analysis.

Stable rank, introduced by
Rudelson & Vershynin 2007
in the context of random matrix theory, provides a continuous relaxation of matrix rank that is more robust to small perturbations.
Vershynin 2018
provides comprehensive treatment of stable rank properties and its applications in high-dimensional probability. In neural network analysis, stable rank has been used to derive generalization bounds:
Neyshabur et al. 2017
used stable rank to obtain PAC-Bayes bounds.
Li et al. 2018
analyzed intrinsic rank dynamics during optimization, finding that it correlates with model compressibility.
Sanyal et al. 2020
proposed stable rank regularization to improve generalization. Our work reveals a novel connection between stable rank dynamics and training stability, showing that stable rank collapse precedes and causes gradient explosion.

Empirical Observations: Training Failure Phenomena

We identify reproducible training failures in transformer models under moderate learning rates. Our analysis reveals two consistent phenomena preceding training collapse.

3.1
Experimental Setup

We construct a reproducible failure scenario using a modified NanoGPT configuration with standard hyperparameters (refer to Appendix

From

Model and Jacobian Notation.

We consider a standard decoder-only transformer with
L
stacked blocks. At each block
\ell
, hidden states
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
(sequence length
T
, hidden dimension
d
) are first processed by multi-head self-attention with query/key/value/output projections
\mathbf{W}_{Q}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{W}_{K}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{W}_{V}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{W}_{O}^{(\ell)}
, and then by a position-wise MLP with weights
\mathbf{W}_{1}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\mathrm{ff}}}
and
\mathbf{W}_{2}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{ff}}\times d}
, with residual connections and LayerNorm around each sublayer:

\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}=F^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell-1)}),\quad\ell=1,\dots,L.

(1)

We define the
layer Jacobian
as
\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)}=\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)})}{\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell-1)})}
. Later references to the stable ranks of
\mathbf{W}_{Q},\mathbf{W}_{K},\mathbf{W}_{V},\mathbf{W}_{O},\mathbf{W}_{1},\mathbf{W}_{2}
and to ”Jacobian alignment between adjacent layers” all refer to this architecture and these
\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)}
.

3.2
Observations during training failure

Observation 1: Sharp Stable Rank Decline

The first observable phenomenon is the rapid decline in weight stable rank. As shown in Figure
1
(left panel), the geometric averaged stable rank of the first several layers drops sharply around step 20000, preceding the gradient explosion. This phenomenon indicates energy concentration in top singular values.

Definition 3.1

(Stable Rank)
.

The stable rank of a matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is:

\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})=\frac{\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}}{s_{1}^{2}},

(2)

where
s_{1}\geq s_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0
are the singular values.

Observation 2: Increasing Jacobian Alignment Between Adjacent Layers

The second critical phenomenon is the increasing alignment between Jacobians of adjacent layers, as illustrated in Figure
1
(right panel).

Definition 3.2

(Matrix Alignment)
.

For any two matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
for which the product
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
is well-defined, their alignment is the cosine similarity between the top right singular vector of
\mathbf{A}
and the top left singular vector of
\mathbf{B}
:

\text{Align}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=|\mathbf{v}_{A,1}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{B,1}|,

(3)

where
\mathbf{v}_{A,1}
is the first right singular vector of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{u}_{B,1}
is the first left singular vector of
\mathbf{B}
. For simplicity, we denote the alignment of Jacobians for adjacent layers,
\text{Align}(\mathbf{J}^{(\ell+1)},\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)})
, as
\text{Align}(\ell+1,\ell)
.

High alignment correlates strongly with both weight scale growth and stable rank decline, as well as gradient growth during the failure phase.

3.3
Conjecture: Low Stable Rank + Jacobian Alignment Drives Training Failure

Based on these two consistent phenomena, we conjecture that the combination of low weight stable rank and high Jacobian alignment creates a destabilizing feedback mechanism that leads to training failure. The remainder of this paper develops this hypothesis theoretically and proposes a solution.

4
Theoretical Analysis: Understanding the Failure Mechanism

We now provide theoretical analysis to explain the observed phenomena and their causal relationships. Our analysis is divided into two parts: (1) explaining why Jacobian alignment and low stable rank lead to training failure, and (2) analyzing the positive feedback mechanism that prevents stable rank from increasing and accelerates its collapse.

4.1
Part I: From Observations to Training Failure

In this section, we establish the causal chain:
Low stable rank + Jacobian alignment

\Rightarrow

High total Jacobian norm

\Rightarrow

Large weight gradient norm
(
Training instability
).

Remark 4.1

(Simplifying Assumption)
.

We adopt the standard assumption that large gradient norms indicate training instability
(
Pascanu et al. 2012
;
Philipp et al. 2017
)
. Our analysis derives lower bounds for gradient norms, with the understanding that such bounds indicate increased risk of divergence.

4.1.1
High Jacobian Alignment + High Layer Jacobian Norm
\Rightarrow
High Total Jacobian Norm

The total Jacobian
\mathbf{J}_{total}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)}
determines how perturbations at the input propagate to the output. In general, the norm of a matrix product can be much smaller than the product of norms due to cancellation effects. However, when the singular subspaces of adjacent Jacobians are
aligned
, these cancellations are suppressed, and the norms multiply constructively.

To formalize this, we use the definition of matrix alignment (Definition 3.2).
This quantity measures how well the ”output direction” of
\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)}
matches the ”input direction” of
\mathbf{J}^{(\ell+1)}
, determining whether the composition
\mathbf{J}^{(\ell+1)}\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)}
preserves or diminishes the spectral norm.

Theorem 4.2

(Jacobian Product Norm Lower Bound)
.

For a deep network with
L
layers, let
\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(\ell-1)}}
denote the Jacobian at layer
\ell
. If each layer Jacobian satisfies
\|\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)}\|_{2}\geq M
and the alignment between adjacent Jacobians satisfies
\text{Align}(\ell+1,\ell)\geq a>0
for all
\ell
, then the total Jacobian from input to output has 2-norm:

\|\mathbf{J}_{total}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{J}^{(L)}\mathbf{J}^{(L-1)}\cdots\mathbf{J}^{(1)}\|_{2}\geq\frac{(aM)^{L}}{a}.

(4)

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.1
.

Remark 4.3

(Exponential Growth Condition)
.

The key insight is that when
aM>1
, the lower bound in Theorem
4.2
grows
exponentially
with depth
L
, providing a sufficient condition for large total Jacobian norms. Observation 1 (stable rank collapse) suggests that
M
can become large (via Theorem
4.4
under approximately fixed Frobenius norms), and Observation 2 shows that
a
tends to increase during training. In the failure regimes we study, these trends empirically drive
aM
above
1
, which is consistent with the observed gradient explosion, although we do not claim that
aM>1
holds at all times or is necessary for failure.

4.1.2
Low Stable Rank
\Rightarrow
High Layer Jacobian Norm

We analyze the relationship between stable rank and layer Jacobian norm for the three primary layer types in transformers.

Linear Layers.

Theorem 4.4

(Stable Rank Controls Jacobian Norm: Linear Layer)
.

For a linear layer with weight matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, given fixed Frobenius norm
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}=F
, the operator norm satisfies:

\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}=\frac{F}{\sqrt{\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})}}.

(5)

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.2
. This establishes the basic principle: as stable rank decreases while the Frobenius norm is kept fixed (or approximately fixed over short training windows, e.g., under Adam-like updates with bounded step sizes), the operator norm increases proportionally.

Attention Layers.

Theorem 4.5

(Jacobian Norm Bound: Attention Layer)
.

Consider a single-head attention layer with projections
\mathbf{W}_{Q},\mathbf{W}_{K},\mathbf{W}_{V},\mathbf{W}_{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{k}}
. Let
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be the input,
\mathbf{A}=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{H}^{T}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)
be the attention matrix. The Jacobian of the attention output
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{Y}}{\partial\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{V}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}

(6)

\displaystyle+\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\|\mathbf{H}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{V}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}.

Defining the logit margin
\gamma_{\min}=\min_{i}(\max_{j}\mathbf{S}_{i,j}-\text{second\_max}_{j}\mathbf{S}_{i,j})
where
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{H}^{T}
, the attention gradient pathway is bounded by:

\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{4\min((n-1)e^{-\gamma_{\min}},1)}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\|\mathbf{W}_{Q}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{K}\|_{2}.

(7)

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.3
. Substituting
\|\mathbf{W}_{i}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{W}_{i}\|_{F}/\sqrt{\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W}_{i})}
shows that low stable rank in any projection matrix amplifies the Jacobian norm.

MLP Layers.

Theorem 4.6

(Jacobian Norm Bound: MLP Layer)
.

Consider a two-layer MLP with weights
\mathbf{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\text{ff}}}
,
\mathbf{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}}\times d}
, and activation
\phi
. The Jacobian satisfies:

\left\|\mathbf{J}_{\text{MLP}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{L_{\phi}\|\mathbf{W}_{1}\|_{F}\|\mathbf{W}_{2}\|_{F}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W}_{1})\cdot\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W}_{2})}},

(8)

where
L_{\phi}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\phi
(
L_{\phi}\approx 1.13
for GELU,
L_{\phi}\approx 1.1
for SiLU).

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.4
. Across all layer types, the Jacobian norm is inversely related to the square root of stable rank, creating conditions for exponential gradient growth when combined with Jacobian alignment (Theorem
4.2
).

4.1.3
High Total Jacobian Norm
\Rightarrow
Large Weight Gradient Norm

We now show that high total Jacobian norms translate into large weight gradients. By the chain rule, the gradient with respect to weight vector
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}
at layer

decomposes as:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}\right)^{T}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}\right)^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}.

(9)

Assumption 4.7

(Gradient Alignment Conditions (Informal))
.

We assume: (1) uniform local gradient lower bound
\gamma>0
; (2) local-Jacobian alignment:
\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}
is aligned with the top right singular direction of the
local
layer Jacobian
\mathbf{J}^{(i+1)}
, with alignment
\geq a
; (3) terminal alignment: the last layer Jacobian
\mathbf{J}^{(L)}
’s top left singular direction is aligned with the loss gradient
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}
, with alignment
\geq a
. The formal statement is provided in Assumption
B.2
in the Appendix.

Theorem 4.8

(Weight Gradient Norm Lower Bound)

Under Assumption
4.7

when
\|\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)}\|_{2}\geq M>1

\geq a

\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}\right\|_{2}\geq a\gamma(aM)^{L-i}\cdot\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}.

(10)

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.5
. Figure

(Total Gradient Norm Lower Bound)
.

Summing over all layers:

\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{(i)}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\geq C\cdot\frac{(aM)^{2L}-1}{(aM)^{2}-1},

4.2
Part II: The Positive Feedback Mechanism

Having established why low stable rank and Jacobian alignment lead to training failure, we now analyze why these conditions tend to
intensify
during training.

4.2.1
Low-Rank Hidden States Lead to Low-Rank Gradients

We first establish that low-rank structure propagates through the network and affects gradient structure.

Theorem 4.10

(Low-Rank Propagation in Attention Layers)
.

Consider an attention layer with query, key, value, and output projection matrices
\mathbf{W}_{Q},\mathbf{W}_{K},\mathbf{W}_{V},\mathbf{W}_{O}
. If the hidden states
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell-1)}
and cohidden states
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{(\ell)}
have rank at most
r
, then the gradients
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{Q}}L,\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{K}}L,\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{V}}L,\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{O}}L
all have rank at most
r
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.7
.

Remark 4.11
.

This result extends to MLP layers due to their two-layer structure. For MLP up-projection
\mathbf{W}_{1}
:
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{1}}L=\mathbf{H}^{T}\cdot(\text{something})
, so rank is bounded by
\text{rank}(\mathbf{H})
. The key insight is that outer-product gradients inherit the rank of their lower-rank factor.

4.2.2
Aligned Input-Weight-Output Structure Accelerates Stable Rank Decline

In general, characterizing how gradient descent affects stable rank dynamics is challenging, as the update direction depends on the complex interplay between input statistics, weight structure, and backpropagated gradients. Here we provide a simplified analysis in a stylized linear setting under strong alignment assumptions that captures the essential feedback mechanism. While these assumptions are restrictive and do not aim to exactly describe full transformer dynamics, they offer theoretical insight into why stable rank can tend to decline during training.

Theorem 4.12

(Stable Rank Feedback Mechanism)
.

Consider a linear layer with weight matrix
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^{T}
where the input hidden states and output cohidden states satisfy:

•

Input covariance:
\Sigma_{in}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{h}_{in}\mathbf{h}_{in}^{T}]=\mathbf{V}\Lambda_{in}\mathbf{V}^{T}
(aligned with
\mathbf{W}
’s right singular vectors)

•

Output gradient covariance:
\Sigma_{out}=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out}^{T}]=\mathbf{U}\Lambda_{out}\mathbf{U}^{T}
(aligned with
\mathbf{W}
’s left singular vectors)

If the correlation between input and output gradient projections
\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})
is negative and satisfy

\frac{\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})}{\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})}>\frac{s_{1}}{s_{i}},\forall 1<i\leq n

(12)

then gradient descent causes the stable rank of
\mathbf{W}
to
decrease
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.8
. This theorem should be interpreted as an existence result in a highly aligned regime that illustrates one concrete way in which gradient descent can decrease stable rank, rather than as a statement about typical training trajectories.

The MSign Optimizer: Breaking the Feedback Loop

Based on our theoretical analysis and empirical observations, we propose a new optimizer to prevent stable rank collapse: the MSign optimizer. This method directly addresses the root cause by periodically restoring the stable rank of weight matrices via the matrix sign operation.

5.1
The Matrix Sign Operation

Definition 5.1

(Matrix Sign Operator)
.

For any matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
reduced
(thin) SVD
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^{T}
, where
r=\text{rank}(\mathbf{W})
,
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
,
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, and
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, we define:

\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}.

(13)

This operation sets all non-zero singular values to 1, creating a partial isometry that maximizes stable rank for a given matrix rank. Note that using the reduced SVD ensures the product
\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^{T}
has the correct shape
m\times n
.

5.2
Practical Implementation

Scaling.

The matrix sign operation alters the scale of the weight matrix, necessitating rescaling. A straightforward approach is to preserve the original Frobenius norm:

\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}}=\frac{\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}}{\|\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{W})\|_{F}}\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{W}).

(14)

In our current implementation, we adopt this Frobenius-norm preserving rescaling for MSign; however, when the stable rank is sufficiently low, this choice may excessively amplify minor singular values, and designing more principled rescaling schemes is left for future work.

Approximation Strategies.

Computing full SVD at every step is prohibitively expensive. We employ several practical strategies to reduce computational cost:

Periodic Application.

MSign is applied every
P
steps (e.g.,
P=100
) rather than at every update. Our experiments demonstrate that this periodic application maintains effectiveness while substantially reducing computational overhead.

Selective Layer Targeting.

Based on our empirical findings, MSign can be selectively applied to the most critical layers:

•

Attention-only
: Apply only to self-attention weights (
\mathbf{W}_{Q},\mathbf{W}_{K},\mathbf{W}_{V},\mathbf{W}_{O}
)

•

2D-parameters-only
: Apply to all 2D parameter tensors (excluding biases and layer norm parameters)

7
Conclusion

We identify and analyze the stable rank collapse feedback loop as a fundamental mechanism underlying LLM training instability. Low weight stable rank amplifies layer Jacobian norms, which combined with inter-layer alignment causes exponential gradient growth. The MSign optimizer breaks this feedback loop by periodically restoring stable rank via the matrix sign operation, effectively preventing training failures with minimal overhead (
<7\%
). Future directions include adaptive scheduling, fused kernels for reduced latency, and extensions to other training pathologies.

While our theoretical results provide a rigorous foundation for understanding the positive feedback mechanism of stable rank collapse (see Theorem 4.12), they rely on strong assumptions—particularly, the uniform negative correlation of input and output gradient projections. These structural conditions may not universally hold in practice. Explicitly characterizing the full range of scenarios where the feedback loop provably dominates remains an open problem. We leave a complete characterization of these conditions and their relaxation to future work, which will further clarify the generality and boundaries of our theory.

Appendix B
Proofs of Main Results

B.1
Proof of Theorem
4.2
(Jacobian Product Norm Lower Bound)

We first establish a key lemma for two-matrix products.

Lemma B.1

(Alignment-Preserving Product Bound)
.

For matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
with SVD
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}_{A}\mathbf{S}_{A}\mathbf{V}_{A}^{T}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{U}_{B}\mathbf{S}_{B}\mathbf{V}_{B}^{T}
:

\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}\geq\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{2}\cdot|\mathbf{v}_{A,1}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{B,1}|,

(20)

where
\mathbf{v}_{A,1}
is the top right singular vector of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{u}_{B,1}
is the top left singular vector of
\mathbf{B}
.

Proof:
Let
\sigma_{A,1}=\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}
and
\sigma_{B,1}=\|\mathbf{B}\|_{2}
. We have:

\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}\geq\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}_{B,1}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{A}\sigma_{B,1}\mathbf{u}_{B,1}\|_{2}=\sigma_{B,1}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{B,1}\|_{2}.

(21)

Expanding
\mathbf{u}_{B,1}
in the basis of
\mathbf{A}
’s right singular vectors:

\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{B,1}\|_{2}=\|\sum_{i}\sigma_{A,i}(\mathbf{v}_{A,i}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{B,1})\mathbf{u}_{A,i}\|_{2}\geq\sigma_{A,1}|\mathbf{v}_{A,1}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{B,1}|,

(22)

where the inequality uses
|\mathbf{v}_{A,1}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{B,1}|\leq 1
and that
\{\mathbf{u}_{A,i}\}
is orthonormal.

Since
\text{Align}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=|\mathbf{v}_{A,1}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{B,1}|
by definition, we obtain:

\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}\geq\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{2}\cdot\text{Align}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}).

(23)

Applying this recursively to the Jacobian product:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{J}^{(L)}\cdots\mathbf{J}^{(1)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathbf{J}^{(L)}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{J}^{(L-1)}\cdots\mathbf{J}^{(1)}\|_{2}\cdot\text{Align}(L,L-1)

(24)

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathbf{J}^{(L)}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{J}^{(L-1)}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{J}^{(L-2)}\cdots\mathbf{J}^{(1)}\|_{2}\cdot a^{2}

(25)

\displaystyle\geq\cdots\geq M^{L}\cdot a^{L-1}=\frac{(aM)^{L}}{a}.

(26)

\square

B.2
Proof of Theorem
4.4
(Stable Rank Controls Jacobian Norm: Linear Layer)

Consider a linear layer that computes
\mathbf{h}_{out}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}_{in}
, where
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the weight matrix. The Jacobian of this transformation is:

\mathbf{J}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{out}}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{in}}=\mathbf{W},

(27)

since the linear map
\mathbf{h}\mapsto\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}
has constant derivative equal to
\mathbf{W}
itself.

The operator norm (also called spectral norm or 2-norm) of a matrix equals its largest singular value:

\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}=\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{W})=\max_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}.

(28)

This represents the maximum factor by which the matrix can stretch any unit vector, i.e., the worst-case signal amplification.

From the definition of stable rank (Definition 1):

\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})=\frac{\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}},

(29)

where
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\ldots\geq 0
are the singular values of
\mathbf{W}
.

Let
F=\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}
denote the Frobenius norm, which is fixed by assumption. Substituting into the stable rank definition:

\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})=\frac{F^{2}}{\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}^{2}}.

(30)

Solving for the operator norm:

\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{F^{2}}{\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}=\frac{F}{\sqrt{\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})}}.

(31)

This formula reveals a fundamental trade-off: for a matrix with fixed total “energy” (Frobenius norm
F
), the operator norm, which determines signal amplification, is inversely proportional to the square root of stable rank.

Intuitively, stable rank measures how “spread out” the singular values are. When
\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})\approx\text{rank}(\mathbf{W})
(high stable rank), the singular values are roughly uniform, and
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}\approx F/\sqrt{\text{rank}(\mathbf{W})}
is relatively small. When
\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})\approx 1
(low stable rank), almost all energy is concentrated in the top singular value, and
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}\approx F
, the maximum possible for given Frobenius norm.
\square

B.3
Proof of Theorem
4.5
(Jacobian Norm Bound: Attention Layer)

We analyze the Jacobian of a single-head attention layer. The output is
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O}
, where
\mathbf{A}=\text{softmax}(\frac{\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{H}^{T}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}})
. The derivative of
\mathbf{Y}
with respect to
\mathbf{H}
is a 4th-order tensor. To manage this, we use the Fréchet derivative, which is a linear operator
D_{\mathbf{H}}(\Delta\mathbf{H})
that approximates the change in
\mathbf{Y}
for a small perturbation
\Delta\mathbf{H}
.

Applying the product rule for Fréchet derivatives:

D_{\mathbf{H}}(\Delta\mathbf{H})=[D_{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{A}(\Delta\mathbf{H})](\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O})+\mathbf{A}[D_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O})(\Delta\mathbf{H})].

(32)

The second term is straightforward:
D_{\mathbf{H}}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O})(\Delta\mathbf{H})=(\Delta\mathbf{H})\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O}
.
Taking operator norms:

\|D\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{H})[\Delta\mathbf{H}]\|_{2}\leq\|D\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{H})[\Delta\mathbf{H}]\|_{2}\|\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}\|\Delta\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}.

(33)

Dividing by
\|\Delta\mathbf{H}\|_{2}
and taking the supremum gives:

\|D\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{H})\|_{2}\leq\|D\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{H})\|_{2}\|\mathbf{H}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{V}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{V}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}.

(34)

Bounding the value gradient pathway.
For the second term, define
f(\mathbf{H})=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O}
. Since this is a linear function of
\mathbf{H}
, its Fréchet derivative at any point
\mathbf{H}
is:

Df(\mathbf{H})[\Delta\mathbf{H}]=\Delta\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O}.

(35)

The operator norm of this linear map is:

\|Df(\mathbf{H})\|_{2}=\sup_{\|\Delta\mathbf{H}\|_{2}=1}\|\Delta\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{W}_{V}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}.

(36)

Bounding the attention gradient pathway.
The first term requires analyzing how the attention matrix
\mathbf{A}
changes with
\mathbf{H}
. The attention matrix is computed as:

\mathbf{A}=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{S}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right),\quad\text{where }\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{T}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{H}^{T}.

(37)

Note that
\mathbf{S}
depends
quadratically
on
\mathbf{H}
, making this pathway more complex to analyze.

Softmax Fréchet derivative.
Consider the softmax function
f(\mathbf{s})=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{s})=\frac{e^{\mathbf{s}}}{\sum_{j}e^{s_{j}}}
applied row-wise to
\mathbf{S}
. For the
i
-th row, let
a_{i}=f(\mathbf{s}_{i})
where
\mathbf{s}_{i}
is the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{S}
. The Fréchet derivative
Df(\mathbf{s}_{i}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear map with matrix representation:

Df(\mathbf{s}_{i})[\Delta\mathbf{s}_{i}]=(\text{diag}(a_{i})-a_{i}a_{i}^{T})\Delta\mathbf{s}_{i}.

(38)

This can be verified by computing: for each component,
\frac{\partial a_{i,k}}{\partial s_{i,j}}=a_{i,k}(\delta_{jk}-a_{i,j})
.

Bounding the operator norm.
Let
\mathbf{J}_{i}=\text{diag}(a_{i})-a_{i}a_{i}^{T}
. To bound
\|Df(\mathbf{s}_{i})\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{J}_{i}\|_{2}
, we examine its quadratic form. For any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{J}_{i}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{T}\text{diag}(a_{i})\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{T}a_{i}a_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}

(39)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}a_{i,j}x_{j}^{2}-\left(\sum_{j}a_{i,j}x_{j}\right)^{2}

(40)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[X])^{2}=\text{Var}(X),

(41)

where we interpret
j
as a random index sampled with probability
a_{i,j}
and
X=x_{j}
as the corresponding random variable. Since variance is non-negative,
\mathbf{J}_{i}\succeq 0
.

For a positive semi-definite matrix, the spectral norm equals the largest eigenvalue, which is bounded by the trace:

\|Df(\mathbf{s}_{i})\|_{2}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{J}_{i})\leq\text{tr}(\mathbf{J}_{i})=\sum_{j}a_{i,j}-\sum_{j}a_{i,j}^{2}=1-\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

(42)

Here we used
\sum_{j}a_{i,j}=1
. Factoring gives:

\|Df(\mathbf{s}_{i})\|_{2}\leq 1-\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}=(1-\|a_{i}\|_{2})(1+\|a_{i}\|_{2})\leq 2(1-\max a_{i}),

(43)

where the last inequality follows from
\|a_{i}\|_{2}\geq\max a_{i}
and
1+\|a_{i}\|_{2}\leq 2
.

Connecting to logit margin.
The bound
1-\max a_{i}
measures how “spread out” the attention distribution is. When attention is sharply peaked,
\max a_{i}\approx 1
and
\|Df(\mathbf{s}_{i})\|_{2}
is small. We can express this in terms of the logit margin
\gamma_{i}=\max_{j}\mathbf{S}_{i,j}-\text{second\_max}_{j}\mathbf{S}_{i,j}
. By definition of softmax:

\max a_{i}=\frac{e^{\max\mathbf{s}_{i}}}{\sum_{j}e^{\mathbf{s}_{i,j}}}\geq\frac{e^{\max\mathbf{s}_{i}}}{e^{\max\mathbf{s}_{i}}+\sum_{j\neq\arg\max_{k}\mathbf{s}_{i,k}}e^{\mathbf{s}_{i,j}}}.

(44)

Since there are at most
n-1
non-maximal terms, each at most
e^{\max\mathbf{s}_{i}-\gamma_{i}}
:

\max a_{i}\geq\frac{e^{\max\mathbf{s}_{i}}}{e^{\max\mathbf{s}_{i}}+(n-1)e^{\max\mathbf{s}_{i}-\gamma_{i}}}=\frac{1}{1+(n-1)e^{-\gamma_{i}}}.

(45)

Rearranging and using
\frac{x}{1+x}\leq\min(x,1)
for
x\geq 0
:

1-\max a_{i}\leq\frac{(n-1)e^{-\gamma_{i}}}{1+(n-1)e^{-\gamma_{i}}}\leq\min((n-1)e^{-\gamma_{i}},1)\triangle\chi.

(46)

Since the rows of
\mathbf{A}
are computed independently, the Fréchet derivative of the full softmax is block-diagonal and its operator norm is the maximum over rows:

\left\|D(\text{softmax})(\mathbf{S})\right\|_{2}=\max_{i}\|Df(\mathbf{s}_{i})\|_{2}\leq 2\chi.

(47)

Jacobian of attention logits.
For the attention logits
\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{H})=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{H}^{T}
, we compute the Fréchet derivative. Using the product rule for bilinear forms:

D\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{H})[\Delta\mathbf{H}]=\Delta\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{H}^{T}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}(\Delta\mathbf{H})^{T}.

(48)

The operator norm is bounded by:

\displaystyle\|D\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{H})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|\Delta\mathbf{H}\|_{2}=1}\|\Delta\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{H}^{T}+\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}(\Delta\mathbf{H})^{T}\|_{2}

(49)

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{H}^{T}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\|_{2}

(50)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\mathbf{W}_{Q}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{K}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{H}\|_{2}.

(51)

Combining via chain rule.
Define
g(\mathbf{S})=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{S}/\sqrt{d_{k}})
and recall
\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{H})=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{H}^{T}
. By the chain rule for Fréchet derivatives:

D\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{H})[\Delta\mathbf{H}]=Dg(\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{H}))[D\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{H})[\Delta\mathbf{H}]],

(52)

and the operator norms satisfy:

\|D\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{H})\|_{2}\leq\|Dg(\mathbf{S})\|_{2}\cdot\|D\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{H})\|_{2}.

(53)

From the softmax analysis,
\|Dg(\mathbf{S})\|_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\left\|\frac{\partial\text{softmax}}{\partial\mathbf{S}}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{2\chi}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}
. Combining:

\|D\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{H})\|_{2}\leq\frac{2\chi}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\cdot 2\|\mathbf{W}_{Q}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{K}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{H}\|_{2}=\frac{4\chi}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\|\mathbf{W}_{Q}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{K}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{H}\|_{2}.

(54)

Final bound.
Substituting the bound on
\|D\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{H})\|_{2}
and noting that
\|\mathbf{V}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{V}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{H}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{V}\|_{2}
:

\|D\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{H})\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{V}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}+\frac{4\chi\|\mathbf{H}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\|\mathbf{W}_{Q}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{K}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{V}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}.

(55)

\square

Discussion: Connection to Stable Rank.

Substituting
\|\mathbf{W}_{i}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{W}_{i}\|_{F}/\sqrt{\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W}_{i})}
into the bound reveals that low stable rank in
any
of the projection matrices amplifies the Jacobian norm. In particular, for attention layers where V and O projections typically have lower stable rank than Q and K, the dominant contribution to gradient magnitude comes through the V-O pathway, which scales as
\|\mathbf{W}_{V}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{O}\|_{2}\propto(\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W}_{V})\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W}_{O}))^{-1/2}
.

B.4
Proof of Theorem
4.6
(Jacobian Norm Bound: MLP Layer)

Consider the forward pass through a two-layer MLP. Given input
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we first compute the hidden representation:

\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{h}\mathbf{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}}},

(56)

where
\mathbf{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\text{ff}}}
projects from hidden dimension
d
to feedforward dimension
d_{\text{ff}}
(typically
d_{\text{ff}}=4d
).

Next, we apply the element-wise activation function
\phi
(such as GELU or SiLU):

\mathbf{a}=\phi(\mathbf{z})=[\phi(z_{1}),\phi(z_{2}),\ldots,\phi(z_{d_{\text{ff}}})]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}}}.

(57)

Finally, we project back to the hidden dimension:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{a}\mathbf{W}_{2}=\phi(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(58)

where
\mathbf{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}}\times d}
.

To compute the Jacobian
\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial\mathbf{h}}
, we apply the chain rule through this three-stage computation:

\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial\mathbf{h}}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial\mathbf{a}}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{a}}{\partial\mathbf{z}}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}}{\partial\mathbf{h}}.

(59)

Each factor has a simple form:

•

\frac{\partial\mathbf{z}}{\partial\mathbf{h}}=\mathbf{W}_{1}
: the Jacobian of a linear transformation is the weight matrix itself.

•

\frac{\partial\mathbf{a}}{\partial\mathbf{z}}=\text{diag}(\phi^{\prime}(\mathbf{z}))
: since
\phi
acts element-wise, its Jacobian is diagonal with entries
\phi^{\prime}(z_{i})
.

•

\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial\mathbf{a}}=\mathbf{W}_{2}
: again a linear transformation.

Combining these:

\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial\mathbf{h}}=\mathbf{W}_{1}\cdot\text{diag}(\phi^{\prime}(\mathbf{z}))\cdot\mathbf{W}_{2}.

(60)

To bound the operator norm, we use the submultiplicativity property
\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{2}
:

\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial\mathbf{h}}\right\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{W}_{1}\cdot\text{diag}(\phi^{\prime}(\mathbf{z}))\cdot\mathbf{W}_{2}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{W}_{1}\|_{2}\cdot\|\text{diag}(\phi^{\prime}(\mathbf{z}))\|_{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{W}_{2}\|_{2}.

(61)

The operator norm of a diagonal matrix is the maximum absolute value of its diagonal entries:

\|\text{diag}(\phi^{\prime}(\mathbf{z}))\|_{2}=\max_{i}|\phi^{\prime}(z_{i})|\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}|\phi^{\prime}(z)|=L_{\phi},

(62)

where
L_{\phi}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\phi
. For commonly used activations: GELU has
L_{\text{GELU}}\approx 1.13
(achieved near
z\approx-0.75
), and SiLU has
L_{\text{SiLU}}\approx 1.1
(achieved near
z\approx 1.28
).

Thus:

\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial\mathbf{h}}\right\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{W}_{1}\|_{2}\cdot L_{\phi}\cdot\|\mathbf{W}_{2}\|_{2}.

(63)

To express this in terms of stable rank, recall from Definition 1 that
\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})=\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2}/\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}^{2}
, which can be rearranged to:

\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}=\frac{\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})}}.

(64)

Substituting this for both weight matrices:

\left\|\mathbf{J}_{\text{MLP}}\right\|_{2}\leq L_{\phi}\cdot\frac{\|\mathbf{W}_{1}\|_{F}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W}_{1})}}\cdot\frac{\|\mathbf{W}_{2}\|_{F}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W}_{2})}}=\frac{L_{\phi}\|\mathbf{W}_{1}\|_{F}\|\mathbf{W}_{2}\|_{F}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W}_{1})\cdot\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W}_{2})}}.

(65)

This shows that when stable ranks are low (denominator small) while Frobenius norms remain approximately constant or grow moderately over short training windows (numerator large), the Jacobian norm increases, potentially leading to gradient explosion.
\square

Discussion: Unified View Across Layer Types.

Across all three layer types, linear, attention, and MLP, we observe the same fundamental pattern: the layer Jacobian norm is inversely related to the square root of the stable rank. This means that as training progresses and stable ranks collapse (Observation 1), the per-layer Jacobian norms tend to grow, even when Frobenius norms remain approximately bounded over moderate training windows. Combined with Jacobian alignment (Observation 2), this creates the conditions for exponential gradient growth characterized by Theorem
4.2
.

B.5
Proof of Theorem
4.8
(Weight Gradient Norm Lower Bound)

We first state the formal version of Assumption
4.7
.

Assumption B.2

(Gradient Alignment Conditions (Formal))
.

For a deep network with
L
layers, we assume the following conditions hold for all layers
i\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
:

1.

Uniform local gradient lower bound
: There exists
\gamma>0
such that
\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}\right\|_{2}\geq\gamma
for all weight vectors
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}
.

2.

Local-Jacobian alignment
: Let
\mathbf{J}^{(i+1)}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i+1)}}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}
be the
local
layer Jacobian at layer
i+1
, with top right singular vector
\mathbf{v}_{1}^{(i+1)}
. The local gradient
\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}
satisfies:

\left|\left\langle\frac{\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}}{\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}\right\|_{2}},\mathbf{v}_{1}^{(i+1)}\right\rangle\right|\geq a.

(66)

3.

Terminal alignment
: Let
\mathbf{J}^{(L)}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L-1)}}
be the last layer Jacobian, with top left singular vector
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(L)}
. The loss gradient satisfies:

\left|\left\langle\frac{\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}}{\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}},\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(L)}\right\rangle\right|\geq a.

(67)

The proof proceeds by carefully tracking how the loss gradient propagates backward through the network.

Starting from the decomposition (
9
):

\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}\right)^{T}\left(\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)}\right)^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2},

(68)

where
\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)}=\mathbf{J}^{(L)}\mathbf{J}^{(L-1)}\cdots\mathbf{J}^{(i+1)}
is the cumulative Jacobian.

The key insight is that the alignment conditions in Assumption
B.2
are stated in terms of
local
layer Jacobians
\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)}
, which then propagate through the chain rule to yield bounds on the cumulative Jacobian
\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)}
.

Step 1: Terminal alignment implies loss gradient projects onto cumulative Jacobian.

Let
\mathbf{J}^{(L)}=\mathbf{U}^{(L)}\mathbf{S}^{(L)}(\mathbf{V}^{(L)})^{T}
be the SVD of the last layer Jacobian. By Assumption
B.2
.3, the loss gradient has alignment
\geq a
with
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(L)}
:

\left|\left\langle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}},\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(L)}\right\rangle\right|\geq a\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}.

(69)

Applying
(\mathbf{J}^{(L)})^{T}
, the component along
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(L)}
maps to
\mathbf{v}_{1}^{(L)}
with amplification
\sigma_{1}^{(L)}=\|\mathbf{J}^{(L)}\|_{2}\geq M
:

\left\|(\mathbf{J}^{(L)})^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}\geq a\cdot M\cdot\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}.

(70)

Step 2: Recursive application through layers.

By the Jacobian alignment condition (Definition 3.2 and the conditions used in Theorem
4.2
), adjacent Jacobians have alignment
\geq a
. This means the top right singular direction of
\mathbf{J}^{(\ell+1)}
aligns with the top left singular direction of
\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)}
. Applying the alignment-preserving product bound recursively from layer
L
down to layer
i+1
:

\left\|(\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)})^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}\geq a\cdot\|\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)}\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}.

(71)

Moreover, by Theorem
4.2
, the cumulative Jacobian norm satisfies:

\|\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)}\|_{2}\geq\frac{(aM)^{L-i}}{a}.

(72)

Step 3: Local-Jacobian alignment at layer
i
.

The result
(\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)})^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}
has its energy concentrated along the direction that propagated through the aligned chain. By Assumption
B.2
.2, the local gradient
\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}
is aligned with the top right singular direction of the
local
Jacobian
\mathbf{J}^{(i+1)}
. Since the Jacobian chain is aligned (Theorem
4.2
), this direction is consistent with the dominant direction of the backpropagated signal.

Combined with the uniform lower bound
\left\|\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}\right\|_{2}\geq\gamma
, this yields:

\left\|\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}\right)^{T}\left(\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)}\right)^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}\geq a\cdot\gamma\cdot a\cdot\|\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)}\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}.

(73)

Step 4: Final bound.

Substituting the lower bound from Theorem
4.2
:

\|\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)}\|_{2}\geq\frac{(aM)^{L-i}}{a},

(74)

we obtain:

\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}\right\|_{2}\geq a^{2}\gamma\cdot\frac{(aM)^{L-i}}{a}\cdot\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}=a\gamma(aM)^{L-i}\cdot\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}.

(75)

\square

Discussion: Gradient Decomposition Interpretation.

The three-part decomposition in (
9
) has a clear interpretation:

•

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}
: The
loss gradient
at the final layer output. This is the “signal” that backpropagation aims to transmit.

•

\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}=\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)}
: The
cumulative Jacobian
from layer
i
to layer
L
. This acts as the “transmission channel” whose gain is bounded by Theorem
4.2
.

•

\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}
: The
local gradient
at layer
i
. This represents how changes in the weight affect the layer’s output.

Discussion: Justification of Alignment Assumptions.

All three assumptions in Assumption
B.2
are stylized but capture a highly aligned regime that we empirically observe near failure in our experiments. Importantly, these assumptions are stated in terms of
local
layer Jacobians
\mathbf{J}^{(\ell)}
rather than the cumulative Jacobian
\mathbf{J}^{(i+1:L)}
. This is consistent with our theoretical framework where Theorem
4.2
establishes how local Jacobian properties (norms and alignments) propagate to yield bounds on the cumulative Jacobian.

The terminal alignment assumption excludes the degenerate case where
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\approx\mathbf{0}
or is nearly orthogonal to the last layer Jacobian’s dominant direction. The uniform local gradient lower bound excludes the trivial case where the weight has no effect on the output. The local-Jacobian alignment condition requires
\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(i)}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out}^{(i)}}
to align with the
local
Jacobian
\mathbf{J}^{(i+1)}
, which is consistent with the structured gradient flow we observe empirically in regimes with strong Jacobian alignment, but is not guaranteed in arbitrary settings.

B.6
Proof of Theorem
4.9
(Total Gradient Norm Lower Bound)

We aggregate the per-weight-vector bounds from Theorem
4.8
across all weights and layers.

Recall that a weight matrix
\mathbf{W}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
can be viewed as a collection of
n_{w}=n
column vectors
\{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out,j}^{(i)}\}_{j=1}^{n_{w}}
, where each vector
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out,j}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The Frobenius norm of the gradient matrix at layer
i
equals the sum of squared 2-norms of the gradients with respect to each column:

\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{(i)}}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{w}}\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out,j}^{(i)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(76)

This follows from the definition of Frobenius norm:
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^{2}=\sum_{j}\|\mathbf{a}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}
where
\mathbf{a}_{j}
are the columns of
\mathbf{A}
.

From Theorem
4.8
, we have a lower bound for each weight vector:

\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out,j}^{(i)}}\right\|_{2}\geq a\gamma(aM)^{L-i}\cdot\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}.

(77)

Squaring both sides (which preserves the inequality since both sides are non-negative):

\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out,j}^{(i)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq a^{2}\gamma^{2}(aM)^{2(L-i)}\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(78)

Note that the right-hand side is independent of the column index
j
. Summing over all
n_{w}
columns:

\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{(i)}}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{w}}\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{out,j}^{(i)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n_{w}}a^{2}\gamma^{2}(aM)^{2(L-i)}\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=n_{w}a^{2}\gamma^{2}(aM)^{2(L-i)}\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(79)

Now we sum over all
L
layers. Since the bound for each layer is independent:

\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{(i)}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{L}n_{w}a^{2}\gamma^{2}(aM)^{2(L-i)}\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(80)

Factoring out terms that do not depend on
i
:

\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{(i)}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\geq n_{w}a^{2}\gamma^{2}\left\|\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}^{(L)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{L}(aM)^{2(L-i)}.

(81)

The remaining sum can be evaluated by a change of variables. Let
k=L-i
. When
i=1
, we have
k=L-1
; when
i=L
, we have
k=0
. Therefore:

\sum_{i=1}^{L}(aM)^{2(L-i)}=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}(aM)^{2k}=1+(aM)^{2}+(aM)^{4}+\cdots+(aM)^{2(L-1)}.

(82)

This is a geometric series with first term
1
, common ratio
r=(aM)^{2}
, and
L
terms. When
r\neq 1
, the sum formula gives:

\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}r^{k}=\frac{r^{L}-1}{r-1}=\frac{(aM)^{2L}-1}{(aM)^{2}-1}.

(83)

When
aM>1
, we have
(aM)^{2L}\gg 1
for large
L
, so the sum is approximately:

\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}(aM)^{2k}\approx\frac{(aM)^{2L}}{(aM)^{2}-1}=O((aM)^{2L}).

(84)

This shows that the total gradient norm grows
exponentially
with network depth
L
.
\square

Discussion: Connection to Observations.

From Observation 1, the stable rank of attention weights drops to near 1, implying
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}/\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}\approx 1
and thus high layer Jacobian norms. From Observation 2, Jacobian alignment
a
increases toward 1. Together, the product
aM
exceeds 1, triggering the exponential gradient explosion characterized by Theorem
4.9
. This explains why training becomes unstable as stable rank declines and Jacobians align.

B.7
Proof of Theorem
4.10
(Low-Rank Propagation in Attention Layers)

The key insight is that gradients in neural networks are computed as outer products. For any weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
in a linear layer
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}
, the gradient with respect to
\mathbf{W}
is:

\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}L=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{x}^{T}=\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{x}^{T},

(85)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{y}}
is the cohidden state (gradient of loss w.r.t. the output).

This outer product structure implies a fundamental constraint:
\text{rank}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{x}^{T})\leq\min(\text{rank}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}),\text{rank}(\mathbf{x}^{T}))=1
for single vectors. When we batch over
B
samples, the gradient becomes:

\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}L=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{(b)}(\mathbf{x}^{(b)})^{T}=\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{T}\mathbf{X},

(86)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d_{out}}
and
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d_{in}}
. By the rank inequality for matrix products:

\text{rank}(\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{T}\mathbf{X})\leq\min(\text{rank}(\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}^{T}),\text{rank}(\mathbf{X}))\leq\min(\text{rank}(\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}),\text{rank}(\mathbf{X})).

(87)

For attention layers specifically, let’s trace through each gradient:

Query gradient:
The query projection is
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}_{Q}
. By the chain rule:

\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{Q}}L=\mathbf{H}^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}.

(88)

This is a product of
\mathbf{H}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
(where
n
is sequence length) and
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{k}}
. If
\mathbf{H}
has rank at most
r
, then
\mathbf{H}^{T}
also has rank at most
r
, and hence
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{Q}}L
has rank at most
r
.

The same argument applies to
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{K}}L
and
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{V}}L
, since they all have
\mathbf{H}^{T}
as the left factor:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{Q}}L

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}^{(\ell-1)T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{Q}^{(\ell-1)}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{rank}\leq\text{rank}(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell-1)})\leq r,

(89)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{K}}L

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}^{(\ell-1)T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{K}^{(\ell-1)}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{rank}\leq r,

(90)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{V}}L

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}^{(\ell-1)T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{V}^{(\ell-1)}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{rank}\leq r.

(91)

Output projection gradient:
For
\mathbf{W}_{O}
, the computation is
\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V})\mathbf{W}_{O}
. The gradient is:

\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{O}}L=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V})^{T}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{y}}=(\text{Attn Output})^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{(\ell)}.

(92)

If the cohidden states
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{(\ell)}
have rank at most
r
, then
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{O}}L
has rank at most
r
.

This completes the proof: all four attention gradients have rank bounded by
\max(\text{rank}(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell-1)}),\text{rank}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{(\ell)}))\leq r
.
\square

B.8
Proof of Theorem
4.12
(Stable Rank Feedback Mechanism)

The gradient of the loss with respect to the weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
in a linear layer
\mathbf{h}_{out}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}_{in}
is given by the outer product of the output gradient (cohidden state) and the input:

\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}L=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out}\mathbf{h}_{in}^{T}],

(93)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{out}}
is the gradient backpropagated from later layers.

Since
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^{T}
(SVD), and we assume the input/output covariances are aligned with these singular vectors, we can project both
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out}
and
\mathbf{h}_{in}
onto the singular bases. Define the projected coordinates:

\alpha_{i}=\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\quad\beta_{j}=\mathbf{v}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in}.

(94)

Then the gradient can be written in the singular vector basis as:

\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}L=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out}\mathbf{h}_{in}^{T}]=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}]\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{T}=\mathbf{U}M\mathbf{V}^{T},

(95)

where
M_{ij}=\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}]=\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})
(assuming zero-mean projections for simplicity).

Under the alignment assumption, the covariance matrix
M
is approximately diagonal because the input and output gradient are each concentrated along their respective top singular directions. Specifically:

M_{ii}=\text{Cov}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})=\rho\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{i}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[\beta_{i}^{2}]}=\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in}),

(96)

where
\lambda_{out,i}=\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out})^{2}]
and
\lambda_{in,i}=\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})^{2}]
are the variances of the projections.

The gradient descent update is
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}=\mathbf{W}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}L
. Substituting the SVD forms:

\mathbf{W}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^{T}-\eta\mathbf{U}M\mathbf{V}^{T}=\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{S}-\eta M)\mathbf{V}^{T}.

(97)

Since
M
is approximately diagonal, the updated matrix
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
has the same singular vectors
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}
(to first order), but with modified singular values.

Using first-order perturbation theory for SVD (valid when
\eta M
is small relative to the gaps between singular values), the updated singular values are:

s_{i}^{\prime}=s_{i}-\eta M_{ii}=s_{i}-\eta\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in}).

(98)

Since
\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})
(negative correlation, typical in gradient descent where the gradient points toward decreasing loss), we have:

\Delta s_{i}=s_{i}^{\prime}-s_{i}=-\eta\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})>0.

(99)

Thus all singular values increase, which is consistent with the observed weight norm growth.

Now we analyze how stable rank changes. Recall
\mathrm{srank}(\mathbf{W})=\frac{\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}}{s_{1}^{2}}
. Taking the differential:

d(\mathrm{srank})=\frac{2\sum_{i}s_{i}\,ds_{i}}{s_{1}^{2}}-\frac{2(\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2})s_{1}\,ds_{1}}{s_{1}^{4}}=\frac{2}{s_{1}^{3}}\left(\sum_{i}s_{i}(s_{1}\Delta s_{i}-s_{i}\Delta s_{1})\right).

(100)

Substituting
\Delta s_{i}=-\eta\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})
:

\displaystyle\Delta\mathrm{srank}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2}{s_{1}^{3}}\left(\sum_{i}s_{i}(s_{1}\Delta s_{i}-s_{i}\Delta s_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{s_{1}^{3}}\left(\sum_{i}-\eta s_{i}(s_{1}\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})-s_{i}\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})\right).

(101)

To determine the sign of
\Delta\mathrm{srank}
, we analyze the term in parentheses. Under the assumption, the covariance satisfy:

\frac{\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})}{\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})}>\frac{s_{1}}{s_{i}},\forall 1<i\leq n

(102)

This gives:

s_{1}\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})>s_{i}\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in}),\forall 1<i\leq n.

(103)

This means the term in parentheses is
\leq 0
, and therefore:

\Delta\mathrm{srank}=\frac{2}{s_{1}^{3}}\left(\sum_{i}-\eta s_{i}(s_{1}\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})-s_{i}\text{Cov}(\mathbf{u}_{1}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{out},\mathbf{v}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{in})\right)\leq 0.

(104)

Thus
stable rank decreases
under gradient descent with aligned input-output structure.
\square
