Spatiotemporal evolution and driving mechanisms of ecological quality in shanxi province based on XGBoost-SHAP
Authors: Yumiao Yuan, Zhongwu Zhang, Shixiong Hu, Kunwei Zhang, Shuo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurately assessing the spatiotemporal evolution of regional ecological quality is critical for advancing China’s ecological civilization and achieving its carbon peaking and carbon neutrality targets. As a typical resource-based region, Shanxi Province faces significant ecological fragility and spatial heterogeneity resulting from the interplay of an arid/semi-arid climate and intensive coal mining activities. This study utilized the Remote Sensing Ecological Index (RSEI) from 2000 to 2025 as a core indicator. The Theil–Sen slope estimator and Mann–Kendall test were employed to identify trends, while the Hurst exponent was used to assess the persistence of changes. Furthermore, the XGBoost-SHAP framework was applied to disentangle the driving factors. The results indicate that: (1) From 2000 to 2025, the ecological quality of Shanxi exhibited a fluctuating upward trend, with the mean RSEI increasing by approximately 8.7%. (2) Trend analysis indicated that the proportions of areas exhibiting upward and downward trends in RSEI were 42.3% and 35.64%, respectively, while 22.05% of the region remained stable. Significant improvement areas were mainly concentrated in the Lüliang and Taihang Mountains. (3) Future trend predictions reveal that stable areas comprise the majority of the province (55.89%). However, the areas of persistent improvement and persistent degradation account for 9.54% and 10.88%, respectively, indicating a characteristic of “polarization” in ecological environmental quality. (4) Regarding driving mechanisms, anthropogenic factors exerted a stronger influence than natural factors. Precipitation was the primary positive natural driver, whereas coal mining intensity and population density were key negative stressors. Crucially, coal mining intensity exhibited a non-linear interaction with precipitation, significantly weakening the ecosystem’s positive response to rainfall. The ecological environment constitutes a complex system formed by the interactions among society, the economy, and nature1, and its quality is directly linked to regional sustainable development and human well-being2. With the rapid socioeconomic development in China, anthropogenic activities—such as population growth, industrial expansion, and accelerated urbanization—have intensified disturbances to the ecological environment. These activities have led to ecosystem degradation and triggered a series of environmental crises, including soil erosion, desertification, and forest decline3–6. As a critical ecological barrier in northern China, the Yellow River Basin plays a pivotal role in regional ecological security, and its ecological protection and high-quality development have been elevated to a national strategic level7,8.

Situated in the middle reaches of the Yellow River and the Loess Plateau, Shanxi Province represents a typical resource-based region. However, long-term and high-intensity coal mining has exacerbated soil erosion, vegetation degradation, and overall environmental deterioration9. As a key coal energy base in China, Shanxi holds a strategic position in the ecological protection of the Yellow River Basin. Therefore, dynamic monitoring and evaluation of its ecological quality are of profound significance for promoting regional ecological restoration10.

In recent years, comprehensive assessment frameworks and driving force analysis methods for regional ecological quality have matured. Regarding remote sensing monitoring, Xu proposed the Remote Sensing Ecological Index (RSEI) in 2013, which integrates Greenness (NDVI), Wetness (Wet), Dryness (NDBSI), and Heat (LST) based on remote sensing imagery11. By utilizing Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to assign weights objectively, RSEI effectively overcomes the subjectivity inherent in traditional methods, enabling a more objective and comprehensive evaluation. Subsequently, numerous scholars have improved and applied RSEI. For instance, modified versions incorporating the Comprehensive Salinity Index (CSI) and water network density have been developed for arid regions12, and the index has been implemented on the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform for long-term monitoring in the Greater Bay Area and the Yangtze River Delta13,14.

Concerning the analysis of driving forces, traditional methods such as correlation analysis15, Structural Equation Modeling (SEM)16, Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR)17, Multiscale GWR (MGWR)18, and Geodetector19 have been widely applied. However, as research deepens, these methods exhibit limitations in handling high-dimensional data, capturing complex non-linear relationships, and assessing feature importance. Recently, the rapid development of machine learning (ML) technologies has provided new avenues for revealing complex driving mechanisms due to their robust non-linear fitting capabilities20. Among them, the XGBoost algorithm is widely used across various fields due to its high prediction accuracy, ability to handle high-dimensional data, and resistance to overfitting21. Although ML models are often criticized as “black boxes,” integrating them with SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) values allows researchers to quantify the contribution of each factor, thereby unveiling the complex driving mechanisms behind ecological changes22.

Consequently, this study utilizes the RSEI to analyze the spatiotemporal evolution of ecological quality in Shanxi Province from 2000 to 2025. The Theil–Sen slope estimator and Mann–Kendall test are combined to identify change trends, while the Hurst exponent is employed to reveal spatial patterns and predict future persistence. Furthermore, the XGBoost model coupled with SHAP values is applied to identify key driving factors and elucidate their contribution rates and directional effects. This study aims to provide a deep scientific basis for ecological restoration and high-quality development in resource-based regions.

Materials and methods

Study area

Shanxi Province (34°34′N–40°44′N, 110°14′E–114°33′E) is situated in the middle reaches of the Yellow River, encompassing the eastern flank of the Loess Plateau and the western margin of the North China Plain. Spanning a total area of approximately 156,700 km², it is recognized as a typical resource-based province in China. The topography is generally higher in the west and lower in the east, bordered by the Taihang Mountains to the east and the Lüliang Mountains to the west. The province extends approximately 682 km from north to south and 385 km from east to west. Climatically, Shanxi falls within the temperate continental monsoon zone, featuring distinct seasons and the synchronous rain and heat.

The annual average temperature ranges from 3.7 to 13.8 °C, and annual precipitation varies between 400 and 650 mm, exhibiting significant spatial heterogeneity due to topographic influences. The ecological environment of Shanxi possesses distinct regional characteristics and complexity. On the one hand, the widespread mountainous terrain results in generally superior ecological quality in mountainous areas compared to basins; however, these mountain ecosystems remain inherently fragile. On the other hand, the coupling effects of an arid to semi-arid climate, dense population, and high-intensity coal mining have led to prominent localized ecological degradation. Over the past two decades, serving as both a key national energy base and a typical ecologically fragile zone, the evolution of Shanxi’s ecological quality is critical to the sustainable development of the Yellow River Basin and North China. Therefore, selecting Shanxi as the study area not only helps reveal the spatiotemporal evolution characteristics of ecological quality in resource-based regions but also provides a scientific reference for ecological restoration and high-quality development in similar areas nationwide (Fig. 1).

Data sources and preprocessing

The remote sensing data utilized in this study were primarily acquired from the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. To ensure long-term consistency and high data quality, we selected Landsat Collection 2 Level 2 (Tier 1) Surface Reflectance (SR) products. This dataset encompasses imagery from Landsat 5 TM (2000–2011), Landsat 7 ETM+ (2012), and Landsat 8 OLI (2013–2025). All SR products have undergone rigorous atmospheric correction by the USGS using the LEDAPS algorithm (for TM/ETM+ sensors) and the LaSRC algorithm (for OLI sensors), as well as orthorectification.

To generate high-quality annual representative imagery, a multi-step preprocessing workflow was implemented on the GEE platform. First, cloud and cloud shadow masking was performed using the QA_PIXEL bitmask generated by the CFMask algorithm to ensure a cloud-free observation environment.23 Second, to minimize the impact of seasonal phenological variations, we filtered all available scenes from the plant growing season (June to September) for each target year. Third, a hierarchical three-step gap-filling procedure was implemented on the GEE platform to address data gaps in Landsat 7 ETM+ imagery (the bad lines caused by the Scan Line Corrector failure). A median compositing reducer was applied to all cloud-masked scenes from June to September to fill the majority of stripes by leveraging orbital overlaps. For minor residual gaps, a neighborhood mean filtering (focal_mean) with a 2-pixel radius was employed as a localized spatial interpolator. Any remaining NoData pixels were substituted with the global mean values of the study area via the unmask function. This multi-tiered approach ensures the spatial integrity of the annual representative imagery, which is essential for the subsequent Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Fourth, to ensure spectral consistency across different sensors, we applied the band-specific Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) transformation coefficients (including slopes and intercepts) to harmonize the reflectance values of Landsat 5/7 with the Landsat 8 OLI standard. This step eliminates systematic spectral offsets between different sensors over the 26-year study period.24 Finally, for the driving force analysis, while the RSEI was calculated at a 30 m spatial resolution, all layers were projected to the WGS84 coordinate system and subsequently resampled to a uniform 1 km resolution using bilinear interpolation to match the spatial scale of the anthropogenic and natural drivers (Tables 1 and 2).

To investigate the driving factors of ecological environmental quality in Shanxi Province, ten representative drivers were selected (Table 2), including natural factors (Temperature, Precipitation, Elevation, Terrain Undulation) and anthropogenic factors (Land Use, Population Density, Road Density, Nighttime Light, Coal Mining Intensity, Human Footprint). These drivers were selected based on established ecological principles and regional environmental characteristics to ensure that the model captures physically meaningful processes. Specifically, Coal Mining Intensity (CMI) was generated as a 1 km resolution raster using the Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) method, based on the raw coal output of each county combined with the distribution of coal mine points. IDW was selected over other geostatistical methods (e.g., Kriging) because mining-induced ecological disturbance is primarily a localized phenomenon that follows a clear distance-decay law. Unlike natural continuous variables (e.g., temperature), coal mining intensity is characterized by high spatial non-stationarity and discrete hotspots. IDW is a deterministic approach that effectively captures this localized attenuation without the need for a stable variogram or the assumption of spatial stationarity required by Kriging, thereby avoiding the over-smoothing of intensive mining signals. To ensure spatial consistency, all datasets were projected to the WGS84 coordinate system and resampled to a uniform spatial resolution of 1 km.

Methodology

Calculation of the remote sensing ecological index

The RSEI integrates four indicators representing Greenness, Wetness, Heat, and Dryness. Specifically, these correspond to the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI),25 the Wetness component (WET) from the Tasseled Cap Transformation,26–28 the Land Surface Temperature (LST),29,30 and the Normalized Difference Built-up and Soil Index (NDBSI),11 which combines the Index-based Built-up Index (IBI) and the Soil Index (SI). To ensure the comparability of RSEI over the 26-year study period, several strategies were implemented for cross-sensor and cross-year consistency. Cross-sensor consistency was achieved by acquiring all images as Surface Reflectance products and applying sensor-specific coefficients for the Tasseled Cap transformation and land surface temperature retrieval. For cross-year consistency, all indicators were normalized using a global minimum-maximum approach across the entire study period (2000–2025) rather than year-by-year rescaling, ensuring that temporal variations reflect actual environmental changes rather than processing artifacts. Theil–sen median trend analysis and mann–kendall test

To quantify the rate and significance of RSEI changes, the Theil–Sen Median slope estimator31 combined with the Mann–Kendall (M-K) test32 was employed for pixel-level (pixel-by-pixel) trend analysis at a spatial resolution of 1,000 m. Compared to simple linear regression, the Theil–Sen estimator is robust against outliers, thereby enhancing the accuracy of trend detection33. By calculating the median of all pairwise slopes, this estimator is highly resistant to short-term anomalies and extreme weather events that might otherwise bias the long-term trend, ensuring the results reflect true ecological shifts.

Hurst index analysis

The Hurst exponent (H),34 based on Rescaled Range (R/S) analysis, was utilized to evaluate the persistence or anti-persistence of the RSEI time series. This method is widely applied in ecology and climatology.33 The value ranges from 0 to 1. When H is near 0.5, the RSEI exhibits randomness; when H is in the range of 0–0.5, the RSEI shows significant anti-persistence; when H is within 0.5–1, the RSEI demonstrates persistence, with stronger persistence as H approaches 1. In this study, H values between 0.45 and 0.55 are defined as stable. By coupling the Hurst exponent with the Theil–Sen slope, future trends were classified into five categories (Table 5). This integrated approach provides an additional layer of statistical validation for the identified ecological trends. By measuring the long-term memory of the time series, it distinguishes consistent ecological trajectories—which possess high statistical persistence—from transient fluctuations and data noise, ensuring that the identified trends represent robust ecological shifts.

XGBoost Model

Xtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a scalable machine learning system for tree boosting.35 It is highly effective for regression and feature ranking tasks, often outperforming other models (e.g., Random Forest, Geodetector) in processing raster data. XGBoost optimizes the model by iteratively adding decision trees to correct the residual errors of previous models, incorporating regularization and parallel computing to prevent overfitting and accelerate training36–39.

Results and analysis

Spatiotemporal distribution characteristics of ecological quality

Based on the “Technical Criterion for Ecosystem Status Evaluation” issued by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of China in 2015 and relevant studies41, the RSEI values were classified into five grades using an interval of 0.2: Bad (0 ≤ RSEI < 0.2), Poor (0.2 ≤ RSEI < 0.4), Medium (0.4 ≤ RSEI < 0.6), Good (0.6 ≤ RSEI < 0.8), and Excellent (0.8 ≤ RSEI < 1).

Figure 2 illustrates the spatial distribution of RSEI in Shanxi Province from 2000 to 2025. Temporally, the mean RSEI increased from 0.5519 (95% CI: [0.5509, 0.5529]) in 2000 to 0.5997 in 2025 (95% CI: [0.5992, 0.6003]), exhibiting an overall trend of “improvement amidst fluctuations” characterized by phased “V-shaped” patterns. A significant ecological trough occurred around 2005 (0.4966), followed by a rebound to 0.6011 in 2007. After several fluctuation cycles, the RSEI stabilized at 0.5997 in 2025, representing an increase of approximately 8.7% compared to 2000. Linear regression analysis on the 26-year time series confirms a highly significant upward trend (F = 21.45, P < 0.001), with an annual growth rate (slope) of 0.0025 yr⁻¹ (95% CI: [0.0014, 0.0036] ). This evolution indicates that despite multi-stage fluctuations, the ecological environment has achieved overall recovery and improvement.

Spatially, the ecological quality exhibited typical geographical heterogeneity: mountainous areas generally outperformed basins, and the southern region was superior to the northern region. High-altitude areas such as the Taihang, Lüliang, and Taiyue Mountains consistently maintained RSEI values above 0.6, reflecting robust forest and grassland coverage. In contrast, the Fenhe River Valley, the Yellow River banks in western Shanxi, and the coal mining areas in northern Shanxi remained at low levels for extended periods, showing characteristics of fragmentation. This suggests that urban expansion, harsh natural conditions, and energy development have caused significant disturbances to local ecosystems.

To quantify the changes in each grade, area statistics were calculated for selected years (Fig. 3; Table 6). Figure 3 illustrates the structural evolution of RSEI grades. Between 2005 and 2015, substantial transitions occurred, with “Poor” and “Medium” grades gradually shifting towards “Good” and “Excellent,” indicating a stabilization of the ecological structure.

Trend analysis of ecological quality

Using the Theil–Sen Median slope estimator and Mann–Kendall test, the pixel-wise trend characteristics of RSEI from 2000 to 2025 were analyzed (Fig. 4). To provide direct spatial validation, Fig. 4 presents the trend results and significance test outcomes in a joint layout. The results (Table 7) reveal significant spatial heterogeneity, forming a pattern of “large-scale improvement with fluctuations and localized clustered degradation.”

From a temporal perspective, the study area witnessed a juxtaposition of improvement and deterioration over the 26-year period. The proportions of areas exhibiting upward and downward trends in RSEI were 42.3% and 35.64%, respectively, while 22.05% of the region remained stable. Specifically, significant improvement areas—comprising highly, significant, and marginally significant increases—spanned 32,445.29 km². These areas showed a prominent “mountain-clustering” distribution, mainly concentrated in high-altitude terrains such as the Lüliang, eastern Taihang, and Taiyue Mountains. This spatial pattern is largely attributed to the sustained implementation of major ecological restoration initiatives, including the “Grain for Green” Program and the Natural Forest Protection Project. Conversely, despite substantial recovery in certain areas, degradation persisted in 35.64% of the region. Significant degradation categories (highly, significant, and marginally significant decreases) collectively accounted for 18.86% of the total area (approx. 29,563.92 km²). Spatially, these declined regions bore the distinct imprint of anthropogenic interference, being heavily clustered in the urbanized Fenhe River Valley, large-scale coal mining bases in Northern Shanxi, and the peri-urban expansion zones of Southeastern Shanxi. These findings underscore the localized ecological degradation driven by construction land sprawl and intensive energy extraction amidst rapid urbanization.

The decadal evolution of ecological quality was further elucidated through a standard departure analysis (Fig. 5). The results show a clear phase-based trajectory. The 2010s (Recovery and Peak), a strong recovery phase was observed, with standard departures remaining predominantly positive. The highest peak occurred around 2016, reflecting the cumulative benefits of ecological restoration projects. The 2020s (a period of stability), ecological quality remained relatively stable compared to the beginning of the study. However, the standard deviations for 2024 and 2025 were both negative, indicating that although the ecological situation has recovered significantly from the trough in 2005, there is still a trend of deterioration, thus requiring continuous environmental monitoring.

Future trend prediction

To reveal the persistence and potential risks of ecological evolution, the Hurst exponent was overlaid with the Sen slope to construct a future trend map (Fig. 6). Area statistics for different evolution patterns are presented in Table 8.

The findings reveal that the ecosystem evolution in Shanxi Province is characterized by widespread stability and a notable proportion of trend reversals. Approximately 23.69% of the region is projected to undergo a trend reversal (including 12.86% from improvement to degradation and 10.83% from degradation to improvement), suggesting that nearly a quarter of the area may deviate from its historical evolutionary trajectory. The areas of persistent improvement account for 9.54%, predominantly located in mountainous terrain. These regions demonstrate strong ecosystem resilience, and the sustainability of this improvement reflects the long-term effectiveness of ecological conservation policies. Conversely, 10.88% of the province faces a risk of persistent degradation, primarily clustered in energy mining bases and urban centers. Constrained by the irreversible nature of intensive resource extraction, achieving a reversal in these zones remains a challenge. Notably, stable areas comprise the majority of the province, accounting for 55.89%. Located in the transition zones between degrading and improving areas, as well as the urban-rural fringe, these regions are hypersensitive to human activity. Therefore, they warrant prioritized monitoring to prevent the potential deterioration of their ecological quality.

Analysis of driving factor importance

Based on the XGBoost model, SHAP values were calculated to quantify the contributions of natural and anthropogenic factors (Fig. 7).

As shown in Figure. 7a, the feature importance ranking indicates that Precipitation (X3), Coal Mining Intensity (X9), and Population Density (X6) are the three most critical factors influencing RSEI. Overall, while both natural factors (precipitation) and anthropogenic factors (mining, population) play significant roles, the explanatory power of anthropogenic factors is more prominent. As shown in Figure. 7b, as precipitation(X3) increases, SHAP values gradually rise, corresponding to an increase in RSEI. This confirms precipitation as the primary positive natural driver. In contrast, Coal Mining Intensity (X9) primarily exerts a negative effect. High mining intensity generally corresponds to lower SHAP values, reflecting ecological effects such as surface disturbance, vegetation destruction, and soil erosion. Notably, a small number of samples showed positive contributions under high mining intensity. This anomaly may be attributed to “Green Mine” policies, where high-intensity artificial greening and landscape reconstruction in certain mining areas create artificial ecosystems superior to the surrounding natural environment. In summary, precipitation constitutes the basic environmental constraint, while anthropogenic factors act as the primary stressors.

Analysis of interactive effects

The interaction between the key drivers, Precipitation (X3) and Coal Mining Intensity (X9), was analyzed to reveal their complex non-linear relationship (Fig. 8). The dramatic fluctuation of SHAP interaction values in the figure indicates that high-intensity mining activities significantly enhance the uncertainty and instability of the ecosystem’s response to precipitation.

In areas with low mining intensity, the SHAP interaction values are concentrated near zero with minimal fluctuation. This suggests that in natural ecosystems undisturbed by mining, the ecological effect of precipitation is consistent and stable. In areas with high mining intensity the ecosystem’s response to precipitation becomes highly sensitive and unpredictable. Although high positive interaction values appear within certain precipitation ranges (reflecting temporary benefits of artificial greening), the overall fluctuation range is much larger than in low-intensity areas. Crucially, pronounced negative values frequently appear in high precipitation zones. This clearly demonstrates a high-risk response mode dominated by the coupling of high-intensity precipitation and mining. Mechanistically, mining destroys the surface structure and weakens water retention capacity. Consequently, while moderate precipitation may promote vegetation recovery, excessive precipitation is prone to triggering secondary ecological risks such as soil erosion, subsidence, and pollutant migration, leading to rapid environmental decline.

Discussion

Temporally, the RSEI in Shanxi Province exhibited an overall trend of fluctuating improvement from 2000 to 2025. The mean annual slope indicates a rise in ecological quality, with the mean RSEI increasing by approximately 8.7%. This improvement is closely related to the continuous implementation of ecological restoration projects and favorable climate changes in recent years, a finding that aligns with previous studies42. However, a distinct decline in mean RSEI and a significant expansion of “Bad” grade areas occurred between 2000 and 2005. This significant ecological trough coincided temporally with a period of rapid expansion in coal production and intensive mining in Shanxi Province, which intensified surface disturbance and vegetation destruction. Subsequent ecological recovery is consistent with the implementation of environmental management measures such as the “green mining” initiative and the “ecological protection red line” policy, reflecting the positive impact of policy interventions on regional ecological restoration43,44.

Spatially, the areas of ecological improvement (accounting for 42.3%) are predominantly concentrated in key ecological engineering zones, such as the Taihang Mountains, the eastern Taihang region, and western Shanxi. This distribution spatially coincides with the implementation of large-scale regional initiatives, most notably the “Grain for Green Project” and the “Three-North Shelterbelt Project.“44 Mechanistically, these ecological restoration measures promoted the conversion of steep arable land into forest and grassland, significantly enhancing vegetation coverage and improving soil water retention capacity, which contributed to the observed rise in RSEI. In contrast, although the degraded areas (approximately 35.64%) are slightly smaller in extent than the improved areas, they exhibit a high degree of spatial clustering. These areas are primarily concentrated in the Fenhe River Valley, the coal mining districts of the north, and the zones of urban expansion and economic growth in the southeast. This pattern directly reflects the ecological pressure imposed by high-intensity anthropogenic activities.

The XGBoost-SHAP analysis identified precipitation (X3) as the primary positive natural driver, particularly in regions with limited baseline rainfall where vegetation is highly sensitive to water availability. This corroborates the consensus that precipitation is the key climatic factor for ecological quality in Shanxi43–46. Conversely, coal mining intensity (X9) constitutes the dominant negative anthropogenic driver. Crucially, the SHAP interaction analysis revealed that high-intensity mining significantly reshapes the ecosystem’s response to climatic factors. While precipitation generally promotes vegetation growth, mining activities disrupt soil structure and alter hydrological connectivity. In mining-intensive areas, these disturbances weaken the effectiveness of rainfall in supporting vegetation recovery and may instead increase the risk of soil erosion and subsidence during heavy precipitation events. This mechanism highlights the deep-seated conflict between intensive energy extraction and regional ecological preservation. The robustness of this nonlinear interaction is further supported by the structured and continuous transition patterns observed in the SHAP interaction results, rather than the irregular or fragmented distributions that typically characterize stochastic modeling artifacts. Combined with stable model performance across five-fold cross-validation and the implementation of consistent data denoising, these findings suggest that the identified interactions represent genuine ecological associations grounded in regional environmental processes.

Transforming identified driving mechanisms into actionable management strategies is crucial for regional sustainable development. Based on our quantitative research findings and SHAP interaction analysis, we propose a tailored “regional management” strategy. In mining-intensive areas, we recommend a “microbe-phytoremediation” model to stabilize soil structure and restore hydrological connectivity. In mountainous barrier zones, the results of the “returning farmland to forest and grassland” program should be consolidated by introducing drought-resistant, species-rich mixed forests, thereby enhancing ecological resilience. In urbanized areas, strict adherence to the “ecological red line” and the implementation of the “sponge city” concept are essential for buffering the negative impacts of construction land expansion. These targeted measures based on identified stress factors can provide a scientific basis for achieving long-term ecological goals in resource-based regions.

Furthermore, it is important to consider potential confounding effects in these driving mechanisms. Multiple anthropogenic drivers may act simultaneously; for example, high collinearity often exists among population density, nighttime light, and road density, which means the impact of human activities is dispersed across multiple related variables. Therefore, the relationships identified in this study should be interpreted as complex associations rather than explicit causal chains. Additionally, neglecting factors such as soil properties and groundwater depth may introduce uncertainties into the model within the framework of statistical associations.

Despite revealing the evolution of ecological quality, this study has several limitations. First, regarding data uncertainty, the use of Landsat median composite imagery may still leave residual cloud contamination and sensor calibration differences between Landsat 5, 7, and 8. Additionally, some driving factors (e.g., human footprint) are themselves model-based products, which may introduce secondary uncertainties into the XGBoost-SHAP analysis. Second, while temporal autocorrelation was addressed, spatial autocorrelation (the tendency for adjacent pixels to exhibit similar characteristics) was not explicitly modeled. Although 1 km resolution alleviates this to some extent, spatial dependence can still lead to an underestimation of standard errors. Future research should better address this by employing spatial regression methods or applying effective sample size correction. Third, methodological limitations, the performance of XGBoost depends on hyperparameter tuning, and different settings can lead to subtle variations in feature importance ranking. Furthermore, predictions based on the Hurst model should be interpreted as probabilistic indicators of persistence rather than deterministic predictions. While the index effectively captures the “ecological inertia” of the system based on historical statistical patterns, it remains susceptible to rapid “external shocks” such as major policy shifts or extreme climate events that could break this predicted persistence. Therefore, these results serve primarily as a spatial risk assessment tool, alerting policymakers to areas where current ecological trends are statistically likely to remain stable or face reversal risks. Potentially important variables, such as specific soil properties and detailed land management measures, were not considered, which could lead to omission bias.

To address these issues, future research could: employ higher-resolution datasets to capture fine-scale ecological variability; integrate in-situ observational data (e.g., soil and vegetation measurements) to validate remote sensing results; apply causal inference methods to better distinguish policy impacts from contextual environmental changes; and combine data-driven models with process-based ecosystem models and climate scenarios to support more robust and policy-relevant predictions.

Conclusions

Based on the RSEI, Theil-Sen Median slope estimator, Mann-Kendall test, Hurst exponent, and XGBoost-SHAP model, this study systematically analyzed the spatiotemporal evolution, future trends, and driving mechanisms of ecological quality in Shanxi Province from 2000 to 2025. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) The ecological environmental quality showed an overall trend of fluctuating improvement. From 2000 to 2025, the mean RSEI increased by approximately 8.7%. Spatially, the pattern is characterized by “significant improvement in mountains and intensified degradation in urban and mining zones.” The optimization of the ecological structure is evidenced by the steady expansion of “Medium” and “Good” grades.

(2) Trend analysis demonstrates a juxtaposition of improvement and degradation. While 42.3% of the province showed an improving trend, predominantly in key ecological engineering areas like the Lüliang and Taihang Mountains, 35.64% of the region exhibited a degrading trend, heavily clustered in the Fenhe River Valley and northern coal mining bases.

(3) Future trend projections highlight a lack of long-term ecological stability. Although 55.89% of the area remains stable, the risk of persistent degradation (10.88%) is concentrated in high-intensity industrial and mining zones. The projections suggest that trend reversals (affecting 23.69% of the region) further contribute to ecological uncertainty, necessitating strategic policy adjustments.

(4) The driving mechanism analysis reveals that precipitation is the primary positive natural driver, whereas coal mining intensity and population density are the dominant negative factors. Specifically, coal mining intensity weakens the ecosystem’s positive response to climatic factors through non-linear interactions, underscoring the contradiction between energy extraction and ecological preservation.

These findings provide a scientific basis for ecological restoration and high-quality development in Shanxi and similar resource-based regions. Future efforts should prioritize maintaining ecological stability in risk-prone areas and regulating anthropogenic disturbances.
