Associations of metabolic score for insulin resistance and new-onset stroke in middle-aged and older adults: a national cohort study
Authors: Xuexue Zhang, Rui Wei, Huanrong Hu, Xujie Wang, Qiuyan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR) is acknowledged as a reliable indicator of insulin resistance and cardiometabolic risk. This study aims to explore the relationship between cumulative METS-IR (cumMETS-IR) and METS-IR change patterns with new-onset stroke.Methods4344 participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) with follow-up until 2020 were utilized in this study. cumMETS-IR was calculated as the area under the curve of METS-IR values from 2011 to 2015. K-means clustering analysis was employed to discern distinct METS-IR change patterns over this interval. Associations of cumMETS-IR and METS-IR change patterns with incident stroke were examined using Cox proportional hazards models and restricted cubic splines (RCS). Mediation analysis assessed whether diastolic blood pressure (DBP) accounted for the association between cumMETS-IR and incident stroke.ResultsThere were 385 (8.86%) new stroke events over 5-years median follow-up. RCS analysis showed that cumMETS-IR was positively correlated with incident stroke (P-overall = 0.013, P-nonlinear = 0.252). Compared with the lowest quartile, the highest quartile was associated with a higher incidence of stroke (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 1.72, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.22–2.42, P = 0.002). Four distinct METS-IR change patterns were identified using k-means clustering: low-stable (30.5%), low-to-moderate stable (35.9%), moderate-to-high stable (24.7%), and high-decreasing (8.9%). Participants following the high-decreasing pattern was associated with the highest incidence of stroke compared to those in the low-stable patterns (adjusted HR 1.61, 95% CI 1.08–2.42, P = 0.021). The core findings remained robust across a series of sensitivity analyses. Mediation analysis revealed that DBP partially explained this association, with a mediation proportion of 9.6%.ConclusionBoth cumMETS-IR and METS-IR change were associated with increased incidence of stroke in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Monitoring changes in METS-IR over time may help characterize metabolic status more comprehensively and offer supportive information for stroke-related health assessment.Graphical Abstract Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03232-x.

Introduction

Stroke poses a substantial global health challenge, with approximately 12 million new cases and more than $2 trillion in macroeconomic losses annually [1, 2]. Over the past three decades, stroke incidence among the elderly has been increasing [3]. This burden is particularly pronounced in China, where stroke is the predominant cause of mortality and disability [4]. In 2020, about 3.4 million Chinese adults suffered an incident stroke, representing an 86.0% increase in incidence over the preceding 30 years [5, 6]. Abnormal metabolism of blood pressure, glucose, and lipids, as key metabolic risk factors, account for 69% of this burden, which disproportionately affects socioeconomically disadvantaged populations [7, 8]. Therefore, identifying easily monitored composite biomarkers offers a cost-efficient strategy for implementing targeted early intervention and primary prevention strategies to mitigate the stroke burden [9, 10].

As is well known, insulin resistance (IR) serves as an early indicator of metabolic dysfunction and represents a key contributor to the pathogenesis of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [11]. Although the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp is widely recognized as the benchmark technique for quantifying insulin sensitivity, its application in clinical practice is constrained by factors such as procedural invasiveness, prohibitive expense, and operational complexity [12]. The metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR), a simple composite index based on fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglycerides (TG), body mass index (BMI), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), has been confirmed to be a robust marker of insulin resistance and cardiometabolic risk [13]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that METS-IR is positively correlated with hypertension [14, 15], heart failure [16], cardiometabolic multimorbidity [17], and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [18–20]. Notably, recent investigations have extended these findings to incident stroke among Chinese adults with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome [21, 22].

Current understanding, however, is predominantly based on evaluations at a single time point, which provides limited information on changes in metabolic status over time. Although repeated measures may offer additional information beyond a single baseline assessment [23], the associations of cumulative METS-IR (cumMETS-IR) and METS-IR change patterns with incident stroke has not been fully elucidated. Therefore, the present study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to examine these associations in the Chinese population.

Methods

Data source and participants

This longitudinal study employed data from CHARLS database, a nationally representative cohort study encompassing middle-aged and elderly residents in both rural and urban areas across China [24]. The initial survey was established during the 2011–2012 period (wave 1), with biennial follow-up assessments were performed in 2013–2014 (wave 2), 2015–2016 (wave 3), 2017–2018 (wave 4), and 2019–2020 (wave 5). Health condition was evaluated through standardized questionnaire-based interviews and physical examinations across waves 1 to 5, while blood biomarker data were collected only in waves 1 and 3, with detailed methodological descriptions provided in previous studies [24, 25]. Participants were excluded if they lacked METS-IR data in either wave 1 or wave 3, had a documented history of stroke before wave 4, or were lost to follow-up [26]. Consequently, 4344 eligible participants were incorporated into the final analysis (Fig. 1). The study protocol for CHARLS received ethical approval from the Peking University Biomedical Ethics Review Committee (IRB00001052-11015).

Assessment of METS-IR, cumMETS-IR and new-onset stroke

METS-IR was calculated using the published formula [27]: METS-IR = ln [2 × FPG (mg/dL) + fasting serum TG (mg/dL)] × BMI (kg/m2)/ln [HDL (mg/dL)]. cumMETS-IR was calculated as the time-weighted average of METS-IR values measured in 2011 and 2015, using an established approach in the CHARLS literature when serial measurements are limited, thereby providing a pragmatic estimate of cumulative metabolic burden as follows [28]: cumMETS-IR = (METS-IR2011+METS-IR2015)/2 × time2011−2015. METS-IR2011 and METS-IR 2015 represent the values obtained at wave 1 and wave 3, respectively, and time2011−2015 denotes the interval between the two examinations. The following question based on self-reported physician diagnoses was used to determine stroke cases:"Have you ever been diagnosed with a stroke by a doctor?"If participants answered"Yes"in wave 4 or wave 5 and had no prior history of stroke before wave 4, they were classified as having new-onset stroke [29].

Assessment of baseline variables

Baseline characteristics were collected via structured questionnaires [17]. Baseline information encompassed: (1) demographic and lifestyle variables, such as age, gender, education level, marital status, smoking status, and drinking status; (2) anthropometric and clinical measures, including BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP); (3) laboratory examination indicators, such as HDL, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), total cholesterol (TC), TG, FPG, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and C-reactive protein (CRP); (4) self-reported history of chronic diseases (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia) and corresponding medication use. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, a self-reported physician diagnosis, or current antihypertensive treatment. Diabetes was diagnosed based on FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, a self-reported physician diagnosis, or current use of antidiabetic medication. Dyslipidemia was defined by TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, TC ≥ 240 mg/dL, HDL < 40 mg/dL, LDL ≥ 160 mg/dL, current use of lipid-lowering medication, or a self-reported physician diagnosis [29].

Statistical analysis

Whereas continuous variables with a skewed distribution were summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR), those with a normal distribution were shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between groups for continuous variables were assessed using ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test, while categorical variables were compared using chi-square test. Missing values were addressed through multiple imputation based on chained equations, and the proportion of missing data for each variable is provided in Table S1.

Based on repeated measurements of METS-IR obtained from 2011 to 2015, individuals were categorized into distinct change patterns by employing K-means clustering, an unsupervised machine learning approach that captures inter-individual heterogeneity [30]. The elbow method was employed to select the appropriate number of clusters. To assess clustering robustness, both overall and cluster-specific stability were performed. Overall agreement between repeated clustering solutions and the reference solution was quantified using the adjusted Rand index (ARI), which is insensitive to arbitrary relabeling of clusters and corrects for chance agreement. In addition, cluster-specific reproducibility was assessed using Jaccard similarity in bootstrap analyses. Both continuous cumMETS-IR and cumMETS-IR quartiles were used in subsequent analyses to investigate its association with incident stroke.

To assess the relationship between cumMETS-IR and METS-IR change patterns with incident stroke, Cox proportional hazards models were fitted. This analysis comprised three sequential models adjusted for potential confounding variables: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 included adjustment for age, gender, marital status, education level, smoke status, and drink status; Model 3 further adjusted for heart disease, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and the use of medications for these conditions. To ensure the validity of the Cox proportional hazards assumption, Schoenfeld residuals were employed. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with four knots positioned at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles using median as the reference was incorporated into the Cox proportional hazards model to investigate the linear correlation between cumMETS-IR and incident stroke. Cumulative incidence of stroke across METS-IR change patterns was illustrated through Kaplan-Meier curves, with the log-rank test employed for between-group comparisons. In order to evaluate the predictive capacity of cumMETS-IR for stroke events, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were conducted, with the area under the curve (AUC) serving as a metric to evaluate the incremental predictive value contributed by cumMETS-IR.

To assess the consistency of the associations of cumMETS-IR and METS-IR change patterns with stroke, stratified analyses across several predefined subgroups were performed. Participants were stratified according to age, gender, smoking, drinking, and pre-existing conditions including heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Interaction terms between cumMETS-IR, METS-IR change patterns and each stratification variable were included, with Wald tests employed to determine the statistical significance.

Multiple sensitivity analyses were implemented to test the stability of the core findings. First, complete-case analysis following the exclusion of participants with any missing values was performed. Second, analyses were repeated after removing individuals who died early during follow-up. Third, as an alternative analytical strategy, logistic regression modeling was employed to assess the robustness of the findings. Fourth, the Cox models for cluster-based METS-IR patterns were further adjusted for baseline METS-IR to examine whether the observed associations were primarily explained by baseline metabolic status. Fifth, to confirm the consistency of the dose-response shape, the RCS model was refitted with five knots located at the 5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th, and 95th percentiles.

Mediation analysis was performed as an exploratory analysis to estimate the indirect effect of cumMETS-IR on stroke through DBP. The indirect effect, along with its 95% CI were estimated using bootstrap resampling. All analyses were conducted using R software 4.5.1, with statistical significance defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants stratified by METS-IR clusters

From the CHARLS cohort, 4344 participants were enrolled in this study. The population of this study had a mean age of 58.34 ± 8.44 years, with 43.8% being male. During a median follow-up period of 5 years, a total of 385 (8.86%) new-onset stroke cases were documented. The K-means solution showed excellent reproducibility, with a mean ARI of 1.000 across repeated random starts and a mean bootstrap ARI of 0.945 (Table S2). Cluster-specific Jaccard similarities were consistently high (0.952–0.974), indicating excellent stability of all identified grouped patterns (Table S3). Using K-means clustering, four distinct change patterns of METS-IR were identified: low-stable group (cluster 1), low-to-moderate-stable group (cluster 2), moderate-to-high-stable group (cluster 3), and high-declining group (cluster 4). The corresponding median of cumMETS-IR were 85.39, 103.68, 124.08, and 149.86, respectively (Table 1; Fig. 2). The stroke incidence increased progressively across clusters (6.3%, 9.1%, 10.1%, and 12.9%, respectively). Compared to lower clusters, participants in clusters with higher cumMETS-IR were characterized by a greater proportion of females, younger age, higher BMI, higher education levels, along with an increased likelihood of chronic diseases. Additionally, these participants had significantly higher SBP, DBP, HbA1c, FPG, CRP, TG, and TC, but lower LDL and HDL.

Associations of cumMETS-IR and METS-IR change patterns with new-onset stroke

As displayed in Table 2, compared with cluster 1, clusters 2 (HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.11–1.95, P = 0.006), clusters 3 (HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.08–2.04, P = 0.015), and clusters 4 (HR 1.61, 95% CI 1.08–2.42, P = 0.021) were associated with a higher incidence of stroke after adjusting for potential confounders. When cumMETS-IR was analyzed by quartiles, the incidence of stroke increased across quartiles. Compared with Q1, the adjusted HRs for Q2, Q3, and Q4 were 1.24 (95% CI 0.89–1.71, P = 0.198), 1.55 (95% CI 1.12–2.14, P = 0.008), and 1.72 (95% CI 1.22–2.42, P = 0.002), with a significant linear trend (P for trend = 0.001). Furthermore, each SD increment in cumMETS-IR was associated with a 19% higher incidence of stroke (HR 1.19, 95% CI 1.06–1.33, P = 0.003).

Sensitivity analyses

The reliability of the core findings was evaluated via multiple sensitivity analyses. First, stratified analyses by age, gender, smoking, drinking, and history of heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia demonstrated that the significant positive association between cumMETS-IR and incident stroke persisted across all strata. No significant interactions emerged between cumMETS-IR and the above stratification factors (P for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 4). This consistency persisted when participants were categorized by METS-IR clusters (P for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 5). Second, the observed findings remained essentially unchanged after removing participants with missing data, and after excluding those who experienced early death events (Tables S4 and S5). Third, the results were consistent when using logistic regression models as an alternative analytical approach (Table S6). Fourth, the Cox models for METS-IR clusters were further adjusted for baseline METS-IR. The associations were materially unchanged after this additional adjustment, including for the high-decreasing group, suggesting that the observed excess risk was not solely explained by baseline METS-IR level (Table S7). Finally, RCS analysis with five knots validated a positive linear association between cumMETS-IR and stroke (Figure S3).

Discussion

This large-scale prospective study revealed that both cumMETS-IR and METS-IR change were associated with a higher incidence of new-onset stroke among middle-aged and older adults in China. Compared with the participants in the low-stable pattern, those in the high-decreasing pattern had the highest rate of incident stroke. Similarly, a significantly elevated incidence of stroke was observed among individuals in the highest quartile of cumMETS-IR relative to those in the lowest quartile. RCS analysis demonstrated a positive linear correlation between cumMETS-IR and stroke. The core findings remained robust across a series of sensitivity analyses. Notably, mediation analysis further revealed that DBP partially explained the association between cumMETS-IR and incident stroke. These findings highlight the potential value of incorporating repeated METS-IR assessments to better characterize metabolic status and its association with stroke occurrence.

The World Stroke Organization has emphasized that the importance of identifying modifiable risk factors in stroke prevention [7]. In resource-limited settings, it is significant to focus on biomarkers that are not only strongly associated with stroke but also accessible, cost-effective, and repeatable. Previous studies have reported positive associations between baseline METS-IR and cardiovascular or cerebrovascular outcomes [31, 32]. The current study extends this literature by showing that cumulative METS-IR derived from the two available measurements, as well as METS-IR change patterns, were associated with incident stroke. These findings were consistent with emerging evidence on cumulative triglyceride-glucose index and other metabolic measures [30, 33–35], supporting the view that repeated metabolic assessment may provide additional information beyond a single baseline measurement.

Cluster-based analysis demonstrated that high-decreasing pattern was associated with the highest incidence of stroke warrants particularly cautious interpretation. Participants in this group also exhibited a markedly greater burden of cardiometabolic comorbidities (i.e. heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia) and presented an adverse metabolic profile (elevated glycemic, lipid, and inflammatory parameters). These characteristics suggest that this group may represent a subgroup with initially unfavorable metabolic status and multimorbidity, both of which have been associated with stroke risk and prognosis [36]. Although one possible explanation is that these individuals experienced a high baseline metabolic burden before a subsequent decline, this pattern should not be interpreted as representing improvement with persistently high risk. In our additional sensitivity analysis, further adjustment for baseline METS-IR did not materially change the association, suggesting that the observed excess risk was not solely explained by baseline METS-IR level. At the same time, several alternative explanations remain possible, including treatment initiation, behavioral modification, and reverse causation related to preclinical disease. Therefore, this group is better interpreted as a heterogeneous subgroup with adverse baseline metabolic characteristics and subsequent change of uncertain origin, rather than as evidence that a declining METS-IR pattern itself confers excess risk.

METS-IR, which combines TG, HDL-C, FPG, and BMI, is considered a reliable marker of systemic insulin resistance. In the present study, cumMETS-IR extends this concept by providing a pragmatic estimate of cumulative metabolic burden based on the two available biomarker measurements, although it does not fully characterize long-term exposure. Given the multifactorial pathogenesis of stroke, higher METS-IR may be associated with vascular and cardiometabolic conditions that are linked to stroke risk, including atrial fibrillation [37, 38]. At the microvascular level, dysfunction of adipose tissue promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby disrupting endothelial integrity and vascular homeostasis. These changes impair vasodilatory capacity, establish a pre-thrombosis environment, and accelerate the process of atherogenesis [39]. In addition, aging is accompanied by a reduced capacity of the lipid buffering system, which contributes to an increased severity of systemic lipotoxicity [40]. In individuals with high cumMETS-IR, cumulative vascular lipotoxicity undermines intracellular signaling, triggers mitochondrial dysfunction and endoplasmic reticulum stress, and promotes oxidative stress via excessive reactive oxygen species generation, which surpasses the capacity of the endogenous antioxidant defense system [41]. Within this framework, individuals in cluster 4, marked by high FPG, TG, and CRP and low HDL-C, display a phenotype of chronic inflammation and lipotoxic stress. This adverse profile may partly underlie the observed association with stroke, although these mechanistic interpretations remain speculative in the context of the present observational study [42].

Mediation analysis suggested that DBP accounted for 9.6% of the observed association between cumMETS-IR and incident stroke. Although this finding may imply that blood pressure was involved in the association between adverse metabolic status and stroke, it should be interpreted cautiously. In observational studies, mediation analysis relies on strong assumptions, including the absence of unmeasured confounding and correct temporal ordering, which could not be fully verified in the present study. Insulin resistance is a recognized promoter of arterial stiffening and impaired endothelial dysfunction, both of which play a key role in the development of hypertension [43, 44]. Persistently elevated DBP leads to a constant pressure load on the vascular wall across the cardiac cycle. This hemodynamic disturbance may directly damage the cerebral microvasculature and trigger secondary pathological changes, including the development of cerebral white matter lesions, which has previously been shown to be linked to stroke risk [45, 46]. In line with this, a meta-analysis indicated that DBP was strongly associated with white matter lesions, especially among people below 70 years old [47]. Considering that SBP often shows an increasing tendency with growing age, while DBP generally exhibits a plateau or decreasing trend in later-life, our results may highlight the clinical significance of controlling diastolic hypertension as part of strategies for preventing stroke for elderly people.

This investigation is characterized by a number of substantive strengths. First, the use of a large, nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults improves the external validity of the results. Second, the repeated measurements allow this study to characterize dynamic, intra-individual metabolic patterns better than single time point measurements. Third, the application of comprehensive sensitivity analyses and mediation analysis which further established the robustness of findings and provided mechanistic explanations on driving associations. Nonetheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, incident stroke was defined using self-reported physician diagnosis rather than adjudicated clinical records or imaging-based confirmation, and stroke subtype information was unavailable. This may have introduced recall bias or non-differential misclassification and likely biased the effect estimates toward the null. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. Second, because stroke status was assessed by survey waves rather than exact onset dates, the time-to-event analysis was based on approximate event timing and may have been affected by interval censoring. Third, cumMETS-IR and clusters-based METS-IR change patterns were derived from only two measurements, limiting their ability to fully characterize cumulative exposure or true longitudinal trajectories. Accordingly, cumMETS-IR should be viewed as an approximation of longer-term exposure. Fourth, despite adjustment for multiple covariates, residual confounding due to substantial baseline differences across groups and unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Finally, the study population was restricted to middle-aged and older Chinese adults, and therefore the generalizability of our findings to younger individuals or other ethnic populations requires further external validation.

Conclusion

The cumulative and change patterns of METS-IR were associated with a higher incidence of new-onset stroke. Repeated assessment of METS-IR over time may provide a practical way to characterize changes in metabolic status and thus help guide ongoing clinical attention in middle-aged and older adults.
