Prevalence of malaria and its major parasite species in Bangladesh: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jibon Kumar Sarker, Nourin Sultana, Tajin Ahmed Jisa, Mst Dilara Pervin, Md Kaderi Kibria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria remains a significant public health challenge in Bangladesh with ongoing transmission driven primarily by Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax. Understanding its current prevalence and parasite distribution is essential for guiding control and elimination strategies. This study aims to estimate the pooled prevalence of malaria and its major parasite species across the country. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Science Direct, EMBASE and Google Scholar for studies published between 2005 to 2025. Eligible studies were assessed for quality using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool. Pooled prevalence estimates were calculated using random-effects models while heterogeneity and inconsistencies were evaluated using the I2 index and Cochran’s Q-test. Publication bias was assessed with funnel plots and Egger’s test. Of 10,152 identified studies, 14 met inclusion criteria. The pooled malaria prevalence in Bangladesh was 16% (95% CI 6%–37%) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.6%, p = 0). Subgroup analyses showed prevalence's of 14% (95% CI 9%–21%) for P. vivax, 80% (95% CI 68%–88%) for P. falciparum and 7% (95% CI 4%–13%) for mixed infections. Malaria prevalence was higher in males (20%, 95% CI 7%–47%) than females (12%, 95% CI 3%–35%). Funnel plot asymmetry was observed but Egger’s test indicated no significant publication bias. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings. However, the findings should be interpreted with caution as reliance on microscopy and rapid diagnostic tests may have limited the detection of non-falciparum, mixed-species and submicroscopic malaria infections. This study highlights the substantial malaria burden in Bangladesh, driven mainly by P. falciparum and P. vivax and underscores the need for targeted, species-specific and gender-sensitive interventions to accelerate malaria control and elimination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12936-026-06014-8.Keywords: Malaria, Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium falciparum, Mixed infections, Random-effects model, Systemic review and meta-analysis, Bangladesh

Introduction

Malaria is a life-threatening disease caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Plasmodium, which includes six species known to infect humans such as P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale curtisi, P. ovale wallikeri, P. malariae and P. knowlesi [1]. It remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide which imposing substantial medical, social and economic burdens particularly in tropical and subtropical regions [2, 3]. According to WHO’s latest World malaria report, there were an estimated 263 million cases and 597000 malaria deaths worldwide in 2023 [4]. Although P. falciparum causes most severe cases and deaths, non-falciparum species including P. vivax, P. malariae, P. ovale as well as mixed-species infections are increasingly recognized as important contributors to malaria transmission and persistence [5–7]. Notably, P. vivax possesses a dormant liver-stage (hypnozoites), which can cause relapses weeks to months after the initial infection, necessitating species-specific radical cure with drugs such as primaquine or tafenoquine [8]. In contrast, P.falciparum is associated with high parasitemia and severe complications but does not relapse. Mixed infections further complicate clinical management and surveillance due to overlapping symptoms and differential drug responses. Additionally, a substantial proportion of non-falciparum and mixed infections may remain asymptomatic or submicroscopic, acting as hidden reservoirs that sustain transmission and challenge elimination efforts [9, 10]. Conventional microscopy and rapid diagnostic tests, primarily targeting P. falciparum, often underestimate these infections, underscoring the need for species-inclusive surveillance and control strategies [8, 10]. Despite their widespread use in routine surveillance and clinical practice, conventional malaria diagnostic tools such as light microscopy and rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) have notable limitations, particularly for detecting non-Plasmodium falciparum species, mixed-species infections and submicroscopic parasitemia [8, 11, 12]. Microscopy requires substantial expertise and may fail to accurately distinguish morphologically similar species or identify infections with low parasite densities. Similarly, commonly used RDTs largely optimized for P. falciparum often exhibit reduced sensitivity and specificity for P. vivax, P. malariae and P. ovale [13]. These diagnostic constraints can result in underestimation of true malaria prevalence and misclassification of parasite species, thereby limiting the effectiveness of surveillance systems and targeted control strategies, particularly in settings where species diversity is epidemiologically relevant [8, 13, 14].

In Bangladesh, malaria transmission is geographically heterogeneous and largely concentrated in specific high-risk areas. Historically, malaria was substantially reduced during the Malaria Eradication Program through widespread indoor residual spraying with DDT prior to 1971 [15]. However, following independence and the subsequent ban on DDT in 1985, malaria cases resurged due to weakened vector control measures and insufficient funding [15, 16]. This neglect led to an epidemic in the 1990 s, with over 500 deaths and 70,000 laboratory-confirmed cases alongside nearly 900,000 clinical cases reported in that decade [17]. Currently, Bangladesh is considered a low-transmission (hypo-endemic) setting within the WHO South-East Asia Region, but malaria remains a persistent public health issue in endemic districts [18, 19]. Transmission is primarily confined to 13 districts located along the borders with India and Myanmar, with the Chittagong Hill Tracts (Bandarban, Khagrachari and Rangamati) accounting for the majority of reported cases [20]. The epidemiology of malaria in these areas is influenced by ecological, socio-economic, and behavioral factors, including forest-related occupations, cross-border movement, and limited access to healthcare. While P. falciparum predominates in most endemic areas, P. vivax and mixed infections are also present and may be underreported due to diagnostic limitations [19, 20]. Population mobility plays an important role in malaria transmission dynamics in Bangladesh, particularly in border regions. Seasonal migration associated with agriculture, plantation work, and forest-related activities can facilitate the spread of malaria parasites, including drug-resistant strains, as observed in other parts of Southeast Asia [21]. However, the contribution of internal and cross-border movement to malaria transmission remains insufficiently characterized due to limited high-resolution data. National surveillance systems provide valuable insights but are widely recognized to underestimate the true burden of malaria, especially in detecting asymptomatic and submicroscopic infections [22]. Despite ongoing control efforts, comprehensive and up-to-date epidemiological evidence on malaria prevalence, species distribution, and demographic patterns in Bangladesh remains fragmented. In particular, variations by sex, age, and geographic location are not consistently reported across studies. Such detailed and systematically synthesized evidence is essential to support the WHO Global Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030 and to guide evidence-based, context-specific interventions. To address these gaps, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to estimate the pooled prevalence of malaria in Bangladesh and to characterize the distribution of major Plasmodium species, including mixed infections. By synthesizing available evidence, this study seeks to provide a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of malaria epidemiology in Bangladesh, thereby informing policy decisions and strengthening national malaria control and elimination strategies.

Materials and methods

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines for the purpose of this review [23]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420251101725) to ensure methodological transparency and reduce the risk of bias. The protocol predefined the research objectives, eligibility criteria, search strategy, and analytical approach.

Search strategy and study identification

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was performed to identify all relevant studies reporting malaria prevalence and Plasmodium species distribution in Bangladesh. The following electronic databases were searched: PubMed, ScienceDirect, EMBASE, and Google Scholar. The search covered publications from January 2005 to January 2025 and was conducted between February 1 and March 15, 2025. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms to maximize sensitivity and specificity. Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”) were applied appropriately. The search keywords included: (prevalence OR epidemiology) AND (malaria[MeSH Terms] OR Plasmodium) AND (Bangladesh[MeSH Terms] OR Bangladesh) AND (Plasmodium[Title/Abstract] OR malaria[Title/Abstract]). Additional filters and field-specific terms including Title/Abstract were applied where relevant. Reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews were also manually screened to identify additional eligible studies. Only studies published in English were included.

Eligibility criteria for study selection

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (i) conducted in Bangladesh and reported the prevalence of malaria and/or distribution of Plasmodium species; (ii) employed cross-sectional, cohort, or surveillance-based study designs; (iii) published in peer-reviewed journals or as credible reports; and (iv) available in English. Studies were excluded if they: (i) did not report original prevalence data; (ii) review articles, case reports, conference abstracts, letters, or editorials; (iii) lacked clear diagnostic criteria or methodological details; or (iv) were duplicates of previously published work. When multiple publications reported data from the same population, the most comprehensive or most recent study was included. The study selection process, including the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and included at each stage, is presented in a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Quality assessment of individual studies

The methodological quality of included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (KUK and NS) using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for cross-sectional studies [24, 25]. This tool evaluates key domains including sampling methodology, measurement validity, confounding factors, and statistical analysis. Each study was assessed against eight criteria, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Studies were categorized as low, moderate, or high quality based on their overall scores, following previously established approaches [26, 27]. The results of the quality assessment are summarized in Table S1 and were considered during data interpretation.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form was collaboratively developed by all authors using Microsoft Excel to ensure consistent and comprehensive data collection. The form captured key study characteristics to ensure comprehensive data collection. These characteristics were the first author, publication year, geographic region, study design, participant age groups, diagnostic methods, gender distribution, sample size, overall malaria prevalence and species-specific prevalence for P. vivax and P. falciparum. For studies employing multiple diagnostic methods (e.g., RDT combined with microscopy or molecular assays), prevalence estimates were extracted based on the most sensitive diagnostic method reported. Specifically, data from molecular diagnostics (PCR or qPCR) were prioritized over conventional methods (RDT or microscopy), as they provide higher sensitivity for detecting low-density, mixed-species, and submicroscopic infections. For studies using only RDT and microscopy, microscopy-based results were preferred if species-specific information was available; otherwise, RDT-based prevalence was used. For studies reporting a single diagnostic method, the reported prevalence was directly included. Following initial extraction, the dataset underwent rigorous verification to identify and resolve discrepancies. Any inconsistencies or disagreements were thoroughly discussed among the authors until consensus was reached.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

A random-effects model was used to estimate pooled prevalence, as substantial methodological and epidemiological heterogeneity was anticipated across studies. This model accounts for both within-study and between-study variability and is considered more appropriate than a fixed-effect model in such contexts. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test. Statistical significance was evaluated using the corresponding p-value < 0.05 and heterogeneity was quantified by the I2 statistic. I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Given the high expected heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted where data permitted to explore potential sources of variability. Potential publication bias was evaluated by visually inspecting funnel plots for asymmetry and statistically tested using Egger’s regression test which detects small-study effects that may indicate bias. To assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach, in which each study was sequentially excluded to evaluate its influence on pooled estimates. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.2 with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Results

Basic characteristics of the selected studies

A total of 14 published studies on malaria prevalence in Bangladesh met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis [18, 28–40]. The initial database search identified 10,152 publications. After removing 64 duplicates, 10,088 records were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 25 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility and 14 studies were deemed suitable for inclusion. The selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). The number of studies retained at each stage is also shown. The included studies were conducted between January 2005 and January 2025 and spanned multiple malaria-endemic regions of Bangladesh including Bandarban, Chittagong, Rajasthali, Khagrachari, Savar, and other high-risk areas (see Table S2). All studies employed cross-sectional designs. The sample sizes ranged from 115 to 9750 participants. Study populations encompassed diverse age groups, from infants to adults aged 90 years. Diagnostic approaches varied including rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), light microscopy, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and quantitative PCR (qPCR). To improve comparability across studies, prevalence estimates were extracted using a predefined hierarchy prioritizing molecular methods (PCR/qPCR), followed by microscopy and RDTs, as described in the Methods section. Study-specific weights were derived from the random-effects model and are presented in Table S2.

Reported malaria prevalence ranged from 0.14% to 72%, with several studies stratifying prevalence by sex and parasite species. Where data were available, male participants generally exhibited a higher proportion of malaria cases than females. P.falciparum was the predominant parasite in most settings, with prevalence reaching up to 80% in some studies, followed by P. vivax and mixed infections (P. falciparum + P. vivax). The wide variation in reported prevalence and species distribution reflects substantial heterogeneity in study settings, diagnostic methods and population characteristics which was formally addressed using random-effects modeling and subgroup analyses.

Pooled prevalence estimates of malaria in Bangladesh

Figure 2 presents the pooled prevalence of malaria across the included studies. In the forest plot, each square denotes the prevalence reported by an individual study, with its size proportional to the study’s weight in the meta-analysis. Reported prevalence ranged substantially from as low as 0.14% (0.0014) in the study by Alam MS, et al. (2016) to as high as 72% (0.72) in the study by Alam MS, et al. (2017). Many studies exhibited wide confidence intervals, reflecting variability and some uncertainty in individual estimates. The overall pooled prevalence of malaria in Bangladesh was 16% (95% CI 6%–37%). Between-study heterogeneity was extremely high (I2 = 99.6%, p = 0), indicating substantial variability in prevalence estimates. Given this high heterogeneity, the pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously, as it likely reflects differences in geographic distribution, transmission intensity, diagnostic methods and study populations rather than a single underlying prevalence.

Forest plot showing the pooled prevalence of malaria in Bangladesh based on random-effects meta-analysis. Squares represent study-specific prevalence estimates. Their sizes are proportional to study weight, and horizontal lines indicate 95% confidence intervals

Sub-analysis of malaria prevalence

Pooled prevalence of Plasmodium falciparum

Based on the included primary studies, the prevalence of P. falciparum varied considerably. It ranged from 57% in the study by Huwe T, et al. (2022) conducted in Chittagong to 92% in the study by Alam MS, et al. (2017) conducted in Alikadam, Bandarban. Such variation is likely influenced by geographic differences, particularly the higher transmission intensity in remote and forested area such as Bandarban. Overall, P. falciparum was the predominant malaria parasite. It contributed to an estimated pooled prevalence of 80% (95% CI 68%–88%). Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 93.6%) (Fig. 3). This heterogeneity suggests that the dominance of P.falciparum is not uniform across all regions and should be interpreted within specific epidemiological contexts.

Pooled prevalence of P. falciparum among malaria cases in the included studies

Pooled prevalence of Plasmodium vivax

This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that the prevalence of P. vivax in Bangladesh ranged from 5% in Haque U, et al. (2009) conducted across thirteen malaria endemic districts to 30% in Huwe T, et al. (2022) conducted in Chittagong. The pooled analysis identified that P. vivax as the second most prevalent malaria parasite in the country, with an estimated prevalence of 14% (95% CI 9%–21%). Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 82.1%) (Fig. 4). The variability in P.vivax prevalence may partly reflect differences in diagnostic sensitivity, as conventional methods may underdetect low-density or relapsing infections.

Pooled prevalence of P. vivax among malaria cases in the included studies

Pooled prevalence of mixed infection

In this study, 9 of the 14 included studies reported malaria cases caused by mixed infections. The prevalence ranged from 2% in the study by Alam MS et al. (2013) which investigated the overall Bangladeshi population with relatively lower transmission intensity. It reached as high as 17% in the study by Starzengruber P et al. (2014) conducted in the high-endemic district of Bandarban where co-circulation of multiple Plasmodium species is more common. The pooled analysis indicated that mixed infection is the least prevalent form of malaria in Bangladesh with an estimated prevalence of 7% (95% CI 4%–13%). Substantial heterogeneity was observed between studies as reflected by variations in study location, transmission intensity and diagnostic methods (I2 = 87%) (Fig. 5). This variability may be influenced by differences in diagnostic methods and the ability of studies to detect co-infections, particularly in low-density infections.

Pooled prevalence estimate of malaria cases caused by mixed infection in Bangladesh

Gender based pooled prevalence of malaria

Five of the fourteen included studies reported malaria prevalence separately for males and females [22–27]. Among males, prevalence ranged from 6% in Huwe T, et al. (2022) to 48% in Starzengruber P, et al. (2014). The pooled estimate under a random-effects model was 20% (95% CI 7%–47%), with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 98.5%) (see Fig. 6a), reflecting wide differences in study settings or population characteristics. For females, prevalence varied from 3% in Huwe et al. (2022) to 38% in Starzengruber et al. (2014). The pooled prevalence was lower than that of males, estimated at 12% (95% CI 3%–35%) again with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 98.8%) (see Fig. 6b).

Forest plot showing the pooled prevalence of malaria among a male and b female participants in Bangladesh

Temporal variation in malaria prevalence

To assess temporal changes in malaria endemicity, we performed separate random-effects meta-analyses for three periods: 2005–2010, 2011–2016, and 2017 onward (Figure S1). The pooled prevalence estimates were 15, 28, and 18%, respectively which is indicating a peak in 2011–2016 followed by a decline in recent years. Within each period, prevalence ranged widely 3–50% (2005–2010), 11.7–55.9% (2011–2016), and 5–72% (2017 onward) with high and significant heterogeneity observed across studies (I2 = 99.8, 99.5, and 99.5%, respectively).

Assessment of publication bias

The funnel plot demonstrated some asymmetry, with fewer studies on the left side and clustering of estimates on the right (Fig. 7). This pattern suggests potential small-study effects. However, Egger’s regression test was not statistically significant (p = 0.1312). While statistical evidence of publication bias was not detected, the observed asymmetry and small number of included studies limit the power of formal tests; therefore, the possibility of bias cannot be completely excluded.

Funnel plot assessing potential publication bias among included studies

Assessing robustness of pooled estimates

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of the pooled effect size. Log odds estimates ranged from − 1.83 to − 1.26 when individual studies were excluded (see Figure S2) and no single study materially altered the overall estimate. The largest effect occurred with Alam MS et al. (2017) included, while the smallest effect followed the exclusion of Alam MS et al. (2016). Despite these variations, the pooled estimate remained largely unchanged, suggesting that heterogeneity arises from broader study-level differences rather than the influence of any single study. This reinforces the reliability of the meta-analytic results.

Discussion

Malaria remains one of the most significant vector-borne diseases globally and continues to disproportionately affect vulnerable populations, including children under five and pregnant women [41]. Despite regional progress in malaria control, transmission persists in localized high-risk settings, emphasizing the need for context-specific epidemiological understanding. In Bangladesh, malaria continues to be a public health concern, particularly in southeastern regions where seasonal transmission contributes to increased healthcare burden [42]. Accurate identification of high-risk areas is therefore essential for effective resource allocation and targeted intervention strategies [43]. Malaria transmission in Bangladesh is geographically heterogeneous. The southeastern districts report substantially higher prevalence than the northeastern endemic districts, with the Chittagong Hill Tracts (CHT) representing the epicenter of malaria burden [33]. However, the magnitude of this prevalences varies across studies, reflecting differences in study design, study time and surveillance intensity. The hilly terrain, dense forest cover, and abundant water bodies in the CHT create favorable ecological conditions for Anopheles mosquito breeding and sustained transmission [44]. Similar ecological associations have been reported in other forested and hilly regions of Asia, where environmental factors strongly influence vector abundance and transmission intensity [29, 45]. These environmental and ecological factors likely contribute to localized transmission patterns rather than uniform national-level prevalence. Given its disproportionate contribution to national malaria morbidity, sustained and intensified control efforts in the CHT remain essential [46].

Our systematic review and meta-analysis, which included 14 eligible studies encompassing 33,010 malaria infections, provides updated national-level estimates of malaria prevalence and species distribution in Bangladesh. The pooled overall prevalence was 16.45% (95% CI 6.18–37.04), with extremely high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.6%, p = 0.00). This high heterogeneity indicates that the pooled estimate should be interpreted as an average across diverse settings rather than a precise national prevalence. P. falciparum accounted for the majority of infections, with a pooled prevalence of 80% (95% CI 68–88), while P. vivax represented 14% (95% CI 9–21). Although P.falciparum predominance was consistently observed, its relative contribution varied across geographic locations and study populations. The predominance of P. falciparum in Bangladesh, especially in CHT, is particularly concerning, given its higher virulence, potential for severe disease, and the global threat of antimalarial drug resistance [47–50]. However, the contribution of non-falciparum species and mixed infections may be underestimated due to diagnostic limitations, as indicated in both the current analysis and previous studies. These findings are broadly consistent with reports from other parts of South and Southeast Asia but also reveal important regional variation. Higher prevalence estimates have been reported in India (44%) [51] and Thailand (33%) [52]. In contrast, studies in Afghanistan and China reported substantially lower prevalence 0.15% (95% CI 0.10–0.21; I2 = 83.3%) and 9% (95% CI 7–12%), respectively [53, 54]. Such differences should be interpreted in the context of varying surveillance systems, diagnostic methods, and transmission settings rather than direct epidemiological comparability. Pakistan, which shares ecological and epidemiological similarities with Bangladesh, reported a pooled prevalence of 23.3% between 2006 and 2021, with P. vivax predominating over P. falciparum and mixed infections accounting for 4% of cases [55]. The exceptionally high heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis is likely multifactorial. Differences in diagnostic methods, study area, population characteristics, and sampling strategies are key contributors to variability across studies. Microscopy and rRDTs remain the cornerstone of malaria surveillance in endemic settings, yet both have well-documented limitations. Microscopy requires skilled personnel and may fail to detect low-density parasitemia or accurately differentiate morphologically similar species, particularly in mixed infections [56, 57]. RDTs are rapid and operationally convenient but are largely optimized for P.falciparum detection through HRP2 antigens and exhibit reduced sensitivity for non-falciparum species and mixed infections [58, 59]. These limitations may have resulted in underestimation of true prevalence and biased species distribution in some studies included in the analysis. In addition, RDT performance can be affected by sustained antigenemia after parasite clearance and by genetic deletions (e.g., HRP2/3), further reducing diagnostic accuracy in certain settings [60].

In contrast, molecular methods such as PCR and qPCR offer substantially higher sensitivity and improved species resolution, enabling the detection of low-density, mixed-species and submicroscopic infections [61, 62]. Accordingly, in studies reporting multiple diagnostic approaches, PCR or qPCR-based prevalence estimates were preferentially used in the meta-analysis to minimize underestimation. Nonetheless, variation in diagnostic strategies and reporting standards across studies likely contributed to residual heterogeneity [63]. Studies relying solely on microscopy or RDTs may have underestimated the true prevalence, particularly of non-falciparum and mixed infections [64]. Seasonal timing, study design, and population characteristics further contributed to observed variability, as noted in previous malaria meta-analyses [65–67]. Therefore, the observed heterogeneity reflects true epidemiological diversity as well as methodological differences across studies. Malaria control interventions may differentially affect Plasmodium species. While artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) and HRP2-based RDTs are highly effective against P. falciparum, P. vivax presents additional challenges due to its dormant liver stage (hypnozoites), which can cause relapses if radical cure with primaquine or tafenoquine is not administered [68, 69]. This biological difference underscores the importance of species-specific diagnosis and treatment strategies, particularly in settings where multiple species co-circulate. Mixed infections may be underdiagnosed and incompletely treated, potentially sustaining transmission.

Gender-based analysis indicated higher malaria prevalence among males (20%) compared to females (12%), although heterogeneity was very high (I2 > 98%). Given the limited number of studies and substantial variability, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. The observed difference may reflect behavioral and occupational exposures, particularly among males engaged in outdoor activities [70, 71]. However, the lack of consistent age-stratified data limits more detailed interpretation of demographic risk patterns. Temporal analysis revealed that pooled prevalence of malaria peaked during 2011–2016 (28%) compared to 15% in 2005–2010 and 18% after 2017 [72, 73]. Although fluctuations were observed, malaria burden remained substantial over time, indicating persistent endemic transmission despite ongoing control efforts. This pattern aligns with WHO global estimates, which show a steady decline in malaria cases from 2005 to 2014, followed by a sustained resurgence from 2015 onward, with the highest global case counts reported in 2023–2024 (Table S3). This reversal has been attributed to disruptions in malaria prevention and treatment services, emerging insecticide and drug resistance and health system constraints [74]. The parallel between global trends and national patterns in Bangladesh highlights the influence of global malaria dynamics on local transmission.

From a programmatic perspective, the predominance of P.falciparum highlights the need for continued focus on this species, whereas the presence of P.vivax and mixed infections necessitates integrated strategies, including radical cure for hypnozoites and improved diagnostic capacity [75]. However, intervention strategies should be tailored to local epidemiological contexts rather than relying solely on aggregated national estimates. In high-burden areas such as the CHT, intensified vector control, strengthened surveillance, and improved access to diagnosis and treatment are essential. Furthermore, enhancing diagnostic capacity, including the integration of molecular tools in surveillance systems, may improve detection of low-density and asymptomatic infections, which are increasingly recognized as important contributors to ongoing transmission. Regular, well-designed epidemiological studies are needed to monitor changing malaria patterns and to support evidence-based malaria elimination strategies in Bangladesh [76, 77].

Limitations of the study

This meta-analysis relied on published studies which may be subject to publication bias despite no statistically significant evidence from Egger’s test. Substantial heterogeneity was observed, likely reflecting variations in study design, population characteristics, geographic settings, and diagnostic approaches. Importantly, many included surveys had limited geographic coverage, being conducted in selected high-risk or localized endemic areas rather than representing nationwide populations. Age coverage was also inconsistent, with several studies focusing primarily on specific age groups, such as under-five children or selected community subgroups, rather than encompassing all age ranges. In addition, time coverage was often restricted to short survey periods, which may not fully capture seasonal or interannual fluctuations in malaria transmission. Furthermore, reliance on microscopy and RDTs methods with reduced sensitivity for low-density parasitemia, non-falciparum species, and mixed infections may have led to underestimation of true prevalence and species misclassification. Variations in local transmission intensity, environmental conditions, and intervention coverage likely contributed further to the observed heterogeneity. Analytical limitations, including limited availability of age- and region-specific data, also constrain our ability to fully assess temporal trends and species-specific patterns. Consequently, the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, as they may not be fully generalizable to the entire country. Nevertheless, the findings provide an important synthesized overview of malaria epidemiology in Bangladesh and offer a useful reference for guiding surveillance, control, and elimination efforts.

Conclusions

Malaria continues to pose a substantial public health challenge in Bangladesh which is predominantly driven by P. falciparum and P. vivax. The pooled prevalence of malaria was 16%, with P. falciparum accounting for the majority of cases followed by P. vivax, while mixed infections were less frequent. Higher prevalence in males compared to females highlights gender-specific differences in malaria risk. Despite considerable heterogeneity among studies, sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings. These results underscore the need for targeted malaria control and elimination strategies that consider parasite species distribution and gender-specific risks. Enhanced surveillance, preventive measures and tailored interventions are critical to reducing the malaria burden and advancing elimination efforts in Bangladesh.
