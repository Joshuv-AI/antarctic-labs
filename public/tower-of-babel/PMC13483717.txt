Liver cancer derived high core fucosylation sEV elict malignancy by activating PI3K/AKT signaling pathway
Authors: Zhuo Li, Na Zhang, Wei Zhang, Jian Zhou, Juan Gao, Xin Wang, Yingjie Ren, Yunjie Wang, Ruiman Geng, Chunyan Yang, Feng Guan, Zhen Zhai, Lin Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall extracellular vesicles (sEV) play a key role in liver cancer (LC) by supporting cancer cell survival, local invasion, and distant metastasis. Although sEV surfaces are known to be richly glycosylated, how these glycans influence sEV function remains poorly understood. In this study, we observed elevated levels of core fucosylation in LC tissues, serum, and cells, as well as in serum-derived sEV. sEV isolated from highly metastatic MHCC97H cells or from LC patient serum enhanced the proliferation and migration of recipient cells. Conversely, inhibiting core fucosylation on sEV suppressed these malignant phenotypes. Mechanistically, integrated proteomic and glycoproteomic profiling identified lectin galactoside-binding soluble 3-binding protein (LGALS3BP) as significantly enriched in LC serum-derived sEV and modified by core fucosylation. Knockdown of LGALS3BP in donor cells attenuated the malignant behavior of recipient cells by inhibiting PI3K-AKT signaling. Our findings reveal a crucial role for core fucosylation in mediating the pro-tumor functions of LC-derived sEV and suggest that targeting sEV glycosylation may offer a novel therapeutic strategy for LC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02944-7.Keywords: Small extracellular vesicle, Core fucosylation, Liver cancer, LGALS3BP

Introduction

Small extracellular vesicles (sEV), ranging in diameter from 40 to 160 nm [1], are released by virtually all cell types and function as crucial mediators of intercellular communication. They transfer bioactive molecules—including nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, and metabolites—to recipient cells, thereby influencing various physiological and pathological processes [2]. In cancer, sEV contribute to tumor development and progression by remodeling the tumor microenvironment, promoting angiogenesis, metastasis, and invasion, as well as facilitating immune evasion [3–6]. sEV are enriched in glycoconjugates, with many of their protein and lipid cargos being extensively glycosylated [7]. This glycosylation plays a key role in sEV biogenesis, cargo sorting, cellular recognition, and uptake [8]. For instance, syndecan heparan sulfate proteoglycans regulate sEV formation through interactions with the cytoplasmic adaptor syntenin-1 and the Endosomal Sorting Complexes Required for Transport (ESCRT) accessory protein Alix [9, 10]. Our previous work further demonstrated that bisecting N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) modifications attenuate the pro‑metastatic activity of breast cancer-derived sEV [11].

A notable form of N-glycosylation is core fucosylation, in which fucose is attached to the innermost GlcNAc residue of N-glycans. This modification is catalyzed by fucosyltransferase 8 (FUT8) [12], and has been implicated in immune regulation and tumor progression [13–15]. Elevated FUT8 expression and core fucosylation levels are closely associated with the advancement of LC [16]. For example, Li Jia et al. reported that increased core fucosylation of folate receptor 1 (FOLR1) enhances folate uptake in LC cells, promoting epithelial‑mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastasis [17]. In clinical practice, serum alpha‑fetoprotein (AFP) is a widely used diagnostic marker for LC [18]. Glycoform analysis reveals that core‑fucosylated AFP (AFP‑L3) can distinguish cirrhosis from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and serves as a predictor of malignancy risk. Despite its established role in cellular glycosylation, whether and how core fucosylation modifies sEV in LC and how it influences their function remains poorly understood.

In this study, we evaluated FUT8 expression and core fucosylation levels in LC tissues, cells, and serum. We further investigated how core fucosylation affects the biological functions of sEV derived from LC cells and patient serum. Using integrated proteomic and glycoproteomic analyses, we identified LGALS3BP as highly enriched in LC sEV and modified by core fucosylation. Building on this finding, we aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which core fucosylation regulates the oncogenic functions of vesicular LGALS3BP in LC.

Materials and methods

Patients and specimens

Human tissue specimens were collected from 10 patients with HCC. Serum samples were obtained from 55 patients with HCC, 7 patients with other types of LC, and 62 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HC). The basic information of the patients of the specimens used in the text is listed in Table S1. All samples were provided by The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Medical University (Xi’an, China). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Xi’an Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection.

Cell lines and culture

Human LC cell lines HepG2 and HCCLM3, along with the human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T, were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The highly metastatic LC cell line MHCC97H was generously provided by the laboratory of Prof. Shu Zhang at Fudan University (Shanghai, China). All cells-HEK293T, HepG2, HCCLM3, and MHCC97H-were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; GENOM, Hangzhou, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Viva Biotech, Shanghai, China) and penicillin‑streptomycin, and were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Equal amounts of proteins were separated by SDS‑PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST (Tris‑buffered saline with 0.1% Tween‑20) and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: FUT8 (sc‑271244, Santa Cruz, USA), Lens culinaris agglutinin (LCA, L‑1040, Vector Laboratories, USA), GAPDH (60004‑1‑Ig, Proteintech, USA), P85 (sc‑1637, Santa Cruz), p‑P85 (17366, CST, USA), AKT (9272, CST), p‑AKT (4060, CST), TSG101 (T55985, Abmart, China), Alix (A2215, ABclonal, China), CD63 (EPR5702, Abcam, UK), and CD81 (T55472, Abmart). Protein bands were visualized using an ECL PicoLight Chemiluminescence Kit (Epizyme, Shanghai, China) and imaged with a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Flow cytometric analysis

Following trypsinization, cells were harvested and blocked with 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. They were then incubated with biotin-conjugated LCA (Vector Laboratories), followed by staining with HRP-labeled streptavidin (Beyotime). After washing with PBS, cells were resuspended in staining buffer and analyzed immediately using a flow cytometer (ACEA Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were processed and visualized with NovoExpress software (ACEA Biosciences).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Cells were cultured on glass coverslips in 24-well plates. After reaching appropriate confluence, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature (RT) and subsequently blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. For glycan detection, samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with biotin-conjugated LCA, followed by a 30-minute incubation with fluorophore-conjugated streptavidin (YEASEN, Shanghai, China). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime) for 10 min at RT. Following PBS washes, coverslips were mounted with antifade glycerol medium. Images were captured using an Eclipse E600 confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The expression level of target glycans was quantified by measuring the average fluorescence intensity in at least three independent fields using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Background subtraction was applied using a rolling ball radius of 50 pixels. The mean fluorescence intensity of LCA staining within each region of interest (ROI) was measured using the “Measure” function [19].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described [11]. In brief, tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an antibody against FUT8 (Ab198741, Abcam). Following washes with PBS, the sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB reagent (Maxim; Fuzhou, Fujian, China), and images were captured with a transmission electron microscopy (model H-7650; Hitachi; Tokyo, Japan). Quantitative immunohistochemistry was performed using imageJ to analyze the mean optical density of the staining signal. Staining resulting from the peroxidase-DAB reaction was separated and segmented using the color deconvolution plug-in (ImageJ). The separated immunohistochemical signals were then converted to 16-bit images comprising different gray values. The numerical data obtained from each image reflected integrated gray intensities of each particular cell, thereby rendering the average gray value used for further statistical analyses. The average gray value was measured for each case analyzing five randomly selected microscopic fields per slide [20].

Isolation of sEV

Cells were cultured in DMEM complete medium until confluence were reached 80%-90%, then incubated in serum-free medium for 48 h. Conditioned medium (CM) was collected and subjected to sequential centrifugation at 500 × g for 10 min, 2,000 × g for 20 min, 11,000 × g for 30 min, and twice at 110,000 × g for 1 h using an ultracentrifuge (Optima XE-100, Beckman Coulter Life Sciences; Indianapolis, IN, USA). The pellets were resuspended in PBS and designed as sEV. The total protein concentration of the sEV preparations was quantified using the BCA assay.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

To assess the level of core fucosylation on sEV, serum samples or CM were added to multi-well ELISA plates pre-coated with anti-CD63 antibody and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The plates were then washed with PBS, blocked with 3% BSA, and incubated with biotinylated LCA for 30 min at 37 °C. After another wash with PBS, the plates were incubated with HRP-conjugated streptavidin for 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Promega; Madison, USA) was added, and the reaction was terminated with 2 M sulfuric acid. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

To measure core fucosylation and LGALS3BP levels in serum or cell CM, serum samples or cell CM were added to ELISA plates and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The plates were then blocked, followed by incubation with either biotinylated LCA or anti-LGALS3BP antibody. Detection was carried out using TMB substrate as described above.

To assess levels of core fucosylated LGALS3BP on serum-derived sEV, sEV isolated from serum by ultracentrifugation were added to multi-well ELISA plates pre-coated with anti-LGALS3BP antibody. After blocking with BSA, the captured sEV were incubated with biotinylated LCA, and binding was visualized using TMB reagent as described above.

Immunoprecipitation (IP)

Serum samples from HC and LC patients were subjected to ultracentrifugation for sEV isolation. The isolated sEV were lysed, and the lysate was split: one portion served as the Input control, while the other was incubated overnight with an anti‑LGALS3BP antibody. The antibody‑antigen complex was then captured using Protein A/G beads during a 2‑hour incubation at 4 °C. After low‑speed centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in loading buffer, heated at 95 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was analyzed by western blot.

Uptake of sEV

Purified sEV (100 µg) was incubated with ExoTracker (5 mM, 1:500, v/v) [21] for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. The labeling reaction was terminated by adding 100 µM glycine. The labeled sEV were then incubated with cells for 1 h at 37 °C, after which the cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cell proliferation

Cells were cultured in 70%-80% degree of confluence, incubated with EdU (10 µM, Promega; Madison, Wisconsin, USA) for 8 h, harvested by EDTA-Trypsin digestion at 37 °C, fixed in 5% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized in 0.2% TritonX-100 for 10 min, and labeled with fluorescence via iclick reaction. The percentage of cell proliferation was determined using flow cytometry.

Transwell assay

Cell migration assays were performed using Transwell inserts (pore size 8 μm; Corning; Corning, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1 × 104 cells were cultured in serum-free medium supplemented with sEV (100 µg/mL) for 24 h, then seeded into the upper chambers. The lower chambers were filled with DMEM complete medium, and the cells were allowed to migrate for 48 h. After incubation, non‑migrated cells on the upper surface of the inserts were gently removed with cotton swabs. Cells that had migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, and imaged under a microscope.

Cell apoptosis

Apoptosis was assessed using the APC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with 7-AAD (BioLegend; San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested with EDTA‑free trypsin, washed with PBS, and pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min. The cell pellets were then resuspended in 200 µL of 1× binding buffer containing 2 µL of APC‑Annexin V and 2 µL of 7‑AAD, and incubated for 20 min in the dark. Stained cells were subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry. For the analysis of apoptotic cell populations, live cells were defined as Annexin V-negative/7-AAD-negative (lower left quadrant), early apoptotic cells were defined as Annexin V‑positive/7‑AAD‑negative (lower right quadrant), late apoptotic cells were defined as Annexin V‑positive/7‑AAD‑positive (upper right quadrant), necrotic cells were defined as Annexin V-negative/7-AAD-positive (upper left quadrant).

Proteomics and glycoproteomics

Protein lysis and digestion

serum samples from healthy controls and patients with LC were collected, and sEV were isolated by ultracentrifugation. The sEV pellets were denatured with 8 M urea/50 mM NH₄HCO₃, followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant for protein quantification, reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), alkylated with 15 mM iodoacetamide (IAM), and the alkylation reaction was then quenched by adding DTT to a final concentration of 2.5 mM, followed by incubation at 37 °C. The sample was digested with trypsin (Promega; Madison, WI, USA).

Peptide desalting

The resulting peptide mixture was acidified with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to a final concentration of 1% (pH < 2) and collected by centrifugation and purified using Oasis HLB cartridges (Waters; Milford, MA, USA). Desalted peptides were collected, lyophilized, and then subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis for proteomics.

For glycoproteomics, N-glycopeptides were enriched by MAX column (Waters) using desalted peptides. The cartridges were washed with 0.1% TFA in water, and peptides were eluted with 60% acetonitrile (ACN) containing 0.1% TFA. The eluate was collected, lyophilized, and stored at -80 °C until mass spectrometry analysis.

Data analysis

Raw mass spectrometry data of proteomics were processed and quantified using the MaxQuant software platform. For glycoproteomics, the raw mass spectrometry data were processed using Glyco-Decipher. Relative intensity was analyzed and generated using FlexAnalysis software (Bruker Daltonics; Bremen, Germany) based on MALDI-TOF/TOF–MS intensity. m/z data were obtained and annotated using GlycoWorkbench software program.

All omics raw data generated in this study have been deposited into a jPOSTrepo repository [22].

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) at least three biologically replicates. For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was determined using two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***). Statistical analysis were performed using SPSS software 29.0 (IBM; Amonk, NY, USA), and graphical representations were generated with GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Company; San Diego, California, USA).

Results

Levels of core fucosylation in LC

Aberrant expression of FUT8 and the subsequent elevation of core fucosylation have been implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple human cancers [23, 24]. In the present study, interrogation of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets revealed that FUT8 transcript levels were significantly upregulated in LC tissues compared with non‑malignant liver tissues (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, elevated FUT8 mRNA expression was observed across diverse tumor stages and histological grades, suggesting a potential association with disease progression (Fig. 1B and C). Immunohistochemical analysis corroborated these findings, demonstrating increased FUT8 protein expression in LC tissues relative to paired adjacent non‑tumor tissues (Fig. 1D). In line with these observations, the level of core fucosylation was markedly elevated in serum samples obtained from LC patients compared with those from HC (Fig. 1E).

To further investigate the correlation between FUT8 expression, core fucosylation, and hepatocellular malignancy, we examined a panel of LC cell lines with distinct tumorigenic potentials. Notably, the highly aggressive MHCC97H and HCCLM3 cells have high levels of FUT8 and core fucosylation modification (Fig. 1F). Consistently, FUT8 mRNA levels were elevated in MHCC97H cells relative to HepG2 cells (Fig. 1G), and this was accompanied by enhanced core fucosylation in the highly malignant cell lines (Fig. 1F, H and I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that both FUT8 expression and core fucosylation are progressively upregulated in the context of LC progression and aggressiveness.

Effects of core fucosylated sEV from LC serum on malignant phenotypes of recipient cells

To investigate whether core fucosylation is aberrantly regulated on sEV in LC, sEV were isolated from the serum of LC patients and healthy controls using differential ultracentrifugation. These preparations were termed LC sEV and HC sEV, respectively. Characterization of the isolated vesicles confirmed the presence of canonical sEV markers, including CD81, TSG101, and Alix, as assessed by western blot (Fig. 2A). Transmission electron microscopy revealed the typical cup‑shaped morphology of sEV (Fig. 2B), and nanoparticle tracking analysis demonstrated that the isolated vesicles exhibited a mean diameter of approximately 136.4 nm (Fig. 2C), consistent with previously established sEV characteristics. Notably, lectin blot analysis using biotinylated LCA revealed that core fucosylation levels were markedly elevated on LC sEV compared to HC sEV (Fig. 2A&D), indicating that sEV from LC patients carry enhanced core-fucosylated glycans. To assess the functional relevance of this modification, sEV were labeled with the fluorescent probe ExoTracker and incubated with recipient HepG2 cells. Both LC sEV and HC sEV were efficiently internalized by recipient cells, as visualized by flow cytometer (Fig. 2E). However, only LC sEV significantly increased core fucosylation levels within the recipient cells (Fig. 2F), suggesting that the fucosylated cargo of LC sEV can be functionally transferred to target cells.

We next examined the impact of LC sEV uptake on the malignant phenotypes of recipient cells. Compared with HC sEV treatment, exposure to LC sEV significantly enhanced the proliferative capacity of HepG2 cells, as determined by EdU incorporation assays (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, Transwell migration assays demonstrated that LC sEV markedly increased the migratory ability of recipient cells (Fig. 2H&I). Conversely, flow cytometric analysis of Annexin V/7‑AAD staining revealed that LC sEV treatment suppressed apoptosis in HepG2 cells relative to HC sEV treatment (Fig. 2J).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that sEV derived from the serum of LC patients carry elevated levels of core fucosylation and are capable of promoting malignant phenotypes, including enhanced proliferation, migration, and reduced apoptosis in recipient cells. These findings suggest that core fucosylated sEV may serve as mediators of intercellular communication that contribute to LC progression.

Effects of core fucosylated sEV from LC cells on malignant phenotypes of recipient cells

To further elucidate the biological role of core fucosylation on sEV in the context of LC, sEV were isolated from three LC cell lines with distinct malignant potentials: HepG2, MHCC97H, and HCCLM3. Western blot analysis confirmed that sEV derived from all three cell lines expressed the canonical sEV markers CD81, TSG101, and Alix (Fig. 3A). Notably, lectin blot analysis using biotinylated LCA revealed that core fucosylation levels were markedly higher on sEV derived from the highly malignant MHCC97H and HCCLM3 cells compared to those from the low malignancy HepG2 cells. Among these, MHCC97H‑derived sEV exhibited the highest levels of core fucosylation, whereas HepG2‑derived sEV displayed the lowest levels (Fig. 3A&B). Based on these observations, MHCC97H cells were selected as donor cells and HepG2 cells as recipient cells for subsequent functional studies. MHCC97H‑derived sEV were isolated and characterized (Fig. S1A&B), then labeled with the fluorescent probe ExoTracker to visualize their uptake. Fluorescence microscopy demonstrated that MHCC97H‑derived sEV were efficiently internalized by recipient HepG2 cells (Fig. 3C). Importantly, incubation with MHCC97H‑derived sEV significantly elevated core fucosylation levels in recipient cells compared to controls (Fig. 3D), indicating functional transfer of fucosylated cargo. We next assessed the impact of MHCC97H‑derived sEV on the malignant phenotypes of recipient cells. sEV from donor cells were fed to recipient cells, showing that MHCC97H derived sEV enhanced proliferation (Fig. 3E), migratory ability (Fig. 3F). Conversely, flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that MHCC97H‑derived sEV suppressed apoptosis in recipient cells (Fig. 3G&H). To corroborate these findings, CM from donor cells was collected and applied to recipient cells. Consistent with the sEV treatment results, CM from MHCC97H cells significantly enhanced proliferation and suppressed apoptosis in recipient HepG2 cells compared to CM from HepG2 cells (Fig. S1C&D). Collectively, these results suggest that highly core fucosylated sEV derived from highly malignant cells promoted the malignancy of recipient cells.

The oncogenic role of ectopic core fucosylation on sEV from LC cells

To test the hypothesis that the oncogenic activity of sEV derived from the serum of LC patients and highly malignant cells is driven by elevated core fucosylation, we manipulated FUT8 expression in donor cells and examined the consequent effects on sEV‑mediated phenotypic modulation in recipient cells. FUT8 was ectopically overexpressed in HepG2 cells, resulting in marked upregulation of FUT8 at both protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 4A and B), accompanied by a substantial increase in global core fucosylation (Fig. 4A). sEV were subsequently isolated from control and FUT8‑overexpressing cells (hereafter referred to as NC sEV and FUT8 sEV, respectively), and their identity was confirmed by the presence of the canonical sEV markers TSG101 and CD81 (Fig. 4C). Notably, FUT8 overexpression in donor cells significantly enhanced core fucosylation levels on the derived sEV (Fig. 4C). Importantly, this ectopic core fucosylation did not affect sEV biogenesis or release, as evidenced by comparable particle numbers and protein yields between NC sEV and FUT8 sEV preparations (Fig. 4D). Both NC sEV and FUT8 sEV were efficiently internalized by recipient cells, and the introduction of sEV did not alter their uptake efficiency (Fig. 4E). However, treatment with FUT8 sEV significantly elevated core fucosylation levels in recipient cells compared to NC sEV treatment (Fig. 4F). Functionally, FUT8 CM treatment enhanced the proliferative capacity of recipient cells (Fig. S2A). Furthermore, FUT8 sEV markedly promoted the migratory and proliferation ability of recipient cells (Fig. 4G and H), while flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that FUT8 sEV conferred resistance to apoptosis (Fig. 4I and J). Similar phenotypic effects were observed in recipient cells treated with CM from FUT8‑overexpressing cells (Fig. S2B).

Consistently, knockdown of FUT8 in donor cells (Fig. 4K and L) reduced core fucosylation levels on sEV (Fig. 4M). Compared with the sh-NC sEV group, the FUT8-silenced sEV group showed lower core fucosylation levels in recipient cells (Fig. 4N), resulting in decreased cell proliferation, migration and anti-apoptotic ability (Fig. 4O-Q), without affecting sEV release and uptake (Fig. S2C&D). These findings indicated an oncogenic role of core fucosylation on sEV from LC cells.

Core fucosylated LGALS3BP facilitated the malignant function of sEV from LC cells

To identify vesicular glycoproteins bearing core fucosylation, we performed proteomics and glycoproteomics analysis on sEV isolated from the serum of HC and LC patients. Proteomic profiling identified a total of 315 proteins in serum‑derived sEV from HC and LC groups (Fig. S3A). Among these, 129 proteins exhibited differential abundance in LC serum sEV compared to HC, with 47 proteins significantly upregulated and 82 downregulated (Fig. 5A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the upregulated proteins were primarily associated with response to stress, signaling receptor binding, extracellular space, and complement and coagulation cascades (Fig. S3B). Glycoproteomic analysis further enabled the identification of core fucosylated glycoproteins in serum sEV. A total of 64 glycoproteins were confidently identified in HC and LC serum sEV (Fig. S3C). Based on the diagnostic mass spectrometry signatures corresponding to core fucosylated glycopeptides (pep‑N1F1 to pep‑N2H3F1), LGALS3BP was identified as a carrier of core fucosylation (Fig. 5B). By comparing the glycosylation of LGALS3BP in sEV between the HC and LC group, it was found that LGALS3BP in LC sEV was modified by more types of core fucosylated (Fig. 5C). Galectin3 has been shown to bind LGALS3BP and activate relative signal transduction [25]. The interaction between LGALS3BP and Galectin3 was stronger in recipient cells treated with LC sEV than in HC sEV (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, quantitative analysis by IP combined with lectin‑based ELISA demonstrated that core fucosylation levels on LGALS3BP were significantly elevated in LC serum sEV relative to HC (Fig. 5E&F). Collectively, these findings identify LGALS3BP as a core fucosylated glycoprotein enriched on sEV from LC patients. Bioinformatic analysis of TCGA dataset revealed that LGALS3BP mRNA expression was significantly elevated in LC tissues compared to normal liver tissues (Fig. S3D&E). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that LC patients with high LGALS3BP expression exhibited significantly shorter overall survival than those with low expression (Fig. S3F), suggesting a potential prognostic value of LGALS3BP in LC. Consistent with the tissue-level findings, LGALS3BP protein levels were markedly increased in sEV derived from the serum of LC patients compared to HC (Fig. S3G&H). These results indicate that LGALS3BP is not only upregulated in LC tissues but also enriched on sEV. We next investigated whether core fucosylation modulates LGALS3BP expression in the context of sEV-mediated intercellular communication. Ectopic induction of core fucosylation via FUT8 overexpression in donor cells significantly increased LGALS3BP levels both in donor cell lysates and in their derived sEV (Fig. 5H&I). Furthermore, treatment of recipient cells with highly core fucosylated sEV—either derived from FUT8-overexpressing cell lines or isolated from LC patient serum—resulted in elevated LGALS3BP levels in recipient cells (Fig. 5G and Fig. S3I). Collectively, these findings suggest that core fucosylation facilitates the enrichment and intercellular transfer of LGALS3BP via sEV, which may contribute to its pro-tumorigenic role in LC progression.

To further investigate the biological function of vesicular LGALS3BP in LC, LGALS3BP was silenced in donor cells MHCC97H (Fig. 7J&K). Subsequently, recipient cells were co-cultured with MHCC97H cells, revealing that LGALS3BP knockdown contributed to a decreased cell proliferation, migration abilities and increased apoptosis (Fig. 7L-N). Core fucosylated sEV facilitates LC by activating PI3K/AKT pathway

A study found LGALS3BP regulated oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) proliferation and migration via PI3K/AKT pathway [26]. In serum proteomics, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway was also enriched by the GO and KEGG pathway analysis of significantly upregulated proteins (Fig. S3B). Therefore, we investigated whether the PI3K/AKT pathway was modulated in recipient cells treated with sEV from LC cells. The phosphorylation levels of P85 (p-P85), the regulatory subunit of PI3K, and phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) were elevated in recipient cells treated with sEV from serum of LC patients (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, receptor cells were treated with LC sEV, and sucrose and lactose were added separately to observe the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. The results showed that the addition of lactose could reverse the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway caused by LC sEV, while sucrose could not, indicating that LGALS3BP functions in a galectin-3 dependent manner (Fig. 6B). Similarly, treatment of recipient cells with sEV from MHCC97H cells (Fig. 6C), as well as sEV from FUT8 overexpressed cells (Fig. 6D), p-P85 and p-AKT were elevated. Vice versa, the levels of p-P85 and p-AKT were decreased in recipient cells treated with sEV derived from FUT8 silenced MHCC97H cells (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, levels of p-P85 and p-AKT were reduced in recipient cells cocultured with LGALS3BP silenced MHCC97H (Fig. 6I). The proliferation, migration and anti-apoptosis of recipient cells treated by sEV from serum of LC patients were suppressed by the treatment of PI3K inhibitor (LY294002) and AKT inhibitor (MK2206) (Fig. 6F-H). Taken together, these findings suggested that core fucosylated vesicular LGALS3BP acted a pivotal role in LC via the activation of PI3K/AKT pathway.

Discussion

Glycans, as essential biological macromolecules, participate in the composition of cellular communication networks and play critical roles in diverse biological processes. Aberrant glycosylation patterns have been implicated in tumor metastasis across multiple cancer types, including colon [27], pancreatic [28], breast [29], oral squamous [30], and HCC [31]. Clinically, several validated cancer biomarkers are glycoconjugates, such as CA19-9 (a sialyl-Lewis antigen) for pancreatic cancer [32], prostate-specific antigen (PSA) for prostate cancer [33], AFP and AFP-L3 for HCC, and HER2 for breast cancer [34–36]. In this study, we observed elevated levels of core fucosylation and its corresponding glycosyltransferase FUT8 in LC tissues, serum, and cell lines (Fig. 1). Consistently, Yanru Guo et al. reported the upregulation of FUT8 and core-fucosylated HSP90 in LC cells [16]. Dysregulated FUT8 expression in LC may result from multiple pathogenic factors, including aberrant activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, which enhances FUT8 transcription via T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor binding [37]. Moreover, the lncRNA HOTAIR has been shown to promote FUT8 transcription by recruiting p300 to facilitate STAT3 binding, thereby contributing to LC progression [16]. FUT8 expression is also regulated by miR-26a, miR-34a, and miR-146a through binding to its 3’UTR, suppressing LC progression [38]. Together, these findings highlight the aberrant core fucosylation status in LC and summarize the key regulatory mechanisms governing FUT8 expression.

Plant lectin LCA was used to detect FUT8-dependent core fucosylation in our experiments. Actually, α1,6-Fucosidase treatment can also serve a complementary approach for detecting core fucosylation. α1,6-fucosidase is a specific enzyme that cleaves α1,6-linked core fucose. Its substrate specificity is primarily restricted to free or oligosaccharide substrates. However, in the context of intact glycoproteins—particularly those with complex N-glycan structures that are further processed by enzymes such as GnT-V—the core fucose moiety is often sterically shielded within the compact three-dimensional conformation of the glycoprotein. This leads to a limited cleavage efficiency of α1,6-fucosidase toward core-fucosylated glycoproteins.

To compensate for this limitation, FUT8 overexpression or knock-down cells were constructed. FUT8 is the sole enzyme responsible for adding α1,6-fucose to N-glycans in mammals. Therefore, manipulating FUT8 levels specifically alters core fucosylation without affecting other fucose linkages. Overexpression of FUT8 specifically increased LCA signals (Fig. 4A), while FUT8 knockdown abolished them (Fig. 4K). This direct correlation between FUT8 enzymatic activity and LCA binding strongly indicates that the signals reflect core fucosylation.

Accumulating evidence indicates that the surface of sEV is extensively modified by glycans, and such glycosylation patterns critically influence sEV function, including cellular uptake, secretion, cargo sorting and biodistribution. For instance, removal of N-glycans from B16-BL6 sEV enhanced their uptake by peritoneal macrophages [39]. Hirosawa et al. demonstrated that sEV internalization occurs via clathrin-independent endocytosis mediated by Galectin3, which recognizes glycans containing LacNAc structure, and lysosome-associated membrane protein-2 C [40]. In our study, sEV derived from LC serum and cells exhibited altered core fucosylation levels, yet these variations did not affect sEV secretion (Fig. 4D&Fig. S2C) or their endocytosis by recipient cells (Fig. 4E&Fig. S2D). Multiple mechanisms mediate sEV uptake, including direct interactions between sEV surface proteins and target cell membrane receptors, membrane fusion, and various endocytic pathways such as phagocytosis, micropinocytosis, and receptor-mediated endocytosis involving clathrin, caveolin, or lipid rafts [41]. Notably, sialylation of vesicular integrin β1 promotes sEV entry into recipient cells by enhancing fibronectin binding [42]. We identified several core-fucosylated sEV membrane proteins, including ADAM10, TFRC, LRP1, ITGA2B, ITGB3, CD9, and CD14. Among these, ITGA2B, ITGB3, CD9, TFRC, and LRP1 have been implicated in sEV endocytosis. Specifically, ITGB3 facilitates vesicle internalization through activation of the FAK signaling pathway [43], while LRP1 mediates sEV uptake via endocytic mechanisms [44]. Our results indicate that core fucosylation alterations do not significantly impact sEV internalization in LC cells, suggesting that sEV uptake in this context occurs through a core fucosylation-independent mechanism.

Glycans also play pivotal roles in protein folding, degradation and secretion. Recent studies have shown that alterations in complex-type N-glycans regulate the recruitment of EWI-2 into extracellular microvesicles [45]. Additionally, galectin-5, which binds galactose-bearing glycoconjugates, is associated with the surface of rat reticulocyte vesicles and modulates vesicle secretion and macrophage-mediated uptake [46]. In our study, LGALS3BP was identified as a target protein bearing core fucosylation in sEV (Fig. 5B). LGALS3BP levels were elevated in cells with high core fucosylation activity and in their corresponding sEV (Fig. 5I), as well as in LC-derived sEV (Fig. 5F). Consistent with our observations, recent studies have reported upregulated LGALS3BP in LC-derived urinary sEV compared to normal controls [47], and in clinical LC tissues and cell lines [48]. We hypothesize that core fucosylation modulates LGALS3BP expression primarily by affecting its degradation through proteasomal and lysosomal pathways [49], thereby influencing its abundance in sEV. Whether core fucosylation directly regulates the sorting of LGALS3BP into sEV warrants further investigation.

Upon internalization by recipient cells, vesicular cargo—including proteins, lipids, DNA, and RNA—can modulate cellular phenotypes and biological functions. We observed that treatment with LC serum-derived sEV containing LGALS3BP activated the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in recipient cells (Fig. 6A). This finding is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that increased LGALS3BP expression facilitates LC progression via PI3K/AKT activation [48]. Stampolidis et al. further showed that LGALS3BP functions as a ligand for integrin membrane receptors, triggering downstream signaling cascades including AKT, JNK, and Raf-ERK pathways in cancer cells [50]. Moreover, LGALS3BP has been identified as a secretory protein present on the surface of pancreatic cancer-derived sEV via surfaceome analysis [51]. Other secretory proteins, such as Gas6 [52] and fibronectin [53], are also abundantly enriched in exosomes, potentially extending their half-lives and facilitating endocrine transmission. These findings suggest that elevated LGALS3BP on LC-derived sEV contributes to oncogenic pathway activation and promotes malignant phenotypes.

In conclusion, our study highlights the important role of core fucosylation in modulating protein expression and sEV characteristics in LC. By identifying core-fucosylated proteins such as LGALS3BP and their regulatory mechanisms, this work provides new insights into the molecular basis of LC progression (Fig. 7). sEV can be detected in the circulation at relatively early stages of disease, and this provides significant opportunities for early diagnosis and clinical therapy [54]. Therefore, highly expressed exosomal LGALS3BP will be a new diagnostic marker for LC.

Currently, several fucosylation inhibitors have been developed, such as B2FF1P, P-D-Rha6F2-1P and P-D-Rha6F3-1P [55]. However, the specificity of these inhibitors for core fucosylation requires further validation. Notably, the inhibition of FUT8 may affect normal cellular physiological processes, which limits the application of FUT8 inhibitors in cancer therapy. Targeted inhibitor development will be a promising avenue for the LC therapy [56]. Meanwhile, a combined therapeutic strategy focusing on FUT8 and the PI3K/AKT pathway may achieve better therapeutic outcomes.

These findings may inform the development of novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic strategies targeting glycosylation pathways or sEV-mediated communication in LC. Future investigations into the functional consequences of core fucosylation on additional sEV-associated proteins will further elucidate their contributions to tumor biology and therapeutic potential.
