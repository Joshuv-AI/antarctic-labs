An innovative course-based undergraduate research experience (CURE) in HPV-associated cervical cancer: integrating molecular oncology research and scientific writing in undergraduate education
Authors: Sadaf Khursheed Baba, Tooba Jawwad, Asma Al Menhali, Sameer Mirza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCourse-based undergraduate research experiences (CUREs) widen access to authentic research by embedding open-ended investigations into undergraduate curricula. Here, we describe a semester-long CURE in cancer biology that integrates molecular biology techniques, data analysis, and structured scientific writing within an upper-level undergraduate course. Students engaged in the study of HPV-associated cervical carcinogenesis by investigating the role of a candidate gene using siRNA-mediated knockdown and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis in cervical cancer cell lines. The primary aims of the course were to increase students’ conceptual understanding of molecular oncology and to develop proficiency in experimental design, primary literature interpretation, data analysis, and scientific communication. Analytical rubrics for laboratory performance, written reports, posters, and oral presentations were used to evaluate student learning, along with a post-course survey measuring perceived learning gains and research self-efficacy. All students successfully completed the experimental workflow and demonstrated increased confidence in molecular techniques, data analysis, and interpretation of scientific data. A distinctive feature of this CURE was the integration of structured scientific writing, which led to student co-authorship of a peer-reviewed review article, reinforcing the linkage between laboratory research and scholarly dissemination. Although implemented with a small cohort, this pilot study provides a scalable and adaptable model for incorporating clinically relevant research and scientific communication into undergraduate science education.

INTRODUCTION

Research is one type of experiential learning, and the Association of American Colleges and Universities considers it to be a high-impact teaching method (1). Providing undergraduate students with opportunities for active research strategies has demonstrated beneficial effects on their learning and attitudes toward science, as well as heightened interest in pursuing graduate education (2–4). These strategies are effective means of boosting students' interest in STEM and their subsequent participation in research. Two strategies are available for acclimating undergraduate students to the research process: undergraduate research courses and course-based undergraduate research experiences (CUREs) (5). The initial paradigm is the one-on-one faculty mentorship, in which a faculty member collaborates with individual students to formulate and implement research projects (6). While CUREs enable instructors to involve larger numbers of students concurrently in organized, research-focused activities throughout the semester, thereby expanding access to research experiences (5).

Cancer biology provides an exemplary framework for CUREs owing to its transdisciplinary characteristics, societal significance, and rapid scientific advancement (7). It also serves as a suitable and interdisciplinary model for students interested in cellular biology, molecular biology, biochemistry, health sciences, and genetics. HPV-associated cervical cancer offers a clinically meaningful context through which students can explore the molecular mechanisms of disease while developing practical laboratory skills. In the United Arab Emirates, cervical cancer is among the most common cancers affecting women, and awareness of its association with HPV remains inadequate despite advanced healthcare infrastructure (8, 9). Introducing regionally relevant health challenges within undergraduate curricula provides a meaningful and context-driven approach that may enhance student engagement and highlight the practical value of scientific research.

At the molecular level, HPV-driven carcinogenesis is mediated by the overexpression of oncoproteins E6 and E7, which disrupt key cellular pathways controlling cell cycle regulation and apoptosis (10). Several molecular targets have been studied for their role in regulating HPV oncoproteins (11), providing opportunities for students to explore emerging questions in molecular oncology. In this course, students investigated Deleted in Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia (DPCD), a protein involved in multiprotein complex assembly and cellular regulatory pathways (12), as a candidate regulator of HPV E6 expression. This biological question served as a platform for teaching experimental design, quantitative data analysis, and scientific reasoning, rather than focusing on biological discovery.

Despite the growing body of literature on CURE in molecular and cell biology, many emphasized general laboratory skills or model systems that are not directly connected to clinically relevant questions (13, 14). In contrast, the present CURE engages students in a biologically and clinically meaningful context by focusing on HPV-associated cervical cancer, a disease of clear global and regional importance. In addition, the course integrates experimental techniques such as gene knockdown and gene expression analysis with structured training in scientific writing and communication. This combination allows students to engage with both the practical and conceptual aspects of research, providing a more comprehensive learning experience than many previously described CURE models.

This manuscript presents a systematic and reproducible CURE that integrates molecular biology techniques with scientific writing and assessment strategies. The activity is implemented as a laboratory-based course, combining hands-on experimentation with data analysis and scientific communication, and is designed to be adaptable and implementable by instructors seeking to incorporate authentic research experiences into undergraduate curricula.

Intended audience

The laboratory activities described in this study are intended for upper-level undergraduate students (junior or senior level) in biochemistry, molecular biology, or related life science disciplines. The course requires prior knowledge of cell biology and laboratory techniques, as students perform mammalian cell culture, siRNA-mediated gene knockdown, and qPCR analysis. Selected components of the activity may be adapted for earlier undergraduate levels with appropriate scaffolding and instructor support.

Learning time

This CURE is implemented over the course of a 12-week academic semester, with 4-h laboratory sessions each week. Additional time outside scheduled sessions is required for preparation of written reports and presentations, data analysis, and literature reviews.

Prerequisite student knowledge

To participate in the activities outlined in this CURE, students must have a basic understanding of cell biology, molecular biology, and basic biochemistry, along with familiarity with standard laboratory practices such as solution preparation, micropipetting, and laboratory safety.

Overview of CURE

The CURE is implemented as a semester-long, laboratory-based course consisting of weekly 4-h sessions over a 12-week period. The course is designed to guide students through a structured progression of activities, beginning with literature analysis and experimental planning, followed by hands-on laboratory work, and concluding with data analysis and scientific communication. Initially, students engage with primary literature to develop research questions and hypotheses related to HPV-associated cervical cancer followed by training in core molecular biology techniques, including mammalian cell culture, siRNA-mediated gene knockdown, RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and quantitative PCR (qPCR). In the later stage of the course, students analyze experimental data using tools such as Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism, enabling them to interpret gene expression results and generate publication-quality figures. The course culminates in students communicating their findings through a written report, scientific poster, and oral presentation. Materials

The following materials are required to implement this CURE. Materials can be scaled based on the number of students or groups. Standard culture media and reagents were used according to manufacturer instructions.

Cell culture and reagents

HeLa (HPV18) and CaSki (HPV16) cell lines; MEM Alpha culture medium; fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10%); penicillin–streptomycin (1%); and Opti-MEM reduced-serum medium.

Transfection reagents

siRNA targeting DPCD; siRNA targeting SRSF2 (positive control); and transfection reagent (e.g., Mirus).

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

TRIzol reagent; chloroform; isopropanol; ethanol (70%); RNase-free water; and iScript cDNA synthesis kit.

qPCR reagents

SYBR Green master mix; forward and reverse primers for DPCD, SRSF2, E6, and 18S rRNA.

Equipment

CO2 incubator (37°C, 5% CO2); biosafety cabinet (Class II); centrifuge; NanoDrop spectrophotometer; PCR/qPCR machine; micropipettes and sterile tips; six-well plates; and microcentrifuge tubes.

Software

Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism.

Personal protective equipment (PPE)

Laboratory coat; gloves; and safety goggles.

Materials should be arranged on laboratory benches prior to each session to facilitate efficient workflow. Shared equipment such as centrifuges, biosafety cabinets, and qPCR instruments can be used by multiple student groups. Depending on class size, students may work individually or in small groups.

Faculty instructions

Before the course begins, instructors should make sure that all of the equipment, cell lines, and reagents are available and working. Cell cultures should be prepared beforehand to guarantee viability and consistency for student use.

The course is organized into weekly modules, providing a clear session-by-session timeline for implementation. At the beginning of the course, instructors should provide training in laboratory safety, sterile technique, and proper handling of cell cultures. Before students carry out these processes on their own, demonstrations of important methods such as cell seeding, siRNA transfection, RNA extraction, and qPCR setup are recommended.

To guarantee experimental accuracy and adherence to safety procedures, close observation is necessary during laboratory sessions, especially during the transfection and RNA extraction stages. Instructors should guide students in troubleshooting common issues such as contamination in cell culture, low transfection efficiency, and inconsistent qPCR results.

Additionally, instructors should help students with data analysis by explaining how to calculate gene expression (Example: 2−ΔΔCt method) and using software tools like Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism.

To support the development of students' scientific communication skills, enough time should be set out for feedback on written reports, posters, and presentations.

Safety considerations

All laboratory activities were conducted in accordance with biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) guidelines due to the use of human-derived cell lines (HeLa and CaSki). Students were required to wear appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE), including laboratory coats, gloves, and safety goggles, at all times. Students received prior training in laboratory safety, including sterile technique, proper handling of human cell lines, and safe use of laboratory equipment.

Work involving cell culture and transfection was performed in a certified Class II biosafety cabinet to maintain sterile conditions and prevent contamination. Chemical hazards associated with reagents such as TRIzol and chloroform were addressed through instructor supervision and adherence to institutional safety protocols. These reagents were handled in a well-ventilated area or fume hood, and students were instructed on safe handling procedures.

All biological and chemical waste was disposed of according to institutional safety guidelines. Biological waste, including cell culture materials, was discarded in designated biohazard containers, while chemical waste was collected and disposed of following approved laboratory procedures. The need for BSL-2 facilities, including access to a biosafety cabinet and dedicated cell culture space, may limit how easily this activity can be implemented in some teaching settings. However, it can still be adapted by simplifying the experimental components or focusing more on data analysis and literature-based activities in environments where such facilities are not available.

All safety practices followed the ASM Guidelines for Biosafety in Teaching Laboratories (15).

Integration of scientific writing and scholarly communication

In addition to the course, students actively participated in the writing of a review paper. They were taught and given the chance to apply an organized approach to scientific writing. Students are trained to use databases like PubMed and Google Scholar, organize content logically, and paraphrase and cite sources appropriately to maintain academic integrity. With the help of their instructor, they were able to make a significant contribution to a collaborative review manuscript that was subsequently published. This publication illustrated the effective conversion of experiential learning into scholarly output and showcased the academic advancement attained through active student involvement in undergraduate research courses.

DISCUSSION

Field testing

This CURE was implemented as a semester-long laboratory-based course with a small cohort of four upper-level undergraduate students. The activity was conducted over a 12-week period with weekly laboratory sessions, allowing students to engage in a complete research workflow from hypothesis development to data analysis and scientific communication. Informal observations and course feedback indicated high levels of student engagement and participation throughout the course.

Evidence of student learning

Evidence of student learning was obtained through multiple assessment methods aligned with the stated learning objectives, including laboratory performance, written reports, and presentations. All students successfully completed the experimental workflow and demonstrated the ability to perform molecular biology techniques and analyze gene expression data.

Student performance on graded assignments indicated strong achievement of learning objectives, particularly in data analysis and scientific communication. While post-course surveys indicated increased student confidence and engagement, these findings were supported by performance-based assessments, including laboratory work and written reports, which demonstrated students’ ability to apply experimental and analytical skills.

Possible modifications

This CURE can be adapted for use in different instructional settings by modifying the scope of laboratory activities or reducing the number of experimental components. For example, instructors with limited laboratory resources may focus on data analysis and literature-based modules, while larger classes may implement group-based experimental work.

The timeline of the activity can also be adjusted to fit shorter course durations by reducing the number of experimental replicates or simplifying molecular techniques. Additionally, alternative gene targets or disease models may be incorporated to align with different course objectives or institutional resources.

Limitations and future directions

This pilot CURE was implemented with a small, selectively recruited cohort, which restricts generalizability of the results. Assessment data are limited to course artifacts (reports, posters, and grades) and a short post-course survey, which constrain the depth of inference about student learning. These constraints nonetheless provide a foundation for future expansion. In subsequent iterations, we plan to adapt the model to larger cohorts, less selective cohorts by modularizing wet-lab activities, incorporating peer-mentoring structures, and adding more systematic assessment tools (pre–post concept inventories, validated self-efficacy scales, and structured reflection prompts). A scaled version could be embedded in upper-level molecular biology or biochemistry laboratory courses, thereby extending authentic cancer-biology research experiences to a broader population of students.

Summary

This course-based undergraduate research experience (CURE) successfully integrated cancer biology concepts at the molecular and cellular level within an upper-level undergraduate curriculum. The course fostered conceptual knowledge, technical competency, and scientific communication skills by involving students in a real-world inquiry about HPV-associated cervical cancer. This educational program provides students with real-world research experiences that encourage participation, boost confidence, and improve readiness for future scientific careers. Despite being a small pilot, this CURE offers a replicable paradigm for integrating scholarly writing and clinically relevant research into undergraduate science.
