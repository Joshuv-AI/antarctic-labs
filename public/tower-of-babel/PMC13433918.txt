Electron-rich dianion vacancies boost diazenide intermediates for efficient chemical looping ammonia synthesis
Authors: Jiaqi Wang, Bo Dai, Zichuang Li, Jiang Li, Xiaojun Lu, Wen-Qian Li, Ruoqian Jiang, Kailong Qian, Sijia Zheng, Shuang Liu, Masaaki Kitano, Hideo Hosono, Yangfan Lu, Jie-Sheng Chen, Tian-Nan Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Activating the nitrogen molecule under mild conditions remains a great challenge in ammonia synthesis. Electron-rich active sites are highly sought after to lower the activation barrier and enable efficient ammonia formation. In this work, we engineer electron-rich dianion vacancies using rare-earth carbide (LaC2) as a platform, where N2 undergoes horizontal adsorption and lattice embedding at the C2 dianion vacancy sites. In situ experimental characterization, combined with computational calculations, reveals that the adsorbed N2 molecules accept electrons from the C2 dianion vacancies, forming highly reactive (N = N)δ− intermediates. These species are crucial for promoting ammonia synthesis via an associative pathway. Utilizing the chemical looping ammonia synthesis process, LaC2 emerges as an optimal candidate for N2 activation and the Ni/LaC2 catalyst demonstrates exceptional performance, achieving 6.9 mmol·g⁻1·h⁻1 at 400 °C and 0.1 MPa, exceeding the activity of previously reported metal catalysts for chemical looping ammonia synthesis process and even surpasses the performance of the state-of-the-art 3d transition metal catalyst for catalytic ammonia synthesis under identical conditions. These findings provide new insights into electron-rich, defect-mediated activation processes and offer a promising strategy for the design of efficient and stable catalysts for ammonia synthesis under mild conditions.

Introduction

Ammonia is not only a key precursor for fertilizers and chemicals but also an important energy carrier, offering high energy density and carbon-free hydrogen storage1–4. Although ammonia synthesis is exothermic and thermodynamically feasible even at ambient temperature, it is strongly limited by kinetics because of the high stability of the N ≡ N triple bond (bond energy of about 945 kJ·mol−1)5–9. Activating N2 generally requires electron donation into its π antibonding orbitals, which lowers the bond order and weakens the N ≡ N bond10–13. Thus, designing electron-rich active sites is critical to promote electron transfer to N214,15. In line with this, both 1st-generation Fe-based catalysts used in the Haber-Bosch process16–18 and 2nd-generation Ru-based catalysts in the KAAP process rely on electronic promoters19–24, such as alkali and alkaline earth metals (e.g., K, Cs, Ba), to increase the electron density at the metal sites and thereby facilitate N2 activation25–31. However, even when N2 dissociation is enabled on these electron-rich metal sites, ammonia formation still proceeds only on transition-metal (TM) surfaces, where the overall performance remains constrained by scaling relations between nitrogen adsorption strength and TM surface properties32–35.

In this context, Ni/LaN catalyst was developed for ammonia synthesis based on a dual-active-site strategy36–38. This approach enables N2 activation at nitrogen vacancy (VN) sites on the LaN support, thereby overcoming the scaling limitation associated with the intrinsically weak nitrogen binding energy of Ni. In this catalytic system, the rate-determining step (RDS) is the hydrogenation of lattice nitrogen. Thus, facilitating the hydrogenation of nitrogen species trapped at vacancy sites is crucial for efficient NH3 formation. Subsequently, it was demonstrated that polyanionic NH2− defects are also effective for N2 activation in TM-loaded alkaline earth imides (TM/AeNH, where TM = Fe, Co, Ni; Ae = Ca, Sr, Ba)39–41. These polyanionic defects can substantially lower the energy barrier for N2 hydrogenation, likely due to a steric effect whereby larger defect cavities enable easier hydrogenation of adsorbed nitrogen species. Therefore, exploring materials with larger and more electron-rich defect sites holds great promise for further enhancing catalytic activity.

Recently, Ni/BaC2 catalyst with C2 defect sites was found effective for a horizontal adsorption configuration of N2, which promotes an alternative hydrogenation pathway of N2 by dissociated H* species from Ni, reducing the reaction barrier for ammonia synthesis42. Notably, BaC2 provides defect sites of just the right size to accommodate horizontally adsorbed N2. However, the electron-donating capability of C2 defects in this alkaline earth carbide is limited, which restricts the degree of electronic promotion for N2 activation over Ni/BaC2. Considering that rare earth metals generally exhibit higher valence states than alkaline earth metals, and that their dicarbides share the same crystal structure, we hypothesize that rare earth dicarbides can deliver higher electron density to the defect sites, generating even more electron-rich and effective defect structures. This strategy is therefore highly attractive for achieving more efficient N2 activation and subsequent ammonia synthesis.

In this work, we engineer large and electron-rich dianion defect sites within the rare earth metal carbide LaC2 and demonstrate that LaC2 is an excellent support for chemical looping ammonia synthesis (CLAS) when loaded with Ni. The electron-rich C2 dianion vacancy sites in LaC2 selectively anchor and activate N2 molecules into highly reactive (N = N)δ⁻ intermediates. After that, dissociated H* from Ni can directly hydrogenate both nitrogen atoms of these (N2)δ⁻ intermediates via an alternative hydrogenation pathway, substantially lowering the energy barrier for N2 hydrogenation to NH3. As a result, Ni/LaC2 exhibits markedly enhanced ammonia synthesis activity with a reduced apparent activation energy compared with other reported metal catalysts for CLAS, and even surpasses the performance of the state-of-the-art 3d transition metal catalyst for catalytic ammonia synthesis under identical conditions. Furthermore, in sharp contrast to conventional CLAS systems, these active surface dianion vacancy sites display significant catalytic stability during prolonged cycling.

Results

Figure 1a presents the crystal and electronic structures of the rare-earth metal carbide LaC2. LaC2 crystallizes in a tetragonal structure (space group I4/mmm)43, in which La cations and C22– units adopt a distorted rock-salt-type arrangement, with the C2 dimers aligned parallel to the c axis. Upon formation of a C2 dianion vacancy, electron localization function (ELF) analysis reveals pronounced electron accumulation at the vacancy site. This finding is corroborated by the density of states (DOS) and partial charge density in the energy range –2.1 eV <E–EF < 0 eV (Supplementary Fig. 1). When an N2 molecule is introduced into the dianion vacancy, it coordinates with neighboring La cations and interacts with these localized anionic electrons. The trapped N2 is elongated to 1.35 Å (Fig. 1a), a bond length comparable to that of a double-bonded (N = N)δ⁻ anion species44. This N2 activation at the C2 vacancy is likely driven by electron transfer via π back-bonding between the vacancy-adsorbed N2 and La 5d orbitals. Such (N = N)δ⁻ anionic intermediates, in which electrons are incorporated in anti-bonding state, are unstable and have only been observed under high pressure or in specific inorganic and organic complexes45,46. DFT calculations confirm that N2 can be efficiently activated at C2 dianion vacancies in LaC2, motivating us to investigate LaC2 as a support for ammonia synthesis catalysts. To isolate the inherent nitrogen activation ability of the electron-rich C2 vacancies in LaC2, we selected metallic Ni as a co-catalyst. Ni is cost-effective and excels at H2 activation, yet interacts only weakly with N2, unlike traditional active metals like Ru or Fe (Supplementary Fig. 2). This allowed us to design a dual-active-site catalyst, Ni/LaC2, where H2 and N2 are activated on Ni and the C2 dianion vacancies, respectively. H2-TPD experiments confirmed Ni’s role as the primary H2 activation site, as bare LaC2 showed minimal H2 desorption (Supplementary Fig. 3). Correspondingly, bare LaC2 produced negligible ammonia (Supplementary Fig. 4), demonstrating that Ni is crucial for initiating the reaction by activating H2 and enabling subsequent ammonia formation. For comparison, we selected BaC2, a related cubic-phase carbide lacking one valence electron, and LaN, a cubic nitride analog of LaC2 containing monatomic anionic nitrogen. The catalytic performances of Ni/LaC2, Ni/BaC2, and Ni/LaN (each with optimized Ni loading) were evaluated for both CLAS and conventional catalytic ammonia synthesis at 400 °C and 0.1 MPa. In the CLAS process, Ni/LaC2 achieves an NH3 formation rate of 6.9 mmol·g⁻1·h⁻1 (Fig. 1b), substantially higher than that of Ni/BaC2 (4.1 mmol·g⁻1·h⁻1) and Ni/LaN (2.5 mmol·g⁻1·h⁻1). These pronounced differences among the reference catalysts indicate that the enhanced CLAS performance stems primarily from the support materials rather than from the Ni sites. The necessity of C2 vacancies containing support materials is also supported by the negligible ammonia production from Ni/C and Ni/Al2O3, and by the relatively low activity of non‑pretreated Ni/LaC2, all three materials lack C2 vacancy active sites (Supplementary Fig. 5). Unlike Ni/LaC2V, the catalytic activity of Ni/LaC2 reflects only its initial reaction activity. Interestingly, the non-pretreated Ni/LaC2 still surpasses the CLAS process. This is because H2 in the catalytic feed reductively generates C2 vacancies in situ, enabling the reaction to proceed. Although Ni/LaC2 exhibits an excellent ammonia production rate during the CLAS process (Fig. 1b). Conversely, its catalytic activity for ammonia production is inferior and similar to that of Ni/BaC2 and Ni/LaN (Fig. 1b).

The apparent activation energy (Ea) for the CLAS process over Ni/LaC2 is as low as 25.6 kJ·mol⁻1 in the temperature range of 320 °C–400 °C, approximately 54% lower than that of the catalytic process (56.2 kJ·mol⁻1) (see Fig. 1c). This reduction in Eₐ is attributed to the presence of vacant C2 sites (Supplementary Fig. 6), which enable horizontal adsorption of N2 resulting from its separate feeding. Such a configuration facilitates dual-sides hydrogenation of the trapped N2 at the vacancies via an alternating hydrogenation pathway, thereby enhancing N ≡ N bond cleavage and NH3 formation, a mechanism that will be discussed in detail later. In contrast, the Ni/LaN catalyst exhibits similar Ea values (~60 kJ·mol⁻1) for both processes (Fig. 1c), indicating that a single nitrogen vacancy has minimal impact on N2 activation and hydrogenation. Importantly, Ni/LaC2 demonstrates higher specific activity than previously reported CLAS catalysts under comparable conditions (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Table 1), and even surpasses the performance of the state-of-the-art 3d transition metal catalyst for catalytic ammonia synthesis under identical conditions (Supplementary Table 2). Furthermore, this high performance is maintained across a broad temperature range (150 ~ 500 °C). Remarkably, at a temperature as low as 150 °C, it still achieves an ammonia formation rate of 0.26 mmol·g⁻1·h⁻1 (Supplementary Fig. 7). Furthermore, the active surface dianion vacancies in Ni/LaC2 can be regenerated during reaction and sustain stable performance for at least 2040 cycles (Fig. 1d). The catalyst maintains its structural integrity under reaction conditions, as shown by the negligible change in its XRD patterns (Supplementary Fig. 8) and the absence of significant Ni nanoparticle agglomeration after extended cycling (Supplementary Fig. 9). Thus, LaC2 is an effective support for chemical looping in ammonia synthesis, providing both low activation energy and exceptionally high catalytic activity.

Motivated by the remarkable ammonia synthesis activity of Ni/LaC2, we further explored the mechanism underlying C2 dianion vacancy formation during the initial H2 treatment in the CLAS process. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to determine the formation energies of surface carbon vacancies on a Ni8/La64C128 slab model (Fig. 2a). The calculated desorption energy (Edes) for a C2 dianion vacancy is 0.13 eV, while that for a C1 vacancy is 1.22 eV, indicating that C2 vacancies are more readily formed on the Ni/LaC2 surface. To experimentally verify the generation of C2 dianion vacancies, H2-temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) was performed on fresh Ni/LaC2. Figure 2b shows the temperature dependence of mass numbers m/z = 15 and 16, corresponding to CH3 and CH4, respectively. It was confirmed that a substantial amount of CH4 could be generated by only a supply of H2 gas. No phase modification was identified on the hydrogen-treated LaC2 based on powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 8). Phase decomposition, such as hydride formation, was also not confirmed, indicating that H2 treatment specifically induced the creation of surface C2 dianion vacancies. Surface-sensitive X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) shows that the C 1 s peak at ~283.5 eV (corresponding to M-C bonds in metal carbides) decreases for Ni/LaC2 after H2 treatment (Fig. 2c), while the La 3d and Ni 2p peaks remain unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 11). The La 3d5/2 binding energy (834.1 eV) of LaC2 is negatively shifted relative to reported La2O3 (834.8 eV)48 (Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating a lower valence state of La in LaC2. The Ni 2p3/2 peak at 852.7 eV confirms the presence of metallic Ni0 (Supplementary Fig. 11)49. Any O 1 s signal from the fresh Ni/LaC2 sample (Supplementary Fig. 12) is attributed to unavoidable surface oxidation from adsorbed oxygen species under experimental conditions. After Ar sputtering, the C 1 s peak returns to its original level, indicating that C2 vacancies mainly form at the surface of LaC2. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra (Fig. 2d) of H2-treated LaC2 display a sharp, symmetrical signal (g-factor = 2.0030), suggesting the presence of unpaired electrons (S = 1/2) associated with surface C2 vacancies50,51. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM, Fig. 2e) along the [111] direction reveals local lattice distortion in LaC2 after H2 treatment, further indicating the formation of significant C2 vacancies. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping results shows that Ni nanoparticles are uniformly dispersed on LaC2 with an average size of ~50 nm (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 13). Collectively, the decreased C 1 s signal in XPS, the EPR signal, and the observed lattice distortion in HRTEM all strongly support that H2 treatment induces the formation of abundant C2 dianion vacancies on the Ni/LaC2 surface.

Both computational and experimental studies confirm that C2 vacancies readily form on Ni/LaC2. This finding motivated us to study the activation and hydrogenation behavior of N2 at these vacancy sites. Accordingly, we conducted N2 temperature-programmed reduction (N2-TPR) test on H2-treated Ni/LaC2 (denoted Ni/LaC2V). The results revealed that N2 (m/z = 28) can adsorb onto Ni/LaC2V surface at room temperature (Fig. 3a), indicating that the C2 vacancies possess a strong affinity for capturing N2. Considering that Ni itself exhibits weak nitrogen adsorption, these C2 dianion vacancies on the LaC2 surface serve as the primary sites for N2 adsorption and activation. To rule out interference from CO (m/z = 28), which shares the same molecular weight as N2, we monitored both m/z = 28 and 14 mass signals (Fig. 3a). To further elucidate the chemical state of adsorbed N2, we performed XPS analyses on both fresh and N2-treated Ni/LaC2V samples. The fresh Ni/LaC2V sample showed no detectable N 1 s signal, confirming its nitrogen-free composition (Fig. 3b). Upon N2 adsorption, a distinct N 1 s peak appeared at 399.2 eV, shifted to lower binding energy relative to N2 on Fe surfaces (~400 eV)52 and similar to potassium-intercalated carbon (399.2 eV)53. Although the catalyst systems differ, we speculate that this lower binding energy corresponds to an activated (N2)δ⁻ species formed by electron transfer from Ni/LaC2V (Fig. 3b). Subsequent H2 treatment resulted in the appearance of two new peaks at 398.2 and 396.8 eV, which are consistent with the formation of N2Hx or NHx intermediates, as expected in the hydrogenation process of (N2)δ⁻54,55.

Complementary diffuse-reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) measurements of N2-treated Ni/LaC2V revealed a distinct peak at 1461 cm⁻1 (Fig. 3c), corresponding to the N–N stretching vibration of the (N = N)δ⁻ species. Isotopic labeling experiments with 15N2 produced a characteristic peak at 1411 cm⁻1 (Fig. 3c), confirming that the observed feature is attributable to the activated (N = N)δ⁻ species44, consistent with the N 1s XPS results (Fig. 3b). Importantly, unlike N2 adsorbed at C2 vacancies in BaC2, which largely remains in the N ≡ N state, N2 at C2 vacancies in LaC2 is significantly more activated, forming a (N = N)δ⁻ state similar to that observed in Ba2N electride56. This highly activated (N = N)δ⁻ species greatly facilitates the hydrogenation of adsorbed N2, thereby enabling highly efficient ammonia synthesis.

To further evaluate the N2 activation and hydrogenation at C2 dianion vacancies in Ni/LaC2 relevant to CLAS, we compared these sites to N vacancies in LaN as a reference to assess the differences in N2 adsorption strength and subsequent hydrogenation behavior. N2-TPD results showed that local electron transfer from C2 vacancies to the N ≡ N bond generates (N = N)δ⁻ species, enhancing nitrogen adsorption and resulting in a higher N2 desorption temperature for Ni/LaC2V compared to Ni/LaNV (Fig. 3d). H2-TPR experiments confirmed ammonia production upon hydrogenation of N2-adsorbed Ni/LaC2V and Ni/LaNV (Fig. 3e). Notably, the NH3 signal from Ni/LaC2 peaked at 160 °C, whereas that from Ni/LaN appeared at 400 °C, indicating that highly activated N2 on Ni/LaC2V undergoes hydrogenation to ammonia more readily than on Ni/LaNV. For N2 hydrogenation to ammonia, Ni/LaC2V exhibited an initial reaction rate of 10.1 mmol·g⁻1·h⁻1 within the first 40 s, with ammonia production remaining constant for the next 170 s (Fig. 3f). This rapid rate suggests that (N = N)δ⁻ species on Ni/LaC2V are quickly and completely hydrogenated to NH3 within 40 s of reaction. In contrast, Ni/LaN displayed a much lower initial rate of 2.4 mmol·g⁻1·h⁻1, with the rate declining gradually over time. The longer reaction duration for Ni/LaN is attributed to the involvement of deep lattice nitrogen of bulk LaN in ammonia production57. Overall, the much higher activity of Ni/LaC2V is ascribed to the highly activated (N = N)δ⁻ species at the electron-rich C2 vacancies, which facilitate more efficient hydrogenation to ammonia compared to nitrogen vacancies on LaN.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were conducted to explore ammonia formation pathways on La64C128 substrates loaded with Ni8 clusters. For the associative mechanism (Fig. 4), N2 initially adsorbs at the C2 dianion vacancy site of LaC2V with an adsorption energy of 2.51 eV (I → II), adopting a horizontal configuration. Upon adsorption, the two nitrogen atoms can accept hydrogen atoms dissociated from Ni (III → IV), forming N2H intermediates through hydrogenation with an energy barrier of 1.51 eV. Next, three hydrogenation pathways, distal, alternative, and semi-alternative, are detailed in Fig. 4. The distal hydrogenation of adsorbed N2 exhibits an energy barrier 0.36 eV higher than the alternative route (IV → V). For the hydrogenation of *HNNH2, we analyzed both alternative and semi-alternative pathways; the latter entails hydrogenation at the NH2 side of *HNNH2. Clearly, the activation energy for the semi-alternative route is approximately 1.61 eV (Fig. 4), 0.11 eV greater than that of the alternative route (ca. 1.50 eV) (VII → VIII), which indicates that formation of *H2NNH2 via the alternative route is kinetically more favorable. Successive hydrogenation steps yield *H2NNH3 (VIII → X), after which two NH3 desorb from the vacancy site (X → XII), regenerating the Ni/LaC2V surface (Fig. 4). Ultimately, two NH3 molecules are released exothermically, with energy releases of 1.80 and 1.24 eV per molecule, respectively. Overall, the RDS in the associative pathway is the hydrogenation of *N2 to form *N2H (III → IV), with a barrier of ca. 1.51 eV (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 3). Notably, direct dissociation of N2 at the C2 vacancy is highly improbable. We determined the N ≡ N bond cleavage barrier at the C2 site (absent hydrogen), finding it to be 2.26 eV (Supplementary Fig. 14). This high barrier renders direct dissociation energetically unfavorable. For comparison, we also examined the dissociative activation of N2 on Ni metal of Ni/LaC2 (Supplementary Fig. 15). The weak adsorption of N2 on Ni led to negligible adsorption energy in its most stable configuration, and the corresponding activation barrier was as high as 2.77 eV, reflecting minimal N2-Ni interaction. These findings support the mechanism by which electron-rich C2 vacancies on LaC2 activate N2, while Ni particles act as hydrogen activation sites. To emphasize the impact of electron-rich C2 vacancies, we extended our investigation to other rare earth metal carbides (ReC2), specifically CeC2 and YC2. Strikingly, clear differences in reaction rates were observed between the CLAS process and catalytic methods (Fig. 5a). These findings further demonstrate that the separate introduction of N2 and H2 notably enhances NH3 formation over Ni/ReC2 catalysts. The catalytic activities for ammonia synthesis via the CLAS process follow the trend: Ni/LaC2 > Ni/CeC2 > Ni/YC2. Because these Ni/ReC2 samples exhibit comparable specific surface areas and Ni particle sizes (Supplementary Fig. 13), we attribute the observed trend in catalytic performance primarily to the nature of the carbide support, rather than the Ni active sites. The Ea for Ni/LaC2 (25.6 kJ·mol⁻1) and Ni/CeC2 (26.4 kJ·mol⁻1) were found to be much higher than for Ni/YC2 (42.3 kJ·mol⁻1), yet still nearly half those of catalytic processes (Fig. 5b). To further probe the role of C2 lattice components, H2-TPR measurements were carried out, which revealed significant CH4 desorption from the Ni/ReC2 samples (Fig. 5c). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of N2-adsorbed Ni/ReC2 shows that the N 1 s binding energy on Ni/LaC2 (399.2 eV) is lower than that on Ni/CeC2 (399.5 eV, Fig. 5d), indicating that more electrons are transferred from Ni/LaC2 to N2, facilitating the formation of active (N = N)δ⁻ species and highlighting the superior electron-donating capability of LaC2. Notably, Ni/YC2 exhibits an even lower N 1 s binding energy at 398.0 eV (Fig. 5d), which correlates with the formation of N3⁻ species58. This suggests that N2 is dissociated at the C2 vacancies on Ni/YC2, with the resulting N3⁻ strongly binding to Y to form a surface Y nitride, making N extraction for ammonia formation challenging and resulting in poor CLAS performance for Ni/YC2.

To assess kinetic differences in N2 activation at the C2 vacancy, N2-TPD measurements were performed. The onset temperature for N2 desorption from Ni/LaC2 is 530 °C (Fig. 5e), significantly higher than that from Ni/CeC2, but slightly lower than that from Ni/YC2. This trend aligns with DFT calculations: the N2 adsorption energies were determined to be −3.06 eV (YC2) > − 2.51 eV (LaC2) > −2.37 eV (CeC2), with corresponding N2 bond lengths of 1.39 Å, 1.35 Å, and 1.34 Å, respectively, indicative of an ionic (N = N)δ⁻ species (Supplementary Fig. 16). These results suggest that the C2 vacancy enhances N2 adsorption by facilitating localized electron transfer from the dianion vacancy to the N ≡ N bond, thus promoting N2 activation. The higher desorption temperature observed for the three carbide samples may also be associated with the formation of dissociated N3⁻ species; for example, the strong Y–N interaction is analogous to that seen in the YN reference sample (Supplementary Fig. 17).

Regarding the hydrogenation step in the CLAS process, H2-TPR measurements (Fig. 5f) reveal that the NH3 desorption temperature for Ni/LaC2 (160 °C) is markedly lower than those for Ni/CeC2 (239 °C) and Ni/YC2 (485 °C). This demonstrates that N2 removal from Ni/LaC2 by H2 proceeds more readily than from Ni/CeC2 and Ni/YC2. No water signal (m/z = 18) was detected in any sample, eliminating interference in the mass spectrometry analysis (Supplementary Fig. 18). These findings are consistent with the N2-TPD results (Fig. 5d), confirming that Ni/LaC2 exhibits suitable N2 activation capability, which facilitates subsequent hydrogenation. For Ni/YC2, the strongly bound N3⁻ species on Y resist extraction, limiting ammonia formation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Ni/LaC2 supports a greater surface concentration of activated (N = N)δ⁻ diazenide species, enabling more efficient hydrogenation and NH3 production.

Discussion

In this study, we have successfully developed a rare-earth metal carbide catalyst, Ni/LaC2, featuring electron-rich C2 defect sites that enable efficient N2 activation and NH3 formation via the CLAS process. N2 cleavage occurs at these electron-rich C2 vacancies, generating key (N = N)δ⁻ intermediates, while H2 dissociation takes place on the supported Ni sites. The presence of highly active (N = N)δ⁻ species significantly lowers the energy barrier for hydrogenation, thereby promoting NH3 synthesis through an alternative association mechanism. The Ni/LaC2 catalyst achieved an impressive ammonia production rate of 6.9 mmol·g⁻1·h⁻1 and maintained full activity over 2040 consecutive reaction cycles, demonstrating outstanding catalytic activity and stability. The electron-rich defect sites in LaC2 play a crucial role in facilitating chemical looping reactions, resulting in low activation energies and enhanced performance in the CLAS process. Overall, this work provides new mechanistic insights into defect-mediated activation and establishes an efficient pathway for ammonia synthesis.

Methods

Synthesis of Ni/ReC2 catalyst

Rare earth metal carbides (ReC2, where Re = La, Ce, or Y) were synthesized via the arc melting method. In the process, 0.5 g of rare earth metals (La, Ce, or Y) were separately mixed with 0.086 g, 0.085 g, and 0.135 g of graphite, respectively. The mixtures were then fully melted at high temperatures using an electric arc to obtain bulk rare earth metal carbides. Subsequently, the resulting rare earth metal carbides were cooled to room temperature. The obtained bulk rare earth metal carbides were ground into a powder using an agate mortar. Nickelocene was used as the precursor for Ni metal and thoroughly ground with the powdered rare earth metal carbides for 20 min. The mixture was then placed in an H2 atmosphere and heated to 250 °C for 2 h to obtain the Ni/ReC2 catalyst. Finally, the catalyst was sealed in a glass vial filled with Ar gas for storage. All the above operations were carried out in an Ar-filled glove box. Subsequently, for the synthesis of comparative catalysts, Ba metal was reacted with pure hydrogen at 1 MPa pressure and 400 °C for 20 h to prepare a BaH2 catalyst. The BaH2 was then mixed with graphite (molar ratio = 1:2) and pressed into cylindrical pellets. These pellets were annealed at 800 °C for 36 h to obtain bulk BaC2. La metal was reacted with pure hydrogen at 1 MPa for 20 h to prepare the LaH3 catalyst. The LaH3 was then maintained at 600 °C under a N2 flow for 8 h to obtain LaN. Nickelocene was separately ground and mixed with BaC2 and LaN. The resulting mixtures were then treated in a hydrogen atmosphere at 250 °C for 2 h to obtain Ni/BaC2 and Ni/LaN, respectively.

Catalytic reaction

The catalytic reaction was performed in a fixed-bed flow reactor loaded with 0.1 g of catalyst. A thermocouple was inserted directly into the catalyst bed inside the fixed-bed reactor to minimize temperature measurement errors. Prior to the chemical looping ammonia synthesis (CLAS) reaction, the catalyst was first pretreated with hydrogen at 400 °C to generate C2 defect sites, yielding the ReC2V catalyst. Subsequently, at the set temperature, nitrogen was introduced at a flow rate of 60 mL·min−1 for 20 s to allow N2 capture by the C2 defect sites on the catalyst surface. The nitrogen supply was then shut off and immediately switched to hydrogen at the same flow rate. Ammonia produced during specified time intervals was collected in a 5 mM sulfuric acid solution, and the NH₄+ concentration in the solution was determined by ion chromatography coupled with a conductivity detector. Sample characterization

To preserve the sample’s surface state, a strict air-free transfer protocol was followed. After the reaction, the catalyst bed was cooled to room temperature under a continuous Ar flow. The quartz reactor tube was then detached, and both ends were immediately sealed with paraffin film. The sealed tube was transferred into an argon-filled glovebox for subsequent characterization.

Temperature-programmed reaction (TPR) and temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) experiments were performed using a BELCAT-II instrument. 0.1 g of the catalyst sample was loaded into a quartz glass tube and positioned within the BELCAT-II instrument for subsequent measurements. For N2-TPR, a gas flow of 5% N2/Ar was used with a heating rate of 10 °C·min−1, and nitrogen consumption was monitored by mass spectrometry. For H2-TPR, a gas flow of 5 % H2/Ar was employed at a heating rate of 10 °C·min−1, and methane formation was monitored by mass spectrometry. TPD experiments were also conducted on 0.1 g of catalyst under an Ar atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C·min−1, with nitrogen concentration changes during desorption monitored by mass spectrometry. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the catalyst under an Ar atmosphere were obtained using a Thermo Scientific Equinox 3500 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation. Before BET surface area measurements, the samples were degassed by heating at 250 °C for 10 h. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured at −196 °C using liquid nitrogen with an automated gas adsorption analyzer (Autosorb-IQ3, Anton Paar) to determine the BET specific surface area. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on a Thermo Scientific Escalab Xi+ instrument under vacuum using Mg Kα radiation. Binding energy was calibrated using the Au 4 f₇/₂ peak from a gold-deposited sample as a reference. Raman spectroscopy was performed on a Horiba LabRAM Soleil instrument equipped with a 50× objective lens, using a 532 nm laser for excitation. Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) measurements were carried out at room temperature using a Bruker EMXmicro-6/1 EPR spectrometer under air-free conditions. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) were conducted using a Thermo Fisher Spectra 300 instrument. The catalyst was uniformly dispersed in hexane and added dropwise onto the carbon-coated copper support membrane for imaging and observation of the catalyst.

DRIFTS of N2 absorption experiments

The diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform (DRIFTS) was conducted using a spectrometer (FT/IR-6XV, JASCO Corporation) equipped with a mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector. The sample was placed in an alumina cup set in a stainless-steel heat chamber equipped with a BaF2 window. The sample chamber was evacuated, and low pressure (5 kPa) of pure 14N2 (99.99995%) or heavy 15N2 (98%) was then introduced to monitor the N2 adsorption at room temperature.

DFT calculations

All calculations were conducted using density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP 6.4.3)59,60. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was employed in these DFT calculations, with core electrons described via the projector augmented wave (PAW) method61,62. All structure relaxations were fully optimized until the energy and forces converged to 1 × 10−5 eV and 0.03 eV Å⁻1, respectively. For the reaction calculations, slab models were constructed based on the (110) facet with La64C128. A vacuum layer thickness of 25 Å was utilized, along with a K-mesh of 2×2×1.
