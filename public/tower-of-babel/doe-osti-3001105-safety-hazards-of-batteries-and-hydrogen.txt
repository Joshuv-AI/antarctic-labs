ABSTRACT
This report addresses the safety concerns and mitigations for battery failures and
their impact on hydrogen storage systems. Through an analysis of failure modes,
this report highlights the risks posed by thermal runaway and chemical emissions
caused by batteries. Although rare, battery thermal failure events may prompt the
opening of the relief valve on the hydrogen tank. Strategies such as battery
management systems, thermal management systems, and multiple thermally
activated pressure relief devices can mitigate these risks. Potential simulations and
experiments to better quantify the unique risks posed by lithium-ion batteries near
tanks are suggested. Improving safety standards will enable integration of batteries
and hydrogen storage systems in various energy storage technologies.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

As new high-capacity energy storage of hydrogen and battery power is becoming
more commonplace, the need for exploring and understanding the risks associated
with battery systems near hydrogen equipment becomes more important. This
report identifies safety concerns and mitigations for battery failures in hydrogen
systems, focusing on the impact on hydrogen storage systems. Lithium-ion
batteries, particularly those with nickel manganese cobalt and lithium iron
phosphate chemistries, dominate the market for electric vehicles and battery
storage banks. Type IV hydrogen tanks, made from composite-wrapped polymer
materials, are widely used due to their high pressure storage capacity and
resistance to hydrogen embrittlement. However, these tanks are vulnerable to
thermal and chemical damage, such as during battery failures.

Fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs) typically have smaller battery capacities
compared to battery electric vehicles (BEVs), reducing the risk and consequence of
thermal runaway. For example, the battery on the Tesla Model 3 BEV is 78.1 kWh,
while the Toyota Mirai FCEV is only 1.24 kWh. However, heavy duty FCEVs, such as
the Kenworth T680 with a 200 kWh battery, may pose a more substantial risk. The
all-electric version of the same vehicle is the Kenworth T680E with a battery
capacity of 396 kWh. Studies show the vehicle body is responsible for the majority
of heat release rates and total heat release in vehicle fires and that batteries only
contribute around 15% of the total heat release from a BEV fire. That said, batteries
contribute significantly to vehicle fire growth since the flame from the battery could
ignite materials and lead to a more rapidly developing fire.

Current safety codes and standards, such as ASME BPVC, ISO 11119-3, SAE J2464,
SAE J2578, SAE J2579, ISO 6469-3, UNECE Regulation No. 100, NFPA 1 Chapter 52,
NFPA 68, and NFPA 855, provide guidelines for the design, testing, and operation of
hydrogen tanks and batteries. However, these standards often address components
independently, without considering the integrated risks of batteries and hydrogen
storage systems.

To mitigate these risks, strategies include using battery management systems to
detect early signs of thermal runaway, implementing thermal management systems

 10

to prevent heat transfer between cells, and installing multiple thermally activated
pressure relief devices on hydrogen tanks. Careful selection of battery chemistries
and innovative battery designs can further reduce the likelihood and impact of
battery failures.

Further research is needed to characterize the hazardous effects of battery fires,
explosions, and chemical emissions on hydrogen tanks through simulations and
experimental studies. This includes understanding the physical limits of composite
tanks, evaluating hydrogen tank protective barrier materials, investigating
interactions between high-pressure hydrogen and battery emissions, and integrating
quantitative risk assessment tools for both batteries and hydrogen storage systems.
Addressing these knowledge gaps and improving safety standards will enhance the
safe integration of batteries and hydrogen storage systems in energy storage
applications.

1. INTRODUCTION
There is a need to understand holistic, system-specific risks that may be associated
with novel and larger-scale applications like hydrogen and battery energy solutions.
Hydrogen fuel not only is of interest for passenger cars but can also provide benefit
to the heavy-duty transportation and energy storage sectors, which have high
associated energy demand. Hydrogen-electric systems are currently topics of
research for passenger vehicles, trucks, buses, locomotives, marine freight vessels,
stationary energy storage, and other energy needs [1], [2], [3], [4], [5].

However, safety risks posed by hydrogen-electric systems, such as the risks of fire
or explosion due to unintentional hydrogen release and ignition, have been
characterized in existing research, including some vehicle-specific considerations
like release of hydrogen from a vehicle in a tunnel [6]. Releases may result from
wear and degradation during normal operations across component lifetimes or due
to rare, acute events such as impacts or crashes [7]. One potential concern that has
been relatively unexplored in research literature and regulations is the impact that
other components onboard the vehicle, such as traction batteries, may have on the
hydrogen tanks.

The focus of this work is to identify the safety concerns and mitigations for the
failure modes of batteries in proximity to hydrogen storage, specifically the potential
impacts and safety implications of battery failures on the hydrogen storage system.
Although there is no known documented event of a battery damaging hydrogen
storage tanks or other hydrogen infrastructure, proactive safety research can help
minimize the risk of such an occurrence. This work will focus on high-voltage and
high-power batteries, such as traction batteries on vehicles. Low voltage and low
power batteries, such as those for starting a vehicle, will not be discussed. It is
important to note that while FCEVs are an obvious example of the co-location of
hydrogen storage and high-power batteries, the same concerns are present for other
applications, including heavy duty vehicles, rail locomotion, and high-capacity
stationary energy storage. Indeed, the increased scale of these heavier-duty
applications make the topics discussed here even more important.

 13

This paper will focus exclusively on the risk to gaseous hydrogen tanks, especially
type IV and type V composite tanks usually used in the transportation industry.
Considerations of hazards associated with a cryogenic liquid hydrogen system are
not considered although scenarios could be imagined in which such a co-location
may occur.

Due to the wide range of battery capacities, it is important to examine whether
battery failures could result in the release of significant amounts of energy that
could cause thermal or chemical damage to a nearby hydrogen tank. This work
focuses on identifying these risks and the conditions when battery failure could
impact a hydrogen system. This work provides a brief overview of hydrogen storage
tank technology and reviews their typical placement in FCEVs as an example of co-
location of hydrogen and battery energy storage. Failure mechanics for both
hydrogen storage tanks and batteries are then discussed. A review of three possible
battery failure modes that could cause substantial damage to a hydrogen system is
discussed, including: thermal runaway (TR), chemical release, and electrical
hazards. Considerations are given for battery selection to minimize risks and future
research in this area is recommended. A review of safety codes and standards is
discussed to better define the current procedures for ensuring the safety of battery
and hydrogen systems. Finally, future research to better quantify risks to a
hydrogen system posed by batteries are proposed to answer knowledge gaps in the
current literature.

2. OVERVIEW OF HYDROGEN TANK TECHNOLOGY

Hydrogen tanks are generally made entirely of metal (Type I), metal with carbon
fiber composite hoop wrap (Type II), a metal liner with a composite overwrap (Type
III), a polymer liner (no metal) with a composite overwrap (Type IV), or entirely of
composite (no liner) (Type V) [8]. The composite is typically carbon fiber in a
thermoset matrix (such as epoxy or vinyl ester) or thermoplastic grade matrix (such
as polyether ether ketone, polyamide, and polyphenylene sulfide) [9]. The liner of a
Type IV tank is typically high-density polyethylene or polyamide material [10]. Type
IV tanks are appealing for some applications compared to Type I-III tanks, since
Type IV tanks are lighter, unsusceptible to hydrogen embrittlement of metals, and
better capable of storing hydrogen at high pressures, thus increasing the volumetric
energy density [11].

2.1. Hydrogen Tank Release
Though not necessarily considered a failure (i.e. an unintended hydrogen release),
hydrogen tanks may discharge their contents when temperature levels are exceeded
and a thermally activated pressure relief device (TPRD) activates to rapidly release
the contents of the tank. This mechanism is used to preserve the integrity of the
tank by avoiding overpressure and possible tank rupture and explosion, which may
result in tank projectiles. When released from a TPRD, the contents of a hydrogen
tank may ignite immediately in a jet fire or, if hydrogen is released into a confined
space or does not ignite immediately, it can accumulate and later ignite, causing an
explosion.

These discharges of hydrogen are initiated when the TPRD senses a temperature of
approximately 110°C or higher, prompting the valve to open [12]. Such temperature
levels should be avoided near hydrogen tanks to reduce the likelihood of hydrogen
discharges, and because the mechanical integrity of the tank is compromised at or
above this temperature. Anomalous incidences such as a vehicle or battery fire, may
cause rapid heating of the tank, which could trigger release of the tank contents via
the TPRD.

3. OVERVIEW OF BATTERY TECHNOLOGIES

3.1. Battery Type Prevalence
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are ubiquitous in consumer power tools and electronics
[13], advanced gear found onboard aircraft [14], and medical devices [15], and have
captured the market as the battery technology of choice for EVs and FCEVs. LIBs
have become the most widely used due to their low maintenance needs, constant
voltage during discharge, high energy density, long shelf life, and comparatively
good low-temperature performance (despite considerable below-freezing
performance issues) [16]. LIBs typically employ nickel manganese cobalt (NMC) or
nickel manganese cobalt aluminum (NMCA) cathodes and graphite anodes, with a
lithium salt electrolyte dissolved in organic solvents such as ethylene carbonate or
dimethyl carbonate [17]. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries are another type of
LIB that are prevalent in the market and may be of greater importance in coming
years [17]. NMC cathodes can be used in EV LIBs to obtain long range capabilities,
given their higher energy density [18]. Studies of consumer behavior and barriers to
EV adoption reveal that driving range is a critical performance aspect [19], and EV
manufacturers consider battery energy density as an important design specification.

NMC batteries are generally more expensive than LFP batteries [26], due to the
price of cobalt, an important raw material for NMCs [17]. However, LFP batteries

manufacturers like Tesla and Ford have been using LFPs in their entry-level EVs,

developing LFP manufacturing in the U.S. [29]. These developments suggest that
LFP may become a more prevalent battery chemistry in the coming years [17].

BESSs represent another rapidly growing application of battery technology [30].
BESS facilities are used to store hundreds of thousands of kWhs to regulate the grid
by providing power during peak demand time and to store power during times of low
energy usage. Energy storage systems like BESS or hydrogen are especially
important for maximizing the capacity of non-dispatchable energy sources by storing
energy that is not immediately used. BESSs are emerging more widely as a solution
for bulk energy storage to regulate the grid and provide localized power for later use,
thereby facilitating more reliable distribution, especially when generation is
intermittent [7].

 18

These battery chemistries are important to consider in terms of the potential
hazards they pose to hydrogen tanks. Co-location or integration of BESS
technologies like NMC and LFP LIBs with hydrogen infrastructure is already an
emerging trend, both in stationary applications like grid management facilities, as
well as in transportation applications [31]. For example, the need for BESS at a
large-scale hydrogen production facility was recently identified at Sinopec’s 260 MW
facility in China [32]. Analysis of the site showed that BESS could increase the
usable energy produced by 20% to 70% by keeping the electrolyzers operational
during lulls in solar production [32]. A 160 kW PEM electrolyzer and 5 MWh BESS
facility have recently been commissioned in South Australia, potentially
demonstrating the use of BESS with hydrogen infrastructure [33]. The
implementation of these stationary, larger scale technologies demonstrates the
need for more insight into the safety implications of co-location and integration of
hydrogen systems and BESS.

Since NMC and LFP chemistries are of primary concern given their current
prevalence in multiple applications, the mechanisms and failure modes for these
types of batteries will be discussed in the following sections with a focus on how
their associated hazards could impact hydrogen storage tanks.

4. REVIEW OF SYSTEM FAILURES
Though this section describes the potential impact of battery failure on nearby
hydrogen storage systems, it is important to note that LIBs have a low failure rate
when utilized according to manufacturer specifications [34]. Unfortunately, batteries
are often pushed beyond manufacturing specifications and accidents can cause
failure rates to exceed this value. The U.S. National Transportation Safety Board
reported that of every 100,000 EVs sold there are 25 EV fires, or about 1 in 4,000
[35]. For comparison, the same study reported 1,530 (1 in 65) fires related to
gasoline-powered vehicles and 3,475 (1 in 29) for hybrid vehicles [36]. While EV
battery fire frequency warrant evaluating their hazards in applications, the analysis
presented here should not be used to imply that LIB failures are normal or occur
routinely. If implemented correctly, the likelihood of failure of LIBs is relatively low,
so the overall risk of a hydrogen tank failure or release due to a nearby LIB is also
correspondingly low.

Carbon fiber composite (Type II-IV) tanks can experience different types of failures
including blistering/cracking, deformation, and potential collapse of the polymer
liner [37]. Hydrogen can permeate through the liner into the interstitial space
between the liner and the tank wall over time because of pressurization and
depressurization cycling during normal use of the tank. It is also possible that a
rapid release of hydrogen from the tank may lead to sudden decompression and
migration of the remaining hydrogen to this interstitial space. The presence of
hydrogen between the liner and the tank wall may compress the liner and cause its
plastic deformation and collapse [9], [37]. Another major failure is overpressure
within the tank, which can lead to fiber and matrix damage in the composite and
possible catastrophic bursting of the tank [37]. Additionally, leakage from the tank
at the location where the plastic liner and metal end boss meet is a concern
because it may be difficult to manufacture a leak tight seal between the material
interface; the vulnerability of this location to leaks increases as the components
wear over time [38]. These types of failures may be accelerated or exacerbated by
thermal or chemical emissions from batteries.

 20

Two modes of failure for batteries are rupture and thermal runaway. Battery rupture
involves failure of the battery casing, often due to pressure-induced expansion [39].
Thermal runaway is an internal, chemically induced self-sustaining heating of the
battery which can result in heat emission, fires, or explosions [39]. Battery
emissions can chemically, physically, and thermally impact nearby hydrogen tanks.
For example, battery rupture and release of hazardous materials could cause
corrosion and weakening of the tank material. TR of the battery could degrade the
hydrogen tank material or cause the space in which the batteries and hydrogen tank
are located to heat up rapidly and could potentially initiate a release of hydrogen
from the tank. Explosive battery failure could lead to puncturing of the tank with
projectiles, or general loss of integrity due to pressure wave generation.

4.1. Thermal Runaway
Thermal runaway (TR) of a battery is a process in which the temperature cannot be
controlled, leading to gas generation, pressure build up, venting (or gas leakage),
ignition of gases, conflagration, propagation, and/or explosion [40]. In short, heat
generation is greater than heat dissipation. Battery failures stemming from TR result
in several forms of hazards which may impact a nearby hydrogen storage system.
First, release of heat from TR can result in very high temperatures from 200°C to
900°C, which could compromise the integrity of a storage tank [41]. An impinging
fire may quickly damage a hydrogen tank before a TPRD activates, potentially
leading to a tank rupture, explosion, and/or rapid tank release. Second, projectiles
pose a mechanical deformation risk to hydrogen tanks. Finally, corrosive chemicals
that are normally internal to the battery could be released, like lithium
hexafluorophosphate, which is used as an electrolyte.

Thermal failures often result in TR where the heat release in one cell triggers a
failure in the adjacent cell. TR results can result in the entire battery burning out
with a release of all the stored chemical energy as heat. These failures can
sometimes be very energetic, causing explosions and the release of large amounts
of gases ranging from 1.5 L/Ah up to 3.2 L/Ah [16] [42]. In this section, the
pathways to each of these failures and the potential hazards they pose to the
hydrogen storage system are discussed.

 21

4.1.1. Expected Temperatures During TR
TR is associated with the most extreme type of battery failure conditions where
large amounts of heat and flammable gases are released rapidly. TR can be caused
by battery overcharging, external mechanical abuse like crushing or piercing, or
manufacturing defects [16]. Most TR events are caused by short circuits of a cell
due to external forces or a design/manufacturing flaw [43]. For an FCEV, this failure
may occur in the form of a vehicle collision that crushes the battery or perhaps by
intruding water from flooding, which could result in a battery short. Examples of this
were seen in Florida during Hurricane Milton in 2024 and Hurricane Ian two years
earlier [44]. Another abuse scenario is a vehicle in a wildfire, which recently caused
many TR events during a fire in Maui, Hawaii in 2023 [45]. Early stages of TR can
occur at temperatures as low as 80°C. Separators in LIBs are made of a polyolefin
material that break down between 130° and 170°C [34], exposing the battery cells
to even more heat. Once a cell has surpassed its safe operating temperature, it
enters a heat-temperature-reaction loop where heat initiates chemical reactions
that release additional heat which leads to chain reactions and increasing heat
release [43]. Table 4-1 lists the failure temperature of different lithium ion battery
components as TR progresses.

Various studies conducted by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) found TR
onset at temperatures around 116 to 142°C with maximum temperatures of 654 to
903°C [16]. Different battery chemistries performed differently, with one study
showing lithium cobalt oxide (LiCoO2, or LCO) cells reaching a maximum
temperature of 708°C while lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide (LiNiMnCoO2)
cells reached a maximum temperature of 665.6°C [16], [46]. Studies at Sandia
National Laboratories observed temperatures as high as 1000°C during single cell
experiments [47]. A hydrogen storage tank adjacent to a battery undergoing TR may
likely experience elevated temperatures far beyond their failure point. At such high
temperatures, Type IV hydrogen composite tanks would be vulnerable to thermal
failure. At 100°C, the resin in the composite tanks begins to weaken, which could

 23

lead to a large hydrogen release as the temperature continues to climb, [48]
assuming the TPRD fails to activate at a temperature of around 110°C or higher.

4.1.2. TPRD Opening as a Result of TR
The most likely outcome for a hydrogen tank during a battery TR event is the
opening of the TPRD. This depends on two conditions: sufficient heat evolution from
the battery and the TPRD detecting high temperature conditions before tank
rupture.

As stated in the Introduction, TPRDs are designed to open and release the contents
of the hydrogen tank at around 110°C. If the TPRD fails to actuate, tank rupture
and possible production of high-speed tank projectiles as well as a sudden violent
release of hydrogen could ensue. TPRDs may fail to open if they are not directly in
the vicinity of a localized heat source such as a failing battery. A localized impinging
fire from the battery could lead to such a tank failure.

For this reason, hydrogen storage tank TPRDs should be positioned closer to the
battery or other potentially flammable components if possible. Multiple TPRDs or
sensors could be installed, such as one on either end of the tank, to ensure
adequate detection of the hydrogen tank’s temperature. However, multiple TPRDs
introduce other risk given they open avenues for additional leakage points and
damage. Other solutions could include protective barriers between the battery and
the hydrogen storage system or intumescent paints.

4.1.3. Emissions from TR
In addition to these thermal hazards, TR poses a projectile hazard to hydrogen tanks
due to the build-up of vented gases that could lead to an explosion [16]. A review of
the vented gas during LIB fires shows that mostly hydrogen, carbon monoxide,
hydrocarbons, and carbon dioxide are emitted [49].

Flammable gas emissions of hydrogen and carbon monoxide increase, while carbon
dioxide emissions decrease, when the state of charge (SOC) of the battery (the
charge remaining in the battery) is higher. Thus, batteries at a higher state of charge
will more likely vent gases within a flammable concentration, hence increasing the
possibility of jet flame formation [50]. At around 40 to 50% SOC there is ~30% by

 24

volume flammable gas produced, however, that number increases to around 40% by
volume at 80% SOC [50]. The lower flammability limit (LFL) for vented battery gases
of LIBs can range from 6.1 to 11.8 vol% in air depending on battery chemistry, based
on two LFL calculation models [49]. An immediate ignition is potentially more likely
to occur during TR if the cells reach temperatures high enough to ignite the
flammable gas mixture. However, immediate ignition outcomes are not always the
case, especially when gas releases occur within compartments. Such an explosion
could damage hydrogen tanks stored in the same compartment or nearby with
maximum overpressure anywhere between 7.38 to 8.44 bar-g if peak laminar flame
speed is 0.37 to 1.07 m/s, depending on battery chemistry [49].

In addition, gases can also be emitted from a battery in a narrow and directed jet
flame that occurs when one vent point develops early in battery failure and the
gases ignite. This could pose a direct, isolated heating risk to a hydrogen tank. Fu et
al. showed that LIB axial flame temperatures can reach 590°C, which can trigger
the opening of the TPRD on the hydrogen tank at around 110°C and cause possible
catastrophic damage to the tank [51]. One study of single cell LIB with a typical
capacity of only 11 Wh observed jet flames up to 21.7 cm in length with ejected
battery fragment velocities of 30 m/s [52]. A small handheld LIB tablet fire was
observed to have flame heights of 40 cm [53]. Much larger capacity batteries could
exhibit potentially larger jet flames or explosions in the case of TR.

4.1.4. Overall Thermal Hazard Risk and Comparison to Battery Electric
 Vehicles
While physically separating the hydrogen storage tanks from the battery system as
much as possible may help minimize hazards, other parts of EVs besides the battery
system may also pose a risk to the hydrogen storage tanks. One study measured the
peak heat release rate (pHRR) and total heat release (THR) for three BEVs, an
internal combustion engine vehicle (ICEV), and an FCEV without hydrogen fuel
present [54]. The ICEV had the highest pHRR but only by a few tenths of a MW, with
the FCEV pHRR being the lowest. Interestingly, the FCEV with the smallest LIB of
around 1.5 kWh produced the greatest THR. For the vehicles used in this test, the
mass of the FCEV was the largest at 1,820 kg compared to the others which ranged

 25

from 1,206 kg to 1,685 kg [69]. The larger vehicle size provided more material to
burn and hence resulted in a greater THR, even without hydrogen fuel present.
These results emphasize that the battery only contributes a small portion to an
overall vehicle fire. The BEV body and LIB, when involved in separate fire events,
yielded a pHRR of 7.81 MW and 1.54 MW respectively, and a THR of 7.53 GJ and 1.3
GJ respectively. These results show that, of all the heat released, only around 15% is
from the battery. In fact, the study reported that the combustion of the passenger
cabin, rather than the TR of the LIB, was the dominant contribution to the HRR.
Given this observation, the ICEV and FCEV, without their petroleum or hydrogen fuel
stores, appear to burn with similar intensity as BEVs. Another study accumulated
vehicle fire research and found, similarly, that there is no substantial difference in
heat release between BEVs or ICEVs [70].

However, due to the unique hazards of EV battery fires, namely high temperatures,
jet fires, and explosions, the fire generally spreads much more quickly in larger EVs,
resulting in larger heat release rates, fast temperature increases, and elevated
temperature duration [34]. In any vehicle with a battery, the risk to hydrogen tanks
increases because the rate of fire spread can increase. This rapid propagation gives
first responders less time to control the fire before the hydrogen tanks are affected.
Kang et al. experimentally compared BEV fires to other fire types and showed that a
HRR of 1 MW due to a LIB fire was achieved in 343 seconds without a jet flame
present, but only 224 seconds with a jet flame present [54]. This two-minute
difference could have implications during emergency response, when every second
matters.

An FCEV may have lower associated risk of hazardous battery failure compared to a
BEV because of its much smaller battery capacity. For example, the Tesla Model 3
BEV has a high voltage battery capacity of 78.1 kWh [55] while the capacity of the
Toyota Mirai FCEV is only 1.24 kWh [56]. This much lower battery capacity for
FCEVs lowers the potential consequence of a TR because the risk of fire, chemical,
or energetic emissions is diminished.

4.1.5. Thermal Runaway Mitigations
Current battery technology offers several ways to reduce the impact of a TR event.
For example, battery management systems (BMS) collect data from the battery to
aid in charging, discharging, temperature management, and more [74]. Detection of
a voltage drop by the BMS could indicate a problem that could lead to TR. Having a
robust BMS is often the best protection for a battery system.

 28

Another tool which can prevent or reduce the consequence of TR is a thermal
management system. These systems can feature either circulating liquid or large
heat barriers between battery cells to reduce or prevent heat transfer from a
damaged cell to an adjacent cell, potentially halting TR. Generally, a highly heat
conductive material is used as a barrier, to help transfer heat from the batteries to
the superstructure of an EV or BESS. However, this thermal management system
method is typically avoided due to its significant battery weight contribution.

If all passive and active forms of mitigation fail, then the intervention of emergency
personnel while adhering to appropriate standoff distances may be necessary. LIB
battery fires can be particularly difficult to extinguish for first responders because
they involve the breakdown of lithium metal oxides in the cathode to generate
oxygen which can make LIB fires self-sustaining [34]. A delay in extinguishing such
a fire can enable its spread and possibly impact a hydrogen storage system near the
battery. Current best practices to extinguish battery fires involve using copious
amount of water. Other suppressants such as F-500, AVD, Stat-X, and Firexo may
extinguish battery fires more quickly [17]. Even so, water is often the best
suppressant for LIB fires due to its high heat capacity, which can reduce battery
temperatures and stop TR [34]. Reignition risks exist even after a battery fire is
extinguished due to the further decomposition of the cells as stranded energy in the
battery continues to be released. For this reason, in the event a hydrogen tank is
near a battery fire, battery reignition should be closely monitored.

To complicate things further, while spraying water on surrounding equipment such
as batteries may prevent fire spread, first responders are taught not to spray water
on a hydrogen storage systems as doing so could cause uneven tank cooling or plug
hydrogen release from TPRDs and their vent stacks [75]. Uneven tank cooling, such
as from spraying one portion of a hydrogen tank with cold water while another
portion is at or above its maximum allowable temperature, could cause a TPRD to
fail to activate and lead to tank rupture or damage. If water is sprayed on the
TPRD’s vent stack, this may plug hydrogen release passageways, causing pressure
to increase in the tank. This is a hazard for gaseous hydrogen, but especially for

 29

liquid hydrogen tanks. Water applied to a gaseous hydrogen tank can block the vent
stack, causing potential tank overpressure, rupture, and release. For a liquid
hydrogen tank, such blockage may lead to tank rupture and a boiling liquid
expanding vapor explosion (BLEVE). Additionally, water spray can potentially trap a
flammable mixture of hydrogen and air within the vent stack, potentially leading to
an explosion in the vent system.

4.2. Chemical Release
Release of chemicals normally internal to a battery can occur for several reasons.
The battery can leak, for example, due to loss of battery structure integrity from
acute impacts, manufacturing defects, or general wear and degradation over time.
Overcharging a battery can also lead to generation and off-gassing of different
chemicals [76]. In addition, battery fires such as the ones discussed in the previous
section may lead to electrolyte combustion and battery chemical leakage or off-
gassing. Between 20 to 200 mg/Wh of battery capacity hydrogen fluoride (HF) gas
can be generated during a LIB fire [77]. Phosphorus oxyfluoride (POF3) can also be
produced in a LIB fire [34]. These gases and other potential emissions from
batteries are toxic to humans and may pose a hazard to the hydrogen tanks onboard
the vehicle. If a vehicle experiences a battery venting event (even minor), sensing
and inspection of nearby components for chemical residue or damage is helpful
toward assessing damage.

Mitigation requirements related to battery chemical emissions on hydrogen tanks is
currently not discussed substantially in codes and standards. For example, in terms
of standards requirements, GTR 13 testing describes hydrogen tank chemical
exposure as “chemicals found in the on-road environment,” including fluids like
battery acid [78]. As more information, testing, and validation of the hazards of
battery fires in proximity of hydrogen tanks becomes available, such regulations may
provide greater clarity on the types of testing which should be conducted. Though
battery fire-induced chemical reactions that are damaging to hydrogen tanks are not
well explored in literature, this may change as battery research progresses and
different battery chemistries are characterized.

 30

4.2.1. Corrosive risks on Hydrogen Tanks
Battery chemical releases may pose multiple hazards on hydrogen tanks. There is a
possibility that reactions between the battery chemicals and the hydrogen tank
could cause corrosion and weakening of the tank material, though as explained
below, the probability of this is low unless a severe exposure event occurs.

A specific benefit of Type IV tanks is their high corrosion-resistance because, unlike
Type I, II, and III tanks, they contain no metal besides the boss at the ends [9], [79].
The polymer lining and fiber-reinforced composite wrapper are unlikely to corrode in
the same way that a tank made partially or entirely from a metal such as aluminum
might corrode from contact with lithium salts from a lithium-ion battery or other
battery chemicals [80]. It is possible for a liner with compromised structural integrity
to be more susceptible to chemical corrosion [79].

The liner can plastically deform, buckle, and collapse. While some liner deformation
may occur with general use over time, a more severe event such as the rapid
decompression from a TPRD release or puncture is more likely to cause deformation
of the liner within the period of its usable lifetime. However, a rapid decompression
event is unlikely – tanks are designed and manufactured to withstand routine
pressure cycling as hydrogen is used and replenished in the tank, and this normal
cycling does not create the liner deformation associated with rapid decompression.

Similarly, the tank and its ancillary components such as the TPRD are designed and
sized so that tank integrity is not compromised when the TPRD is activated [81].
Thus, a rapid decompression event affecting the liner integrity would most likely
only occur due to unplanned events such as large leaks or a rupture that could lead
to swift evacuation of hydrogen from the tank. Standards such as FMVSS No. 307
and No. 308 require hydrogen tank inspections following motor vehicle accidents to
determine whether the tank is still fit for service, and it is unlikely that a tank with a
large leak or a severely deformed liner would be put back into service after this type
of event. Therefore, the possibility of a plastically deformed tank liner being in
service is low, and the possibility of corrosion from a potential battery chemical
release is also low. Additionally, the timescale for battery chemicals to inflict
corrosion-based damage on the hydrogen tank is longer than the timescale for other

 31

failures (i.e., exposure of the hydrogen tank to battery chemicals would need to be
long-term for corrosion or other degradation of the tank to occur). While leaking or
off-gassing of battery chemicals may be caused by a high-consequence acute event
like a battery failure or fire, these emissions would likely be detected using onboard
sensors and addressed before severe corrosion occurs. It is also possible that low-
severity, long-term battery failure may cause leaking or off-gassing and may remain
undetected over a period long enough for some hydrogen tank corrosion to occur.

There is also a possibility that chemicals released from a battery are flammable. For
example, lead-acid batteries can release flammable hydrogen sulfide gas if they are
overcharged, and this gas is particularly likely to accumulate in enclosed spaces
such as the equipment compartments on a vehicle due to its density [17]. In this
case, if the hydrogen sulfide ignites, the fire could impact the hydrogen tanks in
ways discussed in Section 4.1.4.

The corrosion and flammability hazards posed by battery chemicals to hydrogen
storage tanks are mitigated through system compartmentalization required by SAE
J2578 and J2579. Since the battery system and the hydrogen storage system are
required to be isolated in a way that prevents cascading failures from occurring – via
distance from each other or physical separation barriers – the likelihood of leaking
battery chemicals reaching the hydrogen tank is low. The risk posed by a fire from
ignited battery chemicals is generally higher than the risk of corrosion or other
chemical-related hazards because heat, rather than chemicals, may be transferred
more easily between compartments within the vehicle.

4.2.2. False Detection Risk
As already mentioned, hydrogen can be released by batteries undergoing TR. This
off-gassing occurs normally in the operation of lead-acid batteries in small
quantities. Hydrogen is not usually released during normal LIB operation, instead
only when they are failing, with the greatest release of hydrogen occurring during
TR. Though a sensor detecting increased levels of hydrogen is almost always
beneficial, this could cause some conflation between the source of the failure. For
example, sensors detecting a small leak from the hydrogen storage tanks may be
detecting emissions from a battery pack in proximity. In this case, an alarm would

 32

falsely indicate a leak in the hydrogen storage tanks or associated piping. This may
cause a delay in identifying the root cause of a leak which could allow battery
failures to develop further. For this reason, hydrogen gas sensors for both the
battery system and the hydrogen storage tank should be physically isolated to avoid
leak misdiagnosis.

4.2.3. Chemical Release During Emergency Response
Emergency response may have an impact on the release of chemicals in batteries. In
the case of vehicle fires, extinguishment using water is a standard emergency
response action for hydrogen FCEVs in addition to traditional internal combustion
engine vehicles [82]. Existing research and published experiments on the impacts of
extinguishing water on battery chemical release suggests that water that was used
for battery extinguishing may contain metals like lithium, manganese, nickel, and
cobalt, in addition to PFAS, fluoride, and microtoxins [83]. The application of water
to BEV fires is also associated with various gaseous chemical emissions due to the
stifling or incompleteness of combustion. Incomplete combustion may lead to
increased hydrogen and carbon content, decreased soot, decreased carbon dioxide,
and decreased HF in combustion gases [84]. These dissolved chemicals and
gaseous emissions on their own may have corrosive or integrity impacts on the
hydrogen tanks as discussed above, but, again, the likelihood is low that either the
hydrogen tank would be exposed to fire-related chemical releases for a timeframe
conducive to corrosion or that the tank would be approved for continued use after
the occurrence of a vehicle fire. Literature on chemical releases from emergency
response extinguishment activities is currently mostly focused on environmental
impacts (e.g., air and water quality) rather than impacts on parts of the vehicle like
the hydrogen tanks.

4.3. Electrical Hazards
It is also possible for the battery to pose an electrical hazard to the vehicle overall
and to the hydrogen tanks. Automotive and building standards require high-voltage
isolation (e.g., insulation monitoring and automatic shutdown of the battery upon
fault), which makes this scenario unlikely. However, should those protections fail or
be absent, several hazards could result. The battery may discharge a high voltage

 33

through the tank, which could potentially cause heating of the tank or electrostatic
charging of the hydrogen that could ignite when air is encountered, for example, if
leakage occurs during refueling [85]. Heating of the tank from a single high voltage
discharge may not lead to immediate or catastrophic consequences, but over time it
could lead to deformations or loss of integrity in the tank structure that could make
it more susceptible to leakage. In addition, any high voltage arc flash would ignite
any flammable gases that could be around the battery. Gases and arcing to ground
are likely in the event of a major crash that damages the battery.

Compartmentalization of the hydrogen and battery systems, as well as management
of voltage settings through the BMS, can help prevent high voltage discharge from
the battery reaching areas of the vehicle that contain hydrogen. If a high voltage
discharge does reach the hydrogen tank or hydrogen supply lines, some of the
properties of the composite tanks, like their low electrical and thermal conductivity,
can protect the system from these electrical hazards. Some hydrogen supply lines
may be made of more electrically conductive material, such as stainless steel, and it
is possible that using a polymer or insulating material may further protect the areas
of the vehicle that contain hydrogen. The effects of electrical hazards like
electrostatic or high voltage discharges on hydrogen tanks or even polymers in
general are still a developing area of research.

5. BATTERY SELECTION CONSIDERATIONS
There are benefits and drawbacks for each type of battery, and considerations such
as thermochemical behavior may affect the battery’s performance in an integrated
battery-hydrogen system like an FCEV, BESS or other device. The application,
hydrogen tank materials, battery chemistry, and safety features are aspects that
may impact an integrated system manufacturer’s selected battery type.

5.1. Battery Trade-Offs
Material selection can be used to reduce the likelihood of TR. An FAA study found
that LFP batteries undergo 80% less temperature rise during external heating
compared to other LIBs tested, and LFPs did not propagate into thermal runaway
during testing [16]. The temperature rise for LFP cells (122°C) was much less than
LCO (372°C) or LMN (552°C) cells [16]. This observation suggests that LFP cells
are more thermally stable compared to other chemistries. Also, not all types of cells
resulted in TR propagation. For example, some cells failed to propagate TR due to
ejection of cell material, which led to removal of heat from the system [16]. Figure

There are numerous battery shapes and sizes which impact their susceptibility to

TR due to the proximity of each cell [16]. An FAA study found that all but one pouch
design experienced runaway that propagated across all five cells studied, which is
more than cylindrical type designs [16]. This indicates that certain battery designs

to nearby hydrogen tanks.

Research into new solid-state batteries is very active given their greatly improved
energy density and the potential advantages of the elimination of highly flammable
liquid electrolytes [88]. Two Chinese companies have produced semi-solid-state
batteries that aim to bridge the gap toward fully solid-state batteries [17], however

Sodium ion batteries (SIBs) have also been promoted as an LIB replacement.
Replacing lithium with sodium increases battery affordability since sodium ion
batteries are only 2 to 3% the cost of LIBs [89]. However, current SIBs feature only
have half the energy density as LIBs and require very long charging times [90]. From
a safety perspective, some studies have shown that SIBs may have advantages over
LIBs due to the higher thermal stability of sodium compared to lithium, and lower
recorded peak TR temperatures of SIBs compared to LIBs. The likelihood of TR may
also be lower because SIBs are less likely to short circuit than LIBs [91]. SIBs do,
however, utilize a flammable organic electrolyte like LIBs [91], which could pose a
similar risk of TR and damage to nearby hydrogen tanks in an integrated system.
Thus, it is unlikely that LIBs will be replaced in the near term. However, continued
research could provide the breakthroughs needed for this type of chemistry to
become practical.

7. REGULATIONS, CODES, AND STANDARDS FOR BATTERIES AND
 HYDROGEN TANKS

7.1. Codes and Standards Related to Tank Design and Testing
There are multiple existing codes and standards for hydrogen tank design and
testing that address potential tank failure modes. The American Society of
Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC) Section VIII
and Section X provide testing, manufacturing, construction, and operation
requirements for high-pressure vessels and fiber-reinforced plastic vessels,
respectively [94]. Standards published by the International Standards Organization
(ISO) and the Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE), such as ISO 11119-3 and SAE
J2601, require testing for hydrogen tanks on fuel cell vehicles, including pressure
and temperature cycling, burst, ambient/environmental/thermal cycling, gunfire,
bonfire, drop, and flame tests [95], [96]. These evaluations ensure that the tanks
can withstand some level of compression-decompression cycling without leaking,
and that there is a sufficient safety factor between the expected service pressure
and the burst pressure.

Annex D of ISO 11119-3 (Gas cylinders – Design, construction and testing of
refillable composite gas cylinders and tubes Part 3: Fully wrapped fiber reinforced
composite gas cylinders and tubes up to 450 l with non-load-sharing metallic or
non-metallic liners or without liners) covers manufacturing requirements for
composite tanks [97]. This document describes a fire test requirement which
requires that tanks be subjected to direct flame impingement temperatures of
590°C or greater until the tank ruptures or the TPRD opens, however, there are no
specific requirements for how much time the tank needs to remain functional or at
what temperature it is allowed to fail. Fueling Protocols for Light Duty Gaseous
Hydrogen Surface Vehicles (SAE J2601) has a requirement that hydrogen tank
temperatures must not exceed 85°C during refueling to avoid overheating of the
tank [98]. While these standards focus on the tank itself, independent of
consideration of the system in which the tank is being used, they require safeguards
against potential adverse events such as fire and accidents. Other applications,

 41

including heavy duty transportation and stationary energy storage, would likely use
similar types of requirements for larger-scale tanks.

In addition to manufacturing and performance requirements, there are also
standards for periodic inspection and maintenance. For example, the Compressed
Gas Association (CGA) standard, Installation Standard for Bulk Hydrogen Supply
Systems (CGA H-5), requires regular recertification of tanks and annual
maintenance by qualified entities and representatives [99]. Similarly, the ISO
standard, Gas cylinders – Composite cylinders and tubes – Periodic inspection and
testing (ISO 11623), describes requirements for periodic inspection and testing of
composite compressed gas cylinders with volumes between 0.5 and 3,000 L,
including Type IV tanks [100].

In terms of integration of hydrogen tanks in a larger system, the National Fire
Protection Association (NFPA) code, Standard on Explosion Protection by
Deflagration Venting (NFPA 68), provides venting requirements for hydrogen
systems in enclosures to reduce the consequence explosion; these venting
requirements rely on laminar flame speed and maximum overpressures to determine
venting requirements [101].

7.2. Battery Design and Testing Codes and Standards
There are also battery design requirements and testing regulations for vehicles
including SAE J2464 (Electric and Hybrid Electric Vehicle Rechargeable Energy
Storage System [RESS] Safety and Abuse Testing) [102]. SAE J2464 contains
required tests for batteries, including monitoring of hazardous substances and
mechanical, thermal, and electrical abuse testing. The severity of RESS responses,
or consequences, to the different abuse tests is used to inform the hazard
assessment and risk mitigation approach. Battery failures of interest include short-
circuiting of the battery cell, lithium plating at the anode through overcharging, and
copper plating on the cathode through over-discharging. Abuse tests described by
standards such as SAE J2464 include crushing, piercing, dropping, vibration, rolling,
mechanical shock, fuel fire exposure, rapid charge and discharge, high-temperature
exposure, and submersion in water, and these tests are meant to induce failures

 42

and observe the response of the battery. Responses may range from no-
consequence outcomes where the battery is not affected at all, possibly due to
built-in passive protection measures, to more severe consequences like release of
electrolyte or vapor from the battery cell, battery rupture, ignition into a sustained
flame, and energetic failure leading to projectile movement or pressure wave
generation.

ISO 6469-3 (Electrically propelled road vehicles – Safety specifications) is another
standard containing electrical safety requirements for EVs [103]. These regulations
are meant to protect people in the vicinity of the vehicle from electric shock and
thermal incidents, including in crash scenarios.

There are also similar international standards with performance requirements for
batteries. The International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) standard, IEC 62133
(Safety Testing for Lithium-Ion Batteries), regulates electrical, mechanical, and
chemical safety and performance for lithium-ion batteries, to identify safety hazards,
reduce the risk of fire or explosion incidents, and improve product quality [104].
GB/T 31485 (Safety Requirements and Test Methods for Traction Battery of Electric
Vehicle) is a Chinese standard with requirements for testing and inspection,
specifically for the battery in an electric vehicle [105].

7.3. Codes and Standards for Integrated Hydrogen and/or Battery Systems
 and Vehicles
The conditions for many of the battery failures leading to hazardous material venting
and TR result in high temperature and gaseous emissions that are beyond the scope
of the hydrogen tank tests outlined above. Therefore, it is helpful to also use codes
and standards specific to vehicles or energy storage systems that include both
hydrogen and battery electric systems when designing their safe integration and
usage.

Notably, none of these standards explicitly tests or evaluates the scenario of a
battery failure next to a hydrogen tank. Each standard mitigates risks within its
domain (tank-only or battery-only), but integrated testing or design guidelines for
co-located systems are lacking.

 43

SAE J2578 (Recommended Practice for General Fuel Cell Vehicle Safety) [96] and
SAE J2579 (Standard for Fuel Systems in Fuel Cell and Other Hydrogen Vehicles)
[95] are both standards with guidelines for safe integrated design of fuel cell
vehicles that may contain battery equipment. SAE J2578 specifies that hazards
should be isolated and separated from each other, for example, using different
compartments. It also recommends that ignition sources, which are defined in the
document as thermal or electric energy sources that may have the capability to
ignite flammable gas mixtures (such as sources of thermal, electrical, or static
discharge), be isolated from hazardous fluid systems. Since the battery system has
the potential to spark and be an ignition source, it should be separated from the
onboard hydrogen storage. The standard explicitly states that this requirement is
meant to prevent cascading failures, which would include battery failure that
induces a hydrogen tank failure. Further, the document stipulates that a local area
regularly containing flammables should not have any equipment installed that is or
could be an ignition source, which reinforces that the battery system and hydrogen
storage system should be physically isolated. In addition, fail-safe design of both
electrical and hazardous fluid systems is required in order to operationally isolate
the electrical and fuel sources from each other.

The scope of SAE J2579 pertains more to the overall design, construction, operation,
and maintenance of hydrogen fuel storage on road vehicles than to the specific
integration of the systems within a fuel cell vehicle. Requirements for the hydrogen
storage system include protection against hazards including overpressure, over-
temperature, and chemical exposure conditions. The overpressure and thermal
protection are intended to prevent any part of the system from bursting. The
chemical protection is meant to ensure that the hydrogen storage system can
withstand exposure to typical fluids that it might encounter in the operational
environment, including acids, bases, and salts, which are all materials that may be
present in a battery system. To ensure that overpressure, over-temperature, and
chemical exposure protections are sufficient, the hydrogen storage tank could be
evaluated in various service conditions and performance verification tests. For
example, fire tests at the tank’s normal working pressure are used to verify that the

 44

hydrogen tank does not burst or lose structural integrity due to thermal degradation.
Generally, the hydrogen storage system is evaluated for rupture in the case of
“extreme service-terminating events.” While not explicitly stated, service terminating
events could potentially refer to battery failures such as TR or hazardous chemical
release.

Addendum 13 of the global technical regulation (GTR) on hydrogen and fuel cell
vehicles, also referred to as GTR 13, also provides some overall guidance for on-
road hydrogen vehicles [78]. Like SAE J2579, GTR 13 requires specific performance
testing, including exposure of the hydrogen storage tanks to fluids that would be
used on the vehicle. The performance tests, including the exposure of the hydrogen
tank to representative vehicle fluids and mechanical tests verifying structural
integrity, are meant to ensure that the tank can still contain pressurized hydrogen
even when subjected to extreme wear. A U.S. DOT regulation, Federal Motor Vehicle
Safety Standard (FMVSS) No. 307 (Fuel system integrity of hydrogen vehicles) also
describes performance standards and requirements for hydrogen vehicles, such as
leak tightness of hydrogen fueling receptacles, overpressure protection, hydrogen
gas discharge direction, and automatic shutoff valves [106]. The associated
standard FMVSS No. 308 (Compressed Hydrogen Storage System [CHSS] Integrity)
is a vehicle-level standard that requires an on-road performance test to ensure that
the hydrogen storage system does not leak or burst in driving conditions [107].
FMVSS No. 308 also specifies that the CHSS must be tested for its ability to contain
or safely release hydrogen in the case of exposure to a fire somewhere on the
vehicle. This standard has requirements for placement and integrity of closure
devices, as well as labeling.

The United Nations Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE) standard, UNECE
Regulation No. 100 (Uniform provisions concerning the approval of vehicles with
regard to specific requirements for the electric power train), is an international
regulation that specifies requirements for electric vehicles, including requirements
for protection against hazards like electrical shock and water effects and warnings
in the event of failure or low battery charge [108]. Like GB/T 31485, UNECE
Regulation No. 100 also stipulates testing for failure modes such as battery over-

 45

discharge, overcharge, dropping, heating, crushing, and short-circuiting, and the
observation that no leakage, fire, or explosion events occur during these tests. The
standard also describes other performance and acceptance tests for the vehicle like
thermal shock and cycling and mechanical impact, and the ability to protect against
conditions like low-temperature, over-discharge, and overcharge. During acceptance
testing, there should be no electrolyte leakage, battery rupture, venting, fire, or
explosion from the battery. Additionally, the overall vehicle should have a method of
managing any gases emitted from the battery to ensure that vehicle occupants are
not exposed if a failure occurs.

7.4. Safety Responsibility
It is important to clarify the parties responsible for hydrogen fuel cell technologies
codes and standards compliance. Most standards are self-enforced and
performance-based where responsibility falls on the authority having jurisdiction or
OEM. System- or vehicle-level standards can be more ambiguous since different
stakeholders are involved in design and construction. For example, SAE J2464
requires abuse testing for rechargeable energy storage systems on electric vehicles.
Involved parties such as the manufacturer of the energy storage device and the
manufacturer of the vehicle may both have some responsibility in ensuring
compliance (i.e., the battery manufacturer must perform the testing or ensure it is
performed before the battery can be sold, while the vehicle manufacturer must
ensure the individual parts of the vehicle, such as the battery, comply with their
specific standards). SAE J2578 and SAE J2579 both state requirements related to
overall design, construction, operation, and maintenance of fuel cell vehicles, which
means parties involved in each of those roles have some responsibility to ensure
equipment and vehicle compliance. ISO 6469-3 specifies electrical safety
requirements for electric circuits within electric vehicles. This document states that
it does not provide comprehensive safety requirements for manufacturing,
maintenance, and repair parties, which suggests that the vehicle manufacturer or
others involved in designing and manufacturing the electrical subsystems for the
vehicle carry most of the responsibility. The lack of clarity in guidance for parties
responsible for ensuring compliance with all relevant codes and standards is one

 46

area where discussions between stakeholders such as hydrogen equipment- and
vehicle-level designers and manufacturers would be beneficial to ensuring system
compliance and safety.

8. AREAS OF POTENTIAL FUTURE SIMULATION OR EXPERIMENTAL
 STUDY
To enable informed revision of both battery and hydrogen codes and standards, the
hazardous effects of battery fire, explosion, and chemical emissions on hydrogen
tanks should be better characterized. We have identified several future simulation
and experimental efforts which may aid in more specific characterization of the
hazards posed by integration and co-location of batteries and hydrogen tanks. This
suggested mix of experiments and modeling is based on some of the current
scientific and engineering gaps in understanding of battery and proximal tank
hazards.

1. Integration of battery quantitative risk assessment tools with hydrogen
 quantitative risk assessment tools - The work presented in this report represents
 an initial qualitative examination of the risks posed by lithium-ion batteries near
 hydrogen tanks. Quantitative risk assessment, especially related to battery TR or
 overheating, should be applied to estimate the probability of a nearby TPRD
 activating and pressurized hydrogen release as well as the TPRD not activating
 as designed leading to an explosion. This information can be used to determine
 other metrics such as fatality risk to passengers or bystanders as a function of
 distance from the vehicle. Additionally, heat and flame lengths emitted from the
 battery or the tank can also be simulated to help characterize their severity.
 Quantification of these risks can help inform future research and implementation
 decisions from a variety of stakeholders, as more information about tradeoffs can
 be incorporated in these decisions. Combining tools that consider battery and
 hydrogen storage risks and their associated uncertainties can help
 manufacturers and regulators develop a more holistic understanding of overall
 risk for specific conditions. Applications of such quantitative risk assessment
 tools can be useful for identifying potential failure modes and scenarios,
 determining high-risk battery and tank conditions, and prioritizing risk mitigation
 actions. Additionally, this work may inform separation distances for batteries and
 hydrogen tanks on vehicle frames or in other applications.

 48

2. Performing additional heat and chemical testing of Type IV and V tanks to
 determine the physical limits of these materials - Developing analytical/physics
 models of heat transfer from batteries (or another generic heat source) to tanks
 comprised of carbon fiber resin and high-density polyethylene/polyamide or
 other polymer liners can help quantify resulting tank pressures and temperatures
 and provide a starting point for characterizing convective heat effects and the
 potential for TPRD discharge or tank failure. Thermal and fluid dynamics
 software can aid in quantifying the temperature and time scale for heat transfer
 between batteries and tanks. Experiments involving a hydrogen storage tanks
 near a battery in TR would help validate models and inform the progression
 failure mechanism.

3. The effect of physical barrier materials in protecting hydrogen tanks from
 thermal and chemical battery emissions and identification of the optimal
 materials for these barriers – Characterizing different barrier materials can help
 further the understanding of their interactions with thermal and chemical
 hazards (i.e., isolation, absorption, dissipation, or a combination of functions
 related to hazard mitigation). Barriers may also serve a proactive role by cooling
 fires or safely neutralizing chemical emissions. This evaluation should also
 consider drawbacks such as barrier lifetime and degradation pathways. Since
 there are many possibilities for barrier types and functions, this area will likely
 have continued interest and investment, especially as batteries are more broadly
 implemented for different applications.

4. The effects of high pressure gaseous hydrogen, such as from a TPRD release,
 reacting with or igniting chemical emissions from batteries – Experiments which
 identify the main, side, cascade, chain, or decomposition reaction pathways
 when high pressure gaseous hydrogen interacts with typical chemical emissions
 from batteries (such as CH4, HF, HCl, CO, and others) can help identify relevant
 reactions and their starting, intermediate, and product species under an
 emergency release scenario. This characterization is important for quantifying
 heat evolution and production of potentially toxic chemicals.

 49

5. ESD (electrostatic discharge) testing on composite tank materials or simulations
 of current injection into a tank wall. Electric discharge or current injection in
 tanks testing and modeling can identify the conditions where high-voltage
 components can induce currents in the hydrogen system during faults or if any
 recorded incidents of arcing in hydrogen systems exist.

Collectively, these potential areas of future modeling or experiments could help
promote safe component design and improve risk-informed decision-making,
enabling safer coexistence of batteries and hydrogen tanks, especially as both
technologies become more ubiquitous. Figure 8-1 below details further research
initiatives.

9. CONCLUSION

As the use of hydrogen and battery storage systems continues to grow,
understanding the holistic, system-level risks associated with these technologies is
crucial. This work has focused on identifying the safety concerns and mitigations for
the failure modes of batteries, specifically the potential impacts and safety
implications of battery failures on hydrogen storage systems. The findings and
recommendations in this report are intended to help improve safety as hydrogen
storage system technology is further implemented.

LIBs, particularly those containing NMC and lithium iron phosphate LFP
chemistries, dominate the market for electric vehicles and BESS. While LIBs offer
high energy density and performance, possible thermal runaway, chemical release,
and explosions may occur in rare circumstances. These risks may be exacerbated
when batteries are near hydrogen storage tanks.

Composite hydrogen tanks, constructed from composite-wrapped polymer materials,
are widely used in multiple applications due to their high volumetric energy density
and resistance to hydrogen embrittlement. However, these tanks may be vulnerable
to thermal and chemical damage under certain conditions, including during battery
failures. Type IV tanks can begin to break down at temperatures as low as 85°C
while temperatures during thermal runaway in LIBs can be as high as 1000°C,
which can lead to the TPRD opening and the release of the tanks’ contents,
compromise the structural integrity of hydrogen tanks, and/or potentially lead to
catastrophic failures.

Current safety codes and standards, such as ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code
(BPVC) Section VIII and Section X, ISO 11119-3, SAE J2464, SAE J2578, SAE J2579,
ISO 6469-3, UNECE Regulation No. 100, NFPA 1 Chapter 52, NFPA 68, and NFPA
855, provide guidelines for the design, testing, and operation of hydrogen tanks and
batteries. However, these standards often address components independently,
without considering the integrated risks posed by the coexistence of batteries and
hydrogen storage systems. To mitigate these risks, several strategies can be
employed, including the use of barriers between hydrogen storage systems and

 52

batteries, battery management systems to detect and respond to early signs of
thermal runaway, the implementation of thermal management systems to prevent
heat transfer between cells, and the installation of multiple TPRDs on hydrogen
tanks to ensure adequate detection of high-temperature conditions. Additionally, the
selection of safer battery chemistries, such as LFP, and innovative battery designs,
such as blade-shaped or structural batteries, can further reduce the likelihood and
impact of battery failures.

Future research should focus on better characterizing the hazardous effects of
battery fires, explosions, and chemical emissions on hydrogen tanks through a
combination of simulations and experimental studies. This includes, but is not
limited to, understanding the physical limits of composite tanks, evaluating the
effectiveness of barrier materials, investigating the interactions between high-
pressure hydrogen and battery emissions, and integrating quantitative risk
assessment tools for both batteries and hydrogen storage systems.

A collaborative effort between various stakeholders including component
manufacturers, standard-development organizations, and researchers may be
beneficial toward implementing these mitigation strategies and proposed research
areas. Proactive efforts conducted early on can ensure safer deployment of co-
existent batteries and hydrogen storage technologies.
