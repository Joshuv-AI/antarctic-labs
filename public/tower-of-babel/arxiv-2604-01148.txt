Antu Saha, Atish Kumar Dipongkor, Sam Bennett, Kevin Moran, Andrian Marcus, Oscar Chaparro

Abstract
. Most defects in mobile applications are visually observable on the device screen. To track these defects, users, testers, and developers must manually submit bug reports, especially in the absence of crashes. However, these reports are frequently ambiguous or inaccurate, often omitting essential components such as the Observed Behavior (OB), Expected Behavior (EB), or Steps to Reproduce (S2Rs). Low-quality reports hinder developers’ ability to understand and reproduce defects, delaying resolution and leading to incorrect or unresolvable fixes. In this paper, we posit that providing specific app-related information ( e.g. , GUI interactions or specific screens where bugs appear) to LLMs as key points of context can assist in automatically generating clear, detailed, and accurate OB, EB, and S2Rs. We built and evaluated a novel approach, BugScribe , that generates bug reports in this way. To support the evaluation, we introduce a unified quality framework that defines correctness and completeness dimensions for OB, EB, and S2Rs. Using 48 bug reports from 26 Android apps, we show that BugScribe produces higher-quality and more accurate components than the original reports and outperforms recent LLM-based baselines. We envision that BugScribe can serve as a practical assistant for testers and developers by enhancing incomplete bug reports with reliable and accurate OB, EB, and S2Rs, thereby streamlining bug resolution and improving mobile app quality.



1.
Introduction

Most defects in mobile applications are visually observable on the device screen
(
Johnson et al. 2022
)
. Since automated mechanisms for detecting and reporting such bugs are typically unavailable—especially in the absence of explicit oracles like crashes—users, testers, and developers must manually submit bug reports (BRs) during app use or testing
(
Song et al. 2022
)
. Bug reports enable developers to understand, diagnose, reproduce, and ultimately correct the issues
(
Bettenburg et al. 2008
)
and generally contain natural language descriptions, sometimes accompanied by screen captures, replay videos, or execution traces.

A high-quality bug report should include detailed and unambiguous descriptions of at least three essential components
(
Zimmermann et al. 2010
;
Bettenburg et al. 2008
)
: the
Observed Behavior (OB)
, the
Expected Behavior (EB)
, and the
Steps to Reproduce (S2Rs)
. The OB describes the faulty behavior of the application, the EB specifies how users expect the app should behave, and the S2Rs provide instructions that allow developers to reproduce the problem and create test cases to validate fixes.

Unfortunately, bug reports are often submitted with incomplete, ambiguous, and/or inaccurate descriptions, sometimes omitting the EB or S2Rs entirely
(
Chaparro et al. 2017b
)
. Low-quality reports hinder developers’ ability to understand the defects
(
Chaparro et al. 2019a
)
, cause delays in resolution
(
Guo et al. 2010
;
Zimmermann et al. 2012
)
, and may even lead to reopened issues
(
Zimmermann et al. 2012
)
, or unresolvable defects
(
Guo et al. 2010
)
.
Writing complete, clear, and detailed bug reports with correct information is essential for streamlining issue resolution and improving app quality.

However, constructing high-quality bug reports is challenging for both developers and end users, particularly for UI-centric software such as mobile apps
(
Song et al. 2022
;
Moran et al. 2015a
)
.
The challenge stems from difficulty in understanding and describing program behavior at an appropriate level of abstraction. End users, for example, often rely on a feature-oriented understanding of an app and may describe observed faulty behavior using ambiguous or overly high-level language, sometimes omitting critical details.
In contrast, producing unambiguous and complete reports requires a detailed understanding of UI-centric aspects of an app (
e.g.
, UI components, screen flow, allowable user interactions, and supported features), as well as knowing how and when to use this information to
precisely
describe defective behavior. Ultimately, the core challenge of high-quality bug reporting lies in extracting and relating UI-anchored execution information to the observed defect.

Prior work has proposed techniques to improve bug reporting, including annotating problematic S2Rs
(
Mahmud et al. 2025
;
Chaparro et al. 2019a
)
, detecting missing OB/EB/S2Rs
(
Song and Chaparro 2020
;
Bo et al. 2024
)
, providing feedback on report quality
(
Mahmud et al. 2025
;
Chaparro et al. 2019a
)
, and developing interactive reporting tools
(
Song et al. 2022
;
Song et al. 2023
;
Fazzini et al. 2023
;
Moran et al. 2015b
)
. However, these approaches do not automatically enhance existing reports; instead, they offer guidance and leave users responsible for making corrections.
More recent work leverages large language models (LLMs) to restructure unstructured bug summaries into well-formed reports
(
Acharya and Ginde 2025
)
and to generate missing components (
e.g.
, EB)
(
Bo et al. 2024
)
. While promising, these methods still cannot assess or ensure the quality of generated bug content based on how well it reflects concrete app information, such as UI execution paths and associated metadata.
As a result, these methods may still produce incomplete or inaccurate reports; for instance, S2Rs that describe generic interaction sequences unrelated to the user’s actual GUI actions, or OB descriptions that inaccurately specify the system’s buggy state (see
Section

5.4
).
Such inaccuracies can mislead developers, hindering bug understanding, diagnosis, and reproduction.
Our
key insight
is that extracting and selectively supplying app UI information most relevant to each bug report component (
i.e.
, OB, EB, and S2Rs) enables LLMs to generate bug reports that are both complete and precise by connecting natural language descriptions of a bug in a report to UI-based program components, overcoming the limitations of prior approaches.

In this paper, we introduce
BugScribe
, an LLM-powered approach that automatically generates high-quality bug reports from existing reports.

BugScribe
is designed to produce detailed and accurate bug report components (OB, EB, and S2Rs) by combining various app-related information with the reasoning and language generation capabilities of an LLM.

BugScribe
leverages four types of app-related information: (1) OB, EB, and S2R sentences from the original report; (2) GUI-level user interactions that capture app usage; (3) the app screens on which those interactions occur; and (4) the screen suspected to contain the defect.
BugScribe
automatically extracts or infers this information.
Specifically,
BugScribe
constructs a graph-based execution model in which nodes represent app screens and edges represent GUI interactions, enriched with UI metadata such as layouts, widgets, and events. It then (i) identifies OB, EB, and S2R sentences in the report using LLM-based semantic reasoning, and (ii) retrieves relevant screens, interactions, and the likely buggy screen from the execution model via LLM-based analysis. Finally,
BugScribe
leverages LLM reasoning to align report text with execution data to infer a plausible reproduction scenario and generate accurate, detailed OB, EB, and S2R descriptions.

BugScribe
leverages and augments existing techniques for app execution modeling and buggy screen localization, and introduces novel zero-shot, task-decomposition prompts
(
Khot et al. 2022
;
Dua et al. 2022
)
to (1) translate complex execution data into natural language suitable for LLM reasoning, and (2) generate high-quality bug report components (see
Section

3
).

We used a data-driven methodology to identify the best combinations of app-specific information for generating each component: OB, EB, and S2Rs (Section
4
).

We systematically evaluated combinations of the four information types used as context for the LLM, generating bug descriptions for 10 existing bug reports across 9 Android apps spanning diverse domains and defect types.
Based on manually curated ground truth and the bug report quality model we developed (Section
2
), we assessed which combinations produced the highest-quality components.
The quality model builds on an existing S2R quality model
(
Chaparro et al. 2019a
)
and extends it to include OB and EB descriptions and their constituents.
The results show that different combinations of specific app-related information yield the best performance for each component.

We further evaluated
BugScribe
on
a dataset
of
48
bug reports from
26
Android apps, with corresponding ground truth OB, EB, and S2Rs (Section
5
), collected from prior work
(
Saha et al. 2024
;
Chaparro et al. 2019a
)
.
Using our bug report quality model, we compare
BugScribe
against the original bug reports, a recent state-of-the-art LLM-based approach,
U2Sbr

(
Acharya and Ginde 2025
)
, which generates structured reports from bug summaries, and two LLM baselines that do not use app-specific information as context. Our results show that
BugScribe
produces more detailed, complete, and accurate S2Rs than the baselines and the original reports (44.1% to 82.3% relative improvement) and generates OB and EB descriptions that are more detailed and accurate than the original reports and the LLM baselines (3.8% to 35.2% relative improvement).

In summary, the paper makes the following contributions:

•

BugScribe
, an LLM-powered approach that leverages dynamic app analysis, app-related information, and zero-shot, task decomposition prompting to generate detailed, complete, and accurate OB, EB, and S2R descriptions from existing bug reports.

•

A data-driven evaluation showing the benefits of combining multiple types of specific app information elements to generate high-quality OB, EB, and S2R descriptions.

•

An empirical evaluation showing that
BugScribe
improves the quality of original bug reports and generates higher-quality reports compared to three state-of-the-art LLM-based baselines.

•

A replication package that includes a manually curated dataset of 58 bug reports with high-quality ground truth for OB, EB, and S2Rs, along with
BugScribe
’s source code, documentation, and experimental infrastructure to facilitate the reproduction and validation of our results
(
pac 2026
)
, and to support future research.

2.
Bug Report Quality Model

Our quality model defines quality categories for the three main bug report components: the OB, EB, and S2Rs. A high-quality bug report must contain complete, detailed, and accurate descriptions of these components.
The model targets reports of GUI-based applications, in particular Android apps.

S2R Quality Model.
The S2Rs in a high-quality bug report are an enumerated list of GUI interactions that a user performs on the app sequentially to reproduce the bug. Each step should be atomic: it should represent a single GUI action (
e.g.
, a tap, type, or swipe) on a particular GUI component (
e.g.
, button or text field) located in an app screen (
e.g.
, an activity, window, or pop-up dialog). An example of high-quality S2R is “On the main screen, tap the ‘More options’ button”.
We adapt the
S2R quality model
from prior work
(
Chaparro et al. 2019a
)
and define the following categories:

•

Correct Step (CS):
the step corresponds to a specific GUI interaction in the application.

•

Ambiguous Step (AS):
the step corresponds to multiple interactions on GUI components.

•

Extra Step (ES):
the step is not required to reproduce the bug.

•

Missing Step (MS):
a GUI interaction required to reproduce the bug is missing from the report.

Compared to the original quality model
(
Chaparro et al. 2019a
)
, we include the Extra Step (ES) category because automated approaches may generate steps that are unnecessary to reproduce a bug, and we discarded the Vocabulary Mismatch (VM) category
(
Chaparro et al. 2019a
)
because we expect LLMs to always generate well-written steps that map to GUI interactions—we also did not observe this quality issue in our experiments.
An example of an
Ambiguous
S2R would be “Restore from backup” (see
fig.

1
) because multiple individual steps are required to complete the data restoration in the app.

OB Quality Model.
The OB in a high-quality report should be a clear, detailed, and self-contained description of the bug and should specify three main information elements: 1) the
buggy app behavior
(the observed app misbehavior), (2) a
buggy screen reference
(the screen where the user observed the bug), and (3) the
triggering GUI interaction
(the interaction that triggers the buggy behavior). An example of a high-quality OB is “The app crashes if I tap the ‘Restore from backup’ option on the pop-up menu”. Our model defines four quality attributes for each of the OB elements. A
quality OB element
can be:

•

Correct:
the element corresponds to plausible buggy app behaviors, screens, or GUI interactions.

•

Incomplete:
the element lacks information to fully map it to plausible buggy app behavior, screens, or GUI interactions.

•

Ambiguous:
the element contains ambiguous or generic information for mapping it specifically to plausible buggy app behavior, app screens, or GUI interactions.

•

Incorrect:
the element describes information that does not map to plausible buggy app behaviors, screens, or GUI interactions.

•

Missing:
the element is not provided in the OB description.

For example, in the OB “The app crashes if I tap the ‘Restore from backup’ option”, the
buggy screen reference
is
Missing
, and if it is written as “The app does not work if I tap the ‘Restore from backup’ option”, it would also contain an
Ambiguous

buggy app behavior
, and if it was written as “The app does not work if I ‘Restore from backup”’, it would also contain an
Incomplete

triggering GUI interaction
.

EB Quality Model.
The EB in a high-quality report should be a clear, detailed, and self-contained description of the
intended behavior
(the expected app behavior), and it is tied to the OB as it describes the opposite (or an alternative) to the OB’s buggy behavior. For example, for the OB “The app crashes if I tap the ‘Restore from backup’ option on the pop-up menu”, the EB should be “The app should successfully restore the backup and display a confirmation dialog instead of crashing”. We define the same five quality attributes as for the OB elements (
i.e.
, correct, incomplete, ambiguous, missing, and incorrect).
For instance, an EB with an
Ambiguous

intended behavior
would be “The app should work”.

3.
BugScribe
: Generating Quality Bug Reports

We introduce
BugScribe
, an LLM-based automated approach that receives an existing bug report and produces a high-quality report that more effectively describes the equivalent defect from the original report.
BugScribe
leverages dynamic app analysis, LLM-based language reasoning/generation, and uses app-related information as context for the LLM to generate bug reports with clear, detailed, and accurate OB, EB, and S2Rs. Based on these components,
BugScribe
also generates a one-line bug summary as bug report title and adds environment details described in the original bug report,
including mobile device information and Android version.

In its current version,
BugScribe
uses
GPT-5.4

(
OpenAI 2026
)
, a state-of-the-art reasoning LLM.
We selected this model because: (1) it offers reasoning of multi-modal information, as our problem involves reasoning about app execution information, app screen data, and natural language
and (2) it is a frontier model with state-of-the-art performance in reasoning, coding, and tool use
(
gpt 2026
)
.
In
BugScribe
’s evaluation, described in
Section

5
, we instantiate
BugScribe
with
ClaudeOpus-4.6

(
Anthropic 2025
)
, another state-of-the-art LLM, to assess whether using different frontier models impacts the effectiveness of
BugScribe
.

As shown in
Figure

2
,
BugScribe
consists of three main phases:

(1)

App Execution Model Generation
:
BugScribe
constructs a graph-based execution model of the application using both automated and manual app exploration (
Section

3.1
).

(2)

Contextual Information Extraction
:
BugScribe
extracts and formats app-related information,
e.g.
, GUI interactions and screen descriptions, used as context for the LLM (
Section

3.2
).

(3)

Bug Report Generation
:
BugScribe
generates high-quality bug reports, including a title, OB, EB, and S2Rs, leveraging the app-specific information and LLM-based reasoning/generation via zero-shot, task-decomposition prompting (
Section

3.3
).

.
BugScribe
’s architecture

3.1.
App Execution Model Generation

In the first phase,
BugScribe
constructs a graph-based app execution model that captures GUI interactions made on app screens, including corresponding app screen metadata such as the UI component hierarchy and events (
Figure

2
.a). Later,
BugScribe
extracts and formats the GUI interactions and the screen information from the execution model as context for the LLM to generate high-quality bug reports (
Sections

3.2
and
3.3
). We leveraged prior work that uses graph-based representations and dynamic app execution strategies to implement this phase
(
Song et al. 2022
;
Saha et al. 2024
)
.

Specifically,
BugScribe
constructs an app execution model as a directed graph
G=(V,E)
, where the nodes
V
correspond to the unique GUI screens of the app and the edges
E
capture the possible user interactions with GUI components on those screens. Each screen (
i.e.
, node) is defined by its hierarchy of components and layouts; two screens are treated as distinct nodes if their component hierarchies differ. An interaction (
i.e.
, edge) is represented as a tuple (
v_{x},v_{y},e,c
), where
c
is a GUI component on screen
v_{x}
(
e.g.
, a button or text field),
e
is the action performed on
c
(such as tap, swipe,
etc.
), and the interaction leads to a transition to screen
v_{y}
.
Edges also contain additional metadata describing the interaction, including the component type, ID, label (
i.e.
, text), and description.

BugScribe
builds the execution model from GUI interaction traces collected through both automated exploration and manual app usage. For automated exploration,
BugScribe
uses a modified version of
CrashScope

(
Moran et al. 2016
;
Moran et al. 2017
)
, which employs multiple UI exploration strategies to maximize screen and component coverage while collecting screenshots, XML-based UI hierarchies, and UI metadata.
Because automated exploration may miss interactions commonly performed by real users,
BugScribe
can also incorporate traces from manual usage or testing. For the bug reports studied in this paper, we use traces collected in prior work
(
Saha et al. 2024
;
Mahmud et al. 2025
)
, which capture complete interaction sequences from app launch to feature completion. In practice, such manual traces can be collected throughout app evolution, for example, via user monitoring and record-and-replay during in-house or crowd-sourced testing
(
Du et al. 2022
)
.

3.2.
Contextual Information Extraction

BugScribe
annotates the user-submitted bug report to specify three components (OB, EB, and S2Rs) and extracts three app information elements (app screen information, GUI interactions, and buggy screen) as context for the LLM to generate high-quality bug reports.

3.2.1.
OB/EB/S2R Sentence Annotation.

BugScribe
analyzes the input bug report and identifies the
sentences that express the OB, EB, and S2Rs
(
Figure

2
.b). We frame this task as a text classification problem in which
BugScribe
leverages the LLM to classify each sentence in the report as OB, EB, S2R, or other.
BugScribe
first decomposes the report into individual sentences, then
provides them as context to
GPT-5.4
via zero-shot, rule-based prompting to obtain sentence labels.
The original bug report with labeled sentences is passed to the next phase.
This labeling allows
BugScribe
to prioritize OB/EB/S2R content during component generation, while retaining the full bug report context, which may include extra information such as environment details (build number, device, operating system,
etc.
), code snippets, or stack traces.

We extended the S2R classification prompt template from Mahmud
et al.

(
Mahmud et al. 2025
)
following their data-driven methodology for classifying OB, EB, and S2R sentences. The template includes the definition of OB, EB, and S2R with detailed guidelines to classify all sentences in the bug report. The prompt template and the OB/EB/S2R identification results are found in our replication package
(
pac 2026
)
.

3.2.2.
App Screen Description Generation.

BugScribe
uses
app screen descriptions
generated by a vision-capable LLM as the next piece of app context. These natural language summaries convey the functionality and purpose of the given app screens and may include functional and visual details about screen layouts, GUI components, and functions offered. An example of an app screen description is: “This popup menu provides options such as changing the date range, exporting to CSV, … It appears as a dialog over the main task list.”
BugScribe
generates a description for each app screen
in the execution model, which is annotated by a unique ID.
The description is generated by the LLM using zero-shot prompting, with screen-level metadata (
e.g.
, layout and UI component information) used as context.
This information is expected to help the LLM identify the required interactions to reproduce the bug, used to generate atomic S2Rs, as well as a suspected buggy screen (
Section

3.2.4
). Prompts for this step can be found in our replication package
(
pac 2026
)
.

3.2.3.
GUI Interaction Extraction.

GUI interactions
from the execution model are the building blocks for
BugScribe
to generate atomic S2Rs, because the required interactions to reproduce the bug are a subset of all the interactions that can be made on the application.
From all the possible GUI interactions,
BugScribe
uses the LLM to map the provided S2Rs in the original bug report to a navigable interaction sequence from the model that leads to the buggy behavior (Figure
2
.b). More precisely,
BugScribe
extracts all the GUI interactions of an application from the execution model and parses their metadata into a list of interactions given as context to the LLM (
Figure

2
.b). Each GUI interaction from the graph is a tuple that contains (1) the action (
e.g.
, tap), (2) the GUI element that the action operates on (
e.g.
, “OK button"), (3) the ID of the
source screen
where the interaction is performed, and (4) the ID of the
target screen
(the resulting screen after the action is performed). This sequence is then synthesized into descriptions of atomic S2Rs. If the S2Rs are missing, the LLM can infer potential steps based on the OB, EB, other information from the bug report, or from the execution model itself.

3.2.4.
Buggy Screen Localization.

The
buggy screen
is the app screen on which faulty behavior is displayed. It serves as the stopping point for
BugScribe
’s S2R generation phase, as it should be the final screen during bug reproduction. It can also assist in the OB/EB description generation step.

BugScribe
receives the descriptions of all app screens from the
screen description generation
step, and relies on LLM-reasoning of the reported bug and the screen description to identify potential buggy screens automatically (
Figure

2
.c). We formulate this task as a screen retrieval problem, similar to prior work
(
Saha et al. 2024
)
, where the goal is to retrieve a ranked list of potential buggy screens for the given bug report from all possible application screens in the execution model.
BugScribe
uses a zero-shot prompt with detailed instructions asking the LLM to reason over the input (described below) to return a ranked list of potential buggy screens.
BugScribe
then considers the top-ranked screen as the buggy screen, using it as context for bug component generation.

While prior work
(
Saha et al. 2024
)
relies on three deep learning models—
e.g.
,
SBert

(
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
)
,
Clip

(
Radford et al. 2021
)
, and
Blip

(
Li et al. 2022
)
—we leverage LLMs for this task, which requires careful prompt design and a systematic evaluation of different prompting strategies.
Specifically,
we designed two experiments for buggy screen localization with two prompts that used different inputs and instructions.
The first experiment uses the labeled bug report and the screen descriptions as the input, while the second includes all the app GUI interactions as well. We found that the GUI interactions provides information that helps
BugScribe
localize buggy screens more effectively (the correct buggy screen is ranked first in 80% cases, compared to 60% cases), hence,
BugScribe
uses all these information elements as context.
Our replication package contains the prompt templates and the results of these experiments
(
pac 2026
)
.

3.3.
Bug Report Generation

3.3.1.
S2R Generation.

To generate an improved bug report,
BugScribe
first generates atomic S2R descriptions for the input bug report with
GPT-5.4
(
Figure

2
.d).

This step uses a zero-shot, task-decomposition prompt to guide the LLM to map the labeled S2R sentences to GUI interaction sequences and infer plausible reproduction scenarios that lead to the buggy screen.

The prompt instructs the LLM to identify the most likely reproduction scenario given the described bug and context and generate a detailed, enumerated list of reproduction steps. The prompt starts with the task description, followed by a description of the input (
i.e.
, app-related and report information used as context), the output format, the inputs (structured into well-defined sections), and detailed instructions to complete the task. To determine the combination of context information elements that lead to the highest-quality generated S2Rs, we employed a data-driven methodology described in
Section

4
.

3.3.2.
OB/EB Generation.

The last step in
BugScribe
’s pipeline generates the OB and EB for the input report using
GPT-5.4
(
Figure

2
.e). This step uses a zero-shot, task decomposition prompt to guide the LLM to map the identified OB and EB from the original bug report to the contextual app information, including the suspected buggy screen, the S2Rs generated in the previous pipeline step, and the source and target screen descriptions for each generated S2R. This step also generates a bug report title and additional environmental information, such as the Android version and device.

The prompt specifies the task description, the inputs (structured into well-defined sections) with their descriptions, detailed instructions to complete the task, and the output format. To determine the combination of contextual information elements that lead to the highest-quality OB and EB, we employed the data-driven methodology described in
Section

4
.

Finally,
BugScribe
assembles the complete bug report by appending the generated output into labeled sections in the following order: title, OB, EB, S2Rs, and additional information.

4.
Identifying App-specific Information for Developing
BugScribe

We used a data-driven approach to identify the most effective combinations of app-specific information (
e.g.
, GUI interactions and buggy screen) for
BugScribe
’s S2R and OB/EB generation. Using a development set of 10 bug reports, we evaluated reports generated by
BugScribe
against ground truth OB, EB, and S2Rs that we manually created based on our bug report quality model.

4.1.
Development Dataset Construction

4.1.1.
Bug Report Collection.

We collected the 10 bug reports from the development set from prior work on S2R quality assessment
(
Mahmud et al. 2025
)
.
The bug reports are a subset of Saha
et al.
’s dataset
(
Saha et al. 2024
)
and span diverse bug types and apps.

The bugs span nine Android apps from different domains (
e.g.
, finance tracking, file management, multimedia,
etc.
) and report various bug types: crashes (3), cosmetic issues (3), output problems (3), and a navigation bug (1). On average, the app execution models associated with the 10 bug reports contain 37.2 UI screens (ranging from 12 to 74 across) and 80.5 GUI interactions (ranging from 19 to 159).

The dataset includes OB/EB/S2R sentences, videos that show how to reproduce the bugs, and screenshots of the exercised app screen during automated/manual app exploration and execution. However, it does not include data required to validate report quality (
e.g.
, ground truth OB, EB, and S2R descriptions).

4.1.2.
Ground Truth Creation.

To create ground truth S2Rs, we first identified a minimal reproduction scenario (a path of GUI interactions) in the app execution model that maps to the S2Rs in the reports. Once identified, we created natural language descriptions of the GUI interactions using GUI interaction metadata (
i.e.
, the action and the interacted GUI component).

For each of the 10 reports, an author read the reported bug, watched the reproduction video, and manually inspected the graph nodes and edges to identify a minimal reproduction scenario.

This author created the natural language description of each S2R following the format: [action] [GUI component] (
e.g.
, "tap the OK button").

A second author validated the identified path and S2Rs description, noting potential mistakes and disagreements. The two authors engaged in a discussion session to resolve any issues and reach a consensus to finalize the S2R ground truth. The agreement rate was high, 91.7%, with the most common reason for the disagreement being the misinterpretation of execution model metadata.

During the ground truth creation process, the authors also identified the buggy screen in the execution model: the resulting screen of the last GUI interaction in the reproduction scenario. Based on this screen and the OB/EB in the original reports, one author wrote ground truth OB and EB. For OBs, the format used was: “On [buggy screen reference], if the user [triggering GUI interaction], [buggy app behavior]” (
e.g.
, “On the confirmation dialog, if the user clicks the OK button, the app crashes”). The EB format used was: “[subject] should/shouldn’t [intended app behavior]” (
e.g.
, “the app should take me to the previous screen without crashing”). These templates were designed based on the OB/EB discourse patterns identified in prior work
(
Chaparro et al. 2017b
)
, which were derived from manual analysis of hundreds of real-life bug reports. A second author validated the ground truth OBs/EBs, and both authors agreed on the final ground truth if changes were needed (
e.g.
, due to mistakes).

4.1.3.
Development Dataset Summary.

Across the 10 bug reports, ground truth S2Rs include 103 atomic steps (10.3 per bug on average), each corresponding to one specific GUI interaction. For each bug report, we created one OB and EB description with the correct OB/EB information elements.

4.2.
Configurations of App-related Information Used as Context to the LLM

4.2.1.
Configurations for S2R generation.

We designed four configurations that combine three app information elements: GUI interactions, screen descriptions, and the buggy screen.

(1)

No Info.
uses the original report and no app-related information, allowing us to understand the LLM’s ability to generate high-quality S2Rs without any context.

(2)

Interac.
adds
GUI interactions
to generate S2Rs, assuming that access to all app interactions enables the LLM to identify and generate the required steps based on the original bug report.

(3)

Interac.+Scrn.

adds the screen descriptions. Screen descriptions can help the LLM map interaction sequences to the original bug report when generating S2Rs.

(4)

Interac.+Scrn.+BuggyScrn.
adds the suspected buggy screen.
The buggy screen can inform the LLM about the stopping point in the reproduction scenario because it is the final app screen where the bug manifests.

4.2.2.
Configurations for OB/EB generation.

We designed four configurations combining three app-specific contexts: the buggy screen,
BugScribe
-generated S2Rs, and screen descriptions.

(1)

No Info.
does not use any app context, serving as a bare-minimum baseline for OB/EB generation.

(2)

Buggy Scrn.
adds the buggy screen description to generate the OB/EB. As the bug should be visible on this screen, it can provide key information such as the OB’s buggy screen reference.

(3)

S2Rs+BuggyScrn.
adds atomic S2Rs generated in the S2R phase to the buggy screen description, helping the LLM identify the triggering GUI interaction.

(4)

S2Rs+BuggyScrn.+Scrns.
adds screen descriptions of the S2Rs, helping the LLM reason about the steps and identify the triggering GUI interaction.

4.3.
Prompt Development Methodology

To evaluate the app-related information configurations, we designed two core prompt templates for the S2Rs and OB/EB generation tasks (described in
Section

3.3
). We adapted the templates with specific instructions for handling the different app-related information elements, thus creating different instances of the prompts, corresponding to the configurations. The wording and structure of all the prompts are nearly the same. The main difference lies in the inputs (
i.e.
, combinations of app information elements) and specific instructions to handle them. For example, one S2R generation instruction to handle the buggy screen is: “Starting from the initial transition [GUI interaction],
i.e.
, the “open app” transition, your goal is to reach the buggy screen by identifying the most relevant transitions for the user-reported S2Rs.” An example instruction to handle GUI interactions is: “The generated S2Rs must form a
valid and complete path
in the transition graph, starting from the initial screen and ending at the screen where the bug occurs.”

For each task, one author designed the structured prompt template with detailed instructions for both generation tasks following zero-shot, task-decomposition prompting
(
Khot et al. 2022
;
Dua et al. 2022
)
, the OpenAI guidelines
(
ope 2025b
)
, and the best practices
(
White et al. 2024
;
Santana Jr et al. 2025
;
Schulhoff et al. 2024
)
. For refining and improving the prompts, the author experimented with three bugs from the development set and consulted with the web version of ChatGPT with the example bugs to design an optimized prompt (
i.e.
, we used meta-prompting
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
). A second author reviewed the wording and structure of the designed prompt templates and experimented with the three bug reports. This second author made suggestions to improve the prompts where necessary. Both authors met and discussed the suggestions to finalize the prompt templates.

4.4.
Prompt Execution, Metrics, and Evaluation

We executed the eight prompt configurations (four prompts per generation task) using the OpenAI API
(
ope 2025a
)
with the
GPT-5.4
model. Note that the latest GPT models do not have temperature control, so we used the model as is.
We assessed the consistency of LLM generation across three prompt executions and reported the average bug report component quality using the evaluation metrics described next.

4.4.1.
Evaluation Metrics.

We evaluated the quality of the generated S2Rs, OBs, and EBs using the bug report quality model described in
Section

2
, by manually comparing them against ground truth. For S2Rs, we assigned three quality labels: 1)
Correct Step (CS):
the generated step is found in the ground truth S2Rs (
True Positive or TP)
, 2)
Extra Step (ES):
the generated step is not found in the ground truth S2Rs (
False Positive or FP
, and 3)
Missing Step (MS):
a ground truth S2R is not found in the generated steps (
False Negative or FN)
. Using the quality labels assigned to the S2Rs, we computed
precision
,
recall
, and
F1 score
to measure S2R quality.

For OB and EB evaluation, we assessed each OB/EB information element (
e.g.
, buggy behavior or triggering GUI interaction) using four quality labels (see
Section

2
for their definition):
Correct
,
Incomplete
,
Ambiguous
,
Incorrect
, and
Missing
.
We counted the number of cases within each quality category for each component element (
e.g.
, the OB’s buggy behavior) to measure OB/EB quality.

4.4.2.
Methodology for Quality Assessment.

We conducted a multi-coder qualitative evaluation to mitigate the potential biases. Two authors independently compared the generated S2Rs/OB/EB with the respective ground truth and assigned quality labels according to the quality model. To ensure reliable and consistent evaluation, we designed detailed evaluation criteria for each bug report component and specific guidelines with instructions (see our replication package
(
pac 2026
)
). The authors engaged in a discussion session to resolve the disagreements following the criteria to reach a consensus. If the two annotators could not agree, a third annotator evaluated such cases to make a final decision via consensus.

We assessed inter-annotator reliability using observed agreement, Cohen’s Kappa (
\kappa
)
(
Cohen 1960
)
and Krippendorff’s Alpha (
\alpha
)
(
Krippendorff 2018
)
, obtaining overall high agreement:
99.5%
agreement for S2Rs (
\kappa={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}0.98}
, almost perfect agreement
(
Viera et al. 2005
)
;
\alpha={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}0.99}
, reliable agreement
(
Marzi et al. 2024
)
) and

87.5%
agreement for OBs/EBs (
\kappa={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}0.6}
, moderate agreement
(
Viera et al. 2005
)
;
\alpha={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}0.94}
, reliable agreement
(
Marzi et al. 2024
)
).

We implemented

5.2.1.
GPTno-info
and

These baselines generate bug reports following a similar generation pipeline as

Acharya
et al.

(

proposed an LLM-based approach

.
We implemented this baseline using its companion replication package
(

)
.

5.3.
Methodology and Metrics

We ran
BugScribe
and all baseline techniques on the
48

Given that our development experiments found near-perfect consistency in LLM responses under the same inputs across three executions, we executed all the approaches only once in this evaluation.
To address

{1}

Overall, the agreement rates between the two evaluators for S2Rs is 97.3% (

(

Comparison Protocol.
As
GPTno-info
,
CLAUDEno-info
, and
U2Sbr
do not use application information and are not designed to generate atomic S2Rs, they can produce compound S2Rs (
i.e.
, multiple steps in one S2R sentence), contain different wording than the application vocabulary, or implicitly mention multiple steps.
To ensure a fair comparison, we adopted the following strategies. If there are
m
atomic steps in the generated bug report, and among them
n
are present in the ground truth as individual steps, we counted
n
correct steps. Moreover, we considered generated steps with different wording or presentation but similar meaning to the ground truth step as
correct

We also used these strategies to compare the S2R quality of the original bug reports against the ground truth, which is required to compare the reports generated by
BugScribe
and the baselines with the original reports using our evaluation metrics.

A complete comparison protocol to ensure a fair comparison is found in our replication package
(
pac 2026
)

Analysis of Failed Cases.

Manual inspection of the incomplete and incorrect triggering GUI interactions generated by
BSgpt
/
BSclaude
reveals that extra or unnecessary reproduction steps in the original bug report can misguide
BugScribe
. For example, bug report #1066
(
bug 2026b
)
(Focus-android app) contains the line:
“Repeat moving cursor and clicking system status bar…”
, where the user attempted to circumvent the bug. This operation is not a part of the reproduction steps; however,
BugScribe
consider this as the triggering GUI interaction, resulting in an incomplete/incorrect element.

We analyzed the ambiguous buggy screen references generated by
BugScribe
and found that, although imprecise, they refer to the correct buggy screens. These ambiguities arise because
BugScribe
often uses generic, functionality-based names rather than the exact UI wording. For instance, in bug report #154 for the Camfahrplan app
(
bug 2026a
)
, the correct buggy screen is
“schedule screen”
, whereas
BSclaude
refers to it as
“event details screen”
. This discrepancy stems from limitations in the app metadata used to build the app execution model, which may omit or differ from the user-visible screen names, prompting
BugScribe
to infer names from the UI screen descriptions used as context. To address this, our future work will incorporate visual information from UI screenshots via multimodal models to improve screen naming. Besides ambiguous cases, incorrect buggy screen were primarily generated because the buggy screen localization phase failed to identify the correct buggy screen. Our future work will experiment with the inclusion of multiple buggy screen suggestions,
i.e.
, not only leverage the most suspicious screen detected by the localization phase as context.

6.
Threats to Validity

Construct Validity.

The manual construction of high-quality ground truth OB, EB, and S2Rs and the manual evaluation of generated bug reports may introduce subjectivity.
To mitigate this, two authors performed these tasks using well-defined, replicable procedures. In-depth discussion sessions were conducted to resolve the misunderstandings and disagreements. We further quantified the consistency of their work by computing inter-rater agreement, which was consistently very high across all cases, reinforcing the reliability of
BugScribe
’s development and evaluation.

Internal Validity.

The performance of
BugScribe
can vary depending on the wording and structure of the prompt templates. To mitigate this, we systematically designed the templates following the best practices
(
White et al. 2024
;
Santana Jr et al. 2025
;
Schulhoff et al. 2024
)
and evaluated the prompts with different configurations on the development dataset.
LLM non-determinism may cause results to fluctuate across executions. We addressed this by running our development experiments three times, reporting consistent results across runs.
While the baselines were not designed to produce atomic S2Rs, we applied consistent and transparent rules to ensure a fair comparison with
BugScribe
,
documenting our procedure to ensure reproducibility.

External Validity.

The results may or may not generalize to bug reports and apps outside from our datasets. However, these datasets contain bug reports of various bug types (
e.g.
, crashes, UI issues, navigation problems,
etc.
) and
26
Android apps of various kinds (
e.g.
, web browsing, WiFi diagnosis, finance tracking,
etc.
) and sizes.
BugScribe
is developed using a distinct dataset,
i.e.
, a development set of 10 bug reports from nine apps of various domains (
e.g.
, file management, multimedia,
etc.
) and different bug types.

BugScribe
’s evaluation using a different data set (the test set) and the fact that the quality results between both datasets are similar gives confidence in the generalizability of the results.

Bug Report Quality Improvement.

Prior research on enhancing the bug report quality has primarily focused on detecting missing components (
e.g.
, OB/EB/S2R) and generating structured reports by leveraging LLMs. For instance, Acharya
et al.

(

)
proposed an LLM-based technique to transform unstructured bug summaries into structured reports with explicit OB, EB, and S2Rs. Bo
et al.

(
Bo et al. 2024
)
introduced ChatBR, which first identifies components completely missing in the report using a fine-tuned BERT classifier and then employs an LLM to generate the missing content.

Compared to these approaches,
BugScribe

By combining dynamic analysis with LLM reasoning through novel prompt designs,
BugScribe
produces OB, EB, and S2Rs that are explicitly grounded in concrete application behavior, resulting in more accurate and complete bug reports (as the evaluation shows).

Other work has used LLMs to analyze bug reports and recommend relevant screenshots

8.
Conclusions

We learned that by leveraging different types of app-related information (
i.e.
, GUI interactions, screen metadata, and buggy screen references), LLMs can be used to generate high-quality bug reports better than existing, more generic approaches, which rely only on the user-provided textual descriptions of the bug.
Our tool,
BugScribe
, generates bug reports with higher quality than those generated by a state-of-the-art baseline technique and the original user-written bug reports.
