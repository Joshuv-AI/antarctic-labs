Scattering in strong field QED in a non-null background
Patrick Copinger, James P. Edwards, Karthik Rajeev

Abstract
We examine scattering amplitudes for an arbitrary number of photons in a class of non-null background electromagnetic fields, studying tree-level and one-loop amplitudes in scalar and spinor quantum-electrodynamics in backgrounds defined by a gauge field A μ ​ ( 𝔫 ⋅ x ) A_{\mu}(\mathfrak{n}\cdot x) for 𝔫 2 ≠ 0 \mathfrak{n}^{2}\neq 0 . Motivated to account for more physically realistic laser-plasma dispersive properties, our approach overcomes prior work studying such amplitudes in a constant background field and relaxes the familiar null criterion assumed for plane waves. Master Formulae for the N N -photon amplitudes dressed by the non-null background are constructed using the first-quantised worldline formalism, which can systematically account for all orders in the non-null parameter, 𝔫 2 \mathfrak{n}^{2} , treated here as an expansion parameter. These are derived from worldline representations of the coordinate and momentum space propagators (and their LSZ-truncated amplitudes) and the effective action, each incorporating the non-null background non-perturbatively. We then outline a partial resummation of their expansions in 𝔫 2 \mathfrak{n}^{2} . A special exactly solvable case of non-null constant crossed fields without photon insertion in the effective action is explored to test the Master Formulae that result. The validity of the presented master formulae is further checked against known expressions for the wavefunction and non-linear Compton scattering in a non-null background to lowest order in the non-null parameter.

Introduction

Theoretical studies of quantum field theory in electromagnetic backgrounds (i.e., strong-field quantum electrodynamics – SFQED) have long relied on approximating the electromagnetic backgrounds as being some combination of constant and/or plane wave fields
44
, which have become central analytics tools. In such fields, the Dirac and Klein–Gordon equations admit closed-form (Volkov) solutions
98
;
82
;
74
;
75
, from which the field-dressed matter wavefunctions and propagator can be derived exactly. This allows standard S-matrix techniques to be applied to determine scattering amplitudes analytically (for arbitrary background profiles, these are integral representations) and non-perturbatively in the background coupling strength
51
;
63
. The earliest studies go back almost to the birth of quantum field theory itself, considering the quantum effective action
56
;
97
, light-by-light scattering
43
;
12
;
61
;
62
;
25
;
26
and the Sauter-Schwinger pair creation effect

85
;
91
– for a contemporary review of these developments see
86
;
48
;
32
;
5
.

In particular, amplitudes in a plane-wave background, characterised by a gauge field of the form
A_{\mu}(n\cdot x)
with
n^{2}=0
null, play a role in strong-field QED analogous to the hydrogen atom in quantum mechanics: they provide a rare, highly symmetric setting in which nonlinear interactions can be treated exactly, and they supply benchmark results against which approximations and numerical methods can be tested. Much of our present understanding of strong-field processes—nonlinear Compton scattering
78
;
31
, nonlinear Breit–Wheeler pair creation
72
, and the nonlinear trident process
95
derives from this exceptional solubility.

More recently, the so-called worldline formalism of quantum field theory

;

;

;

;

. This first quantised approach, initially proposed in

;

;

It is especially well-adapted to studying higher-multiplicity scattering processes, because the Master Formulae turn out to combine multiple Feynman diagrams related by permutation of external legs

;

In parallel to theoretical developments, modern high-intense laser facilities

;
20
;
99
;

;
101
;

71
;
81
;
29
;
76
. In realistic ultra-intensity laser experiments, the laser often interacts with residual gas, ionised matter, or even intentionally injected plasmas — leading to non-trivial medium responses such as dispersion, refractive index shifts
24

69
;
68

In particular, when propagating through a plasma, the phase-four vector describing the field wavefronts ceases to be strictly null, and the field no longer behaves as a classical plane wave in vacuum. As a result, the background vector potential can often be better modeled by a form akin to
eA_{\mu}(x)=\delta_{\mu}^{\perp}a_{\perp}(\mathfrak{n}\cdot x)
, with
\mathfrak{n}^{2}\neq 0
even though the wavefronts remain planar
24
;
17
;
54
and perpendicular to the light-front directions (see below); such gauge fields we refer to as
non-null
fields. This departure from
nullness
, in turn, encodes the medium’s dispersive properties (effective photon “mass”, refractive index, group velocity effects, etc.), thereby capturing some of the realistic physics of laser–plasma interactions in strong-field regimes
64
;
35
.

The exact analytic machinery of vacuum plane-wave QED, however, ceases to work in these non-null backgrounds. Our central idea is to exploit the fact that these backgrounds can still be viewed as controlled perturbations of a plane wave, with the non-nullness
\mathfrak{n}^{2}\equiv\rho^{2}
, serving as an expansion parameter. The plane wave-like part of the background can still be treated exactly (in this case, to all orders in
\rho^{2}
). An obvious advantage of this approach is that it preserves much of the analytic structure and clarity of the plane-wave case, while revealing how key physical quantities are modified once the null condition is relaxed. Physically, the approach can be thought of as retaining nonperturbative dressing of charged particles by the background through a Volkov-type exponentiation to ensure that part of the all-order interactions with the strong background is resummed
71
;
79
;
80
;
54
, while incorporating effects such as medium-induced dispersion only at the perturbative level (see, Fig.
1
.)

From a methodological standpoint, our analysis is carried out using the worldline path integral formalism, which allows background-field effects to be incorporated in a transparent and often remarkably compact way
89
;
40
;
23
. In this approach, propagators, amplitudes, and other relevant objects are represented as path integrals over relativistic point particle trajectories. Over the past decade, the worldline formalism has seen significant development in the context of strong-field processes, including exact and near-exact treatments of plane-wave backgrounds
88
;
41
;
42
;
22
, where it reproduces Volkov physics in a natural and economical manner. In particular, the integrability of the plane-wave background shows up in the worldline formalism as a hidden Gaussianity of the path integral, making it semi-classically exact (see
60
;
41
). These features make the worldline approach especially well suited for the present problem: it allows nonperturbative plane-wave effects to be treated exactly, while accommodating systematic perturbative insertions associated with deviations from null propagation.

More broadly, the framework we present here fits within a larger effort to extend strong-field QED to realistic electromagnetic backgrounds by exploiting the unique analytic control afforded by plane waves. In this perspective, the exact plane-wave amplitudes—already resumming the dominant nonperturbative dynamics –serve as an analytic core from which amplitudes in more general, non-plane-wave fields can be generated through systematic deformations. A recent example in this direction was taken in
21
, where amplitudes in pp-wave backgrounds were expressed as statistical averages over plane-wave ones. The present work represents another concrete step in this program – systematically incorporating small deviations from the nullness of phase directions – and motivates the long-term goal of extending the framework to fields with even more realistic features like transverse structure or finite spatial extent.

II
Non-Null Background Fields

Plane waves are solutions to Maxwell’s equations characterised by wavefronts that are 3-dimensional planes. The normal to these planes is therefore
n\propto d\phi
, where
\phi\propto n\cdot x
is the wavefront phase. The symmetry of this background suggests a convenient set of coordinates and a choice of gauge, whereby
the plane wave vector potential can be written as
eA_{\mu}(x)=a_{\mu}(n\cdot x)=\delta_{\mu}^{\perp}a_{\perp}(x^{+})
with light-front direction
x^{+}=n\cdot x
defined to align with the phase, the normal
n_{\mu}
is null (
n^{2}=0
), and the transversality to the direction of propagation of the plane wave is manifest (the “
\perp
” direction is orthogonal to
n
so that
n\cdot a=0
). Fields with such a gauge potential have a field strength tensor

\mathrm{f}_{\mu\nu}({x^{+}})=n_{\mu}a^{\prime}_{\nu}(x^{+})-n_{\nu}a^{\prime}_{\mu}(x^{+})\,

(1)

with field invariants

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{f}_{\mu\nu}\mathrm{f}^{\mu\nu}=|\mathbf{E}|^{2}-|\mathbf{B}|^{2}=0\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{f}_{\mu\nu}\widetilde{\mathrm{f}}^{\mu\nu}=\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}=0\,,

(3)

where
\widetilde{\mathrm{f}}^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\mathrm{f}_{\alpha\beta}
is the dual field strength tensor. So the electric and magnetic fields are orthogonal (“crossed”) and of equal magnitude (in natural units); with our convention, the fields point only in the transverse directions to the light-front coordinates. Such plane wave fields are relevant to SFQED because, as outlined in the introduction, the fields of high-intensity lasers are often approximately modeled as plane waves
98
;
84
; moreover, boosting an arbitrary electromagnetic field from the lab frame to the rest frame of an ultra-relativistic electron results in it seeing transverse fields significantly enhanced by the Lorentz factor, such that the electric and magnetic field are approximately perpendicular and of almost equal magnitude:

\frac{\mathbf{E}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{B}^{\prime}}{|\mathbf{E}^{\prime}||{\mathbf{B}^{\prime}|}}\sim\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\frac{\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}}{|\mathbf{E}||{\mathbf{B}|}}\sim 0\,,\qquad\qquad\frac{|\mathbf{E}^{\prime}|^{2}-|\mathbf{B}^{\prime}|^{2}}{|\mathbf{E}^{\prime}||{\mathbf{B}^{\prime}|}}\sim\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\frac{|\mathbf{E}|^{2}-|\mathbf{B}|^{2}}{|\mathbf{E}||{\mathbf{B}|}}\sim 0

(4)

(where we are omitting dimensionless constants). One approximation regularly applied in QED calculations in very strong background fields is the “Locally Constant Field Approximation” (LCFA
15
;
74
;
57
;
92
) in which the (often exact)
rates
for given processes in crossed constant fields (of equal magnitude) are integrated over the true, varying electromagnetic field profile. This models the process as taking place in a field that is instantaneously constant and generally works well when the variation of the fields is slow with respect to the Compton wavelength of particles participating in the process and its formation scale
18
;
13
;
50
. The LCFA is not, however, universally applicable and it is well known that it can miss important physical effects
53
;
27
;
30
;
77
.

Although it is possible to add derivative corrections to the LCFA
16
;
65
;
28
, this does not always lead to a sensible expansion that reliably improves numerical results
58
. Going beyond the plane-wave limit, here we instead examine electromagnetic waves whose wavefronts remain planar, but whose normal one-form
d\phi\propto\mathfrak{n}
is no longer null (
\mathfrak{n}^{2}\neq 0
). This means we can still choose to maintain
eA_{\mu}(x)=\delta_{\mu}^{\perp}a_{\perp}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})
as transverse and depending on a single combination of coordinates,
x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}\equiv\mathfrak{n}\cdot x
, but relax the condition that this be a null direction. To describe this generalisation of a plane wave background field, it will be convenient to retain a reference null vector,
n\cdot a=0
, defined such that in the limit
\mathfrak{n}^{2}\rightarrow 0
, we recover
\mathfrak{n}\rightarrow n
. This ensures that the background can be viewed as a well-defined deformation of a given plane wave. Our overall aim is then to extend the first-quantised worldline formulation of QFT to this class of backgrounds and to obtain Bern–Kosower–type master formulae for scattering amplitudes in these non-null background fields, building on existing developments for the plane-wave case.

To describe our deformation of the plane wave fields in a covariant way, it is useful to introduce a complementary null vector,
\bar{n}
with
\bar{n}^{2}=0
, normalised such that
n\cdot\bar{n}=2
. This allows us to define the corresponding light-cone coordinate
x^{-}\equiv\bar{n}\cdot x
. For example, we may choose a coordinate system where
n_{\mu}=(1,0,0,1)
and
\bar{n}_{\mu}=(1,0,0,-1)
. We can then express our non-null direction as
x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}=\mathfrak{n}\cdot x
, with

\displaystyle\mathfrak{n}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=n_{\mu}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\bar{n}_{\mu}\,,\qquad\rho^{2}\equiv\mathfrak{n}^{2}\,,

(5)

which further leads to
\mathfrak{n}\cdot n=\rho^{2}/2
and
\mathfrak{n}\cdot\bar{n}=2
.
In the choice of coordinate system suggested above we then have
\mathfrak{n}_{\mu}=(1+\rho^{2}/4,0,0,1-\rho^{2}/4)
. As such,
\rho^{2}
(which can be positive or negative) serves as the deformation parameter away from a plane wave field, which satisfies
\lim_{\rho^{2}\rightarrow 0}\mathfrak{n}_{\mu}(\rho^{2})=n_{\mu}
. Note additionally that
\rho^{2}\rightarrow\infty
also corresponds to a plane-wave limit, although the normal one-form for the wavefronts, in this limit, is proportional to
\bar{n}
rather than
n
. Therefore, without loss of generality, we can restrict
-4\leq\rho^{2}\leq 4
and still cover the full space of deformations, as going outside of this range is equivalent to relabeling
n\longleftrightarrow\bar{n}
. Consequently,
|\rho^{2}/4|
is bounded by unity, providing a well-defined deformation of the underlying plane wave.

To illustrate the above characterisation of our deformed plane-wave backgrounds, consider, as an example, transverse plane waves in a homogeneous cold plasma. The Maxwell equation relevant to this case (see, e.g.,
19
) can be reduced to

\displaystyle\partial^{2}A_{\perp}-\Omega^{2}_{p}A_{\perp}=0\,,\quad n\cdot A=\bar{n}\cdot A=\partial^{\perp}A_{\perp}=0\,,

(6)

where
\Omega_{p}
is the plasma frequency. The above equation can be solved exactly, for instance, by

\displaystyle eA_{\perp}(x^{+},x^{-})=\frac{eE_{0}}{\Omega}\sin\left[\Omega\left(x^{+}+\frac{\Omega_{p}^{2}}{4\Omega^{2}}x^{-}\right)\right]\epsilon_{\perp}\,,

(7)

where
E_{0}
is a constant and
\epsilon_{\perp}
is a constant transverse polarisation vector. Comparing the above to the general form
eA_{\mu}(x)=\delta_{\mu}^{\perp}a_{\perp}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})
, we recognise that the field varies along a non-null direction of the same class, with the deformation parameter given explicitly by
\rho^{2}=\Omega^{2}_{p}/\Omega^{2}
. Then
\rho^{2}\ll 1
limit, in this context, translates to the case of the transverse wave’s frequency
\Omega
, in the rest frame of the plasma, far exceeding the plasma-frequency
\Omega_{p}
.

Denoting the phase-momentum of the background field as
\omega_{\mu}=\Omega\,\mathfrak{n}_{\mu}
, the parameter
\rho^{2}
quantifies the
smallness
of
\omega^{2}
. However, this notion of smallness is not unique, since the physical problem at hand may involve several other dimensionful parameters that can also set relevant scales. In the example above, denoting the four-velocity of plasma by
u^{\mu}
, the relevant smallness-condition may be stated covariantly as

\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{2}}{(\omega\cdot u)^{2}}\sim\rho^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{4})\ll 1\,.

(8)

However, as noted for example in
71
, a careful analysis of the corrections to the Volkov wavefunction in such non-null fields leads to a less restrictive criterion
54
, namely

\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega\cdot p}\ll 1\,,

(9)

where
p_{\mu}
is the typical momentum of the charged probe particle, and in which case it is natural to choose
\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega\cdot p}\sim\left(\frac{\Omega}{p^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}}\right)\rho^{2}
as the expansion parameter. Another possible small parameter emerges from an ultra-relativistic-type approximation in which

\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{2}m^{2}}{(\omega\cdot p)^{2}}\ll 1\,,

(10)

corresponding to the expansion parameter
\frac{\omega^{2}m^{2}}{(\omega\cdot p)^{2}}\sim\frac{m^{2}}{p^{+\,2}}\rho^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{4})\,
.

In all of these cases, although the specific expansion parameters may vary,
\rho^{2}
conveniently serves as a bookkeeping parameter for the expansion. Therefore, to cover a wider range of physical situations within our class of non-null plane waves, we formally treat
\rho^{2}
as the expansion parameter; the correct physical scaling can always be reinstated at the end of the analysis through an appropriate rescaling of
\rho^{2}
(i.e.,
\rho^{2}\rightarrow\left(\frac{\Omega}{p^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}}\right)\rho^{2}
,
\rho^{2}\rightarrow\frac{m^{2}}{p^{+\,2}}\rho^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{4})\,
, and so-on, as the case may be). This approach allows us, for example, to remain agnostic about the precise notion of smallness and instead focus on the strong-field QED consequences for scattering processes with arbitrary photon multiplicity in backgrounds of the form

eA_{\mu}(x)=\delta_{\mu}^{\perp}a_{\perp}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})\,.

We will also have the opportunity to provide a
worldline-centric
perspective on what constitutes a small parameter and show how this relates to the physical, spacetime quantities discussed above.

II.1
Non-null coordinates and fields

Analogous to how light-front coordinates simplify calculations in a plane-wave background, aligning one coordinate with
x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}
renders computations in the deformed plane-wave background considerably more tractable. Specifically, it is convenient to change the coordinates to

(x^{+},x^{-},\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp})\rightarrow(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}\,,x^{-},\boldsymbol{x}^{\perp})\,,

(11)

where the “minus” and transverse coordinates are carried over from the (limiting) plane-wave case (so
x^{-}=\bar{n}\cdot x
and
\boldsymbol{x^{\perp}}=(x^{1},x^{2})
). This coordinate choice offers a notable simplification in the worldline path-integral approach. As we present below, the kinematic term in the worldline action arises from the line-element, which in these coordinates is given by

ds^{2}=dx^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}dx^{-}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}(dx^{-})^{2}-dx^{\perp}\cdot dx^{\perp}\,,

(12)

or equivalently, from the Minkowski metric written in this non-standard coordinate chart,

\eta_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathfrak{n}_{\mu}\bar{n}_{\nu}+\mathfrak{n}_{\mu}\bar{n}_{\nu})-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\bar{n}_{\mu}\bar{n}_{\nu}-\sum_{i=1}^{2}(\epsilon^{\perp}_{i\mu}\epsilon^{\perp}_{i\nu})\,,

(13)

where
\epsilon^{\perp}_{i}
denotes the two orthonormal unit transverse vectors. As we will use these coordinates throughout, let us briefly examine how our characteristic vectors defining the electromagnetic field are represented in this coordinate system. The following components can be straightforwardly derived:

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{n}_{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}},\mathfrak{n}_{-},\mathfrak{n}_{\perp})

\displaystyle=(1\,,0,0)\quad;\quad(\mathfrak{n}^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}},\mathfrak{n}^{-},\mathfrak{n}^{\perp})=(\rho^{2}\,,2,0)\,,

(14)

\displaystyle(\bar{n}_{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}},\bar{n}_{-},\bar{n}_{\perp})

\displaystyle=(0,1,0)\quad;\quad(\bar{n}^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}},\bar{n}^{-},\bar{n}^{\perp})=(2,0,0)\,,

from which the non-zero components of the inverse-metric can be found to be
\eta^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}-}=\eta^{-\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}=4

\eta^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}=\rho^{2}
and
\eta^{ij}=-\delta^{i}_{j}
, where
i=1,2
indicate the transverse coordinates. For an arbitrary four vector,
p
, with the contravariant and covariant components given respectively by
p^{\mu}=(p^{0},p^{1},p^{2},p^{3})
and
p_{\mu}=(p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})
in the standard Minkowski coordinates, the corresponding components in the
non-null
coordinate system are given in terms of the familiar components adapted to the null frame by

\displaystyle(p^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}},p^{-})

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{n}\cdot p,\bar{n}\cdot p)=\left(p^{+}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}p^{-},p^{0}-p^{3}\right)\,,

(15)

\displaystyle(p_{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}},p_{-})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}p^{-},\frac{1}{2}p^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}p^{-}\right)=\left(p_{+},\frac{1}{2}p^{+}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{8}p^{-}\right)=\left(p_{+},\frac{1}{2}p^{+}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}p_{+}\right)\,,

(16)

where
p^{+}=n\cdot p
and
p^{-}=\bar{n}\cdot p
etc. The mass shell condition in the non-null coordinates then takes the form

p^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}=\frac{\mathbf{p}^{2}+m^{2}}{p^{-}}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}p^{-}\,,

(17)

or indeed
p^{-}=\frac{2}{\rho^{2}}\Big(p^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}-\sqrt{p^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}2}-\rho^{2}(\mathbf{p}^{2}+m^{2})}\Big)
, being the solution with the correct limit as
\rho\to 0
.

With the coordinates and conventions established, we now return to a discussion of the background field. As mentioned above, the choice of dependence for the gauge potential (
eA_{\mu}(x)=\delta_{\mu}^{\perp}a_{\perp}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})
, absorbing the electromagnetic coupling to the background into
a_{\perp}
) leads to a one-parameter family of fields deforming the plane waves. The corresponding physical fields remain transverse, with the field strength tensor now taking the form

\displaystyle f_{\mu\nu}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})

\displaystyle=\left(n_{\mu}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\bar{n}_{\mu}\right)a^{\prime}_{\nu}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})-\left(n_{\nu}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\bar{n}_{\nu}\right)a^{\prime}_{\mu}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})

(18)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{n}_{\mu}a^{\prime}_{\nu}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})-\mathfrak{n}_{\nu}a^{\prime}_{\mu}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})

(19)

As such the field invariants are now

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\rho^{2}a^{\prime 2}

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(21)

so that the electric and magnetic fields remain orthogonal but are no longer of equal magnitude. In fact we can give closed formulae for arbitrary products of
f
with itself:

\displaystyle f^{2k}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\big(\rho^{2}a_{\perp}^{\prime 2}\big)^{k-1}f^{2}_{\mu\nu}

(22)

\displaystyle f^{2k-1}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\big(\rho^{2}a_{\perp}^{\prime 2}\big)^{k-1}f_{\mu\nu}

(23)

where
f^{2}_{\mu\nu}=(n_{\mu}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\bar{n}_{\mu})(n_{\nu}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\bar{n}_{\nu})a_{\perp}^{\prime 2}-\rho^{2}a^{\prime}_{\mu}a^{\prime}_{\nu}
.

At the level of the physical fields, in the transverse subspace, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\perp}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})

\displaystyle=\big(1+\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\big)\big(a_{1}^{\prime}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}),a_{2}^{\prime}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})\big)

(24)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\perp}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})

\displaystyle=\big(1-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\big)\big(a_{2}^{\prime}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}),-a_{1}^{\prime}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})\big)

(25)

so that

\frac{\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}}{|\mathbf{E}||{\mathbf{B}|}}=0\,,\qquad\qquad\frac{|\mathbf{E}|^{2}-|\mathbf{B}|^{2}}{|\mathbf{E}||\mathbf{B}|}=\frac{\rho^{2}}{1-\big(\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\big)^{2}}\,.

(26)

The second equality above establishes a field-centric interpretation of the deformation parameter
\rho^{2}
. Note that the expression on the right-hand side acts as a bijection in the range
-4\leq\rho^{2}\leq 4
which, as we already established, is enough to describe all possible deformations, modulo a coordinate change
1
1

1

We note in passing that the right-hand-side is invariant under
\rho^{2}\rightarrow-16\rho^{-2}
, under which
[-4,4]
maps to
(-\infty,-4]\cup[4,\infty)
.
.
The fields are constant along lines of fixed
x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}
, pointing in the
(\bar{n}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}n)
-direction, and these can be spacelike (
\rho^{2}>0
) or timelike (
\rho^{2}<0
). As deformations of the plane wave fields, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{E}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})&=\mathbf{E}_{0}(x^{+})+\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\big(\mathbf{E}_{0}(x^{+})+x^{-}\mathbf{E}_{0}^{\prime}(x^{+})\big)+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{4})\,\\
\mathbf{B}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})&=\mathbf{B}_{0}(x^{+})-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\big(\mathbf{B}_{0}(x^{+})-x^{-}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{\prime}(x^{+})\big)+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{4})\,,\end{split}

(27)

where the fields labeled with argument
x^{+}
and subscript zero are exactly those that would appear in the plane wave case. Hence, we can also interpret our non-null parameter,
\rho^{2}
, as characterising the mixing of the two light-front directions in the electromagnetic fields and their difference in squared magnitude. In this sense, the chosen coordinate dependence of the field allows us to relax the strict vanishing of field invariants assumed by plane waves by allowing a non-zero difference in the fields’ magnitudes and include all orders of derivative corrections relative to the constant field case (within the chosen family of fields). Let us also point out that the fields above establish that our deformation does not just include higher-derivative corrections but also a variation in the zeroth-order fields in order to satisfy the non-vanishing field invariant.

To illustrate this, we give the example, which we will return to again below, of constant crossed fields. For a plane wave such fields are described by
a_{\mu}(x^{+})=a_{0}\delta_{\mu\perp}x^{+}
so that with respect to a standard coordinate system
\mathbf{E}=a_{0}(1,1,0)
and
\mathbf{B}=a_{0}(1,-1,0)
(constant, orthogonal fields of equal magnitude, which is the
\rho^{2}\to 0

x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}

\Omega\rightarrow 0
limit (keeping
\rho^{2}=\Omega_{p}^{2}/\Omega^{2}
fixed) of (

a_{\mu}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})=a_{0}\delta_{\mu\perp}x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}=a_{0}\delta_{\mu\perp}x^{+}+\rho^{2}/4a_{0}\delta_{\mu\perp}x^{-}
, the same potential can also be thought of as describing the field of two counter propagating plane waves in the CCF limit. So with
a_{\mu}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})=a_{0}\delta_{\mu\perp}x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{E}&=a_{0}\big(1+\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\big)(1,1,0)\,\\
\mathbf{B}&=a_{0}\big(1-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}\big)(1,-1,0)\,.\end{split}

(28)

This deviation from the CCF has important physical consequences – for instance, unlike the null plane wave, the non-null field can generate pairs via the Schwinger effect, which manifests, for example, in a nonzero imaginary part of the effective action (see below).

Before proceeding, let us note that the above discussion presented the fields in a fixed but arbitrary reference frame. However, it is known

, it may be convenient to Lorentz transform to a frame in which the field takes a specific form. Consider first the case that

\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}_{\mu}=(\rho,0,0,0)
. The electromagnetic field is then a spatially homogeneous but time-dependent electric field (“electric case”). If, on the other hand,
\rho^{2}<0
there is a Lorentz transformation (along
x^{3}
,
\gamma=\frac{1-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}{|\rho|}
) such that
\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}_{\mu}=(0,0,0,\rho)
is purely spacelike and the electromagnetic field is static but spatially varying magnetic field (‘‘magnetic case’’)
2
2

2

The field for
\rho^{2}>0
is in the same Lorentz class of fields as that of a plane wave propagating through a medium with refractive index
\textrm{n}<1
; for
\rho^{2}<0

. This classification will be useful when we discuss the Schwinger effect in our non-null fields below.

In the following section, we present a systematic framework for analysing such effects in our class of non-null deformed plane waves, by examining how the photon-dressed amplitudes are modified at both tree and loop level. We do this by forming the worldline representations of the matter propagator and the one-loop effective action dressed by both the non-null background field and external scattering photons.

III
Scalar QED in Non-Null Backgrounds

Having established the class of non-null plane-wave backgrounds and fixed our conventions, we now turn to the analysis of scattering processes in such fields. As outlined in the Introduction, our primary tool will be the worldline path-integral formalism, which provides a unified framework for treating both propagators and amplitudes in background-field QED, and treats scalar and spinor matter fields in a more similar way. In this section, we focus on scalar QED and we begin by reviewing the worldline approach to calculating the “
N
-photon-dressed propagator” and “
N
-photon-dressed effective action” in the presence of an additional semi-classical electromagnetic background defined by the vector potential
eA_{\mu}(x)=a_{\mu}(x)

N
(already LSZ-truncated) photon lines and upon LSZ reduction yields the tree-level
N+2

N
-photons with two external matter legs. The effective action corresponds to the one-loop
N
-particle scattering amplitude of the
N
-photons mediated by a virtual matter loop. In
22
, building on
8
;
4
;
2
(respectively
88
;
42
;
41
, building on
33
;
87
), worldline representations for these objects in an exactly plane wave background were derived. In particular, the condition
n^{2}=0
implied particular simplifications that allowed for the analytic calculation of the worldline path integral. Here we return to the derivation of this result and show how the method can be systematically adapted to go beyond the null-field limit.

III.1
N-photon dressed propagator and effective action

The worldline representation of the matter propagator in scalar QED, as presented in
22
, is the following path integral over open worldlines propagating between fixed endpoints in arbitrary proper time:

\mathcal{D}^{x^{\prime}x}[A]=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}T\,\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}T}\,\int^{x(T)=x^{\prime}}_{x(0)=x}\hskip-15.00002pt\mathcal{D}x(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{iS[x(\tau),A]}\;,

(29)

where
S[x(\tau),A]
denotes the action for a particle worldline,
x^{\mu}(\tau)
, propagating in propertime
T
, minimally coupled to the gauge field
A_{\mu}
, and is given by

S[x(\tau),A]=-\int_{0}^{T}\!{\mathrm{d}}\tau\,\Big[\frac{\dot{x}^{2}}{4}+e{A(x(\tau))\cdot\dot{x}(\tau)}\Big]\;,

(30)

with dots denoting propertime differentiation, i.e.
\dot{x}(\tau)\coloneqq\tfrac{d}{d\tau}x(\tau)
. Similarly, as in
41
, a worldline representation of the one-loop effective action (here adapted to Minkowski signature) involves a path integral over closed worldlines

\Gamma[A]=-i\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}T}{T}\,\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}T}\,\int_{x(0)=x(T)}\hskip-15.00002pt\mathcal{D}x(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{iS[x(\tau),A]}\,.

(31)

Equations (
29
)–(
31
) make clear that the worldline formalism naturally accommodates both open and closed trajectories, depending on whether one is considering tree-or loop-level objects (and the formalism extends to higher loop order
40
;
89
;
47
;
36
). Moreover, this structure allows us to systematically incorporate background fields and scattering photons by splitting the gauge potential and treating interactions in a controlled expansion, as follows.

In our cases of interest, the gauge potential,
A_{\mu}(x)
is decomposed as
eA_{\mu}(x)=a_{\mu}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})+eA_{\mu}^{\gamma}(x)
, in which
a_{\mu}
is the non-null plane wave background field as defined in Section
II
, for which the coordinate dependence enters solely through
\mathfrak{n}\cdot x=x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}
and
\mathfrak{n}\cdot a=0
, and
A_{\mu}^{\gamma}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i\mu}\mathrm{e}^{ik_{i}\cdot x}
represents the external (scattering) photons. The interaction with the scattering photons is then treated by expanding to multi-linear order in the photon polarisation vectors, leading to the
N
-photon dressed propagator and effective action in the background:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{x^{\prime}x}_{N}[a]

\displaystyle=(-ie)^{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}T\,\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}T}\,\int^{x(T)=x^{\prime}}_{x(0)=x}\hskip-15.00002pt\mathcal{D}x(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{iS[x(\tau),A]}\prod_{i=1}^{N}V^{x^{\prime}x}[\varepsilon_{i},k_{i}]\;,

(32)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N}[a]

\displaystyle=-i(-ie)^{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}T}{T}\,\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}T}\,\int_{x(0)=x(T)}\hskip-15.00002pt\mathcal{D}x(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{iS[x(\tau),A]}\prod_{i=1}^{N}V^{x^{\prime}x}[\varepsilon_{i},k_{i}]\;,

(33)

where the scattering photons are represented by the vertex operator (as in string theory)

V^{x^{\prime}x}[\varepsilon,k]\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\,\varepsilon\cdot\dot{x}(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{ik\cdot x(\tau)}\,.

(34)

The presence of the vertex operators seems to spoil the otherwise standard structure of the path integral. To return to a more amenable path integral, we first exponentiate the prefactors in the vertex operators as

V^{x^{\prime}x}[\varepsilon,k]=\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\,\mathrm{e}^{ik\cdot x(\tau)+\varepsilon\cdot\dot{x}(\tau)}\Big|_{\textrm{lin}\,N}\,\,,

(35)

where the instruction
\textrm{lin}\,N
simply means that the result should be projected onto the contribution linear in
\varepsilon
. From a path integral viewpoint, this exponentiated representation incorporates the interaction with the
N
-photons simply by adding a linear source term
-\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{J}_{\rm(0)}(\tau)\cdot x(\tau)d\tau
, directly into the action, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\mu}_{\rm(0)}(\tau)\equiv i\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(ik_{i}^{\mu}-\varepsilon_{i}^{\mu}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}\big)\delta(\tau-\tau_{i})

(36)

is supported at the insertion points of the photons along the worldline. Next, to facilitate the integral over
x(\tau)
, it is convenient to expand the worldline trajectories as
x(\tau)=x_{\rm cl.}(\tau)+q(\tau)
, where
x_{\rm cl.}(\tau)
is the classical solution to the worldline equations of motion and boundary conditions in
vacuum
, specifically

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\ddot{x}_{\rm cl.}^{\mu}(\tau)=\mathcal{J}^{\mu}_{\rm(0)}(\tau)\quad\&\quad\begin{cases}x_{\rm cl.}(0)=x,x(T)=x^{\prime}\quad&;\quad\textrm{(Line)}\,,\\
x_{\rm cl.}(0)=x(T)=x_{0}\quad&;\quad\textrm{(Loop)}\,.\end{cases}

(37)

For the open line, this classical solution is a straight line between the endpoints, and for the loop, we choose the solution corresponding to expansion about the loop centre of mass.

Finally, we note that for arbitrary gauge potential,
a_{\mu}(x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}})
, the path integral is not, apparently, Gaussian in
q^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}
. In order to deal with the functional integral over
q^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}
, we follow
88
and introduce two auxiliary worldline fields
\xi(\tau)
and
\chi(\tau)
such that the non-trivial interaction term can be rewritten as

\mathrm{e}^{-i\int{\mathrm{d}}\tau\,a(x_{\rm cl.}^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}(\tau)+q^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}(\tau))\cdot\dot{q}(\tau)}=\int\mathcal{D}\xi(\tau)\mathcal{D}\chi(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{i\int{\mathrm{d}}\tau\,\big[\chi(\tau)(\xi(\tau)-q^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}(\tau))-a(x_{\rm cl.}^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}(\tau)+\xi(\tau))\cdot\dot{q}(\tau)\big]}\,.

(38)

The point is that the path integral over
q(\tau)
is then Gaussian, so can be computed analytically. We now turn to the details of this calculation, beginning with the space-time representation of the
N
-photon dressed propagator.

III.1.1
N

For the open line, we have Dirichlet boundary conditions (DBC) on
q(\tau)

q(\tau)

\int_{DBC}\hskip-10.00002pt\mathcal{D}q(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{i\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}\tau\big[-\frac{\dot{q}^{2}}{4}-\delta\mathcal{J}\cdot q\big]}={-i(4\pi T)^{-2}}\,\exp\Big[-i\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}\tau{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}\,\delta\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(\tau)\Delta(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\delta\mathcal{J}^{\mu}(\tau^{\prime})\Big]\;,

It remains to integrate over
\chi(\tau)
and
\xi(\tau)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}d\tau d\tau^{\prime}\,\delta\mathcal{J}(\tau)_{\mu}\Delta(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\delta\mathcal{J}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\!{\mathrm{d}}\tau{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}\,a(x_{\rm cl.}^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}(\tau)+\xi(\tau))\cdot a(x_{\rm cl.}^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}(\tau^{\prime})+\xi(\tau^{\prime})){}^{\bullet}\!\Delta^{\hskip-1.42262pt\bullet}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})+\rho^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\!{\mathrm{d}}\tau{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}\,\chi(\tau)\chi(\tau^{\prime})\Delta(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

(41)

in which
{}^{\bullet}\!\Delta_{ij}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau_{i}}\Delta_{ij}
,

etc – notation defined in
23

. This replacement occurs repeatedly in what follows, making it central to the structure of the deformation from nullness, and thus it is convenient to introduce a compact notation. Given a functional
\mathcal{F}[a]=\mathcal{F}[a(x_{\rm cl.}^{+}(\tau))]
, we denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[a(\bullet+\xi)]\equiv\mathcal{F}[a(x_{\rm cl.}^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}(\tau)+\xi(\tau))]\,.

(42)

By contrast, the second term in (

One may directly evaluate the path integral

III.1.2
N
-photon dressed propagator: momentum-space representation

The preceding discussion has outlined the techniques required to handle worldline path integrals in a non-null plane-wave background, focusing on the space-time representation of the
N
-photon dressed propagator
\mathcal{D}^{x^{\prime}x}_{N}[a]
. For the computation of scattering amplitudes—which are ultimately the quantities of interest—it is more natural to work in momentum space. We therefore turn next to
\mathcal{D}^{p^{\prime}p}_{N}[a]
, the momentum-space representation of the
N
-photon dressed propagator, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{p^{\prime}p}_{N}[a]=\int d^{4}x\,d^{4}x^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{ip^{\prime}\cdot x-ip\cdot x}\,\mathcal{D}^{x^{\prime}x}_{N}[a]\,.

(49)

A direct, brute-force approach to computing
\mathcal{D}^{p^{\prime}p}_{N}[a]
would be to evaluate the Fourier transform above. In a non-null background, however, performing these Fourier integrals is considerably more subtle than in the plane-wave case, and, moreover, the intermediate steps are largely superfluous. For this reason, we delegate the explicit calculation to Appendix
B
for completeness.

Alternatively, and more elegantly, we can instead perform the worldline path integral with a mixed-boundary condition to first compute
\mathcal{D}^{p^{\prime}x}_{N}[a]
so that the desired momentum space propagator can be written in the form
\mathcal{D}^{p^{\prime}p}_{N}[a]=\int\mathrm{e}^{-ip\cdot x}\mathcal{D}^{p^{\prime}x}_{N}[a]d^{4}x
, in direct analogy with the approach outlined in
22
. Both approaches ultimately lead to the representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{N}^{p^{\prime}p}[a]=i(-ie)^{N}\int d^{4}x\int_{0}^{\infty}dT\int\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\tau_{i}\,\,\,\mathrm{e}^{i\rho^{2}\int{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime\prime}\tilde{\Delta}(\tau^{\prime},\tau^{\prime\prime})\frac{\delta}{\delta\xi(\tau^{\prime})}\frac{\delta}{\delta\xi(\tau^{\prime\prime})}}\mathrm{e}^{i\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm tr.}_{\rm pw.}[a(\bullet+\xi)]}\Big|_{\mathrm{lin}\,N}^{\xi(\tau)\rightarrow 0}\,,

(50)

where the worldline Green’s function with mixed boundary conditions is this time
23

\tilde{\Delta}_{ij}\coloneqq\tilde{\Delta}(\tau_{i},\tau_{j})=\frac{1}{2}|\tau_{i}-\tau_{j}|-\frac{1}{2}(\tau_{i}+\tau_{j})\,,

(51)

and the exponential is changed to (recall that the plane wave path integral is semi-classically exact)

\displaystyle i\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm tr.}_{\rm pw.}[a]=i\int_{0}^{T}\left(P_{N}\cdot P_{N}-m^{2}\right)d\tau-i(p^{\prime}+K-p)\cdot x

(52)

with the dressed worldline momentum given by

\displaystyle P_{N}=\tilde{p}^{\prime}-a(n\cdot\tilde{x}_{\rm cl.}(\tau))-\sum_{i=1}^{N}[{}^{\bullet\!}\tilde{\Delta}^{\!\bullet}(\tau,\tau_{i})\varepsilon_{i}^{\mu}+i\,{}^{\bullet\!}\tilde{\Delta}(\tau,\tau_{i})k_{i}^{\mu}]\,

(53)

and the appropriate classical solution
\tilde{x}_{\rm cl.}(\tau)
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{\rm cl.}^{\mu}(\tau)=x^{\mu}+2p^{\prime\mu}\tau-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[|\tau-\tau_{i}|-(\tau+\tau_{i})\right]k^{\mu}_{i}-2i\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Theta(\tau-\tau_{i})\epsilon^{\mu}_{i}\,.

(54)

While obvious, we also emphasis that the transformation
a(\bullet+\xi)
in this context translates to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[a(\bullet+\xi)]=\mathcal{F}[a(\tilde{x}_{\rm cl.}^{\Delta}+\xi)]\,.

(55)

Interestingly, when viewed in light of the spacetime representation (
46
) discussed earlier, the momentum-space expression in (
50
) takes precisely the form one would naturally expect once the change in boundary conditions of the worldline path integral is properly accounted for.

III.1.3
N
-photon dressed effective action

For closed trajectories,
q(\tau)
is periodic and the expansion about the loop center of mass (
x_{0}:=\langle\!\langle x\rangle\!\rangle
) requires that it integrate to zero (“string inspired” boundary conditions: SI), and we have

\int_{SI}\hskip-2.5pt\mathcal{D}q(\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{i\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}\tau\big[-\frac{\dot{q}^{2}}{4}-\delta\mathcal{J}\cdot q\big]}={-i(4\pi T)^{-2}}\,\exp\Big[-\frac{i}{2}\int_{0}^{T}{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{i}{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{j}\,\delta\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(\tau_{i})G_{ij}\delta\mathcal{J}^{\mu}(\tau_{j})\Big]\;,

(56)

where now the (bosonic) worldline Green function is

G_{ij}\coloneqq G(\tau_{i},\tau_{j})=|\tau_{i}-\tau_{j}|-\frac{(\tau_{i}-\tau_{j})^{2}}{T}-\frac{T}{6}\,.

(57)

Then, repeating the same steps as for the propagator, we arrive at a similar replacement

a_{\mu}\big(x_{\rm cl.}^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}+\xi(\tau)\big)\longrightarrow a_{\mu}(\tau):=a_{\mu}\big(x_{\rm cl.}^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}(\tau)\big)\,,

(58)

where this time the classical solution is given by

\displaystyle x_{\rm cl.}^{\mu}(\tau)=x_{0}^{\mu}-i\sum_{j=1}^{N}[\dot{G}(\tau,\tau_{j})\varepsilon_{j}^{\mu}-iG(\tau,\tau_{j})k_{j}^{\mu}]\,,

(59)

and integrating over the position of the loop centre of mass, one finds that the path integral for the scalar effective action evaluates to

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N}[a]=(-ie)^{N}\int d^{4}x_{0}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dT}{T}(4\pi iT)^{-2}\int_{0}^{T}\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\tau_{i}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{i}{2}\rho^{2}\int{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}\tau^{\prime\prime}\,G(\tau^{\prime},\tau^{\prime\prime})\frac{\delta}{\delta\xi(\tau^{\prime})}\frac{\delta}{\delta\xi(\tau^{\prime\prime})}}\mathrm{e}^{i\mathcal{S}^{\rm lp.}_{\rm pw.}[a(\bullet+\xi)]}\Big|_{\textrm{lin}\,N}^{\xi(\tau)\rightarrow 0}\,.

(60)

Here
\mathcal{S}^{\rm lp.}_{\rm pw.}[a]

\displaystyle i\mathcal{S}^{\rm lp.}_{\rm pw.}[a]

\displaystyle=-iM^{2}(a)T+i\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}\cdot x_{0}+2\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big[\big(\langle\!\langle a\rangle\!\rangle-a(\tau_{j})\big)\cdot\varepsilon_{j}+i\big(I(\tau_{j})-\langle\!\langle I\rangle\!\rangle\big)\cdot k_{j}\big]

(61)

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}[G_{ij}k_{i}\cdot k_{j}-2i\dot{G}_{ij}\varepsilon_{i}\cdot k_{j}+\ddot{G}_{ij}\varepsilon_{i}\cdot\varepsilon_{j}]\,.

\displaystyle i\mathcal{S}^{\rm lp.}_{\rm pw.}[a]=i\int_{0}^{T}\left(Q_{N}\cdot Q_{N}-m^{2}\right)d\tau-iK\cdot x_{0}

(62)

\displaystyle Q^{\mu}_{N}(\tau)

(63)

\int d^{4}x_{0}\,\mathrm{e}^{iK\cdot x_{0}}=\hat{\delta}(K_{-})\hat{\delta}^{2}(\mathbf{K})\frac{1}{2}\int dx_{0}^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{iK_{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}x_{0}^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}}\,,

(64)

although now
\delta(K_{-})\sim\delta(K^{+}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}K^{-})
with

x^{\mathchoice{{\scalebox{0.6}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.575}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.55}{$\triangle$}}}{{\scalebox{0.4}{$\triangle$}}}}

To summarise, a unified picture emerges for both the photon-dressed propagator and the photon-dressed effective action in a non-null plane-wave background. To make this structure explicit, we introduce the abstract notation
\mathbb{X}_{N}[a]

III.2
Multi-photon tree-level scattering: LSZ, amplitudes and wavefunction

The above analysis, while treating the non-null field exactly, does so at the expense of expressing the dressed propagator and effective action in functional derivative form. To arrive at expressions more amenable to phenomenological applications, we now turn to a resummed expansion in the non-null parameter,
\rho^{2}
. Led by our success in treating the gauge field’s dependence on
\rho^{2}
through its argument
exactly

\rho^{2}

We will later return to the issue of the physical interpretation of the small parameter
\rho^{2}
and to the question of how it is rendered dimensionless so as to justify such an expansion; it suffices to note – and we will demonstrate this explicitly in what follows – that the coupling of the background and the photons to the classical worldline velocity,

\rho^{2}/|\dot{x}_{\textrm{cl.}}(\tau)|

The appropriate object

In this compact worldline representation, the amplitude encodes tree-level scattering with an arbitrary number

While this concludes our main results for the case of open worldlines, we now briefly return to the question of the formal expansion parameter, before moving to the discussion at one loop order.

III.2.1
\rho^{2}

Going through the conversion from the propertime representation to spacetime coordinates, while cumbersome, serves the dual purpose of providing a check on the wavefunction and non-linear Compton scattering, and furnishing us with a reliable expansion parameter. Since

IV
Spinor QED in Non-Null Backgrounds

The spinor QED version of the preceding discussion proceeds in a largely analogous manner, with the new feature being the coupling of spin degrees of freedom to the background field (and, of course, the external photons as usual). We incorporate these effects within the worldline formalism by introducing Grassmann variables. In particular, one finds that the worldline path integrals in non-null backgrounds again give rise to exact formal relations analogous to (
65
), for appropriately defined quantities. However, explicitly deriving the spinor analogue of (
65
) would largely amount to a repetition of the steps already carried out in the scalar case. Accordingly, in this section, we focus on highlighting the spin-dependent technical aspects, rather than reproducing the direct analogue of (
65
) step by step. For concreteness, we illustrate these points explicitly using the leading-order non-null corrections to both one-loop and tree-level quantities in spinor QED.

V
Conclusions

In this work, we have developed a systematic framework for describing scattering processes in strong-field QED in the presence of non-null plane-fronted electromagnetic backgrounds, i.e., backgrounds whose wavefronts remain planar but whose phase direction satisfies
\mathfrak{n}^{2}\neq 0
. Such fields arise naturally as effective descriptions of laser propagation in dispersive media, for instance, plasmas, and therefore provide a controlled step beyond the idealised vacuum plane-wave backgrounds that underpin much of the existing strong-field QED literature.

Our central technical result is the construction of
N
-photon–dressed master formulae for both tree-level propagators and one-loop effective actions in scalar and spinor QED, valid to all orders in the background field and systematically expandable in the non-nullness parameter
\rho^{2}=\mathfrak{n}^{2}
. By exploiting the worldline path-integral formalism, we were able to retain the non-perturbative resummation characteristic of plane-wave (Volkov) physics while incorporating deviations from null propagation as controlled insertions acting on corresponding plane-wave-like expressions. In this way, the familiar plane-wave results emerge smoothly in the
\rho^{2}\to 0
limit, while dispersive effects are captured order by order beyond it.

A key conceptual feature of our approach is that results in the non-null background can be expressed entirely in terms of classical solutions to the worldline equations of motion in
vacuum
, a property shared with the case of exact plane-wave backgrounds. This structure naturally leads to both space-time and momentum-space representations of dressed propagators, as well as their LSZ-truncated amplitudes, thereby providing a unified description of scattering processes at both tree and loop level (see (
65
)). The fundamental reason behind this is the semi-classical exactness of the worldline path integral in our class of backgrounds.

As nontrivial checks on our framework, we have shown that known results are recovered in appropriate limits, including the exact plane-wave expressions and previously derived leading-order non-null corrected wavefunctions and scattering amplitudes. We also examined a special exactly solvable case of constant crossed fields with
\rho^{2}\neq 0
, illustrating how the non-null deformation leads to qualitative new physical effects – most notably the appearance of a non-vanishing imaginary part of the effective action, reflecting the (known) fact that pair production may no longer be forbidden once the null condition is relaxed.

The formalism developed here is also well-suited to extending the by now long-running efforts to find closed-form expressions for the low energy limit of the
N
-photon scattering amplitudes (see
11
;
10
;
67
;
38
;
70
). This limit corresponds to projecting onto the multi-linear contribution to the amplitudes in the external photon momenta,
k_{i}
, valid when their characteristic energy,
\omega_{i}/m\ll 1
, with
m
the mass of the matter particles represented by the line or loop. This is significant because it generally allows for the remaining worldline parameter integrals to be computed in closed form.

The approach presented opens several natural directions for further investigation. On the phenomenological side, it provides a systematic tool for assessing the impact of medium-induced dispersion and non-vanishing field invariants on strong-field processes such as nonlinear Compton scattering and Breit-Wheeler pair production in laser–plasma environments. One might also use this formalism, as it stands, to determine (at least perturbatively in
\rho^{2}
) the one-loop vacuum polarisation in our class of non-null fields and test whether the 1 particle reducible contributions to processes (known to be present in constant fields but corresponding simply to an additional renormalisation in a pure plane wave
7
) are relevant in a non-null background. On the theoretical side, it would be interesting to extend the present analysis to backgrounds with additional structure, such as slowly varying transverse profiles, finite-duration pulses, or focusing effects, using the plane-wave sector as an analytic core around which further deformations are organised. More broadly, our results contribute to an ongoing effort to bridge the gap between exactly solvable strong-field QED models and the increasingly realistic electromagnetic fields encountered in modern high-intensity experiments and those expected in the future.

Finally, there are no fundamental obstacles to going beyond one-loop order. The worldline formalism extends naturally to higher loop calculations
40
;
89
;
66
;
47
whilst retaining its favourable properties such as summing over sets of Feynman diagrams related by exchange of photon insertions around the loop. Here we would expect the worldline path integral to be computable in closed form, although the contributions arising from the expansion in
\rho^{2}
will no doubt be more complicated than their tree-level or one-loop counterparts presented here.

Appendix D
Action of Quadratic Differential Operators on Gaussian Functions

In this Appendix, we derive a useful identity for the action of the exponential of a quadratic differential operator on a Gaussian function. The functional generalisation of this result is used to arrive at (
91
).

D.1
The result

Let
A
and
B
be symmetric, positive-definite matrices. Then

\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}A_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-x^{T}\cdot B\cdot x}\Big)\Big|_{x=0}=\Big[\det\!\left(\frac{A^{-1}+2B}{A^{-1}}\right)\Big]^{-1/2}

(196)

D.2
Proof

We start with the ‘Fourier representation’ of the Gaussian operator:

\displaystyle\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2}A_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\Big)\,=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi A)}}\int\mathcal{D}q\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2}q^{T}A^{-1}q\Big)\exp\left(q_{i}\partial_{i}\right)\,.

(197)

Now applying the above operator on to
f(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-x^{T}Bx}
and using
\mathrm{e}^{q_{i}\partial_{i}}f(x)=f(x+q)
leads to

\displaystyle e^{\frac{1}{2}A_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-x^{T}Bx}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi A)}}\int\mathcal{D}q\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2}q^{T}A^{-1}q-(x+q)^{T}B(x+q)\Big).

(198)

Evaluating this expression at
x=0
yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}A_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-x^{T}Bx}\Big)\Big|_{x=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi A)}}\int\mathcal{D}q\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2}q^{T}(A^{-1}+2B)q\Big).

(199)

The remaining integral is a standard Gaussian integral, giving

\int\mathcal{D}q\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2}q^{T}(A^{-1}+2B)q\Big)=\Big[\det\Big(\frac{2\pi}{A^{-1}+2B}\Big)\Big]^{1/2}.

(200)

Combining terms, we arrive at

\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}A_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-x^{T}Bx}\Big)\Big|_{x=0}=\Big[\det\!\Big(\frac{A^{-1}+2B}{A^{-1}}\Big)\Big]^{-1/2},

(201)

which completes the proof.
