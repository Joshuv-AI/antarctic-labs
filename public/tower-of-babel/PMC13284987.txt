Use and characteristics of free/libre and open source electronic health records: an overview of reviews
Authors: Arthur Thives Mello, Daniel Henrique Scandolara, Júlia Meller Dias De Oliveira, Vanessa Pereira Corrêa, João Paulo Lucchetta Pompermaier, Francisco Martins Hoffmann, Miliane Dos Santos Fantonelli, Ianka Cristina Celuppi, Jades Fernando Hammes, Célio Luiz Cunha, Raul Sidnei Wazlawick, Eduardo Monguilhott Dalmarco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo synthesize and consolidate the existing evidence on the use and characteristics of Free/Libre and Open Source Software (FLOSS)-based electronic health records (EHRs) in global healthcare settings.Materials and MethodsWe conducted an overview of reviews, systematically searching 14 information sources using a peer-reviewed strategy. Pairs of reviewers independently and in duplicate screened studies, extracted predefined data items, and assessed methodological quality. Findings were synthesized thematically and categorized by use and system characteristics.ResultsNine systematic reviews (2012-2024) met the inclusion criteria. Methodological quality was generally low. We identified 26 themes related to use and 19 related to system characteristics. FLOSS-based EHRs were predominantly implemented in low- and middle-income countries, driven by cost advantages, adaptability, and community support. Reported challenges included organizational barriers, uncertainties about the capacity of volunteer-led development communities, limited comparative evidence with proprietary systems, and concerns over set-up, customization and training costs. System characteristics varied, especially regarding interoperability, security, and governance.DiscussionThe implementation of FLOSS-based EHRs appears to be influenced more by institutional and organizational factors than by technical features. Their adaptability supports use in resource-limited settings, but persistent challenges may raise concerns about long-term sustainability.ConclusionTo realize the potential of FLOSS-based EHRs, stakeholders must strengthen institutional readiness and governance structures, while the research community must improve the methodological quality of supporting evidence.

Background and significance

Health-system digitalization, driven by information technology, has transformed how clinical data are recorded, accessed, and used. Among the key milestones of this digital transformation are electronic health records (EHRs), which have contributed to improving the accuracy and efficiency of clinical data collection, the accessibility of patient information, and the continuity of care. These systems have also played a role in enhancing the quality of healthcare delivery and containing operational costs.1–5

Recognizing these benefits, the World Health Organization (WHO) endorses expanding health information systems as a global policy priority.6,7 However, the implementation of proprietary EHR systems entails considerable costs, which are often unaffordable in low and middle-income countries (LMICs).8,9 In response to these constraints, free/libre and open-source software (FLOSS) solutions have emerged as viable alternatives, offering not only economic advantages but also autonomy and greater adaptability to local contexts.10–14 This became evident during the West African Ebola outbreak, for instance, when the FLOSS-based EHR OpenMRS-Ebola was rapidly developed based on OpenMRS to support evolving clinical workflows under severe resource and biosafety constraints.15

Despite their potential, the widespread adoption of FLOSS EHRs remains limited due to technological, organizational, and socio-environmental barriers. Existing reviews16–20 have produced a fragmented and, at times, contradictory body of literature, possibly due to the diversity of methodological approaches, scopes, and contexts. Some reviews emphasize advantages in cost, while others highlight that set-up, customization and training can still entail high costs and strain local capacity. The variability of these barriers across countries and health systems underscores the importance of context-sensitive analysis.16

This heterogeneity hinders the clear synthesis of findings and complicates evidence-informed decision-making. To address this gap, the present study adopts an overview of reviews methodology (also referred to as an umbrella review), aiming to critically appraise, consolidate, and interpret the existing body of evidence on the topic.21

The primary objective of this review is to assess the use and characteristics of FLOSS EHRs in healthcare. In addition, it seeks to answer the following complementary questions: Are there differences in the use and characteristics of these systems across countries or specific software? What is the overall methodological quality of the reviews in this field? How does this quality affect the certainty of the available evidence? By addressing these questions, this review aims to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the current knowledge, identify critical gaps in the literature, and propose directions for future research and policy related to the use of open-source technologies in health information management.

Methods

The protocol for this overview of reviews was registered at the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/Y9QZG), before the beginning of data analysis. This article was written based on the PRIOR statement.22 All data associated with this project are available at the Open Science Framework project page (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/QS2GT).

Information sources

We searched 14 information sources, including MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase (Excerpta Medica Database), the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, LILACS (Literatura Latino-Americana e do Caribe em Ciências da Saúde), Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus, Compendex (Engineering Village), the ACM Digital Library, ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global, the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), the Open Science Framework (OSF) registry, and Google Scholar [https://scholar.google.com/] (first 100 results26). Additionally, we manually searched reference lists of included studies (backward and forward citation searching). The latest search was conducted on August 21, 2024. We also contacted subject matter experts on November 8, 2024 (File S1).

Search strategy

One researcher (ATM) developed the search strategy for MEDLINE (via PubMed) iteratively using the 2DSearch application. A second researcher (JMDO) peer-reviewed the strategy in accordance with the PRESS criteria.27 To maximize sensitivity, we used controlled vocabulary (MeSH/Emtree) and free-text terms, covering synonyms, acronyms, and spelling variants across population, context, and study-design domains.

To identify systematic reviews and other types of research syntheses, we applied a non-validated search filter developed by librarians at the University of Pittsburgh.28 The MEDLINE search strategy was adapted for the remaining databases using the Polyglot Translator (SR-Accelerator). We reviewed all controlled vocabulary mappings individually and adjusted terms when necessary to ensure appropriate translation and inclusion of relevant subject headings. We verified translations against each database’s syntax.

We hand-searched reference lists of included and related studies. Backward and forward citation searching was conducted to supplement the strategy. No language or date restrictions were applied. We also searched the PROSPERO and OSF registries to identify all pre-registered systematic reviews. The entire search process was documented to comply with the PRISMA-S reporting guidelines.29

All search strategies are available in File S1.

Selection process

Search results were imported into EndNote X9 (Clarivate, Philadelphia) by one researcher (JMDO), who removed exact duplicates. Another researcher (ATM) uploaded the deduplicated records to the Systematic Review Accelerator Online Platform, where flagged duplicates were manually assessed using the Deduplicator tool.30 We used Rayyan® (Qatar Computing Research Institute, Qatar) to manage the screening process.

Two researchers (ATM and DHS) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts in duplicate using predefined eligibility criteria, resolving disagreements by consensus or by involving a second pair of researchers.

We used the GROOVE tool31 to assess the degree of overlap between included reviews, including calculation of the CCA (corrected covered area) index and pairwise overlap. We classified overlap as slight (CCA < 5%), moderate (5%-10%), high (10%-15%), and very high (>15%). In cases of moderate to very high overlap, we considered including only the highest quality review to avoid duplication.32 We also verified whether reviews genuinely overlapped, as high CCA values may occur when reviews include the same studies but extract different data due to differing objectives.

Data collection process

One researcher (ATM) extracted data using a predefined Google Sheets spreadsheet, and a second (DHS) cross-checked it. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or expert consultation. All findings were extracted, regardless of overlap, due to their qualitative nature. For narrative data, direct quotes were flagged by ATM and reviewed by DHS. Concise summaries were provided when quotes were too lengthy. Main data items included aspects of EHR use (adoption, implementation, benefits, challenges) and characteristics (customization and extensibility, development community, integration and interoperability, licensing, localization and multilingual support, security and privacy, sustainability, updates, usability, and user experience). Contextual data (number and quality of contributing studies, relevant OS-EHRs and countries) were also recorded. Full data items are available in File S1.

Methodological quality assessment

We assessed the methodological quality of included reviews using the Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews and Research Syntheses.21 We considered the original nine domains in the tool, but created a total of 26 subitems to standardize the assessment in each domain. More detail on this process is presented in File S1. Two researchers (ATM and ICC) conducted all assessments independently and in duplicate, using a pre-piloted spreadsheet. We did not reassess primary studies, even when critical appraisal in the included reviews could be flawed, incomplete, or missing.

Assessment of meta-biases

We considered the risk of bias at the synthesis level. This included identifying pre-registered systematic reviews that were not ultimately published (potential publication bias) and comparing the registered protocols of included reviews to their final publications to identify deviations from the original methodology (eg, selective reporting).

Data synthesis

We synthesized the findings by summarizing previously synthesized evidence, without conducting re-analysis. We primarily used a narrative synthesis approach with tabular presentation. We created a summary of findings table that included the main results, the context of each finding (eg, relevant EHR systems and relevant countries), and either the methodological quality of the review or the certainty of the evidence (eg, GRADE) when reported.

Subgroup analyses

To answer our additional research questions, we explored differences in the use and characteristics of free/libre and open-source EHRs across countries. Countries were grouped by World Bank income classification because it is a proxy for structural constraints, and it is available for every country. We did not group findings by software type because this was not consistently reported within the included reviews. When possible, we restricted analyses to the highest-quality reviews (ie, those that met the most criteria in the critical appraisal tool).

Results

The systematic search of databases and registries resulted in 9903 records after removing duplicates. We also identified two additional records through citation searching. The full texts of all potentially eligible studies have been located and after confirming their eligibility, nine studies were included in this overview. Studies excluded at the full-text screening phase are described in the File S1. A PRISMA flowchart is presented in Figure 1.

Characteristics and methodological quality of the included reviews

In total, 200 primary studies were included in the nine reviews, 171 were included in only one review, 22 were included in two reviews, and seven were included in three reviews. Overall, there was only a slight overlap in the literature (CCA of 2.25%). A full report on the overlap among the included studies is presented in the File S1.

Table 2 shows the general characteristics of the nine included reviews. The full characteristics of the included studies are presented in File S2. The studies were published between 2012 and 2024. Web of Science (67%), PubMed (56%), Scopus (44%), MEDLINE (44%), and IEEE Xplore (44%) were the most common information sources. Most reviews focused exclusively on FLOSS-based EHRs, whereas the remainder adopted a broader scope or focused on contexts in which FLOSS-based EHRs are the most frequently used systems. Regarding context, the reviews mostly looked at LMICs or other low resource settings. The reviews were also quite diverse in their eligibility criteria, often focusing on papers published only in specific languages and on studies employing specific methodological approaches. In some cases, researchers limited the inclusion of studies to a specific set of systems or to a minimum number of systems. The number of included studies by the identified reviews ranged from ten to 80 studies. The reviews included studies that collectively identified 69 systems, with OpenMRS (89%), OSCAR (44%), GNU Health (44%), and OpenEMR (44%) being the most frequent. Regarding phenomena of interest, the included studies often focused on specific domains within the characteristics category, including most of the anticipated domains in the protocol, with only findings related to ethical standards, maintenance, performance, and scalability lacking.

The assessment of methodological quality is summarized in Figure 2. All studies presented substantial methodological shortcomings, namely: review questions not clearly and explicitly stated, sources for searching often missing important engineering-related databases and platforms, lack of critical appraisal of the included studies, and data extraction most likely performed by a single reviewer. Only two studies18,36 critically appraised the literature, and we are not sure the assessments were conducted independently and in duplicate by at least two reviewers. Aside from the criteria in the critical appraisal tool, none of the included reviews had a pre-registered protocol. The full assessments including justifications are presented in File S3.

The FLOSS-based EHR systems also showed benefits by allowing the access of LMICs to the general benefits of EHRs (five reviews; underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 8). The identified literature also indicated a number of challenges associated with the use of FLOSS-based EHRs, which are summarized in File S4. The challenges were often similar to general challenges faced by the implementation of EHRs, but a few seemed to be specific to the FLOSS-based nature or to the specific context of low resource settings.

The main challenges specific to FLOSS-based EHRs appear to be organizational barriers (two reviews; 29%-33% high quality studies or not available; synthesis 16), questioning the ability of often voluntary-based development communities to meet the implementation and support requirements of healthcare systems (one review, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 19). There are also differing views in the literature on whether the benefits of FLOSS-based EHRs are well substantiated with evidence, especially when compared to proprietary systems (one review, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 21). Cost was also mentioned as a factor, since FLOSS-based EHRs might still require a high cost of set-up and maintenance (two reviews; 22%-25% high quality studies; synthesis 9), besides the costs associated with often-needed in-house customization and training before full implementation (one review, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 25). Lastly, FLOSS-based EHRs, which are often based in development communities, might encounter resistance from senior management, which tends to be conservative in maintaining in-house teams (one review, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 20).

Characteristics

The integration and interoperability findings varied across reviews, focusing on compatibility, standards adopted, whether the systems were considered interoperable or not, and semantic interoperability. The systems were found to be compatible with various other systems and followed standards such as FHIR (HL-7), ANSI X12, and DICOM. In an older review, most of the relevant systems were found not to be interoperable (one review, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 29). The OpenEHR approach to semantic interoperability, featuring archetype formalism, was also a key finding, though it also faces challenges (two reviews, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 31).

On security and privacy (Table 4), features such as often-used encryption protocols and access control mechanisms were employed to protect patient data (four reviews, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 33). Additionally, some systems gave more control to patients over their own data (one review, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 32). However, weaknesses were also identified, including the failure of some systems to implement strong access control mechanisms and security vulnerabilities identified (two reviews, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 34).

Strengths and weaknesses were synthesized regarding customization and extensibility, localization and multilingual support, and the sustainability of the systems. Some systems offered significant customization, allowing healthcare organizations to adapt the software to their specific needs. Additionally, the extensibility of some systems was noted as a strength, with modular architectures enabling the addition of new features (two reviews, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 38). Localization and multilingual support in FLOSS-based systems were highlighted as superior over commercial software (one review, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 43). Regarding sustainability, concerns have been raised about the frequent dependence of FLOSS-based EHRs on funding from international partnerships (one review, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 44).

Other aspects, such as licensing, usability and user experience, and the frequency of updates have also been described. The systems were found to be distributed under permissive licenses, such as the GNU GPL Public Licenses (two reviews, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 40). Usability was identified as a potential issue in some systems, with reports highlighting that certain systems had interfaces that were difficult to navigate or that were not user-friendly for healthcare professionals (two reviews, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 41). The frequency of updates was only mentioned for the OpenEMR system, featuring two significant releases per year (one review, underlying study quality unknown; synthesis 45).

Discussion

This overview found that FLOSS-based EHRs offer relevant alternatives to proprietary systems, particularly in LMICs. Benefits such as customizability, cost reduction, and improved accessibility were frequently cited in the literature, yet persistent organizational, infrastructural, and socio-technical challenges continue to hinder implementation. The findings suggest that the success of FLOSS-based EHRs depends less on technical aspects and more on the presence of local capacity, institutional support, and development communities able to sustain the needs of global health systems, though the quality of evidence hinders firm conclusions. These insights reinforce the need for context-sensitive strategies in digital health policy and system design, as well as higher research standards in the field.

While FLOSS-based EHRs are used in some HICs, they are more commonly adopted in LMICs, where budget constraints and limited infrastructure drive the search for cost-effective, adaptable alternatives to proprietary systems.37–39 In HICs, adoption is often framed in terms of digital sovereignty and public-sector control over critical infrastructure, interoperability and data portability mandates, and efforts to mitigate vendor lock-in while preserving flexibility for local configuration.40,41 In LMICs, FLOSS systems offer a pathway to digital health record implementation, allowing customization to local needs and fostering regional technical capacity.36,42,43 However, their success depends heavily on government support and consistent public policies. Without institutional backing, challenges such as lack of maintenance, updates, training, administrative resistance, and hidden costs may hinder sustainability. Thus, governments can play crucial roles not only as funders but as coordinators of governance structures that align technical efforts with health system needs, enabling a secure and sustainable ecosystem for FLOSS adoption.42,44–46

Epidemic responses in LMICs underscore the value of FLOSS EHRs. OpenMRS, for example, has been deployed in HIV and tuberculosis programs and adapted during the Ebola crisis to create OpenMRS-Ebola.19,47 These cases illustrate the adaptability and responsiveness of FLOSS communities in contrast to proprietary systems limited by legal restrictions on modification/redistribution. Still, the reliance on short-term or informal resourcing, whether time-limited project funding or volunteer time, raises concerns about sustainability without institutional engagement.19,36 Long-term viability requires structured governance, technical capacity, and partnerships with health authorities and funders. The OpenMRS experience shows that, with coordinated support, FLOSS solutions can enhance public health responses during crises.

There is limited but growing evidence on the role and structure of development communities supporting FLOSS-based EHRs. Health systems present particularly demanding environments for digital solutions, requiring secure, interoperable, and continuously updated platforms that support complex clinical workflows, adhere to regulatory standards, and provide reliable technical support. A key concern raised in the literature is the voluntary nature of many FLOSS communities.19 While much of the development is indeed volunteer-driven, many projects incorporate paid roles for maintenance, support services, and specific development tasks, often funded by institutions or governments.48

Another frequently cited limitation is the dependence of FLOSS-based EHRs on multiple development communities, which may complicate integration and sustainability.19 However, this is not unique to FLOSS, as open-source components underpin much of commercial software. A key difference may lie in governance arrangements: in some FLOSS implementations, coordination across components may be less centralized when a foundation, consortium, or lead implementer does not provide integration governance, thereby increasing the risk of misalignment. Strengthening institutional support and hybrid governance models may help mitigate these issues while preserving the collaborative strengths of open development ecosystems.49,50

Organizational challenges related to the implementation and use of FLOSS-based EHRs have been identified, namely a lack of leadership and planning. Studies have shown a positive influence of more structured technology implementation methodologies and models, with institutional support and user training.51–53 These organizational challenges may be associated with a lack of capacity within open source communities to establish an effective implementation and support model. Other experiences show that FLOSS-based systems often face challenges such as lack of resources, schedule changes, sustainability issues, and the complexity of maintaining quality and continuity.54–56

Privacy and security are critical concerns in health records due to the sensitivity of the data.57 In open-source systems, these concerns are heightened since the publicly available code may expose vulnerabilities to malicious actors.58 Key issues include access control, encryption, secure communication, and legal compliance.59 For example, HIPAA governs any U.S. entity handling patient data, from hospitals to cloud providers. Yet some systems lack basic security features or have major vulnerabilities, as noted by Kiah et al.19 and Shaikh et al.20 Ensuring security in FLOSS-based systems requires active community involvement through regular audits, rapid patching, threat detection, team training, automated vulnerability scanning, community contributions, and adherence to secure development practices.60 Without proper implementation, security gaps pose risks for both users and the broader open-source ecosystem.

Higher standards in conducting and reporting research syntheses are urgently needed, as this was a major limitation in our overview. Although systematic reviews can inform decision-making, adherence to best practices is essential to ensure credible results and meet the expectations of policymakers and stakeholders.61,62 Most included reviews lacked critical appraisal, undermining a core purpose of systematic reviews: identifying the best available evidence.62 We also found eligibility criteria that limited the comprehensiveness of the evidence, such as restricting studies by journal prestige or narrow publication windows, practices misaligned with current standards. Language restrictions require careful consideration, particularly since FLOSS-based EHRs are often implemented in non-English-speaking LMICs. Tools to support the inclusion of non-English studies are widely available. Adoption of reporting guidelines like PRISMA63 would improve reporting quality. In many reviews, it was unclear whether key steps like data extraction and critical appraisal were conducted independently by at least two reviewers, a standard feature of rigorous reviews.

The overall applicability and relevance of the included evidence is limited by several factors. For instance, the body of evidence in the field can be impacted by the potential overrepresentation of specific systems—not because they are widely used or are the most consolidated, but because they might attract the attention of researchers. Consequently, we cannot rule out the possibility that other FLOSS-based EHRs might exist, facing different challenges and presenting completely different characteristics than those presented here. Even in the identified literature, several characteristics were only available for a specific subset of the identified systems, precluding the applicability of our overall conclusions to FLOSS-based EHRs generally.

This overview had several limitations. Relevant studies may have been missed due to the use of an unvalidated search filter, though this was mitigated by searching multiple databases and using Supplementary Methods (eg, citation searching). Our critical appraisal approach may also be a limitation, as the tool used was not solely focused on risk of bias. Additionally, since most included reviews did not assess risk of bias in primary studies, a full reappraisal of the literature would be warranted if resources permitted. Finally, limited reporting of implementation context and funding streams in included studies constrained our synthesis, particularly given recent shifts in global health funding.

Conclusion

This overview highlights the nuanced, context-dependent potential of FLOSS-based EHRs in healthcare. While these systems offer advantages, their implementation and sustainability may depend on institutional support, governance, and local technical capacity. In LMICs, FLOSS-based EHRs can support digital health transformation, particularly when backed by public policies that foster capacity building and long-term maintenance. Nevertheless, challenges related to interoperability, privacy, usability, and organization remain, and must be addressed through coordinated efforts balancing innovation, regulatory compliance, and sustainability.

The evidence base remains limited in both quantity and quality, with many reviews lacking methodological rigor, transparent reporting, and inclusion of non-English sources. Real-world implementations also reveal challenges such as instability in development communities and pressure to secure sustainable financing. These issues could complicate sustainability, especially in low-resource settings. Future research should emphasize robust methods, context-specific questions, and broader representation of systems and regions to support equitable digital health policies. It is also important to examine the operational realities of FLOSS projects, including community dynamics and sustainable financial and governance models.
