Development and validation of continuous cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome severity score (cCKMS-S) complementing CKM staging
Authors: Safdar Masoumi, Mohamadamin Tarighat-Payma, Farhad Shaker, Ladan Mehran, Ramin Assempoor, Fereidoun Azizi, Maryam Adib, Atieh Amouzegar, Davood Khalili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome integrates metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular dysregulation into a unified construct. Current CKM staging systems rely on categorical thresholds that can obscure within-stage heterogeneity, leading to misclassification due to population-specific cut-offs. This study aimed to develop and validate a continuous CKM severity score (cCKMS-S) to mitigate these limitations of categorical staging as a continuous adjunct severity index.MethodsUsing data from 7,343 adults aged 20–80 years in phase III (2005–2008) of Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was applied to derive age- and sex-specific latent CKM constructs. Temporal validation was performed in TLGS VI, and independent external validation in National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999–2018. Within-stage heterogeneity was assessed by stratifying Stage 2 into tertiles. Discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and mortality associations using Cox models.ResultsIn temporal external validation, area under the curves (AUCs) for stage classification were ≥ 0.83 for identifying CKM stage ≥ 2 and ≥ 0.90 for stage ≥ 3, across population subgroups. Similar discriminative performance was observed in NHANES (AUC 0.90 for stage ≥ 2 and 0.84 for stage ≥ 3). Each 1-SD increase in cCKMS-S was associated with a higher risk of all-cause (HR = 3.2;95%CI:2.7–3.9) and cardiovascular (CVD) mortality (HR = 4.7;95%CI:3.6–6.2). In NHANES, cCKMS-S also demonstrated consistent associations with mortality outcomes, with each 1-SD increase associated with a higher risk of all-cause (HR = 1.2;95%CI:1.2–1.3) and CVD mortality (HR = 1.4;95%CI:1.3–1.5), with adjusted C-indices for all-cause and CVD mortality (0.83 and 0.86, respectively) comparable to those observed in the TLGS cohort. Within Stage 2, cCKMS-S identified a marked gradient in all-cause mortality risk across tertiles (HR = 2.16, 3.33, and 9.43). Time-dependent ROC analysis for all-cause mortality in TLGS showed AUCs of 0.71 and 0.77 for cCKMS-S versus 0.66 and 0.69 for CKM staging at 10 and 18 years, improving to 0.85 and 0.84 after adjustment.ConclusionscCKMS-S introduces a continuous severity score that complements CKM staging by capturing within-stage heterogeneity and demonstrating generalizability across populations. It provides a practical tool for longitudinal monitoring and assessment of intervention efficacy, and preventive risk stratification in clinical practice. Background

Cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and metabolic disorders represent the three major health conditions that significantly contribute to global mortality and morbidity, as demonstrated in recent Global Burden of Disease (GBD) reports [1, 2]. Because of their interconnected pathophysiology, these conditions are frequently observed concomitantly, affecting more than 25% of the U.S. adult population between 2015 and 2020 [3, 4]. In response to the need for a more integrated approach to managing these interrelated conditions, the American Heart Association (AHA) proposed the framework of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome in 2023 [5, 6]. CKM syndrome is divided into five distinct stages, ranging from individuals with no CKM risk factors to those with the clinical coexistence of CVD, CKD, or significant metabolic risk factors. A recent analysis of National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data from 2011 to 2020 demonstrated that roughly 90% of U.S. adults satisfied the criteria for CKM syndrome (stage 1 or higher), with approximately half (49.0%) classified as stage 2 and 14.6% having advanced stages (3 or 4) [7].

Despite its clinical relevance, CKM staging has limitations, including interclass heterogeneity and potential misclassification due to threshold-based definitions [8, 9]. Moreover, the contribution of CKM components to disease burden varies across populations, limiting generalizability. A continuous CKM severity score (cCKMS-S) may address these limitations by integrating key components such as waist circumference (WC), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), systolic blood pressure (SBP), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), smoking, and medication use. Such a score could improve sensitivity to within-person changes, enhance statistical power, and reduce misclassification associated with population-specific thresholds. Unlike traditional risk prediction tools that estimate future events, a latent severity construct reflects underlying CKM burden and enables more stable longitudinal assessment.

Although cCKMS-S conceptually evolves from earlier CFA-based metabolic syndrome severity scores [10, 11], those models were confined to metabolic components. In contrast, cCKMS-S extends this framework by incorporating renal, behavioral, and therapeutic factors to capture the full CKM spectrum.

While CKM staging provides a clinically useful categorical framework for classification, it was not designed as a quantitative severity metric. Continuous indices may therefore serve as complementary tools rather than replacements, enabling within-stage differentiation and longitudinal tracking of physiologic burden.

The current study aimed (1) to develop and validate age- and sex-specific cCKMS-S based on data from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS) using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA); (2) to evaluate its robustness across demographic subgroups; (3) to develop a publicly available online calculator for cCKMS-S, facilitating disease severity assessment in clinical practice; (4) to assess cCKMS-S association with all-cause and CVD mortality, and its added value beyond CKM staging.

Methods

Study design

The present study was conducted within the framework of TLGS, a prospective community-based cohort designed to investigate the incidence and determinants of non-communicable diseases in Iran [12]. TLGS consists of six examination and survey phases (TLGS I-VI), performed at three-year intervals for more than 20 years of follow-up (1999–2018).

To develop cCKMS-S, CFA was applied to data from participants in the third phase (TLGS III; 2005–2008). Furthermore, data from the sixth phase (TLGS VI; 2014–2017) were used to perform external validation of the score developed in the present study. 12,581 participants of TLGS phase III aged between 20 and 80 years were screened and excluded if they had missing data or were pregnant at any phase during the follow-up period (Table S1), which resulted in the inclusion of 7,343 participants in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Data collection

At each study visit, data were obtained using standardized procedures. Information on participants was gathered through structured, interviewer-administered questionnaires and physical examinations conducted by trained doctors. Blood pressure was measured twice using a sphygmomanometer following a 15-minute seated rest period, and the average of SBP and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) readings was recorded. Anthropometric measurements, including WC, height, and weight, were taken with precision to the nearest 0.1 cm and 0.1 kg, respectively. After an overnight fasting period of 12–14 h, venous blood samples were collected. Laboratory assessments of plasma glucose, TG, total cholesterol, HDL-C, and serum creatinine were performed using standardized protocols and equipment, as described in detail elsewhere [13].

Variable and outcomes definition

Participants were followed on a yearly basis for the occurrence of medical events throughout 18 years of follow-up. Outcome ascertainment was performed by a dedicated adjudication committee consisting of clinical specialists, an epidemiologist, and the physician in charge of data recording. This committee systematically reviewed and evaluated all available information to determine the final outcome status for each participant.

CVD was defined as a composite outcome including coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, and non-CHD conditions. CHD comprised definite or probable myocardial infarction identified through positive electrocardiographic findings together with cardiac biomarkers and clinical signs, angiographically confirmed subclinical coronary atherosclerosis, any history of cardiac surgery, or unstable angina pectoris. Stroke was defined as a definite or probable event characterized by the sudden onset of neurological deficit persisting for at least 24 h. Non-CHD conditions included heart failure and atrial fibrillation. CVD mortality was characterized as deaths attributable to events related to CVDs. To confirm the cause of death, forensic medical reports, death certificates, and verbal autopsy reports were checked and documented. All-cause mortality, in this paper, comprised all documented deaths regardless of their etiology.

Metabolic syndrome was defined according to the Joint Interim Statement (JIS) definition as the coexistence of any three or more of the five components [14]. : Central obesity (WC ≥ 90 cm in both sexes; based on cut-points for the Iranian population [15]), FPG ≥ 100 mg/dL or use of antidiabetic medication, HDL-C < 40 mg/dL in males or < 50 mg/dL in females, TG ≥ 150 mg/dL or use of lipid-lowering therapy, SBP ≥ 130 mmHg, DBP ≥ 85 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medication.

CKD was defined by the eGFR less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, based on Kidney Disease Outcome Quality Initiative (KDOQI) guideline [16]. CKD-EPI (Epidemiology Collaboration) equations were used for eGFR calculation [17]. Table 1 illustrates the definition of adiposity, metabolic, kidney, and cardiovascular components of CKM [8, 18].

CKM staging and definition

The staging of CKM syndrome was determined using a combination of clinical features and laboratory markers representing metabolic status, cardiovascular, and kidney health, respecting the AHA Scientific Statement proposed framework [5]: Stage 0: no CKM risk factors; Stage 1: excess or dysfunctional adiposity (WC ≥ 90 cm, prediabetes, body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2) without existing CKD or other metabolic risk factors or CVD; Stage 2: metabolic risk factors or moderate to high risk CKD (eGFR of 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2) or both without existing CVD; Stage 3: presence of conditions equivalent to subclinical CVD (including patients with high-risk CKD (eGFR of < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) or predicted 10-year CVD risk of ≥ 20% based on the Predicting Risk of CVD Events (PREVENT) score [19]) in patients with metabolic risk factors or high-risk CKD (< 30 mL/min/1.73 m2); Stage 4: clinical CVD presence in cases with metabolic risk factors of CKD.

Statistical analysis

CFA was performed on the identified components of the CKM syndrome, including WC, FPG, SBP, TG, HDL-C, eGFR, smoking status, and medication use in order to evaluate each component’s weighted contribution to the latent, unobserved CKM construct. CFA was applied to derive a continuous latent severity construct that reflects shared variance across CKM domains rather than to redefine or modify categorical CKM staging. Multiple CFA models were fitted among included participants, and the findings were reported in the total sample (20–80) and stratified by age groups (20–59 and 60–80 years) and sex.

Because SBP and DBP are highly correlated, only SBP was included in the CFA models, given its stronger association with insulin resistance [20]. HDL-C and eGFR were included in their original scales and standardized, without transformation for directionality. Because of its skewed distribution, TG levels were log-transformed before analysis. All CKM components entered into the CFA models were standardized to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation (SD) of 1 in the full sample. The means and SDs of the total population were used for this standardization and in the derivation of the cCKMS-S equations. A single-factor CFA model was estimated, and the resulting factor loadings were interpreted as the strength of association of each component with the latent CKM trait. A loading ≥ 0.30 was considered to indicate a moderate correlation. Models were developed in the total sample and across age- and sex-specific subgroups, with and without the assumption of factor loadings equality across subgroups.

Factor scores were generated according to the appropriate linear combinations of the included variables. Factor scores were then calculated and cCKMS-S utilizing linear regression models based on the unstandardized CKM components in order to allow for potentially wider standardized score ranges across age and sex strata. The standardized factor coefficients obtained in the final CFA models were used to compute the cCKMS-S for each individual. For clinical applicability, CKM components and their corresponding CFA-derived were back-transformed so that the equations could incorporate the actual (non-standardized) values of the CKM variables. The resulting cCKMS-S can be standardized in any given population and interpreted as a z-score (mean = 0, SD = 1), where higher scores indicate greater CKM syndrome severity. Measurement invariance across sex and age subgroups was evaluated with multi-group confirmatory factor analysis. Configural, metric (equal factor loadings), and scalar (equal loadings and intercepts) invariance models were sequentially tested. Model fit comparisons were conducted using changes in fit indices, with ΔCFI < 0.01 and ΔRMSEA < 0.015 indicating evidence of invariance.

Model performance for the overall and subgroup-specific CFA models was investigated using multiple goodness-of-fit indices: chi-square test, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; good fit < 0.05), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR; good fit < 0.08), comparative fit index (CFI; good fit > 0.90), goodness-of-fit index (GFI; good fit > 0.90), Bentler-Bonett normed fit index (NFI; good fit > 0.90), and Akaike information criterion (AIC; lower values indicating better fit).

To assess potential overfitting and the stability of model performance across subgroups, internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling within the development dataset (TLGS III). A total of 500 bootstrap samples were generated. Optimism was estimated as the difference between apparent and bootstrap-corrected area under the curve (AUC). Optimism-corrected AUC values were subsequently computed for each age- and sex-specific subgroup.

Temporal external validation of the cCKMS-S was first performed among participants of TLGS VI (2014–2017) using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to investigate its discriminative accuracy for identifying participants with CKM stage ≥ 2 and ≥ 3. To further assess generalizability, external validation was performed with data from NHANES 1999–2018, a nationally representative, multi-ethnic cohort of the United States population. The cCKMS-S was calculated using coefficients derived from the TLGS cohort without recalibration. Its discriminative performance for CKM stage classification and its association with all-cause and CVD mortality were assessed with ROC and Cox proportional hazards models, respectively. CKM staging in NHANES was defined according to the AHA framework, incorporating albuminuria (UACR) where available [21, 22]. Detailed information on NHANES methodology is available via the following official website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx). AUC ≥ 0.80 was considered highly accurate.

To enhance clinical interpretability and assess within-stage heterogeneity, Stage 2 was further subdivided into tertiles based on cCKMS-S values, defined as Stage 2a (lowest tertile), Stage 2b (middle tertile), and Stage 2c (highest tertile). This tertile-based stratification was used to complement conventional CKM staging by refining risk classification within intermediate stages. Hazard ratios (HRs) for all-cause mortality were estimated across these refined categories using Cox models.

Additionally, the mean cCKMS-S values across CKM stages were calculated for exploratory purposes. Finally, Cox models were utilized to evaluate the association between cCKMS-S and all-cause and CVD mortality. Analysis was conducted with R Statistical Software, version 4.1.0 (http://www.r-project.org). Time-dependent ROC analysis was performed to investigate the accuracy of the cCKMS-S at different time points in survival outcomes prediction by utilizing the “timeROC” package. Confidence intervals were estimated using the inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) method as implemented in this package. CFA was performed with “lavaan” package, and Cox models were undertaken using the “survival” package. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P < 0.05.

Results

Development of cCKMS-S

A total of 7,343 individuals were included for CFAs. Age, WC, SBP, HDL-C, TG, FPG, eGFR, smoking status, and medication use varied substantially among age and sex categories. Model fit indices, including RMSEA, SRMR, GFI, NFI, and CFI, for the developed models ranged from 0.10 to 0.14, 0.072 to 0.09, 0.87 to 0.92, 0.74 to 0.86, and 0.75 to 0.87, respectively. Factor loadings of cCKMS-S components varied across age and sex subgroups. FPG and use of antidiabetic medication consistently exhibited the highest factor loadings across all subgroups, ranging from 0.64 to 0.76 and 0.59 to 0.83, respectively. HDL-C and eGFR indicated negative loadings reflecting their protective effects; HDL-C also showed relatively modest loading magnitudes across models, indicating a limited contribution to the shared latent construct. Notably, SBP and eGFR showed higher loadings in women than in men in the corresponding age groups. eGFR factor loadings in both men and women subpopulations were greater in the 20–60 years age group compared to those aged 60–80. Smoking demonstrated consistently low factor loadings across all subgroups, likely reflecting its limited variability and relatively low prevalence in certain subgroups, particularly among females; it was retained as a clinically relevant behavioral risk factor (Table 2).

The finalized equations derived from the CFA for each subgroup are detailed in Table 3, comprising nine distinct models stratified by sex and age categories (20–59, 60–80, 20–80 years). Individual cCKMS-S scores can be computed using data on sex, age, and relevant components through these tailored formulas. The resulting cCKMS-S values range from − 0.61 to 2.11, which consistently increased as the CKM stage progressed, in both males and females. Measurement invariance of the CKM latent construct was assessed across sex subgroups using multi-group confirmatory factor analysis. The configural model demonstrated that the same theoretical factor specification could be applied across groups; however, overall model fit was suboptimal, suggesting limited support for configural invariance (CFI ≈ 0.59, RMSEA ≈ 0.12). Imposing equality constraints on factor loadings (metric invariance) resulted in a decline in model fit (ΔCFI > 0.01), and further constraints on item intercepts (scalar invariance) led to a substantial deterioration in fit (CFI ≈ 0.04, RMSEA ≈ 0.16; ΔCFI > 0.01, ΔRMSEA > 0.015), indicating that full metric and scalar invariance were not supported. These findings suggest that the relationships between observed indicators and the underlying CKM latent construct differ across subgroups, supporting the use of subgroup-specific factor models in the derivation of the cCKMS-S (Table S2).

Validation

To assess internal validity, ROC analysis of the models demonstrated diagnostic performance for CKM classification (Stage ≥ 2). Across all age- and sex-subgroups, cCKMS-S indicated an AUC value greater than 0.86 (Figs. 3 and 4). The highest AUC was observed in women aged 20–80 years (AUC = 0.94), and the lowest was found in men aged 60–80 years (AUC = 0.86). To further evaluate model stability and potential overfitting, internal validation using bootstrap resampling was performed. The estimated optimism was minimal across all subgroups, ranging from − 0.0018 to 0.0018. After correction, AUC values remained virtually unchanged compared to apparent estimates (e.g., total population: 0.9203 vs. 0.9200), indicating high stability of the model. Notably, even in smaller age- and sex-specific subgroups, no meaningful reduction in performance was observed after bootstrap correction, suggesting that the models were not meaningfully affected by overfitting (Table S3).

For temporal external validation, AUC values for the detection of patients with Stage ≥ 2 of CKM, exceeded 0.83 across all groups. In addition, to further explore the discriminative ability of the model, its classifying performance was assessed for patients with Stage ≥ 3 of CKM, which showed an AUC of ≥ 0.90 across all subgroups (Table 4).

External validation in the NHANES cohort

External validation was performed using data from NHANES 1999–2018. Among 55,081 participants aged 20–80 years, 38,909 individuals were excluded due to missing covariates or pregnancy, resulting in a final analytic sample of 16,172 participants (Figure S1).

The distribution of CKM stages in the NHANES cohort was as follows: stage 0 (n = 1462, 9.0%), stage 1 (n = 2739, 16.9%), stage 2 (n = 8990, 55.5%), stage 3 (n = 1365, 8.4%), and stage 4 (n = 1616, 9.9%). The mean age of the study population was 50.04 ± 17.09 years. During a median follow-up of 102.0 months (IQR: 56.75–150.00) with a corresponding mean follow-up of 108.88 ± 63.42 months, 1,927 all-cause deaths (11.9%) and 575 cardiovascular deaths (3.5%) were recorded.

Discriminative performance for CKM stage classification

In external validation, the cCKMS-S demonstrated a strong discriminative ability for identifying individuals with moderate and advanced CKM stages. The AUC for detecting CKM stage ≥ 2 was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.897–0.907) in the overall population, while the AUC for stage ≥ 3 was 0.84 (95% CI: 0.835–0.850). Discrimination remained consistently high across age- and sex-specific subgroups, with AUCs ranging from 0.84 to 0.92 for stage ≥ 2 and 0.69 to 0.84 for stage ≥ 3 (Table 4, Figure S2).

Association with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality

In unadjusted models, each 1-SD increase in cCKMS-S was associated with a 70% increase in all-cause mortality risk (HR = 1.7; 95% CI: 1.7–1.8) and an 80% increase in CVD mortality risk (HR = 1.8; 95% CI: 1.7–1.9), with corresponding C-indices of 73.2% and 78.3%, respectively. In comparison, CKM staging demonstrated lower discriminative performance in unadjusted analyses (C-index: 60.6% for all-cause and 62.0% for CVD mortality).

After adjustment for age, sex, and physical activity, cCKMS-S remained independently associated with mortality outcomes (all-cause mortality HR = 1.2, 95% CI: 1.2–1.3; CVD mortality HR = 1.4, 95% CI: 1.3–1.5), with C-indices of 82.8% and 86.3%, respectively. The corresponding values for CKM staging were 82.6% and 85.8% (Table 5, Figure S3).

Distribution of cCKMS-S across CKM stages

Mean cCKMS-S values differed across CKM stages, with lower values in early stages and generally higher values in advanced stages, although some overlap was observed between stages 3 and 4 (Table S4 and S5).

Refined CKM staging (incorporating within-Stage 2 stratification) and mortality

Using the predefined tertile-based stratification of Stage 2 (refined CKM staging), a graded increase in all-cause mortality risk was observed across CKM categories (Table 6). Compared with Stage 0, individuals in Stage 1 showed a modest borderline rise in risk (HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 0.99–2.69).

Discussions

The present study developed and validated cCKMS-S, a continuous severity score for CKM syndrome, using data from the TLGS cohort, and externally validated the score in the NHANES study. Unlike prior categorical staging systems, cCKMS-S quantifies overall CKM burden as a single, age- and sex-specific latent score derived through CFA, integrating cardiovascular, metabolic, renal, and behavioral domains. The model demonstrated strong discrimination of advanced CKM stages in both internal and external validation (AUC ≈ 0.86 and 0.83), with consistent performance in an independent multi-ethnic cohort (NHANES; AUC 0.90 for stage ≥ 2 and 0.84 for stage ≥ 3). Importantly, cCKMS-S provided incremental prognostic information beyond categorical CKM staging, predicting all-cause and CVD mortality with HRs of 3.2 and 4.7 per 1-SD increase, respectively. Beyond between-stage discrimination, the score identified substantial within-stage heterogeneity: subdivision of Stage 2 into tertiles (2a-2c) revealed a marked gradient in mortality risk (HR 2.16, 3.33, and 9.43), which aligned with a continuous, nonlinear increase in risk observed in spline analyses. These findings support the concept that CKM dysfunction exists along a continuous spectrum and demonstrate that a latent severity score can refine risk stratification beyond traditional staging by capturing clinically meaningful variation within stages.

A population-based study on more than 877,000 Korean participants focusing on the prognostic value of longitudinal changes in CKM stages [8] noted that subtle parameter fluctuations near threshold values (e.g., eGFR) can shift individuals between CKM stages, reflecting the inherent instability of threshold-based categorical classification. This limitation underscores the loss of information that occurs when continuous physiologic variables are discretized into stages. Using cCKMS-S permits quantitative tracking of a patient’s trajectory over time and captures both between-class (movement across categorical stages) and within-class (subtle changes among individuals assigned to the same category) changes that categorical systems miss. Categorical frameworks often respond to this limitation by creating ad-hoc sub-stages, for example, the AHA subdivided certain stages (e.g., stage 4 to 4a/4b) based on CKD status, illustrating how discrete staging can obscure heterogeneity. In this study, substantial within-stage heterogeneity was observed, particularly in Stage 2, where stratification based on cCKMS-S revealed a marked gradient in mortality risk (HR 2.16, 3.33, and 9.43 across increasing tertiles). This gradient was further supported by spline analysis, demonstrating a more coherent alignment of individuals along a continuous risk trajectory after refined classification. Similar heterogeneity in clinical risk among patients within the same CKM stage has been shown in UK Biobank study by Yang et al., which introduced 5 subtypes of patients as a more efficient risk stratification tool in the CKM population, using K-means clustering [9]. A continuous score can differentiate these individuals by providing a within-class distribution of severity. cCKMS-S addresses these issues by quantifying CKM severity on a linear scale, reducing sensitivity to prespecified cut-points, and improving within-person comparability over time.

It is noteworthy that categorical CKM staging applies fixed thresholds that may not reflect the underlying distribution of cardiometabolic risk factors in different populations. In Middle Eastern cohorts such as Iran, hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL-C are highly prevalent, whereas CKD prevalence remains relatively low [23–26]. Also, Western populations demonstrate a distorted pattern, with more than one-third of the overall population having dyslipidemia, and CKD is present in only about 14% [27, 28]. Such skewed component prevalence leads to uneven stage allocation, where certain individuals are over-represented in lipid-driven stages while others with alternative risk constellations remain under-classified. This imbalance can bias population-level risk estimates and impair generalizability across regions. AHA 2023 Scientific Statement on CKM Health also recognized this limitation, noting that component definitions and thresholds may require calibration to a specific population [5]. In contrast, cCKMS-S integrates all components according to their shared variance rather than their prevalence or threshold position. Each variable is z-standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1) before modeling, and factor loadings are derived from CFA weight components by their covariance structure rather than by how frequently abnormalities occur. This design makes cCKMS-S robust against the skewed distribution and prevalence of CKM components in the target population. Supporting this, the performance of cCKMS-S remained consistent across populations for both advanced stage discrimination and mortality outcomes, with comparable AUC and C-index values observed in TLGS and the independent NHANES cohort despite substantial differences in demographic and epidemiological profiles. This consistency underscores the robustness and transportability of the latent construct across diverse settings.

According to the findings of the present study, cCKMS-S demonstrated incremental prognostic information beyond categorical CKM staging for all-cause and CVD mortality compared with categorical CKM staging (stages 0, 1 versus higher stages). This added prognostic value remained consistent after adjustment for age, sex, and physical activity. After adjustments, for cCKMS-S, the C-index elevated for all-cause mortality from 0.76 to 0.85 (absolute increase 0.09, relative increase 11.8%), whereas for CKM stages, the C-index increased from 0.63 to 0.84 (absolute increase 0.21, relative increase 33.3%). The smaller increment for cCKMS-S indicates that it already captures much of the prognostic information conveyed by age and demographics. Furthermore, this age-dependence of CKM staging association with all-cause and CVD mortality was observed in a cohort study within the framework of UK Biobank with more than 319,000 participants [18], which showed a stronger association in cases younger than 60 compared to the group of over 60 years. Patients, in both genders (in males: 14.60 vs. 9.29 for CVD mortality and 3.58 vs. 2.44 for all-cause mortality, and in females: 17.37 vs. 7.53 for CVD mortality and 2.84 vs. 2.53 for all-cause mortality, all P values for age interaction were significant). Furthermore, a notable difference was observed between females and males, as well as smokers and non-smokers, in terms of the association strength of CKM stages and mortality, which highlights the modifying role of these factors for CKM staging prognostic value. The same pattern of age dependency of CKM staging’s association with all-cause mortality was shown in a community-based study of more than 97,000 Chinese participants [29], which similarly showed a more pronounced association in cases younger than 60. Overall, these findings point to the fact that the prognostic efficacy of CKM staging depends notably on age as well as demographic and lifestyle-related factors, which is consistent with the results of this study that CKM C-statistics for mortality were enhanced notably when age, sex, and physical activity were adjusted. It is notable that the C-statistics of cCKM-S for mortality prediction did not notably enhance as much as CKM staging after adjustment for this factor, which highlights the fact that cCKM-S can provide a more comprehensive and robust measure to assess patients’ prognosis.

A key strength of the current study is evaluation of cCKMS-S in an independent, nationally representative cohort (NHANES), investigating the transportability and generalizability of the developed model. The consistent discriminative performance observed across NHANES, with AUC values of 0.90 for stage ≥ 2 and 0.84 for stage ≥ 3, supports the transportability of the latent construct across populations with markedly different demographic and epidemiological profiles. This finding provides empirical support for the theoretical advantage of CFA-based models, which weight components according to their shared variance rather than fixed thresholds, thereby reducing sensitivity to population-specific prevalence patterns.

For all-cause and CVD mortality, cCKMS-S demonstrated superior unadjusted discrimination compared with CKM staging, while adjusted models yielded comparable performance. This pattern, consistently observed in both NHANES and the TLGS cohort, suggests that cCKMS-S intrinsically captures a substantial proportion of the prognostic information conveyed by age, sex, and lifestyle factors. In contrast, the marked improvement in CKM staging after adjustment indicates greater dependence on these external covariates. In general, the current results indicate the interpretation of cCKMS-S considered as a measure of underlying disease burden rather than a construct driven primarily by demographic risk. Furthermore, the results support the generalizability of cCKMS-S across populations while highlighting the importance of further validation in diverse cohorts. Future studies should explore potential recalibration of model coefficients in populations with substantially different CKM component distributions and assess the added value of incorporating albuminuria or other emerging biomarkers into the latent construct.

The absence of albuminuria (UACR) in the derivation cohort represents an important limitation, as it is a central component of CKM staging. However, the preserved discriminative performance of cCKMS-S in NHANES (where UACR was incorporated into CKM stage definitions) provides indirect validation of the model’s robustness. The modest reduction in AUC for identifying stage ≥ 3 compared with stage ≥ 2 likely reflects the increased contribution of albuminuria to advanced CKM classification, particularly in distinguishing subclinical cardiovascular disease. Nevertheless, the maintained overall performance suggests that the latent construct derived from routinely available variables remains informative even when applied to definitions incorporating additional renal markers. From a practical perspective, this may represent an advantage in low-resource settings where albuminuria is not routinely measured.

Despite these strengths, some overlap in cCKMS-S values between stages 3 and 4 was observed. This finding likely reflects the inherent heterogeneity of advanced CKM stages, where clinical cardiovascular disease, treatment effects, and variable combinations of metabolic and renal abnormalities coexist. In such contexts, partial compression of continuous severity scores is expected and does not indicate model failure but rather the shared high-risk profile of these groups.

Collectively, the external validation findings support cCKMS-S as a transportable and clinically meaningful metric that extends beyond the limitations of threshold-based CKM staging. Its consistent performance across populations and outcomes highlights its utility as a scalable tool for integrated cardiometabolic risk evaluation.

Recently introduced CKM2S2-BAG model [30] translates the AHA CKM framework into a point-based CVD-risk score derived from regression coefficients of seven CKM-related variables. Although it produces a numeric value, interpretation is ultimately categorical (low, intermediate, or high risk), making it effectively stepwise rather than truly continuous, which will limit its ability to detect longitudinal progression of disease. Its discrimination for incident CVD events was moderate (C-index ≈ 0.73–0.76), and the model did not include medication use, potentially underestimating risk among treated individuals. In contrast, the present study established cCKMS-S as a continuous latent construct with validated unidimensionality and structural invariance across different age and sex groups, which has not been investigated in CKM2S2-BAG, limiting its generalizability. Within the TLGS cohort, cCKMS-S showed strong prediction efficacy for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (C-index 0.86–0.91), indicating broad prognostic coverage. Taken together, the validated latent dimensionality, notable robustness across age- and sex-subgroups, inclusion of therapeutic variables, and demonstrated mortality discrimination position cCKMS-S as a more comprehensive and generalizable representation of CKM pathophysiology than the categorical CKM2S2-BAG score.

Other contemporary frameworks, including PREVENT [19], ProtRS [31], the CKM Index [32], and Life’s Essential 8 [33], address different aspects of CKM risk. cCKMS-S was not directly compared with event-risk prediction tools such as PREVENT because such models estimate probability of future events rather than underlying syndrome severity; therefore, they address distinct clinical questions. PREVENT generates continuous probabilities for future atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) events based on demographic and clinical inputs but quantifies risk, not underlying syndrome severity. Importantly, event-prediction risk scores such as PREVENT are not suitable for use as outcome measures of disease severity or treatment efficacy, as they quantify predicted risk rather than the biological severity of CKM dysfunction. Using such models as outcomes introduces circularity and makes longitudinal comparisons unstable. In contrast, a latent severity construct like cCKMS-S reflects the shared physiologic burden of CKM components at a given point in time and is therefore appropriate for tracking disease severity or assessing intervention effects. ProtRS applies proteomic signatures to predict CKM outcomes with high discrimination yet requires omics infrastructure, limiting routine applicability. The CKM Index combines echocardiographic, renal, and metabolic parameters to forecast short-term events in hospitalized patients, while Life’s Essential 8 aggregates behavioral metrics to describe lifestyle health rather than physiologic disease burden. None of these models provides a standardized, population-independent quantification of CKM severity using readily obtainable clinical variables. cCKMS-S uniquely fills this gap, offering a continuous, accessible, and biologically validated measure that complements event-risk tools and lifestyle indices alike.

cCKMS-S is well-suited for implementation in primary care as an early-detection and prevention tool, as it relies on routinely obtainable, non-complex clinical and laboratory factors such as SBP, WC, FPG, TG, HDL-C, eGFR, smoking, and common medications. The score not only separates patients with advanced CKM stages but also discriminates lower stages with a high AUC, meaning it can identify at-risk individuals before overt cardiovascular or renal disease manifests. In practice, this allows clinicians to stratify risk, target early lifestyle or pharmacologic prevention, and prioritize closer monitoring or referral for patients who would otherwise appear low-risk under categorical staging. Furthermore, the continuous nature of the score supports tracking of small but clinically meaningful changes over subsequent visits, improving the timing and tailoring of preventive interventions.

Given the inclusion of behavioral and therapeutic variables in the model, their interpretation warrants particular consideration. In this context, smoking represents a behavioral risk factor rather than a direct physiological marker; however, it contributes substantially to cardiovascular and metabolic risk and was included to capture broader CKM risk exposure beyond biochemical parameters. This approach is consistent with the broader perspective of CKM as an integrated, prevention-oriented construct that encompasses behavioral and metabolic determinants of risk [5]. Accordingly, the cCKMS-S should be interpreted as an integrated measure of CKM risk rather than a purely physiological index. The relatively low factor loading of smoking in this study likely reflects its limited variability (as a binary exposure) and weaker covariance with metabolic components in this population, as well as its low prevalence, particularly among females, rather than a lack of clinical relevance.

The inclusion of medication use in the latent construct warrants careful interpretation, as medications represent treatment rather than direct physiological burden. In the framework of the present study, medication variables function as proxies of underlying disease severity and clinical decision-making. Individuals receiving pharmacologic therapy are, by definition, those in whom lifestyle measures alone were insufficient or in whom disease burden was deemed clinically significant. Thus, for a given level of biomarkers (e.g., glucose or blood pressure), treated individuals may represent a higher underlying disease burden than untreated individuals with similar measured values. This reflects the fact that pharmacologic treatment can normalize observable biomarkers without fully eliminating the underlying pathophysiology. This design introduces a conceptual nuance when interpreting longitudinal changes, as initiation or intensification of therapy may increase the score despite improvement in measured biomarkers. In this context, the cCKMS-S should be interpreted as a measure of overall CKM burden and current clinical disease state, particularly when used to monitor treatment response. This approach is consistent with commonly used cardiovascular risk modeling strategies in which treatment variables are incorporated alongside clinical and biological factors, reflecting underlying disease burden rather than functioning as modifiers of physiological burden.

The findings of the current study extend the current CKM staging framework by demonstrating that meaningful variability in risk exists within established stages, particularly in Stage 2. While CKM staging provides a clinically intuitive structure for risk categorization, the observed gradient in mortality across Stage 2a to 2c indicates that individuals within the same stage may differ substantially in their underlying risk profiles.

Spline analysis further complements this observation by illustrating a continuous and nonlinear increase in mortality across the full range of cCKMS-S values. Notably, when Stage 2 was subcategorized, individuals were more coherently distributed along the risk continuum, with lower tertiles aligning with flatter, lower-risk regions and higher tertiles clustering in steeper, higher-risk segments of the curve. Altogether, these findings indicate that cCKMS-S enhances the granularity of CKM staging by capturing within-stage variation that may not be fully reflected by categorical classification alone. Rather than replacing CKM staging, the cCKMS-S may function as a complementary approach to refine risk assessment, particularly in intermediate stages where clinical heterogeneity is most pronounced.

To improve clinical interpretability, data-driven reference ranges were derived based on tertile stratification within Stage 2. Values < -0.12 were associated with relatively lower risk, values between − 0.12 and 0.22 with intermediate risk, and values > 0.22 with substantially higher mortality risk. These thresholds are supported by spline analysis, in which a cCKMS-S value around 0.0 corresponded to an approximate doubling of mortality risk (HR ≈ 2), with a steeper increase at higher values. Accordingly, cCKMS-S may be pragmatically interpreted as follows: values below − 0.1 indicate lower risk, values around 0 reflect an intermediate level of risk corresponding to an approximate doubling of mortality risk, and values above 0.2 identify a higher-risk subgroup that may warrant closer monitoring or more intensive intervention.

Future research should assess the applicability of the cCKMS-S in other ethnic and geographic populations, as component distributions and factor structures may vary across settings. Longitudinal analyses are needed to determine the responsiveness of cCKMS-S to lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions and to quantify within-person changes in CKM burden. Recent longitudinal evidence also suggests that cumulative exposure patterns and timing of cardiometabolic-related physiologic abnormalities may carry important prognostic implications beyond single measurements [34]. In addition, future studies should evaluate the ability of cCKMS-S to predict CKM stage progression and the velocity of disease transitions over time. Integration of emerging biomarkers, such as inflammation or imaging-based indices, could refine the latent construct.

In the present study, formal testing of measurement invariance across sex and age subgroups did not support full metric or scalar invariance. This indicates that the relationships between individual CKM components and the underlying latent construct vary across demographic strata. Such findings are not unexpected given the multidomain nature of CKM syndrome, which integrates metabolic, renal, and behavioral factors that exhibit well-established sex- and age-specific biological patterns.

Rather than imposing strict invariance assumptions, subgroup-specific factor models were retained to derive the cCKMS-S, allowing the construct to more accurately reflect underlying physiological heterogeneity across populations. This approach aligns with prior cardiometabolic risk modeling strategies that account for demographic variability. Importantly, the lack of full invariance implies that direct comparisons of absolute cCKMS-S values between subgroups (e.g., men vs. women) should be interpreted with caution. However, the strong and consistent associations observed between cCKMS-S and all-cause and CVD mortality support its validity as a prognostic marker at the population level.

Strengths and limitations

Several notable strengths could be noted for this study. The current study presents a continuous severity score specifically designed for CKM, developed through robust CFA and validated using both internal and external datasets. By using standardized components and factor loadings derived from their shared variance, cCKMS-S is less sensitive to component prevalence and distribution and population-specific cut-offs than threshold-based classifications. Its outcome-based evaluation, linking the score to long-term all-cause and CVD mortality, demonstrated real prognostic capability rather than relying solely on cross-sectional associations. The extensive follow-up duration (more than 18 years) strengthens temporal validity. Finally, all laboratory measurements were obtained through standardized and homogeneous protocols across TLGS phases, reducing measurement variability and enhancing the reliability of component inputs. The score does not alter CKM staging definitions but quantifies severity within each stage.

This study has certain limitations. First, to ensure the score’s generalizability, its validation in non-Middle Eastern cohorts will be necessary. If the model is applied in populations with markedly different CKM distributions, re-estimation of factor loadings or recalibration of the regression equations may be necessary. Second, although the overall model fit was moderate (RMSEA = 0.14, CFI = 0.80), this level is acceptable for multidomain constructs that combine metabolic, cardiovascular, renal, and behavioral factors. Although these fit indices did not reach conventional thresholds, this may reflect the complexity and multidimensionality of the CKM construct. Previous studies [35, 36] suggest that strict cutoff criteria may be overly conservative in such models. Third, outcomes were limited to mortality, and evaluation of nonfatal CKM events should be pursued in future research. Additionally, some age- and sex-specific subgroups had smaller sample sizes, which likely contributed to the moderate, though still acceptable, AUC values and model fit indices observed in these sub-analyses. This pattern reflects sampling limitations rather than deficiencies in the underlying model structure. Although derived within a single cohort, the score is intended as a quantitative complement to CKM staging rather than a replacement classification system. Fourth, because variables were standardized using the full sample, subgroup-specific variances were not constrained to unity. As a result, comparisons of factor loadings across subgroups should be interpreted carefully, since the observed differences could partially result from variation in subgroup dispersion in addition to underlying associations. Fifth, measurement invariance across sex and age subgroups was not fully supported, indicating that the latent CKM construct may not be directly comparable across these groups. Accordingly, comparisons of absolute cCKMS-S values between subgroups should be interpreted with caution. Sixth, albuminuria (UACR), a key component of the kidney domain in the CKM framework, was not available in the TLGS dataset and therefore was not included in the derivation of the cCKMS-S. This may have led to partial underrepresentation of renal involvement, particularly in advanced CKM stages. However, external validation in NHANES, where UACR was incorporated, demonstrated preserved performance, supporting the robustness of the model. Notably, reliance on routinely available variables may also facilitate application in low-resource settings, although incorporation of albuminuria could further refine risk assessment in future studies.

Conclusions

In the current study, cCKMS-S was developed and validated as a continuous latent severity metric for CKM syndrome, grounded in the 2023 AHA CKM framework. By integrating metabolic, renal, cardiovascular, and behavioral factors into a single, age- and sex-specific latent construct, cCKMS-S addresses the limitations of discrete, threshold-based categorical staging systems. The score demonstrated robust discrimination of CKM stages and consistent performance in an independent multi-ethnic cohort (NHANES), supporting its generalizability across populations. Importantly, cCKMS-S provided incremental prognostic information beyond CKM staging and identified substantial within-stage heterogeneity, with clear risk gradients observed within Stage 2 that were not captured by conventional classification. As an accessible tool based on routinely available clinical variables, cCKMS-S offers a practical adjunct for refining risk stratification, quantifying disease severity, and tracking CKM burden over time. The score may help clinicians identify high-risk individuals earlier, improve monitoring of disease progression, and support more individualized preventive and therapeutic strategies, particularly among patients within the same CKM stage who may have substantially different underlying risk profiles. Overall, this approach captures the continuous nature of cardiometabolic disease more effectively than traditional staging and may support more personalized preventive cardiometabolic care and population-level health management.
