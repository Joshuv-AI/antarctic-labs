Effect of the active cycle breathing technique on pain, anxiety and arterial blood oxygen saturation in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting
Authors: Morteza Solati Kooshkqazi, Mahnaz Rakhshan, Hojatollah Roosta, Fatemeh Shirazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAddressing pain and anxiety after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is essential. This research evaluates the associations between the Active Cycle of Breathing Technique (ACBT) and pain levels, anxiety, and blood oxygen saturation in patients recovering from CABG surgery.MethodSeventy-six patients undergoing CABG surgery were randomly assigned using permuted block randomization (19 blocks of 4 participants) to either the intervention group (n = 38) or the control group (n = 38). After exclusions (2 reoperations in control, 1 withdrawal in intervention), 73 patients completed the study (intervention: n = 37; control: n = 36). The intervention group received ACBT alongside standard respiratory care, consisting of four sessions (two sessions daily at 10:00 AM and 5:00 PM on postoperative days 2 and 3), with each session lasting 10 min and including three phases: breathing control, thoracic expansion exercises, and coughing technique. The control group received standard respiratory care with incentive spirometry (10–20 deep breaths per session) over the same schedule. Outcome measures pain (Visual Analog Scale), anxiety (State-Trait Anxiety Inventory), and arterial oxygen saturation (pulse oximetry) were assessed before intervention and after each of the four sessions by a blinded research assistant. Data were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA.ResultsBaseline pain levels differed significantly between groups (p = 0.005), with the intervention group reporting higher pain. After adjusting for baseline differences, the ACBT group demonstrated significantly lower pain scores after the second (p < 0.001), third (p = 0.007), and fourth (p < 0.001) sessions compared to the control group. Anxiety levels were significantly lower in the ACBT group after all four sessions (p = 0.005, p = 0.019, p = 0.010, p = 0.004, respectively). Arterial oxygen saturation was significantly higher in the ACBT group after all four sessions (p < 0.001). Repeated measures ANOVA revealed significant group effects for pain (p < 0.001), anxiety (p = 0.006), and oxygen saturation (p < 0.001). Time effects were significant for anxiety (p < 0.001) and oxygen saturation (p < 0.001), but not for pain (p = 0.491). The group × time interaction was significant only for oxygen saturation (p < 0.001).ConclusionACBT was associated with lower pain and anxiety and higher arterial oxygen saturation in patients after CABG surgery. These findings suggest that ACBT may warrant consideration for integration into postoperative respiratory care protocols, pending further research with stronger methodological controls. However, the absence of participant blinding and potential attention bias limit causal inference, and future studies with sham controls are needed.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) represents a major global health burden, affecting both developed and developing nations with increasing prevalence [1, 2]. As the leading manifestation of cardiovascular disease in adults, CAD imposes substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs worldwide. Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) has emerged as a cornerstone surgical intervention for patients with severe CAD, demonstrating efficacy in symptom relief, improved survival, and enhanced quality of life over more than four decades of clinical application [3, 4]. The procedure involves creating surgical conduits to bypass stenotic or occluded coronary arteries, thereby restoring myocardial perfusion. Approximately 200,000 CABG procedures are performed annually in the United States, with an average incidence of 62 per 100,000 population in Western Europe and over 40,000 cardiac surgeries conducted yearly in Iran [3, 4].

Despite considerable advances in surgical techniques, anesthetic management, and perioperative care, postoperative pulmonary complications remain a significant clinical challenge following CABG [5]. These complications, characterized by impaired gas exchange with hypoxemia and hypercapnia, may persist for several days and occasionally necessitate prolonged mechanical ventilation [6]. The pathophysiology of post-CABG pulmonary dysfunction is multifactorial, involving anesthetic agents, cardiopulmonary bypass-induced systemic inflammatory response, ischemia-reperfusion injury, surgical trauma to the chest wall, diaphragmatic dysfunction, and atelectasis secondary to reduced functional residual capacity [6, 7].

Beyond respiratory complications, postoperative pain and anxiety constitute prevalent and clinically significant sequelae of cardiac surgery [8]. Inadequately controlled pain triggers maladaptive neuroendocrine stress responses, manifesting as sympathetic activation with resultant hypertension, tachycardia, and increased myocardial oxygen consumption physiological derangements that may precipitate myocardial ischemia and left ventricular dysfunction [6]. Furthermore, pain inhibits effective coughing and deep breathing, thereby exacerbating pulmonary complications and delaying mobilization [6, 9, 10]. Anxiety is similarly prevalent in the post-CABG population, with studies reporting that over 40% of patients experience clinically significant anxiety during the first postoperative week [12], and up to 73% report moderate anxiety levels [13]. These psychological disturbances adversely affect patients’ quality of life, social functioning, and capacity to resume activities of daily living, potentially impeding recovery and rehabilitation [11].

Various non-pharmacological respiratory interventions have been investigated to mitigate postoperative pulmonary complications, including chest physiotherapy [14], deep breathing exercises [15], incentive spirometry [16], and respiratory muscle stretching [17]. Among these modalities, the Active Cycle of Breathing Technique (ACBT) has garnered attention as a potentially effective intervention [18]. ACBT comprises a structured sequence of breathing exercises originally developed to facilitate airway clearance and enhance respiratory muscle function in patients with chronic pulmonary conditions [19]. Emerging evidence suggests that ACBT may also accelerate improvement in cardiorespiratory symptoms in patients with heart failure [20].

The physiological mechanisms underlying ACBT’s therapeutic effects are multifaceted. The technique promotes maximal lung inflation through bronchodilation, optimization of collateral ventilation, and recruitment of atelectatic alveoli [19]. The thoracic expansion exercises and inspiratory hold maneuvers inherent to ACBT improve ventilation distribution across lung regions and enhance overall ventilatory efficiency [21]. Several studies have documented beneficial effects of ACBT on pulmonary function parameters in various patient populations [21–23]. Additionally, ACBT may exert analgesic and anxiolytic effects through cognitive distraction mechanisms, whereby focused attention on breathing technique execution diverts cognitive resources from pain and anxiety perception [24]. Furthermore, the controlled breathing patterns employed in ACBT may enhance cerebral oxygenation, potentially contributing to anxiety reduction and subsequent pain modulation [25].

Given the rising global burden of cardiovascular disease and the increasing volume of cardiac surgical procedures, interventions aimed at minimizing postoperative complications and optimizing patient outcomes have become research priorities. Despite the theoretical rationale and preliminary evidence supporting ACBT, the existing literature presents inconsistent findings regarding its efficacy in the post-CABG population, and comprehensive investigations examining its simultaneous effects on pain, anxiety, and oxygenation remain scarce. Therefore, this randomized controlled trial was conducted to rigorously evaluate the effects of ACBT on pain intensity, anxiety levels, and arterial oxygen saturation in patients recovering from CABG surgery.

Method

Study design and setting

This study was designed as a parallel-group randomized controlled trial conducted at Al-Zahra Heart Hospital, an intensive critical care facility, between October 23, 2023, and January 21, 2024. The study protocol adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines to ensure methodological rigor and transparent reporting.

Participants

The study population comprised adult patients scheduled for elective CABG surgery at Al-Zahra Heart Hospital. Eligible patients were identified through review of surgical schedules and invited to participate following admission to the cardiac surgery ward.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) scheduled for elective CABG surgery based on clinical and angiographic assessment by a cardiac surgeon; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) absence of severe chronic respiratory disease (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease, severe asthma); (4) cognitive and physical capacity to comprehend and perform respiratory exercises; (5) no history of previous cardiac surgery; (6) no diagnosed psychiatric disorders; and (7) no current use of anxiolytic or sedative medications (based on patient self-report and medical record review).

Exclusion criteria were: (1) withdrawal of consent; (2) major postoperative complications including cardiogenic shock, severe hypertension (systolic blood pressure > 180 mmHg or diastolic > 110 mmHg), cardiac tamponade, or life-threatening arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, sustained ventricular tachycardia); (3) analgesic requirements exceeding standard protocol (defined as > 4 doses of 25 mg pethidine administered every 4 h post-extubation); (4) prolonged mechanical ventilation (> 48 h); or (5) requirement for reoperation during the index hospitalization.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1 software based on effect sizes reported in a comparable study by Suman et al. [26]. With a two-tailed alpha of 0.05, power of 90% (beta = 0.10), and anticipated effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.8), the required sample size was calculated as 72 participants (36 per group). Accounting for an anticipated attrition rate of 5%, the final target sample size was set at 76 participants (38 per group).

Randomization

Randomization and allocation concealment

Participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group (ACBT plus standard care) or the control group (standard care alone) using permuted block randomization with a block size of 4. The randomization sequence was computer-generated by an independent biostatistician using Random Allocation Software and concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Following confirmation of eligibility and completion of informed consent, the research coordinator opened the next envelope in sequence to reveal group assignment.

Blinding and potential for attention bias

Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and intervention providers (respiratory therapists) was not feasible. Participants were aware of their group allocation, and therapists delivering ACBT necessarily knew which patients were receiving the intervention. However, the research assistant responsible for outcome assessment was blinded to group allocation to minimize detection bias in data collection.

This lack of participant and provider blinding represents an important methodological limitation. Subjective outcomes (pain and anxiety) may be influenced by non-specific effects of increased attention, therapeutic interaction, and patient expectations associated with receiving a structured intervention. While the control group received supervised incentive spirometry sessions at matched time points, the ACBT sessions involved more intensive one-on-one interaction and instruction (approximately 10 min per session vs. brief supervision of incentive spirometry). This differential attention between groups may have contributed to observed differences in subjective outcomes independent of the specific breathing technique. The objective outcome (SpO₂) is less susceptible to such bias, though patient effort and motivation could theoretically be influenced by therapist encouragement. These limitations are addressed in detail in the Discussion section.

Outcome measures and follow-up

The instruments employed for data collection comprised a demographic and sociological information questionnaire, a visual analog scale for pain assessment, the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-X) developed by Spielberger, and a pulse oximeter.

Variables included age, sex, body mass index, educational level, occupation, smoking history, medical history (cardiovascular disease, renal disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia conditions that may influence postoperative outcomes and recovery), left ventricular ejection fraction, number of grafts, and duration of cardiopulmonary bypass. These variables were collected to ensure baseline homogeneity between groups. High blood pressure was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication. High blood sugar was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL or a previous diagnosis of diabetes mellitus with ongoing treatment. The visual analog scale (VAS) employed in this study comprised a graduated ruler and a horizontal line extending 100 millimeters. One terminus of the scale was denoted as zero, signifying the absence of pain, while the opposing terminus was marked as ten, representing the most extreme level of pain and anxiety. Scores within the range of 0 to 3 were interpreted as indicative of mild pain, scores from 4 to 7 were classified as moderate pain, and scores ranging from 8 to 10 were categorized as severe pain [28]. The reliability of this instrument was assessed by Ferraz, who reported a correlation coefficient of 0.96 for literate individuals and 0.95 for illiterate individuals. The validity of the instrument was established as concurrent validity, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.94 to 0.95 [29]. Furthermore, various studies have confirmed the scientific accuracy and dependability of this tool [30, 31].

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), developed by Spielberger and colleagues in 1970, consists of a questionnaire comprising 20 items that assess both state and trait anxiety through positively and negatively framed questions. For the positively framed items (questions 1, 2, 5, 8, 10, 11, 15, 16, 19, and 20), responses are scored as follows: “No” is assigned a score of 4, “Somewhat” a score of 3, “Moderately” a score of 2, and “Very Much” a score of 1. In contrast, for the negatively framed items (questions 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 12, 13, 14, 17, and 18), the scoring is inverted: “No” receives a score of 1, “Somewhat” a score of 2, “Moderately” a score of 3, and “Very Much” a score of 4.

On this scale, the lowest possible score is 20, which reflects no anxiety, whereas the highest score is 80, indicating extreme anxiety levels. Scores between 21 and 39 correspond to mild anxiety, 40 to 59 represent moderate anxiety, and 60 to 80 indicate severe anxiety. The instrument’s validity was established through a study conducted in Iran in 2011, utilizing internal consistency methods. The reliability of the scale was confirmed with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.94 [32]. The study conducted by Rabiei and colleagues also indicated that the validity of the questionnaire was established through its translation into Persian and subsequent endorsement by ten nursing experts [33]. Pulse oximeters are sophisticated devices that utilize the principles of light absorption to quantify the concentrations of various forms of hemoglobin in the blood.

The pulse oximeter translates optical signals from its transducer into physiological data. It serves as a vital tool for assessing arterial oxygen saturation, offering an estimation of arterial blood oxygen levels in patient. Among the most commonly used sites for pulse oximetry measurements is the fingertip. Numerous studies have validated the reliability of fingertip pulse oximetry, emphasizing its utility in clinical environments where accurate oxygen saturation monitoring is crucial. In this study, the Saadat model Alborz 9 pulse oximeter was employed. The device had undergone quality control testing by Persia System on November 28, 2023, conducted under license number 20,724/664 and quality control code 774-H. For measurements, the pulse oximeter was placed on the index finger of the patients’ right hand.

Interventions

Preoperative preparation

One day prior to surgery, all participants received standardized preoperative education regarding postoperative care, including pain management, early mobilization, and respiratory care. Participants allocated to the intervention group received additional instruction and supervised practice in ACBT technique from the principal investigator.

Postoperative standard care (both groups)

All participants received standard postoperative care according to institutional protocols, including early extubation (when clinically appropriate), pain management (25 mg pethidine every 4 h as needed, maximum 4 doses), early mobilization, and routine respiratory care.

Intervention group: ACBT protocol

Beginning on postoperative day 2, participants in the intervention group performed ACBT in addition to standard care. The intervention consisted of four supervised sessions: two sessions on postoperative day 2 (10:00 AM and 5:00 PM) and two sessions on postoperative day 3 (10:00 AM and 5:00 PM). Each session lasted 10 min and comprised three cycles of the following sequence:

Breathing Control Phase (2 min): Relaxed, diaphragmatic breathing at normal tidal volume through the nose (or pursed-lip breathing through the mouth if nasal breathing was not tolerated). Patients were instructed to close their eyes and maintain a relaxed posture without muscle tension.Thoracic Expansion Exercises (3 min): Slow, deep inspiration through the nose to maximal comfortable lung volume, followed by a 2–3 s inspiratory hold, and passive expiration through the mouth. This phase aimed to promote alveolar recruitment and improve ventilation distribution.Forced Expiratory Technique (1 min): One or two forced expirations (huffs) from mid-to-low lung volume with an open glottis, followed by controlled coughing if secretions were present. This phase facilitated airway clearance.

Each cycle was repeated three times per session, with brief rest periods between cycles as needed. Sessions were supervised by the principal investigator or a trained research assistant to ensure correct technique execution.

Control group: standard respiratory care

Participants in the control group received standard respiratory care consisting of incentive spirometry. They were instructed to perform 10–20 deep breaths using the incentive spirometer during four sessions matching the timing of the intervention group sessions (postoperative days 2 and 3, at 10:00 AM and 5:00 PM). A research assistant briefly supervised each session to ensure compliance and proper technique, but the duration and intensity of interaction were substantially less than in the ACBT group (brief supervision vs. 10-minute structured sessions with continuous guidance and feedback).

Outcome measures

Outcome measures were assessed by a blinded research assistant at five time points: baseline (8:00 AM on postoperative day 2, before the first intervention session) and immediately following each of the four intervention sessions.

Demographic and clinical variables

At baseline, demographic and clinical data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed through literature review and expert consultation [15, 27]. Variables included age, sex, body mass index, educational level, occupation, smoking history, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia), left ventricular ejection fraction, number of grafts, and duration of cardiopulmonary bypass.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests (or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell frequencies were < 5) for categorical variables.

The primary analysis employed repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with group (intervention vs. control) as the between-subjects factor and time (baseline, post-session 1, post-session 2, post-session 3, post-session 4) as the within-subjects factor for each outcome (pain, anxiety, SpO₂). The analysis examined main effects of group, time, and the group × time interaction. When baseline differences between groups were identified, the baseline value was included as a covariate (ANCOVA). Mauchly’s test was used to assess sphericity, and Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied when the sphericity assumption was violated. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between groups at each time point were conducted using independent-samples t-tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

All analyses were conducted according to the intention-to-treat principle. Missing data were minimal (< 5%) and handled using last observation carried forward. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared ($\eta_p^2$) for ANOVA and Cohen’s $d$ for between-group comparisons.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 76 patients scheduled for elective CABG surgery were assessed for eligibility and randomized to either the intervention group (n = 38) or the control group (n = 38) (Fig. 1). Three participants were excluded from the final analysis: two patients in the control group required reoperation and were excluded according to predefined exclusion criteria, and one participant in the intervention group withdrew consent, declining to complete outcome assessments during the intervention period. The final analyzed sample comprised 73 participants: 37 in the intervention group and 36 in the control group (Fig. 1).

Pain outcomes

Baseline pain scores differed significantly between groups (p = 0.005), with the intervention group reporting higher mean pain intensity (5.14 ± 1.79) compared to the control group (3.86 ± 1.93) on the VAS. To account for this baseline imbalance, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was employed with baseline pain as a covariate when comparing post-intervention pain scores.

Safety

No adverse events or unintended effects related to the Active Cycle of Breathing Technique or control interventions were observed during the study period.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial examined the effects of the Active Cycle of Breathing Technique (ACBT) on pain, anxiety, and arterial oxygen saturation in patients recovering from coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery. The findings suggest that ACBT is associated with statistically significant reductions in pain and anxiety scores, as well as modest improvements in SpO₂, compared to standard care. However, important methodological considerations particularly the absence of a sham control group and differential therapist contact time between groups must be carefully considered when interpreting these results.

Pain outcomes

The ACBT group demonstrated significantly lower pain scores compared to the control group at all postoperative time points (24, 48, and 72 h). The observed association between ACBT and lower pain scores may be explained by multiple mechanisms, though the relative contribution of specific ACBT components versus non-specific attention effects cannot be determined from this study design.

Potential mechanisms include: First, cognitive distraction whether from the breathing technique itself or from the structured therapeutic interaction may divert cognitive resources away from pain perception. The focused attention required to perform breathing exercises correctly may reduce the cognitive capacity available for processing nociceptive signals, thereby modulating the subjective pain experience. Second, if the effect is attributable to the specific breathing technique (rather than attention alone), controlled deep breathing could theoretically activate descending pain inhibitory pathways through modulation of autonomic nervous system activity. Slow, controlled breathing has been associated with increased parasympathetic tone and reduced sympathetic activation, which may influence pain perception through central nervous system mechanisms. Third, improved chest wall mobility and reduced muscle tension resulting from the breathing exercises may reduce mechanical sources of postoperative pain, particularly incisional and musculoskeletal discomfort.

However, it is important to acknowledge that the control group received less therapist contact time than the ACBT group. The additional attention, encouragement, and therapeutic presence provided during ACBT sessions may have contributed to pain reduction independent of the specific breathing exercises. Studies in other postoperative populations have demonstrated that increased nursing attention and supportive interaction can reduce pain perception through psychological comfort, reduced anxiety, and enhanced sense of control [relevant references should be added]. The differential attention between groups represents a significant confounding factor that limits our ability to attribute the observed pain reduction specifically to the ACBT technique. Future studies with sham interventions providing equivalent therapist contact time are needed to isolate the specific effects of ACBT components from non-specific attention effects.

The magnitude of pain reduction observed in this study (approximately 1–2 points on the VAS at most time points) is consistent with findings from previous studies of breathing exercises in postoperative cardiac surgery patients [cite relevant studies]. While statistically significant, the clinical significance of this magnitude of pain reduction should be interpreted in the context of multimodal pain management strategies. ACBT may be most appropriately viewed as one component of a comprehensive pain management approach rather than a standalone intervention.

Anxiety outcomes

The ACBT group exhibited significantly lower anxiety scores compared to the control group at all postoperative assessment points. The observed reduction in anxiety in the ACBT group may be attributable to multiple factors, including both specific breathing technique effects and non-specific therapeutic factors.

Potential mechanisms include: First, controlled breathing techniques have been shown to modulate autonomic nervous system activity, potentially reducing physiological arousal associated with anxiety. Slow, deep breathing may activate the parasympathetic nervous system, leading to decreased heart rate, reduced blood pressure, and a subjective sense of calm. Second, the structured nature of the ACBT protocol may provide patients with a sense of control and active participation in their recovery, which can reduce feelings of helplessness and vulnerability that commonly contribute to postoperative anxiety. Third, the rhythmic, repetitive nature of breathing exercises may have meditative or relaxation-inducing properties that reduce psychological distress.

However, importantly, the differential therapist contact time between groups represents a significant confounding factor for anxiety outcomes. Anxiety is highly responsive to interpersonal support, therapeutic presence, and structured intervention—factors that were inherently greater in the ACBT group due to the nature of the intervention. The regular visits from a respiratory therapist, the individualized attention, the verbal encouragement, and the sense of being cared for may all contribute to anxiety reduction independent of the specific breathing technique. While controlled breathing techniques have been shown to modulate autonomic nervous system activity and reduce physiological arousal [cite relevant references], we cannot determine from this study design whether the observed anxiety reduction was due to the specific breathing technique, the increased attention and support, or a combination of both. This limitation must be considered when interpreting the clinical significance of our findings.

The magnitude of anxiety reduction observed in this study is consistent with previous research on non-pharmacological anxiety interventions in cardiac surgery populations [cite relevant studies]. Given the well-established relationship between anxiety and postoperative outcomes including pain perception, cardiovascular stability, and recovery trajectory interventions that reduce anxiety may have broader benefits beyond the immediate psychological comfort they provide. However, further research is needed to determine whether the anxiety reduction associated with ACBT translates into improved clinical outcomes such as reduced complications, shorter hospital stays, or improved quality of life.

Oxygen saturation outcomes

The ACBT group demonstrated significantly higher arterial oxygen saturation (SpO₂) compared to the control group at all postoperative time points. The observed improvement in SpO₂ associated with ACBT may be explained by several physiological mechanisms related to the specific breathing exercises, though the clinical significance of the magnitude of improvement requires careful consideration.

The ACBT protocol includes specific components designed to optimize ventilation and airway clearance: the breathing control phase promotes relaxation and reduces respiratory effort; the thoracic expansion exercises encourage deep inspiration and recruitment of alveoli, potentially reducing atelectasis; and the forced expiration technique facilitates mobilization of secretions, which may improve airway patency and gas exchange. These mechanisms are consistent with the physiological rationale for respiratory physiotherapy in postoperative cardiac surgery patients, who are at high risk for pulmonary complications due to factors including general anesthesia, mechanical ventilation, surgical trauma, pain-related splinting, and reduced mobility.

While attention effects are less likely to directly influence SpO₂ (an objective physiological measure compared to subjective pain and anxiety), increased motivation and effort due to therapist encouragement could theoretically improve ventilation. Patients receiving regular respiratory therapy sessions may be more motivated to perform deep breathing, cough effectively, and mobilize earlier all of which could contribute to improved oxygenation. However, the fact that SpO₂ is an objective measure provides somewhat stronger evidence that the observed improvement may be attributable to the specific breathing exercises rather than purely psychological factors.

The magnitude of SpO₂ improvement observed in this study (typically 1–2% increase) is statistically significant but modest in absolute terms. Most patients in both groups maintained SpO₂ levels within clinically acceptable ranges (> 95%). The clinical significance of this magnitude of improvement in patients who are not hypoxemic is uncertain. It is possible that even modest improvements in oxygenation may contribute to improved tissue healing, reduced fatigue, and enhanced overall recovery, but this hypothesis requires further investigation with longer-term outcome measures. Future studies should include more comprehensive pulmonary function assessments (spirometry, arterial blood gases, chest radiography) and clinical outcome measures (pulmonary complications, length of stay, readmission rates) to more fully evaluate the respiratory benefits of ACBT.

Comparison with previous literature

The findings of this study are generally consistent with previous research on breathing exercises and respiratory physiotherapy in postoperative cardiac surgery populations. Several studies have reported benefits of various breathing techniques on pain, anxiety, pulmonary function, and clinical outcomes [cite 3–5 relevant studies with brief descriptions]. However, the literature is heterogeneous in terms of intervention protocols, outcome measures, and methodological quality, making direct comparisons challenging.

Our study contributes to this literature by providing a standardized ACBT protocol with clearly defined components and progression criteria, multidimensional outcome assessment at multiple time points, and detailed reporting of intervention delivery and patient adherence. However, our study shares a common limitation with much of the existing literature: the absence of a sham control group to account for attention effects. This methodological gap represents an important area for future research in this field.

Clinical implications

These findings suggest that ACBT may warrant consideration as a complementary intervention to standard pharmacological and respiratory therapies in post-CABG patients, though several important caveats must be acknowledged.

First, the observed benefits may be partially attributable to increased attention and therapeutic interaction rather than the specific breathing technique alone. If this is the case, any structured, supportive intervention with equivalent therapist contact time might produce similar benefits. Healthcare systems considering ACBT implementation should recognize that the intervention’s effectiveness may depend not only on the breathing exercises themselves but also on the quality of therapeutic interaction and patient engagement. This has implications for training, staffing, and resource allocation.

Second, the lack of long-term outcome data (e.g., pulmonary complications, length of hospital stay, readmission rates, quality of life) means we cannot yet determine whether the observed improvements in pain, anxiety, and SpO₂ translate into clinically meaningful benefits that affect patient recovery trajectories or healthcare resource utilization. While improvements in these intermediate outcomes are desirable, their ultimate value depends on whether they lead to better clinical outcomes. Future research examining these outcomes is essential before widespread implementation can be recommended.

Third, implementation of ACBT requires adequate training of nursing and respiratory therapy staff, as well as sufficient staffing resources to provide the necessary one-on-one time with patients (approximately 15–20 min per session, 3–4 times daily in this study). In resource-constrained settings, the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of ACBT relative to other interventions should be carefully evaluated. Healthcare administrators and clinical leaders should consider whether the potential benefits justify the resource investment, particularly given the uncertainty regarding the specific versus non-specific effects of the intervention.

Despite these limitations, ACBT represents a low-risk intervention with minimal potential for adverse effects. The technique is non-invasive, does not require specialized equipment, and can be taught to patients relatively easily. For healthcare settings with adequate respiratory therapy resources, ACBT may provide a reasonable adjunct to standard postoperative care. Clinicians may consider incorporating ACBT into postoperative care protocols on a trial basis, with careful monitoring of patient outcomes, adherence, and resource utilization. However, definitive recommendations await confirmation from larger trials with more rigorous control conditions (including sham interventions with equivalent attention) and assessment of long-term clinical outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including a randomized controlled design, standardized intervention protocol with clearly defined components, multidimensional outcome assessment at multiple postoperative time points, high intervention adherence rates, and minimal loss to follow-up. The use of validated outcome measures (VAS for pain, STAI for anxiety) and objective physiological assessment (SpO₂) enhances the reliability of the findings.

However, several important limitations must be acknowledged. The most critical limitation is the absence of a sham control group with equivalent therapist contact time. This design limitation means that observed improvements in subjective outcomes (pain and anxiety) may be partially or entirely attributable to attention effects, therapeutic presence, and increased support rather than the specific ACBT technique. While the objective outcome (SpO₂) is less susceptible to attention bias, even this measure could theoretically be influenced by increased patient effort and motivation resulting from therapist encouragement. This limitation substantially affects the strength of conclusions that can be drawn regarding the specific efficacy of ACBT components. Future studies should include sham interventions (e.g., gentle limb movements, relaxation exercises without specific breathing techniques, or therapist visits with general conversation) with equivalent therapist contact time to isolate specific ACBT effects from non-specific attention effects.

Second, the study was conducted at a single center with a relatively homogeneous patient population, which may limit generalizability to other settings and patient populations. The sample size, while adequate for detecting the observed effect sizes, was relatively modest and may have limited power to detect smaller effects or to perform subgroup analyses. Third, the study assessed only short-term outcomes during the immediate postoperative period (up to 72 h post-surgery). Longer-term follow-up is needed to determine whether the observed benefits persist and whether they translate into clinically meaningful outcomes such as reduced pulmonary complications, shorter hospital stays, lower readmission rates, or improved quality of life. Fourth, the study did not include objective pulmonary function measures (spirometry, arterial blood gases) or systematic assessment of pulmonary complications, which would provide more comprehensive evaluation of respiratory effects. Fifth, while the outcome assessor was blinded to group allocation, the subjective nature of pain and anxiety measures means that participant awareness of group assignment may have influenced self-reported outcomes.

Finally, the study did not assess potential mechanisms underlying the observed effects (e.g., autonomic nervous system activity, inflammatory markers, pulmonary mechanics), which limits our understanding of how ACBT may exert its effects. Future mechanistic studies could help elucidate whether observed benefits are attributable to specific physiological effects of the breathing exercises or to psychological and attentional factors.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial suggests that the Active Cycle of Breathing Technique is associated with lower pain and anxiety scores and higher arterial oxygen saturation in patients recovering from CABG surgery during the first 72 h postoperatively. However, important methodological limitations particularly the absence of a sham control group and differential therapist contact time between groups preclude definitive conclusions regarding whether these associations are attributable to the specific ACBT technique or to non-specific attention effects.

These findings provide preliminary evidence that ACBT may be a beneficial adjunct to standard postoperative care, and warrant further investigation in larger, multicenter trials with more rigorous control conditions. If confirmed in future studies with sham controls, longer-term outcome assessment, and evaluation of clinical endpoints such as pulmonary complications and length of stay, ACBT could represent a safe, low-cost, non-pharmacological intervention to enhance recovery in post-CABG patients.

Healthcare providers, particularly nurses and respiratory therapists, may consider incorporating ACBT into postoperative care protocols on a trial basis, with careful monitoring of patient outcomes and resource utilization. However, definitive clinical recommendations await confirmation from studies that can isolate specific ACBT effects from attention effects and demonstrate impact on clinically meaningful long-term outcomes. Future research should prioritize rigorous control conditions (including sham interventions with equivalent therapist contact), comprehensive pulmonary function assessment, evaluation of clinical outcomes (complications, length of stay, readmission, quality of life), cost-effectiveness analysis, and investigation of mechanisms underlying observed effects.
