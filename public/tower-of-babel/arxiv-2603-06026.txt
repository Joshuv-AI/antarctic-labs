Higher-Order Approximation of Coherent State Dynamics in Self-Interacting Quantum Field Theories
Zied Ammari, Julien Malartre, Maher Zerzeri

Abstract
We study the propagation of coherent states in self-interacting bosonic quantum field theories in the semi-classical (mean-field) regime. Relying on Hepp’s method and a detailed analysis of the associated classical and quantum field dynamics, non-linear and linear respectively, we construct an asymptotic expansion of arbitrary order for the quantum evolution of coherent states. The results are first established for the spatially cutoff P ​ ( ϕ ) 2 P(\phi)_{2} model, under standard assumptions ensuring essential self-adjointness of the Hamiltonian and well-posedness of the classical flow, and are then extended to a class of non-polynomial analytic interactions. This work refines and generalizes earlier results, which identified only the leading-order term of the expansion.



Key words.
Classical limit, Coherent states, QFT, Wick quantization, Fock spaces,
P(\phi)_{2}
model, Analytic interaction, Hypercontractivity.

Introduction

Bohr’s correspondence principle establishes a profound bridge between classical and quantum mechanics, asserting that the predictions of these two theories coincide in the limit of large quantum numbers. From a mathematical perspective, this principle leads naturally to the study of Schrödinger-type equations of the form

(0.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon\,\partial_{t}\psi_{t}=H_{\varepsilon}\,\psi_{t}\\
\evaluated{\psi_t}_{t=0}=\varphi_{\varepsilon}\,,\end{array}\right.

in the semi-classical regime
\varepsilon\to 0
. This is precisely the realm of semi-classical analysis (see for instance
[
16
,
37
]
), a domain that provides rigorous formulations of Bohr’s principle, often referred to as the classical limit.

The connection between classical and quantum dynamics becomes especially striking when the initial datum
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
in
(
0.1
)
is chosen to minimize the uncertainty principle. Such states, known as coherent states, were first introduced by E. Schrödinger in 1926
[
33
]
as the most classically localized quantum states. The terminology
coherent states
was later coined by J. R. Glauber in 1963 in the context of laser physics
[
20
]
. Since then, coherent states have developed into a central theme of both physics and mathematics, see for example
[
12
]
. Coherent states continue to attract increasing attention owing to their involvement in various mathematical and physical fields such as condensed matter, quantum optics and quantum information theory
[
10
,
23
,
28
,
27
]
.

A decisive advance was made in 1974, when K. Hepp
[
24
]
introduced a rigorous framework clarifying the crucial role of coherent states in the classical limit. His groundbreaking method proved remarkably fruitful, inspiring a wide range of subsequent works devoted to semi-classical dynamics with a coherent state approach. In particular, it allows one to derive a full asymptotic expansion in powers of
\varepsilon^{1/2}
for solutions of the Schrödinger equation
(
0.1
)
with coherent state initial data
[
31
]
.

Hepp’s method has since been extended to a variety of asymptotic regimes, including the mean-field limit, where the semi-classical parameter
\varepsilon
corresponds to the inverse particle number, as well as to infinite-dimensional phase space analysis and quantum field models (see respectively
[
19
,
32
,
2
]
,
[
6
]
, and
[
17
]
). In the latter setting, it was shown in
[
7
]
that under a certain class of self-interacting bosonic quantum field Hamiltonians, coherent states remain localized along classical trajectories, while undergoing a squeezing governed by a unitary Bogoliubov transformation.

The aim of the present paper is to further develop this line of research by providing a complete asymptotic description of coherent-state evolution in the classical limit, using Hepp’s method in the spirit of D. Robert’s analysis for Schrödinger operators
[
31
]
. We focus in particular on the spatially cutoff
P(\phi)_{2}
model, chosen for its fundamental role in theoretical physics (see
[
22
]
and also
[
15
]
). For this model, the classical field equation is given by a non-linear Klein-Gordon equation
(
1.40
)
which is globally well-posed for initial data in the energy space, see Theorem
2.9
. More generally, we study certain models of self-interacting bosonic quantum field Hamiltonians with analytic interactions, including a variant of the Høegh-Krohn model
[
26
]
.

Following the framework of
[
7
]
, we adopt a dynamical point of view to study the classical limit. A different approach, based on a variational perspective, is discussed in
[
1
,
8
]
. It is also worth mentioning that an alternative approach was developed in
[
6
]
, based on an extension of Wigner (or semi-classical) measures to infinite-dimensional phase spaces. To date, this method has been applied mainly to many-body Hamiltonians in the mean-field regime, to quantum electrodynamics and to Yukawa and Polaron type models in different scaling (see e.g.
[
5
,
3
,
18
,
13
]
). Its possible extension to self-interacting quantum field theory models lies beyond the scope of the present work and is left for future investigation.

Using Hepp’s method and a systematic expansion of the quantum dynamics around the associated classical non-linear field equation, we construct an asymptotic expansion of arbitrary order for the time evolution of coherent states in the classical limit. The leading order is governed by the classical Hamiltonian flow, while subleading terms are described by a time-dependent quadratic (Bogoliubov) dynamics and higher-order Wick-ordered corrections. For each fixed time, we obtain norm-accurate expansions with explicit control of the remainder. These results refine and generalize previous work of the first and third authors
[
7
]
as well as the work of K. Hepp
[
24
]
, where only the leading-order term of the semi-classical expansion was established.

In particular, we clarify the study of the non-linear Klein-Gordon
P(\phi)_{2}
field equation, by proving the existence and uniqueness of global mild solutions to
(
1.38
)
for each initial data in the physical space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, see Corollary
2.10
. As a consequence, we show the propagation of coherent states along the classical trajectories departing from any initial data in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and for arbitrary finite time below the Ehrenfest time, see Theorem
1.4
. The above result requires an additional assumption on the spatial cutoff function
g
, see
(
1.34
)
, which is taken furthermore to be in the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
instead of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. For further details, see Subsection
2.2
and Remark
2.11
.

For the more general classical non-linear field equation
(
1.46
)
with analytic non-linearity, formulated on an abstract Hilbert space
\mathcal{Z}
, we remark that the argument in
[
7
]
, used to construct global mild solutions to
(
1.46
)
on the
\mathcal{Z}
-space, is incorrect even though the conclusion itself may still hold. Nevertheless, we still have existence and uniqueness of local mild solutions to
(
1.46
)
for any initial data in
\mathcal{Z}
, see Theorem
3.6
. In this setting, we accordingly prove the propagation of coherent states, with higher order approximation, along the classical trajectories during their lifespans and below the Ehrenfest time, see Theorem
1.6
.

1
Background and main results

The symmetric Fock space is the natural Hilbert space where the Hamiltonians of free Bose fields are well described and expressed in a very explicit manner. Furthermore, we have at hand a wave representation of the Canonical Commutation Relations, see Theorem
1.1
below, which enables us to describe quantum field Hamiltonians either in particle representation or in wave representation. With the latter description, we will see that the interaction terms considered in this work take a particularly convenient form, which simplifies their study.

In order to make the paper as self-contained as possible, we introduce in the next subsection some basic notions of quantum field theory, following
[
7
]
.

1.1
Formalism of Quantum Field Theory

Let
\mathcal{Z}
be a separable, complex Hilbert space which inner product
\innerproduct{\cdot}{\cdot}
is antilinear with respect to the left variable, with associated norm
\absolutevalue{u}=\sqrt{\innerproduct{u}{u}}
. We endow
\mathcal{Z}
with a conjugation
\mathfrak{c}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
, that is, a norm-preserving, antilinear involution on
\mathcal{Z}
. We define

(1.1)

\mathcal{Z}_{0}=\{z\in\mathcal{Z},\quad\mathfrak{c}z=z\}\,,

the real subspace of
\mathcal{Z}
.

The bosonic Fock space over
\mathcal{Z}
is defined as the direct Hilbert sum

(1.2)

\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}\mathcal{Z}\,,

where
\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}\mathcal{Z}
denotes the range of
\otimes^{n}\mathcal{Z}
by the orthogonal projection

(1.3)

\mathcal{S}_{n}:u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{n}\longmapsto\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}u_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{\sigma(n)}\,,

where
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is the symmetric group of degree

n
. The space
\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}\mathcal{Z}
, seen as a subspace of
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
, is called the
n
-particle sector. For
n=0
, this gives the vacuum sector
\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{0}\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{C}\,\Omega
where

(1.4)

\Omega=(1,0,0,\dots)\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})

is called the vacuum vector. Thanks to these identifications, any
\psi=\left(\psi^{(n)}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
can be written

(1.5)

\psi=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\psi^{(n)}\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\big\|\psi\big\|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})}^{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\|\psi^{(n)}\|_{\otimes^{n}\mathcal{Z}}^{2}<+\infty\,.

The polarization formula

(1.6)

\mathcal{S}_{n}(u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{n})=\frac{1}{2^{n}\,n!}\sum_{\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n}\in\{\pm 1\}}\rho_{1}\cdots\rho_{n}\,\left[\otimes^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\rho_{j}u_{j}\right)\right]

yields, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(1.7)

\overline{\mathrm{Span}\{\otimes^{n}w,\quad w\in\mathcal{Z}\}}=\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}\mathcal{Z}\,.

The Hilbert space
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
is endowed with the inner product

\innerproduct{\varphi}{\psi}_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,\innerproduct{\varphi^{(n)}}{\psi^{(n)}}_{\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}\mathcal{Z}}\,.

We denote by
\norm{\cdot}
the associated norm.

Finally, the finite particle space, defined as the algebraic direct sum

(1.8)

\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}^{\mathrm{alg}}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}\mathcal{Z}\,,

is dense in
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
.

We now define the standard operators of quantum field theory. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
be a semi-classical parameter. For all
u\in\mathcal{Z}
, the
\varepsilon
-dependent creation and annihilation operators associated with
u
are respectively defined on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})
by the expressions

(1.9)

\evaluated{\crea[\eps]{u}}_{\tensymn{\hi}{n}}=\sqrt{\varepsilon(n+1)}\,\mathcal{S}_{n+1}\Big(\ket{u}\otimes(\otimes^{n}\mathbbm{1})\Big)

and

(1.10)

\evaluated{\anni[\eps]{u}}_{\tensymn{\hi}{n}}=\sqrt{\varepsilon n}\,\mathcal{S}_{n-1}\Big(\bra{u}\otimes(\otimes^{n-1}\mathbbm{1})\Big)\,,

where
\bra{u}:v\in\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\innerproduct{u}{v}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\ket{u}=[\,\bra{u}\,]^{*}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{Z}
. These two operators satisfy the canonical commutation relations (CCR):

(1.11)

\commutator{\anni[\eps]{u}}{\anni[\eps]{v}}=\commutator{\crea[\eps]{u}}{\crea[\eps]{v}}=0\text{ and }\commutator{\anni[\eps]{u}}{\crea[\eps]{v}}=\varepsilon\,\innerproduct{u}{v}\,\mathbbm{1},\,\forall\,u,v\in\mathcal{Z}\,.

For all
u\in\mathcal{Z}
, the Segal field operators

(1.12)

\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\Big(\mathrm{a}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)+\mathrm{a}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)\Big)

are essentially self-adjoint on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})
, see for example
[
29
, Theorem X.41]
, which enables us to define (by Stone’s Theorem) the Weyl operators

(1.13)

\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)}\,.

These operators satisfy, for all
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}
,

(1.14)

\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u_{1}\right)\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u_{2}\right)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mathsf{w}\left(u_{1},u_{2}\right)}\,\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u_{1}+u_{2}\right)\,,

where
\mathsf{w}\left(u_{1},u_{2}\right)=-2\,\imaginary\innerproduct{u_1}{u_2}
is the canonical symplectic form.

In this context, the set of coherent states are the total family of vectors in the Bosonic Fock space
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
given by

(1.15)

\left\{\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\varepsilon}u\right)\Omega,\quad u\in\mathcal{Z}\right\},

where
\Omega
is the vacuum vector
(
1.4
)
. Explicitly, we have

(1.16)

\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\varepsilon}u\right)\Omega=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\absolutevalue{u}^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\varepsilon^{-\frac{n}{2}}\,\frac{\otimes^{n}u}{\sqrt{n!}},\quad\forall\,u\in\mathcal{Z}\,.

For any self-adjoint operator
A:D(A)\subset\mathcal{Z}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}
, the corresponding lifting operator is defined by

\evaluated{\liftop{A}}_{\tensymn{D(A)}{n,\rm alg}}=\otimes^{n}A\,,

where
\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{n,\rm alg}D(A)
is the algebraic symmetric
n
-tensor product; and the second quantization of
A
is the operator
\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(A\right)
given by

\evaluated{\dG[\eps]{A}}_{\tensymn{D(A)}{n,\rm alg}}=\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{n}\,(\otimes^{j-1}\mathbbm{1})\,\otimes\underbrace{A}_{j^{\mathrm{th}}\mathrm{position}}\otimes\,(\otimes^{n-j}\mathbbm{1})\,,

which describes the Hamiltonian of free Bose fields in the particle representation. In particular, the
\varepsilon
-dependent number operator is defined by
\mathrm{N}_{\varepsilon}=\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathbbm{1}\right)
. We also note that
\Gamma\left(\mathfrak{c}\right)
defines a conjugation on the Bosonic Fock space
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
whenever
\mathfrak{c}
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{Z}
.

We conclude this subsection with a crucial theorem, often referred to as the wave representation of Fock space, which arises from representation of Gaussian random processes indexed by real Hilbert spaces (see for instance
[
22
, Proposition 2.1.1]
and also
[
35
, Theorem I.1]
).

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a probability space
(Q,\mathfrak{T},\mu)
and a unitary map

\mathcal{R}:\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})\to L^{2}(Q,\differential\mu)

fulfilling the following conditions:

1.

\mathcal{R}\Omega=1
,

2.

\mathcal{R}\mathfrak{M}\mathcal{R}^{*}=L^{\infty}(Q,\differential\mu)
, where
\mathfrak{M}
denotes the
W^{*}
-algebra generated by the Weyl operators
\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)
,
u\in\mathcal{Z}_{0}
.

Furthermore, for all
V\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
, we have

(1.17)

\mathcal{R}\Gamma\left(\mathfrak{c}\right)V=\overline{\mathcal{R}V}

and, for all
u\in\mathcal{Z}_{0}
,
\varepsilon>0
,

(1.18)

\mathcal{R}^{*}\Big(\mathcal{R}(\Gamma\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)V)\Big)\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)\Omega)=\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)\Gamma\left(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)V\,.

The wave representation of Fock space appears very useful to study the interaction terms considered in this work, as we will see that these terms correspond to unbounded multiplication operators by measurable functions in
L^{2}(Q,\differential\mu)
.

1.2
Polynomial Wick quantization

In this subsection, we introduce the notion of polynomial symbol following
[
6
]
.

A map
b:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be a monomial symbol of order
(p,q)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
if there exists a bounded operator
\widetilde{b}\in\mathcal{L}(\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{p}\mathcal{Z},\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{q}\mathcal{Z})
such that, for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
,

(1.19)

b(z)=\innerproduct{\tensn{z}{q}}{\tilde b(\tensn{z}{p})}_{\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{q}\mathcal{Z}}\,.

In this case, we write
b\in\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(\mathcal{Z})
and we call
m=p+q
the total order of
b
. We define
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
as the space of linear combinations of monomial symbols, called polynomial symbols. For all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{P}_{m}(\mathcal{Z})
, resp.
\mathcal{P}_{\leqslant m}(\mathcal{Z})
, denotes the space of polynomial symbols of total order
m
, resp. at most
m
.

For all
b\in\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(\mathcal{Z})
,
z,h\in\mathcal{Z}
, we can write

(1.20)

b(z+h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant j\leqslant q\\
0\leqslant k\leqslant p\end{subarray}}\,\frac{1}{j!}\,\frac{1}{k!}\,\innerproduct{\tensn{h}{j}}{\gatgatbar{k}{j}{b}(z) (\tensn{h}{k})}\,,

where

(1.21)

\partial_{z}^{k}\partial_{\overline{z}}^{j}b(z)=\,\frac{p!}{(p-k)!}\,\frac{q!}{(q-j)!}\,\mathcal{S}_{j}\Big(\bra{\tensn{z}{q-j}}\otimes(\otimes^{j}\mathbbm{1})\Big)\,\widetilde{b}\,\mathcal{S}_{p}\Big(\ket{\tensn{z}{p-k}}\otimes(\otimes^{k}\mathbbm{1})\Big)\\
\in\mathcal{L}(\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\mathcal{Z},\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{j}\mathcal{Z})\,.

This formula provides a notion of derivative for polynomial symbols. In particular, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(1.22)

\partial_{z}^{k}b(z)=\frac{p}{(p-k)!}\,\bra{\tensn{z}{q}}\,\widetilde{b}\,\mathcal{S}_{p}\Big((\otimes^{p-k}z)\otimes(\otimes^{k}\mathbbm{1})\Big)\in\big(\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\mathcal{Z}\big)^{*}

and

(1.23)

\partial_{\overline{z}}^{k}b(z)=\frac{q!}{(q-k)!}\,\mathcal{S}_{k}\left(\bra{\tensn{z}{q-k}}\otimes(\otimes^{k}\mathbbm{1})\right)\,\widetilde{b}\,(\otimes^{p}z)\in\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\mathcal{Z}\,.

The Wick quantization of a monomial symbol
b\in\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(\mathcal{Z})
is defined on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})
by the formulas

(1.24)

\evaluated{\Wick[\eps]{b}}_{\tensymn{\hi}{n}}=\mathbbm{1}_{[p,+\infty)}(n)\,\frac{\sqrt{n!\,(n+q-p)!}}{(n-p)!}\,\varepsilon^{\frac{p+q}{2}}\,\mathcal{S}_{n+q-p}\left(\widetilde{b}\otimes(\otimes^{n-p}\mathbbm{1})\right)\,,

where
\mathcal{S}_{n+q-p}
is the symmetrization operator given by
(
1.3
)
. The expression
\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)
is extended by linearity to any polynomial symbol
b\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
, and
b
is called the Wick symbol of the operator
\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)
. Let us remark the homogeneity property

(1.25)

\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)=\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}\,\mathrm{Wick}_{1}\left(b\right)\text{ for all }b\in\mathcal{P}_{j}(\mathcal{Z})\,.

For all
\varphi,\psi\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})
and for all polynomial symbol
b
, we have

(1.26)

\innerproduct{\varphi}{\Wick[\eps]{b}\, \psi}=\innerproduct{\Wick[\eps]{\bar{b}}\, \varphi}{\psi}\,,

where
\overline{b}
is the polynomial symbol defined by
\overline{b}(z)=\overline{b(z)}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
. This identity shows that
\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(\overline{b}\right)^{*}
is an extension of
\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)
: in particular, the operator
\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)
is closable. We keep the same notation for its closure.

Identifying
b
and
\widetilde{b}
through the identity
(
1.19
)
, we give below the most important examples of Wick quantizations.

\begin{array}[]{c|c}\widetilde{b}&\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)\\
\hline\cr\lambda\in\mathbb{C}&\lambda\mathbbm{1}\\
\bra{u}&\mathrm{a}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)\\
\ket{u}&\mathrm{a}^{*}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)\\
\sqrt{2}\,\real\innerproduct{u}{z}&\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)\\
\mathbbm{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z})&\mathrm{N}_{\varepsilon}\\
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z})&\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(A\right)\end{array}

We now state the main properties of Wick quantization, see
[
6
]
for the proofs.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
b\in\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(\mathcal{Z})
.

1.

(adjoint) On
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})
, we have

(1.27)

\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)^{*}=\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(\overline{b}\right)\,.

2.

For any self-adjoint operator
A
on
\mathcal{Z}
and for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have, on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})
,

(1.28)

\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(A\right)}\,\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(A\right)}=\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tA}\,\bullet)\right)\,,

where
b\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tA}\,\bullet\right):z\in\mathbb{C}\mapsto b\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tA}\,z\right)
is a polynomial symbol.
As a consequence, for all
u\in\mathcal{Z}
, we have

(1.29)

\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(A\right)}\,\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(A\right)}=\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}tA}\,u\right)\,.

3.

The space

(1.30)

\mathscr{H}_{0}=\mathrm{Span}\left\{\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)\varphi,\quad u\in\mathcal{Z},\,\varphi\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})\right\}

is a core for the operators
\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)
,
b\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
.

4.

(translation) On
\mathscr{H}_{0}
, we have, for all
u\in\mathcal{Z}
,

(1.31)

\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\varepsilon}u\right)^{*}\,\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)\,\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\varepsilon}u\right)=\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b(\bullet+u)\right)\,,

where
b\left(\bullet+u\right):z\in\mathbb{C}\mapsto b(z+u)
is a polynomial symbol.

5.

(composition) For all
b_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{p_{1},q_{1}}(\mathcal{Z})
,
b_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{p_{2},q_{2}}(\mathcal{Z})
,

(1.32)

\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b_{1}\right)\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b_{2}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(p_{1},q_{2})}\frac{\varepsilon^{k}}{k!}\,\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(\partial_{z}^{k}b_{1}\cdot\partial_{\overline{z}}^{k}b_{2}\right)\,,

where
\partial_{z}^{k}b_{1}\cdot\partial_{\overline{z}}^{k}b_{2}(z)=\innerproduct{\gat{k}{b_1}(z)}{\gatbar{k}{b_2}(z)}_{(\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\mathcal{Z})^{*},\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{k}\mathcal{Z}}
. We denote by
b_{1}\ \sharp_{\varepsilon}\ b_{2}
the Wick symbol of the operator
\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b_{1}\right)\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b_{2}\right)
.

We end this subsection by stating the well-known number estimates, see
[
15
, Proposition 3.13]
.

Proposition 1.3
.

We start by stating our main result concerning the propagation of coherent states for spatially cutoff

The self-adjointness of the Hamiltonian

2.3
The quadratic dynamics for the
P(\phi)_{2}
model

In order to study the propagation of coherent states in the classical limit, we need some information about the dynamics of time-dependent quadratic Hamiltonians. Although the results of this subsection concern the
P(\phi)_{2}
model in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{Z}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\differential k)
, we will see later that they can easily be extended to a more general case: for this reason, we prefer to state them in a rather abstract way, following
[
7
, Section 4]
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{+,k}
the space
D\left(\mathrm{N}_{1}^{k/2}\right)
equipped with the inner product

\innerproduct{\varphi}{\psi}_{\mathcal{D}_{+,k}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)^{k}\innerproduct{\varphi^{(n)}}{\psi^{(n)}}\,,

and by
\mathcal{D}_{-,k}
the completion of
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
with respect to the inner product

\innerproduct{\varphi}{\psi}_{\mathcal{D}_{-,k}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)^{-k}\innerproduct{\varphi^{(n)}}{\psi^{(n)}}\,.

This provides the Hilbert rigging

\mathcal{D}_{+,k}\subset\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})\subset\mathcal{D}_{-,k}\,.

We also define

\mathcal{D}_{+,\infty}=\displaystyle\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{D}_{+,k}\,.

Let
\Big(\mathfrak{Q}_{t}\Big)_{t\in I}
be a family of self-adjoint operators on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
. A family of unitary operators
\left(U(t,s)\right)_{t,s\,\in\,I}
is said to be a unitary propagator of the problem

(2.27)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{i}\,\partial_{t}u_{t}=\mathfrak{Q}_{t}\,u_{t}\\
\evaluated{u_t}_{t=0}=u_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{+,k}\end{array}\right.

if it satisfies:

1.

U(t,t)=\mathbbm{1}
for all
t\in I
,

2.

U(t,r)\circ U(r,s)=U(t,s)
for all
r,s,t\in I
,

3.

For each
s\in I
, we have
U(\bullet,s)\in C^{0}\big(I,\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{+,k})\big)\cap C^{1}\big(I,\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{+,k},\mathcal{D}_{-,k})\big)
and, for all
u_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{+,k}
,

\mathrm{i}\,\partial_{t}U(t,s)\,u_{0}=\mathfrak{Q}_{t}\,U(t,s)\,u_{0}\,.

Here,
C^{k}(I,\mathfrak{B})
denotes the space of
k
-continuously differentiable
\mathfrak{B}
-valued functions where
\mathfrak{B}
is endowed with the strong operator topology.

2.3.1

We now return to the
P(\phi)_{2}
model introduced in Subsection
2.1
. From now on, we assume that the spatial cutoff

where

which corresponds to the quadratic polynomial

appearing in the Taylor expansion
(
1.61
)
. We recall that

is the mild solution of
(
2.13
)
given by Corollary
2.10
. The aim of this paragraph is to prove the existence of a unique unitary propagator for the operators
\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(\omega\right)+\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(F_{V_{2}(t)}\right)

We will use the result

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be a closed interval and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
be fixed. Let
(\mathfrak{Q}_{t})_{t\in I}
be a family of self-adjoint operators on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
such that
\mathfrak{Q}_{t}\in\mathcal{L}\big(\mathcal{D}_{+,k},\mathcal{D}_{-,k}\big)
. We assume that

1.

the map

is continuous,

2.

there exists a continuous function

such that, for all

and

(2.33)

\absolutevalue{\braket{(\nbop[1]+1)^k \varphi}{\mathfrak{Q}_t \psi} - \braket{\mathfrak{Q}_t \varphi}{(\nbop[1]+1)^k \psi}}\leqslant f_{k}(t)\,\norm{\varphi}_{\mathcal{D}_{+,k}}\,\norm{\psi}_{\mathcal{D}_{+,k}}\,.

Then, the Cauchy problem
(
2.27
)
admits a unique unitary propagator
(U(t,s))_{t,s\,\in\,I}
.

Let us remark that we have

F_{\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)}^{\mathfrak{c}_{t}}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\innerproduct{\tensn{(z+\conjug_t z)}{2}}{\tilde{V}_2(t)}

where
\mathfrak{c}_{t}z=\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}t\omega}\mathfrak{c}z
and
\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(\omega\right)}\,V_{2}(t)
. The assumption
\Gamma\left(\mathfrak{c}\right)V_{2}(t)=V_{2}(t)
combined with the identity
\mathfrak{c}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t\omega}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\omega}\,\mathfrak{c}
implies

(2.34)

\Gamma\left(\mathfrak{c}_{t}\right)\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)=\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)\,.

Thus, applying Theorem
1.1
with
\mathfrak{c}
replaced by
\mathfrak{c}_{t}
and following Proposition
3.1
, we obtain that the operators
\mathfrak{Q}_{t}
given in
(
2.31
)
are essentially self-adjoint. We keep the same notation for their closure. Let us now verify that these operators fulfill the assumptions of Theorem
2.12
.

1.

For all
T>0
and

, the estimate

(2.35)

\norm{\tensn{(\varphi_t + \conjug\varphi_t)}{j-2} - \tensn{(\varphi_s + \conjug\varphi_s)}{j-2}}_{2}\leqslant 2\,(j-2)\,\norm{\varphi_t - \varphi_s}_{2}\left(\underset{\absolutevalue{\tau}\leqslant T}{\sup}\left(2\norm{\varphi_\tau}_{2}\right)\right)^{j-3}\,,

holding for all

\norm{V_2(t)-V_2(s)}_{2}\leqslant\sqrt{2}\,\norm{\varphi_t-\varphi_s}_{2}\,\sum_{j=3}^{2n}\,j\,(j-1)\,(j-2)\,2^{-\frac{j}{2}}\,\absolutevalue{\beta_j}\left(\underset{\absolutevalue{\tau}\leqslant T}{\sup}(2\norm{\varphi_\tau}_{2})\right)^{j-3}\norm{w_j}_{2}\,,

which shows that the map
t\longmapsto\norm{V_2(t)}_{2}
is continuous. Let us recall that
w_{j}
denotes the Schwartz kernel given by
(
2.9
)
. Now, let us write

(2.36)

F_{\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)}^{\mathfrak{c}_{t}}=b_{2,0}(t)+b_{0,2}(t)+b_{1,1}(t)\,,

where the polynomial symbols
b_{p,q}(t)\in\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(\mathcal{Z})
,

are associated with the operators

\widetilde{b}_{0,2}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\ket{\tilde{V}_2(t)}\,,\quad\widetilde{b}_{1,1}(t)=\sqrt{2}\,\Big(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\bra{\conjug_t \, \bullet}\Big)\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\widetilde{b}_{2,0}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\bra{\tilde{V}_2(t)}\,.

For all

(2.37)

\displaystyle\norm{\Wick[\eps]{F_{\tilde{V}_2(t)}^{\conjug_t}} \psi}_{\mathcal{D}_{-,k}}^{2}\leqslant

\displaystyle\phantom{3\,\Big(}+\norm{\Wick[\eps]{b_{0,2}(t)} \psi}_{\mathcal{D}_{-,k}}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{3\,\Big(}+\norm{\Wick[\eps]{b_{1,1}(t)} \psi}_{\mathcal{D}_{-,k}}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\,9\,\varepsilon^{2}\,\norm{V_2(t)}_{2}^{2}\,\norm{\psi}_{\mathcal{D}_{+,k}}^{2}\,.

Similarly, for all

, we have

\norm{\Wick[\eps]{F_{\tilde{V}_2(t)}^{\conjug_t}}\psi- \Wick[\eps]{F_{\tilde{V}_2(s)}^{\conjug_s}} \psi}_{\mathcal{D}_{-,k}}^{2}\leqslant\,9\,\varepsilon^{2}\,\norm{V_2(t)-V_2(s)}_{2}^{2}\,\norm{\psi}_{\mathcal{D}_{+,k}}^{2}.

Thus, the map

2.

We now establish an estimate of the form
(
2.33
)

\left[(\mathrm{N}_{1}+1)^{k},\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)\right]\psi^{(n)}=\left((n+q-p+1)^{k}-(n+1)^{k}\right)\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right)\psi^{(n)}

with

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon\,\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}_{t}=\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(F_{\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)}^{\mathfrak{c}_{t}}\right)\,\widetilde{u}_{t}\\

admits a unique unitary propagator
\big(\widetilde{U}_{2}^{\circ}(t,s)\big)_{t,s\,\in\,\mathbb{R}}
. Furthermore, we have the estimate

(2.40)

\norm{\tilde{U}_2^\circ(t,s)}_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{+,k})}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{2\,\sqrt{2}\,k\,3^{k/2}\,\absolutevalue{\int_s^t \norm{V_2(\tau)} \dd\tau}}\,.

Finally, if we set
U_{2}^{\circ}(t,s)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(\omega\right)}\,\widetilde{U}_{2}^{\circ}(t,s)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{s}{\varepsilon}\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(\omega\right)}
, the map

is a mild solution of the Cauchy problem

(2.41)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon\,\partial_{t}u_{t}=\left(\differential\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(\omega\right)+\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(F_{V_{2}(t)}\right)\right)u_{t}\\
\evaluated{u_t}_{t=0}=u_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{+,k}\,.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness of

follow from Theorem

. The proof of the operator norm estimate
(

)
is analogous to the one of
(

)
, since the differentiation of the quantity

in the sense of quadratic form yields

\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon\,\dot{u}(t)=\innerproduct{\tilde{U}_2^\circ(t,0)\,\psi}{\comm{(\nbop[1]+1)^k}{\Wick[\eps]{F_{\tilde{V}_2(t)}^{\conjug_t}}}\, \tilde{U}_2^\circ(t,0)\,\psi}\,.

The Grönwall lemma ends the proof.
∎

2.3.2
Quadratic propagation of a polynomial observable

We now state a result due to S. Breteaux
[

concerning the propagation of a polynomial observable under a time-dependent quadratic Hamiltonian.

Let us write

F_{\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)}^{\mathfrak{c}_{t}}(z)=\innerproduct{z}{\alpha_t \, z}+\imaginary\innerproduct{\beta_t}{\tensn{z}{2}}

where
\beta_{t}=-\mathrm{i}\,\sqrt{2}\,\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R},\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\mathcal{Z})
, and
\alpha_{t}=\sqrt{2}\,\Big(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\bra{\conjug_t \, \bullet}\Big)\,\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)
defines a family of bounded, strongly continuous self-adjoint operators. Applying
[
29
, Theorem X.69]
, we obtain that the assumptions

and

of
[
11
, Section 3, page 82]
are satisfied by

The classical evolution associated with this operator is given by

(2.42)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{i}\,\dot{\widetilde{z}}_{t}=\partial_{\overline{z}}F_{\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)}^{\mathfrak{c}_{t}}(\widetilde{z}_{t})\\
\evaluated{\tilde{z}_t}_{t = 0}=z_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}\,,\end{array}\right.

where

\partial_{\overline{z}}F_{\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)}^{\mathfrak{c}_{t}}(u)=\sqrt{2}\,\big(\bra{(u+\conjug_t u)}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\big)\widetilde{V}_{2}(t)

is
\mathbb{R}
-linear with respect to

. The inequality

3.2

In the case of the

Let

and

\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(F_{R_{3}(t)}\right)=\sum_{\ell=3}^{N+2-j}\varepsilon^{\frac{\ell}{2}}\,\mathrm{Wick}_{1}\left(F_{V_{\ell}(t)}\right)+\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(F_{R_{N+3-j}(t)}\right)

where
R_{N+3-j}(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=N+3-j}^{\infty}V_{\ell}(t)\in\displaystyle\bigoplus_{\ell=N+3-j}^{\infty}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{\ell}\mathcal{Z}
.
Using the explicit expression
(
2.48
)
of
\psi_{N}(t)
and Theorem
2.14
, as well as the definition
(
2.61
)
of the polynomial symbols
b_{k}(t)
, we thus obtain

(3.31)

\dot{\Theta}_{N}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{\varepsilon}H_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\delta(t)}{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\varepsilon}\varphi_{t}\right)\times\\
\left(\sum_{j=0}^{N}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}\,\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(F_{R_{N+3-j}(t)}\right)\,U_{2}(t,0)\,\mathrm{Wick}_{1}\left(b_{j}(t)\right)\psi\right)\,.

Let us now conclude the proof of Theorem
1.6
. The left-hand side of
(
1.45
)
is dominated by

\int_{0}^{\absolutevalue{t}}\norm{\dot{\Theta}_N(s)}\,\differential s\leqslant\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\absolutevalue{t}}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}\,\norm{\Wick[\eps]{F_{R_{N+3-j}(s)}}\, U_2(s,0)\ \Wick[1]{b_j(s)} \psi}\,\differential s\,.

Since
V
satisfies
(H3)
with constants
\alpha>0
,
\lambda>1
, the sequence
\Big(V_{\ell}(t)\Big)_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies
(H3)
with constants
0<\gamma<\alpha
and
\lambda>1
.

Thus, we can apply
(
3.25
)
to the potentials
R_{N+3-j}(s)\in\displaystyle\bigoplus_{n=N+3-j}^{+\infty}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}^{n}\mathcal{Z}
and to the functions
\mathrm{Wick}_{1}\left(b_{j}(s)\right)\psi\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})
, which yields

\displaystyle\norm{\Wick[\eps]{F_{R_{N+3-j}(s)}} \, U_2(s,0) \Wick[1]{b_j(s)} \psi}^{2}\leqslant

\displaystyle C\,\varepsilon^{N+3-j}\norm{\e^{\gamma\lambda^{\nbop[1]}} R_{N+3-j}(s)}^{2}\times

\displaystyle\bigg(\norm{\mathrm{\bf g}_s^{1/2}\, \Wick[1]{b_j(s)} \psi}^{2}

\displaystyle+c\,\mathrm{g}_{s}^{\prime}(c)\int_{0}^{\absolutevalue{s}}\norm{V_2(\tau)}\differential\tau\norm{\Wick[1]{b_j(s)} \psi}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C^{\prime}(s,\psi,N,j)\,\varepsilon^{N+3-j}

with
C^{\prime}(s,\psi,N,j)
continuous with respect to
s
and
\mathrm{g}_{s}
given by
(
3.24
)
. Recall that
\mathrm{\bf g}_{s}
is defined like in
(
3.7
)
. Eventually, the left-hand side of
(
1.45
)
is dominated by

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\absolutevalue{t}}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}\,C(s,\psi,N,j)\cdot\varepsilon^{\frac{N+3-j}{2}}\differential s=C(t,\psi,N)\cdot\varepsilon^{\frac{N+1}{2}}\,,

which concludes the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

\square

Appendix A
Weyl operators and Number estimates

The aim of this appendix is to establish the Number-Weyl estimates
(
A.1
)
. Such estimates are standard, see for instance
[
14
, Section 3.5]
for further discussion. However, the proof of Proposition
3.10
requires applying
(
A.1
)
with a sharp quantitative bound tailored to our needs. For this reason, we prefer to include a detailed proof.

Let us denote by

\mathbb{C}_{+}=\{z\in\mathbb{C},\real(z)\geqslant 0\}

the closed right half-plane. For all
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, let
D\Big((\mathrm{N}_{1}+1)^{z}\Big)
be the domain of the closure of the operator
\Big((\mathrm{N}_{1}+1)^{z},\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})\Big)
.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
,
u\in\mathcal{Z}\,\setminus\,\{0\}
and
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
be fixed. Then, the Weyl operator
\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)
preserves the domain
D\Big((\mathrm{N}_{1}+1)^{z}\Big)
. Furthermore, we have an estimate of the form

(A.1)

\norm{(\nbop[1]+1)^z\, \weylop[\eps]{u}\, (\nbop[1]+1)^{-z}}\leqslant M(\absolutevalue{u})\,\absolutevalue{u}^{\real(z)}\,2^{\real(z)^{2}}\,,

where
M(\absolutevalue{u})
is an
\varepsilon
-independent constant.

We state a technical lemma that provides the estimate
(
A.1
)
for
z\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, we deduce the general case thanks to a complex interpolation argument.

Proof for integers

Lemma A.2
.

For all
u\in\mathcal{Z}\,\setminus\,\{0\}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, the operator

(A.2)

T_{k}=\left(\mathrm{N}_{1}+1+\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathrm{i}u\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\absolutevalue{u}^{2}\right)^{k}\,(\mathrm{N}_{1}+1)^{-k}

is bounded on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
. Furthermore, we have an estimate of the form

(A.3)

\norm{T_k}\leqslant M^{\prime}(\absolutevalue{u})\,\absolutevalue{u}^{k}\,2^{k^{2}}\,,

where
M^{\prime}(\absolutevalue{u})
is an
\varepsilon
-independent constant.

Before jumping into the proof of this result, let us explain how to obtain Theorem
A.1
in the case
z=k\in\mathbb{N}
. We recall that the space
\mathscr{H}_{0}
defined by
(
1.30
)
is a core for the Wick operators
\mathrm{Wick}_{\varepsilon}\left(b\right),\,b\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
. In particular, we have
\mathscr{H}_{0}\subset\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)
and
\mathscr{H}_{0}\subset D(\mathrm{N}_{\varepsilon}^{k})
for all
u\in\mathcal{Z}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The translation property
(
1.31
)
yields

(A.4)

\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)^{*}\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(v\right)\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)=\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(v\right)+\varepsilon\,\imaginary\innerproduct{u}{v}\mathbbm{1}

and

(A.5)

\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)^{*}\mathrm{N}_{\varepsilon}\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)=\mathrm{N}_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\,\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathrm{i}u\right)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\,\absolutevalue{u}^{2}\mathbbm{1}

on
\mathscr{H}_{0}
. Hence, the operators
\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)^{*}\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(v\right)\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)
and
\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)^{*}\mathrm{N}_{\varepsilon}\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)
preserve
\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})
, so
\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(v\right)
and
\mathrm{N}_{1}
preserve
\mathscr{H}_{0}
. This enables us to write, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(A.6)

\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)^{*}\,(\mathrm{N}_{1}+1)^{k}\,\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)=T_{k}\,(\mathrm{N}_{1}+1)^{k}\text{ on $\mathscr{H}_{0}$}

with
T_{k}
given by
(
A.2
)
. Hence, for all
\psi\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
and
\varphi\in D(\mathrm{N}_{\varepsilon}^{k})
such that
\norm{\varphi}=1
, we have

\absolutevalue{\braket{\weylop[\eps]{u}^{*}\, (\nbop[1] + 1)^k\, \varphi}{\psi}}\leqslant\norm{T_k}\,\norm{(\nbop[1] + 1)^k \psi}\,.

Extending this inequality to
\psi\in D(\mathrm{N}_{\varepsilon}^{k})
yields, for all
\chi\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z})
,

\absolutevalue{\braket{(\nbop[1] + 1)^k \varphi}{\weylop[\eps]{u} (\nbop[1] + 1)^{-k}\, \chi}}\leqslant\norm{T_k}\norm{\chi}\,.

Finally, we obtain that
\mathrm{W}_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)\,(\mathrm{N}_{1}+1)^{-k}\,\chi\in D\Big([(\mathrm{N}_{1}+1)^{k}]^{*}\Big)=D\Big((\mathrm{N}_{1}+1)^{k}\Big)
, and the estimate
(
A.1
)
for
z=k\in\mathbb{N}
follows from
(
A.3
)
.

We now turn to the proof of Lemma
A.2
, which is inspired from the number-energy estimates
[
14
, Section 3.5]
(see also
[
4
, Proposition 3.5]
for an application of such estimates in another context). Let us define

A=\mathrm{N}_{1}+1+\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathrm{i}u\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\absolutevalue{u}^{2}\text{ and }B=(\mathrm{N}_{1}+1)^{-1}

so that
T_{k}=A^{k}B^{k}
. We define recursively

(A.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{ad}_{B}^{0}(A)=A,\\
\forall\,j\in\mathbb{N},\quad\mathrm{ad}_{B}^{j+1}(A)=\commutator{\ad[B]^j(A)}{B}\,.\end{array}\right.

From the formulas
\commutator{\fieldop[\eps]{v}}{\nbop[\eps]}=\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon\,\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(\mathrm{i}v\right)
and

\commutator{A}{B}=B\commutator{B^{-1}}{A}B\,,

we deduce that the equality

(A.8)

\mathrm{ad}_{B}(A)=\commutator{A}{B}=-\mathrm{i}\,B\,\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(u\right)B

holds on
\mathscr{H}_{0}
and extends to
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{Z}))
. This enables us to obtain by induction on
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
the formula

(A.9)

\mathrm{ad}_{B}^{j}(A)=(-\mathrm{i})^{j}\,B^{j}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(u_{j}\right)B^{j}\text{ where }u_{1}=u\text{ and }u_{j+1}=\mathrm{i}\,u_{j}\,.

We now show that the operators
T_{k},\,k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
are bounded. For
k=1
, the inequality

(A.10)

\norm{\fieldop[\eps]{u} \psi}^{2}\leqslant 2\,\varepsilon\,\absolutevalue{u}^{2}\norm{(\nbop[1]+1)^{1/2}\, \psi}^{2}

yields
\norm{T_1}\leqslant Q(\absolutevalue{u})^{1/2}
where
Q
is an
\varepsilon
-independent polynomial provided that
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
. Furthermore, the Leibniz formula

(A.11)

AB^{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\,B^{k-j}\,\mathrm{ad}_{B}^{j}(A)

and the formula
(
A.9
)
allow us to write

\displaystyle T_{k+1}=A^{k+1}B^{k+1}=

\displaystyle A^{k}(AB^{k})B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{k}B^{k}AB+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k}{j}A^{k}B^{k-j}\ \mathrm{ad}_{B}^{j}(A)\,B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{1}T_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\,(-\mathrm{i})^{j}\,\binom{k}{j}\,T_{k}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}\left(u_{j}\right)\,B^{j+1}\,.

Hence, provided that
T_{k}
is bounded, we obtain that
T_{k+1}
is bounded with

\displaystyle\norm{T_{k+1}}\leqslant

\displaystyle\norm{T_k}\big(\norm{T_1}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\ \norm{\fieldop[\eps]{u_j}(\nbop[1] + 1)^{-1}}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\norm{T_k}\big(Q(\absolutevalue{u})^{1/2}+2^{k}\cdot\sqrt{2}\,\absolutevalue{u}\big)\text{ by {{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}\hyperref@@ii[in\'{e}galit\'{e}_norme_champ_et_nombre]{(\ref*{in\'{e}galit\'{e}_norme_champ_et_nombre})}}}}\,.

From the last inequality, we deduce
(
A.3
)
. Indeed, if we define

a=Q(\absolutevalue{u})^{1/2}\geqslant 0,\quad b=\sqrt{2}\,\absolutevalue{u}>0\,,

we have for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

\displaystyle\log\big(\norm{T_k}\big)-\log\big(\norm{T_1}\big)\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\,\log(a + 2^j\, b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\,\left[j\,\log(2)+\log(b)+\log\left(1+2^{-j}\,\frac{a}{b}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\,\frac{k^{2}}{2}\,\log(2)+k\,\log(b)+\frac{a}{b}\,,

where we have used
\log(1+x)\leqslant x
. We finally obtain

\norm{T_k}\leqslant M^{\prime}(\absolutevalue{u})\,2^{k^{2}}\,\absolutevalue{u}^{k}\,,

whence
(
A.3
)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. This estimate extends to the case
k=0
up to picking
M^{\prime}(\absolutevalue{u})\geqslant 1
.

Proof in the general case

Let
u\in\mathcal{Z}\,\setminus\,\{0\}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed. For all
\varphi,\psi\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathcal{Z})
with
\norm{\varphi}=1
, the function

F_{\varphi,\psi}(w)=\innerproduct{\varphi}{(\nbop[1]+1)^w\, \weylop[\eps]{u}\, (\nbop[1]+1)^{-w}\, \psi}

can be expressed as a finite linear combination of terms of the form

(n+1)^{w}\,(m+1)^{-w}\innerproduct{\varphi^{(n)}}{\weylop[\eps]{u} \psi^{(m)}},\quad m,n\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Hence,
F_{\varphi,\psi}
is entire on
\mathbb{C}
. Applying the three-line Hadamard interpolation theorem
[
36
, Theorem 12.3, page 187]
and taking the supremum over
\varphi
, we obtain

\norm{(\nbop[1]+1)^{k+w}\, \weylop[\eps]{u}\, (\nbop[1]+1)^{-(k+w)}}\leqslant\norm{T_k}^{1-\real(w)}\norm{T_{k+1}}^{\real(w)}

for all
w\in\mathbb{C}
such that
0\leqslant\real(w)\leqslant 1
. This yields, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
\real(z)\geqslant 0
,

\norm{(\nbop[1]+1)^z\, \weylop[\eps]{u}\, (\nbop[1]+1)^{-z}}\leqslant\norm{T_k}^{1-x}\norm{T_{\ell+1}}^{x}\,,

where
k=\left\lfloor\real(z)\right\rfloor
and
k+x=\real(z)
. Applying
(
A.3
)
, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\norm{(\nbop[1]+1)^z\, \weylop[\eps]{u}\, (\nbop[1]+1)^{-z}}\leqslant

\displaystyle M^{\prime}(\absolutevalue{u})\,\absolutevalue{u}^{k(1-x)+(k+1)x}\,2^{k^{2}(1-x)+(k+1)^{2}x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M^{\prime}(\absolutevalue{u})\,\absolutevalue{u}^{\real(z)}\,2^{\real(z)^{2}+(x-x^{2})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle M(\absolutevalue{u})\,\absolutevalue{u}^{\real(z)}\,2^{\real(z)^{2}}\text{ where }M(\absolutevalue{u})=2^{\frac{1}{4}}\,M^{\prime}(\absolutevalue{u})\,.

This concludes the proof of Theorem
A.1
.
