Cryopreservation of Aseel rooster sperm using different glycerol–iodixanol combinations and evaluation of post-thaw quality
Authors: Ebuderda Günay, Ramazan Arıcı, Hatice Şenlikci, Selin Yağcıoğlu, Ahmet Eser, Kamber Demir, Serhat Alkan, Kemal Ak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCryopreservation of avian semen is a critical tool for genetic conservation and poultry breeding programs. However, the use of glycerol, the most common penetrating cryoprotectant, is associated with dose-dependent impairments in sperm function and potential contraceptive effects in the female reproductive tract. In this study, we investigated the potential of iodixanol, a non-penetrating and biocompatible medium, to improve post-thaw sperm quality when used alone or in combination with reduced glycerol concentrations. Semen samples from ten Aseel roosters were pooled and cryopreserved (n = 10) using Beltsville Poultry Semen Extender supplemented with varying concentrations of glycerol (2.5% or 5%) and iodixanol (0%, 2.5%, or 5%) in different combinations. Post-thaw sperm quality was assessed using computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA), eosin-nigrosin staining, hypoosmotic swelling test (HOST), and flow cytometry to evaluate mitochondrial activity, plasma membrane integrity, acrosome damage, apoptosis, and chromatin structure.ResultsExtenders containing 5% glycerol yielded the highest total and progressive motility and improved plasma membrane integrity. In low-glycerol groups, iodixanol supplementation significantly improved total motility, while membrane integrity and mitochondrial activity showed numerical improvement, with the 2.5% glycerol + 2.5% iodixanol group demonstrating the most favorable overall outcomes. Flow cytometric analysis further supported these findings by showing improved mitochondrial membrane potential and plasma membrane integrity, indicative of reduced cryodamage.ConclusionsIodixanol enhances the cryoprotective efficacy of extenders, particularly under low-glycerol conditions, by improving motility, membrane integrity, and mitochondrial activity. The combined use of glycerol and iodixanol may provide a more effective cryopreservation strategy for Aseel rooster sperm, thereby supporting genetic conservation programs and improving artificial insemination outcomes in poultry breeding.

Introduction

Cryopreservation of sperm is one of the fundamental applications of reproductive biotechnologies in poultry, offering broad utility in genetic material preservation, supporting endangered species, and facilitating commercial production processes [1–3]. This technique enables the long-term storage of genetic material and plays a critical role in advancing reproductive technologies [4–6]. Aseel roosters, a native breed originating from the Indian subcontinent, are valued in ornamental and traditional poultry production because of their distinctive physical characteristics, fighting ability, and resilience; thus, the development of efficient cryopreservation protocols is important to support the conservation and dissemination of this unique genetic resource and to help maintain its valuable traits over time [7, 8].

The cryopreservation process allows the storage of sperm at low temperatures; however, it also induces physicochemical stress, resulting in reduced motility, viability, and membrane integrity [9–11]. Glycerol, a widely used cryoprotectant in poultry semen cryopreservation, is commonly used at concentrations ranging from approximately 4% to 11%, depending on the protocol and species, and plays a key role in reducing cryoinjury during freezing; nevertheless, it has also been reported to exert contraceptive effects within the female reproductive tract and to negatively affect sperm function [12, 13]. Therefore, determining optimal glycerol concentrations and investigating alternative cryoprotectants to alleviate these adverse effects are of great importance.

In recent years, cryoprotectants such as iodixanol have been shown to reduce the harmful effects of cryopreservation by preserving sperm membrane integrity and functional properties [14, 15]. Iodixanol, originally developed as a non-ionic X-ray contrast medium, has more recently been utilized as a density gradient medium for the separation and isolation of viable cells due to its non-toxic, osmotically inert, and biocompatible nature [16]. Its cryoprotective potential has been demonstrated in mammalian spermatozoa, where iodixanol supplementation in freezing extenders has been associated with improved post-thaw motility, better membrane integrity, reduced oxidative damage, and enhanced sperm survival, partly through modification of glass transition temperature and ice crystal formation [14, 15]. However, studies on the effects of iodixanol on avian sperm are very limited, and to the best of our knowledge, there is currently no published research investigating its use in rooster semen cryopreservation. In particular, it is hypothesized that combining iodixanol with glycerol may enhance post-thaw sperm quality by mitigating the toxic effects of high glycerol concentrations while improving overall cryoprotection [17, 18].

This study aims to evaluate the effects of glycerol and iodixanol combinations on the spermatological characteristics of Aseel rooster sperm during cryopreservation. The results of this study are expected to provide valuable insights for achieving higher success rates in artificial insemination and reproductive biotechnologies.

Materials and methods

Animals and housing conditions

The study utilized 10 Aseel roosters (Gallus gallus domesticus) aged 2 years, with body weights between 2.1 and 3.4 kg. The roosters were housed individually under standard conditions at the Istanbul University-Cerrahpaşa Veterinary Faculty, Department of Artificial Insemination and Reproduction. The animals belong to this department, and the necessary permissions for the use of animals were obtained prior to the study. A commercial poultry diet and clean water were provided ad libitum. The facility maintained a controlled environment with a temperature of 20–22 °C and a 14-hour light/10-hour dark photoperiod. Semen samples were collected during the spring season (March–May).

Semen processing and cryopreservation

Semen samples were collected twice weekly using the abdominal massage technique, ensuring non-invasive collection. Initial sperm motility was assessed subjectively under light microscopy, and samples were pooled (n = 10) to minimize individual variability and included only if initial motility was ≥ 90% [19].

Beltsville Poultry Semen Extender (BPSE) served as the base cryopreservation medium [20]. BPSE was prepared by sequentially dissolving the following components in 100 mL of ultrapure water: monosodium glutamate (0.5202 g), potassium hydrogen citrate monohydrate (0.0384 g), D-fructose (0.3 g), N-Tris(hydroxymethyl)methyl-2-aminoethanesulfonic acid (TES, 0.3170 g), potassium dihydrogen diphosphate trihydrate (0.0390 g), potassium dihydrogen phosphate (0.7620 g), anhydrous sodium acetate (0.2580 g), and magnesium chloride (0.0204 g). The pH was adjusted to 7.2, and the osmolality was maintained at 320 ± 5 mOsm/kg.

After dilution at room temperature, the samples were allocated into experimental groups and loaded into 0.25 mL mini straws. The straws were placed on racks at room temperature and cooled to 5 °C at a rate of 0.4 °C/min in a programmable automatic freezer (Cryo Bio System Groupe I.M.V. Technologies Nano-Digitcool), followed by an equilibration period of 25 min within the device. After equilibration, the straws were frozen to -35 °C at a rate of 7 °C/min and then to -140 °C at a rate of 60 °C/min using a modified protocol based on previous rooster semen cryopreservation studies [21, 22]. At the end of the freezing process, the straws were removed from the device and immediately plunged into liquid nitrogen. The straws were subsequently placed into separate goblets according to the study week and stored in liquid nitrogen at -196 °C. After at least 2 weeks of storage, the straws were thawed in a 37 °C water bath for 30 s, and the thawed samples were immediately subjected to spermatological analyses [22, 23].

The experimental design involved ten independent replicates (n = 10), with each replicate comprising semen collection from all ten roosters, followed by cryopreservation and subsequent post-thaw quality assessment. This replicate structure ensured sufficient statistical power while maintaining animal welfare standards. All animal handling and experimental procedures were performed in compliance with institutional guidelines for the ethical use of animals in research. Importantly, this study employed a non-terminal design; no animals were subjected to euthanasia upon study completion. All roosters remained healthy throughout the experimental period and were subsequently returned to standard housing conditions where they continued their normal physiological and reproductive activities.

Post-thaw sperm analysis

Assessment of sperm motility

Computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA; SCA®, Sperm Class Analyser, Microptic SL, Barcelona) was employed to evaluate sperm motility and kinetic parameters, including total motility (MOT), progressive motility (pMOT), curvilinear velocity (VCL), straight-line velocity (VSL), and average path velocity (VAP). CASA software was calibrated to analyze at least 200 sperm per field across five fields for each sample [24]. The percentage of motile sperm was defined as the proportion of spermatozoa exhibiting an average path velocity (VAP) > 5 μm/s, while progressive cells were classified as those with a VAP > 50 μm/s and straightness index (STR) > 75%. The CASA system settings were as follows: Area (µm²) (min): 5, Area (µm²) (max): 170, VAP points (pixels): 5, and Connectivity (pixels): 18.

Assessment of plasma membrane functionality

The hypoosmotic swelling (HOS) test was performed to evaluate sperm plasma membrane functionality under hypotonic conditions (100 mOsm/kg). The HOS solution was prepared by dissolving 1 g of sodium citrate in 100 mL of distilled water and the osmolarity was adjusted to approximately 100 mOsm/kg and verified using an osmometer (Vapro 5500, Wescor Inc., South Logan, UT, USA). Semen samples (25 µL) were incubated in 500 µL of HOS solution for 30 min at 25 °C, placed on a pre-warmed slide (37 °C), and fixed with 2% buffered glutaraldehyde. Spermatozoa showing tail coiling or swelling were considered HOS-positive. A total of 200 cells were evaluated under a phase-contrast microscope at 100× magnification [14].

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry was used to assess plasma membrane integrity, acrosome damge, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), apoptosis, and chromatin structure in post-thawed spermatozoa. All analyses were performed using a Guava® EasyCyte™ Flow Cytometer (Luminex Corporation, Austin, TX, USA) equipped with InCyte™ software. A minimum of 10,000 events per sample was recorded.

A gating strategy was applied to ensure accurate identification of sperm populations. Initially, debris was excluded based on forward scatter and side scatter characteristics. The resulting singlet population was used for fluorescence analyses. Fluorescence compensation was adjusted using single-stained controls, and quadrant gates were defined based on unstained and fluorescence-minus-one controls.

For each assay, fluorescence signals were collected in the FL1 (green, ~ 530 nm) and FL3 (red, ~ 610 nm) channels. Acrosome damage (PNA–FITC), membrane integrity (PI), mitochondrial activity (JC-1), and apoptosis (Annexin V–FITC/PI) were evaluated using assay-specific gating within the singlet population. Data were analyzed using standardized quadrant regions to distinguish viable, damaged, and functionally distinct sperm subpopulations.

Acrosome damage was assessed using peanut agglutinin conjugated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (PNA–FITC; PNA-FITC Lectin, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. L7381). Diluted sperm samples were incubated with 2.5 µg/mL PNA–FITC for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, after which fluorescence was analyzed. Cells exhibiting green fluorescence were classified as acrosome-damaged, whereas non-fluorescent cells were considered acrosome-intact [25].

Plasma membrane integrity was evaluated using propidium iodide (PI; Propidium Iodide, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. P4170). Thawed sperm samples were stained for 15 min at 37 °C and examined immediately. PI-negative cells were regarded as possessing intact plasma membranes, whereas PI-positive cells were considered membrane-damaged [25, 26]. Mitochondrial activity was measured using the JC-1 dye (JC-1; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. T4069). Spermatozoa were incubated with 3 µM JC-1 dye for 30 min at 37 °C, protected from light. The ratio of red (aggregated JC-1) to green (monomeric JC-1) fluorescence was calculated to determine mitochondrial activity [25, 27].

Apoptotic changes were assessed using Annexin V–FITC and PI according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with PI; Biolegend Cat. No. 640914). After staining for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, spermatozoa were categorized as viable (Annexin V⁻/PI⁻), early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻) or late apoptotic/necrotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺), and the relative proportions of these populations were recorded [28].

Chromatin integrity was evaluated using the Sperm Chromatin Structure Assay (SCSA) following the protocol described by Donald P. Evenson [29]. Spermatozoa were exposed to an acidic denaturation buffer followed by staining with acridine orange (Acridine Orange, Cat. No. A6014), which emits green fluorescence when bound to double-stranded DNA and red fluorescence when bound to denatured single-stranded DNA. The percentage of spermatozoa exhibiting high DNA stainability (HDS) was calculated as an indicator of chromatin condensation abnormalities [29].

Assessment of viability and morphological integrity

Sperm viability and morphological integrity were evaluated using the eosin-nigrosin staining technique. Semen samples were mixed with eosin stain and smears were prepared on glass slides. A total of 200 spermatozoa were examined under a light microscope at 100× magnification. Unstained cells were considered viable. Morphological abnormalities were classified according to the affected region (acrosome, head, midpiece, or tail), and the percentages of dead and abnormal spermatozoa were recorded [30].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 25.0). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test data normality. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to compare groups. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Post-thaw CASA findings are presented in Table 1. Total motility percentages revealed significant differences among the groups. The lowest total motility was observed in Group 4, which contained 2.5% glycerol without iodixanol (p < 0.05). Groups 5 and 6, which included iodixanol at 2.5% and 5% concentrations respectively, exhibited moderate improvements in total motility compared to Group 4 (p < 0.05). In contrast, Groups 1, 2, and 3 containing 5% glycerol, demonstrated the highest total motility percentages, with no significant differences between them (p > 0.05). Progressive motility was significantly greater in the 5% glycerol groups (Groups 1–3) than in the 2.5% glycerol groups (Groups 4–6) (p < 0.05). Within each glycerol level, however, supplementation with iodixanol at 2.5% or 5% did not significantly affect progressive motility (p > 0.05). Kinetic parameters such as curvilinear velocity (VCL), straight-line velocity (VSL), and average path velocity (VAP) showed varying trends. VCL values were significantly lower in Group 4 compared to Group 3 (p < 0.05). Similarly, VAP and VSL values were reduced in Group 4 compared to Groups 2 and 3 (p < 0.05). STR and LIN values followed a similar pattern, being significantly lower in Group 4 compared to Groups 1 and 2 (p < 0.05). ALH values showed no significant differences among groups (p > 0.05).

The results of the post-thaw flow cytometric analyses are presented in Table 2; Fig. 1A–H. The highest plasma membrane integrity was observed in Groups 2 and 3, whereas the lowest value was found in Group 4 (p < 0.05). Although Groups 5 and 6 showed numerically higher plasma membrane integrity values than Group 4, these differences were not statistically significant, and both groups remained lower than the 5% glycerol groups. However, within the 2.5% glycerol groups, iodixanol supplementation did not result in a statistically significant difference in acrosome damage (p > 0.05). Early and late apoptotic sperm percentages showed no significant differences among the groups (p > 0.05). Similarly, SCSA analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in HDS (%) values among the experimental groups (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Cryopreservation is a crucial technique in reproductive biotechnology, enabling the preservation of genetic material and supporting artificial insemination programs in poultry [5, 31]. However, the process induces significant cellular damage, primarily due to osmotic stress, intracellular ice formation, and oxidative stress, which collectively compromise sperm viability, motility, and membrane integrity [10, 32]. In this study, the effects of different concentrations of glycerol and iodixanol on post-thaw sperm quality in Aseel roosters were evaluated, with findings highlighting the critical role of cryoprotectant combinations in mitigating cryo-induced damage.

Glycerol is the most widely used penetrating cryoprotectant for avian sperm, primarily due to.

its ability to prevent ice crystal formation and reduce osmotic stress [2, 33]. However, its application remains controversial as high concentrations have been associated with reduced fertilization success, likely due to its toxic effects on sperm membranes and its contraceptive properties in the female reproductive tract [12, 13, 34–36]. Consistent with previous studies, the present study demonstrated that 5% glycerol provided better cryoprotection than 2.5% glycerol, resulting in significantly higher total and progressive motility, as well as improved membrane integrity [37, 38]. This aligns with reports indicating that higher glycerol concentrations facilitate better osmotic balance, thereby reducing sperm cryodamage and improving post-thaw survival [39, 40]. In the present study, 5% glycerol was selected as a relatively protective concentration based on its established use in poultry semen cryopreservation, whereas 2.5% glycerol was included to evaluate whether reducing glycerol exposure could lessen its known adverse and contraceptive effects while still maintaining acceptable post-thaw sperm quality. However, the present findings showed that the lower glycerol concentration did not provide sufficient cryoprotection, as reflected by reduced motility and structural integrity [14, 15].

The inclusion of iodixanol, a non-penetrating cryoprotectant, was examined as a potential strategy to enhance cryosurvival by stabilizing sperm membranes and mitigating osmotic stress. Previous studies in mammalian species, particularly in canine spermatozoa, have reported that iodixanol may protect sperm cells during cryopreservation by modulating ice formation and preserving membrane integrity during the freeze-thaw process [14, 15]. The results of the present study showed that iodixanol improved total motility and mitochondrial activity, particularly in the low-glycerol groups, suggesting that it may counteract some of the deleterious effects associated with reduced glycerol concentrations [18, 37]. Similar protective effects have been reported in bull and ram sperm cryopreservation, where iodixanol supplementation enhanced motility, membrane stability, and post-thaw sperm longevity [14–17]. However, in the present study, iodixanol supplementation did not significantly improve progressive motility or reduce apoptosis, indicating that its primary role may be structural rather than functional [41, 42]. Nevertheless, it notably enhanced total motility, mitochondrial activity, and membrane integrity, particularly in the low-glycerol groups. These findings suggest that iodixanol mitigates some of the detrimental effects associated with cryopreservation, likely by stabilizing sperm membranes and reducing osmotic stress. The observed benefits align with previous reports in mammalian species but also highlight potential species-specific differences in sperm physiology and cryotolerance [15, 31, 32, 43].

Total motility is one of the most critical parameters in sperm quality assessment, as it directly influences fertilization potential and overall reproductive success [32, 43]. The results of this study demonstrated that iodixanol supplementation significantly improved total motility, particularly in the low-glycerol groups, mitigating the adverse effects of reduced glycerol concentrations. In the 2.5% glycerol group (group 4), total motility was the lowest, highlighting the detrimental impact of insufficient cryoprotectant levels on sperm movement. However, the addition of 2.5% iodixanol (group 5) increased total motility to 42.04 ± 5.16%, while 5% iodixanol (group 6) resulted in a total motility of 34.45 ± 3.01%, demonstrating a significant improvement over the control group (p < 0.05). These findings align with previous studies reporting that iodixanol stabilizes sperm membranes and reduces osmotic stress, ultimately enhancing post-thaw motility [14–17]. Although total motility in iodixanol-supplemented low-glycerol groups did not reach the levels observed in the 5% glycerol groups (groups 1, 2, and 3, all above 57%), the observed enhancement suggests that iodixanol may compensate for some of the cryoinjuries associated with lower glycerol concentrations. This improvement could be attributed to iodixanol’s role in maintaining plasma membrane integrity and mitochondrial function, as previously suggested in mammalian sperm cryopreservation studies [32, 43]. These findings indicate that iodixanol supplementation provides a protective effect against cryodamage, particularly when glycerol concentrations are suboptimal, making it a promising additive for sperm cryopreservation protocols in poultry species.

Because live spermatozoa and acrosomal status were evaluated using different methodologies, some discrepancies were observed between the results obtained by eosin-nigrosin staining and flow cytometry. While flow cytometry revealed significant differences among groups, eosin-nigrosin staining produced more uniform findings. This discrepancy may be explained by the higher sensitivity of flow cytometry for detecting plasma membrane integrity, viability, and acrosomal status compared with conventional microscopic staining methods. In addition, most acrosomal defects were observed in non-viable spermatozoa, and the lower contrast and more subjective nature of microscopic evaluation may have limited the accuracy of acrosomal integrity assessment. Similarly, Öztürkler et al. [44] reported that morphological defect rates may vary depending on the evaluation method, with Hancock’s solution fixation yielding lower defect percentages than eosin-nigrosin and nigrosin staining. Taken together, these findings suggest that advanced techniques such as flow cytometry may provide a more sensitive and precise assessment of sperm membrane integrity and viability than traditional staining-based methods.

Mitochondrial activity plays a crucial role in sperm motility and overall viability, making it a key parameter in evaluating cryopreservation efficiency [32, 43]. The highest mitochondrial activity was observed in 5% glycerol groups supplemented with iodixanol, reinforcing the idea that glycerol concentration and membrane stabilizers work synergistically to protect mitochondrial function [41, 45]. Cryopreservation-induced oxidative stress is known to impair mitochondrial function, leading to reduced ATP production and motility loss [24, 36]. The observed improvement in mitochondrial activity with iodixanol supplementation is consistent with reports indicating that non-penetrating cryoprotectants stabilize mitochondrial membranes, reducing oxidative stress and ATP depletion [17, 32, 42].

The preservation of acrosomal damage is equally important, as acrosomal damage negatively impacts sperm fertilization potential [46, 47]. In the present study, acrosome damage was evaluated by PNA-FITC staining, where fluorescent cells were classified as acrosome-damaged. The results demonstrated that iodixanol supplementation in the 5% glycerol groups significantly reduced acrosome damage, particularly in Group 2 compared to Group 1 (p < 0.05).

This finding indicates a protective effect of iodixanol on acrosomal structure during cryopreservation. Cryodamage to the acrosome is a major factor limiting fertilization success in frozen-thawed sperm, emphasizing the need for cryoprotectant combinations that maintain acrosomal structure [32, 48]. Reduced acrosome damage observed in iodixanol-supplemented groups is consistent with previous studies in ram and bull sperm, where membrane-stabilizing agents were shown to preserve acrosomal integrity by minimizing osmotic and oxidative stress during freezing and thawing [14, 17, 47].

The HDS (%) values obtained via SCSA analysis serve as a critical biomarker reflecting the effects of cryopreservation on sperm chromatin structure [49–51]. Although HDS levels among the groups were not statistically significant, lower HDS levels observed in the Groups 2 and 3 may suggest a potential stabilizing effect of iodixanol on chromatin structure [42, 52]. These results suggest that glycerol-iodixanol combinations may help maintain chromatin condensation, alongside preserving membrane integrity and mitochondrial function. Future studies should further investigate the impact of these changes on in vivo fertility outcomes [53].

The results of HOST further corroborated the advantages of 5% glycerol supplementation, with the highest membrane integrity observed in these groups. However, iodixanol addition did not significantly affect HOST values, suggesting that glycerol remained the primary determinant of membrane fluidity and structural stability [39, 54]. This is consistent with previous studies identifying glycerol as a crucial factor in maintaining plasma membrane integrity after thawing [5, 33]. Similarly, flow cytometry analysis of plasma membrane integrity showed that the 5% glycerol groups had higher membrane integrity values overall, whereas the low-glycerol groups showed lower values. Although the highest numerical plasma membrane integrity value was observed in the 5% glycerol + 2.5% iodixanol group, no significant difference was detected between the iodixanol-supplemented 5% glycerol groups. Taken together, the HOST and flow cytometry findings highlight the importance of membrane stabilization in maintaining post-thaw sperm functionality, with glycerol as the primary protective factor and iodixanol showing only a limited complementary effect under the conditions of this study.

The combined effect of glycerol and iodixanol presents a potentially useful strategy for optimizing Aseel rooster sperm cryopreservation. The superior outcomes observed in the 5% glycerol groups indicate that this concentration provided better post-thaw protection than 2.5% glycerol under the conditions of the present study, while iodixanol supplementation contributed to improved membrane stability and mitochondrial activity in some treatment groups [17, 38]. However, the absence of significant improvements in progressive motility and apoptosis suggests that the benefits of iodixanol may be more structural than functional [32, 41]. Importantly, although 2.5% and 5% glycerol were evaluated here for their cryoprotective effects, both concentrations remain relevant to the well-known concern that residual glycerol may impair fertility in chickens because of its toxic and contraceptive effects within the female reproductive tract. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted primarily in terms of post-thaw sperm quality rather than direct reproductive performance. The current study was not designed to determine whether glycerol should be removed before practical insemination or whether iodixanol could attenuate glycerol-associated toxicity in the hen oviduct. These questions require dedicated in vivo fertility trials and should be addressed in future studies before practical application in artificial insemination programs. In addition, future research should further optimize iodixanol concentrations and investigate its possible synergistic effects with other cryoprotectants, such as trehalose and antioxidants, to enhance sperm cryosurvival [14, 43].

Conclusion

This study provides strong evidence that optimizing glycerol and iodixanol concentrations can significantly enhance post-thaw sperm quality in Aseel roosters. The findings confirm that 5% glycerol remains the most effective concentration for maintaining sperm viability. However, iodixanol supplementation exhibited protective effects on sperm membrane integrity and mitochondrial activity when combined with glycerol. Notably, the addition of 2.5% iodixanol in low-glycerol groups improved total motility and membrane stability, mitigating some of the adverse effects associated with reduced glycerol concentrations. These results suggest that iodixanol can enhance cryosurvival across different glycerol concentrations, offering a potential strategy to optimize cryopreservation protocols while reducing glycerol toxicity. Overall, this study supports the refinement of sperm cryopreservation techniques, contributing to both genetic conservation efforts and artificial insemination efficiency in poultry breeding programs.
