Deterministic quantum master equation for non-Markovian signal processing
Guilherme De Sousa, Diogo O. Soares-Pinto

Abstract
In this work, we derive a deterministic master equation to model a general, possibly non-Markovian, feedback. The master equation describes a system with a general evolution and measurement operation, with feedback being applied in terms of signal processing. The feedback signal has an arbitrary structure with dimensionality that indicates the degree of non-Markovianity of the information processing. We present examples to illustrate how such a master equation can be used to model systems with memory feedback and non-trivial frequency dependence.

Throughout, we characterize non-Markovianity by a general feedback function that can depend on past values

s_{n+1}=g_{n+1}(x_{n+1},s_{n},s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{n-T}).

\varrho_{n+1}(\vec{y})=\sum_{x^{\prime},\vec{y}^{\prime}}\delta_{\vec{y},\vec{f}_{n+1}(x^{\prime},\vec{y}^{\prime})}\mathcal{M}_{x^{\prime}}(\vec{y}^{\prime})\varrho_{n}(\vec{y}^{\prime})

The dimensionality of

II
Outline of the derivation

Let’s define our system and the steps necessary to apply feedback: we consider a quantum system with discrete or continuous state space, with the quantum state described by a density matrix
\rho_{n}\equiv\rho(t_{n})
, with
t_{n}
corresponding to the observation times when measurement and feedback take place.
At each observation step, we (a) measure, (b) process the information, and (c) change the evolution based on the processed signal, thus applying feedback — see Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
Steps for evolving the conditional quantum state from one observation at
t_{n}
to the next observation at
t_{n+1}
. The sequence involves (a) measuring using the Kraus operators, (b) collecting and processing the information using non-Markovian feedback rule, and (c) applying feedback based on the Markovian embedded high-dimensional signal.

The measurement is modeled using a set
\{K_{x}\}
of Kraus operators that satisfy
\sum_{x}K_{x}^{\dagger}K_{x}=\mathbbm{1}
, with
x
being the measurement outcome index (continuous or discrete), and the probability associate with the measurement is given by Born rule
P(x_{n+1})=\tr{K_{x_{n+1}} \rho_n K_{x_{n+1}}^\dagger}
.
The post measurement state is given by

\rho_{n+1,{\rm post~measurement}}=\frac{K_{x_{n+1}}\rho_{n}K_{x_{n+1}}^{\dagger}}{P(x_{n+1})}.

(3)

It is worthwhile to note that this evolution is stochastic because the measurement outcome
x_{n+1}
is a stochastic process.
Thus, we will refer to the evolution of the density matrix
\rho_{n}
to be
conditioned
on the sequence
x_{1:n}\equiv\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
.

Based on the measurement outcome
x_{n+1}
, the experimentalists implement a signal processing step, consisting of a signal
s_{n}\equiv s(t_{n})
from which feedback is applied.
The evolution of the signal
s_{n}
is deterministic and is given by the update rule in Eq. (
1
).
If the update function only depends on
g_{n}(x_{n+1},s_{n})
the processing is said to be Markovian, and the results follow Ref.
28
.
When the update function depends on past values
\{s_{n-1},\dotsc,s_{n-T}\}
, the signal processing is non-Markovian, thus a
Markovian embedding
is necessary.

The Markovian embedding in this context consists of promoting the scalar signal to a high-dimensional vector
s_{n}\rightarrow\vec{y}_{n}
with appropriate dimension to resolve the non-Markovianity of the feedback rule function
g_{n}
.
This process is model-dependent and is illustrated in Sec.
III
.
Using the signal function
s_{n}
(or its embedded version
\vec{y}_{n}
) we can apply feedback.
In the embedding space, the update function is given by

\vec{y}_{n+1}=f_{n+1}(x_{n+1},\vec{y}_{n}).

(4)

Feedback is modeled as a generic parameter from which the time evolution can be changed.
The time evolution is given by a general CPTP channel

\rho_{n+1}=\mathcal{L}_{n+1}(\vec{y}_{n+1})\rho_{n+1,{\rm post~measurement}}.

(5)

Combining with the measurement step, we can write the evolution of our conditioned density matrix at the next observation time
t_{n+1}
as

\rho_{n+1}=\mathcal{L}_{n+1}(\vec{y}_{n+1})\left[\frac{K_{x_{n+1}}\rho_{n}K_{x_{n+1}}^{\dagger}}{P(x_{n+1})}\right]\equiv\frac{M_{x_{n+1}}(\vec{y}_{n})\rho_{n}}{P(x_{n+1})},

(6)

where
M_{x_{n+1}}(\vec{y}_{n})
defines a quantum instrument
21
;
22
.

The final step to derive Eq. (
2
) is to define an ensemble-resolved density matrix
\varrho_{n}(\vec{y})
that averages all possible conditional quantum states
\rho_{n}
that are consistent with the signal
\vec{y}_{n}
at time
t_{n}
,

\varrho_{n}(\vec{y})={\mathrm{E}}\left[\rho_{n}\delta_{\vec{y},\vec{y}_{n}}\right].

(7)

Here,
{\mathrm{E}}[\cdot]
denotes the average over the stochastic measurement outcomes
x_{1:n}
. This operator is a joint-distribution between quantum degrees of freedom and measurement-outcome (signal-processed) degrees of freedom.
In Appendix
A
, we present a detailed derivation of Eq. (
2
).

III
Application to non-Markovian feedback

Here, we show how one can design a feedback function
\vec{f}_{n}
to have access to
s_{n-1,\dotsc,n-T}
in the embedded space
\vec{y}_{n}
, thus applying a non-Markovian signal processing.
The particular structure of the signal processing
\vec{f}_{n}
is model-dependent and can change according to the internal structure of the high-dimensional vector
\vec{y}
.
As a first example, let’s consider how one can incorporate a single step from the past into the feedback rule.

III.1
Feedback rule with momentum

Let’s consider a general feedback rule that only depends on the instantaneous value of the signal
s_{n}
:

s_{n+1}=s_{n}+g_{n+1}(x_{n+1},s_{n}).

(8)

A modification of the feedback to include a momentum-like term (which adds memory effects) can be done in the following way:

s_{n+1}=s_{n}+(1-\beta)g_{n+1}(x_{n+1},s_{n})+\beta(s_{n}-s_{n-1}).

(9)

Here,
\beta
controls the relative contribution of the momentum (or inertia) of the signal
s_{n}
compared to the feedback rule
g_{n}
to the next iteration of the signal.

To describe this system in a Markovian way, let’s define an auxiliary signal
m_{n}=s_{n}-s_{n-1}
, such that

\displaystyle m_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\beta m_{n}+(1-\beta)g_{n+1}(x_{n+1},s_{n})

(10)

\displaystyle s_{n+1}

\displaystyle=s_{n}+m_{n+1}

The composite signal
\vec{y}_{n}=(s_{n},m_{n})^{T}
evolves under a Markovian update rule
\vec{y}_{n+1}=\vec{f}_{n+1}(x_{n+1},\vec{y}_{n})
.
This particular model of adding momentum into the evolution of the feedback signal
s_{n}
is closely related to the Nesterov accelerated gradient algorithm used in optimization problems
23
.

For the case of continuum feedback
t_{n+1}-t_{n}\equiv\delta t\rightarrow 0
, if we define
\beta\approx 1-\gamma\delta t
, we can write Eq. (
10
) as:

\displaystyle m(t)

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{-\infty}^{t}~\differential s~e^{-\gamma(t-s)}g(x_{s}),

(11)

\displaystyle s(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{t}~\differential s~m(s).

Or equivalently,

s(t)=\gamma\int_{-\infty}^{t}~\differential s~\left(1-e^{-\gamma(t-s)}\right)g(x_{s}),

(12)

showing that the inclusion of momentum is equivalent to a kernel with memory (past values have substantial weight).

III.2
Feedback rule with
T
steps in the past

Now, we turn to the problem of modeling a feedback rule that can depend on
T
steps in the past, as represented by Eq. (
1
).
The exact construction from the non-Markovian model to a Markovian embedding using the vectors
\vec{y}_{n}
and
\vec{f}_{n}
are model-dependent and can vary for different signal processing implementations.
Here, we show one possible implementation that works for a general non-Markovian feedback rule described by Eq. (
1
).

Define
m_{n}^{(k)}=s_{n+1-k}-s_{n-k}
as a set of auxiliary momentum signals, from which one can write
s_{n-k}=s_{n}-\sum_{j}^{k}m_{n}^{(j)}
.
The evolution of signals can be written as:

\displaystyle s_{n+1}

\displaystyle=g_{n+1}\left(x_{n+1},s_{n},s_{n}-m_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,s_{n}-\sum_{j}^{T}m_{n}^{(j)}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle m_{n+1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=s_{n+1}-s_{n}

\displaystyle m_{n+1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=m^{(1)}_{n}

\displaystyle\vdots

\displaystyle m^{(T)}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=m_{n}^{(T-1)}

This system of equations is Markovian because the future values at
n+1
only depend on quantities evaluated at time
n
.
The construction is not unique, but it is general, and shows how one can apply non-Markovian feedback using a Markovian embedding into a high-dimensional signal.
Note that the number of equations matches on how many steps in the past that are taken into account.
From the construction in Eq. (
13
), we can define the vector
\vec{y}_{n}=(s_{n},m_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,m_{n}^{(T)})^{T}
and the appropriate high-dimensional feedback rule
\vec{f}_{n}
to be used in Eq. (
2
).

This construction also works for continuous measurement.
In the limit
t_{n+1}-t_{n}\equiv\delta t\rightarrow 0
, one can use Gaussian Kraus operators in Eq. (
6
) and linear transformation for
g_{n}
to recover the results in Refs.
31
;
7
.

IV
Discussion and outlook

Discussion
—The general deterministic equation for feedback control in Eq. (
2
) can reproduce all previous results in the field for appropriate choices of feedback rule and quantum instruments.
This framework is particularly relevant for quantum control protocols with finite-bandwidth electronics, delayed feedback, or digital filtering, where memory effects are unavoidable.

If the signal
\vec{y}_{n}\rightarrow s_{n}
is a scalar and consequently, the feedback function is Markovian
g_{n}\equiv g_{n}(x_{n+1},s_{n})
, we recover the master equation derived in Ref.
28
.
In their previous work, the authors showed how the Markovian feedback version of Eq. (
2
) can recover quantum jump dynamics, diffusion feedback, and the Quantum Fokker-Planck Master Equation
2
.
By increasing the dimensionality of the signal processing, our general master equation can also recover the quantum Fokker-Planck master equation with general filtering
31
;
7
.
This happens for the case where the instruments are described by Gaussian Kraus operators and the feedback rule is a general linear transformation
\vec{f}_{n+1}={\rm M}\vec{y}_{n}+{\vec{b}}x_{n+1}
.

Equation (
13
) shows how a general a signal processing with memory can be modeled.
This construction is model-dependent because it is affected by the particular signal processing happening in the experiment.
For the case of the quantum Fokker-Planck master equation with general filtering
7
, the particular choices of the momentum signals defines the particular filtering kernel used in the experiment.
Here, we also showed the trade-off between increasing the memory of the signal processing (by accessing more steps in the past) and the dimensionality of the vector signal
\vec{y}_{n}
, which is a direct consequence of the Markovian embedding that allows a deterministic equation to be derived.

Conclusion
—In this work, we extended the results of Ref.
28
to the case of non-Markovian signal processing.
We illustrated how one can incorporate memory effects in signal processing by explicitly constructing a momentum-dependent update rule that has applications in optimization problems.
Then, we show a particular signal processing structure that can implement general non-Markovian processing depending on
T
steps in the past.
This construction shows that, to have access to
T
past values of the signal, one needs to increase the dimensionality of the system to
(T+1)
-dimensions.
Similar dimensionality effects were also found in Refs.
31
;
7
.

We hope our results will add to the far-reaching literature of quantum feedback and to the recent developments of quantum technologies.
The ability to describe quantum feedback in a deterministic way can change how people design and optimize experiments, interact with quantum systems, and even develop new applications to real-world problems.
