Precision culturomics enabled by unlabeled single-cell morphology and Raman spectra
Authors: Qiaoxing Liang, Xihong Lan, Jiayi Wu, Wei Wei, Lili Li, Xiaofang Tang, Guoping Zhao, Ruidong Guo, Huijue Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelective enrichment of target bacteria from complex communities, such as the human microbiome, has remained a challenge. Here, we report precision single-cell culturomics based on label-free morphology, Raman spectrometry, and Laser-Induced Forward Transfer (LIFT) technology. This approach operates at the level of single microbial cells, many generations before these cells form visible colonies. We develop a machine learning-based framework that achieves species-level identification of single cells in complex microbiome and achieve selective culturing for or against specific bacteria in fecal or vaginal samples, and quantify some of the cellular components based on Raman spectra. Genomic analysis of single-cell cultures reveals that short-term antibiotic use promotes both pre-existing resistance and de novo mutations of gut commensals, alongside convergent evolution across species. Our precision culturomics method provides a powerful tool for morphological, metabolic, and genomic analysis of microbial phenotypic variations at the single-cell level in microbiome studies.Subject terms: Microbiome, Bacteria

Introduction

Functional characterization of the microbiome and standardization of animal models both require defined isolates of commensal microbes. Despite recent advances in culturomics, bacterial isolates obtained via plating and colony-picking typically enrich for abundant bacteria in the sample1,2. Colony image analysis has been applied for targeted selection, but the accuracy at the genus level rarely exceeds 50%3. While commensal microbes are no longer considered unculturable, their incubation can take weeks or months4. Over time, plates become increasingly suboptimal due to dehydration and nutrient conversion. To be able to selectively retrieve specific taxa at the single-cell stage would therefore be time-saving, resource-efficient, and ensure recovery of relevant microbes.

Laser-Induced forward transfer (LIFT) is a straightforward method for manipulating microparticles5. With a laser beam deforming the matrix, LIFT is ideally suited for picking particles in the size range of bacteria. The ejected cells can be collected over a short distance, potentially preserving cell viability. LIFT is rarely combined with Raman spectrometry, which is increasingly studied for rapid identification of bacteria such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)6. Label-based methods (e.g., isotope probing or substrate analog probing) do not provide sufficient taxonomic discrimination and may impair cell activity. In contrast, label-free characteristics, including cell morphology detected through optical microscopy and chemical composition detected through Raman spectrometry, provide valuable insights for single-cell taxonomic identification. Moreover, LIFT could retain spatial information of the retrieved microbes in the microbiome samples7.

Here, we established LIFT-based single-cell culturomics, which selects individual cells based on morphology and Raman spectra (Fig. 1a). The approach involves three core steps: (1) collecting single-cell morphological and Raman spectral data from microbiome samples; (2) identifying target taxonomic groups using a machine learning-based framework; and (3) sorting single cells via LIFT and culturing. The approach enables the identification of specific microbial species from complex microbiome samples. We demonstrated the utility of this approach by selectively culturing for or against specific species from the human gut microbiota. This single-cell omics approach is well-positioned to advance pharmaceutical development related to the host-associated microbiome and deepen our understanding of microbial communities. Future development of more specific applications may also include routine quality examination of Live Biotherapeutic products, safety monitoring of fecal transplants, etc.

Results

Single-cell live culture from microliter samples

With precision culturomics in mind, we set out to test whether bacteria are still alive after the single-cell ejection by LIFT using a commercially available device8. One advantage of this method would be the microliter amounts of precious samples required. We performed a Cesarean section for two mice, spotted the amniotic fluid onto the aluminum-coated quartz slide, and used LIFT to transfer all visible bacterial cells (Supplementary Fig. 1). After prolonged anaerobic incubation for 2 weeks, high-density populations emerged in some of the wells. Notable isolates included Oceanobacillus picturae, which could produce ectoine for skin health9. Additionally, we identified Pseudomonas sp. known to be present in the upper reproductive tract of humans and could metabolize estrogen10–13. Other bacterial species included Klebsiella pneumoniae and Enterococcus lactis. We searched for closely related genomes in the human microbiome using the MGnify genome collections of the human gut, oral, and vaginal microbiomes14. The most closely related genomes within the same species (Mash distance < 0.05) were identified in the gut for O. picturae, K. pneumoniae, and E. lactis.

Harnessing microbial single-cell morphology for species-level discrimination

To identify specific taxa prior to culture, we collected single-cell morphological and Raman spectral data from 25 bacterial species (encompassing 14 genera) commonly found in the human microbiome. These data were used to train machine learning models. Using this dataset, which links single-cell morphology and spectra to taxonomy, we first investigated whether single-cell morphology enables species-level bacterial discrimination. Single-cell morphological data were quantified from microscopic images (Fig. 1b; Supplementary Fig. 2). We developed a cell contour analysis pipeline that uses the segment anything model (SAM) for image segmentation and single-cell mask generation. These masks were then analyzed via the computer vision tool OpenCV to compute ten morphological features related to cell size and shape. We performed principal component analysis (PCA) using the 10 quantified morphological features. As expected, spherical bacteria, such as Veillonella, Staphylococcus, Lactococcus, and Streptococcus, tended to be closer to each other, while rod-shaped bacteria like Bacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Clostridium exhibited a similar pattern (Fig. 2a). Cell length and width were the most prominent contributors to Principal Component 1 (PC1) and Principal Component 2 (PC2), respectively (Fig. 2b). In addition to length, features such as perimeter, circularity, and aspect ratio contributed significantly to PC1. As the primary separation axis for different taxa, PC1 explained 53.38% of the morphological variance.

Single cells from distinct species exhibited diverse morphological profiles (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Fig. 3). Moreover, single-cell morphology can vary significantly among different species within the same genus. For instance, at least under the conditions used, Bacteroides cellulosilyticus was relatively short and nearly oval, whereas Bacteroides uniformis and Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron were significantly longer and more similar to typical rod-shaped bacteria (Fig. 2d; Student’s t-test, P < 2.2 × 10−16). Clostridium butyricum and Clostridium tertium clustered together, but C. butyricum was significantly wider than C. tertium (Supplementary Fig. 3; Student’s t-test, P = 2.7 × 10−11). In contrast, Clostridium perfringens was distinct from both in the clustering analysis (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Fig. 3).

To evaluate the relative contribution of each morphological feature to species-level classification, we trained a random forest multi-class classification model using the 10 morphological features. The importance scores, measured as the mean decrease in accuracy (MDA), were >10% for all the features. The most important feature was area, followed by aspect ratio, length, circularity, eccentricity, perimeter, and width (Fig. 2e). In conclusion, single-cell bacterial morphology contains substantial information for species-level discrimination.

Anchoring single-cell Raman spectra to molecular composition

To enable molecular quantification from bacterial single-cell Raman spectra, we employed non-negative least squares (NNLS) fitting to model cellular spectra against reference spectra of pure compounds. Quantification estimates were derived from the weights assigned to each compound in the fitting process. Quality-controlled Raman spectra were converted from intensity to integrated area (±10 cm−1) across the Raman shift range (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Fig. 4). We collected reference Raman spectra for cellular compounds, including nucleobases, amino acids, cell wall components, and other small molecules (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Given the prevalence of overlapping Raman peaks among biomolecules, we first evaluated the robustness of our fitting approach to spectral overlap using mixtures of four amino acids (arginine, histidine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan) with highly overlapping spectral peaks (Supplementary Fig. 6). We prepared eight mixtures with known ratios of these amino acids, acquired their Raman spectra, and performed NNLS fitting. Fitting weights for each amino acid were generally positively correlated with their known molar fractions, and were zero for amino acids absent from a given mixture (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating that the method can distinguish compounds with substantial peak overlap. We further assessed the impact of signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) on fitting performance by adding graded Gaussian noise to the mixture spectra, spanning SNR from high down to ~2. Increasing noise levels led to greater variability in fitting weights, but the positive correlation between fitting weights and molar fractions was largely preserved (Supplementary Fig. 6). We also compared fitting using spectral intensity versus integrated area. Under high-noise conditions, intensity‑based fitting tended to suppress the weights of actually present compounds toward zero (Supplementary Fig. 6), suggesting that the smoothing effect of integration enhances noise tolerance.

To validate the feasibility of Raman-based molecular characterization in bacterial single cells, we collected Raman spectra from Bacillus licheniformis. We assessed the quality of our quantification method based on goodness-of-fit (coefficient of determination R2 and norm of residual) and intra-cell repeatability (intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC). Since our reference library does not cover all cellular metabolites, we quantified the impact of incomplete reference sets by fitting single-cell spectra with the full library and with four subsets (nucleobases, cell wall components, amino acids, and other molecules). Fitting with the full library yielded a mean R2 of 0.786 (SD = 0.117) and a mean relative residual norm of 0.167 (SD = 0.067), with the next-best performance observed for the “other molecules” subset, followed by amino acids, cell wall components, and nucleobases (Fig. 3a). To evaluate repeatability, we collected repeated Raman spectra from individual cells and calculated ICC for each compound. Of the 41 reference compounds (or distinct physical forms) in the library, 31 were detected (fitting weight > 0 in at least one cell), and 16 of these achieved ICC ≥ 0.75 and were included in downstream analyses (Supplementary Fig. 7).

To compare Raman-based quantification with metabolomic measurements, we analyzed B. licheniformis in three states: control vegetative cells, citrate-treated vegetative cells, and spores, with cell states confirmed via microscopy. Spores exhibited distinct Raman spectral profiles compared to vegetative cells (Fig. 3b, c; Supplementary Fig. 8). In Bacillus spores, dipicolinic acid (DPA) exists predominantly as a 1:1 chelate with calcium (Ca-DPA)15. Although metabolomic profiling quantifies total DPA without distinguishing free DPA from Ca‑DPA, their distinct Raman signatures enabled separate quantification via spectral fitting. Fitting weights revealed that both Ca-DPA and DPA were significantly more abundant in spores than in vegetative cells (Fig. 3d; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 0.05), with a more pronounced increase for Ca-DPA, consistent with its role as the dominant form in spores. Citrate-treated vegetative cells showed significantly lower levels of both Ca-DPA and DPA compared to control cells (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 1.0 × 10−5), a trend that aligned with metabolomic measurements of total DPA (Fig. 3e; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 0.01). The early reduction in DPA levels upon citrate treatment may reflect the dual effect of citrate in delaying the onset of sporulation while ultimately enhancing spore yield16. To assess the impact of incomplete reference sets on specific compounds, we compared Ca-DPA using the full library versus each compound subset. Weights from subset fitting showed concordance with those from the full library (Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient > 0.9; Supplementary Fig. 9). Beyond DPA, seven additional compounds were detected in both Raman fitting and metabolomic profiling (Supplementary Fig. 10). While some compounds (e.g., arginine and tryptophan) showed similar trends across methods, others exhibited notable discrepancies, and may require a more exhaustive reference. Collectively, these results indicate that this approach can be used for quantifying molecules of interest from single-cell Raman spectra.

Linking single-cell phenotypic variation reflected in morphology and spectra

Our aforementioned analysis of single-cell morphological features revealed significant morphological heterogeneity among cells of the same species. In addition to morphology, intra-species cellular variation is also reflected in chemical composition, which is detectable via Raman spectroscopy. Simultaneous collection of morphological and Raman spectral data provides an opportunity to characterize single-cell phenotypic variation, linking morphological and molecular heterogeneity. Using data from cells of the same B. licheniformis strain, we investigated associations between single-cell morphology and Raman spectra. To assess the extent to which morphological variance can be explained by Raman spectra, we fitted the morphological features using multivariable linear regression models with the top 100 principal components of spectra. The coefficient of determination (R2) was highest for aspect ratio (R2 = 0.368), followed by length (R2 = 0.340) and width (R2 = 0.254). Although the predicted and observed values of morphological features were significantly correlated (Pearson’s correlation, P < 2.2 × 10−16; Fig. 3f–h), the R2 values were modest. These results indicate that although Raman spectra are not strong predictors of morphological features, they reveal trends linking cellular biochemistry to physical form. We performed a correlation analysis between these features and the integrated area for each Raman shift. This analysis identified five spectral regions that were negatively correlated with aspect ratio and length (Spearman correlation, Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.05). These regions included 736–758, 925–997, 1121–1172, 1294–1400, and 1534–1602 cm−1, among which the range 1121–1172 cm−1 was also positively correlated with width (Fig. 3i). We further fitted cellular spectra to pure compound spectra and identified correlations between specific molecules and morphological features (Fig. 3j). For example, tryptophan was negatively correlated with aspect ratio (False Discovery Rate, FDR = 4.2 × 10−12) and length (FDR = 4.3 × 10−12), and may partially account for the negative correlations in the spectral regions of 736–758, 1294–1400, and 1534–1602 cm−1 (Fig. 3k). Threonine showed similar associations, possibly attributable to signals in the 1294–1400 cm−1 region (Fig. 3l). Peptidoglycan, the primary component of the cell wall, also showed negative correlations with aspect ratio (FDR = 1.1 × 10−9) and length (FDR = 5.4 × 10−8). Peptidoglycan and its building block, N-acetylglucosamine, may partially account for the negative correlations in 1121–1172 and 925–997 cm−1 (Fig. 3m, n). Thus, within the same strain, bacterial single-cell morphological features such as cell length and aspect ratio are partly reflected in the cells’ Raman spectra.

To determine whether the observed correlations between morphological features and Raman spectra are specific to B. licheniformis or reflect general patterns, we extended the same analysis to two additional rod-shaped bacterial species, Bifidobacterium longum and Lactobacillus crispatus (Supplementary Fig. 11). Among the five spectral regions showing significant negative correlations with the aspect ratio and length of B. licheniformis, only the negative correlations between aspect ratio and the 1157–1172 cm−1 region, and between length and 1159 cm−1, were consistently replicated in B. longum and L. crispatus (Spearman correlation, P < 0.05). These results indicate that the majority of correlations between single-cell morphology and Raman spectra exhibit species specificity, which may stem from intrinsic differences in cellular composition, metabolic activity, or cell wall structure across species.

Species-level classification models based on morphological and spectral features

For taxonomic identification at the single-cell level, the machine-learning-based framework consists of two stages. First, a deep neural network extracts feature vectors from single-cell morphology and Raman spectra, designed to minimize intra-taxon distances while maximizing inter-taxon distances. Second, the framework determines if a cell belongs to a target taxon by comparing its feature vector to those of reference cells. Feature vectors of reference cells are calculated using the trained network and stored in a reference library (Fig. 1d). Distances to multiple reference cells of a specific taxon are integrated using multivariable logistic regression to predict whether a cell belongs to that taxon (Fig. 1e).

We adopted contrastive learning based on a Siamese network to extract feature vectors from microbial single-cell morphological and Raman spectral data (Fig. 4a). Specifically, 128-dimensional feature vectors are extracted from the morphological and Raman spectral data of each cell, respectively, and then concatenated to form the final 256-dimensional vector. The microbial cells used for training are organized into positive pairs (cells from the same taxon) and negative pairs (cells from different taxa). The two cells are mapped into a 256-dimensional embedding space via identical deep neural network branches. Contrastive loss encourages the model to learn an embedding space where cells from the same taxon are close together, while cells from different taxa are far apart. After training, the cells used for training were fed into the trained network to extract feature vectors that represent their specific positions within the embedding space. These feature vectors form a reference library, with each taxon represented by multiple cells (Fig. 4b). Similarly, feature vectors are extracted from the morphological and Raman spectral data of novel cells in the microbiome samples. Distances between the feature vector of a novel cell and those of multiple reference cells of a taxon were used as predictors in an elastic net model to predict whether the novel cell belongs to that taxon.

We constructed a Siamese network using data from 25 bacterial species commonly found in the human microbiome. Feature vectors for morphological and spectral data were extracted using a feature tokenizer (FT)-transformer and ResNet, respectively, and then concatenated (Supplementary Fig. 2, 4; Supplementary Table 1). The network was trained at the species level, encouraging cells from the same species to be close together while cells from different species were pushed farther apart. For each species, we calculated the Euclidean distances between reference cells and test cells from the same or different species, using either raw model inputs or feature vectors in the embedding space (Fig. 4c). We observed that the distances calculated from feature vectors were significantly smaller for reference cells and test cells of the same species compared to those of different species. In contrast, distances calculated from raw model inputs for the two groups were less distinguishable. Consistent with these results, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization of raw data showed intermingled cells across species, whereas UMAP visualization of feature vectors revealed species-specific clusters (Fig. 4d, e).

To evaluate the utility of single-cell morphology and Raman spectra and the advantages of integrating both for taxonomic prediction, we additionally constructed deep neural networks that used either morphological or spectral data as input. We trained a distinct elastic net model per species to perform binary classification, using distances derived from the three input types. Even for models using only morphological data, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) approached 0.9 for the majority of species (Fig. 4f; Supplementary Fig. 12). The AUC-ROC for models integrating both data types was significantly higher than that for morphology-only (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 2.3 × 10−5) or spectra-only models (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 1.5 × 10−2). For certain species, morphology-only or spectra-only models achieved performance comparable to that of integrated models. These results demonstrated that single-cell morphological and Raman spectral data are both effective for species discrimination. In general, integrating both data types can enhance model performance.

We further constructed genus-level models by labeling microbial cells from the same genus as positive pairs. The deep neural network successfully learned a feature embedding space where cells formed distinct genus-specific clusters (Supplementary Fig. 13). Consistent with the species-level elastic net models, the genus-level models based on distances derived from both morphology and spectra outperformed those based solely on morphology (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 1.7 × 10−3) and spectra (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = 1.7 × 10−3) (Supplementary Fig. 14). The overall performance assessed by the micro-average AUC-ROC across taxa was slightly lower in genus-level models than species-level models, which may be due to the increased intra-class variation. Nonetheless, the models using both morphological and spectral data achieved a micro-average AUC-ROC > 0.9 at both the species and genus levels.

Given the high diversity of the microbiome, models should avoid misclassifying cells from untrained taxa as target cells. To evaluate this, we constructed leave-one-out models that excluded one species or genus from training at a time and made predictions for cells from the excluded taxa. We observed that the distribution of predicted probabilities for these cells was similar to that of cells from non-target taxa included in the training (Fig. 4g; Supplementary Fig. 13). Excluding non-target taxa led to a slight increase in false-positive rates, yet misclassification patterns remained comparable to those observed when they were included (Supplementary Fig. 15). This attribute enables setting a probability threshold to effectively separate target and non-target cells based on benchmark results from restricted datasets, rather than the entire microbiome.

To dissect the relative discriminative power of morphological and spectral features in integrated models, we performed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis for the deep neural network (Supplementary Fig. 16). Mean absolute SHAP values reflected the average importance of individual features within each modality, while total absolute SHAP values captured the cumulative contribution of all features in a modality. Morphological features exhibited higher mean absolute SHAP values than individual spectral features across most tested species, indicating that morphology is more discriminative than spectra on a per-feature basis. However, the sum of absolute SHAP values for spectral features exceeded that of morphological features. These results indicate that while individual morphological features exerted stronger effects, the greater number of spectral features resulted in a larger overall contribution.

Culturomics from complex microbiome samples with selection

To demonstrate the utility of our experimental and computational approach in complex microbiomes, we performed single-cell cultures from fecal samples. The LIFT module successfully isolated individual bacterial cells without disturbing neighboring cells through laser energy-tuned ejection size adjustment (0.5–10 μm, Supplementary Fig. 17). The LIFT-based single-cell sorting process and the influence of laser pulse energy on sorting efficiency are illustrated in Supplementary Movie 1. We first assessed the single-cell viability rates, which were determined by broth turbidity (>108 cfu/mL according to Escherichia coli) within three weeks. We sorted 96 cells per independent experiment, which was defined as the workflow from sample collection through to the culturing of sorted cells. Samples were collected from four human donors and four mice, with the number of independent experiments per donor ranging from one to ten. Considerable variability in the viability rates was observed both across donors and within samples from the same individual (Supplementary Fig. 18). Across 17 experiments from the four human donors, the mean viability was 42.28% (SD = 14.60%). For one donor with 10 repeated experiments spanning over 8 months, viability ranged from 20.83% to 64.58%, possibly reflecting variation in microbial physiological state at the time of collection17. For mouse fecal samples (n = 4), the mean viability was 40.89% (SD = 11.83%). A random subset of isolates from two human donors was subjected to whole-genome sequencing for taxonomic identification. This analysis revealed a wide range of species, including both abundant species and low-abundance species (Fig. 5a). For instance, Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum and Enterococcus faecium were isolated, despite estimated relative abundances of 0.001–0.01% by Kraken 218 and being reported as zero by the marker gene-based tool MetaPhlAn 419.

To exclude the effect of cultivability limitations on viability, we pre-cultured fecal samples overnight under conditions identical to those used for single-cell cultures. This pre-culturing step increased the mean viability rate to 91.67% based on three independent experiments. Sequencing of randomly selected cultures revealed that 71.91% (64 out of 89) were E. coli (Fig. 5b). This situation represents a typical challenge in traditional culturomics: fast-growing and abundant bacteria in the samples are more likely to be cultured and thus dominate the colonies1,2,20. To demonstrate the utility of machine learning for depleting specific bacteria in culturomics, we applied our pre-trained models for negative selection against E. coli from pre-cultured fecal samples. We used the species-level model for E. coli and the genus-level model for Escherichia, which was represented only by E. coli in the training set. Cells were designated as putative E. coli if either model yielded a prediction probability > 0.3. We sorted both putative E. coli and putative non-E. coli cells in parallel. Among 14 cells sorted as non-E. coli cells, 13 were confirmed as non‑E. coli. For putative E. coli cells, 92.31% (60 out of 65) were verified as true positives. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis of identified E. coli cells revealed strains distinct from the training reference strain, indicating that the models are not restricted to strains genetically identical to the training references (Fig. 5c).

Having demonstrated the utility of our approach in selecting against unwanted dominant taxa, we next applied our method to the enriched cultivation of specific bacterial species. As a proof of concept, we selectively isolated beneficial Clostridium species while excluding pathogenic ones. C. butyricum is an approved Live Biotherapeutic Product renowned for its ability to produce butyrate. It supports gut barrier integrity, regulates inflammation, and aids in restoring gut microbiota after antibiotic use. Furthermore, it may have therapeutic potential for managing conditions like diabetes and gastrointestinal infections21. In contrast, pathogenic C. perfringens produces toxins causing tissue damage and immune dysregulation22. We performed positive selection using the pre‑trained binary models for each of the three Clostridium species included in our training set. Using selection criteria requiring C. butyricum model probability > 0.6 and higher than other Clostridium species, we successfully isolated two C. butyricum cultures. Consistent with the marked morphological differences between C. butyricum and C. perfringens, our models correctly distinguished them using only morphological data (Fig. 5d, e). Specifically, 2 out of 3 cultures predicted as C. butyricum were correctly identified, and 7 out of 11 cultures predicted as C. perfringens were correct, consistent with the probability threshold used. To further challenge the morphology-based discrimination, we targeted Clostridium tertium, a species morphologically similar to C. butyricum (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Fig. 3). One culture was obtained and confirmed as C. tertium, with morphology-only model probabilities of 0.770 (C. tertium), 0.010 (C. butyricum), and 0.002 (C. perfringens). To showcase the capability of our method for rare taxa isolation, we selectively cultured for Akkermansia muciniphila, a beneficial gut species whose relative abundance was estimated at 0.0017% based on Kraken 2 analysis of metagenomic reads. Using a morphology‑only model probability threshold of >0.6, we obtained two cultures, one of which was confirmed as A. muciniphila (Fig. 5f).

The machine‑learning framework is extensible to new target taxa beyond the initial training set. New taxa of interest can be added by collecting reference morphology and spectra for that species alone, without requiring additional data for dozens of classes. To illustrate this scalability and provide an example of our platform applied beyond fecal samples, we added Lactobacillus crispatus, a species associated with vaginal health, to the training set and retrained the models. Applying this updated model to a vaginal sample, we obtained three cultures, all confirmed as L. crispatus (Supplementary Fig. 19). The isolated strains showed an ANI of 98.76% relative to the training reference strain. To evaluate model performance across strains with different degrees of genetic divergence, we compared this strain with a previously isolated strain showing 99.93% ANI to the reference. Both strains were cultured independently, and morphological and spectral data were collected from 300 cells from each strain. The model integrating morphology and spectra showed comparable recall rates across both strains. In contrast, the morphology‑only model showed a slightly lower recall rate for the 98.76% ANI strain, while the spectra‑only model showed a higher rate (Supplementary Fig. 19). These observations imply a complex relationship between genomic divergence and phenotypic shifts in morphology or spectra, which warrants further investigation. In conclusion, our method can be used to culture either for or against specific bacteria from complex microbiome samples.

Resistance of human gut commensal species driven by antibiotic exposure

The impact of short-term antibiotic use on beneficial gut bacteria has become a critical concern in clinical medicine23. Sequencing-based approaches for studying antibiotic effects face computational challenges. These challenges include resolving genetic variants into strains, analyzing low-coverage species, and distinguishing low-frequency mutations from sequencing errors. To illustrate how culturomics can overcome these limitations, we isolated single cells of two beneficial Bifidobacterium species—B. longum and B. adolescentis—from a human volunteer (H1) who received a 5-day course of oral cefuroxime for non-intestinal surgery. Both species were included in our model training dataset. B. longum has been reported to reduce throat pain and symptoms like runny nose and cough in children, alleviate allergic rhinitis and intermittent asthma, improving quality of life24. B. adolescentis is a species that appeared earlier in formula-fed infants compared to breastfed infants25. It has recently been implicated with antibody counts after COVID-19 vaccination26 and increased healthy lifespan in multiple model organisms27. Fecal samples were collected before cefuroxime administration (with no antibiotic use in the preceding year) and 5 weeks after treatment ended. Among the cultures obtained, 58.64% were B. longum or B. adolescentis.

Using a threshold of ANI > 99.5%, we identified two B. longum strains in H1, with a mean ANI of 98.65% between them (Fig. 6a, b). We first analyzed penicillin-binding protein 2 (PBP2), the direct target of cefuroxime in B. longum, and found that each strain carried a distinct PBP2 variant differing at three amino acid positions. For clarity, we designated the two variants as v1 (430H, 546V, 590G) and v2 (430R, 546A, 590S). Based on cell counts across 47 B. longum cells, the strain carrying v1 was dominant, comprising 40 cells, while the strain carrying v2 accounted for the remaining 7 cells. As a control, we isolated B. longum from a volunteer (H2) who reported no antibiotic use in the preceding three years. B. longum from H2 represented another strain with a mean ANI of 98.54% relative to H1 strains and harbored PBP2 variant v3 (168A, 430R, 546A, 568S, 590S). To assess functional implications, we predicted the structures of all three variants and performed cefuroxime docking (Fig. 6c). The mutations distinguishing v1 and v2 localized to PBP2’s transpeptidase domain, where cefuroxime binds (Fig. 6d). Affinity prediction and docking scoring suggested that v2 had reduced binding capacity compared to v1 and v3 (Fig. 6e, f). Notably, both v1 and v2 strains persisted before and after cefuroxime exposure. Although the increase in the v2 population following exposure did not reach statistical significance (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.1), the observed trend suggests potential evolution of pre-existing resistance at the population level.

Similar to B. longum, individual H1 harbored two B. adolescentis strains, one of which was dominant, with both strains persisting before and after cefuroxime exposure (Fig. 6g). The dominant strain showed evidence of sub-strain diversification (Fig. 6h). Specifically, the distribution of ANI values between cells of the dominant strain displays a primary peak near 100% ANI, alongside a small peak at slightly lower ANI values (99.8–99.9%). In contrast to B. longum, all B. adolescentis cells shared identical penicillin-binding protein sequences, underscoring the role of pre-existing resistance mechanisms. We next investigated convergent evolution between the two species based on 47 B. longum and 65 B. adolescentis cell isolates from individual H1. While the number of pairwise single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) between cells was generally lower in B. adolescentis than in B. longum, both species showed an increase in pairwise SNPs after exposure (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.05; Fig. 6i, j), suggesting de novo mutagenesis. To rule out strain-proportion effects, we analyzed allele frequency changes within the same sub-strain before and after exposure. In B. longum, we identified a missense variant in the gene encoding sugar ABC transporter ATP-binding protein, with the T-allele depleted after exposure (Fisher’s exact test, FDR < 0.05; Fig. 6k). The ATP-binding protein and the permease are core components of the ABC transporter system, which is essential for sugar import but can also act as an antibiotic efflux pump. Intriguingly, B. adolescentis exhibited a frequency shift in a missense variant within its ABC transporter permease gene (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.03; Fig. 6l). Together, these findings reveal the distinct yet convergent adaptation strategies employed by beneficial gut bacterial species under short-term antibiotic stress, and demonstrate the utility of the precision culturomics strategy for serial characterization of target species.

Discussion

To achieve precision single-cell culturomics, we developed a system integrating morphology and Raman spectra, machine learning-based taxon identification, LIFT sorting, and single-cell culture. We demonstrated that this system enables label-free sorting and culturing of target taxa from complex microbiome samples.

Our system simultaneously collects imaging and Raman spectral data. The significance of this integration lies in the orthogonality of the data types. While morphology captures physical structure, spectra provide a biochemical fingerprint. This complementarity is crucial for resolving biological ambiguity, as cells may be morphologically identical yet possess distinct metabolic states. Conversely, cells of different species or growth phases may share similar metabolic profiles despite markedly divergent morphologies. Integrating both data types improved taxonomic prediction accuracy and enabled a double-verification mechanism. Beyond taxonomic prediction, this synergy establishes a link between a cell’s physical phenotype and its internal molecular heterogeneity. For instance, we observed correlations between cell length or aspect ratio and specific Raman signals. Although the causal direction between these shifts remains to be fully elucidated, their consistent coupling highlights the integration of physical and chemical responses, providing a more holistic characterization of single-cell variation.

The microbiome typically comprises thousands of species, and including all of them in training datasets would be resource-intensive. This situation can lead to out-of-distribution (OOD) challenges, which are particularly problematic for multi-class classification strategies. Specifically, models may misclassify OOD samples as one of the known classes, leading to erroneous predictions. Our machine learning strategy for taxonomic prediction involves two stages. Initially, deep neural networks extract feature vectors from single-cell morphological and spectral data. Subsequently, the distances between these feature vectors and those of reference cells from specific taxa are integrated to determine taxonomic membership. This approach reframes multi-class classification as reference database matching followed by binary classification. This method enhances interpretability and circumvents challenges associated with confidence calibration for model output probabilities. Furthermore, contrastive learning has shown scalability in face recognition tasks involving tens of thousands of individuals with only a few samples per person. Therefore, few-shot learning for single cells is feasible in the future. A promising starting point would be the construction of a large-scale database of single-cell morphological and spectral data.

Our machine learning approach achieved species-level identification of bacterial cells. Notably, species-level models generally outperformed genus-level models, possibly due to reduced intra-class variation. This finding suggests future applications of our approach in fine-grained scenarios, such as strain-level identification. Additionally, we demonstrated the potential for molecular quantification using Raman spectra. These results imply the possibility of extending precision culturomics from specific microbial taxa to strains with particular metabolic functions, all without the need for labels. It is worth noting that the time requirement of Raman spectral acquisition makes it impractical to scan all cells within a complex microbial community, particularly when targeting rare taxa. One potential application of Raman-based sorting in the future is the isolation of metabolically distinct subpopulations from abundant taxa or pure cultures.

In the present study, we isolated strains with ANI distances from the reference strains used for model training, suggesting that the approach is not limited to retrieving strains genetically identical to the training references. However, the relationship between genomic divergence and differences in morphology or Raman spectra is not necessarily linear. In other words, greater genomic divergence does not always translate into larger differences in morphology or Raman spectra, as the magnitude and functional impact of within-species genomic variation can vary widely across taxa, affected genes, and metabolic states. Therefore, systematic large-scale studies incorporating multiple strains across diverse species are warranted to clarify how genomic variation manifests in the phenotypic features captured by our approach.

This study provides preliminary evidence for the feasibility of molecular quantification from single-cell Raman spectra. While metabolomics remains the gold standard for metabolite profiling of bulk samples, its application to single bacterial cells remains technically unfeasible. Raman spectroscopy offers a complementary alternative by enabling measurements at the single‑cell level. Moreover, it can distinguish between different molecular forms (e.g., DPA and Ca-DPA), which are indistinguishable by conventional metabolomics. However, several limitations should be considered. First, reference spectra acquired from pure compounds in solid or aqueous solution form may not fully recapitulate the spectral signatures of molecules in the highly crowded intracellular environment. In vivo factors, including local pH, ion concentrations, and macromolecular binding states, can induce mode-dependent shifts and line-width changes in Raman spectra28–31, thereby complicating the linear fitting assumptions underlying our single-cell molecular quantification. Second, at present, not all compounds can be reliably quantified, and our strategy relies on a posteriori selection of molecules with high repeatability. Third, conventional metabolomics detects only free small molecules, excluding those covalently incorporated into macromolecules (e.g., amino acid residues in proteins, nucleobases in DNA/RNA). Thus, Raman‑derived estimates and metabolomic measurements are as yet complementary, and both have room for improvement.

Despite the promising applications of our single-cell culturomics approach, several limitations should be noted. While our study provides insights into the adaptive responses of Bifidobacterium species to short-term antibiotic use, it is important to acknowledge that these findings were based on observations from a single individual. This limited sample size may not capture the full spectrum of inter-individual variability in the gut microbiome. Future studies incorporating larger and more diverse cohorts are essential to validate these observations. Additionally, while negative controls were employed throughout the single-cell culturing process to verify that bacterial growth originated from the sorted cells, potential cross-contamination from adjacent tissues or skin during the collection of mouse amniotic fluid cannot be entirely excluded. Nevertheless, the retrieval of live bacteria from such samples remains valuable for downstream functional investigations. Furthermore, clinical samples such as feces or vaginal swabs are inherently complex, containing host cell debris, undigested dietary fibers, and mucins. These particles can morphologically resemble bacteria under microscopy and may be unavoidably co-transferred with target bacterial cells during the LIFT process, potentially introducing latent inhibitors, host factors, or dead cells that could interfere with downstream single-cell cultivation or library construction. Finally, bacterial morphology and Raman fingerprints are highly dependent on growth phase (e.g., logarithmic versus stationary) and environmental stressors (e.g., nutrient deprivation, osmotic stress). Our machine learning models were trained on reference strains under standard in vitro culture conditions. In real-world clinical applications, target bacteria may exist in stress-induced states, causing their morphological and metabolic fingerprints to deviate substantially from those in the training set. Such deviations could potentially lead to a drop in the in situ recall rate and would require the incorporation of in situ morphological or spectral data into the machine-learning models when feasible.

Although we primarily focused on the human microbiome in this study, the system we presented can be applied to microbiome samples from various habitats. The preservation of spatial information can be further explored in combination with host data. The system utilizes commercially available equipment, and we have implemented the proposed machine learning-based framework as an easy-to-use Python package. We believe this system is ready to be adopted in laboratories around the world to facilitate microbiome research.

Methods

This research complies with all relevant ethical regulations. Animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee at Greater Bay Area Institute of Precision Medicine. The study of human microbiome samples was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fudan University.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. As a proof-of-concept methodological study, sample sizes were chosen based on technical capacity and common standards in the field to ensure representation for model training and experimental validation. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.

Microbiome samples

Mice were housed in a specific-pathogen-free facility under a controlled climate with a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, a relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, and a standard 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Healthy adult C57BL/6J inbred mice (Mus musculus) aged 8 weeks were purchased from Zhuhai BesTest Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. (China). Animals had ad libitum access to sterile water and a standard irradiated chow diet (1010088, Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-engineering Co., Ltd., China). No non-standard, high-fat, or chemically modified diets were introduced during any experimental or sampling phases. Amniotic fluid samples were collected from two female mice. Fecal samples were obtained from a sex-balanced cohort of four mice. For human microbiome samples, volunteers were recruited through word-of-mouth invitations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. No participant compensation was provided. The sex of participants was determined based on self-report. Fecal samples were obtained from two female and two male healthy volunteers aged 33–41 years. The vaginal sample was obtained from a healthy female volunteer aged 34 years. Sex was considered in the design as mice amniotic fluid or human vaginal microbiome samples required female donors. For fecal samples, donors were selected without sex-specific restrictions. Sex-based stratification analysis was not performed in the current study aimed at methodology development.

Fecal sample processing

Human fecal samples were self-collected by participants using 50 mL sterile centrifuge tubes (339652PK, Thermo Fisher, Shanghai, China) that had been pre-filled with nitrogen. Participants were instructed to collect samples from the middle of the stool. Mouse fecal samples were collected immediately upon excretion. Samples were immediately transferred to an anaerobic chamber (AL-B, LABIOPHY, China). To maintain strict anaerobic conditions, all procedures, which included sample processing, single‑cell sorting, and culturing, were performed within anaerobic chambers. Because these procedures were carried out in separate, specialized anaerobic chambers to prevent cross-contamination, samples were sealed in gas‑impermeable bags during any brief transfers between chambers. These bags were only opened and sealed inside chambers. All reagents were pre-equilibrated in the anaerobic environment for at least 48 h before use.

A sterile, disposable 3 mL dropping pipette (DG-300, Biosharp, China) was used to take a 1 g fecal sample. For mouse samples, all available fecal pellets were collected. The sample was then transferred to a sterile 15 mL centrifuge tube (339650, Thermo Fisher) and homogenized in 5 mL of sterile, pre-reduced 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (BL550A, Biosharp) by vortex mixing (MIX-2500, Hangzhou Yooning Instrument Co., Ltd., China) for 30 s to release microbial cells from the fecal matrix. To remove dietary debris while retaining single microbial cells, ~1–2 mL of the resulting slurry was first filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer (258368, NEST Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) into a 50 mL centrifuge tube. The filtrate was then passed sequentially through 40 μm (258369, NEST) and 10 μm (43-50010-01, pluriStrainer, Germany) cell strainers. If clogging occurred, an additional 2 mL of PBS was added to dilute the sample. For cell cleaning, 100 μL of the final filtrate was transferred to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube (MCT-001-150, LABSELECT, China) and mixed with 1 mL of sterile, pre-reduced water by pipetting up and down 10–20 times. The mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm (865×g) (Legend Micro17, Thermo Fisher) for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the microbial pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of sterile water.

Vaginal swab sample processing

Fresh vaginal swab samples were collected with a sterile swab from the mid-vaginal wall without the use of lubricants or douches. Samples were processed in an anaerobic environment as described in the previous section. Immediately after collection, each swab was placed into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube containing 1 mL of sterile, pre-reduced 1×PBS. To release microbial cells, the tube was vortexed for 5 min with the swab still inside. The swab was then removed using sterile forceps, and the remaining liquid was centrifuged at 500×g for 10 min to pellet host cellular debris. The supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube and centrifuged at 3000 rpm (865×g) for 5 min to pellet microbial cells. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was washed by gently adding 1 mL of sterile water and pipetting the mixture up and down without introducing air bubbles. The suspension was centrifuged again at 3000 rpm (865×g) for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the microbial pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of sterile water.

Ultra-low biomass sample processing

For microliter-scale samples with ultra-low microbial biomass, such as mouse amniotic fluid, the objective was to maximize the recovery of any available microbial cells rather than achieving high-precision sorting. To minimize cell loss, the washing steps (centrifugation and resuspension) were omitted, and the collected samples were directly used for sample loading. Amniotic fluid was collected from pregnant mice using a sterile syringe inserted into the amniotic sac under direct visualization to avoid damaging surrounding tissues. Aseptic technique was adhered to throughout the procedure.

Laser-induced forward transfer (LIFT) of bacterial cells

The equipment, PRECI SCS-R300 (Hooke Instruments, Ltd., China), was turned on and adjusted according to standards following the manufacturer’s handbook8,32. For all sample types, 1–2 μL of the microbial suspension was aspirated from the middle of the tube and carefully spotted onto a slide (HSC24, Hooke Instruments, Ltd.). For amniotic fluid samples, all visible bacterial cells in the microscopic field were transferred. For selective isolation from complex samples, cells were screened based on morphology to narrow down candidate targets before spectral acquisition and transfer. In practice, morphology‑only models were used as the primary criterion, and cells with prediction probabilities exceeding a predefined threshold (typically > 0.6) were sorted directly. Raman spectral acquisition was performed optionally for confirmation. Visually selected bacterial cells (the manufacturer’s software) were LIFT-transferred into collectors (HSR04, Hooke Instruments, Ltd.) using a laser power of 80–120 nJ and then flipped into individual PCR tubes with brief spinning at 2000×g using a mini centrifuge (Mini-6K, Hangzhou Yooning Instrument Co., Ltd., China). The laser spot size was fixed, while the ejection size was adjustable (0.5–20 μm) via energy modulation to accommodate bacterial dimensions.

Bacterial cell culture

Single cells were transferred to culture tubes and grown under anaerobic conditions (5% H2, 10% CO2, and 85% N2) in an anaerobic chamber (Type B, Coy Laboratory Products, Ann Arbor, MI) at 37 °C. Bacterial cells from mouse amniotic fluid samples were cultured in Wilkins–Chalgren Anaerobe Broth (LA4420, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China). Bacterial cells from human and mouse fecal samples were cultured in Gifu Anaerobic Medium Broth, Modified (HB8518, Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., China). Bacterial cells from the vaginal swab sample were cultured in De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) Broth (HB0384-1, Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology).

Negative controls

A negative control strategy was implemented to monitor potential contamination throughout the workflow. To ensure that observed microbial growth originated from sorted cells, three levels of negative controls were established. First, for each experimental batch, 20 sterile culture tubes containing only broth (without sorted cells) were prepared and incubated under the same conditions as the tubes containing sorted single cells. Second, to monitor the long-term sterility of the anaerobic chamber, broth-only tubes were placed at multiple locations inside the chamber and observed throughout the study to detect any airborne or surface‑borne contamination. Third, before single-cell sorting experiments, the cleanliness of the culture broth and slides was visually verified using the microscope and visualization software of the PRECI SCS-R300 equipment. Specifically, sterilized and uninoculated broth was loaded onto slides and visually inspected to confirm the absence of microbial cells, and the slides were also examined for any contaminating cells. No bacterial growth was expected in any control tube. The single-cell culturing results were considered valid only if all negative controls showed no microbial presence or growth.

Single-cell morphology

The morphological parameters extracted from microbial cells included length, width, aspect ratio, area, perimeter, circularity, eccentricity, convexity, extent, and median centroid–contour distance. Detailed definitions and computational formulas for each morphological parameter are provided in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Fig. 2. Microscopic images were acquired using the microscope and visualization software of the PRECI SCS-R300 equipment. The images were processed with the segment anything model (SAM)33. For each cell, SAM generated a binary segmentation mask, which was then used as input to the OpenCV library (v 4.5.5) for contour processing. Pixel‑based measurements were converted to micrometers using a pre‑calibrated pixel size (0.0763 μm per pixel for the default image resolution of the visualization software). All analyses were implemented in Python.

Random forest classification based on morphological features

To preliminarily assess the contribution of morphological features to species-level classification, a random forest multiclass classifier was implemented using the randomForest package (v 4.6-14) in R with default parameters (ntree = 500, mtry = √p, where p is the number of features). The training dataset comprised 50 randomly selected cells from each of the 25 species, resulting in a total of 1250 cells. The 10 morphological features described above were used as predictors, with the species label serving as the outcome variable. Feature importance was quantified as the mean decrease in accuracy (MDA).

Raman spectrometry

Raman spectra were collected using the PRECI SCS-R300, which is equipped with a 532 nm laser and a −70 °C cooled charge-coupled device detector with a 600 l/mm optical grating. For cultural purposes, the laser power was 1 mW with an acquisition time of 1 s. For reference, the products of biological molecules, the laser power was 10 mW with an acquisition time of 10 s. The Raman signal was collected in the spectral interval from 400 to 3600 cm−1. Pre-processing of the raw spectra included cosmic ray removal, noise reduction, baseline correction, min–max normalization, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) filtering, spectral truncation, and conversion of intensity to integrated area. The detailed workflow and parameters are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 4 and described below.

Cosmic ray removal was performed with a filter size of 9 and a dynamic factor of 3. Savitzky–Golay denoising was conducted using a window size of 5 and a third-order polynomial for modeling. Baseline correction was conducted using the adaptive iteratively reweighted Penalized Least Squares (airPLS) algorithm with a lambda value of 100 and a maximum of 15 iterations. After baseline correction, min–max normalization was applied to scale all intensities to a range of 0–1. Specifically, silent region-anchored normalization was implemented: intensities were scaled relative to the maximum intensity of the spectrum and the median intensity of a silent region (1800–2000 cm−1), which was assumed to lack biological Raman signal and thus served as a stable reference. SNR was calculated as the ratio of the total intensity range of the entire spectrum to the intensity range within the silent region (1800–2000 cm−1). Spectra with an SNR below 2.0 were excluded from subsequent analysis. Spectra were truncated to a wavenumber range of 600–3000 cm−1 for taxonomic identification and 600–1800 cm−1 for molecular quantification. The normalized spectral intensities were converted to the integrated area under the curve for each Raman shift over a window of ±10 cm−1 using Simpson’s rule. The resulting integrated spectra were used as input for modeling.

Collection of ground-truth datasets

A dataset of single-cell morphology and Raman spectra with known taxonomic identities was constructed using bacterial strains, which had been previously isolated from human fecal, salivary, or vaginal sources. Each bacterial species contained approximately 500 cells. Prior to morphological and Raman spectral measurement, bacterial strains isolated from human fecal or salivary sources were cultured in Gifu Anaerobic Medium Broth, Modified, whereas strains of vaginal origin were cultured in MRS Broth. For the processing of microbial culture samples, 1 mL of broth culture was transferred to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube and centrifuged at 3000 rpm (865×g) for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was washed with 1 mL of sterile water followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm (865×g) for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of sterile water. For anaerobic strains, all steps were performed inside the anaerobic chamber.

Training data

The training set for the Siamese network was constructed from pairs of single cells. For each taxon (species or genus), 100 cells were selected as the source for pair formation. From these, 100,000 positive pairs (in which both cells belonged to the same taxon) and 100,000 negative pairs (in which the two cells came from different taxa) were generated. The combined dataset of 200,000 pairs was then randomly shuffled. Positive pairs were sampled uniformly across all taxa, and negative pairs were constructed uniformly across all possible inter‑taxon combinations.

Cells not used for Siamese networking training (i.e., the remaining ~400 cells per taxon) were allocated to train and test a taxon-specific elastic net model. For each taxon, 80% of these cells served as positive samples in the training set, and the remaining 20% constituted the positive samples in the test set. Negative samples for the elastic net were drawn from a pool of cells belonging to all other taxa (also unused in Siamese network training). The number of negative samples was set equal to the number of positive samples, and these negative samples were evenly distributed across all other taxa.

Machine learning

A Siamese network was developed to extract feature vectors for paired bacterial cells. The deep neural network received as input a vector that concatenated 10 morphological features with 2401 Raman spectral features (600–3000 cm−1). The network architectures for the morphological and spectral feature extraction branches were adapted from the feature tokenizer (FT) Transformer34 (Supplementary Fig. 2) and ResNet6 (Supplementary Fig. 4), respectively. Hyperparameters are provided in Supplementary Table 1. A comprehensive description of the network architectures and hyperparameters is provided in the Supplementary Methods. The network was trained using contrastive loss, which was computed from the pairwise Euclidean distance between the feature vectors of cell pairs. Three model variants were introduced: one using only morphological data, one using only spectral data, and one that integrated both data types. In the integrated architecture, each of the two branches outputs a 128‑dimensional feature vector, which was concatenated to produce a final 256‑dimensional vector. Deep neural network training was completed within several hours using one NVIDIA A100 GPU (40 GB VRAM) and eight CPU cores.

The feature vectors of the cells used in training were extracted using the trained models and constituted a reference library for cell matching. The elastic net model was designed to predict whether a cell belonged to the target taxon. Its predictors were the Euclidean distances of feature vectors between the cell and each of the 100 Siamese network training cells of that taxon. During Siamese network training, early stopping was employed. Specifically, for each taxon, training was halted when the training accuracy of its corresponding elastic net model reached a plateau. For cells in microbiome samples, feature vectors were extracted from their morphological and spectral data, and their distances to reference cells were input into the elastic net model to determine whether they belonged to the desired taxa.

Depending on the experimental aim, the corresponding elastic net model was used for positive selection (enrichment) or negative selection (depletion) of target taxa. For positive selection, cells with predicted probabilities above a set threshold for the target taxon were sorted for culture. For negative selection, cells predicted to belong to an undesired taxon with a probability above a threshold were excluded from sorting, thereby enriching for taxa other than the target.

SHAP analysis for feature importance interpretation

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was performed in Python using the GradientExplainer from the shap library (v 0.49.1) to assess the contribution of morphological and Raman spectral features in the deep neural network. The analysis was conducted on a per-species basis using the pre-trained Siamese network. For each target species, 100 cells from the training set were used as references, and another 100 cells not used in training the deep neural network were randomly selected as test samples. Feature vectors of reference samples were extracted using the morphological and spectral branches of the Siamese network separately, and the centroids (mean feature vectors) were computed for each modality to serve as the reference points in the embedding space. A background dataset of 100 cells randomly sampled from the training set (across all species) was used to initialize the explainer. For each test cell, the Euclidean distance between its feature vector and the centroid was computed, and SHAP values were computed for the 10 morphological features and the 2401 Raman spectral features. Mean absolute SHAP values were calculated as the average of absolute SHAP values across all features within a modality (morphology or spectra). Total absolute SHAP values were calculated as the sum of absolute SHAP values of all features within a modality. We further computed the natural log-transformed ratio of morphological to spectral SHAP values (mean or total) to compare their relative importance across different bacterial species.

Whole-genome sequencing

Taxonomy of cultured bacteria was determined by whole-genome sequencing. DNA was extracted using the VAMNE Magnetic Pathogen DNA Kit (DM202-01, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Paired-end libraries (2 × 150 bp) were prepared using the VAHTS Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit (ND617, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd) and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 sequencer (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Each library was sequenced to a target output of ~1 Gb, which corresponds to an estimated average sequencing depth of ~200× for a typical bacterial genome of 5 Mb. Sequencing reads were quality-filtered using fastp35 v0.22.0 and assembled by MEGAHIT36 v1.2.9. GTDB-Tk37 v1.0.2 was used for genomic taxonomy classification. Whole-genome ANI was estimated using FastANI38 v1.33. Mash distance was generated using Mash39 v2.0. SNPs were called using Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy) and SNP-based phylogeny was constructed using FastTree40 v2.1.11. The structures of three variants of PBP2 were initially predicted using NeuralPLexer41 v0.1.0 and then optimized using DynamicBind42 v1.0. The binding affinities were estimated by DynamicBind, and docking poses were scored using the RTMScore function43.

Metagenomic sequencing and taxonomic profiling

The metagenomic sequencing data analyzed in this study were generated as part of a separate project. Metagenomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the MGIEasy Stool Microbiome DNA Extraction Kit (940-000122-00, MGI) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Paired-end libraries (2 × 150 bp) were prepared using the VAHTS Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit (ND617, Vazyme) and sequenced on a DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI) to a target output of ~10 Gb per sample. Sequencing reads were quality filtered using fastp v0.22.0.

Taxonomic profiling was performed using Kraken 2v 2.0.7-beta to assign taxonomic labels to each read and MetaPhlAn 4 v4.0.6 to provide a marker gene-based profile. Kraken 2 was run against the pre-built Standard database (downloaded December 2022) with the confidence threshold set to 0.2. The relative abundance of species was calculated as the percentage of reads classified to that species relative to the total number of reads classified at the species level. MetaPhlAn 4 was run with default parameters using the pre-built marker gene database (mpa_vOct22_CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202212).

Non-negative least-squares fitting of Raman spectra

Non‑negative least-squares (NNLS) fitting was used to fit integrated areas from single‑cell Raman spectra with the integrated areas from spectra of multiple reference compounds. The integrated area was calculated over a window of ±10 cm−1 for each Raman shift in the fingerprint region (600–1800 cm−1). The resulting weights from the NNLS fitting were taken as estimates for the relative quantification of the corresponding molecules. NNLS fitting was performed using the nnls function from the SciPy library (v1.15.3) in Python. An intercept term was included by appending a column of ones to the reference matrix. The fitted spectrum was reconstructed as the linear combination of reference spectra weighted by their coefficients, plus the intercept term. The residual was calculated as the difference between the original and fitted spectra.

Reference spectra of pure biological compounds were collected using a laser power of 10 mW and an acquisition time of 10 s. Spectral preprocessing followed the same procedures as described for single‑cell Raman spectra. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between all pairs of reference spectra to identify highly similar profiles. For any pair of distinct compounds or different physical forms of the same compound (e.g., powder, aqueous solution, crystal) with a Pearson correlation coefficient r > 0.9, only one representative spectrum was retained. All remaining candidate reference spectra were then included in an initial NNLS fit. For compounds with multiple retained forms, the form yielding a higher intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) in reproducibility assessments (see next section) was preferred. If multiple forms of the same compound all achieved ICC > 0.6, they were all retained in the final reference set. For the spectral fitting of cells, four subsets of compounds were also fitted separately: nucleobases (adenine, cytosine, thymine, guanine, and uracil), amino acids (all 20 standard amino acids), cell wall components (peptidoglycan, N‑acetylmuramic acid, and N‑acetylglucosamine), and other compounds (all remaining reference materials not belonging to the above categories). In addition, Ca-DPA and DPA were each added to the above compound subsets to assess how the inclusion of reference spectra influenced the quantification of specific molecules.

Reproducibility and error assessment of Raman spectral fitting

Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis was performed to evaluate the quantification quality of each compound. Ten individual cells were randomly selected, and Raman spectra were acquired five times per cell. All spectra were fitted using the same set of reference compounds, and the resulting coefficients for each compound were used as input for ICC calculation. ICC values and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the ICC function from the psych package (v 2.0.9) in R. For each compound, the ICC estimate was used to assess the consistency of the fitted coefficients across repeated measurements. Only compounds with ICC ≥ 0.75 were retained for downstream analyses.

Benchmarking Raman spectral fitting on defined amino acid mixtures

Benchmarking experiments were performed using aqueous mixtures of four amino acids: arginine, histidine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan. Eight mixtures with different ratios were prepared in a 10 mL aqueous system based on weight proportions. For example, in the 10:1:5:1 mixture, the final concentrations (w/v) were 1.0%, 0.1%, 0.5%, and 0.1% for arginine, histidine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan, respectively. Weight ratios were converted to molar fractions using the molecular weight of each amino acid. The fitted coefficients were compared with the known molar fractions to evaluate quantification accuracy.

Raman spectra of the four pure amino acid aqueous solutions were used as reference spectra for fitting. For noise analysis, additive Gaussian noise with standard deviations ranging from 0.01 to 0.10 (in increments of 0.01) was introduced to the intensity of the mixture spectra. In contrast to the integrated area-based fitting used elsewhere in this study, intensity-based fitting was also performed using intensity values for both reference and mixture spectra.

Metabolomic validation of Raman-based molecular quantification

Bacillus licheniformis cells in three experimental conditions were prepared for Raman spectral fitting and metabolomic profiling. The bacterium was cultured aerobically at 37 °C in Columbia Broth (027411, Huankai Microbial Sci.& Tech. Co., Ltd, China). The citrate-treated group was cultured in 10% Columbia Broth with the pH adjusted to 4.0 using citric acid. Both the control and citrate-treated groups were cultured for two days and contained only vegetative cells with no sporulation observed by microscopic examination. Spores were collected after 14 days of culture, when extensive sporulation was confirmed by microscopy. For each group, Raman spectra were acquired from 100 individual cells using a laser power of 5 mW and an acquisition time of 5 s. Raman spectra acquired for molecular quantification were collected from cells on the slide surface without liquid coverage. For metabolomic profiling, six biological replicates were collected per group, each from an independent culture tube. Pellets were harvested and washed three times with PBS (3000 rpm; 865×g, 5 min) to remove residual medium. Targeted metabolomics was performed using the HM700 panel (BGI) based on the LC–MS/MS platform. Metabolite extraction was carried out by adding 200 μL of methanol (A454-4, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to each 10 mg sample, followed by grinding in a tissue grinder with steel beads at 50 Hz for 300 s. The homogenate was centrifuged at 18,000×g and 4 °C for 20 min. A 160 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a 350 μL 96‑well plate and dried using a vacuum concentrator. The dried pellet was reconstituted in 20 μL of ultrapure water, mixed with 150 μL of sample release agent, shaken at 1200 rpm for 30 min, and centrifuged at 18,000×g and 4 °C for 30 min. A 40 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a new 96‑well plate and mixed with 25 μL of derivatization reagent and 25 μL of EDC working solution. The plate was sealed with aluminum foil and incubated in a thermostatic shaker at 40 °C and 1200 rpm for 60 min. After incubation, the plate was centrifuged at 4000×g and 4 °C for 5 min, and 90 μL of 50% methanol in water was added to each well, followed by mixing at 600 rpm for 10 min, and centrifuging at 4000×g and 4 °C for 30 min. UPLC–MS/MS analysis was performed on a QTRAP 6500 Plus system (SCIEX) equipped with an ESI Turbo ion spray interface, coupled to an ACQUITY UPLC (Waters) system with a BEH C18 chromatographic column (2.1 mm × 10 cm, 1.7 μm, Waters) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (phase A) and 30% isopropanol in acetonitrile (phase B). The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. Metabolite identification and quantification were conducted using Skyline (v 21.1.0.146) with monoisotopic peaks, mass tolerance of 0.6 Da, and a mass range of 50–1500 Da.
