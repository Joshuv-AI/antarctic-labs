Interference visibility as a witness of preparation contextuality via overlap inequalities
Mohd Asad Siddiqui

Abstract
We show that standard multi-path interferometry, using only pairwise visibility measurements, provides an operational route to tests of preparation noncontextuality. Under ideal symmetric conditions, interference visibility directly encodes state overlaps, without requiring tomography or SWAP tests. For three paths, any jointly diagonalizable (coherence-free) description must satisfy 𝒱 12 2 + 𝒱 23 2 − 𝒱 13 2 ≤ 1 \mathcal{V}_{12}^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{23}^{2}-\mathcal{V}_{13}^{2}\leq 1 , where 𝒱 i ​ j \mathcal{V}_{ij} are two-path visibilities. Pure qubit detector states violate this bound, achieving a maximal value of 5 / 4 5/4 . We generalize to arbitrary n n -path interferometers and derive the tight qubit bound S n max = n ​ cos 2 ⁡ ( π / 2 ​ n ) − 1 S_{n}^{\max}=n\cos^{2}(\pi/2n)-1 for all n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , achieved by coplanar pure qubit states with uniform angular separation π / n \pi/n . A robustness analysis yields explicit experimental thresholds. Under the operational equivalences used in overlap-based generalized noncontextuality frameworks, violations of these visibility inequalities also witness preparation contextuality. For n n -cycle inequalities, only the pairwise visibilities appearing in the cycle need to be measured.



I
Introduction

Quantum coherence is a defining feature of quantum theory and a primary
resource for quantum technologies. In the standard resource
theory
1
, coherence is defined relative to a fixed
reference basis. A complementary, basis-independent notion was
introduced by Designolle
et al.

5

characterizes coherence as the impossibility of simultaneously
diagonalizing a set of quantum states in a common basis. For triples of
states with pairwise overlaps

r_{ij}=|\langle d_{i}|d_{j}\rangle|^{2}
, coherence-free models satisfy
nontrivial overlap inequalities
7
;
23
.
Violations of these inequalities witness basis-independent coherence.
Under the operational equivalences assumed in generalized
noncontextuality frameworks
22
;
13
, the same
violations also witness preparation contextuality.

State overlaps are often accessed through tomography or specialized
overlap-estimation protocols such as SWAP tests
6
.
Multi-path interferometry provides a natural operational alternative:
coherence manifests through pairwise interference visibilities

\mathcal{V}_{ij}

16
, which, under ideal symmetric
conditions, directly encode the detector-state overlaps. This connection
is closely related to coherence-based formulations of multipath
wave-particle duality
2
;
15
.

We show that a standard multi-path interferometer, using only pairwise
visibility measurements, provides an interferometric realization of
preparation-noncontextuality tests within the generalized contextuality
framework
22
;
13
. The interferometer prepares a
family of detector states whose pairwise overlaps are operationally
determined by the measured visibilities. In this framework, any jointly
diagonalizable (i.e., coherence-free) description of a three-path
interferometer must satisfy

\mathcal{V}_{12}^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{23}^{2}-\mathcal{V}_{13}^{2}\leq 1,

(1)

while pure qubit detector states achieve the maximal quantum value
5/4
.
We further generalize to arbitrary
n
-cycle inequalities and derive the
exact quantum maximum for qubit detector states

S_{n}^{\max}=n\cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)-1,

(2)

uniquely achieved by
n
pure qubit states lying on a common great circle
with uniform angular separation
\pi/n
(up to global rotations,
reflections, and cyclic relabelings).

Previous work established these overlap-cycle inequalities and demonstrated
numerical quantum violations
23

maximal qubit violation analytically and provide an interferometric
realization.

This provides an experimentally accessible interferometric route to
testing overlap inequalities and preparation contextuality using
standard fringe-visibility measurements.

II
Interferometer and Visibilities

We assume standard quantum mechanics, in which only pairwise
interference terms contribute and higher-order interference is absent.
Consider an
n
-path interferometer in which a quantum particle is
prepared in the pure state

\ket{\Psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\ket{\psi_{i}},\qquad\sum_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}=1,

(3)

where
c_{i}
are path amplitudes and

\{\ket{\psi_{i}}\}
forms an orthonormal path basis.

Which-path information is extracted by coupling each path to a detector
initially prepared in the reference state
\ket{d_{0}}
. The interaction
correlates each path with a distinct detector state,

\ket{\psi_{i}}\ket{d_{0}}\longrightarrow\ket{\psi_{i}}\ket{d_{i}}.

(4)

After the interaction, the joint particle–detector state reads

\ket{\Psi}_{QD}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\ket{\psi_{i}}\ket{d_{i}}.

(5)

Tracing out the path degree of freedom yields the reduced detector state

\rho_{D}=\sum_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}\ket{d_{i}}\bra{d_{i}}.

(6)

We assume that the detector states
\ket{d_{i}}
are independent of which
subset of paths is opened, so that all pairwise visibilities correspond
to a single underlying set of detector states. We further assume stable
path amplitudes and ideal interferometric conditions in which visibility
loss arises solely from detector-state distinguishability.

For any pair of paths
(i,j)
, one may define the corresponding
two-path interference visibility
16

\mathcal{V}_{ij}=\frac{2|c_{i}c_{j}|}{|c_{i}|^{2}+|c_{j}|^{2}}\,|\langle d_{i}|d_{j}\rangle|.

(7)

Related visibility-based duality relations for three-slit interference
were studied earlier in Ref.
21
.

We define the squared overlap

r_{ij}=|\langle d_{i}|d_{j}\rangle|^{2}.

(8)

Operationally,
r_{ij}
is the Born-rule probability of obtaining the
outcome associated with the projector
\ket{d_{i}}\!\bra{d_{i}}
when
measuring the state
\ket{d_{j}}
. While
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
is linear in

|\langle d_{i}|d_{j}\rangle|
, the squared visibility is proportional to

r_{ij}
and coincides with it in the symmetric case discussed below.

In the symmetric case
|c_{i}|=1/\sqrt{n}
for all
i
,
Eq. (
7
) reduces to

\mathcal{V}_{ij}=|\langle d_{i}|d_{j}\rangle|,\qquad\mathcal{V}_{ij}^{2}=r_{ij}.

(9)

This mapping allows pairwise visibilities measured in a standard
interferometric setup to directly access the overlaps
r_{ij}

appearing in the noncontextuality inequalities, without requiring
discrete measurement settings or full state tomography.

The exact identification
\mathcal{V}_{ij}^{2}=r_{ij}
assumes pure
detector states and ideal interferometric conditions. For mixed
detector states, visibility generally no longer coincides exactly with
the pure-state overlap relation used here, and the tight quantum
bounds derived below need not apply directly.

In realistic experiments, imperfections such as decoherence and mode
mismatch reduce the observed visibilities, as studied in interferometric
complementarity under decoherence
20
. Under a calibrated
visibility-reduction model, the observed visibilities provide lower
bounds on the ideal overlap-derived visibilities. Consequently,
observed violations of the overlap inequalities remain sufficient to
certify nonclassicality within the assumed noise model.

In the symmetric case
|c_{i}|^{2}=1/n
, the Hilbert–Schmidt coherence
of the detector state is proportional to the total squared
visibility
17
,

C_{\mathrm{HS}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathcal{V}_{ij}^{2}.

(10)

Thus, Hilbert–Schmidt coherence depends only on the total visibility,
whereas the overlap inequalities derived here constrain the distribution
of visibility among different path pairs. This additional structural
constraint, absent in standard duality relations, underlies the
visibility-based contextuality witnesses developed in this work.

III
Classical polytope and visibility inequality (three paths)

For three detector states

\{|d_{1}\rangle,|d_{2}\rangle,|d_{3}\rangle\}
,
the classically allowed overlap triples

(r_{12},r_{23},r_{13})

form a convex polytope
C
consisting of overlaps realizable by
jointly diagonalizable states. The nontrivial facet inequalities of
this polytope are
7
;
23

r_{12}+r_{23}-r_{13}\leq 1,

(11)

together with its permutations.

Geometrically, these inequalities encode compatibility constraints among
pairwise overlaps. For example, if
r_{12}=1
and
r_{23}=1
, then
necessarily
r_{13}=1
. Assignments such as
(1,1,0)
violate this
compatibility and are therefore excluded.

Equivalently, Eq. (
11
) can be rearranged as

(1-r_{13})\leq(1-r_{12})+(1-r_{23}),

(12)

which takes the form of a triangle inequality for the disagreement
probabilities

P_{ij}(\mathrm{disagree})=1-r_{ij}
.
In any jointly diagonalizable description, the disagreement
probability between states
1
and
3
cannot exceed the sum of the
disagreement probabilities involving the adjacent pairs
(1,2)
and
(2,3)
.

Using Eq. (
7
), this constraint can be written
entirely in terms of measurable visibilities, yielding the

visibility inequality

\displaystyle\frac{(|c_{1}|^{2}+|c_{2}|^{2})^{2}}{4|c_{1}c_{2}|^{2}}\,\mathcal{V}_{12}^{2}+\frac{(|c_{2}|^{2}+|c_{3}|^{2})^{2}}{4|c_{2}c_{3}|^{2}}\,\mathcal{V}_{23}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{(|c_{1}|^{2}+|c_{3}|^{2})^{2}}{4|c_{1}c_{3}|^{2}}\,\mathcal{V}_{13}^{2}\leq 1,

(13)

valid for arbitrary nonzero path amplitudes under ideal
interferometric conditions.

For the symmetric interferometer (
|c_{i}|=1/\sqrt{3}
), this reduces to

\mathcal{V}_{12}^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{23}^{2}-\mathcal{V}_{13}^{2}\leq 1.

(14)

Any set of detector states admitting a jointly diagonalizable
description necessarily satisfies Eq. (
11
), and hence the
visibility inequality (
14
) in the symmetric case.
Therefore, any violation of Eq. (
14
) certifies that the
detector states are not jointly diagonalizable, thereby witnessing
basis-independent coherence. When the operational equivalences of the
overlap-based framework are imposed (Sec.
IV
), the same
inequality also acquires the interpretation of a
preparation-noncontextuality bound.

III.1
Maximal quantum violation for three states

Theorem 1

(Maximal quantum violation for three states)
.

For any three pure qubit detector states with overlaps

r_{ij}=|\langle d_{i}|d_{j}\rangle|^{2}
,

S\equiv r_{12}+r_{23}-r_{13}\leq\frac{5}{4}.

The bound is tight and is achieved by the qubit triplet

|d_{1}\rangle=\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|0\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}|1\rangle,\quad|d_{2}\rangle=|0\rangle,\quad|d_{3}\rangle=\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|0\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}|1\rangle,

for which
r_{12}=r_{23}=3/4
and
r_{13}=1/4
.

Proof.

For fixed
r_{12}
and
r_{23}
, maximizing
S
is equivalent to
minimizing
r_{13}
. The positivity of the Gram matrix constrains the
admissible overlaps, and the maximal value of
S
is attained on the
boundary
\det G=0
. Using the tight lower bound derived in
Appendix
A
,

r_{13}\geq\begin{cases}\bigl(\sqrt{r_{12}r_{23}}-\sqrt{(1-r_{12})(1-r_{23})}\bigr)^{2},&r_{12}+r_{23}>1,\\[4.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

we obtain

S\leq r_{12}+r_{23}-\bigl(\sqrt{r_{12}r_{23}}-\sqrt{(1-r_{12})(1-r_{23})}\bigr)^{2}.

Parametrizing
r_{12}=\cos^{2}\beta
and
r_{23}=\cos^{2}\gamma
, the
inequality becomes

S\leq\cos^{2}\beta+\cos^{2}\gamma-\cos^{2}(\beta+\gamma).

Optimization over
\beta
and
\gamma
yields the unique interior
stationary point
\beta=\gamma=\pi/6
, for which
S_{\max}=5/4
.

The saturating states correspond to Bloch vectors lying on a common
great circle of the Bloch sphere. Since for pure qubit states

r_{ij}=\cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{\theta_{ij}}{2}\right),

the optimal overlaps
r_{12}=r_{23}=3/4
and
r_{13}=1/4

correspond to geodesic separations

\theta_{12}=\theta_{23}=\frac{\pi}{3},\qquad\theta_{13}=\frac{2\pi}{3}.

∎

III.2
Three-path experimental realization

A physical realization uses three which-path detector states encoded in
photon polarization:

|d_{1}\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|H\rangle+\frac{1}{2}|V\rangle,\quad|d_{2}\rangle=|H\rangle,\quad|d_{3}\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|H\rangle-\frac{1}{2}|V\rangle,

(15)

where
|H\rangle
and
|V\rangle
denote horizontal and vertical
polarization states. These are the optimal states saturating the quantum
bound
S=5/4
. In a symmetric three-path interferometer, the corresponding
visibilities are
\mathcal{V}_{12}=\mathcal{V}_{23}=\sqrt{3}/2\approx 0.866

and
\mathcal{V}_{13}=1/2
, yielding

\mathcal{V}_{12}^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{23}^{2}-\mathcal{V}_{13}^{2}=\frac{3}{4}+\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{4}=\frac{5}{4}>1.

(16)

III.3
Uniform visibility reduction

In realistic interferometers, imperfect mode overlap and alignment reduce
the observed two-path visibility. We model this by a common efficiency
factor
0<\eta\leq 1
, assuming uniform visibility reduction across all
path pairs, such that
\mathcal{V}_{ij}^{\mathrm{exp}}=\eta\mathcal{V}_{ij}
.
Substituting into Eq. (
14
), the left-hand side scales as

\eta^{2}S
. Since the classical bound is unity in the three-state case,
a violation requires

\eta>\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}.

For
S=5/4
, this yields
\eta>2/\sqrt{5}\approx 0.894
, within reach
of current integrated photonic platforms
12
;
9
.

III.4
Interpretation: coherence versus contextuality

A violation of Eq. (
14
) certifies that the detector states are
not jointly diagonalizable, thereby witnessing basis-independent
coherence
5
. Under the operational assumptions
discussed in Sec.
IV
, the same inequality also admits an
interpretation as a preparation-noncontextuality inequality in the
generalized noncontextuality framework
22
.

IV
Connection to preparation noncontextuality

For qubit detector states, the operational equivalences needed for a
preparation-noncontextuality test arise naturally from the interferometric
setup. A balanced
n
-path interferometer assigns identical
a priori

probabilities to each path when no which-path information is extracted.
When path
i
is paired with path
j
in a two-path interference
experiment, the observed visibility
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
operationally
determines the detector-state overlap
|\langle d_{i}|d_{j}\rangle|
.
Observed interference visibilities therefore directly determine the
overlaps entering the noncontextuality inequalities. Any jointly
diagonalizable description must therefore satisfy the corresponding
classical overlap inequalities.

The cyclic inequality derived in Sec.
III

provides the operational translation of this noncontextuality condition.
It bounds the admissible pairwise visibility configurations
compatible with coherence-free detector states. Within the framework
of generalized noncontextuality
22
;
13
,
violations of this inequality admit an interpretation in terms of
preparation contextuality, with the detector states

\{|d_{i}\rangle\}
serving as operational preparations and the
projectors
\{|d_{i}\rangle\langle d_{i}|\}
defining the measurements
used to access their overlaps.

In the interferometric realization considered here, the equal-weight
mixtures

\frac{1}{2}P_{i,0}+\frac{1}{2}P_{i,1}

all prepare the same maximally mixed state
\mathbb{I}/2
, independently of
i
.

These operational equivalences may be verified experimentally from the
observed statistics or enforced by symmetry of the implementation.
More general realizations may require approximate operational
equivalences
13
; see also Ref.
14
for
robust preparation-noncontextuality inequalities and
tomographic-completeness assumptions.

In preparation-noncontextual ontological models, operational overlaps
satisfy constraints of the type discussed in Ref.
18
.
In particular, preparation noncontextuality constrains the admissible
pairwise overlaps through triangle-inequality-type relations among the
associated epistemic states. These imply the classical overlap inequality

r_{12}+r_{23}-r_{13}\leq 1,

which is Eq. (
11
). Consequently, a violation of
Eq. (
14
) certifies that the detector states are not jointly
diagonalizable, thereby certifying basis-independent coherence and,
under the operational assumptions above, preparation contextuality.

Unlike existing discrete prepare-and-measure schemes, the present
approach extracts overlap information directly from continuous
fringe-visibility measurements, reducing the reliance on
tomographic-completeness assumptions and extensive switching among
incompatible measurement settings.

The protocol requires only pairwise visibility measurements, without
tomography or multi-copy tests. This provides an experimentally
accessible route to tests of preparation contextuality in photonic
interferometers.

V
Tight qubit bound for general
n

The three-state inequality of Theorem
1

corresponds to the
n=3
case of the cycle expression introduced below.
Equivalent cycle inequalities are obtained by cyclic permutations of the
detector labels. We define the
n
-cycle overlap expression

S_{n}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}r_{i,i+1}-r_{1n},

(17)

For jointly diagonalizable states, the cycle inequalities satisfy the
classical bound
S_{n}\leq n-2

7
;
23
, which follows
recursively from the three-state inequality
r_{ij}+r_{jk}-r_{ik}\leq 1
.

The case
n=2
is trivial, since
S_{2}=r_{12}-r_{12}=0
identically
and therefore admits no nonclassical violation; the first nontrivial
cycle occurs at
n=3
. We now derive the tight qubit bound for arbitrary
cycle length
n\geq 3
.

Theorem 2

(Tight qubit bound for the
n
-cycle)
.

For any
n\geq 3
pure qubit states
\{|d_{1}\rangle,\dots,|d_{n}\rangle\}

with pairwise overlaps
r_{ij}=|\langle d_{i}|d_{j}\rangle|^{2}
,

S_{n}\leq n\cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2n}\right)-1.

The bound is tight. Up to global rotations,
reflections, and cyclic relabelings, it is uniquely
achieved by
n
Bloch vectors lying on a common
great circle with uniform angular separation
\pi/n
.

n
-cycle overlap inequality.
(a)
n=3
: three coplanar detector states with uniform angular separation

\Delta=\pi/3
on the equatorial great circle of the Bloch sphere.
(b)
n=4
: four coplanar detector states with uniform angular separation

\Delta=\pi/4
.

S_{n}^{\max}=n\cos^{2}(\pi/2n)-1
,
uniquely up to global rotations, reflections, and cyclic relabelings of the detectors.

The optimal configurations for
n=3
and
n=4
are illustrated in
Fig.
1
.

Proof.

Step 1: Bloch vector formulation.
—
Represent each pure qubit state by a unit Bloch vector
\vec{v}_{i}\in S^{2}
.
The overlaps satisfy
r_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(1+\vec{v}_{i}\cdot\vec{v}_{j})
, giving

S_{n}=\frac{n-2}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\vec{v}_{i}\cdot\vec{v}_{i+1}-\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{n}\Bigg].

(18)

Define the nearest-neighbor step angles

\theta_{i}\equiv\theta_{i,i+1}=\arccos(\vec{v}_{i}\cdot\vec{v}_{i+1}),\qquad i=1,\dots,n-1,

and the closing angle

\theta_{1n}=\arccos(\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{n}).

All angles lie in
[0,\pi]
. Then

S_{n}=\frac{n-2}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\cos\theta_{i}-\cos\theta_{1n}\Bigg].

(19)

Step 2: Upper bound via the spherical triangle inequality.
—
Let
\Theta=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\theta_{i}
. Since geodesic distance on the
Bloch sphere
S^{2}
satisfies the triangle inequality, repeated application
along the chain
\vec{v}_{1},\vec{v}_{2},\dots,\vec{v}_{n}
gives

\theta_{1n}\leq\Theta.

(20)

Equality is attained when the vectors lie on a common great circle,
with the vectors ordered monotonically along the great circle.

We first analyze the regime
\Theta\leq\pi
; the maximizing configuration
will later be verified to lie within this domain. On
[0,\pi]
,
the function
\cos x
is strictly decreasing, so Eq. (
20
)
implies

\cos\theta_{1n}\geq\cos\Theta,\qquad\text{hence}\qquad-\cos\theta_{1n}\leq-\cos\Theta.

(21)

Substituting into Eq. (
19
) yields the upper bound

S_{n}\leq\frac{n-2}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\cos\theta_{i}-\cos\Theta\Bigg],

(22)

valid for all configurations with
\Theta\leq\pi
.

Step 3: Optimization.
—
The upper bound in Eq. (
22
) depends solely on the

n-1
step angles
\theta_{i}\in[0,\pi]
, constrained by

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\theta_{i}=\Theta\leq\pi.

We first show that any maximizing configuration must satisfy

\theta_{i}\leq\pi/2
for all
i
.
Suppose instead that some angle
\theta_{k}>\pi/2
.
Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\theta_{i}\leq\pi
, the remaining angles cannot all
satisfy
\theta_{i}\geq\pi/2
. Hence, there exists at least one angle

\theta_{j}<\pi/2
.
Keeping the sum

c=\theta_{k}+\theta_{j}

fixed, consider

g(x)=\cos x+\cos(c-x)=2\cos\!\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)\cos\!\left(x-\frac{c}{2}\right).

(23)

Since
c\leq\pi
, we have
c/2\in[0,\pi/2]
, and therefore
\cos(c/2)\geq 0
,
so
g(x)
is maximized at
x=c/2
.
Therefore, replacing
(\theta_{k},\theta_{j})
by
(c/2,c/2)
strictly increases

\cos\theta_{k}+\cos\theta_{j}
unless
\theta_{k}=\theta_{j}
already.
Hence, no maximizing configuration can contain an angle larger than
\pi/2
.

The optimization may therefore be restricted to the domain

\theta_{i}\in[0,\pi/2]
, on which
f(x)=\cos x
is concave.
Applying Jensen’s inequality
4
gives

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\cos\theta_{i}\leq(n-1)\cos\!\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\theta_{i}}{n-1}\right)=(n-1)\cos\phi,

(24)

where

\phi=\frac{\Theta}{n-1}\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right].

Equality holds if and only if

\theta_{1}=\cdots=\theta_{n-1}=\phi.

Substituting into Eq. (
22
) gives

S_{n}\leq H(\phi)\equiv\frac{n-2}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Big[(n-1)\cos\phi-\cos((n-1)\phi)\Big].

(25)

Differentiating,

H^{\prime}(\phi)=\frac{n-1}{2}\Big[\sin((n-1)\phi)-\sin\phi\Big].

(26)

Stationary points satisfy

\sin((n-1)\phi)=\sin\phi.

Restricting to
\phi\in[0,\pi/(n-1)]
, the solutions are

\phi=0,\qquad\phi=\frac{\pi}{n}.

The second derivative is

H^{\prime\prime}(\phi)=\frac{n-1}{2}\big[(n-1)\cos((n-1)\phi)-\cos\phi\big].

(27)

At
\phi=\pi/n
, we have
(n-1)\pi/n=\pi-\pi/n
.
Using
\cos(\pi-x)=-\cos x
, we obtain

\cos((n-1)\pi/n)=-\cos(\pi/n)
. Hence,

H^{\prime\prime}(\pi/n)=-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\cos(\pi/n)<0,

(28)

since
\cos(\pi/n)>0
for all
n\geq 3
. Thus,
\phi=\pi/n
is a
strict local maximum of
H
.

At
\phi=0
, we have
H^{\prime}(0)=0
and

H^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}>0.

Therefore,
\phi=0
is a strict local minimum of
H
, and hence cannot
maximize
H
.

The preceding analysis shows that
\phi=\pi/n
is the unique interior
stationary point of
H(\phi)
on

\left[0,\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right].

It therefore remains only to compare this value with the boundary
points. Since

H(\pi/n)>H(0),

and Appendix
B
proves that

H(\pi/n)>H\!\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right),

it follows that
\phi=\pi/n
is the global maximizer on

\left[0,\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right].

At the optimum,

\theta_{i}=\frac{\pi}{n},

and since
n\geq 3
,

\frac{\pi}{n}\leq\frac{\pi}{3}<\frac{\pi}{2},

all step angles lie in
[0,\pi/2]
, where
\cos x
is concave and
Jensen’s inequality applies.

Moreover,

\Theta=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\theta_{i}=(n-1)\frac{\pi}{n}=\pi-\frac{\pi}{n}<\pi,

so the maximizing configuration also lies within the domain

\Theta\leq\pi
assumed in
Step 2
.

Evaluating at the optimum,

\displaystyle S_{n}^{\max}

\displaystyle=H(\pi/n)

\displaystyle=\frac{n-2}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Big[(n-1)\cos(\pi/n)-\cos(\pi-\pi/n)\Big]

For large

VI
Discussion and experimental feasibility

We provide an interferometric framework for testing overlap inequalities
and generalized noncontextuality based on pairwise visibility measurements.
Overlap inequalities were identified as basis-independent coherence
witnesses by Galvão and Brod
7
, and the event-graph
framework of Wagner
et al.

23
unified coherence,
contextuality, and nonlocality, establishing cycle inequalities as
preparation-noncontextuality inequalities.

Recent experiments have demonstrated direct access to pairwise overlaps
in photonic systems
8
, and
n
-cycle contextuality
inequalities have been tested using interferometric schemes such as
two-photon conditional Young interference
3
. The present
approach reduces such contextuality tests to standard single-photon
visibility measurements. Pairwise visibilities are obtained by opening
one pair of paths at a time and extracting the corresponding fringe
contrast, without joint measurements, coincidence detection, or state
tomography, provided the path amplitudes are known and stable. For cycle
inequalities, only the
n
pairwise visibilities appearing in the cycle
need to be measured.

Wagner
et al.

24
applied this framework to
Mach-Zehnder interferometers using a discrete prepare-and-measure
approach. The present work provides a direct operational mapping from
interference visibilities to these inequalities, enabling contextuality
tests using standard fringe-contrast measurements.

A direct proof-of-principle implementation is provided by the three-path
configuration discussed in Sec.
III.2
, using
polarization-encoded detector states

|d_{1}\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|H\rangle+\frac{1}{2}|V\rangle,\ |d_{2}\rangle=|H\rangle,\ |d_{3}\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|H\rangle-\frac{1}{2}|V\rangle,

which achieves the maximal violation
S_{3}=5/4
and requires visibility

\eta>2/\sqrt{5}\approx 0.894
.

A scalable four-path implementation uses detector polarization states at
angles
0^{\circ}
,
22.5^{\circ}
,
45^{\circ}
, and
67.5^{\circ}
relative to
the horizontal axis, requiring
\eta>\sqrt{2/(1+\sqrt{2})}\approx 0.910

for a violation. Both thresholds lie within reach of current integrated
photonic platforms
12
;
9
.

VII
Conclusion

We have shown that multi-path interferometry provides an operational
route to tests of preparation noncontextuality under the overlap-based
framework using only pairwise visibility measurements. For three paths,
the inequality

\mathcal{V}_{12}^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{23}^{2}-\mathcal{V}_{13}^{2}\leq 1

holds for any jointly diagonalizable set of detector states, while pure
qubit detector states achieve a maximal violation of
5/4
. We proved the
tight qubit bound
S_{n}^{\max}=n\cos^{2}(\pi/2n)-1
for arbitrary
n\geq 3
,
uniquely achieved by
n
coplanar pure qubit states with uniform angular
separation
\pi/n
(up to global rotations, reflections, and cyclic
relabelings). Our results establish interference visibility as a practical
and scalable witness of preparation contextuality within
overlap-based noncontextuality frameworks. Whether higher-dimensional
detector states can exceed the qubit optimum derived here remains an
interesting open question.

Appendix A
Gram matrix constraints and derivation of the overlap bound

For three pure detector states
\{|d_{1}\rangle,|d_{2}\rangle,|d_{3}\rangle\}
,
define the Gram matrix

G=\begin{pmatrix}1&\langle d_{1}|d_{2}\rangle&\langle d_{1}|d_{3}\rangle\\
\langle d_{2}|d_{1}\rangle&1&\langle d_{2}|d_{3}\rangle\\
\langle d_{3}|d_{1}\rangle&\langle d_{3}|d_{2}\rangle&1\end{pmatrix}.

(30)

Physical realizability requires
G\succeq 0
. Since the
1\times 1
and

2\times 2
principal minors are automatically nonnegative
(
0\leq r_{ij}\leq 1
), the determinant condition provides the only
nontrivial positivity constraint:

\det G\geq 0.

Let
r_{ij}=|\langle d_{i}|d_{j}\rangle|^{2}
and write the overlaps as

\langle d_{i}|d_{j}\rangle=\sqrt{r_{ij}}\,e^{i\theta_{ij}}
. By suitable
rephasing of the states, two overlaps may be chosen real and positive,
leaving a single gauge-invariant phase:

\langle d_{1}|d_{2}\rangle=\sqrt{r_{12}},\quad\langle d_{2}|d_{3}\rangle=\sqrt{r_{23}},\quad\langle d_{1}|d_{3}\rangle=\sqrt{r_{13}}\,e^{i\varphi},

(31)

with
\varphi=\theta_{13}-\theta_{12}-\theta_{23}
.

Evaluating the determinant yields

\det G=1+2\sqrt{r_{12}r_{23}r_{13}}\cos\varphi-r_{12}-r_{23}-r_{13}.

(32)

The determinant condition therefore provides the only nontrivial
positivity constraint on the overlaps entering the optimization of

S=r_{12}+r_{23}-r_{13}.

(33)

For fixed
r_{12}
and
r_{23}
, the minimal allowed value of
r_{13}
is
obtained by maximizing the phase-dependent contribution to
\det G
,
which occurs at
\cos\varphi=1
. Defining
x=\sqrt{r_{13}}\geq 0
, the
inequality reduces to

x^{2}-2\sqrt{r_{12}r_{23}}\,x+(r_{12}+r_{23}-1)\leq 0.

(34)

Since the coefficient of
x^{2}
is positive, the inequality is satisfied
for
x
between the two real roots,

x_{\pm}=\sqrt{r_{12}r_{23}}\pm\sqrt{(1-r_{12})(1-r_{23})}.

(35)

For
r_{12}+r_{23}>1
, the lower root is positive and gives the minimal
admissible value of
x
, yielding the tight bound

r_{13}\geq\left(\sqrt{r_{12}r_{23}}-\sqrt{(1-r_{12})(1-r_{23})}\right)^{2}.

(36)

By contrast, when
r_{12}+r_{23}\leq 1
, the lower root is nonpositive,
and the physical condition
r_{13}\geq 0
already permits
r_{13}=0

as the tight lower bound.

Equality in Eq. (
36
) is attained when
\varphi=0
,
corresponding to three pure qubit states whose Bloch vectors lie on a
common great circle. In this case all pairwise overlaps may be chosen
simultaneously real and positive. For triples of pure states, positivity
of the Gram matrix provides necessary and sufficient conditions for the
overlaps to be compatible with a valid quantum realization. The
optimization of
S
is therefore completely determined by the determinant
constraint.

Appendix B
Boundary comparison for the
n
-cycle optimization

To establish that
\phi=\pi/n
is the global maximizer of
H(\phi)
on

[0,\pi/(n-1)]
, it suffices to verify that

H(\pi/n)>H\!\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right).

From the definition of
H(\phi)
one obtains

H(\pi/n)-H\!\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\Big[g(n)-g(n-1)-1\Big],

where
g(x)=x\cos(\pi/x)
.

For
x>2
, we have
0<\pi/x<\pi/2
, hence
\cos(\pi/x)>0
and

g^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{x^{3}}\cos(\pi/x)<0.

Thus
g
is strictly concave on
(2,\infty)
, which implies that the
forward differences
\Delta g(n)=g(n)-g(n-1)
are strictly decreasing.

Since

\Delta g(3)=g(3)-g(2)=3\cos(\pi/3)-2\cos(\pi/2)=\frac{3}{2}>1,

and
\Delta g(n)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, it follows that

\Delta g(n)>1
for all
n\geq 3
.

Consequently,

H(\pi/n)>H\!\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right).

Together with the earlier verification that
H(\pi/n)>H(0)
, this
establishes
\phi=\pi/n
as the unique global maximizer.
