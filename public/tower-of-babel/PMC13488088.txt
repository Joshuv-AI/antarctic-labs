Feasibility of multi-component intervention for blood pressure control in pregnancy in Nigeria and Ghana: a pilot randomized clinical trial

Introduction

Hypertensive disorders in pregnancy affect approximately 18 million pregnancies worldwide and are a leading cause of maternal and fetal morbidities and mortalities [1–5]. They account for about 14% and 15% of the maternal deaths and stillbirths respectively [1–6]. They are 5 times as prevalent in Sub-Saharan Africa compared to higher-income countries (13% vs. 2.73%) with a relatively higher rate of adverse outcomes [2, 3].

The four categories of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy are gestational hypertension, preeclampsia/eclampsia, chronic hypertension, and chronic hypertension complicated with preeclampsia/eclampsia. All four categories complicate about 10% of all pregnancies while chronic hypertension has been found in 3–5% of all pregnancies [1–5]. The relatively greater prevalence of chronic hypertension in blacks with onset at younger ages makes it a common encounter in pregnant black women [7, 8].

Chronic hypertension in pregnancy is associated with several obstetric complications [9–11]. A systematic review revealed that the rate of occurrence of superimposed preeclampsia was about 26% [11], Cesarean delivery rate was 41% [11], preterm delivery occurred in 28%, with 17% of the babies having low birth weight [11]. Neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission was required for 21% of the babies while perinatal death occurred in 4% [11]. Chronic hypertension is associated with a higher risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes than gestational hypertension [5]. The odds of developing myocardial infarction (MI) and stroke were significantly higher in chronic hypertensives compared to gestational hypertension but highest in chronic hypertension with super-imposed preeclampsia [12].

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy have substantial economic repercussions. The impact is especially significant in low- and middle-income countries where health financing systems are limited [13–15]. Access to healthcare in low- and middle- income countries (LMICs) continue to be a challenge, with significant strain on the few existing ones [13–15]. Cost-effective interventions with favourable health outcomes could thus be extremely beneficial in these patients [3, 6]. Physiologic changes in pregnancy and the restricted choice of antihypertensive drugs makes the management of hypertensive disorders very challenging. Thus, evaluating the adoption of non-pharmacological blood pressure control techniques to supplement existing pharmacological therapy in pregnant women with chronic hypertension could be very beneficial in this patient population [16–18].

Studies have shown that the addition of non-pharmacological measures are effective in improving medication adherence, sustained blood pressure control, and reduced complications in patients with chronic hypertension [19–29]. However, pregnant women are mostly excluded from such clinical trials, hence, evidence on its impact on feto-maternal outcomes in the pregnant population remain sparse, particularly on the African continent.

We propose to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of delivering a multi-component intervention, in addition to standard pharmacologic care, to support blood pressure control among pregnant women with chronic hypertension in two African countries (Ghana and Nigeria).

The primary objective was to determine the feasibility of conducting a multi-component intervention study among pregnant women with chronic hypertension by assessing the participant recruitment rates, retention rate, fidelity to the protocol and acceptability.

The secondary objectives were to describe the within-group changes in systolic blood pressure from enrolment to 6 weeks post-delivery and to estimate the proportion of participants who achieved optimal blood pressure control (defined as BP < 140/90 mm Hg) in each study arm.

The tertiary objective was to evaluate the effect of the multi-component intervention on adverse maternal and fetal outcomes.

Methodology

This was a pilot multi-center randomized controlled trial that involved 60 pregnant women with chronic hypertension who presented for booking within the first trimester or before 20 weeks of gestation and randomized into the intervention arm (multi-components intervention) and the control arm (usual care) in 2 tertiary hospitals in Nigeria and Ghana.

The multi-component approach included standard pharmacologic treatment for blood pressure control, home blood pressure monitoring, regular extensive counselling on lifestyle modifications and medication adherence, and regular short message services (SMS) to reinforce counselling.

This study was conducted in 2 tertiary hospitals in Nigeria and Ghana, namely, Kwara State Teaching Hospital Ilorin, Nigeria and Komfo Anokye Teaching hospital, Kumasi, Ghana. It was registered with the Pan-African Clinical trial registry with trial registration number: PACTR202306896247858, dated 20th June 2023.

Participants were recruited using consecutive sampling. All pregnant women attending antenatal clinics who met the study definition of chronic hypertension were approached and enrolled throughout the study period. The study was a feasibility pilot; thus, no formal power calculation was performed. The sample size of 60 women (30 per country) was chosen based on available resources to generate preliminary data for future studies.

Assessment of outcomes

The primary outcomes were the participant recruitment rate, retention rate, fidelity to the protocol and acceptability.

The secondary outcomes were the mean change in systolic BP from enrollment to 6 weeks post- delivery and the proportion of participants who achieved BP control (defined as < 140/90 mm Hg).

The tertiary outcomes were the rates of adverse maternal (i.e. pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, and maternal mortality), mode of delivery (cesarean section specifically for hypertensive severity or spontaneous vaginal delivery) and adverse fetal (Intra-uterine growth restrictions, intra-uterine fetal demise, preterm delivery, neonatal intensive care unit admission, perinatal deaths) outcomes.

The mean change in systolic blood pressure was measured at 6 weeks post-partum while the maternal and fetal outcomes were measured every 4 weeks till 6 weeks post-delivery. The time-point of these outcome measures are as illustrated in Table 1.

Operational definitions

Chronic hypertension in pregnancy was defined as a documented systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg that was present before pregnancy, diagnosed before 20 weeks’ gestation, or known to persist beyond 12 weeks postpartum, in accordance with international obstetric hypertension guidelines [1].

Standard pharmacological care (Usual Care) refers to routine antenatal hypertension management provided at participating health facilities, based on local and national clinical guidelines [1, 8]. This typically included antihypertensive therapy using pregnancy-safe medications (such as methyldopa, labetalol, or nifedipine), dose adjustments at the discretion of the managing clinician, and routine antenatal follow-up without structured behavioral, educational, or adherence-support components beyond usual care.

Multi-component approach for blood pressure control included home Blood Pressure monitoring, lifestyle modification, medication adherence counselling, biweekly text message reminders and motivational messages to reinforce counselling in addition to the standard pharmacologic treatment [21 − 5].

Recruitment rate was defined as the total number of participants enrolled per unit time. It was calculated as the total number of participants divide by the number of months. The numerator was the total number of participants enrolled per site, and the denominator was time (in months) [31, 32].

Acceptability was defined as the extent to which the participants receiving the multi-component intervention perceive it as appropriate, agreeable, or satisfactory. It was evaluated every 4 weeks, using the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM) [30, 31], using a 5-point Likert scale [30–32].

Retention rate was defined as the proportion of participants who remain active in the study and completed all required follow-up assessments through to the final endpoint. It was calculated by dividing the total number of participants who completed the study (Numerator) by the total number enrolled (Denominator) expressed as a percentage, and it was measured at 6 weeks post-partum [30–32].

Fidelity of Participants to protocol was defined as the degree to which an intervention is delivered exactly as it was designed and intended by the study protocol. Fidelity of the participants to protocol was assessed through a checklist, which was assessed every 4 weeks from recruitment until 6 weeks post-delivery. A minimum score of 90% was required [30–32].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All pregnant women aged 18–49 years who had chronic hypertension (sustained systolic blood pressure of ≥ 140/90 mmHg, present at booking or before 20 weeks gestation, or receiving anti-hypertensives before pregnancy) who consented were recruited, while participants with pregnancy-induced hypertension, preeclampsia/eclampsia, diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, sickle cell disease were all excluded from the study.

Study procedures

Sixty (60) participants were recruited, comprising of 30 participants for each study site, with 15 participants in each study arm. All subjects were enrolled at the antenatal clinics in these facilities.

Participants who consented and met the inclusion criteria were allocated into one of the two groups by simple randomization [33]. No blinding was done due to the nature of the interventions. The different study groups were however given separate clinic days to reduce the possibility of inter-group participants’ interaction. The randomization flow is depicted in Fig. 1.

Participants randomized to the control arm received the usual care which is the standard pharmacologic treatment only, as delivered by the Obstetrician as per the hospital guidelines without any interference from the study team.

Participants in both the intervention and control (usual care) groups were followed using an identical visit schedule, as stipulated by the guidelines on management of Chronic Hypertension in pregnancy [1, 12, 21]. The technique of home BP monitoring

The participants were required to check their BPs in the morning on 3 days (alternate days) each week prior to medications. They were required to sit comfortably and calmly for about 30 min before taking two readings, two minutes apart, with the outstretched arm supported at approximately the same level as the heart [36–38]. An appropriate-sized blood pressure cuff with the length of the bladder being at least equal to 80% of the circumference of the upper arm was used in each case. The cuff was wrapped snugly around the upper arm with the cuff’s lower edge one inch above the ante-cubital fossa and the monitor was switched on [36–38]. Elevated values above 140/90mmHg prompted a third reading which was recorded and communicated via text messages to a dedicated line. This allowed for dosage adjustments where necessary. For values of 160/110mmHg or more, participants were instructed to visit the obstetric emergency for further management.

Weight and height were measured by the nurses at the clinic in accordance with the standard protocol, the body mass index (BMI) was then calculated using the standard formular: (Weight(kg)/Height (m2).

Data analysis

Data was analysed using the statistical package for social sciences software SPSS (version 23, 2011, SPSS Inc. Chicago, Illinois). Data collected on the proforma was entered into a master sheet using numerical codes. Frequency distribution tables and cross-tabulation of variables were generated. Measures of central tendency and dispersion of quantitative variables such as mean, median, standard deviation and inter-quartile range, as well as the proportion for categorical variables were also determined. The chi-square and student t-tests were used to identify significant differences for categorical and continuous variables respectively. Yates correction and Fisher’s exact tests were used as appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant. Data analysis was done as per protocol, meaning that only the available data were analysed.

The mean age of subjects in both arms was comparable (p = 0.513). There was no statistical difference in marital status, ethnic group, social class or parity between both arms of the study in each country. (Tables 2 and 3)

All participants from Ghana had health insurance, while none of those from Nigeria had insurance.

There was no difference in the mean weight, height and BMI of participants in the intervention and control arms. The mean systolic and diastolic blood pressures at enrolment in the intervention arm were 141.6 ± 15mmHg and 89.5 ± 10.6mmHg, and these were comparable to the blood pressures in the control arm.

Regarding symptoms recorded at enrolment, headaches (43.3% of all subjects) and dizziness (26.7% of all subjects) were most commonly reported in both arms. A family history of hypertension was present in 50.0% of subjects in the control arm and 36.7% of the intervention group. There was no difference in the distribution of symptoms at presentation and family history of hypertension between both study groups.

Recruitment, fidelity, acceptability retention

There were 5 (8.3%) drop-outs from both countries, 4 from Nigeria and 1 from Ghana. Hence, the retention rates at end of the study were 86.7% and 96.7% in Nigeria and Ghana respectively.

Fidelity to protocol ranged from 80 to 100% in both countries throughout the course of the study.

All the participants in both countries strongly agree on a 5-point Likert scale, that the inclusion of the three additional components to their ante natal care “meets their approval”, “found it appealing”, “liked” and “welcomed” all three.

Comparing all blood pressure readings taken at all study visits, 196 blood pressure readings were in the intervention arm compared to 181 in the control arm. This difference is accounted for by dropouts from the study and time points at which endpoint maternal adverse events occurred. Sixty three (32.1%) of the 196 BP readings at all study visits in the intervention arm were above 140/90mmHg as compared to 64 (35.4%) BP readings in the control arm being above the same cutoff. This difference in the proportion with high blood pressure readings was not statistically significant (p = 0.509).

Only 6 (20.0%) subjects achieved adequate BP control at all study visits following the first visit in the intervention arm compared to 7 subjects (24.1%) in the control arm. This difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.701).

Regarding adverse maternal and fetal outcomes as depicted in Table 6, superimposed pre-eclampsia was most commonly seen in the usual care arm of the study. The odds of superimposed pre-eclampsia in the intervention arm were 27% less than the odds in the control arm (p = 0.027). The cesarean section rate in the intervention arm of 27.6% was lower than that recorded in the control arm (50.0%) but this marginally failed to achieve statistical significance (p = 0.05).

Discussion

We successfully executed the first pilot multi-center randomized controlled trial among pregnant women with chronic hypertension in two tertiary hospitals in Nigeria and Ghana. This study suggests that delivering a multi-components intervention for blood pressure control in women with chronic hypertension in pregnancy is feasible and could be superior to the usual care pathways in which only pharmacological intervention is provided, with the documented reasonable feasibility indices, the suggested significant mean difference in both systolic and diastolic blood pressures in the intervention arm compared with the controls, and the suggested significant odds of developing superimposed preeclampsia in the control arm compared with the intervention arm.

In this current study, the retention, fidelity to protocol and acceptability were generally high and comparable to previous studies [21, 38, 39]. This indicates that conducting this study on a larger scale in Africa is feasible.

The earlier onset of the lowest dip in both systolic and diastolic blood pressures, coupled with the statistically significant mean change in systolic and diastolic BP in the intervention arm from study enrolment to completion, may indicate that optimal BP control-with its anticipated benefits- is achieved much earlier with the multi-component approach than usual care. This contrasts with some previous studies where these differences were not statistically significant [21, 44]. This may be as a result of short duration for the implementation of intervention as patients were recruited till late in the third trimester, and these studies included all categories of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy including preeclampsia and eclampsia [21, 44]. This study also found comparable proportion of blood pressure readings below 140/90mmHg (optimal BP) in both arms of the study, which is similar to findings of Thakur et al. in BUMP 2 trial, where no significant difference was recorded in mean systolic blood pressure in the self-monitoring group as compared to the usual care group. However, it is important to point out that the current study was not sufficiently powered to measure this outcome as this was designed as a feasibility study. Cernota et al. in a systematic review reported some studies with significant blood pressure reduction with non-Pharmacologic intervention, these studies were however done in the non-pregnant population [40].

Twice the number of participants developed super-imposed preeclampsia in the control group compared to the intervention and this difference was significant, indicating direct benefit of the multi-component intervention, furthermore, the odds of developing superimposed pre-eclampsia in the intervention arm were 27% less as compared to the odds in the control arm. This contrasts with previous studies done [21, 44]. Cesarean section for hypertensive severity was more in the control than the intervention, although this difference was not statistically significant, similar to previous studies [21, 38, 44]. Although this study did not detect significant differences in adverse fetal outcomes in both study arms, the marginal differences observed in both groups in each country may be casual as a result of the limited sample size. The comparable maternal and perinatal outcomes in this study contrast with findings by Magee et al. in the CHIPS randomized control trial which indicated that better outcomes were achieved with tighter blood pressure control [45].

The three interventions evaluated together in this study appear to be novel as previous studies had evaluated the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of self-monitoring alone [21, 38, 39], while other studies evaluating the effect of non-pharmacologic methods were conducted in the non-pregnant population [40–43]. Africa bears the greatest burden of pregnancy-related hypertensive disorders globally but there is a paradoxical dearth of data regarding this in this region. Furthermore, while these previous studies were conducted on technologically advanced, well-educated and highly motivated populations, the African population significantly differ genetically, socio-demographically and have relatively poorer access to good quality healthcare.

This study has demonstrated the feasibility of conducting a multi-component intervention for chronic hypertension in pregnancy in this setting and provides important information to guide the design of larger intervention studies that may generate the needed maternal and perinatal benefits. Expectedly, the major limitation of this study is the underpowered sample size which has limited the conclusiveness of the findings as they relate to the secondary and tertiary objectives. A much larger and sufficiently powered study will address this limitation.

Summary of findings

Retention, fidelity to protocol and acceptability were generally high and comparable to other studies done.

The mean change in Systolic and diastolic BP in the intervention arm from study enrolment to completion was found to be statistically significant, while there was no significant difference in SBP and DBP between study enrolment and completion in the control arm.

Optimal blood pressure was recorded in 67% (67.9%) of blood pressure readings in the intervention arm as compared to 64.6% of the readings in the control arm.

The odds of superimposed pre-eclampsia in the intervention arm were 27% less as compared to the odds in the control arm.

There was slightly higher rate of cesarean section in the control arm of the study.

There were no statistically significant differences in the adverse fetal outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of his study was the fact that it was conducted in two different countries in West African countries with diverse range of ethnicities and socioeconomic and educational backgrounds which makes our findings largely generalizable to settings with similar context. This study is however limited by its pilot design and arbitrary sample size determination, and not powered enough to detect differences between randomization groups for the secondary and tertiary outcomes.

Conclusion

The combination of non-pharmacologic interventions with the usual management of chronic hypertension in pregnancy is feasible and found to be acceptable by the participants. A large-scale randomized controlled trial powered to explore the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of this multi-component approach for improved BP control in hypertensive pregnancies will be needed particularly in African Countries who bear the greatest burden of this disease.
