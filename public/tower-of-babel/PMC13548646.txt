Safety of drug-eluting stent in Chinese population with or without diabetes: a multi-center registry study
Authors: Shining Cao, Meirong Ling, Xun Zhang, Wei Wu, Man Luo, Bin Jiang, Jing Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe safety of drug-eluting stents (DES) in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remains largely lacking in China. This study aimed to determine the 1-year adverse outcomes in a large registry of patients undergoing PCI with DES for coronary artery disease (CAD) in China. We also examined clinical outcomes in patients with versus without diabetes.MethodsThis study employs a retrospective cohort design. Data were obtained from the Monitoring Reevaluation Platform for Key Medical Device Adverse Events—the multicenter, observational registry that includes patients undergone PCI in Beijing, Guangdong province, and Guizhou province. The primary outcome measure was the composite of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, or any clinically indicated revascularisation procedure. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the association between MACE and diabetes, estimating adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsA total of 12,448 patients were included in the final analysis. There were 3,359 (26.98%) patients with DM and 9,089 (73.02%) without DM, respectively. The crude incidence rate of MACE was 0.92% (95% CI: 0.68–1.17%) over 12 months following PCI. Patients with diabetes had a slightly higher rate of MACEs than those without DM, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (1.01% vs. 0.88%; P = 0.625). After adjustment for baseline characteristics, patients with DM were not at higher risk of MACEs than those without diabetes (OR: 1.67; 95% CI: 0.44–6.34; P = 0.452).ConclusionsIn this large, multicenter Chinese registry, DES use was associated with a low and acceptable 1-year MACE rate. Diabetes may not be an independent risk factor for adverse outcomes following DES implantation. However, given the high attrition rate in the observational registry and the potential for underestimation of events, further studies with stricter endpoint ascertainment are warranted, particularly among high-risk subgroups such as elderly patients and those with multiple comorbidities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06060-9.Keywords: Stents, Diabetes, Major adverse cardiovascular events, Mortality, PCI

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD), characterised by the existence of atherosclerosis in coronary arteries, has become the leading cause of death and loss of Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) worldwide [1]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is a minimally invasive procedure for treating CAD, usually achieved by deploying a stent to relieve occlusion of the coronary artery.

The new-generation drug-eluting stents (DES) represent remarkable clinical benefits with a substantial reduction in in-stent restenosis [2–6]. However, geographic variations in clinical characteristics, race and ethnicity, and social determinants may also contribute to clinical outcomes after PCI, leading to discrepancies in clinical practice across countries [7–9]. Several clinical trials have highlighted significant geographical differences in clinical outcomes associated with DES, but this has not been extensively studied in China [10, 11]. Indeed, clinical trials tend to apply narrow eligibility criteria for patient enrollment and to require stricter follow-up. As a result, the evidence generated from the clinical trials may not be translated into clinical practice. Thus, data on the safety of DES implantation remain largely lacking in China, especially real-world evidence.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is an established risk factor for CAD [12]. It was estimated that 1 in 4 patients receiving PCI has DM [13]. In addition to the increased risk of CAD, diabetic patients have a higher risk for revascularisation, major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE), and mortality following PCI procedures compared to those without diabetes [3, 14]. DES were reported to have lower rates of restenosis than bare-metal stents [15]. However, there is controversy comparing the safety of DES in diabetic patients and non-diabetics [16]. Specifically, few studies demonstrated that diabetic patients had worse clinical outcomes than non-diabetics following PCI with DES [15, 17], while other studies reported similar rates of restenosis among patients with vs. without DM [6, 18]. Therefore, the impact of DM on clinical outcomes with DES remains uncertain, particularly in China.

While the safety of DES and the prognostic impact of diabetes have been extensively characterised in Western populations and randomised clinical trials, robust real-world evidence from large, nationally representative cohorts in China remains limited. In the context of China’s recently implemented active pharmacovigilance system — which continues to rely predominantly on passive surveillance for medical device safety — there is a critical need to evaluate clinical outcomes through observational registries that reflect actual practice. Given the substantial geographic dissimilarities in healthcare delivery models and practice preferences worldwide, we sought to determine the 1-year clinical outcomes in a large registry of patients undergoing PCI with DES for CAD. We also examined clinical outcomes in patients with versus without diabetes.

Methods

Data source

Data were obtained from the Monitoring Reevaluation Platform for Key Medical Device Adverse Events—the multicenter, observational registry that includes patients undergone PCI in Beijing, Guangdong province, and Guizhou province. As part of the 13th Five-Year Plan for medical device surveillance, the primary goal of the multicenter registry was to evaluate the safety of cardiac stents in routine clinical practice. All stents were approved by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) and are commonly used in China. This registry collected data on device failures, complications, and post-procedure outcomes. All events were assessed at 7 days (or hospital discharge), 1 month, and 12 months post-procedure via phone or during a clinic visit. Medical staff collected the baseline information upon hospital admission, including patient demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, lesion, procedure-related, and angiographic characteristics. All medical staff were trained on the data collection at each hospital. Although the registry was designed to monitor all cardiac stents, the widespread adoption of DES in clinical practice has resulted in a cohort that is predominantly DES. Hence, our analyses focused on patients receiving DES, excluding those with missing stent-type data. In the registry, a total of 12,790 patients who received PCI were enrolled from 13 hospitals. All patients had indications for coronary interventions in accordance with standard hospital practice. All data were anonymised at each hospital.

Study design

This study employs a retrospective cohort design. In this analysis, consecutive patients who received DES were evaluated to determine the rate of MACE at hospital discharge and through 1 year after PCI. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline [19].

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Shanghai Ethics Committee for Clinical Research (Number: SECCR/2023 − 137). Due to the retrospective nature of this study, written informed consent from patients was not required.

Study sample

This retrospective study included patients who underwent PCI and received at least 1 DES between 2017 and 2021. Patients were excluded if (1) they received only a bare-metal stent, (2) cardiac stents were not implanted successfully, or (3) patients required coronary artery bypass surgery. We also excluded patients with missing data on stent type or diabetic status. Eligible samples were further divided into two groups based on whether they had diabetes. Diabetes was defined as a self-reported diagnosis of diabetes, use of insulin or oral antidiabetic drugs, fasting plasma glucose > 7.0 mmol/L, an oral glucose tolerance test 2-hour blood glucose > 11.1 mmol/L, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% upon hospital admission.

Outcomes

The occurrence of adverse events was evaluated for the overall sample and compared between patients with and without DM at three predefined time points: 7 days (or hospital discharge), 1 month, and 12 months following PCI. Device delivery failure, including stent failure and no-reflow/slow-reflow, was recorded and reported by the operating doctors during the index hospitalisation within 7 days of the PCI procedure. This 7-day window was prespecified to capture early procedural complications, based on the previous study and in accordance with the clinical practice in China [20]. Longer-term adverse events were ascertained via telephone interview or clinical visit at 1 month and 1 year after PCI.

The primary outcome measure was the composite of MACE, defined as cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction (MI), or any clinically indicated revascularisation procedure. This definition aligns with the widely adopted definition of the MACE measure in clinical trials and observational studies [21, 22]. Secondary endpoints included in-stent restenosis (ISR), major bleeding, heart failure requiring hospitalisation, and arrhythmias. ISR was defined as having a re-narrowing of ≥ 75% of the reference vessel [23]. Because routine angiography was not mandated at 1 or 12 months, ISR was reported only for the 7-day follow-up. Revascularisation and MI were confirmed via medical record review. Major bleeding was defined as type 3 or 5 bleeding events according to the Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) [24]. Arrhythmias included sustained ventricular tachycardia or atrial fibrillation requiring hospitalisation, which represent serious cardiac events.

Statistical analysis

For descriptive statistics, means with standard deviations (SD) were calculated for continuous variables, and frequencies with percentages were reported for categorical variables. Comparisons between the two groups were performed using a Student’s t-test for continuous variables and a chi-square analysis for categorical variables. The crude incidence of adverse events was calculated for the overall sample and by diabetes status. Patients lost to follow-up at any time point were excluded from analyses for that time horizon to obtain a more conservative estimate of the event rate.

To evaluate the independent association between DM and MACE, multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals. Time-to-event analysis was not performed due to the lack of event time in the registry, which only has the time point at which events were ascertained. The multivariable models were adjusted for baseline characteristics, including age, sex (female vs. male), indication for PCI, target-vessel treated, number of stents implanted, number of diseased vessels, history of prior PCI, current smoking status, presence of comorbidities, and baseline cardiac troponin I level (cTnI). All covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature.

Sensitivity analysis

Given the high attrition rate, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the potential influence of loss to follow-up. First, we compared baseline characteristics between those who were followed up and those who were lost to follow-up. To assess the potential impact of attrition, we estimated MACE rates among lost patients range from 5% to 30%. According to the published literature, the MACE rates for DES stents range from 5% to 20% [21, 22, 25–29]. We assumed that up to 30% of patients lost to follow-up at each time point experienced MACE, yielding the highest possible estimates.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

During the study period, 12,790 patients who underwent PCI were identified in the registry database. 342 patients were excluded from the analysis because of missing data. Hence, a total of 12,448 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Follow-up was complete for all 12,448 eligible patients at 7 days following PCI. At 1 month, follow-up data were available for 10,071 patients, representing a loss to follow-up of 19.10%. By 12 months, 5,845 patients remained in follow-up, corresponding to an attrition rate of 53.04%. Loss to follow-up was slightly lower among patients with diabetes. At 1 month, 2,816 patients with diabetes and 7,255 without diabetes had follow-up, with the attrition rate is 16.17% for diabetic patients and 20.17% for non-diabetic patients; at 12 months, 1,876 with diabetes and 3,969 without diabetes had follow-up, with the attrition rate of 44.08% for diabetic patients and 56.35% for non-diabetic patients.

In 10,071 patients with a 1-month follow-up, 221 (2.19%; 95% CI: 1.91–2.48%) reported MACEs within the first 30 days following PCI with DES, with 178 patients suffered MI (1.77%; 95% CI: 1.51–2.02%), 12 patients need revascularization (0.12%; 95% CI: 0.05–0.19%), and 72 patients died (0.71%; 95% CI: 0.55–0.88%). For the secondary outcomes, a total of 23 patients experienced major bleeding (0.23%; 95% CI: 0.14–0.32%), 33 patients had heart failure (0.32%; 95% CI: 0.21–0.43%), and 78 patients had arrhythmias (0.77%; 95% CI: 0.60–0.95%).

In 5,845 patients with a 12-month follow-up, 54 reported MACE, and the crude incidence rate during the 12 months following DES implantation was 0.92% (95% CI: 0.68–1.17%). Among patients with MACE, 31 patients suffered MI (0.53%; 95% CI: 0.34–0.72%), 4 patients needed revascularisation (0.07%; 95% CI: 0.00-0.14%), and 22 patients died (0.38%; 95% CI: 0.22–0.53%). With respect to secondary outcomes, 1 patient reported major bleeding (0.02%; 95% CI: -0.02-0.05%); 18 patients had heart failure (0.31%; 95% CI: 0.17–0.45%), and 5 patients had arrhythmias (0.09%; 95% CI: 0.01–0.16%).

Comparison between patients with vs. without diabetes

Table 2 compares the incidence rate of MACEs between diabetic and non-diabetic patients. The rate of MACEs at 7 days was similar between patients with DM (1.13%; 95% CI: 0.77–1.49%) and those without DM (1.17%; 95% CI: 0.95–1.39%; P = 0.871). Patients with diabetes had a slightly higher rate of MACEs at 1 month (2.66% vs. 2.01%; P = 0.045) and 12 months (1.01% vs. 0.88%; P = 0.625) than those without DM.

At 1-month follow-up, diabetic patients demonstrated slightly higher rates of major bleeding (0.39% vs. 0.17%, P = 0.033) compared to nondiabetics but did not reach statistical significance (Table 2). There were no statistical differences in the rate of heart failure (0.39% vs. 0.29%; P = 0.415), MI (2.02% vs. 1.67%; P = 0.219), arrhythmias (0.71% vs. 0.80%; P = 0.652), revascularization (0.11% vs. 0.11%; P = 0.821), and cardiovascular death (0.92% vs. 0.63%, P = 0.120), between diabetics and non-diabetics.

At 12-month follow-up, diabetic patients demonstrated significantly higher rates of death (0.64% vs. 0.25%, P = 0.024). Patients with DM had similar rates of heart failure (0.21% vs. 0.35%; P = 0.369), MI (0.37% vs. 0.60%; P = 0.255), arrhythmias (0.11% vs. 0.08%; P = 0.705), and revascularisation (0% vs. 0.10%; P = 0.169) compared with non-diabetic patients.

To determine whether diabetes was associated with the risk of overall MACEs, we performed a multivariate logistic regression analysis (Table 3). After adjustment for baseline characteristics, patients with DM were not associated with a higher odds of having MACEs compared to those without diabetes at 1-month follow-up (OR: 1.42; 95% CI: 0.95–2.12; P = 0.092), and at 12-month follow-up (OR: 1.67; 95% CI: 0.44–6.34; P = 0.452). The factors associated with 1-month MACEs were older age (P = 0.006), STEMI (P = 0.014), implantation of more than 1 cardiac stent (P<0.001), and hypertension (P = 0.049). The factors associated with 12-month MACEs included a diagnosis of unstable angina (P = 0.007).

Sensitivity analysis

Compared with patients in the analysis, those who were lost to follow-up had similar age and gender distributions but were more likely to have > 1 stent, a prior PCI, hyperlipidemia, and cTnI > 0.1 µg/L (Appendix Tables S3 and S4). Compared with patients in the analysis, those who were lost to follow-up were more likely to have > 1 stent, a prior PCI, hyperlipidemia, and cTnI > 0.1 µg/L, suggesting a potentially higher risk of MACE. In the scenario-based sensitivity analysis, in which we assumed the 12-month MACE rate among lost patients ranged from 5% to 30%, the overall 12-month MACE rate for the full cohort remained below 20%, with similar rates between diabetic and non-diabetic patients.

Discussion

In this real-world registry of patients undergoing PCI with DES implantation, the risk of MACE was less than 2% through 1-year follow-up, consistent with rates reported in clinical trials. To our knowledge, this is the first real-world study evaluating the safety of DES in China using large, multicenter registry data. Furthermore, our findings indicated that diabetic patients experienced MACE risks similar to those of non-diabetic patients, consistent with studies conducted in other countries. Diabetes is a progressive condition associated with accelerated atherosclerosis, impaired vascular healing, and an elevated risk of late stent-related complications, which often evolve over time and may not become clinically apparent within the first year following PCI. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant difference in MACE between diabetic and non-diabetic patients at 12 months should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of long-term equivalence. Future research with extended follow-up will be essential to determine whether differences in event rates emerge over time, particularly among high-risk subgroups such as those with long-standing diabetes, insulin dependence, or multiple chronic diseases.

Globally, the safety of the DES following PCI has been extensively evaluated. The incidence rate of MACEs over a 12-month follow-up ranged from 5% to 20% [25–29]. Our analysis of a large, real-world Chinese registry revealed substantially lower 12-month MACE rates; these findings warrant careful interpretation given the study’s design and surveillance method. This discrepancy may reflect differences between clinical trials and real-world registries. While randomised trials often employ intensive, protocol-driven follow-up with systematic adjudication and routine diagnostic testing, e.g., angiography and biomarker monitoring. On the other hand, our registry relied on active surveillance through structured telephone interviews and routine clinical visits, without laboratory-based event ascertainment. As a result, only symptomatic events, such as recurrent symptoms of angina and revascularisation procedures, could be captured, potentially contributing to lower incidence rates. However, it is important to note that the event rates remain substantially higher than those captured by China’s national passive surveillance system. For instance, in 2019, the estimated adverse event rate for cardiac stents in Guizhou Province was approximately 0.20 per 1,000 procedures. In contrast, our registry recorded a 7-day adverse event rate of 10.28 per 1,000 patients, suggesting that active surveillance detects more events than passive systems.

Beyond methodological considerations, biological, behavioural, and healthcare system differences may also contribute to the observed variation. Most patients were elderly and had lower educational attainment, making it difficult to contact them by phone. In addition, race and ethnicity have been identified as important factors determining cardiovascular outcomes [30]. In studies comparing clinical outcomes after PCI with DES in Asia and Europe, Asians were less likely to suffer adverse events than Europeans. Furthermore, differences in patient comorbidities and preferences for invasive treatments, along with varying healthcare delivery systems, may contribute to the adverse outcomes reported across regions [31].

Our analysis also indicated no difference in MACEs between DM and non-DM patients at 1 year after PCI, consistent with several large RCTs [32, 33]. The SPIRIT IV clinical trial (Clinical Evaluation of the XIENCE V Everolimus Eluting Coronary Stent System) showed no difference in the occurrence of target lesion failure between diabetic and nondiabetic patients at 1 year (6.4% vs. 6.9%; P = 0.80) [32]. In a pooled analysis of data from the RESET and NEXT trials, which compared 3-year clinical outcomes by the presence of DM, no statistical difference was found in the risk of MI, stent thrombosis, or cardiovascular death [33]. By using real-world registries of DES in routine clinical practice, our analysis also found similar clinical outcomes among patients with and without diabetes who underwent PCI, providing important information in the era of DES.

DES reduced the risk of restenosis in high-risk patients compared with BMS [26, 34], but it appears to be subject to stent thrombosis, a concern that has been reported especially for diabetic patients [35]. Elevated blood glucose level, along with increased free fatty acid level and blood pressure, can induce endothelial dysfunction and vascular inflammation, resulting in accelerated atherosclerosis and vascular injury in diabetic patients with diabetes [36]. In our analysis, patients with hypertension or unstable angina had a higher risk of MACE, which were mainly driven by stent stenosis and revascularisation.

We also examined factors associated with MACE after PCI in the Chinese population, including LMCA and the number of diseased vessels. Traditionally, PCI for LMCA disease was considered an alternative to coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [37]. With the advancements in the DES, there was increased use of PCI for LMCA. Two landmark clinical trials – EXCEL (Evaluation of XIENCE Everolimus Eluting Stent Versus Coronary Artery Bypass Surgery for Effectiveness of Left Main Revascularization) and NOBLE (Nordic-Baltic-British Left Main Revascularization Study) – investigated the efficacy of PCI with DES for LMCA patients but reached conflicting results [37]. Therefore, more attention should be paid to patients with LMCA who underwent PCI.

Study limitations

Several limitations of our analysis should be noted. Despite substantial efforts made to follow up with patients, the data on adverse events were collected based on patients’ or their caregivers’ reports, and hence, we cannot exclude the possibility of underestimating the risk of MACE. This may also partially explain the low rate of adverse events, particularly revascularisation and stenosis, which generally require imaging confirmation. In addition, incomplete follow-up—particularly at 12 months—may introduce attrition bias and contribute to underestimation of adverse event rates. We excluded patients lost to follow-up at each time point to obtain a more conservative estimate of the event rate. However, we have performed a series of sensitivity analyses to estimate the impact of attrition on the MACE rate, and have found that diabetic and non-diabetic patients still have similar MACE rates. Second, because this study was observational, it could not draw causal inferences about the relationship between DES and adverse events. Third, although sirolimus-eluting stents and everolimus-eluting stents showed distinct safety profiles, the two types of DES were not compared in the analysis because there was only one manufacturer of everolimus-eluting stents in China. The disclosure of specific stent brands was intentionally limited to avoid potential misinterpretation or unintended commercial implications. Fourth, several clinically relevant variables, such as SYNTAX score, duration of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) [38], detailed lesion complexity, and event timing, were not captured in the registry, potentially limiting the precision of our multivariable models.

Conclusions

In this real-world study using multicenter registry data, DES use was associated with a low and acceptable 1-year MACE rate. Diabetes may not be an independent risk factor for adverse outcomes following DES implantation. However, given the high attrition rate in the observational registry and the potential for underestimation of events, further studies with stricter endpoint ascertainment are warranted, particularly among high-risk subgroups such as elderly patients and those with multiple comorbidities.
