Cerebral tuberculoma in pregnancy (Jan 1975–May 2025): a systematic review and descriptive analysis of 33 published cases
Authors: David E. Rebellón-Sánchez, Daniela Vinueza, David E. Castro Restrepo, Julio Alfredo Llanos, Fernando Rosso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral tuberculoma is an infrequent but life-threatening manifestation of tuberculosis whose diagnosis and treatment are uniquely complicated by pregnancy-related physiological changes. Evidence to guide clinicians remains fragmented and largely anecdotal.MethodsWe systematically searched MEDLINE, LILACS and grey literature to May 2025 for reports of cerebral tuberculoma in pregnant or postpartum women. Twenty-seven studies (24 case reports, 2 descriptive studies, 1 case series) from 20 countries met inclusion criteria, yielding individual-level data on 33 patients. Demographics, clinical features, diagnostics, management and maternal–fetal outcomes were extracted and synthesized.ResultsMedian maternal age was 26 years (IQR 23–29). Diagnosis occurred ante-partum in 45% and post-partum in 55%, at a median gestational age of 31 weeks. Headache (59%), fever (56%) and seizures (48%) predominated; cranial-nerve palsies (54%) and focal paresis (50%) were common neurologic signs. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed solitary or multiple contrast-enhancing lesions, chiefly supratentorial but infratentorial in 35%. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) showed proteinorrachia (n = 10/15) and mononuclear pleocytosis (n = 8/12, 66.67%); positivity of CSF or biopsy interferon-γ release assays (100%, n = 3/3) outperformed CSF culture (33.3%, n = 3/9) and CSF polymerase chain reaction (PCR; 12.5%, n = 1/8). Standard four-drug therapy plus corticosteroids was administered in 97%, typically > 12 months; 60% underwent neurosurgical biopsy or resection. Maternal mortality was 4.6%, and persistent neurological sequelae occurred in 33.3% of cases (n = 7/21). Among 26 live pregnancies, preterm delivery occurred in 72%; neonatal complications affected 50%, including congenital tuberculosis (17%) and neonatal death (17%).ConclusionsAlthough cerebral tuberculoma during pregnancy is rare, delayed recognition contributes to substantial maternal morbidity and adverse neonatal outcomes. Our synthesis underscores the diagnostic value of advanced imaging and CSF interferon-γ assays, supports prolonged first-line therapy with adjunctive steroids, and highlights critical gaps in evidence-based guidelines. Multicentre prospective registries are urgently needed to refine diagnostic algorithms and optimise maternal–fetal care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13145-5.

Introduction

The unique physiological state of pregnancy introduces complex challenges in diagnosing and managing neurological conditions. This complexity is particularly pronounced in the context of central nervous system (CNS) tuberculosis (TB), including tuberculomas, which are rare and present with a variety of symptoms that can mimic other neurological conditions. This makes initial diagnosis and subsequent management exceedingly difficult. Pregnant women often first present with neurological episodes, such as seizures or headaches, which do not immediately point to a clear diagnosis, including the possibility of tuberculoma. The broad spectrum of clinical manifestations of tuberculoma in pregnant women, along with their prevalence, remains poorly defined, further complicating the clinical approach to these patients.

Moreover, when tuberculoma is suspected in a pregnant patient, there is a notable absence of clear guidelines on the available diagnostic tests and their interpretation, varying significantly by institution. This includes a lack of clarity on the expected findings from histopathological, imaging, and laboratory studies. Therapeutic options are similarly fraught with uncertainty, with limited evidence on the efficacy and safety of various treatments in pregnant women. Unfortunately, clinical practice guidelines that outline the optimal approach to managing tuberculomas in pregnancy are nonexistent, leaving a significant gap in our knowledge and clinical practice.

CNS TB accounts for about 1% of all TB cases and 8% of extrapulmonary TB cases. Its rarity, combined with the complex presentation and management needs during pregnancy, underscores the need for focused research in this area [1]. Historical documentation since 1898 highlights the ongoing challenge of diagnosing and managing brain tumors, including tuberculomas, in pregnant women. Recent trends indicate an increase in the diagnosis of brain tumors in pregnancy, further emphasizing the urgency for specialized knowledge and guidelines [2, 3].

This systematic review aims to address these knowledge gaps by meticulously compiling and analyzing an extensive collection of documented tuberculoma cases in pregnant patients from the literature. Our review reveals common clinical presentations, diagnostic findings, and management strategies, providing much-needed insights into this complex condition. The lack of comprehensive clinical guidelines and the scarcity of aggregated patient data motivated this review, with the goal of contributing towards a clearer understanding and improved management of tuberculomas in pregnant women.

Materials and methods

Study design and objectives

We undertook a systematic review with individual patient-level descriptive synthesis of published case reports, case series and observational studies describing cerebral tuberculoma in pregnant or postpartum women. The primary objective was to systematically collect and synthesize available data to identify and describe the clinical characteristics, diagnostic approaches, treatment modalities, and outcomes of cerebral tuberculoma in this population. By aggregating these data, we aim to identify consistent patterns and knowledge gaps that may guide future research and inform evidence-based clinical practice.

Search strategy and selection criteria

A predefined PROSPERO protocol guided the review (CRD42023449347). We conducted a comprehensive search of electronic databases including MEDLINE (PubMed), LILACS, and grey literature sources like Google Scholar and Google, articles published in english and spanish were identified and included. Our search strategy employed both MeSH terms and free-text terms such as “tuberculoma”, “tuberculomas”, “tuberculous granuloma”, “pregnant woman”, “pregnancy”, “brain”, “cerebral”, and “nervous system”. These terms were combined using Boolean operators to maximize the search sensitivity and specificity.

We included studies that reported on the clinical presentation, diagnostic modalities, and therapeutic interventions of cerebral tuberculoma during pregnancy. We included articles from January 1975 to May 2025, and there were no restrictions on geographical location.

Data extraction process

Data extraction was facilitated using a standardised Microsoft Excel (Office 16) sheet. Three physicians (DV, DERS and DECR) independently reviewed full-text articles and extracted data. Any discrepancies between the extractors were resolved by a third author (FR). Data extracted included publication details, study period, demographic information, clinical manifestations, diagnostic findings, treatment modalities, outcomes, and reported complications.

During data extraction, we operated under the assumption that the symptoms and clinical findings detailed in the reports represented the complete spectrum of manifestations, treating any characteristics not mentioned as absent. This methodology aligns with our study’s descriptive intent to accurately catalog the reported presentations of cerebral tuberculoma in the target demographic. For the reporting of paraclinical findings and treatments, only positive or negative outcomes explicitly stated in the original manuscripts were included.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

Given that the available evidence consisted predominantly of case reports and a small case series, methodological quality was appraised using a domain-based framework informed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists for Case Reports and Case Series, adapted to the specific characteristics of descriptive clinical evidence on rare conditions.

Drawing on core JBI appraisal items, each report was evaluated across four predefined domains: [1] Selection, assessing clarity of case definition, patient context, and eligibility; [2] Diagnostic ascertainment, evaluating the strength and transparency of evidence supporting cerebral tuberculoma (clinical, radiological, histopathological and/or microbiological confirmation); [3] Causality, assessing temporal coherence and biological plausibility between pregnancy or the postpartum period and disease presentation, including consideration of alternative explanations; and [4] Reporting quality, evaluating completeness of clinical presentation, investigations, management, outcomes, and follow-up.

Each domain was rated as low risk, unclear risk, or high risk according to predefined criteria established a priori. At least two reviewers independently assessed each study (DERS and DV), with disagreements resolved by consensus. The quality appraisal was not used to exclude studies, but to contextualize the certainty and interpretability of the descriptive estimates. Results are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.2. Given the nature of the data derived from individual case reports and series, a meta-analytic approach was not feasible. Instead, we employed a narrative-descriptive synthesis method, concentrating on identifying and interpreting patterns and trends across the compiled cases.

To evaluate the distributional characteristics of the quantitative data, we applied normality tests, specifically the Shapiro-Wilk test and QQ-plots, ensuring that the statistical methods used were well-suited to the nature of our data. Quantitative variables were summarized by calculating medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) to describe central tendency and variability. Frequencies and percentages were used to present categorical variables, highlighting the relative occurrence of each observed category. This approach was in line with the study’s descriptive objectives and laid a robust groundwork for our narrative synthesis, facilitating a coherent interpretation of the data.

Ethical consideration and consent

According to Resolution 8430 in Colombia, this study is classified as no-risk because it utilizes secondary data, thereby eliminating the need for ethical approval. However, it includes data from a case treated at our institution, Fundación Valle de Lili, in Cali, Colombia, for which we obtained the ethics committee’s approval and patient consent (approval number 2023.213). This ensures our adherence to ethical standards and patient rights. The rest of the data, obtained from the public domain, did not require additional ethical approval for inclusion in our systematic review. Thus, our methodology reflects a conscientious balance between utilizing publicly available information and the principled management of patient-derived data.

Results

Study selection

The electronic databases yielded a total of 153 articles. After removing 13 duplicates, 140 articles were screened and 26 met the initial inclusion criteria. Reviewing the references cited in the selected studies and searching the Consensus platform yielded one additional eligible article. In total, 27 studies encompassing 33 cases were included for analysis. The details of the selection process are presented in Fig. 1.

Characteristics and clinical manifestations

The median age of the pregnant women was 26 years, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 23 to 29 years. Diagnosis was made in the postpartum or post-abortion period in 54.6% of cases (n = 18/33), and during pregnancy in the remaining 45.4% (n = 15/33). The median gestational age was 31 weeks (IQR 24 to 37 weeks). Prior TB exposure was noted in 13.8% (n = 4/29) of the participants, with 21.9% (n = 7/32) reporting contact with an active TB case. In vitro fertilization was reported in 9.1% (n = 3/32) of the cases.

The most common symptoms were headache, reported by 59.4% of individuals (n = 19/32), and fever, reported by 56.3% (n = 18/32). Visual disturbances were noted in 25% (n = 8/32) of the cases, seizures in 48.5% (n = 16/33), and respiratory symptoms in 25% (n = 8/32). Other symptoms included strength reduction in 28.1% (n = 9/32), lower back pain in 9.4% (n = 3/32), diplopia in 9.4% (n = 3/32), and vertigo in 3.1% (n = 1/32). Additionally, 12.5% of the patients (n = 4/32) experienced alteration of mental function. Loss of appetite, weight loss and night sweats were present in 9.4% (n = 3/32) of patients. Malaise and urinary retention were the least common symptoms, each documented in one case (3.1%) (Table 1).

The most frequent neurological manifestation was cranial nerve compromise, observed in 53.9% (n = 14/26) of these cases. Paresis was also a common finding, affecting 50% (n = 13/26) of the patients. Other notable observations included altered fundoscopy in 26.9% (n = 7/26) and papilledema in 23.1% (n = 6/26).

Most patients, 88.5% (n = 23/26), presented with normal reflexes, whereas diminished and augmented reflexes were found in 3.8% (n = 1/26) and 7.7% (n = 2/26) of cases, respectively. Meningeal signs were evident in 23.1% (n = 6/26) of the individuals.

Other less frequently reported findings were diminished level of consciousness, seen in 26.9% of patients (n = 7/26), and facial palsy, reported by 19.2% (n = 5/26). Eye movement paresis was documented in 15.4% (n = 4/26) and cerebellar signs in 11.5% (n = 3/26). Lymphadenopathy was the least common symptom, recorded in one patient (3.9%). There were no respiratory signs reported in this group of women (Table 2).

Laboratory diagnostics and histopathological findings

There was a notable lack of data in several reports regarding these findings. Among those tested, 50% (n = 5/10) had positive tuberculin skin tests. The sputum stains performed on seven patients were negative, and only one sputum culture was positive in this group of patients. M. tuberculosis PCR in blood showed positivity in two out of four tested patients (50%), while urine cultures were positive in 66.7% of cases (n = 2/3).

CSF analysis demonstrated elevated proteins in 66.7% (n = 10) of 15 tested patients. Pleocytosis was noted in 66.7% (n = 8/12), with a differential showing monocytes and lymphocytes in 57.1% and neutrophils in 28.6% of cases. CSF lactate levels were raised in 66.7% of those tested (n = 2/3), and glucose levels varied with both low and elevated results in 50% of patients (n = 5/10). AFB staining of the CSF was positive in 15.4% (n = 2/13), and M. tuberculosis culture was positive in three of the nine tested patients (33.3%). Additionally, CSF PCR for M. tuberculosis yielded a positive result in 12.5% of cases. The adenosine deaminase (ADA) test was elevated in one of the three individuals tested (33.3%).

Histopathological examination of cerebral biopsies revealed consistent findings of granulomatous inflammation (47.8%) and caseous necrosis (52.2%) across the majority of samples. Other pathologies such as ischemic necrosis, fibrinoid exudates, and vascular occlusion were less commonly observed, ranging from 4.4% to 8.7%. The presence of Langhans giant cells was significant in 21.7% (n = 5/23) of the cases, with lymphocytic infiltrates seen in 21.7% (n = 5/23). Acid-fast bacillus staining of biopsy samples was positive in 36.4% (n = 4/11) of cases (Table 3).

Imaging findings

Imaging studies, specifically MRI, were utilized in 23 out of the 30 instances where imaging was indicated. MRI was the more frequently chosen modality, used in 76.7% of the cases. The observed lesions commonly presented as oval or rounded (38.1%, n = 8/21) and irregular shapes (33.3%, n = 7/21). Most patients had a unique lesion. (52.2%, n = 12/23).

On computed tomography (CT), lesions were hypodense in 57.1% of cases (n = 4/7), isodense in 28.6% (n = 2/7), and hyperdense in 14.3% (n = 1/7). On T1-weighted MRI, lesions were predominantly isointense (40%, n = 4/10). T2-weighted MRI findings showed a higher frequency of hypointense and hyperintense signals, each category representing 36.4% of cases (n = 4/11). FLAIR imaging primarily revealed hypointense lesions (83.3%, n = 5/6).

Hydrocephalus was identified in 5.9% (n = 1/17) of the cases, a midline shift in 36.8% (n = 7/19), and ventricular dilation was seen in 27.8% (n = 5/18). Signs of ischemia were present in 11.1% (n = 2/18), while perilesional edema was the most frequently associated finding, occurring in 79% (n = 15/19) of patients.

Lesion enhancement following contrast administration was observed in the majority of cases (92.3%, n = 12/13). Meningeal enhancement was present in 31.3% (n = 5/16), while abnormal abdominal imaging and lung imaging suggestive of TB were seen in 25% (n = 1/4) and 54.2% (n = 13/24) of cases, respectively (Table 4).

Treatment strategies

Most of these patients, 97%, reported antituberculosis treatment (n = 32/33), demonstrating a high adherence rate to the first-line regimen which included Isoniazid (n = 25/26, 96.2%), Rifampicin (n = 25/26, 96.2%), Pyrazinamide (n = 22/26, 84.6%), and Ethambutol (n = 24/26, 92.3%). Treatment duration varied, with a 50% of patients undergoing therapy for over 12 months (n = 7/14).

The intensive phase of treatment commonly lasted for 2 months in 66.7% of the cases (n = 4/6), whereas the continuation phase extended beyond 15 months for 28.6% of the patients (n = 2/7). Steroid use was prevalent in 90.5% of cases (n = 19/21), with the majority receiving intravenous administration (n = 9/11, 81.8%). Surgical intervention was required in 64% of the instances (n = 16/25), encompassing surgical resection and biopsy as the most common procedures.

Treatment outcomes and complications

Two patients (6.1%, n = 2/33) required admission to the ICU. The follow-up period concluded with one fatality (4.6%, n = 1/22) and neurological sequelae in 33.3% of the patients (n = 7/21). Imaging follow-ups indicated improvement in 84.6% of cases (n = 11/13), though persistent lesions remained in 15.4% (n = 2/13).

Newborn complications were significant, affecting 50% of the cases (n = 13/26), including neonatal TB (n = 2/12, 16.7%) and neonatal death (n = 2/12, 16.7%). Preterm delivery was observed in 72.2% of the pregnancies (n = 13/18), underlining the substantial impact of tuberculoma on pregnancy outcomes (Table 5).

Discussion

Intracranial tuberculomas are rare but clinically significant complications in pregnancy, representing an important differential diagnosis for intracranial space-occupying lesions in TB-endemic regions [14–18]. In the absence of active TB, presentations may be misattributed to neoplasms. Unlike tuberculous meningitis, no validated staging system exists for tuberculoma, complicating clinical decisions [19]. Diagnostic and therapeutic decisions are further constrained by the physiological changes of pregnancy and the limited available evidence; our review summarizes clinical features, diagnostics, treatment, and maternal–neonatal outcomes.

We identified 33 pregnant patients, most presenting with headache, fever, seizures, and raised intracranial pressure. Lesions were typically round, often supratentorial (with a substantial infratentorial subset), isointense on T1 and hypo/hyperintense on T2/FLAIR with contrast enhancement; CSF commonly showed protein elevation and mononuclear pleocytosis. Fifteen underwent surgery; microbiologic proof was uncommon, while histopathology frequently showed granulomas with caseation. Most received prolonged first-line anti-TB therapy, with radiologic improvement in the majority and one death; neonatal complications were frequent, including preterm birth and congenital TB.

Most patients were younger than 35 years, immunocompetent, and in their third trimester of pregnancy. Although constitutional symptoms were uncommon, approximately one-quarter presented with respiratory complaints, and more than half showed pulmonary abnormalities on imaging—findings consistent with hematogenous dissemination and CNS seeding as described in the pathophysiology of tuberculosis [20–22]. The development of cerebral tuberculomas in these patients likely reflects reactivation of latent infection in a post-primary form [23], facilitated by the immunological adaptations of pregnancy, particularly the downregulation of cell-mediated immunity [24, 25]. The proportion of patients reporting prior TB contact (~ 22%) was similar to that observed in non-pregnant, immunocompetent populations [26], suggesting that altered immune regulation during pregnancy, rather than increased exposure, may predispose to disease manifestation [27].

Seizures occurred in nearly half of cases, markedly higher than in the general obstetric population [28–30]. Symptom overlap with eclampsia (headache, visual changes, altered mental status) can obscure diagnosis [31], yet fever and focal neurologic signs (cranial neuropathies, paresis, meningismus, papilledema) were common and mirror non-pregnant series [32]. Where lateralizing signs or concern for hemorrhage, infarction, or infection exist, targeted investigations are warranted [33]. The predominance of postpartum diagnoses (55%) likely reflects delayed recognition rather than true postpartum onset, driven by overlapping clinical features and by the limited performance of several tuberculosis tests, which are not routinely included in the initial diagnostic workup. Immune reconstitution and reporting bias may also contribute to this observed pattern.

Neuroimaging predominantly showed solitary or multiple supratentorial lesions, aligning with other adult series [34, 35], though infratentorial disease was notable and reminiscent of pediatric patterns [36, 37]. MRI–histopathology correlations follow known evolutionary stages: non-caseating lesions with T1 hypointensity/T2-FLAIR hyperintensity and homogeneous enhancement; caseating solid lesions with iso- to hypointense signals and ring enhancement; and caseating-liquefied lesions with T2 hyperintensity and ring enhancement [32, 34, 38, 39]. In approximately 36% of patients, MRI findings could be directly correlated with histopathological results, consistently revealing caseating granulomas with surrounding edema, that are hallmarks of cerebral tuberculoma. Beyond conventional MRI, advanced modalities such as MRS and contrast-enhanced imaging can further refine diagnostic accuracy: in one case, MRS revealed an elevated choline–creatine ratio, decreased NAA, and lipid peaks [5], while Gd-DTPA–enhanced MRI frequently demonstrated meningeal enhancement and conglomerate ring-enhancing nodules [40–42].

CSF findings in pregnant patients with cerebral tuberculoma typically showed proteinorrachia and mononuclear pleocytosis [43], reflecting the inflammatory response characteristic of CNS tuberculosis. However, glucose and lactate levels displayed no consistent pattern, and ADA testing was infrequently performed, mirroring the heterogeneity observed in other series [5]. Indirect immunologic assays such as the TST and IGRA yielded higher positivity rates than direct microbiological methods [44, 45]. Notably, despite the immune modulation inherent to pregnancy, IGRA performance appears preserved, contrasting with the reduced sensitivity reported in other immunocompromised populations [46]. Meta-analytic evidence from non-pregnant cohorts supports the high diagnostic accuracy of this assay [47], though its performance depends on timely sample processing and careful interpretation [48]. By contrast, direct microbiological confirmation remains elusive, and cultures, stains, and PCR were only rarely positive, underscoring the persistent diagnostic challenges of CNS tuberculosis and the consequent reliance on an integrated clinical, radiological, and immunological approach in pregnant patients [49, 50].

Treatment predominantly consisted of standard first-line regimens administered for prolonged periods (> 9–12 months), frequently complemented with corticosteroids to mitigate perilesional inflammation. Imaging-based assessments often guided treatment duration and therapeutic adjustments [51, 52]. Evidence from a recent meta-analysis indicates that first-line anti-tuberculous drugs do not increase the risk of severe maternal adverse events and may even improve pregnancy outcomes [53], supporting adherence to current guideline-recommended regimens during pregnancy [54]. In the other hand, surgical intervention was reserved for selected cases, primarily when diagnostic uncertainty persisted, when lesions caused significant mass effect or intracranial hypertension unresponsive to medical therapy, or when clinical deterioration occurred despite adequate pharmacologic management. In our synthesis, neurological sequelae were observed in approximately one-third of patients, and mortality reached 5%, slightly exceeding rates reported in non-pregnant cohorts with tuberculous mass lesions [55]. Neonatal outcomes were frequently unfavorable, including preterm delivery, small-for-gestational-age infants, and congenital TB, though no cases of hydrocephalus were reported [56–58].

Although this review is inherently limited by the case-based nature of available evidence, publication bias toward atypical or successfully treated cases, and heterogeneous reporting, it represents the most comprehensive synthesis to date of cerebral tuberculoma in pregnancy. The small number of documented cases precludes formal meta-analysis and definitive conclusions regarding optimal diagnostic or therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, because severe, atypical, or complicated presentations are more likely to be reported, mild or asymptomatic cases are probably underrepresented. Nevertheless, the aggregated evidence highlights the need for prospective multicenter registries with standardized variables encompassing maternal characteristics, imaging, CSF biomarkers, microbiology, treatments, and longitudinal maternal–neonatal outcomes. Such collaborative efforts are essential to clarify how pregnancy-related immunomodulation may influence disease presentation and prognosis, and to establish evidence-based management guidelines tailored to this unique population. Until such data become available, clinical decision-making should rely on extrapolation from non-pregnant cohorts, existing obstetric TB recommendations, and individualized risk–benefit assessment for both mother and fetus.

Conclusions

Cerebral tuberculoma during pregnancy, though rare, represents substantial risks of maternal neurological sequelae and adverse neonatal outcomes. Our review indicates that (i) headache, seizures, and focal neurological deficits are the predominant clinical manifestations; (ii) contrast-enhanced MRI, supported by CSF interferon-γ release assays, provides the greatest diagnostic yield when cultures and PCR are frequently negative; and (iii) prolonged first-line anti-tuberculous therapy, often combined with corticosteroids, results in radiological improvement in most patients, although one-third experience persistent deficits.

Early recognition and timely initiation of treatment are crucial to improving outcomes, yet current evidence remains confined to isolated reports. Based on the accumulated experience, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for tuberculoma in pregnant patients presenting with unexplained intracranial lesions, integrate IGRA testing and advanced neuroimaging into diagnostic pathways, and pursue multidisciplinary management to balance maternal and fetal safety. Establishing prospective multicenter registries and harmonized reporting standards is imperative to define evidence-based therapeutic durations, evaluate fetal safety, and guide prevention strategies. These coordinated efforts are essential to transform fragmented case evidence into robust clinical guidance that improves prognosis for both mother and child.
