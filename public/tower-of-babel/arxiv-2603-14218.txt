Interleaved Resampling and Refitting: Data and Compute-Efficient Evaluation of Black-Box Predictors
Haichen Hu huhc@mit.edu, David Simchi-Levi dslevi@mit.edu

Abstract
We study the problem of evaluating the excess risk of large-scale empirical risk minimization under the square loss. Leveraging the idea of wild refitting and resampling, we assume only black-box access to the training algorithm and develop an efficient procedure for estimating the excess risk. Our evaluation algorithm is both computationally and data efficient. In particular, it requires access to only a single dataset and does not rely on any additional validation data. Computationally, it only requires refitting the model on several much smaller datasets obtained through sequential resampling, in contrast to previous wild refitting methods that require full-scale retraining and might therefore be unsuitable for large-scale trained predictors. Our algorithm has an interleaved sequential resampling-and-refitting structure. We first construct pseudo-responses through a randomized residual symmetrization procedure. At each round, we thus resample two sub-datasets from the resulting covariate–pseudo-response pairs. Finally, we retrain the model separately on these two small artificial datasets. We establish high-probability excess risk guarantees under both fixed-design and random-design settings, showing that with a suitably chosen noise scale, our interleaved resampling and refitting algorithm yields an upper bound on the prediction error. Our theoretical analysis draws on tools from empirical process theory, harmonic analysis, Toeplitz operator theory, and sharp tensor concentration inequalities.

Introduction

Deep Neural Networks (DNN) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) have been very important in modern machine learning
(
60
;
26
)
. More recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have become a revolutionary force, with tremendous applications in science
(

, healthcare
(

, operations management

, education
(

, and so on.

Despite the unprecedented success and widespread deployment of deep neural networks and artificial intelligence (AI) models, one of their most significant, and arguably most concerning characteristics is that they predominantly operate as opaque, black-box predictors
16
;
15
. This opacity is particularly evident in modern Large Language Models (LLMs). Even after extensive empirical observation and rigorous probing, researchers still do not fully understand the internal mechanisms governing the emergent behaviors and decision-making processes of these models. This persistent lack of interpretability stems from two compounding factors of scaling: the astronomical size of the pre-training datasets and the sheer structural complexity of the underlying architectures.

First, modern AI models are pre-trained on text of unprecedented scale, often encompassing billions or even trillions of samples
13
;
27
. Auditing this sheer volume of unstructured, web-scale data makes it nearly impossible to trace specific model outputs back to their original training instances or to fully understand the origins of a model’s learned associations, reasoning pathways, and potential biases.

Second, the underlying neural architectures are immensely complicated. State-of-the-art Large Language Models rely on deep Transformer networks
70
characterized by billions of trainable parameters distributed across numerous layers with massive dimensional widths
13
. The highly non-linear, high-dimensional interactions among these parameters render traditional analytical methods ineffective, completely obscuring the internal logic that transforms an input prompt into a coherent output.

However, rigorously evaluating the reliability and interpretability of these opaque models is a vital prerequisite for real-world deployment
45
. In high-stakes domains, blindly trusting black-box predictors risks catastrophic consequences. For instance, unrecognized algorithmic biases in clinical healthcare can drive severe misdiagnoses and compromise patient safety
59
;
51
. Similarly, in safety-critical systems like autonomous driving, a model’s failure to reliably communicate uncertainty when encountering out-of-distribution data can lead to fatal failures
40
;
2
. Therefore, before such models are integrated into critical societal infrastructure, it is essential to develop efficient and mathematically sound methods for quantifying their reliability.

Therefore, given the widespread deployment and inherent opacity of LLMs, developing a principled framework to rigorously evaluate these black-box models remains a critical open problem. Crucially, such a framework must achieve efficiency across two dimensions:
computational efficiency and data efficiency
.

First, let us consider
computational efficiency
. Training large-scale AI systems, such as the pre-training of LLMs, typically involves processing trillions of data points over months on thousands of GPUs
(
32
;
39
)
. Therefore, any practical validation method must avoid incurring a second computational burden comparable to that of the original training process. To this end, a good evaluation procedure should be able to operate on much smaller datasets so that its computational cost constitutes only a marginal fraction of the original computation cost while still providing a valid evaluation of the pre-trained model.

Second, the evaluation framework must be
data-efficient
. Modern LLMs are data-hungry
(
72
)
, demanding exhaustive quantities of high-quality data to sustain performance gains
(
42
)
. Consequently, practitioners rarely have the luxury of holding out massive, high-fidelity datasets for traditional evaluation techniques like cross-validation or standard data splitting. Instead, in modern large-scale applications, nearly all available high-quality data is typically used directly for training in order to maximize predictive performance. Thus, an ideal evaluation method must be capable of rigorously assessing the model without relying on abundant reserves of unseen validation data.

Our Contributions.

In this paper, we develop an algorithmic framework for evaluating black-box predictors that is efficient in both computation and data usage. Specifically, for an arbitrary black-box training algorithm, we study the problem of estimating its excess risk, arguably the most fundamental metric for assessing generalization performance and the overall quality of a learned model. Our evaluation procedure combines
interleaved resampling

(
85
)
with
wild refitting

(
75
)
, allowing us to decompose an otherwise massive evaluation task into many moderate-scale subproblems. More importantly, our statistical guarantees are model-free and do not rely on classical function-class complexity measures such as Rademacher complexity or covering numbers; instead, we assume only black-box access to the training procedure together with a single dataset. In addition, we establish excess-risk bounds under both fixed-design and random-design settings, whereas previous work on wild refitting has been limited to the fixed-design regime
(
75
;
37
;
36
)
. In fact, our bounds remain valid under a mere square-integrability assumption, even without requiring continuity of the underlying model class. Technically, our analysis draws on tools from harmonic analysis, the spectral theory of Toeplitz operators, and sharp tensor concentration inequalities.

Literature Review

Statistical learning theory
(
69
)
provides the mathematical foundation for the rigorous analysis of learning algorithms, with empirical risk minimization (ERM)
(
68
)
serving as one of its central paradigms. A core objective in this literature is to characterize the excess risk, which quantifies the gap between empirical performance and population risk. Traditionally, controlling this gap has relied on the rich machinery of empirical process theory. Through a variety of capacity measures—including the VC dimension

, intrinsic dimension

(

However, obtaining valid and tractable upper bounds on these complexity measures becomes highly challenging when the underlying function class is very rich, as is often the case for black-box predictors such as deep neural networks and large language models.

Generally, evaluating a training algorithm involves splitting the available data into disjoint training and validation sets, and then using a portion of the data to assess the performance of the trained model
(

;

)
. Variants of this approach include leave-one-out cross-validation

;

)
and

-fold cross-validation
(

. However, because all such methods rely on sample splitting, they do not fully allocate the available data to training and are therefore inherently data-inefficient. This limitation is particularly pronounced in modern large-scale settings such as LLM training, where high-quality data is scarce and valuable.

More recently,
75
introduced the concept of
wild refitting
for regression. This approach upper bounds the excess risk under fixed design by constructing a carefully perturbed synthetic dataset and refitting the model. Subsequently,
37
;
36
broadened this framework to accommodate more general Bregman and convex loss functions. Despite these theoretical advances, existing wild refitting techniques face significant scalability bottlenecks when evaluating massive AI models. Because they require retraining the model on synthetic datasets of a size comparable to the original training corpus, they may be computationally impractical in modern large-scale applications.

Algorithmically, our method is related to resampling in statistics. Classical resampling methods, such as the jackknife
(

;

and the bootstrap

, provide effective tools for inference, especially in settings where classical asymptotic approximations are inadequate. Broadly speaking, resampling methods can be divided into those based on sampling with replacement, such as the bootstrap
(

(
54

While a naive implementation that evaluates all possible subsamples is computationally infeasible, foundational algorithmic advances in sampling without replacement

avoid this combinatorial explosion and make such procedures highly scalable in modern large-scale applications.

3
Algorithm: Interleaved Resampling and Wild Refitting

In this section, we present our evaluation algorithm for the black-box procedure
Alg
. Our algorithm alternates between sequential resampling and wild refitting.

As described by
75
, a traditional wild refitting of an algorithm
Alg
with an input dataset
\mathcal{D}
generally entails two steps. First, we construct perturbed pseudo-responses via Rademacher symmetrization on the residuals and obtain a dataset
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
with the same scale as
\mathcal{D}
. Second, the model is refitted at full-scale to the synthetic dataset
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
to construct the wild predictor.

However, in this paper, without a full-scale retraining budget, we cannot refit the model at the original scale. Thus, we must approximate the behavior of full-scale retraining. In statistics, resampling provides a principled approach for such approximation. Classical methods such as cross-validation and the bootstrap repeatedly sample the data to approximate quantities of interest, including the test error and the variability of estimators. Motivated by this perspective, our evaluation algorithm adopts an interleaved resampling scheme combined with small-scale refitting. In brief, our evaluation algorithm leverages the statistical properties of wild refitting and the computational efficiency of resampling while respecting the available compute budget. This design enables us to approximate the behavior of full-scale retraining without incurring its prohibitive cost.

Evaluation Algorithm Steps.

Our algorithm proceeds as follows. Given the original full-scale training on
\mathcal{D}=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\breve{f}=\texttt{Alg}(\mathcal{D})

v_{i}=y_{i}-\bar{f}(x_{i}),i\in[n].
We then generate an i.i.d. sequence of Rademacher random variables
\varepsilon=\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
\left\{v_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\left\{\varepsilon_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
are used in future repeated sampling and wild refitting.

At a high level, our algorithm employs an interleaved procedure of resampling and wild refitting. In each iteration, we first draw a random subsample from the original training data. Unlike traditional bootstrap methods—which sample with replacement to construct a synthetic dataset of the original size—our approach samples without replacement to generate a strictly smaller subset, with its cardinality explicitly bounded by the available computational budget. This distinction is critical: our objective is not to naively simulate a full retraining pass, but rather to rigorously approximate its statistical behavior under severe computational constraints.

After obtaining the subsample, we construct wild responses and apply a wild refitting procedure to the resulting artificial random datasets in order to produce wild predictors. These predictors are then used to derive a computable upper bound on the excess risk.

Practicality.

At the end of this section, we give some remarks about the practicality of Algorithm

First, uniform resampling without replacement is a classical operation with many efficient implementations, including a permutation-based method that draws a uniform random permutation and keeps the first several indices
(

Second, the choice of the parameters

Moreover, when the training dataset

Roadmap.

In Subsection

Since we care about the empirical excess risk

\|\breve{f}-f^{*}\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\breve{f}(x_{i}))^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-f^{*}(x_{i}))^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\breve{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i}))\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\breve{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})).

4.2
Bounding the Empirical Excess Risk
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{D}}(\breve{f})

In this subsection, we provide our bound on the fixed-design excess risk
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{D}}(\breve{f})
. For clarity of exposition, we begin with a detailed roadmap of the discussion in this subsection.

Roadmap.

We organize our discussion of the fixed-design excess risk
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{D}}(\breve{f})
as follows. We begin by presenting, together with Lemma
6
, several key lemmas that will be used in the proof of the main excess-risk guarantee in Theorem
9
. In particular, Lemma
7
will show that the relevant empirical processes can be controlled directly by the outputs of our interleaved resampling-and-refitting procedure in Algorithm
1
. We will then turn to the comparison between the empirical norms induced by the full-scale dataset and by the subsamples. Using tools from harmonic analysis and Toeplitz operator theory, we will show that these norms are equivalent in some sense, which allows us to transfer estimates obtained on the subsamples back to the full-scale dataset. Finally, by combining these ingredients with the unbiasedness result in Lemma
6
, we will derive the desired upper bound on the fixed-design excess risk.

In statistical learning theory, obtaining excess risk bounds while controlling certain empirical process terms is a fundamentally difficult issue
(
68
;
57
;
71
)
. The classical approach is to upper bound these quantities via Dudley’s entropy integral. However, such arguments depend crucially on having a tractable description of the function class and its complexity, which is often unavailable for modern black-box models such as deep neural networks. As demonstrated in
75
;
37
, wild refitting offers an alternative route: it allows some of the empirical process terms introduced above to be upper bounded by the corresponding
wild optimism
evaluated at suitable radii.

In particular, for any noise scale
\rho
and any subsample
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
, we define the wild optimism quantities
\widetilde{\text{Opt}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\rho}(\widetilde{f}^{k}_{\rho})
and
\widetilde{\text{Opt}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\rho}(\widecheck{f}^{k}_{\rho})
regarding the wild predictors
\{\widetilde{f}^{k}_{\rho},\widecheck{f}^{k}_{\rho}\}_{k=1}^{K}
as follows:

\widetilde{\text{Opt}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\rho}(\widetilde{f}^{k}_{\rho})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varepsilon^{k}_{i}v^{k}_{i}(\widetilde{f}^{k}_{\rho}(x^{k}_{i})-\breve{f}(x^{k}_{i})),\ \widecheck{\text{Opt}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\rho}(\widecheck{f}^{k}_{\rho})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varepsilon^{k}_{i}v^{k}_{i}(\check{f}^{k}_{\rho}(x^{k}_{i})-\breve{f}(x^{k}_{i})).

Then, we have the following important lemma.

Lemma 7
.

For any noise scale
\rho>0
, we have

{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}(\|\widetilde{f}^{k}_{\rho}-\breve{f}\|_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}})=\widetilde{\text{Opt}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\rho}(\widetilde{f}^{k}_{\rho}),\ \mathcal{U}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}(\|\widecheck{f}^{k}_{\rho}-\breve{f}\|_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}})=\widecheck{\text{Opt}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\rho}(\widecheck{f}^{k}_{\rho})

Intuitively, Lemma
7
shows that the wild predictors
\widecheck{f}^{k}_{\rho}
and
\widetilde{f}^{k}_{\rho}
attain the corresponding suprema of the empirical processes
\mathcal{U}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}
, respectively. Moreover, the corresponding wild optimism terms are fully computable from Algorithm
1
. This computability is crucial, as it allows us to bypass the notoriously difficult task of analyzing function class complexity when deriving excess risk bounds for the black box procedure
Alg
.

To control the true optimism
\text{Opt}^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}(\breve{f})
, it is necessary to bound the empirical processes
\mathcal{W}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathcal{D}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathcal{D}}
, both defined through suprema over the full-scale empirical
L^{2}
ball
\mathbb{B}_{r}(\breve{f};\mathcal{D})
with respect to the pseudo-norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{D}}
. In contrast, Lemma
7
shows that our sub-scale wild refitting procedure only provides control of
{\mathcal{T}}^{\varepsilon}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}
and
\mathcal{U}^{\varepsilon}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}
, where the underlying metric is the subsample empirical
L^{2}
norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}
rather than the full-data norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{D}}
. This discrepancy between the two empirical norm structures introduces a second fundamental difficulty in the theoretical analysis.

Therefore, a key step is to establish an appropriate notion of equivalence between the full-scale empirical
L^{2}
norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{D}}
and the subsample empirical norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}
uniformly over the relevant function difference class
\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{F})
. Only in this way can the bounds obtained at the subsample scale be transferred back to the full-scale metric.

Thus, to obtain the equivalence between different empirical norms, the following lemma holds uniformly over
h\in\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{F})=\left\{f-f^{\prime}|f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\right\}
.

Lemma 8
.

For the covariate data
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
in
\mathcal{D}
and its subsample
{\mathcal{S}}=\left\{x_{k_{i}}\right\}_{i=1}^{n^{\beta}}
,
\forall\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-2\delta
, for any
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n^{\beta}}\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\|h\|_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{2}\leq 4\frac{\widebar{w}+3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n^{\beta}}}}{\underline{w}-3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n}}}\|h\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}+\left(2\frac{\widebar{w}+3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n^{\beta}}}}{\underline{w}-3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n}}}+1\right)\frac{8M_{v}^{2}}{2v-1}\frac{1}{N^{2v-1}},

uniformly over
h\in\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{F})
.

The proof of this lemma relies crucially on techniques from harmonic analysis and Toeplitz operator theory. In contrast to many classical excess risk bounds in statistical learning, which are distribution-free and hence independent of the covariate distribution
\nu
, Lemma
8
depends explicitly on the density ratio
\widebar{w}/\underline{w}
associated with
\nu
. The main technical challenge is to establish concentration bounds for the empirical Toeplitz operator
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Phi_{\infty}(x_{i})\Phi_{\infty}(x_{i})^{H}.

In our setting, the expected operator
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}\left[\Phi_{\infty}(x)\Phi_{\infty}(x)^{H}\right]
is non-compact, so standard concentration inequalities for sequences of compact operators do not apply directly. To overcome this difficulty, we replace the infinite-dimensional feature map
\Phi_{\infty}
with a finite-dimensional surrogate
\Phi_{N}
, and then use the Fourier decay condition in Assumption
2
to control the resulting truncation error. Moreover, because Toeplitz operators typically exhibit a continuous spectrum, this truncation argument is fundamentally different from the more familiar eigenvalue-based truncations for compact operators used, for example, in
35
. We defer the full proof to Appendix
C
.

Intuitively, when
n
is large enough, by Lemma
8
, we can set

to obtain:

\|h\|_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{2}\lesssim C_{\underline{w},\widebar{w},v}^{\delta}\left(\|h\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}+\frac{M_{v}^{2}}{n^{(2v-1)\beta}}\right),

where
C_{\underline{w},\widebar{w},v}^{\delta}
is a constant that is only related to
\underline{w},\widebar{w},v
and
\delta
.

One remarkable feature of Lemma

is that it holds uniformly over the function difference class
\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{F})
. In this way, we establish a precise connection between
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{D}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{S}}}
, which allows us to transfer bounds obtained for sub-scale pilot noise complexities
{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}(r)
and
\mathcal{U}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}(r)
into corresponding bounds for the full-scale pilot noise complexities, and thus upper bound the true optimism
\text{Opt}^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}(\breve{f})
.

\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{D}}(\breve{f})

\widehat{r}_{n}

Proof Sketch of Theorem

\text{Opt}^{*}(\widehat{f})\lesssim\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(r)]+\frac{r\tau\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Next, to control the empirical process
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(r)

\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}v_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})).

Therefore, for any

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(r)]
via the sum of
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)
, along with the pilot error term
V_{2r}(\widebar{f})
.

After that, we use Lemma
8
to conclude that the functions that achieve the supremum of
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)
, denoted by
h_{1}
and
h_{2}

\mathbb{B}_{2\widetilde{r}}(\breve{f};{\mathcal{S}}_{k})
uniformly over
k\in[K]
with high probability, where

(2\widetilde{r})^{2}=C_{\underline{w},\widebar{w},v}^{\delta}\left((2r)^{2}+\frac{M_{v}^{2}}{n^{(2v-1)\beta}}\right).

Then, utilizing Lemma
6

\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}v_{i}(h_{1}(x_{i})-\breve{f}(x_{i}))\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}B_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}(h_{1})+\frac{16\tau\sqrt{\log(K/\delta)}}{\sqrt{K}}\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mathcal{T}}^{\varepsilon}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}(2\widetilde{r})+\frac{16r\tau\sqrt{\log(K/\delta)}}{\sqrt{K}}.

In the last inequality, we use the result that
h_{1}\in\mathbb{B}_{2\widetilde{r}}(\breve{f};{\mathcal{S}}_{k})
according to Lemma
8
. Using the same argument, we have

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{U}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathcal{D}}(2\widetilde{r})+\frac{16r\tau\sqrt{\log(K/\delta)}}{\sqrt{K}}.

Finally, with the noise scale condition in Theorem
9
, we use Lemma
7
to obtain

{\mathcal{T}}^{\varepsilon}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}(2\widetilde{r})=\widetilde{\text{Opt}}^{\rho_{1}^{k}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}(\widetilde{f}^{k}_{\rho^{k}_{1}}),\ \text{and}\ \mathcal{U}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathcal{D}}(2\widetilde{r})=\widecheck{\text{Opt}}^{\rho_{2}^{k}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}(\widecheck{f}^{k}_{\rho_{2}^{k}}).

Combining these parts together, we obtain

\text{Opt}^{*}\mathcal{D}(\breve{f})\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\widetilde{\text{Opt}}^{\rho_{1}^{k}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}(\widetilde{f}^{k}_{\rho^{k}_{1}})+\widecheck{\text{Opt}}^{\rho_{2}^{k}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}(\widecheck{f}^{k}_{\rho_{2}^{k}})\right)+R(\delta)+V_{2r}(\widebar{f}),

which completes our proof.

Explanation

Now, we provide some comments regarding Theorem
9
. First, in order to make the conclusion valid, we assume that
n
is large enough. Specifically, for any
\delta
and
K
, we just need
n
to be large enough such that

\displaystyle\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n^{\beta}}}\leq\frac{1}{8}\underline{w},\ N=\frac{n^{\beta}}{\log n}.

This condition is satisfied as long as
n\gtrsim C^{\prime}_{v,\underline{w},\widebar{w}}\frac{1}{\delta^{1-\beta}}
for some constant
C^{\prime}_{v,\underline{w},\widebar{w}}
. Under this condition, we can verify that

\displaystyle\frac{\widebar{w}+3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n^{\beta}}}}{\underline{w}-3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n}}}\leq\frac{\widebar{w}+\frac{3}{8}\widebar{w}}{1-\frac{3}{8}\underline{w}}=\frac{11\widebar{w}}{5\underline{w}}.

Consequently, by Lemma
8
, we get

\|\breve{f}-f^{*}\|_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{9\widebar{w}}{\underline{w}}\widehat{r}_{n}^{2}+\frac{48M_{v}^{2}}{2v-1}\frac{(\log n)^{2v-1}}{n^{\beta(2v-1)}}}\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{\widebar{w}}{\underline{w}}}\widehat{r}_{n}+\frac{7\sqrt{\widebar{w}}M_{v}}{\sqrt{(2v-1)\underline{w}}}\frac{(\log n)^{v-1/2}}{\sqrt{n^{\beta(2v-1)}}},

which is exactly upper bounded by the radius we set in Theorem

.

To interpret our upper bound, note that Inequality
3
shows that the true optimism can be upper bounded by the wild optimism for appropriately chosen noise scales, along with the probability deviation term
R(\delta)
and the pilot error term
V_{2r}(\widebar{f})
.

Intuitively, the excess risk bound consists of three components. The first is the dominant
wild optimism
term,

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big\{\widetilde{\text{Opt}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\rho^{k}_{1}}\big(\widetilde{f}^{k}_{\rho^{k}_{1}}\big)+\widecheck{\text{Opt}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}^{\rho^{k}_{2}}\big(\widecheck{f}^{k}_{\rho^{k}_{2}}\big)\Big\},

which is computed by our interleaved resampling and refitting procedure.

The second component is the
probabilistic deviation term

R(\delta)
. More precisely,
R(\delta)
decomposes into two contributions. The first component is
r\,\frac{10\sqrt{2}\tau\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{\sqrt{n}}
, which arises from the concentration arguments in the refitting analysis when upper bounding
\text{Opt}^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}(\breve{f})
in terms of
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}
. The second one is
\frac{32r\tau\sqrt{\log(K/\delta)}}{\sqrt{K}}

, which captures the sampling error introduced by the resampling step across
K
rounds.

For the
pilot error term

V_{2r}(\bar{f})
, we compare it with the sum of
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)+\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)
. By definition, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)+\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{2r}(\breve{f};\mathcal{D})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})-w_{i})(\breve{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i}))\right\}

\displaystyle+\sup_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{2r}(\breve{f};\mathcal{D})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})-w_{i})(f(x_{i})-\breve{f}(x_{i}))\right\}.

Therefore, the pilot error term takes the same form, except that the noisier quantity
\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})-w_{i}
is replaced by the more informative discrepancy
\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})
. Consequently, as pointed out by
75

\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)+\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(2r)
, and hence to be dominated by the wild optimism term, as long as
\widebar{f}
is a reasonably accurate approximation of
f^{*}
.

More importantly, the following two theorems show that, when
\mathcal{F}
is rich enough,

, we focus on the regime

Theorem 10
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}=\left\{f\in C([0,1])\big|v\in(\frac{1}{2},1),M_{v}>0,\text{s.t.}\forall k,|k|^{v}|\widehat{f}(k)|\leq M_{v}\right\}

x_{i}\neq x_{j},\forall i\neq j
and
|\widehat{f}(k)||k|^{v}<M_{v}
. Then, for any
\delta>0
, with probability at least

.

Suppose that

\forall\delta>0

With Theorems

As for the proofs, Theorems

More generally, a similar intuition should apply to other black-box deep neural networks. If the underlying function class

. Then, it is highly plausible that the pilot error term can also be rigorously shown to be upper bounded by the wild-optimism term.

4.3
Practical Interleaved Resampling and Refitting by Bounding the Radius
\widehat{r}_{n}

To apply Theorem
9
in practice, it is crucial to know at least a feasible radius
r
, that is, a crude upper bound on
\widehat{r}_{n}
. The reason is that the condition
r\geq\widehat{r}_{n}
is needed to determine the appropriate noise scales. In this subsection, we present a practical method for obtaining a valid upper bound on
\widehat{r}_{n}
, and then introduce an adjusted version of Algorithm

suitable for practical implementation.

Specifically, the theoretical foundation of our practical approach is the following theorem.

Theorem 12
.

Assuming that
\mathcal{F}
is convex, for any
t>0
, if
\widehat{r}_{n}>\frac{t^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}
, with probability at least
1-4e^{-t^{2}}
, we have

\widehat{r}_{n}^{2}\leq\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}([2+\frac{1}{t}]\widehat{r}_{n})+\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}([2+\frac{1}{t}]\widehat{r}_{n})+[2+\frac{1}{t}]\widehat{r}_{n}\frac{4\sqrt{2}\tau t}{\sqrt{n}}+V_{[2+\frac{1}{t}]\widehat{r}_{n}}(\bar{f})+4\frac{\tau}{t}\widehat{r}_{n}^{2}+\frac{2\widehat{r}_{n}\tau t}{\sqrt{n}}.

The proof of Theorem
12
is deferred to Appendix
C
.

We now interpret this theoretical result and transform it into a practical approach about finding an upper bound for
\widehat{r}_{n}
. First, based on our previous discussion on the pilot error term, we know that with probability at least
1-4e^{-t^{2}}
,

V_{[2+\frac{1}{t}]\widehat{r}_{n}}(\widebar{f})\lesssim\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}([2+\frac{1}{t}]\widehat{r}_{n})+\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}([2+\frac{1}{t}]\widehat{r}_{n})+C\frac{[2+\frac{1}{t}]\widehat{r}_{n}\tau t}{\sqrt{n}},

for some constant
C
.

Then, we invoke the following lemma.

Lemma 13
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is convex,
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(r)
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(r)
are concave in

. With

, we have that

\frac{\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(s)}{s}\leq\frac{\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(t)}{t},\ \frac{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(s)}{s}\leq\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}^{\varepsilon}(t)}{t},\ \forall s\geq t>0.

Then, for any

denoting

and

The analysis based on these two terms are symmetric, we focus on

Statistical Guarantee on Excess risk

In this section, we upper bound the excess risk

by generalizing an existing bound on the empirical excess risk

Lemma 14
.

Denote
\mathbb{D}(\mathcal{F})^{2}
to be the function class
\left\{(f-f^{\prime})^{2}:f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\right\}
. For any
\delta>0

such that with probability at least

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}[(f(x)-f^{\prime}(x))^{2}]\leq\frac{4\widebar{w}}{\underline{w}}\|f-f^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}+\widetilde{C}_{v}\frac{\widebar{w}}{\underline{w}}\frac{\log n(\log\log n)\log(1/\delta)}{n^{1-\frac{1}{2v}}},

uniformly over

Classical learning theory results
(
12
;
11
)
show that such a generalization typically hinges on the

(or localized complexity) of the underlying function class
\mathcal{F}

Evaluating Black Box Predictors with Multivariate Covariates

In modern deep neural networks, covariate embeddings—such as token representations in large language models—typically reside in high-dimensional spaces. To account for this complexity, we extend our interleaved resampling and refitting procedure from the univariate setting to a d-dimensional covariate domain. Formally, we define the covariate space as
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
. We assume the underlying function class
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
satisfies a coordinate-wise Fourier decay condition; specifically, there exist constants
v>d/2
and
M_{v}>0
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, we have

|k|^{v}\,|\widehat{f}(k)|\leq M_{v},

where
\widehat{f}(k)
denotes the Fourier coefficient of
f
at frequency
k

|k|=|k_{1}|+\cdots+|k_{d}|
is the magnitude of the multi-index
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Throughout this section, we restrict our attention to the regime
d/2<v\leq d
, consistent with the rationale established in Section
2
. As in the univariate setting, the strict lower bound
v>d/2
is essential to guarantee square-integrability via Parseval’s identity. Specifically, for any function
f\in L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
whose Fourier coefficients decay asymptotically as
|\widehat{f}(k)|\sim|k|^{-v}
, the condition
v>d/2
is strictly required to ensure the convergence of the associated multivariate
p

Algorithmically, the core structure of our procedure remains essentially unchanged. The primary technical challenge in the high-dimensional covariate setting arises from the fact that the key mathematical objects in our analysis can no longer be represented naturally as matrices; consequently, standard matrix concentration inequalities are no longer sufficient. Instead, we must establish concentration bounds for higher-order tensors and multi-product empirical processes, which introduce substantial analytical complexity. In particular, to achieve excess risk guarantees comparable to those established in the univariate case, we require a high-dimensional analogue of Lemma
8
applicable to the empirical norm. We formally establish this crucial result in Lemma

Lemma 16

Denoting

Discussion

In this paper, we propose a compute and data-efficient procedure for rigorously evaluating black-box predictors. To ensure broad applicability to almost arbitrary black-box training algorithms, our statistical guarantees completely bypass the classical function class complexity measures that are traditionally relied upon in learning theory. At its core, our evaluation framework introduces a novel interleaved resampling and refitting structure. To achieve data efficiency and fully utilize the training data we have, we leverage the idea of wild refitting
(
75
)
. Furthermore, to overcome the prohibitive computational burden and enable the efficient evaluation of modern models that are trained on a large scale, we introduce an adjoint resampling subroutine. This critical innovation allows us to refit the predictor on several moderately sized sub-datasets rather than retraining on the original massive scale.

Nevertheless, our algorithm has several limitations, each of which points to an important direction for future work. First, our theoretical guarantees depend explicitly on both the covariate density ratio
\widebar{w}/\underline{w}
and the ambient dimension
d
of the covariate space
\Omega
. This dependence is a genuine weakness of the current theory, especially in high-dimensional or distributionally heterogeneous settings, where these quantities may be difficult to control or may lead to overly conservative guarantees. It would therefore be highly desirable to develop a procedure that avoids such dependence while still retaining both computational and statistical efficiency. Second, the practical performance of our method depends on the choice of the noise-scale parameter
\rho
, which currently must be selected manually. This tuning requirement limits the method’s usability in practice and leaves open the possibility that its empirical performance may be sensitive to ad hoc parameter choices. Designing a principled, automatic, and computationally efficient strategy for selecting
\rho
would therefore substantially strengthen the practical value of the approach. Third, our current analysis relies on a mild Fourier decay assumption on the underlying function class
\mathcal{F}
. Although this assumption is mathematically convenient, it is still a nontrivial structural restriction, and it remains unclear whether the main conclusions continue to hold without it or under weaker alternatives. Removing or relaxing this assumption is thus an important open problem. Finally, our evaluation procedure requires full access to the original training dataset, which may be unrealistic in modern large-scale applications where the training data are massive, distributed, private, or otherwise inaccessible after training. This limitation restricts the range of settings in which the method can currently be applied, and it would be particularly valuable to develop variants that remain effective under partial or indirect data access.

.

We apply the chain rule and submultiplicativity of the induced 2-norm. The core of this proof relies on establishing a global upper bound for the Jacobian of
\eta_{\epsilon}
, independent of its input.

Step 1: Global Lipschitz Constant of
\eta_{\epsilon}

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represent an arbitrary row vector. The normalization function is
y(x)=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{\|x\|_{2}^{2}+c}}x
, where
c=d\epsilon>0
. Taking the derivative of
y
with respect to
x
yields the Jacobian matrix:

Dy(x)=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{\|x\|_{2}^{2}+c}}I-\frac{\sqrt{d}xx^{\top}}{(\|x\|_{2}^{2}+c)^{3/2}}

To find the spectral norm
\|Dy(x)\|_{2}
, we analyze its eigenvalues. For any vector
v
:

•

If
v\perp x
,
Dy(x)v=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{\|x\|_{2}^{2}+c}}v
. The eigenvalue is
\lambda_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{\|x\|_{2}^{2}+c}}
.

•

If
v\parallel x
,
Dy(x)x=\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{\|x\|_{2}^{2}+c}}-\frac{\sqrt{d}\|x\|_{2}^{2}}{(\|x\|_{2}^{2}+c)^{3/2}}\right)x=\frac{\sqrt{d}c}{(\|x\|_{2}^{2}+c)^{3/2}}x
. The eigenvalue is
\lambda_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{d}c}{(\|x\|_{2}^{2}+c)^{3/2}}
.

Since
c>0
and
\|x\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0
, the maximum of
\lambda_{1}
occurs when
\|x\|_{2}=0
, giving
\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{c}}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{d\epsilon}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}
. Similarly, the maximum of
\lambda_{2}
also occurs at
\|x\|_{2}=0
, giving
\frac{\sqrt{d}c}{c^{3/2}}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{c}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}
. Therefore, for any input
X
, the spectral norm of the block-diagonal Jacobian of the entire sequence operation
\eta_{\epsilon}
is strictly bounded:

\|D\eta_{\epsilon}(X)\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}

Step 2: Jacobians of Sub-layers Evaluated on Bounded Inputs

By definition of
\eta_{\epsilon}
, the output row vector strictly satisfies
\|\eta_{\epsilon}(X)_{i,:}\|_{2}<\sqrt{d}
. Thus, the Frobenius norm of the normalized sequence matrix

satisfies
\|Z\|_{F}<\sqrt{Nd}
, which implies its spectral norm is bounded:
\|Z\|_{2}<\sqrt{Nd}
.

For the FFN layer
\mathcal{F}^{(l)}(Z)
, the Jacobian norm relies only on the 1-Lipschitz property of

and the weight norms:

\|D\mathcal{F}^{(l)}(Z)\|_{2}\leq\|W_{2}^{(l)}\|_{2}\cdot 1\cdot\|W_{1}^{(l)}\|_{2}=C_{\mathcal{F}}^{(l)}

\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(Z)

. The product rule yields:

d\mathcal{A}^{(l)}=dP\cdot(ZW_{V}^{(l)})+P\cdot(dZW_{V}^{(l)})

Using the property that

\displaystyle\|D\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(Z)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|W_{V}^{(l)}\|_{2}+2\|Z\|_{2}^{2}\|W_{Q}^{(l)}\|_{2}\|W_{K}^{(l)}\|_{2}\|W_{V}^{(l)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|W_{V}^{(l)}\|_{2}\left(\sqrt{N}+2Nd\|W_{Q}^{(l)}\|_{2}\|W_{K}^{(l)}\|_{2}\right)=C_{\mathcal{A}}^{(l)}

Step 3: End-to-End Composition

Using the chain rule for the
l
-th residual block components:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial Y_{l}}{\partial X_{l-1}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|I+D\mathcal{A}^{(l)}(\eta_{\epsilon}(X_{l-1}))D\eta_{\epsilon}(X_{l-1})\|_{2}\leq 1+\frac{C_{\mathcal{A}}^{(l)}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial X_{l}}{\partial Y_{l}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|I+D\mathcal{F}^{(l)}(\eta_{\epsilon}(Y_{l}))D\eta_{\epsilon}(Y_{l})\|_{2}\leq 1+\frac{C_{\mathcal{F}}^{(l)}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}

Taking the product over all
L

\|J\|_{2}\leq\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left\|\frac{\partial X_{l}}{\partial Y_{l}}\right\|_{2}\left\|\frac{\partial Y_{l}}{\partial X_{l-1}}\right\|_{2}\leq\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(1+\frac{C_{\mathcal{A}}^{(l)}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)\left(1+\frac{C_{\mathcal{F}}^{(l)}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right).

, we can invoke Hoeffding’s Lemma. The sub-Gaussian parameter for
Z_{k}
is:

\sigma_{Z_{k}}^{2}\leq\frac{(2c)^{2}}{4}=c^{2}=(\tau\|f-\breve{f}\|_{n})^{2}=\tau^{2}\|f-\breve{f}\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}.

Next, we consider the average deviation across all
K
independent sub-sample folds, denoted by
\Delta:=A_{n}(f)-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}B_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}(f)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}Z_{k}
. Assuming the
K
sub-samples are drawn independently,
\Delta
is the average of
K
independent zero-mean sub-Gaussian random variables. By the properties of independent sub-Gaussian variables, the sub-Gaussian parameter of the average
\Delta
is reduced by a factor of
K
:

\sigma_{\Delta}^{2}=\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{Z_{k}}^{2}=\frac{\tau^{2}\|f-\breve{f}\|_{n}^{2}}{K}.

Applying Lemma
24
(Hoeffding’s Inequality) to
\Delta
, we have for any
t>0
:

\mathbb{P}(\Delta\geq t)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma_{\Delta}^{2}}\right).

We set the right-hand side equal to the confidence level
\frac{\delta}{K}
(which naturally accommodates a subsequent Union Bound over the
K
subsets):

\exp\left(-\frac{Kt^{2}}{\tau^{2}\|f-\breve{f}\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}}\right)=\frac{\delta}{K}.

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides and solving for
t
yields:

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=\frac{\|f-\breve{f}\|_{\mathcal{D}}\tau\sqrt{\log(K/\delta)}}{\sqrt{K}}.

Therefore, with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{K}
,
\Delta\leq t
holds. Rearranging this inequality gives the first desired bound:

A_{n}(f)\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}B_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}(f)+\frac{8\|f-\breve{f}\|_{\mathcal{D}}\tau\sqrt{\log(K/\delta)}}{\sqrt{K}}.

By symmetry, since
C_{n}(f)
and
D_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}(f)
share the exact same algebraic and sampling structure as
A_{n}(f)
and
B_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}(f)
, applying the identical Cauchy-Schwarz separation and Hoeffding bounding procedure directly yields the second inequality:

C_{n}(f)\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}D_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}(f)+\frac{2\|f-\breve{f}\|_{\mathcal{D}}\tau\sqrt{\log(K/\delta)}}{\sqrt{K}}.

We finish the proof.
∎

.

For any

h
by its Fourier series and

\displaystyle\|h\|_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\|h_{N}+\sum_{|k|>N}\widehat{h}(k)e_{k}\|_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(\|h_{N}\|_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{2}+\frac{4M_{v}^{2}}{2v-1}\frac{1}{N^{2v-1}}\right)

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{\widebar{w}+3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n^{\beta}}}}{\underline{w}-3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n}}}\|h_{N}\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}+\frac{4M_{v}^{2}}{2v-1}\frac{1}{N^{2v-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{\widebar{w}+3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n^{\beta}}}}{\underline{w}-3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n}}}\|h-\sum_{|k|>N}\widehat{h}(k)e_{k}\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}+\frac{8M_{v}^{2}}{2v-1}\frac{1}{N^{2v-1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\frac{\widebar{w}+3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n^{\beta}}}}{\underline{w}-3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n}}}\|h\|_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}+\left(2\frac{\widebar{w}+3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n^{\beta}}}}{\underline{w}-3\widebar{w}\sqrt{\frac{2N\log(2N/\delta)}{n}}}+1\right)\frac{8M_{v}^{2}}{2v-1}\frac{1}{N^{2v-1}}.

We finish the proof.

Proof of Theorem
10
.

For the pilot error term
V_{2r}(\widebar{f})
, we first consider the term

\sup_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{2r}(\breve{f};\mathcal{D})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i}))(f(x_{i})-\breve{f}(x_{i}))\right\}.

We define
the triangle pulse basis function
\Lambda_{\epsilon}(x)=\max\left\{0,1-\frac{|x|}{\epsilon}\right\}
. For any vector
g^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\|g^{1}\|_{n}\leq 2r
, we define the following interpolation function.

f_{\epsilon}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}g^{1}_{j}\Lambda_{\epsilon}(x-x_{j}),

where
\epsilon
is some small number to be determined later.
We compute the Fourier coefficient
\widehat{f}_{\epsilon}(k)
by directly evaluating the integral. By definition, we have:

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{\epsilon}(k)=\int_{0}^{1}f_{\epsilon}(x)e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}kx}dx=\sum_{j=1}^{n}g^{1}_{j}\int_{0}^{1}\Lambda_{\epsilon}(x-x_{j})e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}kx}dx.

Assuming
\epsilon
is sufficiently small such that the support of each basis function
\Lambda_{\epsilon}(x-x_{j})
is strictly strictly within
[0,1]
(i.e.,
\epsilon<\min(x_{j},1-x_{j})
), we can evaluate the integral for each
j
by applying the change of variables
u=x-x_{j}
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\Lambda_{\epsilon}(x-x_{j})e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}kx}dx=\int_{-x_{j}}^{1-x_{j}}\Lambda_{\epsilon}(u)e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}k(u+x_{j})}du=e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}kx_{j}}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\left(1-\frac{|u|}{\epsilon}\right)e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}ku}du.

Using Euler’s formula
e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}ku}=\cos(2\pi ku)-\mathrm{i}\sin(2\pi ku)
, and noting that
(1-|u|/\epsilon)\sin(2\pi ku)
is an odd function over the symmetric interval
[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
, its integral evaluates to zero. Thus, we are left with the even cosine term:

\displaystyle\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\left(1-\frac{|u|}{\epsilon}\right)e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}ku}du

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\left(1-\frac{u}{\epsilon}\right)\cos(2\pi ku)du.

We proceed with integration by parts. Let
v=1-\frac{u}{\epsilon}
and
dw=\cos(2\pi ku)du
, which gives
dv=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}du
and
w=\frac{\sin(2\pi ku)}{2\pi k}
. Applying
\int vdw=vw-\int wdv
:

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\left(1-\frac{u}{\epsilon}\right)\cos(2\pi ku)du

\displaystyle=2\left[\left(1-\frac{u}{\epsilon}\right)\frac{\sin(2\pi ku)}{2\pi k}\right]_{0}^{\epsilon}-2\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\frac{\sin(2\pi ku)}{2\pi k}\left(-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)du

\displaystyle=0+\frac{1}{\pi k\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\sin(2\pi ku)du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi k\epsilon}\left[-\frac{\cos(2\pi ku)}{2\pi k}\right]\Bigg|_{0}^{\epsilon}=\frac{1-\cos(2\pi k\epsilon)}{2\pi^{2}k^{2}\epsilon}.

Using the identity
1-\cos(\theta)=2\sin^{2}(\theta/2)
, we rewrite this as
\frac{1-\cos(2\pi k\epsilon)}{2\pi^{2}k^{2}\epsilon}=\epsilon\left(\frac{\sin(\pi\epsilon k)}{\pi\epsilon k}\right)^{2}.

Substituting this result back into the sum, we obtain the explicit expression for
\widehat{f}(k)
:

\widehat{f}_{\epsilon}(k)=\epsilon\left(\frac{\sin(\pi\epsilon k)}{\pi\epsilon k}\right)^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}g^{1}_{j}e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}kx_{j}}.

Thus, we have

|k|^{v}|\widehat{f}_{\epsilon}(k)|\leq\epsilon|k|^{v}n\frac{1}{\pi^{2}\epsilon^{2}k^{2}}\leq 2nr\epsilon^{1-v}\max_{z\geq 0}\{z^{v}\min(1,\frac{1}{\pi^{2}z^{2}})\}.

Since
\max_{z\geq 0}\{z^{v}\min(1,\frac{1}{\pi^{2}z^{2}})\}=\frac{1}{\pi^{v}}
, we set
\epsilon=\min\{|x_{i}-x_{j}|,(\frac{M_{v}\pi^{v}}{2nr})^{\frac{1}{1-v}}\}.

Therefore, we find a function
f_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
f_{\epsilon}(x_{j})=g^{1}_{j},\forall j
. Thus, we consider the function
\varphi=\widehat{f}+f_{\epsilon}
,
\varphi\in\mathbb{B}_{2r}(\breve{f};\mathcal{D})
. Since this argument holds for any
\|g^{1}\|\leq 2r
, we define
g^{1}
to be

g^{1}:=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\|g\|_{n}\leq 2r}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{j}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i}))\right\}.

By Lemma
26
,
g^{1}_{i}=2r\frac{\bar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})}{\|\widebar{f}-\breve{f}\|_{\mathcal{D}}}
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{2r}(\breve{f};\mathcal{D})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})-w_{i})(f(x_{i})-\breve{f}(x_{i}))\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})-w_{i})f_{\epsilon}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})g^{1}_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}f_{\epsilon}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\max_{\|g\|_{n}\leq 2r}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{j}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i}))\right\}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}f_{\epsilon}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{2r}(\breve{f};\mathcal{D})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i}))(f(x_{i})-\breve{f}(x_{i}))\right\}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}f_{\epsilon}(x_{i})

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}f_{\epsilon}(x_{i})

f_{\epsilon}
is independent of
\varepsilon_{i}
and
w_{i}

.

|\widehat{f}(k)|<\frac{M_{1}}{|k|}

\rho_{k}>0
. Since
\widehat{f}\in C^{1}
, then its Fourier coefficients is
o(1/|k|)
. Thus, the residual capacity
\rho_{k}
scales as
\Theta(1/|k|)
and the series
\sum_{k\neq 0}\rho_{k}
diverges.

We construct
h(x)
as a linear combination of truncated residual kernels. Define the truncated kernel up to frequency
N
:

K_{N}(x)=\sum_{0<|k|\leq N}\rho_{k}e^{i2\pi kx}

We propose the function form
h_{N}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}K_{N}(x-x_{j})
. The Fourier coefficients of
h_{N}(x)
are given by:

\widehat{h}_{N}(k)=\begin{cases}\rho_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}e^{-\mathrm{i}2\pi kx_{j}},&\text{if }0<|k|\leq N\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

By the triangle inequality, for any
k
, we can bound the magnitude:

|\widehat{h}_{N}(k)|\leq\rho_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|c_{j}|=\rho_{k}\|c\|_{1}.

To satisfy the condition
|\widehat{h}_{N}(k)|\leq\rho_{k}
, it is sufficient to guarantee that
\|c\|_{1}\leq 1
.

Now we enforce the interpolation condition
h_{N}(x_{i})=y_{i}
, where
y_{i}=g_{1}^{*}(x_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
. This yields a linear system
A^{(N)}c=y
, where the matrix entries are
A^{(N)}_{i,j}=K_{N}(x_{i}-x_{j})
.

Let us analyze the behavior of the matrix
A^{(N)}
as
N\to\infty
:

•

Diagonal entries:
For
i=j
,
A^{(N)}_{i,i}=K_{N}(0)=\sum_{0<|k|\leq N}\rho_{k}
. Since the series diverges,
A^{(N)}_{i,i}\to+\infty
as
N\to\infty
.

•

Off-diagonal entries:
For
i\neq j
,
A^{(N)}_{i,j}=\sum_{0<|k|\leq N}\rho_{k}e^{i2\pi k(x_{i}-x_{j})}
. Since
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
and
\rho_{k}
decreases monotonically to
0
, by Dirichlet’s test, this series converges to a finite value
L_{i,j}<\infty
.

Because the diagonal entries grow unbounded but the off-diagonal entries remain bounded, the matrix
A^{(N)}
becomes strictly diagonally dominant for sufficiently large
N
. Therefore,
(A^{(N)})^{-1}
exists, and its
\ell_{1}
matrix norm vanishes:
\lim_{N\to\infty}\|(A^{(N)})^{-1}\|_{1}=0
.

Therefore, the coefficient vector
c=(A^{(N)})^{-1}y
satisfies:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\|c\|_{1}\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}\|(A^{(N)})^{-1}\|_{1}\|y\|_{1}=0.

Since
\|y\|_{1}
is a fixed finite constant determined by
g_{1}^{*}
, there exists a sufficiently large integer
N^{*}
such that
\|c\|_{1}\leq 1
.

By choosing
h(x)=h_{N^{*}}(x)
, we have successfully constructed a continuous function that exactly interpolates
g_{1}^{*}
at all sample points (i.e.,
h(x_{i})=g_{1}^{*}(x_{i})
), and satisfies that

|\widehat{h}(k)|\leq\rho_{k}\|c\|_{1}\leq\rho_{k}=\frac{M_{1}}{|k|}-|\widehat{f}(k)|.

This completes the construction.

For function
h
, we have
\widehat{f}+h\in\mathbb{B}_{2r}(\breve{f};\mathcal{D})
and that
\widehat{f}+h\in\mathcal{F}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{2r}(\breve{f};\mathcal{D})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})-w_{i})(f(x_{i})-\breve{f}(x_{i}))\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathbb{B}_{2r}(\breve{f};\mathcal{D})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\widebar{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})-w_{i})(f(x_{i})-\breve{f}(x_{i}))\right\}+\frac{4\tau r\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{\sqrt{n}}.

|k|=\|k\|_{2}\geq\|k\|_{\infty}
. Thus,
|k|^{-2v}\leq\|k\|_{\infty}^{-2v}
. This yields:

\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}>N}|\widehat{h}(k)|^{2}\leq 4M_{v}^{2}\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}>N}\|k\|_{\infty}^{-2v}.

To estimate the right-hand sum, we partition the summation domain into shells based on the maximum norm. Let
S_{r}=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\|k\|_{\infty}=r\}
for integers
r>N
. The number of integer points on the surface of the hypercube of radius
r
is exactly
|S_{r}|=(2r+1)^{d}-(2r-1)^{d}
. By the mean value theorem, for
r\geq 1
, we can bound
|S_{r}|
by:

|S_{r}|\leq 2d(2r+1)^{d-1}\leq 2d(3r)^{d-1}=S_{d}r^{d-1},

where
S_{d}
is a constant depending solely on the dimension
d
. Rewriting the multiple sum as a single sum over
r
, we have:

\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}>N}\|k\|_{\infty}^{-2v}=\sum_{r=N+1}^{\infty}\sum_{k\in S_{r}}r^{-2v}\leq\sum_{r=N+1}^{\infty}S_{d}r^{d-1}r^{-2v}=S_{d}\sum_{r=N+1}^{\infty}r^{d-1-2v}.

Since
v>d/2
, we have
d-1-2v<-1
. The function
x\mapsto x^{d-1-2v}
is strictly monotonically decreasing for
x>0
. We can therefore bound the series by an integral:

\sum_{r=N+1}^{\infty}r^{d-1-2v}\leq\int_{N}^{\infty}x^{d-1-2v}dx=\left[\frac{x^{d-2v}}{d-2v}\right]_{N}^{\infty}=\frac{N^{d-2v}}{2v-d}.

Combining all the inequalities, we obtain
\sum_{\|k\|_{\infty}>N}|\widehat{h}(k)|^{2}\leq\frac{4M_{v}^{2}S_{d}}{2v-d}N^{d-2v}
.
Now, we study
h_{N}(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k_{i}|\leq N}\widehat{h}(k)e_{k}(x)
. By definition, we have

\|h_{N}(x)\|_{{\mathcal{S}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{S}}}(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k_{i}|\leq N}\widehat{h}(k)e_{k}(x_{i}))^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{\beta}}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\sum_{k,l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k_{i}|,|l_{i}|\leq N}\widehat{h}(k)\widehat{h}(l)e_{l}(x_{i})e_{k}(x_{i})\right).

We denote the covariate
x_{i}=(x_{i}^{1},x_{i}^{2},\cdots,x_{i}^{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the vector-valued mapping

\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle v_{N}(t):=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}Nt}\\
\vdots\\

Proof of Lemma
21
.

By the Plancherel theorem, there is an isometric isomorphism between sequences
v\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and functions
V\in L^{2}([0,1])
given by the Fourier series
V(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}v_{k}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}kx}
, which satisfies
\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|V\|_{L^{2}}
.

Now, consider the operator
A
acting on the vector
v
, yielding
u=Av
. The
i
-th component of this matrix-vector multiplication is a discrete convolution:

u_{i}=(Av)_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{i,j}v_{j}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{w}(i-j)v_{j}.

According to the convolution theorem for Fourier transforms, a discrete convolution in the frequency domain corresponds strictly to pointwise multiplication in the time domain. Thus, the continuous function
U(x)
corresponding to the sequence
u
is exactly the product of
w(x)
and
V(x)
:

U(x)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{i}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}ix}=w(x)V(x).

Therefore, the operator norm of
A
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is equivalent to the operator norm of the multiplication operator
M_{w}:V(x)\mapsto w(x)V(x)
on
L^{2}([0,1])
:

\|A\|_{\text{op}}=\sup_{v\neq 0}\frac{\|Av\|_{\ell^{2}}}{\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}}=\sup_{V\neq 0}\frac{\|wV\|_{L^{2}}}{\|V\|_{L^{2}}}.

Next, we prove that the norm of this multiplication operator is exactly
\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

For any
V\in L^{2}([0,1])
, we have:

\|wV\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}|w(x)V(x)|^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}|V(x)|^{2}\,dx=\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|V\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Taking the square root and the supremum yields
\|A\|_{\text{op}}\leq\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

On the other hand, by the definition of the essential supremum, for any given
\epsilon>0
, the set
S_{\epsilon}=\{x\in[0,1]:|w(x)|>\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}-\epsilon\}
has a strictly positive Lebesgue measure, i.e.,
\mu(S_{\epsilon})>0
.
We construct a specific indicator function:

V_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu(S_{\epsilon})}}\mathbf{1}_{S_{\epsilon}}(x).

Clearly,
\|V_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}=1
. Applying the operator to this function gives:

\displaystyle\|wV_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{\epsilon}}|w(x)|^{2}\frac{1}{\mu(S_{\epsilon})}dx\geq\int_{S_{\epsilon}}(\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}-\epsilon)^{2}\frac{1}{\mu(S_{\epsilon})}dx=(\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}-\epsilon)^{2}.

This shows that for any
\epsilon>0
,
\|A\|_{\text{op}}\geq\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}-\epsilon
. Letting
\epsilon\to 0
gives
\|A\|_{\text{op}}\geq\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}
.
Combining the upper and lower bounds, we establish that

\|A\|_{\text{op}}=\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Furthermore, since
w(x)
is real-valued,
A
is self-adjoint. By the Rayleigh-Ritz theorem, the spectral lower bound of
A
is given by the infimum of its Rayleigh quotient. Using the same isomorphism, we evaluate the quadratic form:

\langle v,Av\rangle_{\ell^{2}}=\int_{0}^{1}\overline{V(x)}(w(x)V(x))\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}w(x)|V(x)|^{2}\,dx.

Under the assumption that
w(x)\geq c>0
, we have:

\int_{0}^{1}w(x)|V(x)|^{2}\,dx\geq\inf_{x\in[0,1]}w(x)\int_{0}^{1}|V(x)|^{2}\,dx\geq c\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

Taking the infimum over all unit vectors
\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}=1
yields the desired spectral lower bound:

\lambda_{\min}(A)=\inf_{\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}=1}\langle v,Av\rangle_{\ell^{2}}\geq\inf_{x\in[0,1]}w(x)\geq c.

Finally, if
w(x)\geq c>0
almost everywhere on
[0,1]
, the absolute value of
w(x)
is bounded below by
c
. Then,
\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}\geq\inf_{x\in[0,1]}|w(x)|\geq c.

Combining this with

Proof of Lemma
22
.

Let
v
be an arbitrary sequence in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
with finite support. We can associate
v
with a
d

By Parseval’s identity, we know that
\|f\|_{L^{2}([0,1]^{d})}^{2}=\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}
.

Now, we consider the quadratic form associated with the operator
A
:

\langle v,Av\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\sum_{\mathbf{j}}\bar{v}_{\mathbf{i}}a_{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}}v_{\mathbf{j}}=\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\sum_{\mathbf{j}}\bar{v}_{\mathbf{i}}\widehat{w}(\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j})v_{\mathbf{j}}.

Substituting the definition of the Fourier coefficient
\widehat{w}(\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j})
into the summation yields:

\langle v,Av\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\sum_{\mathbf{j}}\bar{v}_{\mathbf{i}}v_{\mathbf{j}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}w(\mathbf{x})e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j})\cdot\mathbf{x}}d\mathbf{x}.

Since the summation is finite, we can interchange the order of integration and summation:

\langle v,Av\rangle=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}w(\mathbf{x})\left(\sum_{\mathbf{j}}v_{\mathbf{j}}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{j}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\right)\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\bar{v}_{\mathbf{i}}e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\right)d\mathbf{x}.

Recognizing the terms inside the parentheses as
f(\mathbf{x})
and its complex conjugate
\overline{f(\mathbf{x})}
, we obtain the fundamental identity:

\langle v,Av\rangle=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}w(\mathbf{x})|f(\mathbf{x})|^{2}d\mathbf{x}.

To bound the operator norm
\|A\|_{op}
, we take the absolute value of the quadratic form:

|\langle v,Av\rangle|\leq\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|w(\mathbf{x})||f(\mathbf{x})|^{2}d\mathbf{x}\leq\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|f(\mathbf{x})|^{2}d\mathbf{x}=\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

Taking the supremum over all such
v
with
\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}=1
, we conclude that
\|A\|_{op}\leq\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

For the lower bound of the spectrum, since
w(\mathbf{x})>0
almost everywhere, the operator
A
is self-adjoint, and its spectrum lies on the real line. Utilizing the same identity, we have:

\langle v,Av\rangle=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}w(\mathbf{x})|f(\mathbf{x})|^{2}d\mathbf{x}\geq\left(\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d}}w(\mathbf{x})\right)\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|f(\mathbf{x})|^{2}d\mathbf{x}.

This implies
\langle v,Av\rangle\geq\inf w(\mathbf{x})\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}
. By the Rayleigh-Ritz theorem for self-adjoint operators, this establishes that
\inf\{\sigma(A)\}\geq\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d}}w(\mathbf{x})
.
