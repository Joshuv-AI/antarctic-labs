Parent–adolescent psychological distress (in)congruence and adolescent NSSI: a dyadic response surface analysis
Authors: Guanghui Shen, Yang Zou, Mengting Wang, Wenqian Jian, Hong Pan, Yawen Zheng, Li Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) represents a significant public health concern among adolescents with mood disorders. While individual psychological distress is established as a risk factor, the impact of parent-adolescent emotional congruence/incongruence on NSSI remains underexplored. This study aimed to examine the associations between adolescent and parental psychological distress and adolescent NSSI, focusing on the effects of dyadic congruence and incongruence.MethodsThe study included 147 parent-adolescent dyads (94 girls, 53 boys; mean age = 15.18 years) with adolescents diagnosed with depression or bipolar disorder. Participants completed the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21) and the Self-Injurious Behavior Scale. Polynomial regression analysis and response surface analysis were employed to examine the effects of congruence and incongruence in psychological distress between parents and adolescents on adolescent NSSI.ResultsAdolescent psychological distress was positively associated with NSSI frequency (depression: r = 0.38, p < 0.001; anxiety: r = 0.42, p < 0.001; stress: r = 0.31, p < 0.001). Response surface analyses revealed that emotional incongruence between parents and adolescents significantly predicted higher levels of NSSI for all three dimensions of psychological distress. For anxiety specifically, a U-shaped curvilinear relationship emerged (a₄=0.29, p < 0.001), indicating that extreme incongruence in either direction was particularly detrimental.ConclusionThe current study highlights that beyond individual psychological distress, emotional incongruence between parents and adolescents represents a significant risk factor for NSSI in clinically depressed adolescents. This underscores the importance of family-based interventions that promote emotional understanding and co-regulation within parent-adolescent relationships to reduce NSSI risk in clinical populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13034-026-01108-2.

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) defined as the deliberate damage to one’s body tissue without suicidal intent, represents a major public health concern among adolescents [1]. Common NSSI behaviors include cutting, burning, hitting, biting, or punching oneself. The prevalence of NSSI among adolescents is notably high, with lifetime rates ranging from 13% to 24% in non-clinical samples [2–4] and 12-month rates between 46.7 and 59.0% among psychiatric inpatients [5]. Notably, adolescents diagnosed with clinical mood disorders exhibit a considerably higher prevalence of NSSI, with estimates as high as 44.8% in adolescents with anxiety and 48.3% in adolescents with depression [6]. Furthermore, NSSI correlates with several negative outcomes, such as increased risk for suicide attempts, substance abuse, and poor psychosocial functioning [7].

Psychological distress-encompassing symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress-is a well-established risk factor for NSSI among adolescents [8]. Adolescents often engage in NSSI as a mechanism to cope with negative emotions, express emotional pain, or assert control in their lives [9]. Although the association between adolescent psychological distress and NSSI is consistently demonstrated, the specific influence of parental psychological distress, particularly its congruence and incongruence with adolescent psychological distress, remains an under-explored area concerning NSSI risk. Previous research has highlighted how parental psychological distress can affect parent-child relationships and contribute to adolescent psychopathology [10, 11]. However, the specific influence of parental and adolescent emotional congruence and incongruence on adolescent NSSI has not been extensively studied.

This research aims to fill this gap by investigating how the consistency of emotional experiences between parents and adolescents relates to NSSI behavior in a clinical sample of adolescents diagnosed with depression or bipolar disorder. By examining the interaction between parental and adolescent psychological distress in the context of NSSI, this study could inform the development of targeted interventions and prevention strategies for NSSI in at-risk adolescent populations.

The role of psychological distress in adolescents’ NSSI

Psychological distress is a broad construct that refers to the unpleasant emotional and mental suffering experienced by an individual. It encompasses a range of symptoms and experiences, including feelings of anxiety, depression, stress, irritability, anger, and somatic manifestations such as sleep disturbances and fatigue [12]. Depression, anxiety, and stress are three key components of psychological distress that have been consistently linked to NSSI in adolescents [13]. For instance, a recent network analysis study identified depressive symptoms as a central node in the NSSI behavior network, and the aggravation of depression can lead to uncontrollable self-harm [14]. Similarly, anxiety symptoms have also been demonstrated to correlate with NSSI, Bock et al.(2021) found that anxiety symptoms were independently associated with NSSI, and that individuals suffering from anxiety seek escape from their suffering through NSSI. Perceived stress has also been linked to an increased likelihood of engaging in NSSI among adolescents [15].

Parental psychological distress demonstrably exerts a profound impact on parent-child relationships, ultimately affecting adolescents’ mental health [16]. Extensive research indicates that parental depression and anxiety precipitate heightened family conflict, characterized by frequent arguments, disagreements, and tension [17–19]. This environment of conflict and hostility compromises adolescents’ emotional well-being, thus increasing their susceptibility to various psychological difficulties [20, 21]. Furthermore, parental stress diminishes the warmth and involvement in the parent-child bond, which can leave adolescents feeling unsupported, misunderstood, or emotionally distant from their parents, heightening their vulnerability to mental health problems [22–24]. Moreover, prior research reported that parents of adolescents engaging in NSSI often experience significant psychological distress, such as anxiety, depression, guilt, shame, and feelings of responsibility. This distress can impact the parent-child relationship, leading to heightened control and hypervigilance, potentially exacerbating the situation [25, 26].

Congruence/incongruence in parent–adolescent psychological distress and adolescents’ NSSI

The relationship between adolescent or parental psychological distress and NSSI has been well-established, but the role of parental psychological distress’s congruence or incongruence with adolescent distress in shaping NSSI risk remains insufficiently examined. Emotional congruence/incongruence constitutes a core construct in developmental and family psychology, which highlights the significance of emotional congruence/incongruence between parents and adolescents on the emotional development and regulation strategies of the latter [27]. In this study, we define emotional congruence as the degree of similarity in psychological distress levels between parent and child, serving as an indicator of emotional attune. Conversely, incongruence represents a significant discrepancy in emotional states. Although direct empirical evidence on the impact of congruence/incongruence on NSSI is limited, several studies provide indirect support. For instance, incongruence in educational aspirations between parents and adolescents has been associated with heightened internalizing and externalizing symptoms in adolescents [28]. Similarly, research by Lohman & Jarvis reported that incongruence in parent and adolescent perceptions of family functioning were associated with higher levels of psychological distress and maladaptive coping strategies, such as NSSI, in adolescents [29]. While these studies did not directly investigate the impact of psychological distress congruence or incongruence, they collectively suggest that incongruence between parents and adolescents may influence NSSI development. Further evidence comes from Victor et al. (2019) who found that poorer parent–adolescent relationship quality predicted higher levels of NSSI, implying that relational difficulties—potentially reflective of distress incongruence—may function as a developmental risk factor [30]. This implies that a negative parent-adolescent relationship, potentially characterized by psychological distress incongruence, could pose a risk factor for NSSI development in adolescents.

Family systems theory posits that family members’ emotional states are interdependent and that disruption within one member affects the broader family system [31]. This theory offers theoretical backing for the correlation between psychological distress and its influence on family dynamics. When there is an incongruence in the levels of psychological distress between parents and adolescents, it can have a significantly negative impact on overall family functioning. In clinical settings, such distress incongruence often manifests as dyadic disharmony: parents and adolescents experience and express emotional states that do not resonate with each other, leading to misunderstandings, diminished attunement, and conflict. This mismatch can lead to reduced emotional attunement and, critically, emotional invalidation, whereby adolescents perceive their feelings as misunderstood, minimized, or dismissed. Emotional invalidation has been identified as a key interpersonal risk factor for maladaptive emotion regulation. According to cognitive-emotional models of NSSI, invalidating interpersonal environments hinder the development of adaptive regulatory strategies, increasing the likelihood that adolescents rely on NSSI as an immediate means of modulating overwhelming distress [9]. From an attachment perspective, persistent emotional incongruence may be associated with reduced emotional security, which in turn could heighten vulnerability to self-injurious behaviors [3, 32].

The current research has yet to adequately examine the complex emotional interactions between parents and adolescents and the way in which these interactions may influence adolescent NSSI. This gap is concerning, as emotional incongruence within these relationships could potentially impact NSSI behaviors. A more nuanced investigation into these emotional processes is therefore essential for advancing understanding of NSSI development. Identifying patterns of emotional congruence or incongruence between parents and adolescents may offer researchers and clinicians critical insights into mechanisms underlying NSSI and inform more targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

Polynomial regression analysis and response surface analysis for studying informant incongruence

Traditionally, parent–adolescent incongruence has been assessed by calculating score differences between the two parties [33], which are then examined in relation to the criterion variable using simple linear regression. However, this approach has been widely criticized for lacking validity [34]. As a more robust alternative, polynomial regression has been recommended, as it allows for modeling the relationship between dependent and independent variables while incorporating quadratic and interaction terms [35, 36]. Complementing this approach, Response Surface Analysis (RSA) has been proposed to examine congruence and incongruence patterns between informants with respect to a criterion variable [37]. RSA graphically depicts the polynomial regression equation as a three-dimensional surface, allowing for statistical testing of surface parameters and facilitating interpretation of congruence effects [38, 39]. As Schönbrodt (2018) emphasizes, RSA mitigates the conceptual and statistical limitations of difference scores and extends beyond the constraints of moderated regression by directly modeling congruence hypotheses and providing interpretable surface parameters [34]. Empirical work supports its utility, for instance, Wang et al.(2024) demonstrated that incongruence in parent–child relationships was positively associated with adolescent depression through RSA [40]. In the context of NSSI, these analytic techniques offer a powerful framework for examining how congruence or incongruence between parent and adolescent psychological distress influences the development and maintenance of NSSI. Specifically, they can shed light on whether high congruence between parent and adolescent distress is linked to increased NSSI, or if specific patterns of incongruence have a more detrimental impact. Ultimately, these methods are valuable for understanding the effects of congruence or incongruence between parent and adolescent psychological distress on NSSI.

The present study

The association between parent-adolescent congruence/incongruence in psychological distress and adolescent NSSI remains insufficiently examined, especially among adolescents with clinical mood disorders. Existing research in this area also faces limitations in distinguishing between types of psychological distress and in the use of more sophisticated statistical methods. To address these gaps, the present study employed polynomial regression analysis combined with response surface analysis to investigate the effect of three types of psychological distress, depression, anxiety and stress, on adolescent NSSI in adolescents with clinical mood disorders (Aim 1) and further to examine the relationship between parent-adolescent congruence and incongruence in psychological distress and adolescent NSSI (Aim 2).

Based on prior empirical evidence, we hypothesized that higher levels of adolescent psychological distress (depression, anxiety and stress) will be positively associated with increased levels of NSSI in adolescents (Hypothesis 1) and higher levels of parental psychological distress (depression, anxiety and stress) will be positively associated with increased levels of NSSI in adolescents (Hypothesis 2). Regarding the effects of parent-adolescent congruence and incongruence, the current study posited that incongruence between parent and adolescent psychological distress will be positively associated with higher levels of NSSI in adolescents (Hypothesis 3).

Method

Participants

Data collection was conducted in the psychological clinics and psychiatric clinics of three hospitals in Wenzhou and Huzhou, Zhejiang Province. A total of 150 parent–adolescent dyads were initially recruited. Among them, 3 dyads were excluded due to failed matching caused by encoding problems, resulting in a final valid sample of 147 dyads. As the missing data accounted for less than 5% of the total sample and its loss is due to researcher coding errors (completely random in nature), listwise deletion was adopted. The inclusion criteria for the adolescent participants were: (1) diagnosed with either depressive disorder or bipolar disorder by two or more psychiatrists using the criteria outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th ed.; DSM–5); and (2) absence of any diagnosed organic diseases. Diagnoses were made by psychiatrists at the study hospitals. One parent per adolescent participated in the study, with both mothers and fathers being eligible. Specifically, the parent who accompanied the adolescent to the psychiatric outpatient clinic at the time of assessment was invited to participate. Finally, the study included 94 girls (64%) and 53 boys (36%) adolescents, with an average age of 15.18 years (SD = 7.71). The parents, on the other hand, had an average age of 43.09 years (SD = 4.63).

A priori power analyses was conducted using G*Power 3.1 to decide the minimum required sample size for multiple linear regression analysis. Based on conventional parameters for a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired statistical power of 0.80, with 9 predictors included in the model (encompassing the five polynomial terms and covariates), the analysis showed that a sample size of at least 114 participants was required. Our final sample of 147 exceeded this threshold, providing sufficient statistical power to detect medium-sized effects in the regression models.

Procedure

Informed consent was obtained from both adolescents and parents prior to participation. Data collection was conducted in a double-blind manner. Psychiatrists who were blind to the purpose of the study provided clinical information and confirmed diagnoses using DSM-5 criteria. Graduate students with a master’s degree in psychology were responsible for questionnaire data collection across the study hospitals. All graduate students participated in questionnaire management training prior to data collection to ensure consistency of administration procedures. Questionnaire data were collected on a one-to-one basis at the hospital sites following participants’ consent. Questionnaire responses were de-identified and coded for analysis to maintain participant confidentiality. Data from the questionnaires was entered by graduate students into SPSS version 26.0. Multiple checks were performed to ensure the accuracy of data entry. All study procedures were approved by the Wenzhou Medical University Review Board (No.2023022).

Measurements

Demographic information

Data on age and sex was gathered from adolescents, with age being recorded as a continuous variable in years and sex coded as a dichotomous variable (0 = male, 1 = female). Similarly, age data for parents was collected as a continuous variable denoting year.

Adolescents’ psychological distress

Adolescents’ psychological distress was evaluated using the 21-item Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21) [41]. This scale comprises three subscales that measure symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress. Participants rated the extent to which each statement applied to them in the past week on a 4-point severity scale ranging from 0 (not applicable at all) to 3 (highly applicable or most of the time). Scores for each subscale were totaled, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. In this study, the internal consistency of the three DASS-21 subscales was found to be good: depression (Cronbach’s α = 0.83), anxiety (Cronbach’s α = 0.85), and stress (Cronbach’s α = 0.88).

Adolescents’ NSSI

Adolescents’ non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) was evaluated using the 7-item Self-Injurious Behavior Scale adapted from the Functional Assessment of Self-Mutilation(FASM) [42] and modified by You et al. for use among Chinese adolescents [43]. Participants were asked to indicate how frequently they had engaged in behaviors such as cutting, burning, hitting, biting, and punching themselves during the previous 6 months. The response options ranged from 0 to 6 or more times. The responses were then added together to calculate a total NSSI frequency score, with higher scores indicating more frequent self-injurious behaviors. The internal consistency of the scale was found to be good in this study (α = 0.85).

Parents’ psychological distress

Parents’ psychological distress was also evaluated using the 21-item Depression Anxiety Stress Scale. Among the parents, the three DASS-21 sub-sequences also have good internal consistency: depression (Cronbach’s α = 0.82), anxiety (Cronbach’s α = 0.84), and stress (Cronbach’s α = 0.84).

Statistics analysis

Descriptive statistics and Pearson’s correlation analyses were performed to delineate the demographic composition of the participants. For descriptive purposes, scores were standardized and categorized to illustrate patterns of parent–adolescent (in)congruence. A difference greater than half a standard deviation was used as an indicative threshold for notable incongruence, consistent with prior research suggesting that 0.5 standard deviation represents a meaningful difference in psychological and clinical measures [44].

Then, dyadic polynomial regressions using R software (R version 4.3.2) were conducted to examine the effect of parents’ anxiety and adolescents’ psychological distress on adolescents’ NSSI. The dyadic polynomial regression model was constructed from a polynomial regression model, in which X1 and X2 stand for adolescents’ psychological distress and parents’ psychological distress, respectively. Y stands for adolescents’ NSSI frequency, this polynomial regression equations can be written as follows:

Finally, response surface analysis was conducted. Rather than directly interpreting the results from the polynomial regression analysis, RSA uses effects from the polynomial regression model to generate a response surface that makes overall comparisons of concordance and discordance and provides tests for four coefficients to show (a1) whether concordance is better than discordance, (a2) whether direction of discordance matters, (a3) whether concordance in different levels of the measure differs, and (a4) whether there is a curvilinear effect, and (a5) whether the response surface exhibits rotational asymmetry. Specifically, the a1 coefficient (i.e., b1 + b2) represents the slope of the line of congruence (LOC), and the a2 coefficient (i.e., b3 + b4 + b5) represents the curvature of the LOC. A significant a1 indicates a linear relationship, whereas a significant a2 indicates a curvilinear relationship between parent–adolescent congruence and adolescent NSSI. Similarly, the a3 coefficient (i.e., b1 − b2) represents the slope of the line of incongruence (LOIC), and the a4 coefficient (i.e., b3 − b4 + b5) represents the curvature of the LOIC. A significant a3 indicates a linear relationship, whereas a significant a4 indicates a curvilinear relationship between parent–adolescent incongruence and adolescent NSSI. In addition, the a5 coefficient (i.e., b3 − b5), often referred to as the “twisting” effect, indicates whether the principal axes of the response surface are rotated away from the LOC and LOIC(Supplementary Table S1). All statistical analyses in this study were executed using R (version 4.3.2), employing the “state” “rsm”, and “RSA” packages, with a significance level set at p < 0.05 for two-sided tests.

Result

Preliminary analysis

Table 1 provides means, standard deviations, and correlations between the study variables. Correlation analyses indicated that adolescent NSSI was positively associated with depression, anxiety, and stress (ps < 0.001). Additionally, adolescents’ NSSI showed correlations with sex (p < 0.001) and age (p = 0.001) of the adolescents. Specifically, girls exhibited higher NSSI frequency compared to boys, and adolescents’ NSSI engagement tended to decrease with age. There were no significant correlations found between parental psychological distress and adolescents’ NSSI (depression: p = 0.58; anxiety: p = 0.95; stress: p = 0.74).

Paired samples t-tests were conducted to examine the differences in levels of psychological distress between adolescents and their parents. The results revealed that adolescents’ psychological distress was significantly higher than that of their parents (Depression: t = 8.50, p < 0.001; Anxiety: t = 9.28, p < 0.001; Stress: t = 4.86, p < 0.001). Further, as for the occurrence of consistency/inconsistency, in terms of depression, 53 (36.05%) adolescents reported higher levels than their parents, 42 (28.57%) were consistent with their parents, and 52 (35.37%) adolescents reported lower levels than their parents. For anxiety, 61 (41.50%) adolescents reported higher levels than their parents, 34 (23.13%) were consistent with their parents, and 52 (35.37%) adolescents reported lower levels than their parents. Regarding stress, 60 (40.82%) adolescents reported higher levels than their parents, 28 (19.05%) were consistent with their parents, and 59 (40.13%) adolescents reported lower levels than their parents.

Dyadic polynomial regression for adolescents’ NSSI

Three polynomial regression models were analyzed to investigate how parents’ and adolescents’ psychological distress (depression, anxiety, and stress) predict adolescents’ NSSI frequency, as presented in Table 2.

In model 1, which examined the predictive effect of adolescents’ depression and parents’ depression on adolescent’s NSSI, the entire model explained 22% of the variance in adolescents’ NSSI (R2 = 0.22, p < 0.001). It was found that higher scores in adolescents’ depression (b1−1= 0.39, p < 0.001) were associated with more frequent engagement in adolescents’ NSSI, while the effect of parents’ depression was not significant (p = 0.94). Additionally, the quadratic terms for adolescents’ depression (p = 0.22) and parents’ depression did not show significance (p = 0.86), and neither did their interaction term (p = 0.65).

In model 2, which examined the predictive effect of adolescents’ anxiety and parents’ anxiety on adolescents’ NSSI, the entire model explained 23% of the variance in adolescents’ NSSI (R2 = 0.27, p < 0.001). It was found that higher scores in adolescents’ anxiety (b2−1 = 0.39, p < 0.001) were associated with more frequent engagement in adolescents’ NSSI, while the linear term for parents’ anxiety (p = 0.07) and the quadratic term for adolescents’ anxiety (p = 0.16) were not significant. However, the quadratic terms for parent’s anxiety (b2−5 = 0.11, p < 0.01) was significant, suggesting a nonlinear effect. There were no significant interaction effects between the anxiety of adolescents and parents (p = 0.58).

In model 3, which examined the predictive effect of adolescents’ stress and parents’ stress on adolescent’s NSSI, the entire model explained 11% of the variance in adolescents’ NSSI (R2 = 0.16, p < 0.001). It was found that higher scores in adolescents’ stress (b3−1 = 0.30, p < 0.001) were associated with more frequent engagement in adolescents’ NSSI, while the effect of parents’ stress was not significant (p = 0.89). Additionally, the quadratic terms for adolescents’ stress (p = 0.81) and parents’ stress (p = 0.90) did not show significance, and neither did their interaction term (p = 0.98).

Response surface analysis on (in)congruence effect

The results of the response surface analyses were shown in Table 3. Furthermore, the RSA plots were provided in Figs. 1, 2 and 3, providing a visual illustration of the results for three models.

Adolescents’ depression and parents’ depression (in)congruence on adolescents’ NSSI

The RSA examined congruence (a1−1 and a1−2) and incongruence (a1−3 and a1−4) effects of adolescents’ depression and parents’ depression on adolescents’ NSSI, as shown in Table 3; Fig. 1A and B. RSA yielded a significant linear effect (a1−1 = 0.40, p < 0.01) and a nonsignificant curvilinear effect of LOC (a1−2 = 0.16, p > 0.05), indicating that when both adolescents’ depression and parents’ depression were higher, adolescents’ NSSI tended to be higher. Moreover, the significant positive linear effect (a1−3 = 0.39, p < 0.01) and nonsignificant curvilinear effect (a1−4 = 0.13, p > 0.05) of LOIC indicated that increased incongruence between adolescents’ depression and parents’ depression was linked to higher levels of adolescent NSSI. The a₁₋₅ was not significant (p > 0.05).

Adolescents’ anxiety and parents’ anxiety (in)congruence on adolescents’ NSSI

The response surface analysis examined congruence (a2−1 and a2−2) and incongruence (a2−3 and a2−4) effects of adolescents’ anxiety and parents’ anxiety on adolescents’ NSSI, as shown in Table 3; Fig. 2A and B. RSA yielded a nonsignificant linear effect (a2−1 = 0.21, p > 0.05) and a nonsignificant curvilinear effect of LOC (a2−2 = 0.19, p > 0.05). However, the significant positive linear effect (a2−3 = 0.56, p < 0.001) and significant curvilinear effect (a2−4 = 0.29, p < 0.001) of LOIC indicated that increased incongruence between adolescents’ anxiety and parents’ anxiety was associated with greater engagement in NSSI by adolescents. Discussion

The present study aimed to explore the correlation between emotional (in)congruence between parents and adolescents and NSSI among adolescents with mood disorders. By analyzing data on parent-adolescent matching and using dyadic response surface analysis, the findings indicated that higher incongruence in emotional distress between adolescents and parents, specifically in terms of depression, anxiety, and stress, was linked to a higher frequency of NSSI among adolescents. Additionally, a curvilinear relationship was observed between parent-adolescent emotional incongruence and adolescent NSSI, particularly in relation to anxiety, suggesting that extreme levels of incongruence could have a particularly negative impact on adolescent mental health. These results underscore the intricate connection between familial emotional dynamics and their direct influence on self-injurious behavior in adolescents who struggle with emotional regulation.

Adolescents’ psychological distress and NSSI

The study findings demonstrated a notable positive linear correlation between psychological distress and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) in adolescents with mood disorders. Specifically, higher levels of depression, anxiety, and stress were associated with increased frequency of NSSI engagement among adolescents. This correlation aligns closely with existing research on NSSI, such as the work of Miller and Klonsky, which has previously identified NSSI as a maladaptive coping strategy used by adolescents to cope with intense emotional distress [45, 46]. This perspective is further enriched by considering the developmental trajectory of NSSI within the context of emotional disorders. Adolescents, who are undergoing crucial emotional and psychological development, often struggle with processing and managing complex emotional states [47]. When confronted with overwhelming feelings of depression, anxiety, or stress, NSSI may serve as a tangible method to regain control over their emotional experiences or temporarily alleviate psychological pain [48]. Inadequate coping mechanisms, combined with the intensity of psychological distress in individuals with mood disorders, create a susceptibility where NSSI becomes a harmful yet accessible response [9].

Parents’ psychological distress and adolescents’ NSSI

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, bivariate correlations revealed no significant linear associations between parental psychological distress (depression, anxiety, stress) and adolescent NSSI. Two considerations may account for this result. First, parents’ mean distress levels were substantially lower than those of adolescents, possibly below the threshold needed for a direct linear influence. Second, as shown below, the relationship appears to be nonlinear rather than linear. Bivariate correlations are unable to capture such curvilinear patterns. The significant nonlinear relationship was discovered between parental anxiety and adolescent NSSI in a dyadic polynomial regression analysis. This relationship was illustrated by a U-shaped curve, indicating that both high and low levels of parental anxiety were linked to higher occurrences of NSSI in adolescents. This study highlights a dual risk process involving parental anxiety and adolescent NSSI, where both excessive and insufficient parental anxiety expressions contribute to an elevated risk of NSSI in adolescents. Excessive parental anxiety creates an emotionally unstable environment that negatively impacts the child, aligning with the principles of Attachment Theory [49]. According to Attachment Theory, adolescents naturally seek security and stability within their relationships with parents. From a family perspective, elevated parental anxiety undermines the overall emotional climate of the household, weakening the sense of security that adolescents derive from parental relationships and thereby heightening their vulnerability to anxiety and emotional dysregulation [50]. Evidence from Krizova’s longitudinal study further demonstrates that parental anxiety exerts long-term effects on children’s emotional regulation difficulties [51]. On the other hand, insufficient parental anxiety expression (parental positivity bias) also increases the risk of NSSI. Parents who lack emotional engagement or expression may unintentionally communicate emotional disengagement or unavailability, making adolescents feel neglected or unsupported [52]. This is particularly relevant for the current study’s sample of adolescents with mood disorders, such as depression and bipolar disorder. In this context, parents with notably low levels of anxiety might underestimate the seriousness of their child’s emotional states, leading to a lack of emotional validation and support [53]. The study of Lagattuta et al. (2012) indicated parents underestimate their childrens’ worries and anxiety and overestimate their optimism (suggesting a parental positivity bias) [53]. According to Emotion Regulation Theory [54], the absence of adequate emotional support from parents hinders the development of effective emotion regulation strategies in adolescents.

Parent–adolescent incongruence and adolescents’ NSSI

The core findings of this research illuminate a novel aspect of family dynamics: the significance of dyadic emotional patterns between parents and adolescents in predicting NSSI. First, regarding the congruence effect, our results revealed a positive linear relationship along the Line of Congruence (LOC) for depression and stress. This indicates that while congruence at low levels of distress (i.e., both parent and adolescent are well-regulated) is associated with the lowest NSSI frequency, congruence at high levels of distress (i.e., “ high-high” congruence) predicts severe NSSI outcomes. This finding aligns with the “cumulative risk” hypothesis in family systems. When both dyad members experience high psychological distress, the family system may face resource depletion. Parents who are themselves overwhelmed by depression or stress may lack the emotional capacity to act as effective “external regulators” for their children. Consequently, the adolescent is exposed to a double burden: their own internal distress and a lack of parental scaffolding, creating an environment of shared toxicity that fuels NSSI.

Beyond this risk of shared distress, response surface analyses indicated that the degree of incongruence between parents’ and adolescents’ psychological distress also significantly predicted higher rates of NSSI, across all three dimensions. Specifically, a greater incongruence in the levels of psychological distress experienced by parents versus adolescents is associated with more frequent engagement in NSSI by adolescents. This pattern aligns with theoretical models that highlight the importance of emotional attunement and validation between parents and adolescents for healthy development of emotion regulation [55]. As posited by Attachment Theory, a secure attachment relationship requires parents to be emotionally available and responsive to their adolescents’ affective needs [56]. However, when there is emotional incongruence, parents may struggle to recognize, understand, and validate their adolescents’ internal experiences. In the absence of constructive coping resources and adequate emotional guidance, adolescents experiencing unresolved emotional turmoil may resort to non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) as a maladaptive strategy to regulate their affective states.

The response surface analyses quantify this risk, demonstrating that more extreme incongruence predicts higher NSSI frequency. Particularly on parental anxiety, the additive quadratic effect specifies that the relationship is nonlinear, following a U-shaped curve. This signifies that maximum incongruence, at either extreme where adolescent or parent anxiety disproportionately outweighs the other, confers greatest risk for adolescent NSSI. Drawing from parental buffering theory [57, 58], it is reasonable that if a parents’ level of anxiety is substantially lower than that of their child, the parents may lack the necessary insight and experiential understanding to fully comprehend the adolescent’s intense emotional state. It is also possible that this discrepancy stems partly from structural factors, such as reduced parent-adolescent contact or communication frequency. Limited interaction physically restricts the opportunities for parents to perceive and resonate with their child’s distress, creating a gap that manifests as statistical incongruence. This gap in emotional understanding can render the parent ill-equipped to offer the requisite emotional support and validation needed by the adolescent. Consequently, the adolescents’ intense emotional needs remain unmet and poorly managed. This unresolved anxious arousal in the adolescents may significantly elevate the risk of resorting to NSSI as an urgent coping mechanism. Furthermore, it is important to consider that adolescents engage in NSSI as a method to attract the attention of their parents. This behavior could be viewed as a cry for help or a way to communicate distress in a situation where they feel emotionally neglected or misunderstood. This aspect of NSSI highlights the complex interplay of communication, emotional needs, and coping strategies within parent-adolescent relationships. It underscores the necessity for parents to be attuned to their adolescents’ emotional states and responsive to their emotional signals, particularly when conventional communication methods fail to convey the depth of an adolescents’ emotional distress. Conversely, when parents exhibit substantially greater anxiety surrounding their adolescent’s emotional problems compared to the adolescents’ actual distress levels, this discrepancy can also augment the risk of self-harming behaviors. Drawing from family systems theory [59], disproportionately heightened parental anxiety regarding an adolescents’ mental health issues prompts various manifestations of over-involvement - such as excessive monitoring, advice-giving, or insistence on participating in all therapy activities [60]. Adolescence represents a critically sensitive phase, excessive anxiety and over-involvement from parents can overload adolescents’ finite coping resources lead to NSSI. Alternatively, intense resentment over inappropriate parental control could also trigger self-harm as an act of defiance or self-assertion. A clinical interview study by Fu et al. (2020) reinforces the finding that discrepancies in parent-adolescent attitudes toward NSSI elevate the risk of NSSI behaviors [61].We posit that such attitudinal discrepancies often underlie the emotional incongruence observed in our study. When parents and adolescents hold fundamentally different views regarding the meaning and function of NSSI, this creates a significant barrier to emotional congruence. For instance, an adolescent may engage in NSSI for an emotion-focused function (i.e., coping with overwhelming distress), whereas their parents may perceive the behavior primarily through a behavioral-focused attitude (i.e., attention-seeking or manipulative). This profound lack of emotional understanding fosters feelings of neglect or isolation in adolescents, which in turn reinforces the reliance on NSSI as a maladaptive strategy for affective regulation.

Conclusion

The present study examined the role of parent–adolescent congruence and incongruence in psychological distress in relation to adolescent NSSI using polynomial regression and response surface modeling. Results showed that higher levels of adolescent depression, anxiety, and stress were significantly associated with increased NSSI frequency. Beyond these individual effects, incongruence between parents’ and adolescents’ psychological distress emerged as a robust predictor of NSSI, with a U-shaped pattern indicating that extreme incongruence in distress levels posed the highest risk. Furthermore, parents’ anxiety demonstrated a nonlinear association with adolescent NSSI, where both insufficient and excessive parental anxiety were linked to greater engagement in self-injurious behaviors. Collectively, these findings highlight that both adolescents’ own psychological distress and the emotional alignment between parents and adolescents are critical in understanding NSSI among adolescents with mood disorders.

Clinical and intervention implications

The present findings carry important implications for future intervention research and clinical practice. First, the identification of emotional incongruence as a risk factor suggests that future interventions might benefit from targeting parent-adolescent emotional alignment, rather than focusing solely on individual symptom reduction. Potential approaches worthy of investigation include dyadic emotional regulation strategies, where parents and adolescents learn to recognize and communicate emotional states. Techniques such as emotion coaching, reflective listening exercises, and joint mindfulness practices represent promising directions for future research to explore whether they can help families develop synchronized emotional awareness. Second, clinicians might consider assessing parental emotional responsiveness patterns, though the present cross-sectional data do not establish causal mechanisms. Parent training programs could be developed and tested to examine whether calibrating emotional responsiveness—validating adolescent distress without becoming overwhelmed—reduces NSSI risk. Finally, our findings suggest the hypothesis that standard individual assessments may miss critical dyadic risk patterns. Future research should evaluate whether routinely assessing parent-child emotional congruence using parallel measures improves treatment outcomes.

Limitation

While the present study provides valuable insights into the relationship between parent-adolescent emotional incongruence and adolescent NSSI, it is important to acknowledge several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about the direction of the relationship between parent-adolescent emotional incongruence and adolescent NSSI. Second, the reliance on self-report measures may be subject to biases or inaccuracies in emotional awareness and reporting. Third, the focus on a clinical sample of adolescents with mood disorders limits the generalizability of the findings to community populations or other clinical groups. Fourth, the study did not explicitly assess cohabitation status or the frequency of daily interaction between parents and adolescents. Variations in the degree of contact could potentially influence the extent of emotional congruence, preventing us from controlling for this potential confounding factor. Fifth, the study did not systematically record which parent participated (mother vs. father), precluding examination of potential differences between maternal and paternal effects on adolescent NSSI. Parent sex may influence psychological distress levels and parent-adolescent interaction patterns. Finally, given that the clinical sample was exclusively recruited from hospitals in China, the observed associations between parent-adolescent emotional incongruence and adolescent NSSI may be modulated by cultural factors inherent to a Chinese context. For instance, traditional Chinese culture often emphasizes emotional restraint and implicit communication within the family, which may lead parents and adolescents to minimize or internalize distress. Future cross-cultural research is needed to determine whether these patterns replicate in other cultural settings and to clarify how culturally embedded communication norms shape parent–adolescent emotional dynamics. Future studies should also incorporate longitudinal designs and detailed assessments of family interaction patterns to better understand the mechanisms linking parent–adolescent incongruence to adolescent NSSI.
