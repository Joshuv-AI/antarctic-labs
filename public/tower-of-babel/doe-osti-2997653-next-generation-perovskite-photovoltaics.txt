 SECTION II: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 Major Goals and Objectives
 In the proposed program we plan to improve perovskite photovoltaic performance by developing
 (1) orientational control of 3D/2D perovskite heterostructures to simplify device architectures,
 thus improving device efficiencies and stability; (2) high-throughput optical measurements and
 real-time device simulations for device optimization; (3) robust, dual-pronged lead-sealing and
 oxygen/moisture/UV barrier films for long-term stability. Specifically, we seek to develop an in-
 depth understanding of the perovskite film formation, interface passivation, device stability, and
 environmentally friendly encapsulation, which together will lead to perovskite devices with PCE
 of over 28%, stability of T80 at 85/85 (85% humidity at 85 degrees C) for 10,000, expected to be
 equivalent to T90 of 100 hours), and architectures that would satisfy the U.S. EPA and RoHS limits
 of lead-leaching. The knowledge generated with this project will be applicable to tandem devices
 with wider-bandgap perovskites.

Impact:
The project focuses on increasing the PCE of solar cells based on 3D/2D perovskite
heterostructures toward the theoretical efficiency limit as well as developing interfacial
passivation and device encapsulation strategies to obtain perovskite solar cells with a lifetime
exceeding 20 years and meeting regulatory requirements. We first aim to gain a fundamental
understanding of the important parameters that can increase PCEs towards 28% which should
allow us to develop and apply novel strategies to increase the stability of perovskite PV and de-
risk perovskite PV for commercialization. Furthermore, the high throughput characterization
tools that we are developing will accelerate the advancement of perovskite technology. The
continued development of perovskite PV from MIT combined with our extensive experience with
taking new technology to commercial products presents a unique opportunity to bring
perovskites closer to commercialization. In addition to technological innovation and
development, the talent development with the proposed project will be significant. This
interdisciplinary project of material chemistry and electrical engineering will train researchers in
this highly interdisciplinary and promising new area in semiconductor research.

Five Significant Project Achievements:
1. Chemical bath deposition (CBD) is widely used to deposit tin oxide (SnOx) as an electron-
transport layer in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). In this work, a volatile oxalic acid (OA) is
introduced as an alternative to TGA. OA, a dicarboxylic acid, functions as a chemical linker for
the nucleation and attachment of particles to the substrate in the chemical bath. This rational design
of a CBD SnOx layer leads to devices with T85 ≈1500 h, a significant improvement over the TGA-
based device with T80 ≈250 h. The champion device reached a power conversion efficiency of
24.6%.

Y. Lu, et al., “Rational Design of a Chemical Bath Deposition Based Tin Oxide Electron-
Transport Layer for Perovskite Photovoltaics,” Adv. Mater. 35, 2304168 (2023).

2. We report bis(2-oxo-3-oxazolidinyl) phosphinic chloride (BOP-Cl) as a perovskite ink additive
with the following benefits: (1) The phosphoryl and two oxo groups form six-membered
intermolecular hydrogen-bonded rings with the formamidinium cation (FA), mitigating ion

4. We have successfully developed a solution-based interface engineering method for perovskite
solar cells that yields a pristine, phase-pure n = 2 2D/3D heterostructure, eliminating excess PbI₂
and mixed 2D phases. This approach enhances energy band alignment and surface passivation,
resulting in a 250% increase in surface diffusion coefficient and a 47% increase in carrier lifetime.
Devices fabricated with this strategy demonstrated improved stability (80% PCE retention over
1270 hours), negligible hysteresis, and a champion power conversion efficiency of 25.3%,
representing a significant advancement in scalable, high-performance perovskite photovoltaics.

M.-C. Shih, et al., “A 2D/3D Heterostructure Perovskite Solar Cell with a Phase-Pure and
Pristine 2D Layer,” Adv. Mater. 37, 2416672 (2025).

In this section we describe activities that were performed toward completion of tasks, subtasks,
and milestones outlined in the SOPO.

Project 1: Templated 2D perovskites formation on 3D perovskites
 We worked on a novel fabrication method to control the orientation of 2D perovskites on
3D perovskites. We found that the following factors contribute to affect the 2D perovskite
formation: (1) Intermediate phases at the early stages of 3D perovskite crystallization which
facilitate ion exchange to template the 2D perovskite growth. (2) The rigidity of 2D ligands (i.e
Dion-Jacobson versus Ruddlesden-Popper) which allow an ordered 2D formation. (3) Solvent-
induced (IPA) reconstruction of the 3D perovskite surface. We probed the paramter space of the
above mentioned factors using grazing incidence wide angle xray scattering.
 80+ samples with various conditions were screened for potential templated 2D formations
at Brookhaven National Lab. We investigated several different perovskite compositions including
pure FA, with 0.8%-5% MAPbBr3 and 0-5% Cs doping. Moreover, we looked at two types of 2D
ligands: hexylammonium bromide (RP) in chloroform and Hexane-1.6-diammonium bromide (DJ)
in 2-propanol (IPA). The results were consistent with our hypothesis. By depositing 1.6-
diammonium bromide (DJ) in 2-propanol (IPA) at early stage of the 3D perovskite formation (20
mins into annealing and 0.8% MAPbBr3), we found a new 2D perovskite phase (at 0.65 A-1)
formed with similar orientation distribution to that of bulk perovskites at 1 A-1. After 40 mins, a
different group of 2D perovskite phases formed at (0.36, 0.72 and 1.08 A-1) where the orientation
distributions are distinct from the 3D bulk. In between, a mixture of both 2D phases formed as
shown in Figure 1.1. These data fulfill the requirement of Milestone 1.1.2

 We further confirm the templated growth by characterizing the micro interface of the
heterostructure using HRTEM. Figure 1.4 shows an epitaxial growth of 2D perovskites on 3D
perovskites. By taking the FFT of the image, we identify 3D perovskite phases and 2D perovskite
phases. We observed that the surface of the 3D perovskite is partly converted into a 2D perovskite
phase. The 2D perovskites grow out of plane relative to the 3D perovskites, which can facilitate
charge transfer from the 3D to 2D perovskite.

 Figure 1.4 HRTEM image of the templated 3D/2D interface

 We used photoluminance measurements to investigate the charge separation at the 3D/2D
interface. By characterizing the novel heterostructure using power dependent photoluminescence,
we found that the PL shifts to 810 nm for the novel 3D/2D heterostructure while the conventional
treatment leads to a PL centered at 790 nm. Moreover, the increase of PL versus power has a slope
~ 1 while the bulk 3D perovskite has a slope of 1.44 in Figure 1.5. A power dependent slope of
1.44 in the control film indicates that the recombination is a mixture of free carrier bimolecular
and monomolecular recombination. In Figure 1.6a and c, we illustrate that the conventional 2D,
which has a type I alignment, blocks both types of carriers so that the majority of them recombine

Project 2: Stabilization of Organic Cations in Lead Halide Perovskite Solar Cells using
Phosphine Oxides Derivatives

  Figure 2.1. Summary of the degradation pathways related to the organic cations in a perovskite solar
  cell and mechanisms of BOP-Cl on stabilizing organic cations in a perovskite solar cell device.

 Most state-of-the-art PSCs utilize a formamidinium (FA) based A site lead halide
composition. However, ion migration and ionic loss of the organic cation trigger various
degradation pathways. Within the bulk perovskite, hydrogen bonding between the halide and the
proton of the ammonium ion in FA plays an important role in stabilizing crystal phases. However,
the strength of these interactions could be significantly varied at the surfaces depending on
different terminations of the crystal structure, leading to much more pronounced ion migrations at
the surface and grain boundary of the devices. Furthermore, the presence of an unprotected proton
on the ammonium group can adversely affect the chemical and phase stability of the perovskite
layer due to its acidity. In a PSC device, extrinsic reactions can happen at the interface between
the metal oxide charge transport layer and perovskites, leading to the deprotonation of ammonium
groups to volatile amines and the subsequent formation of lead iodides.
 Phosphine oxide derivatives are widely used in perovskite LEDs because oxygen can
donate electron density to the exposed Pb2+ ions and passivate surface non-radiative recombination
pathways. Similarly, the electron-rich oxygen can also form hydrogen bonds with protons in the
ammonium group of FA, which is often understated. Our previous work utilized
triphenylphosphine oxide in the device because of its benefits in reducing metastable behavior
(e.g. initial burn-in at timescale of minutes to hours) and improving the long-term stability of the
solar cell. Because cation migration can cause an initial burn-in of the device under reverse bias
(i.e. at flat band condition), we therefore pivot to believe that interactions between the phosphine
oxide and the organic cations (and not the exposed Pb2+ ions) are the dominating cause of this
improvement in a solar cell device.

Project 3: New simplified device architecture based on stable, dopant-free spiro-OMeTAD
 Conventional spiro-OMeTAD hole transporting layer is a highly complex mixture as it has
to be doped with various additives and dopants to oxidize pristine neutral spiro-OMeTAD (spiro0)
to increase its hole conductivity. These dopants include lithium
bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), cobalt bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide
(CoTFSI) salts, 4-tert-Butylpyridine, and acetonitrile. These dopants are the primary reason
underlying the well-known instability issues of spiro-OMeTAD. The dopants are not only
hydroscopic, but they significantly decrease the glass transition temperature of spiro-OMeTAD
from 110 °C of pristine spiro-OMeTAD to ~55 °C of doped spiro-OMeTAD. This also explains
the thermal instability of devices utilizing spiro-OMeTAD operated above 55 °C. Moreover, the
diffusion of the Li+ and Co2+ cations towards the perovskite interface are potential reasons for
many abnormal degradation behaviors experienced by devices utilizing spiro-OMeTAD, such as
initial burn in and hysteresis behavior.
 The above disadvantages motivated us to simplify the conventional spiro-OMeTAD recipe.
We first synthesized pre-oxidized spiro2+, and by precisely controlling the proportion of oxidized
spiro2+ to pristine spiro0, we are able to achieve a dopant-free spiro-OMeTAD hole transporting
layer that functioned without the need for any additional of the dopants mentioned above. In
addition, we further incorporated ionic liquid additives based on bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide
anions to tune the work function and band alignment between the spiro-OMeTAD and perovskite
layer.
 We fabricated devices based on the regular n-i-p architecture (Figure 1(a)) incorporated
with the new dopant-free spiro-OMeTAD. Three separate batches were fabricated on three
different days (March 2nd, March 15th, March 21st). Figures 1(b)-(e) plot the distribution of the
photovoltaic parameters of the devices. The devices were not only highly efficient, but were also
reproducible batch-to-batch, with average PCEs of 25.0±0.1% (March 2nd batch), 24.8±0.3%
(March 15th batch), and 24.9±0.2% (March 21st batch). The device photovoltaic parameters across
the three different batches are the same within statistical uncertainty, fulfilling the goal of GNG-
2C. Furthermore, the device active area is ~10 mm2, larger than the 1 mm2 goal of the GNG.

Project 4: Simplified and Stable Electron Transport Layer Based on Chemical Bath
Deposition of SnO2

 Dense and conformal SnO2 electron transport layers (ETLs) deposited by chemical bath
deposition (CBD) have led to several record efficiency perovskite solar cells (PSC). CBD-grown
layers, however, may contain ligand contaminants from the growth reaction, or unwanted
impurities from the growth solution, and these may reduce device stability. As a result, the most
stable n-i-p device structures are based on commercial SnO2 colloidal nanoparticles. Improving
the long-term stability of CBD grown tin oxide-based PSCs requires understanding the function
of each component in the chemical bath reaction and the potential role of these components in
device degradation processes.
 To date, all high efficiency CBD based tin oxide devices contain the following four
components: SnCl2-2H2O, HCl, urea, and thioglycolic acid (TGA), a composition which can be
traced back to the fabrication of tin oxide electrodes for lithium-ion battery. Fluorine doped tin
oxide (FTO) is preferred as the substrate because it is chemically compatible with the bath solution
and functions as a natural seed layer to ensure a conformal and adherent deposition. Previous
studies show the growth of the film is dominated by the attachment of nanocrystalline particles
formed in the solution to the substrate. Thus, the essential part of this reaction is to control the
kinetics of the tin oxide growth so that heterogeneous attachment of particles onto the substrate is
favored over homogeneous precipitation in the solution.
 To deposit tin oxide selectively on the substrate, the initial pH of the bath solution is kept
low enough by optimizing HCl concentration so that the hydrolysis of the metal ions is retarded.
A slow and controlled hydrolysis is induced by the decomposition of urea at an elevated
temperature. The reaction is stopped when bulk precipitation is observed; that is when particle
sizes are too large to be incorporated into the film. During the deposition window, conditions for
heterogeneous growth need to be optimized so that a film of desired thickness can be grown during
the process. An essential consideration is that the attachment of particles formed in solution is
affected by colloidal forces including van der Waals and electrostatic forces. Since the
isoelectric point of SnO2 is ~5.5-6.0, the film deposition is impeded by positive charges on the tin
oxide particles and FTO surface at low pH. Capping ligands containing negatively charged
carboxylate group like thioglycolic acid (TGA) can bind strongly to nanoparticles, neutralize
charges and facilitate their attachment to the FTO substrate, leading to a dense and conformal
nanoparticle coating. In the field of dye sensitized solar cells, TGA was widely reported to act as
a dual functional linker in the chemical bath deposition of quantum dots (QDs) on zinc oxide
surface, where the thiol group was chosen to facilitate binding of CdS QDs to zin oxide. There is,
however, no obvious reason for including a thiol functional group for tin oxide CBD, considering
the weaker interaction between sulfur and tin compared to oxygen and tin. This statement is

Page 25 of 54

supported by the fact that bond dissociation energy between tin and sulfur (464 KJ/mol)[12] is
smaller than that of tin and oxygen (548 KJ/mol). Moreover, TGA is known to cause pinholes and
defects in the perovskite layer which negatively affects the long term stability of the devices. Due
to the nonvolatile nature of TGA, we infer that TGA as a chemical impurity may remain at the tin
oxide/perovskite interface after thermal annealing. Following this logic, we suggest that a volatile
dicarboxylic acid is a better alternative to TGA by ensuring connectivity between individual tin
oxide nanoparticles and allowing efficient attachment of nanoparticles to the substrate.
Furthermore, long term stability of the device may be improved if organic residues can be fully
removed by post treatments at a relatively low temperature.
 Here, we report the use of the volatile and tin-chelating linker molecule oxalic acid (OA)
as a replacement to the conventional TGA ligand. We conclude that the ditopic carboxylic acid
OA can chemically link the substrate to the particles in the bath solution and accelerate subsequent
condensation of colloidal particles. Moreover, volatile OA (sublimation temperature 157 °C) can
be removed by thermal annealing and a mild surface treatment with H2O2. The surface oxygen
vacancies of the SnOx layer are synergistically selectively passivated. The rational design of the
ligand in the chemical bath plus the controlled oxidation of the surface tin oxide layer leads to
improved long-term device stability (T85 = 1500 hours) compared to control devices with TGA
CBD tin oxide (T80 = 250 hours). The champion device using OA reached a power conversion
efficiency of 24.6% (EOP-A).

Project 5: 2D/3D interface reconstruction for perovskite solar cells
 In the conventional 2D/3D perovskite passivation method, previously published works
encountered challenges such as an impure 2D perovskite phase, a wide bandgap type I band
alignment and a residual PbI2. To solve these challenges, we propose and test a new 2D/3D
interface reconstruction method to grow the 2D perovskite. The procedure and the in-situ GIWAX
result of the method are shown in Figures 5.1.a and 1.b, respectively. First, instead of loading the
optimized amount of the 2D ligand on top of the 3D perovskite, we overload the 2D ligand amount.
The excess of the 2D ligand forces most of the residual PbI2 to react with the 2D ligand and form
the 2D perovskite. As shown in Figure 5.1.b, most of the PbI2 disappears after overloading, and
two 2D perovskite phases (n=1 and n=2) form after heating. Second, to remove the excess 2D
ligand and control the 2D perovskite thickness, we use isopropanol (IPA) to wash the 2D
perovskite surface. As shown in Figure 5.1.b, we are effective in washing away n=1 2D perovskite
and keeping just the right amount of n=2 2D perovskite on the surface. Finally, we anneal the film
to form a more crystalline 2D perovskite structure.

 Figure 5.1. a. The procedure of the 2D/3D interface reconstruction method.
 b. The corresponding in-situ GIWAX data for the 2D/3D interface reconstruction method.

 To further understand the mechanism of the interface reconstruction, we compared the
static XRD spectrum of several methods for forming the perovskite films: the conventional 2D
deposit method (2D control), 2D control washed with IPA, and the interface reconstruction
method, as illustrated in Figures 5.2a and 5.2b. After washing the 2D control film with IPA, the

Page 28 of 54

2D perovskite on the film is washed away, and there is additional residual PbI2, as shown in Figure
5.2a. This is because the 2D perovskite film in the 2D control sample is one thin layer, and IPA
can dissolve both the 2D perovskite and the organic molecules. Therefore, IPA can wash away all
the 2D perovskite and also some of the methylammonium halide and formamidine halide inside
the 3D perovskite, which then exposes more PbI2. In contrast, after the interface reconstruction
method, we can simultaneously remove most of the PbI2 and n=1 2D perovskite. In this method
we obtain just the right amount of n=2 2D perovskite on top, as shown in Figure 5.2b. The key
difference between the 2D control washed with IPA and the interface reconstruction is the
overloading step. The overloading step provides two functions for the interface reconstruction
method: First, because of the overloading of the 2D ligand, we make PbI2 become a limiting
reagent and the 2D ligand becomes an excess reagent, which then forces all the PbI2 to form the
2D perovskite with the 2D ligand. Second, because of the overloading, the formed 2D perovskite
is very thick, with the n=1 2D perovskite and extra 2D ligand on the top surface and the n=2 2D
perovskite on the bottom. The top surface of the 2D perovskite serves as a protection layer for the
bottom layer during the washing. After washing, we wash away the top surface and finally keep
just the right amount of n=2 2D perovskite remaining. Therefore, after the interface reconstruction,
we can simultaneously remove most of the PbI2 and obtain phase pure n=2 2D perovskite phase,
as summarized in Figure 5.2c.

Project 7: Modeling Techniques to Screen Novel Perovskite Formulations for Improved PV
Performance
 Our first work explored physics-based models as we detailed in Brenes et al. (Phys. Rev.
Applied 12, 014017 (2019)). Briefly, the model considers losses due to bulk Shockley-Read-Hall
(SRH) and Auger recombination, as well as incorporating gains due to photon recycling. This helps
to assess the maximum potential power-conversion efficiency (PCE) for perovskite thin films. In
Fig. 7.1 we show fits the two intensity-dependent TRPL data collected on FAPb(IxBr1-x)3 films
passivated with hexylammonium bromide. Through the recombination rate constants extracted
from these fits, we determined that these passivated films have a maximum potential PCE ~28%
while the devices have achieved maximum efficiencies of ~25%, indicating small amounts of loss
due to the presence of either transport loss during charge extraction or interfacial recombination.

 Figure 7.1. Intensity dependent TRPL data taken at carrier densities 1014 cm-3 (blue), 1015 cm-3 (orange)
  and 1016 cm-3 (green). The solid lines represent fits to the data using a rate equation considering SRH,
 radiative and Auger recombination.

 This initial model has limitations for predicting the actual device operation, since it does
not incorporate interfacial recombination between layers in the solar cell stack, band-bending at
interfaces and internal electric fields developed due to the charge extraction layers and charge
transport losses. These non-idealities could be included using a drift-diffusion model and using
fits to experimental data to determine the relevant simulation parameters. Nevertheless, the number
of free parameters in these models require extensive measurements and fitting models that hinders
their usefulness as a predictive tool.
 Therefore, we began exploring the use of machine learning to take the measured physical
parameters of the perovskite layers within a solar cell structure as input to directly predict the JV
curve, thereby predicting the cell efficiency performance. These “Blackbox models” allow us to
potentially predict the performance of full devices with data that might contain many physical
processes that could be too complex to predict accurately with an analytical model. Hence, we

Project 8: Device Stability and Lifetime Correlation Under Different Stressors and
Environments

 This section relates to our studies on how the device stability is affected by different
stressors (humidity, temperature, and light intensity), and to determine the correlation between the
stress condition and device lifetime. Encouraged by the promising stability results discussed in
Project 5 above, all devices now reported in this section are based on our new simplified spiro-
MeOTAD hole-transporting layer.
 In terms of thermal stability, we heated our unencapsulated devices at different
temperatures of 25°C, 45°C, 65°C, and 85°C inside a nitrogen glovebox. Periodically before each
I-V measurement, the devices would be cooled first at room temperature in the glovebox. Figure
8.1a plots the average PCEs (absolute values) of 4 devices each at the different temperatures except
for 85°C (2 best devices). No obvious degradation was observed for over 500 hours for the devices
heated at 25°C, 45°C, and 65°C. On average, only the devices aged at 85 °C showed noticeable
degradation. Therefore, the results in Figure 8.1a indicate that there seems to be no correlation
between temperature and device stability (Milestone 1.2.4).
 Inspecting the 85°C aged devices individually in Figure 8.1b, there seems to be a large
variation in stability among each of the 4 devices. We further observed “white spots” dotting after
thermal aging (Figure 8.1c). We suspect that the “white spots” are some forms of impurity (and/or
pinhole) that was already originally present within the fresh devices (possibly introduced during
fabrication) and was responsible for catalyzing/accelerating the device degradation under thermal
stress. The gold diffusion into devices, for example, would be accelerated because of the pinhole.
This intrinsic thermal stability of our new simplified spiro-MeOTAD might be still sufficient to
withstand heating at 85 °C. To further verify the argument, we are currently testing 16 of our
simplified spiro structure devices made using TPPO as a barrier layer under 85 °C. The champion
device as shown in green maintains its initial efficiency (~20%) after 225 hours of aging. On
average, these devices retain 92% of its initial efficiency after 225 hours of aging (Figure 8.2),
which shows some improvement in stability. Taken together, this suggests a way forward for us to
achieve GNG-2B.

Project 9: Lead Sealing Barrier Layers

 The lead sealing barrier film is based on inexpensive, non-toxic polymers and calcium
phosphate, as listed in Figure 9.1(a). The component materials are poly(methyl methacrylate-co-
methacrylic acid) (PMMA), poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO), butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) binder,
and calcium phosphate salt. The barrier film is designed to convert dissolved Pb2+ into more stable,
less soluble forms, preventing Pb from leaching out into the surrounding environment from
damaged perovskite solar cells. A cation exchange reaction takes place between Ca2+ from the
calcium phosphate in the barrier film and Pb2+ in solution to form less soluble lead phosphate.
 Photographs of the devices deposited with the barrier film (yellow colored) are shown in
Figure 9.1(b). We first assessed whether the barrier film had any impact on the device
performance and stability. Figures 9.1(c) and 9.1(d) respectively show the absolute and
normalized efficiency of the devices under maximum power point tracking at AM1.5G continuous
illumination. The control device (without barrier film) had an initial PCE of ~22%. With the
addition of the barrier film, the efficiency dropped to ~21%. However, the device with barrier film
is observed to be more stable under maximum power point tracking, retaining ~72% of its initial
PCE after 220h, as compared to ~64% PCE retention for the control device. This is a surprising
result, and we hypothesize that either the thickness of the film is preventing or increasing the water
or atmospheric diffusion path length or that the phosphates present in the lead barrier are minorly
passivating the perovskite layer in the device itself.

 The Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP) was performed according to the

 The same devices that underwent the maximum power point tracking in Figure 9.1(c), (d)

 The ideal chemical barrier film for perovskite solar cells should be robust and able to
effectively capture and contain Pb even after significant weathering. We then aged our devices
with barrier film to the damp heat double 85°C/85% relative humidity (RH) test for 50 h in the
dark. These devices utilized our new dopant-free spiro-MeOTAD at the hole-transporting layer.
As shown in Figure 9.2(a), the aged device with the barrier film had a leached Pb content of

2E). In summary, the results presented in Figure 9.2 collectively fulfill Milestone 1.3.2, 1.3.3,

 On a final note, the barrier film is a cheap solution and consists of readily available
materials, as shown in Figure 9.1(a), with a cost of $0.002 per gram based on bulk purchasing
from commercial suppliers. For example, for a 15% power conversion efficient solar panel, this
would equate to a materials cost of $0.002 W-1 for a 50 μm thick barrier film, much lower than the
$0.33 W-1 hazardous waste disposal cost for glass perovskite modules. Moreover, the barrier film
is also intrinsically flexible and therefore compatible with flexible solar cell applications.
