Sex-related differences in pathological characteristics of colorectal cancer: a retrospective study
Authors: Siqi Lan, Zhigang Mao, Deng Tang, Yali Song, Si Chen, Yuemei Chen, Mi Su, Chengyi Zhu, Ruiting Yan, Ji Zhang, Yufang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeColorectal cancer (CRC) exhibits substantial sex-related differences in incidence, prognosis, and clinicopathological features. However, existing evidence on these disparities remains limited and inconclusive. This study aimed to investigate sex-specific patterns in tumor differentiation, metastasis, and mismatch repair (MMR) status, and to determine whether these associations persist after adjustment for clinicopathological covariates.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted in 2,912 patients who underwent surgery for CRC at West China Hospital between November 2008 and June 2023. Sex differences were assessed across subgroups stratified by tumor differentiation grade, metastatic status, and age at onset. Three multivariable Firth penalized logistic regression models were constructed to assess whether sex remained an independent predictor of metastasis, deficient MMR (dMMR), and poor differentiation (G3) after adjustment for age, body mass index (BMI), stage, tumor location, and relevant molecular markers.ResultsSignificant sex-related differences were observed in tumor differentiation, metastasis, and MMR status. Female patients had higher rates of poorly differentiated (G3) tumors, dMMR, and metastasis. However, these differences were markedly heterogeneous and were most evident in moderately differentiated (G2) tumors, late-onset CRC, and non-metastatic disease, whereas no significant sex differences were identified in early-onset disease. In multivariable analysis, female sex remained an independent predictor of metastasis (adjusted OR 1.36, 95% CI 1.04–1.79, P = 0.024), but not of dMMR (adjusted OR 1.28, P = 0.108) or G3 differentiation (adjusted OR 1.15, P = 0.146), suggesting that the latter associations were largely explained by tumor location.ConclusionSex-related differences in CRC are context-dependent and vary according to tumor differentiation, metastatic status, and age at diagnosis. The higher metastatic risk observed in female patients appears to be an independent association, whereas differences in dMMR and tumor grade mainly reflect the greater prevalence of right-sided tumors in women. These findings support the incorporation of sex as a biological variable in metastatic risk stratification, while highlighting the importance of accounting for anatomical confounding when interpreting molecular and histological sex differences.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05472-5.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Sex differences, Clinicopathological features, Metastasis, Differentiation, Mismatch repair status

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) exhibits substantial sex-related differences in incidence, mortality, and clinicopathological presentation. Globally, age-standardized incidence and mortality rates are higher in men than in women, at 23.4 versus 16.2 per 100,000 and 11 versus 7.2 per 100,000, respectively [1]. However, these epidemiological patterns appear to vary by age at diagnosis. In particular, early-onset CRC (diagnosed before 50 years of age) shows a more balanced sex distribution, with females accounting for approximately 50.7% of cases, compared with 34.2% in late-onset CRC [2]. This pattern suggests that sex-related disparities in CRC may arise through partly distinct biological or clinical pathways in younger and older patients.

Sex differences are also evident in tumor location and molecular features. Female patients are more likely to develop right-sided colon tumors, which are more frequently associated with deficient mismatch repair (dMMR), microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) phenotype, and BRAF mutations [3, 4]. Population-based studies have further shown that women more often present with poorly differentiated or undifferentiated tumors and have a higher prevalence of dMMR than men [5]. In contrast, male patients more commonly develop left-sided tumors, which are more often linked to the chromosomal instability pathway and may show greater sensitivity to anti-EGFR therapy [6]. These differences are clinically relevant because dMMR/MSI-H tumors are more likely to respond to immune checkpoint inhibitors, whereas proficient mismatch repair tumors are generally managed with conventional systemic strategies.

Despite these observations, the current anatomical-molecular framework does not fully explain sex-related variation in CRC [3–6]. Most previous studies have focused primarily on tumor location and mismatch repair (MMR) status, with less attention to whether sex differences vary according to differentiation grade, metastatic status, or age at onset. As a result, it remains unclear whether the reported disparities are driven mainly by anatomical distribution or reflect broader sex-associated differences in tumor behavior.

To address these gaps, a retrospective analysis was performed in 2,912 patients with histologically confirmed CRC. Tumor differentiation, anatomical location, metastatic status, and MMR status were systematically compared between male and female patients, with additional stratification by age at onset, differentiation degree, and metastatic status. The aim of this study was to characterize context-dependent sex-related pathological patterns beyond the established anatomical-molecular associations and to provide evidence to support more refined risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (No. 2023 − 958). Patients diagnosed with CRC at West China Hospital between November 2008 and June 2023 were identified from a prospectively maintained database. Case retrieval was based on diagnostic keywords including colon, rectum, tumor, neoplasm, cancer, and dysplasia.

Data collection and case selection

Patient information was retrieved using unique identification numbers and included demographic variables such as age, sex, height and weight. Histopathological and clinicopathological data were obtained from the pathology records, including histopathological type, tumor size, tumor grade, inflammatory status, metastasis, vascular invasion, lymph node status, perineural invasion, T stage, N stage, M stage, and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining results.

MMR status was determined by IHC staining for MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2 on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections. Staining results were independently reviewed by two experienced gastrointestinal pathologists who were blinded to clinical information. Loss of nuclear staining in tumor cells for any of the four proteins was classified as deficient mismatch repair (dMMR), whereas intact nuclear staining for all four proteins was classified as proficient mismatch repair (pMMR).

Metastasis was defined as the presence of distant metastatic disease (M1) documented in clinical records, including synchronous metastases identified preoperatively by imaging or intraoperatively, as well as cases undergoing concurrent resection of metastatic lesions. TNM stage was assigned according to the pathological findings of the colorectal specimen based on the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system.

Cases with discordant or non-CRC primary tumors, including neuroendocrine carcinoma, lymphoma, melanoma, endometrial cancer, thyroid cancer, gastrointestinal stromal tumors, were excluded. After manual review, 23,414 patients were identified in the source population, from which 2,912 patients with colorectal adenocarcinoma, surgical records, and complete pathological data were included in the final study cohort.

Variable definitions

Tumor stage was classified according to the 8th edition of the AJCC TNM staging system. Tumor differentiation was categorized as well differentiated (G1), moderately differentiated (G2), and poorly differentiated (G3).

The primary tumor location was classified as right-sided colon, left-sided colon, or rectum. Right-sided colon tumors were defined as those located proximal to the splenic flexure, including the cecum, ascending colon, and transverse colon. Left-sided colon tumors were defined as those located from the splenic flexure to the sigmoid colon. Rectal tumors were classified separately.

Body mass index (BMI) was categorized into four groups according to World Health Organization criteria: ≤18, 18–24.9, 25–29.9, and ≥ 30 kg/m². Age at diagnosis was dichotomized as early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC), defined as CRC diagnosed before 50 years of age, and late-onset colorectal cancer (LOCRC), defined as CRC diagnosed at or after the age of 50.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R x64 version 3.5.1. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, Anderson-Darling test, D’Agostino-Pearson test, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and visual inspection of normal Q-Q plots. Consistent evidence of non-normality across formal tests and graphical assessment supported the use of nonparametric methods (Supplementary Figure S1). Detailed normality test statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and were compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All tests were two-sided. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

To determine whether the observed sex-related differences were independent of potential confounders, three multivariable Firth penalized logistic regression models were constructed. Firth penalized logistic regression was used to reduce small-sample bias and to address potential separation in subgroup analyses. Sex (female vs. male) was the primary exposure variable in all models.

Model 1 evaluated metastasis (yes vs. no) as the dependent variable and included age (continuous), BMI (continuous), T stage, N stage, tumor differentiation grade, MMR status (pMMR, unknown, dMMR), and tumor location as covariates.

Model 2 evaluated dMMR status (dMMR vs. pMMR) as the dependent variable. Cases with unknown MMR status (n = 105) were excluded from this model. Covariates included age (continuous), BMI (continuous), T stage, N stage, tumor location, and tumor differentiation grade.

Model 3 evaluated poor differentiation (G3 vs. G1/G2) as the dependent variable and included age (continuous), BMI (continuous), T stage, N stage, tumor location, and MMR status (pMMR, unknown, dMMR) as covariates.

Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P-value. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), and model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 23,414 patients were initially screened, of whom 2,912 patients with colorectal adenocarcinoma, surgical records, and complete pathological data were included in the final analysis. The median age of the study cohort was 58.0 years (IQR 49–69), and 1,194 patients (41.0%) were female. Most tumors were moderately differentiated (G2, 76.8%), located in the rectum (50.9%), non-metastatic (88.3%), and pMMR (88.8%). Multivariate logistic regression analyses

In Model 2, with dMMR (vs. pMMR) as the outcome, female sex was not independently associated with dMMR after multivariable adjustment (adjusted OR 1.28, 95% CI 0.95–1.73, P = 0.108). This analysis included 2,807 patients after excluding cases with unknown MMR status.

In Model 3, with poor differentiation (G3 vs. G1/G2) as the outcome, female sex was not an independent predictor of poorly differentiated histology (adjusted OR 1.15, 95% CI 0.95–1.40, P = 0.146).

Taken together, these findings indicate that the higher prevalence of metastasis in female patients represents an independent association, whereas the unadjusted female predominance of dMMR and G3 tumors appears to be largely explained by tumor location and other clinicopathological covariates. Model 1 showed good discrimination (AUC 0.878, 95% CI 0.862–0.894) and excellent calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow χ² = 3.15, df = 8, P = 0.925, Fig. 1).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 2,912 patients with CRC, we found that sex-related differences in clinicopathological features were present but highly context-dependent. In univariate analyses, female patients had higher rates of metastasis, deficient mismatch repair (dMMR), and poorly differentiated (G3) tumors. However, after multivariable adjustment, only the association between female sex and metastasis remained significant. By contrast, the associations with dMMR and poor differentiation were attenuated, suggesting that these differences were largely explained by tumor location and other clinicopathological factors.

These findings refine current understanding of sex differences in CRC. Previous studies have shown that women are more likely to develop right-sided tumors and dMMR/MSI-H disease, whereas men more often present with left-sided tumors [3–6]. Our results are broadly consistent with this framework, but further show that these differences are not uniform across all disease contexts. In stratified analyses, the excess metastatic burden in female patients was most evident in the G2 subgroup, whereas no significant sex differences were detected in G1 or G3 tumors. Likewise, sex differences in tumor differentiation and MMR status were mainly observed in non-metastatic CRC and were not evident in metastatic disease. These patterns suggest that sex-related variation may be more apparent in intermediate or less advanced disease states, while in biologically aggressive tumors such effects may be obscured by dominant progression-related mechanisms.

The multivariable analyses further clarified the nature of these associations. Female sex remained independently associated with metastasis after adjustment for age, BMI, T stage, N stage, tumor differentiation, MMR status, and tumor location. In contrast, female sex was no longer independently associated with dMMR or G3 differentiation. This finding supports the interpretation that the higher unadjusted prevalence of dMMR and poorly differentiated tumors in female patients is largely attributable to anatomical distribution, particularly the higher frequency of right-sided tumors in women, rather than an independent sex-specific effect [3–5]. By comparison, the higher metastatic propensity observed in female patients appeared to be more robust.

Another notable finding was the attenuation of sex-related differences in EOCRC. In this subgroup, BMI was the only variable that differed significantly between males and females. In contrast, LOCRC showed significant sex differences in metastasis, tumor differentiation, and MMR status. This pattern suggests that sex-related disparities may be more pronounced in older patients, consistent with prior work indicating that the epidemiology and biology of EOCRC differ from those of late-onset disease [2]. Whether this reflects age-related biological changes, differences in hormonal milieu, or other clinical factors remains uncertain.

The biological basis of these findings was not directly examined in the present study. Hormonal mechanisms have been proposed as one possible explanation for sex differences in CRC, particularly in relation to postmenopausal changes and tumor biology [7, 8]. However, our data do not allow direct testing of these hypotheses, and any mechanistic interpretation should therefore be considered speculative. Similarly, although female patients showed a higher unadjusted prevalence of dMMR, this association was no longer significant after accounting for tumor location, suggesting that anatomical distribution is the principal explanatory pathway [9–12].

In addition to biological factors, sociobehavioral and healthcare-related determinants may contribute to the observed differences. Women are often reported to have higher participation in CRC screening programs than men, but right-sided lesions may present with less specific symptoms, such as anemia or fatigue, which can delay recognition and diagnosis [3, 4, 13, 14]. Men, in contrast, may show lower healthcare utilization overall, yet left-sided tumors more often cause obstructive or bleeding symptoms that prompt earlier evaluation [9, 14]. Because our retrospective dataset did not include detailed information on screening history, symptom duration, or diagnostic delay, these unmeasured factors should be recognized as potential confounders.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, long-term survival and recurrence data were not sufficiently complete for robust prognostic analysis. Third, the mechanisms underlying the observed sex differences were not directly investigated. Fourth, metastasis was defined from clinical documentation, whereas TNM stage was assigned on the basis of pathological examination of the primary resection specimen; this may have introduced some inconsistency in retrospective data integration.

In conclusion, sex-related differences in CRC were evident in this cohort but varied according to disease context. Female patients showed higher unadjusted prevalences of metastasis, dMMR, and poorly differentiated tumors, but only metastasis remained independently associated with sex after adjustment. Differences in dMMR and tumor grade appear to be largely explained by tumor location, particularly the higher frequency of right-sided tumors in women. These findings support the consideration of sex as a relevant variable in metastatic risk assessment while underscoring the importance of accounting for anatomical and clinicopathological confounding when interpreting sex-related differences in molecular and histological features.
