1.1

For
b\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathrm{Re}b>0,
or equivalently with
\arg b\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}),
let
S_{b}
be the
double sine function
given by

S_{b}(z)=\exp\Bigg(\int_{\Omega}\frac{\sinh\Big(\big(\frac{Q}{2}-z\big)t\Big)}{4t\sinh(\frac{bt}{2})\sinh(\frac{t}{2b})}dt\Bigg),

where
Q=b+b^{-1}
and the contour
\Omega

with
0<\mathrm{Re}z<\mathrm{Re}Q,
defining a holomorphic function in such

For a six-tuple of complex numbers

\begin{split}&t_{1}=a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3},\quad t_{2}=a_{1}+a_{5}+a_{6},\quad t_{3}=a_{2}+a_{4}+a_{6},\quad t_{4}=a_{3}+a_{4}+a_{5},\\

Then the six-tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{6})\in\mathbb{C}^{6}

for all
i,j\in\{1,2,3,4\}.

In particular, if
(a_{1},\dots,a_{6})

-admissible, then

\max\{\mathrm{Re}t_{1},\mathrm{Re}t_{2},\mathrm{Re}t_{3},\mathrm{Re}t_{4}\}<\min\{\mathrm{Re}q_{1},\mathrm{Re}q_{2},\mathrm{Re}q_{3},\mathrm{Re}q_{4}\}.

6j

Let

Theorem 1.3
.

Let

be the dihedral angles of a truncated hyperideal hyperbolic tetrahedron
\Delta.
Then as
b\to 0

(1)

with a fixed
\arg b\in[-\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}],

(2)

with a fixed
\arg b\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},-\frac{\pi}{4})\cup(\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{2})

satisfying the Geometric Hypothesis

,

we have

\bigg\{\begin{matrix}\frac{Q}{2}\pm\frac{\theta_{1}}{2\pi b}&\frac{Q}{2}\pm\frac{\theta_{2}}{2\pi b}&\frac{Q}{2}\pm\frac{\theta_{3}}{2\pi b}\\
\frac{Q}{2}\pm\frac{\theta_{4}}{2\pi b}&\frac{Q}{2}\pm\frac{\theta_{5}}{2\pi b}&\frac{Q}{2}\pm\frac{\theta_{6}}{2\pi b}\end{matrix}\bigg\}_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{e^{-\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(\Delta)}{\pi b^{2}}}}{\sqrt[4]{-\det\mathrm{Gram}(\Delta)}}\Big(1+O\big(b^{2}\big)\Big),

where in each entry of the
b
-
6j
symbol the sign

or
-
can be chosen arbitrarily,
\mathrm{Vol}(\Delta)
is the hyperbolic volume of
\Delta

Remark 1.4
.

Numerical computations suggest that a very large portion of the dihedral angles

\big|F(u)\big|<K^{8}e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{\pi}{2}\mathrm{Im}\big((u-t_{i})(u-t_{i}-Q)\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{4}\frac{\pi}{2}\mathrm{Im}\big((q_{j}-u)(q_{j}-u-Q)\big)}=K^{8}M^{-1}e^{-2\pi\mathrm{Re}Qt},

As a consequence, the integral in the proposition converges absolutely .

Next, we show that the integral is independent of the choice of the vertical line
\Gamma.
Suppose
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are two vertical lines passing the interval
(\max\{\mathrm{Re}t_{i}\},\min\{\mathrm{Re}q_{j}\}).
Let

be sufficiently large, and for

We also let

be the piece of the horizontal line

lying in between
\Gamma_{1,+}
and
\Gamma_{2,+},
and let

be the piece of the horizontal line

\Gamma_{1,-}
and

\Gamma_{1,+},

\Gamma_{1,-},

\Gamma_{2,+},

\Gamma_{2,-},

Then by the analyticity of

as

As the integrals over

\Gamma_{2,+}
and

To this end, let

(2.12)

Let
L>0
be sufficiently large so that for each

for each

and for each

Let
\Gamma_{+}=\{u\in\Gamma\ |\ \mathrm{Im}u\geqslant L\}

Then by Lemma

From (

Proposition 2.2

(Tetrahedral symmetry)

\begin{Bmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
a_{4}&a_{5}&a_{6}\end{Bmatrix}_{b}=\begin{Bmatrix}a_{2}&a_{1}&a_{3}\\

-

(Reflection symmetry)
.

If
(a_{1},\dots,a_{6})
is
b
-admissible, then
(Q-a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{6})
is also
b
-admissible, and

\bigg\{\begin{matrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\

Moreover, the same result holds if we change

for any

Proof.

The
b
-admissibility part follows verbatim that of
[
13
, Proposition 3.4]
. The reflection symmetry is proved in
[
13
, Proposition 3.4]
for all real

and the case for a complex

follows immediately from the analytic dependence on

stated in Proposition
1.2
.
∎

The following Proposition

states the behavior of the complex

-
6j

and the index

Proposition 2.4

(Complex conjugation)
.

(\overline{a_{1}},\dots,\overline{a_{6}})
is
\overline{b}
-admissible if and only if
(a_{1},\dots,a_{6})
is
b
-admissible, and

\bigg\{\begin{matrix}\overline{a_{1}}&\overline{a_{2}}&\overline{a_{3}}\\
\overline{a_{4}}&\overline{a_{5}}&\overline{a_{6}}\end{matrix}\bigg\}_{\overline{b}}=\overline{\bigg\{\begin{matrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
a_{4}&a_{5}&a_{6}\end{matrix}\bigg\}_{b}}.

Proof.

For
i,j\in\{1,2,3,4\},

0<\mathrm{Re}(\overline{q_{j}}-\overline{t_{i}})<\mathrm{Re}\overline{Q}

if and only if
0<\mathrm{Re}(q_{j}-t_{i})<\mathrm{Re}Q,
hence
(\overline{a_{1}},\dots,\overline{a_{6}})
is
\overline{b}
-admissible if and only if
(a_{1},\dots,a_{6})
is
b
-admissible.

To show the equality, we first claim that for
b\in\mathbb{C}
with
\arg b\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
that is not a pole of
S_{b}(z),

S_{\overline{b}}(\overline{z})=\overline{S_{b}(z)}.

(2.16)

Indeed, let
F_{z,b}(t)=\frac{\sinh\big((\frac{Q}{2}-z)t\big)}{4t\sinh(\frac{bt}{2})\sinh(\frac{t}{2b})}
be the integrand in (
1.1
). Then we have
F_{\overline{z},\overline{b}}(\overline{t})=\overline{F_{z,b}(t)}.
As a consequence,
\int_{\Omega}F_{\overline{z},\overline{b}}(t)dt=\int_{\overline{\Omega}}F_{\overline{z},\overline{b}}(\overline{s})d\overline{s}=\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\overline{F_{z,b}(s)}d\overline{s}=\overline{\int_{\Omega}F_{z,b}(s)ds},
where the first equality comes from the change of variable
t=\overline{s};
and
S_{\overline{b}}(\overline{z})=\exp\big(\int_{\Omega}F_{\overline{z},\overline{b}}(t)dt\big)=\exp\big(\overline{\int_{\Omega}F_{z,b}(s)ds}\big)=\overline{\exp\big(\int_{\Omega}F_{z,b}(s)ds\big)}=\overline{S_{b}(z)},
where again the second equality comes from the change of variable
t=\overline{s}.

Then for
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{6}),
we let
G_{\bm{a},b}(u)=\prod_{i=1}^{4}S_{b}(u-t_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{4}S_{b}(q_{j}-u)
be the integrand in (
1.2
). By (
2.16
), we have
G_{\overline{\bm{a}},\overline{b}}(\overline{u})=\overline{G_{\bm{a},b}(u)}.
Therefore,
\int_{\Gamma}G_{\overline{\bm{a}},\overline{b}}(u)du=\int_{\overline{\Gamma}}G_{\overline{\bm{a}},\overline{b}}(\overline{v})d\overline{v}=\int_{\overline{\Gamma}}\overline{G_{\bm{a},b}(v)}d\overline{v}=\overline{\int_{\Gamma}G_{\bm{a},b}(v)dv},
where the first equality comes from the change of variable
u=\overline{v};
and the desired equality follows immediately.
∎

2.3
Complex

-
6j

In this subsection, we focus on the case that
a_{k}=\frac{Q}{2}\pm\frac{\theta_{k}}{2\pi b}
for each
k\in\{1,\dots,6\}
with the sign
+
or
-
chosen arbitrarily, and with
(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{6})
the dihedral angles of a hyperideal hyperbolic tetrahedron. In this case, the following Proposition
2.5
gives us a flexibility to choose the contour of integral in the computation of the
b
-
6j
symbols, which is a crucial step in the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

To state the result, we first recall that the double sine function
S_{b}
satisfies the functional equation (see e.g
[
15
, A.15]
)

S_{b}(z+b^{\pm 1})=2\sin(\pi b^{\pm 1}z)S_{b}(z),

(2.17)

and extends to a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
with the set of poles
\{-nb-mb^{-1}\ |\ m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\}
and the set of zeros
\{Q+nb+mb^{-1}\ |\ m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\}.
See Figure
2
.

The poles and zeros of
S_{b}(z).

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{6})
be the dihedral angles of a hyperideal hyperbolic tetrahedron. For each
k\in\{1,\dots,6\},
let
a_{k}=\frac{Q}{2}\pm\frac{\theta_{k}}{2\pi b}
with the sign
+
or
-
chosen arbitrarily.
Let
t_{\text{max}}\in\{t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}\}
be the one with
\mathrm{Re}t_{\text{max}}=\max\{\mathrm{Re}t_{i},\mathrm{Re}t_{2},\mathrm{Re}t_{3},\mathrm{Re}t_{4}\}
and let
q_{\text{min}}\in\{q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4}\}
be the one with
\mathrm{Re}q_{\text{min}}=\min\{\mathrm{Re}q_{1},\mathrm{Re}q_{2},\mathrm{Re}q_{3},\mathrm{Re}q_{4}\}.
Let

\Gamma^{*}
be a contour as depicted in (the shaded region of) Figure
3
, i.e.,

(1)

for each
u\in\Gamma^{*},

\arg(u-t_{\text{max}})\notin[\pi-\arg b,\pi+\arg b]\quad\text{and}\quad\arg(u-q_{\text{min}})\notin[-\arg b,\arg b],

and

(2)

one end of
\Gamma^{*}
is a ray of argument in
[\arg b,\pi-\arg b],
and the other end of
\Gamma^{*}
is a ray of argument in

Then for
b\in\mathbb{C}
with
\arg b\in\big(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\big),
the

-
6j
symbol can be computed by

\bigg\{\begin{matrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
a_{4}&a_{5}&a_{6}\end{matrix}\bigg\}_{b}=\Bigg(\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{4}\prod_{j=1}^{4}S_{b}(q_{j}-t_{i})}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\Gamma^{*}}\prod_{i=1}^{4}S_{b}(u-t_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{4}S_{b}(q_{j}-u)du.

\Gamma^{*}.

The proof of Proposition
2.5
relies on the relationship between the double sine function and
Faddeev’s quantum dilogarithm function

\Phi_{b}(z)\doteq\exp\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\frac{e^{-2\mathbf{i}zt}}{4t\sinh(bt)\sinh\big(\frac{t}{b}\big)}dt\bigg)

for
b\in\mathbb{C}
with
\arg b\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}).

The integral absolutely converges for
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
-\mathrm{Re}\big(\frac{Q}{2}\big)<\mathrm{Im}z<\mathrm{Re}\big(\frac{Q}{2}\big),
defining a holomorphic function in such
z;
and by the functional equation
[
1
, Formula (48)]

\Phi_{b}\bigg(z-\frac{b^{\pm 1}\mathbf{i}}{2}\bigg)=\Big(1+e^{2\pi b^{\pm 1}z}\Big)\Phi_{b}\bigg(z+\frac{b^{\pm 1}\mathbf{i}}{2}\bigg),

(2.18)

\Phi_{b}(z)
extends to a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
with the set of poles
\big\{\frac{Q}{2}\mathbf{i}+nb\mathbf{i}+mb^{-1}\mathbf{i}\ \big|\ m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\big\}
and the set of zeros
\big\{-\frac{Q}{2}\mathbf{i}-nb\mathbf{i}-mb^{-1}\mathbf{i}\ \big|\ m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\big\}.
By
[
15
, A4, A5, A13]
, Faddeev’s quantum dilogarithm function
\Phi_{b}
and the double sine function
S_{b}
are related by

\Phi_{b}(z)=S_{b}\bigg(\mathbf{i}z+\frac{Q}{2}\bigg)e^{\frac{\pi\mathbf{i}z^{2}}{2}+\frac{\pi\mathbf{i}}{24}(b^{2}+b^{-2})}.

(2.19)

We need the following infinite product formula for Faddeev’s quantum dilogarithm function.

Proposition 2.6
.

For
b\in\mathbb{C}
with
\arg b\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}),

\Phi_{b}(z)=\frac{\prod_{n=0}^{+\infty}\Big(1+e^{2\pi bz+(2n+1)\pi\mathbf{i}b^{2}}\Big)}{\prod_{n=0}^{+\infty}\Big(1+e^{2\pi b^{-1}z-(2n+1)\pi\mathbf{i}b^{-2}}\Big)}

for each
z\in\mathbb{C}
that is not a pole of
\Phi_{b}(z).

Proof.

Observe that for
b\in\mathbb{C}
with
\arg b\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}),
we have
\mathrm{Im}(b^{2})>0.

Then by
[
1
, Formula (44)]
, the formula holds for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
-\mathrm{Im}\big(\frac{Q}{2}\big)<\mathrm{Im}z<\mathrm{Im}\big(\frac{Q}{2}\big);
and to prove the formula for other
z\in\mathbb{C},
it suffices to show that the right hand side of the equality defines a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
that shares the same set of poles with
\Phi_{b}.

To this end, we let
N_{b}(z)=\prod_{n=0}^{+\infty}\big(1+e^{2\pi bz+(2n+1)\pi\mathbf{i}b^{2}}\big)
be the numerator and
M_{b}(z)=\prod_{n=0}^{+\infty}\big(1+e^{2\pi b^{-1}z-(2n+1)\pi\mathbf{i}b^{-2}}\big)
be the denominator of the right hand side of the equality.

We first claim that both
N_{b}(z)
and
M_{b}(z)
define holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{C},
as a consequence of which
\frac{N_{b}(z)}{M_{b}(z)}
is a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}.
Indeed, for each
z\in\mathbb{C},
we have

\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\big|e^{2\pi bz+(2n+1)\pi\mathbf{i}b^{2}}\big|\leqslant\big(\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}e^{-(2n+1)\pi\mathrm{Im}(b^{2})}\big)e^{2\pi|b||z|}

and

\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\big|e^{2\pi b^{-1}z-(2n+1)\pi\mathbf{i}b^{-2}}\big|\leqslant\big(\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}e^{(2n+1)\pi\mathrm{Im}(b^{-2})}\big)e^{2\pi|b^{-1}||z|}.

Since
\mathrm{Im}(b^{2})>0
and
\mathrm{Im}(b^{-2})<0,
both of the series above converge uniformly on the disk
|z|\leqslant r
for each
r>0.
Then by e.g.
[
11
, Theorem on p. 437]
, both of the infinite products
N_{b}(z)
and
M_{b}(z)
converge, and define holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{C}.

Next, we compute the set of poles of
\frac{N_{b}(z)}{M_{b}(z)}.

To see this, we first observe that the set of zeros of

N_{b}(z)
is

Z_{N_{b}}=\bigg\{-\Big(n+\frac{1}{2}\Big)b\mathbf{i}+\Big(m+\frac{1}{2}\Big)b^{-1}\mathbf{i}\ \bigg|\ n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0},m\in\mathbb{Z}\bigg\}

and the set of zeros of
M_{b}(z)
is

Z_{M_{b}}=\bigg\{\Big(n+\frac{1}{2}\Big)b\mathbf{i}+\Big(m+\frac{1}{2}\Big)b^{-1}\mathbf{i}\ \bigg|\ n\in\mathbb{Z},m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\bigg\};

and all these zeros are simple zeros.
As a consequence, all the points in
Z_{N}\cap Z_{M}
are removable singularities of
\frac{N_{b}(z)}{M_{b}(z)},

and hence the set of poles of
\frac{N_{b}(z)}{M_{b}(z)}

is

Z_{M_{b}}{\smallsetminus}Z_{N_{b}}=\bigg\{\Big(n+\frac{1}{2}\Big)b\mathbf{i}+\Big(m+\frac{1}{2}\Big)b^{-1}\mathbf{i}\ \bigg|\ m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\bigg\}.

Finally, the set of poles of
\Phi_{b}
is
\big\{\frac{Q}{2}\mathbf{i}+nb\mathbf{i}+mb^{-1}\mathbf{i}\ \big|\ m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\big\},

which coincides with
Z_{M_{b}}{\smallsetminus}Z_{N_{b}}.
This completes the proof.
∎

Using Proposition
2.6
, we obtain the following estimate of the double sine function near infinity, which is the key ingredient of the proof of Proposition
2.5
. A similar result can also be found in
[

, Formula (A.11)]

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

be a

-smooth function with the series
\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}f(n)
converges, the integral
\int_{0}^{+\infty}f(x)dx

\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}f(n)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}f(x)dx+\frac{f(0)}{2}-\frac{f^{\prime}(0)}{12}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime\prime}(x)P_{2}(x)dx,

is the second Bernoulli polynomial of
x-[x],
and the Gauss symbol
[x]
is the largest integer less than or equal to
x.

Proof.

By the Second-derivative form of Euler’s Summation Formula (see e.g.
[
2
, Theorem 3]
), we have for each

that

\sum_{n=0}^{k}f(n)=\int_{0}^{k}f(x)dx+\frac{f(k)+f(0)}{2}+\frac{f^{\prime}(k)-f^{\prime}(0)}{12}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{k}f^{\prime\prime}(x)P_{2}(x)dx.

(3.8)

Indeed, the original statement of

-smooth functions, applying which respectively to the real and the imaginary parts of

we get (

). To prove the result, since

and
\int_{0}^{+\infty}f(x)dx
converge, and
f(x)\to 0
and

it suffices to show that the integral

To this end, as

and
\int_{0}^{k}f(x)dx

4.2
The Geometric Hypothesis

Let
(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{6})
be the dihedral angles of a hyperideal hyperbolic tetrahedron
\Delta.
For each

let
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\pi}{2}\pm\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}

Let

in
(\max\{\tau_{i}\},\min\{\eta_{j}\});

The Geometric Hypothesis needed in Theorem
1.3
(2) considers the behavior of
\mathrm{Im}U_{\bm{\alpha}}(\xi)
on the whole
\mathbb{R}.
To state the hypothesis, recall that
\mathrm{Im}L(x)=-2\Lambda(x)
for all
x\in(0,\pi),
where
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

is the Lobachevsky function defined by

\Lambda(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}\log|2\sin t|dt.

Then by (
3.2
), (
3.3
), we can continuously (but not
C^{1}
-smoothly) extend
\mathrm{Im}L(x)
from
H=\mathbb{C}{\smallsetminus}\big((-\infty,0)\cup(\pi,\infty)\big)

\mathbb{C}
by letting

\mathrm{Im}L(x)=-2\Lambda(x)

for each
x\in\mathbb{R}.

As a consequence, we can continuously extend
\mathrm{Im}U_{\bm{\alpha}}
from
D=\mathbb{C}{\smallsetminus}(-\infty,\max\{\tau_{i}\})\cup(\min\{\eta_{j}\},\infty)
to
\mathbb{C}
by letting

\mathrm{Im}U_{\bm{\alpha}}(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\Lambda(\eta_{j}-\tau_{i})-2\sum_{i=1}^{4}\Lambda(\xi-\tau_{i})-2\sum_{j=1}^{4}\Lambda(\eta_{j}-\xi)

for each
\xi\in\mathbb{R}.

Geometric Hypothesis 4.13
.

Let
(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{6})
be the dihedral angles of a hyperideal hyperbolic tetrahedron. For each
k\in\{1,\dots,6\},
there is a choice of the sign
+
or
-
in
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\pi}{2}\pm\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}
such that, with this choice of
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{6}),

\mathrm{Im}U_{\bm{\alpha}}(\xi)\geqslant\mathrm{Im}U_{\bm{\alpha}}(\xi^{*})

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R};
and the equality holds if and only if
\xi=\xi^{*}+k\pi
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}.

Remark 4.14
.

Numerical computations show that this Geometric Hypothesis is not always satisfied. For example, for a sufficiently small
\delta>0,

(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{6})=\big(\delta,\delta,\delta,\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta,\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta,\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta\big)
provides a counter example.

Remark 4.15
.

On the other hand, numerical computations suggest that a very large portion of the dihedral angles
(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{6})
should satisfy the Geometric Hypothesis. We examined the dihedral angles taking values from the set
\{0.01,0.26,0.51,0.76,1.01,1.26,1.51,1.76,2.01,2.26,2.51,2.76,3.01\},
evenly distributed in the space
[0,\pi]^{6}.
There are in total
104321
values that form the set of dihedral angles of a hyperideal
hyperbolic tetrahedron, and
103213
of them satisfy the Geometric Hypothesis, with a probability of
\sim 98.94\%.
The same computations also suggest that as long as none of the
\theta_{k}
’s is as extremely small as
0.01,
the Geometric Hypothesis is satisfied.
