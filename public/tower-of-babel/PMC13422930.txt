Clinical benefit, price and epidemiology of orphan drugs approved in China: a cross-sectional analysis of orphan drug policy (ODP) in China
Authors: Jing Yuan, Minghui Li, Bin Jiang, Z Kevin Lu, Jing Zhao, Xiangxiang Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the regulatory approval, epidemiology, clinical benefits and pricing of orphan drugs approved by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) in China.DesignCross-sectional analysis.Study sampleOrphan drugs approved by the NMPA for rare disease indications between 1 January 2005 and 31 December 2022.Outcome measuresThe outcomes included regulatory approval lag time, National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL) coverage, monthly treatment costs, clinical benefit (SMR, Service Médical Rendu) and additional therapeutic value (ASMR, Amélioration du Service Médical Rendu) ratings from the French National Health Authority (HAS, Haute Autorité de santé). Data were analysed at the drug indication level. The approval time lag was defined as the interval between the earliest global marketing authorisation and subsequent approval by China’s NMPA.ResultsA total of 59 orphan drugs were approved by the NMPA for 32 rare disease indications in 2005–2022. Of the 24 indications with epidemiological data, the incidence ranged from 0.098 to 9.62 cases per 100 000 persons and the prevalence ranged from 0.017 to 25.7 cases per 100 000 population. A total of 51 drug-indication pairs had SMR ratings: 40 (63.5%) were rated as important, 2 (3.2%) as moderate, 6 (9.5%) as weak and 5 (7.9%) as insufficient. For the 61 indications with pricing data, the median annual treatment price was US$18 259.8 (IQR: US$6504.6 to US$184 011.9), while negotiated drugs, those included in the NRDL through price negotiation, had significantly lower prices than non-negotiated drugs (US$13 677.8 vs US$62 870.0, p=0.015). The ASMR ratings indicated that 5 (7.9%) were rated as important (II), 10 (15.9%) as moderate (III), 11 (17.5%) as minor (IV), and 25 (39.7%) as no improvement (V).ConclusionsThe orphan drug policy in China has improved access to and affordability of orphan drugs, particularly through priority reviews and NRDL price negotiations. However, substantial barriers remain in accessing orphan drugs, including limited definitions of rare diseases, the unavailability of overseas-approved orphan drugs and the high costs of non-NRDL drugs. Therefore, ‘Three Medical Linkage’ healthcare reform, which integrates health insurance, medical service delivery and pharmaceutical regulation, should continue to incentivise the development of orphan drugs and improve patient accessibility and affordability.

Background

Orphan drugs are typically defined as medicines intended for rare diseases, as defined by jurisdiction-specific rarity thresholds.1 The definitions of rare diseases vary among countries.2 3 Although there is no official definition of rare disease in China, the Catalogue of the First Batch of Rare Diseases—jointly released by national authorities—serves as the principal operational reference list for rare disease recognition and the implementation of rare disease-related policies. An academic association defines them as having an adult prevalence rate of less than 0.01‰, a neonatal incidence rate of less than 1/10 000 or fewer than 140 000 cases.4 It is estimated that rare diseases affect more than 20 million Chinese individuals. In addition to the lack of a definition of rare diseases, another major challenge involving orphan drug treatment in China is its limited availability and accessibility.5 6 Recent studies have shown that less than half of the orphan drugs approved in the USA, European Union (EU) and Japan are available in China.7 Furthermore, orphan drugs are often priced significantly higher than non-orphan drugs owing to limited patient populations and the lack of therapeutic alternatives. High drug prices may cause poverty for families coping with catastrophic health expenditures.8 9 A national survey conducted by the China-Dolls Center for Rare Disorders found that medical costs for patients with rare diseases were 1.9 times their family incomes.10 Therefore, it underscores the unmet need to improve accessibility and affordability for patients with rare diseases in China. 10

Globally, the establishment of policy frameworks aimed at optimising the development, licensing, pricing and reimbursement processes for orphan drugs has been considered a potentially effective approach to address access and unmet needs for patients with rare diseases.2 In 1983, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) enacted the Orphan Drug Act, the first legislation to regulate orphan drugs worldwide.11 Since then, most high-income countries have included the development of orphan drug policies (ODPs) in their policy agendas.2 12 Various initiatives related to regulatory policies, Research and Development (R&D) and pricing and reimbursement strategies have been implemented to improve access to orphan drugs in high-income countries. Despite these initiatives, significant challenges remain, such as delayed access to innovative treatments, limited availability of orphan drugs and high therapeutic costs, all of which vary considerably across regions.2 13–16 Recently, some low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly China, established ODPs to improve the availability of orphan drugs.17 18

China’s regulatory and reimbursement framework for orphan drugs has evolved rapidly since 2017. The National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) offers special review (SR), priority review (PR) and the Urgently Needed Overseas Drugs (UNODs) designation to expedite the approval of orphan drugs. For the reimbursement and coverage of the National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL), orphan drugs are prioritised in price negotiations administered by the National Healthcare Security Administration (NHSA). The recent ‘Three Medical Linkage’ reform, which integrates national health insurance, medical care and drug regulation, plays a central role in coordinating drug policies to improve accessibility for both orphan and non-orphan drugs. However, most of the current literature on ODP focuses on specific initiatives, countries or regulatory systems.19–24

ODPs in China

The definition of an ODP is any ‘formal decision or a plan of action that has been taken by, or has involved, a state organisation’ in relation to the access and regulation of orphan drugs.2 25 Based on the ODP themes developed by Chan et al,2 we categorised the key ODPs in China into four themes (online supplemental appendix table 1).

Orphan drug designation: In 2018, five Chinese government authorities—the National Health Commission (NHC), the Ministry of Science and Technology, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, the NMPA and the National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine—jointly formulated the Catalog of the First Batch of Rare Diseases (hereafter referred to as Catalog), which identified 121 conditions/diseases as rare diseases.26 According to subsequent official procedural guidance, diseases considered for inclusion were required to meet the following criteria: low incidence or prevalence; substantial harm to patients and their families; the availability of clear diagnostic methods; and either the existence of treatment options or inclusion in a national research programme where no effective treatment was available. The NHC Expert Committee on the Diagnosis, Treatment and Protection of Rare Diseases provided technical support and policy advice during the development of the Catalog. Candidate diseases could be nominated by government authorities, national academic or professional societies and associations or registered civil organisations.

Marketing authorisation: Since 2016, China has implemented Accelerated Drug Marketing Registration Procedures for drugs, including PR and SR pathways, to shorten the time from development to market approval, particularly for orphan drugs or to address unmet medical needs.27 The PR pathway is granted to innovative drugs with recognised clinical value for rare diseases, paediatric populations and other conditions associated with substantial unmet needs. The SR pathway was originally established as an emergency review mechanism for drugs needed to prevent or treat public health emergencies, but its scope was subsequently expanded to include drugs for serious or life-threatening conditions, including rare diseases, as well as other clinically urgently needed products.28 In 2018, China introduced the UNOD policy to expedite the review of drugs for rare or serious diseases. For UNOD-designated drugs, ethnic sensitivity data may be waived at the time of market application.29 Neither the PR nor the SR pathway lowers the evidentiary requirements for safety, efficacy or quality; rather, both prioritise regulatory resources and shorten review timelines. Under the PR pathway, the standard review period may be reduced from 200 working days to 130 working days, or to 70 working days for UNOD-designated drugs, while under the SR pathway, the review timeline may be shortened to 20 working days. Sponsors are required to apply for inclusion in the PR and SR pathways.

In addition to orphan drug-specific policies, national drug policies have been implemented for orphans and other drugs to address unmet medical needs. In 2017, the General Office of the State Council issued the Opinions on Deepening the Reform of the Review and Approval System and Encouraging Innovation in Drugs and Medical Devices,28 which stated that clinical trials for orphan drugs and other drugs with substantial clinical benefits that have been marketed abroad could be waived to accelerate approval through priority review.28 Furthermore, in the same year, the NMPA initiated the accelerated approval for UNOD-designated drugs, in which orphan drugs accounted for a large proportion.18 With regard to price regulation, since 2017, many drugs have been gradually added to the NRDL through price negotiations.33 In 2019, the NHSA issued The Work Plan for the Adjustment of Medicines List for National Medical Insurance 2019 (Consultation),34 which stated that the national health insurance formulary would be regularly adjusted in line with treatment priorities for severe diseases, including orphan drugs.34

Several studies have examined the effects of the ODPs in China.7 35–37 However, these studies are limited to a very narrow scope of rare diseases7 35 or are restricted to orphan drugs approved by the FDA and other regulatory authorities.36 Little is known about the status quo and trends in orphan drugs approved under policies aimed at improving accessibility in China. This study aims to evaluate the regulatory approval, epidemiology, clinical benefits, and pricing of orphan drugs approved by the NMPA in China.

Methods

Data source and identification of orphan drugs

This analysis included all new drugs indicated for rare diseases listed in the 2018 ‘Catalog’ issued by government authorities.26 We first accessed publicly available data to obtain a list of orphan drugs approved by the NMPA between 1 January 2005 and 31 December 2022, as reported in previous studies by Yuan et al and Zhang et al.38 39 Orphan drugs were defined as NMPA-approved drugs indicated for rare diseases listed in the ‘Catalog’. Because many drugs have both rare and common disease indications or multiple indications, only rare disease indications were analysed. The analyses were conducted at the drug-indication level. Orphan drugs approved before 2005 were excluded from the study due to the lack of official approval records on the NMPA website.39 We also cross-checked our list of orphan drugs against the Pharmacodia database, one of the largest commercial databases compiled from the NMPA’s official websites,40 NHSA and other public resources.38 We extracted the following information: the drug approval date, indications, approval type, drug price and reimbursement status.40 41 As described previously, the characteristics of the orphan drugs were extracted and manually verified.42 Two independent reviewers extracted and manually verified the data, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist.

Epidemiology of rare diseases

For each indication, we conducted a scoping review to determine the incidence and prevalence of rare diseases listed in the ‘Catalog’. The data were retrieved from the Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Rare Diseases.43 For epidemiological data reported across multiple studies, meta-analyses were performed to estimate the mean prevalence and incidence. For epidemiological data reported as ranges across various studies, we calculated weighted averages, using sample size as the weight, to aggregate the ranges into a single estimate. In accordance with the global health technology assessment agencies,44 we dichotomised indications into ultra-rare (<2 cases per 100 000 people) and common-rare (≥2 cases per 100 000 people).

Regulatory approval

For each orphan drug included in our analysis, we retrieved and reviewed the latest label and regulatory review documents to collect data on indications and expedited programmes granted by the NMPA, including SR, PR and UNOD.45 Two reviewers independently reviewed NMPA websites and cross-checked expedited designations and approval dates against NMPA, the Pharmacodia database and news and announcement websites.41 To ensure data accuracy, the data extraction process involved multiple steps. Regulatory documents were extracted from the NMPA’s ‘Drug Approval Information Publicity Platform’. We then verified the expedited designations by cross-referencing NMPA announcements and Pharmacodia’s ‘Expedited Approval Drugs’ module. The FDA/European Medicines Agency approval dates for foreign-approved drugs were retrieved from the official databases. Further details regarding the data-extraction approach are described elsewhere.38

To understand the impact of ODPs on market availability, we evaluated the approval lag time, which was calculated as the interval between a new orphan drug approved by international regulatory agencies (eg, the FDA) and the time it was subsequently approved in China.36 To evaluate approval lag time, orphan drugs were limited to those originally approved outside China. If a drug had multiple indications, the earliest approval date was used. We categorised the drugs according to their Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification at level 1 and innovation status (first-in-class vs next-in-class). The innovation status was defined by whether the drug provided a novel mechanism of action for rare diseases.38

Clinical benefits

Because clinical and economic evaluation reports are not publicly available for most orphan drugs in China, we extracted the clinical value ratings—SMR (Service Médical Rendu, clinical benefit) and ASMR (Amélioration du Service Médical Rendu/additional therapeutic value rating)―from the French National Health Authority (Haute Autorité de santé (HAS)).46 Clinical value ratings were extracted at the drug-indication level, consistent with the HAS appraisal practice. In France, all innovative drugs undergo a health technology assessment before being included in the positive list of reimbursed products. The SMR rates the drug’s clinical benefit based on five criteria: (1) the severity of the disease and its impact on morbidity and mortality, (2) the clinical efficacy/effectiveness and safety of the drugs, (3) the treatment goal of the drugs (eg, preventive or curative), (4) the existence of therapeutic alternatives and (5) unmet clinical needs. The SMR was classified as insufficient, mild, moderate or important. ASMR rates a drug’s added value compared with existing therapies and classifies them as I–V. We included both SMR and ASMR ratings based on the evaluations and re-evaluations of orphan drugs published as of December 2023. To ensure accuracy, the data were extracted independently by two reviewers.

Reimbursement coverage and drug prices

In China, prescription drugs are procured through centralised bidding at the national or provincial levels, leading to price variations across the country. Hence, we extract the annual winning two times a day prices for 2022 from the Pharmacodia database and calculate the average unit price for each orphan drug.41 For orphan drugs procured through national volume-based procurement, we extracted the bid-winning prices announced on the Shanghai Sunshine Medical Procurement All-In-One.47 For those drugs negotiated with NHSA, we extracted negotiated prices from the NRDL. We calculated pricing information from the hospital platform for certain orphan drugs using confidential pricing data, as described in previous studies.38 To examine the impact of price negotiations on the orphan drugs included in the NRDL, we compared drug prices before and after the negotiations.

To conduct a price comparison, we calculated the annual treatment prices for chronically used drugs and the cost per full treatment course for single-use or cycle therapies, based on a defined daily dose (DDD). DDD refers to the average daily maintenance dose for adults. If the DDD was unavailable for a specific drug, the average daily dose was calculated based on the indications approved by the NMPA.38 For drugs that required dosing based on body weight or body surface area (BSA), we used 60 kg body weight or 1.5 m2 BSA for dosing calculations, which is consistent with the requirements of the NHSA and previous studies.38 For prescription drugs covered by the NRDL, Class A drugs are fully covered, whereas Class B drugs are generally covered by 50%. For NRDL-covered prescription drugs, Class A drugs are reimbursed at 90% of the listed reimbursement price, whereas Class B drugs typically require patients to pay approximately 50% of the out-of-pocket cost after reimbursement. These reimbursement rates were used to estimate the out-of-pocket costs. Therefore, out-of-pocket costs were estimated by applying the NRDL reimbursement rate.38 47

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported; continuous variables were reported as means (SD) or medians (IQR), and categorical variables were reported as numbers (percentages). Because the lag time and cost data are highly skewed, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test was used to assess differences between the data. Furthermore, multiple ordinary least squares regression analysis was applied to examine factors related to drug prices, including price negotiation status, innovativeness (first-in-class vs next-in-class), rarity of indication (ultra-rare vs common-rare), SMR, ASMR and NMPA approval year. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the study.

Results

Characteristics of orphan drugs

Our analysis included 59 orphan drugs that were approved by the NMPA. Of these drugs, 32 indications were included in the 121 rare diseases in the ‘Catalog’ (online supplemental appendix table 2). Four (6.8%) orphan drugs had more than one rare disease indication, resulting in a total of 63 indications included in the analysis. The most frequent therapeutic areas were antineoplastic and immunomodulatory (table 1; n=16, 25.4%), followed by the alimentary tract and metabolism (n=14, 22.2%), blood and blood-forming organs (n=10, 15.9%) and cardiovascular system (n=10, 15.9%).

Clinical benefits

As shown in table 1, 40 (63.5%) drugs were rated as having important clinical benefits, 2 (3.2%) as moderate, 6 (9.5%) as weak, 5 (7.9%) as insufficient and 10 (15.9%) as not rated. Regarding ASMR scores, 5 (7.9%) were rated as having important additional therapeutic value, 10 (15.9%) as moderate, 11 (17.5%) as minor, 25 (39.7%) as insufficient, and 12 (19.0%) as no additional therapeutic value.

Epidemiology of rare diseases

Among the 63 rare disease indications included in the analysis, 21 (35.6%) were ultra-rare (<2/100 000), 38 (64.4%) were common-rare (≥2/100 000) and 4 (6.3%) lacked sufficient epidemiological data and were classified as unknown. Among the 32 rare disease conditions, 4 had incidence data and 24 had prevalence data for the Chinese population. The incidence rate ranged from 0.098 to 9.62 cases per 100 000 persons, and the prevalence rate ranged from 0.017 to 25.7 cases per 100 000 population (figure 1). Most of the approved indications are for ultra-rare diseases, except for haemophilia, severe congenital neutropenia, hyperphenylalaninaemia and tuberous sclerosis complex. The prevalence of severe congenital neutropenia is 25.7 cases per 100 000 people in China, which is higher than the definition of rare diseases.48 More details are provided in online supplemental appendix table 3.

Of the 63 indications included in the analysis, 40 (63.5%) were included in the NRDL. The median coverage lag time between NMPA approval and NRDL coverage was 2.9 years (IQR: 1.5–5.3). Indications with important or moderate additional therapeutic value had a shorter lag time than those with minor or no additional value (1.3 (IQR: 1.0–2.9) vs 3.1 (IQR: 2.3–6.0) years; p=0.108).

Orphan drugs price and medication affordability

Of the 63 indications approved by the NMPA, price data were unavailable for 2. For the resulting sample of 61 indications with price data, the median annual treatment price was US$18 259.8 (table 4; IQR: 6504.6–184 011.9). Indications with price negotiation had significantly lower prices than those that were not negotiated (US$13 677.8 (IQR: 6087.4–20 188.7) vs US$62 870.0 (IQR: 7315.0–456 552.7); p=0.015). Higher prices were observed for ultra-orphan drugs than for common orphan drugs (US$22 058.6 (IQR: 7381.9–235 078.8) vs US$12 570.6 (IQR: 1717.5–68 965.1); p=0.029). In the regression analysis, orphan drug price was associated with price negotiation but not with SMR, ASMR, innovativeness, rarity of indication and NMPA approval year (table 5).

Discussion

This analysis identified significant variations in NMPA approval, innovativeness, clinical benefits and pricing of orphan drugs for rare disease indications. Recently, ODPs introduced in China have been impressively effective at expediting regulatory approval and increasing the availability of orphan drugs, consistent with evidence from other countries, which suggests that ODPs are associated with faster regulatory approval and greater market availability.49 The ODPs have successfully stimulated the development of orphan drugs, particularly for ultra-rare conditions, to address critical and unmet medical requirements.49 50 The price negotiation approach empowers the NHSA to negotiate prices directly with manufacturers and represents an effective and promising measure to address drug pricing and affordability issues.49 However, there are concerns about the clinical benefits of orphan drugs, which are often supported by small-sample clinical trials that may overestimate efficacy.49

Rare disease designation and clinical needs

ODPs were introduced in China to recognise rare diseases. Unsurprisingly, we found that the majority of the orphan drugs approved by NMPA were associated with significant unmet needs. Most drugs have been approved for the treatment of rare diseases, defined as those with a prevalence of <10 cases per 100 000 people in the Chinese population. In fact, the majority of these rare indications were defined as ultra-rare (<2 per 100 000) in other countries. Globally, different countries have different approaches to defining rare diseases. Most countries followed the EU’s definition of a low prevalence of <0.05% (or 1 per 10 000),23 24 whereas some countries (eg, the USA) adhered to the number of prevalent cases.2 As of 2023, China’s NHC has released two catalogues covering 207 rare diseases.51 Between 2017 and 2022, the orphan drugs approved by the NMPA accounted for nearly 20% of the 121 rare diseases listed in the first ‘Catalog’. In fact, before the regulatory reforms, 21 rare diseases on the ‘Catalog’ had no disease-specific medications approved in China, while they were readily available in the USA, according to a report.20 Our study found that among the 17 orphan drugs approved by the NMPA, 5 were the only treatment option for specific rare diseases.

However, a few challenges remain in incentivising orphan drug discovery and development, which should be addressed through more comprehensive policy measures. First, there is no official definition of rare diseases in China, which creates uncertainty for domestic companies that allocate resources to the development of orphan drugs. Orphan drugs for rare diseases are clearly defined in several countries and regions.2 Second, the types of diseases listed in the ‘Catalog’ are limited compared with other countries and regions, resulting in many orphan drugs approved overseas not receiving orphan designations in China. According to the WHO, 5000–8000 rare diseases exist worldwide.25 Studies have reported that many rare diseases are not yet recognised in China.4 5 9 Therefore, it is urgently needed to further expand the rare diseases in the ‘Catalog’ to benefit more patients with rare diseases who urgently need treatments that are not available in China.

Regulatory policy and market access

Waiting for new drug approvals, especially for orphan drugs, has long been ignored in China. The combination of legislation, regulations and policies has demonstrated promising effects in expediting regulatory approval and improving the market availability of orphan drugs.49 Our findings indicate that the traditional long approval lag time will be significantly shortened from 2016 to 2022. For example, sodium phenylbutyrate, approved by the FDA in 1996, will be approved for ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency in 2021, with a 25-year regulatory approval lag, which refers to the time interval between the earliest global marketing authorisation (primarily in the USA and the EU) and subsequent approval in China. In addition, orphan drugs that received priority and special reviews had numerically shorter approval lag times than those without these designations, although these differences were not statistically significant. In this analysis, we did not find a difference in approval lag times between drugs listed in the UNOD and those not listed. Drugs on the UNOD lists were not required to provide additional local clinical data by conducting bridging trials or additional local clinical studies to extrapolate foreign clinical trial data to the Chinese population after obtaining overseas approval. However, this policy was implemented in 2018, and its effects have not yet been demonstrated.

A series of ODPs was implemented to incentivise drug development for rare diseases with low commercial returns. However, we found that a considerable number of rare diseases currently have treatments available globally but not in China. For example, sodium benzoate/sodium phenylacetate was initially approved for hyperornithinaemia-hyperammonaemia-homocitrullinuria syndrome in the USA in 1987, but remains unavailable in China as of 2022. Hence, more strategies should be developed to incentivise drug R&D. For example, the tax credit could be increased, and the market exclusivity period could be prolonged to boost economic returns.49

Pricing, coverage and reimbursement policies

Making orphan drugs affordable is another global challenge52 particularly for LMICs such as China. Our findings showed that the series of ODPs was effective in reducing prices and improving the affordability of orphan drugs. With national price negotiations, the time lag between regulatory approval and NRDL coverage has significantly shortened, which is a long-standing issue in China. Orphan drug costs have been successfully reduced through pooled procurement and price negotiations. For example, nusinersen saw an average price drop of over 95% after negotiations, greatly reducing costs for patients with spinal muscular atrophy.

This analysis also highlights the significant financial burden of orphan drugs on patients and families. Orphan drugs remain unaffordable or are deemed catastrophic for the family, particularly for those not covered by the NRDL. For orphan drugs not included in the NRDL, the average monthly treatment price was more than 800 times an individual’s daily disposable income (US$21.9).47 Moreover, the annual treatment price was nearly twice the disposable annual household income (US$14 358)53 suggesting that current pricing exceeds a household’s capacity to pay. If the NRDL covers orphan drugs, most are reimbursed by up to 70%; hence, they are deemed affordable. With recent progress in national price negotiations, the affordability of orphan drugs is expected to improve.

Substantial price cuts in China also shed light on the global challenges of achieving affordable prices for orphan drugs. International reference prices have been used to benchmark or negotiate prices in China’s national price negotiation, and may contribute to price cuts and cross-country price convergence through spillover effects.54 Due to the small market and the difficulty of research and development, few domestic pharmaceutical companies in China are developing orphan drugs, and most are imported, leading to high economic burdens on patients, their families and society as a whole.25 As a result, the affordability of orphan drugs remains a significant concern in China. To improve medicine accessibility and affordability, China has launched the ‘Three Medical Linkage’ reform—a joint reform of health insurance, medical care and the drug industry—to govern the healthcare system more efficiently. Generic drug substitution and local manufacturing should be encouraged through legislation, treatment guidelines and public education.

Limitations of this study

This study had several limitations. First, we analysed only drugs approved by the NMPA for rare disease indications. Therefore, the policy implications may not be generalisable to other therapeutic areas. Second, we cross-checked the drugs approved by the NMPA; however, we could not rule out the possibility of missing data. Third, the clinical benefits were assessed by the French National Health Authority, which may represent different assessments of clinical and economic value. Fourth, epidemiological data for the rare disease indications were derived from scoping reviews and meta-analyses of existing published studies rather than from systematic reviews. This data collection approach may introduce potential biases into the estimated incidence and prevalence rates of rare diseases. Fifth, this study focused only on orphan drugs, without comparison with non-orphan drugs, and the observed improvements in orphan drug access and affordability are attributable to ODPs or to broader national healthcare reforms targeting all drugs that address unmet medical needs, which have been widely implemented in China in recent years. For instance, the time lag between regulatory approval and NRDL coverage for oncology drugs was reduced from 8.7 years in 2016 to 1.8 years in 2022,38 which is similar to that of orphan drugs reported in this analysis. Therefore, future studies should perform comparative analyses to evaluate the effects of national drug policies on orphan and non-orphan drugs. Finally, the first-in-class status is an approximate measure of innovativeness and does not necessarily indicate a major therapeutic advance.

Conclusion

ODPs in China have improved the access and affordability of orphan drugs, filling significant unmet clinical needs. However, many orphan drugs were still not approved or were deemed unaffordable in China. Therefore, ‘Three Medical Linkage’ healthcare reform should be further developed to incentivise the development of orphan drugs and to improve patient accessibility and affordability.
