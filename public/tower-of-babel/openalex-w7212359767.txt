Abstract
Sepsis is characterized by a dysregulated inflammatory response that frequently progresses to profound
immunosuppression. In this context, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) play a central role in immune
regulation by limiting tissue damage while simultaneously contributing to immune paralysis. This study aimed to
evaluate the therapeutic potential of MDSCs as targets for sepsis treatment. An integrative literature review was
conducted using the PubMed database with descriptors related to immunotherapy and immunomodulation. The
selected studies demonstrated that early MDSC expansion is associated with poor clinical outcomes and increased
mortality. At the molecular level, factors such as NFI-A, C/EBPβ, and microRNAs regulate the differentiation and
immunosuppressive activity of these cells. Experimental evidence indicates that MDSC modulation through
phenotypic reprogramming or inhibition of specific signaling pathways can restore immune competence and improve
survival. Furthermore, approaches including progenitor cell infusion and ulinastatin administration support the
potential of MDSC-targeted interventions as adjunctive therapies for sepsis. Overall, targeting MDSCs represents a
promising therapeutic strategy to restore immune homeostasis during different stages of sepsis.
Keywords: Sepsis; MDSC; Immunomodulation; Immunosuppression; Adjunctive Therapy.

1. Introduction
Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome characterized by a dysregulated host response to infection that frequently results
in organ dysfunction and high mortality (Singer et al., 2016). Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and intensive care
management, sepsis remains a major global public health challenge due to its high incidence, elevated healthcare costs, and
substantial mortality, particularly among critically ill patients (Rhodes et al., 2017). Current therapeutic approaches primarily
focus on infection control and hemodynamic support, whereas strategies targeting immune dysregulation remain limited.
The pathophysiology of sepsis is characterized by a dynamic interplay between excessive inflammation and
subsequent immunosuppression. Although the initial hyperinflammatory response contributes to pathogen clearance, many
patients subsequently develop persistent immune dysfunction characterized by lymphocyte apoptosis, impaired antigen
presentation, reduced natural killer (NK) cell activity, and dysfunction of macrophages and dendritic cells. This
immunosuppressive state predisposes patients to secondary infections, multiple organ dysfunction, and unfavorable clinical
outcomes (Schrijver et al., 2019; Cao et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023).
Among the cellular populations involved in sepsis-associated immune dysfunction, myeloid-derived suppressor cells
(MDSCs) have emerged as key regulators of immune homeostasis. MDSCs comprise a heterogeneous population of immature
myeloid cells with potent immunosuppressive properties that expand under chronic inflammatory conditions, including sepsis
(Gabrilovich & Nagaraj, 2009). These cells suppress both innate and adaptive immune responses by inhibiting T-cell
proliferation, impairing NK-cell cytotoxicity, modulating dendritic cell maturation, and regulating cytokine production,
thereby contributing to the maintenance of immune paralysis (Brudecki et al., 2012; Delano et al., 2007; Veglia et al., 2021).
Two major MDSC subsets are recognized: polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) and monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs),
which differ in their phenotypic markers, morphological characteristics, and distinct suppressive mechanisms. Despite the
growing number of studies, the heterogeneity of these cells remains a significant challenge for their standardized identification
and characterization (Bronte et al., 2016). MDSC expansion is associated with chronic inflammatory conditions, including
cancer (Gabrilovich & Nagaraj, 2009; Pereira et al., 2026), autoimmune diseases (Bekić et al., 2023), viral infections (Norris et
al., 2013), bacterial infections (Lai et al., 2014), fungal infections (Kaminski et al., 2025), and pregnancy (Köstlin et al., 2016;
Fragoso et al., 2026).
Bronte et al. (2016) proposed international guidelines for the classification of MDSCs, emphasizing the need to
consider molecular, immunophenotypic, and functional aspects when studying these cell populations. According to these
criteria, in mice, M-MDSCs are defined as CD11b⁺ Ly6Chigh Ly6G⁻, whereas PMN-MDSCs are characterized as CD11b⁺
Ly6Clow Ly6G⁺ (Bronte et al., 2016). In humans, MDSCs are classified into three main subtypes: monocytic MDSCs
(mMDSCs), characterized by HLA-DR⁻/low CD33⁺ CD11b⁺ CD14⁺; polymorphonuclear or granulocytic MDSCs (gMDSCs),
identified as Lin⁻ HLA-DR⁻/low CD33⁺ CD11b⁺ CD15⁺; and early-stage MDSCs (eMDSCs), which are Lin⁻ (including CD3,

Growing evidence suggests that MDSCs also possess diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic relevance in sepsis.
Increased circulating levels of monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) have been associated with disease severity, secondary
infections, prolonged intensive care unit stay, and increased mortality in patients with sepsis and septic shock (Waeckel et al.,
2020; Schrijver et al., 2022). Experimental studies further demonstrate that modulation of MDSC expansion or function
through selective depletion, phenotypic reprogramming, or inhibition of key molecular pathways can restore immune
competence and improve survival in preclinical models (Brudecki et al., 2012; Schrijver et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023).
Despite these advances, important knowledge gaps remain regarding the biological heterogeneity of MDSC subsets,
the optimal timing for therapeutic intervention, and the identification of reliable biomarkers for clinical application.
Furthermore, the available evidence is heterogeneous with respect to experimental models, phenotypic characterization, and
therapeutic approaches, highlighting the need for a comprehensive synthesis of current findings. Therefore, this study aimed to
evaluate the therapeutic potential of MDSCs as targets for sepsis treatment.

2. Methodology
This study is an integrative literature review (Page et al., 2021; Snyder, 2019), employing a quantitative approach to
describe the 16 articles selected for the research corpus and a qualitative approach to analyze and discuss the role of myeloidderived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in the mechanisms underlying the immune response suppression in sepsis (Risemberg et al.,
2026; Pereira et al., 2018), with emphasis on their immunological functions and therapeutic potential. The literature search was
conducted in the PubMed database using the following search strategy: ("Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells"[MeSH] OR

immune modulation, and therapeutic interventions in sepsis. To improve the relevance of the retrieved studies, publication
types including autobiographies, bibliographies, interviews, letters, news articles, comments, and case reports were excluded
using PubMed filters. The initial search identified 24 publications.
Subsequently, titles and abstracts were independently screened according to the eligibility criteria. When necessary,
full-text articles were assessed to determine their relevance. Studies were excluded if they did not specifically investigate the
role of MDSCs in sepsis, focused primarily on other pathological conditions such as cancer or transplantation, were duplicate
records, had no accessible full text, or consisted of narrative reviews lacking substantial analytical discussion. The selected
studies were analyzed according to study design, experimental model, principal findings, and the reported role of MDSCs in
the immunopathogenesis and therapeutic modulation of sepsis. Data were synthesized descriptively to identify the main
molecular mechanisms, immunological pathways, and therapeutic strategies reported in the literature.

3. Results
Following the eligibility assessment, 16 studies were included in the qualitative analysis. The selected publications
comprised predominantly experimental animal models, together with observational clinical studies, reflecting the current stage
of translational research on MDSCs in sepsis. The principal characteristics and findings of the included studies are summarized
in Table 1.
Overall, the analyzed studies consistently demonstrated that MDSCs expand during sepsis and play a dual role in
disease progression. While these cells contribute to limiting excessive inflammatory responses during the early stages of

4. Discussion
Walker et al. (2016) investigated the effects of pneumococcal choline-binding protein A (PcpA) on the recruitment of
innate immune regulatory cells during bacteremic pneumonia. The presence of PcpA was associated with increased bacterial
burden, elevated IL-6 levels, reduced TRAIL production, and decreased infiltration of polymorphonuclear cells into the lungs.
Notably, PcpA expression negatively modulated the recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor-like cells, which have been
implicated in the clearance and control of bacterial pneumonia. These findings suggest that bacterial virulence factors can
influence the recruitment of immunoregulatory myeloid populations and thereby contribute to impaired host defense and
increased bacterial burden during pneumococcal pneumonia (Walker et al., 2016).
Immunosuppression is already established during the early stages of sepsis. Ruan et al. (2020) demonstrated that,
concomitantly with the early increase in splenic apoptosis and sustained depletion of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T lymphocytes, there is a
rapid and persistent increase in PD-1 expression on T cells. In this context, MDSCs, particularly PMN-MDSCs, exhibit
elevated PD-L1 expression from the earliest days of disease and have been shown to suppress lymphocyte proliferation in coculture assays. The temporal overlap among lymphopenia, increased PD-1 expression on T cells, and progressive MDSC
expansion suggests that the PD-1/PD-L1 axis contributes to early immune blockade, establishing the basis for subsequent
immunoparalysis. These findings indicate that the functional interaction between MDSCs and T lymphocytes is an early event
in sepsis rather than merely a late consequence of the disease (Ruan et al., 2020).
Corroborating these experimental findings, Mathias et al. (2017) demonstrated in patients with severe sepsis that
MDSC expansion occurs within the first hours after diagnosis and persists for at least 28 days. This expansion is
predominantly granulocytic, whereas monocytic MDSCs remain reduced compared with healthy controls. Functionally, these
cells strongly suppress T-cell proliferation and reduce the production of cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-4, and IL-10.
Transcriptomic analysis revealed downregulation of genes associated with the HLA complex and increased ARG1 expression,
indicating impaired antigen presentation and enhanced arginine consumption. Clinically, sustained high MDSC levels were
associated with a higher incidence of nosocomial infections, prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stays, and poorer functional
status at discharge, further supporting the relevance of these cells in the transition toward the persistent inflammation,
immunosuppression, and catabolism (PICS) phenotype observed after sepsis (Mathias et a., 2017).
McPeak et al. (2017a) demonstrated that the transcription factor C/EBPβ is indispensable for activation of this
immunosuppressive program. Deletion of Cebpb in Gr1⁺CD11b⁺ myeloid cells significantly increased survival in mice during
late sepsis, abolished MDSC expansion, and prevented the maturation blockade of these cells. The absence of C/EBPβ resulted
in lower IL-10 and TGF-β levels during the prolonged phase of disease, whereas TNF-α and IL-6 production remained
relatively preserved during the early phase. These findings demonstrate that C/EBPβ is essential for late immunosuppression
and the accumulation of suppressive cell populations during sepsis progression (McPeak et al., 2017a).
In this context, multiple studies have identified the transcription factor NFI-A as a central regulator of aberrant
myeloid differentiation during sepsis. McPeak et al. (2017b) demonstrated that myeloid-specific deletion of NFI-A virtually
abolishes MDSC expansion in murine models, preserves normal myeloid differentiation, significantly reduces bacterial burden
at the site of infection, and markedly improves survival during the late phase of disease. These animals maintained adequate
TNF-α production, low IL-10 expression, and the ability to respond to subsequent immune challenges, indicating that NFI-A
deficiency specifically prevents sepsis-induced immunosuppression without compromising basal immunity. The authors
further demonstrated that NFI-A regulates pathways involving p21, cyclin D1/CDK4, and NF-κB, which contribute to the loss
of effector function and acquisition of the suppressive MDSC phenotype, positioning this factor as a highly promising
therapeutic target (McPeak et al., 2017b).

Convergent findings were reported by Dai et al. (2018), who demonstrated that sustained NFI-A activation promotes
the reprogramming of Gr1⁺CD11b⁺ myeloid precursors into functionally suppressive MDSCs, thereby inhibiting CD4⁺ T-cell
proliferation and function. NFI-A deletion disrupted this program, restored T-cell function, and partially re-established the
balance of the adaptive immune response. These findings reinforce the concept that MDSC expansion during sepsis results
from an active and regulated process and that sepsis-associated immunosuppression may be reversible through modulation of
its central transcriptional regulators (Dai et al., 2018).
Upstream of NFI-A induction, the regulatory axis mediated by the microRNAs miR-21 and miR-181b is particularly
relevant. McClure et al. (2016) demonstrated that activation of these microRNAs ultimately induces NFI-A, thereby blocking
myeloid differentiation through p21 suppression and maintaining cells in an immature and immunosuppressive state. Inhibition
of miR-21, miR-181b, or NFI-A restored differentiation into mature, functionally competent monocytes and dendritic cells,
thereby reducing suppressive activity. These findings establish the miR-21/miR-181b–NFI-A axis as a key mechanism
underlying sustained immunosuppression in sepsis (McClure et al., 2016).
Extending this model, Dai et al. (2017) elucidated that C/EBPβ cooperates with Stat3 to activate the promoters of
miR-21 and miR-181b, thereby inducing NFI-A and promoting the generation of immunosuppressive Gr1⁺CD11b⁺ MDSCs.
Ectopic reintroduction of C/EBPβ into deficient cells restored microRNA expression, reduced differentiation into macrophages
and dendritic cells, and conferred suppressive activity against CD4⁺ T cells. Co-transfection with miR-21 and miR-181b
reproduced this phenotype, whereas individual transfection of either microRNA was ineffective, demonstrating their
synergistic action. These findings position C/EBPβ as an early and indispensable regulator of the molecular cascade
culminating in MDSC-mediated immunosuppression during sepsis (Dai et al., 2017).
The functional maintenance of the miR-21/miR-181b–NFI-A axis also depends on the S100A9 protein. Alkhateeb et
al. (2019) demonstrated that S100A9 is essential for stabilization of the nuclear pSTAT3–C/EBPβ complex, thereby enabling
its binding to the promoters of miR-21 and miR-181b. In the absence of S100A9, even with activation of the other components
of the pathway, these microRNAs were not induced and the suppressive phenotype was not acquired. Reintroduction of
S100A9 restored the ability to suppress T lymphocytes, identifying this protein as a central component in the transition from
inflammation to immunoparalysis during chronic sepsis (Alkhateeb et al., 2019).
Additional studies have demonstrated that the cytokine IL-10 plays a critical role in this process by promoting both
S100A9 expression and its nuclear translocation. Bah et al. (2018) showed that, although both IL-6 and IL-10 increase S100A9
expression, only IL-10 induces its nuclear localization, a condition essential for MDSC suppressive function. IL-10 blockade
in vivo reduced MDSC expansion during the late phase of disease, whereas S100A9 knockdown restored myeloid
differentiation and immune function, indicating that prolonged IL-10 signaling sustains the chronic immunosuppression
observed in sepsis (Bah et al., 2018).
Moreover, epigenetic mechanisms also contribute to stabilization of this phenotype. Bah et al. (2022) demonstrated
that the demethylase KDM6A removes H3K27me3 from the promoter of the lncRNA Hotairm1, thereby enabling its
transcription and facilitating the nuclear transport of S100A9. Pharmacological inhibition of KDM6A with GSK-J4 suppressed
Hotairm1 expression, altered the subcellular localization of S100A9, and reversed the immunosuppressive phenotype of
MDSCs in both murine models and human cells. These findings reveal that the interplay among IL-10, KDM6A, and Hotairm1
establishes an epigenetic environment permissive to sustained immunosuppression during prolonged sepsis (Bah et al., 2022).
Other inflammatory pathways also modulate MDSC function. Ledo et al. (2018) demonstrated that TNFR1 signaling
is critical for acquisition of the suppressive phenotype by these cells during Staphylococcus aureus sepsis, regulating the
expression of arginase-1, iNOS, and IL-10. Although MDSC expansion does not directly depend on TNFR1, their suppressive

From a therapeutic perspective, several strategies for modulating MDSCs have been explored. Chang et al. (2018)
demonstrated that administration of TDCA induces the selective expansion of CD11b⁺Gr1hi granulocytic MDSCs with a
distinct immunoregulatory profile, associated with attenuation of systemic inflammation and targeted suppression of CD4⁺ Tcell activation. These findings reinforce that MDSC expansion is not homogeneous and that functionally distinct
subpopulations may exert protective effects when appropriately modulated (Chang et al., 2018).
Similarly, Morales-Mantilla et al. (2022) demonstrated that early infusion of naïve HSPCs significantly increases
survival in murine models of severe sepsis, even in the absence of changes in pathogen burden. This effect was associated with
restoration of the myeloid compartment and expansion of functional MDSCs capable of suppressing activated T lymphocytes
and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines. These findings suggest that, during the early stages of sepsis, controlled
amplification of immunosuppressive mechanisms may be beneficial by limiting systemic inflammatory injury (MoralesMantilla et al., 2022).
Additional immunomodulatory interventions include the use of immunoglobulins. Kyvelidou et al. (2018)
demonstrated that early administration of IgG or IgM during endotoxemia significantly reduces MDSC and Treg expansion
and arginase-1 activity, in addition to decreasing inflammatory mediators and improving survival. These findings indicate that
reversal of immunosuppressive circuits may contribute to clinical and functional recovery (Kyvelidou et al., 2018).
Finally, translational approaches further support the clinical relevance of MDSC modulation. Chen et al. (2022) using
single-cell RNA sequencing, demonstrated that sepsis is associated with the expansion of inflammatory and
immunosuppressive myeloid populations consistent with MDSCs, accompanied by impaired antigen presentation and
dysfunctional immune communication. Ulinastatin was able to modulate these populations, attenuating MDSC functional
characteristics and promoting re-equilibration of the immune response, thereby providing a molecular basis for its use as an
adjunctive therapy in sepsis (Chen et al., 2022).

5. Final Considerations
In summary, the available evidence indicates that immunosuppression in sepsis is established early as a result of an
active process of immune reprogramming, rather than merely representing a manifestation of the late inflammatory response.
In this context, MDSC-mediated immunosuppression and their interaction with T lymphocytes, modulated by transcription
factors, microRNAs, inflammatory signals, and epigenetic mechanisms, play a central role in the suppression of adaptive
immunity. The integration of evidence from murine models demonstrates that targeted modulation of these mechanisms is
associated with increased survival, whereas clinical evidence indicates that persistent MDSC expansion is associated with
poorer outcomes following sepsis, supporting the hypothesis that these processes represent promising therapeutic targets both
during the late stages of disease and in the early phases of excessive inflammation. These advances provide a more robust
conceptual framework for understanding human sepsis and serve as a foundation for the future development of more precise
therapeutic strategies, with the potential to improve clinical outcomes.
