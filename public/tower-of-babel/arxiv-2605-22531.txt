Disentanglement Beyond Generative Models with Riemannian ICA
Edmond Cunningham

Abstract
There is a gap between the theoretical foundations of disentanglement and the practice of modern representation learning. Existing theoretical frameworks, particularly Independent Component Analysis (ICA) and its nonlinear variants, assume a generative model with statistically independent latent variables underlying the data so that disentanglement amounts to identifying the latents that could have generated the data. This generative framework is interpretable and theoretically justified, but its strong modeling assumptions make it difficult to apply to modern representation learning. Modern pretrained encoders often learn features that exhibit disentangled properties without making generative assumptions, yet there is no general theory for interpreting these features as independent factors of variation. We take a step toward such a theory by introducing Riemannian ICA (RICA), which replaces ICA’s global generative model with local geometric structure. RICA is founded on the observation that in ICA, the factors of variation underlying a data point can be understood through radial curves emanating from the point that map to axis-aligned lines in the latent space. We formalize this perspective using tools from Riemannian geometry and introduce our theory in a way that is consistent with the existing generative approach. Our main contribution is the disentanglement tensor , which encodes a second-order notion of disentanglement that we call pointwise disentanglement. This tensor depends on the Hessian of the data log likelihood as well as the Ricci curvature induced by the model. In a controlled source recovery setting with known ground-truth sources, RICA recovers sources across several manifolds, while the success of ICA baselines depends on the coordinates used to represent the observations. Our work provides a theoretical basis for studying local disentanglement without assuming a global generative model.

Introduction

A long-standing goal of representation learning has been to disentangle the factors of variation underlying high-dimensional data
[
5
]
.
A representation is said to be
disentangled
when a change in one factor underlying the data corresponds to a change in a single component of the representation
[
5
,
42
]
.
For example, varying the pose of a face image should move exactly one component of its representation, and the components encoding eye color and hair length should remain fixed
[
45
]
.
Such representations are interpretable because each component corresponds to a distinct property of the data, and it has been argued that they should enable downstream supervised learning, transfer learning, few-shot learning, and scientific analysis
[
5
,
73
,
42
,
80
]
.
The intuition that disentangled representations should cause semantically meaningful changes to data while simultaneously being useful for downstream tasks has motivated the study of disentanglement through the lens of generative models
[
39
,
34
,
16
,
42
,
70
,
30
]
.
These approaches assume that data is generated from a set of independent latent variables and that the latents can also be recovered from data.
After fitting the generative model to real data, the latents can be interpreted as factors of variation directly, which enables applications such as controllable editing
[
16
,
34
,
85
,
86
]
and counterfactual analysis
[
23
]
, as well as theoretically justified statistical analyses of the recovered latent distribution
[
70
,
14
]
.
Despite their interpretability, these generative approaches have not been shown to consistently deliver representations that are useful for downstream tasks in practice
[
56
]
.
On the other hand, models based on self-supervised learning
[
68
,
62
,
2
,
83
]
learn features with properties that are consistent with disentanglement even though they were not explicitly built for it
[
37
,
60
]
.
But despite their downstream utility, theoretically justified interpretations of these features remain an open problem
[
58
,
64
,
74
,
37
,
60
]
.
In this paper, we offer one such interpretation by building upon a classical framework for generative disentanglement, called independent component analysis.

Independent component analysis (ICA)
[
17
,
39
]
, and its nonlinear extensions
[
42
,
9
,
30
,
89
,
41
]
, are generative frameworks for disentanglement with strong theoretical guarantees that follow from strict modeling assumptions
[
17
,
89
,
9
]
.
The most prevalent assumption is that the map between data and latents is globally invertible.
This assumption enables the strongest form of latent identification because each data point corresponds to a unique latent vector, and the latent distribution can be computed directly through the change of variables formula
[
39
]
.
However, the extra modeling assumptions required for this identification either use auxiliary information that is not available in every setting
[
41
]
or impose rigid modeling constraints that may be misspecified in practice
[
89
,
9
,
36
]
.
These restrictions make ICA difficult to use as a theory for modern disentangled representations, where the goal is often to interpret useful features rather than recover a globally valid generative model.
Our main insight is that the interpretation offered by generative models does not require global invertibility, but can instead be built from
local invertibility
around each data point.

A generative interpretation of disentanglement requires that variations in the data be attributable to variations of individual latent components.
To understand this, observe that a variation of a data point traces a curve through the point.
If the encoder maps this curve to an axis-aligned line in the latent space and is locally invertible at the point, then we can interpret the component associated with this axis as the factor of variation underlying the data point.
Disentanglement is then a question of whether the data’s probability density function factors along these curves.
We use tools from Riemannian geometry
[
55
]
to make this picture precise.

In this paper, we introduce Riemannian ICA (RICA), a local geometric analogue of ICA that defines independent factors of variation from a data density and a Riemannian metric.
RICA is motivated by pretrained models that can be represented by Riemannian metrics, and interprets
Riemann normal coordinates

[
55
]
as latent variables that are defined locally around a chosen base point.
This modeling choice preserves the local change-of-variables structure used by ICA, can be applied to non-invertible encoder models through the pullback metric, and gives a clear geometric interpretation of the factors of variation as geodesics through the base point.
Since the map to normal coordinates is invertible in a neighborhood of the base point, we are able to directly pushforward the data density through it and use the change of variables formula to obtain a probability density for the latent variables.
If this latent density factors into a product of independent distributions, then the latents have independent components.
However, it is possible that a pretrained model cannot disentangle data in any neighborhood of a point.
We therefore introduce
pointwise disentanglement
, a necessary pointwise condition for disentanglement that captures second-order statistical independence at the point.
Our main theoretical contribution is a tensor that encodes pointwise disentanglement called the
disentanglement tensor
.
This tensor depends on the data through the second derivatives of the log likelihood function and on the metric through Ricci curvature.
Our algorithm for pointwise disentanglement diagonalizes this tensor, which amounts to solving a generalized eigenvalue problem.
We validate this theory in a controlled source recovery setting where the ground-truth sources, metric, and density are known.
Across the tested manifolds and coordinate charts, RICA recovers the ground-truth sources, whereas the baselines are sensitive to the chart in which the observations are presented.
RICA recasts disentanglement as a local geometric property of a Riemannian metric relative to a data density, rather than as a property of a global invertible generative model.
This shift makes it possible to study disentanglement in pretrained representations without treating them as the latent variables of a generative model.

2.1
Independent component analysis

Independent component analysis (ICA)
[
17
,
39
,
42
]
is a classical algorithm for disentanglement that assumes data is generated by the model

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=J\mathbf{s},\quad\mathbf{s}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{s,i}(s_{i})

(2)

where
J\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is an invertible matrix and at most one of the source densities,
p_{s,i}
, is Gaussian.
Under these assumptions, the sources can be identified from data up to permutations and scaling
[
17
]
.
ICA has had success in problems where data actually satisfies these assumptions, such as the cocktail party problem
[
39
]
where observations are generated by a linear mixture of independent audio signals.
However, this linear model is unrealistic for high-dimensional datasets encountered in practice, such as ImageNet
[
21
]
, which bars the interpretation of the sources recovered by ICA as factors of variation underlying the data (
Figure

1(a)
).

To overcome this limitation, nonlinear ICA
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
was originally proposed as a more expressive generalization of ICA.
Its model is
\mathbf{x}=f(\mathbf{s}),\;\mathbf{s}\sim p_{s}(\mathbf{s})
where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an invertible function, often parameterized by a normalizing flow
[
71
,
63
]
, and
\mathbf{s}
has independent components.
The model of nonlinear ICA is flexible enough to represent any non-degenerate distribution of data
[
63
]
, but is not identifiable without extra constraints because infinitely many latent parameterizations can produce the same data distribution
[
40
,
59
,
9
]
.
This leads to models like the one shown in
Figure

1(b)
that are able to model the data’s distribution well but cannot recover meaningful latent structure.
Several constraints have been proposed in pursuit of identifiability, such as incorporating auxiliary variables
[
41
,
36
,
89
,
53
]
or making structural assumptions about the decoder
[
9
,
10
,
30
,
36
]
.
Despite a push to use nonlinear ICA for disentanglement
[
42
]
, research in this area shifted toward other approaches in recent years
[
73
,
10
,
7
,
57
,
69
,
72
]
.

A core limitation of ICA and its variants is the assumption that the decoder is globally invertible.
This assumption makes ICA and nonlinear ICA fundamentally misspecified when data has a non-trivial topology
[
18
]
, and excludes non-invertible neural network architectures as encoders.
There are two key reasons why this restrictive assumption has not been relaxed.
The first reason is the problem setting.
Disentangling the factors of variation underlying data implies both the ability to recover sources from data and the ability to vary data from sources because we must know exactly how the data varies.
This is not possible without invertibility unless we take a fundamentally different approach, such as employing ELBO-based methods like the
\beta
-VAE
[
34
,
70
]
.
The second reason is the change of variables formula, which relates the density function of data to the density function of the latents.
Under an invertible decoder where
h=f^{-1}
, the change of variables formula is given by

\displaystyle\log p_{s}(\mathbf{s})=\log p_{x}(\mathbf{x})+\log|\det J(\mathbf{x})|\quad\text{ where }\mathbf{s}=h(\mathbf{x})\text{ and }J(\mathbf{x})=\frac{df(\mathbf{s})}{d\mathbf{s}}

(3)

If
h
is not invertible, then the formula no longer holds, making the analysis of
p_{s}(\mathbf{s})
considerably more difficult
[
3
]
.
As a result, non-invertible pretrained encoders lack the generative structure needed to analyze whether their features define independent factors of variation.

Our main insight is that disentanglement is fundamentally a local question about how an individual data point varies with respect to latent variations, so global invertibility is not actually required (
Figure

1(c)
).
The intuitive picture we adopt is to think about how position on a curve in the data space corresponds to an axis-aligned variation in the latent space.
Suppose we have a base point
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which we compute its sources
\mathbf{s}_{0}=h(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, and suppose that we vary the
i
’th source a small amount
\Delta s_{i}
,
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}_{0}+\Delta s_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the
i
’th standard basis vector.
Because
h
may not be invertible, we cannot directly compute the new data point
\mathbf{x}=h^{-1}(\mathbf{s})
.
What we can do instead is search for a curve
\mathbf{x}(t)
that starts at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and ends at some
\mathbf{x}
so that the latent curve,
\mathbf{s}(t)=h(\mathbf{x}(t))
, is a straight line that starts at
\mathbf{s}_{0}
and ends at
\mathbf{s}
.
However, the standard ICA toolkit does not contain the necessary tools or even language for formalizing this.
Fortunately, Riemannian geometry does.

2.2
Riemannian geometry and normal coordinates

We review the necessary tools from Riemannian geometry following
[
54
,
55
]
, but adopt a more suggestive notation to make the connections to ICA as direct as possible.
See
Appendix

A
for a more detailed review.
We emphasize that every geometric object we introduce in this paper is parameterized and computed in the data space.

A Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M},g)
is a smooth manifold
\mathcal{M}
equipped with a positive-definite inner product
g
, called the
Riemannian metric
.
We focus on the setting where
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
because data is usually collected in a Euclidean space, but our approach is directly applicable to general smooth manifolds.
In this setting, the metric is represented by a symmetric, positive-definite matrix
g_{x}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
It is used to define a pointwise inner product between two tangent vectors
u
and
v
at some
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, whose components are represented by
u_{x},v_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively.
The inner product of
u
and
v
with respect to
g
is given by
g(u,v)=u_{x}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x})\,v_{x}
.

g
,
u
, and
v
are
tensors
, meaning that they exist independently of any coordinate system.
In particular,
u
and
v
are
(1,0)
-tensors, while
g
is a
(0,2)
-tensor.
These tensor types determine how their coordinate representations transform under a change of coordinates in order to preserve the meaning of the underlying objects.
If
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a smooth, invertible function where
\mathbf{x}=\phi(\mathbf{z})
, then the components of the tangent vectors and metric in
z
-coordinates are related by

u_{z}=\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{z})}{\partial\mathbf{z}}^{-1}u_{x},\quad v_{z}=\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{z})}{\partial\mathbf{z}}^{-1}v_{x},\quad g_{z}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{z})}{\partial\mathbf{z}}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{z})}{\partial\mathbf{z}}

(4)

These transformation rules preserve the inner product
g(u,v)=u_{x}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x})v_{x}=u_{z}^{\top}g_{z}(\mathbf{z})v_{z}
.
Additionally, this inner product induces a notion of
g
-orthogonality
.
Suppose
J_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a matrix whose columns represent components of
n
tangent vectors.
Then
J_{x}
is
g
-orthogonal if
J_{x}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x})\,J_{x}=I_{n}
.

A Riemannian metric determines distances on the manifold.
Let
\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be two points.
If the points are sufficiently close, then there exists a unique shortest path between them called a
geodesic
.
Geodesics are completely characterized by their base point
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and an initial velocity
v_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The map that sends the initial velocity to the point reached by the geodesic after
1
unit of time is called the
exponential map
, and is denoted by
\mathbf{x}=\exp_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(v_{x})
.
In a sufficiently small neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, the exponential map is invertible, and its inverse is called the
logarithmic map
,
\log_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(\mathbf{x})
, which recovers the initial velocity from an endpoint
\mathbf{x}
.

\log_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(\mathbf{x})
can be computed directly from the metric
g_{x}
, but is computationally expensive in general.
Under the Euclidean metric, the logarithmic map is simply
\log_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}
.

The preceding machinery leads to
Riemann normal coordinates
, which are an orthonormal coordinate system at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.
Normal coordinates are defined on a normal neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, where the exponential map is invertible
[
55
]
.
They are unique up to the choice of a
g
-orthogonal matrix that orients the coordinate system.
Let
J_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denote this orientation matrix.
Then the normal coordinate map, also called the
normal coordinate chart
, is given by

\displaystyle h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}(\mathbf{x}):=J_{x}^{-1}\log_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(\mathbf{x})

(5)

We denote these normal coordinates by
\mathbf{s}=h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}(\mathbf{x})
.
At the base point, the normal coordinate chart satisfies
\mathbf{0}=h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{s}}h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}^{-1}(\mathbf{0})=J_{x}
.

A useful property of normal coordinates is that the metric is Euclidean at the origin and has zero first derivatives there.
Let
g_{s}(\mathbf{0})=J_{x}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})J_{x}
be the components of the metric in normal coordinates at the origin.
Then the value, gradient, and Hessian of the log determinant of the metric are given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\det g_{s}(\mathbf{0})=0,\quad\frac{\partial\frac{1}{2}\log\det g_{s}(\mathbf{0})}{\partial\mathbf{s}}=\mathbf{0},\quad\frac{\partial^{2}\frac{1}{2}\log\det g_{s}(\mathbf{0})}{\partial\mathbf{s}\partial\mathbf{s}^{\top}}=-\frac{1}{3}\Ric_{s}(\mathbf{0})

(6)

where
\Ric_{s}(\mathbf{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the Ricci curvature tensor in normal coordinates at the origin.
The Ricci tensor is a
(0,2)
-tensor, and so it transforms according to
Equation

4
.
For simplicity, we use
Equation

6
as an operational definition of Ricci curvature.
In our main contributions, Ricci curvature appears as a correction to the second derivative of the change of variables formula that is needed on a curved manifold.

Lastly, the Hausdorff density
[
84
]

\rho_{x}(\mathbf{x})=p_{x}(\mathbf{x})/\sqrt{\det g_{x}(\mathbf{x})}
is an intrinsic density function that is defined with respect to the Riemannian volume element
dV_{g}=\sqrt{\det g_{x}(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}
.
The Hausdorff density gives a coordinate-independent way to express probability, which we use to derive a change of variables formula on a Riemannian manifold.
See
Appendix

A
for more details.

3
Method

In this section, we present our contributions.
Our method takes as input a probability density function,
p_{x}(\mathbf{x})
, and a Riemannian metric
g_{x}(\mathbf{x})
.
There are many ways to construct Riemannian metrics
[
31
]
.
For example, metrics can be constructed from point clouds using diffusion geometry
[
44
,
4
]
, from probability distributions
[
33
,
84
]
, or from generative models
[
1
,
23
]
.
Our method is motivated by the setting where one is given a pretrained encoder,
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for which the pullback metric is a natural choice.

Suppose
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a smooth, pretrained encoder and, for the moment, assume that
d=n
.
The pullback metric of the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
through
h
is given by
g_{x}(\mathbf{x})=\scriptstyle\tfrac{\partial h(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\tfrac{\partial h(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}
.
When
h
is locally invertible, this metric measures the length of a path in data space by the Euclidean length of its image under
h
.
This is consistent with the intuitive picture of factors of variation at the end of
Section

2.1
because local geodesic variations under the pullback metric correspond to linear changes in the latent space.
When
d\neq n
, the metric may be degenerate, so we adopt a simple heuristic to ensure that it has full rank.
We use the metric
g_{x}(\mathbf{x})=(1-\lambda)I_{n}+\lambda\scriptstyle\frac{\partial h(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\frac{\partial h(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}
, where
\lambda\in(0,1)
, to blend the Euclidean metric with the pullback metric.
In the rest of this section, we introduce our contributions using an abstract Riemannian metric.

3.1
Normal coordinates are adaptive latent variables

Recall that ICA requires an invertible encoder
h(\mathbf{x})\mapsto\mathbf{s}
.
Our first contribution is to interpret the normal coordinate chart from
Equation

5
as a
local
encoder for ICA.
Let
h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}
be the normal coordinate chart centered at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and oriented by
J_{x}
.
Because this chart maps the base point to the origin,
h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathbf{0}
, varying the
i
’th component by
\Delta s_{i}
gives
\mathbf{s}=\Delta s_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the
i
’th standard basis vector.
The corresponding data variation follows the radial geodesic
\mathbf{x}=\exp_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(J_{x}\mathbf{s})
.
Thus, the factors of variation through
\mathbf{x}_{0}
are geodesics under the metric, formalizing the intuitive picture we described at the end of
Section

2.1
.

Next, we need to understand how probability density fits into the picture.

h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}
is invertible in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, so we can pushforward the restriction of the data density to this neighborhood and apply the change of variables formula.
Therefore, by
Definition

1
, a normal coordinate chart disentangles the data near
\mathbf{x}_{0}
when the pushed-forward density factors across its coordinates.
This lets us define what it means for a metric to disentangle data on a neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.
However, such a disentangling neighborhood may not exist
[
51
,
78
]
.
Since we do not know which models fail to disentangle data a priori, we propose to check a necessary condition for disentanglement that we call
pointwise disentanglement
.

3.2
Pointwise disentanglement

Pointwise disentanglement examines whether a normal coordinate chart disentangles data directly at its base point.
To formalize this, we first need to express the change of variables formula at the base point.

Proposition 2

(Pointwise change of variables formula)
.

Let
g_{x}
be a Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the base point,
J_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a
g
-orthogonal matrix, and
h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}
be the normal coordinate chart.
At the origin, the change of variables formula is given by

\log p_{s}(\mathbf{0})=\log p_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})+\log|\det J_{x}|\quad\text{ where }\mathbf{0}=h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})

(7)

Additionally, the Hausdorff density at the base point equals the latent density,
\rho_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=p_{s}(\mathbf{0})
.

See
Appendix

B
for the derivation.
This expression is consistent with the standard change of variables formula in
Equation

3
because
J_{x}
is the Jacobian matrix of the map from latents to data,
h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}^{-1}(\mathbf{s})\mapsto\mathbf{x}
, at
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{0}
.

Proposition

2
lets us define pointwise disentanglement.

Definition 3

(Pointwise disentanglement)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a base point,
J_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a
g
-orthogonal matrix, and let
h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}
be the corresponding normal coordinate chart.

h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}
pointwise disentangles the data distribution
p_{x}
at the base point
\mathbf{x}_{0}
if the latent log-likelihood has a diagonal Hessian at the origin:

\frac{\partial^{2}\log p_{s}(\mathbf{0})}{\partial\mathbf{s}\partial\mathbf{s}^{\top}}\text{ is diagonal.}

(8)

It is easy to confirm that this condition follows from statistical independence by differentiating the latent log-likelihood twice.
This definition is closely related to
Definition

1
, except that pointwise disentanglement uses the normal coordinate chart in place of an explicit encoder, and the independence statement is only made to second order at a point instead of globally.
Like
Definition

1
, this definition says nothing about how to choose
J_{x}
to make the Hessian diagonal.
The next subsection shows how to choose
J_{x}
so that the recovered latents are pointwise disentangled.

3.3
Disentanglement tensor and Riemannian ICA

Next, we present our primary contribution, which is a tensor that captures pointwise disentanglement.
We call this tensor the
disentanglement tensor
because its diagonalization identifies a normal coordinate chart whose latents are pointwise disentangled.

(a)
RICA consists of a log map followed by a
g
-rotation.

(b)

Theorem 4

(Disentanglement tensor)
.

Let
p_{x}
and
g_{x}
be the probability density function of data and the metric on the data space, respectively, and let
\rho
denote the Hausdorff density.
The
disentanglement tensor
is defined as

\mathcal{D}=\nabla^{2}\log\rho-\tfrac{1}{3}\Ric

(9)

where
\nabla^{2}
is the covariant Hessian under the Levi-Civita connection and
\Ric
is the Ricci curvature tensor.
For any base point
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any
g
-orthogonal matrix
J_{x}
, let
h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}
be the corresponding normal coordinate chart and let
p_{s}=(h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}})_{\#}p_{x}
be the induced latent density on this normal coordinate neighborhood.
Then the Hessian of the normal log-likelihood is given by

\frac{\partial^{2}\log p_{s}(\mathbf{0})}{\partial\mathbf{s}\partial\mathbf{s}^{\top}}=\mathcal{D}_{s}(\mathbf{0})

(10)

where
\mathcal{D}_{s}(\mathbf{0})
is the matrix containing the components of the disentanglement tensor in the normal coordinate system.

The derivation in
Appendix

B
follows by differentiating
Equation

7
twice and identifying the resulting tensorial terms.
In practice, the disentanglement tensor can be computed in whichever coordinate system is most convenient and then transformed to normal coordinates using the
(0,2)
tensor transformation rule.
For example, in data-space coordinates, this computation is given by the following proposition.

Proposition 5

(Coordinate formula for the disentanglement tensor)
.

Let
p_{x}
and
g_{x}
be the probability density function and metric on data space, respectively.
Let
\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{k}_{ij}
denote the Christoffel symbols of the Levi-Civita connection induced by
g_{x}
at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, which are defined by

\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{k}_{ij}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}g_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{km}\left(\frac{\partial g_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})_{im}}{\partial x^{j}}+\frac{\partial g_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})_{jm}}{\partial x^{i}}-\frac{\partial g_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})_{ij}}{\partial x^{m}}\right)

(11)

where
g_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{km}
denotes the
(k,m)
index of the inverse matrix
g_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{-1}
.
The
(i,j)
component of the disentanglement tensor in data space coordinates is given by

\mathcal{D}_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})_{ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}r(\mathbf{x}_{0})}{\partial x^{i}\partial x^{j}}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{k}_{ij}\frac{\partial r(\mathbf{x}_{0})}{\partial x^{k}}-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{a}_{ij}}{\partial x^{a}}+\frac{1}{3}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{a}_{ib}\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{b}_{aj}

(12)

where
r(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\log p_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\frac{1}{3}\log\det g_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
.
Additionally, if
J_{x}
is a
g
-orthogonal matrix, then the components of the disentanglement tensor in normal coordinates
\mathbf{s}=h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}(\mathbf{x})
are given by

\mathcal{D}_{s}(\mathbf{0})={J_{x}}^{\top}\mathcal{D}_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0}){J_{x}}

(13)

See
Appendix

B
for the derivation.
This coordinate formula makes
\mathcal{D}
computable, but also shows why exact computation can be expensive.
In closed form, it requires second order derivatives of the metric and the log likelihood, which are not always tractable in practice.
We discuss these practical limitations in
Section

3.4
.
When
\mathcal{D}_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is available, pointwise disentanglement reduces to choosing
J_{x}
so that
Equation

13
yields a diagonal matrix.
This gives our algorithm for performing pointwise disentanglement.

Proposition 6

(RICA)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the coordinate matrix of the disentanglement tensor at the base point
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.
Since
\mathcal{D}_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is symmetric and
g_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is positive definite, there exists a
g
-orthogonal matrix
J^{*}_{x}
solving

{J^{*}_{x}}^{\top}\mathcal{D}_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\,J^{*}_{x}=\Lambda,\quad{J^{*}_{x}}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\,J^{*}_{x}=I_{n}

(14)

where
\Lambda
is diagonal.
Then
\mathbf{s}=h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J^{*}_{x}}(\mathbf{x})
is pointwise disentangled at the base point, and is identifiable up to sign flips and permutations if the entries of
\Lambda
are distinct.

See
Appendix

B
for the proof.

Proposition

6
gives an algorithm for performing pointwise disentanglement that is equivalent to solving a generalized eigenvalue problem, which differs from standard eigenvalue problems only in how orthogonality is defined
[
28
]
.
As a consequence, the uniqueness of the recovered latents depends on the uniqueness of the eigendecomposition of
\mathcal{D}_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
.

3.4
Practical limitations

RICA provides a theoretically justified algorithm for performing pointwise disentanglement with metrics induced by encoders such as those based on self-supervised learning
[
68
,
12
,
62
,
75
]
.
However, its computational cost is prohibitive for typical pretrained models encountered in practice because, under the pullback metric, computing
Equation

12
requires third-order automatic differentiation through the encoder.
Additionally, RICA requires the ability to compute the log likelihood of the data density in closed form, which is not always available.
We leave the development of a scalable empirical algorithm for future work.

4
Experiments

We validate our theory in a source recovery setting where we generate data from ground-truth sources and test the ability of RICA and the baselines to recover the sources.

4.1
Source recovery

We evaluate whether RICA can recover known sources better than ICA and a nonlinear ICA baseline.
This experiment is designed as a controlled validation of the theory.
Complete experimental details are given in
Section

C.2
.
We evaluate four tractable geometries: the sphere, torus, hyperbolic space, and SPD matrices.
To test the role that the coordinates used to represent the observations plays, we generate observations for each geometry in both an intrinsic coordinate chart and an alternate coordinate chart, which are specified in
Section

C.2
.
For each geometry, we fix a base point
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and randomly draw a
g
-orthonormal matrix
J_{x}
.
We then generate
N
paired samples
(\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{s}^{(\ell)})_{\ell=1}^{N}
from the following generative model:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\exp_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(J_{x}\mathbf{s}),\quad\text{where}\quad s_{i}\sim\operatorname{Logistic}(0,b_{i}),\quad b_{i}=b\,r_{s}^{\,i-1},\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,n

where
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a fixed base point,
J_{x}
is
g
-orthonormal at
\mathbf{x}_{0}

control the overall spread of

and the anisotropy of the sources, respectively.
For these source-recovery experiments, the problem scale is small enough that the disentanglement tensor can be computed exactly using

Each method we evaluate recovers sources

We evaluate these sources using the mean correlation coefficient (MCC)
[

\mathrm{MCC}\bigl(\mathbf{s},\hat{\mathbf{s}}\bigr)\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\,\max_{\pi\in S_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl|\mathrm{corr}\bigl(\hat{s}_{i},\,s_{\pi(i)}\bigr)\bigr|,\qquad\mathrm{TC}(\hat{\mathbf{s}})\;=\;\mathrm{KL}\Bigl[p_{\hat{s}}(\hat{\mathbf{s}})\,\Big\|\,\textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{\hat{s},i}(\hat{s}_{i})\Bigr]

(16)

where

is the set of permutations of

is the sample Pearson correlation across the
N

MCC equals

when the recovered sources match the ground truth up to signed permutation.

We estimate

with the nearest-neighbor estimator of
[

]
at

The methods we use to estimate the sources from observations are RICA

[

]

For FastICA and conformal nonlinear ICA, we recover sources from the latent spaces of their models.

We use a conformal model for the nonlinear ICA baseline because it has identifiability guarantees

[

5
Related work

There is no single definition of disentanglement, and the concept appears in many different contexts
[
80
]
.
A large portion of the literature studies generative models with structured latent variables.
This includes ICA and nonlinear ICA (
Section

2.1
), GAN-based methods
[
29
,
16
]
, and VAE-based methods
[
49
,
34
,
11
,
48
,
14
,
52
,
70
]
.
These approaches typically define factors as statistically independent latent variables that generate the data.
Other approaches define disentanglement without starting from statistical independence of latent coordinates.
Group-theoretic approaches characterize disentangled representations by how transformations act independently on latent subspaces
[
35
,
13
]
.
Causal approaches instead tie factors to mechanisms and interventions
[
76
,
86
,
73
]
.
RICA is closest to ICA and nonlinear ICA because it defines disentanglement through an invertible map from data to latent variables.
However, RICA finds local factors of variation from a metric and a density rather than from a global latent-variable model.

Recently, interpretability research in large language models has studied features that exhibit disentangled behavior
[
61
,
24
,
77
]
.
In particular, sparse autoencoders have been used to decompose neural representations into interpretable features
[
37
,
74
]
.
However, these features are not always isolated factors of variation
[
60
]
.
Although RICA is also motivated by interpretability, it does not offer a global interpretation of features in the way these methods do and instead recovers local factors of variation.
An interesting future research direction could be to construct global interpretations that locally agree with RICA.

Geometric tools are widely used in machine learning
[
8
,
31
,
23
,
84
]
.
Some methods study learning problems on manifolds other than
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
27
,
15
]
, whereas our experiments focus on Euclidean data spaces.
This focus does not restrict the applicability of RICA because normal coordinates exist on every smooth Riemannian manifold
[
55
]
, and even in Euclidean space, the choice of metric can induce nontrivial geometric structure.
Other methods learn structure from data, distributions, or generative models
[
31
,
4
,
33
,
84
,
1
,
44
]
.
RICA takes the geometry as input and can therefore be applied with the metrics learned by these methods.

The geometric methods most closely related to RICA are generalizations of PCA to manifolds.
Tangent PCA
[
66
]
, principal geodesic analysis
[
25
,
87
,
88
]
, and barycentric subspace analysis
[
67
]
extend PCA to manifold-valued data for dimensionality reduction.
Like RICA, these methods recover directions or subspaces that summarize local variation in the data.
However, they optimize variance or reconstruction criteria
[
66
,
26
]
, whereas RICA uses an eigendecomposition of the second derivatives of the log density in normal coordinates to find local factors of variation.

6
Conclusion

We introduced Riemannian ICA as a local geometric extension of ICA that replaces the global invertible generative model with a Riemannian metric and a data density.
This allows us to interpret normal coordinates around a base point as adaptive latent variables and factors of variation as geodesics.
Our central theoretical contribution is the disentanglement tensor,
\mathcal{D}=\nabla^{2}\log\rho-\tfrac{1}{3}\Ric
, whose eigenspaces identify pointwise disentangled directions.
This gives an algorithm for pointwise disentanglement by solving a generalized eigenvalue problem.
In a controlled source recovery setting where the metric, likelihood, and ground truth sources are known, RICA recovers the tangent space sources across several tractable manifolds and coordinate charts.
An open challenge is to scale RICA to modern pretrained encoders and determine whether the resulting local factors are useful for interpreting model representations.
If this challenge can be met, RICA may provide a route toward theoretically grounded interpretability of pretrained representations.

Appendix A
Background on Riemannian geometry

We briefly review the tools from Riemannian geometry that we use in Riemannian ICA, following
[
54
,
55
]
.
Throughout this appendix we use Einstein summation convention, so repeated upper and lower coordinate indices are summed unless stated otherwise.

Smooth manifolds

A
smooth manifold

\mathcal{M}

[
54
, Chapter 2]
is a space that can be locally parameterized by a set of coordinates on an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
When referring to an abstract manifold, we write
\mathsf{p}\in\mathcal{M}
for a point on the manifold.
We reserve
x
for the coordinate system in which we observe data, so that
\mathbf{x}=x(\mathsf{p})
represents a data point.
Regions of a manifold can be parameterized by different coordinate systems, and by definition of a smooth manifold, there exists a diffeomorphism between any two coordinate systems called a transition map.
For example, if
x
and
z
are two coordinate systems on a manifold
\mathcal{M}
, then the transition map between
\mathbf{z}=z(\mathsf{p})
and
\mathbf{x}=x(\mathsf{p})
is given by
\mathbf{x}=\phi(\mathbf{z})
, where
\phi=x\circ z^{-1}
.

A tangent vector
v\in T_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{M}

[
54
, Chapter 3]
is a geometric object representing a direction of infinitesimal change on the manifold that lives in the tangent space
T_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{M}
at the point
\mathsf{p}
.
In the
x
-coordinate system, we let
v_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the column vector of components of
v
, so that
v=v_{x}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}
.
Tangent vectors are coordinate-independent objects, so in a different coordinate system
z
, we have that
v=v_{z}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{i}}
, where
v_{x}=\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{z})}{\partial\mathbf{z}}\,v_{z}
, equivalently
v_{z}=\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{z})}{\partial\mathbf{z}}^{-1}v_{x}
, and
\phi(\mathbf{z})
denotes the transition map from
z
-coordinates to
x
-coordinates.

Riemannian manifolds

A
Riemannian manifold

[
55
]
is a smooth manifold equipped with a
Riemannian metric

[
54
, Chapter 13]
.
The metric assigns to each point
\mathsf{p}\in\mathcal{M}
a positive-definite inner product
g_{\mathsf{p}}\colon T_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{M}\times T_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, and this assignment varies smoothly with
\mathsf{p}
.
We denote the component matrix of
g_{\mathsf{p}}
in
x
-coordinates at
\mathbf{x}=x(\mathsf{p})
by
g_{x}(\mathbf{x})
.
The inner product of two tangent vectors
u,v\in T_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{M}
is then
g(u,v)=u_{x}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x})\,v_{x}
.
We write
g(u,v)
rather than
g_{\mathsf{p}}(u,v)
because the basepoint
\mathsf{p}
is already carried by
u
and
v
.
These components can be computed in the
z
-coordinate system as
g_{z}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{z})}{\partial\mathbf{z}}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x})\,\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{z})}{\partial\mathbf{z}}
.

A Riemannian metric induces a notion of orthogonality.
Two tangent vectors
u,v\in T_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{M}
are
g
-orthogonal if
g(u,v)=0
.
A set of tangent vectors
(v^{(1)},\ldots,v^{(n)})
at
\mathsf{p}
is
g
-orthonormal if
g(v^{(i)},v^{(j)})=\delta_{ij}
, so distinct vectors are
g
-orthogonal and each vector has unit
g
-norm.
An orthonormal basis of
T_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{M}
is a set of
g
-orthonormal tangent vectors that span the tangent space.
Suppose we extract the components of a
g
-orthonormal basis of tangent vectors and arrange them into a matrix
J_{x}
, where the
i
-th column contains the component vector
v_{x}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then we say that
J_{x}
is a
g
-orthogonal matrix and it satisfies
J_{x}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x})\,J_{x}=I_{n}
.
This equation also shows that its left inverse is
J_{x}^{-1}=J_{x}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x})
.

Tensors

The tangent vectors
u,v
and the metric
g
are
tensors
.
A tensor is a geometric object that exists independently of any coordinate system.
Each tensor has a type
(k,l)
, where
k
is the number of upper indices and
l
the number of lower indices of its components.
A tangent vector is a
(1,0)
-tensor, and so its component form is written as
v=v_{x}^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}
.
The metric is a
(0,2)
-tensor, and so its component form is written as
g=g_{x,ij}dx^{i}\otimes dx^{j}
.
The type fixes how the components of a tensor transform under a change of coordinates to keep the underlying object fixed while its components change.
We have already seen this transformation rule for tangent vectors and the metric.
The components of a
(1,0)
-tensor transform as
v_{z}=\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{z})}{\partial\mathbf{z}}^{-1}v_{x}
, and the components of a
(0,2)
-tensor transform as
g_{z}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{z})}{\partial\mathbf{z}}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x})\,\frac{\partial\phi(\mathbf{z})}{\partial\mathbf{z}}
.
Together these rules keep the inner product invariant, so that
g(u,v)=u_{x}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x})\,v_{x}=u_{z}^{\top}g_{z}(\mathbf{z})\,v_{z}
.

Geodesics

A
geodesic
, under the Levi-Civita connection
[
55
, Chapter 5]
, is a curve
\mathbf{x}(t)
with an initial velocity
v\propto\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}(t)}{\partial t}\big|_{t=0}
,
\|v\|_{g}=1
, that satisfies the geodesic equation
[
55
, Theorem 4.10]

\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{x}(t)}{\partial t^{2}}^{k}+\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x}(t))\,^{k}_{ij}\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}(t)}{\partial t}^{i}\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}(t)}{\partial t}^{j}=0\quad\forall k=1,\ldots,n

(17)

where
\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x})^{k}_{ij}
are the
Christoffel symbols
of
g
in the
x
-coordinate system, defined by

\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x})^{k}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}{g_{x}^{-1}(\mathbf{x})}^{kl}\left(\frac{\partial g_{x}(\mathbf{x})_{jl}}{\partial x^{i}}+\frac{\partial g_{x}(\mathbf{x})_{il}}{\partial x^{j}}-\frac{\partial g_{x}(\mathbf{x})_{ij}}{\partial x^{l}}\right)

(18)

The Christoffel symbols express how neighboring points of the manifold are “connected” (via the Levi-Civita connection
[
55
, Chapter 5]
) so that it is possible to define an abstract notion of a directional derivative on a Riemannian manifold that does not require a choice of coordinate system
[
55
, Chapter 4]
.

Exponential and logarithmic maps

The exponential map
\exp_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(v_{x})
is the point on the geodesic that is
1
unit of time away from
\mathbf{x}_{0}
with initial velocity
v_{x}
.
It can be computed by simulating the geodesic equation with initial velocity
v_{x}
and integrating for
1
unit of time.
The corresponding geodesic between nearby endpoints can also be characterized variationally.
For a nearby endpoint
\mathbf{x}
, let
\mathbf{x}^{*}(t)
denote the energy-minimizing curve from
\mathbf{x}_{0}
to
\mathbf{x}
.

\mathbf{x}^{*}(\cdot)=\arg\min_{\bm{\gamma}\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial\bm{\gamma}(t)^{\top}}{\partial t}\,g_{x}(\bm{\gamma}(t))\,\frac{\partial\bm{\gamma}(t)}{\partial t}\,dt\quad\text{subject to }\bm{\gamma}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0}\text{ and }\bm{\gamma}(1)=\mathbf{x}

(19)

In a normal neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, this curve is unique, and the logarithmic map recovers its initial velocity.

\log_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}^{*}(t)}{\partial t}\bigg|_{t=0}

(20)

Equivalently, the exponential map is the inverse operation that follows the unique geodesic with initial velocity
v_{x}
for
1
unit of time.

Riemann normal coordinates

Let
\mathsf{p}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
with coordinates
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and let
J_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a
g
-orthogonal matrix at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, satisfying
J_{x}^{\top}g_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\,J_{x}=I_{n}
.
The columns of
J_{x}
form an orthonormal basis of
T_{\mathsf{p}_{0}}\mathcal{M}
under the metric
g
.
Riemann normal coordinates
[
55
, Chapter 5]
form a coordinate system in which variations of the coordinates trace geodesics whose initial velocity vectors are the columns of
J_{x}
.
In a normal neighborhood of
\mathsf{p}_{0}
, the normal coordinates, denoted by
\mathbf{s}
, are defined by
\mathbf{s}=h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}(\mathbf{x}):=J_{x}^{-1}\log_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(\mathbf{x})
, where
\log_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}
is the logarithmic map.
At the origin, the value and first two derivatives of the normal coordinate chart are given by

h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathbf{0},\quad\frac{\partial h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}=J_{x}^{-1},\quad\frac{\partial^{2}h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}^{i}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}{\partial x^{j}\partial x^{k}}=\frac{\partial h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}^{i}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}{\partial x^{l}}\,\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{l}_{jk}

(21)

This means that given a coordinate description of a geometric object at a particular point, it is possible to explicitly construct the normal coordinate chart to perform computations.
An important consequence of this is that the inverse of the normal coordinate chart,
h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}^{-1}(\mathbf{s})\mapsto\mathbf{x}
, can be treated as a decoder from normal coordinates to the data space.
At the origin, the Jacobian of this decoder is
J_{x}
:

\frac{\partial h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}^{-1}(\mathbf{0})}{\partial\mathbf{s}}=J_{x},\quad\text{where }\mathbf{0}=h_{\scriptstyle\mathbf{x}_{0},J_{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})

(22)

Ricci Curvature Tensor

The Ricci curvature tensor is a
(0,2)
-tensor that is defined by

\displaystyle\Ric_{x}(\mathbf{x})_{ij}

\displaystyle=g_{x}(\mathbf{x})^{kl}\,R_{x}(\mathbf{x})_{kijl}

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x})^{k}_{ij}}{\partial x^{k}}-\frac{\partial\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x})^{k}_{ik}}{\partial x^{j}}+\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x})^{a}_{ab}\,\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x})^{b}_{ij}-\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x})^{a}_{ib}\,\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x})^{b}_{aj}

(24)

where
R_{x}(\mathbf{x})_{kijl}
is the
k,i,j,l
component of the Riemann curvature tensor evaluated at
\mathbf{x}
.
We will not discuss the Riemann curvature tensor here.
See
[
55
, Chapter 7]
for more details.
The curvature tensor appears in the second derivative of the metric components in normal coordinates at the origin.
In particular, the metric in normal coordinates at the origin is Euclidean to first order so that its value and first two derivatives are given by
[
81

g_{s}(\mathbf{0})_{ij}=\delta_{ij},\quad\frac{\partial g_{s}(\mathbf{0})_{ij}}{\partial s^{k}}=0,\quad\frac{\partial^{2}g_{s}(\mathbf{0})_{ij}}{\partial s^{k}\partial s^{l}}=\frac{1}{3}\bigl(R_{s}(\mathbf{0})_{kilj}+R_{s}(\mathbf{0})_{likj}\bigr)

(25)

This expression can be used to derive the value and first two derivatives of the log determinant of the metric in normal coordinates at the origin, which are given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\det g_{s}(\mathbf{0})=0,\quad\frac{\partial\frac{1}{2}\log\det g_{s}(\mathbf{0})}{\partial\mathbf{s}}=\mathbf{0},\quad\frac{\partial^{2}\frac{1}{2}\log\det g_{s}(\mathbf{0})}{\partial\mathbf{s}\partial\mathbf{s}^{\top}}=-\frac{1}{3}\Ric_{s}(\mathbf{0})

(26)

Through this equation and how it is used in
Appendix

, we interpret Ricci curvature as specifying how the change of variables formula for normal coordinates on a Riemannian manifold differs from its Euclidean counterpart.

Covariant Hessian

For a smooth scalar function
\psi\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, the
covariant Hessian

\nabla^{2}\psi(\mathsf{p}_{0})
at a basepoint
\mathsf{p}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
is a
(0,2)
-tensor.
It is given by

\nabla^{2}\psi(\mathsf{p}_{0})(u,v)=\Bigl(\frac{\partial^{2}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{0})}{\partial x^{i}\partial x^{j}}-\Gamma_{x}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{k}_{ij}\,\frac{\partial\psi(\mathbf{x}_{0})}{\partial x^{k}}\Bigr)u_{x}^{i}\,v_{x}^{j}=\frac{\partial^{2}\psi(\mathbf{0})}{\partial s^{i}\partial s^{j}}\,u_{s}^{i}\,v_{s}^{j}

(27)

The covariant Hessian is the coordinate-invariant generalization of the Hessian of a function on a Euclidean space.
In normal coordinates at the origin the Christoffel symbols vanish, so it reduces to the ordinary Hessian, as the second equality in
Equation

27
shows.

A_{\ell}
, and unconstrained raw vectors
\tilde{a}_{\ell},\tilde{b}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The parameters used in
Equation

49
are computed via
\alpha_{\ell}=\exp(4\tanh(\tilde{\eta}_{\ell}))
,
O_{\ell}=\exp(A_{\ell}-A_{\ell}^{\top})
,
a_{\ell}=2\sqrt{n}\tilde{a}_{\ell}/\|\tilde{a}_{\ell}\|
, and
b_{\ell}=2\sqrt{n}\tilde{b}_{\ell}/\|\tilde{b}_{\ell}\|
.

We train this Möbius flow by maximum likelihood under a logistic prior whose scales are matched to the source scales of the generative model.
Training runs for
30{,}000
AdamW steps at batch size
128
, and we return latents from the trained encoder.

NL-ICA performs worse than FastICA on nearly every cell, and the gap widens at
n\approx 64
because of the spread of the source scales.
With
r_{s}=0.85
the scales
b_{i}
are spread over two orders of magnitude at
n=32
and over four at
n=64
.
FastICA does not have this issue because it whitens the observations first, which puts every source on a common scale before separation.
NL-ICA, on the other hand, is trained by maximum likelihood directly on the raw anisotropic data and is only able to recover the large-scale sources.
