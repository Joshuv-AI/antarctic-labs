Abstract
. We develop new tools for determining the existence of models of specific Scott ranks under countability conditions. Using these, we improve a result of Sacks by showing that any counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture must have at least two models of every parameterized Scott rank—a result that contrasts with the unparameterized case, where minimal counterexamples have only one model at many ranks. We further prove that theories with fewer than continuum many models have trivial Scott spectra and provide a general, systematic classification of low Scott rank models when only countably many Σ α \Sigma_{\alpha} -types are realized. Additionally, we classify the Scott complexity spectra for many Ehrenfeucht theories, and prove the ω \omega -Vaught’s conjecture in this setting, answering an infinitary strengthening of a question of Pillay and Tanović. We demonstrate that the Scott complexity of prime models for ω \omega -stable first-order theories is commensurate with the complexity of the theory itself. Along the way, we apply our methods to concrete theories like p-groups, trees, and Boolean algebras, answering questions of Harris–Montalbán and Alvir–Csima–MacLean regarding specific structures.

Much of contemporary mathematics comes down to investigating the properties of structures that satisfy some list of axioms, what logicians call a theory.
One of the most fundamental of these properties is about counting:
If you write down a theory, how many structures of a fixed size satisfy it?
While investigating theories with only finitely many models, Robert Vaught conjectured that the number of countable models of a theory is countable or continuum, but never in between
[
33
]
.
In other terms, unlike general sets, the conjecture states that the countable isomorphism types of a fixed theory satisfy the continuum hypothesis without further set theoretic assumptions.
This problem has been open for over sixty years and continues to inspire work in various parts of mathematical logic, including model theory, descriptive set theory, and computability theory.
It is unclear what part of mathematical logic will resolve Vaught’s conjecture, or what further interactions its continued investigation will promote.

Important early progress on Vaught’s conjecture was achieved by Morley, who demonstrated that the number of countable models of a theory is countable, continuum, or exactly
\aleph_{1}
, the second smallest cardinal, and never anything else
[
24
]
.
Morley’s approach centrally informs many contemporary approaches to Vaught’s conjecture and similar model counting problems, including the work in this article.
Morley considered the
infinitary Vaught’s conjecture
, a stronger version that lets theories be axiomatized by a formula in infinitary logic,
L_{\omega_{1}\omega}
, instead of just a first-order theory.
This is the setting with which we presently concern ourselves; henceforth “Vaught’s conjecture” refers to the infinitary version.
This setting has the advantage that one can exploit properties of infinitary sentences not satisfied by finitary theories, for example, Scott’s theorem that every countable structure is characterized up to isomorphism by an
L_{\omega_{1}\omega}
sentence
[
30
]
.
Scott’s work allows for several invariants to be associated with a countable structure, all of which roughly measure the difficulty of describing the isomorphism type of the structure.
These invariants are often called the
Scott rank
or
Scott complexity
of the structure.
The field of Scott analysis aims to understand how these invariants relate to other important notions throughout logic, how to effectively calculate Scott invariants, and which sorts of Scott invariants emerge from models of a particular theory as a means of comparing them.
Scott analysis has emerged as an independently interesting field touching various parts of logic in addition to its usefulness for problems like Vaught’s conjecture (see the survey
[
10
]
for many examples and more background on Scott analysis).

Sacks considered Morley’s analysis closely and noted that every theory satisfying Vaught’s conjecture has an associated countable ordinal that witnesses this satisfaction
[
29
]
.
The
Vaught ordinal
of a theory,
vo(T)
, is the least ordinal that witnesses one of two behaviors.

(1)

The first possibility, corresponding to the continuum outcome, is that there are continuum many different formulas true of the various models of
T
at level
vo(T)
.

(2)

The second possibility, corresponding to the countable outcome, is that there are no models of Scott rank greater than
vo(T)
.

The existence of a countable Vaught ordinal for
T
is equivalent to
T
’s satisfaction of Vaught’s conjecture, whereas a counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture has
vo(T)=\omega_{1}
.

The present article concerns Scott analysis below the Vaught ordinal of a theory.
In other words, we consider theories
T
and ordinals
\alpha
such that
T
has models of Scott rank at least
\alpha
, but there are only countably many different
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
types realized by the models of
T
.
There are two ways to understand this effort.
The first is that we are attempting to understand Vaught’s conjecture.
A counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture, should it exist, would have
vo(T)=\omega_{1}
, so our analysis would apply to all levels of the quantifier-complexity hierarchy.
Through this effort, we obtain a better understanding of counterexamples to Vaught’s conjecture, whether in an attempt to construct one or an attempt to disprove their existence.
The second is to develop general Scott analytic tools for concrete theories of interest below their Vaught ordinal.
For example, the work of Harris and Montalbán
[
8
]
calculates that the Vaught ordinal of Boolean algebras is
\omega
and describes the structure of Boolean algebras up to finitely many alternations of quantifiers.
Similarly, work of the first two authors
[
6
]
explained the behavior of linear orderings below their Vaught ordinal of 3.
The present work provides tools for similar future efforts within a more abstract, widely applicable framework.
We understand our work in both contexts and emphasize applications in each framework.

We use notions of Scott rank introduced by Montalbán in
[
18
]
that we more precisely define in the preliminaries section.
More specifically, we refer to the
(unparamaterized) Scott rank
of a structure,
SR(\mathcal{M})
, and the
parametrized Scott rank
of a structure,
SR_{p}(\mathcal{M})
.
The difference between these notions is that the parameterized Scott rank allows for finitely many parameters to be named to aid in the description of the isomorphism type of the structure (e.g., a basis of a finite-dimensional vector space).
These notions relate closely to
Scott complexity
, or the syntactic complexity of the simplest Scott sentence of a structure.

We take care to note the variety of Scott invariants used because, unlike most previous work, we observe qualitative differences between the behavior of theories based on these details.
This is most apparent when it comes to counting models of a particular Scott rank in a hypothetical counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture.
This line of inquiry is due to Sacks
[
28
]
, who identifies two reasons to understand Vaught’s conjecture from this perspective.
The first is that there are counterexamples to strong versions of Vaught’s conjecture that allow more expressive logics in describing the theory and have very particular shapes from the perspective of Scott rank.
Sacks, giving credit to H. Friedman, describes the set of
1
-transitive linear orderings (those for which any pair of singletons is in the same automorphism orbit) as a
PC_{\omega_{1},\omega}
class with exactly
\aleph_{1}
models, yet there is only (exactly) one such model of each Scott rank.
Showing that this sort of Scott analytic behavior is impossible in
L_{\omega_{1},\omega}
demonstrates the futility of adapting such examples to a true
L_{\omega_{1},\omega}
counterexample.
The second reason to study this is that a possible route to proving Vaught’s conjecture is to consider a hypothetical counterexample and show that it must have far too many models at a given Scott rank.
Any progress towards “thickening” the profile of a theory that is a counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture by demonstrating the necessity of many models works towards this goal.
Sacks himself in
[
28
]
demonstrates that there must be multiple models of each
\Sigma_{2}^{1}
-admissible Scott rank, and this has not been previously improved since his original paper.
We offer the following improvement.

{restatable*}

If

We also demonstrate limits to this methodology by showing that if Vaught’s conjecture fails, then there must be a counterexample with exactly one model of unparameterized Scott rank

That said, this contrast offers a different approach to proving Vaught’s conjecture.
Although distinct, the parameterized and unparameterized Scott ranks are very closely related.
If it were possible to strengthen one of our proofs to show that either a) counterexamples to Vaught’s conjecture have at least two models of every unparameterized Scott rank or b) there must be a counterexample with exactly one model of each parameterized Scott rank, then Vaught’s conjecture would be proven by a direct contradiction.
Perhaps it is even possible to find a notion of rank that sits between parameterized and unparameterized Scott rank that would allow such a conclusion.
It is tantalizing to consider; the only thing stopping this proof idea from going through currently is the influence of a few parameters.
That said, it is not clear how to remove those parameters for now.

1.
Preliminaries

When speaking about
L_{\omega_{1}\omega}

Using this normal form, we can describe the complexity of a formula as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

We let
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{0}=\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{0}
be the class of finitary quantifier-free formulas. For countable ordinals
\alpha>0
and
\varphi\in L_{\omega_{1}\omega}

•

\varphi\in\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
if it is logically equivalent to a formula of the form
\mathop{\mathchoice{\bigvee\mkern-15.0mu\bigvee}{\bigvee\mkern-12.5mu\bigvee}{\bigvee\mkern-12.5mu\bigvee}{\bigvee\mkern-11.0mu\bigvee}}_{i}\exists\bar{x}\psi_{i}(\bar{x})
where
\psi_{i}\in\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\gamma_{i}}
for some
\gamma_{i}<\alpha
;

•

\varphi\in\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
if it is logically equivalent to a formula of the form
\mathop{\mathchoice{\bigwedge\mkern-15.0mu\bigwedge}{\bigwedge\mkern-12.5mu\bigwedge}{\bigwedge\mkern-12.5mu\bigwedge}{\bigwedge\mkern-11.0mu\bigwedge}}_{i}\forall\bar{x}\psi_{i}(\bar{x})
where
\psi_{i}\in\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\gamma_{i}}
for some
\gamma_{i}<\alpha
.

Furthermore,
\varphi\in d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
if there is
\psi\in\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
and
\theta\in\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
so that
\varphi
is logically equivalent to
\psi\land\theta

Note that the negation of a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}

\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
formula and vice-versa.
Also,
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha},\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}\subseteq d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha+1},\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha+1}
.
A fundamental theorem of Scott
[
30
]
is that every countable structure has an
L_{\omega_{1}\omega}
formula that describes it up to isomorphism among countable structures.
Such a formula for a structure
\mathcal{M}
is called a
Scott sentence
for
\mathcal{M}
.
This gives us a way to associate formulas with structures.
The ranking on formulas, therefore, gives us a ranking on structures by association.
This ranking generally measures the difficulty of describing the structure up to isomorphism.
To be more precise, we will work with three variations of this rank.
Montalban defined the first two in
[
18
]
.
The
(unparamaterized) Scott rank
of a structure,
SR(\mathcal{M})
, is given by the least
\alpha
such that
\mathcal{M}
has a
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha+1}
Scott sentence.
Meanwhile, the
paramaterized Scott rank
of a structure,
SR_{p}(\mathcal{M})
, is given by the least
\alpha
such that
\mathcal{M}
has a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha+2}
Scott sentence.
The parameterized Scott rank can also be understood as the minimal unparamaterized Scott rank of
\mathcal{M}
after taking some fixed finite set of parameters.
Both of these measures are robust in the sense that there are many other equivalent formulations of these notions.
Notable for our purposes is that
SR(\mathcal{M})
is also the least
\alpha
such that every automorphism orbit in
\mathcal{M}
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
definable.
This is further equivalent to saying that the
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
type of each tuple is supported by a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
formula.
Similarly,
SR_{p}(\mathcal{M})
is the least
\alpha
such that every automorphism orbit in
\mathcal{M}
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
definable over some fixed finite set of parameters.
We lastly refer to

,

, a finer invariant introduced in
[

]
.
There it is shown that every structure
\mathcal{M}
has a simplest Scott sentence of the form

or

for some ordinal

.
The Scott complexity of

is the complexity among
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha},\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
and

.
We note that infinite structures have Scott complexity

We also frequently use the notion of the

of a theory, in the vein of

Given a theory

The Vaught ordinal is so named because of its relationships to Vaught’s conjecture.

A useful tool to understand lower bounds for Scott rank or Scott complexity is the back-and-forth relations.

Definition 1.2
.

The

•

•

The relation

\mathcal{M}
and
\gamma<\beta

\exists

2.1.
Type omitting style results

In this subsection, we prove two of the major technical tools used in this paper.
In particular, we show how to construct a model of bounded Scott rank given sufficient countability conditions.
These insights are later used to produce concrete Scott analytic results, given assumptions that are based on counting.
We will use the following convention, introduced in
[
21
]
, to parsimoniously refer to theories without too many types realized among their models.

Definition 2.2
.

A theory
T
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
-small if there are only countably many
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
types realized among its models.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
T
is a counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture, then
T
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
small for all
\alpha<\omega_{1}
.

Proof.

Say that
T
is not
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
small for some
\alpha<\omega_{1}
.
Consider the equivalence relation on tuples in models of
T
given by
\equiv_{\alpha}
.
This is a Borel equivalence relation, so by Silver’s theorem
[
31
]
, it either has countably many or continuum many equivalence classes.
By assumption, it does not have countably many equivalence classes, so it has continuum many.
As every model of
T
only has countably many elements, it can only realize countably many
\equiv_{\alpha}
classes.
Therefore, there must be continuum many models of
T
, and so
T
satisfies Vaught’s conjecture.
∎

2.2.
Finding exact Scott complexities

The previous subsection was dedicated to constructing structures with given Scott analytic bounds.
This subsection provides a more exact analysis.
In particular, we aim to give conditions for the existence of structures that achieve exact Scott complexity invariants.
We begin with a counting condition that demonstrates the existence of
\Pi
Scott sentences.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
T\in\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}
has

\mathcal{B}\models T

\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}

\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}

\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}

T
is non-empty, as

must have a model of Scott rank greater than or equal to

\mathcal{B}\models T

\mathcal{B}\not\in S_{\beta}

\mathcal{B}
has Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}

\beta\in(\alpha,vo(T))

\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}

.

We can push this argument further to guarantee the existence of multiple models if we sacrifice some control over the exact Scott invariants of those models.
This involves carefully chaining multiple applications of
Scott Analysis below the Vaught Ordinal
and
Scott Analysis below the Vaught Ordinal
.

Lemma 2.5
.

If

2n
models of Scott sentence complexity at most
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+n+1}
and Scott rank at least
\beta
.

Proof.

Start with the structure
\mathcal{B}
with Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}
from
Lemma

2.4
. Let
\psi
be a Scott sentence for
\mathcal{B}
and consider
\neg S_{\beta}\land T\land\neg\psi
where
S_{\beta}
is the set from the proof of the above lemma. This is a consistent
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
formula as
T
has models of Scott complexity at least
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}
.
Scott Analysis below the Vaught Ordinal
gives a model of
\neg S_{\beta}\land T\land\neg\psi
with Scott complexity less than
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
. Call this model
\mathcal{A}
and its Scott sentence
\theta
. We then have that
\neg S_{\beta}\land\neg\psi\land\neg\theta
is a consistent
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}

\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}

\mathcal{B}_{i}

\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+i}

\mathcal{B}_{1}=\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{A}_{i}

\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+i+1}

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{A}
.
∎

We now examine the analogous proposition for
\Sigma

This does not work out as nicely as the general situation with
\Pi

Proposition 2.6
.

If
T
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
and has

\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
, or there exists infinitely many structures with parameterized Scott rank
\beta
.

Proof.

Let
R_{\beta}=\{\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{A}\models T\land SR_{p}(\mathcal{A})<\beta\}
. This set is countable as
T
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}
-small and axiomatized by a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}
formula given by the disjunction of all
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}
Scott sentences for models in
R_{\beta}
. The intersection of the complement of
R_{\beta}
with models of
T
is non-empty because
T
has a model of Scott rank at least
\beta+2
. So by
Scott Analysis below the Vaught Ordinal
, there is
\mathcal{B}_{0}\models T
, with
SR_{p}(\mathcal{B}_{0})\leq\beta
and
\mathcal{B}_{0}\not\in R_{\beta}
. Note that this is the same as saying
SR_{p}(\mathcal{B}_{0})=\beta
. Consider the case where
\mathcal{B}_{0},\cdots,\mathcal{B}_{i}
are models of
T
with parameterized Scott rank
\beta
that do not have Scott complexity exactly
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
. In this case, we note that the complement of
R_{\beta}
along with the condition that a model is not isomorphic to any of
\mathcal{B}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{B}_{i}
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
. So by
Scott Analysis below the Vaught Ordinal
, there is
\mathcal{B}_{i+1}\models T
, with
SR_{p}(\mathcal{B}_{i+1})=\beta
. Continuing in this fashion, there is either some
i
with
SC(\mathcal{B}_{i})=\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
, or the process continues giving infinitely many models with parameterized Scott rank
\beta
.
∎

Note that the above proposition is equally provable with the weaker assumption that
T
has some model of parameterized Scott rank at least
\beta
and is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}
small.
This makes the assumption more analogous to the unparameterized version.
The asymmetry comes from the use of the unparamaterized Scott rank in the definition of the Vaught ordinal.

Corollary 2.7
.

If
T
is
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}
and

, then there are at least two structures of parameterized Scott rank
\alpha
for every
\alpha\in[\beta,\gamma)
.

Proof.

Fix such an
\alpha
. By
Proposition

2.6
, we may assume that there is a structure of
T
with Scott complexity
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
. By
Scott Analysis below the Vaught Ordinal
, there is also a structure of
T
with Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}
.
∎

The above corollary yields a strengthening of Sack’s theorem
[
28
]
, who showed that a
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture must have two models of every
\Sigma_{2}^{1}
admissible Scott rank.

\twomodelsvc

We will see in the subsequent section that the above corollary fails for the unparameterized Scott rank.
Indeed, it fails at
\omega_{1}
many ranks.

3.
Labeling Back-and-Forth Classes

This section focuses on conclusions that can be drawn about the back-and-forth relations below the Vaught ordinal.
We begin with some general theory regarding the back-and-forth relations.
Combining this with the work of the previous section, we see four applications.
The first is a contrast to
Scott Analysis below the Vaught Ordinal
, showing that failure of Vaught’s conjecture implies the existence of a counterexample
T
with many ordinals
\alpha\in\omega_{1}
such that there is only one

with

We then provide a sharpening of

from the previous section, exploring the necessity of models whose parameterized Scott ranks differ from the unparamaterized Scott rank.
The third subsection gives a procedure to understand the classification of

-small setting, focusing on particular orderings as key examples.
Finally, we examine non-splitting types and Boolean algebras to answer a question of Harris and Montalbán.

Our first proposition has nothing to do with Vaught ordinals or other counting assumptions.
Instead, it is a generally useful fact regarding the behavior of the back-and-forth relations.

Say that

If

.

3.1.
Unparameterized ranks and Vaught’s conjecture

We now have established the needed tools to give an unparamaterized contrast to
Scott Analysis below the Vaught Ordinal
.
The key is to analyze a minimal counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture.
We define this notion before moving on.

Definition 3.3

(
[
7
]
)
.

T
is a
minimal counterexample
to Vaught’s conjecture if for any
\varphi\in L_{\omega_{1},\omega}
either
T\land\varphi
or
T\land\lnot\varphi
has only countably many models.

In
[
7
]
, Harnik and Makkai showed that if there is any counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture, then there must be a minimal one.

Theorem 3.5
.

Given
\varphi
a minimal counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture, there is a function
G:\omega_{1}\to\omega_{1}
with
G(\alpha)\geq\alpha
such that for all
\alpha\in\omega_{1}
,
\varphi
has exactly one model of unparameterized rank
G(\alpha)
.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha\in\omega_{1}
.
List the
\equiv_{\alpha}
-classes of
\varphi
as
C_{1,\alpha},C_{2,\alpha},\cdots
.
Note that there are countably many, as
\varphi
is a counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture.
Say that
C_{i,\alpha}
and
C_{j,\alpha}
have uncountably many models.
By
Lemma

3.2
,
C_{i,\alpha}
and
C_{j,\alpha}
are disjoint, axiomatizable subclasses of
\varphi
.
Because
\varphi
is a minimal counterexample, this is a contradiction.
Therefore, there is a unique
i
such that
C_{i,\alpha}
is uncountable.
For each
\alpha
we write
C_{\alpha}
to denote the unique uncountable
\equiv_{\alpha}
-class of
\varphi
.
Note that if
\lambda
is a limit ordinal, then
C_{\lambda}=\bigcap_{\beta<\lambda}C_{\beta}
, as each
\equiv_{\lambda}
-class is an intersection of
\equiv_{\beta}
classes and the
C_{\beta}
are the only ones of the appropriate size.

Define the following function
f:\omega_{1}\to\omega_{1}

f(\alpha)=\min\{\beta\in\omega_{1}|(\forall\mathcal{M}\not\in C_{\alpha})SR(\mathcal{M})<\beta\}.

This function is well-defined because there are only countably many
\mathcal{M}
with
\mathcal{M}\not\in C_{\alpha}
.
Consider a fixed point
f(\alpha)=\alpha
.
In this case, every model
\mathcal{N}
outside of
C_{\alpha}
has Scott rank below
\alpha
.
This means that every
\mathcal{M}
with
SR(\mathcal{M})\geq\alpha
has
\mathcal{M}\in C_{\alpha}
.
However, by
Corollary

3.1
,
C_{\alpha}
has at most one model
\mathcal{K}
with
SR(\mathcal{K})\leq\alpha
.
Because
C_{\alpha}
has more than one model, in fact,
SR(\mathcal{K})=\alpha
.
As there are no such models outside of
C_{\alpha}
, there is only one model of Scott rank
\alpha
and so
G(\alpha)=\alpha
has the required properties.
Furthermore, if for some
n\in\omega
,
f^{n}(\alpha)=f^{n+1}(\alpha)
, then setting
G(\alpha)=f^{n}(\alpha)
has the required properties as
f^{n}(\alpha)
is an
f
fixed point.

Consider instead the case where, for all
n
,
f^{n}(\alpha)<f^{n+1}(\alpha)
.
In this case, we claim that
G(\alpha)=\sup_{n\in\omega}f^{n}(\alpha)
has the required properties.
This comes down to showing that every model outside of
C_{G(\alpha)}
has Scott rank below
G(\alpha)
; from there, the argument above demonstrates the claim.
Let
\mathcal{N}\not\in C_{G(\alpha)}
.
Because
G(\alpha)
is a limit ordinal, this means that there is some
\beta<G(\alpha)
where
\mathcal{N}\not\in C_{\beta}
.
More saliently, there is an
n\in\omega
such that
\mathcal{N}\not\in C_{f^{n}(\alpha)}
.
By the definition of
f
, this gives that
SR(\mathcal{N})<f^{n+1}(\alpha)<G(\alpha)
, as desired.
∎

3.2.
Models for which parameters matter

We establish a sharpening of
Proposition

2.6
.
This stronger result allows us to conclude that there is no avoiding the existence of structures whose parameterized and nonparameterized Scott ranks differ in the context of countability.
For example, counterexamples to Vaught’s conjecture must have such models.

Proposition 3.6
.

If
T
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
and has
vo(T)>\beta+2
, then either there exists a structure with Scott complexity exactly
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
or there exists infinitely many structures with Scott complexity exactly
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}

Proof.

Fix a model
\mathcal{B}\models T
with Scott complexity at least
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
.
By
Lemma

3.2

E(\mathcal{B},\beta):=\{\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{A}\models T\land\mathcal{A}\equiv_{\beta}\mathcal{B}\}
is
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}
definable.
In particular,
T\land E(\mathcal{B},\beta)
is a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
theory that still has a model of Scott complexity at least
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
and is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}
small (indeed
vo(T\land E(\mathcal{B},\beta))>\beta+2
).
By applying
Proposition

2.6
to
T\land E(\mathcal{B},\beta)
, we obtain that there is either a model of Scott complexity
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
that is
\equiv_{\beta}
equivalent to
\mathcal{B}
or there are infinitely many such models with
SR_{p}(B_{i})=\beta
.
In the former case, we are done at once.
In the latter case, by
Corollary

3.1
, at most one of these structures has Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}
.
In the absence of any models of Scott complexity
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
, this means that the remaining infinitely many must have Scott complexity
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+1}
, as desired.
∎

Note that the above proposition works equally well with the assumption that
T
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}
-small and has a model of Scott complexity at least
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta+2}
, just like
Proposition

2.6
before it.
The claim mentioned about counterexamples to Vaught’s conjecture follows at once.

Corollary 3.7
.

If
T
is a counterexample to Vaught’s conjecture, then for every
\beta\in\omega_{1}
there is a structure
\mathcal{M}
such that
SR_{p}(\mathcal{M})=\beta<SR(\mathcal{M})
.

An unexpected consequence of
Proposition

3.6
is an entirely abstract resolution of a question posed by Alvir, Csima, and MacLean
[

, Question 5.2]
.
In particular, we show that there is an Abelian p-group with a non-
\Pi
Scott complexity.

\abelianpsigma

Proof.

Khisamiev showed that the theory of Abelian p-groups is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}-
small
[

]
.
It is well known that Abelian p-groups have models of arbitrarily high Scott rank (see e.g.
[

]
).
The result follows at once from
Proposition

3.6
.
∎

This is, in some sense, an unsatisfactory answer to the question.
Ideally, one would like to have an actual construction of such a group, as it is likely the concrete group could be modified to create non-
\Pi
Scott complexity at higher levels as well.
While it is now clear that a search for such a non-
\Pi
Scott complexity construction would not be in vain, we do not attempt the search here.
To be more explicit, we ask the following question, updating Alvir, Csima, and MacLean’s question with our new knowledge.

Question 1

Explicitly construct an Abelian p-group of parameterized Scott rank
1
and Scott rank greater than
1
. Which non-
\Pi
Scott complexities are attainable for Abelian p-groups?

Given the work of Harris and Montalbán
[

who calculated that the Vaught ordinal of Boolean algebras is

\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n+2}
Scott complexity Boolean algebras or infinitely many
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n+1}
Boolean algebras for each

.

We note that both of the possibilities in
Proposition

3.6
are necessary, and that this is apparent at even the lowest levels of the hierarchy.

Proposition 3.8
.

There is a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}
-small theory with a Scott complexity
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{3}
model but no Scott complexity
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}

\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}
-small theory with no Scott complexity
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{3}

d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}

Proof.

The first theory is given by
Example

2.2
. An example for the second theory is the theory of linear orderings.
It is shown in
[

]
that there is no Scott complexity
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{3}

d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}

The classic fact that linear orderings are
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}

[

20
, Section 4]
, even though the original proof does not reckon with these results at all.
In particular, Knight’s result that linear orderings are
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
-small, along with
Corollary

3.4
, immediately gives that
LO_{\leq 2}
is a minimal
3
-universal class (i.e.
(1)
implies
(2)
above).
Furthermore,
Corollary

3.1
yields that there can only be one element of
LO_{\leq 2}
in each
\equiv_{2}
class (i.e.
(1)
implies
(3)
above).
Lastly, to get an explicit description of
K
(i.e., (1) above), it suffices by
Corollary

3.4
to look at each
\equiv_{2}
class and find the simplest structure in it; this can be done by careful examination of Montalban’s explicit
\equiv_{2}
classification for linear orderings.
In total, this approach gives a general outline for moving from results that count types and back-and-forth equivalence classes to results that classify the simplest structures of a theory.

This idea also provides insights into theories where the Scott analysis is not as completely understood.
For example, we may examine the class of order-theoretic trees
Tr
.

Tr
is more formally defined as the set of partial orderings
P
such that the set of elements less than any given
x\in P
is linearly ordered.
It was shown by Richter
[
27
]
that
Tr
is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}
small.

Corollary

3.4
gives that there are countably many trees with Scott rank 1, one for each
\equiv_{1}
class.
This can be understood in terms of finite trees in the following, more structural manner.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
FinTr
denote the finite models of
Tr
.
Given a finite tuple
\bar{U}\in FinTr^{<\omega}
, let
C(\bar{U})\subseteq FinTr
be the elements of
FinTr
that do not take any embeddings from any elements of
\bar{U}
.

Theorem 3.10
.

There is an mapping
\Theta:Tr_{\leq 1}\to FinTr^{<\omega}
such that

(1)

T\cong S\iff\Theta(T)=\Theta(S)
,

(2)

Age(T)=C(\Theta(T))
,

(3)

\bar{U}\in Im(\Theta)
if and only if
C(\bar{U})
has the Joint Embedding Property and
\bar{U}
is an anti-chain.

Proof.

We begin by defining
\Theta
.
Fix
T\models Tr
and consider
X(T)
, the complement of
Age(T)
in
FinTr
.
By Kruskal’s tree theorem
[
15
]
,
FinTr
is well quasi-ordered by embeddability.
In particular,
X(T)
is empty or has finitely many embeddability-minimal elements.
If
X(T)
is empty, we let
\Theta(T)=\emptyset
(note that
C(\emptyset)=FinTr
).
If
X(T)
has finitely many embeddability-minimal elements, we let
\Theta(T)
be the collection of those elements.

We now prove the claimed properties for
\Theta
.
We begin with (2).
In the case that
\Theta(T)=\emptyset
, we have that
C(\Theta(T))=FinTr
.
We also have that
X(T)
is empty, or, what is the same,
Age(T)=FinTr
.
Now say that
\Theta(T)
is not empty.
Note that
X(T)
is upwards embeddability-closed.
In particular, it is the upward closure of its minimal elements,
\Theta(T)
.
This means that
Age(T)
is the complement of the embeddability cones above
\Theta(T)
.
By defintion, this gives that
Age(T)=C(\Theta(T))
.

We now show (1).
Because
Age(T)
is isomorphism invariant, it follows at once that
\Theta(T)
is too.
Now consider
T
and
S
with
\Theta(T)=\Theta(S)
.
By (2),
Age(T)
is determined by
\Theta(T)
, so we have that
Age(T)=Age(S)
.
We now demonstrate that
Age(T)=Age(S)
implies that
T\cong S
.
More specifically, we show that
T\equiv_{1}S
and note that
Corollary

3.1
yields
T\cong S
.
Say that the
\forall
-player plays
\bar{p}\in T
on their move.

\bar{p}
has an induced ordering on it, making it an element of
Age(T)
.
Because
Age(T)=Age(S)
, we obtain that there is a
\bar{q}\in S
with the same induced ordering.
The
\exists
-player may play this
\bar{q}
in response, winning the game.
A symmetrical strategy gives that
T\equiv_{1}S
, and so
T\cong S
as desired.

We lastly show (3).
If
\bar{U}\in Im(\Theta)
, then, by (2),
C(\bar{U})
is the age of a structure.
Therefore, it must have JEP.

\bar{U}
is an anti-chain because it is the set of minimal elements of
X(T)
for some structure
T
.
Say that
C(\bar{U})
has JEP and
\bar{U}
is an anti-chain.
If
F\in C(\bar{U})
and
G\subseteq F
, then certainly no elements of
\bar{U}
embed into
G
.
Therefore,
G\in C(\bar{U})
, and so
C(\bar{U})
has the Hereditary Property.
By Fraïssé’s theorem
[
11
, Chapter 7.1]
, there is some countable structure
\mathcal{M}
with
Age(\mathcal{M})=C(\bar{U})
.
Let
Age(\mathcal{A})\neq Age(\mathcal{M})
.
Then there is some
\bar{p}
in a finite arrangement in one of
\mathcal{A}
or
\mathcal{M}
not witnessed in the other structure.
Playing this tuple gives a winning strategy for the
\forall
-player to demonstrate that
\mathcal{A}\not\equiv_{1}\mathcal{M}
.
Taken together, we obtain that every structure 1-equivalent to
\mathcal{M}
has the same age.
By
Corollary

3.4
, we have that there is some
\mathcal{A}
with
SR(\mathcal{A})=1
and
\mathcal{A}\geq_{2}\mathcal{M}
.
It follows that
Age(\mathcal{A})=C(\bar{U})
, and so
\bar{U}=\Theta(\mathcal{A})
.
Therefore,
\bar{U}\in Im(\Theta)
, as desired.
∎

A more explicit description of the Scott rank 1 trees can therefore be given by answering the following combinatorial question, which we leave open.

Question 2
.

For which finite anti-chains
\bar{U}\in FinTr^{<\omega}
does
C(\bar{U})
have the Joint Embedding Property?

3.4.
Non-splitting types

In their investigation into Boolean algebras, Harris and Montalbán
[
8
]
described the collection of
n
-back-and-forth types for finite
n\in\omega
.
A direct consequence of their result is that they showed that the theory of Boolean algebras is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n}
small for every
n
, as mentioned in the introduction of this paper.
A fundamental feature of their study was an interest in the “descendant” relation.
Formally speaking, for
m\geq n
, they defined an
m
-type to be a descendant of an
n
-type if the
n
-type is a consequence of the
m
-type.
We give an analogous definition here, including the transfinite case.

Definition 3.5
.

Given
\alpha,\beta\in\omega_{1}
with
\alpha\geq\beta
, a theory
T\in L_{\omega_{1},\omega}
,
\mathcal{A}\models T
and
\mathcal{B}\models T
, we say that
Th_{\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}}(\mathcal{A})
is a
descendant
of
Th_{\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}}(\mathcal{B})
if
Th_{\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}}(\mathcal{A})=Th_{\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\beta}}(\mathcal{B})
.

We also make the following definition, analogous to Harris and Montalbán.

Definition 3.6
.

A
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
theory is
non-splitting
if it has exactly one
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha+1}
theory as a descendant.

Harris and Montalbán conjectured that, for Boolean algebras, non-splitting types at finite levels are exactly the isomorphism types, though they were unable to provide a general proof (instead only demonstrating the claim for
n=1,2,3,4
).
We show a more abstract result that follows immediately from our previous investigation, and confirm their full conjecture as a corollary.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
T
be a
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
-small theory and
\rho
be the
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
type of
\mathcal{A}\models T
.
If
\rho
is non-splitting, then for any
\mathcal{B}\models\rho\land T
,
\mathcal{B}\cong\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

By
Corollary

3.3
,
\rho
is labeled, or, in other words, there is a
\mathcal{C}
such that

\mathcal{C}\equiv_{\alpha}\mathcal{B}\equiv_{\alpha}\mathcal{A}

with
SR(\mathcal{C})\leq\alpha
.
Because
\rho
is non-splitting, we obtain that

\mathcal{C}\equiv_{\alpha+1}\mathcal{B}\equiv_{\alpha+1}\mathcal{A}.

Because
\mathcal{C}
has a
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha+1}
Scott sentence, we obtain that

\mathcal{C}\cong\mathcal{B}\cong\mathcal{A},

as desired.
∎

As promised, we confirm the conjecture from
[
8
, Page 21]
.

Corollary 3.12
.

Every non-splitting
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{n}
type of Boolean algebras is an isomorphism type.

Proof.

It was demonstrated in
[
8
]
that the theory of Boolean algebras is
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n}
-small.
By
Proposition

3.11
, the result follows at once.
∎

4.
Ehrenfeucht theories

We now turn our attention to Ehrenfeucht theories, or theories with only finitely many models.
The study of Ehrenfeucht theories is inextricably linked with the study of Vaught’s conjecture (see
[
25
]
).
Indeed, Vaught originally made his conjecture in
[
33
]
, where he excludes the possibility of a complete, first-order theory having exactly two models.
Fortunately, our methods give us insights into the Scott analytic behavior of Ehrenfeucht theories alongside the previously stated insights into Vaught’s conjecture.

Our goal is to explain which Scott complexities may arise as models of an Ehrenfeucht theory.
We formalize this using the Scott complexity counting functions introduced in
[
4
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

Given a theory
T\in L_{\omega_{1},\omega}
and a complexity
\Gamma\in\{\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha},d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha},\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\omega_{1}}
, the
Scott complexity counting function

I
is defined by

I(T,\Gamma)=|\{\mathcal{M}\models T:SSC(\mathcal{M})=\Gamma\}|.

Understanding the behavior of the Scott complexity counting function gives the finest possible Scott analysis for a given theory.
We will call the full behavior of the Scott complexity counting function the
Scott complexity spectrum
of the theory.
We aim to understand the possible Scott complexity spectra for Ehrenfeucht theories, starting with the case where
T
has exactly two models.
It is readily possible for an infinitary theory to have exactly two models (unlike the complete first-order case).
We state our results for
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
-theories but want to emphasize that our setting is still fully general. Given any
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{\alpha}
-theory
T
we can change the vocabulary using Morleyization to obtain a
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
theory whose models are precisely the expansions of models of
T
(see
[
23
, Chapter II.5]
).

Proposition 4.1
.

A
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
formula
T
with exactly two models has one of the following two Scott complexity spectra:

(1)

\displaystyle I(T,\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2})=2\text{ and }I(T,\Gamma)=0\text{ if $\Gamma\neq\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}$};

(2)

\displaystyle I(T,\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2})=1,I(T,d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2})=1,\text{ and }I(T,\Gamma)=0\text{ if $\Gamma\not\in\{\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2},d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}\}$.}

Both possibilities are realized.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.4
there must be at least one model
\mathcal{A}
of complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.
The other model
\mathcal{B}
is axiomatized by saying
\mathcal{B}\not\cong\mathcal{A}\land\mathcal{B}\models T
.
This is a
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
condition.
This leaves only the two possibilities described in the proposition statement.

To obtain a
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
theory
T
with two models, each of Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
, simply consider two structures in the same vocabulary with
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
Scott complexity and take as
T
the disjunction of their Scott sentences.

It remains to describe a
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
theory with two models, one of which has Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
and the other has Scott complexity
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.
Let
T
be in the vocabulary of the binary relation
<
stating that
<
is a linear ordering.
Furthermore,
(\forall x<y)\exists z\ x<z<y\in T
and
\forall x\exists y\ x<y\in T
, or in other words, any model of
T
must be a dense linear ordering without last element.
It is straightforward to confirm that
\eta
and
1+\eta
are the only two models of
T
.
At last, note that
1+\eta\leq_{2}\eta
(see
[
6
, Lemma 2.4]
), so the structures must have the desired complexities.
∎

We have a similar characterization of theories with three models.
It will be convenient for us to establish some operations on theories to describe how to realize the given Scott complexity spectra in this more complicated case.

Definition 4.2
.

Given a theory
T
over the vocabulary
L
,
T^{+}
is a new theory defined over the vocabulary
L\cup\{R,U\}
where
R
is a fresh binary relation symbol and
U
is a fresh unary relation symbol.

T^{+}
is the conjunction of

(1)

\forall x\ \lnot U(x)\to(T\land\forall y,z\lnot R(y,z))

(2)

for any relation
S\in L
,
\exists x\ U(x)\to\forall\bar{z}\lnot S(\bar{z})

(3)

\exists x\ U(x)\to\exists!y\ \lnot U(y)
and for any constant
c\in L
,
y=c

(4)

for any function
f\in L
,
\exists x\ U(x)\to\forall\bar{z}\ \lnot U(f(\bar{z}))

(5)

the existence of a
U
point implies the relation
R
defines a dense linear ordering without endpoints on the
U
points.

We now explain how changing
T
to
T^{+}
changes the Scott complexity counting function and the complexity of
T
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
T
over a vocabulary
L
,
I(T^{+},\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2})=I(T,\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2})+1
and
I(T^{+},\Gamma)=I(T,\Gamma)
for
\Gamma\neq\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.

Proof.

Consider an
M\models T^{+}
.
If
M\models\forall x\ \lnot U(x)
, then by the first axiom of
T^{+}
,
M
is a model of
T
with the
U
and
R
predicates empty.
If
M\models\exists x\ U(x)
, all relations from
L
are empty, there is exactly one
\lnot U
point
y
that serves as the interpretation of all constants and the range of all functions in
L
, and the
R
-order of the
U
-points is isomorphic to the ordering of the rationals.
In particular, there is exactly one model with
M\models\exists x\ U(x)
defined by the above axioms.
This model has Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
because it is infinite and the orbits are defined in a quantifier-free manner (by the unary relation
U
along with the
R
-order).
In total,
T^{+}
has a model for each model of
T
, plus one extra of Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
as required.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

If
T
is
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
, then so is
T^{+}
.

Proof.

This is immediate from counting quantifiers in the definition of
T^{+}
and the fact that the dense linear ordering of the rationals has a
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
Scott sentence.
∎

We next provide a binary operation on theories that preserves their more complicated models, but preserves the property of having exactly one
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
model.

Definition 4.3
.

Fix two theories
T
and
T^{\prime}
over vocabularies
L
and
L^{\prime}
with exactly one
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
model each
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
Define the theory
T^{\wedge}T^{\prime}
over a two sorted version of the vocabulary
L\sqcup L^{\prime}
as follows:

(1)

the first sort is a model of
T

(2)

the second sort is a model of
T^{\prime}

(3)

either the first sort is isomorphic to
\mathcal{M}
or the second sort is isomorphic to
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any theories
T
and
T^{\prime}
with unique
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
models
\mathcal{M}
, respectively,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
,
I(T^{\wedge}T^{\prime},\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2})=1
and
I(T^{\wedge}T^{\prime},\Gamma)=I(T,\Gamma)+I(T^{\prime},\Gamma)
for
\Gamma\neq\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.

Proof.

Note that for a two-sorted structure with no interaction between the sorts, it is straightforward to see that
SC((\mathcal{N},\mathcal{K}))=\max\{SC(\mathcal{N}),SC(\mathcal{K})\}
.
By the third axiom for
T^{\wedge}T^{\prime}
, either the first sort is a copy of
\mathcal{M}
or the second sort is a copy of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
We have
SC((\mathcal{M},\mathcal{K}))=SC(\mathcal{K})
, so for each model of
T^{\prime}
there is a corresponding model with the same complexity in
T^{\wedge}T^{\prime}
.
In the case that the first sort is not a copy of
\mathcal{M}
, the second is a copy of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and
SC(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{M}^{\prime})=SC(\mathcal{N})
.
So, each model of
T^{\prime}
has a corresponding model with the same complexity in
T^{\wedge}T^{\prime}
.
The only overlap between these two types of structures is
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}^{\prime})
, so there is still exactly one
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
model, but at each other complexity
I(T^{\wedge}T^{\prime},\Gamma)=I(T,\Gamma)+I(T^{\prime},\Gamma)
as desired.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

If
T
and
T^{\prime}
are
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2},
so is
T^{\wedge}T^{\prime}
.

Proof.

This is immediate from the definition of
T^{\wedge}T^{\prime}
.
∎

We also observe the following general fact for theories with finitely many models.
This will restrict some of the possible behaviors of the Scott complexity counting function.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
T
be
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
with finitely many models and
n\geq 2
.
If there is
\mathcal{M}
such that
\mathcal{M}\models T
and
SC(\mathcal{M})>d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n}
then there are models
\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2}\models T
with
SC(\mathcal{M}_{1})=\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n+1}
and
SC(\mathcal{M}_{2})=\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{n+1}
.

Proof.

As in
Lemma

2.4
, let
S_{n}=\{\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{S}\models T\land SC(\mathcal{S})\leq d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n}\}
.
Note that
S_{n}/{\cong}
is finite, so
S_{n}
is a
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n}
-definable.
Furthermore, as
Mod(T)\setminus S_{n}
is non-empty and both
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{n+1}
and
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n+1}
-definable,
by
Scott Analysis below the Vaught Ordinal
and
Scott Analysis below the Vaught Ordinal
, there are
\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2}\in Mod(T)\setminus S_{n}
with
SC(\mathcal{M}_{1})\leq\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n+1}
and
SC(\mathcal{M}_{2})\leq\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{n+1}
.
As
\mathcal{M}_{1}
has no
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n}
Scott sentence, it must be that
SC(\mathcal{M}_{1})=\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{n+1}
.
Symmetrically,
SC(\mathcal{M}_{2})=\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{n+1}
, giving the desired result.
∎

We are now ready to classify the behavior of the Scott complexity counting function when the theory has exactly three models.

Proposition 4.7
.

A
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
formula
T
with exactly three models has one of the following four Scott complexity spectra:
for some
n
with
1\leq n\leq 3

(1)-(3)

\displaystyle I(T,\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2})=n,I(T,d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2})=3-n,\text{and }I(T,\Gamma)=0\text{ if $\Gamma\not\in\{\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2},d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}\}$}

(4)

\displaystyle I(T,\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2})=1,I(T,\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{3})=1,I(T,\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{3})=1,\text{and }I(T,\Gamma)=0\text{ if $\Gamma\not\in\{\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2},\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{3},\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{3}\}$.}

All possibilities are realized.

Proof.

We first show that these are the only possibilities.
By
Lemma

2.4
there must be at least one model of
T
of complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.
By
Proposition

4.6
if there is any model with complexity above
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2},
there must be at least one model of complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{3}
and
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{3}.

As there are only three models in total, this forces the last spectrum to be the one in question.
Otherwise, all models are at most
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
, and each of those possibilities is covered by the first three listed spectra.

We now demonstrate how to realize each of the Scott spectra.
Let
S
be a
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
Scott sentence.

S^{++}
has
3
models of Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
and no more.
Let
D
be the theory from
Proposition

4.1
with exactly one structure of Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
and one structure of Scott complexity
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.

D^{+}
has
2
models of Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
and one of complexity
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.

D^{\wedge}D
has one model of Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
and two of complexity
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.
Lastly, the theory
C
from
Example

2.2
has three models; one of complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
, one of complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{3}
, and one of complexity
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{3}
.
∎

Proposition

4.6
also helps us obtain a general fact about theories with finitely many models, which partially generalizes what we have observed about theories with
2
or
3
models.

Proposition 4.8
.

Say that
T
is a
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
theory with
2N+1
(
2N+2
) models.
If
\mathcal{M}\models T
, then
SC(\mathcal{M})<d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{N+2}
(
SC(M)\leq d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{N+2}
).

Proof.

We begin with the case where
T
has
2N+1
models.
For the sake of contradiction, say that there is a
\mathcal{M}\models T
with
SC(\mathcal{M})\geq d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{N+2}>d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{N+1}
.
By
Proposition

4.6
, this means that for
2\leq k\leq N+1,
there are models
\mathcal{M}^{k}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}^{k}_{2}
with
SC(\mathcal{M}^{k}_{1})=\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{k+1}
and
SC(\mathcal{M}^{k}_{2})=\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{k+1}
.
Furthermore, by
Lemma

2.4
there must be at least one model
\mathcal{A}\models T
of complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.
In total, the
\mathcal{M}^{k}_{i}
and
A
are
2N+1
models, all of complexity strictly less than
d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{N+2}
.
This means that
\mathcal{M}
is not among these models, so
T
has at least
2N+2
models, a contradiction.

We now look at the case where
T
has
2N+2
models.
This will proceed similarly to the above case.
For the sake of contradiction, say that there is
\mathcal{M}\models T
with
SC(\mathcal{M})>d\mathchar 45\relax\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{N+2}
.
By
Proposition

4.6
, this means that for
2\leq k\leq N+2,
there are models

5.
Prime models in complete first-order theories

Vaught’s conjecture was originally stated in the context of complete first-order theories.
We now examine some consequences of our tools in this context.
In particular, we examine the general Scott analysis for prime models of first-order theories.
Perhaps unsurprisingly, prime models often have fairly tame Scott complexity.
Below, we say that a first-order theory is
\Pi_{m}
if all of its axioms are
\Pi_{m}
.
We also use the following standard definition.

Definition 5.1
.

A complete theory
T
is
\omega
-stable
if for every
\mathcal{M}\models T
and
A\subseteq M
,
\mathcal{M}
realizes at most
\max(\aleph_{0},|A|)
many types with parameters from
A
.

Note that
\omega
-stable theories only have countably many first-order types (over the empty set) realized among their models.
Furthermore, it is well known that
\omega
-stable theories have prime models
[
16
, Chapter 7]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
T
be a complete
\Pi_{2}
, first-order theory that is
\omega
-stable or has fewer than continuum many countable models.
The prime model
\mathcal{P}
must have Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.

Proof.

If
T
is
\omega
-stable or has fewer than continuum many countable models, then it has only countably many first-order types realized among its countable models.
Note that all
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}
formulas true of a tuple are disjunctions of finitary
\Sigma_{1}
formulas.
This means that the finitary type of a tuple determines its
\Sigma_{1}
type, and so
T
is
\Sigma_{1}
-small.
By
Scott Analysis below the Vaught Ordinal
,
T
must have a model
\mathcal{M}
of Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.
We have that
\mathcal{P}\preceq\mathcal{M}
, say via the elementary embedding
\sigma
.
In particular,
\mathcal{P}
embeds into
\mathcal{M}
in a way that preserves all
\Sigma^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}
formulas.
We claim that
\mathcal{P}\geq_{2}\mathcal{M}
.
The
\exists
-player may play along the elementary embedding
\sigma
on the first move of the back-and-forth game with the guarantee that
(\mathcal{P},\bar{a})\leq_{1}(\mathcal{M},\sigma(\bar{a}))
for their winning strategy.
Because
\mathcal{M}
has Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
, we see that
\mathcal{P}\cong\mathcal{M}
, and so
\mathcal{P}
has Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is of Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
, then
\mathcal{A}
is a prime model of its first-order theory.

Proof.

The structure
\mathcal{A}
has existentially definable orbits and thus every
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}
-type is existentially supported. In general, for any
\bar{a}

aut(\bar{a})=\{\bar{c}:\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}\text{-}tp(\bar{a})=\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}\text{-}tp(\bar{c})\}=\{\bar{c}:\Pi_{1}\text{-}tp(\bar{a})=\Pi_{1}\text{-}tp(\bar{c})\}\supseteq\{\bar{c}:tp(\bar{a})=tp(\bar{c})\},

and by the minimality of orbits it follows that any element sharing
\bar{a}
’s type must be automorphic. This shows that every first-order type is isolated and hence
\mathcal{A}
is countable atomic, and therefore also prime.
∎

We note that
Propositions

5.1
and
5.2
has been independently proven by Jason Block.

Corollary 5.3
.

A complete, first-order theory has at most one model of Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.

These observations limit the coding power of complete first-order theories.
Below, an effective bi-interpretation is a strong manner of transforming models of one theory into the models of another theory while preserving all salient properties studied in computable structure theory.
This includes invariants like Scott rank and Scott complexity.
More information about effective bi-interpretation can be found in
[
22
, Chapter VI.4]

Our corollary only needs the fact that effective bi-interpretation preserves (unparamaterized) Scott rank, so it also applies to any notion of reduction with this property.

Corollary 5.4
.

If
T
is a complete first-order theory, then
T
is not on top for effective bi-interpretation or any other reduction notion that preserves (unparamaterized) Scott rank.

Proof.

As seen in

T
has only one

Therefore,
T
can have at most one model of Scott rank 1 by

.
Therefore,
T
cannot accept an effective bi-interpretation or any other reduction that preserves Scott rank from a theory with more than one model of Scott rank 1.
∎

Note that the

Proposition 5.5

There is a complete,
\Pi_{2}

Proof.

\mathcal{M}\models\lnot P_{n}(x)

\varphi
that is not automorphic of
x
.
This yields the desired contradiction, so
\mathcal{M}
has no models of Scott complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}

∎

Proposition

5.1
iterates up the arithmetic hierarchy in the expected manner.

\pinprime

Proof.

Say that
T
is defined over the vocabulary
L
.
We Morleyize
L
to include new predicates for all
\Sigma_{n}
sets.
This can be done in a manner where the definitions of each new predicate are
\Pi_{2}
(see
[
23
]
Definition II.25).
In particular, we obtain a
\Pi_{2}
theory
T^{\prime}
that is an expansion by definitions of
T
over the vocabulary
L^{\prime}\supseteq L
.
If
T
is
\omega
-stable, as
T^{\prime}
is an expansion by definitions,
T^{\prime}
is also
\omega
-stable.
If
T
is complete and has fewer than continuum many countable models,
T^{\prime}
similarly is complete and has fewer than continuum many countable models.
By
Proposition

5.1
,
T^{\prime}
has a prime model
P^{\prime}
with complexity
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
.
Let
P
be the reduct of
P^{\prime}
with
P\models T
.
Consider the type of some
x\in P
.
In
P^{\prime}
, the
L^{\prime}
-type of
x
is isolated by some
\varphi\in L^{\prime}
, because
P^{\prime}
is a prime model.
Over
T^{\prime}
, we can expand the
L^{\prime}
predicates in
\varphi
to their definitions in
L
.
This gives a formula
\hat{\varphi}\in L
that isolates the
L^{\prime}
-type of
x
in
P^{\prime}
.
In particular,
\hat{\varphi}
is an
L
-formula that isolates the
L
-type of
x
in
P
.
Therefore, every type realized in
P
is isolated, so
P
is prime.
Consider now
\chi
, the
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
Scott sentence for
P
.
Proceeding as above, we may define
\hat{\chi}
, an
L
-formula equivalent to
\chi
, and therefore also a Scott sentence for
P
.
Furthermore, as each quantifier-free formula in
\chi
is replaced by a formula of quantifier depth
n
,
\hat{\chi}
is
\Pi^{\mathrm{in}}_{n+2}
.
Lastly,
\chi
is an
L
-Scott sentence for
P^{\prime}
, so it is also a Scott sentence for
P
, as desired.
∎

\Sigma_{n}
for any
n
. An example of models where this Scott rank is attained are prime models of non-standard completions of Peano arithmetic

, see also
