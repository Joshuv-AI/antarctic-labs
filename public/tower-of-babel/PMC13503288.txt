KLF12-mediated suppression of androgen receptor accelerates metastasis in endometrioid endometrial carcinoma by activating epithelial–mesenchymal transition
Authors: Jian Li, Ling Zhou, Sihui Yang, Yanping Zhu, Haimeng Shi, Feifei Guo, Xiangyi Kong, Lulu Wang, Mengdan Miao, Qi Guo, Ruilin Chen, Tong Yan, Junnan Wang, Qi Liu, Ling Ding, Huaijun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFor patients with advanced endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (EEC), prognosis remains poor due to the lack of effective therapeutic targets. Loss of androgen receptor (AR) expression is associated with the progression of EEC. However, the regulatory mechanisms remain unknown, limiting its application in prognostic prediction and targeted therapy. In this study, overexpression of KLF12 or loss of AR was strongly correlated with advanced clinical stage and poor patient outcomes. Functional experiments demonstrated that KLF12 upregulation suppressed AR expression and promoted EEC metastasis by enhancing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) both in vitro and in vivo. As determined by bioinformatics predictions and CUT&Tag analysis, KLF12 suppressed the transcriptional activity of the AR gene by binding to the promoter and intronic regions. These direct interactions were further confirmed through electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA), ChIP-PCR, ChIP-qPCR and dual-luciferase reporter assays. This study identifies a novel KLF12–AR regulatory axis driving EEC metastasis and provides potential molecular targets for therapeutic intervention and prognostic evaluation.Subject terms: Endometrial cancer, Transcription, Nuclear receptors

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma ranks as the sixth most common malignancy among women worldwide. The American Cancer Society released data showing an increase in the frequency of endometrial cancer, with approximately 66,200 new cases and 13,303 fatalities in the United States in 2023. However, the survival rates for these women have not demonstrated any significant improvement, primarily because of an incomplete understanding of the disease’s pathogenesis and the limited efficacy of treatments for advanced or recurrent cases [1]. About 80% of endometrial cancers are known to be endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (EEC), which are thought to be related to excessive or unregulated estrogen stimulation [2]. In recent years, regarding the clinical therapy of EEC, progestins or aromatase inhibitors have been utilized extensively, with a reported clinical response rate of 21.6% and 10%, respectively [3, 4]. However, their efficacy remains limited in patients with advanced or recurrent disease [5]. The coordination of cyclical changes in the normal endometrium by progestins, estrogens, androgens, and their receptors suggests the crucial role of androgens and their receptors in developing endometrial cancer. Most current EEC research overlooks the significance of androgens and their receptors in favor of concentrating on estrogen, progesterone, and their receptors. Investigating the biological activities and processes by which androgens and androgen receptors (AR) function in the EEC may provide new theoretical knowledge for understanding the pathogenesis and treatment of endometrial carcinoma.

Androgen stimulation leads to the dissociation of AR from its chaperone proteins, resulting in the formation of dimers that translocate to the nucleus to regulate transcription [2]. AR has a role in several pathogenic processes in malignancies, such as invasion, proliferation, cancer cell stemness, and chemoresistance. Recent research suggests that dihydrotestosterone, a high-affinity ligand for AR, may prevent the proliferation of EEC cells [6]. Moreover, AR expression levels are correlated with the invasiveness and metastatic potential of endometrial carcinoma, with reduced AR expression in endometrial carcinoma being associated with lower survival rates [7]. Therefore, AR serves as an essential marker for assessing the response to treatment and predicting prognosis in advanced endometrial cancer. Elucidating the mechanisms responsible for the decrease in AR expression during the progression of endometrial carcinoma is crucial for developing precise treatment approaches.

KLF12 is a member of the KLF family and is involved in controlling transcription in eukaryotic cells. It also has a significant impact on the angiogenesis, invasion, migration, and proliferation of different types of tumor cells [8–11]. Previous research has demonstrated that KLF12 expression increases with cancer staging in endometrial carcinoma tissues and is correlated with patient prognosis. Furthermore, KLF12 suppresses apoptosis while promoting proliferation and metastasis of EEC cells [12]. CUT&Tag analysis revealed the genomic binding landscape of KLF12, suggesting potential regulatory occupancy at the AR locus. These studies suggest that KLF12 could play a major role as a repressive factor in suppressing AR expression.

This work established for the first time that increased KLF12 expression and reduced expression of AR stimulate the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), facilitating EEC invasion and metastasis. Next, a negative correlation was established between KLF12 and AR expression. Finally, it was demonstrated that KLF12 directly binds to the promoter and intron regions of the AR gene, inhibiting AR transcription. This study elucidated the biological role of AR in EEC and the molecular mechanism by which KLF12 regulates AR, providing a new theoretical basis for endocrine and molecular targeted therapies in EEC.

Materials and methods

Cells and patient samples

MFE296 and AN3CA cell lines were acquired from Cobioer Biosciences. HEC-1B and RL95-2 cell lines were acquired from the Cell Research Center at Shanghai Institutes for Biological Science. All cell lines were maintained in DMEM (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10%, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. We selected the MFE296, AN3CA, HEC-1B, and RL95-2 cell lines based on their differential expression profiles of AR and KLF12. MFE296 cells exhibit strong AR expression, while AN3CA cells show moderate AR positivity, lower than that in MFE296. In contrast, HEC-1B cells display negative AR expression. All four cell lines express KLF12 at varying levels.

Between 2018 and 2020, endometrial tissue samples were collected from 79 pathologically identified EEC patients at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital approved the study. Informed consent was obtained from each patient. The original pathology reports were used to establish the tumor histology, tumor involvement, and stage [using the surgical staging guidelines from the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO)].

Lentivirus infection

The viral solution was used to infect MFE296, AN3CA, HEC-1B, and RL95-2 cells. In MFE296 cells, KLF12 was overexpressed and AR was knocked down. In AN3CA cells, KLF12 and AR were knocked down, and AR was also overexpressed. In HEC-1B cells, AR was overexpressed and KLF12 was knocked down, while in RL95-2 cells, KLF12 was overexpressed. Single-cell clones were isolated by limiting dilution. The cells transfected with the corresponding plasmids were defined as follows: MFE296 OE-KLF12, MFE296 Vector, MFE296 shAR, MFE296 shCtrl, AN3CA shKLF12, AN3CA shAR, AN3CA shCtrl, AN3CA OE-AR, AN3CA Vector, HEC-1B shKLF12, HEC-1B shCtrl, HEC-1B OE-AR, HEC-1B Vector, RL95-2 OE-KLF12, and RL95-2 Vector.

Cell transwell assays

Matrigel was 1:10 diluted with FBS-free DMEM and added to the upper chamber. The cells were added to the upper chamber. A total of 2 × 104 cells were introduced to each well. A total of 600 μL of medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. The cells on the lower side were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The migration of cells was conducted in chambers devoid of Matrigel, and the rest of the procedure was the same as in the transwell invasion assay.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

After extracting total RNA from the cells, 1 μg of total RNA was converted to cDNA using reverse transcription kits (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Using the MyiQ Single Color Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), qRT-PCR was performed. Using GAPDH as an internal reference, the 2–ΔΔCT technique was used to determine the relative expression of AR and KLF12 mRNA. The primer sequences used for qPCR were provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed with appropriate dilutions of specific primary antibodies. AR (5153S, Cell Signaling Technology), KLF12 (ab129459, Abcam), Snail1 (13099-1-AP, Proteintech), MMP2 (40994S, Cell Signaling Technology), E-cadherin (3195, Cell Signaling Technology), β-Tubulin (5568, Cell Signaling Technology) and GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech).

Establishment of a mouse EEC metastasis model

All animal studies were authorized by the animal ethics committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital and carried out in compliance with the relevant regulations and guidelines. Each group of cells was administered into the female BALB/c-nude mice at the right axillary region. After four weeks, the tumor was removed and cut into 1 × 1 × 3 mm3 strips. A total of 24 six-week-old female BALB/c-nude mice were randomly assigned to eight groups, with three animals in each group. The tumor tissue was transplanted into the uteri of mice. Four weeks later, the mice were scanned by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI, Bruker BioSpec 94/20 USR) to detect metastases. MRI scans were acquired with a slice thickness of 1.0 mm. For each mouse, ten consecutive anatomically matched slices centered at the porta hepatis were analyzed, and metastatic foci were counted for statistical comparison. Mice were anesthetized and sacrificed. The orthotopic uterine tumors and abdominal metastases were examined. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured with a digital caliper, and tumor volume (V) was calculated using the formula V = (L × W²)/2. Tumor weight was recorded, and representative gross images were captured.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation technology (CUT&Tag)

The Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (Vazyme TD904) was used to perform the CUT&Tag assay. Cells were adsorbed using concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads. The primary antibody (ab129459, Abcam) and corresponding secondary antibody were used to bind the target protein, and the pG-Tn5/pA-Tn5 transposon was employed to cleave DNA sequences near the target protein. PCR was used to purify and amplify DNA. The samples underwent Illumina sequencing.

Nuclear extract preparation and electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

Nuclear extracts were prepared from MFE296 cells using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (E101, Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Double-stranded DNA oligonucleotides were biotin-labeled using the EMSA Probe Biotin Labeling Kit (GS008, Beyotime). Binding reactions were performed at room temperature for 30 min using the Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (GS009, Beyotime). For cold competition assays, a 50-fold molar excess of unlabeled probe was added to the reaction mixture prior to the addition of the biotin-labeled probe. For supershift assays, 1 μg of anti-KLF12 antibody (ab129459, Abcam) was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature before electrophoresis. Samples were resolved on 6% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gels, transferred onto nylon membranes, and detected according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA probe sequences used for wild-type and mutant were provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP assays were performed using the SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads, #9003, Cell Signaling Technology) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. MFE296 cells were crosslinked with formaldehyde, lysed, and chromatin was partially digested with Micrococcal Nuclease to generate fragments of ~1‒5 nucleosomes in length. Immunoprecipitation was carried out using the anti-KLF12 antibody (ab129459, Abcam) and Protein G magnetic beads, with Normal Rabbit IgG (#2729, CST) as a negative control and Histone H3 (D2B12) XP® Rabbit mAb (ChIP Formulated, #4620, CST) as a positive control. After reversal of cross-links, DNA was purified using spin columns and analyzed by PCR and qPCR with specific primers for the AR promoter and AR intronic regions. ChIP enrichment was calculated as the percentage of input DNA (% input). The primer sequences used for PCR and qPCR were provided in Supplementary Tables S3, S4.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The AR promoter region sequence was inserted into the pGL3-basic luciferase vector, resulting in the creation of the pGL3-basic ARwt construct. The sequence of the AR intronic region was inserted into the pGL3-promoter luciferase vector (pGL3-promoter ARwt). The primers used for cloning the AR promoter sequence were: forward, 5’-ATCTGCGATCTAAGTAAGCTTTTCTCCAAAGCCACTAGGCA-3’; reverse, 5’-CAGTACCGGAATGCCAAGCTTGAGGTGGAGAGCAAATGCAAC-3’. The forward 5’-AGATAGGATTCCTCTGGGGTCC-3’ and the reverse 5’-TGGACCACTATACGTCCCAGTTAC-3’ primer sequences were utilized to clone the AR intronic sequence from the genomic DNA of MFE296.

Site-directional mutational analysis

The AR promoter sequence was mutated by site-directed mutagenesis using pGL3-basic ARwt as a template (pGL3-basic ARmut). The primer sequence for mutating the AR promoter sequence was 5’- CAGGCGTTAGCGCGCGGTGAGGGCTCCGGAGAAAAGGAAA-3’, with mutated nucleotides underlined. The AR intronic sequence was mutated by site-directed mutagenesis, using the pGL3-promoter ARwt as a template (pGL3-promoter ARmut). The AR intronic sequence was mutated using the primer sequences 5’- AGTGGATGGCAGTGATATCTTGGGTGCGTTGAGGGAAGTG -3’, where the mutated nucleotides were underlined. DNA sequencing confirmed that the mutated nucleotides were accurate.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were statistically analyzed by SPSS version 17.0 (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois, United States). Data are presented as the mean ± SD. Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA was used to analyze the differences between the experimental groups. The Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate the correlation between KLF12 or AR expression levels and clinicopathological factors. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to compare the survival rate among the groups. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and p < 0.01 was regarded as highly significant.

Western blot analysis confirmed AR overexpression in AN3CA and HEC-1B cells (Fig. 1D, E and Supplementary Fig. S1A, B) and AR knockdown in MFE296 and AN3CA cells (Fig. 1F, G and Supplementary Fig. S1C, D). Using CCK-8, flow cytometry, and Transwell assays, we evaluated the biological functions of AR in EEC cells, including proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion. The results showed that modulation of AR expression did not significantly affect the proliferation of MFE296, AN3CA, or HEC-1B cells (Supplementary Fig. S2A–D), but markedly influenced apoptosis, migration, and invasion. Specifically, AR overexpression in AN3CA and HEC-1B cells significantly promoted apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. S3A, B) and inhibited migration and invasion (Fig. 1D, E and Supplementary Fig. S2E, F). In contrast, AR knockdown in MFE296 and AN3CA cells enhanced migration and invasion (Fig. 1F, G and Supplementary Fig. S2G, H). Notably, in MFE296 cells, AR knockdown caused a slight reduction in apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. S3C), whereas this effect was not observed in AN3CA cells (Supplementary Fig. S3D).

To further evaluate the tumor-suppressive function of AR in vivo, orthotopic endometrial cancer models were established in BALB/c nude mice using tumor tissues derived from AN3CA Vector, AN3CA OE-AR, MFE296 shCtrl, and MFE296 shAR cells. Tumor growth in the uterus was monitored for four weeks. Representative images of orthotopic tumors and intra-abdominal metastases, including liver lesions confirmed by MRI and H&E staining, are shown in Fig. 1H–K. Quantitative analyses revealed no significant differences in tumor volume or weight among the groups (Fig. 1L, N). However, the AN3CA OE-AR group exhibited fewer liver metastatic nodules (Fig. 1M), whereas the MFE296 shAR group showed a markedly increased metastatic burden compared with their respective controls (Fig. 1O).

Together, these findings identify AR as a suppressor of EEC metastasis, with limited influence on primary tumor growth.

KLF12 and AR regulate EEC cell migration and invasion through EMT induction

KLF12 and AR exhibit opposite impacts on EEC cell invasion and migration. Furthermore, modifications in KLF12 and AR led to changes in cellular morphology. AN3CA cells, derived from metastatic lymph nodes of EEC, initially had a spindle-shaped morphology. After the overexpression of AR or knockdown of KLF12, the cells transformed and transitioned to a cobblestone-like morphology (Fig. 3A, B). The normal polygonal, cobblestone morphology of epithelial cells was lost, and a spindle-shaped mesenchymal morphology was acquired in MFE296 cells as the result of AR knockdown or overexpression of KLF12 (Fig. 3C, D).

This morphological change aligns with the process of EMT. Thus, it was speculated that these transformations in EEC cells were associated with EMT activation. The expression of E-cadherin, Snail1, and MMP2 was determined by Western blotting. The AN3CA OE-AR cells (Fig. 3E) and AN3CA shKLF12 cells (Fig. 3F) exhibited reduced expression levels of Snail1 and MMP2 while showing elevated expression levels of E-cadherin. Both MFE296 shAR cells (Fig. 3G) and MFE296 OE-KLF12 cells (Fig. 3H) showed increased expression of Snail1 and MMP2 and conversely showed decreased expression of E-cadherin.

Furthermore, IHC analysis in xenograft tumor tissues derived from AN3CA OE-AR and AN3CA shKLF12 indicated a significant decrease in the expression levels of Snail1 and MMP2, accompanied by an increase in E-cadherin expression relative to the control group (Fig. 3I). In contrast, MFE296 shAR and MFE296 OE-KLF12 xenografts exhibited elevated levels of Snail1 and MMP2, while E-cadherin expression was markedly reduced (Fig. 3J). These results suggest that KLF12 could activate EMT in EEC, whereas AR may exert a suppressive effect on this process.

The regulatory relationship between KLF12 and AR was validated by qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses. In MFE296 cells, overexpression of KLF12 markedly reduced AR mRNA (Fig. 4A) and protein expression (Fig. 4B), whereas knockdown of KLF12 in AN3CA cells increased AR mRNA (Fig. 4C) and protein expression (Fig. 4D).

KLF12 directly binds to the promoter and intronic regions of the AR gene to repress its transcriptional activity

CUT&Tag was performed in MFE296 OE-KLF12 and MFE296 Vector cells to identify genomic regions bound by KLF12. Metagene profile analysis showed that KLF12 binding peaks were predominantly enriched around transcription start sites (TSS) (Fig. 5A). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that KLF12 target genes were mainly involved in metabolic pathways, focal adhesion, and pathways in cancer (Fig. 5B). Motif enrichment analysis revealed a strong enrichment of the canonical Gli-binding site (GliBS) motif in KLF12-bound regions (Fig. 5C). Representative CUT&Tag signal tracks showed enrichment of KLF12 binding in the intronic region of the AR gene (Fig. 5D). Given that KLF12 generally binds to promoter regions of downstream genes, we further explored potential upstream binding motifs using the JASPAR database. This analysis predicted a sequence within the AR promoter region that closely resembled the canonical KLF12-binding motif, suggesting an additional potential regulatory site (Fig. 5E). To experimentally verify these findings, electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) were conducted. Biotin-labeled double-stranded DNA probes containing the wild-type (WT) KLF12-binding motifs in the AR promoter and intronic regions were synthesized based on the JASPAR predictions and CUT&Tag analysis, while unlabeled WT and mutant (Mut) probes were used in competition assays (Fig. 5E, F). Nuclear extracts from MFE296 cells showed distinct shifted bands when incubated with the biotin-labeled WT probes, indicating specific KLF12–DNA complex formation. Addition of a 50-fold molar excess of unlabeled WT probes led to the disappearance of the shifted bands, whereas the unlabeled mutant probes did not compete for binding, confirming the specificity of the KLF12–DNA interaction. Furthermore, the addition of 1 μg of anti-KLF12 antibody resulted in a marked reduction of the shifted bands, indicating that KLF12 is indeed a component of the DNA–protein complex.

I ChIP-qPCR analysis quantifying KLF12 occupancy at the AR promoter and intronic regions. J, K Dual-luciferase reporter assays showing transcriptional activity of wild-type (wt) and mutant (mut) AR promoter (J) and intronic (K) constructs after KLF12 overexpression. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments. ** p < 0.01, n.s. not significant.

Consistent with the EMSA findings, ChIP-PCR and ChIP-qPCR analyses revealed significant enrichment of KLF12 at both the promoter and intronic regions of the AR gene relative to the IgG (negative) control (Fig. 5G–I), thereby confirming its specific in vivo occupancy at the AR locus.

Finally, dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that KLF12 overexpression markedly reduced the transcriptional activity of wild-type AR promoter and intronic constructs, whereas point mutations at these binding sites abolished this inhibitory effect (Fig. 5J, K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that KLF12 suppresses AR transcription by directly binding to its promoter and intronic regions.

Discussion

In this study, we identified KLF12 as a novel oncogenic driver of metastasis in EEC, whereas AR functioned as a tumor suppressor. Clinically, KLF12 and AR protein levels were inversely correlated in EEC specimens, defining a clinically relevant KLF12–AR regulatory axis. Overexpression of KLF12 or low expression of AR predicted poor patient prognosis, consistent with the results of our in vivo metastasis models. Mechanistically, multiple complementary assays, including CUT&Tag, ChIP-PCR, ChIP-qPCR, EMSA, and dual-luciferase reporter analysis, confirmed that KLF12 directly binds to both promoter and intronic regions of the AR gene, repressing its transcription and thereby inducing EMT to promote tumor metastasis. Collectively, the results of the current study elucidated the molecular mechanism by which KLF12 regulates AR expression and provided new insights into the metastatic progression and potential therapeutic targeting of EEC.

Biological and prognostic implications of AR expression in endometrial carcinoma remain incompletely understood [13, 14]. Earlier small-cohort studies reported no significant association between AR expression and clinicopathological parameters such as myometrial invasion, tumor size, or lymphovascular space invasion [15, 16]. In contrast, a meta-analysis including 1485 endometrial carcinoma patients revealed strong correlations between AR expression and FIGO stage, lymphovascular invasion, and lymph-node involvement [7]. Moreover, AR-positive tumors were associated with longer disease-free survival and delayed recurrence [17, 18]. Consistent with these findings, our data demonstrated that reduced AR expression was significantly correlated with advanced-stage EEC, further supporting the notion that AR functions as a tumor suppressor in this hormone-related malignancy.

AR plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of hormone-dependent cancers, particularly prostate and breast malignancies [19–25]. The role of AR in tumor biology, however, is complex and context-dependent. In prostate cancer, androgens activate AR to promote tumor growth, but reactivation of AR signaling in castration-resistant prostate cancer remains therapeutically relevant, conferring clinical benefit in a subset of patients [19, 20, 26]. Because of the partially overlapping response elements among steroid receptors, extensive crosstalk occurs among AR, estrogen receptor (ER), and progesterone receptor (PR) signaling pathways. The biological outcome of AR activation largely depends on the expression pattern of these receptors. In breast cancer, the function of AR is determined by ER status. AR promotes tumor growth in ER-negative subtypes but exerts an antiproliferative effect in ER-positive disease [27, 28]. Mechanistically, AR inhibits breast cancer cell proliferation by preventing ER from binding to its transcriptional coactivators and repressing the transcription of ER-regulated cell-cycle genes [29]. The AR antagonist enzalutamide further highlights this complexity, activating ERα signaling in breast cancer cells with high AR expression, while exerting ER-mediated inhibitory effects in those with low AR levels. Interestingly, enzalutamide treatment also upregulates PR expression in endometrial carcinoma cells [30]. Collectively, these findings suggest that AR, ER, and PR constitute an interdependent steroid receptor network, and that the full spectrum of AR function in EEC must be interpreted within this broader hormonal context.

KLF12 has been identified as an oncogene in several malignancies. In ovarian cancer, KLF12 inhibits Sp1-mediated transcription of survivin by competing for its binding site on the survivin promoter, thereby reducing resistance to anoikis [31]. KLF12 is also highly expressed in multiple cervical cancer cell lines [32]. These findings confirm the oncogenic role of KLF12 at the cellular, animal, and clinical levels, underscoring its importance in cancer development and progression. We previously demonstrated that KLF12 is highly expressed in endometrial carcinoma tissues and is associated with poor prognosis [12].

The association between KLF family members and hormone receptors has been previously reported. Prior studies have shown that KLF12 reduces progesterone sensitivity in endometrial cancer by inhibiting the PR signaling pathway [33]. Moreover, KLF9 directly binds to the promoter regions of the ER and PR, thereby facilitating progesterone-dependent gene transcription in uterine epithelial cells [34, 35]. In androgen-dependent prostate cancer cells, KLF9 suppresses proliferation and induces apoptosis through the AR pathway [36]. Our study demonstrated that inhibition of KLF12 partially restores AR expression, further supporting the role of KLF proteins in modulating nuclear receptor signaling. These observations collectively underscore the critical involvement of KLF family members in hormone receptor regulation. Given this, we sought to determine whether KLF12 affects AR expression in EEC.

Given the pivotal role of AR in prostate cancer, we further examined whether KLF12 also regulates AR expression in this context. In 22Rv1 prostate cancer cells, Western blot analysis showed that KLF12 overexpression had little effect on full-length AR (AR-FL) but markedly increased the expression of the splice variant AR-V7 (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Conversely, silencing KLF12 reduced both AR-FL and AR-V7 levels, with AR-V7 remaining more prominently affected in this model (Supplementary Fig. S6B). AR-V7 was not detected in EEC cells, indicating that AR signaling in EEC is primarily mediated by the AR-FL. In contrast to prostate cancer, KLF12 acts as a transcriptional repressor of AR-FL in EEC, suppressing its expression at the transcriptional level. These findings suggest that KLF12 may regulate AR signaling in a tumor-specific manner. Notably, AR-V7 lacks the ligand-binding domain, rendering it constitutively active and independent of androgen regulation, and its elevated expression has been strongly associated with therapeutic resistance in castration-resistant prostate cancer [37]. Therefore, the modulation of AR-V7 by KLF12 may provide a potential entry point for investigating the mechanisms underlying therapy resistance in prostate cancer.

During our investigation, we observed that KLF12 overexpression or AR knockdown induced morphological changes reminiscent of EMT. KEGG analysis of CUT&Tag data revealed that KLF12-associated genes were enriched in pathways related to cell adhesion and tumor metastasis. Functional assays further demonstrated that KLF12 overexpression or AR depletion promoted EMT activation. Previous studies have shown that both KLF12 and AR are involved in the regulation of EMT [38, 39]. Among EMT regulators, Snail1 suppresses epithelial markers such as E-cadherin and induces mesenchymal genes including MMP2 [40]. Notably, EMT activation is governed by complex epigenetic regulatory programs that occur independently of DNA sequence alterations [41]. Accordingly, we examined the protein expression of Snail1, MMP2, and E-cadherin to more accurately assess EMT activity in our model.

While promoter-associated regulation has been extensively characterized, the biological significance of intronic regions remains less understood. Accumulating evidence indicates that intronic sequences, traditionally regarded as noncoding, can act as silencers or enhancers that modulate promoter activity and even affect post-transcriptional processing [42–44]. Recent chromatin-mapping studies have provided deeper insight into how KLF proteins influence transcription through enhancer–promoter interactions and chromatin accessibility. KLF5 preferentially occupies distal enhancer regions and activates oncogenic transcriptional programs such as ACTN4-driven cytoskeletal remodeling and metastasis [45]. Likewise, KLF4 functions as a canonical pioneer factor capable of binding compact chromatin, promoting nucleosome remodeling, and facilitating enhancer–promoter looping by recruiting cofactors and architectural proteins [46]. In this context, our study revealed that KLF12 directly binds to both the promoter and intronic regions of the AR gene, thereby suppressing its transcriptional activity. Mutations within the AR intronic region attenuated KLF12-mediated transcriptional repression, implying that these intronic elements may function as silencers. Given the chromatin-regulatory features of other KLF family members, it is plausible that KLF12 modulates AR expression through chromatin-based silencing mechanisms involving intronic regulatory elements. Future integrative studies will be instrumental in defining the epigenetic mechanisms underlying this regulation in EEC.

In summary, this study provides the first CUT&Tag-based genome-wide mapping of KLF12 in endometrial carcinoma. Through comprehensive multi-angle validation, including CUT&Tag, EMSA, ChIP-PCR, ChIP-qPCR, and dual-luciferase reporter assays, we demonstrate that KLF12 directly represses AR transcription to drive EMT and metastasis. These findings uncover the mechanistic basis of the KLF12–AR regulatory axis and offer a rationale for targeted therapeutic intervention in EEC. A proposed model illustrating the regulatory mechanism of the KLF12–AR axis in EEC.

KLF12 directly binds to the promoter and intronic regions of the AR gene, repressing AR transcription. Reduced AR expression weakens its tumor-suppressive function and induces EMT-related changes, including upregulation of Snail1 and MMP2 and downregulation of E-cadherin, thereby promoting cell migration and invasion, which consequently facilitate metastatic progression in EEC.
