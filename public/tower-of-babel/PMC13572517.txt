Anticancer activity with physical property analysis of Mollugo cerviana (L). Ser whole plant extract on hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines (HepG2)
Authors: Sherin Rebecca Flavian, Ramanathan Sharmila, Sahaya Vijay Jeyaraj, Fikadu Tesgera Tolasa, Rajamanickam Pon Nivedha, Tony Augustine, Janani Ezhilan, Sagni Ragassa Wadajo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma is a rapidly advancing malignancy and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality. Recently, plant-based pharmaceuticals have garnered increased attention compared to synthetic medications due to their non-toxic nature and accessibility. Mollugo cerviana, a prevalent weed in the Mollugnaceae family, is recognised for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. The ethanolic extract of the plant was evaluated for its cytotoxic effects on hepatocarcinoma cell lines (HepG2). The vitality of the treated cells was evaluated using the MTT test. The induction of oxidative stress was assessed by quantifying intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and the mechanism of cell death was analysed using ethidium bromide/acridine orange (EB/AO) dual staining. Additionally, the DNA damage was assessed via the comet test. To ascertain the probable bioactive chemicals responsible for the observed cytotoxicity, LC–MS analysis of the plant extract was conducted. The primary components found in the plant extract underwent molecular descriptor analysis and statistical correlations were established to evaluate their notable drug-likeness and possible therapeutic significance. The plant extract induces cell death in a dose-dependent manner, as demonstrated by the MTT assay, with HepG2 cell viability decreasing from 98 to 44% at concentrations ranging from 1 to 500 µg/mL. HepG2 cells exposed to 356.03 µg/ml (IC50) of plant extract exhibited an elevated amount of ROS production. The primary form of cell death was apoptosis, with 54% of cells identified as apoptotic and 6% as necrotic. The extent of DNA damage in HepG2 cells, as assessed by the comet assay was markedly elevated. The research indicates that the ethanolic extract of M.cerviana promotes apoptosis in HepG2 cells, likely facilitated by increased ROS levels and DNA damage. The combination of phytochemical profiling and molecular descriptor analysis substantiates the therapeutic potential of M.cerviana as a promising natural therapy for hepatocellular cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58687-5.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the lethal types of primary malignancy that represents 75% of all liver cancer among the human population and it accounts for the third leading cause of cancer deaths1. The risk factors that contribute to the development of HCC in humans include chronic viral infections caused by HBV (Hepatitis B Virus) and HCV (Hepatitis C Virus), as well as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Undiagnosed HBV infections can trigger viral replication that promotes the progression of inflammation, which in turn induces hepatocarcinogenesis. Additionally, factors such as Type II diabetes and prolonged exposure to aflatoxin have also been implicated as contributors to the development of HCC2. The treatment strategies for HCC are still evolving and require a multidisciplinary approach that depends on patient-specific factors that include the stage of liver cirrhosis, the functioning ability of the liver, its size and the extent of carcinogenesis3. The curative options in the management of HCC comprise liver resection, liver transplantation, radiofrequency ablation and selective internal radiation therapy, which is patient-specific and tailored to suit individual needs4. Despite the availability of more sophisticated and advanced treatments, recent research has shifted to a focus on herbal medicine, which is nontoxic, effective and has fewer side effects. Plant-based drugs play a key role in primary healthcare systems in both developing and developed countries, as they are accessible than synthetic drugs5. M.cerviana is a common weed found in wheat and paddy fields across southern India6. This plant has been used as a source of medicine in most of the rural areas in treating liver-related disorders and the plant extracts have been proven to possess good antioxidants and antidiabetic properties7. Rich in minerals and diverse phytochemicals, the plant serves as a promising source of clinically important bioactive compounds8. To investigate the anticancer potential of the plant extract, ethanol was chosen as the extraction solvent due to its polarity and proven efficiency in extracting diverse phytochemical constituents9. With this evidence, the present study aimed to evaluate the anticancer potential of the ethanolic extract of M.cerviana on HepG2 cell lines through various assay methods, such as cell viability assays, ROS generation, EB/AO staining and comet assays. Furthermore, LC–MS analysis was performed to identify the bioactive compounds present in the extract and molecular descriptors of the selected active compounds were calculated to understand and correlate their potential biological activity.

Methodology

Ethanolic extraction of the plant material

The total plant material of M.cerviana for the present study was collected from the fields of Tirunelveli and the plant material was authenticated by a taxonomist at Xavier Research Foundation, Palayamkottai. The collected plant material was pulverized into a fine powder and subjected to solvent extraction. Plant powder of 10 g was dissolved in 100 ml of 100% ethyl alcohol at room temperature. It was then agitated for 4 hours. The ethanolic extracts were then retrieved by drying the filtrates in a rotary evaporator at 40 °C. The collected ethanolic extract was stored in an Eppendorf tube in a cool and dry place. The yield % was calculated using the formula;

Percentage of yield = (Yield in grams/loaded sample in grams) × 100

Cell culture

HepG2 (Human hepatocellular carcinomic cell lines) was used to evaluate the cytotoxic potential of the ethanolic extracts of the M.cerviana plant. The HepG2 cell lines were obtained from NCCS Pune, and the cell lines were supplemented with FBS (10%), NaHCO₃ (0.2%) and an antibiotic solution (100 µg/ml in culture medium). The HepG2 cells were carefully seeded and cultivated at 37 °C in a CO₂ incubator.

MTT assay for cytotoxicity assessment

The cytotoxic potential of the ethanolic extract of M.cerviana was analyzed by MTT assay (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]−2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide). The HepG2 cells were carefully cultured in a 96-well format with a concentration density of 1×104cells in respective wells. These wells were incubated for 24 hours, after which the plant extracts of various concentrations (1µg- 500 µg/ml) were added to the wells. The control sets were maintained with only the culture medium. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 hours. After the incubation period, the cell viability was analyzed by the colorimetric MTT assay10. A 20 µl of MTT solution (5mg/ml) was added to each well and incubated for 4 hours at 37º. Finally, the MTT-supplemented medium was removed from the wells, and the wells were washed with 200 µL of 1× PBS. The supernatant was removed and the DMSO was used to dissolve the purple formazan crystals, and the absorbance was analyzed at 570nm in a microplate reader (Model: Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). IC50 was calculated using Graph pad Prism 8.0 (USA). Paclitaxel (10–100 µg/mL) was used as a positive control to validate the assay performance that helps to correlate a direct comparison with the cytotoxic potential of the M.cerviana extract.

Ethidium bromide/acridine orange (EB/AO) dual staining

Apoptosis induction in HepG2 cells was assessed using Ethidium Bromide/Acridine Orange (EB/AO) dual staining11. Cells were seeded at a density of 1×104 cells/well in a 96-well plate and allowed to adhere for 24 hours. Subsequently, cells were treated with the IC50 concentration of M.cerviana extract for 24 hours. After treatment, the cells were washed with PBS and stained with a mixture of acridine orange and ethidium bromide (100 µg/ml each) for 5 minutes. A 25 µl aliquot of the stained suspension was transferred to a glass slide, covered with a coverslip and observed under a fluorescence microscope (ZOE-BIO-RAD) using appropriate filter sets (AO: 520 nm, EB: 590 nm). Apoptotic and necrotic cells were quantified from randomly selected fields (n = 200 cells) and results were expressed as mean ± SD from three independent experiments.

ROS measurement

Intracellular ROS levels in HepG2 cells were measured using DCFH-DA staining using standard Protocol12. Cells were seeded at a density of 1×104 cells/well in black-walled 96-well plates and incubated for 24 hours at 37 °C in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were then treated with the IC50 concentration of M.cerviana extract for 24 hours. After treatment, cells were incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 30 minutes in the dark at 37 °C. Excess dye was removed by washing with PBS and fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (ZOE-BIO-RAD) at 485 nm excitation and 535 nm emission. ROS levels were quantified from three independent experiments and expressed as mean ± SD. Untreated and H₂O₂-treated cells served as negative and positive controls, respectively.

Measurement of DNA damage by comet assay

The cultured HepG2 cells at a density of 10,000 cells/well were incubated at 37° C for 24 hours. The incubated cells were treated with the IC50 of plant extract, the treated cells after incubation were collected and the comet assay protocol was followed13. The microscopic slides were sequentially coated with 200 µl of 0.75% agarose and again layered with 100 µl of 0.5% low melting agarose as a second layer. To these slides, 20µl of cell suspension was distributed in the form of thin film and the coated sample slides were suspended in the cell lysis buffer which is composed of 2.5 M Sodium Chloride, 0.2 Sodium hydroxide, 100 mM Na2EDTA, 10 mM Tris–hydrochloric acid, 1% Triton X-100 and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide and this maintains the pH level equals to 10.0 and was subsequently incubated overnight at 4°C. The prepared slides were rinsed with double distilled water and subjected to incubation in 3M NaOH which promotes DNA unwinding. The samples were separated through electrophoresis in 2% gel for a period of 20 minutes at 25V. Eventually, the neutralization process of the slides was carried out with 0.4MolarTris buffer with the pH 7.5. To ensure proper visualization, the slides were subjected to staining with ethidium bromide with the concentration of 5mg/ml. The stained samples were observed in a fluorescent microscopy (ZOE-BIO-RAD). The results are expressed as mean ± S.D. from three independent experiments and the significant level was p<0.05.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectroscopy (LC–MS)

The separation and identification of phytochemical constituents in the ethanolic extract M.cerviana were performed using LC-MS. The analysis was conducted on a Hypersil GOLD C18 reversed-phase column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.9 µm particle size). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid), delivered in a gradient mode. Detection was carried out using an Orbitrap mass spectrometer operating at a resolution of 60,000, with a scan range of 10–1000 m/z and a maximum injection time of 50 ms.

Compound identification was based on accurate mass measurements and fragmentation patterns using the NIST 20 spectral library and reference standards including quercetin and kaempferol. Quantification was performed by peak area normalization. Residual solvent analysis confirmed ethanol and hexane levels were below detection limits (<0.01%). A total of 38 bioactive compounds were identified and selected for further topological and molecular descriptor analysis to evaluate their drug-likeness and therapeutic potential.

Molecular descriptors calculation

While experimental methods are essential for validating biological activity, theoretical analysis plays a critical role in predicting molecular behavior and optimizing compound design. In this study, we incorporated a cheminformatics-based theoretical framework to complement our experimental findings. Cheminformatics enables the prediction of molecular properties using computational models, among which molecular descriptors serve as key indicators of structural and physicochemical characteristics. Specifically, we employed topological descriptors graph-theoretical indices derived from molecular structure as predictive tools to assess drug-likeness and therapeutic relevance. Results

Assessment of cytotoxicity of ethanolic extracts of M.cerviana extract by MTT assay

The cytotoxic effect of the ethanolic extract of M.cerviana on human hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HepG2) was evaluated using the MTT assay. As shown in Figure 1A, cell viability decreased in a dose-dependent manner across the concentration range of 1–500 µg/ml. The viability of HepG2 cells was recorded as 98% at 1 µg/ml and progressively declined to 80%, 64%, 48% and 44% at higher concentrations, indicating significant cytotoxicity (P < 0.05). The IC50 value, calculated using nonlinear regression analysis in GraphPad Prism, was determined to be 356.03 µg/ml. These results confirm that the ethanolic extract of M.cerviana exerts a concentration-dependent toxic effect on HepG2 cells. Representative morphological changes observed after treatment are shown in Figure 1B. Ethidium bromide/acridine orange (EB/AO) dual staining

Ethidium Bromide/Acridine Orange (EB/AO) dual staining followed by fluorescent microscopy is a reliable and extensive tool to detect the morphological features and Apoptosis in culture cells14. In the current study, the changes in the ratio of apoptotic and necrotic cells were evaluated upon exposure to M.cerviana plant extract. The findings record an incredible increase in apoptotic cells of 54 and 6 % necrosis in the cultured cells after 24 hours of treatment. (Fig 4A). Analysis of DNA damage by comet assay

Comet Assay is a rapid and sensitive technique to quantify the level of DNA damage in individual cells. The degree of DNA damage is measured by the number of comet events that are often been classified into five classes (Class 0 – Class 4), whereas each class denotes the level of DNA breakage. Class 0 indicates no DNA damage and class 4 indicates severe damage16. The M.cerviana plant extract induces DNA damage in HepG2 cells to a proximal extent and is depicted in Figs. 5 and 6. As represented here, the treated samples show an increased percentage of comets in class 3 followed by classes 2 and 4. Discussion

Modern medicine has made a lot of progress in cancer treatment and management, but in developing countries, herbal drugs made from plants are becoming more popular. Various plant extracts are considered to be the abundant source of innovative anticancer pharmaceuticals and numerous lead compounds have emerged from these plant extracts are in various phases of clinical trials and regarded as promising chemotherapeutic agents17. Numerous herbal extracts have been evaluated for their efficacy against HCC, however, M.cervianaa medicinally significant plant, has been selected due to its rich phytochemical profile and preliminary evidence indicating antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer properties18. Nonetheless, the cytotoxic efficacy of M.cerviana against human HCC cell lines HepG2 has not been previously documented. Consequently, the present study was designed to evaluate the in vitro cytotoxicity and anti-cancer properties of the plant extract against HepG2 cell lines through various assays. There are many available methods to test the cytotoxicity of the cells, and they are usually carried out in two frames: cell viability assays and cell proliferation assays. Colorimetric and fluorometric assays are the most used in vitro methods to investigate the cell viability of the cancer cells because of their reliability and accuracy. Four assay methods have been chosen and tested to find out how well the plant extract exerts cytotoxicity against HepG2 cells19. The MTT colorimetric assay is regarded as the most effective method for evaluating cell viability in vitro. The enzymatic reduction of MTT (3–4, 5 dimethylthiazol-2yl-2, 5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) to MTT formazan crystals is contingent upon mitochondrial respiration, which indirectly evaluates the energy capacity of the cells20. The current study unequivocally demonstrates that the influence of plant extracts on HCC cells exhibits a dose-dependent cytotoxic effect. Higher amounts of plant extract make cells less viable, which means that higher amounts are needed to stop HCC cells from growing. The viability of HepG2 cells diminished from 98 to 44% at a concentration range of 1–500 µg/ml. At higher concentrations of 500 µg/ml to 100 µg/ml, the viability of the cells dropped significantly below 50%, which shows that the plant extract has a good cytotoxic effect against HCC cells. The cytotoxic effect infers that the plant is highly rich in secondary metabolites and many bioactive compounds21. The plant extract demonstrates a dose-dependent inhibitory effect, with an IC50 of 356.03 µg/ml, indicating a good anti-cancer agent. Inducing apoptosis is recognized as an effective approach for cancer treatment. Research conducted over the years has demonstrated that apoptosis is initiated by two main pathways: cell-intrinsic pathway involving the ligation of cell surface death receptors and the mitochondrial release of cytochrome C22. Ethidium Bromide/Acridine Orange (EB/AO) dual staining is a versatile method for detecting the mechanism of apoptosis in vitro. Acridine Orange stains both the cytoplasm and nucleus, emitting green fluorescence, while Ethidium Bromide diffuses through the cells and intercalates with DNA, emitting red fluorescence. Consequently, apoptotic cells exhibit orange to red fluorescing nuclei23. The cytological alterations noted in HepG2 cells exposed to 356.03 µg/ml indicates that 54% of the cells were subjected to undergo death due to chromatin condensation, while merely 6% experienced necrosis. Prior research has indicated that varying doses of Aloe vera extracts exert a significant anticancer effect on HepG2 cells through the induction of apoptosis24. One of the early and important steps in apoptosis is the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which causes oxidative stress and kills cancer cells25. ROS is very important for cell signalling and is involved in apoptosis pathways that are controlled by death receptors and cell organelles. Increase production can also denature proteins, nucleic acids, lipid membranes and cell organelles which can lead to apoptosis26. This study found that the ethanolic extract of M.cerviana increased the amount of ROS Production. This was evident by a strong green, fluorescent image observed in fluorescent microscope. This high level of ROS production shows that the plant has a strong root for fighting cancer cells The study’s findings were corroborated by Mohammed, 2021 who demonstrated that Krameria lappacea plant extract effectively targeted the breast cancer cell line (MCF) by producing robust reactive oxygen species (ROS) by initiating the apoptotic pathway27. DNA breakage is also one of a confirmatory approach to analyse the dead and damaged cells. The comet assay was used to look at DNA breakage, and the visual scoring of cells was used to determine the intensity of damage confined. Comet score can be analysed by the measure of the size of the head and tail and the intensity of the tail28. The plant extract examined in the study demonstrates a moderate to significant DNA damage, indicated by class 3 and class 4 comets, which enhances cellular apoptosis. A plethora of apoptotic regulatory molecules were involved in modulating the activity. Raissa et al, 2022 reported that the leaf extracts of Azadirachta indica Juss diminish HepG2 proliferation by inducing apoptosis and decreasing cell viability29. The study offers a significant insight into the anticancer efficacy of the plant M.cerviana against HepG2 cancer cells and this potent effect inherited by the plant is linked to the presence of various bioactive compounds within the plant. Detailed investigations need to be carried out to rule out the most potent and active compounds responsible for this cytotoxic activity exerted by the plant. Darvesh et al, 2013 reported that the polyphenol compound epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) from green tea induces oxidative stress in HCC cancer cells by generating reactive oxygen species ROS and that the proliferation of these cancer cells is inhibited through the induction of apoptosis30. Abdul Hamid et al, 2020 elucidated the function of various bioactive herbal compounds derived from plants that aid in the prevention and treatment of HCC by impeding the advancement of cancer cells, obstructing metastasis and suppressing tumour cell proliferation31. The results of reactive oxygen species generation, DNA damage and significant cell accumulation clearly proves that the plant extract targets the cancer cells by cell inhibition and shuts down the cells predominantly through apoptosis. The study reveals that the plant extract was found to be very effective in targeting HepG2 cells and can be considered a drug target in future studies. However, molecular analysis of pre- and post-apoptotic genes and protein expression assays should be carried out to find out the detailed functions of the genes with them on and off regulatory mechanisms, and the sequence of events leading to apoptosis. Topological indices and molecular characteristics (MW, EM, TP, and XlogP) are the key parameters in cheminformatics and drug design, and their correlation coefficient graph depicts their interrelationships, Though TP exhibits mild oscillatory behavior relevant to bioavailability and drug transport, MW and EM consistently display substantial positive correlations, establishing them as strong predictors of molecular behavior. X Log P, on the other hand, exhibits a great deal of variability, even going as far as negative correlations, underscoring its intricate reliance on molecular structure and its crucial function in drug solubility and permeability. While X Log P’s erratic connection highlights the necessity of cautious assessment in pharmaceutical research, the stability of MW and EM points to their potential use in drug candidate screening. These results have significant cheminformatics applications, especially in material science and drug discovery. When forecasting molecule stability, reactivity and biological activity, topological indices are essential. To uncover structural characteristics that influence molecular interactions, such as binding affinity in drug-receptor interactions, highly changing indices, such as those found in TI-1, can be helpful. On the other hand, for generalized molecular attribute predictions like solubility, lipophilicity, or transport processes, indices with more stable distributions (like those in TI-2) might be helpful. Scientists can improve drug-likeness, bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy by optimizing the molecular design of pharmaceuticals through the analysis of these indicators. In material science, these indices also aid in the classification of molecular structures, which helps in the creation of novel polymers and nanomaterials with desired characteristics.

Conclusion

The present study concludes that the ethanolic extract of M.cerviana had cytotoxic potential against HepG2 cells. The results displayed a dose-dependent cytotoxic activity of the plant extract with increasing concentration which is confirmed by MTT assay. The plant inhibits the proliferation of HepG2 cells and induces cell death predominantly by apoptosis. These preliminary findings suggest that the plant M.cerviana has the potential to target HepG2 cells and may therefore be considered as a good anticancer target against Hepatocellular carcinoma. Quantitative molecular descriptor predictions, when compared with the experimental data, revealed a strong correlation between the physical properties and drug-likeness characteristics of the compounds, indicating their ability to reach the desired biological targets effectively. This scientific insight provides a valuable basis for selecting promising anticancer candidates for future cancer-related research. Furthermore, cytotoxicity should also be validated in the normal healthy cell lines to assess complete nature of the plant extract. In addition, comprehensive molecular characterization with gene and protein analysis will facilitate the progression of the plant extract toward potential therapeutic application.
