Characteristics and influencing factors of self-management behaviors of hypertensive patients in areas with concentrated ethnic minorities on plateau: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Zhuoya Zhang, Dongli She, Jinjiu Hu, Haixia Wu, Lin Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundsHypertension remains a major public health challenge in China, especially in high-altitude ethnic minority areas where prevalence is high but awareness, treatment, and control rates remain low. Self-management is crucial for delaying complications and improving quality of life, yet previous studies have mainly focused on low-altitude areas and Han Chinese populations and have largely relied on total-score comparisons. Because self-management is multidimensional, such approaches may obscure population heterogeneity. Latent profile analysis (LPA) can identify subgroups with distinct behavior patterns, but evidence in hypertensive patients from high-altitude ethnic minority areas is scarce. This study aimed to identify latent profiles of self-management behaviors in this population and to examine factors associated with profile membership.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between December 2024 and May 2025 among hypertensive patients attending Gannan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture People’s Hospital. Latent profile analysis was performed to identify distinct self-management behavior profiles. Multinomial logistic regression was subsequently used to examine factors associated with profile membership, including self-efficacy, social support, chronic disease health literacy, age, ethnicity, smoking, and drinking.ResultsThree distinct profiles of self-management behaviors were identified: 1) a comprehensive management type, characterized by consistently good performance across all behavioral dimensions; 2) a low treatment management type, marked by poor medication adherence and inadequate blood pressure monitoring; and 3) a low risk factor management type, characterized by weak control of smoking, alcohol use, and work-related stress. Multiple logistic regression analyses demonstrated that self-efficacy played a role in each patient group, while social support, chronic disease-related health literacy, ethnicity, and dietary habits only exerted corresponding effects in specific patient subgroups.ConclusionsSelf-management behaviors among patients with hypertension in high-altitude ethnic minority regions exhibited substantial heterogeneity, yielding three distinct latent profiles with clearly differentiated characteristics. The study found that self-efficacy played a role across all three behavioral categories, facilitating early identification of individuals who require intervention. Concurrently, clinical practice and public health interventions can implement stratified, precision-based management strategies tailored to the primary factors that influence different patient populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27956-3.

Introduction

Hypertension is one of the most common chronic noncommunicable diseases worldwide [1]. It is often asymptomatic in its early stages, but as the disease progresses, it can lead to severe cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications and is therefore often referred to as a “silent killer” [2, 3]. In China, hypertension prevention and control has long been characterized by high prevalence but low awareness, treatment, and control rates [4, 5]. This pattern is even more pronounced in high-altitude ethnic minority areas. A large-scale cross-sectional survey conducted in the Tibet Autonomous Region in 2025 reported a hypertension prevalence of 46.5%, nearly twice the national average. The awareness, treatment, and control rate were only 45.2%, 30.8%, and 3.0%, respectively, and the control rate was less than one-fifth of the national average [6].

In this context, enhancing self-management among patients in the region is crucial, as it helps delay complications and improve quality of life [7]. Key components of self-management include medication adherence, home blood pressure monitoring, risk factor control, and maintenance of a healthy lifestyle. However, previous studies have predominantly focused on low-altitude areas and the Han Chinese population [8], limiting the generalizability of their findings to high-altitude ethnic minority areas. Even studies conducted in high-altitude areas [6] primarily addressed single or limited risk factors (e.g., age, alcohol consumption history) and have largely relied on total-score comparisons. Yet self-management behaviors in these regions are shaped by multiple factors [9–11]. More importantly, self-management is a multidimensional construct, and reliance solely on total scores may obscure heterogeneity within the population.

Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a person-centered approach that identifies subgroups of individuals with similar patterns across multiple continuous indicators [12]. Currently, LPA has been applied in studies involving patients with diabetes, hypertension, and other chronic diseases, demonstrating its value in identifying latent profiles, revealing population heterogeneity, and informing targeted interventions [13–16]. However, studies using LPA to examine self-management profiles and associated factors among hypertensive patients in high-altitude ethnic minority areas remain scarce. Therefore, this study aimed to identify latent profiles of self-management behaviors in this population and to examine factors associated with profile membership.

Participants and methods

Study population and setting

Using convenience sampling, hypertensive patients from a tertiary hospital in Gannan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Gansu Province, China, were selected as study participants between December 2024 and May 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of hypertension according to the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension (2024 Revised Edition) [17], defined as office systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg measured on 3 separate occasions on different days, or a previous confirmed diagnosis of hypertension with ongoing antihypertensive treatment; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their family members before participation in the study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe hypertension-related complications; (2) critical illness, including severe heart failure, renal failure, or malignant tumors; and (3) major psychiatric disorders, cognitive impairment, hemiplegia, or other conditions that prevented patients from clearly expressing their views or cooperating with the survey.

Sample size estimation

There is no universally accepted standard for sample size estimation in latent profile analysis (LPA). The required sample size is influenced by several factors, including the number of indicators, the number of latent profiles, profile separation, and the size of the smallest profile [18]. Therefore, in this study, the sample size was estimated with reference to the approach commonly used in quantitative research, the sample size should be at least 5–10 times the number of variables [19]. In this study, there are 22 independent variables: 18 from the general information questionnaire, and four scales—the hypertension knowledge scale, the self-efficacy scale for patients with hypertension, the social support rating scale, and the health literacy scale for patients with chronic disease—each counted as one variable. Allowing for an anticipated 20% rate of invalid responses, the required sample size is therefore 138–275 participants.

Preliminary experiment

Using convenience sampling and the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, we conducted a pilot survey in November 2024 at the chronic-disease outpatient clinic of a tertiary hospital in the Gannan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, China. Ten hypertensive patients were enrolled in the pilot study. The pilot study was conducted to assess the clarity, comprehensibility, and feasibility of the questionnaire in the target population. Based on the pilot findings, only minor revisions were made to the wording and respondent instructions of the general information questionnaire to improve clarity and comprehension in the local context. In addition, a Tibetan medicine practitioner was involved in the formal survey to provide language assistance when needed, so as to facilitate participants’ understanding of the questionnaire instructions and item content. This adjustment was intended to improve comprehension in the local context and did not alter the conceptual meaning of the original items. The final questionnaire was then confirmed for use in the main study.

Measures

General information questionnaire

Based on a review of the relevant literature and the aims of this study, the researchers developed a self-administered questionnaire for hypertensive patients living in high-altitude areas. The questionnaire included sociodemographic and disease-related characteristics. The sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex, ethnicity, economic status, educational level, and type of medical insurance. The disease-related characteristics included duration of hypertension and the number of comorbid chronic conditions.

Hypertension Knowledge-Level Scale (HK-LS)

Patients’ knowledge of hypertension was assessed using the Hypertension Knowledge-Level Scale (HK-LS). The scale was originally developed by Erkoc et al. in 2012 [20], and the Chinese version used in this study was translated by Zhang Juan [21]. The HK-LS consists of 22 items across 6 domains: definition (2 items), medical treatment (4 items), medication adherence (4 items), lifestyle (5 items), diet (2 items), and complications (5 items). Each item has 3 response options: Yes / No / Don’t know. Items were scored according to the answer key, with correct answers coded as 1 and incorrect or “Don’t know” responses coded as 0, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 22. Higher scores indicate greater knowledge of hypertension. Previous studies have reported acceptable reliability for the Chinese version of the scale, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.82,and the HK-LS has been used in studies involving hypertensive patients [22]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.918.

Self-Management Scale for Patients with Hypertension (SMSPH)

Self-management was assessed using the Self-Management Scale for Patients with Hypertension (SMSPH) developed by Liu Ning [23]. The scale consists of 21 items across 4 domains: treatment management (8 items), diet and physical activity management (5 items), lifestyle habit management (5 items), and risk-factor management (3 items). Total score ranges from 21 to 105, with higher scores indicating better self-management. Scores < 63 indicate a low level, scores of 63–83 indicate a moderate level, and scores of 84–105 indicate a high level of self-management. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the SMSPH was 0.854, and it has been used in studies of self-management among hypertensive patients [24]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.727.

Self-efficacy scale for patients with hypertension

Patients’ self-efficacy was assessed using the Self-Efficacy Scale for Patients with Hypertension developed by Yang Bipin et al. [25]. The scale consists of with 11 items 4 domains. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“completely unable”) to 4 (“completely able”), with higher total scores indicating stronger self-efficacy. The scale has been used in studies involving hypertensive patients [26]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.80 in the original study and 0.615 in the present study.

Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS)

Social support was assessed using the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) developed by Xiao Shuiyuan [27]. The scale consists of 10 items across 3 domains: objective support (3 items), subjective support (4 items), and utilization of support (3 items). Total score ranges from 12 to 66, with higher scores indicating greater social support. The scale has been used to assess social support among hypertensive patients in China [28]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.92 in the original study and 0.627 in the present study.

Health Literacy Management Scale (HeLMS)

We used the Chinese version of the Health Literacy Management Scale (HeLMS), translated and adapted by Sun Haolin [29], to evaluate patients’ ability to obtain, understand, and use health information. The scale contains 24 items across 4 domains: information acquisition, communicative and interactive ability, willingness to improve health, and willingness to provide financial support. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“very difficult”/“very unwilling”) to 5 (“not difficult at all”/“very willing”), yielding a total score of 24–120. Scores > 96 indicate adequate health literacy for chronic disease. The scale has been used in studies involving hypertensive patients [30, 31]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.894 in the original study and 0.865 in the present study.

Data collection and quality control

Before the formal survey, the researchers explained the purpose and significance of the study to each participant. After informed consent was obtained, an individual record was created and a unique identification number was assigned to each participant. During data collection, paper-based questionnaires were distributed, and participants were encouraged to complete them independently whenever possible. For participants who were illiterate or unable to complete the questionnaire independently, trained researchers read the questionnaire items aloud and recorded the participants’ responses verbatim, without prompting, interpretation, or translation. For participants who were unable to understand Mandarin, a Tibetan medicine practitioner provided language translation when needed. The translation was limited to the original questionnaire content, without additional explanation, interpretation, or prompting. All questionnaires were collected on site immediately after completion. Questionnaires with identical responses to all items were considered invalid and excluded. Before data entry, each completed questionnaire was checked independently by 2 researchers to ensure completeness and accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencys and percentages, and continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data.

LPA performed using Mplus version 8.3. The 4 domain scores of the Self-Management Scale for Patients with Hypertension (SMSPH)—treatment management, diet and physical activity management, lifestyle habit management, and risk-factor management—were used as continuous indicators. The original domain scores were entered directly into the model, and no standardization was performed before the analysis.

Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and sample-size–adjusted BIC (aBIC), with smaller values indicating better fit; entropy, with values closer to 1.00 indicating higher classification accuracy and values ≥ 0.80 indicating good classification quality; and the Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) and bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), which were used to compare models with k versus k − 1 classes. A P value < 0.05 for the LMR-LRT or BLRT indicated that the k-class model fit the data better than the k − 1 class model [32].

For univariate analyses, categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, normally distributed continuous variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance, and non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analyses were further assessed for multicollinearity. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) ≥ 5 were considered indicative of high multicollinearity, and variables with high collinearity were excluded before regression analysis. The remaining variables were then entered into multinomial logistic regression model to identify factors associated with membership in different self-management behavior profiles. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [33].

Result

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 250 hypertensive patients were included in this study, including 137 men (54.8%) and 113 women (45.2%). The age distribution was as follows: <55 years, 78 (31.2%); 55–64 years, 104 (41.6%); and ≥ 65 years, 68 (27.2%). By ethnicity, 45 participants (18.0%) were Hui, 85 (34.0%) were Tibetan, and 120 (48.0%) were Han. Detailed information is provided in Appendix 1.

Scores on self-management and related scales

The mean total self-management score was 67.67 ± 8.65. Among the 4 self-management domains, treatment management had the highest score (26.46 ± 5.25), followed by lifestyle habit management (15.64 ± 2.53), diet and physical activity management (14.18 ± 3.54), and risk-factor management (11.39 ± 3.43). The median scores for hypertension knowledge, self-efficacy, social support, and chronic disease health literacy were 15 (interquartile range [IQR]: 12–18), 28 (23–32), 32 (28–36), and 89 (80–96), respectively.

Latent profile analysis was performed using the 4 domain scores of the self-management scale as continuous indicators. Models with 1 to 5 profiles were fitted, and the model fit indices are presented in Table 1. As the number of profiles increased, the AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased progressively, suggesting improved model fit. Entropy values for all models were greater than 0.80, indicating acceptable classification quality, with the 4-profile model showing the highest entropy. However, the 4-profile and 5-profile solutions each included at least 1 profile comprising fewer than 10% of participants, which limited their practical interpretability.

For all models except the 3-profile model, both the LMR and BLRT were statistically significant (P < 0.05). For the 3-profile model, the BLRT remained significant, whereas the LMR was not. Previous research has suggested that the BLRT may provide more robust performance in model selection, particularly in studies with relatively limited sample sizes [34]. Therefore, BLRT results were given greater weight. Considering the model fit indices together with profile size and interpretability, the 3-profile solution was selected as the optimal model [35].

The estimated proportions of the 3 profiles were 16.0%, 24.4%, and 59.6%, respectively. Based on the patterns of scores across the 4 self-management domains (Fig. 1), the profiles were labeled as follows: Profile 1, characterized by a relatively lower treatment management score, was labeled the low treatment-management profile; Profile 2, characterized by a relatively lower risk-factor management score, was labeled the low risk-factor-management profile; and Profile 3, characterized by relatively balanced scores across the 4 domains, was labeled the overall balanced-management profile.

Univariate analysis of latent classes of self-management behavior

Univariate analyses showed significant differences among the self-management behavior profiles with respect to sex, age group, ethnicity, Mandarin listening and reading proficiency, educational level, occupation, place of residence, household monthly per-capita income, duration of hypertension, medication adherence, dietary habits, BMI, smoking history, drinking history, self-efficacy, social support, and chronic disease health literacy(See Appendix 2 for details).

Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated high collinearity between smoking and drinking history (VIF for smoking history = 20.123; VIF for drinking history = 20.640). After drinking history was excluded, multicollinearity was reassessed for the remaining variables, and all VIF values from 1.154 to 3.431.

Multivariable analysis of classes of self-management behavior profiles

Multinomial logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with membership in the self-management behavior profiles among hypertensive patients. The overall balanced-management profile (Profile 3) was used as the reference group. Variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analyses were entered into the model as independent variables. The coding of variables is presented in Table 2. For categorical variables, the category with the highest code value was used as the reference category.

Discussion

Patients classified as the low treatment management profile mainly exhibited insufficient behaviors such as regular medication and blood pressure monitoring. Similar self-management heterogeneity has also been reported in other potential categories of hypertensive populations [36]. The possible reason is that treatment-related behaviors are more dependent on long-term, stable, and repetitive daily execution than diet, exercise, or general lifestyle adjustments, and therefore are more likely to become a weak link in hypertension self-management. Previous domestic and international studies have also indicated that the adherence to hypertension medications [37] and the continuous implementation of home blood pressure monitoring often face multiple obstacles [38, 39]. In ethnic minority areas at high altitudes, this problem may also be further affected by factors such as the accessibility of medical services [40], the way of obtaining health information [6], language communication [41], and cultural environment. Based on this, for patients classified in this profile, the management focus should be on strengthening regular medication and routine blood pressure monitoring behaviors. Measures such as strengthening doctor-patient communication and negotiating the establishment of clear and achievable management goals can be taken to help patients enhance their behavioral motivation and promote the long-term maintenance of treatment management behaviors [42].

Patients classified as belonging to the low-risk factor management profile mainly exhibit relatively weak behaviors such as smoking, alcohol control, and sleep schedule management. Previous studies have also indicated that controlling risk factors is often one of the most difficult aspects to maintain in the long-term management of hypertension [43, 44].In areas where ethnic minorities live in high-altitude regions, this issue may be related to local occupations. Previous studies suggest that young working populations often face high work pressure and usually relieve fatigue by smoking and drinking [45, 46]; while middle-aged and elderly people are more engaged in heavy physical labor such as herding, their work locations are scattered and highly mobile, making it difficult to have fixed rest time s [47, 48]. Moreover, the health risks brought about by smoking, drinking, and irregular sleep schedules often have long-term and latent characteristics [49], some patients may have difficulty perceiving the harm and thus experience the situation of “knowing it is harmful but having difficulty in continuous control”. Therefore, for patients classified in this profile, the intervention focus should be more on the long-term implementation of risk factor management behaviors, and individualized health education plans should be formulated based on the actual living situations of different occupational groups [50].

Patients classified as belonging to the overall balanced management profile exhibit relatively balanced performance across all dimensions and have relatively high management levels. They can be regarded as the relatively ideal management group in the current research population. Previous studies have also shown that comprehensive hypertension management can further improve patients’ blood pressure control and self-management levels [51, 52]. For patients in ethnic minority areas at high altitudes, this relatively stable management state has certain practical significance, suggesting that under the influence of factors such as local medical service accessibility, health resources, and cultural background, most patients have formed a basic self-management model. However, compared with some domestic plain areas [53] and foreign research reports [54, 55], the overall self-management level of the subjects in this study is still relatively low. Based on this, for patients classified in this profile, the management focus should not only remain at maintaining the current level, but also through continuous follow-up, culturally adapted health education, family and community support [55], and the comprehensive application of digital management methods [56, 57], promote patients to gradually transform the existing behavioral improvements into long-term stable self-management behaviors.

Self-efficacy, age, and dietary pattern may be important factors related to membership in the low treatment-management profile. Higher self-efficacy was associated with lower odds of being classified in this profile, which is consistent with previous studies showing that self-efficacy is important for treatment adherence among hypertensive patients [58, 59]. In addition, patients with a high-salt, high-fat, and high-protein diet pattern have a harder time entering this profile, suggesting that patients in this profile may have relatively better dietary management. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies [60, 61]. Considering the significant deficiencies in treatment management in this group, this result indicates that some patients may be more inclined to focus on lifestyle adjustments and may not attach sufficient importance to regular medication and active blood pressure monitoring, which implies a tendency to “replace treatment management with lifestyle management”. Finally, participants under the age of 55 are less likely to enter the low-treatment-management profile. This indirectly suggests that elderly patients may be more prone to poor treatment management. This is consistent with the results of some previous studies [62, 63], but there are also studies suggesting that younger adults may also have deficiencies in hypertension awareness and treatment [64], indicating that the relationship between age and treatment management may still be influenced by specific research subjects and management contexts. Based on this, for the low-treatment-management group, priority should be given to enhancing self-efficacy, strengthening compliance management, and including elderly patients as key intervention targets, while also providing dietary behavior guidance.

In the low risk-factor-management profile, self-efficacy and social support both showed protective effects, suggesting that patients with stronger self-efficacy and more adequate family and community support were less likely to show weaknesses in risk-factor control [65]. Notably, higher chronic disease health literacy was associated with a greater likelihood of membership in the low risk-factor-management profile, a finding that is not fully consistent with most previous studies [66]. One possible explanation is the existence of a “knowledge–action gap” [67], that is, although patients may have a certain ability to obtain, understand, and apply health information, the long-term maintenance of risk-factor management behaviors, such as smoking control, alcohol control, and regular daily routines, still depends on multiple factors including behavioral motivation [68], self-regulation [69], and social support; In areas where ethnic minorities live in the highlands, it may also be related to local occupations and climatic conditions. For instance, the physical labor intensity for herders is relatively high, and some residents might view drinking alcohol as a way to keep warm. Another possible explanation is that the effect of chronic disease health literacy on self-management behaviors may not be entirely direct, but may instead operate indirectly through factors such as disease acceptance [70], self-efficacy [71], and family support [72]. However, the present study did not directly measure these mediating factors, and therefore these interpretations should be treated with caution. At the same time, it cannot be ruled out that this result is related to differences in measurement dimensions. HeLMS primarily assesses patients’ ability to acquire, understand, and apply health information, without directly measuring implementation barriers, behavioral motivation, or maintenance capacity for behaviors such as smoking control, alcohol control, and regular sleep patterns. Regarding ethnic differences, the results showed that Hui patients were less likely to be classified in the low risk-factor-management profile. This difference may not simply stem from ethnic identity itself, but may instead be more closely related to sociocultural background, such as not smoking or drinking [73]. However, the present study still observed that some younger Hui individuals engaged in smoking or drinking, suggesting that adherence to cultural norms may vary under the influence of factors such as age and occupation. Therefore, in high-altitude ethnic minority areas, this finding still needs to be understood in light of the specific sociocultural context. Based on these findings, interventions for the low risk-factor-management profile should not remain limited to the transmission of health knowledge alone, but should also strengthen the transformation from “knowing” to “doing” while enhancing self-efficacy and social support. Targets for smoking restriction, alcohol reduction, and regular daily routines should be specified in terms of frequency, dose, and time periods, combined with simple record-keeping and regular follow-up feedback, so as to help patients gradually transform “knowing what should be done” into “being able to do it consistently over the long term.” At the same time, more targeted individualized strategies should be implemented in conjunction with ethnic and cultural backgrounds.

Limitations

First, convenience sampling was used, and the participants were recruited from a tertiary hospital in the Gannan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, which limits the representativeness of the sample and requires caution in generalizing the findings. Second, although standardized administration procedures were adopted to minimize prompting and interpretation, the use of different questionnaire administration methods may still have introduced interviewer-related bias, social desirability bias, or translation-related bias. In addition, the internal consistency of some scales in the present sample was lower than that reported in the original studies, particularly for the self-efficacy scale and the Social Support Rating Scale, suggesting that these instruments still require further validation in hypertensive patients from high-altitude ethnic minority areas.

Conclusion

Among hypertensive patients in high-altitude minority regions, LPA identified three distinct self-management types—overall (balanced) management, low treatment-management, and low risk-factor management—confirming marked heterogeneity in self-management behaviors. Self-efficacy emerged as the core psychological predictor distinguishing profile membership, while social support, chronic-disease health literacy, age group, ethnicity, and dietary habits showed profile-specific predictive effects. Accordingly, interventions for the low treatment-management group should focus on enhancing treatment adherence, whereas those for the low risk-factor–management group should prioritize control of specific lifestyle risks (smoking, alcohol use, stress). Going forward, management of hypertension should move beyond one-size-fits-all approaches toward precision, evidence-based, stratified interventions to better use limited health-care resources and improve overall management effectiveness.
