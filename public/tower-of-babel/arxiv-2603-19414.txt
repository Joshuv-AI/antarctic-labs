Abstract
. We study a problem of optimal allocation in a discrete-time multi-period pure-exchange economy, where agents have preferences over stochastic endowment processes that are represented by strongly time-consistent dynamic risk measures. We introduce the notion of dynamic Pareto-optimal allocation processes and show that such processes can be constructed recursively starting with the allocation at the terminal time. We further derive a comonotone improvement theorem for allocation processes, and we provide a recursive approach to constructing comonotone dynamic Pareto optima when the agents’ preferences are coherent and satisfy a property that we call equidistribution-preserving . In the special case where each agent’s dynamic risk measure is of the distortion type, we provide a closed-form characterization of comonotone dynamic Pareto optima. We illustrate our results in a two-period setting.



1.
Introduction

The study of efficiency in pure-exchange economies has served as the foundation for the characterization of Pareto-optimal (PO) allocations of an aggregate endowment among a population of economic agents. The historical roots of this field can be traced back to the seminal work of
Borch 1960
;
Borch 1962
and
Wilson 1968
, within the framework of Expected Utility (EU) Theory with risk-averse agents. Under this framework, the celebrated Borch rule states that an allocation is PO if and only if the ratio of marginal utilities between the agents is constant. Furthermore, optimal allocations are non-decreasing deterministic functions of the aggregate endowment. In other words, PO allocations are comonotone when agents are risk averse. This has motivated a series of developments related to comonotone improvement results in the literature, including
Landsberger and Meilijson 1994
,
Chateauneuf et al. 1997
,
Carlier et al. 2012
, and
Denuit et al. 2025
, for instance.

Characterization results for Pareto optima have also been obtained for models beyond the EU framework. For instance, economies with ambiguity-sensitive preferences of the Choquet-Expected Utility (CEU) type
(
Schmeidler 1989
)
were examined by
Chateauneuf et al. 2000
,
Dana 2004
,
De Castro and Chateauneuf 2011
, and
Beissner and Werner 2023
.
Dana 2002
;
Dana 2004
and
De Castro and Chateauneuf 2011
examine economies with Maxmin Expected Utility (MEU) preferences axiomatized by
Gilboa and Schmeidler 1989
. Exchange economies with variational preferences (e.g.,
Maccheroni et al. 2006
) were studied by
Dana and Le Van 2010
and
Ravanelli and Svindland 2014
, for instance.
Strzalecki and Werner 2011
consider general ambiguity-sensitive convex preferences.
Embrechts et al. 2018
;
Embrechts et al. 2020
study PO risk exchange for agents with quantile preferences. Some recent work has focused on obtaining crisp characterizations that can be numerically implemented
(
Liu 2020
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Ghossoub and Zhu 2026
)
.
Embrechts et al. 2018
examine preferences given by quantile-based risk measures (specifically, the Range-Value-at-Risk) and the work is generalized to tail risk measures in
Liu et al. 2022
. In
Liu 2020
, the author studies a weighted risk-sharing problem and gives an explicit characterization when agent preferences are given by distortion risk measures. This characterization is further generalized to positively homogeneous monetary utilities (or equivalently, law-invariant coherent risk measures) in
Ghossoub and Zhu 2026
.
Jouini et al. 2008
and
Filipović and Svindland 2008
also studied the case of economies with law-invariant monetary utilities. Recently,
Hara et al. 2026
study Pareto optimality in pure-pure exchange economies with heterogeneous smooth ambiguity preferences (e.g.,
Klibanoff et al. 2005
;
Denti and Pomatto 2022
).

While the aforementioned literature considered a single-period framework, which we henceforth refer to as the static setting, the nature of investment and consumption is dynamic in reality. As a result, the literature has also devoted some effort to the study of the multi-period optimal-allocation problem. For instance, in a discrete-time multi-period setting with agents having recursive utility preferences, the optimal consumption problem is studied in
Benhabib et al. 1988
, and a generalized market with multiple consumption goods is considered in
Dana and Le Van 1990
. For more sophisticated preferences that account for uncertainty in future consumption, see
Epstein and Zin 1989
,
Chew and Epstein 1990
,
Chew and Epstein 1991
, and
Kan 1995
, for instance. Further work in the discrete-time setting include
Epstein and Wang 2004
, who study equilibrium asset prices for consumption and investment problems; and
Anderson 2005
, who define and characterize steady states in infinite horizon problems. For a broad overview and summary of results in the discrete-time setting, we refer to
Backus et al. 2004
.

In the continuous-time setting, recursive preferences are given by stochastic differential utilities (SDU), which have been introduced in
Duffie and Epstein 1992b
, and their applications to asset pricing are first discussed in
Duffie and Epstein 1992a
. In
Duffie et al. 1994
, the existence of Pareto optima under SDU is established, and a characterization is derived. This characterization is improved in
Dumas et al. 1998
, who show that Pareto optima can be computed by optimizing one value function, instead of one for each agent. For problems with consumption and investment, optimal strategies in various market settings are studied in a series of papers by Schroder and Skiadas. The case of unconstrained trading is considered in
Schroder and Skiadas 1999
, and its results are extended to agents with trading constraints in
Schroder and Skiadas 2003
and
Skiadas 2007
. Non-tradable income is introduced in
Schroder and Skiadas 2005
, and discontinuous market information is studied in
Schroder and Skiadas 2008
.

In the above literature on optimal consumption, or optimal allocations, in a dynamic framework, Pareto efficiency is defined from the perspective of time-
0
preferences. Future consumption streams, future endowment streams, or future risk profiles are first discounted to time
0
, and then evaluated. Our contention is that this leads to an inherently myopic view that fails to capture the dynamic structure of endowment profiles. We propose a novel approach that allows to incorporate the dynamic structure of risk profiles into the decision-making process, through dynamic risk measures; and we introduce the associated notions of dynamic individual rationality and dynamic Pareto optimality.

Specifically, in this paper, we assume that each agent’s initial endowment is given by a discrete-time stochastic process, and the initial endowments are aggregated and reallocated at each point in time. Moreover, agent preferences over discrete-time stochastic processes are represented by strongly time-consistent dynamic risk measures, which are extensively studied in the risk management literature
(
Cheridito et al. 2006
;
Ruszczyński 2010
;
Bielecki et al. 2018
;
Moresco et al. 2024
;
Coache and Jaimungal 2024
;
Dentcheva and Ruszczynski 2024
;
Pesenti et al. 2025
;
Bielecki et al. 2025
)
. Unlike the existing literature on recursive utilities, we assume that agent preferences are (conditionally) translation invariant, which is a classical assumption for risk measures. Therefore, the risk of a deterministic endowment is equal to the size of the endowment and agent preferences admit a recursive representation, as in
Cheridito et al. 2006
and
Ruszczyński 2010
. This recursive representation allows us to define a notion of optimality for all time points, contrasting with classical notions of efficiency which are defined from the perspective of time
0
only, and hence are inherently myopic. Our new notion of optimality, which we call
dynamic Pareto optimality
(or just Pareto optimality for simplicity), not only considers the risk of an allocation process at time
0
, but also incorporates the dynamic structure of risk profiles into the decision-making process.

In our first main result (Theorem
4.3
), we show that an allocation is PO if and only if it is the solution to a series of recursive (backward in time) optimization problems. When the problem is static, we recover the classical result that PO allocations optimize a given social welfare function. In Section
5
, we extend the classical notion of comonotonicity to the dynamic setting and derive a comonotone improvement theorem for dynamic risk measures that preserve the convex order. We further study Pareto optima in the comonotone market, and we show that each PO allocation in the comonotone market is also PO in the unconstrained market, therefore providing justification for studying the comonotone market. Our second main result (Theorem
5.21
) provides a crisp characterization of comonotone Pareto optima when agent preferences are coherent and satisfy a property that we call
equidistribution-preserving
. This allows for a useful algorithmic implementation, which we demonstrate for a two-period setting in Section
6.2
. Our numerical example highlights the differences between dynamic and static models, and it demonstrates how future risks affect allocations in earlier periods.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. Section
2
introduces the necessary notation and recalls some relevant properties of dynamic risk measures. Section
3
presents the dynamic optimal-allocation problem and defines a notion of Pareto optimality for this framework. Section
4
characterizes Pareto optima, and our main characterization of comonotone Pareto optima is provided in Section
5
. A numerical example is provided in Section
6
, and Section
7
concludes. Some background material is presented in Appendix
A
.

Notation and Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

We consider a finite (known) time horizon
T\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
and a filtered non-atomic probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}},{\mathbb{P}})
. For each
t\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}
, define
{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}:=L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{t},{\mathbb{P}})
to be the set of essentially bounded and
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable random variables, and denote by
{\mathcal{L}}_{t+}^{\infty}\subseteq L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{t},{\mathbb{P}})
the subset of non-negative random variables. Furthermore, let
{\mathcal{L}}_{0,T}^{\infty}:={\mathcal{L}}_{0}^{\infty}\times{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{\infty}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{L}}_{T}^{\infty}
be the space of essentially bounded discrete-time stochastic processes, and denote by
X_{0:T}:=\{X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{T}\}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{0,T}^{\infty}
a real-valued stochastic process. For
0\leq t<s\leq T
, we use the notation
{\mathcal{L}}_{t,s}^{\infty}:=0\times\cdots\times 0\times{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{L}}_{s}^{\infty}\times 0\times\cdots\times 0
and denote by
X_{t:s}:=\{0,\ldots,0,X_{t},\ldots,X_{s},0,\ldots,0\}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t,s}^{\infty}
the projection of
X_{0:T}
onto the space
{\mathcal{L}}_{t,s}^{\infty}
. Similarly, we denote the space of
n
-dimensional stochastic processes by
{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}_{0,T}^{\infty}
. We further denote random vectors with capital boldface letters, real-valued vectors with lowercase boldface letters, and use
{\bm{X}}_{0:T}
for vector valued
n
-dimensional stochastic processes, where for each
t\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}
,
{\bm{X}}_{t}:=(X_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{t}^{(n)})
with
X_{t}^{(i)}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Throughout, (in)equalities between random vectors and stochastic processes are to be understood component-wise and in a
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost sure sense. Finally, we denote by
\,{\overset{d}{=}}\,
equality in distribution.

2.2.
Risk Measures

We assume that each agent has a preference given by a dynamic risk measure. Before defining a dynamic risk measure, we first recall the definition of a conditional risk measure.

Definition 2.1

(Conditional Risk Measure)
.

A conditional risk measure is a functional
\rho_{t,s}\colon{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1,s}^{\infty}\to{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}
, where
t\in\mathfrak{T}:=\{0,1,\ldots,T-1\}
and
t<s\leq T
.

A conditional risk measure may satisfy some of the following properties. These properties were first introduced in
Artzner et al. 1999
;
Kusuoka 2001
;
Föllmer and Schied 2002
in the context of static risk measures.

Definition 2.2

(Properties of Conditional Risk Measures)
.

For
t\in\mathfrak{T}
and
t<s\leq T
, let
Y_{t+1:s}
,
{\bm{Z}}_{t+1:s}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\infty}_{t+1,s}
. A conditional risk measure
\rho_{t:s}
is:

(i)

Monotone if
\rho_{t,s}(Y_{t+1:s})\leq\rho_{t,s}(Z_{t+1:s})
whenever
Y_{t+1:s}\leq Z_{t+1:s}
. If, in addition,
Y_{t+1:s}\leq Z_{t+1:s}
and
{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{t+1:s}<Z_{t+1:s})>0
implies
{\mathbb{P}}\big(\rho_{t,s}(Y_{t+1:s})<\rho_{t,s}(Z_{t+1:s})\big)>0
, then
\rho_{t,s}
is strictly monotone.

(ii)

Positive homogeneous if for all
c\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t+}^{\infty}
and
Y_{t+1:s}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\infty}_{t+1,s}
,
\rho_{t,s}(c\,Y_{t+1:s})=c\,\rho_{t,s}(Y_{t+1:s})
.

(iii)

Subadditive if for all
Y_{t+1:s},\,Z_{t+1:s}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\infty}_{t+1,s}
,
\rho_{t,s}(Y_{t+1:s}+Z_{t+1:s})\leq\rho_{t,s}(Y_{t+1:s})+\rho_{t,s}(Z_{t+1:s})
.

(iv)

Translation invariant if for all
Y_{t:s}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t,s}^{\infty}
,
\rho_{t,s}\big((Y_{t}+Y_{t+1},Y_{t+2:s})\big)=Y_{t}+\rho_{t,s}(Y_{t+1:s})
.

(v)

Convex if for all
\lambda\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}
with
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
and
Y_{t+1:s},\,Z_{t+1:s}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\infty}_{t+1,s}
,
\rho_{t,s}\big(\lambda\,Y_{t+1:s}+(1-\lambda)\,Z_{t+1:s}\big)\leq\lambda\,\rho_{t,s}(Y_{t+1:s})+(1-\lambda)\rho_{t,s}(Z_{t+1:s})
.

(vi)

Normalized if
\rho_{t,s}({\bm{0}})=0
, where
{\bm{0}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s-t}
is the zero vector.

(vii)

Equidistribution-preserving
\rho_{t,s}(Y_{t+1:s})\,\,{\overset{d}{=}}\,\rho_{t,s}(Z_{t+1:s})
whenever
Y_{t+1:s}\,{\overset{d}{=}}\,Z_{t+1:s}
.

When
s=t+1
, we omit the second subscript of the conditional risk measure, i.e.
\rho_{t,t+1}:=\rho_{t}
, and call
\rho_{t}
a one-step conditional risk measure. For
t=0
and
s=1
, the risk measure
\rho_{0}
is a static risk measure, and the property of equidistribution-preserving reduces to the classical notion of law invariance. Moreover, a conditional risk measure is coherent if it is monotone, positive homogeneous, subadditive, and translation invariant. Next, we recall the definition of a dynamic risk measure.

Definition 2.3

(Dynamic Risk Measure)
.

A dynamic risk measure on
\mathfrak{T}
is a sequence of conditional risk measures
\{\rho_{t,T}\}_{t\in\mathfrak{T}}
.

A dynamic risk measure satisfies one of the properties in Definition
2.2
if
\rho_{t,T}
satisfies the property for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. Next, we recall the notion of strong time consistency (which we henceforth refer to as just time consistency) and the recursive relation for time-consistent dynamic risk measures
(
Cheridito et al. 2006
;
Ruszczyński 2010
)
. Time consistency means that if one stochastic process has a larger risk than another at a future time
\theta
and the two processes are almost surely equal at all times
t
satisfying
\tau+1\leq u\leq\theta
, then the former process should also have a larger risk at time
\tau
. This ensures that in this case the timing of risk evaluation does not change the ordering of the risk assessment.

Definition 2.4

(Time Consistency)
.

A dynamic risk measure
\{\rho_{t,T}\}_{t\in\mathfrak{T}}
is time consistent if for all
0\leq\tau<\theta<T
and
X_{\tau+1:T},\,Y_{\tau+1:T}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\tau+1,T}^{\infty}
, the conditions

X_{t}=Y_{t},\,\text{ for all }\ \tau+1\leq t\leq\theta,\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \rho_{\theta,T}(X_{\theta+1,T})\leq\rho_{\theta,T}(Y_{\theta+1,T})

imply that

\rho_{\tau,T}(X_{\tau+1,T})\leq\rho_{\tau,T}(Y_{\tau+1,T}).

It is well known that time-consistent dynamic risk measures admit the following recursive representation. A proof of this result can be found in
Cheridito et al. 2006
and
Ruszczyński 2010
.

Theorem 2.5

(Recursive Relation)
.

Let
\{\rho_{t,T}\}_{t\in\mathfrak{T}}
be a normalized, monotone, and translation-invariant dynamic risk measure. Then,
\{\rho_{t,T}\}_{t\in\mathfrak{T}}
is time consistent if and only if there exists a family of normalized, monotone, and translation-invariant one-step conditional risk measures
\{\rho_{t}\}_{t\in\mathfrak{T}}
such that for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
and
X_{t+1:T}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1,T}^{\infty}
,

(1)

\rho_{t,T}(X_{t+1:T})=\rho_{t}\Big(X_{t+1}+\rho_{t+1}\big(X_{t+2}+\cdots+\rho_{T-1}(X_{T})\big)\Big).

Theorem
2.5
allows us to write any conditional risk measure as a sequence of one-step conditional risk measures. Moreover, any sequence of one-step conditional risk measures gives rise to a time-consistent dynamic risk measure through (
1
). This recursive structure is essential to construct optimal allocation processes recursively backwards in time.

3.
Pareto Optimality in a Dynamic Setting

In this section, we extend the static notions of individual rationality and Pareto optimality to the dynamic setting. We first describe the problem in Section
3.1
and define two different notions of Pareto optimality in Section
3.2
and Section
3.3
. The first notion, which we call myopic Pareto optimality, is motivated by the existing literature on recursive preferences, and only considers the risk of the process at time
0
. We discuss the shortfalls of this definition when agent preferences are translation invariant, and propose a second notion of optimality that is compatible with translation-invariant and time-consistent dynamic risk measures in Section
3.3
.

3.1.
Problem Setup

We consider a problem with
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
agents, and for each
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, we assume that the
i
-th agent has initial endowment
X_{1:T}^{(i)}:=\{X_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,X_{T}^{(i)}\}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{1,T}^{\infty}
. Furthermore, for each
t\in\mathcal{T}:=\{1,\ldots T\}
, we denote by
{\bm{X}}_{t}:=(X_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{t}^{(n)})
the vector of all agents’ endowments at time
t
and assume that
T\geq 2
unless otherwise stated. The case of
T=1
(i.e., the static problem), is well studied in the literature and we refer to Appendix
A
for a collection of key results from the static case. Additionally, the information available to the agents is encapsulated in the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}}
, and we assume that
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
is the trivial sigma algebra. At each time
t\in\mathcal{T}
, the
n
agents pool together their endowments to create the so-called aggregate endowment
S_{t}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}X_{t}^{(i)}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}
, and we denote by
S_{1:T}:=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{T}\}
the aggregate endowment process (or aggregate risk process in a risk-sharing problem). Under this setting, we have the following definition of an allocation.

Definition 3.1

(Allocation - Dynamic)
.

A
n
-dimensional process
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}_{1,T}^{\infty}
is an allocation of
S_{1:T}
if

(2)

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}Y_{t}^{(i)}=S_{t},\ \text{for all}\ t\in\mathcal{T}\,.

We denote by
\mathcal{A}_{1:T}
the set of allocation processes. Moreover, if the (sub)-process
{\bm{Y}}_{r:s}
satisfies (
2
) for all
t\in\{r,\ldots,s\}
, then we say that
{\bm{Y}}_{r:s}
is an allocation of
S_{r:s}
and denote by
\mathcal{A}_{r:s}
the set of allocations of
S_{r:s}
.

Note that for each
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
t\in\mathcal{T}
, the random variable
Y_{t}^{(i)}
represents the portion of the time-
t
aggregate endowment that is allocated to the
i
-th agent. The agents wish to choose
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}
based on individual preferences characterized by time-consistent dynamic risk measures with representation (
1
), that is, for
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, agent-
i
uses a dynamic risk measure
\{\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}\}_{t\in\mathfrak{T}}
. Then, by Theorem
2.5
, the
i
-th agent’s preference at time
t
is given by a normalized, monotone, and translation-invariant one-step conditional risk measure
\rho_{t}^{(i)}
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
and
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Consequently, we make the following assumption all throughout.

Assumption 3.2
.

For
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, the
i
-th agent’s risk preference is given by a normalized, monotone, translation-invariant, and time-consistent dynamic risk measure
\{\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}\}_{t\in\mathfrak{T}}
.

3.2.
Myopic Pareto Optimality

In this section, we define a notion of optimality that only considers the time
0
conditional risk measure of each agent. We refer to this notion of optimality as “myopic” since the agents do not care about risk valuation at any future time point.

Definition 3.3

(Myopic Individual Rationality)
.

An allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}
is myopic individually rational (IR) if

(3)

\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{(i)})\leq\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(X_{1:T}^{(i)})\,,\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}\,.

We denote by
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
the set of all myopic IR allocations.

Condition (
3
) states that agents will not be worse off with respect to their conditional risk measure
\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}
after participating in the market. Thus, agents have an incentive to participate in reallocation if and only if the optimal allocation is myopic IR. Furthermore, note that
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\neq\emptyset
since
{\bm{X}}_{0:T}\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
. Next, we define a notion of Pareto optimality for this myopic setting, which we call myopic Pareto optimality. Myopic Pareto optimality means that an agent cannot deviate from the optimal allocation and be strictly better off without making another agent strictly worse off.

Definition 3.4

(Myopic Pareto Optimality)
.

An allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}
is myopic Pareto optimal (PO) if

(i)

{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}

(ii)

there does not exist an allocation process
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
that satisfies
\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{(i)})\leq\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)})
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and at least one inequality is strict.

We denote by
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
the set of all myopic PO allocations.

Note that myopic individual rationality and myopic Pareto optimality reduce to the classical notions of individual rationality and Pareto optimality when
T=1
. Furthermore, Assumption
3.2
allows us to find myopic Pareto optima by finding Pareto optima in a related static problem instead. For each
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

\displaystyle\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{(i)})

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}^{(i)}\Big(Y_{1}^{(i)}+\rho_{1}^{(i)}\left(Y_{2}^{(i)}+\cdots+\rho_{T-1}^{(i)}(Y_{T}^{(i)})\right)\Big)

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}^{(i)}\left(\rho_{1}^{(i)}\Big(\cdots\rho_{T-1}^{(i)}\big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}^{(i)}\big)\Big)\right)=:\overline{\rho}^{(i)}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}^{(i)}\right)\,.

We observe from the above equation that by translation invariance, the
i
-th agent’s risk evaluation of the process
Y_{1:T}^{(i)}
is equivalent to evaluating the risk of
\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}^{(i)}
with a real-valued risk measure
\overline{\rho}^{(i)}\colon{\mathcal{L}}_{T}^{\infty}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. We denote by
\bar{Y}^{(i)}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}^{(i)}
the total allocation of the
i
-th agent and
\bar{X}^{(i)}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}^{(i)}
the initial total endowment of the
i
-th agent. Then, (
3
) holds if and only if

(4)

\overline{\rho}^{(i)}(\bar{Y}^{(i)})\leq\overline{\rho}^{(i)}(\bar{X}^{(i)})\,,\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}\,.

In other words, under Assumption
3.2
, a process
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}
is myopic IR if and only if the associated total allocation of the agents
\bar{{\bm{Y}}}
is IR (in the static sense) when the
i
-th agent uses risk measure
\overline{\rho}^{(i)}
and has initial endowment
\bar{X}^{(i)}
. Furthermore, myopic IR (and hence myopic PO) is a condition on the total allocation across all time periods
\bar{{\bm{Y}}}
and not the individual allocations
Y_{t}^{(i)}
. We summarize the above discussion with the following proposition.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}
and
\bar{Y}^{(i)}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}^{(i)}
for
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\bar{{\bm{Y}}}
is PO (in the static sense) when for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
the
i
-th agent uses risk measure
\overline{\rho}^{(i)}
and has initial endowment
\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}^{(i)}
.

(ii)

{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
.

Proposition
3.5
implies that we can find myopic Pareto optima using tools from the static case. In particular, by combining Proposition
3.5
with the well-known (static) inf-convolution result (see Proposition 3.3 from
Ghossoub and Zhu 2026
or Proposition
A.5

(i)
in Appendix
A
), we obtain that an allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}
is myopic PO if and only if the associated total allocation vector of the agents
\bar{{\bm{Y}}}
attains the infimum

(5)

\underset{{\bm{Z}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}}}{\inf}\ \sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\overline{\rho}^{(i)}(Z^{(i)}),

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}}
is the set of total allocation vectors such that (
4
) holds.

Furthermore, observe that the representation (
5
) is equivalent to the classical notion of
\alpha
-efficiency for recursive utilities with
\alpha=(1,\ldots,1)
, where here the recursive utility is replaced by
\overline{\rho}
. The assumption of translation invariance, which is typically not used in the recursive utility literature, has a significant impact on the set of optimal allocations. In particular, given a myopic PO allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}
, translation invariance implies that any allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{\prime}
satisfying
\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}^{\prime(i)}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}^{*(i)}
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
is also myopic PO. In other words, myopic Pareto optimality is a condition on the total allocation for each agent over time and never defines a unique stochastic process
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}
. Thus, it is necessary to consider a different notion of optimality for translation-invariant dynamic preferences.

3.3.
Dynamic Pareto Optimality

In this section, we remove the assumption that agents only care about evaluating the risk of an allocation process at time
0
and assume that each agent is concerned with their future risk
\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})
at all time points
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. This leads to a different notion of individual rationality and Pareto optimality, which we call dynamic individual rationality and dynamic Pareto optimality. For simplicity, we drop the word dynamic whenever there is no confusion and simply use the terms IR and PO.

Definition 3.6

(Dynamic Individual Rationality)
.

An allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}
is dynamic IR if for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}

\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\leq\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}(X_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\,,\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}\,.

We denote by
\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
the set of all dynamic IR allocations.

Definition 3.7

(Dynamic Pareto Optimality)
.

An allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}
is dynamic PO if

(i)

{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}

(ii)

there does not exist an allocation process
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
such that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
t\in\mathfrak{T}
,

\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\leq\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)}),

and
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})<\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right)>0
for some
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
t\in\mathfrak{T}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
the set of all dynamic PO allocations.

Unlike myopic Pareto optimality, we assume here that agents care about risk evaluation at intermediate time steps. Hence, even when agent preferences are translation invariant, it does not suffice to study the sum of each agent’s allocation over time. To characterize PO allocations, we first define the notion of Pareto optimality at a single time point
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. Then, we show that an allocation is PO if and only if it is PO at time
t
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. This allows us to characterize PO allocations via backwards recursion. To simplify the notation in our recursive arguments, we introduce the risk-to-go process.

Definition 3.8

(Risk-to-Go)
.

The risk-to-go process associated with
\{\rho_{t,T}\}_{t\in\mathfrak{T}}
, denoted
R_{0:T}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{0,T}^{\infty}
, is defined recursively as follows:

(i)

R_{T}=0
.

(ii)

For
t\in\mathfrak{T}
, we define
R_{t}(Y_{t+1:T}):=\rho_{t}\big(Y_{t+1}+R_{t+1}(Y_{t+2:T})\big)
.

Definition 3.9

(Individual Rationality at Time
t
)
.

For
t\in\mathfrak{T}
, an allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}
is IR at time
t
if for all
\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(X_{t+1:T}^{(i)}).

Furthermore, we denote by
\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
the set of all processes
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}
that are IR at time
t
, i.e.,

\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}:=\left\{{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}~|~R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(X_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}\right\}.

It follows from the definition that
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
if and only if
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{0}=\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
and all notions of individual rationality in this paper are equivalent to the static notion of individual rationality when
T=1
. Next, we define Pareto optimality at time
t
in a similar manner.

Definition 3.10

(Pareto Optimality at Time
t
)
.

For
t\in\mathfrak{T}
, an allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}
is PO at time
t
if

(i)

{\bm{Y}}^{*}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}

(ii)

there does not exist a random vector
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1}
with
\{{\bm{Y}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
such that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big),

and
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)<\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\right)>0
for some
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
the set of all processes
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}
that are PO at time
t
.

We use the convention that
\{{\bm{Y}}_{T},{\bm{Y}}_{T+1:T}^{*}\}={\bm{Y}}_{T}
. In other words, an allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{T}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{T-1}
is PO at time
t
if there does not exist a random vector
{\bm{Y}}_{T}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{T-1}
such that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,
R_{T-1}^{(i)}(Y_{T}^{(i)})\leq R_{T-1}^{(i)}(Y_{T}^{*(i)})\ \text{and}\ {\mathbb{P}}\left(R_{T-1}^{(i)}(Y_{T}^{(i)})<R_{T-1}^{(i)}(Y_{T}^{*(i)})\right)>0\ \text{for some}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}.

Our next result links
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
with
\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
. In particular, an allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}
is PO if and only if each sub-allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}
is PO at time
t
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. Hence, we can characterize PO allocations by characterizing allocations that are PO at time
t
instead.

Lemma 3.11
.

{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
if and only if
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
.

Proof.

We first prove that
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
implies
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
. Suppose
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. Then,
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
, which implies that
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
. Hence,
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}
satisfies the first condition of Pareto optimality and it remains to check the second condition. Let
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
such that for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
and
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

(6)

R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)}).

To verify the second condition of Pareto optimality, it suffices to show that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
s\in\mathfrak{T}
,

(7)

R_{s}^{(i)}(Y_{s+1:T}^{(i)})=R_{s}^{(i)}(Y_{s+1:T}^{*(i)}).

We prove (
7
) by induction. For
s=T-1
, we have
{\bm{Y}}_{T}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{T-1}
since
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
. Hence, the assumption that
{\bm{Y}}_{T}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{T-1}
implies that at least one of the following holds:

(8)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(R_{T-1}^{(i)}(Y_{T}^{(i)})\leq R_{T-1}^{(i)}(Y_{T}^{*(i)})\right)<1\ \text{for some}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}},

(9)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(R_{T-1}^{(i)}(Y_{T}^{(i)})<R_{T-1}^{(i)}(Y_{T}^{*(i)})\right)=0\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}.

Since (
8
) contradicts (
6
), it must be the case that (
9
) holds. Combining (
6
) and (
9
) yields (
7
) for time
T-1
. Next, we proceed by induction backwards in time. For this, assume that (
7
) holds for all
s\geq t
. It suffices to show that (
7
) also holds for
t-1
. Note that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\big)

\displaystyle=\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{*(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(X_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(X_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\big),

where the first equality holds by the induction hypothesis, the first inequality follows from (
6
), and the last inequality follows from the fact that
{\bm{Y}}_{t:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t-1}
. In other words,
\{{\bm{Y}}_{t},{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t-1}
. Hence, the assumption that
{\bm{Y}}_{t:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t-1}
implies that at least one of the following holds:

(10)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{*(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)<1\ \text{for some}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}},

(11)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t-1:T}^{*(i)})\big)<\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{*(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)=0\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}.

Since (
7
) holds for
s=t
, (
10
) is equivalent to

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\big)\leq\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{*(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)<1\ \text{for some}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}},

which contradicts (
6
). Therefore, it must be the case that (
11
) holds. Since (
7
) holds for
s=t
, (
11
) is equivalent to

(12)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\big)<\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{*(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)=0\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}.

Combining (
6
) and (
12
) yields (
7
) for
s=t
.

To prove the converse, let
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
. Assume, by way of contradiction, that
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\notin\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
for some
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. Since
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
, this implies that
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. Therefore,
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\notin\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
implies that there exists
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1}
with
\{{\bm{Y}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
such that

(13)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)=1\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}\ \text{and},

(14)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)<\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)>0\ \text{for some}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}.

Let
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}
be the process
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}
with the
(t+1)
-st vector replaced by
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1}
. Then, for all
s\in\mathfrak{T}
and
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

(15)

R_{s}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{s+1:T}^{(i)})\leq R_{s}^{(i)}(Y_{s+1:T}^{*(i)})\leq R_{s}^{(i)}(X_{s+1:T}^{(i)}),

where the first inequality follows from the monotonicity of
\{\rho_{s}^{(i)}\}_{s\in\mathfrak{T}}
and (
13
). Finally, for
s=t
and
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
satisfying (
14
), the first inequality in (
15
) is strict with positive probability, contradicting the assumption that
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
. Therefore,
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
.
∎

4.
Characterization of Dynamic Pareto Optimal Allocations

In this section, we generalize the well-known static inf-convolution result (
Embrechts et al. 2018
Proposition 1,
Ghossoub and Zhu 2026
Proposition 3.3) to the dynamic setting. Then, we combine this result with Lemma
3.11
to derive a recursive approach for finding PO allocations. We start with a lemma that gives an alternative representation for
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}=\biggl\{{\bm{Z}}_{t+1:T}\biggr.

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}~\big|~{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Z_{t+2:T}^{(i)})\big)\leq\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}R_{t}^{(i)}(Z_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\right)<1\ \text{or}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Z_{t+2:T}^{(i)})\big)<\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}R_{t}^{(i)}(Z_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\right)=0

\displaystyle\biggl.\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{for all}\ {\bm{Y}}_{t+1}\neq{\bm{Z}}_{t+1}\ \text{such that}\ \{{\bm{Y}}_{t+1},{\bm{Z}}_{t+2:T}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\biggr\}.

Proof.

Fix
t\in\mathfrak{T}
and let
\Gamma
denote the set on the right-hand side of the equation in the lemma.

We first show that
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
. Let
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
and assume that
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}\notin\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
. Then, by definition of
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
, there exists a random vector
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1}
with
\{\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{(i)})\big)\leq\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{(i)})\big)\right)=1\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}\ \text{and},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{(i)})\big)<\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{(i)})\big)\right)>0\ \text{for some}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}.

Hence, neither condition of the set
\Gamma
is satisfied, so
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
.

Next, we show that
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}\subseteq\Gamma
. Let
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
. Assume, by way of contradiction, that
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\notin\Gamma
. Then, by definition of
\Gamma
, there exists a random vector
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1}
with
\{\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
such that

(16)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)=1\ \text{and}

(17)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)<\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)>0.

For
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, let
v^{(i)}:=\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)-R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}
and
w^{(i)}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}
such that

(i)

For all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,
0\leq w^{(i)}\leq\max\{0,\,-v^{(i)}\}
.

(ii)

There exists
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
such that
{\mathbb{P}}\big(w^{(i)}<\max\{0,\,-v^{(i)}\}\big)>0
.

(iii)

(18)

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}v^{(i)}{\mathds{1}}_{v^{(i)}\geq 0}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}w^{(i)}{\mathds{1}}_{v^{(i)}<0}.

Note that random variables satisfying
(i)
and
(ii)
exist since (
17
) implies that
{\mathbb{P}}\big(-v^{(i)}>0\big)>0
for some
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Furthermore, (
16
) and (
17
) imply that there exists
\{w^{(i)}\}_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}
that also satisfies
(iii)
. For
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, let

{\varepsilon}^{(i)}:=w^{(i)}{\mathds{1}}_{v^{(i)}<0}-v^{(i)}{\mathds{1}}_{v^{(i)}\geq 0}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty},\ \widehat{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}:=\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+{\varepsilon}^{(i)},\ \text{and}\ \widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+2:T}={\bm{Y}}^{*}_{t+2:T}.

We first show that
\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}
. Since
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
, it follows that
\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+2:T}={\bm{Y}}^{*}_{t+2:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+2:T}
. Furthermore, by (
18
) and the fact that
\{\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}
,

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\widehat{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\left(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+{\varepsilon}^{(i)}\right)=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}=S_{t+1},

and therefore
\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}
. Next, we show that
\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
. Since
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
, we have
R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(X_{t+1:T}^{(i)})
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Hence, to show that
\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
, it suffices to show that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widehat{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right)=1.

Indeed, for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widehat{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ {\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)+{\varepsilon}^{(i)}\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ {\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\{\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)-v^{(i)}\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right\}\cap\left\{v^{(i)}\geq 0\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\ +{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\{\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)+w^{(i)}\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right\}\cap\left\{v^{(i)}<0\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ {\mathbb{P}}\left(v^{(i)}\geq 0\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(\{w^{(i)}\leq-v^{(i)}\}\cap\{v^{(i)}<0\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ {\mathbb{P}}\left(v^{(i)}\geq 0\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(v^{(i)}<0\right)=1,

where the first equality follows from translation invariance, the third equality follows from the fact that
v^{(i)}=\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)-R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})
, and the fourth equality follows from
(i)
. Finally, for
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
satisfying
(ii)
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widehat{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}

\displaystyle+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)<R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\Big)

\displaystyle=\ {\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)+{\varepsilon}^{(i)}<R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\ {\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\{\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)+w^{(i)}<R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right\}\cap\left\{v^{(i)}<0\right\}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\{w^{(i)}<-v^{(i)}\right\}\cap\left\{v^{(i)}<0\right\}\right)>\ 0,

where the first equality follows from translation invariance and the last inequality follows from
(ii)
. Hence,
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\notin\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

The quantity
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}R_{t}^{(i)}(Z_{t+1:T}^{(i)})
is the total risk-to-go for all agents at time
t
for the allocation process
{\bm{Z}}_{t+1:T}
. Thus, Lemma
4.1
states that
{\bm{Z}}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
if and only if we cannot obtain a strict improvement in the total risk-to-go at time
t
by changing only the allocation of
S_{t+1}
. Furthermore, Lemma
4.1
allows us to derive an inf-convolution result for allocations that are PO at time
t
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
t\in\mathfrak{T}
and
{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}
be an allocation of
S_{t+2:T}
. Then,
\{{\bm{Z}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
if and only if
{\bm{Z}}_{t+1}
attains the infimum

(19)

\underset{\left\{{\bm{W}}_{t+1}~|~\{{\bm{W}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\}}{\inf}\,\psi\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\left(W_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}\left(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)}\right)\right)\right),

for some monotone
\psi:{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that is strictly monotone on the set

\aleph:=\left\{V\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}~|~V=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(V_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\ \text{ for some }\ \{{\bm{V}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
\{{\bm{Z}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
and
Z:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Z_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)
. Define the functional
\psi_{Z}:{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
\psi_{Z}(W):={\mathbb{E}}[\max\{W,Z\}]
. We first show that
{\bm{Z}}_{t+1}
attains the infimum (
19
) for the mapping
\psi_{Z}
. For
\{{\bm{Y}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
, let
Y:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)
. Then,

\psi_{Z}(Y)={\mathbb{E}}[\max\{Y,Z\}]\geq{\mathbb{E}}[Z]=\psi_{Z}(Z).

It remains to show that
\psi_{Z}
is strictly monotone on
\aleph
. Let
\widetilde{Y},\,\widehat{Y}\in\aleph
. Then, by definition of
\aleph
, there exist some
\{\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\},\,\{\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
, such that

\widetilde{Y}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\ \ \hbox{ and }\ \ \widehat{Y}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widehat{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big).

Assume, without loss of generality, that
\widetilde{Y}\leq\widehat{Y}
. Then,

\psi_{Z}(\widehat{Y})-\psi_{Z}(\widetilde{Y})={\mathbb{E}}[\max\{\widehat{Y},Z\}]-{\mathbb{E}}[\max\{\widetilde{Y},Z\}]\geq 0.

To show strict monotonicity on
\aleph
, we need to show that
\psi_{Z}(\widetilde{Y})<\psi(\widehat{Y})
whenever
\widetilde{Y}\leq\widehat{Y}
and
{\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}<\widehat{Y})>0
. Assume, by way of contradiction, that
{\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}<\widehat{Y})>0
and
\psi_{Z}(\widetilde{Y})=\psi_{Z}(\widehat{Y})
. By definition of
\psi_{Z}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}[\max\{\widehat{Y},Z\}-\max\{\widetilde{Y},Z\}]=0.

Since the non-negative random variable
\max\{\widehat{Y},Z\}-\max\{\widetilde{Y},Z\}
is strictly positive whenever
{\mathbb{P}}(\widehat{Y}>Z)
or
{\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}>Z)
, we have
{\mathbb{P}}(\widehat{Y}\leq Z)={\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}\leq Z)=1
. Furthermore, as
\{{\bm{Z}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
, Lemma
4.1
implies that
{\mathbb{P}}(\widehat{Y}<Z)={\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}<Z)=0
. Consequently,

{\mathbb{P}}(\widehat{Y}=Z)={\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}=Z)={\mathbb{P}}(\widehat{Y}=\widetilde{Y})=1,

thereby contradicting the assumption that
{\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}<\widehat{Y})>0
. Therefore,
\psi
is strictly monotone on
\aleph
.

Conversely, suppose that
{\bm{Z}}_{t+1}^{\prime}
attains the infimum (
19
), for some
\psi
that is strictly monotone on
\aleph
. Assume, by way of contradiction, that
\{{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}^{*}_{t+2:T}\}\notin\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
. By Lemma
4.1
, there exists
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1}
such that
\{{\bm{Y}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}^{*}_{t+2:T}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
,

(20)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Z_{t+1}^{\prime(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)=1,\ \text{and}

(21)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)<\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Z_{t+1}^{\prime(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)>0.

Since
\psi
is strictly monotone on
\aleph
, (
20
) and (
21
) imply that

\psi\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)<\psi\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Z_{t+1}^{\prime(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right),

which contradicts the optimality of
{\bm{Z}}_{t+1}^{\prime}
for (
19
). Thus,
\{{\bm{Z}}^{\prime}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
.
∎

The set
\aleph
is the set of attainable risks at time
t
given an allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}
. Moreover, when
T=1
, we recover Proposition 3.3 from
Ghossoub and Zhu 2026
by taking
\psi
to be the identity function. For
T\geq 2
, the total risk-to-go at all future time points is a random variable and thus we need to introduce a real-valued functional
\psi
into our optimization problem. When the functional
\psi
is strictly monotone on the set of attainable risks at time
t
, then the solution to the optimization problem (
19
) corresponds to a PO allocation at time
t
. Conversely, for any allocation
\{{\bm{Z}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}
that is PO at time
t
, there exists a functional
\psi
that is strictly monotone on the set of attainable risks at time
t
such that
{\bm{Z}}_{t+1}
solves the optimization problem (
19
). Combining Proposition
4.2
and Lemma
3.11
yields the main characterization result for this section.

Theorem 4.3
.

{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
if and only if for each
t\in\{T,\ldots,1\}
,
{\bm{Y}}_{t}^{*}
is defined recursively as a solution to

(22)

\underset{\left\{{\bm{Y}}_{t}~|~\{{\bm{Y}}_{t},{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t-1}\right\}}{\argmin}\,\psi_{t-1}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right),

for some
\psi_{t-1}:{\mathcal{L}}_{t-1}^{\infty}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying the conditions of Proposition
4.2
.

Proof.

Suppose that
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}
is constructed recursively according to the theorem. Then,
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
by Proposition
4.2
and therefore
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
by Lemma
3.11
.

Conversely, suppose that
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
. Then,
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
by Lemma
3.11
and hence
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}
has the desired recursive construction by Proposition
4.2
.
∎

Theorem
4.3
states that an allocation is PO if and only if it is the solution to a series of recursive (backward in time)
\psi
inf-convolution problems of the form (
22
). Moreover, the only requirement on
\psi
is strict monotonicity on the set of attainable risks at time
t
. Since the expected value is strictly monotone on
{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}
for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
, any solution to the expected value inf-convolution problem corresponds to an allocation that is PO at time
t
. We state this formally in the following corollary.

Corollary 4.4
.

For
t\in\{T,\ldots,1\}
, define
{\bm{Y}}_{t}^{*}
recursively as a solution to

(23)

\underset{\left\{{\bm{Y}}_{t}~|~\{{\bm{Y}}_{t},{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t-1}\right\}}{\argmin}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right].

Then,
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
.

5.
Comonotone Allocations

In this section, we define a new notion of comonotonicity for allocation processes and study processes that are comonotone dynamic PO (which we just call
c
-PO). After introducing a dynamic comonotone improvement theorem in Section
5.1
, we define the notions of comonotone myopic Pareto optimality and comonotone Pareto optimality in Sections
5.2
and
5.3
. Then, we prove an analogue to Theorem
4.3
for comonotone Pareto optima in Section
5.4
and discuss the connection between the comonotone and unconstrained markets in Section
5.5
. Finally, we derive an explicit characterization of
c
-PO allocations in Section
5.6
.

5.1.
Comonotone Improvement

Before defining a new notion of comonotonicity that is compatible with our dynamic setting, we first recall the classical definition of comonotonicity for random vectors. We say that a random vector
{\bm{Z}}
is comonotone if there exists a random variable
W
and non-decreasing functions
f^{(i)}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
Z^{(i)}=f^{(i)}(W)
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Moreover, by
Denneberg 1994
Proposition 4.5, comonotonicity of
{\bm{Z}}
is equivalent to the existence of non-decreasing functions
g^{(i)}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, it holds that
Z^{(i)}=g^{(i)}(\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}}Z^{(j)})
. When PO allocations are comonotone in the static setting, then they are also comonotone with the aggregate, and thus agents have no incentive to under-report their initial risks in a risk-sharing problem. In an insurance setting, policyholders have no incentive to over-report their losses in a comonotone market, and hence comonotonicity is often referred to as the no-sabotage condition
(
Carlier and Dana 2003
)
.

In the dynamic setting, agents are not only concerned with the risk of the aggregate endowment at a fixed time, but also the risk-to-go. Hence, we propose the following notion of a comonotone process with respect to the dynamic risk measures
\{\rho^{(i)}_{t:T}\}_{t\in\mathfrak{T},i\in{\mathcal{N}}}
. Unlike the static setting, our notion of comonotonicity depends on the preferences of the agents. However, as all agent preferences are fixed throughout, we simply say that
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}
is comonotone and omit the dependence on
\{\rho^{(i)}_{t:T}\}_{t\in\mathfrak{T},i\in{\mathcal{N}}}
whenever there is no confusion.

Definition 5.1

(Comonotone Process)
.

A process
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}
is comonotone with respect to
\{\rho^{(i)}_{t:T}\}_{t\in\mathfrak{T},i\in{\mathcal{N}}}
if for any
t\in\mathcal{T}
, the random vector
\big(Y_{t}^{(1)}+R_{t}^{(1)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(1)}),\ldots,Y_{t}^{(n)}+R_{t}^{(n)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(n)})\big)
is comonotone (in the classical sense). We denote by
\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
the set of comonotone processes.

For
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
, if the random vector
\big(Y_{s}^{(1)}+R_{s}^{(1)}(Y_{s+1:T}^{(1)}),\ldots,Y_{s}^{(n)}+R_{s}^{(n)}(Y_{s+1:T}^{(n)})\big)
is comonotone for all
s\geq\tau
, then we say that
{\bm{Y}}_{\tau:T}
is a comonotone allocation of
S_{\tau:T}
and denote by
\mathcal{A}_{\tau:T}^{C}
the set of comonotone allocations of
S_{\tau:T}
. Moreover, as the risk-to-go term vanishes at the terminal time,
{\bm{Y}}_{T}
is a comonotone allocation of
S_{T}
in the classical sense (with respect to any dynamic risk measure). Hence, comonotonicity ensures that agents have no incentive to misreport their endowment or loss at the terminal time. Before the terminal time, agents do not have the incentive to misreport the sum of their endowment and the risk-to-go.

Before we state the dynamic comonotone improvement theorem, we first recall the definition of the convex order. We refer to
Shaked and Shanthikumar 2007
for a detailed discussion of stochastic orders.

Definition 5.2

(Convex Order)
.

Let
Y,\,Z\in{\mathcal{L}}_{T}^{\infty}
. We say that
Z
dominates
Y
in the convex order, denoted
Y\preccurlyeq_{\hskip-1.13809pt{}_{cx}}Z
, if for every convex function
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,

{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(Y)]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(Z)],

whenever the expectations are well defined. Moreover, if the above inequality is strict for some strictly convex function
\phi
, then we say that
Z
strictly dominates
Y
in the convex order, and use the notation
Y\prec_{\hskip-1.13809pt{}_{cx}}Z
.

We are now ready to state the dynamic comonotone improvement theorem. The proof of this result relies heavily on the comonotone improvement from the static case (see Theorem 3.1 of
Carlier et al. 2012
or Proposition
A.5

(ii)
in Appendix
A
).

Lemma 5.3

(Dynamic Comonotone Improvement Theorem)
.

For each
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}
, there exists
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
such that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
s\in\mathcal{T}
,

(24)

\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{(i)}+R_{s}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{s+1:T}^{(i)})\preccurlyeq_{\hskip-1.13809pt{}_{cx}}Y_{s}^{(i)}+R_{s}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{s+1:T}^{(i)}).

Moreover, if
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\notin\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
, then
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}
can be chosen such that for some
s\in\mathcal{T}
, there exists
j\in{\mathcal{N}}
such that

(25)

\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{(j)}+R_{s}^{(j)}(\widetilde{Y}_{s+1:T}^{(j)})\prec_{\hskip-1.13809pt{}_{cx}}Y_{s}^{(j)}+R_{s}^{(j)}(\widetilde{Y}_{s+1:T}^{(j)}).

Proof.

For
s=T
,
{\bm{Y}}_{s:T}
is a random vector, so the existence of
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{T}\in\mathcal{A}_{T}^{C}
such that (
24
) holds follows from Theorem 3.1 of
Carlier et al. 2012
. Next, we proceed by induction backwards in time. For this, assume that there exists
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t:T}
such that (
24
) holds for all
s\geq t
and let

{\bm{Z}}_{t-1}:=\left(Y_{t-1}^{(1)}+R_{t-1}^{(1)}(\widetilde{Y}_{t:T}^{(1)}),\ldots,Y_{t-1}^{(n)}+R_{t-1}^{(n)}(\widetilde{Y}_{t:T}^{(n)})\right).

Then,
{\bm{Z}}_{t-1}
is an allocation of
S_{t-1}+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}R_{t-1}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{t:T}^{(i)})
. By Theorem 3.1 of
Carlier et al. 2012
, there exists a comonotone allocation vector
\widetilde{{\bm{Z}}}_{t-1}
of
S_{t-1}+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}R_{t-1}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{t:T}^{(i)})
such that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

\widetilde{Z}_{t-1}^{(i)}\preccurlyeq_{\hskip-1.13809pt{}_{cx}}Y_{t-1}^{(i)}+R_{t-1}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{t:T}^{(i)}).

Let
\widetilde{Y}_{t-1}^{(i)}:=\widetilde{Z}_{t-1}^{(i)}-R_{t-1}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{t:T}^{(i)})
. Then,
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t-1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{t-1:T}^{C}
and
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t-1:T}
satisfies (
24
) for all
s\geq t-1
.

Finally, (
25
) follows from the same recursive construction (by applying the second half of Theorem 3.1 in
Carlier et al. 2012
).
∎

For
T=1
, the random vector
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}
in Lemma
5.3
is referred to as a comonotone improvement in the static case literature. For
T\geq 2
, the comonotone improvement
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}
differs from the classical notion due to the additional risk-to-go term. Furthermore, if
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}
is a comonotone process as defined in Definition
5.1
, then we can take
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1:T}={\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}
, in which case (
24
) automatically holds for
s\geq t+1
. We state this formally in the following corollary.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
t\in\mathcal{T}
and
{\bm{Y}}_{t:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{t:T}
with
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}
. Then, there exists
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}
such that
\{\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t},{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}\}\in\mathcal{A}_{t:T}^{C}
and

\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\preccurlyeq_{\hskip-1.13809pt{}_{cx}}Y_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)}),

for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Moreover, if
{\bm{Y}}_{t:T}\notin\mathcal{A}_{t:T}^{C}
, then
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t}
can be chosen such that there exists
j\in{\mathcal{N}}
with

\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{(j)}+R_{t}^{(j)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(j)})\prec_{\hskip-1.13809pt{}_{cx}}Y_{t}^{(j)}+R_{t}^{(j)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(j)}).

Proof.

Let
{\bm{Z}}_{t}:=\big(Y_{t}^{(1)}+R_{t}^{(1)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(1)}),\ldots,Y_{t}^{(n)}+R_{t}^{(n)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(n)})\big)
. Then, construct
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t}
in the same manner as in the proof of Lemma
5.3
.
∎

To make use of comonotone improvements, we require agent preferences to be monotone with respect to the convex order. Risk measures with this property are said to be convex order preserving, which we define next.

Definition 5.5

(Convex Order Preserving)
.

A one-step conditional risk measure
\rho_{t}
is convex order preserving if for all
Y,\,Z\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1}^{\infty}
such that
Y\preccurlyeq_{\hskip-1.13809pt{}_{cx}}Z
, we have
\rho_{t}(Y)\leq\rho_{t}(Z)
. If, in addition,
{\mathbb{P}}\big(\rho_{t}(Y)<\rho_{t}(Z)\big)>0
whenever
Y\prec_{\hskip-1.13809pt{}_{cx}}Z
, then we say that
\rho_{t}
is strictly convex order preserving.

Assumption 5.6
.

For
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
t\in\mathfrak{T}
, the
i
-th agent’s one-step conditional risk measure
\rho_{t}^{(i)}
preserves the convex order.

Note that if we further assume that agent preferences are monotone (i.e., Assumption
3.2
holds), then the risk-to-go at any time
t
of a comonotone improvement is upper bounded by the risk-to-go of the original allocation. We refer to Section
6.2
for an example of when this assumption is satisfied.

5.2.
Comonotone Myopic Pareto Optima

In this section, we revisit the notion of myopic Pareto optimality introduced in Section
3.2
. When agents are restricted to allocation processes that are comonotone in the sense of Definition
5.1
, we have the following definition of comonotone myopic Pareto optimality.

Definition 5.7

(Comonotone Myopic Pareto Optimality)
.

An allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}
is comonotone myopic PO if

(i)

{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}

(ii)

there does not exist an allocation process
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
that satisfies
\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{(i)})\leq\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)})
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and at least one inequality is strict.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
the set of all comonotone myopic PO allocations.

For
T=1
, comonotone myopic Pareto optimality reduces to the classical notion of comonotone Pareto optimality. Moreover, in Section
3.2
, we showed that myopic Pareto optima solve an inf-convolution problem of the form (
5
). Similarly, consider the following problem:

(26)

\underset{{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\,\in\,\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\,\cap\,\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}}{\inf}\ \sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{(i)})=\underset{{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\,\in\,\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\,\cap\,\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}}{\inf}\ \sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\overline{\rho}^{(i)}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}^{(i)}\right).

The next result shows that comonotone myopic Pareto optima solve the inf-convolution problem (
26
). The arguments in the proof are similar to the static case, which we include below for completeness.

Proposition 5.8
.

An allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}
is comonotone myopic PO if and only if
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}
solves (
26
).

Proof.

We first show that any allocation attaining the infimum (
26
) is comonotone myopic PO. Suppose that
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\notin\mathcal{C}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
. Then, there exists
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
such that
\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})\leq\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)})
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and at least one inequality is strict. Hence,

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})<\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)}).

Thus,
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}
does not attain the infimum (
26
) and we conclude that any allocation attaining the infimum (
26
) must be comonotone myopic PO.

Conversely, assume, by way of contradiction, that there exists
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
that does not attain the infimum (
26
). Then, there exists
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
such that

(27)

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})<\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)}),

Define a partition
{\mathcal{N}}_{1},{\mathcal{N}}_{2},{\mathcal{N}}_{3}
of
{\mathcal{N}}
such that

\displaystyle\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})>\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)}),\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{1},

\displaystyle\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})=\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)}),\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{2},

\displaystyle\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})<\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)}),\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{3}.

Note that
{\mathcal{N}}_{3}\neq\emptyset
by (
27
), and
{\mathcal{N}}_{1}\neq\emptyset
since
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
. Moreover, for
i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
, let

{\varepsilon}^{(i)}:=\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)})-\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})<0.

By (
27
), there exists
\{{\varepsilon}^{(i)}\}_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{3}}
such that

(i)

{\varepsilon}^{(i)}\leq 0
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{3}
;

(ii)

\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})-{\varepsilon}^{(i)}\leq\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)})
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{3}
, with at least one strict inequality; and

(iii)

\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{1}}{\varepsilon}^{(i)}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{3}}{\varepsilon}^{(i)}
.

Let

\widehat{Y}_{1}^{(i)}:=\begin{cases}\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{(i)}+{\varepsilon}^{(i)},&i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{1}\\
\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{(i)},&i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{2}\\
\widetilde{Y}_{1}^{(i)}-{\varepsilon}^{(i)},&i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{3}\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{2:T}=\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{2:T}.

Note that
\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}
since
(iii)
holds and
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{1:T}
. Moreover,
\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}
is comonotone since
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}
is comonotone and
{\varepsilon}^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}
for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{N}}_{3}
. Therefore,
\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
. Finally, by translation invariance,

\displaystyle\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widehat{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})=\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})+{\varepsilon}^{(i)}=\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)})\leq\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(X_{1:T}^{(i)}),\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all}\quad i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{1},

\displaystyle\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widehat{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})=\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)})\leq\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(X_{1:T}^{(i)}),\qquad\qquad\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all}\quad i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{2},

(28)

\displaystyle\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widehat{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})=\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{1:T}^{(i)})-{\varepsilon}^{(i)}\leq\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(Y_{1:T}^{*(i)})\leq\rho_{0,T}^{(i)}(X_{1:T}^{(i)}),\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all}\quad i\in{\mathcal{N}}_{3}.

Thus,
\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{M}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
. Moreover, at least one inequality in (
28
) is strict, contradicting the assumption that
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
.
∎

Similar to the case of myopic Pareto optimality in the unconstrained market, comonotone myopic Pareto optimality is a condition on the total allocation for each agent over time, and never defines a unique stochastic process. Hence, it is necessary to consider an alternative notion of optimality in the comonotone market.

5.3.
Comonotone Dynamic Pareto Optima

Next, we define the notions of comonotone Pareto optimality and comonotone Pareto optimality at time
t
. We further show that under Assumptions
3.2
and
5.6
, an allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}
is
c
-PO if and only if the sub-allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}
is
c
-PO at time
t
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. This result allows us to construct comonotone Pareto optima recursively.

Definition 5.9

(Comonotone Dynamic Pareto Optimality)
.

An allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}
is comonotone dynamic PO if

(i)

{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}

(ii)

there does not exist an allocation process
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
such that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
t\in\mathfrak{T}
,

\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\leq\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)}),

and
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})<\rho_{t,T}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right)>0
for some
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
t\in\mathfrak{T}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
the set of all comonotone dynamic PO allocations.

Definition 5.10

(Comonotone Pareto Optimality at Time
t
)
.

For
t\in\mathfrak{T}
, an allocation
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}
is comonotone PO at time
t
if

(i)

{\bm{Y}}^{*}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}

(ii)

there does not exist a random vector
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1}
with
\{{\bm{Y}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}
such that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big),

and
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)<\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\right)>0
for some
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
the set of all processes
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}
that are
c
-PO at time
t
.

The following result is the analogue of Lemma
3.11
for comonotone processes. This result allows us to characterize
c
-PO allocations by characterizing allocations that are
c
-PO at time
t
instead.

Lemma 5.11
.

If
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
, then
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
. Moreover, the converse also holds under Assumption
5.6
.

Proof.

Suppose that
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. Since
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
, it follows that
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
. Hence,
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}
satisfies the first condition of comonotone Pareto optimality. Next, let
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
such that for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
and
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)}).

To verify the second condition of comonotone Pareto optimality, it suffices to show that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
s\in\mathfrak{T}
,

(29)

R_{s}^{(i)}(Y_{s+1:T}^{(i)})=R_{s}^{(i)}(Y_{s+1:T}^{*(i)}).

The case when
s=T-1
holds by the same arguments as in the proof of Lemma
3.11
. Next, we proceed by induction backwards in time. For this, assume (
29
) holds for all
s\geq t
. It suffices to show that (
29
) also holds for
t-1
. By the arguments in Lemma
3.11
, we have

\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(X_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(X_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\big),\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}.

Thus,
\{{\bm{Y}}_{t},{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t-1}
. Next, we show that
\{{\bm{Y}}_{t},{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{A}_{t:T}^{C}
. Since
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
, the random vector

\left(Y_{s}^{*(1)}+R_{s}^{(1)}(Y_{s+1:T}^{*(1)}),\ldots,Y_{s}^{*(n)}+R_{s}^{(n)}(Y_{s+1:T}^{*(n)})\right),

is comonotone for all
s\geq t+1
. Moreover, by the induction hypothesis,

\left(Y_{t}^{(1)}+R_{t}^{(1)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(1)}),\ldots,Y_{t}^{(n)}+R_{t}^{(n)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(n)})\right)=\left(Y_{t}^{(1)}+R_{t}^{(1)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(1)}),\ldots,Y_{t}^{(n)}+R_{t}^{(n)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(n)})\right).

Since
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
, it follows that
\{{\bm{Y}}_{t},{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{A}_{t:T}^{C}
. The remainder of the induction argument follows from the arguments in the proof of Lemma
3.11
.

For the converse, let
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
and suppose that Assumption
5.6
holds. Assume, by way of contradiction, that
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\notin\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
for some
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. Since
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
, we have
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}
for all
t\in\mathfrak{T}
. Thus,
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\notin\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
implies that there exists
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1}
with
\{{\bm{Y}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)=1\ \text{for all}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}\ \text{and},

(30)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)<\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)>0\ \text{for some}\ i\in{\mathcal{N}}.

Let
\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}
be the process
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}
with the
(t+1)
-st vector replaced by
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1}
. Let
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
be a comonotone improvement of
\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}
(which exists by Lemma
5.3
). Next, we want to show that for all
s\in\mathfrak{T}
and
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

(31)

R_{s}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{s+1:T}^{(i)})\leq R_{s}^{(i)}(\widehat{Y}_{s+1:T}^{(i)})\leq R_{s}^{(i)}(Y_{s+1:T}^{*(i)}).

The second inequality follows from Equation (
15
) in the proof of Lemma
3.11
, so it remains to prove the first inequality. We proceed by induction. For
s=T-1
, (
31
) holds since
\rho_{T-1}^{(i)}
preserves the convex order for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Next, assume that (
31
) holds for
s\geq t
. We want to show that (
31
) holds for
s=t-1
. Note that for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

\displaystyle R_{t-1}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{t:T}^{(i)})

\displaystyle=\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(\widehat{Y}_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}\big(\widehat{Y}_{t}^{(i)}+R_{t}^{(i)}(\widehat{Y}_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\big)

\displaystyle=R_{t-1}^{(i)}(\widehat{Y}_{t:T}^{(i)}),

where the first inequality holds since
\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}
preserves the convex order for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
and the second inequality follows from monotonicity of
\rho_{t-1}^{(i)}
and the induction hypothesis. Hence, (
31
) holds for all
s\in\mathfrak{T}
and
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Finally, for
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
satisfying (
30
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(\widetilde{Y}_{t+2:T}^{(i)})\big)<R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right)\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)<R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right)>0,

where the first inequality follows from (
31
) and the last inequality follows from (
30
). Finally, (
31
) implies that
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}
and hence
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}\cap\mathcal{A}_{1:T}^{C}
. This contradicts the assumption that
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}
, so
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
.
∎

5.4.
Characterization of Comonotone Dynamic Pareto Optimal Allocations

In this section, we derive characterization results for
c
-PO allocations. We start with an analogue of Lemma
4.1
, which gives a useful representation of allocations that are
c
-PO at time
t
.

Lemma 5.12
.

For
t\in\mathfrak{T}
, let

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\biggl\{{\bm{Z}}_{t+1:T}\biggr.

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}~\big|~{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Z_{t+2:T}^{(i)})\big)\leq\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}R_{t}^{(i)}(Z_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\right)<1\ \text{or}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Z_{t+2:T}^{(i)})\big)<\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}R_{t}^{(i)}(Z_{t+1:T}^{(i)})\right)=0

\displaystyle\biggl.\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{for all}\ {\bm{Y}}_{t+1}\neq{\bm{Z}}_{t+1}\ \text{such that}\ \{{\bm{Y}}_{t+1},{\bm{Z}}_{t+2:T}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}\biggr\}.

Then,
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
. If, in addition, Assumption
5.6
holds (i.e., agent preferences preserve the convex order), then
\Gamma=\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
.

Proof.

The statement
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
follows from similar arguments as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
(with
\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}
instead of
\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
and
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
instead of
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
).

To show that
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}\subseteq\Gamma
, suppose that Assumption
5.6
holds and let
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
. Assume, by way of contradiction, that
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\notin\Gamma
. Then, by definition of
\Gamma
, there exists a random vector
\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1}
with
\{\widetilde{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)=1,\ \text{and}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)<\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{*(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\right)>0.

Let
\widehat{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}:=\widetilde{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+{\varepsilon}^{(i)}
, where
{\varepsilon}^{(i)}
is defined as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
. As
\{\widehat{{\bm{Y}}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}
and
{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+2:T}^{C}
, by Corollary
5.4
, there exists
{\bm{Y}}^{\prime}_{t+1}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+1}
such that
\{{\bm{Y}}^{\prime}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}
and

Y_{t+1}^{\prime(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\preccurlyeq_{\hskip-1.13809pt{}_{cx}}\widehat{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)}),

for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Furthermore, for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{\prime(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widehat{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\leq R_{t}^{(i)}(X_{t+1:T}^{(i)}),

where the first inequality holds since
\rho_{t}^{(i)}
is convex order preserving, the second inequality follows from similar arguments as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, and the last inequality holds since
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}
. Moreover, for some
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(Y_{t+1}^{\prime(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)<R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(\widehat{Y}_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)<R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{*(i)})\right)

\displaystyle>0,

where the last inequality follows from similar arguments as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
. Finally, since
\{{\bm{Y}}^{\prime}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}
, this contradicts the assumption that
{\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
.
∎

Lemma
5.12
states that if
{\bm{Z}}_{t+1:T}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
, then we cannot obtain a comonotone strict improvement in the total risk-to-go at time
t
by changing only the allocation of
S_{t+1}
. Furthermore, Lemma
5.12
allows us to derive an inf-convolution result for allocations that are
c
-PO at time
t
. The result is similar to Proposition
4.2
, except the infimum is now taken over all comonotone IR processes instead of all IR processes.

Proposition 5.13
.

Suppose that Assumption
5.6
holds. Let
t\in\mathfrak{T}
and
{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{t+2:T}^{C}
. Then,
\{{\bm{Z}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{O}_{t}
if and only if
{\bm{Z}}_{t+1}
attains the infimum

(32)

\underset{\left\{{\bm{W}}_{t+1}~|~\{{\bm{W}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}\right\}}{\inf}\,\psi\left(\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\left(W_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}\left(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)}\right)\right)\right).

for some monotone
\psi:{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that is strictly monotone on the set

\aleph^{C}:=\left\{V\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}~|~V=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\rho_{t}^{(i)}\big(V_{t+1}^{(i)}+R_{t+1}^{(i)}(Y_{t+2:T}^{*(i)})\big)\ \text{for some}\ \{{\bm{V}}_{t+1},{\bm{Y}}_{t+2:T}^{*}\}\in\mathcal{I}\mathcal{R}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}_{t+1:T}^{C}\right\}.

Proof.

This follows from similar arguments as in the proof of Proposition
4.2
(with Lemma
5.12
instead of Lemma
4.1
).
∎

The set
\aleph^{C}
is the set of attainable risks at time
t
in the comonotone market. Combining Proposition
5.13
and Lemma
5.11
yields the following characterization, which is the analogue of Theorem

Corollary

states that any PO allocation that is also comonotone is

5.6.
Explicit Characterization of Comonotone Dynamic Pareto Optima

In this section, we derive an explicit characterization of
c
-PO allocations. Before stating our main result, we introduce some additional notation. For an allocation process
{\bm{Y}}_{1:T}
, we let
{\overline{R}}_{t}({\bm{Y}}_{t+1:T}):=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}R_{t}^{(i)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(i)})
denote the total risk-to-go at time

its essential infimum. Whenever there is no confusion, we omit the dependence on

and simply write

\mathfrak{G}:=\left\{\{g^{(i)}\}_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}~|~g^{(i)}:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\ \text{is non-decreasing and}\ \sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}g^{(i)}=\text{Id}\right\},

where Id denotes the identity function. In the static case, a comonotone allocation can always be written as the sum of functions in

and a constant (see Lemma 3.13 in
Ghossoub and Zhu 2026

Lemma 5.18

For

and
\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}c_{t}^{(i)}=\underline{s}_{t}+\underline{r}_{t}
.

Proof.

By definition, the random vector
\big(Y_{t}^{(1)}+R_{t}^{(1)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(1)}),\ldots,Y_{t}^{(n)}+R_{t}^{(n)}(Y_{t+1:T}^{(n)})\big)

Ghossoub and Zhu 2026
.
∎

We refer to the functions

S_{t}
and the total risk-to-go (at time
t

i

Definition 5.19

A Choquet integral with respect to a distortion function

is the mapping

given by

(43)

I_{k}(X):=\int_{0}^{\infty}k\left(\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\right)\,dx+\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left[k\left(\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\right)-1\right]\,dx,\ \text{for all}\ X\in L_{t}^{\infty},

where

and

k

Many properties of a Choquet integral are determined by its distortion function

is a coherent risk measure. We refer to
Denneberg 1994

Lemma 5.20

Let

where
\mathcal{K}
is a convex set of concave distortion functions that is sequentially closed under pointwise convergence.

Lemma
5.20

6.1.
An Algorithmic Approach to Finding Dynamic Pareto Optima

In summary, we propose the following recursive approach for finding

(i)

For the terminal time

(ii)

For each solution

\displaystyle\underset{k\in{\mathcal{N}}}{\min}\,\left\{k_{t}^{*(k)}\Big({\mathbb{P}}\left(S_{t}+{\overline{R}}_{t}>x+\underline{r}_{t}+\underline{s}_{t}\right)\Big)\right\}\biggr\}.

This set represents the agents with the most optimistic assessment of the likelihood of the tail event

and the risk-to-go at time

(iii)

Identify all

satisfying the necessary condition in Part

6.2.
Pareto-Optimal Allocations in a Two-Period Setting

In this section, we study an example with
n=2

periods. Assume that
S_{1}
follows an exponential distribution with mean
200
, and that the conditional distribution of
S_{2}

is exponential with mean
S_{1}
. We are interested in the retention structure of the agent allocations (i.e., the functions

given by Theorem

). Since the constants
c_{t}^{*(i)}
only shift the retention functions and do not change the shape of the retention structure, we will not discuss them in our example.

To evaluate the risk at time 1, the first agent uses the Expected Shortfall (ES) at level
\alpha=0.9
while the second agent uses the expected value. Recall that the ES at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
is given by

{\mathrm{ES}}_{\alpha}(X):=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{\alpha}^{1}F_{X}^{-1}(t)\,dt,

where
F_{X}^{-1}(\alpha):=\inf\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}|F_{X}(x)\geq\alpha\}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, is the (left-continuous) quantile function of the random variable
X
. Thus, the ES is a distortion risk measure with distortion weight function
\gamma(u):=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}{\mathds{1}}_{u>\alpha}
. To evaluate the risk at time 2, both agents will use the ES, but the parameter
\alpha
will depend on the realization of
S_{1}
. In other words, the risk measure of agent 1 is given by

\rho_{0:2}^{(1)}(Y_{1:2})={\mathrm{ES}}_{0.9}\big(Y_{1}+{\mathrm{ES}}_{\alpha^{(1)}(S_{1})}(Y_{2})\big),

where
\alpha^{(1)}(S_{1})=\begin{cases}0,&S_{1}\leq F_{S_{1}}^{-1}(0.2)\\
0.9,&S_{1}>F_{S_{1}}^{-1}(0.2)\end{cases}.

The risk measure of agent 2 is given by

\rho_{0:2}^{(2)}(Y_{1:2})={\mathbb{E}}[Y_{1}+{\mathrm{ES}}_{\alpha^{(2)}(S_{1})}(Y_{2})],

where
\alpha^{(2)}(S_{1})=\begin{cases}0,&S_{1}\leq F_{S_{1}}^{-1}(0.6)\\
0.99,&S_{1}>F_{S_{1}}^{-1}(0.6)\end{cases}.

In this example, agent 2 uses a less conservative risk measures to evaluate the risk at time 1, and hence we expect that they will retain all of the risk at time 1. For both agents, their assessment of the time 2 risk depends on the realized value of
S_{1}
. For the first agent, if the realization of
S_{1}
is less than the
0.2
quantile, then they will use the expected value to evaluate the risk at time 2. In contrast, if the realization of
S_{1}
is large, then they will continue to use the ES with level
\alpha=0.9
. For the second agent, if the realization of
S_{1}
is less than the
0.6
quantile, then they will continue using the expected value. Otherwise, they will switch to the ES with level
\alpha=0.99
. In other words, agent 2 becomes more conservative than agent 1 if
S_{1}
is large. Since
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are positively correlated, we expect agent 1 to retain the tranche of the aggregate endowment
S_{2}
when
S_{2}
is large. The following calculations show that the PO retention structure aligns with this intuition.

We begin with Step
(i)
of the algorithm for the terminal time
T=2
. That is, we calculate the distortion functions of the risk measures
\varrho_{2}^{(i)}(\cdot):={\mathbb{E}}[\rho_{1}^{(i)}(\cdot)]
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
. For the first agent,

\displaystyle\varrho_{2}^{(1)}(X)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[{\mathrm{ES}}_{\alpha^{(1)}(S_{1})}(X)]

\displaystyle=0.2\,{\mathbb{E}}[X]+0.8\,{\mathrm{ES}}_{0.9}(X)

\displaystyle=0.2\int_{0}^{1}F_{X}^{-1}(u)\,du+0.8\int_{0}^{1}10\,{\mathds{1}}_{u>0.9}\,F_{X}^{-1}(u)\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(0.2+8\,{\mathds{1}}_{u>0.9})\,F_{X}^{-1}(u)\,du.

Hence,
\varrho_{2}^{(1)}(X)
is a distortion risk measure with distortion weight function
\gamma_{2}^{(1)}(u)=0.2+8\,{\mathds{1}}_{u>0.9}
. Since

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we study Pareto optima in a discrete-time multi-period pure-exchange economy, where agents have preferences over stochastic endowment processes that are represented by strongly time-consistent dynamic risk measures, which have been extensively studied in the risk management literature. Whereas the literature on dynamic optimal allocations studies Pareto efficiency from the perspective of time-
0
preferences, which we refer to as
myopic Pareto optimality
, we introduce the notion of
dynamic Pareto-optimal
allocation processes that takes into account the dynamic structure of risk profiles in the decision making process. We show that such dynamic Pareto-optimal allocation processes can be constructed recursively backwards in time, starting with the allocation at the terminal time, and that the allocation at each time point is the solution to a social welfare optimization problem.

Furthermore, we extend the classical notion of comonotonicity to the dynamic setting, and we derive a dynamic comonotone improvement theorem for allocation processes. This motivates the study of dynamic Pareto optima in a comonotone dynamic market. We provide a crisp characterization of Pareto optima when agent preferences are coherent and satisfy a property that we call
equidistribution-preserving
, and we provide a clear and easily implementable recursive algorithm for finding these optima. In the special case of the single period problem, we recover the characterization of
Ghossoub and Zhu 2026
. Finally, we demonstrate our algorithmic approach in a two-period numerical example.

In this appendix, we briefly discuss the optimal-allocation problem in the static setting, which is well studied in the literature. In the static setting, we work with the set of essentially bounded random variables
{\mathcal{L}}^{\infty}
on a non-atomic probability space

. We consider a problem with

i

that is realized at the end of the period. The
n

based on their preferences characterized by a (static) risk measure. That is, agent-
i
uses the risk measure

i\in{\mathcal{N}}
. We recall below some classical definitions in the static setting.

(Allocation - Static)

A random vector

An allocation
