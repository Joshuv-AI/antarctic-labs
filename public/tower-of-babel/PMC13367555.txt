Pulmonary tuberculosis and the surgeon
Authors: Sabyasachi Bal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThough medical management is the cornerstone of management of pleuropulmonary tuberculosis today, surgery may play a significant collaborative role, both in its diagnosis and in its treatment in well defined clinical scenarios. In drug resistant tuberculosis, empyemas and bronchial stenosis, surgery may play a very important role and may also successfully resolve symptoms produced by TB sequelae such as symptomatic bronchiectasis and aspergillomas. Recent advances in thoracic surgery and the use of minimally invasive surgical techniques has made surgical options safer and with a significant decrease in morbidity and mortality, thereby enabling surgical solutions for a bigger number of tuberculosis related problems. It is important for all clinicians treating tuberculosis to understand the indications for surgery and to work together with surgeons so that the most appropriate treatment can be offered to the patient at the correct time. Perhaps the time has also come to explore the role of multidisciplinary teams to enable this to happen. This review attempts to define the role and timing of surgery in the management of pleuropulmonary tuberculosis.

While the burden of tuberculosis (TB) continues to be high and most cases still occur in less developed countries, there has been, in recent years, an increase in the incidence even in the Western world, especially in densely populated urban areas.[1] Globally, an estimated 10 million people develop TB, and over a million deaths occur annually.[2] India accounts for about 25% of the global TB burden, with an estimated TB incidence of 2.77 million in 2022.[3]

Historically, surgery has had a valuable role to play in the management of tuberculosis but with the advent of effective medical anti-tubercular therapy, there has been a paradigm shift, and surgery has been relegated to a secondary player. Furthermore, in recent years, there has been a resurgence of interest in surgery for TB for and there is today a better understanding of its role as an effective modality in a variety of clinical scenarios. The re-emergence of surgery in the management of TB has occurred for a number of reasons.

This review will define the surgeon’s role in the management of TB and will elaborate on the clinical scenarios where the surgeon can play a role. Whenever surgery is being considered, it is prudent to discuss the clinical details in a multi-disciplinary team meeting to define the aim of surgery, to assess the risks of anesthesia and surgery, the anticipated results including probability and severity of complications with consequent prolonged hospital stay and a realistic assessment of the potential benefits of surgery. Combined decision making and patient counseling allay fears and enable true informed consent for the proposed procedure.

THE SURGEON’S ROLE IN DIAGNOSIS

The surgeon may be called upon to help in clarifying clinical presentations where the diagnosis is uncertain or where alternate possibilities exist, notably where a malignancy cannot be ruled out. Common scenarios where diagnostic confusion may exist are in patients with the following:

1. Indeterminate nodules and mass lesions as in tuberculomas, tuberculomas are round, granulomatous tumors of tubercles that are primarily observed in healed primary tuberculosis infection but can occasionally also exist in post-primary tuberculosis.[6] Most are smaller than 3 cm but large mass lesions have been reported. Satellite smaller lesions may exist.[7] Pulmonary tuberculoma develop in approximately 7–9% of tuberculosis patients.[8]

A tissue diagnosis is obviously mandatory. With a roadmap provided by contrast-enhanced dynamic CT parameters[9] and a PET scan, and using EBUS, newer navigational bronchoscopic techniques and CT-guided biopsies in selected cases, a diagnosis is usually possible. Where uncertainty exists, a video-assisted wedge excision of the lesion will provide a diagnosis. Recent advances using non-intubated VATS techniques have made the procedure better.[10]

In many cases, bronchoscopic or needle biopsy may not be practically possible, and VATS provides an excellent minimally invasive option.[1112] Needless to say, a diagnosis is always obtained in these cases because inadequacy of sampling is never an issue.

2. Significant mediastinal lymphadenopathy,

Accurate diagnosis of patients with isolated mediastinal lymphadenopathy poses a challenge because a myriad of pathologies can have similar presentations, and a definitive diagnosis is important.[13]

The diagnosis of isolated mediastinal TB lymphadenitis, which occurs in approximately 6-10%, is difficult. In these cases, the lung parenchyma is not involved, the bacterial load is low, and bronchoscopy is unhelpful. The computed tomography (CT) characteristics of mediastinal TB lymphadenitis are non-specific, and positive microbiologic and/or pathologic confirmation is necessary.[1415]

EBUS-TBNA has emerged as the primary investigation for this condition. Claims of very high sensitivity and specificity have been made,[1617] but the yield is perhaps (using EBUS-TBNA and TB-PCR more like around 60-70%.[1819]

If the success rate is around 70% only, the logical question that should arise is ‘what methods of diagnosis were used to diagnose the remaining patients, and what was the role of surgical methods in these patients?’. Unfortunately, this issue is not addressed in any paper that deals with EBUS-TBNA as a diagnostic modality,[20] so we have no way of knowing the diagnostic algorithm, and any conclusion is bound to be speculative.

As far as surgical techniques are concerned, the mediastinum can be surgically explored principally with parasternal mediastinotomy (Chamberlain procedure), mediastinoscopy (both cervical and video-assisted), and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery. Surgery is currently reserved for those cases in which an endoscopic procedure is not indicated or does not provide a diagnosis, and furthermore, surgery may also be therapeutic by removing lymph nodes[17]

3. Tissue invasion and consolidation without response to medical treatment or progression, and those with an atypical clinical course. In these patients, again, the underlying fear is that of missing a more ominous, yet treatable pathology. Poor response or progression also needs to be explained satisfactorily. TB and malignancy can mimic each other. Furthermore, TB and cancer can also coexist, and cancer can also arise in areas earlier affected by TB, the so-called ‘scar carcinoma’. It seems to be common practice to add additional drugs in this group of patients without a basis, which seems unjustified. A tissue diagnosis with relevant microbiology is important, and VATS biopsy may play an important role if the specimen cannot be obtained by less invasive means. Similarly, it seems to be common practice to start anti-tubercular treatment when a patient who has been treated for TB presents with fresh symptoms on the assumption that this is a reactivation or reinfection. This is very fallacious, and a routine diagnosis of recurrent TB (simple or drug-resistant) is not justified without tissue or microbiological confirmation. Empirical starting of anti-tubercular treatment in these clinical scenarios cannot be justified.

It is well understood that the search for diagnosis obviously moves from non-invasive methods to surgical biopsy. It is also important to understand that no radiological imaging can yet confirm a diagnosis of TB affecting the lung parenchyma. Even modern CT and positron emission tomography have proven too non-specific, especially in the Indian scenario. In patients who have clinical manifestations and radiological features that suggest TB and for whom less invasive investigative modalities have failed to yield a positive diagnosis, surgical biopsy is definitely indicated.

Percutaneous imaging-guided biopsy of suspicious lung lesions yields highly variable rates of positive diagnosis. Published series have suggested rates of positive diagnosis for TB ranging from 80% to as low as 20%.[21]

Fiber-optic bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage and/or transbronchial biopsy may give a positive diagnosis in only 30 to 58% of smear-negative TB cases.[21222324]

Surgical lung biopsy, therefore, remains necessary to confirm the diagnosis in a proportion of patients suspected to have TB. The approach is usually by VATS, with the exception of those patients who do not tolerate one-lung ventilation, though this is increasingly being overcome by performing VATS biopsies using non-intubated anaesthesia. Histological and microbiological confirmation additionally helps in accurate management and picking up MDR and XDR variants and allows accurate treatment.

4. Undiagnosed, persistent or recurrent pleural effusions.

The diagnosis of tubercular pleural effusion can be difficult and the yield from various investigations to diagnose tubercular pleural effusion has been well summarised[25] and clearly shows the superiority of invasive methods to establish etiology. The gold standard for definitively diagnosing pTB is detection of Mtb in pleural fluid or pleural tissue, or demonstration of caseating granulomata on pleural biopsy, ideally in the presence of acid-fast bacilli (AFB) and this is better achieved with a biopsy.[2526]

A rational diagnostic approach should begin with comprehensive pleural fluid analysis and, where appropriate, analysis of sputum. In high TB prevalence areas, a presumptive diagnosis may be made on the basis of a lymphocyte-predominant exudate with a high ADA; however, despite the high sensitivity and specificity of ADA, many patients of tubercular pleural effusion have their ADA levels below the accepted cut off values and therefore, will ideally require pleural biopsy to confirm their diagnosis.[27] Empirical therapy without biopsy has often been used, but empirical treatment should always be balanced with the risk of drug-resistant TB in that individual. Where the pre-test probability of drug-resistant TB is high, invasive testing to definitively identify the organism and its drug sensitivity profile is definitely indicated.[2627]

Closed pleural biopsy with or without image guidance was the commonly used procedure in the past, but pleuroscopy (also called medical thoracoscopy) or VATS is better to achieve the diagnostic goals.[27] The yield with surgical VATS seems to be better than medical pleuroscopy, and false negatives have been reported with medical thoracoscopy. The results of closed pleural biopsy, medical thoracoscopy, and VATS have been summarised succinctly.[25]

In patients who are symptomatic, the pleural fluid is usually aspirated for relief but complete evacuation may not be possible and may encourage formation of loculations which increases the chance of therapeutic intervention by VATS. Patients who have been aspirated successfully may have recurrence of the effusion due to a number of reasons including incomplete evacuation, thickened pleura,[282930] loculation, drug resistance and paradoxical increase of activity, and a VATS procedure may help to evacuate all the fluid and remove all fibrinopurulent flakes and carry out an early decortication if necessary and t0 obtain tissue for histological and microbiological examination. Although logical, whether complete removal of fluid by an Intercostal tube (vis-à-vis pleural aspiration) reduces the incidence of thickness of peel and prevents lung restriction is controversial and probably needs further dedicated studies before a definitive opinion can be formed. The role of intrapleural streptokinase is controversial. It is true, however, that residual pleural thickening of > 10 mm is seen in 20–46% of patients and has been associated with chronic chest pain, dyspnoea and lung function impairment.[2829303132] Early complete drainage may therefore be beneficial in avoiding these sequelae and early VATS decortication may provide better drainage and avoid the above complications. This definitely needs multidisciplinary discussion and further clinical studies.

THE SURGEON’S ROLE IN THERAPY

Failure of medical therapy might require surgery, provided the patient is medically fit and will tolerate anaesthesia and anatomical lung resection. The decision for surgery should ideally be taken in a multidisciplinary team meeting, which discusses the safety, extent and indication of the proposed intervention so that accurate counseling and risk stratification can be done.

THE PROBLEM OF CAVITARY TUBERCULOSIS AND POST-TREATMENT RESIDUAL FOCUS

Cavitary TB carries a poor prognosis. There is a higher risk of treatment failure and relapse if cavities are radiographically present during the first two months of therapy.[46] Then, if cavities persist after 6 months of treatment, the risk of relapse doubles compared to those whose cavities close by treatment completion.[47] studies suggest that pulmonary resection combined with anti-TB chemotherapy for MDR-TB can achieve high success rates.[484950]

Guidelines also suggest that surgery for drug-resistant TB be done early in the course of the disease when the patient’s risk of additional morbidity and mortality is low.[51] Unfortunately, the lack of robust trials evaluating the true role of surgery are lacking thus making definitive opinions difficult if not impossible.[52]

Between 20 and 50% of cavitary TB patients have persistent cavities after completion of anti-TB treatment.[53] Their management evokes strong diverse opinions. The prevalent opinion is that surgery for residual cavitary lesions after successful medical treatment in a sputum-negative patient without symptoms is not warranted or justified but certain caveats exist. It is known that the healing response may be incomplete and may result in fibrotic scarring which can lead to open (cavity airspace remains) or closed healing (cavity resolved to scar tissue or calcified foci).[5455] Whether the belief that cavities which have become smaller or fibrosed do not need surgery is true or not is uncertain because occasional case reports provide contrary evidence.[56] Certainly, greater acceptance for surgery exists for cavitary lung lesions with negative sputum/culture and where the lesion is larger than 8-10 cm because these lesions have been shown to be prone to reactivation and relapse.[5758]

The transformation of the cold cavity to a hot one happens in 20-30% according to Russian experience,[35] justifying an active surgical attitude even in culture-negative cases though controversy exists, especially in countries with low incidence but a 10% relapse rate in medically treated patients is cannot be easily dismissed.[4159] Therapy-resistant caseous pneumonia, infiltrative pulmonary tuberculosis with positive sputum after 6–8 month of treatment have also been seen as indications for resection though again, there is no consensus.[6061]

Tuberculomas come to surgery (VATS resection) because of the difficulty of differentiating them from a lung cancer. If the diagnosis is made by non-invasive means then a tuberculoma larger than 2 cm in a sputum/culture negative patient, is usually treated with extended anti-tuberculous medication. Others argue that the inflammatory barrier is the hindrance of the sufficient drug concentration and advise resection.[6101235] The possibility of a malignancy should not be forgotten in a patient whose tuberculoma is refractory to treatment.[62]

SEQUELAE OF TREATED TUBERCULOSIS

Even after successful medical treatment, the tuberculosis afflicted lung may become totally non-functional. The term “destroyed lung” has been used in these cases and has the potential for repeated secondary infections, hemoptysis and causing infection in the contralateral lung.

Patients with this medical condition may have a history of exposure to multiple TB therapies, with delayed recovery and/or drug resistance.[6364] The surgical rationale is to interrupt further M. tuberculosis transmission and to prevent life-threatening complications. In most cases, a pneumonectomy will be necessary.

Secondary (tuberculous) pneumothorax may occur and its management is often made difficult because of the inability of the remaining lung to expand.[65] Thoracic empyema with or without bronchopleural fistula is treated with established methods. In case of a bronchopleural fistula the tube thoracostomy is usually the initial treatment option. Open thoracostomy is usually recommended for severely ill patients with a high operative risk. Two-stage management with open thoracostomy and subsequent muscle plombage of the cleaned residual space is also suggested as a reasonable alternative in patients with poor general conditions and extensive calcifications precluding decortications,[416667] regardless of the causative organism. Haemoptysis in most cases, can be stabilised by a combination of conservative measures supplanted with bronchial artery embolisation. Thus, emergency surgery with its higher morbidity and mortality can be avoided. Persistent or recurrent hemoptysis, however, will need surgery.[41] The risk factors associated with recurrence after successful embolisation for life-threatening haemoptysis are a lack of complete cessation of haemoptysis within seven days after the procedure, the need for blood transfusion, the presence of aspergillomas and the absence of TB.[68]

Fibrothorax might benefit from decortication, stripping the encapsulating cortex and freeing the trapped lung. Re-expansion oedema, cytokine storm of a suddenly expanding lung, needs a close cooperation between surgeon and anesthetist.[1221] Many of these decortications can now be done by minimally invasive techniques.[2169]

Tuberculous involvement of the bronchi can occur and may result in stenosis of the major airway with distal lung collapse. These need surgery but only when the active disease process has been controlled. No surgery is advised in the central airways during an active process. It is also important to ensure viability of the distal parenchyma. Pre-operative angiography and even positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) might help in this decision. Permanent chest wall sinuses (with or without rib osteomyelitis) need active intervention following proven local sterilisation.[35]

PROTECTION OF HEALTHCARE PERSONNEL

It is important to remember the risks posed by surgical procedures on TB patients to healthcare personnel involved in the care of such patients. TB is an airborne infection and can spread through the air even during a surgical procedure. OR personnel have been reported to develop infection after surgery.[70] This can be prevented by mandatory adoption of strict precautions against airborne infections during medical procedures on a patient with confirmed or suspected active TB. This also applies to staff in endoscopy units and pathology departments during handling of respiratory tract specimens. Excellent protocols exist and can be adopted by individual units.[21]
