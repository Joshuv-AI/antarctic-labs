Family Empowerment for Stunting Prevention in Children Aged 0–2 Years: A Concept Analysis
Authors: Titin Sutini, Windy Rakhmawati, Henny Suzana Mediani, Tria Astika Endah Permatasari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStunting in children aged 0–2 years remains a major global health challenge, particularly in low‑ and middle‑income countries, with long‑term consequences for growth and development. Although family empowerment is widely promoted as a strategy for stunting prevention, its conceptualization within stimulation, early detection, and family‑centered care remains fragmented.MethodsGuided by Walker and Avant’s concept analysis framework, this study systematically examined literature retrieved from PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar (July 2025). Studies were screened for relevance to family empowerment, caregiving, health literacy, and growth monitoring in the context of children aged 0–2 years.ResultsTwenty‑seven eligible studies were synthesized to refine the construct of family empowerment in stunting prevention. Across the reviewed literature, family empowerment was consistently conceptualized as a multidimensional process characterized by five interrelated attributes—reflective decision‑making, self‑efficacy, participation, health literacy integration, and resource mobilization—positioning families as active agents in growth monitoring, stimulation, and timely intervention during the first 1000 days of life.ConclusionThis analysis reframes family empowerment as a strategic, family‑centered pathway for stunting prevention. By advancing conceptual clarity within nursing and culturally diverse caregiving contexts, the findings inform practice and policy through the integration of empowerment indicators alongside growth monitoring and highlight priorities for future research focused on culturally responsive validation of empowerment measures.Keywords: concept analysis, early childhood, child nutrition, family empowerment, family-centre care, stunting prevention

Introduction

Stunting in children aged 0–2 years remains a critical global health challenge, associated with impaired linear growth and delayed cognitive development.1 Despite extensive global and national initiatives, stunting prevalence remains disproportionately high in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.2 From a nursing perspective, early childhood stunting is closely linked to family caregiving contexts and caregiver capacity, highlighting the importance of family-centered care, health literacy, and empowerment as key domains for nursing practice and intervention.3

Although family empowerment is increasingly recognized as important for child health, its application within stunting prevention remains conceptually and operationally fragmented.4 Many studies continue to equate empowerment with maternal education alone, overlooking its multidimensional nature that encompasses caregiver agency, decision‑making, and linkage with health and community systems.5 Instruments such as the Family Empowerment Scale (FES) and Parenting Sense of Competence Scale (PSOC), originally developed in Western contexts, may have limited cultural transferability in LMIC settings characterized by collective caregiving and distinct sociocultural norms.6

In LMIC contexts where caregiving practices are strongly shaped by cultural and family systems, evidence from Indonesia demonstrates that family empowerment is influenced by transcultural caregiving factors and is closely associated with the quality of complementary feeding practices, underscoring contextual variation in how empowerment is defined and operationalized within stunting prevention efforts.7 Recent stunting prevention initiatives in several LMICs, including Indonesia and Ethiopia, report increased parental participation; however, many rely on adapted programmatic frameworks rather than culturally validated empowerment measures, leaving important contextual dimensions underexplored.4,8 This gap has been consistently noted in recent literature, highlighting persistent challenges in validity, contextual adaptability, and operationalization of empowerment‑oriented approaches across diverse caregiving systems.9

Building on prior concept analysis scholarship, this study reframes family empowerment as a multidimensional construct encompassing knowledge, caregiver agency, and linkage with health and community systems, functioning simultaneously as both a process and an outcome of family‑centered care.10 Guided by Walker and Avant’s concept analysis method, empowerment is situated within established nursing theoretical perspectives to demonstrate its relevance for stunting prevention.11 Accordingly, this study aims to systematically examine and conceptually integrate the antecedents, defining attributes, and consequences of family empowerment in the context of stunting prevention, in order to develop a theoretically grounded conceptual model to inform family‑centered practice.

To date, no concept analysis has explicitly delineated family empowerment in the context of stunting prevention during the critical first 1,000 days of life, resulting in limited theoretical clarity and constraining the development of culturally responsive family‑centered interventions.

Methods

Concept Analysis Approach

This study employed Walker and Avant’s concept analysis method, a structured framework widely used in nursing scholarship to refine theoretical constructs.12–14 The eight steps—selecting the concept, determining the purpose, identifying uses, defining attributes, constructing model and alternative cases, identifying antecedents and consequences, and determining empirical referents—were applied systematically. Each step was interpreted to ensure both theoretical fidelity and practical relevance, allowing family empowerment to be examined as a conceptual construct and as a pathway for nursing interventions in stunting prevention. To strengthen theoretical integration, findings were consistently interpreted within nursing frameworks such as Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory, Roy’s Adaptation Model, and Family Systems Nursing.

Search Strategy and Selection

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in July 2025 using PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar, yielding a total of 472 records. After duplicate removal, records were screened by title and abstract against predefined eligibility criteria. Studies were included if they examined parents, mothers, or families in relation to stunting prevention or nutrition in children aged 0–2 years and explicitly addressed empowerment, caregiving, health literacy, or growth monitoring. To ensure conceptual breadth, studies outside direct stunting contexts were also retained if they discussed empowerment in caregiving, parental participation, or family‑centered health literacy. Studies were excluded if they focused solely on biomedical interventions without family‑centered components, addressed irrelevant topics or populations, or presented opinion‑based content without sufficient analytic depth. In line with PRISMA‑informed guidelines for transparent reporting of the search and selection process,15 a total of 27 articles were retained for analysis, comprising 14 stunting‑specific studies and 13 studies from related health contexts, including Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) care, chronic illness, maternal health literacy, and empowerment scale development. These studies provided sufficient conceptual depth to support the concept analysis.

Data Extraction and Analysis

Data were extracted on definitions, attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents, and organized into a comparative matrix (Supplementary Table 1). Core studies informed stunting‑specific attributes, while supporting studies were used to enhance theoretical clarity across related health contexts. Each element was systematically mapped to Walker and Avant’s eight steps to maintain methodological consistency. To enhance analytic rigor, concept identification and categorization were conducted iteratively using constant comparison across studies.

Results

Uses of the Concept of Family Empowerment

The review of 27 articles shows that family empowerment has been applied across diverse health contexts, with its meaning evolving over time. In NICU and Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), it was described as strengthening parental knowledge, confidence, and participation. In maternal health literacy, the focus shifted to autonomy and reflective questioning. Community-based interventions expanded it to social independence, collective action, and caregiving in nutrition and stimulation. In chronic illness, empowerment was defined through self-efficacy, educational participation, and collaborative family management.

Recent studies in LMICs highlight empowerment as directly relevant to stunting prevention, linking it to maternal decision-making, household resource use, and nutrition-sensitive practices. Across contexts, empowerment consistently involves parental involvement, reflective decision-making, and active engagement with health services.

This trajectory shows a shift from coping strategies toward a multidimensional, proactive approach to child health promotion. Empowerment now bridges individual agency with systemic support, moving beyond isolated caregiving to a holistic framework integrating confidence, reflective decision-making, and collaboration with health systems.

Identify a Model Case

The cases presented (Mrs. A, Mrs. B, Mrs. C, and Mrs. D) are illustrative examples developed for the purpose of concept clarification in accordance with Walker and Avant’s concept analysis method. They do not represent real individuals or empirical data.

Mrs. A, a 27‑year‑old mother of a 10‑month‑old child, attends routine growth monitoring at a local health center. She demonstrates reflective decision-making by assessing weight trajectory and discussing feeding adequacy, shows self-efficacy in adjusting complementary feeding and stimulation, actively participates in consultations, integrates health literacy into daily routines, and mobilizes resources through parenting groups and professional communication. As a result, her child shows improved growth and responsiveness. This case exemplifies how all five attributes operate synergistically, transforming empowerment into a lived pathway that strengthens resilience and supports stunting prevention.

Identify Borderline, Related, Contrary Cases

Borderline Case

Mrs. B, a 25‑year‑old mother of an 8‑month‑old child, attends growth monitoring. She shows limited reflective decision-making, fragile self-efficacy, and minimal participation, relying heavily on the nurse. Knowledge integration is inconsistent and resource use sporadic. This case illustrates partial empowerment, resulting in limited effectiveness for child growth.

Related Case

Mrs. C, a 30‑year‑old mother of a 1‑year‑old child, applies feeding guidelines and engages with health workers, showing literacy integration and resource use. However, empowerment remains program-driven, with low autonomy and reflective decision-making. This case demonstrates attributes present but constrained by external guidance.

Contrary Case

Mrs. D, a 22‑year‑old mother of a 9‑month‑old child, shows no reflective decision-making, lacks self-efficacy, avoids participation, fails to integrate knowledge, and is isolated from resources. This case illustrates absence of empowerment, leading to poor caregiving and heightened stunting risk.

Identify Antecedents and Consequences

Antecedents

Family empowerment in stunting prevention among children aged 0–2 years is preceded by enabling conditions: adequate parental knowledge and health literacy, supportive health services with clear communication, social and community support, psychological readiness, and cultural-contextual factors shaping caregiving practices. These antecedents provide the foundation for reflective decision-making, self-efficacy, participation, literacy integration, and resource mobilization (Figure 1).

Consequences

Empowered families demonstrate improved caregiving practices (feeding, growth monitoring, stimulation), enhanced self-efficacy and resilience, active collaboration with professionals, and effective resource utilization. Collectively, these outcomes strengthen continuity of care, community engagement, and child growth trajectories, reducing stunting risk (Figure 1).

Empirical Referents

Family empowerment can be operationalized through validated instruments: the Family Empowerment Scale (FES)16,17 for decision-making, the Parenting Sense of Competence (PSOC)18–20 for self-efficacy, the Parental Empowerment through Awareness, Relationships, and Resources (PEARR)21 for participation, the Health Literacy Questionnaire (HLS-EU-Q)22 for literacy integration, and the Family Resource Scale (FRS)23 for resource use (Table 1). While developed in Western contexts, their application in LMICs requires cultural adaptation to capture collective caregiving and contextual nuances. This underscores the need for validation studies that move beyond knowledge transfer toward empowerment-driven, culturally responsive interventions.

Discussion

This concept analysis situates family empowerment as a multidimensional construct central to stunting prevention among children aged 0–2 years. The five attributes—reflective decision-making, self-efficacy, participation, literacy integration, and resource mobilization—function interdependently, enabling families to engage in stimulation, early detection, and timely intervention. By reframing parental education, health literacy, and community support, the study advances a holistic caregiving framework.

Unlike prior models that emphasize individual agency through self-efficacy and parental decision-making,24,25 this analysis positions empowerment as a dynamic process embedded in relational, cultural, and systemic contexts. Cross-country comparisons show consistent emphasis on strengthening family capacity, but strategies differ: in Asia (Indonesia, Japan, Thailand), empowerment is rooted in community values through nutrition education and health worker support, while in Western countries (Canada, Finland) it is formalized via shared decision-making and equal partnerships (Supplementary Table 1). Yet many interventions remain compliance-driven, measuring attendance or knowledge rather than autonomy.4

The proposed model suggests a pathway where antecedents such as literacy and supportive environments activate attributes—particularly reflective decision‑making and self‑efficacy—which then translate into improved caregiving and child growth. Evidence links parental education to improved nutrition, but most studies emphasize maternal education.26 Comparative analyses in LMICs indicate that paternal education is equally influential, yet often overlooked,27 while maternal–paternal interactions remain underexplored, leaving empowerment framed as an individual rather than a collective process.28

Health literacy is often treated as static knowledge; however, without supportive environments and confidence‑building, knowledge alone is unlikely to translate into sustained caregiving practices.29 Embedding empowerment strategies into health services enables health literacy to be coupled with self‑efficacy and participatory engagement,30 aligning with Orem’s Self‑Care Deficit Theory.31 Identifying resource mobilization as a defining attribute further critiques compliance‑oriented community approaches by emphasizing families’ capacity to actively access and utilize social and institutional supports,32 consistent with Roy’s Adaptation Model.33

From a policy perspective, empowerment should be embedded alongside growth monitoring to move beyond compliance‑driven approaches,4 while also addressing structural inequities such as gendered caregiving roles.34 Within this framing, nursing emerges not only as a site of clinical practice but also as a driver of systemic change in child health and equity.35

Future research should prioritize the cultural adaptation and validation of empowerment measures, ensure the inclusion of paternal and extended family roles, and explicitly examine empowerment components as potential mediators of child growth outcomes.

Conclusion

This concept analysis clarifies family empowerment as a multidimensional construct essential to stunting prevention during early childhood. Defined through five interrelated attributes—reflective decision‑making, self‑efficacy, participation, literacy integration, and resource mobilization—empowerment functions as both a process and an outcome in strengthening caregiving capacity and family resilience. By situating empowerment within nursing theories and diverse cultural contexts, this study advances theoretical clarity while underscoring its practical relevance for early childhood health promotion.

From a practice perspective, family‑centered approaches should embed empowerment indicators alongside growth monitoring. At the policy level, frameworks must address gendered caregiving roles and ensure systemic support for families. For research, priority lies in the cultural adaptation and validation of empowerment measures, including paternal and extended family roles, and in examining empowerment components as potential mediators of child growth outcomes.
