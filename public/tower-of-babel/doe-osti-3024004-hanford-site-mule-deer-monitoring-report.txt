 1.0 INTRODUCTION

The U.S. Department of Energy, Hanford Field Office (HFO) conducts ecological monitoring on
the Hanford Site to collect and track data needed to ensure compliance with environmental laws,
regulations, and policies governing Department of Energy activities. The vision for the HFO-
managed portion of the Hanford Site, hereby referred to as Central Hanford, focuses not only on
the cleanup of nuclear facilities and waste sites but on the protection and restoration of the Hanford
Site lands. As the HFO moves toward accomplishing this vision, understanding of the ecological
resources present and the need for conservation and/or protection of those resources will be critical
for making informed decisions for responsible site stewardship. Ecological monitoring data
provides baseline information about the plants, animals, and habitats under HFO stewardship at
Central Hanford required for decision-making under the National Environmental Policy Act of
1969 (NEPA) and Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act of
1980.
DOE/EIS-0222-F (1999), the Hanford Site Comprehensive Land Use Plan (CLUP), is the
Environmental Impact Statement that evaluates the potential environmental impacts associated
with implementing a comprehensive land-use plan for Central Hanford, which ensures that HFO,
its contractors, and other entities conduct activities on the Hanford Site in compliance with NEPA.
DOE/RL-96-32 (2017), Hanford Site Biological Resources Management Plan, is identified by
DOE/EIS-0222-F (1999) as the primary implementation control for managing and protecting
natural resources on Central Hanford. DOE/RL-96-32 ranks wildlife species and habitats (Levels
0 to 5) based on the level of concern for each resource, providing a graded approach to monitoring
biological resources.
Mule deer (Odocoileus hemionus), a habitat generalist found on Central Hanford, can thrive in a
variety of environments and are often associated with sagebrush-steppe habitat in Washington
State. While common in the Columbia Plateau, they are listed on the Washington Department of
Fish and Wildlife (WDFW) Priority Habitat and Species List (WDFW, 2008) and have statewide
management goals that include preservation and protection of mule deer and their habitat (WDFW,
2016). Additionally, they are a trustee resource of interest and importance to wildlife resource
agencies and local Tribes (WDFW, 2016). Due to these factors, mule deer are considered a BRMP
Level 3 species, which dictates a moderate degree of monitoring for the species on Central
Hanford, with listed goals to conserve and sustain the species (DOE/RL-96-32, 2017). With over
30 years of ongoing data collection of the same population of mule deer, there has been a unique
opportunity to review trends over time. Long-term monitoring of the population has supplied
baseline and ongoing data that provides distinctive insight into overall population status and
contributes to an understanding of site-wide biological diversity, as well as overall management
of natural resources on the Site.
Population characteristics of mule deer on Central Hanford have been monitored since fiscal year
(FY) 1995. Since then, roadside surveys have been conducted at various times in the fall and winter
to assess age and sex ratios, and the frequency of testicular atrophy in males.
Between FY 1995 and FY 2011, variable numbers of surveys were performed each year between
September and February. No surveys were conducted in FY 2012, and since FY 2013, surveys
have been conducted every two to five years, with four surveys performed in survey years during
the post-hunt period of December through February. During each survey, individual animals were
identified according to sex and age class (fawn or adult). For male deer, the presence of misshapen,

 1-5

velvet-covered antlers was used as an indicator of testicular atrophy. This report documents survey
results from FY 2024 and FY 2026 and compares the results to historical trends from past surveys.
In the early 1990s, testicular atrophy and sterility were observed in some male mule deer on Central
Hanford. Extensive investigation found no relationship between the presence of testicular atrophy
and contaminant levels, diet, disease, or natural conditions (e.g., aging or genetics) (PNNL-11518,
1997). Testicular atrophy in male mule deer is associated with abnormal antler growth manifested
as misshapen, velvet-covered antlers, which can be observed in field surveys. The observed
frequency of misshapen antlers in mule deer has ranged from a high of 17% in the southern region
in FY 1998 and 13% in the northern region in FY 2005 to a low of 0% in both regions seen multiple
years starting in FY 2004. These frequencies need to be interpreted with caution because the small
sample sizes may not fully reflect population conditions and misshapen antlers have not been seen
in any survey since FY 2016.
Trends from survey results can be compared to past data and used to broadly monitor changes in
mule deer population size and health and mule deer populations provide a rough indication of
overall habitat quality. It is also important to continue observing male’s antlers to monitor if
misshapen antlers begin to appear again.
Additionally, Rocky Mountain Elk (Cervus elaphus) data (locations, gender, and herd counts) were
collected while deer surveys were conducted. It was not until 1972 when elk were first documented
on the Hanford Site, and in recent years the population has grown drastically. These surveys
provide a valuable opportunity to document areas regularly occupied by elk and the status of
population. While roadside surveys may not represent a dependable long-term survey
methodology, these observations may be sufficient to maintain an ongoing record of the relative
abundance of elk on Central Hanford, particularly the size of the main herd.

 2.0 METHODS

Although hunting is not permitted on Central Hanford, wildlife can enter and leave freely. Due to
this movement, surveys are conducted after deer hunting season has ended, which runs from
September through early December. Additionally, during the winter months following the fall rut,
deer tend to herd into tighter groups, simplifying monitoring efforts.
In both FY 2024 and FY 2026, the northern and southern driving routes were surveyed on the same
day four times during the post-hunting period from December through February. FY 2024 surveys
were conducted on January 4, January 11, January 25, and February 1. FY 2026 surveys were
conducted on January 12, January 13, January 21, and January 29. Surveys in both regions
typically alternate between the morning and evening survey periods and survey direction.
Surveys were conducted from a vehicle along a specified route (Figure 2-1). The route is
approximately 37 mi (60 km) long; the northern end of the route is near 100-B/C, the southern end
is just north of the 300 Area. The survey route is divided into a northern region and a southern
region, with the break occurring at the north end of the Hanford Townsite. Surveys begin at dawn
or mid-afternoon (to end near dusk) and are driven alternatively from north-to-south and south-to-
north. Deer and elk are most active during early morning and late evening. Therefore, to attain
maximum sample sizes and help attain representative estimates for these population
characteristics, surveys were performed within 4 hours of twilight and dusk, when deer and elk
were most likely to be active.
Two staff conducted each survey - the driver and a second observer. Survey speed was 5 to
35 mi/hr (8 to 56 km/hr) with higher speeds on Central Hanford primary roads and slower speeds
on secondary and dirt roads. Surveyors stop periodically at good vantage points to scan expansive
areas using spotting scopes or binoculars. When deer were spotted, the driver stopped and/or pulled
off the road. A global positioning system position was collected, along with specific data about
the herd including the number of deer, their sex and age class, and the presence of misshapen
antlers on any of the bucks. Care was taken to avoid duplicate counts of the same deer. Similar
data were collected for elk observed along the driving transects.

 3.0 RESULTS

Over the four survey dates in FY 2024, 261 mule deer were counted (Table 3-1). There were a
higher number of observations in the southern region (61.7%) than in the northern region (38.3%).
This was particularly notable in bucks, with 78.4% of bucks found in the southern region compared
to 21.6% of bucks found in the norther region. Combined, the FY 2024 surveyed population was
made up of 47.1% does, 16.5% fawns, and 14.2% bucks. Another 22.2% were categorized as
antlerless, and were either fawns or does, but age could not be accurately determined.
The number of mule deer observed in the northern region averaged 25.0 (± 28.9) deer in FY 2024.
In the southern region, there was an average of 40.3 (± 11.3) deer surveyed. When combining daily
counts from both regions in FY 2024, the average number of mule deer was 65.3 (± 32.7), with a
range of 39 to 113 deer observed (Figure 3-3).

Over the four survey dates in FY 2026, 227 mule deer were counted (Table 3-2). There were
similar numbers of observations in the southern region (53.3%) and the northern region (46.7%).
However, the southern region contained over twice as many bucks (69.4%) as the southern region
(30.6%). Combined, the FY 2026 surveyed population was made up of 53.7% does, 11.5% fawns,
and 15.9% bucks. Another 18.9% were categorized as antlerless, and were either fawns or does,
but age could not be accurately determined.
The number of mule deer observed in the northern region averaged 26.5 (± 12.2) deer in FY 2026.
In the southern region, there was an average of 30.3 (± 13.7) deer surveyed. When combining daily
counts from both regions in FY 2026, the average number of mule deer was 56.8 (± 18.8), with a
range of 35 to 73 deer observed (Figure 3-3).

The means of the total daily mule deer counts were calculated for both FY 2024 and FY 2026.
These averages are similar to averages calculated since data collection started in 1995 and the 95%
confidence interval falls within the range of the confidence intervals for all other recorded survey
years, indicating that the averages from FY 2024 and FY 2026 are not statistically different than
previous years (Figure 3-1). The wide confidence interval associated with the averages is likely
due to a variety of factors including imperfect detection and immigration/emigration of deer in the
survey areas. Increasing the number of surveys could tighten the confidence interval and provide
a better assessment of changes in deer numbers over time.

The maximum number of deer observed in the northern region in FY 2024 was 68 deer on January
11, 2024, and the maximum number of deer observed in the southern region was 50 deer on
February 1, 2024 (Figure 3-2). In FY 2026, the maximum number of deer observed in the northern
region was 40 deer on January 21, 2026, and the maximum number of deer observed in the
southern region was 48 deer on January 13, 2026 (Figure 3-2). The maximum counts for both FY
2024 and FY 2026 were consistent with averages over the past 30 years, although peaks have been
documented in multiple years in the past.
A negative binomial generalized linear model was used to evaluate trends in maximum deer
counts. Counts were strongly overdispersed relative to a Poisson distribution (φ=6.69). After
accounting for overdispersion, no significant trend over time was detected in either region
(Northern: +1.2% per year, p=0.63, Southern: -1.0% per year, p=0.54). The counts are highly
variable among years but stable across the duration of the analysis for both regions.

antler growth (Figure 3-6). Upon further evaluation of photos, it was determined the animal was
most likely an antlered doe. The animal had very small velvet covered antlers but had the outward
appearances of a doe such as a longer snout, slender neck, and lack of tarsal gland staining. For
this reason, the animal was not included in the graph of abnormal bucks above. There are cases of
antlered mule deer does in the literature, although rare (Wislocki 1954).

Historically, incidental observations of elk along the established mule deer survey routes have
been recorded during the surveys and documented in the mule deer monitoring reports. In FY
2024, as part of a larger monitoring effort, elk surveys were conducted across the entire Hanford
Site rather than the more limited mule deer survey routes. In FY 2026, the maximum count was
550 elk observed in the northern route survey area on January 29, 2026. This included a herd of
almost 400 elk seen off Route 2 North approximately 2 km (1.3 mi) east of Gable Mountain, that
was not fully counted due to geographical obstructions preventing full visibility of the herd, and
a second large herd of more than 100 located 1.3 km (0.8 mi) north of the White Bluffs bank off
Route 2 North. There were seven additional observations of elk during that survey with sizes
ranging from 2 – 26 elk. The size of elk herds observed on the Hanford Site during deer surveys
has grown drastically in recent years (Figure 3-7). In FY 2012 and FY 2013 the elk herd
occupying central Hanford was limited to less than 40 individuals (HNF-54666; HNF-55551).
Since that time, the main herd has grown to 77 individuals in FY 2016 (HNF-60304) and 118
individuals observed in FY 2019 (HNF-65027). FY 2026 observations show that the main herd
has split in two, although they typically reside nearby and it is unknown how they interact and if
they move between groups or come together as one larger herd. FY 2026 results show the elk
population on Central Hanford has grown from 77 individuals to 550 individuals in 10 years.

 4.0 SUMMARY

Monitoring conducted during FY 2024 and FY 2026 found the average daily counts and maximum
counts of mule deer were similar to previous years. The fawn-to-doe average has remained
consistent over the last 30 years, with the exception of a notable sharp increase that was observed
in FY 2019 due to a decrease in does observed in FY 2019 compared to typical years. There were
zero observations of misshapen antlers in both FY 2024 and FY 2026, other than the suspected
antlered doe, and this phenomenon has not been seen in Central Hanford mule deer bucks since
FY 2016.
The current management goal for mule deer on Central Hanford is conservation. With a growing
elk population competing for the same resources, regular monitoring should continue and mule
deer data should be analyzed to track the population trends as well as examine if corresponding
elk population trends are impacting mule deer. While the elk population has drastically increased
on Central Hanford, for now, the deer population has stayed relatively steady. Information from
continued deer monitoring surveys may aid future game-management decisions.
Due to the increased presence of elk in all areas of the Hanford Site, including Central Hanford,
and rapid growth of the size of the herd, more comprehensive elk-specific monitoring is necessary.
Incidental observations of elk during the limited routes of routine mule deer surveys no longer
accurately measure the population and will be discontinued from future deer surveys in favor of
other methods of counting elk. More recent comprehensive monitoring information on the Hanford
Site elk herd is located in the HFO’s ecological monitoring report, Evaluating Movement Patterns
of the Rattlesnake Hills Elk Herd on Hanford for Calendar Years 2019-2024 (HNF-71433) and
can also be found in each Hanford Annual Site Environmental Report from 2020 to present
(DOE/RL-2021-15, 2021; DOE/RL-2022-08, 2022; DOE/RL-2023-20, 2023; DOE/RL-2024-10,
2024; DOE/RL-2025-12, 2025).
