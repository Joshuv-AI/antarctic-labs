Association between ScholarRx Qmax question bank engagement and NBME CBSE and USMLE Step 1 performance
Authors: Emma C Sherman, Jack I Stomberger, Kirsten A Porter-Stransky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn recent years, the pass rate of the USMLE Step 1 exam has declined. Failing or delaying Step 1 can cost students time, money, and morale. Undergraduate medical curricula are increasingly supplemented with third-party resources to support successful completion of the curriculum and board exams. Empirical research on the utility of specific third-party resources for student success is needed to inform evidence-based resources and strategies to support students’ success on standardized exams. To contribute to filling this gap, the present study examined the relationship between usage of specific components of the third-party resource ScholarRx and student performance on the Comprehensive Basic Science Exam (CBSE) and passing the USMLE Step 1 exam before clerkships began.MethodsTwo cohorts of students from a public medical school in the United States were included in the study: one from a Traditional discipline-based curriculum (n = 97) and one from an Integrated Organ System-based (IOS) curriculum (n = 103). ScholarRx data including usage of bricks, practice Qmax questions, flash facts, and express videos; biomedical science course averages; CBSE scores; and Step 1 outcomes were matched, de-identified, and analyzed (Traditional curriculum n = 97; Integrated Organ System curriculum n = 103). Multiple linear regression was performed to assess associations between engagement with specific ScholarRx tools and CBSE performance. Multiple logistic regression was used to investigate statistical predictors of taking and passing Step 1 on time.ResultsMultiple linear regression revealed that student use of ScholarRx practice questions (Qmax; Unstandardized B = 0.004–0.005) and biomedical science grades (Unstandardized B = 1.414–1.610) were significantly, positively associated with CBSE scores (R2 = 0.64–0.79, p < 0.05) but bricks, flash facts, and express videos were not. Similarly, logistic regression found that Qmax usage (Unstandardized B = 0.002–0.003) and biomedical science grades (Unstandardized B = 0.342–0.401) were statistically significant predictors of taking and passing Step 1 on time in both curricula (Traditional: x2(2) = 52.800, p < 0.001; Nagelkerke R2 = 0.575; IOS: x2(2) = 36.706, p < 0.001; Nagelkerke R2 = 0.532).ConclusionThis study found that students’ voluntary use of ScholarRx practice questions was associated with higher performance on the CBSE and taking and passing the Step 1 exam on schedule in both a Traditional curriculum and an IOS curriculum.Keywords: ScholarRx, Step 1, CBSE, Academic support, Practice questions, Medical education, External resources, Third-party resources, Assessment

Background

The typical medical school curriculum in the United States spans four years. Generally, the first 1.5-2 pre-clerkship years focus on the biomedical sciences, providing students with foundational knowledge to build upon during third-year clerkships. A “Traditional” pre-clerkship curriculum begins with students learning the normal functioning of each body system (year 1), followed by the abnormal pathophysiology of each body system (year 2). An “Integrated” curriculum covers each organ system once, teaching the normal and abnormal functioning of each organ system together. According to the 2025 Liaison Committee on Medical Education Annual Medical School Questionnaire-Part II, 24% of MD-granting medical schools in the United States have a Traditional curriculum and 76% teach with an Integrated Organ System (IOS) curriculum [1]. At many medical schools, the third and fourth years focus on the application of medical science knowledge in real-world clinical practices during clerkships and post-clerkships.

Passing the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) Step 1 Board exam is a required component of allopathic medical schools and at many schools is positioned between pre-clerkship and clerkship medical training. This exam evaluates a student’s ability to apply basic science concepts to the practice of medicine [2]. In 2022, the Step 1 exam transitioned from numeric scoring to pass/fail [3] in part to address the concern that student well-being and engagement with the formal curriculum suffered due to the importance of Step 1 scores on residency application success. However, the pass rates of Step 1 decreased from 96% in 2021 to 93% in 2022 and 92% in 2023 [3]. The Step 1 pass rate has continued to remain lower than pre-pass/fail times with 88% in 2024 and 91% in 2025 [4]. As a result, medical educators continue to discuss the most effective ways to support students and gauge learners’ readiness for the Step 1 exam.

The literature has identified risk factors that correlate with poor performance on the Step 1 exam, including lower total practice questions completed and poor performance on pre-clerkship in-house and practice exams [3]. The Comprehensive Basic Science Exam (CBSE) is a secure practice exam written by the National Board of Medical Examiners (NBME) that can be used as a metric of student readiness for the Step 1 examination [5]. The question format consists of clinical vignettes, similar in style and content to questions often seen on the Step 1 board exam. The CBSE exams provide students with a few metrics including a total equated percent correct (EPC) score on a 0-100 scale, a “likely score range” which indicates how much one’s score could change without forgetting or learning any information, and the estimated probability of passing Step 1 within 1 week of taking the CBSE if the exam is taken in a similar testing environment. The literature has supported that higher CBSE scores are associated with greater probability of passing Step 1 [2]. For the purposes of this study, we will be using the EPC score.

Of note there is wide discourse within the field of medical education on how to best support student learning. Prior studies recommend that students implement spaced-repetition, practice exams, and question banks from third-party resources to promote success [3, 6]. The value of completing board-style practice questions has been well supported in the literature. These effects are thought to be mediated in part by retrieval practice [7], which is when the “the act of recalling information from memory through questions or testing causes the memory of that information to be strengthened” [8]. A growing sector within medical education is the utilization of third-party resources. Many studies have described the steady increase of medical student third-party resource usage [9], which is often influenced by a desire to save time. Medical learners are more likely to use third-party resources if they are perceived as more efficient than traditional lectures and are less likely to use resources if they are unfamiliar to them and require additional time to explore how to use the resource [10–12]. Additionally, medical students have reported increased satisfaction with digitally based resources due to schedule flexibility. The growth in utilization of digitally based learning, including third-party resources, in medical education was catalyzed by the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, when remote learning became necessary [13, 14]. Opinions of third-party resources vary, from support of independent student learning, the foundation of medical professional life-long learning, to disagreement due to the fear that medical school curriculum will become obsolete [6]. Despite this discourse, the evidence remains clear: student use of third-party resources remains the new status quo in medical education. Furthermore, the literature supports medical school sponsored subscriptions and recommendations of third-party resources to address financial barriers, equity, and the cognitive burden of selecting resources [3, 6, 15].

As schools have been navigating how best to support their students to successfully matriculate into clerkships, selecting the optimal third-party resource is of importance. Factors to assess the quality of the resource include evidence of supporting student success, user-friendliness, ability to parallel the resource to medical school curriculum, financial cost, and available modalities for various learning preferences.

ScholarRx is a third-party online resource that utilizes multiple modalities to facilitate interactive student learning of biomedical sciences. These modalities include:

ScholarRx also links to the First Aid textbook, a long-standing resource utilized by medical students aimed at identifying “high yield” content reported to appear on board exams [16].

Although ScholarRx was founded 20 years ago and has been implemented into many medical schools worldwide [16–18], there is a paucity of research examining the association of students’ use of specific ScholarRx tools and academic performance. To our knowledge, no published studies have examined the association between student usage of specific ScholarRx modalities and outcomes on board exams. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between students’ use of specific ScholarRx resources, CBSE EPC, and passing USMLE Step 1. Due to the impact of retrieval practice, we hypothesized that greater usage of Qmax would be associated with higher CBSE EPC scores and passing USMLE Step 1 on schedule.

Methods

Ethics approval

The project (Proposal: Pro00131225) was reviewed by the University of South Carolina Institutional Review Board (IRB) which determined the study to be exempt under 45 CFR 46.104(d)(1). Because this study used retrospective deidentified data collected as part of normal educational practices, the need for informed consent was deemed unnecessary by the IRB. The study was conducted in accordance with the IRB’s standards.

Study design, curricular context, and participants

The study design was a retrospective quantitative study, which was chosen to utilize objective measures of student performance in association with their voluntary use of ScholarRx modalities. The study was conducted at the University of South Carolina School of Medicine Greenville [19]. Data from all students who completed the pre-clerkship biomedical sciences curriculum by the end of academic years 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 were included in the study. Students were granted free access to ScholarRx (paid by the medical school). The ScholarRx variables of interest included the total number of practice questions (Qmax), flash facts, bricks, and express videos completed per student. Usage metrics were obtained from ScholarRx for the duration of each biomedical science block. Usage during each of these periods was summed to calculate total usage scores for each participant within this study. For students in the IOS curriculum, this included years 1 and 2. For students in the Traditional curriculum, this included year 2 (because the institution had not yet purchased ScholarRx during their first year). ScholarRx usage data were matched with internal student data including course grade averages, CBSE EPC scores, and whether students took and passed the USMLE Step 1 exam before the scheduled start of clerkships. Data were then de-identified to ensure student academic privacy was upheld.

The medical school was undergoing curricular revision. Due to this timing, the variables of interest were examined within two distinct curricula: a Traditional curriculum and an Integrated Organ System (IOS) curriculum. The Traditional curriculum was designed to have the first year of learning focusing on the ‘normal’ anatomy and physiology, and the second year of learning devoted to the ‘abnormal’ pathophysiology. The IOS curriculum was a systems-based, block curriculum with each block including the anatomy, physiology, pathology, and pharmacology associated with the organ system. ScholarRx was implemented at the start of the Traditional cohort’s second year of medical school and the IOS cohort’s first year of medical school. While comparing the effectiveness of the two curricula is beyond the scope of the current study, the timing of this new curriculum provided an ideal opportunity to examine the relationship between usage of ScholarRx resources and standardized exam outcomes in both a Traditional and IOS curriculum at the same institution. In both curricula, students took the CBSE at least twice in preparation for Step 1, and students were expected to pass Step 1 during spring semester of the second year of medical school before beginning clerkships. Each student’s highest CBSE EPC was analyzed. For the overwhelming majority of students, their highest CBSE EPC was their final CBSE EPC. The final CBSE was after the last biomedical science course for the Traditional cohort and near the end of the final biomedical sciences course for the IOS cohort. To sit for Step 1, students should have successfully passed all pre-clerkship biomedical sciences courses and have reached a minimum EPC of 62% on the CBSE.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed with SPSS version 31.0.0.0 and graphed using GraphPad Prism version 10.6.1. The data sets were examined and verified to meet the assumptions of parametric statistics. Due to differences in curricular instruction and length of access to ScholarRx, data from students in the Traditional and IOS curricular groups were analyzed separately. An alpha level of 0.05 was used for determining statistical significance.

Multiple linear regression was performed to assess associations between ScholarRx engagement and academic outcomes, allowing us to explore whether more extensive use of the platform was statistically related to better academic performance on standardized exams. The dependent variable of the linear regression was the highest CBSE EPC during the second year of medical school. Independent variables included average biomedical course grade, total Qmax questions, flash facts, express videos, and bricks completed.

Although usage of ScholarRx resources were the primary variables of interest in this study, we also included students’ average pre-clerkship biomedical science grade in the regressions, because course grades have been shown to associate with standardized test scores and could account for some of the variance in the models [20]. Pearsons’s correlation and best-fit line with 95% confidence intervals were calculated to depict the relationship between average biomedical science grade and Qmax.

An overarching goal of this research project was to identify potential resources and variables associated with students successfully taking and passing the Step 1 examination on schedule. Therefore, we performed binary logistic regressions with the dependent variable of taking and passing Step 1 on time. Taking and passing Step 1 on time was defined by a student taking and earning a “pass” on the Step 1 exam before the scheduled start of third-year clerkships. The independent variables were average biomedical science grade and number of Qmax questions completed, because these were the two uncorrelated variables identified in the multiple linear regression that were significantly associated with CBSE EPC, a measure of Step 1 preparedness.

Additionally, to provide supporting descriptive analysis of the association between Qmax usage and Step 1 performance, one-tailed unpaired t-tests were conducted to compare the number of Qmax questions completed between students who took and passed Step 1 on time and those who did not in both cohorts. One-tailed t-tests were chosen for this a priori comparison because we were specifically interested in testing the directional hypothesis that students who passed Step 1 on time had completed more Qmax practice questions than students who did not pass Step 1 on time. Cohen’s d was conducted and reported as an indicator of effect size.

Discussion

The present study found that ScholarRx Qmax question bank usage was a statistically significant predictor of CBSE performance (Tables 2 and 3) as well as taking and passing USMLE Step 1 on schedule (Table 4) within both an IOS and Traditional curriculum. The unstandardized B coefficients revealed that each Qmax question completed was associated with a 0.004-0.005-point increase in CBSE EPC (Tables 2 and 3) and 0.002–0.003 increase in log odds of taking and passing Step 1 on time (Table 4). To scale for perspective, based upon the linear regression results, if a student completed 659 Qmax practice questions, which was the average completed in the IOS group (Table 1), that would be associated with a 3.3-point increase on the CBSE EPC (based off the 0.005 B coefficient, Table 3). Similarly, the results of the logistic regression indicate that for a 659-unit increase in Qmax questions, the log odds of taking and passing Step 1 on time increased by 1.32–1.98. In other words, the odds ratio showed that a 659-question increase in Qmax was associated with a 274% (Traditional curriculum)-622% (IOS curriculum) increase in the odds of taking and passing Step 1 on time (Table 4). Furthermore, students who took and passed Step 1 on time completed more Qmax practice questions than students who did not (Fig. 2). Based upon the Cohen’s d values, these results were of a moderate effect size. Considering the number of practice questions that many medical students complete (often thousands) and the high-stakes nature of these exams, we believe that these effects are not just statistically significant but also educationally meaningful. Previous studies have shown that increased utilization of practice questions correlates with higher performance on CBSE and Step 1 [3, 6], and our study builds upon these findings through evaluating the impact of a specific question bank, ScholarRx Qmax.

The present study also found that the biomedical science grade average was the largest statistically significant predictor of CBSE performance as well as taking and passing Step 1 on time. This result confirms previous research at other institutions showing that student performance within the medical school’s curriculum is associated with Step 1 success [3, 6, 21]. The finding that Qmax and biomedical science grade average were not correlated suggests that the association between Qmax completion and CBSE score are not attributed to improvements within course grades. Rather, biomedical science grades and Qmax are two separate factors that statistically contribute to CBSE score and taking and passing Step 1 on schedule.

The literature supports completing board-style practice questions due to the benefits of retrieval practice [7, 8] and helping learners identify gaps in their knowledge to direct future studying. Our study’s findings regarding academic performance and ScholarRx Qmax question bank usage in relation to Step 1 outcomes are consistent with previous research investigating other question banks. One such study within an IOS curriculum found that number of Kaplan USMLE Step 1 Qbank practice questions attempted was positively associate with Step 1 scores [22]. A different study found that within a Traditional curriculum, self-reported completion of the UWorld USMLE Step 1 Qbank and academic performance were both positively associated with Step 1 scores [23]. Therefore, while the present results demonstrate a positive association between use of ScholarRx Qmax and CBSE EPC and Step 1 outcomes, such associations may occur with other quality third-party question banks due to the value of retrieval practice.

In both curricula, students’ use of ScholarRx bricks, flash facts, and express videos did not have statistically significant relationships with CBSE EPC. Relatively low usage of bricks, flash facts, and express videos may have contributed to the non-significant findings. Future research with mandated or higher voluntary usage of these resources may yield different results. The lack of statistical significance for bricks, flash facts, and express videos in the regression analyses is not evidence that these tools are ineffective [24]. Students had autonomy in which ScholarRx tools, if any, they chose to use, and usage rates were relatively low for these resources. Usage could vary within future cohorts or at other medical schools with different study cultures or recommendations from near-peers or faculty members, resulting in different outcomes. For example, a recent study found that brick usage positively corresponded to scores on NBME customized assessments; however, compared to the present study, bricks were more formally and consistently integrated into their curriculum [18]. While not reaching statistical significance (p = 0.052), the IOS ScholarRx express videos’ confidence interval spanned only negative values up to zero (-0.069-0.000; Table 2). This result warrants further investigation in future studies with larger samples or higher usage rates. Video usage was generally low in both groups; thus, this result should be interpreted with caution [25]. However, if future studies with greater usage of express videos detect a negative relationship to CBSE EPC, that would be consistent with prior research suggesting that passive video watching is negatively correlated to Step 1 exam performance [26].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the relationship between students’ voluntary usage of ScholarRx modalities and their performance on the CBSE and USMLE Step 1. Strengths of the study include analysis of four specific ScholarRx resources (Qmax, bricks, flash facts, and express videos), two standardized exams (CBSE and USMLE Step 1), two different types of pre-clerkship curricula (IOS and Traditional), and multiple quantitative analyses (linear regressions, logistic regressions, and t-tests) with converging results.

This study has notable limitations. Firstly, due to when ScholarRx was purchased for the medical school, the two cohorts of students had access to ScholarRx for different lengths of time. Students in the Traditional cohort had one year of access to ScholarRx (their second year of medical school), and students in the IOS curriculum had two years of access (their first and second years of medical school). This difference in length of access prevented combining both cohorts into one regression analysis and would explain why average usage of each ScholarRx modality was higher in the IOS cohort (Table 1). Despite these differences in length of access, usage of Qmax remained a statistical predictor of CBSE performance and taking and passing Step 1 on time in each cohort, strengthening the interpretation that Qmax practice questions could support learners in both types of curricula.

Secondly, the results do not necessarily indicate causation. Students were neither randomly assigned to different groups nor mandated to use each of the resource modalities. For ethical reasons, all students were provided access to ScholarRx. Despite the correlational nature of these results, the significant Qmax results in multiple analyses (the linear regressions, logistic regressions, and t-tests) in both curricula support the interpretation that Qmax practice questions may be used as a study tool to support students’ success on the CBSE and Step 1.

Thirdly, the Qmax variable was total completed practice questions. We do not have records of whether students answered each question correctly or not. However, performance on practice questions while studying throughout the curriculum is likely less important than completing them. Our measure is consistent with previous research that focuses on practice question completion and the roles of retrieval practice and using practice questions to identify gaps in knowledge to direct studying [8, 22, 23, 26].

Fourthly, this study was conducted at one public medical school in the United States. Results could potentially vary at other schools depending on student demographics, academic preparedness, Step 1 timing, and culture of other medical schools. Despite these possibilities, the fact that Qmax usage was significantly related to CBSE scores and taking and passing Step 1 on time within two different cohorts with different curricula and different lengths of time with access to ScholarRx is promising that these results may transfer to other schools and curricula.

Fifthly, although delaying Step 1 is usually attributed to academic reasons [21], personal reasons (health, family, etc.) could contribute to delaying this exam. To protect students’ privacy, the research team did not have knowledge of personal circumstance surrounding exam delays; thus, the study could not account for potential non-academic factors influencing Step 1 timing. Additionally, it is possible that personal characteristics and other variables not accounted for in the regression models could impact a student’s voluntary choice to use or not use specific ScholarRx resources. Despite these limitations, the findings that biomedical science course grade is associated with CBSE EPC (Tables 2 and 3) and Step 1 success (Table 4) is consistent with the interpretation that academic preparedness is the major factor.

The present study was not designed to directly compare the effectiveness of specific ScholarRx resources to other third-party study tools. Areas for future research include comparing other external study resources and their relationships to CBSE performance and Step 1 success. Additionally, USMLE Step 1 timing varies across medical schools. While Step 1 functions as the final hurdle between pre-clerkship biomedical science classes and clinical clerkships at many medical schools, some schools have positioned Step 1 either during or after clerkships such that it is closer to Step 2 [26–28]. Future research should investigate whether the relationship between use of specific resources and Step 1 outcomes are similar in curricula where students take Step 1 after clerkships. Given differences in educational experiences, study strategies, and student populations at schools that position Step 1 after further clinical exposure, it is unknown if the findings of the present study would translate to these contexts.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that voluntary ScholarRx Qmax question bank usage and biomedical science course performance are statistically significant predictors of CBSE EPC and passing the Step 1 exam on time within both a Traditional and IOS curriculum. Importantly, Qmax and biomedical course average were not statistically associated with each other, further strengthening these findings. Other ScholarRx modalities, such as bricks, flash facts, and express videos did not show a statistically significant association with CBSE performance. Despite limitations related to cohort differences and potential confounders, the consistency of results across two curricular models suggests that ScholarRx can be integrated into diverse medical education models to support Step 1 readiness. Medical schools implementing ScholarRx could consider recommending Qmax question bank usage particularly for students who may be at risk for delayed Step 1 progression. Future research should explore whether these findings translate to other curricular models, including taking Step 1 after clerkships, and compare the impact of using ScholarRx versus other third-party resources on CBSE and Step 1 performance. The present study highlights the importance of evidence-based resource recommendations in guiding medical students toward successful Step 1 performance.
