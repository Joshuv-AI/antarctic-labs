Model Capability Dominates: Inference-Time Optimization Lessons from AIMO 3
Natapong Nitarach

Abstract
Majority voting over multiple LLM attempts improves mathematical reasoning, but correlated errors limit the effective sample size. A natural fix: assign structurally different reasoning strategies to different voters to decorrelate errors. We test this — Diverse Prompt Mixer — in the AIMO 3 competition ( 6 ) : 3 models, 23+ experiments, and 50 IMO-level problems on a single H100 80 GB with a 5-hour limit. Every intervention fails. High-temperature sampling already decorrelates errors sufficiently; weaker prompt strategies reduce per-attempt accuracy more than they reduce correlation. Across a 17-point model capability gap and every inference-time optimization we tried, model capability dominates by an order of magnitude.



1
Introduction

Majority voting across
N
inference attempts — self-consistency
(
14
)
— is the standard approach for competition mathematics. It rests on the Condorcet Jury Theorem
(
4
)
: with per-attempt accuracy
p>0.5
and independent errors, the majority converges to the correct answer as
N
grows.

In practice, errors are correlated. The same model with the same prompt makes the same systematic mistakes regardless of random seed. The effective sample size becomes

N_{\text{eff}}=\frac{N}{1+(N-1)\rho}

(1)

where
\rho
is the mean pairwise error correlation. At
\rho=0.3
, eight attempts reduce to 2.6 effective votes.

The natural fix: assign different reasoning strategies to different voters. If “small cases first” and “work backwards” lead the model through different paths, their errors should be less correlated. We call this
Diverse Prompt Mixer
.

The idea is intuitive. It is also wrong. This paper explains why (Figure
1
).

2
System Architecture

Our system runs entirely on a single H100 80 GB within Kaggle’s 5-hour wall-clock limit. No external APIs, no multi-GPU setups, no pre-computed solutions.

2.1
Model and Serving

We use
gpt-oss-120b
: 116.8B total parameters, 5.1B active via Mixture-of-Experts (MoE). The model is served locally via vLLM
(
7
)
with FP8 quantization for both weights and KV cache. Model weights consume
{\sim}75
GB of GPU memory; the remaining
{\sim}5
GB supports KV cache for up to
N{=}8
concurrent sequences at 65536-token context.

Server startup takes
{\sim}160
s (including weight loading to page cache). We pre-load weights via sequential file reads before launching vLLM to avoid cold-start latency during inference.

2.2
Inference Pipeline

Each problem passes through a 5-stage pipeline:

1.

Budget allocation
: Divide remaining wall-clock time equally among remaining problems. No bonus, no inflation — this guarantees total solving time
\leq
notebook limit.

2.

Parallel attempts
: Launch
N{=}8
concurrent inference threads, each with a different random seed but identical system prompt and temperature
T{=}1.0
.

3.

Tool-integrated reasoning
: Each attempt can generate Python code via
<tool_call>
tags. Code executes in a persistent Jupyter kernel sandbox with access to
sympy
,
numpy
,
mpmath
. Results feed back into the conversation for multi-turn reasoning.

4.

Early stopping
: If 4 of 8 attempts agree on a non-trivial answer (
>1
), remaining attempts are cancelled.

5.

Entropy-weighted voting
: Final answer selected by weighted majority vote, where weight
w=1+1/(\text{entropy}+0.1)
. Low-entropy (confident) attempts contribute more.

2.3
Time Management

The 5-hour limit is the binding constraint. Our budget system:

•

Total: 18000 s. Reserve 540 s for infrastructure variance, 360 s for startup.

•

Solving budget: 17100 s for 50 problems.

•

Per-problem budget:
\text{time\_left}/\text{problems\_remaining}
. Pure equal division — no bonus from saved time, no inflation. Mathematically guarantees completion.

•

Hard deadline: if
<
30 s remain, return 0 immediately. This ensures the inference server answers all 50 problems before Kaggle’s hard kill.

3

1.

2.

: Enumerate
n{=}1,2,3,\ldots
, discover patterns, conjecture, prove.

3.

4.2
Pairwise Correlation Is Already Near Zero

Equation
1
assumes constant pairwise correlation
\rho
across attempts. We measure
\hat{\rho}
directly using the method-of-moments estimator for a binary exchangeable sequence:

\hat{\rho}=\frac{v_{c}(v_{c}-1)/[N(N-1)]-\hat{p}^{2}}{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}

where
v_{c}
is the number of correct votes and
\hat{p}=v_{c}/N
. We compute this across all three models:
gpt-oss-120b
(
N{=}8
), Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (
N{=}16
), and Nemotron-Super-120B (
N{=}3
). Problems with
\hat{p}\in\{0,1\}
yield undefined
\hat{\rho}
and are excluded.

This gives 8 computable points (Figure
3
). All eight are negative. The five estimates from models with
N\geq 7
are
\hat{\rho}\in\{-0.167,-0.167,-0.100,-0.067,-0.067\}
, mean
-0.113
. The three Nemotron points all give
\hat{\rho}=-0.500
, which is the mathematical minimum for
N{=}3
,
v_{c}{=}1
(any single-correct-vote outcome at
N{=}3
forces this value); they confirm sign but not magnitude. Across all 8 points the mean is
-0.258
. When the model is wrong, wrong answers scatter across many distinct values rather than clustering—so two draws are less likely to share the correct answer than
\hat{p}^{2}
predicts. This leaves no correlation headroom for diversity strategies to exploit. Diverse prompts cannot decorrelate what is already at or below zero.

4.3
Weaker Strategies Reduce Accuracy

The Mixer modifies two parameters simultaneously: it
reduces

\rho
(intended) but also
reduces

\bar{p}
(unintended: weaker prompts lower per-attempt accuracy). For the intervention to succeed, decorrelation must exceed accuracy loss.

The critical test is E10: Equal mix (2+2+2+2) at
T{=}0.5
, where prompt diversity should help most (low stochastic diversity). It scores 36 —
worse
than
T{=}0.5
alone (38). Prompt diversity does not compensate even when temperature-based diversity is deliberately suppressed.

7.2
Inference-Time Scaling

Recent work on scaling test-time compute
(
12
;
3
)
suggests that repeated sampling with selection can substitute for model scaling. Our results qualify this: on IMO-level problems near the capability frontier (
\hat{p}\approx 0.69
), repeated sampling with majority voting works, but
modifying
the sampling (prompt diversity, temperature tuning, strategy mixing) does not improve over vanilla self-consistency. The returns to inference-time optimization flatten once the base system is reasonably configured.

8
Submission as Lottery

With
\hat{\mu}=39.7
and
\hat{\sigma}=1.7
over 13 baseline runs, each submission is a lottery ticket:

P(\text{score}\geq 44\mid\text{single})\approx 0.6\%,\qquad P(\max\geq 44\text{ in }K)=1-0.994^{K}

(2)

The Mixer reduces expected score (
\mu
drops to
{\sim}39.0
) without improving tail probability: the lower mean is partly offset by a slightly wider spread (
\sigma{\approx}2.0
), leaving
P(\text{single}\geq 44)
nearly unchanged at
{\approx}0.6\%
. There is no advantage to mixing. The optimal strategy is to submit the unmodified baseline repeatedly (Figure
7
).

9
Infrastructure Noise

Benchmark scores on shared GPU infrastructure exhibit significant variance from non-algorithmic sources
(
1
)
: resource contention, memory allocation timing, and kernel scheduling can shift scores by
{\sim}6
percentage points. Our 13-run baseline (
\sigma=1.7
, range 37–44) likely reflects a combination of genuine sampling variance and infrastructure noise. We mitigate this by reporting 13-run statistics rather than single-run scores, and by cross-validating findings across 3 different models.

10
When Might It Work

Prompt diversity is not universally useless. It may help when: (1) temperature is low (
T\leq 0.3
), leaving insufficient stochastic diversity; (2) problems are easier, so weaker strategies still achieve high
p
; (3) diverse prompts can be designed with comparable accuracy to the original; or (4) the model is
prompt-robust
— its accuracy
p
remains stable across diverse instruction formats. On IMO-level problems at
T{=}1.0
with a model near its capability frontier, conditions 1–3 do not hold. Condition 4 is the deeper barrier.

Most mathematical reasoning models are trained on datasets with very few instruction formats: NuminaMath
(
2
)
uses two (CoT, TIR), OpenMathInstruct-2
(
13
)
uses one (CoT), and OpenMathReasoning
(
8
)
uses three (CoT, TIR, GenSelect). A model trained exclusively on these formats has not learned to maintain accuracy under format perturbation. When a different strategy prompt — “Work Backwards” or “Small Cases First” — is substituted, accuracy drops not because the math is harder but because the instruction distribution has shifted.
9
demonstrate this directly: training with diverse instruction formats (Mixture of Formats, MOF) improves prompt robustness and raises accuracy on held-out benchmarks. Our results are consistent — the strategies that hurt least (Code-First:
-0.7
; Work Backwards:
-0.7
) are those most similar in structure to the training distribution; the strategies that hurt most (Classify & Select:
-3.7
; Small Cases:
-2.7
) require unfamiliar reasoning scaffolds. Diverse Prompt Mixer may become viable once models are trained on MOF-style data and prompt robustness improves — but that requires a training-time intervention, not an inference-time one.

11
Conclusion

We proposed Diverse Prompt Mixer to decorrelate errors in majority voting for mathematical reasoning. It does not work. High-temperature sampling already provides sufficient diversity; structured prompt diversity is redundant at best, harmful at worst. Across 3 models, 23+ experiments, and 50 IMO-level problems, model capability dominates all inference-time optimizations by an order of magnitude. For hardware-constrained competitions: use the largest model that fits, keep temperature high, and spend your submission budget on lottery tickets, not on prompt engineering.
