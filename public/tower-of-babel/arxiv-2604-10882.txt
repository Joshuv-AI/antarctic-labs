DIB-OD: Preserving the Invariant Core for Robust Heterogeneous Graph Adaptation via Decoupled Information Bottleneck and Online Distillation *Corresponding Author: Qiudong Yu
Yang Yan, Qiuyan Wang, Tianjin Huang, Qiudong Yu *, Kexin Zhang

Abstract
Graph Neural Network pretraining is pivotal for leveraging unlabeled graph data. However, generalizing across heterogeneous domains remains a major challenge due to severe distribution shifts. Existing methods primarily focus on intra-domain patterns, failing to disentangle task-relevant invariant knowledge from domain-specific redundant noise, leading to negative transfer and catastrophic forgetting. To this end, we propose DIB-OD , a novel framework designed to preserve the invariant core for robust heterogeneous graph adaptation through a D ecoupled I nformation B ottleneck and O nline D istillation framework. Our core innovation is the explicit decomposition of representations into orthogonal invariant and redundant subspaces. By utilizing an Information Bottleneck teacher-student distillation mechanism and the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion, we isolate a stable invariant core that transcends domain boundaries. Furthermore, a self-adaptive semantic regularizer is introduced to protect this core from corruption during target-domain adaptation by dynamically gating label influence based on predictive confidence. Extensive experiments across chemical, biological, and social network domains demonstrate that DIB-OD significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods, particularly in challenging inter-type domain transfers, showcasing superior generalization and anti-forgetting performance.

Introduction

Graph Neural Networks have revolutionized representation learning across diverse fields, from molecular property prediction to social network analysis.
To mitigate the scarcity of labeled data, the ”pre-train then fine-tune” paradigm has emerged, aiming to capture transferable structural patterns from vast unlabeled graph repositories.
This pursuit of cross-domain generalization is also a fundamental challenge in many AI fields.
Despite its success, a critical generalization wall persists: most existing GNN pre-training and domain adaptation frameworks are designed for intra-domain shifts—addressing distribution variations within the same category of data (e.g., regional shifts in citation networks or sensor noise in protein graphs).

Recently, an emerging trend has seen the development of universal graph models that aim to aggregate knowledge across disparate data sources
24
;
9
;
6
.
While these pioneer efforts have demonstrated the potential of large-scale graph learning, a critical representation bottleneck remains largely unaddressed across the field.
Existing pre-training strategies, including those emerging universal frameworks, often treat latent features as monolithic entities.
This results in a fundamental information entanglement: domain-specific structural styles are deeply entangled with the task-relevant topological laws.
In heterogeneous transfer scenarios, the noise’s representation from a source domain often acts as a spurious correlation that misleads the model when it encounters a target domain with a completely different connectivity logic, leading to negative transfer.

Furthermore, a significant hurdle for pre-trained GNNs is their vulnerability during target-domain adaptation.
When a model that has captured universal structural principles is fine-tuned on a new domain, it risks the deficiency of catastrophic forgetting.
Without an explicit mechanism to isolate and protect the transferable core, the target-domain-specific signals often contaminate and overwrite the hard-won generalizable knowledge.
This tension between transferability and specificity undermines the very purpose of cross-domain pre-training.

To address these limitations, we propose DIB-OD, a universal pre-training framework based on Decoupled Information Bottleneck and Online Distillation.
Our approach shifts the focus from mere ”data aggregation” to ”knowledge purification.”
Built upon a multi-view distillation architecture, DIB-OD explicitly decomposes the learned representation into two orthogonal components: (1) a domain-invariant core representation that captures universal, transferable graph principles, and (2) a domain-specific redundant representation that absorbs category-specific noise.
Guided by the Information Bottleneck principle and enforced by the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion
8
(HSIC for short), our framework ensures the two subspaces are statistically independent.

To further combat knowledge corruption during adaptation, we introduce a self-adaptive semantic regularizer.
Unlike fixed constraints, this regularizer dynamically modulates the influence of target labels based on predictive confidence, effectively shielding the invariant core from being overwritten by noisy target signals.

•

A Purification Paradigm for Heterogeneous Transfer:
We identify the ”information entanglement” issue in universal graph learning and propose a novel decoupling framework that bridges fundamentally different graph categories.

•

Decoupled Information Bottleneck Distillation:
We introduce an optimization scheme that utilizes IB-guided distillation and HSIC-based orthogonality to isolate invariant graphical structures, ensuring its purity and transferability.

•

Self-adaptive Knowledge Preservation:
We design a confidence-based adaptive regularizer and a “frozen teacher” strategy to protect universal knowledge during adaptation, effectively mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

•

Extensive Benchmarking:
Extensive experiments on several cross-domain benchmarks demonstrate that DIB-OD significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods, especially in challenging inter-type domain transfers (e.g., Chem
\rightarrow
Bio), showcasing superior generalization.

Related Works

2.1
From Intra-domain Adaptation to Heterogeneous Generalization

Graph Domain Adaptation mitigates distribution shifts, conventionally focusing on intra-domain scenarios where patterns vary but categories remain consistent
21
;
6
. However, these methods rely on structural overlap, proving ineffective for heterogeneous cross-domain pre-training where disjoint feature spaces create a “generalization wall.”
To break this barrier, recent research pivots toward Out-of-Distribution (OOD) generalization and invariant learning.
Works such as
11
;
3

attempt to discover stable substructures invariant to environmental changes.
Recent domain generalization works like GraphAny
24
and UniGraph
9
address this by capturing universal inductive biases or aligning disparate datasets into a unified space. Despite this, most frameworks treat representations as monolithic entities, prioritizing global alignment over internal disentanglement. This necessitates a shift from data aggregation to explicit knowledge purification, motivating our proposal to mathematically decouple the invariant core from redundant noise.

2.2
Information-Theoretic Disentangled Representation

Multiview Disentangled Representation Learning separates shared consistency from view-specific information using the Information Bottleneck principle
20
;
5
or adversarial constraints
17
.
Existing frameworks, however, are largely confined to in-domain datasets.
In heterogeneous settings, the failure to separate transferable principles from domain-specific noise (
e.g.
connectivity styles) leads to negative transfer.
DIB-OD addresses this by extending the Information Bottleneck principle to heterogeneous pre-training, explicitly enforcing orthogonality between invariant and redundant subspaces to ensure only “universal graph laws” are transferred.

2.3
Knowledge Decomposition via Online Distillation

While Knowledge Distillation typically involves student-teacher imitation
14
;
2
, existing graph KD methods
10
lack mechanisms to distinguish the generalizable core from redundant noise.
This “imitation-based” approach leaves models vulnerable to catastrophic forgetting, as fine-tuning on new domains can overwrite pre-trained structural wisdom.
Unlike these paradigms, our DIB-OD utilizes online distillation to deconstruct knowledge components, employing a Self-adaptive Semantic Regularizer to shield the invariant core from corruption by target-specific noise.

Methodology

3.1
Framework Overview

As Figure
1
illustrated, our DIB-OD framework consists of three main components: (1) a multi-view encoder that processes various augmented graph views, (2) a teacher model that learns a fused representation via the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle, and (3) student models that disentangle the representation into invariant core and redundant subspaces through online contrastive distillation.

3.2
Multi-view Graph Disentangled Auto-Encoder

Given a graph, we generate multiple augmented views
\mathbf{X}_{\Phi}=\{\mathbf{x}_{\phi_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\phi_{V}}\}
via stochastic augmentations (e.g., node dropping, edge perturbation).
A multi-view encoder
E_{\Phi}
with parameters
\Theta_{\Phi}
fuses these views into a holistic representation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}=\sum_{i=1}^{V}\mathbf{\theta}_{\phi_{i}}E_{\phi_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{\phi_{i}})=\mathbf{\Theta}_{\Phi}\mathbf{X}_{\Phi}

(1)

A corresponding decoder
\Psi_{\Phi}
reconstructs the original views from
\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}
, with reconstruction loss
\mathcal{L}_{R}^{T}
(Equation
2
), where
N
is the number of nodes.

\displaystyle L_{R}^{T}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{V}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{\phi_{j},i}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{j}(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi,i})\right\|^{2}

(2)

3.3
View-Invariant Teacher Learning via Information Bottleneck

The core of our MVDIB-OD framework lies in its ability to disentangle the fused representation
\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}
into a domain-invariant, task-relevant “core” representation (
\mathbf{z}_{vs}
) and a domain-specific, redundant representation (
\mathbf{z}_{vr}
).
This is accomplished through a novel synergy of the Information Bottleneck principle and an online knowledge distillation process.

First, we apply the IB principle to the teacher model to regulate the information flow from input views
\mathbf{X}_{\Phi}
to the fused representation
\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}
.
The objective is to learn a representation
\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}
that is maximally informative about the target labels
\mathbf{Y}
while being minimally compressive with respect to the input.
This trade-off is governed by the teacher’s IB loss,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IBT}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{IBT}}=-\mathcal{L}_{OPT}(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi},\mathbf{Y})+\beta_{T}\cdot I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{X}_{\Phi})

(3)

where
I(\cdot;\cdot)
denotes the mutual information, and
\beta_{T}
is a hyperparameter balancing prediction and compression.
This step ensures that the teacher model focuses on learning the most salient features for the task at hand.
We use the notation
\mathcal{L}_{OPT}(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi},\mathbf{Y})=\sup I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y})
to represent the optimal objective for mutual information computation.

Lemma 1
.

Multi-view Redundancy Equivalence.

Our
\mathcal{L}_{OPT}
computing objective is equivalent to its conditional form
\mathcal{L}_{OPT}(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi},\mathbf{Y})=\sup I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y}|\Phi)
if and only if two conditions hold:

(1) The label
\mathbf{Y}
is independent of the view source
\Phi
, i.e.,
I(\mathbf{Y};\Phi)=0
, and (2) The fused representation
\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}
is conditionally independent of the view source
\Phi
given the label
\mathbf{Y}
, i.e.,
I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\Phi|\mathbf{Y})=0
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{OPT}(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi},\mathbf{Y})=\sup I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y})

is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{OPT}(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi},\mathbf{Y})=\sup I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y}|\Phi)\text{, if }I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\Phi|\mathbf{Y})=0

Proof.

We want to prove
I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y})=I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y}|\Phi)
.
By definition,
I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y})=H(\mathbf{Y})-H(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{Z}_{\phi})
and
I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y}|\Phi)=H(\mathbf{Y}|\Phi)-H(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{Z}_{\phi},\Phi)
.
From condition (1),
I(\mathbf{Y};\Phi)=0\implies H(\mathbf{Y})=H(\mathbf{Y}|\Phi)
.
From condition (2),
I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\Phi|\mathbf{Y})=0
. By the chain rule of mutual information, this implies
H(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{Z}_{\phi})=H(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{Z}_{\phi},\Phi)
.
Substituting these two equivalences into the definitions, we find
H(\mathbf{Y})-H(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{Z}_{\phi})=H(\mathbf{Y}|\Phi)-H(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{Z}_{\phi},\Phi)
, thus
I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y})=I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}\mathbf{Y}|\Phi)
.
∎

The condition
I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\Phi|\mathbf{Y})=0
mathematically defines our goal: the fused representation
\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}
contains all predictive information about
\mathbf{Y}
in a way that is completely redundant and independent of the view source
\Phi
.

Lemma 2
.

Aggregated Decisive Information.

Given the ideal redundancy condition
I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\Phi|\mathbf{Y})=0
, the optimal objective
\mathcal{L}_{OPT}=\sup I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y}|\Phi)
decomposes into the weighted sum of all decisive information aggregated from each individual view.

Proof.

We begin by expressing the conditional mutual information
I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y}|\Phi)
as the expected value of the mutual information over all possible views
\phi\in\Phi
, i.e.,
I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y}|\Phi)=\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim P(\Phi)}[I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\mathbf{Y}|\Phi=\phi)]
.

By definition, mutual information can be expressed as the KL divergence between the joint distribution and the product of the marginal distributions. This allows us to expand the expectation as follows:

\displaystyle I(\bm{Z}_{\Phi};\bm{Y}|\Phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi\in\Phi}P(\phi)\cdot D_{KL}\left(P_{\bm{Z}_{\Phi},\bm{Y}|\Phi=\phi}\mid\mid P_{\bm{Z}_{\Phi}|\Phi=\phi}P_{\bm{Y}|\Phi=\phi}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi\in\Phi}P(\phi)\sum_{\bm{z}_{\phi},y}(P_{\bm{Z}_{\Phi},\bm{Y}|\Phi}(\bm{z}_{\phi},y|\phi)

(4)

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\log\frac{P_{\bm{Z}_{\Phi},\bm{Y}|\Phi}(\bm{z}_{\phi},y|\phi)}{P_{\bm{Z}_{\Phi}|\Phi}(\bm{z}_{\phi}|\phi)P_{\bm{Y}|\Phi}(y|\phi)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi_{i}\in\Phi}\bm{\theta}_{i}\cdot I(\bm{Z}_{\phi_{i}};\bm{Y}|\phi_{i})

(5)

The step from Equation
4
to
5
is the core of this lemma.
It asserts that under the ideal redundancy condition
I(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi};\Phi|\mathbf{Y})=0
, the total information captured by the fused representation
\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}
(conditioned on knowing the view source) can be represented as a linear aggregation of the “decisive” information
I(\bm{Z}_{\phi_{i}};\bm{Y}|\phi_{i})
captured by each individual view’s representation.
The weights
\bm{\theta}_{i}
correspond to the parameters of our multi-view encoder.

This proves that our objective, when optimized to be view-invariant, effectively aggregates all non-redundant, decisive information from the multi-view inputs.
∎

3.4
Disentangled Student Learning and Online Contrastive Distillation

The teacher’s representation
\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}
is distilled into two student models that learn disentangled representations:
the invariant core

\mathbf{z}_{vs}
and the
redundant part

\mathbf{z}_{vr}
.
The student’s IB objective is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{IBS}}=

\displaystyle-\kappa\cdot I(\mathbf{z}_{vs};\mathbf{Y})+\beta_{y}\cdot I(\mathbf{z}_{vr};\mathbf{Y})~+

(6)

\displaystyle\beta_{vs}\cdot I(\mathbf{z}_{vs};\mathbf{z}_{vr})+I(\mathbf{z}_{vs};\mathbf{Z}_{\phi})

We deploy contrastive losses
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CKD}}^{vs}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CKD}}^{vr}
align student representations with the teacher’s representations
\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}
. (Detailed derivations in Appendix A. ).

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{CKD}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{CKD}}^{vs}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{CKD}}^{vr}

(7)

Additionally, we enforce orthogonality between
\mathbf{z}_{vs}
and
\mathbf{z}_{vr}
via the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Orth}}=\operatorname{HSIC}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{vs}^{S},\mathbf{Z}_{vr}^{S}\right)=\frac{1}{(N-1)^{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{K}_{vs}^{S}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}_{vr}^{S}\mathbf{H}\right)

(8)

where
\mathbf{K}_{vs}^{S}
and
\mathbf{K}_{vr}^{S}
are kernel matrices computed from the respective representations, and
\mathbf{H}
is a centering matrix.
The total distillation loss is
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CKD}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{Orth}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Orth}}
.

3.5
Self-adaptive Semantic Regularizer

In our DIB-OD, the key to preventing catastrophic forgetting lies in the adaptive formulation of the student’s Information Bottleneck loss,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IBS}}
.
The Self-adaptive Semantic Regularizer
\kappa
(SSR for short defined in Equation (
6
)) , which dictates the importance of the invariant representation
\mathbf{z}_{vs}
, is dynamically determined.
The intuition is that knowledge corresponding to semantic classes that the pre-trained model can predict with high stability and confidence is likely part of the generalizable, domain-invariant core.
This is precisely the knowledge we must preserve. In contrast, knowledge for unstable or uncertain classes may be domain-specific or poorly learned, thus requiring more adaptation.

To quantify this invariant core, we devise a three-step process to compute a dynamic class-aware
\kappa
.
Let
\mathring{y}
be the prediction of the pre-trained teacher model and
y^{\ast}
be the ground-truth label.

1.
Quantifying Pre-trained Confidence
. We first establish a confidence baseline
t_{b}
for each class
b
in Equation (
9
).
This threshold represents the teacher model’s average predictive confidence for all nodes it identifies as class
b
, serving as a measure of its self-assessed certainty from pre-training.

\displaystyle t_{b}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{X}_{\mathring{y}=b}|}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}_{\mathring{y}=b}}\hat{p}(\mathring{y}=b;\mathbf{x})

(9)

2.
Identifying Stable Knowledge via Observation
.
We then construct an observation matrix
\mathbf{O}_{\mathring{y},y^{\ast}}
.
An element
\mathbf{O}_{\mathring{y},y^{\ast}}
counts the instances in which the model predicts class
\mathring{y}
for a node of a ground-truth class
y^{\ast}
, but only when its confidence in the true label surpasses the established baseline
t_{b}
.
The diagonal elements
O_{c,c}
thus represent the amount of high-confidence, correct knowledge the model possesses for class c.

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{a,b}=|\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}_{\mathring{y}=a}\mid y^{\ast}_{\mathbf{x}}=b\text{ and }\hat{p}(y^{\ast}=b;\mathbf{x})\geq t_{b}\}|

(10)

3.
Deriving a Class Invariance Score
.
From this, we compute a normalized estimation matrix
\mathbf{E}_{\mathring{y},y^{\ast}}
in Equation
11
.
Finally, we set
\kappa_{i}=\mathbf{E}_{\mathring{y}=y_{i},y^{\ast}=y_{i}}
with the diagonal element for each sample in the
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IBS}}
loss.

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{a,b}=\frac{\frac{\mathbf{O}_{a,b}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbf{O}_{a,k}}\cdotp|\mathbf{X}_{\mathring{y}=a}|}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\mathbf{O}_{j,b}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbf{O}_{j,k}}\cdotp|\mathbf{X}_{\mathring{y}=j}|}

(11)

Theorem 1
.

For dataset
\mathbf{\hat{X}}=(\mathbf{x},\mathring{y})^{N}
, where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\mathring{y}\in\lfloor 1,m\rfloor
, if
\hat{p}(\mathring{y},y^{\ast})
is ideal, the ideal threshold for
t_{k}=\mathrm{E}_{x\in\mathbf{x}_{\mathring{y}=k}}\hat{p}(\mathring{y};\mathbf{x})

Proof.

For prediction
\mathring{y}=c
, its theoretical probability threshold mainly from: (1) the model makes a correct prediction. (2) the model making mistaken predictions as
c
from other classes.
The above process can be mathematically summarized as:

\displaystyle t_{c}=\displaystyle{\sum_{k\in\lfloor 1,m\rfloor}}p(\mathring{y}=c|y^{\ast}=k)\cdot p(y^{\ast}=k|\mathring{y}=c)

(12)

In ideal condition, the Equation (
12
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle t_{c}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle{\sum_{k\in\lfloor 0,m\rfloor}}\hat{p}(\mathring{y}=c|y^{\ast}=k)\cdotp\mathrm{E}_{x\in\mathbf{x}_{\mathring{y}=c}}\hat{p}(y^{\ast}=k;\mathbf{x})

(13)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{x\in\mathbf{x}_{\mathring{y}=c}}\displaystyle{\sum_{k\in\lfloor 0,m\rfloor}\hat{p}(\mathring{y}=c|y^{\ast}=k)}\cdotp\hat{p}(y^{\ast}=k;\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{x\in\mathbf{x}_{\mathring{y}=c}}\displaystyle{\sum_{k\in\lfloor 0,m\rfloor}\hat{p}(\mathring{y}=c|y^{\ast}=k;\mathbf{x})}\cdotp\hat{p}(y^{\ast}=k;\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{x\in\mathbf{x}_{\mathring{y}=k}}\hat{p}(\mathring{y};\mathbf{x})

∎

Dataset.
To evaluate our model performance, we perform experiments on several well-known datasets

Baseline Models.

To situate our work within the existing literature, we compare against key representative methods.

Default settings.

We evaluate our framework on downstream graph classification tasks, adopting Accuracy as the primary evaluation metric.
During the pre-training phase, we employ a 10-fold cross-validation strategy to optimize both the teacher and student models.
For cross-domain fine-tuning, we split the labeled data using 10-fold cross-validation.
The detailed graph augmentation parameters are listed in Appendix C.

The GNN backbone consists of a three-layer Graph Convolutional Network, which is followed by an MLP-based logistic regression layer for the final classification.
Adhering to the online distillation paradigm, we simultaneously update the parameters of both the teacher and student models during pre-training.
Subsequently, during the fine-tuning phase, the teacher model’s parameters are frozen, and only the student model is trained.
For both pre-training and fine-tuning, all models are trained for

4.3

To rigorously evaluate the robustness and transferability of our pre-training framework, we further design a challenging set of experiments that involve knowledge transfer across heterogeneous domains with significant distribution shifts.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed DIB-OD, a novel framework designed to overcome the ”generalization wall” in heterogeneous graph transfer learning.
By integrating a decoupled information bottleneck with online distillation, our approach explicitly disentangles representations into an orthogonal invariant core and a redundant subspace.
This purification process, coupled with a self-adaptive semantic regularizer, effectively protects transferable structural principles from being overwritten by domain-specific noise, thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting.
Extensive experiments across chemical, biological, and social network domains demonstrate that DIB-OD significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods, particularly in challenging inter-type transfers.
Our work provides a robust paradigm for building universal graph models that are both generalizable and stable across disparate data distributions.
