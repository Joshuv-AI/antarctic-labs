Admission Uric Acid, Troponin, and C-Reactive Protein Levels and In-Hospital Mortality in Acute Stroke Patients
Authors: Ömer Yüceer, Abdussamed Vural
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveStroke remains a leading cause of in-hospital mortality worldwide. Routinely available laboratory biomarkers obtained at emergency department admission may facilitate early risk stratification. This study aimed to evaluate the associations between admission serum uric acid (SUA), troponin, and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels and in-hospital mortality in patients with acute stroke.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included 403 adults diagnosed with acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke between January 1, 2019, and January 1, 2022. Demographic data, comorbidities, and admission laboratory findings were extracted from electronic hospital records. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of in-hospital mortality. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to evaluate discriminatory performance.ResultsOf 403 patients (mean age 73.8 ± 12.4 years; 54.8% male), 16.6% (n=67) died during hospitalization. Non-survivors were significantly older and had higher admission SUA, troponin, and CRP levels (all p<0.001). In multivariable analysis, advanced age (OR: 1.07; 95% CI: 1.03–1.08; p=0.007), troponin >14 ng/L (OR: 2.23; 95% CI: 1.21–4.01; p=0.012), CRP >5 mg/L (OR: 2.93; 95% CI: 1.59–5.67; p=0.001), and SUA >7 mg/dL (OR: 1.93; 95% CI: 1.04–3.53; p=0.041) were independently associated with in-hospital mortality. CRP showed the highest discriminatory performance (AUC: 0.77), while troponin (AUC: 0.67) and SUA (AUC: 0.66) demonstrated limited discriminatory ability.ConclusionAdmission CRP, troponin, and SUA levels were independently associated with in-hospital mortality in acute stroke patients. However, given their moderate discriminatory performance and the absence of standardized stroke severity scores, these biomarkers should be interpreted as supplementary risk indicators rather than standalone prognostic tools. Prospective multicenter studies incorporating stroke severity scales are warranted.Keywords: stroke, C-reactive protein, troponin, serum uric acid, in-hospital mortality, prognostic biomarkers

Introduction

Stroke remains one of the leading causes of global mortality and long-term neurological disability, imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden on healthcare systems worldwide.1–3 The incidence of stroke increases progressively with age and is strongly associated with vascular risk factors, including hypertension and diabetes mellitus.4,5

Serum uric acid (SUA), the terminal product of purine catabolism, exerts antioxidant activity under physiological conditions; however, elevated SUA levels have been associated with oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular inflammation.6–8 Hyperuricemia has been implicated in both stroke occurrence and adverse clinical outcomes, as demonstrated in previous observational studies and meta-analyses.6,9

Cardiac troponin is a highly sensitive and specific marker of myocardial injury. Elevated troponin levels occur frequently in patients with acute stroke, even in the absence of acute coronary syndrome, principally through neurogenic myocardial injury mediated by sympathoadrenal activation and catecholamine-induced cardiac stress.10,11 Multiple studies have demonstrated that troponin elevation is independently associated with increased mortality in both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke.12–15

C-reactive protein (CRP) is a well-established acute-phase reactant reflecting systemic inflammatory burden. Elevated admission CRP has been consistently associated with poor functional outcomes and increased mortality in patients with acute stroke.16–18

Although each of these biomarkers has been individually investigated, evidence evaluating their combined independent associations with in-hospital mortality in a cohort including both stroke subtypes remains limited. This study therefore aimed to evaluate the relationships between admission SUA, troponin, and CRP levels and in-hospital mortality in patients presenting with acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, while acknowledging the inherent limitation of lacking standardized stroke severity assessments.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective single-center cohort study was conducted in the Emergency Department of Niğde Ömer Halisdemir Training and Research Hospital, a tertiary referral center in Türkiye. Adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke between January 1, 2019, and January 1, 2022, were consecutively screened. Acute stroke diagnosis was confirmed by clinical assessment and computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Demographic characteristics, comorbid conditions, neuroimaging findings, and admission laboratory parameters were extracted from electronic medical records.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Patients aged ≥18 years with neuroimaging-confirmed acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke were eligible. Patients were excluded if they had: (i) absence of neuroimaging confirmation; (ii) incomplete medical records; (iii) active infection at presentation; (iv) pregnancy; (v) acute coronary syndrome requiring cardiology consultation; or (vi) documented primary cardiac disease, to minimize confounding effects on troponin interpretation. The final study population comprised 403 patients.

Laboratory Measurements and Outcome Definition

All laboratory parameters were measured from venous blood samples collected at emergency department admission using standardized validated assays in the hospital’s certified laboratory. In-hospital mortality was defined as death from any cause occurring during the index hospitalization.

The timing of blood sampling relative to stroke onset could not be standardized owing to the retrospective design; however, all included patients were in the acute phase at presentation. This temporal variability represents a recognized limitation, as biomarker levels may vary with time from symptom onset.

Statistical Analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as frequency and percentage.

Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Univariate logistic regression was performed for all clinically plausible variables. Variables with p<0.10 in univariate analysis and those considered clinically relevant (age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke subtype) were included in the multivariable model. Adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported. Model calibration was assessed by the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and explained variance was estimated using Nagelkerke R2. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis.

Optimal cut-off values for each biomarker were determined using the Youden index derived from ROC analysis. Area under the curve (AUC) with 95% CIs was calculated for individual biomarkers. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the absence of formal power calculations for secondary analyses, results should be considered hypothesis-generating.

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics Statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from Çukurova University Faculty of Medicine Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval date: January 4, 2024; Meeting No: 140; Decision No: 44). Informed consent was waived by the ethics committee owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized patient data.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 403 patients with acute stroke were included. Mean age was 73.8 ± 12.4 years, and 54.8% were male. Ischemic stroke accounted for 78.2% of cases and hemorrhagic stroke for 21.8%. Overall in-hospital mortality was 16.6% (n=67). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Non-survivors were significantly older than survivors (78.9 ± 10.2 vs 72.7 ± 12.6 years; p=0.002). ROC Analysis

ROC analysis identified CRP as the biomarker with the highest discriminatory performance (AUC: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.70–0.84; cut-off: 47.8 mg/L; sensitivity: 76.2%; specificity: 71.5%). Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of patients presenting with acute stroke, elevated admission levels of CRP, troponin, and SUA were each independently associated with in-hospital mortality after adjustment for age and traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Among these biomarkers, CRP demonstrated the strongest discriminatory performance. These findings support the potential utility of routinely available laboratory tests in supplementing early prognostic assessment in the emergency department setting.

The independent association between elevated admission SUA and in-hospital mortality in the present study is directionally consistent with prior evidence. Liu et al demonstrated in a retrospective cohort of acute cerebral infarction patients that hyperuricemia was independently associated with in-hospital mortality on multivariate analysis adjusted for NIHSS score; crucially, this association was statistically significant only among patients with impaired renal function (p=0.002), while no significant difference was observed in those with normal renal function (p=0.321).19 These findings underscore that elevated SUA may partly function as a surrogate marker for renal insufficiency rather than exerting a purely independent neurotoxic effect. In a large prospective study of 701 African stroke patients, hyperuricemia independently predicted both in-hospital mortality (OR=2.07; 95% CI: 1.45–2.95) and poor functional outcome within three months (OR=2.48; 95% CI: 1.40–4.40), with the independent mortality association confirmed on Cox proportional hazards regression after multivariable adjustment (adjusted HR=1.74; 95% CI: 1.31–2.32).20 Taken together, these data suggest that while the SUA–mortality association is biologically plausible—mediated through xanthine oxidase–driven oxidative stress, purine catabolism from ischemic neuronal injury, and pro-inflammatory endothelial effects—the absence of eGFR data in the present study introduces residual confounding that substantially qualifies the independence of the observed association. Future prospective studies must adjust for renal function before drawing definitive conclusions about the independent prognostic value of SUA in acute stroke.

The independent association between elevated admission troponin and in-hospital mortality observed in the present cohort aligns with a substantial and growing body of evidence. Zhang et al conducted an updated systematic review and meta-analysis encompassing 17 studies and more than 15,000 acute ischemic stroke patients, confirming that baseline troponin elevation independently predicted both in-hospital and short-term mortality; this finding held across subgroups stratified by troponin assay type, geographic region, and whether acute coronary syndrome had been formally excluded.21 Understanding why troponin rises in the absence of primary cardiac disease requires consideration of the broader concept of stroke–heart syndrome. This term captures the spectrum of neurogenic cardiac injury that can follow cerebral insult, and its mechanistic underpinnings have been well characterised by Scheitz et al22 Their work highlights that lesions involving the insular cortex can disrupt central autonomic regulation, setting off a cascade of sympathoadrenal overactivation, excessive catecholamine release, microvascular coronary dysfunction, and ultimately direct myocardial toxicity all of which may produce measurable troponin elevations without any epicardial coronary occlusion. This framework offers a coherent explanation for the clinical observation that patients with more extensive neurological injury tend to exhibit higher troponin concentrations and poorer outcomes. It also underscores an important caveat of our study design: excluding patients with known primary cardiac disease was necessary to limit non-neurogenic confounding of troponin values, yet roughly 30–50% of real-world stroke patients carry an underlying cardiac diagnosis. The external validity of our findings to this broader population therefore warrants cautious interpretation.

Among the three biomarkers, CRP demonstrated the strongest discriminatory performance (AUC: 0.77), consistent with the substantial evidence base supporting its prognostic role in acute stroke. A 2023 meta-analysis by Chen et al synthesizing 39 studies searched through October 2022 found that elevated admission hs-CRP was independently associated with all-cause mortality in acute ischemic stroke patients (RR=3.84; 95% CI: 2.41–6.11; p<0.001), as well as with recurrent stroke and poor functional prognosis.23 An earlier meta-analysis by Yu et al of eight studies encompassing 3,604 acute ischemic stroke patients similarly demonstrated that the highest CRP quartile was independently associated with a more than two-fold increase in all-cause mortality after multivariable adjustment (HR: 2.07; 95% CI: 1.60–2.68).24 Mechanistically, CRP elevation in acute stroke reflects convergent pathophysiological processes: activation of the complement cascade, induction of pro-inflammatory cytokine release, promotion of blood–brain barrier disruption, amplification of neuronal apoptosis, and expansion of the ischemic penumbra, collectively accelerating early neurological deterioration and systemic organ injury.23,24 Nevertheless, the moderate AUC of 0.77 observed here, while the strongest among the three biomarkers, remains insufficient for standalone clinical prognostication and underscores the need for CRP to be interpreted alongside neuroimaging findings and standardized stroke severity assessments rather than in isolation.

A notable finding was the attenuation of hypertension and diabetes mellitus to non-significance in the multivariable model despite their significant univariate associations with mortality. This pattern is consistent with mediation: the adverse effects of these chronic vascular risk factors on stroke outcomes may be substantially expressed through the promotion of systemic inflammation, endothelial injury, and neurogenic cardiac stress pathways captured by the biomarker variables included in the model.

Limitations

Several important methodological limitations must be emphasized. Most critically, standardized stroke severity assessments specifically the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) were unavailable. Stroke severity is the dominant determinant of in-hospital mortality, and failure to adjust for it introduces the possibility that elevated biomarker levels in this study partially reflect greater stroke severity rather than independent biological toxicity. This confounding cannot be excluded and substantially qualifies the interpretation of our results. Additionally, the combined predictive performance of the three-biomarker model was not formally evaluated; future studies should assess whether a composite model incorporating all three markers together with clinical covariates provides incremental prognostic value over clinical assessment alone. The retrospective single-center design, absence of eGFR data and medication histories (including statins and uric acid–lowering agents), the mixing of ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke subtypes without subgroup analysis, and the inability to account for reperfusion therapy, ICU admission, or hematoma/infarct volume further limit the generalizability and causal interpretation of these findings.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. The sample size of 403 patients with a clinically meaningful in-hospital mortality rate (16.6%), the rigorous exclusion of primary cardiac disease to minimize non-neurogenic troponin elevation, and the comprehensive statistical approach including model calibration, multicollinearity assessment, and ROC-based threshold derivation support the internal validity of the associations observed.

In clinical practice, these three biomarkers are already routinely collected on emergency department admission, incur no additional cost, and are available within minutes. Their independent associations with mortality, even after adjustment for traditional risk factors, suggest that they may contribute useful supplementary prognostic information when integrated with clinical judgment, stroke severity assessments, and neuroimaging findings. Prospective multicenter studies incorporating standardized stroke severity scales, renal function parameters, and subtype-specific analyses are required to rigorously validate and extend these preliminary observations.

Conclusion

Higher admission levels of CRP, troponin, and SUA were independently associated with in-hospital mortality in patients presenting with acute stroke. CRP demonstrated the strongest discriminatory performance among the evaluated biomarkers. However, in the absence of standardized stroke severity assessments and renal function data, these associations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and these biomarkers should be regarded as supplementary risk indicators rather than standalone prognostic tools. Prospective multicenter studies incorporating stroke severity scales, eGFR measurements, and combined biomarker models are warranted to better define the clinical utility of these markers in acute stroke prognostication.
