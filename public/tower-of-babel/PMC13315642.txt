Efficacy of tannins as silage additives to inhibit deamination process during ensiling: a meta-analysis and in silico approach
Authors: Irwan Susanto, Fachrul R Mahendra, Farisha R Azzahra, Mardiah Rahmadani, Roni Ridwan, Komang G Wiryawan, Erika B Laconi, Anuraga Jayanegara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnsiling, the anaerobic preservation of forage, is widely used to ensure year-round feed availability. However, high-protein forages are prone to protein degradation and deamination during ensiling, leading to ammonia formation and reduced feed quality. This study evaluated the efficacy of tannins as natural additives for inhibiting protein deamination using a meta-analysis of published studies, complemented by an in silico approach. Because tannins possess protein-binding properties, their potential role in inhibiting deamination was further examined through interactions with glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), an enzyme associated with deamination pathways in Clostridium species commonly found in silage. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted using OpenMEE, and molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock4 to predict the binding affinity of tannin-derived compounds toward GDH. The meta-analysis showed that tannin supplementation significantly (p < 0.001) reduced NH₃-N, non-protein nitrogen (NPN), and soluble nitrogen concentrations, while also lowering butyric acid concentration, indicating improved silage quality. Meta-regression analysis further demonstrated a linear decrease in ammonia concentration with increasing tannin levels (equation: −0.842 − 0.048 × level). MM/GBSA analysis further suggested that terflavin B was the most stable ligand, with the lowest binding free energy (−51.06 kcal/mol), followed by corilagin (−43.11 kcal/mol) and punicalin (−34.48 kcal/mol), indicating strong interactions with the GDH active site. In addition, bioavailability and toxicity prediction indicated generally favorable safety characteristics of these tannin-derived compounds. Overall, the integrated findings support the potential of tannins as bioactive agents for reducing protein degradation in silage, thereby improving nutrient preservation and feed quality during storage.Keywords: Deamination, Meta-analysis, Molecular docking, Phenol, Silage

Introduction

Sustainability of livestock production is highly dependent on feed availability, as it is a primary factor influencing animal productivity and health. In ruminants, feed is a critical input for maintaining essential life processes such as lactation, reproduction, and growth. However, seasonal variation in forage availability poses a major constraint, including in tropical countries. Green fodder is readily available during the rainy season, but its availability declines considerably during the dry season, leading to feed shortages (Daniel et al., 2019; Susanto et al., 2023a). Among the various methods adopted to ensure a continuous feed supply, silage production is one of the most widely used, in which green fodder is preserved under anaerobic conditions. The primary principle of silage making is to lower the pH of the ensiled material, thereby creating acidic conditions that prevent spoilage (Muck et al., 2020). However, ensiling requires a certain period to achieve optimal preservation, during which undesirable microbial activity may occur.

One of the most significant challenges in silage production is the development of spoilage bacteria such as Clostridium sp., which can lead to nutrient loss and deterioration of silage quality. Protein deamination, a key process contributing to nutrient loss, involves the conversion of silage proteins into ammonia (Jayanegara et al., 2019). This process reduces protein content and amino acid availability in feed, thereby lowering its nutritional value. In addition, ammonia accumulation negatively affects the organoleptic quality of silage, resulting in reduced palatability and lower feed intake by livestock (Queiroz et al., 2018). Because feed palatability directly influences animal intake and, consequently, productivity, preventing excessive deamination is essential for maintaining both the quality and acceptability of silage. Palatability refers to the preference of livestock for consuming a particular feed. Low palatability may reduce feed intake and, consequently, impair livestock growth performance and production (Baumont, 2011). Therefore, the development of effective additives to prevent protein deamination is crucial for optimizing silage preservation.

Bioactive plant compounds, particularly secondary metabolites with antimicrobial properties, have attracted considerable interest as natural feed additives. Among these, tannins are promising alternatives because of their protein-binding capacity (Jayanegara et al., 2018) and antimicrobial activity (Daglia, 2012). Tannins have been reported to interfere with microbial metabolism, potentially inhibiting spoilage bacteria such as Clostridium sp. and fungi that contribute to silage deterioration. However, the interaction between tannins and microbial communities in silage is complex, as tannins may also influence protein digestibility (Jayanegara & Palupi, 2010). Despite their well-established antimicrobial and protein-binding properties, their specific mechanism of action in inhibiting deamination under silage conditions remains poorly understood. It is still unclear to what extent specific tannin molecules can selectively inhibit deaminase-producing bacteria without adversely affecting protein availability. Additionally, recently published studies have shown that nitrate accumulation of >90% can be achieved in the presence of phenolic acids (Huang et al., 2024a). Moreover, variation in tannin composition among different plant sources may influence their efficacy, indicating that their bioactive composition should also be taken into consideration.

Although several studies have reported the effects of tannins on fermentation quality and protein protection during silage, the results still show high variability between studies due to differences in tannin sources, forage types, application doses, and silage conditions (Syahniar et al., 2018; Jayanegara et al., 2019). Therefore, a meta-analysis approach is essential to produce a more comprehensive quantitative synthesis of the biological effects of tannins on protein deamination and silage quality parameters empirically. However, meta-analyses are only able to demonstrate biological response patterns and statistical relationships, without explaining the mechanisms underlying the antideamination activity of secondary metabolites.

A molecular docking approach was used to overcome these limitations, as a complement to evaluate the possible interactions between tannin bioactive compounds and key enzymes involved in amino acid degradation and deamination processes by silage microorganisms (Susanto et al., 2025). This in silico approach allows for the identification of binding affinity and stability of molecular interactions between secondary metabolites and protein targets (Ranaweera & Baik, 2025), providing a mechanistic explanation for the empirical results obtained through meta-analysis. Thus, the integration of meta-analysis and molecular docking not only provides a quantitative evaluation of tannin effectiveness but also strengthens the biological understanding of the deamination inhibition mechanism at the molecular level.

To provide deeper insight into the efficacy of tannins in silage preservation, the present study employed both meta-analysis and in silico approaches. Meta-analysis integrates data from previous studies to generate an overall assessment of the role of tannins in inhibiting protein deamination by evaluating trends, variability, and factors affecting their effectiveness. Additionally, meta-regression can be used to explore the relationship between tannin use levels and fermentation response, particularly ammonia concentration. This approach was used to empirically demonstrate the biological effects of tannins on fermentation parameters and protein protection during ensiling. Concurrently, molecular docking analysis complements this approach by providing a mechanistic understanding at the molecular level, identifying secondary metabolites such as punicalagin, punicalin, and catechin as compounds with the potential to interact with bacterial enzymes that catalyze protein deamination (Falade et al., 2021; Susanto et al., 2025). Molecular docking was used to elucidate the possible molecular mechanisms of deamination inhibition through the interaction of tannin compounds with target enzymes involved in amino acid degradation.

The combination of these two approaches allows for a more robust evaluation: meta-analysis summarizes empirical evidence to assess the overall effectiveness of tannins and the effectiveness of tannin levels in preventing deamination through meta-regression, whereas molecular docking elucidates their possible mode of action at the biochemical level. Through the integration of these complementary techniques, this study aimed to establish a scientific basis for the selection of tannin-based additives and to identify the most active bioactive compounds for preventing protein degradation. We hypothesized that tannins are able to reduce protein deamination during the ensiling process through the inhibition of key enzymes involved in amino acid degradation, and that this mechanism can be partially explained through molecular interaction analysis using molecular docking or in silico study.

Materials and methods

Literature search and selection

The study commenced with a literature search for research articles on the application of tannins as silage additives using the PRISMA protocol (Liberati et al., 2009; Rahmadani et al., 2026). The literature was retrieved from ScienceDirect (https://www.sciencedirect.com/), Semantic Scholar (https://www.semanticscholar.org/), PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and the Scopus database (https://www.scopus.com/) using institutional access provided by IPB University. The literature search was conducted using the keywords “silage”, “tannin”, “ensile”, and “extract”, and the data were collected from publications published between 1999 and 2025. A total of 949 articles were initially identified and subsequently screened using the following criteria: (1) English-language publications; (2) inclusion of both treatment and control (without additive) groups in the experiment; (3) presence of tannin supplementation as a silage additive during ensiling; (4) reporting of chemical composition and silage fermentation results; and (5) reporting of replication and variance, including standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). The selection PRISMA process is presented in Fig. 1.

The selection process of the studies using the PRISMA protocol.

Based on the literature search and selection process, 24 articles comprising 82 experiments were included in this analysis and are presented in Table 1. The substrates used for silage production varied widely and included grasses, legumes, agricultural by-products, and total mixed ration (TMR). The selected articles also reported the use of extracts from various plants, such as acacia, chestnut, quebracho, and several other plant sources. Some articles also reported different units of measurement for certain parameters. In such cases, the data for each parameter were standardized by conversion into predetermined units. Nutritional quality parameters, including dry matter (DM), pH, crude protein (CP), ether extract (EE), neutral detergent fiber (NDF), acid detergent fiber (ADF), acid detergent insoluble crude protein (ADICP), and neutral detergent insoluble crude protein (NDICP), were integrated into the database. The fermentation products included water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC), lactic acid, ethanol, acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, NH3-N, weight loss (WL), total nitrogen, non-protein nitrogen (NPN), and soluble-N.

Publication bias was assessed by using funnel plot and Fail-safe number value (Rosenthal, 1979; Ridla et al., 2024). The meta-analysis software used in this study referred to Susanto et al. (2023b) and Albarki et al. (2024), namely the OpenMEE (http://www.cebm.brown.edu/openmee/). Effect sizes from individual studies were synthesized using a one-way random-effects model to estimate the pooled Hedges’ d along with the associated 95% confidence interval. Publication bias was evaluated through both visual inspection of funnel plots and statistical assessment using Egger’s test. Furthermore, the trim-and-fill method was implemented to identify and account for the potential influence of unpublished or missing studies, thereby improving the robustness of the meta-analysis (Shi & Lin, 2019). For publication bias statistical analyses were performed using JASP software.

Meta regression and sub group meta-analysis

Regression analysis was performed to evaluate the effect of tannin inclusion levels on their inhibitory efficacy against deamination, using ammonia concentration as the primary response parameter based on both dosage level and effect size. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were conducted, where the effect sizes represented comparisons between different dietary or substrate types, tannin sources, and their respective control treatments. The control groups consisted of treatments without tannin supplementation.

To accurately account for variations in experimental design among studies, subgroup analyses were performed separately for each comparison category. Importantly, each substrate type and tannin source was analyzed within the context of its original experimental framework to preserve the integrity of the individual study comparisons. Consequently, the subgroup estimates reflected the pooled mean differences attributable to the application of specific substrate and tannin sources as evaluated in each study, thereby enabling a more precise interpretation of the effects of tannin source and substrate type on ammonia concentration on deamination inhibition.

Receptor and ligand preparation

Molecular docking was performed using the glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) receptor from Clostridium sp., obtained from the RCSB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) with PDB code 1BGV. The receptor was selected based on its resolution of 1.90 Å and wild-type (non-mutated) status. The obtained 3D receptor structure was then processed by removing water molecules and separating the protein receptor structure from the co-crystal ligand (glutamic acid). The next step involved converting both the protein and co-crystal ligand from *.pdb format to *.pdbqt format by adding charges and hydrogen atoms.

The test ligands, consisting of secondary metabolite derivatives of tannin compounds and condensed tannins, as well as the comparison control ligand Bithionol (2,2′-Thiobis(4,6-dichlorophenol)), were obtained from several sources, such as PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), KNApSAcK (https://www.knapsackfamily.com/KNApSAcK/), and literature reviews (Li et al., 2009; Falade et al., 2021; Susanto et al., 2025). The structures of 11 tannin-related compounds were identified, namely terflavin B, punicalagin, punicalin, ellagic acid, corilagin, gallagic acid, hexahydroxydiphenic acid, gallic acid, catechin, chebulinic acid, and catechol (Susanto et al., 2025).

Gridbox and docking validation

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock4 (AutoDock-GPU v1.6) to evaluate the binding interactions of 11 tannin ligands with glutamate dehydrogenase (PDB ID: 1BGV), with Bithionol used as the control comparison ligand. The receptor structure was prepared by removing non-essential molecules and correcting missing atoms using PyMOL™ v3.1.6.1 and pdbfixer (https://github.com/openmm/pdbfixer) (Trott & Olson, 2010; Mahendra et al., 2025).

To validate the docking protocol, a redocking analysis was conducted by re-docking the co-crystallized native ligand into the active site of glutamate dehydrogenase. A total of 50 independent redocking runs were performed using identical docking parameters. The accuracy of the docking setup was assessed by calculating the root mean square deviation (RMSD) between the predicted poses and the crystallographic ligand conformation. The grid box configuration that consistently produced the lowest RMSD values was selected for subsequent screening. Based on this validation, the grid box was centered at X = −15.845, Y = 15.479, and Z = 20.243, with dimensions of 55 × 55 × 55 Å along the X, Y, and Z axes, ensuring full coverage of the binding pocket. The optimal docking pose for each ligand was selected based on the lowest binding affinity obtained from the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm runs. The structural accuracy of the predicted complexes was evaluated by calculating the RMSD between the docked pose and the reference conformation using RDKit v2025.3.3. Docking results were considered valid when the RMSD value was below 2.0 Å, indicating reliable reproduction of the binding orientation within the active site.

Ligand preparation for screening was conducted using ADFRsuite v1.0 to obtain PDBQT files. The Grid Parameter Files (GPFs) were generated using MGLTools v1.5.7 and executed with AutoGrid4. Docking simulations were carried out using the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA), the default search method in AutoDock4. Both the number of LGA runs and the population size were set to 100. For each ligand, the docking pose with the most favorable binding affinity was selected as the representative conformation.

MM-GBSA scoring interaction

To further refine and validate the docking results obtained from AutoDock4 (AutoDock-GPU v1.6), the MM/GBSA approach was employed to provide a more reliable estimation of binding free energy. Unlike conventional docking scoring functions, MM/GBSA incorporates solvation effects and partially accounts for entropic contributions, thereby improving the energetic accuracy of ligand–receptor interaction assessment. This method enhances the discrimination between true binders and false-positive docking hits. Binding free energy calculations were performed using the Uni-GBSA pipeline following the protocol described by Mahendra et al. (2025). The receptor structures (.pdb) and ligand structures (.sdf), generated from the best docking poses (converted from * format using Open Babel), were used as input files for the MM/GBSA analysis. The calculations were executed through the unigbsa-pipeline command-line tool in a Linux Bash environment, using the docking outputs from the AutoDock4 GPU screening step. The resulting MM/GBSA binding free energy values were used to validate and re-rank the selected docking poses.

Bioavailability and toxicity assessment

Lipinski’s Rule of Five was used to evaluate compound bioavailability in this study based on the PKCSM webserver (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/). This principle is used to predict the pharmacokinetic feasibility of a compound, particularly in relation to its ability to be absorbed orally. According to these rules, a compound is likely to be bioavailable if: (1) its molecular weight does not exceed 500 Daltons; (2) its lipid–water partition coefficient (log P) does not exceed 5; and (3) it does not have more than five hydrogen bond donors. These parameters were analyzed computationally using the PKCSM web server to predict the oral bioavailability feasibility of the tested compounds (Pires et al., 2015; Fagerholm et al., 2024). The results of this analysis provide a more comprehensive picture of the compounds’ potential as drug candidates and additives.

Compound toxicity was also analyzed using the PKCSM webserver (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/) to predict various important toxicological parameters. These included the Ames toxicity test, maximum tolerated dose (MTD), acute and chronic oral toxicity in animals, and hepatotoxicity. The Ames toxicity test was used to predict the mutagenic potential of a compound, namely its potential to cause genetic mutations that may lead to cancer. Meanwhile, the predicted maximum tolerated dose (MTD), expressed in log mg/kg/day, estimates the highest dose that can be administered to animals without causing significant toxic effects. Chronic oral toxicity reflects the effects of long-term daily exposure and is expressed in log mg/kg body weight/day. In contrast, acute oral toxicity indicates severe toxic effects or death resulting from a single exposure to a compound and is expressed in mol/kg. The liver is the primary organ responsible for the metabolism of drugs and other compounds. Therefore, hepatotoxicity analysis was performed to determine whether a compound may cause liver damage. The results of these predictions provide an initial overview of the potential hazards associated with the tested compounds, thereby allowing the elimination of compounds with potentially significant harmful effects before further experimental testing.

Meta-regression and subgroup effects

The results of the regression analysis evaluating the effect of tannin extract levels in silage showed that increasing tannin levels linearly decreased ammonia production (Fig. 2). These findings indicate a reduction in ammonia concentration with increasing tannin levels, as described by the regression equation: −0.842 − 0.048 × tannin level. This result suggests that tannins are effective in inhibiting ammonia formation during silage storage. To further explore these findings, the analysis was subsequently continued using subgroup analysis.

The regression model between ammonia concentration and level of tannin.

The subgroup analysis results indicated that ammonia concentration in silage was affected by both the tannin source and forage substrate. In the tannin source subgroup analysis (Fig. 3A), several tannin sources were associated with a reduction in ammonia concentration, although the magnitude of the response varied among sources. Gallnut and acacia exhibited the strongest reductions, whereas myrabolam, quebracho, mimosa, and tannic acid showed no significant effect on ammonia concentration. In the forage substrate subgroup analysis (Fig. 3B), most forage types showed decreased ammonia concentration following tannin application. The strongest response was observed in king grass silage, while alfalfa, lucerne, TMR, and pea + wheat substrates also demonstrated significant reductions. In contrast, ryegrass, mulberry leaves, cassava, and tall fescue substrates did not exhibit significant changes in ammonia concentration.

Publication bias

Fig. 4 presents the funnel plot analysis used to evaluate publication bias for the ammonia concentration parameter. Panel A displays the distribution of the observed studies using funnel plot, whereas Panel B illustrates the distribution after adjustment using the trim-and-fill method. Visual examination of the funnel plot showed an asymmetrical pattern, indicating the potential presence of publication bias and small-study effects among the included studies. Several studies were distributed unevenly outside the funnel area, particularly those with larger standard errors, suggesting that studies reporting smaller or non-significant effects may be underrepresented in the dataset. The statistical analyses supported this observation, where the meta-regression test demonstrated significant funnel plot asymmetry (z = −9.169; P < 0.001). Likewise, Egger’s weighted regression test also indicated significant asymmetry (t = −4.756; P < 0.001), confirming the existence of small-study effects and possible selective reporting bias.

Publication bias effect of tannin on ammonia concentration.

After adjustment using the trim-and-fill procedure, the pooled effect size changed to −1.165 (95% CI: −3.016 to 0.686; P = 0.217), indicating that potentially missing studies may have influenced the estimated overall effect size. This finding suggests that studies reporting stronger reductions in ammonia concentration were more likely to be published compared to studies showing weaker or non-significant effects. The presence of asymmetry may also be associated with heterogeneity among the included studies, including differences in tannin sources, application levels, silage substrates, and experimental conditions. Similar findings were reported by Rahmadani et al. (2026), who stated that significant Egger’s test results in meta-analysis may reflect the presence of missing studies and publication bias related to heterogeneity across studies.

Discussion

During ensiling, pH and dry matter (DM) are important indicators of fermentation quality and silage stability. In the present study, tannin supplementation was associated with higher DM content, suggesting that tannins may help preserve the nutritional components of silage during storage. Generally, a reduction in DM is expected during the ensiling process because of plant respiration and microbial fermentation activities (Jayanegara et al., 2019). However, DM losses of approximately 3–5% are still considered acceptable under normal ensiling conditions (Tabacco et al., 2006). Previous studies have reported that DM losses may occur at several stages of silage production, including forage harvesting, plant respiration, microbial fermentation in the silo, and oxygen exposure during storage and feed-out (Borreani et al., 2017). Therefore, maintaining a higher DM content in tannin-treated silage may indicate improved preservation efficiency during ensiling.

The EE content in silage can be influenced by microbial activity during fermentation. Lactic acid bacteria utilize lipids as an additional nutrient source for microbial growth, which may contribute to changes in fat composition during silage (Ridla et al., 2024). During fermentation, microbial populations increase and produce various enzymes that degrade complex plant materials. For example, cellulolytic microorganisms produce cellulase enzymes that break down lignocellulosic and hemicellulosic bonds within plant substrates (Thapa et al., 2020), facilitating nutrient transformation during fermentation. In addition, the crude fat fraction in fermented feed may be affected by the biomass of microbial cells that grow and reproduce within the fermentation medium (Kitessa, 2024). The high heterogeneity value of EE content in silage is influenced by several factors, including the dry matter content and the type of substrate used in the ensiling process (Kara et al., 2022).

Neutral detergent insoluble crude protein (NDICP) represents the fraction of protein bound within the neutral detergent fiber (NDF) residue and is generally considered resistant to ruminal degradation (Licitra et al., 1996). This fraction is commonly associated with rumen undegradable protein, which can bypass ruminal degradation and become available for post-ruminal digestion. The presence of tannin compounds in feed ingredients may enhance the formation of protein–tannin complexes, thereby limiting proteolysis during ensiling and increasing the proportion of fibre-bound protein fractions. Previous studies have reported that NDICP and ADICP fractions can provide valuable information regarding the amount of bypass protein present in feed ingredients, including silage (Jayanegara et al., 2016). This study highlights the limited number of studies evaluating NDICP and ADICP, resulting in high heterogeneity and findings that may not fully represent the expected outcomes due to the limited available data. However, these findings remain important to report in order to increase researchers’ awareness and encourage further investigation of NDICP and ADICP parameters in future studies.

On the other hand, the reduced formation of certain fermentation products may be associated with the antimicrobial properties of tannins. Tannins are known to contain secondary metabolites that can disrupt bacterial cell wall structures and inhibit microbial growth (Daglia, 2012). Therefore, high concentrations of tannins may suppress the activity of LAB, which play a central role in converting WSC into lactic acid during ensiling. Reduced LAB activity may limit lactic acid production and alter the overall fermentation pathway. Recent studies also demonstrated that excessive tannin concentrations may reduce LAB abundance and alter fermentation pathways during ensiling, leading to lower lactic acid accumulation and slower acidification processes (Azzahra et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). In addition, WSC availability is an important factor regulating silage fermentation, as these carbohydrates serve as the primary substrate for LAB metabolism. Previous studies have shown that silage fermentation characteristics are strongly influenced by WSC concentration and pre-ensiling management practices, such as forage wilting (Ridla et al., 2024).

The formation of fermentation acids, such as propionic and butyric acids, is closely related to microbial activity and environmental conditions during ensiling. Tannin compounds exhibit antimicrobial properties that can influence the growth of specific microbial populations involved in silage fermentation. In particular, environmental factors such as pH can regulate the activity of lactic acid bacteria and other competing microorganisms, thereby affecting the composition of the fermentation end products (Harahap et al., 2023).

Butyric acid is commonly associated with undesirable fermentation because it is produced by spoilage microorganisms, such as Clostridium spp., which are active under unfavourable fermentation conditions (Kung et al., 2018). Therefore, the presence of butyrate in silage is often considered an indicator of protein degradation and spoilage during fermentation. Ideally, butyrate production should remain minimal in well-preserved silage (Wang et al., 2024). However, microbial dynamics during ensilage may vary depending on the type of forage substrate. Leguminous forages, for example, often carry higher populations of spoilage microorganisms, including Clostridium spp. and certain fungi, than lactic acid bacteria (McDonald et al., 2002). Consequently, the fermentation characteristics of legume-based silage may differ from those of other forage types.

Nitrogenous fermentation products, such as NH₃-N, non-protein nitrogen (NPN), and soluble nitrogen, are commonly used as indicators of protein degradation during ensiling. Ammonia formation generally results from the microbial deamination of amino acids during proteolysis, which is frequently associated with the activity of spoilage microorganisms such as Clostridium spp. During secondary fermentation, these microorganisms convert lactic acid into butyric acid while simultaneously degrading proteins, peptides, and amino acids into amines and ammonia (Adesogan et al., 2004). Consequently, excessive ammonia accumulation is widely regarded as a sign of undesirable fermentation and nutrient loss in silages.

The presence of tannin compounds in forage may influence this process through both antimicrobial activity and the formation of tannin–protein complexes, which limit microbial proteolysis. Tannins interact with proteins via hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, thereby reducing protein accessibility to the microbial enzymes. However, the stability of tannin–protein complexes can be influenced by environmental factors such as pH, as the ionization of phenolic hydroxyl groups may reduce the tannin–protein affinity (Adamczyk et al., 2012). In addition, improved ensiling management practices, including appropriate moisture levels and controlled fermentation conditions, may further suppress the growth of Clostridium spp. during the later stages of fermentation (Lemos et al., 2024). In addition to microbial activity, nitrogen transformation during silage fermentation may also depend on forage characteristics, including plant chemical composition and associated microbial communities (Carvalho et al., 2024). These findings highlight the potential role of tannin-containing additives in preserving protein fractions and reducing nitrogen loss during silage storage. Furthermore, complementary bioinformatics approaches, such as molecular docking, may help identify specific tannin-derived compounds that interact with enzymes involved in deamination pathways.

The ammonia concentration is an important indicator of protein degradation and amino acid deamination during silage fermentation. The reduction in ammonia observed in most treatments suggests that tannins can protect plant proteins from excessive proteolysis by forming relatively resistant tannin-protein complexes that are not easily degraded by microbes (Grote et al., 2023; Jayanegara et al., 2018; Sucu et al., 2025). In addition, tannins may inhibit the activity of proteolytic microorganisms and their associated enzymes, thereby reducing amino acid deamination and subsequent ammonia formation (Jayanegara et al., 2019). Among the tannin source subgroups, gallnut and acacia exhibited the strongest reductions in ammonia concentration, whereas quebracho, mimosa, and myrabolam showed no significant effects. Acacia and quebracho tannins are both rich in profisetinidin-type condensed tannins. However, quebracho tannins are considered to have lower biological reactivity because of their more compact polymer structure, which may limit their interaction with proteins and microbial enzymes (Mueller-Harvey, 2006). Nevertheless, the present findings differ from those reported by Fitri et al. (2022), who found that quebracho tannin was more effective than acacia tannin in reducing protein degradation. Such inconsistencies among studies may be attributed to differences in tannin extraction sources, supplementation levels, molecular weight, and degree of polymerization, all of which can influence tannin bioactivity and protein-binding capacity (Yanza et al., 2021).

In the forage substrate subgroup, most forage types showed reduced ammonia concentration following tannin application. This finding suggests that the effectiveness of tannins in suppressing protein degradation is influenced by forage characteristics, including forage species, crude protein content, fiber fractions, and water-soluble carbohydrate concentration (Hilgers et al., 2025; Chaikong et al., 2017). These results are supported by Gülümser et al. (2024) where the use of Humulus lupulus L. in alfalfa silage was able to improve silage quality, lowered pH, and reduced methane production. Leguminous forages, such as alfalfa, lucerne, and Indigofera, exhibited significant reductions in ammonia concentration, indicating that high-protein forages may be more responsive to tannin-mediated protein protection. Higher protein availability in legume silages may enhance the formation of tannin-protein complexes, thereby reducing proteolysis during fermentation. In some forage species, endogenous polyphenol oxidase activity may also contribute to protein protection through the formation of protein-bound phenolic compounds that are more resistant to enzymatic degradation (Hilgers et al., 2025). These findings are consistent with those of Herremans et al. (2019), who suggested that tannins may interact more effectively with proteins in high-protein silages than with grass proteins. In contrast, ryegrass and mulberry leaves did not show significant reductions in ammonia concentration. This variation related to differences in nutrient composition, protein solubility, cell wall characteristics, and the composition of fermentative microbial populations, all of which can influence tannin reactivity and the extent of protein protection during ensiling.

This study integrated meta-analysis and in silico approaches to better understand the role of tannins in inhibiting deamination during ensiling. While meta-analysis synthesizes evidence from previous studies to evaluate the effects of tannin supplementation on silage quality, molecular docking provides mechanistic insights by examining the interactions between tannin-derived compounds and enzymes involved in nitrogen metabolism. This combined approach offers a broader understanding of the potential role of tannins as functional additives in the preservation of silage.

Glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) is an enzyme that catalyzes oxidative deamination reactions involved in nitrogen metabolism. Structurally, GDH contains several functional domains, including an amino acid dehydrogenase-like domain and an NAD(P)-binding domain that participate in the reversible conversion of glutamate to α-ketoglutarate and ammonia, respectively (Stillman et al., 1993; Baker et al., 1995). This enzymatic activity plays an important role in regulating ammonia formation during the metabolism of amino acids. The virtual screening of GDH receptors and tannin derivatives demonstrated the suitability of this method for scoring complex molecular docking interactions (Xu et al., 2022). Molecular docking analysis provided insights into the interactions between tannin-derived compounds and the GDH catalytic pocket. Tannin molecules, which are rich in hydroxyl and aromatic groups, can interact with enzymes through hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions, potentially altering the conformation of the active site or limiting substrate binding (Türkan et al., 2019; Zhong et al., 2024). Such interactions may contribute to the inhibition of deamination reactions and the reduction of ammonia production. In addition, tannin compounds are known to exhibit antimicrobial properties that can influence microbial activity during silage fermentation, supporting their potential application as functional additives to improve forage preservation and limit excessive proteolysis (Huang et al., 2024b; Azzahra et al., 2025).

Molecular docking analysis indicated that several tannin-derived compounds exhibited stronger binding affinities toward the GDH receptor than the reference ligand and natural substrate. Ellagitannin derivatives, such as punicalagin, punicalin, and corilagin, showed particularly strong interactions within the GDH catalytic pocket, suggesting their potential to interfere with substrate access and inhibit the oxidative deamination of glutamate into 2-oxoglutarate and ammonia (Shen et al., 2021; Bianchetti et al., 2024). These interactions are mainly stabilised by hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and π-related interactions, which may alter the conformational environment of the enzyme’s active site and reduce its catalytic activity. These findings provide a molecular explanation for the inhibitory effect of tannins on deamination processes associated with ammonia formation during silage fermentation. However, conventional molecular docking has several limitations. Docking simulations often simplify biological systems by treating proteins and ligands as relatively rigid structures and neglecting environmental factors such as solvent effects, cofactors, and dynamic conformational changes. Therefore, further studies using molecular dynamics simulations may provide a more comprehensive understanding of ligand–receptor interactions and the stability of tannin binding to GDH (Ma et al., 2012; Menchon et al., 2018; Richard & Pantazes, 2024).

Bioavailability prediction based on Lipinski’s Rule of Five was used as an initial approach to evaluate the potential bioavailability of each tannin-derived metabolite compound. The results indicated that only a few compounds, including gallic acid, catechin, and catechol, fully satisfied the criteria, likely due to their relatively simple molecular structures. However, several tannin-derived compounds that violate Lipinski’s rule, such as punicalagin and punicalin, have demonstrated strong inhibitory potential. This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that polyphenolic compounds with higher molecular weights and multiple hydroxyl groups may still exhibit strong enzyme-inhibitory activity because of their ability to form multiple hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with catalytic residues (Yoshida et al., 2000; Alalawi et al., 2023). In addition, the structural complexity and degree of polymerization of polyphenols have been reported to enhance their inhibitory activity by increasing their interactions with target enzymes (Sugiyama et al., 2007). These findings suggest that the inhibitory potential of tannin-derived compounds is not solely determined by Lipinski’s criteria but also by their structural properties and interaction capabilities.

Toxicity prediction analysis indicated that most tannin-derived compounds exhibited low toxicity potential across several toxicity parameters, including mutagenicity, oral toxicity, and hepatotoxicity. The absence of mutagenic signals in the AMES test suggests a low probability of genetic toxicity, supporting the safety profile of these compounds. The AMES test is often used in evaluating the potential of a compound to cause genetic mutations in bacterial organisms, so it is often used in carcinogenic potential indicators (Vijay et al., 2018). Overall, these results indicate that tannin-derived metabolites may possess favorable safety characteristics while maintaining strong inhibitory activity, highlighting their potential as bioactive compounds for regulating deamination-related enzymatic processes. However, further in vitro and in vivo studies are needed to validate the molecular docking results and strengthen the reliability of these preliminary findings regarding the potential of tannins as deamination inhibitors.

Conclusions

This study showed that tannin supplementation is a promising strategy to reduce protein deamination during ensiling, as reflected by the lower concentrations of NH₃-N, non-protein nitrogen, and soluble nitrogen in tannin-treated silage. These findings indicate that tannins may help preserve silage quality by reducing nitrogen loss during fermentation. Meta-regression analysis further supported these findings by demonstrating a linear decrease in ammonia concentration with increasing tannin levels in silage. Complementary molecular docking and MM-GBSA analyses further suggested that tannin-derived compounds can strongly interact with glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), providing a plausible mechanistic explanation for their role in inhibiting deamination. Among the compounds evaluated, terflavin B, corilagin, and punicalin emerged as the most promising candidates based on their favorable MM-GBSA binding energies. Overall, this integrated meta-analysis and in silico approach strengthens the evidence for tannin-based additives as functional agents in silage preservation and highlights specific secondary metabolites with potential roles in limiting protein degradation during ensiling.
