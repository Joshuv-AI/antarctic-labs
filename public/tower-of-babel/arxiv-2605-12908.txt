The Mechanism of Weak-to-Strong Generalization: Feature Elicitation from Latent Knowledge
Ryoya Awano, Taiji Suzuki, [0.3em] University of Tokyo, [0.2em] awano-ryoya284@g.ecc.u-tokyo.ac.jp

Abstract
Weak-to-strong (W2S) generalization, in which a strong model is fine-tuned on outputs of a weaker, task-specialized model, has been proposed as an approach to aligning superhuman AI systems. Existing theoretical analyses either fix the student’s representations or operate in restricted settings. Whether multi-step SGD can succeed in feature learning while preserving diverse pre-trained capabilities remains open. We study W2S in the setting of reward-model learning with two-layer neural networks. The strong model has pre-trained representations organized into low-dimensional subspaces V k V_{k} , and is fine-tuned under the supervision of a weak model specialized on task κ \kappa . We prove that the strong model efficiently learns task κ \kappa , eliciting its pre-trained knowledge while retaining general capabilities. This establishes W2S generalization in the feature-learning regime, in the sense that the strong model acquires the target feature direction through W2S training, rather than having it given a priori. Moreover, W2S preserves pre-trained off-target features, whereas standard supervised fine-tuning causes catastrophic forgetting when off-target feature directions are correlated with the target’s. Numerical experiments on synthetic data confirm our theoretical results.

Introduction

Burns et al. 2023
showed that fine-tuning a strong language model on the outputs of a weaker, task-specialized model can produce a student that surpasses its teacher on that task (e.g., GPT-4
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
fine-tuned on GPT-2
(
Radford et al. 2019
)
labels outperforms GPT-2 across NLP benchmarks).
This phenomenon,
weak-to-strong
(W2S) generalization, has become a workhorse for studying how imperfect supervision by a less capable model can nonetheless elicit useful behavior from a more capable one. It underlies the superalignment program, in which humans act as the weak supervisors of models beyond their own capabilities.

Empirically,
Burns et al. 2023
demonstrate that W2S can successfully elicit capabilities even under linear probing, where the strong model’s representations are fixed.
This may partly explain why many early theoretical treatments of W2S have focused on the linear regime.
However, they also show that full fine-tuning—allowing for feature learning—unlocks better performance.
To explain this gain, they hypothesize that feature learning makes the concepts acquired during pre-training more "salient."
W2S would thus elicit latent knowledge rather than instill entirely new capabilities.

The theoretical study of W2S has addressed either abstract frameworks that do not analyze gradient-based optimization
(
Lang et al. 2024
;
Shin et al. 2025
;
Charikar et al. 2024
;
Xue et al. 2025
)
or linear and random feature models
(
Wu and Sahai 2025
;
Ildiz et al. 2025
;
Dong et al. 2025
;
Medvedev et al. 2025
)
.
Neither line derives the difference between weak and strong model features from the optimization process itself.
Motivated by the empirical advantage of full fine-tuning, recent theoretical works
(
Moniri and Hassani 2025
;
Oh et al. 2025
)
took the first steps toward W2S feature learning, but their theoretical settings leave significant gaps regarding architecture, pre-training priors, and task specialization.

Oh et al. 2025
analyze W2S by employing a linear CNN as the weak model and a two-layer ReLU CNN as the strong model, with convergence proved under full-batch gradient descent.
However, this CNN-based architecture differs from the LLMs used in
Burns et al. 2023
, assumes no pre-training prior on the strong model, and does not specialize the weak model to a particular task.

Moniri and Hassani 2025
use a two-component nonlinear model rather than a neural network (NN), assume the strong model already knows the exact direction of the off-target task at initialization, and their analysis tracks only a single gradient step.

A key open question is whether multi-step SGD can provably succeed at W2S feature learning while preserving diverse capabilities, in a more realistic setting.
Answering this question would shed light on the mechanism by which W2S makes pre-trained latent knowledge salient.
We study this question in the setting of reward-model learning, where, as in
Burns et al. 2023
, the strong model is trained on the weak model to estimate a reward directly.

Pre-trained models have been observed to localize individual tasks in distinct subsets of neurons
(
Dai et al. 2022
;
Panigrahi et al. 2023
)
.
We therefore adopt an additive reward
r^{*}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}r^{*}_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\top}x)
, where each component
r^{*}_{k}
corresponds to one task.
Furthermore, consistent with empirical findings that pre-trained models encode concepts in low-dimensional subspaces
(
Beaglehole et al. 2026
)
, we assume the strong model has pre-training knowledge of the task subspaces
V_{k}
.

We focus on the setting where the weak model is a small two-layer NN specialized to task
\kappa
, trained to approximate
r^{*}_{\kappa}
.
The strong model, a larger two-layer NN, queries the weak model based on its prior knowledge.
It then trains by multi-step SGD on the weak model’s outputs with nonlinear transformation.

Contributions

•

Feature elicitation via SGD analysis of W2S.

We study W2S in a setting closer to pre-trained LLMs, with an additive multi-feature reward and task subspaces as pre-training knowledge.
To our knowledge, we give the first multi-step SGD convergence guarantee for W2S in the feature-learning regime, with sample complexity
\tilde{O}(s^{3/2})
(
1
).
This sample complexity depends only on the subspace dimension
s
, not the input dimension
d
, and gives the provable account of how W2S elicits pre-trained latent features.

•

Feature preservation under W2S.

The strong model’s pre-trained input distribution, concentrated on
V_{\kappa}
, and the nonlinear teacher transformation together act as an implicit regularizer within the task subspaces.
We prove that the feature directions of off-target tasks are preserved, provided those tasks are allocated more neurons than the target task (
2
).
This preservation, combined with feature elicitation on task
\kappa
, provides a theoretical account of W2S generalization.

•

Advantages over standard SFT.

While
2
shows that W2S preserves off-target features, we also analyze the behavior of standard SFT on the same strong model, replacing the W2S algorithm with isotropic sampling and the direct teacher
r^{*}_{\kappa}
.

2
shows that off-target tasks with feature directions similar to
\theta_{\kappa}
suffer catastrophic forgetting.
Together with
1
, this provides a theoretical account of the advantages of W2S over standard SFT in terms of sample complexity and feature preservation.

Weak-to-strong generalization.

One line characterizes when W2S succeeds through abstract frameworks without analyzing gradient-based optimization
(

;

.
Moving beyond these settings,

2.1
Sample complexity and the information exponent

We define the information
(
Dudeja and Hsu 2018
;
Ben Arous et al. 2021
)
and generative
(
Damian et al. 2024b
)
exponents, which govern the sample complexity of learning a single-index model.

Definition 1

(Information and generative exponents)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be square-integrable under
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
with Hermite expansion
f(z)=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\sqrt{j!}}\,\mathrm{He}_{j}(z)
.
The
information exponent
of
f
is
\mathop{\mathrm{IE}}(f)=\min\{j>0:\alpha_{j}\neq 0\}
.
The
generative exponent
of
f
is
\mathop{\mathrm{GE}}(f)=\inf_{\mathcal{T}\in L^{2}}\,\mathop{\mathrm{IE}}(\mathcal{T}\circ f)
.

For a polynomial
f
of degree
q
, we have
\mathop{\mathrm{GE}}(f)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{IE}}(f)\leq q
; moreover,
\mathop{\mathrm{GE}}(f)=2
if
f
is even, and
\mathop{\mathrm{GE}}(f)=1
otherwise
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
.
Intuitively, a larger
p=\mathop{\mathrm{IE}}(f)
means the signal about the direction
\theta
appears only in higher-order Hermite components of
f
, making it harder to detect from samples
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
.
When
p>2
, online SGD requires
\tilde{O}(d^{p-1})
samples to find the hidden direction
\theta
from observations
(x,f(\theta^{\top}x)+\zeta)
of the single-index model, with
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

(
Ben Arous et al. 2021
)
.
However, by applying a nonlinear transformation,
Chen and Meka 2020
achieve
\tilde{O}(d)
sample complexity for polynomial link functions.

2.2
Additive reward model

As motivated in
Section

1
, we model the true reward as an additive combination of
K
single-index components.
Following
Oko et al. 2024a
, we study the case where each link function is a polynomial.
Each feature direction
\theta_{k}
is in a pre-trained subspace
V_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, motivated by empirical evidence that LLMs encode concepts in low-dimensional subspaces
(
Beaglehole et al. 2026
)
.
A similar assumption appears in
Oko et al. 2024b
and
Nishikawa et al. 2025
, where feature vectors are drawn from a low-dimensional subspace identified by pre-training.

Assumption 1

(Additive reward model with task subspaces)
.

The reward
r^{*}
decomposes additively across
K
tasks indexed by
k\in[K]
, with target task
\kappa
.

(i)

Additive reward.

The true reward is

\displaystyle r^{*}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}r^{*}_{k}(\theta_{k}^{\top}x),\qquad r^{*}_{k}=\pi_{k}\sigma^{*}_{k},

(1)

observed as
y=r^{*}(x)+\zeta
with
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
,
\zeta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
\|\theta_{k}\|=1
.
Each link function
\sigma^{*}_{k}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{N}(0,1))
is normalized so that
\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\sigma^{*}_{k}(t)]=0
and
\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[(\sigma^{*}_{k}(t))^{2}]=1
.
The target link function
\sigma^{*}_{\kappa}
is a degree-
q
polynomial with
\mathop{\mathrm{IE}}(\sigma^{*}_{\kappa})=p>2
, as in
Oko et al. 2024a
;
Simsek et al. 2025
;
Ren et al. 2025
.
The weights
\pi_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\pi_{K}\geq 0
satisfy
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}^{2}=1
.

(ii)

Task subspaces.

Each task
k
is associated with a subspace
V_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of dimension
s=d^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, containing its feature direction
\theta_{k}\in V_{k}
.
The subspaces
V_{k}
need not be mutually orthogonal;
2
exploits nonzero inner products
\theta_{k}^{\top}\theta_{\kappa}
across tasks.
We write
\Sigma_{k}
for the orthogonal projection onto
V_{k}
.

Remark
.

The Gaussian assumption
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is standard in the theoretical study of feature learning
(
Ben Arous et al. 2021
;
Damian et al. 2024b
)
.
Writing
\sigma^{*}_{\kappa}(t)=\sum_{i=p}^{q}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\sqrt{i!}}\mathrm{He}_{i}(t)
, the normalization condition reads
\sum_{i=p}^{q}\alpha_{i}^{2}=1
.
The subspaces
V_{k}
encode the pre-training prior that the strong model has identified the subspace
V_{k}
containing
\theta_{k}
, but not
\theta_{k}
itself, extending the setting of
Moniri and Hassani 2025
where off-target feature vector is known exactly.

2.3
Weak model

We consider that the weak model is small and specialized to task
\kappa
.
We model it as a two-layer network whose neurons are nearly aligned with
\theta_{\kappa}
and whose output approximates

in

.

Assumption 2

(Weak model)
.

The weak model, fine-tuned on task
\kappa\in[K]
, is a two-layer NN

\displaystyle r^{w}(x)=\frac{1}{N^{w}}\sum_{n=1}^{N^{w}}a^{w}_{n}\sigma^{w}_{n}(w_{n}^{\top}x+b^{w}_{n}),

(2)

where each activation

is a degree-

\|w_{n}\|=1

\|a^{w}\|_{1}=\tilde{O}(\pi_{\kappa}N^{w})

The weak model has learned feature

(i)

There exists
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{w}>0

\theta_{\kappa}^{\top}w_{n}\geq 1-\tilde{\varepsilon}_{w}

(ii)

Proposition 1

(Weak model construction; informal)

Under

Remark
.

The analysis builds on the literature

Ben Arous et al. 2021

Damian et al. 2024b

However, these results guarantee only (ii) in

2.4
Strong model

Task localization
(
Dai et al. 2022
;
Panigrahi et al. 2023
)
in pre-trained models motivates partitioning the strong model’s neurons by task.
We model the strong model as a pre-trained two-layer network with
K

\displaystyle r(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}(x),\quad r_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}a_{k,n}\,\sigma_{k,n}(w_{k,n}^{\top}x+b_{k,n}),

(4)

where
\|w_{k,n}\|=1
.
Unlike
Oko et al. 2024a

For task
\kappa

\Sigma_{\kappa}w^{0}_{\kappa,n}
uniform on

and perpendicular component satisfying
\|\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\perp}w^{0}_{\kappa,n}\|^{2}=o(s^{-1/2})
(see

For

\|\Sigma_{\kappa}w^{0}_{k,n}\|^{2}

k

Small values of this quantity preserve pre-trained features for

The strong model generates responses from its pre-trained distribution, in line with recent W2S literature
(
Ji et al. 2024

Tao and Li 2025
)
rather than the original setup of
Burns et al. 2023

We model the resulting embedding distribution as

Assumption 3

(Generative distribution)
.

When the strong model is queried on task

\displaystyle\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}\,\mu_{k},\quad(\lambda_{k})_{k=1}^{K}\in\Delta^{K-1},\quad 1-\lambda_{\kappa}=o(s^{-1/2}),

where

With

The factor

W2S Generalization.

1

\kappa

The strong model surpasses the weak model in that it acquires

via W2S training while retaining its initial knowledge of off-target tasks

\kappa
alone, does not possess.
For instance, task
\kappa

\kappa

Our results are complementary to analyses that assume fixed strong-model representations
(

Unlike

The bound on forgetting is enabled by the localization parameter

Remark
.

Empirically,

Generative finetuning may, however, pull off-target neurons toward

Proof sketch of Theorem
1

Intuitively, the gradient dynamics keep

approximately within
V_{\kappa}
throughout training, so
\theta_{\kappa}
is learned without leakage outside the subspace.
Two quantities govern the training dynamics, the alignment
z^{t}:=\theta_{\kappa}^{\top}w^{t}_{\kappa,n}
and the complement-subspace deviation
D^{t}:=\|\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\perp}w^{t}_{\kappa,n}\|^{2}

The following lemma gives a decomposition of the negative gradient
g^{t}_{\kappa}
with respect to

Lemma

(Gradient decomposition; informal,
22
)
.

g^{t}_{\kappa}=y^{t}\tilde{\nabla}_{w^{t}_{\kappa}}r_{\Theta}(x^{t})
decomposes as

\displaystyle g^{t}_{\kappa}=\bar{\alpha}_{2}\beta_{\kappa,n,2}\lambda_{\kappa}z^{t}\,\theta_{\kappa}+Z^{t}+R^{t},

(8)

where
\bar{\alpha}_{2},\beta_{\kappa,n,2}
are the second Hermite coefficients of
\bar{r}^{w}\exp(\bar{r}^{w})
and
a_{\kappa,n}\sigma_{\kappa}(\cdot+b_{\kappa,n})
,
Z^{t}
is mean-zero, and
\|R^{t}\|=\tilde{O}((\mathcal{E}_{s}\vee D^{t})\,s^{1/2})
.

Since
\mathop{\mathrm{GE}}(\sigma^{*}_{\kappa})=2
,
18
gives
\bar{\alpha}_{2}\neq 0
, so the alignment satisfies
z^{t+1}\approx(1+\eta^{t}\bar{\alpha}_{2}\beta_{\kappa,n,2}\lambda_{\kappa})\,z^{t}
.
By contrast, online SGD on
r^{*}_{\kappa}
has drift
(z^{t})^{p-1}

(
Ben Arous et al. 2021
)
.
The W2S signal reduces this to a linear drift, which accounts for the improved sample complexity.
The following lemma bounds
D^{t}
over each sub-interval of training.

Lemma

(Subspace deviation bound; informal,
24
)
.

For any step
t
with
z^{t}\geq 0
,
D^{t+1}-D^{t}=\tilde{O}(\eta^{t})
.
Moreover, over any sub-interval
[\tau,\tau+\tau^{\prime}]
with
z^{t}\geq 0
for all
t\in[\tau,\tau+\tau^{\prime}]
, with probability
1-\delta^{\prime}
,
D^{\tau+t}\leq Q^{t}
for all
t=0,\dots,\tau^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle Q^{0}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{D^{\tau}}_{\text{initial}}+\underbrace{\tilde{O}(\eta\,\mathcal{E}_{s}\,\tau^{\prime})}_{\text{weak-model error}}+\underbrace{\Theta((1-\lambda_{\kappa})^{1/2}\delta^{\prime-1/2}\eta\tau^{\prime 1/2}+(1-\lambda_{\kappa})\delta^{\prime-1}\eta^{2}s\tau^{\prime})}_{\text{off-subspace perturbation}},

(9)

\displaystyle Q^{t}

\displaystyle=Q^{t-1}+\tilde{O}(\eta(Q^{t-1})^{2}).

(10)

Here
\delta^{\prime}=1/\operatorname{polylog}d
.

Controlling this bound requires Doob’s maximal inequality rather than standard concentration, which forces a smaller learning rate and drives the overall complexity to
\tilde{O}(s^{3/2})
; see
1
for details.
A large

reduces the effective drift on

to
\bar{\alpha}_{2}\beta_{\kappa,n,2}\lambda_{\kappa}z^{t}-O(D^{t})
(
22
), so the condition
D^{t}=O(z^{t})
must be maintained throughout training.
Since the per-step increment of
D^{t}
is
O(\eta(D^{t})^{2})
(
24
) while
z^{t}
grows at rate
\Omega(\eta z^{t})
(
25
and
27
), the ratio
D^{t}/z^{t}
is non-increasing during the initial phase where
z^{t}=o(1)
.

25
and
27
shows that the condition
D^{t}=o(z^{t})
is maintained throughout each sub-interval with probability
1-o(1)
, using the bound on
D^{t}
established in
24

Alignment dynamics.

Three stages complete the argument (

,

,

In the first two stages the learning rate is

In weak recovery (

Lemma

(Subspace deviation decay; informal,

Suppose

The total cost

Remark 1

(Why
s^{3/2}
rather than
s
)
.

For online SGD on a single-index target with
\mathrm{IE}=2
, the standard complexity is
\tilde{O}(s)
at learning rate
\eta\lesssim s^{-1}

(
Ben Arous et al. 2021
)
.
The difficulty stems from the heavy-tailed structure of
\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\perp}Z^{t}
. When
x^{t}\sim\mu_{k}
for
k\neq\kappa
, the sample falls outside
V_{\kappa}
and causes an
O(1)
perturbation, even though this event has probability
1-\lambda_{\kappa}=O(s^{-1/2})
.
Concentration inequalities control sums of martingale differences only through their per-step maximum, so the rare
O(1)
jumps dominate and the resulting bound is too loose.
Doob’s maximal inequality, applied to a supermartingale in
24
, instead exploits the low probability of the large-increment event and yields a tight bound.
Since Doob’s inequality bounds the running maximum of each martingale by its cumulative variance, the bound becomes vacuous if the interval is too long. The horizon is therefore partitioned into
\tilde{O}(s^{1/2})
sub-intervals and a union bound is taken over all of them.
This forces
\eta\lesssim s^{-3/2}
rather than
s^{-1}
, yielding
\tilde{O}(s^{3/2})
steps.

SFT forgetting

While
2
shows that W2S preserves off-target features, we show that SFT does not.
This is consistent with empirical observations of catastrophic forgetting in large language models during fine-tuning
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Kotha et al. 2024
;
Luo et al. 2025
)
.
In our setting, SFT on task
\kappa
applies online SGD to the same strong model (
Eq.

4
) with isotropic inputs
x^{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and direct teacher labels
y^{t}=r^{*}_{\kappa}(\theta_{\kappa}^{\top}x^{t})+\zeta^{t}
as in
Ben Arous et al. 2021
.
The first-layer update is

\displaystyle w_{k,n}^{t+1}\leftarrow w_{k,n}^{t}+\eta^{t}\,y^{t}\,\tilde{\nabla}_{w_{k,n}}r_{\Theta}(x^{t}),\quad w_{k,n}^{t+1}\leftarrow w_{k,n}^{t+1}/\|w_{k,n}^{t+1}\|.

(11)

2
confirms that neurons initialized toward
\theta_{k}
are driven to align with
\theta_{\kappa}
whenever
\theta_{k}^{\top}\theta_{\kappa}
is non-negligible, so the pre-trained feature
\theta_{k}
is lost before task
\kappa

Theorem 2

(SFT forgetting; informal)
.

Assume

and
5
.
Let
t_{1,\kappa}
be the first time at which
\theta_{\kappa}^{\top}w^{t}_{\kappa,n}\geq o(1)
for some

Under
Algorithm

\theta_{\kappa}^{\top}w^{t}_{k,n}\geq 1-o(1)
for every neuron

satisfying the sign condition, all

k\neq\kappa

6

Appendix

F

The off-target neurons thus reach
\theta_{\kappa}
no later than the task-
\kappa

\theta_{k}^{\top}w^{t}_{k,n}
with
\theta_{k}
is lost once task
\kappa
is learned.
Under the random initialization in

Proof idea.

The base online SGD analysis follows the same framework as
1
and
1
and prior work
(
Ben Arous et al. 2021
;
Damian et al. 2024a
;
Lee et al. 2024
;
Oko et al. 2024a
)
.
Under isotropic sampling the gradient propagates to every neuron, and the Hermite expansion of the direct teacher has
\mathop{\mathrm{IE}}(\sigma^{*}_{\kappa})=p

\theta_{\kappa}^{\top}w^{t}_{k,n}
scales as
\chi_{k}(\theta_{\kappa}^{\top}w^{t}_{k,n})^{p-1}

By

\theta_{\kappa}^{\top}w^{0}_{k,n}

A Bihari–LaSalle ODE comparison (formalized in

\theta_{k}^{\top}w^{t}_{k,n}
with the pre-trained feature is lost.

W2S versus SFT.

Our results establish that W2S improves over SFT in both sample complexity and feature preservation.

1
,
2
and
2
share the same model architecture, so the difference between W2S and standard SFT is purely algorithmic.
Unlike SFT, which trains on a fixed labeled dataset, W2S treats the weak model as an oracle and queries it on inputs drawn from the strong model’s generative distribution
\mu
.
W2S further applies a nonlinear transformation to the weak model output, achieving feature elicitation with
\tilde{O}(s^{3/2})
samples, whereas SFT under isotropic sampling requires
\tilde{O}(d^{p-1})
samples
(
Ben Arous et al. 2021
)
.
Under the intended setting where task-
k
neurons satisfy
\theta_{k}^{\top}w^{0}_{k,n}\approx 1
,
6
reduces to
\theta_{k}^{\top}\theta_{\kappa}\gtrsim s^{-1/2}
, meaning forgetting occurs when tasks
k
and
\kappa
are more similar than two random directions in the
s

Even in this setting,
2
guarantees preservation of
\theta_{k}
, since
\|\Sigma_{\kappa}w^{0}_{k,n}\|^{2}\gtrsim(\theta_{\kappa}^{\top}\theta_{k})^{2}\gtrsim s^{-1}
, in contrast to SFT, under which the alignment
\theta_{k}^{\top}w^{t}_{k,n}
with the pre-trained feature is lost.

Remark
.

W2S succeeds in a setting where SFT cannot, even when SFT applies the same nonlinear teacher transformation as
Algorithm

1

Since SFT trains on a fixed labeled dataset without a weak model, it draws inputs isotropically from
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
; any such algorithm requires
\tilde{\Omega}(d)
samples to learn a feature
(
Dudeja and Hsu 2024
;
Damian et al. 2024b
)

When
s<d^{2/3}
, the
\tilde{O}(s^{3/2})
complexity of
Algorithm

1
is below
d
, so W2S learns the feature from fewer samples than SFT can, regardless of its teacher transformation.

6
Discussion

Limitations.

Second-layer learning for the strong model is not formally analyzed.
Our results characterize W2S generalization in terms of feature alignment rather than function approximation, and whether second-layer learning preserves reward quality for off-target tasks remains open. This limitation is shared with
Moniri and Hassani 2025
, who similarly establishes preservation at the feature level.

2
is algorithm-specific rather than an information-theoretic lower bound, so forgetting can likely be avoided by other algorithms with explicit regularization.
The generative-exponent reduction requires
\sigma^{*}_{\kappa}
to be even, which excludes odd link functions.

Future work.

Extending second-layer learning to preserve off-target features remains open, with two obstacles.
Following
Oko et al. 2024a
, the second layer can be learned by ridge regression, but since
r^{w}\approx r^{*}_{\kappa}
, the supervision provides no signal for off-target neurons, driving
a_{k,n}\to 0
for
k\neq\kappa
.
Moreover, standard practice resets the bias parameters
b_{k,n}
before second-layer training, destroying
r_{k}
preserved by first-layer W2S training.
A penalty on the deviation of second-layer parameters from their initialization may resolve both obstacles, analogous to the KL regularization used in LLM alignment.
The analysis of deeper architectures such as Transformers is also a natural future direction.

Linear representation hypothesis.

The linear representation hypothesis posits that high-level concepts are encoded as linear subspaces in the representation space of neural networks
(
Mikolov et al. 2013
;
Arora et al. 2016
;
Elhage et al. 2022
;
Park et al. 2024

This structure has been observed in Transformer LLMs across diverse domains, including relational knowledge
(

Hernandez et al. 2024

and spatial and temporal concepts
(

A related body of work shows that these representations can be linearly probed and used to steer model behavior
(

Turner et al. 2023
)

At scale,
Beaglehole et al. 2026
extract linear subspace representations for over 500 concepts in large-scale AI models, providing direct evidence for our assumption that the strong model’s pre-training knowledge is organized into task subspaces

Training Algorithm

Algorithm

2
describes the weak model training procedure.
It follows the layer-wise training paradigm commonly used in feature learning theory
(
Damian et al. 2022
;
Ba et al. 2022
;
Bietti et al. 2022
;
Abbe et al. 2023
;
Mousavi-Hosseini et al. 2023
)
.

In Phase I, the first-layer weights
w_{n}
are trained by online SGD to minimize the correlation loss
\mathcal{L}=-yr^{w}_{\Theta}(x)

(
Oko et al. 2024a
;
Lee et al. 2024
)
, which matches the behavior of squared loss when the learning rate is sufficiently small
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
.
The update uses the spherical gradient
\tilde{\nabla}_{w}r(x)=(I-ww^{\top})\nabla_{w}r(x)
, where
\nabla_{w}
denotes the Euclidean gradient.

After Phase I, sufficiently many neurons satisfy
|\theta^{\top}_{\kappa}w^{T_{1}}_{n}|\geq 1-\tilde{\varepsilon}_{w}
, but some do not.
Phase II filters these out by computing the empirical covariance matrix of
(\hat{w}_{n})_{n}
, extracting its leading eigenvector
\hat{\theta}
, and retaining only neurons with
|\hat{\theta}^{\top}\hat{w}_{n}|\geq 1-2\tilde{\varepsilon}_{w}
, which guarantees all remaining neurons satisfy
2
(i).
The biases
b_{n}
are then re-randomized before Phase III, in which the second-layer coefficients
a_{n}
are learned by
\ell^{2}
-regularized least squares regression.

Input:

Initialized weak model
r^{w}_{\Theta}
with
\Theta=(a_{n},b_{n},w_{n}^{0})_{n=1}^{\tilde{N}^{w}}
; learning rate schedule
\eta^{t}
; regularization parameter

; sample sizes

; bias scale

; filtering threshold

.

Phase I: first-layer training

for

do

Draw

,

.

w_{n}^{t+1}\leftarrow w_{n}^{t}+\eta^{t}y^{t}\tilde{\nabla}_{w}r^{w}_{(a_{n},b_{n},w_{n}^{t})_{n=1}^{\tilde{N}^{w}}}(x^{t})

w_{n}^{t+1}\leftarrow w_{n}^{t+1}/\|w_{n}^{t+1}\|,\quad(n=1,2,\dots,\tilde{N}^{w})

\hat{w}_{n}\leftarrow w_{n}^{T_{1}}

Phase II: neuron filtering

W_{\tilde{N}^{w}}\leftarrow\frac{1}{\tilde{N}^{w}}\sum_{n=1}^{\tilde{N}^{w}}\hat{w}_{n}\hat{w}_{n}^{\top}

\hat{\theta}\leftarrow
leading unit eigenvector of

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{i!}\mathbb{E}[(\bar{r}^{*}(z))^{i+1}\mathrm{He}_{p}(z)]\right|\leq\frac{1}{i!}\mathbb{E}[\bar{r}^{*}(z)^{2(i+1)}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{He}_{p}(z)^{2}]^{1/2}\lesssim\frac{1}{i!}(\log d)^{-(i+1)},

(116)

giving

.
Combining,
I_{1}=\Theta((\log d)^{-C_{\rho}e_{p}})
dominates
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
, giving
r_{p}=\Theta((\log d)^{-C_{\rho}e_{p}})
.

We now treat the case
\mathrm{ge}(r^{*})>p\geq 1

Write

\displaystyle r_{p}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\bar{r}^{*}(z)\exp(\bar{r}^{*}(z))\mathrm{He}_{p}(z)]

(117)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\rho^{-1}r^{*}(z)\exp(\rho^{-1}r^{*}(z))\mathrm{He}_{p}(z)]

(118)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\rho^{-1}r^{*}(z)\exp(\rho^{-1}r^{*}(z))-\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(r^{*}(z))(\log d)^{-1}\exp((\log d)^{-1})\right)\mathrm{He}_{p}(z)\mathbf{1}(|r^{*}(z)|\geq\rho/\log d)\right].

(119)

Since

,
\mathbb{E}[\rho^{-1}r^{*}(z)\exp(\rho^{-1}r^{*}(z))\mathrm{He}_{p}(z)]=0

17
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\rho^{-1}r^{*}(z)\exp(\rho^{-1}r^{*}(z))\mathrm{He}_{p}(z)\mathbf{1}(|r^{*}(z)|\geq\rho/\log d)|\right]

(120)

(121)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(r^{*}(z))(\log d)^{-1}\exp((\log d)^{-1})\mathrm{He}_{p}(z)\mathbf{1}(|r^{*}(z)|\geq\rho/\log d)|\right]

(122)

\displaystyle\leq(\log d)^{-1}e^{(\log d)^{-1}}\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{He}_{p}(z)|^{2}]^{1/2}\mathbb{P}[|r^{*}(z)|\geq\rho/\log d]^{1/2}=O(d^{-C}).

(123)

Therefore
r_{p}=O(d^{-C})
.
∎

Lemma 19

E.3
Error of the Teacher Signal in the Subspace

Lemma 21
.

Let
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
.
Let
\sigma_{n}
be a degree-
q
polynomial with uniformly bounded coefficients over

, and let

\theta\in S^{d-1}

be a subspace, and let

,
P_{V}^{\perp}
denote the orthogonal projections onto
V
and its complement respectively.

L^{1}
norm of

restricted to
V

(i)

If
\theta\in V
, then
\mathbb{E}_{x}[|f(P_{V}x)|]\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}[|f(x)|]+\tilde{O}(N^{-1}\|a\|_{1}\tilde{\varepsilon})
.

(ii)

If
\theta\perp V
, then
\mathbb{E}[|f(P_{V}x)|]=\tilde{O}(N^{-1}\|a\|_{1}+\tilde{\varepsilon}).

Proof.

Set
u_{n}=w_{n}-(w_{n}^{\top}\theta)\theta
, so that

\displaystyle\|u_{n}\|=\sqrt{\|w_{n}-(w_{n}^{\top}\theta)\theta\|^{2}}=\sqrt{1-(w_{n}^{\top}\theta)^{2}}\leq\sqrt{2\tilde{\varepsilon}}.

(124)

For
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, write
x_{1}=P_{V}x
and
x_{2}=P_{V}^{\perp}x
; these are independent.

Proof of (i).

Set
\delta_{n}=w_{n}^{\top}P_{V}^{\perp}x
and expand
\sigma_{n}(w_{n}^{\top}x+b_{n})
in a Taylor series around
w_{n}^{\top}P_{V}x+b_{n}
:

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\sigma(w_{n}^{\top}P_{V}x+b_{n}+\delta_{n})

(125)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}\sigma(w_{n}^{\top}P_{V}x+b_{n})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{i!}a_{n}(\delta_{n})^{i}\sigma_{n}^{(i)}(w_{n}^{\top}P_{V}x+b_{n})

(126)

\displaystyle=f(P_{V}x)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{i!}a_{n}(\delta_{n})^{i}\sigma_{n}^{(i)}(w_{n}^{\top}P_{V}x+b_{n})

(127)

Since
\mathbb{E}_{P_{V}^{\perp}x}[\delta_{n}]=0
and
\frac{(P_{V}^{\perp}u_{n})^{\top}}{\|P_{V}^{\perp}u_{n}\|}x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, we have by (
124
)

\displaystyle|\delta_{n}|=|w_{n}^{\top}P_{V}^{\perp}x|=|u_{n}^{\top}P_{V}^{\perp}x|\leq\|u_{n}\|\left|\frac{(P_{V}^{\perp}u_{n})^{\top}}{\|P_{V}^{\perp}u_{n}\|}x\right|=\tilde{O}(\tilde{\varepsilon}^{1/2})

(128)

with high probability.
Taking the expectation of (
125
) over
x_{2}=P_{V}^{\perp}x
,

\displaystyle|f(x_{1})|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{x_{2}}[f(x_{1}+x_{2})]-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{i!}a_{n}\mathbb{E}_{x_{2}}[(\delta_{n})^{i}]\sigma_{n}^{(i)}(w_{n}^{\top}x_{1}+b_{n})\right|

(129)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x_{2}}[|f(x_{1}+x_{2})|]+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=2}^{q}\frac{1}{i!}\left|a_{n}\mathbb{E}_{x_{2}}[(\delta_{n})^{i}]\sigma_{n}^{(i)}(w_{n}^{\top}x_{1}+b_{n})\right|

(130)

Taking the expectation over
x_{1}=P_{V}x
as well, and using the high-probability bound
|\delta_{n}|^{i}=\tilde{O}(\tilde{\varepsilon}^{1/2})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},x_{2}}[|f(x_{1})|]\leq\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},x_{2}}[|f(x_{1}+x_{2})|]+\tilde{O}(N^{-1}\|a\|_{1}\tilde{\varepsilon}).

(131)

Proof of (ii).

Since
\theta\perp V
, we have
w_{n}^{\top}P_{V}x+b_{n}=u_{n}^{\top}P_{V}x+b_{n}
, so

\displaystyle f(P_{V}x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i=0}^{q}\frac{1}{i!}a_{n}(u_{n}^{\top}P_{V}x)^{i}\sigma_{n}^{(i)}(b_{n}).

(132)

By the same argument as in (i),
\mathbb{E}_{x}[u_{n}^{\top}P_{V}x]=0
and
\mathbb{E}_{x}[|u_{n}^{\top}P_{V}x|^{i}]=\tilde{O}(\tilde{\varepsilon}^{1/2})
for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[|f(P_{V}x)|]\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}|a_{n}\sigma_{n}(b_{n})|+\tilde{O}(\tilde{\varepsilon})=\tilde{O}(N^{-1}\|a\|_{1}+\tilde{\varepsilon}).

(133)

t_{1,\kappa}\geq t_{1,k}+t_{2,k}
, it follows that
\theta^{\top}_{\kappa}w^{t}_{k}>1-c_{1}
for every
t\geq t_{1,\kappa}
.
∎

Lemma 38

(Bihari–LaSalle and Gronwall inequalities; cf.
Oko et al. 2024a
;
Ben Arous et al. 2021
)
.

Let
p\geq 3
,
c>0
, and let
(a^{t})_{t=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence of positive reals satisfying
a^{t+1}=a^{t}+c(a^{t})^{p-1}
. Then

\displaystyle a^{t}\leq\frac{a^{0}}{\bigl(1-c(p-2)(a^{0})^{p-2}t\bigr)^{\frac{1}{p-2}}}.

(324)

Moreover, if
a^{t}\leq 1
for all
t\leq T-1
, then

\displaystyle a^{t}\geq\frac{a^{0}}{\bigl(1-c(1+c)^{-(p-1)}(p-2)(a^{0})^{p-2}t\bigr)^{\frac{1}{p-2}}}.

(325)

Proof.

Since the integrand
x^{-(p-1)}
is decreasing,

\displaystyle c=\frac{a^{t+1}-a^{t}}{(a^{t})^{p-1}}\geq\int_{a^{t}}^{a^{t+1}}\frac{dx}{x^{p-1}}=\frac{1}{p-2}\left[\frac{1}{(a^{t})^{p-2}}-\frac{1}{(a^{t+1})^{p-2}}\right].

(326)

Summing from
0
to
t-1
gives

\displaystyle c(p-2)t\geq(a^{0})^{-(p-2)}-(a^{t})^{-(p-2)},

(327)

and rearranging yields the upper bound

\displaystyle a^{t}\leq\frac{1}{\bigl((a^{0})^{-(p-2)}-c(p-2)t\bigr)^{\frac{1}{p-2}}}=\frac{a^{0}}{\bigl(1-c(p-2)(a^{0})^{p-2}t\bigr)^{\frac{1}{p-2}}}.

(328)

For the lower bound, note that
a^{t}\leq 1
implies
a^{t+1}\leq(1+c)a^{t}
, so

\displaystyle c=\frac{a^{t+1}-a^{t}}{(a^{t})^{p-1}}=\int_{a^{t}}^{a^{t+1}}\frac{dx}{(a^{t})^{p-1}}\leq\int_{a^{t}}^{a^{t+1}}\frac{(1+c)^{p-1}}{(a^{t+1})^{p-1}}dx\leq\frac{(1+c)^{p-1}}{p-2}\left[\frac{1}{(a^{t})^{p-2}}-\frac{1}{(a^{t+1})^{p-2}}\right].

(329)

Summing and rearranging gives

\displaystyle a^{t}\geq\frac{a^{0}}{\bigl(1-c(1+c)^{-(p-1)}(p-2)(a^{0})^{p-2}t\bigr)^{\frac{1}{p-2}}}.

(330)

∎

Lemma 39

(Matrix Bernstein inequality
(
Vershynin 2018
)
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be independent, mean-zero, symmetric
d\times d
random matrices with
\|X_{i}\|\leq K
almost surely for all
i
. Then for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right\|\geq t\right]\leq 2d\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+Kt/3}\right),

(331)

where
\sigma^{2}=\bigl\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]\bigr\|
is the operator norm of the variance of the sum.
