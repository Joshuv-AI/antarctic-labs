Ultrasound Features and Clinical Pathological Characteristics of Acquired Periungual Fibrokeratoma: Two Case Reports
Authors: Dandan He, Hailiang Tao, Hongsheng Xia, Zehu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcquired periungual fibrokeratoma (APF) is a rare benign neoplasm that can cause longitudinal nail plate malformation. Although the significance of high-frequency ultrasound (HFUS) in the assessment of nail unit lesions has been well - established, its sonographic characteristics remain insufficiently documented. To describe the sonographic characteristics of acquired periungual fibrokeratoma in two cases, offering early observations to inform future studies.MethodsTwo patients with histopathologically confirmed acquired periungual fibrokeratoma were examined using high-frequency ultrasound (HFUS), and the sonographic features were correlated with clinical and histopathological findings.ResultsHigh-frequency ultrasound (HFUS) accurately delineated the size, location, and morphology of acquired periungual fibrokeratoma as well-defined, hypoechoic, nail matrix-based nodules with minimal internal vascularity. Introduction

Acquired digital fibrokeratoma (ADF) was first reported by Burt et al1 in 1968. It is a rare, benign fibrous neoplasm that predominantly occurs in the acral regions, with a predilection for the fingers and toes. When the lesion arises in the periungual or subungual tissues, it is referred to as acquired periungual fibrokeratoma (APF) or acquired subungual fibrokeratoma (ASF).2 APF typically manifests as a solitary, skin - colored or hyperkeratotic, firm nodule with a slow, progressive growth pattern and no tendency for spontaneous regression. Histopathologically, the tumor is characterized by a dense proliferation of collagenous fibrous tissue accompanied by overlying acanthosis and marked hyperkeratosis, which accounts for its hard, horny apex.

Although the lesion is biologically benign, its close anatomical relationship with the nail matrix and nail bed often results in nail plate deformities, including longitudinal grooving, onycholysis, or fissuring, leading to functional and aesthetic problems.3 Clinically, APF poses a diagnostic challenge as its appearance frequently overlaps with that of other subungual or periungual masses, such as subungual glomus tumor, subungual exostosis, common wart, and myxoid cyst. Therefore, an accurate diagnosis based solely on physical examination can be unreliable, emphasizing the need for reliable imaging.

HFUS has become a valuable non - invasive method for evaluating the nail unit, as it can delineate the delicate anatomical layers and precisely determine the location, size, and internal structure of small space - occupying lesions, thus providing crucial information for preoperative planning.

A review of previous case studies indicates that, to date, an examination of the existing case reports on acquired periungual fibrokeratoma reveals that the literature has mainly focused on clinical manifestations, etiology, and histopathological features, while the sonographic characteristics have rarely been documented. This significant lack of imaging data limits the role of high-frequency ultrasound as a diagnostic tool for this entity.

This report describes the sonographic characteristics in two patients with histopathologically confirmed APF, with the aim of providing early, hypothesis - generating observations that may inform future studies. No attempt is made to define diagnostic criteria or to assess accuracy; the findings are presented solely as a clinical - imaging correlation in two cases.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This is a retrospective case series study conducted at Hangzhou Third People’s Hospital between July and August, 2025. Two patients with histopathologically confirmed acquired periungual fibrokeratoma were included.

Patient Selection

Patients presenting with a periungual or subungual nodule that was subsequently diagnosed as acquired digital fibrokeratoma by post-operative pathology were included. The extracted clinical data comprised age, sex, symptom duration, history of trauma, and any relevant past medical history.

Imaging Protocol

All patients underwent pre-operative HFUS examination. The examinations were performed using two ultrasound system according to the clinical workflow: a Siemens Medical Solutions USA system equipped with an 18H6 linear array probe (frequency range: 6–18 MHz) and an Esaote MyLab ClassC system (Italy) with an SL3116 linear array probe (frequency range: 10–22 MHz).

To optimize near-field resolution, a generous amount of coupling gel was used as a stand-off pad. Gray-scale and color Doppler images were acquired in both longitudinal and transverse planes. For each lesion, the following characteristics were systematically recorded: maximum diameter, location relative to the nail matrix, shape, margin, internal echogenicity (homogeneous or heterogeneous), presence of posterior acoustic enhancement, and relationship with the nail plate and underlying phalanx. Vascularity was assessed by color Doppler imaging and classified as hypovascular, moderately vascular, or hypervascular.

Pathological Examination

Surgical excision was performed in all cases. The excised specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histopathological diagnosis was made by an experienced pathologist based on the presence of marked hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and a dermal proliferation of thick collagen bundles and fibroblasts.

Ethical Approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study of this case was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Medical Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Third People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2026KA045). According to the policy of this institution, separate institutional approval for publishing the case details is not required. Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient (or legally authorized representative).

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 52-year-old female presented with a painless nodule and concave deformity of the right great toenail that had persisted for over 2 years. She had a history of vitiligo (over 2 years) and onychomycosis (over 8 months), but no history of trauma. Physical examination revealed a flesh-colored, rough-surfaced nodule (approximately 5.0×5.0 mm) located between the proximal nail fold and the dorsal nail plate, with longitudinal grooving of the nail plate (Figure 1A).

HFUS detected a well-defined, heterogeneous, hypoechoic nodule in the dermis beneath the proximal nail fold, measuring approximately 9.8 mm×5.3 mm × 7.9 mm. The lesion was “gourd”-shaped with a wide base, situated in the nail matrix region. It protruded from the proximal nail fold and compressed the nail plate, without posterior acoustic enhancement. Color Doppler imaging showed minimal intralesional blood flow. The underlying distal phalangeal cortex was smooth and intact (Figure 1B).

The lesion was completely excised surgically. Intraoperatively, the tumor’s broad base was confirmed to overly the nail matrix and without infiltration, consistent with the pre-operative ultrasound assessment. Grossly, the excised specimen measured approximately 10.0 mm×6.5 mm×8.0 mm, closely matching the sonographic dimensions (error <5%).

Histopathological examination revealed epidermal hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and dermal proliferation of collagen fibers and fibroblasts, consistent with acquired Periungual fibrokeratoma (Figure 1C).

Case 2

A 6-year-old male presented with a 2–3 month history of left great toenail deformity. The parents denied any prior trauma. On examination, a shallow longitudinal depression was visible on the nail plate, with a subtle, flesh-colored swelling (approximately 2 mm) partially visible under the proximal nail fold. There was no Periungual erythema, swelling, or ulceration (Figure 2A).

Ultrasound examination demonstrated an oval, well-defined, heterogeneous, hypoechoic nodule in the dermis beneath the proximal nail fold, measuring approximately 2.2 mm× 1.2 mm×2.0 mm. The base of the nodule was located in the nail matrix; the lesion lay mainly within the dermis and extended partially to the epidermis of the proximal nail fold, causing a shallow longitudinal concave deformity of the nail plate (Figure 2B). Color Doppler imaging revealed minimal internal vascularity. No abnormality of the distal phalanx was observed.

The lesion was surgically excised. Intraoperatively, the lesion location and size confirmed the ultrasound findings. Gross specimen measurement was 2.5 mm×1.5 mm× 2.0 mm, again within minimal deviation from sonographic measurements. Histopathological findings were consistent with acquired digital fibrokeratoma, showing features identical to Case 1 (Figure 2C).

Discussion

Acquired periungual fibrokeratoma (APF) is an uncommon, benign fibrous tumor that most frequently occurs on the fingers and toes but has also been reported on the palms, proximal nail folds, and dorsum of the hand; it is usually asymptomatic.3 Although the exact etiology remains unclear, chronic trauma or repeated mechanical stimulation has been suggested as a possible contributing factor.1,4 APF is typically solitary, presenting as a firm, skin-colored nodule generally less than 1 cm in diameter, though larger lesions exceeding 1 cm have been documented.5 Yasuki6 classified the disease into two major categories and five subtypes based on anatomical relationship and histological structure. HFUS features of APF has been scarcely described in the literature.HFUS in our two cases revealed well-defined, heterogeneous, hypoechoic dermal nodules with a broad base overlying the nail matrix and minimal internal vascularity. These sonographic findings correlated closely with the histopathological features: the heterogeneous hypoechogenicity reflected the haphazardly arranged dense collagen bundles and proliferating fibroblasts, while the well-defined margin corresponded to the non-infiltrative, expansile growth pattern of the tumor. The minimal color Doppler signal was consistent with the paucity of blood vessels within the fibrotic stroma. Moreover, in both cases the ultrasound measurements closely matched the gross specimen dimensions (error < 5%), confirming the accuracy of HFUS in delineating these superficial lesions. Although HFUS findings of APF have rarely been reported, a recent series by Sobrino García-Yanes et al7 described four histopathologically confirmed digital fibrokeratomas evaluated with 18–22 MHz probes. Their observation of well-defined lesions with minimal or absent internal Doppler flow is consistent with our two cases. Additionally, Tognetti et al8 reported the first high-resolution ultrasound of an ADFK, demonstrating a well-defined hyperechoic structure with a hyperkeratotic collarette. While our lesions appeared hypoechoic rather than hyperechoic—possibly due to differences in tumor composition or location—both studies confirm the utility of HFUS in delineating these benign fibrous tumors and excluding vascular-rich mimickers.

APF is typically more prevalent among middle - aged patients, and pediatric cases are exceedingly scarce. The youngest patient reported previously was 12 years old,9 while a more recent report documented a 6 - year - old child.10 Our Case 2 (a 6 - year - old) aligns with the youngest age reported in,10 validating that APF can manifest at this age, further indicating that APF can occur in both children and adults.

Notably, there were disparities in the clinical histories between our two cases. The adult patient (Case 1) had a longer disease duration (exceeding 2 years), with the nodule partially protruding from the proximal nail fold. In contrast, the pediatric patient (Case 2) had a relatively brief history (2–3 months), and the nodule slightly protruded from the proximal nail fold. This divergence may mirror differences in tumor growth rate or the timing of seeking medical consultation. Nevertheless, the core ultrasound characteristics and the compressive effect on the nail matrix were consistent between the two cases.

Notably, the study10 did not delineate the clinical differences between pediatric and adult cases. Our observation that the pediatric case had a shorter history and less protrusion provides novel insights.

The differential diagnosis of periungual nodules encompasses several entities that can mimic APF both clinically and on imaging, and HFUS offers unique value in distinguishing them:

Subungual glomus tumor: Typically presents as a painful subungual nodule with homogeneous hypoechogenicity and markedly rich internal vascularity on color Doppler (often described as a “fireball” sign), readily distinguishable from the minimal flow pattern of APF.

Subungual exostosis: Appears as a hyperechoic lesion with posterior acoustic shadowing, demonstrating continuity with the underlying bone cortex. This calcified nature contrasts sharply with the purely soft-tissue echotexture of APF.

Verruca vulgaris: Manifests as a hyperechoic epidermal thickening with prominent internal vascularity reflecting dilated capillaries in the elongated dermal papillae; these features differ from the predominantly dermal, hypovascular fibrotic nodule of APF.

Digital myxoid cyst: Presents as a well-demarcated, anechoic or mixed-echoic cystic lesion with posterior acoustic enhancement and no internal blood flow, clearly distinct from the solid, although minimally vascular of APF.

Koenen tumor (periungual fibroma in tuberous sclerosis complex): May share similar fibrous echotexture with APF but is frequently multiple and associated with other systemic manifestations of tuberous sclerosis. Tchernev et al11 recently described atypical broad-based periungual fibrokeratomas, highlighting the diagnostic challenge in atypical presentations.

The ability of imaging to non-invasively characterize these lesions has been increasingly recognized. Ankit Gupta et al12 emphasized the importance of differentiating APF from more aggressive conditions such as squamous cell carcinoma and pyogenic granuloma, and pointed to the value of imaging modalities in avoiding misdiagnosis. Our report adds sonographic evidence to this growing recognition by demonstrating a characteristic combination of morphology, echogenicity, and vascular pattern that can aid in narrowing the differential diagnosis.

Beyond the diagnostic function, HFUS offers crucial information for surgical planning. It clearly reveals the location of the nodule within the dermis beneath the proximal nail fold and precisely delineates its relationship with the nail matrix, nail bed, and underlying phalanx. This is of utmost significance because intraoperative damage to the nail matrix may lead to permanent nail dystrophy. In both of our cases, the bone cortex was intact, excluding deeper osseous involvement and guiding a more conservative surgical approach. This preoperative mapping is especially valuable in pediatric patients like Case 2, where minimizing tissue trauma is essential for preserving normal nail growth.

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size is small (n = 2), and both lesions were located on the great toe; the generalizability of these sonographic features to APFs arising at other anatomical sites remains to be validated. Second, the retrospective nature of this report precludes a prospective evaluation of the diagnostic accuracy of ultrasound. Third, while our observations suggest that APF can be sonographically differentiated from common mimickers, direct head-to-head sonographic comparison with other periungual tumors within the same cohort was not performed. Four,HFUS can accurately map the lesion and its compressive effect, it may have limitations in definitively excluding superficial matrix involvement in very small lesions.Larger prospective studies with histopathological confirmation are needed to establish reliable sonographic criteria for APF and to further define its place in the diagnostic algorithm of periungual soft-tissue lesions. Future investigations may also explore high-frequency transducers (>22 MHz) or novel imaging techniques such as line-field confocal optical coherence tomography (LC-OCT) for even finer delineation of nail unit pathology.

Conclusion

This report presents high-frequency ultrasound (HFUS) findings of two histopathologically confirmed cases of acquired periungual fibrokeratoma (APF) — each showing a well-defined, heterogeneous, hypoechoic dermal nodule with a broad base overlying the nail matrix and minimal internal vascularity. These sonographic features, though based on a limited number of cases, add to the sparse imaging literature on APF. In patients presenting with an unexplained longitudinal nail groove or a suspicious periungual nodule, HFUS may be considered as a complementary preoperative tool to differentiate APF from common mimickers (eg, glomus tumor, exostosis) and to map the nodule’s relationship with the nail matrix, thereby helping to avoid surgical injury. However, the conclusions are preliminary; larger prospective studies are needed to validate these sonographic criteria across different anatomical sites and to establish the diagnostic accuracy of HFUS for APF.
