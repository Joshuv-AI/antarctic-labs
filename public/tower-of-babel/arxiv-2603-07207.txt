Online Bidding for Contextual First-Price Auctions with Budgets under One-Sided Information Feedback
Zeng Fu † \text{Zeng Fu}^{{\dagger}}, Jiashuo Jiang ‡ \text{Jiashuo Jiang}^{{\ddagger}}, Yuan Zhou § ​ ¶ \text{Yuan Zhou}^{\lx@sectionsign\lx@paragraphsign}

Abstract
In this paper, we study the problem of learning to bid in repeated first-price auctions with budget constraints. In each period, the decision maker needs to submit a bid to win the auction and maximize the total collected reward, subject to a budget constraint throughout the horizon. We focus on the setting with one-sided information feedback where only the winning bid is revealed to the decision maker at each period. Different from previous papers that assume homogeneous competitors’ bids, we assume that the highest bid of other bidders depends on the context of the impression, which is initially unknown and needs to be learned over time. To tackle the learning difficulty, we propose a novel robust regression method based on conditional quantile invariance to learn the contextual parameter. Further combined with a dual update procedure, we develop a new bidding algorithm and prove that our algorithm achieves O ~ ​ ( T ) \widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T}) regret, which is order-optimal. We further extend our approach to the multi-dimensional setting and demonstrate the practical efficiency of our algorithm through numerical experiments.

Introduction

Driven by the explosive growth of e-commerce, digital advertising has become the dominant marketing force in the global economy, with US spending exceeding $129 billion in 2019. The engine generating this revenue is the online ad auction. While the truthful Second-Price (Vickrey) auction was historically the industry standard, the ecosystem has recently undergone a paradigm shift toward First-Price auctions. This transition, completed by major exchanges like AppNexus, OpenX, and Google Ad Manager by 2019, now accounts for the majority of the display ad market
(
8
)
.

This shift fundamentally alters bidder incentives. Unlike in second-price settings, truth-telling is no longer a dominant strategy; bidders must strategically “shade” their bids below private values to generate surplus. Furthermore, platforms like Google have introduced new transparency mechanisms, providing the “minimum-bid-to-win” only to the winner, or sometimes to all participants, creating a complex “one-sided feedback” environment. Despite this, existing research often relies on static competitor distributions, ignoring the rich contextual data (e.g., user demographics) that drives modern pricing. In this paper, we address the challenge of learning to bid in repeated first-price auctions with
budgets, contextual competitors, and unknown noise
.

1.1
Problem Formulation

We consider the online bidding problem for a bidder participating in a population of bidders during a time horizon
T
, with a total budget
B
. At the beginning of each time step
t=1,2,\dots,T
, the bidder observes an impression with context
x_{t}
, and receives a private value
v_{t}
based on the context. We assume
v_{t}
is an invertible function of
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
, specifically
v_{t}=f(x_{t})
. Based on past observations, the bidder submits a bid
b_{t}
for the impression. Let
d_{t}
be the highest bid of other bidders at time
t
.

If
b_{t}>d_{t}
, then the bidder wins the auction and pays
b_{t}
for the impression; if
b_{t}<d_{t}
, the bidder doesn’t get the impression and pays nothing. The reward of the bidder is:

r_{t}=(v_{t}-b_{t})\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b_{t}>d_{t}\}.

(1)

Feedback Model
. We investigate the setting of
one-sided information feedback
. In this setting, the bidder observes
d_{t}
only if
b_{t}<d_{t}
. Thus, the available feedback is the pair
(\mathbf{1}\{b_{t}>d_{t}\},\mathbf{1}\{b_{t}<d_{t}\}d_{t})
. This models the transparency mechanisms in modern exchanges and is significantly more challenging to learn from compared to full information feedback, which also has been commonly adopted in previous studies (e.g.
1
,
10
).

Competitor Model
. To model other bidders’ bids, we assume they are linearly related to the context
x_{t}
plus an error term
z_{t}
, where
z_{t}
is i.i.d. drawn from an unknown distribution
G
. To be specific, we have

d_{t}=\alpha x_{t}+z_{t},\quad z_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textit{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}G,

(2)

where both
G
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
are unknown to the bidder.

Due to the one-sided information feedback setting, the parameter
\alpha
is hard to estimate, necessitating a novel algorithm to achieve a sub-linear regret bound. Note that the competitor model distinguishes our work from the previous ones. To be specific, though the contextual setting has been studied in previous literature (e.g.
1
), they all make the limited assumption that the competitors’ bid is i.i.d. and independent of the impression context.

The expected reward at time
t
is:

\mathbb{E}_{v,d}\left[R_{t}(x_{t},v_{t},b_{t})\right]=(v_{t}-b_{t})G(b_{t}-\alpha x_{t}).

(3)

Optimization Goal
. Let
\Pi
denote the set of all budget-feasible strategies. The optimization problem is:

\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\quad

\displaystyle R(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{v,d}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_{t}(x_{t},v_{t},b_{t})\right]

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}b_{t}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b_{t}>d_{t}\}\leq B.

(4)

Define
\pi^{*}=\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi}R(\pi)
as our benchmark strategy. The regret is defined as:

\text{Regret}(\pi)=R(\pi^{*})-R(\pi).

(5)

Throughout the paper, we make the following assumptions:

{assumption}

[Superlinear Growth]

For all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
with
x_{1}>x_{2}
, the following inequality holds:

f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})\;>\;\alpha(x_{1}-x_{2}).

{assumption}

[Lipschitz Continuity]

The distribution function
G
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, and its density
g
satisfies
0<g_{\min}\leq g(z)\leq g_{\max}<\infty
for all
z
in the support.

{assumption}

[Boundedness]

Values, bids, and contexts are bounded:
v_{t}\in[0,\bar{v}]
,
b_{t}\in[0,\bar{v}]
,
x_{t}\in[0,\bar{x}]
almost surely.

{assumption}

[Identifiability]

The context distribution has non-zero variance:
\text{Var}(x_{t})\geq\sigma^{2}>0
, and the conditional quantile difference
|q_{1}(\alpha)-q_{2}(\alpha)|
is uniquely minimized at
\alpha=\alpha_{0}
with
\kappa
-strong convexity in a neighborhood.

These assumptions are standard in the literature. Assumption
1.1
ensures that the optimal bid increases with the impression context. Assumption
1.1
guarantees that the noise distribution
G
is sufficiently smooth and has bounded density. Assumption
1.1
ensures all relevant quantities lie in a compact set, which is typical for all online auction settings and necessary for obtaining meaningful regret bounds. Assumption
1.1
ensures the parameter
\alpha
can be consistently estimated from the data, requiring enough variability in the contexts.

1.2
Our Main Results and Contributions

Our main contribution is a novel bidding algorithm for the contextual first-price auction problem under budget constraints (Algorithm
2
), which achieves a regret bound of
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
(Theorem
4.1
). This result advances the existing literature in several directions:

•

Contextual Bidding with Budgets:
Prior work by
10
attains a
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret bound with one-sided feedback but lacks budget constraints and contextual information. Similarly,
22
achieved
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret with budgets but assumed no contextual information. Our formulation captures the realistic heterogeneity of modern ad markets where competition varies with observable contexts.

•

Removing Distributional Assumptions:
While
1
studied contextual first-price auctions, achieving
O(\sqrt{dT})
regret, their analysis assumes the noise distribution
G
is known to the bidder. We remove this impractical assumption, operating in a setting where both
\alpha
and
G
are unknown.

•

Novel Estimation Technique:
The introduction of contextual pricing with one-sided feedback presents a significant challenge: we must learn the parameters without observing losing bids. We overcome this by developing a robust regression method based on conditional quantile invariance constraints (Algorithm
1
). We prove that the estimation error of this method is upper bounded by
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
, matching the optimal rate. The novel estimation technique developed in our paper is of independent interest and enjoys the potential to be applied to other problems.

In summary, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to study repeated first-price auctions that simultaneously incorporate budget constraints, contextual competitors, and one-sided information feedback. This unique intersection creates a realistic but challenging environment where standard estimators fail due to the complex nature of censored, context-dependent data. To address this, we introduce a novel robust regression algorithm based on conditional quantile invariance constraints. This methodological innovation allows us to reconstruct competitor behavior without knowing the underlying noise distribution, enabling us to achieve an optimal
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret bound in a setting where prior approaches are inapplicable.

1.3
Other Related Work

The Shift to First-Price Auctions and Limitations of Classical Theory

With the industry-wide transition away from second-price auctions, the property of truthfulness no longer holds, significantly complicating bidding strategies. While the existing literature has explored these dynamics, it falls short in addressing the realities of modern online advertising. The classical game-theoretic approach relies on a Bayesian setup, assuming bidders can model competitors’ private values as probability distributions to derive Nash equilibria
(
25
,
16
,
17
,
26
)
. Despite its theoretical elegance, this approach is ill-suited for online ad auctions for two reasons. First, bidders lack the information necessary to model competitors’ private values; indeed, accurately estimating even one’s own value is difficult. Second, classical theory focuses on one-shot bidding, whereas online display ads function as repeated auctions where past information should inform future strategy.

The Emergence of Online Learning and Existing Gaps
.
To address these shortcomings, an online decision-making framework has emerged that allows for adaptive strategies without modeling competitor values. However, this literature has predominantly focused on second-price auctions, emphasizing seller-side floor price optimization
(
15
,
4
,
19
,
27
)
or buyer-side bidding with imperfect value knowledge
(
14
,
24
)
. While
3
addressed budget constraints in repeated second-price auctions via bid shading and dual-gradient descent, a principled framework for adaptive bidding in repeated first-price auctions remains largely unexplored.

State of the Art: Unconstrained First-Price Bidding
.
Recent work on repeated first-price auctions has focused on surplus maximization without budget constraints, categorized by the level of feedback provided by the exchange:

•

Binary Feedback:
2
showed that when the highest competing bid
m_{t}
is i.i.d., one achieves a minimax optimal regret of
\tilde{\Theta}(T^{\frac{2}{3}})
, while an adversarial
m_{t}
yields
\tilde{\Theta}(T^{\frac{3}{4}})
.

•

Winning-Bid-Only Feedback:
11
established a regret of
\tilde{\Theta}(T^{\frac{1}{2}})
against i.i.d. competitors.

•

Full-Information Feedback:
12
demonstrated that
\tilde{\Theta}(T^{\frac{1}{2}})
is achievable even against adversarial competitors.

The Challenge of Budget Constraints
.
In practice, advertisers entrust demand-side platforms with fixed budgets, transforming the problem from pure surplus maximization to a constrained optimization challenge. A bidder must be prudent, avoiding auctions with low surplus that consume a disproportionate amount of the budget. While equilibrium characterizations for budget-constrained first-price auctions exist in game theory literature (
5
,
6
,
13
,
2
,
23
), the fundamental learning-theoretical question remains: Can a bidder learn to adaptively bid in repeated first-price auctions with budget constraints? We answer this affirmatively, even under one-sided information feedback.

Broader Applications
.
The theory of online bidding extends well beyond advertising. Similar dynamic pricing and bidding challenges appear in cloud storage markets
(
7
)
, natural gas management
(
20
)
, and online cultural markets
(
21
)
. Most notably, the electricity market has seen significant application, from Segmented Pay-as-Clear markets
(
9
)
to strategic bidding for profit maximization
(
18
)
, underscoring the universality of these adaptive decision-making problems.

Robust Regression

2.1
Problem Formulation and Challenges

The core statistical challenge in our contextual first-price auction setting is estimating the linear parameter
\alpha
from censored observations. Specifically, we observe pairs
\{(x_{t},d_{t}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b_{t}<d_{t}\})\}_{t=1}^{n}
, where
d_{t}=\alpha x_{t}+z_{t}
with
z_{t}\sim G
being i.i.d. noise. The censoring indicator
\mathbf{1}\{b_{t}<d_{t}\}
depends on our bidding policy
b_{t}
, which in turn depends on the context
x_{t}
. This creates a bid-dependent censoring mechanism where we only observe
d_{t}
when we lose the auction (
b_{t}<d_{t}
).

Traditional regression methods (e.g., ordinary least squares) fail in this setting because:

1.

The censoring is not random but depends on our adaptive bidding decisions.

2.

When
b_{t}\geq d_{t}
, we observe only that
d_{t}\leq b_{t}
, not the actual value of
d_{t}
.

2.2
Quantile-based Estimator: Intuition

Our approach exploits the following key insight: although the full distribution of
d_{t}|x_{t}
is affected by censoring, certain conditional quantiles remain identifiable under appropriate assumptions. Specifically, we leverage the fact that if we split the samples into two groups based on
x_{t}
, the difference in conditional quantiles of the residuals can identify
\alpha
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
be a fixed quantile level. We select
p
sufficiently high such that all censored observations (
d_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b_{i}<d_{i}\}=0
) lie strictly to the left of the p-quantile in the residual distribution.

For any candidate
\alpha
, define the residuals:

R_{i}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}d_{i}\mathbf{1}_{\{b_{i}<d_{i}\}}-\alpha x_{i},&\mathbf{1}_{\{b_{i}<d_{i}\}}\neq 0,\\
-\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(6)

The censored observations (
d_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b_{i}<d_{i}\}=0
) are treated as
-\infty
so they do not affect the quantile calculations, as they represent left-censored data points. Then the algorithm selects
\hat{\alpha}=\arg\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}Q(\alpha)
, the objective function
Q(\alpha)=|q_{1}(\alpha)-q_{2}(\alpha)|
compares only the p-quantiles from the two groups, which are computed exclusively from uncensored observations. This makes our estimator robust to the bid-dependent censoring pattern, as the censored points do not directly influence the quantile comparison that identifies
\alpha
.

Algorithm 1

Quantile-Based Estimator for
\alpha

1.

Input:

n

S_{i}:=\{x_{i},d_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b_{i}<d_{i}\}\}

2.

Split samples by median of
x_{i}
:
I_{1}=\{i:x_{i}\leq\mathrm{median}(x)\}
,
I_{2}=\{i:x_{i}>\mathrm{median}(x)\}

3.

For each
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
:

(a)

Define residuals:

R_{i}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}d_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b_{i}<d_{i}\}-\alpha x_{i}&\text{if }d_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b_{i}<d_{i}\}\neq 0\\
-\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(b)

Compute sample
p
-quantiles:

q_{k}(\alpha)=\inf\{y:\frac{1}{|I_{k}|}\sum_{i\in I_{k}}\mathbf{1}\{R_{i}(\alpha)\leq y\}\geq p\}
for

Q(\alpha)=|q_{1}(\alpha)-q_{2}(\alpha)|

We establish the following theoretical guarantees for Algorithm
1
:

Lemma 2.1

(Uniform Concentration of Sample Quantiles)

Under Assumption
1.1
(Lipschitz continuity of
G
) and Assumption
1.1
(identifiability), for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\sup_{\alpha}|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha)-q_{k}(\alpha)|\leq 2C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}},\quad k=1,2.

Lemma 2.2

(Estimation Error Bound)

Under the identification condition
\kappa=\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}>0
, let
\mathcal{E}
be the event in Lemma
2.1
. Then on
\mathcal{E}
, for
n
sufficiently large,

|\hat{\alpha}_{n}-\alpha_{0}|\leq\frac{4\epsilon_{n}}{\kappa},

where
\epsilon_{n}=4C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}}
.

Using the lemmas above we can reach the following theorem:

Theorem 2.3

(Main Estimation Result)

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
A,M,c,\kappa
such that

\mathbf{P}\left(|\hat{\alpha}_{n}-\alpha_{0}|>C\sqrt{\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}}\right)\leq\delta.

2.3
Proof Sketch

The proof of Theorem
2.3
involves four key steps:

1.

Grid approximation
: Construct a finite grid
\mathcal{G}=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\}\subset[-A,A]
with spacing
\eta
.

2.

Concentration on the grid
: Apply the Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz inequality to bound the deviation between sample and population quantiles on the grid:

\mathbf{P}\Bigl(\max_{j,k}|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha_{j})-q_{k}(\alpha_{j})|>t\Bigr)

\qquad\leq 4N\exp(-2mc^{2}t^{2}),

where
m=|I_{k}|\approx n/2
.

3.

Extension to the continuum
: For any
\alpha\in[-A,A]
, choose
\alpha_{j}\in\mathcal{G}
with
|\alpha-\alpha_{j}|\leq\eta
. Using the Lipschitz properties of residuals and quantiles:

|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha)-q_{k}(\alpha)|\leq M\eta+t+L_{q}\eta.

Choosing
\eta=t/[2(M+L_{q})]
yields
|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha)-q_{k}(\alpha)|\leq 2t
.

4.

Identification and final bound
: Using the identification condition
\kappa>0
, we show that the minimizer
\hat{\alpha}_{n}
satisfies

|\hat{\alpha}_{n}-\alpha_{0}|\leq\frac{4\epsilon_{n}}{\kappa}=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}}\right).

The
\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
estimation error achieved by Algorithm
1
contributes directly to the overall
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret bound of our bidding algorithm.

3
Algorithm Design

We design an algorithm to minimize regret by considering the Lagrangian dual of the constrained optimization problem:

\max_{\pi}R(\pi)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}_{v,d}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{b_{t}^{\pi}\geq d_{t}\}b_{t}^{\pi}\right]\leq\rho.

(7)

The Lagrangian dual is:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(v_{t}-(1+\lambda)b_{t}^{\pi})G(b_{t}^{\pi}-\alpha x_{t})\right]+\lambda\rho\right).

(8)

From weak duality, it holds that

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}R(\pi)\leq\min_{\lambda\geq 0}T\Bigg(

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v}\Big[\max_{b}\big(v-(1+\lambda)b\big)

(9)

\displaystyle\times G(b-\alpha x)\Big]+\lambda\rho\Bigg).

Our formal algorithm is presented in Algorithm
2
. Our algorithm uses online gradient descent to approximate
\lambda
, at each time step, we update
\lambda_{t}
using the submitted bid
\tilde{c_{t}}(b)
and average budget
\rho
. We also update the remaining budget
B_{t}
in order to stop when the remaining budget is lower than
\bar{v}
.

On the other hand, the distribution
G
and parameter
\alpha
are unknown. We decompose the objective into two estimators:
c(v,b)=(v-b)G(b-\alpha x)
and
r(b,x)=bG(b-\alpha x)
. We estimate these quantities using the following approach:

1.

Initial exploration:
During the first
2\sqrt{T}
rounds, we set
b_{t}=0
to observe
d_{t}
and estimate
\hat{\alpha}_{0}
using ordinary least squares:

\hat{\alpha}_{0}=\dfrac{\sum_{i\in T_{0}}d_{i}x_{i}-\hat{d}_{i}\hat{x}_{i}}{\sum_{i\in T_{0}}(x_{i}^{2}-\overline{x}_{i}^{2})}.

(10)

2.

Phase-based learning:
We partition the remaining time horizon into
\frac{1}{2}\lceil\log_{2}T\rceil
phases:
A_{1},B_{1},A_{2},B_{2},\dots,A_{n},B_{n}
, where
n=\frac{1}{4}\log_{2}T
, with:

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle=[2^{i}\sqrt{T},2^{i-1}\cdot 3\cdot\sqrt{T}],

\displaystyle B_{i}

\displaystyle=[2^{i}\cdot 3\cdot\sqrt{T},2^{i+1}\sqrt{T}],\quad\text{if }i\neq n,

\displaystyle B_{i}

\displaystyle=[2^{i-1}\cdot 3\cdot\sqrt{T+1},T],\quad\text{if }i=n.

Thus
|A_{i}|=|B_{i}|=2^{i-1}\sqrt{T}
.

We use phase
A_{i}
to estimate
\hat{\alpha}_{i}
and phase
B_{i}
to estimate
\hat{r}_{i}
and
\hat{c}_{i}
, ensuring independence between estimates.

4
Regret Analysis

Theorem 4.1

(Main Result)

Conclusion

We studied contextual first-price auctions with budget constraints under one-sided information feedback. We proposed a novel algorithm that combines online gradient descent with robust quantile-based parameter estimation. Our algorithm achieves

A.1
Proof of Lemma
2.1

We prove the uniform concentration bound for
\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha)
. The proof follows four steps.

Step 1: Lipschitz properties.
First, note that the residual function
r_{i}(\alpha)=z_{i}+(\alpha_{0}-\alpha)x_{i}
satisfies the Lipschitz condition:

|r_{i}(\alpha)-r_{i}(\alpha^{\prime})|\leq M|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\quad\text{for all }\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in[-A,A],

since
|x_{i}|\leq M
by Assumption
1.1
.

For the population quantile function
q_{k}(\alpha)
, we compute its derivative. By the implicit function theorem applied to
F_{k,\alpha}(q_{k}(\alpha))=p
, where
F_{k,\alpha}
is the conditional CDF of
r_{i}(\alpha)
given group
k
, we obtain:

q_{k}^{\prime}(\alpha)=-\frac{\partial F_{k,\alpha}/\partial\alpha}{f_{k,\alpha}(q_{k}(\alpha))},

where
f_{k,\alpha}
is the conditional density. The numerator satisfies:

\left|\frac{\partial F_{k,\alpha}}{\partial\alpha}\right|=|\mathbf{E}[x_{i}f_{k,\alpha}(q_{k}(\alpha)\mid x_{i})\mid\mathrm{Group~}k]|\leq M.

By Assumption
1.1
, the denominator satisfies
f_{k,\alpha}(q_{k}(\alpha))\geq g_{\min}>0
. Therefore,

|q_{k}^{\prime}(\alpha)|\leq\frac{M}{g_{\min}}=:L_{q}.

Thus
q_{k}(\alpha)
is
L_{q}
-Lipschitz.

Step 2: Grid construction.
Fix
\eta>0
to be chosen later. Construct a grid
\mathcal{G}=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\}\subset[-A,A]
with
\alpha_{j}=-A+(j-1)\eta
,
j=1,\ldots,N
, where
N=\lceil 2A/\eta\rceil
.

Step 3: Concentration on the grid.
For a fixed grid point
\alpha_{j}
and group
k
, the sample quantile
\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha_{j})
is computed from
m=|I_{k}|\approx n/2
i.i.d. observations. By the Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz inequality for quantiles, we have for any
t>0

\mathbf{P}\left(|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha_{j})-q_{k}(\alpha_{j})|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-2mg_{\min}^{2}t^{2}\right).

Applying a union bound over all grid points and both groups:

\mathbf{P}\left(\max_{j=1,\ldots,N}\max_{k=1,2}|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha_{j})-q_{k}(\alpha_{j})|>t\right)\leq 4N\exp\left(-2mg_{\min}^{2}t^{2}\right).

Step 4: Extending to the whole interval.
For any
\alpha\in[-A,A]
, choose
\alpha_{j}\in\mathcal{G}
such that
|\alpha-\alpha_{j}|\leq\eta
. Then

|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha)-q_{k}(\alpha)|\leq|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha)-\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha_{j})|+|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha_{j})-q_{k}(\alpha_{j})|+|q_{k}(\alpha_{j})-q_{k}(\alpha)|.

We bound each term:

1.

By the Lipschitz property of the residual function, the empirical distribution functions satisfy:

\hat{F}_{k,\alpha}(y-M\eta)\leq\hat{F}_{k,\alpha_{j}}(y)\leq\hat{F}_{k,\alpha}(y+M\eta).

This implies
|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha)-\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha_{j})|\leq M\eta
.

2.

On the event complementary to the bound in equation (7),
|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha_{j})-q_{k}(\alpha_{j})|\leq t
.

3.

By the Lipschitz property of
q_{k}
,
|q_{k}(\alpha_{j})-q_{k}(\alpha)|\leq L_{q}\eta
.

Thus,

|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha)-q_{k}(\alpha)|\leq M\eta+t+L_{q}\eta.

Choose
\eta=\frac{t}{2(M+L_{q})}
. Then
(M+L_{q})\eta=t/2
, and

|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha)-q_{k}(\alpha)|\leq\frac{t}{2}+t+\frac{t}{2}=2t.

The grid size satisfies
N\leq\frac{2A}{\eta}+1\leq\frac{4A(M+L_{q})}{t}+1
. For
t
small enough,
N\leq\frac{5A(M+L_{q})}{t}
.

Step 5: Determining
t
.
From equation (7), the probability that the uniform bound fails is at most:

4N\exp(-2mg_{\min}^{2}t^{2})\leq\frac{20A(M+L_{q})}{t}\exp(-2mg_{\min}^{2}t^{2}).

Set
m=|I_{k}|\geq n/4
for
n\geq 4
. We want this probability to be at most
\delta/2
:

\frac{20A(M+L_{q})}{t}\exp\left(-\frac{ng_{\min}^{2}t^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

Taking logarithms:

-\frac{ng_{\min}^{2}t^{2}}{2}+\log\left(\frac{20A(M+L_{q})}{t}\right)\leq\log\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right).

The term
\log(1/t)
is dominated by
t^{2}
for small
t
. Solving asymptotically yields
t\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}}
. More precisely, there exists a constant
C_{1}
depending on
A,M,L_{q},g_{\min}
such that for
n
sufficiently large,

t=C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}}

satisfies the inequality. Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
:

\sup_{\alpha\in[-A,A]}|\hat{q}_{k}(\alpha)-q_{k}(\alpha)|\leq 2C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}}.

This completes the proof of Lemma
2.1
.

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\bar{v}\cdot\mathbb{E}[T-\tau]}_{\mathrm{budget\ depletion}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{t\in T_{0}}[r(v_{t},\pi^{*}(v_{t}))-r(v_{t},b_{t})]}_{\mathrm{exploration\ phase}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t\in A_{i}\cup B_{i}}[r(v_{t},\pi^{*}(v_{t}))-r(v_{t},b_{t})]}_{\mathrm{commit\ phase}}.

We bound each term separately.

By Lemma
B.2
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
T-\tau\leq C_{0}\sqrt{T\ln(T/\delta)}
, so the budget depletion term is
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
.

The exploration phase lasts
|T_{0}|=\sqrt{T}
rounds, and since per-round reward is bounded by
\bar{v}
, the total regret in this phase is
O(\sqrt{T})
.

For each round
t
in the commit phases
A_{i}\cup B_{i}
, we compare the reward
r(v_{t},b_{t})
obtained by our algorithm with the reward
r(v_{t},\alpha^{*}(v_{t}))
of the optimal stationary strategy from Lemma
B.1
. Recall the relation between the original reward and its scaled version:

r(v_{t},b)-\lambda_{t}c(v_{t},b)=(1+\lambda_{t})r\left(\frac{v_{t}}{1+\lambda_{t}},b\right).

Define the discretized value
v^{m(t)}=\max\{u\in\mathcal{V}:u\leq v_{t}/(1+\lambda_{t})\}
. By Lipschitz continuity of
r
and
c
, the discretization error satisfies:

|r(v_{t},b)-(1+\lambda_{t})r(v^{m(t)},b)|\leq\frac{\bar{v}}{\sqrt{T}}.

Using Lemma
B.3
and the filtering process in Algorithm
2
(line 25), we can show that with high probability:

(1+\lambda_{t})r(v^{m(t)},b_{t})\geq(1+\lambda_{t})r(v^{m(t)},\tilde{b}(v^{m(t)}))-4w_{t}^{m},

where
w_{t}^{m}=\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{n_{i}^{k}})
is the estimation error.

Combining these inequalities and summing over all rounds in the commit phase, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathrm{commit}}

\displaystyle[r(v_{t},\alpha^{*}(v_{t}))-r(v_{t},b_{t})]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}(1+\bar{\lambda})2^{i+1}\sqrt{T}\cdot 2\frac{\bar{v}}{\sqrt{T}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}(1+\bar{\lambda})2^{i+1}\sqrt{T}w_{i}^{m}

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}[c(v_{t},b_{t})-c(v_{t},\alpha^{*}(v_{t}))],

where
\bar{\lambda}=\max_{t}\lambda_{t}
.

The term

\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}(1+\bar{\lambda})2^{i+1}\sqrt{T}\cdot 2\frac{\bar{v}}{\sqrt{T}}=(1+\bar{\lambda})2^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T+1}\bar{v}=O(\sqrt{T}),

because
\bar{\lambda}=O(1)
and
2^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}=\sqrt{T}
.

For the remaining two terms we invoke Lemma
B.4
and Lemma
B.5
to obtain, with high probability,

\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}(1+\bar{\lambda})2^{i+1}\sqrt{T}w_{i}^{m}\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}),\qquad\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}[c(v_{t},b_{t})-c(v_{t},\alpha^{*}(v_{t}))]\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}).

Therefore the total regret contribution from the commit phase is
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
.

Appendix C
Proof of Lemma
4.2

We prove the lemma by constructing a stationary optimal strategy via Lagrangian duality.

Consider the constrained optimization problem (1):

\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mathbf{d}}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{b_{t}^{\pi}\geq d_{t}\}(v_{t}-b_{t}^{\pi})\right]\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{b_{t}^{\pi}\geq d_{t}\}b_{t}^{\pi}\leq\rho T,\ \forall\mathbf{v},\mathbf{d}.

Since
d_{t}=\alpha x_{t}+z_{t}
with
z_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}G
, the expected reward and cost for a bid
b
given context
x_{t}
and value
v_{t}
are:

r(v_{t},b,x_{t})=(v_{t}-b)G(b-\alpha x_{t}),\quad c(v_{t},b,x_{t})=bG(b-\alpha x_{t}).

Define
\mathcal{A}_{1}
as the set of stationary strategies that map
(v_{t},x_{t})
to a distribution over
[0,\bar{v}]
without using history. For any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
, the total expected reward and cost are:

R(\alpha)=T\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(v,x)\sim F\times H}\left[(v-\alpha(v,x))G(\alpha(v,x)-\alpha x)\right],

C(\alpha)=T\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(v,x)\sim F\times H}\left[\alpha(v,x)G(\alpha(v,x)-\alpha x)\right],

where
H
is the distribution of contexts. The budget constraint becomes
C(\alpha)\leq\rho T
.

Consider the Lagrangian with multiplier
\lambda\geq 0
:

\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\lambda)=R(\alpha)-\lambda(C(\alpha)-\rho T)=T\left(\mathbb{E}_{(v,x)}\left[(v-(1+\lambda)\alpha(v,x))G(\alpha(v,x)-\alpha x)\right]+\lambda\rho\right).

For fixed
\lambda
, the pointwise maximizer defines a stationary strategy:

\alpha_{\lambda}^{*}(v,x)\in\arg\max_{b\in[0,\bar{v}]}(v-(1+\lambda)b)G(b-\alpha x).

By Assumptions
1.1
and
1.1
, the objective is strictly concave in
b
, so the maximizer is unique.

Let
\lambda^{*}\geq 0
be the optimal Lagrange multiplier solving
\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\max_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\lambda)
. The primal problem is convex and satisfies Slater’s condition (bidding 0 yields zero cost, which is strictly less than
\rho
), so strong duality holds:

\max_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}R(\alpha)\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ C(\alpha)\leq\rho T=\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\max_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\lambda).

Define
\alpha^{*}=\alpha_{\lambda^{*}}^{*}
. By complementary slackness,
\lambda^{*}(C(\alpha^{*})-\rho T)=0
, and by construction,
\alpha^{*}
maximizes
\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\lambda^{*})
over
\mathcal{A}_{1}
.

For any feasible strategy
\pi\in\Pi_{0}
(including
\pi^{*}
), we have:

R(\pi)\leq\max_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\lambda^{*})=\mathcal{L}(\alpha^{*},\lambda^{*})=R(\alpha^{*})-\lambda^{*}(C(\alpha^{*})-\rho T).

If
C(\alpha^{*})\leq\rho T
, then
\alpha^{*}
is feasible and
R(\alpha^{*})\geq R(\pi)
for all feasible
\pi
. If
C(\alpha^{*})>\rho T
, then
\lambda^{*}=0
by complementary slackness, and
\alpha^{*}
maximizes the unconstrained reward
R(\alpha)
. Since any feasible
\pi
satisfies
C(\pi)\leq\rho T<C(\alpha^{*})
, we still have
R(\alpha^{*})\geq R(\pi)
.

Thus, in both cases,
\alpha^{*}
is a stationary strategy satisfying
R(\alpha^{*})\geq R(\pi^{*})
.

Appendix D
Proof of Lemma
4.3

We analyze the budget consumption process to bound the stopping time
\tau
. Recall the budget update:
B_{t+1}=B_{t}-b_{t}\mathbf{1}\{b_{t}>d_{t}\}
, with initial budget
B=\rho T
. The stopping time
\tau
is defined as the last period where the remaining budget is at least
\bar{v}
. If
\tau<T
, then
B_{\tau+1}<\bar{v}
, which implies

\sum_{t=2}^{\tau}c_{t}>\rho T-\bar{v},\quad\text{where }c_{t}=b_{t}\mathbf{1}\{b_{t}>d_{t}\}.

Here we note that
c_{1}=0
due to the initial exploration round with
b_{1}=0
. Rearranging this inequality gives

\sum_{t=2}^{\tau}(\rho-c_{t})<-\rho(T-\tau)+\bar{v}.

We now establish an upper bound for the left-hand side of (2). Decompose the sum into three terms:

\sum_{t=2}^{\tau}(\rho-c_{t})=\underbrace{\sum_{t=2}^{\tau}(\rho-\hat{c}_{t}(b_{t}))}_{(A)}+\underbrace{\sum_{t=2}^{\tau}(\hat{c}_{t}(b_{t})-c(b_{t}))}_{(B)}+\underbrace{\sum_{t=2}^{\tau}(c(b_{t})-c_{t})}_{(C)},

where
\hat{c}_{t}(b_{t})
is the cost estimator used in Algorithm
2
, and
c(b_{t})=b_{t}G(b_{t}-\alpha x_{t})
is the expected cost conditional on the bid
b_{t}
and context
x_{t}
.

Bounding (A).
From the dual variable update rule in Algorithm
2
(line 13), we have

\lambda_{t+1}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\lambda\geq 0}(\lambda_{t}-\eta(\rho-\hat{c}_{t}(b_{t})))\geq\lambda_{t}-\eta(\rho-\hat{c}_{t}(b_{t})).

Summing this inequality from
t=2
to
\tau
, and using the fact that
\lambda_{t}\in[0,\bar{v}/\rho-1]
for all
t
, we obtain

\sum_{t=2}^{\tau}(\rho-\hat{c}_{t}(b_{t}))\leq\frac{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{\tau+1}}{\eta}\leq\frac{\bar{v}/\rho}{\eta}.

Bounding (B).
By Lemma
B.3
, with probability at least
1-\delta/3
, for all
t\geq 2
:

\sum_{t=2}^{\tau}|\hat{c}_{t}(b_{t})-c(b_{t})|\leq C_{1}\bar{v}\sqrt{T\ln(T/\delta)}

for some constant
C_{1}>0
.

Bounding (C).
Define the martingale difference sequence
M_{t}=\sum_{s=2}^{t}(c_{s}-c(b_{s}))
for
t\geq 2
, with
M_{1}=0
. Note that
\mathbb{E}[c_{t}-c(b_{t})\mid\mathcal{H}_{t},v_{t},b_{t}]=0
, since
c(b_{t})
is precisely the conditional expectation of
c_{t}
given the history, current value, and current bid. Moreover, each term is bounded:
|c_{t}-c(b_{t})|\leq\max\{c_{t},c(b_{t})\}\leq\bar{v}
, as both
c_{t}
and
c(b_{t})
are bounded by
\bar{v}
(since
b_{t}\leq\bar{v}
and
G(\cdot)\leq 1
). Therefore,
\{M_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
is a martingale adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{H}_{t}\}
, with increments bounded by
\bar{v}
.

Applying the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality to the martingale
M_{\tau}
(noting that
\tau
is a stopping time bounded by
T
), we have for any
\epsilon>0
:

\mathbb{P}\left(|M_{\tau}|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\bar{v}^{2}\tau}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\bar{v}^{3}\tau}\right).

Setting
\epsilon=\bar{v}\sqrt{2T\ln(6/\delta)}
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{t=2}^{\tau}(c_{t}-c(b_{t}))\right|\geq\bar{v}\sqrt{2T\ln(6/\delta)}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{3}.

Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta/3
:

\left|\sum_{t=2}^{\tau}(c_{t}-c(b_{t}))\right|\leq\bar{v}\sqrt{2T\ln(6/\delta)}.

Taking a union bound over the three events (3), (4), and (5), they hold simultaneously with probability at least
1-\delta
. Substituting these bounds into the decomposition, we obtain

\sum_{t=2}^{\tau}(\rho-c_{t})\leq\frac{\bar{v}/\rho}{\eta}+C_{1}\bar{v}\sqrt{T\ln(T/\delta)}+\bar{v}\sqrt{2T\ln(6/\delta)}.

Finally, from (2) and (6):

-\rho(T-\tau)+\bar{v}\leq\frac{\bar{v}/\rho}{\eta}+C_{1}\bar{v}\sqrt{T\ln(T/\delta)}+\bar{v}\sqrt{2T\ln(3/\delta)}.

Rearranging and setting
\eta=\Theta(1/\sqrt{T})
(as in Algorithm
2
), we obtain

T-\tau\leq\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\frac{\bar{v}/\rho}{\eta}+C_{1}\bar{v}\sqrt{T\ln(T/\delta)}+\bar{v}\sqrt{2T\ln(3/\delta)}\right)\leq C_{0}\sqrt{T\ln(T/\delta)}

for some constant
C_{0}
independent of
T
and
\delta
. When
\tau=T
, the inequality holds trivially. This completes the proof.

Appendix E
Proof of Lemma
4.4

We prove the bound for
\hat{r}(v^{m},b^{k})
; the proof for
\hat{c}(v^{m},b^{k})
follows similarly. Recall that
d_{s}=\alpha x_{s}+z_{s}
where
z_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}G
, and
r(v^{m},b^{k})=(v^{m}-b^{k})G(b^{k}-\alpha x_{t})
.

First, we decompose the error as:

\displaystyle|r(v^{m},b^{k})-\hat{r}(v^{m},b^{k})|

\displaystyle=\left|(v^{m}-b^{k})G(b^{k}-\alpha x_{t})-\frac{1}{n_{t}^{k}}\sum_{s\in B_{1}}\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq b_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq d_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}(v^{m}-b^{k})\right|

\displaystyle\leq(v^{m}-b^{k})\cdot\left|G(b^{k}-\alpha x_{t})-\frac{1}{n_{t}^{k}}\sum_{s\in B_{1}}\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq b_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq d_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\bar{v}\cdot\left|G(b^{k}-\alpha x_{t})-\frac{1}{n_{t}^{k}}\sum_{s\in B_{1}}\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq b_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq d_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}\right|.(7)

Now, define for each
s\in B_{1}
:

Y_{s}=\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq b_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq d_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}.

We need to compare the empirical average
\frac{1}{n_{t}^{k}}\sum_{s\in B_{1}}Y_{s}
with
G(b^{k}-\alpha x_{t})
.

Consider the following approximation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{s}|\mathcal{H}_{s-1},b_{s},x_{s}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq b_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq d_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}|\mathcal{H}_{s-1},b_{s},x_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq b_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}\cdot G(b^{k}-\alpha x_{t}-(\hat{\alpha}-\alpha)(x_{s}-x_{t})).

The second term is bounded by:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n_{i}^{k}}\sum_{s\in B_{1}}\mathbf{1}\{b^{k}\geq b_{s}-\hat{\alpha}(x_{s}-x_{t})\}\cdot(G(b^{k}-\alpha x_{t}-(\hat{\alpha}-\alpha)(x_{s}-x_{t}))-G(b^{k}-\alpha x_{t}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{n_{i}^{k}}),(9)

using the fact that the indicator is bounded by 1.

Combining all bounds, we obtain:

\left|\frac{1}{n_{i}^{k}}\sum_{s\in B_{1}}Y_{s}-G(b^{k}-\alpha x_{t})\right|\leq\tilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{i}^{k}}}\right).

Finally, substituting back into (7) gives:

\left|r(v^{m},b^{k})-\hat{r}(v^{m},b^{k})\right|\leq\bar{v}\cdot\tilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{i}^{k}}}\right)=\tilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{i}^{k}}}\right).

The proof for
\hat{c}(v^{m},b^{k})
is analogous, replacing
(v^{m}-b^{k})
with
b^{k}
in the derivation. Taking a union bound over all
m\in[M]
and
k\in[K]
, we obtain the stated high probability bound.

Appendix F
Proof of Lemma
4.5

Recall that
w_{i}^{m}=\bar{v}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{4\ln T\log(KT/\delta)}{N_{i}^{m}}}
, where
N_{i}^{m}=\min_{b^{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{i-1}^{m}}n_{i}^{k}
. The factor
2^{i+1}\sqrt{T}
corresponds to the length of phase
i
(both
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
combined).

We need to bound:

S=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}(1+\bar{\lambda})2^{i+1}\sqrt{T}w_{i}^{m}\right]=\bar{v}(1+\bar{\lambda})\sqrt{4\ln T\log(KT/\delta)}\sqrt{T}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}2^{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{i}^{m}}}\right].

Let
C=\bar{v}(1+\bar{\lambda})\sqrt{4\ln T\log(KT/\delta)}
. Then

S\leq C\sqrt{T}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}2^{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{i}^{m}}}\right].

Now, for each phase
i
, we have:

N_{i}^{m}\geq 1+\sum_{s=2}^{t-1}\mathbf{1}\left\{v_{s}\leq\frac{\rho}{\bar{v}}v_{t}\right\},

where we used
\lambda_{t}\leq\bar{v}/\rho-1
.

Since phase
i
has length
2^{i}\sqrt{T}
(combining
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
), let
t_{i}
be the round in phase
i
that achieves the minimum
N_{i}^{m}
. Then the number of rounds before
t_{i}
is at least
\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}2^{j}\sqrt{T}=(2^{i}-2)\sqrt{T}
.

Thus, conditioned on
v_{t_{i}}
:

N_{i}^{m}\geq 1+X_{i},

where
X_{i}
stochastically dominates a binomial random variable with parameters
(2^{i}-2)\sqrt{T}
and
F\left(\frac{\rho}{\bar{v}}v_{t_{i}}\right)
.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{i}^{m}}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{v_{t_{i}}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\mathrm{Bin}((2^{i}-2)\sqrt{T},F(\frac{\rho}{\bar{v}}v_{t_{i}}))}}\right]\right].

The binomial expectation can be bounded as:

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)}}\right]\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{C_{1}}{\sqrt{np}}\right\}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\sqrt{np}}.

Thus,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{i}^{m}}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{v_{t_{i}}}\left[\frac{C_{1}}{\sqrt{(2^{i}-2)\sqrt{T}\cdot F\left(\frac{\rho}{\bar{v}}v_{t_{i}}\right)}}\right]\leq\frac{C_{2}}{2^{i/2}T^{1/4}}.

Now, we can bound this expectation by:

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{i}^{m}}}\right]\leq\frac{C_{1}}{2^{i/2}T^{1/4}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{F\left(\frac{\rho}{\bar{v}}v_{t_{i}}\right)}}\right]\leq\frac{C_{2}}{2^{i/2}T^{1/4}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\bar{v}}{\rho}},

for some constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
.

Multiplying by
2^{i}
and summing over
i=1
to
\frac{\log_{2}T}{2}
:

\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}2^{i}\cdot\frac{C_{2}}{2^{i/2}T^{1/4}}=\frac{C_{2}}{T^{1/4}}\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}2^{i/2}\leq\frac{C_{2}}{T^{1/4}}\cdot\frac{2^{\frac{1}{4}\log_{2}T+1/2}-1}{\sqrt{2}-1}.

Now, note that:

2^{\frac{1}{4}\log_{2}T}=T^{1/4},\quad 2^{1/2}=\sqrt{2}.

Thus,

\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}2^{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{i}^{m}}}\right]\leq\frac{C_{2}}{T^{1/4}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}T^{1/4}}{\sqrt{2}-1}=\frac{C_{2}\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}-1}=O(1).

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}T}2^{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{i}^{m}}}\right]=O(1).

Substituting back:

S\leq C\sqrt{T}\cdot O(1)=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}).

This completes the proof.

Appendix G
Proof of Lemma
4.6

Recall from Algorithm
2
that
b_{t}
is chosen as:

b_{t}=\inf\mathcal{B}_{t}^{m(t)}\quad\text{where}\quad v^{m(t)}=\max\{u\in\mathcal{V}:u\leq v_{t}/(1+\lambda_{t})\}.

From the construction of the active sets
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{m}
and Lemma B.3 (which shows that the optimal bid
\tilde{b}(v^{m})
remains in the active set with high probability), we have that for any
\lambda\geq 0
:

\hat{r}_{t}(v^{m(t)},b_{t})-\lambda_{t}\hat{c}_{t}(b_{t})\geq\hat{r}_{t}(v^{m(t)},\tilde{b}(v^{m(t)}))-\lambda_{t}\hat{c}_{t}(\tilde{b}(v^{m(t)}))-3w_{t}^{m(t)}.

Applying Lemma
B.3
with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have:

r(v^{m(t)},b_{t})-\lambda_{t}c(b_{t})\geq r(v^{m(t)},\tilde{b}(v^{m(t)}))-\lambda_{t}c(\tilde{b}(v^{m(t)}))-(4w_{t}^{m(t)}+\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{n_{t}^{k}})).

Now, recall the relationship between the scaled and unscaled rewards:

r(v_{t},b)-\lambda_{t}c(v_{t},b)=(1+\lambda_{t})r\left(\frac{v_{t}}{1+\lambda_{t}},b\right).

Let
\alpha^{*}
be the optimal stationary strategy from Lemma
B.1
. Then:

\displaystyle r(v_{t},b_{t})-\lambda_{t}c(v_{t},b_{t})

\displaystyle=(1+\lambda_{t})r\left(\frac{v_{t}}{1+\lambda_{t}},b_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\lambda_{t})\left[r\left(\frac{v_{t}}{1+\lambda_{t}},\tilde{b}\left(\frac{v_{t}}{1+\lambda_{t}}\right)\right)-\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{t}^{m(t)}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq(1+\lambda_{t})\left[r\left(\frac{v_{t}}{1+\lambda_{t}},\alpha^{*}(v_{t})\right)-\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{t}^{m(t)}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=r(v_{t},\alpha^{*}(v_{t}))-\lambda_{t}c(v_{t},\alpha^{*}(v_{t}))-(1+\lambda_{t})\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{t}^{m(t)}}}\right),

where the second inequality follows from the optimality of
\tilde{b}(v)
for value
v
in the discretized bid set.

Rearranging terms, we get:

\lambda_{t}[c(v_{t},b_{t})-c(v_{t},\alpha^{*}(v_{t}))]\leq r(v_{t},\alpha^{*}(v_{t}))-r(v_{t},b_{t})+(1+\lambda_{t})\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{t}^{m(t)}}}\right).

Summing from
t=1
to
T
:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}[c(v_{t},b_{t})-c(v_{t},\alpha^{*})]\leq\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}[r(v_{t},\alpha^{*}(v_{t}))-r(v_{t},b_{t})]}_{(A)}+\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(1+\lambda_{t})\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{t}^{m(t)}}}\right)}_{(B)}.

Now we bound each term separately.

Bounding (A):
This term represents the regret compared to the optimal stationary strategy. From the regret analysis in Theorem
4.1
, we know that with high probability,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}[r(v_{t},\alpha^{*}(v_{t}))-r(v_{t},b_{t})]\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}).

Bounding (B):
Since
\lambda_{t}\leq\bar{v}/\rho-1
(from Lemma
4.3
), we have
1+\lambda_{t}\leq\bar{v}/\rho
. Thus,

(B)\leq\frac{\bar{v}}{\rho}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{t}^{m(t)}}}\right).

From Lemma
B.4
, we have:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{t}^{m(t)}}}\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}).

Therefore,

(B)\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}).

Combining the bounds for (A) and (B), we obtain:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{t}[c(v_{t},b_{t})-c(v_{t},\alpha^{*})]\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})+\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}).

This completes the proof of Lemma
4.6
.
