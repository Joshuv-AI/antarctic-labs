Utility of Tumor Sphericity on CT for Differentiating Fat-Poor Angiomyolipoma from Renal Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Jaein Song, Jung Jae Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo assess the utility of tumor sphericity on CT as a quantitative shape parameter for distinguishing fat-poor angiomyolipoma (AML) from renal cell carcinoma (RCC) in small renal masses (SRMs, maximum diameter ≤4 cm).Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively evaluated 238 solitary sporadic SRMs that were pathologically confirmed as either AML or RCC and showed no visible fat on unenhanced CT. Tumor volume and sphericity were obtained through volumetric analysis using dedicated software. To distinguish fat-poor AML from RCC, clinical and imaging findings were compared, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify variables independently associated with fat-poor AML.ResultsAmong the 238 SRMs, 22 were diagnosed as AMLs and 216 as RCCs, comprising 173 clear cell, 25 papillary, and 18 chromophobe subtypes. Fat-poor AMLs were significantly more prevalent in female and had lower tumor volumes than RCCs (both p < 0.001). The tumor sphericity was significantly lower in fat-poor AMLs than in RCCs (0.755 ± 0.042 vs. 0.824 ± 0.026, p < 0.001). For distinguishing fat-poor AML from RCC, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for sphericity was 0.909 (optimal cutoff, 0.801; sensitivity, 86.4%; specificity, 84.7%). Female sex, lower tumor volume, and lower sphericity were independent predictors of fat-poor AML (all p < 0.005). The AUC for the multivariate model was 0.965 (sensitivity, 90.9%; specificity, 92.6%).ConclusionTumor sphericity serves as a quantitative shape parameter for distinguishing fat-poor AML from RCC in SRMs.

INTRODUCTION

Small renal masses (SRMs), defined as tumors measuring 4 cm or less in diameter and corresponding to clinical stage T1a, are commonly detected incidentally on abdominal imaging in asymptomatic patients (1). These lesions encompass a heterogeneous group of tumors, including benign and malignant entities such as renal cell carcinoma (RCC). Although SRMs have an excellent prognosis even when they represent RCC, identifying benign SRMs has become increasingly important because of the widespread use of cross-sectional imaging and growing concerns regarding overtreatment (23).

Angiomyolipoma (AML), the most common benign renal tumor, is usually easily diagnosed on CT because of its gross fat content (4). However, fat-poor AML, accounting for approximately 5% of AMLs, often demonstrates imaging features similar to those of RCC because of the absence of macroscopic fat, making differentiation difficult on conventional CT (567). Fat-poor AML may appear either hyperattenuating or isoattenuating in the unenhanced phase, depending on the distribution of diffusely scattered fat cells, which are not macroscopically detectable, and typically demonstrates relatively homogeneous enhancement after contrast enhancement (89). However, differentiation is particularly challenging when RCC demonstrates homogeneous enhancement without internal degenerative changes, a situation that is more commonly encountered in SRMs. Thus, reliable differentiation between the two tumor types based solely on CT imaging characteristics remains challenging.

Across imaging modalities, tumor shape has been suggested as a useful feature for distinguishing fat-poor AML from RCC. Based on the morphologic difference between the relatively spherical shape of RCC and the more compressed or distorted configuration frequently seen in AML, several imaging features suggestive of AML, including the angular interface and the ice-cream cone sign, have been well described (1011121314). However, these approaches have limitations because tumor morphology is evaluated using qualitative or semiquantitative methods or assessed only on selected two-dimensional (2D) planes, rather than through volumetric 3D analysis of the entire tumor.

Sphericity is a radiomic shape feature calculated using the following equation: Sphericity = π1/3 × (6V)2/3 ÷ A

Where V and A represent the volume and surface area of the object, respectively. It is a measure of how closely the shape of an object approximates that of a perfect sphere in three dimensions, with values decreasing from 1 as the shape deviates from a perfect sphere. We assumed that the sphericity of AML would differ from that of RCC because of their different morphologic characteristics; therefore, we aimed to assess the utility of tumor sphericity on CT as a quantitative morphologic parameter for distinguishing fat-poor AML from RCC in SRMs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

CASE ENROLLMENT

This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board, which waived the requirement for informed consent owing to the study’s retrospective design (IRB No. 2026-03-044). From the electronic medical records, we identified 587 patients who underwent surgical intervention (either partial or radical nephrectomy) or radiofrequency ablation with biopsy for renal tumors at our institution between January 2018 and December 2025. Among these, AML or RCC (clear cell, papillary, or chromophobe RCC) was pathologically confirmed in 544 patients. Malignant renal tumors other than the aforementioned renal tumor pathologies were excluded. Radiologic reports and preoperative CT images were reviewed to determine whether the tumors met all of the following inclusion criteria: adequate preoperative CT images consisting of unenhanced and at least one contrast-enhanced phase (slice thickness, ≤3 mm) without significant artifact; a solitary, sporadic renal tumor (maximum diameter ≤4 cm on CT image); and no macroscopic fat on the unenhanced phase. A total of 238 SRMs in 238 consecutive patients who met all of the inclusion criteria were included in the analysis.

DATA COLLECTION AND IMAGE ANALYSIS

We retrieved clinical data, including patient sex and age at the time of diagnosis. Pathologic data were retrospectively obtained by reviewing reports recorded by one of the abdominal and genitourinary pathologists at our institution. All preoperative CT images were obtained within 2 months prior to treatment either at our institution (165 patients, 69.3%) or at other hospitals (73 patients, 30.7%). To reduce bias, a radiologist with 15 years of experience in genitourinary imaging evaluated the images while blinded to the clinical and pathologic data. All contrast-enhanced CT images were transferred to a workstation in DICOM format at their original quality. For multiphase contrast-enhanced CT, a single post-contrast phase in which the tumor margin was most clearly delineated from the adjacent normal tissue was selected for analysis. Tumor segmentation and volumetric evaluation were performed using 3D Slicer (version 5.10.0; https://www.slicer.org/) with the PyRadiomics extension to extract radiomic shape features, including tumor volume and sphericity (15). Tumor segmentation was performed semiautomatically using the Grow from Seeds algorithm implemented in 3D Slicer. The initial seed regions were placed within the tumor and adjacent background tissues, and the algorithm automatically propagated the segmentation. Because it was difficult to achieve accurate tumor segmentation using the Grow from Seeds algorithm alone, the results were reviewed and manually corrected to ensure accurate delineation of the tumor margins in all cases (Fig. 1). The CT images were resampled to an isotropic voxel size of 1 mm3 (1 × 1 × 1 mm) using B-spline interpolation before shape feature extraction. The same image set was independently evaluated by another radiologist with two years of experience in abdominal imaging using the same procedure to assess interobserver agreement for tumor volume and sphericity measurements.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

We compared the clinical and radiologic findings between patients with fat-poor AML and those with RCC using the independent t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, or chi-square test, depending on the variable type. Differences in sphericity among RCC subtypes were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed, and the optimal cutoff value was determined by maximizing the Youden index to assess the diagnostic performance of tumor sphericity and volume in distinguishing fat-poor AML from RCC. Binary logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of fat-poor AML among the variables. Inter-reader agreement for tumor volume and sphericity measurements between the two radiologists was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value <0.05.

RESULTS

Fat-poor AML was identified in 9.2% (22 of 238) of patients. Among the 216 RCCs, clear cell, papillary, and chromophobe subtypes accounted for 173 (80.1%), 25 (11.6%), and 18 (8.3%), respectively. A comparison of the clinical and radiologic findings between patients with fat-poor AMLs and those with RCC is summarized in Table 1. The mean age did not differ significantly between the two groups; however, fat-poor AML was significantly more prevalent in female, as previously reported. The mean sphericity was significantly higher in RCC (0.824 ± 0.026) than in fat-poor AML (0.755 ± 0.042) (p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference in mean sphericity was observed among the pathological subtypes of RCC (clear cell RCC, 0.824 ± 0.025; papillary RCC, 0.825 ± 0.032; and chromophobe RCC, 0.825 ± 0.032) (all p > 0.05) (Fig. 2). The tumor volume was significantly greater in RCC (9.4 ± 7.2 cm3) than in fat-poor AML (3.1 ± 2.6 cm3) (p < 0.001). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for sphericity for distinguishing fat-poor AML from RCC was 0.909 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.865–0.942). Using a cutoff value of 0.801, the sensitivity and specificity were 86.4% (95% CI, 65.1%–97.1%) and 84.7% (95% CI, 79.2%–89.2%), respectively. The AUC for tumor volume for distinguishing fat-poor AML from RCC was 0.819, with an optimal cutoff value of 3.4 cm3. In multivariate binary logistic regression analysis (Table 2), female sex (odds ratio [OR], 9.4), lower tumor volume (OR, 9.8), and lower tumor sphericity (OR, 32.4) were independently associated with fat-poor AML (all p < 0.005). The AUC for the multivariate model for distinguishing fat-poor AML from RCC was 0.965 (sensitivity, 90.9%; specificity, 92.6%). The reliability of tumor volume and sphericity measurements between the readers was excellent, as indicated by an ICC of 0.92 (95% CI, 0.90–0.94) and 0.88 (95% CI, 0.84–0.91), respectively.

DISCUSSION

Previously, renal mass biopsy was not routinely recommended when the therapeutic plan had already been determined based on the suspicion of malignancy, except in specific situations in which a lesion was suspected to be hematologic, metastatic, infectious, or inflammatory (16). However, current guidelines increasingly recommend percutaneous biopsy to avoid unnecessary treatment of benign SRMs (17). Therefore, the role of imaging is becoming increasingly important not only in detecting SRMs that require treatment but also in identifying benign lesions that may not require treatment. In previous studies analyzing the pathologic outcomes of SRMs lacking visible fat on CT imaging, AMLs accounted for approximately 10% of tumors confirmed in posttreatment specimens (111213181920). Similarly, 9.2% of SRMs in our study cohort were presumed to be malignant and subsequently underwent potentially unnecessary treatment. This proportion may be reduced if imaging findings raise the possibility of AML, thereby supporting the use of percutaneous biopsy or imaging follow-up rather than treatment (21).

According to a systematic review and meta-analysis, CT demonstrated moderate sensitivity and excellent specificity for the diagnosis of fat-poor AML versus RCC (22). The diagnostic limitations of CT suggest that the conventional imaging features of AML are absent in a substantial proportion of cases, highlighting the need for additional imaging characteristics to distinguish fat-poor AML from RCC. Even if an imaging finding does not definitively establish the benign nature of an SRM, it may still be meaningful if it supports the use of percutaneous biopsy in the context of current clinical trends. Recently, CT radiomics has been applied to explore imaging biomarkers for differentiating fat-poor AML from RCC because quantitative texture and shape analysis can objectively evaluate image features, enabling the detection of subtle changes and heterogeneity not visible to the human eye (23). However, diagnostic models or nomograms derived from CT radiomics are often too complex for routine clinical application and may have limited generalizability because they rely on the specific conditions of the original analyses. MRI has provided additional value for differentiating fat-poor AML from RCC (24). Multiparametric kidney MRI provides comprehensive tissue characterization and functional information at the microenvironmental level and offers several advantages in the evaluation of renal tumors. However, MRI also has limitations in determining the benign or malignant nature of certain renal tumors, particularly when a lesion shows diffuse low signal intensity on T2-weighted images, because fat-poor AML and non-clear cell RCC show considerable overlap in imaging features, even on MRI (20).

Differences in tissue composition between AML and RCC may result in variations in tumor stiffness, which may affect lesion shape by influencing the boundary between the tumor and surrounding tissue (25). RCCs tend to be more spherical overall, whereas AMLs often demonstrate a characteristic morphology with compression at the interface with the renal parenchyma and relative expansion toward the surrounding perirenal fat (1226). Consequently, imaging features such as the angular interface and ice-cream cone sign have been described as useful in the diagnosis of AML (1011). Tumor shape is an intuitive metric that is clinically useful because it is independent of imaging modality; however, visual assessment is inherently limited by observer subjectivity. Furthermore, because not all AMLs display characteristic imaging signs and their absence does not necessarily indicate RCC, particularly in SRMs, these signs have been reported to show limited sensitivity despite their relatively high specificity (10111213). To address the limitations of subjective assessment, several studies have shown that quantitative shape parameters, including the long-to-short axis diameter ratio or tumor circularity on cross-sectional imaging, can help differentiate fat-poor AML from RCC (131819). Quantitative shape parameters may aid in identifying AMLs that lack characteristic imaging signs, as they provide a comprehensive evaluation of tumor morphology. A previous study reported that tumor circularity measured on axial contrast-enhanced CT images outperformed radiologic signs of AML in differentiating fat-poor AML from RCC (13). However, the authors acknowledged that the evaluation of tumor shape on a 2D plane still has potential limitations, such as limited representativeness or selection bias, depending on the plane selected for evaluation.

Our results demonstrated that tumor sphericity on CT imaging may have diagnostic value in distinguishing fat-poor AML from RCC in SRMs (Figs. 3, 4). Sphericity, obtained from 3D volumetric analysis, generates a single value per tumor and is not affected by selection bias, thereby reflecting the entire tumor morphology. To date, tumor sphericity has mainly been investigated in cancers of the lung, breast, and brain in relation to survival outcomes, such as tumor aggressiveness and treatment response (272829). In the kidney, a few studies have reported that sphericity is useful for assessing the aggressiveness of clear cell RCC or differentiating clear cell RCC from non-clear cell RCC (303132). Sphericity has rarely been reported or utilized as a shape parameter, even in studies applying CT radiomics to renal tumors, as most analyses have been performed using 2D images (23). Only one recent study applied 3D CT radiomic features to differentiate benign from malignant SRMs; however, specific values or results regarding sphericity were not reported (33). Our study results alone are insufficient for the definitive differentiation of benign and malignant renal tumors because of the study limitations and overlap in sphericity between RCC and AML. However, our findings provide a theoretical basis for incorporating the quantitative shape analysis of renal tumors into a comprehensive diagnostic model. Furthermore, these results may serve as a radiological basis for adopting a more proactive approach to active intervention in SRMs or, conversely, for deferring aggressive procedures when AML is suspected, particularly in equivocal cases in which the typical radiological features of AML cannot be confidently identified.

Although SRMs were selected as the target population for this study, additional considerations should be made when interpreting the findings for particular subsets of renal tumors. Smaller lesions are inherently more susceptible to measurement errors because even minor inaccuracies in segmentation may result in proportionally larger deviations. Smaller tumors may not exhibit sufficiently developed or distinctive imaging features, which may limit their radiological differentiation. Therefore, considering the potential role of tumor volume as a confounding factor, we attempted propensity score matching. However, the assessment of the covariate balance after matching demonstrated a considerable residual imbalance, which limited the validity of the matched results and precluded their inclusion in the main analysis. The observed imbalance was likely driven by the substantial difference in tumor volume between the two groups. Consequently, we cannot rule out the possibility that tumor size may have served as a source of bias or as a confounding factor. Although multivariate regression analysis did not demonstrate a significant influence of tumor volume, this potential influence should be considered when interpreting the results. Furthermore, tumor margin characteristics likely reflect the interactions between the tumor and the surrounding tissues. As sphericity is influenced by the interface among the tumor, renal parenchyma, and perirenal fat tissue, the relative location of the tumor in the kidney may affect its measured sphericity. In particular, these anatomical constraints may bias tumors toward a more spherical morphology, especially when the tumor is located either completely within the renal parenchyma or entirely exophytic, thereby altering the optimal cutoff value and potentially reducing the discriminatory performance of sphericity-based assessments. In our exploratory subgroup analysis, spherical AMLs above the threshold value showed minor differences in tumor volume and location (completely endophytic versus partially exophytic) compared with non-spherical AMLs below the threshold value; however, these trends were not statistically significant. The limited statistical power of our relatively small cohort may have influenced these observations. Consequently, the application of our findings to very small tumors, as well as to tumors that are completely endophytic or exophytic, should be interpreted with caution. Further studies with larger cohorts are required to validate the utility and optimal threshold for sphericity in these subgroups.

This study has certain limitations. First, the results of our study may be subject to measurement errors because the semiautomated segmentation process requires manual correction. In addition, the CT imaging protocols used for the analysis were not standardized and were heterogeneous across cases. Therefore, further validation in larger cohorts using controlled imaging protocols is required to support the clinical application of these findings. Second, the degree and pattern of tumor enhancement were not evaluated because of the variability in the CT equipment and acquisition protocols in our cohort. Instead, a shape factor, such as sphericity, can be quantified using any imaging modality if the method provides adequate cross-sectional images of the lesion. This suggests that our results may be applicable regardless of the CT protocol or imaging modality used. Third, a wide spectrum of neoplasms may arise in the kidneys, and we did not include rare renal tumors in our analysis. AML and RCC represent the majority of benign and malignant renal tumors, respectively, and are among the most common renal tumors in clinical practice. Furthermore, categorizing heterogeneous tumor subtypes into single benign or malignant categories may introduce potential bias in the results. Fourth, we did not analyze the radiological features of AML, such as the ice-cream cone sign or angular interface, which are commonly used in clinical practice. Therefore, the association between sphericity and these imaging features was not assessed separately owing to the limited number of cases. Additional analyses evaluating the correlation between sphericity and well-established imaging features of AML may help strengthen the significance of our findings. Finally, fat-poor AMLs were defined according to imaging features, and the degree of the fat component was not quantified on pathological examination. However, the absence of macroscopic fat on unenhanced CT raises the clinical suspicion of malignancy in SRMs, warranting further imaging evaluation, biopsy, or even treatment. Therefore, fat-poor AMLs were defined based on imaging findings, in accordance with the primary aim of this study. In conclusion, tumor sphericity serves as a quantitative shape parameter for distinguishing fat-poor AML from RCC in SRMs.
