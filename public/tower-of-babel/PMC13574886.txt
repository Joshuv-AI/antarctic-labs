Identifying coagulation-related biomarkers in steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head: implications for early diagnosis and treatment
Authors: Congqin Xie, Jian Du, Zhi Liu, Yanlei Lv, Huaijun Ni, Fangyuan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SONFH) is closely associated with coagulation dysfunction, but its molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to identify coagulation-related biomarkers for SONFH and evaluate their diagnostic relevance. The GSE123568 dataset from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GEO) was analyzed, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were intersected with coagulation-related genes (CRGs) from the Molecular Signatures Database to identify coagulation-related differentially expressed genes (CRDEGs). Functional enrichment analysis was performed to explore the biological processes and pathways associated with these genes, while protein-protein interaction network analysis and three machine learning algorithms were applied to identify key genes. A nomogram model was constructed and evaluated using bootstrap internal validation. Gene set enrichment analysis, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis, competing endogenous RNA network, and Drug Signature Database analyses were performed to explore potential regulatory mechanisms. The expression levels of the key genes were preliminarily assessed by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). In total, 49 CRDEGs were identified and were mainly enriched in immune response, blood coagulation, and complement and coagulation cascades. PECAM1, LGALS3, and ANXA5 were identified as key genes. The prediction model constructed based on these genes showed favorable diagnostic discrimination, with a bootstrap AUC of 0.939 (95% CI: 0.840–0.977). RT-qPCR showed decreased PECAM1 and LGALS3 expression and increased ANXA5 expression in SONFH patients. These findings suggest that PECAM1, LGALS3, and ANXA5 may serve as potential coagulation-related biomarkers for SONFH, providing a basis for further clinical validation and mechanistic investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10238-026-02245-w.

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a frequent and challenging orthopedic disorder, characterized by inadequate blood supply to the femoral head, which results in bone necrosis and the failure of bone tissue repair, ultimately leading to bone collapse [1, 2]. ONFH is typically divided into traumatic and non-traumatic categories, with steroid-induced ONFH (SONFH) being the primary cause of the non-traumatic form [3]. Epidemiological studies show that around 8.12 million individuals aged 15 and older in China are affected by non-traumatic ONFH, with 38.27% of these cases linked to prior glucocorticoid use [4]. Due to ineffective early diagnosis and treatment, the majority of SONFH patients ultimately need total hip replacement surgery [5, 6]. It significantly impacts patients’ quality of life and places a heavy burden on society [7, 8]. Consequently, finding new diagnostic biomarkers may present innovative targets for the early diagnosis and treatment of SONFH.

Coagulation dysfunction is an imbalance between procoagulant and anticoagulant factors, leading to excessive thrombosis while fibrinolytic function is weakened, making it difficult for thrombi to be effectively cleared [9]. More evidence suggests that coagulation dysfunction significantly contributes to the pathogenesis of SONFH [10, 11]. Steroids impair vascular function, notably by preventing the formation of H-type vessels, which interferes with vascular and bone remodeling, worsening bone ischemia, inhibiting bone repair, and accelerating femoral head necrosis [12]. Therefore, further exploration of the role of coagulation-related genes (CRGs) in SONFH is of great significance for understanding the pathogenesis and treatment of SONFH.

The research identified coagulation-related differentially expressed genes linked to SONFH using bioinformatics and conducted an enrichment analysis of their biological process pathways. Then, key genes were selected using PPI and machine learning, and a nomogram prediction model was constructed. In addition, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, Single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, ceRNA regulatory networks and the Drug Signature Database were employed to further explore the potential regulatory mechanisms of the key genes. Finally, the expression levels of key genes were validated through the collection of clinical samples. Therefore, these results may help uncover the role of CRGs in SONFH pathogenesis and offer fresh perspectives for its clinical validation and mechanistic investigation.

Materials and methods

Figure 1 illustrates the study’s flowchart.

Fig. 1Study workflow chart.

Study workflow chart.

Data collection

The GSE123568 dataset was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). This dataset was generated on the GPL15207 ([PrimeView] Affymetrix Human Gene Expression Array) platform and included 30 peripheral blood samples from SONFH patients and 10 control peripheral blood samples. Platform annotation information was extracted using the “GEOquery” package in R, and probe IDs were converted into corresponding gene symbols. The expression matrix was then preprocessed and normalized using the “limma” package in R for subsequent analyses. In addition, 281 coagulation-related genes (CRGs) (Supplementary File S1) were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) using the keyword “coagulation”.

Identification of coagulation-related differentially expressed genes

Differential expression analysis of the GSE123568 dataset was performed using the limma package in R software. To control false positives arising from multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted at the genome-wide level using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Genes with an adjusted P value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.585 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) [13]. The DEGs were then intersected with coagulation-related genes to identify Coagulation-Related Differentially Expressed Genes (CRDEGs), and the overlap was visualized using a Venn diagram.

Functional enrichment analysis

To explore the underlying mechanisms of CRDEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the “clusterProfiler” package in R software [14]. An adjusted P value < 0.05 was considered indicative of significant enrichment, and the results were visualized using the “ggplot2” package in R.

Protein-protein interaction network construction

The Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network was built using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/). CRDEGs were uploaded to STRING, and an interaction score threshold of 0.4 was applied for network analysis. The resulting data were then imported into Cytoscape (version 3.9.1) for PPI network construction and visualization [15]. Additionally, three algorithms from the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape, namely Degree, MCC, and MNC, were employed to identify hub genes. Finally, the hub genes were obtained by taking the intersection of the results from the three algorithms.

Machine learning for key gene selection

Three machine learning algorithms were utilized in this study to identify key genes: Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Support Vector Machine Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE), and Random Forest (RF). The LASSO algorithm was applied using the “glmnet” package in R software, with 10-fold cross-validation employed to identify potential key genes [16]. For the SVM-RFE algorithm, the “e1071” package in R was used, utilizing a 10-fold cross-validation approach combined with a stepwise elimination procedure to select candidate key genes [17]. The Random Forest (RF) algorithm was executed using the “randomForest” package in R, where the importance of candidate key genes was evaluated based on the mean decrease in the Gini index [18]. Ultimately, the key genes were determined by intersecting the results obtained from the three machine learning approaches.

Additionally, sensitivity analyses using alternative DEGs screening thresholds were further performed to examine the robustness of the identified three key genes.

Construction and evaluation of the prediction model

A nomogram-based prediction model was constructed using the three predefined key genes with the rms package in R software. Calibration curve analysis and decision curve analysis (DCA) were performed to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes and the potential clinical utility of the model. The discriminatory ability of the model was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated. The prediction model was further internally validated using bootstrap resampling. Specifically, 1,000 bootstrap resampling iterations with replacement were conducted. In each bootstrap sample, the logistic regression model was refitted, and the AUC was calculated in the original dataset. The bootstrap AUC and its 95% confidence interval were estimated from the bootstrap distribution. Model stability was assessed by comparing the apparent AUC with the mean bootstrap AUC, with a smaller difference indicating better stability.

Supplementary validation of key genes

To further examine the diagnostic relevance of the identified key genes, GSE10311 was used as a supplementary glucocorticoid-related dataset for single-gene ROC analysis. GSE10311 is an in vitro glucocorticoid-related dataset generated on the GPL570 platform (Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array). GSE10311 included 3 human primary osteoblast-like samples treated with dexamethasone for 24 h and 3 untreated human primary osteoblast-like samples at the same time point. Because GSE10311 is a cell-based glucocorticoid-related dataset rather than an independent clinical SONFH cohort, it was used only as a supplementary dataset for single-gene analysis.

Gene set enrichment analysis

To explore the potential pathways linked to the key genes and disease, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the “clusterProfiler” package in R software. A significance threshold of P value < 0.05 was applied, and the top five enriched pathways are presented.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

To evaluate the relative abundance of 28 immune cell types infiltrating different samples, immune infiltration analysis was carried out using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) [19]. Correlation analysis between the key genes and the immune cell types was then performed using the Spearman method, with the results visualized using the “corrplot” package in R software [20].

Construction of the ceRNA regulatory network

Targeted miRNAs for the key genes were predicted using the miRDB database (https://mirdb.org/). The SpongeScan database was then utilized to predict lncRNAs associated with these miRNAs. A ceRNA regulatory network, consisting of mRNA-miRNA-lncRNA interactions, was constructed using Cytoscape software.

Prediction of compounds associated with key genes

To explore predicted compound-gene associations related to the key genes, compound enrichment analysis was performed using the DSigDB database (http://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/). Compound enrichment analysis was subsequently conducted with the ‘clusterProfiler’ and ‘enrichplot’ packages in R software, and the results were visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.9.1).

Clinical sample collection

A total of 8 peripheral blood samples were collected in this study, including samples from 4 SONFH and 4 healthy controls. All patients met the inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria for SONFH samples were: [1] long-term corticosteroid use; [2] clinically and radiographically diagnosed as SONFH; [3] adults aged 18 to 60 years. The criteria for including healthy controls were: [1] no prior corticosteroid use; [2] no clinical or radiographic features of SONFH; [3] adults aged 18 to 60 years. The exclusion criteria were: [1] autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus and ankylosing spondylitis; [2] malignant tumors; [3] musculoskeletal diseases, such as osteoporosis and osteoarthritis; [4] secondary osteonecrosis caused by trauma or infection. The basic clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Supplementary File S2. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee at the Eighth Medical Center of the PLA General Hospital. All participants gave informed consent.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Peripheral blood samples were used to extract total RNA with TRIzol reagent. RNA was then converted into cDNA according to the method provided in the reverse transcriptase reagent kit. SYBR Green qPCR master mix was used to perform reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Each sample was tested in triplicate. The mRNA expression levels of the key genes were normalized to GAPDH, and their relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Statistical analysis

R software (version 4.4.1) and GraphPad Prism 9 were employed for data analysis. Each experiment was conducted with three biological replicates, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Independent t-tests were used to compare groups, with statistical significance set at P value < 0.05.

Results

Identification of CRDEGs

The “limma” package in R was used to analyze the GSE123568 dataset, resulting in the identification of 1794 DEGs, including 1072 upregulated genes and 722 downregulated genes (Fig. 2A, B). Intersecting these DEGs with CRGs identified 49 CRDEGs, including 39 upregulated genes and 10 downregulated genes (Fig. 2C).

Functional enrichment analysis

GO enrichment analysis results showed that, in the biological process (BP) category, these CRDEGs are primarily involved in processes such as regulation of peptidase activity, protein processing, immune response-activating signaling pathway, regulation of inflammatory response, and positive regulation of inflammatory response. In the cellular component (CC) category, these CRDEGs are primarily involved in processes such as secretory granule lumen, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, vesicle lumen, tertiary granule, and ficolin-1-rich granule. In the molecular function (MF) category, these CRDEGs are mainly involved in processes such as endopeptidase activity, cysteine-type endopeptidase activity, cysteine-type peptidase activity, peptidase regulator activity, and serine-type endopeptidase activity (Fig. 3A). Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis results showed that these CRDEGs are primarily involved in pathways such as Apoptosis, Lysosome biogenesis, Complement and coagulation cascades, C-type lectin receptor signaling pathway, and Acute myeloid leukemia (Fig. 3B).

Machine learning for screening key genes

Three machine learning algorithms were applied in this study to identify key genes. The LASSO algorithm identified 4 candidate key genes: ANXA5, LGALS3, LYN, and PECAM1 (Fig. 5A, B). The SVM-RFE algorithm identified 4 candidate key genes: PECAM1, IRF1, LGALS3, and ANXA5 (Fig. 5C, D). The RF algorithm identified 6 candidate key genes: IRF1, CTSS, PECAM1, LGALS3, ANXA5, and LYN (Fig. 5E, F). Finally, the overlap among the results of the three machine learning algorithms identified 3 key genes: PECAM1, LGALS3, and ANXA5 (Fig. 5G).

Sensitivity analysis of key genes

Sensitivity analyses using alternative DEGs screening thresholds demonstrated differential robustness among the three key genes. All three genes were retained under the stricter adjusted P value cutoff, whereas progressively stricter fold-change criteria led to the sequential loss of ANXA5 and then LGALS3. Specifically, PECAM1 and LGALS3 remained at |log2FC| > 0.8, while only PECAM1 was retained at |log2FC| > 1. Collectively, these results indicate that PECAM1 exhibited the greatest stability across alternative analytic thresholds (Supplementary File S3).

Construction and evaluation of the prediction model

A prediction model based on the three key genes was constructed to evaluate the risk of SONFH development (Fig. 6A). The calibration curve showed acceptable agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities, and the bias-corrected curve was generally close to the ideal curve, suggesting favorable model calibration (Fig. 6B). Decision curve analysis indicated that the model provided potential clinical net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 6C). Bootstrap internal validation further showed favorable diagnostic discrimination, with a bootstrap AUC of 0.939 (95% CI: 0.840–0.977) (Fig. 6D).

Immune cell infiltration analysis

To explore differences in immune cell between the SONFH and control groups, ssGSEA analysis was performed. The results showed that, compared to the control group, Immature B cells, Plasmacytoid dendritic cells, MDSC, Neutrophils, T follicular helper cells, central memory CD4 T cells, immature dendritic cells, effector memory CD8 T cells, monocytes, natural killer T cells, natural killer cells, activated B cells, eosinophils, regulatory T cells, central memory CD8 T cells, activated CD8 T cells, type 1 T helper cells and memory B cells showed significant increases; however, CD56bright natural killer cells were notably reduced (Fig. 9A). Furthermore, the results of the correlation analysis indicated that PECAM1 exhibited a positive correlation with eosinophils, immature dendritic cells, monocytes, natural killer cells, neutrophils, plasmacytoid dendritic cells and type 1 T helper cells, while showing a negative correlation with activated CD4 T cells and activated CD8 T cells. In contrast, LGALS3 was negatively correlated with CD56dim natural killer cells and immature dendritic cells. ANXA5 was positively correlated with Central memory CD8 T cells, Gamma delta T cells, Macrophages, Monocytes and Type 2 T helper cells (Fig. 9B). These findings suggest potential associations between SONFH, immune cells, and the identified key genes.

Discussion

SONFH is an orthopedic disease caused by long-term use of glucocorticoids, leading to the degeneration of the femoral head’s active components, ultimately resulting in hip joint pain and dysfunction [21, 22]. The pathological mechanisms of SONFH include coagulation dysfunction, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired angiogenesis, with coagulation dysfunction leading to excessive coagulation of femoral head microvessels and insufficient blood supply, thereby contributing to the onset of the disease [23, 24]. A growing number of studies indicate that SONFH is closely related to coagulation dysfunction, but its pathogenesis remains unclear [25]. Thus, this study seeks to investigate the involvement of CRGs in SONFH using bioinformatics and machine learning approaches, offering new perspectives on the pathogenesis of SONFH.

In this study, 49 CRDEGs were discovered. The functional enrichment analysis revealed that CRDEGs are mainly linked to immune response, blood coagulation, and complement and coagulation cascades. It has been established that the immune response plays a crucial role in the progression of SONFH [26]. Jiang et al. discovered that reducing the number of M1 macrophages helps alleviate arthritis symptoms, which could potentially reduce the risk of SONFH [27]. Interestingly, blood coagulation as well as complement and coagulation cascades have been demonstrated to play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of SONFH [28, 29]. These findings suggest the possible involvement of immune response, blood coagulation and complement and coagulation cascades in SONFH pathogenesis, providing exploratory clues for future experimental validation.

Additionally, 8 hub genes were identified using the PPI network. Subsequently, three key genes were identified using three machine learning methods: PECAM1, LGALS3, and ANXA5. Platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1, CD31), which belongs to the lectin family, is mainly involved in inflammation, platelet aggregation, leukocyte migration and angiogenesis [30, 31]. Research has indicated that CD31 can enhance bone formation, thereby participating in the coupling process of angiogenesis and osteogenesis [32]. During the treatment of SONFH, CD31 not only promotes the proliferation of endothelial cells but also inhibits thrombus formation, indicating that it plays a key role in angiogenesis [33, 34]. Interestingly, the high expression of CD31 and endothelial membrane proteins in H-type vessels can secrete osteogenic factors and participate in the coupling process of angiogenesis and osteogenesis, thereby affecting the development of SONFH [35, 36]. This discovery offers a fresh viewpoint for further investigating the involvement of CD31 in the pathogenesis of SONFH. Galectin-3 (LGALS3, Gal-3), a member of the lectin family, is crucial for processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, cell adhesion, angiogenesis, and tissue repair [37, 38]. Additionally, Gal-3 is a key regulatory factor in bone homeostasis, playing an important role in bone remodeling regulation by affecting bone resorption and bone formation [39, 40]. Interestingly, Gal-3 has been found to play a role in angiogenesis across various diseases, including breast cancer [41], oral lichen planus [42], neuroblastoma [43] and hepatocellular carcinoma [44]. However, no studies have yet indicated the role of LGALS3 in SONFH. Therefore, further exploration of the relationship between LGALS3 and SONFH may help to gain a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of SONFH. Annexin A5 (ANXA5) is a calcium-related protein that is mainly involved in cell growth, differentiation and inflammatory responses [45, 46]. Additionally, ANXA5 is also involved in osteoblast differentiation, bone formation, and angiogenesis [47, 48]. Previous research has demonstrated that silencing ANXA5 notably inhibits the proliferation of osteoprogenitor cells, indicating that ANXA5 could be crucial in the process of bone formation [49]. Interestingly, ANXA5 participates in the blood coagulation cascade by indirectly inhibiting the prothrombin-specific complex, suggesting that ANXA5 could be involved in the regulation of blood coagulation in the development of SONFH [50]. While previous research has suggested a possible connection between ANXA5 and SONFH, the exact mechanism is still unknown. Hence, further investigation is needed to better understand the role of ANXA5 in SONFH. In conclusion, this study identified three key genes that could play a part in the progression of SONFH.

Sensitivity analyses suggested a gradient of robustness across the three key genes, with PECAM1 showing the greatest stability across alternative analytic thresholds, whereas LGALS3 and ANXA5 were more sensitive to stricter fold-change criteria. In addition, we further evaluated the diagnostic performance of the three-gene model using bootstrap internal validation. The predictive nomogram based on PECAM1, LGALS3, and ANXA5 showed favorable diagnostic discrimination, with a bootstrap AUC of 0.939 (95% CI: 0.840–0.977). Although these findings support the potential diagnostic relevance of the three key genes in SONFH, further validation in larger independent clinical cohorts is still required.

In addition, some GSEA-enriched pathways, such as olfactory transduction and porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism, showed limited direct relevance to the known pathophysiology of SONFH. These findings may be partly attributable to the relatively small sample size and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that immune cells are crucial in the pathogenesis of SONFH [51]. In this study, ssGSEA analysis showed that a variety of immune cells, including T cells, neutrophils, and B cells, infiltrate SONFH. These results align with previous studies. Chen et al. observed that the numbers of CD3+, CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, along with the CD4+/CD8 + ratio, were markedly increased in SONFH [52]. Additionally, neutrophils have been demonstrated to be crucial in the onset and progression of SONFH [53]. Interestingly, elevated levels of CD86+ and CD19+ activated B cells may be involved in the development of SONFH [54]. Additionally, this study also found correlations between key genes and immune cells. PECAM1 was associated with neutrophils, LGALS3 with CD56dim natural killer cells and ANXA5 with macrophages. These findings suggest potential associations between SONFH, immune cells and the identified key genes, and may provide clues for further investigation of disease pathogenesis.

Furthermore, DSigDB analysis of the three key genes identified several top-ranked compound terms, which may provide preliminary biological clues to SONFH-related pathological processes. It should be emphasized that the DSigDB results were derived solely from computational enrichment analysis and should not be interpreted as therapeutic recommendations. Some top-ranked compounds, including PTAQUILOSIDE, beryllium sulfate, and RUTHENIUM-related compounds, are pharmacologically implausible or potentially hazardous in clinical settings; therefore, they were not considered actionable therapeutic candidates in this study and were excluded from further therapeutic discussion.

Among the remaining predicted compounds, sodium salicylate has been reported to inhibit ADP-induced platelet aggregation, suggesting a possible link with platelet-mediated hemostatic processes [55]. Similarly, C2-ceramide has also been reported to inhibit ADP-induced platelet aggregation, suggesting its possible involvement in platelet-mediated hemostatic and coagulation-related processes [56]. These platelet aggregation-related findings may be relevant to coagulation dysfunction and microcirculatory impairment in SONFH, providing preliminary clues for future drug-gene interaction studies. However, the pharmacological effects, safety profiles, contraindications, and clinical applicability of these predicted compounds in SONFH were not evaluated in the present study and require further experimental and clinical validation.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size of the available peripheral blood dataset may have limited the robustness and generalizability of our findings. Although bootstrap internal validation was performed to assess model stability and reduce potential overestimation of diagnostic performance, this approach remains an internal validation strategy and cannot replace validation in an independent clinical cohort. Therefore, the diagnostic performance and generalizability of the three-gene model require further confirmation in larger independent peripheral blood cohorts. Second, complete matched data on standard coagulation markers, such as D-dimer, fibrinogen, and thrombin generation-related parameters, were not available in the same cohort. Therefore, we were unable to directly compare the discriminatory performance of the three-gene panel with conventional coagulation markers. Future studies using larger independent clinical cohorts that integrate peripheral blood gene expression profiles and routine coagulation markers are needed to evaluate the added diagnostic value of the three-gene panel beyond conventional coagulation tests. Third, the specific mechanisms of immune cell infiltration in SONFH have not been fully elucidated. Because this study was based on peripheral blood transcriptomic data and no composition-adjusted differential expression analysis was performed, the observed biomarkers and immune-related findings may have been influenced by changes in blood cell composition. Therefore, these findings should be considered preliminary. Future studies integrating experimental evidence and more refined analytical strategies are needed to further clarify the role of immune cells in SONFH. Finally, although the expression of the key genes was preliminarily validated in a small clinical cohort at the mRNA level, the limited sample size and experimental approaches may still affect the strength of the biological conclusions. In particular, protein-level assays were not performed in the current study. Future studies should include protein-level validation in larger clinical cohorts, as well as in vitro and in vivo experiments, to further clarify the biological role and translational potential of these genes in SONFH.

Conclusion

PECAM1, LGALS3, and ANXA5 may serve as potential coagulation-related biomarkers for SONFH. These findings provide a basis for further mechanistic investigation and additional clinical validation.
