Beyond the clinic gate: barriers to childhood immunization access and strategies for improvement at a rural Zimbabwean clinic
Authors: Eustina Shava, Faith Chiwungwe, Miriam Chitura
Published: 2026

Background

Vaccination is one of the most cost-effective public health interventions for preventing infectious disease morbidity and mortality in children under five years of age [1]. The Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), launched by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1974, was designed to ensure equitable access to life-saving vaccines for every child regardless of geographic location or socioeconomic status [2]. Over five decades, EPI has grown from protecting children against six diseases to offering 13 universally recommended vaccines across the life course [3].

Despite this progress, immunization coverage remains below target in many low- and middle-income countries. In 2023, globally 14.5 million children received no vaccines, defined as zero-dose children, and coverage of diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis third dose (DTP3) stood at 84%, below the 90% global target [4]. In sub-Saharan Africa, DTP3 coverage varies from 56% to 97% across countries, and more than 30 million children under five continue to suffer from vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) annually, with over half a million dying each year [4, 5].

In Zimbabwe, EPI services are offered nationwide with support from WHO, GAVI, and UNICEF. Nonetheless, national DTP3 (pentavalent 3) and measles-rubella first dose (MR1) coverage each stood at 83% in 2023, below the 90% target [4]. Goromonzi District in Mashonaland East Province is among five Zimbabwean districts that have not attained pentavalent 3 coverage above 80% according to the District Health Information Software (DHIS2). Within this district, Nyaure Clinic recorded pentavalent 3 coverage of 65%, 62%, and 67% in 2021, 2022, and 2023 respectively, and MR1 coverage of 55%, 50%, and 55% in the same years. The clinic also recorded the highest number of confirmed measles cases in Goromonzi District during the 2022 national measles outbreak.

Low immunization coverage compromises herd immunity, increases the risk of VPD outbreaks, contributes to child disability and death, and places economic strain on health systems [6]. Understanding the specific barriers operating at facility and community level is essential to designing targeted interventions. Previous studies have identified distance, socio-demographic factors, maternal education, religious and cultural beliefs, vaccine availability, and health worker practices as determinants of immunization uptake in sub-Saharan Africa [7–9]. However, evidence on the specific barriers operating at Nyaure Clinic and the strategies most acceptable to the community and health workers in this context is lacking.

This study aimed to: (1) determine caregivers’ knowledge of EPI services; (2) identify barriers to EPI access at Nyaure Clinic; (3) describe health workers’ perspectives on causes of low coverage; and (4) identify strategies to improve EPI coverage at Nyaure Clinic in Goromonzi District, Zimbabwe.

Methods

Study design and setting

A quantitative cross-sectional survey was conducted at Nyaure Clinic in Goromonzi District, Mashonaland East Province, Zimbabwe. Goromonzi is a predominantly rural district located approximately 45 km east of Harare. Nyaure Clinic serves a catchment population of 261 caregivers of children under five, 10 community health workers (CHWs), and 3 nurses. Data were collected over two weeks in October 2024.

Study population and sampling

The target population comprised caregivers of children under five years, CHWs, and nurses within the Nyaure catchment area. From the caregiver population of 261, a sample size of 55 caregivers was determined using the Raosoft sample size calculator. A 90% confidence level and a 10% margin of error (significance level of 0.1) were adopted in preference to the conventional 95% confidence level and 5% margin. This decision reflected the exploratory, descriptive nature of the study, the single-site setting, and the limited resources available for an unfunded bachelor’s-level project; the wider margin yielded a feasible sample while still permitting indicative estimates of the proportions of interest. The less stringent threshold is acknowledged as a limitation. Caregivers were selected using stratified random sampling to ensure proportional representation of sub-groups, with each caregiver having an equal probability of selection. For the health workers, a census approach was used: all 10 CHWs and all 3 nurses serving the catchment area were eligible and were approached. Of these 13 eligible health workers, 6 enrolled and completed questionnaires (3 CHWs and 3 nurses), and all 6 were included in the final analysis; the remaining 7 were unavailable during the two-week data collection window. The final analysed sample was therefore 61 participants: 55 caregivers and 6 health workers.

Data collection instruments

Three structured, self-administered questionnaires were developed specifically for this study: one for nurses, one for caregivers, and one for CHWs (see Additional file 1). Questionnaires contained closed-ended items covering demographic characteristics, knowledge of EPI, barriers to vaccination access, vaccine availability, distance, religious and cultural beliefs, and strategies to improve coverage. The questionnaires were pilot-tested with 10 respondents and refined based on feedback. Research supervisors reviewed the instruments to ensure content validity. Questionnaires were administered in October 2024 following approval from the Medical Research Council of Zimbabwe (MRCZ/B/2795) and the Makumbe Hospital ethical review board.

Data analysis

Completed questionnaires were grouped by similar responses for computation. Data were entered into and analysed using Microsoft Excel. Results are presented as frequencies and percentages in tables and figures. Descriptive statistics are reported for all study variables.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Research Council of Zimbabwe (MRCZ/B/2795) and Makumbe Hospital. Written permission was obtained from the District Medical Officer. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Participants were informed of the study purpose, their right to withdraw at any time, and the confidentiality of their responses. No personal identifying information was collected. Participation was voluntary.

Among caregivers, the 25 to 31 years age group was the most represented. Young mothers aged 18 to 31 years accounted for 58.2% of caregivers. Most caregivers (63.6%) had attained secondary education, and 58.2% were not formally employed. Health workers ranged in age from 32 years to 39 years and above, all had attained at least secondary education, all nurses held a Primary Care Nurse (PCN) qualification, and all health workers had served in the Nyaure catchment area for five or more years.

Knowledge of EPI services

Of the 55 caregivers, 96.4% reported having received information about the importance of EPI services, while 3.6% had not. Figure 1 summarises these findings. Of those who received information, 70.9% cited health facility workers as the primary source. Village health workers and public media accounted for the remaining sources. 91% of caregivers reported having received EPI education during antenatal or postnatal care visits. All 6 health workers (100%) reported discussing EPI coverage with caregivers most often.

Availability of vaccines

Among caregivers, 83.6% reported that vaccines were available at Nyaure Clinic most of the time. However, 14.5% reported that vaccines were sometimes not available. This intermittent non-availability was identified as a contributing factor to low coverage.

Barriers to EPI access

Distance was reported as a barrier to EPI access by 29.1% of caregivers (n = 55) and all 6 health workers (n = 6). The majority of caregivers (70.9%) did not perceive distance as a barrier, though the health workers reported areas within the catchment that they considered far from the facility, served by poor terrain and limited public transport.

Religious and cultural beliefs, particularly those of Apostolic sects, were reported by respondents as contributing to vaccine hesitancy and low coverage. Poverty and political interference were not perceived as barriers by caregivers or health workers. Young maternal age (18 to 31 years accounting for 58.2% of caregivers) was noted as a socio-demographic characteristic of the sample; younger maternal age has been associated with lower vaccination uptake in other settings [10], although the present study did not test this association.

Discussion

This study investigated perceived barriers to EPI access and strategies for improvement at Nyaure Clinic, a rural primary healthcare facility in Goromonzi District, Zimbabwe, where pentavalent 3 coverage has remained between 62% and 67% for three consecutive years. The barriers most commonly reported by respondents were distance, intermittent vaccine shortages, and religious and cultural beliefs, while outreach, health education, and door-to-door vaccination were the most frequently proposed improvement strategies.

Knowledge of EPI services

The high level of EPI awareness among caregivers (96.4%) is encouraging and reflects the active information dissemination role of health workers at Nyaure Clinic. Health facility workers were the primary source of EPI information for 70.9% of caregivers. This finding aligns with Ota et al., who demonstrated that maternal knowledge of vaccine benefits directly influences vaccination uptake and that positive attitudes toward vaccine safety are crucial for coverage improvement [11]. However, 9.1% of caregivers reported receiving EPI information from public media, a source that during the COVID-19 pandemic contributed substantially to vaccine misinformation and hesitancy [12]. This proportion, though small, warrants targeted media engagement strategies.

High knowledge levels did not translate directly into high coverage at Nyaure Clinic, suggesting that structural and social barriers may operate independently of awareness. This is consistent with Ogundele et al., who found that incomplete childhood vaccination in West African settings was predicted by supply-side and community-level factors alongside caregiver knowledge [7].

Barriers to EPI access

Distance was reported as a barrier by 29.1% of caregivers and all health workers. The discrepancy between these proportions may reflect the health workers’ direct knowledge of geographically remote villages within the catchment area. Nyaure Clinic is in a rural setting served by poor road infrastructure and limited public transport. This perception accords with evidence that gaps in health system provision contribute to unmet need for preventive services, particularly where infrastructure and service availability are limited [13]. The WHO Reach Every Child (REC) strategy specifies that children within three kilometres of a facility should access routine services at the facility, those within five kilometres should be served through outreach, and those beyond ten kilometres through mobile clinics [14]. The persistent coverage deficit at Nyaure Clinic raises the possibility that these thresholds are not being met for a proportion of the catchment population, although the study did not directly measure travel distances.

Intermittent vaccine shortages, reported by 14.5% of caregivers, represent a modifiable supply-side barrier. Vaccine stock-outs disrupt immunization schedules and reduce caregiver confidence in facility services. District-level EPI coordinators bear responsibility for ensuring consistent cold chain maintenance and stock availability at peripheral facilities.

Religious and cultural beliefs, specifically those of Apostolic sects, were reported by respondents as contributing to vaccine hesitancy. This is in keeping with evidence that parental knowledge, attitudes, and cultural or religious convictions shape vaccine acceptance and decision-making [15]. Apostolic sect vaccine refusal has been documented in prior Zimbabwean literature and represents a community-specific barrier that would require culturally sensitive engagement strategies to address.

Contrary to some of the wider literature, poverty and political interference were not perceived as barriers by either caregivers or health workers at this site. This contrasts with evidence linking health system financing and socioeconomic disadvantage to unmet need for preventive care [13]. A possible explanation is that the predominantly rural, subsistence-farming population in Nyaure has flexible time availability for clinic visits, and that financial cost is not a direct barrier since EPI services are offered free of charge. This context-specific observation underscores the importance of locally grounded research rather than generalising from broader literature.

The sample comprised a high proportion of young caregivers (58.2% aged 18 to 31 years). Younger maternal age has been associated with vaccination status elsewhere, for example by Akanpaabadai et al. in a rural Ghanaian district, potentially due to lower appreciation of vaccine-preventable disease consequences and less accumulated experience with the health system [10]. The present descriptive study did not test for such an association, and this observation should be interpreted as a characteristic of the sample rather than a determinant identified by the study.

Strategies to improve EPI coverage

Both caregivers and health workers reported outreach programmes, health education, and door-to-door vaccination as priority strategies. These preferences align with the broader literature. Siddiqui et al. identified outreach, educational interventions, reminder systems, and multicomponent approaches as effective strategies for improving vaccination coverage [16]. In a regional case study, Micek et al. found that consistent outreach and strong frontline health worker engagement were among the critical success factors for routine immunization performance in Zambia [17]. At a system level, Zhu et al. highlight that sustained investment and supply reliability are needed to accelerate immunization progress in middle-income countries [18].

A small number of respondents, specifically the six health workers, expressed support for legislation criminalising vaccine hesitancy. This view was confined to this subgroup and should be read as a perception reflecting frontline frustration with persistent refusal among some Apostolic sect communities, rather than a finding or a policy position supported by the study. The study generated no evidence on the effectiveness, acceptability, or appropriateness of punitive legislation, and we therefore make no recommendation on this matter. Any consideration of legislative or mandatory approaches in low-resource settings would require careful attention to community trust, human rights, and enforcement capacity, and would need to be informed by dedicated research rather than the perceptions reported here.

Some respondents also proposed building additional clinics so that more community members would live within three kilometres of a facility, in line with the WHO REC strategy, reflecting a perception of a structural infrastructure deficit in the catchment area. Such an approach would carry resource implications that would require assessment and prioritisation by health authorities.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample was drawn from a single clinic catchment area, which restricts generalisability to other rural Zimbabwean or sub-Saharan African settings. Second, the sample size of 61 is small, and it was calculated using a 90% confidence level and a 10% margin of error rather than the conventional 95% confidence level and 5% margin; this less stringent threshold widens the uncertainty around the reported proportions, so findings should be treated as indicative rather than definitive. Third, self-administered questionnaires with closed-ended items may introduce social desirability bias and precluded exploration of the depth and nuance of participant experiences; a mixed-methods design incorporating in-depth interviews would have strengthened the evidence. Fourth, the study relied on caregiver self-report for vaccine receipt and perceived barriers, which may not accurately reflect actual vaccination status or the full range of barriers operating in the catchment area. Fifth, the cross-sectional design captures a single point in time and cannot establish causal relationships between the reported barriers and low coverage. Sixth, the health worker sub-group was small (n = 6), limiting the weight that can be placed on findings specific to that group, including the expressed support for legislation on vaccine hesitancy. Future research should use a larger, multi-site design and incorporate qualitative methods to capture caregiver and community narratives around vaccine hesitancy.

Conclusions

EPI coverage at Nyaure Clinic has remained persistently below national and global targets for three consecutive years. This study identified distance to the health facility, intermittent vaccine shortages, and religious and cultural beliefs as the barriers most commonly reported by caregivers and health workers in this rural Zimbabwean setting. Notably, high caregiver awareness of EPI benefits did not correspond with adequate coverage, suggesting that structural and social barriers may operate independently of knowledge, though the cross-sectional design of this study does not permit causal conclusions.

Health workers and caregivers consistently endorsed outreach programmes, health education, door-to-door vaccination, and CHW expansion as priority improvement strategies. These findings suggest that policymakers and district health managers should consider strengthening outreach services, ensuring consistent vaccine supply at peripheral facilities, and investing in healthcare infrastructure to reduce travel distances. District EPI coordinators, nursing managers, and community health workers each have roles to play in translating these strategies into coverage improvements. Given the study’s limitations, further research using larger, multi-site designs is needed before broader recommendations can be made with confidence.
