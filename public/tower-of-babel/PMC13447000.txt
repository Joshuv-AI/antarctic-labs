Hepatitis B in the vaccine era: persistent burden and clinical implications
Authors: Cherechi Nwabueze, Eunice Omeludike, Ridwan Lawal, Kedife Nwabueze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a leading global cause of advanced liver disease and imposes a substantial economic burden. Early prevention is critical, as perinatally acquired HBV carries a high risk of progression to cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Hepatitis B vaccination has been proven effective in preventing cancer, yet coverage of the complete vaccine series remains suboptimal. This implementation gap perpetuates the clinical burden, manifesting decades later as hepatic decompensation and liver cancer. This narrative review synthesizes the current HBV landscape, highlighting how gaps in vaccination policy and delivery contribute to real-world gastrointestinal outcomes. By reframing HBV vaccination as a core component of gastrointestinal and hepatology practice, clinicians can play a pivotal role in preventing cirrhosis and HCC while improving population health.Keywords: Hepatitis B virus, Early detection of cancer, Liver neoplasms, Global health

BACKGROUND

Chronic infection with hepatitis B virus (HBV) remains a major global cause of advanced liver disease, accounting for a substantial proportion of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). With an estimated 254 million people living with chronic infection and over 820,000 annual deaths, HBV accounts for 37% of all liver cancer deaths globally, making it the single leading etiology [1,2,3]. The economic impact is equally profound, with HBV-attributable noncommunicable diseases estimated to have cost $41 billion in 2021 and projected to reach $84 billion by 2035 [4].

The natural history of HBV infection underscores the importance of early prevention. Up to 40% of men and 15% of women with perinatally acquired HBV infection will die from cirrhosis or HCC. In this context, the hepatitis B vaccine remains one of the most remarkable achievements in preventive medicine and the first vaccine proven to prevent cancer [5,6]. Recombinant hepatitis B vaccines induce protective antibody responses in more than 95% of infants, children, and young adults after the complete series, with long-term follow-up studies demonstrating protection lasting at least 30 years due to immune memory, even as antibody titers decline [5]. Despite this proven efficacy, critical implementation gaps perpetuate the global burden of HBV. Although 98% of countries have adopted universal infant HBV vaccination, coverage of the complete 3-to-4 dose series reaches only 84% globally, and timely birth-dose (hepatitis B birth-dose vaccine) coverage remains at 45% as of 2024, being as high as 79% in the World Health Organization (WHO) Western Pacific Region, while it is estimated at only 17% in the WHO African Region [7].

Consequently, the clinical burden of HBV continues to be borne by gastroenterologists and hepatologists who manage downstream consequences, portal hypertension, hepatic decompensation, and liver cancer, often decades after the initial failure of prevention. These failures occur across multiple levels of the HBV prevention cascade. Obstetricians and midwives are central to timely administration of the birth-dose vaccine, which remains the most critical intervention for preventing vertical transmission. Pediatricians and primary care providers are responsible for ensuring completion of infant vaccination series and implementing catch-up immunization in unvaccinated children and adults. Public health authorities, in turn, play a decisive role in vaccine policy, financing, supply chain infrastructure, and equitable delivery systems. Breakdowns at any of these levels propagate forward, ultimately shifting the burden of disease to subspecialty care. Achieving the WHO goal of eliminating viral hepatitis as a public health threat by 2030 requires coordinated, system-wide interventions that address these structural inequities simultaneously. Without aggressive intervention, annual global deaths from HBV are projected to increase by 39% between 2015 and 2030 [2]. Conversely, effective interventions could reduce HBV-related liver cancer mortality by approximately 30% by 2040 [8]. However, gaps in birth-dose vaccination, inequities in vaccine delivery, incomplete adult immunization, and vaccine nonresponse in high-risk populations have collectively limited the population-level impact of an otherwise highly successful intervention [9]. As a result, preventable infections acquired early in life or adulthood continue to progress silently to cirrhosis and HCC.

This narrative review is designed as a clinician-focused synthesis of the current HBV landscape, with explicit integration of stakeholder responsibilities across the prevention continuum. Rather than providing an exhaustive systematic appraisal, we connect specific failures in upstream actors to real-world gastrointestinal outcomes encountered in clinical practice. By tracing the pathway from missed immunization opportunities, whether at birth, during migration, or in immunocompromised adults, to advanced liver disease, this review aims to reframe HBV vaccination as a core component of gastrointestinal and hepatology practice. This perspective is essential not only for population health but also for the prevention of cirrhosis and liver cancer encountered daily at the bedside.

METHODOLOGY

To identify evidence for this narrative review, a comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and ScienceDirect to identify relevant evidence relevant to HBV infection, vaccination, elimination efforts, and clinical outcomes published between January 2010 and December 2025. The search strategy employed keywords including “Hepatitis B virus,” “Hepatitis B vaccine,” “vaccination coverage,” “WHO 2030 elimination targets,” and “hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance.” Studies were selected based on their relevance to the global HBV burden, implementation gaps in vaccination, and clinical management strategies. To enhance the clinical and policy relevance of this review, priority was given to systematic reviews, meta-analyses, clinical guidelines (including those from the WHO and major hepatology societies), and large-scale epidemiological studies. To ensure a global perspective, epidemiological reports from WHO, and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) were also integrated. Articles were excluded if they were not published in English, lacked peer-reviewed data, or did not focus on the vaccination-prevention-clinical outcomes HBV cascade. The identified literature was synthesized narratively to map the role of multidisciplinary stakeholders in overcoming implementation gaps. Given the narrative nature of this work, formal meta-analysis was not performed. Instead, evidence was synthesized qualitatively, with emphasis on identifying recurring themes, implementation gaps across the HBV prevention cascade, and the linkage between upstream vaccination failures and downstream gastrointestinal and hepatologic outcomes.

GLOBAL EPIDEMIOLOGY AND HEPATITIS B VACCINATION INEQUITIES

The global epidemiology of hepatitis B reveals profound geographic and socioeconomic disparities that persist despite the availability of an effective vaccine. With about 296 million people living with chronic HBV infection globally, the burden is heavily concentrated in specific regions. The highest prevalence remains in the Western Pacific (7.1%) and African (6.5%) regions, while the lowest prevalence reported in the European region (1.1%). Notably, about 17 countries account for over 75% of the global HBV burden, with China, India, Indonesia, Nigeria, and Ethiopia alone representing over 50% of all hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)-positive individuals worldwide (Fig. 1) [9,10,11].

This unequal distribution reflects deep-rooted inequities in vaccination access, healthcare infrastructure, and social determinants of health that threaten the WHO 2030 elimination goals. High-endemicity regions are characterized by a cycle of perinatal and early childhood transmission, in which up to 90% of infections acquired during infancy progress to chronic disease. Early-life infection therefore establishes a long latency period during which individuals remain asymptomatic while progressive hepatic injury accumulates, ultimately manifesting as cirrhosis or HCC decades later [5,6,9,10,11,12].

In contrast, low prevalence regions experience predominantly adult-acquired infections through sexual contact or injection drug use, with fewer than 5% progressing to chronic infection. This fundamental difference in transmission dynamics has important implications for prevention strategies. While universal infant vaccination has been a transformative success in high-burden regions, low-endemicity countries must pivot towards aggressive adult vaccination and screening strategies, particularly for migrant populations arriving from endemic areas [10,11,12,13,14,15].

Hence, the global HBV burden serves as a mirror of broader inequality. The disproportionate mortality seen is a direct consequence of service gaps where the lack of birth-dose infrastructure, high costs of antiviral therapy, and fragile cold-chain logistics transform a preventable infection into a fatal malignancy [12]. Therefore, understanding that HBV burden mirrors structural inequality rather than biological susceptibility is essential for advocating for the systemic changes required to meet WHO 2030 targets.

THE BIRTH-DOSE VACCINE: THE MISSED FIRST OPPORTUNITY

Despite 98% of countries adopting universal infant HBV vaccination policies by 2020, substantial implementation gaps continue to perpetuate global health inequities. While global coverage of the 3-dose series has reached approximately 84%, timely birth-dose coverage remains around 45% worldwide (Fig. 2) [7]. This gap is particularly consequential as the birth-dose administration within 24 hours of delivery is critical for preventing mother-to-child transmission, the primary driver of chronic HBV infection in high-prevalence regions [6,7,8,9,10]. These gaps reflect failures across the HBV prevention cascade, involving multiple stakeholders whose roles are interdependent. Obstetricians and midwives are responsible for ensuring timely birth-dose administration at delivery, particularly in facility-based births. Pediatricians and primary care providers are central to completion of the vaccine series and implementation of catch-up vaccination strategies in unvaccinated children, adolescents, and adults. Public health authorities oversee vaccine policy, financing, supply chains, and integration of maternal and immunization services. Breakdowns at any of these levels, whether due to missed opportunities at birth, incomplete follow-up vaccination, or systemic infrastructure failures propagate forward and ultimately manifest as chronic liver disease managed by subspecialists.

The consequences of these failures are especially pronounced in Africa, where overall hepatitis B vaccine coverage (3-dose hepatitis B vaccine series) rose only minimally from 68.1% in 2010 to 69.4% in 2022 [13]. Coverage remains below 40% across large areas of the Sahel, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Angola, and northern Mozambique, with several countries reporting districts where coverage is less than half their national average. In 2022, birth-dose coverage in the WHO African Region stood at just 14%, the lowest globally, despite this region having the highest prevalence of HBV infection among children under 5 years of age (1.7% compared to 0.7% globally) [10,11,12,13]. Current projections suggest that current trajectories will likely fall short of WHO elimination targets; mean African vaccine coverage is expected to reach only 77% by 2030, with fewer than ten countries on track to achieve the 90% target [13].

HEALTH SYSTEM BARRIERS IN LOW- AND MIDDLE-INCOME COUNTRIES (LMICs)

At the health system level, these inequities are driven by identifiable and modifiable barriers. In high-burden settings like Nigeria, studies identify common reasons for missed birth-dose vaccination: vaccine unavailability at place of delivery, delivery not occurring on immunization day, lack of awareness regarding the timing of the birth-dose vaccination, and long travel distances to health facilities. Furthermore, in many LMICs, timely birth-dose prophylaxis is often restricted to the private sector, forcing families to pay out-of-pocket for a life-saving intervention. The challenge is magnified in regions where home delivery is customary and linkage between maternal and immunization services is weak [15].

SOCIOECONOMIC AND RACIAL INEQUITIES IN HIGH-INCOME SETTINGS

In high-income countries, the burden shifts but the theme of inequality remains. The epidemiology of HBV has shifted toward adult-acquired infections, driven primarily by sexual transmission, healthcare-associated transmission, injection drug use, and migration from endemic regions. HBV incidence and care insufficiencies are concentrated within populations marginalized by migration status, housing insecurity, and social exclusion. Among community-dwelling adults, the likelihood of initiating vaccination is strongly associated with being younger, identifying as White (versus Black or Hispanic), having health insurance, and possessing higher levels of education. Racial and ethnic disparities remain evident, for example, in 2019, the rate of HBV infection among non-Hispanic Black adults in the United States was approximately 3 times that of Asian or Pacific Islander adults and nearly twice that of Hispanic adults [14,15,16,17]. These disparities reflect structural inequities rather than biological differences.

ADULT VACCINATION: CLOSING THE IMMUNIZATION GAP

While universal infant vaccination has been the cornerstone of global HBV prevention, adult vaccination remains a critical yet underutilized component of the prevention cascade, particularly in low-endemic settings and among high-risk populations. In contrast to pediatric immunization programs, which are often structured and policy-driven, adult vaccination is frequently opportunistic, fragmented, and highly dependent on healthcare access and provider initiative. In high-income countries, the epidemiology of HBV has shifted toward adult-acquired infections, driven primarily by sexual transmission, injection drug use, and migration from endemic regions. As a result, adult vaccination plays a central role in preventing incident infections and interrupting transmission chains. Multiple barriers contribute to low uptake, including lack of awareness among both patients and providers, missed opportunities during healthcare encounters, absence of systematic vaccination programs, and structural barriers such as cost, insurance status, and fragmented care delivery. In contrast to childhood immunization, adult vaccination is rarely integrated into routine care pathways, resulting in significant gaps in coverage even among high-risk groups [16,17,18].

STRATEGIC INTERVENTIONS AND PRIORITY ACTIONS

Addressing the social determinants of health requires a shift from passive policy to intervention. Strategies must be effective across multiple domains, including community education, screening, immunization access, linkage to care, and upstream prevention. Strategies that have proven effective include free or reduced-cost services, community outreach, culturally tailored education, and harm reduction programs. High-resolution subnational mapping of vaccination coverage and dropout rates may further guide precision immunization efforts to close persistent gaps. Priority actions include expanding facility-based delivery in high-burden regions, strengthening cold chain infrastructure, eliminating out-of-pocket costs, implementing catch-up vaccination programs, and developing community-based strategies for populations with limited healthcare access.

Ultimately, the HBV global burden is an equity crisis. The tools for prevention are highly effective and widely endorsed, yet their benefits remain unevenly distributed along lines of geography, income, and social marginalization. Without sustained intervention, annual global HBV-related deaths are projected to rise by 39% by 2030 [2]. Closing these gaps will require sustained political commitment, reliable financing, and deliberate efforts to reach the populations and regions left furthest behind, thereby interrupting the earliest and most preventable pathway to chronic liver disease. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

Gastroenterologists occupy a critical position in addressing the hepatitis B paradox, serving as both primary diagnosticians and long-term care coordinators for a disease that remains profoundly undertreated despite its prevalence. The clinical implications of HBV management span the entire patient journey, from initial case identification and complex treatment decision-making to the rigorous, lifelong monitoring required to prevent poor hepatic outcomes.

Screening recommendations have evolved substantially in recent years toward a more aggressive, no-miss approach to case identification. The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening adults at increased risk for HBV infection, including persons born in countries with HBsAg prevalence ≥2%, US-born individuals with parents from regions where prevalence ≥8%, and those with behavioral risk factors. The CDC has expanded this guidance to recommend universal one-time screening for all adults aged 18 years and older regardless of risk, with periodic testing for individuals with ongoing factors (Fig. 4) [18,19].

In clinical practice, this translates to the routine screening of any patients with chronic liver disease, unexplained aminotransferase elevations, or inflammatory bowel disease requiring immunosuppression, and those undergoing evaluation for hepatobiliary disorders. Screening should employ a 3-test panel (HBsAg, hepatitis B surface antibody [anti-HBs], and hepatitis B core antibody [anti-HBc]) to provide comprehensive assessment of current infection, immunity, and prior exposure. This approach not only identifies chronic infection but also detects isolated anti-HBc positivity, which may indicate prior exposure or occult infection, both of which carry a significant risk for HBV reactivation during immunosuppressive therapy or chemotherapy [20,21].

Pregnancy represents the most critical intervention point in the HBV prevention cascade. Mother-to-child transmission remains the dominant route of chronic HBV infection globally, particularly in high-prevalence regions. Without intervention, transmission rates can exceed 90% in infants born to highly viremic mothers. Success at this stage relies on the coordination of obstetricians and midwives, who are responsible for HBsAg screening and the initiation of antiviral prophylaxis. Current guidelines recommend that all HBsAg-positive pregnant women undergo HBV DNA quantification during pregnancy to assess transmission risk. For women with high viral loads (typically ≥200,000 IU/mL), antiviral prophylaxis with tenofovir is recommended during the third trimester to reduce vertical transmission. Equally essential is the role of pediatricians in ensuring timely neonatal prophylaxis, including administration of the birth-dose hepatitis B vaccine within 24 hours of delivery, with or without hepatitis B immunoglobulin, depending on maternal status [20,21]. Failure at this stage represents the single most consequential missed opportunity in HBV prevention.

Despite the availability of potent nucleos(t)ide analogs (NAs), the global care cascade reveals profound attrition at every stage. As of 2022, only 13% of the 254 million people living with chronic HBV were aware of their diagnosis, and a mere 3% were receiving treatment [1]. This gap persists even in well-resourced settings. In the United States, data shows that only 25% of patients receive the recommended annual alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and HBV DNA monitoring. Surveillance for HCC is equally deficient, with fewer than 40% of high-risk patients, including those with cirrhosis and certain high-risk non-cirrhotic populations [20,21,22]. Across multiple studies, specialist involvement remains the strongest predictor of comprehensive evaluation, treatment initiation, and appropriate surveillance, underscoring the essential role of gastroenterologists in strengthening the HBV care cascade.

Treatment decisions have evolved significantly in recent years, with increasing emphasis on earlier and broader antiviral therapy. While traditional management frameworks categorize chronic HBV infection into phases: immune tolerant, immune-active, inactive carrier (hepatitis B e-antigen [HBeAg]-negative), and reactivation based on HBeAg status, ALT, and HBV DNA levels, current guidelines from the WHO and major liver societies advocate for simplified and expanded treatment criteria to improve access and reduce disease progression. All patients with cirrhosis and detectable HBV DNA should receive antiviral therapy regardless of ALT level or HBeAg status [19,20,21,22]. These approaches prioritize clinical factors such as viral load, fibrosis stage, age, and comorbidities over rigid phase classification, reflecting the recognition that disease progression can occur across all phases and that delayed treatment contributes to preventable morbidity. Among non-cirrhotic patients, treatment is recommended for those with immune-active disease characterized by elevated ALT and significant viremia, or for those with at least moderate fibrosis on non-invasive assessment or biopsy. Recent trials have demonstrated that earlier antiviral initiation in patients with moderate-to-high viremia, even in the setting of normal or minimally elevated ALT, reduces disease progression and serious clinical events. These findings have informed updated recommendations from the WHO, which expand treatment eligibility based more heavily on HBV DNA thresholds, age, and clinical context rather than strict ALT levels alone. Broader eligibility has the potential to substantially increase the proportion of patients receiving therapy and reduce long-term complications [20,22]. First-line therapies remain high-potency, low-resistance NAs, including tenofovir disoproxil fumarate or tenofovir alafenamide, and entecavir. Given that functional cure (HBsAg loss) is uncommon, treatment is often long-term and frequently lifelong in patients with advanced disease [20,21,22,23].

HCC surveillance represents one of the most consequential gaps in HBV management. Current guidelines recommend ultrasound, with or without alpha-fetoprotein measurement, every 6 months for all patients with cirrhosis and for selected high-risk non-cirrhotic individuals, including Asian men over 40 years, Asian women over 50 years, persons of African ancestry over 20 years, and those with a family history of HCC [20,21]. Yet surveillance rates remain suboptimal, limiting early tumor detection and curative intervention. Hence, to optimize hepatitis B care, gastroenterologists should implement structured, evidence-based strategies, including standardized screening protocols, complete baseline staging with fibrosis assessment and HBV DNA quantification, simplified, evidence-based treatment algorithms, proactive HCC surveillance tracking, and integrated vaccination and revaccination plans for susceptible patients [23,24]. By strengthening each link in the care cascade from identification to long-term monitoring, gastroenterologists can play a decisive role in reducing the burden of HBV-related cirrhosis and HCC and in closing the gap between vaccine-era potential and clinical reality.

CONCLUSION

The persistence of the global hepatitis B burden in an era of highly effective vaccination is a profound implementation failure rather than a biological or technological one. This review has traced the HBV prevention and care cascade to reveal that the clinical burden managed by gastroenterologists is the direct consequence of missed opportunities by upstream stakeholders. The gap between infant series completion and timely birth-dose administration remains the most significant driver of chronic infection globally. Closing this gap requires a multisectoral commitment: obstetricians and midwives must prioritize birth-dose delivery; pediatricians and primary care providers must ensure catch-up vaccination and universal adult screening; and public health authorities must eliminate the socioeconomic and structural barriers that perpetuate vaccine inequity.

For the gastroenterologist, the shift toward universal one-time screening and simplified, expanded treatment criteria provides a powerful framework to interrupt disease progression. By serving as both a primary diagnostician and a coordinator of long-term surveillance, the subspecialist acts as the final safeguard in the care cascade. Ultimately, achieving the WHO 2030 elimination targets will require moving beyond passive clinical management toward an integrated model of care that emphasizes aggressive case identification and a commitment to health equity. Only by addressing these structural and clinical gaps simultaneously can we shift the trajectory of HBV from a rising cause of global mortality to a preventable disease of the past.
