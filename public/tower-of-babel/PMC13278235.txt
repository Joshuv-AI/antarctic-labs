Free Vertical Rectus Abdominis Musculocutaneous Flap for Reconstruction of Large Defect After Re-recurrent Sarcoma Resection
Authors: Tadashi Iwai, Masaya Aoyama, Makoto Ieguchi, Kiyohito Takamatsu, Hidetomi Terai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Wide resection of advanced recurrent high-grade soft tissue sarcomas often leaves large defects that are challenging to reconstruct. The options for defect closure vary. A free vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous (VRAM) flap could be an alternative for closing a significantly large defect, particularly for recurrence after wide resection using a pedicled flap. We present the case of a 79-year-old man with recurrent soft tissue sarcoma in the right upper arm after wide resection using a pedicled latissimus dorsi flap, which was treated with wide resection and subsequent defect closure using a free VRAM flap. The VRAM flap was initially often used to reconstruct the chest and abdominal walls; therefore, there have been few reported uses for upper arm defects. The repair site defect was viable even with a less aesthetic donor site, and all defects were closed without any signs of infection. The free VRAM flap is a promising option for large defect closure in the upper arm region, particularly after wide resection in cases where a pedicled flap is unavailable.

Contemporary treatment strategies for soft tissue sarcomas have focused on limb salvage; however, approximately 20% of patients develop local recurrence by 10 years after surgery alone.1 Moreover, the disease-free interval of some patients may be prolonged or cured after local recurrence resection.1 Herein, we describe a rare case of a free vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous (VRAM) flap for the reconstruction of a large upper arm defect (approximately 30 × 8 cm defect) after wide resection of a re-recurrent soft tissue sarcoma.

CASE PRESENTATION

Approximately 5 years ago, at the first visit to the plastic surgery department, a 79-year-old Japanese man complained of a small mass located in the right upper outer arm. Although this mass was marginally resected, the pathological diagnosis was myxofibrosarcoma; therefore, an additional wide resection and split-thickness skin grafting from the left groin were performed based on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, histological examination revealed incomplete resection (R1 resection). Although chemoradiotherapy was recommended, this patient needed to be treated for seizures and epilepsy after a stroke; therefore, his upper arm remained untreated after the nonradical surgery. Three years later, on MRI, the tumor appeared as a heterogeneous mass (38 × 27 × 37 mm) in the right upper arm with a solid component and a cyst with a large portion of mucus. Furthermore, tumor progression from the biceps brachii to the brachialis muscle was observed. Although the patient underwent wide tumor resection using a pedicled latissimus dorsi flap, R1 resection was revealed again. Consequently, the patient decided to undergo radiotherapy (60 Gy) after nonradical surgery. Regrettably, approximately 1.5 years later, sarcoma recurrence occurred from the shoulder to the upper arm, which rapidly enlarged for 1 month (Fig. 1).

Clinical photograph of the patient after the second local recurrence.

In our orthopedic department, MRI revealed a large heterogeneous mass (132 × 71 × 80 mm) and an additional mass (31 × 30 × 82 mm) in the right upper arm. Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging showed predominantly strongly enhancing masses. The additional tumor involved the radial nerve on the distal side of the upper arm, although no sensory or motor disorder was observed before reoperation. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography showed no metastases, so the patient underwent a repeat wide tumor resection using a free VRAM flap (end-to-side anastomosis of the brachial artery, with 2 veins anastomosed in a direct end-to-end fashion) (Fig. 2). Harvesting of the anterior layer of rectus sheath was minimized, and the mesh was inset on top of the sutured fascia for reinforcement against abdominal hernia. After that, the mesh was also circumferentially secured to the fascia using nonabsorbable sutures. Finally, a subcutaneous suture was performed. Histopathologic examination, including margin status, revealed a large amount of cellular spindle cell proliferation in the myxoid background, which showed negative stump margins (×40, respectively). (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the histopathologic images, including the margin status, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E978.) At a postoperative outpatient clinic visit 2 months after hospital discharge, the flap remained viable without any signs of infection. Additionally, contrast-enhanced MRI showed no evidence of local recurrence. Due to radial nerve resection, the patient needed a full cock-up hand splint for postoperative radial nerve palsy to stabilize and support the wrist in an extended position. Therefore, the postoperative Musculoskeletal Tumor Society score was 20 out of 30 (66.7%). For oncological follow-up, the patient will be examined every 3 months for 2 years postoperatively, every 6 months for 3–5 years postoperatively, and annually after 5 years postoperatively. Physical examinations (including neurological evaluation), right upper arm contrast-enhanced MRI for follow-up of local recurrence, and chest computed tomography for follow-up of lung metastases will be performed at every follow-up visit.

DISCUSSION

Reoperation strategies for recurrent large sarcomas in the upper arm area always cause controversy between shoulder disarticulation and limb-salvage surgery.2 Limb-salvage surgery may have difficulty achieving adequate oncological margins compared with shoulder disarticulation; however, a previous report indicated that the oncological outcome of amputation was not statistically different from that of limb-salvage surgery.3 Therefore, salvage surgery (repeat wide resection) was chosen based on informed consent in surgical practice. However, a wide resection often leaves a large defect, necessitating further reconstruction procedures.

The most essential factor was selecting an appropriate flap. Humeral periosteum excision (periosteal stripping) was performed to obtain a wide margin (Fig. 2A). Such extensive excision disrupts the vascularity of the outer bone cortex and may impair both bone and wound healing. In addition, in this older adult patient with a history of stroke, vascular flow after radiotherapy was suspected to be compromised. Furthermore, the prevalence of vascular anomalies in the anterolateral thigh has been reported to be approximately 5%,4 whereas such anomalies were very rare in the VRAM flap. Therefore, we considered it more appropriate to select a large musculocutaneous flap rather than a fasciocutaneous flap (eg, anterolateral thigh flap) to ensure flap survival without postoperative complications based on recent reports.5,6 Pedicled muscle flaps include the pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, and rectus abdominis flaps.7–9 In our patient, a latissimus dorsi flap was used in a previous wide resection procedure. Furthermore, the pectoralis major flap was unsuitable owing to its inability to cover such a large defect. Although the rectus abdominis flap was adequate for covering this wide range of defects, a free flap was required because of the distance between the wide resection area and the harvesting site. Therefore, we performed a free VRAM flap transposition to fill the 30-cm defect in the upper arm. The VRAM flap contains relatively abundant tissue that may allow bulk transfer to the recipient site.9 Compared with the transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous flap, the VRAM flap is associated with a better blood supply owing to its advantage of maximum capture of perforating vessels at the defect.9

Total flap loss, partial flap necrosis, abscess formation, skin necrosis, and intraoperative arterial thrombosis have been reported as major perioperative surgical complications of free flaps,10 whereas, in our patient, the flap was viable without skin necrosis or infection. The limitation of this case report is the lack of long-term follow-up. However, the VRAM flap may be a promising method for closing a large upper arm defect in cases where pedicled flaps are not available.
