Sex-specific adaptive strategies and rhizosphere microbiome responses to drought stress in Bouteloua dactyloides
Authors: Chuwen Huang, Zijian Ding, Yucheng Guo, Xiaoyu Ma, Jiahuan Li, Lizhu Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrought is becoming more frequent and severe under global climate change. Bouteloua dactyloides (Nutt.) (hereafter, B. dactyloides) is a dioecious, drought-tolerant warm-season turfgrass, but whether males and females use different adaptive strategies under drought remains unclear. We conducted a pot experiment to compare male and female plants under well-watered (90% field capacity) and drought-stressed (30% field capacity) conditions. We hypothesized that male and female B. dactyloides plants would exhibit sex-specific adaptive strategies in physiological traits and rhizosphere microbial communities under drought stress. Drought increased malondialdehyde and proline contents and enhanced superoxide dismutase and ascorbate peroxidase activities in both sexes, while peroxidase activity decreased. Under well-watered conditions, females had a higher drought resistance index than males, whereas no significant sex difference was detected under drought. Sex-specific responses were still evident; females showed a higher root-shoot ratio, whereas males exhibited increased catalase (CAT) activity. Drought and plant sex also jointly altered rhizosphere microbial communities. Drought increased fungal alpha diversity only in males, whose rhizospheres were enriched in Basidiomycota and Glomeromycota. Drought suppressed bacterial aerobic metabolism and sulfur respiration functions, as well as saprotrophic and pathogenic fungi in both sexes. Notably, male rhizospheres were significantly enriched in symbiotic fungi, particularly arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF). Overall, female B. dactyloides mainly enhances drought adaptation through morphological plasticity, whereas males rely more on a microbiome-mediated strategy centered on AMF recruitment. Introduction

Global climate change is one of the greatest threats to ecosystem functions in the 21st century, with more frequent and intense regional droughts projected for many areas, particularly arid and semi-arid regions [1–3]. This challenge imposes significant pressure on water availability, especially in water-intensive agricultural and managed ecosystems [4]. As a typical water-intensive managed ecosystem, turfgrass systems are widely used in urban and suburban landscapes for their recreational, aesthetic, and ecological services [5]. Maintaining these services under drought stress typically requires substantial supplemental irrigation, which places significant strain on local water resources. Moreover, the lack of sufficient rainfall or irrigation water severely affects the growth and quality of turfgrass in various regions [6]. Consequently, enhancing the inherent drought resistance of turfgrass is crucial to ensure its viability and sustainability under limited water condition.

The physiology of plant responses to drought includes optimizing resource allocation between shoots and roots, synthesizing osmolytes or osmoprotectants such as proline, soluble sugars, and glycine betaine, and enhancing the efficiency of antioxidant defense system to mitigate oxidative damage [7]. Beyond these intrinsic adaptations, plants actively engage with external biotic partners. Specifically, plants can recruit and shape rhizosphere microbial communities through changes in the rhizosphere environment, thereby enhancing nutrient acquisition, growth, and tolerance to environmental stressors such as drought [8–11]. For example, a previous study revealed that drought triggers plants to recruit specific rhizospheric Proteobacteria, which promotes stress memory formation and strengthens host drought tolerance [12]. Another study on wheat also indicated that enrichment of Mortierella alpina can activate plant gene expression and trigger plant drought adaptation mechanisms [13].

Bouteloua dactyloides (Nutt.) (hereafter, B. dactyloides) is a perennial, dioecious, warm-season turfgrass native to North American prairies, renowned for its exceptional drought tolerance and low maintenance requirements [14]. It is a unique dioecious grass [15]. As such, it represents a valuable resource for sustainable turfgrass cultivation in water-limited environments. In China, B. dactyloides has been widely cultivated and used in applications such as airport landscaping and slope stabilization because of its excellent drought tolerance, strong stress resistance, and ability to grow well in infertile soils [16]. It is also increasingly planted throughout the southern Great Plains of the United States as a low-maintenance ground cover requiring minimal management inputs [17]. Previous studies have shown that B. dactyloides can allocate more genetic resources to regulate photosynthesis, thereby enhancing its drought resistance under drought stress [18]. Interestingly, as one of the few dioecious turfgrass species, B. dactyloides shows a variety of physiological differences between male and female plants, such as the growth ability of stolons [18].

In many dioecious plant species, male and female plants exhibit sexual dimorphism in growth, defense and storage [19]. For instance, male individuals of Juniperus communis L. and Populus euphratica Oliv. have been reported to exhibit stronger resistance or tolerance to environmental stresses than female individuals [20, 21]. However, despite its pronounced sexual dimorphism, it remains unexplored whether and how male and female B. dactyloides individuals differ in their drought resistance. More importantly, the potential role of the rhizosphere microbiome, a key mediator of plant stress adaptation, in driving such sex-specific drought responses remains entirely unknown.

It is widely accepted that plants can mitigate drought stress through a suite of physiological and biochemical adaptations [22]. In recent years, recruitment of beneficial rhizosphere microorganisms has emerged as a critical complementary strategy for plant drought tolerance, forming the basis of microbiome-driven plant adaptation [23]. Herein, we investigated physiological responses and rhizosphere microbiome shifts in male and female B. dactyloides to explore their sex-specific drought adaptation strategies. We specifically addressed the following questions: (1) Do male and female plants exhibit differentiated physiological responses to drought? (2) How does drought alter the rhizosphere microbial community composition in male and female plants? (3) What are the differences in drought responses between male and female plants?

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The B. dactyloides cultivar ‘Texoka’ was used in this experiment. Seeds of ‘Texoka’ were obtained from a commercial turfgrass seed supplier. No specific permissions were required for the use of this commercially available cultivar. The experimental procedures complied with institutional and national guidelines for plant research. Plants were initially grown individually in pots filled with field soil from a site previously planted with B. dactyloides. During the flowering period, plant sex was determined by visual identification of male and female inflorescences. Healthy stolons from identified male and female plants were then collected and clonally propagated to establish unisexual clonal populations with a uniform genetic background. A pot experiment was conducted in a greenhouse. Plants were transplanted into pots (40 cm length; 40 cm width; 20 cm height) filled with field soil collected from a site previously planted with B. dactyloides. Each pot was filled with 10.23 kg of this soil. The physicochemical properties of the soil were as follows (mean ± SD, n = 9): SOC 11.51 ± 1.46 g/kg, TN 1.30 ± 0.07 g/kg, TP 0.54 ± 0.10 g/kg, AP 2.78 ± 1.54 mg/kg, TK 14.37 ± 9.33 g/kg, AK 157.82 ± 13.87 mg/kg, pH 7.57 ± 0.14, EC 280.58 ± 116.44 µS/cm, NO₃⁻-N 104.21 ± 22.64 mg/kg, and NH₄⁺-N 13.23 ± 0.86 mg/kg. The soil was slightly alkaline, with relatively abundant available potassium and nitrate nitrogen but low available phosphorus. Each pot contained 16 evenly spaced clonal ramets of a single sex. Drought treatment was initiated after the plants had fully covered the pot surface (two months after transplanting). Two soil moisture treatments were established, including a control (90% of field capacity, CK) and a drought treatment (30% of field capacity, D). A total of 4 treatment groups were established based on the combination of plant sex and soil moisture treatment. Each treatment was replicated three times, resulting in 12 pots in total. Soil moisture was maintained by daily weighing and supplemental watering.

Sample collection and measurements

After 30 days of drought treatment, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters (Fv/Fm) were determined using a plant efficiency analyzer (Handy PEA +, PP SYSTEMS, UK). Subsequently, plants from one quarter of each pot were harvested. Fresh leaf samples were collected and approximately 2 g of fresh leaves were placed in individual centrifuge tubes, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 ℃ for physiological assays. The contents of malondialdehyde (MDA) and proline (PRO), and the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) were measured using commercial assay kits from Suzhou Keming Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (MDA-2-Y, Pro-2-Y, SOD-1-Y, POD-1-Y, CAT-1-Y, APX-1-W). All assays were performed strictly following the manufacturer’s protocols. Calibration curves were constructed using the standard substances provided in each kit. Absorbance measurements were conducted using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV2600, Techcomp, China) according to the specific requirements of each assay. Results were expressed as nmol/g fresh weight (FW) for MDA, µg/g FW for PRO, and U/g FW for all enzyme activities.

For leaf relative water content (RWC) determination, approximately 1 g of fresh leaves was weighed to obtain the FW. The leaf samples were then immersed in distilled water. After 24 h of saturation, the leaves were gently blotted with filter paper to remove surface moisture, and the saturated weight (SW) was recorded. The leaves were subsequently oven-dried at 65°C to constant weight to determine the dry weight (DW). RWC was calculated using the following formula: .

The remaining aboveground and belowground biomass from three-quarters of each pot was separated, oven-dried at 65 ℃ to constant weight, and weighed to determine shoot biomass, root biomass, and root-shoot ratio. Meanwhile, the rhizosphere soil was carefully collected from the root system of the harvested plants and stored at -80 °C for microbial community analysis.

Bacterial and fungal high-throughput sequencing

All measurements were performed with three biological replicates and three technical replicates per biological replicate. The soil microbial community was characterized by high-throughput sequencing. Total DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of each soil sample using the Omega Mag-Bind DNA Kit for Soil (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quantity and quality of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-2000 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA). For bacterial community analysis, the V4–V5 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the universal primers F (ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA) and R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT). For fungal community analysis, the ITS1 region was amplified using the primers ITS1F (GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG) and ITS2R (GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC). The PCR products were purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) and quantified using a Microplate Reader FLx800 (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Equimolar amounts of purified amplicons were then pooled and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw sequence data were first demultiplexed using the demux plugin in QIIME 2 (version 2019.4), and primer sequences were trimmed using the cutadapt plugin [24]. The reads were then quality-filtered (truncQ = 2, maxEE = 2, maxN = 0), denoised, merged, and checked for chimeras using the DADA2 plugin [25] implemented in QIIME 2 [26]. The resulting sequences were defined as amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). After denoising, ASV feature tables and representative sequences were merged across all samples, and singleton ASVs were removed. An average of 209,412 reads were generated per bacterial sample and 124,214 reads per fungal sample. An R script was used to evaluate the length distribution of high-quality sequences across all samples. Taxonomic assignment of bacterial ASVs was performed against the SILVA database (version 138.1), whereas fungal ASVs were assigned using the UNITE database (version 8.0) with the VSEARCH consensus taxonomy classifier implemented in QIIME 2.

Data analysis

Prior to data analysis, assumptions of error distributions were verified using Levene’s test for homoscedasticity and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for normality. Physiological and biochemical responses of plants were quantified using ln-transformed response ratios, and significance was evaluated using 95% bootstrap confidence intervals based on 1000 bootstrap resamples. Effects were considered significant when confidence intervals did not overlap zero. A composite drought resistance index was constructed using multiple physiological traits, including root-shoot ratio, Fv/Fm, RWC, MDA, PRO, POD, SOD, CAT, and APX. All indicators were standardized using min–max normalization across all samples. These parameters reflect key drought-adaptive traits, including biomass allocation, photosynthesis, leaf water status, membrane stability, osmotic adjustment and antioxidant defense. Traits positively associated with drought resistance (root-shoot ratio, Fv/Fm, RWC, PRO, POD, SOD, CAT, and APX) were normalized directly, whereas MDA was treated as a negative indicator and inversely transformed. Indicator weights were determined using the CRITIC (Criteria Importance Through Intercriteria Correlation) method, integrating both trait variability and inter-indicator correlations [27]. The composite drought resistance score was calculated as the weighted sum of normalized indicators. The effects of plant sex, water treatment, and their interaction on the composite index and diversity indices were evaluated using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Sidak-adjusted pairwise comparisons (p < 0.05).

ASV count tables were normalized using cumulative sum scaling (CSS) implemented in the metagenomeSeq R package to account for differences in sequencing depth and subsequently converted to relative abundances for downstream analyses. Microbial alpha diversity was evaluated using ACE richness and Pielou’s evenness indices calculated with the vegan R package. Microbial community dissimilarity was assessed using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis distances, and statistical significance was tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations implemented in the vegan package. Differentially abundant taxa among treatments were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) implemented in the microeco R package [28]. Functional profiles of bacterial and fungal communities were predicted using the FAPROTAX and FungalTraits databases implemented in the microeco R package. Differences among treatments were assessed using two-way ANOVA, with p-values across functional groups adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. Significant effects were further examined using Sidak-adjusted pairwise comparisons.

Results

Physiological and biochemical responses of male and female B. dactyloides to drought stress

Under drought stress, significant changes were observed in most physiological and biochemical parameters (Fig. 1a; Table S1). Specifically, MDA and PRO contents, together with SOD and APX activities, increased significantly in both male and female plants. By contrast, POD activity decreased significantly in both male and female plants. CAT activity increased significantly in male plants but showed no significant change in females. The root-shoot ratio increased significantly only in female plants. Neither Fv/Fm nor the leaf RWC was significantly affected by drought in either sex. A comprehensive evaluation of drought resistance revealed that under the well-watered conditions, female plants exhibited a significantly higher drought resistance index than males (Fig. 1b). However, under drought stress, there was no significant difference between male and female plants.

Rhizosphere microbiome community composition of male and female B. dactyloides under drought stress

For bacterial communities, the alpha diversity indices, including ACE and Pielou’s evenness, showed no significant differences between male and female plants or between drought and CK (Fig. 2a, 2b; Table S2). For fungal communities, the ACE index was significantly higher in male plants than in female plants under drought stress (Fig. 2c, 2d). Compared with CK, drought stress also significantly increased the ACE index in male plants. Pielou’s evenness showed no significant differences among all treatments. Furthermore, the overall community composition of bacteria and fungi differed significantly among all treatments, indicating that plant sex and soil moisture jointly shaped the rhizosphere microbiome (Fig. 2e and 2f).

Responses of rhizosphere microbial diversity in male and female Bouteloua dactyloides (Nutt.) to drought stress. a, b Bacterial alpha diversity; c, d fungal alpha diversity; e bacterial beta diversity; and (f) fungal beta diversity

The dominant bacterial phyla across all samples were Proteobacteria, Acidobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Planctomycetota, and Patescibacteria, while the dominant fungal phyla were Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Mucoromycota, Chytridiomycota, and Rozellomycota (Fig. 3a and 3d). LEfSe analysis further identified treatment- and sex-specific core microbial taxa (LDA > 4). In the bacterial community, the rhizosphere of male plants under CK conditions was significantly enriched with Proteobacteria. Male plants under drought stress were enriched with Actinobacteriota, Actinomycetia, Vicinamibacterales and Vicinamibacteria. For females under CK conditions, plants were significantly enriched with Parcubacteria, Patescibacteria, Gammaproteobacteria and Burkholderiales, whereas plants under drought stress were enriched with Acidobacteriota (Fig. 3b, 3c). For fungi, all taxa identified as significantly enriched were detected in the rhizosphere of male plants under drought stress (Fig. 3e, f), including Agaricomycetes, Basidiomycota, Agaricales, Bolbitiaceae, Conocybe, Glomeromycota, Glomerales, Glomeromycetes, Capnodiales, and Cladosporiaceae.

Functional profiles of rhizosphere microbiomes in male and female B. dactyloides under drought stress

FAPROTAX analysis was employed to evaluate the functional potential of bacterial communities (Table S3). Functional groups with high relative abundance included chemoheterotrophy, aerobic chemoheterotrophy, anaerobic chemoheterotrophy, nitrification, aerobic ammonia oxidation, nitrate reduction, predatory or exoparasitic, phototrophy, dark hydrogen oxidation, and photoautotrophy (Fig. 4a). Drought stress induced significant shifts in bacterial functional profiles, which also varied between male and female plants. Aerobic processes, including aerobic chemoheterotrophy, nitrification, aerobic ammonia oxidation, were significantly suppressed under drought stress compared with CK in both sexes (Fig. 4c, 4e, 4f). Concurrently, processes related to sulfur respiration, including sulfate respiration and respiration of sulfur compounds, were also significantly reduced (Fig. 4h, 4i). In contrast, anaerobic chemoheterotrophy was significantly enhanced under drought (Fig. 4d). Notably, the response of anaerobic chemoheterotrophy exhibited a significant difference between male and female plants. Anaerobic chemoheterotrophy was inherently higher in males under CK conditions and increased further with drought stress, while it showed no significant change in females (Fig. 4d). Other sex-specific responses included a significant increase in aromatic compound degradation specifically in drought-stressed males (Fig. 4g).

Responses of rhizosphere microbial functions in male and female Bouteloua dactyloides (Nutt.) to drought stress. a Top 10 predicted bacterial functions, the size of the circle represents the absolute value of the Z-score normalized relative abundance; b fungal trophic modes and major guilds; and (c–n) significant differences in microbial functions between sexes and drought treatments

The functional profiles of fungal communities were predicted using the FungalTraits database (Table S4). The dominant functional guilds included saprotroph, pathotroph, symbiotroph, plant pathogen, wood saprotroph, endophyte, animal pathogen, dung saprotroph, soil saprotroph, and plant saprotroph. Under drought stress, the relative abundances of saprotroph, wood saprotroph, and animal pathogen significantly decreased in both male and female plants (Fig. 4j, 4l and 4m). The relative abundances of symbiotroph guilds and AMF were significantly higher in male plants than in female plants under drought stress (Fig. 4k and 4n). Compared with CK, drought stress also significantly increased the relative abundances of symbiotroph guilds and AMF in male plants.

Discussion

This study reveals a fundamental divergence in the drought response strategies of male and female B. dactyloides. We propose that female plants primarily rely on a morphological adjustment strategy, characterized by root system expansion. In contrast, male plants adopt a microbiome-mediated strategy, enhancing drought resistance through the specific recruitment of symbiotic fungi, particularly AMF. These findings provide an important theoretical basis for selecting and managing turfgrass cultivars to improve resilience under the increasingly drought-prone climates driven by global change.

Drought stress typically induces membrane lipid peroxidation, with MDA serving as a key biomarker of cellular damage [29, 30]. Consistent with previous reports, both male and female B. dactyloides exhibited a significant rise in MDA content under drought, indicating that male and female plants suffered similar degrees of physiological damage [31]. To mitigate such damage induced by drought, plants often employ morphological and physiological adjustments [32]. A common morphological adaptation is increasing the root-shoot ratio, which expands the root system for enhanced water absorption [33]. In dioecious species, this strategy can be sexually dimorphic. For instance, female Populus cathayana increase specific root length to maximize uptake efficiency, whereas male plants adopt an energy-conserving strategy to cope with phosphorus deficiency [34]. Similarly, we found that female B. dactyloides plants significantly increased their root-shoot ratio under drought, whereas males did not. This indicates that female plants may have a stronger capacity for morphological plasticity, directly investing in root architecture to cope with water deficit [35].

At the physiological level, plants accumulate organic osmolytes such as PRO to maintain cell turgor, and activate antioxidant enzymes to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) [36, 37]. In our study, both male and female plants showed significantly increased PRO contents, together with SOD and APX activities. Furthermore, CAT activity increased significantly only in males. As a critical enzyme mitigating oxidative stress, elevated CAT activity suggests that male plants either experienced higher oxidative stress or possessed a more potent antioxidant defense system [38, 39]. Notably, both male and female plants showed a significant decrease in POD activity under drought stress, which contrasts with the widely reported increase in POD activity under water deficit [40–42]. This suggests that POD may not be a core component of the antioxidant response in B. dactyloides, and its activity variation is highly species- and context-dependent [41].

The rhizosphere microbiome is a key mediator of plant stress adaptation, with its assembly being strongly influenced by host plant physiology and root exudation [43]. Our results demonstrate sex-specific differences in the microorganisms enriched in the rhizosphere of male and female B. dactyloides. In male plants, drought did not significantly alter bacterial alpha diversity but induced a profound shift in community composition. Under drought stress, male plants were significantly enriched with Actinobacteriota, Actinomycetia, Vicinamibacterales and Vicinamibacteria, whereas female plants were enriched with Acidobacteriota. Actinobacteriota and Actinomycetia are widely reported to promote plant drought tolerance [44]. Acidobacteria, Vicinamibacterales and Vicinamibacteria are typically considered oligotrophic bacteria characterized by slow growth, low metabolic rates and high energy reserves, which enable them to persist long-term in resource-poor arid environments [45, 46]. The differential enrichment of these bacterial taxa in male and female B. dactyloides plants likely reflects their distinct microbial-mediated strategies for drought adaptation. More critically, the fungal community in drought-stressed males exhibited a significant increase in diversity (ACE index) and a targeted enrichment of symbiotic fungi, most notably taxa belonging to Glomeromycota, Glomerales and Glomeromycetes. This recruitment of AMF is a cornerstone of the male strategy in response to drought stress [47]. AMF are well-recognized mutualists that expand the functional root system via extraradical hyphae, enhancing host water and nutrient acquisition under drought stress [48–51]. AMF symbiosis can further modulate plant endogenous hormone metabolism, upregulate the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, POD and CAT, and reduce MDA accumulation in host plants, which ultimately enhances plant drought resistance [52]. Compared with females, male B. dactyloides allocate less biomass to underground roots under drought, and such resource allocation limitation makes males more dependent on AMF symbiosis. The significant enrichment of these taxa suggests that male plants actively foster this mutualism to compensate for their lower investment in root biomass. This process may be mediated by changes in root exudation [53], a well-documented pathway for plant-microbe signaling that requires direct experimental validation in future studies.

Conversely, female plants displayed a contrasting pattern. Their rhizosphere response was dominated by an enrichment of bacterial taxa such as Acidobacteriota under drought. We propose that this lack of targeted AMF recruitment is a consequence of the female morphological strategy. Drought is a major abiotic stress limiting plant growth, which induces significant shifts in plant biomass allocation [54, 55]. It has been reported that drought significantly increases root mass fraction while reducing allocation to stem, leaf and reproductive biomass, and herbaceous plants in particular exhibit pronounced root biomass allocation responses to drought stress [56]. The significant increase in root-shoot ratio directly enhances water absorption capacity, potentially reducing the immediate net benefit of investing carbon in an extensive AMF symbiosis [57]. Furthermore, functional predictions of the microbiome robustly support this strategy. The relative abundance of predicted symbiotrophic functions, and specifically the arbuscular mycorrhizal pathway, was significantly higher in drought-stressed males compared with both CK and females. This confirms that the observed enrichment of Glomeromycota translates into a functional shift in the rhizosphere microbiome. The male strategy is thus functionally rooted in enhancing mutualistic associations.

Beyond these sex-specific strategies, drought also induced general shifts in rhizosphere microbial functions that may influence nutrient cycling. Key nitrogen transformation processes, including nitrification and aerobic ammonia oxidation were significantly suppressed under drought in both sexes, consistent with reports of reduced nitrification activity in dry soils [58]. Sulfur respiration pathways, including sulfate respiration were also weakened, indicating reduced activity of sulfate-reducing bacteria that participate in sulfur cycling and plant stress responses [59]. These coordinated declines in aerobic nitrogen and sulfur metabolism suggest that drought may alter the coupled C-N-S cycles in the rhizosphere [60], potentially modulating nutrient availability for both plant sexes. Furthermore, the broad reshaping of saprotrophic and pathogenic fungal guilds under drought also reflects an altered rhizosphere nutrient environment [61, 62]. The activity of these fungal guilds is closely linked to carbon availability in the rhizosphere, which is often reduced under drought stress [63].

We must acknowledge that this study has inherent limitations in exploring AMF. We adopted non-specific high-throughput sequencing approaches, which cannot precisely identify specific AMF species involved in drought-alleviating symbiosis. Future complementary studies should integrate specific AMF molecular identification, targeted inoculation assays and in-situ field observations to uncover the underlying regulatory mechanisms.

Conclusions

In response to drought stress, both male and female B. dactyloides plants exhibited common physiological adjustments, including the accumulation of proline for osmotic adjustment and elevated antioxidant enzyme activities to mitigate oxidative damage. Male plants demonstrated a more pronounced biochemical response, characterized by greater increases in antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT). More distinctively, they employed a microbiome-mediated strategy, characterized by the significant enrichment of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) in the rhizosphere. This symbiosis enhances water and nutrient acquisition through extensive hyphal networks, constituting a key mechanism for male drought tolerance. Conversely, female plants invested more heavily in morphological adaptation, significantly increasing their root-shoot ratio to directly enhance water uptake. These findings not only deepen our understanding of sex-specific ecological adaptations in dioecious plants, with males relying on a microbiome-mediated strategy and females on morphological plasticity, but also lay a solid theoretical foundation for optimizing turfgrass cultivation and management in arid and water-limited environments.
