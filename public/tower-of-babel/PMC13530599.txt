Poster 155. Squat as a Functional Vital Sign: Association Between a Novel Squat Classification and SF-36 Scores in Orthopaedic Patients
Authors: Omar Abdalla, Haytham Alqasmi, Ahmad Almaat, Fahad Saadat, Alexa Jaume, Ameen Suhrawardy, Rahul Vaidya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:The squat, often used informally in clinics, is a foundational compound movement that engages multiple joints and associated soft tissue structures. To perform a full flexion squat, a patient must demonstrate (1) adequate mobility of the hips, knees, and ankles, (2) suppleness of ligaments and tendons, (3) lower extremity (LE) proprioception, (4) sufficient muscle strength and balance, particularly involving the extensor mechanism, and (5) a non-restrictive body weight. This makes it a powerful surrogate for comprehensive LE function that can serve as a rehabilitative benchmark. Currently, there are no routine screening recommendations from the United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) for degenerative joint disease, and no simple laboratory test analogous to a cholesterol panel exists for musculoskeletal health. To address this gap, we developed a novel squat classification system for clinical use (Table 1). The current study represents the first real-world clinical application of this squat classification system. We deployed it in several orthopaedic clinics and explored its relationship with a well-established functional Patient-Reported Outcome Measure (PROM), the SF-36. To our knowledge, this is the first study to rigorously classify squat performance in a general orthopaedic clinic and test its association with a validated quality-of-life measure. We hypothesized that higher squat classification grades would significantly correlate with higher SF-36 functional scores.Methods:In a cross-sectional design, 56 patients (age 14-82) in outpatient orthopaedic clinics were assessed. Each eligible patient attempted a maximal squat. Exclusion criteria were kept minimal to mirror real practice. We excluded only those who could not attempt any form of squat due to severe disability, or anyone who declined to participate. Squat performance was graded by trained assessors using the novel classification described in Table 1. We administered a short-form SF-36, focusing on four key subscales relevant to physical health: (1) Physical Function; (2) Role Limitations; (3) Bodily Pain; and (4) General Health. We calculated an overall SF-36 physical health score from 0 - 100 with 100 being the best health state possible. A linear regression was performed to analyze the association between squat classification and SF-36 score, with one-way ANOVA testing differences across squat grades.Results:A total of 56 patients were enrolled and completed both the squat test and SF-36 survey. The cohort's mean age was 51 ± 17.5 years (range 14 to 82). The distribution of squat grades was skewed toward the lower end, reflecting that many patients presented with functional limitations. The most frequent category was 2C (n = 26), followed by 1C (n = 16), 3C (n = 7), 4C (n = 3), 2B (n = 2), 1B (n = 1), and 1A (n = 1). SF-36 composite scores ranged from 39.6 to 98.1, with a mean of 73.9 ± 15.8 and a median of 77.4. Higher squat classification moderately correlated with higher SF-36 scores (multiple R = 0.31, R² = 0.098), indicating approximately 9.8% of variance in patient-reported function was explained by squat ability. The regression was significant (ANOVA F = 5.89, p < 0.05), and the slope indicated that each stepwise increase in squat grade was associated with a 3.74-point higher SF-36 functional score on average (95% CI: 0.65 to 6.84; p < 0.05). Thus, patients who near optimal squat performance (4C) tended to report better functional health than those with limited squat ability.Conclusions:In this first real-world use of the novel squat classification system, we demonstrated that squat performance is significantly correlated with SF-36 patient-reported functional status in the orthopaedic clinic. Patients who could squat deeper and rise without assistance generally reported better physical function and less pain on the SF-36 survey. These findings suggest that the squat classification captures clinically meaningful aspects of lower-extremity health, functioning as a concise indicator of overall performance. The results support the utility of the squat test as a simple, time-efficient surrogate for overall lower-extremity function in clinical examinations. With further validation, this classification may also serve as a tool for monitoring rehabilitation progress and potentially predicting clinical outcomes. Routine use of the squat classification system could help clinicians identify deficits early and direct patients toward targeted rehabilitation, shifting the focus from treatment to prevention.
