How few channels suffice? A hierarchical validation framework for minimal-channel wearable sEMG in upper-limb rehabilitation gesture recognition
Authors: Xiangyang Li, Huayue Liu, Aoying Shangguan, Huanghe Zhang
Published: 2026

Background

Surface electromyography (sEMG) serves as a critical neural interface for decoding human motor intent, enabling intuitive control in prosthetics, rehabilitation robotics, and human–computer interaction [1]. In upper-limb rehabilitation, sEMG-driven systems hold the promise of restoring functional independence for patients with neuromotor impairments—such as those recovering from stroke or peripheral nerve injury—by translating residual muscle activity into device commands. However, achieving robust user-independent gesture recognition with a practical wearable form factor remains a significant challenge [2]. A core obstacle is the prevailing dependence on high channel counts. Contemporary systems, ranging from 8-channel commercial armbands to 64+ electrode high-density sEMG (HD-sEMG) arrays [3], attempt to compensate for inter-subject variability and signal noise by maximizing spatial sampling. While effective for offline accuracy, this “additive” strategy incurs severe penalties on power consumption, electrode preparation time, and patient burden—factors that are especially critical in rehabilitation settings where user compliance and device wearability directly impact therapeutic outcomes [4, 5].

From a clinical perspective, the practical burden of multi-channel systems extends beyond hardware cost. In rehabilitation contexts, patients often have limited dexterity or caregiver support, making electrode donning and doffing a significant barrier to daily use. Every additional electrode increases preparation time, skin irritation risk, and the probability of poor electrode-skin contact [6]. These usability constraints directly erode patient compliance, which is widely recognized as one of the most important predictors of rehabilitation success. Therefore, identifying the minimum viable number of sEMG channels is not merely an engineering optimization question—it is a clinically meaningful design objective that can determine whether a wearable rehabilitation device is adopted or abandoned by its intended users.

Motivated by these usability constraints, recent studies have increasingly examined channel number, electrode placement, and wearable form factor as coupled design factors in sEMG gesture recognition. Reviews of wearable hand-gesture interfaces emphasize that rehabilitation-oriented systems must combine accurate decoding with daily robustness and unobtrusive sensing [7]. Soft epidermal and wireless sEMG platforms further demonstrate the clinical value of conformal sensors for post-stroke hand-function assessment, although reliable decoding is still commonly supported by multi-channel acquisition [8]. In parallel, electrode-optimization studies suggest that channel count can be reduced when informative locations are selected strategically. Pelaez Murciego et al. showed that online gesture classification can retain high performance with fewer forearm electrodes, but the optimal channels were highly subject-specific [9]. Prakash and Kunju reported that a four-electrode wrist configuration balances accuracy, latency, cost, and comfort [10]. Wrist and wrist–forearm comparative studies also indicate that compact wrist recordings can support fine hand-gesture recognition, especially with suitable learning models [11, 12]. Together, these studies motivate a systematic evaluation of whether fixed, user-independent minimal-channel topologies can support rehabilitation-grade gesture decoding.

The question of how few channels are truly sufficient for reliable gesture decoding has not been rigorously answered. Existing channel selection studies often employ heuristic feature ranking or wrapper methods [13], yielding subject-specific subsets that lack generalization. More fundamentally, these analyses are typically conducted on signals of varying and uncontrolled quality, making it impossible to disentangle the contributions of signal fidelity from spatial redundancy. We hypothesize that when signal quality is maximized at the physical layer, the information density per channel increases sufficiently to permit a drastic reduction in sensor count without compromising cross-subject decoding performance [14]. A model-centric paradigm, which deploys computationally intensive deep learning architectures such as 3D CNNs and Transformers to compensate for noisy inputs, may therefore represent an over-engineered response to a fundamentally physical-layer problem [4, 5].

Unlike previous studies that focused on signal segmentation algorithms or hardware–software co-design [15–19], this paper addresses a distinct and complementary question: What is the minimum viable channel topology for user-independent rehabilitation gesture recognition, and under what hardware conditions does this minimum hold? To answer this, we propose a Hierarchical Validation Framework that integrates three orthogonal pillars of evidence:

Signal Fidelity Verification: We developed a custom high-fidelity wearable acquisition system and verified that inter-channel variance stems purely from physiology rather than hardware artifacts via strict multi-harmonic injection protocols [20]. This ensures a reliable physical ground truth upon which channel optimization can be built.

The specific contributions of this work are: (1) Through rigorous leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation across 24 subjects, we identify a 3-channel configuration as the Efficiency Knee Point and a 4-channel configuration as the recommended optimal for rehabilitation applications; (2) We validate the cross-subject universality of this topology via Overlap Ratio and Regret Analysis; (3) We demonstrate via dual-track sensitivity analysis that the identified minimal configuration is conditionally dependent on signal fidelity, providing a 16% robustness advantage; and (4) We show that the data-driven optimal topology converges on exactly the four forearm muscles spanning the wrist’s two kinematic degrees of freedom, providing an anatomically grounded and clinically interpretable design principle for rehabilitation wearable devices.

Participants

Twenty-four healthy, right-handed participants (Mean ± SD: age 21.3± 2.1 years, height 1.73± 0.08 m, weight 64.5± 10.2 kg, Body Mass Index 21.4± 2.3 kg/m) were recruited for this study. Exclusion criteria included history of neuromuscular disorders, upper-limb surgery, cardiovascular disease, implanted electronic devices, or dermatological conditions affecting electrode placement. Participants were instructed to refrain from strenuous upper-limb exercise, caffeine, and alcohol for 24 h prior to testing to minimize physiological variability. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects under a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shandong University (IRB-SBMS-SDU(L)2025-01-3), strictly adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants also provided specific consent for the publication of identifiable images and experimental setup photographs (e.g., Fig. 2).

Development of custom high-fidelity instrumentation

To ensure the channel optimization analysis was not confounded by commercial instrument noise, black-box filtering, or proprietary compression, we developed a custom high-fidelity wearable sEMG acquisition system. The system overview, including its wearing configuration, physical form factor, internal architecture, signal processing characteristics, hardware verification results, and classification benchmarking, is presented as an integrated schematic in Figs. 2 and 3.

Hardware performance characterization. a Hardware denoising effect: time-domain and power spectral density comparisons between raw and denoised signals for both active and resting states, showing 50 Hz power-line interference suppression at -33.7 dB. b Classification accuracy (LOSO) of five machine learning models under three signal conditions: Standard Hardware (Baseline), Proposed Hardware (Raw Signal), and Proposed System (Full Pipeline). c Radar chart comparing key hardware specifications (CMRR, PSRR, 1/Offset Voltage, 1/Noise Density) between a generic baseline and the proposed circuit

The system utilizes a vertical Stacked-PCB Architecture that physically isolates the sensitive Analog Front-End (AFE) layer from the digital processing (MCU/Bluetooth Low Energy) layer using an electromagnetic shielding ground plane, effectively minimizing high-frequency switching noise coupling [24]. The AFE is implemented as a discrete-component, multi-stage differential topology comprising four cascaded stages: a precision OPA2227 dual operational amplifier configured as a high-impedance unity-gain buffer for common-mode voltage isolation, an INA283 instrumentation amplifier providing the primary differential gain with optimized common-mode rejection, an INA128-based secondary programmable-gain stage for dynamic range optimization, and a hardware Twin-T notch network for active analog suppression of power-line interference prior to digitization. Synchronous sampling of all six channels is coordinated by an STM32F103C8T6 microcontroller (Cortex-M3, 72 MHz) using DMA-driven dual-ADC acquisition at 1000 Hz per channel with inter-channel skew below 10 μ s, and data are streamed via BLE or high-speed UART. The stacked-PCB module is housed in a custom 3D-printed enclosure mounted at the waist to mechanically decouple the electronics from forearm electrode sites. The AFE features a multi-stage differential topology achieving a Common Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR) greater than 112 dB at power line frequencies (50/60 Hz) and an input-referred noise density lower than 15 nV/ [25], as quantified in the radar chart comparison (Fig. 3c). The hardware denoising effect is illustrated in Fig. 3a, showing effective attenuation of 50 Hz power-line interference (-33.7 dB) and wideband noise suppression in both active and resting states.

To strictly validate signal purity, we performed a Fidelity Verification Protocol. We utilized a precision signal generator to inject synthesized complex waveforms containing fundamental frequencies (20–30 Hz) and rich harmonic components (up to 480 Hz), verifying that the system preserves phase linearity and amplitude integrity across the sEMG spectrum (Fig. 2f). Note that although the downstream classification pipeline band-limits the physiological signal to 20–450 Hz (“Signal preprocessing and feature extraction” section), the harmonic test extends to 480 Hz to verify spectral margin and the absence of unexpected roll-off near the upper band edge. Additionally, we tested stability across three conditions: intra-session (short-term consistency), Inter-session (30 min drift check), and power cycle (reboot consistency), with all indices exceeding 0.989 (Fig. 2e). The results confirmed zero spectral distortion, establishing a reliable physical ground truth.

Although the channel-optimization analysis was performed on our custom platform, the proposed framework is not intended to be tied to this specific AFE implementation. Rather, the fidelity criteria defined in “Evaluation metrics” section should be interpreted as methodological safeguards for reducing hardware-induced confounds before drawing channel-topology conclusions. Because sEMG recordings are sensitive to electrode–skin@@ interface conditions, crosstalk, electrode placement, and recording noise, acquisition systems with comparable signal fidelity may be more likely to produce similar qualitative topology trends. However, these criteria should not be interpreted as sufficient conditions guaranteeing identical optimal subsets or classification accuracy on other hardware. When signal fidelity is lower, the per-channel information content may decrease, and additional spatial redundancy may be required to compensate for degraded signal quality, consistent with the sensitivity trends observed in “Evaluation of hardware-dependency via sensitivity analysis” section. Therefore, the portability of the proposed topology should be regarded as conditional rather than hardware-invariant, and cross-platform validation on independent acquisition systems remains necessary.

Electrode placement and gesture vocabulary

Six bipolar Ag/AgCl electrode pairs (20 mm Inter-Electrode Distance) were placed across the dominant upper limb, including two upper-arm sites (BB, TB) and four forearm sites (ECR, ECU, FCR, FCU), adhering to SENIAM guidelines [26]. Channels were positioned to capture the primary actuators of hand and wrist movement: Biceps Brachii (BB), Triceps Brachii (TB), Extensor Carpi Radialis (ECR), Extensor Carpi Ulnaris (ECU), Flexor Carpi Radialis (FCR), and Flexor Carpi Ulnaris (FCU). The ground electrode was placed on the olecranon process. It should be noted that BB and TB are upper-arm muscles primarily responsible for elbow-level movements, whereas the remaining four channels (ECR, ECU, FCR, FCU) are forearm muscles directly governing wrist and hand articulation. The gesture vocabulary comprised eight functional movements essential for daily living and rehabilitation assessment: Elbow Flexion (EF), Elbow Extension (EE), Wrist Ulnar Deviation (WUD), Wrist Radial Deviation (WRD), Wrist Flexion (WF), Wrist Extension (WE), Fist Clench (FC), and Hand Open (HO).

Data collection protocol

Participants were seated with the device secured at the waist. Following signal quality verification, each session adhered to a standardized timeline. A trial commenced with a 10-s rest, followed by a 5-s Unconstrained Natural Motion window. Adopting the benchmark framework by Atzori et al. [27], this 5-s duration captures the complete physiological envelope (onset, plateau, offset). Participants performed the designated gesture naturally without rigid speed constraints. Following the action, another 10-s rest period was observed [1]. Each participant completed four sessions, totaling 20 repetitions per gesture per participant.

Signal preprocessing and feature extraction

Raw signals sampled at 1000 Hz were processed via a 4th-order Butterworth bandpass filter (20–450 Hz) to attenuate motion artifacts and high-frequency noise. Adaptive notch filters were applied to remove 50 Hz power line interference.

Robust signal segmentation is a prerequisite for calibration-free gesture recognition. To overcome the limitations of fixed thresholds, we employed the noise-aware double-threshold adaptive energy segmentation (DT-AES) algorithm. Unlike static methods prone to noise leakage, DT-AES dynamically estimates the ambient noise floor to establish a floating “Safety Line.” It utilizes a topological region-growing mechanism that expands from high-confidence seeds to low-threshold boundaries. This decoupling of segmentation from rigid timing constraints ensures precise detection of variable-speed gestures even under fluctuating SNR conditions.

We extracted 10 features spanning time domain, frequency domain, and time-frequency domain from each 200 ms window with 50 ms increment. The per-window feature vector is 60-dimensional (10 features × 6 channels). Time domain features include mean absolute value, root mean square, variance, zero crossing, and Willison Amplitude; frequency domain features include maximum power, median frequency, and mean power frequency; time-frequency domain features include wavelet maximum and wavelet energy derived from wavelet analysis [28].

Dual-track sensitivity analysis

To rigorously evaluate the system’s robustness without circular logic, we implemented a dual-track strategy that strictly differentiates between training augmentation and testing degradation.

Track A: training for generalization (physiology-informed augmentation)

A critical innovation of our augmentation strategy is that each transformation is explicitly derived from a documented physiological or biomechanical phenomenon in sEMG literature, rather than applying generic noise. This grounding ensures that the augmented samples populate physiologically plausible regions of the feature space.

(i)Motor Unit Recruitment Variability: Random amplitude scaling (0.5× –1.5× ) simulates the well-documented inter-trial variability in motor unit recruitment levels. During voluntary contractions, the number of active motor units and their firing rates fluctuate with force output, electrode-skin contact pressure, and subcutaneous tissue thickness, producing amplitude variations of up to 50% even for the same gesture [23].

(ii)Fatigue-Induced Spectral Compression: We inject a controlled downward shift (5–15%) in the median frequency of each channel’s power spectrum, mimicking the spectral compression that occurs during sustained or repeated contractions. This phenomenon arises from decreased muscle fiber conduction velocity and increased motor unit synchronization under fatigue [29].

Track B: testing for sensitivity (measurement-artifact degradation)

To stress-test the minimal channel configuration, we subjected the model to structural hardware defects it had never seen during training: (i) 50/60 Hz Harmonics: Simulating power line coupling in unshielded environments. (ii) Baseline Drift: Simulating impedance instability due to sweat or skin-electrode interface changes. (iii) Motion Artifact Bursts: Simulating transient spikes caused by cable traction or electrode movement. (iv) SNR Decay: Sweeping the signal-to-noise ratio from 30 dB down to 5 dB using structural noise profiles. (v) Sensor Stochasticity: Random channel masking during inference to simulate electrode detachment [23].

Classification and cross-subject validation

To focus on the contribution of channel topology and signal quality rather than model architecture, we employed a Lightweight Ensemble Classifier (LEC) as the primary model, benchmarked against Support Vector Machine (SVM) with RBF kernel, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and a Feedforward Artificial Neural Network (ANN). Hyperparameters were optimized via grid search on the training set.

We utilized a rigorous Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation scheme, directly addressing the inter-subject variability that is critical in rehabilitation contexts where per-user calibration is impractical. Statistical validation was performed using a One-way Repeated Measures ANOVA to evaluate the main effect of channel count (2–6). When significant main effects were found, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni correction.

Evaluation metrics

To rigorously quantify the minimum viable channel configuration, we established a tiered evaluation framework encompassing signal fidelity and classification performance.

Signal fidelity metrics

Before feature extraction, the absolute reliability of the acquired signals was quantified using the Similarity Index. We computed the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC, model [1, 2]) and Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) across repeated trials (Intra-session) and restart conditions (Power Cycle) [33]. Results

The results are presented following the hierarchical logic of our framework: first validating the physical ground truth, then optimizing the topological configuration, and finally verifying robustness under stress.

Validation of signal source fidelity

The foundation of our channel optimization lies in the physical quality of the data. As shown in Fig. 2e, the proposed hardware demonstrated exceptional stability. Pearson’s r exceeded 0.999 for Intra-session trials and remained above 0.990 even after a full system reboot (Power Cycle). This exceeds reliability benchmarks reported in recent comparable wireless EMG sensor designs [37]. Furthermore, the Fidelity Index (1-NRMSE) for the harmonic injection test was >0.989 (detailed in “Appendix”). Figure 3c further illustrates that the proposed circuit outperforms a generic baseline across all key specifications, including CMRR, PSRR, offset voltage, and noise density. These results confirm that the variance observed in subsequent classification tasks stems purely from physiological information loss (channel reduction), not hardware artifacts or drift, thereby providing a trustworthy foundation for the channel optimization that follows.

Topological convergence and the 3 versus 4 channel trade-off

Figure 6 reveals the physiological mechanism governing the trade-off between the minimal 3-channel and the robust 4-channel configurations.

Topological convergence and regret analysis. a Physiological anchor analysis: heatmap of channel selection frequency across subset sizes. Ch3 and Ch5 are immutable cores. At k=3, a split occurs between Ch4 and Ch6, which is resolved at k=4 via a union strategy. b Regret analysis (3-channel): comparison between individual optimized subsets (Green Dot) and the global fixed 3-channel topology (Orange X). Only 8/24 subjects achieve Zero Regret, with a maximum regret of 10.5%, revealing substantial inter-subject variability. c Regret analysis (4-Channel): Comparison between individual optimized subsets (Green Dot) and the global fixed 4-channel topology (Red X). 16/24 subjects achieve Zero Regret, with a maximum regret of only 3.0%, confirming the robustness of the 4-channel configuration

The physiological anchor analysis (Fig. 6a) demonstrates that Ch3 (ECR) and Ch5 (FCR) serve as the immutable core, selected by 23/24 subjects even in the sparse Best-3 subset. However, at k=3, a topological divergence emerges: the auxiliary channel selection is split between Ch4 (ECU, 12 votes) and Ch6 (FCU, 10 votes). This indicates that while a fixed 3-channel topology (e.g., Ch3+Ch5+Ch6) captures the primary antagonistic drivers, it inevitably incurs a “mismatch penalty” for subjects whose biomechanics favor the alternative auxiliary muscle (Ch4). This mismatch is reflected in the 3-channel worst-case recall of 84%, where specific gestures involving ulnar deviation or synergistic co-activation remain partially ambiguous.

The regret analysis for the 3-channel configuration (Fig. 6b) quantitatively exposes this inter-subject variability. Under the global fixed 3-channel topology, only 8 out of 24 subjects (33%) achieve a Zero Regret state, and the maximum regret reaches 10.5%. This indicates that for certain individuals, the best globally fixed 3-channel subset incurs a clinically meaningful performance gap relative to their personalized optimum, underscoring that the 3-channel configuration, while meeting the 90% average accuracy threshold, cannot guarantee consistent performance across all users.

The transition to the optimal subset size of 4 (best-4) resolves this ambiguity. As shown in Fig. 6a, the inclusion of the fourth channel allows both Ch4 (18 votes) and Ch6 (19 votes) to be integrated simultaneously. This “Union Strategy” effectively covers the inter-subject variability that fragmented the 3-channel solution.

This superiority is quantified in the 4-channel Regret Analysis (Fig. 6c). Using the global fixed 4-channel topology (Ch3, Ch4, Ch5, Ch6), 16 out of 24 subjects (67%) achieve a Zero Regret state, meaning the global solution is mathematically identical to their personalized optimum. Even for the remaining outliers, the maximum regret is capped at a negligible 3.0%. The contrast between the 3-channel regret profile (max 10.5%, 33% zero regret) and the 4-channel regret profile (max 3.0%, 67% zero regret) provides direct evidence that the fourth channel is not merely an incremental improvement but a qualitative shift in cross-subject robustness. Furthermore, the Overlap Ratio analysis confirms this consistency: the mean Overlap Ratio of 0.78 indicates a high degree of alignment between the generalizable model and individual physiological needs.

Clinical implication While the 3-channel configuration offers the highest efficiency ratio (η = 30.6) by capturing the ECR-FCR backbone, its substantial inter-subject regret (up to 10.5%) limits its reliability as a universal solution in rehabilitation settings where consistent performance across diverse patients is essential. The 4-channel configuration represents the recommended standard for rehabilitation. By accommodating both auxiliary extensors and flexors (ECU and FCU), it eliminates the topological blind spots caused by individual anatomical variations, which is critical for ensuring equitable performance across diverse patient populations. The 3-channel configuration may nonetheless remain suitable for resource-constrained or screening applications where moderate inter-subject variability is acceptable.

Physiological validation of the optimal topology

Beyond the statistical analysis, we explicitly validated the data-driven channel selection result against established musculoskeletal physiology. This subsection demonstrates that the mathematically optimal topology is not a numerical artifact but a direct reflection of the biomechanical structure of the forearm.

Kinematic basis The wrist joint possesses two primary Degrees of Freedom (DoF): flexion/extension and radial/ulnar deviation. As detailed in Table 1, Ch3 (ECR) and Ch5 (FCR) form an antagonistic pair spanning the flexion–extension axis, while Ch4 (ECU) and Ch6 (FCU) resolve the ulnar deviation component. The fact that the exhaustive search independently converges on exactly these four channels, without any anatomical prior imposed on the algorithm, constitutes a strong theory-data alignment: the optimal topology is the minimal set that spans the wrist’s kinematic workspace.

Muscle synergy interpretation Rehabilitation science recognizes that functional upper-limb movements are generated not by isolated muscles but by coordinated muscle synergies [40]. Clinical relevance for rehabilitation For patients recovering from stroke or peripheral nerve injury, the forearm muscles targeted by our 4-channel topology (ECR, ECU, FCR, FCU) are among the first to be assessed during functional rehabilitation [39]. The BB and TB channels (Ch1 and Ch2), which are consistently excluded from the optimal subset, primarily serve elbow-level movements (EF and EE) that are biomechanically independent of wrist articulation. This exclusion is physiologically rational: elbow flexion/extension can be decoded by the remaining wrist-level channels through their secondary mechanical coupling, whereas the converse is not true. Our data confirm this asymmetry: the recall for EF and EE remains above 91% even in the 3-channel configuration, whereas wrist-level gestures benefit most from the fourth channel.

This convergence of data-driven optimization and established anatomy provides a principled physiological guarantee that the proposed topology is not overfitted to our specific cohort but reflects a generalizable musculoskeletal invariant, supporting its adoption in rehabilitation device design without per-patient channel recalibration.

Discussion

The convergence of our three evidence pillars provides a principled answer to the “Minimum Viable Channel” question for rehabilitation-oriented sEMG. In this section, we interpret the key findings in the context of clinical rehabilitation practice and position our contributions relative to existing literature.

The physiological evidence suggests that since the wrist has 2 primary Degrees of Freedom (DoF), 2 sensors theoretically span the workspace. However, physiological co-activation introduces non-linearities. Our data show that 2 channels achieve only 73.85%, which is insufficient for clinical use. Three channels (91.65%) cross the clinical reliability threshold, but the Regret Analysis confirms that a 4th independent source is required to resolve topological splits (Ch4 vs Ch6) and achieve zero regret for the majority of users. Notably, the 3-channel Regret Analysis (Fig. 6b) reveals that only 8/24 subjects achieve zero regret under the global 3-channel topology, with a maximum regret of 10.5%, indicating that while the 3-channel mean accuracy exceeds 90%, its cross-subject reliability is insufficient for universal clinical deployment. In contrast, the 4-channel configuration reduces the maximum regret to 3.0% and achieves zero regret for 16/24 subjects (Fig. 6c), representing a qualitative improvement in generalizability. The complete accuracy progression (2Ch: 73.85%, 3Ch: 91.65%, 4Ch: 94.44%, 5Ch: 94.81%, 6Ch: 95.26%) reveals that the marginal gain beyond 4 channels is negligible (<0.4% per added channel), strongly supporting 4 channels as the recommended optimum for rehabilitation deployment.

The Dual-Track Sensitivity Analysis proves that this optimum is strictly conditional on signal quality. The steep performance decay of the minimal system under low SNR (Fig. 8) demonstrates the “Glass Ceiling” of low-fidelity hardware [49]. It confirms that high-fidelity acquisition is not a luxury, but the functional prerequisite that makes minimal architectures viable. This finding has direct implications for rehabilitation device design: investing in analog front-end quality yields a more sustainable pathway to channel reduction than compensating with computational complexity. For rehabilitation engineers and clinicians, the practical message is clear: a well-designed 4-channel system with high-quality analog electronics may offer a more favorable trade-off than a poorly designed 8-channel system, while simultaneously reducing patient burden and extending device battery life.

A distinguishing strength of the present work is the explicit physiological grounding of the channel optimization results. Unlike purely algorithmic channel selection methods, our framework independently validates the data-driven topology against musculoskeletal anatomy (“Physiological validation of the optimal topology” section). The fact that the exhaustive search converges on exactly the four forearm muscles spanning the wrist’s two kinematic DoF, without any anatomical constraint imposed a priori, provides a level of interpretability and clinical confidence that is absent from black-box selection schemes. This theory-data alignment also explains why the topology generalizes across subjects: the underlying musculoskeletal structure is shared across individuals, even as surface-level parameters (adipose thickness, electrode impedance, motor unit density) vary. For rehabilitation applications, this anatomical invariance is particularly valuable, as it eliminates the need for per-patient recalibration and enables a fixed hardware layout.

It is important to clarify the scope of the channel pool examined in this study. Our six-channel setup includes two upper-arm channels (BB, TB) and four forearm channels (ECR, ECU, FCR, FCU). The exhaustive search consistently excludes the upper-arm channels from the optimal subsets, converging on four forearm muscles. This result demonstrates that electrodes on the upper arm are largely unnecessary for wrist and hand gesture recognition, which is the primary finding of the topology analysis. However, because our candidate pool was limited to six channels with only four on the forearm, we do not claim equivalence to high-density forearm-only arrays that may capture additional spatial information through dense electrode placement on the forearm alone. The potential benefits of such concentrated forearm arrays remain outside the scope of this study and warrant future investigation.

Prior channel selection often relies on black-box criteria (e.g., mRMR, PCA, GA), as seen in studies by Phinyomark et al. [50] and Khushaba et al. [51]. Although these methods can reduce channel count, they often result in subject-specific topologies that lack generalization. In contrast, our results (Fig. 6) demonstrate that the “Universal Physiological Anchor” (ECR+FCR) possesses cross-subject universality, eliminating the need for individual recalculation and enabling a unified hardware layout—which is essential for practical rehabilitation devices that must serve diverse patient populations without per-user recalibration. To mitigate low spatial resolution, studies like Côté-Allard et al. [46] and Atzori et al. [52] have successfully utilized Deep Learning architectures (e.g., Transfer Learning, ConvNets) to enhance performance. However, this approach shifts the burden from hardware to software, incurring high computational latency and power consumption. Our results demonstrate that with sufficient signal fidelity, a lightweight ensemble classifier on just 3–4 channels can achieve competitive efficiency ratios compared to deep learning baselines requiring 8–128 channels, enabling true edge-computing efficiency and extending battery life for wearable rehabilitation applications.

Previous studies utilizing High-Density sEMG (HD-sEMG) (e.g., Stango et al. [53]) often view channel reduction as a trade-off, advocating that only high spatial resolution provides sufficient redundancy against electrode shift. Conventional wisdom dictates that dropping below 8 channels compromises robustness. We challenge this convention. By rigorously analyzing the Regret (Fig. 6c), we demonstrate that our 4-channel configuration achieves Zero Regret for 67% of users, with a maximum penalty of only 3.0% for the remaining subjects. This suggests that, under high-fidelity acquisition conditions, massive channel redundancy may not be strictly necessary for functional gesture recognition in rehabilitation contexts.

The transition from offline LOSO analysis to real wearable deployment remains an important consideration. In practical use, model complexity, inference latency, energy consumption, electrode-skin impedance drift, electrode shift, limb-position variation, and transient motion artifacts may all affect real-time performance [54, 55]. Therefore, the recommended 4-channel topology should not be interpreted as an accuracy-only optimum, but as a sensor-level configuration that still requires platform-specific evaluation with respect to computational cost, latency, and embedded implementation. Because the 4-channel configuration provides a larger safety margin against worst-case recall than the 3-channel knee point, it is expected to be more tolerant to online degradation; nevertheless, closed-loop and session-spanning validation will be required before clinical deployment.

Electrode layout and material may also influence the minimal viable channel configuration. The present topology was derived from bipolar Ag/AgCl electrodes placed according to SENIAM guidelines with a 20 mm inter-electrode distance, and should therefore be interpreted within this specific spatial and material setting. Denser forearm grids or circumferential armbands may provide greater tolerance to electrode shift, whereas dry, textile, or epidermal electrodes may improve comfort and donning convenience but introduce different impedance and stability characteristics [53, 56, 57].

The present findings should also be interpreted within the context of a neurologically intact cohort. Although the use of healthy participants limits direct clinical generalization, healthy-subject sEMG data provide a useful normative neuromuscular reference for rehabilitation-oriented sensor design. Recent upper-limb kinematic and EMG datasets have used healthy cohorts to establish reference values for physiological movement behavior and to identify deviations in motor-impaired populations [58]. Transfer-learning studies further suggest that representations learned from healthy sEMG can be adapted to clinical cohorts with altered neuromuscular activation when patient-specific adaptation is incorporated [59]. In addition, post-injury muscle coordination abnormalities may reflect preserved, merged, or fractionated forms of normal muscle synergies [60]. Therefore, the proposed topology should be regarded as a candidate reference configuration derived from normative neuromuscular organization, rather than as direct evidence of patient-level clinical validity. Its generalizability should be further evaluated in heterogeneous patient cohorts with different impairment types, severities, and recovery stages.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the current study recruited only healthy young participants; although such data provide a useful normative reference, the transferability of the identified optimal topology to patient populations with neuromotor impairments (e.g., post-stroke hemiparesis, peripheral neuropathy) remains to be validated. Pathological conditions may alter muscle activation patterns and necessitate topology modifications. Second, the 8-gesture vocabulary, while representative of fundamental wrist and hand movements, does not cover the full spectrum of activities of daily living. Third, the current analysis focuses on discrete gesture classification; extending the framework to continuous proportional control [61, 62], where the minimum viable channel count may differ, is an important next step. Fourth, longitudinal studies examining day-to-day variability and long-term device compliance in rehabilitation settings are needed. Fifth, although the study compares up to 6 channels, the candidate pool includes only four forearm and two upper-arm sites; the comparison does not replicate high-density forearm-only configurations (e.g., 8 channels concentrated on the forearm), and therefore the conclusions regarding channel reduction should be interpreted within the context of this specific electrode placement strategy rather than as a general claim against dense forearm arrays. Sixth, the current evaluation was performed offline using a fixed Ag/AgCl electrode layout; online closed-loop control, alternative electrode materials, and different wearable layouts were not directly assessed. Future work will focus on validating this architecture on pathological datasets [63, 64], investigating continuous proportional control paradigms, and conducting clinical feasibility trials with stroke survivors to establish the translational potential of the proposed minimal-channel design.

Conclusions

This study presented a Hierarchical Validation Framework to identify the minimum viable channel architecture for rehabilitation-oriented wearable sEMG. Through the integration of high-fidelity instrumentation verification, exhaustive subset search with regret analysis, and Dual-Track Sensitivity Analysis across 24 subjects, we demonstrated that a 3-channel configuration serves as the Efficiency Knee Point (91.65%, ), though with notable inter-subject variability (maximum regret of 10.5%, zero regret for only 33% of users), which limits its suitability as a universally reliable clinical solution. The 4-channel configuration offers the Recommended Optimal (94.44%) with zero regret for 67% of users and a maximum regret of only 3.0%, establishing it as the robust standard for rehabilitation deployment. The data-driven optimal topology converges on the four forearm muscles spanning the wrist’s two kinematic degrees of freedom (ECR, ECU, FCR, FCU), providing an anatomically grounded design principle that showed strong consistency across individuals. Notably, the two upper-arm channels (BB, TB) are consistently excluded from the optimal subsets, suggesting that, within the present six-site candidate pool, upper-arm electrodes are largely unnecessary for wrist and hand gesture recognition. Our data further indicate that this channel efficiency is physically contingent on signal fidelity: under low-SNR conditions, the minimal system suffers disproportionate degradation. These findings support a practical design principle for rehabilitation wearables: under the present electrode-placement strategy and high-fidelity acquisition condition, systematic channel optimization can reduce electrode count relative to conventional 8-channel systems while maintaining rehabilitation-relevant performance, offering a pathway toward more comfortable, lightweight, and patient-friendly rehabilitation interfaces that could meaningfully improve therapeutic compliance and outcomes.
