Hepatic steatosis affects the outcome of anti-tuberculosis treatment in pulmonary tuberculosis patients
Authors: Gang Xue, Fang-Qun Chang, Zhi-Xiang Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) remains a major cause of infectious-disease mortality worldwide. Host metabolic status may shape treatment response, but whether hepatic steatosis affects sputum smear conversion in PTB remains poorly defined.MethodsWe performed a retrospective single-center cohort study including newly diagnosed, smear-positive PTB patients treated at Taizhou People's Hospital between January 2022 and December 2025. Patients were classified according to sputum smear conversion status, with delayed conversion defined as persistent smear positivity beyond 8 weeks after initiation of standard anti-tuberculosis therapy. Baseline demographic, clinical, ultrasonographic, laboratory, and cytokine data were collected and compared between groups. Potential factors associated with delayed conversion were evaluated using correlation analysis, multicollinearity assessment, change-in-estimate confounder analysis with the R package chest, and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsClinical screening identified 11 candidate factors. After prioritisation by clinical relevance, correlation structure, collinearity and confounding assessment, six factors were entered into the multivariable model. Hepatic steatosis and IL-18 were positively associated with delayed smear conversion (hepatic steatosis: β = 2.401, OR = 11.036, 95% CI = 2.489–48.934; IL-18: β = 2.396, OR = 10.984, 95% CI = 1.832–65.862). By contrast, T-SPOT positivity was inversely associated with delayed smear conversion (β = −4.199, OR = 0.015, 95% CI = 0.001–0.172).ConclusionsHepatic steatosis was independently associated with delayed smear conversion in PTB. Baseline cytokine profiles, including IL-6 and IL-18, also differed according to treatment response. These findings suggest that PTB patients with hepatic steatosis may benefit from closer monitoring of treatment response during anti-tuberculosis therapy.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Hepatic steatosis, Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Smear conversion

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the world's leading causes of death from infectious disease [1]. Its burden is closely linked to poverty and nutritional status. Low body mass index (BMI) increases susceptibility to TB, whereas higher BMI has been associated with lower risk of infection [2]. Yet higher BMI can also signal metabolic disease, including type 2 diabetes mellitus and hepatic steatosis [3], [4]. The International Union Against Tuberculosis and Lung Disease and the World Health Organization recommend systematic TB screening for patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus in China [5]. HIV infection and diabetes also shape TB outcomes. Together, these observations indicate that nutritional and metabolic status influence TB prognosis in complex, rather than uniformly protective, ways.

Between 7.3% and 25.4% of patients with PTB remain sputum smear positive at the end of the intensive phase of anti-tuberculosis treatment [6]. Age, sex, BMI, smoking, HIV infection and diabetes have all been associated with 2-month sputum conversion [7]. Hepatic steatosis is a common metabolic abnormality and an early feature of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [8]. It may also modify immune function and hepatic drug metabolism. A recent meta-analysis estimated that China has more than 240 million people with NAFLD and the highest annual NAFLD-related mortality rate [9]. NAFLD has been associated with recurrent urinary infections and pneumonia [10], [11]. However, its effect on PTB treatment outcomes remains unclear.

Simple steatosis without cirrhosis is often considered to carry a low short-term risk of liver-related complications. Its systemic metabolic and inflammatory consequences have nevertheless received increasing attention [8], [9]. In TB management, hepatic steatosis has historically been under-recognised, which may partly explain why its relation to TB progression and prognosis remains poorly characterised. Genetic susceptibility, metabolic dysfunction, insulin resistance and inflammatory cytokine dysregulation may all contribute to interactions between NAFLD, metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) and TB [12], [13], [14], [15].

Here, we analysed clinical characteristics in PTB patients with and without delayed sputum smear conversion. We then identified factors independently associated with treatment response and explored links between hepatic steatosis, cytokine dysregulation and delayed smear conversion.

Materials and methods

Study population

We screened 758 patients with active TB who were treated at Taizhou People's Hospital between January 2022 and December 2025. TB was diagnosed according to WHO guidelines [1]. Eligible patients had positive sputum smears for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, compatible imaging findings and typical clinical manifestations, including cough, expectoration for more than 2 weeks, haemoptysis, night sweats, fatigue, afternoon fever, anorexia or weight loss. We excluded patients with retreatment PTB, multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB), tuberculous pleurisy, diabetes, hypertension, autoimmune disease, tumours, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchiectasis or missing key clinical data. MDR-TB was excluded because drug-resistance patterns and prolonged individualised regimens could confound the association between hepatic steatosis and smear conversion during standard treatment. The final cohort comprised 174 patients with initial-treatment active PTB. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Taizhou People's Hospital (TZRY-LL-AF/SQ-014-2.0; protocol KY201803901).

Data collection and study design

All patients with active PTB received standard first-line anti-TB treatment: rifampicin (R), 10 mg/kg/day; isoniazid (H), 5 mg/kg/day; pyrazinamide (Z), 25 mg/kg/day; and ethambutol (E), 20 mg/kg/day for 2 months during the intensive phase, followed by daily isoniazid and rifampicin for 4 months during the continuation phase (2HRZE/4HR). When adverse drug effects occurred, infectious disease physicians adjusted treatment according to clinical practice. Patients who remained sputum smear positive beyond 8 weeks were assigned to the delayed-conversion group. Patients who converted within 8 weeks were assigned to the conversion group. Patient inclusion, exclusion and study design. PTB, pulmonary tuberculosis. Patients with positive sputum smears beyond 8 weeks were classified as the delayed-conversion group, whereas those who achieved smear conversion within 8 weeks were classified as the conversion group.

All participants provided informed consent for the use of their clinical data. Baseline variables included age, sex, BMI, anti-tuberculosis treatment outcome, intensive-phase duration, T-SPOT result and liver ultrasonography findings. Liver ultrasonography was used to identify liver masses, fibrosis and hepatic steatosis. Reports were generated during routine clinical care before final outcome grouping. Because of the retrospective design, formal operator blinding could not be fully confirmed. Laboratory variables included routine blood indices (RBC, NEU and related measures), liver function (TBIL, ALB, ALT, AST and related measures), kidney function (BUN, CREA, B2MG and related measures) and inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-18). Tumour markers (CA-125, CEA and CA19–9) were collected as part of routine clinical screening but were not central to mechanistic interpretation. Cytokines were measured in baseline serum samples collected at admission or before final outcome classification. Patients with missing key exposure, outcome or laboratory data were excluded from analysis.

Statistical analysis

Clinical and laboratory variables were summarised as frequencies (n), percentages (%), means ± standard deviations (s.d.) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate. All analyses were performed in R version 4.2.1. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared using Student's t-test, and non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate correlations among differential factors. The R package chest (version 0.3.7) was used to apply the change-in-estimate method for confounder assessment, which helped identify covariates that meaningfully affected associations between candidate factors and delayed smear conversion [16]. Logistic regression was used to examine relationships between selected factors and delayed sputum smear conversion. Variables were retained in the multivariable model on the basis of clinical relevance, correlation results, collinearity diagnostics and confounding assessment, even when individual variables did not reach statistical significance. Two-sided P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

The final cohort included 174 patients with PTB. BMI was higher in the delayed-conversion group than in the conversion group (22.55 ± 3.73 versus 20.52 ± 3.46 kg/m2; P = 0.004). Bilateral lung involvement was also more frequent in the delayed-conversion group (P < 0.05). Intensive-phase duration was longer in the delayed-conversion group than in the conversion group (5.00(4.00, 6.00) versus 1.00 (1.00, 2.00) months; P < 0.05). T-SPOT positivity was lower in the delayed-conversion group (72% versus 99%; P < 0.05). Liver ultrasonography showed hepatic steatosis in 75% of patients with delayed conversion and 16% of patients with conversion. This difference was substantial (univariable OR = 15.520, 95% CI = 6.210–38.800; P < 0.001). Other baseline variables did not differ significantly between groups (Table 1).

Selection of candidate factors

Nine differential continuous variables were identified in the preceding analyses. Spearman correlation analysis was then used to assess relationships among these variables (Fig. S1). LYM was strongly correlated with MON and NEU (r > 0.84, P < 0.05). IL-10 correlated with IL-18, NEU, LYM and MON (r > 0.50, P < 0.05), and BUN correlated with CREA (r > 0.50, P < 0.05). Collinearity diagnostics showed variance inflation factor values greater than 5 for NEU, LYM and MON (Table S1). No substantial multicollinearity was observed among the remaining continuous variables (VIF < 5, R2 < 1). We then used the R package chest to assess confounding across 11 candidate factors (Fig. S2). The technical details of the correlation and confounding assessments are summarised in Fig. 1, Fig. S2 and Table S1.

Multivariable logistic regression of candidate factors

The multivariable logistic regression model included six factors, hepatic steatosis, T-SPOT, BUN, IL-6, IL-10 and IL-18, selected by clinical relevance, correlation analysis, collinearity diagnostics and confounder assessment. Three factors were independently associated with delayed smear conversion (Table 5 and Fig. 2). Hepatic steatosis and IL-18 were positively associated with delayed smear conversion (hepatic steatosis: β = 2.401, OR = 11.036, 95% CI = 2.489–48.934; IL-18: β = 2.396, OR = 10.984, 95% CI = 1.832–65.862). T-SPOT positivity was inversely associated with delayed smear conversion (β = −4.199, OR = 0.015, 95% CI = 0.001–0.172). BUN, IL-6 and IL-10 were retained because of clinical relevance or their contribution to confounding assessment, although they were not statistically significant in the final model.

Discussion

Nutritional status is closely linked to the development and outcome of active tuberculosis. Lower BMI, mid-upper-arm circumference, skinfold thickness and muscle mass have been associated with increased mortality and relapse in active TB [17]. Serum cholesterol concentrations are also lower in active TB, and nutritional supplementation may reduce adverse drug reactions and improve outcomes during anti-tuberculosis chemotherapy [18]. By contrast, the relation between hepatic steatosis and anti-tuberculosis treatment response has been poorly characterised.

Evidence linking abnormal lipid metabolism to TB is complex. A retrospective cohort study from Taiwan reported a nonlinear association between obesity, diabetes and TB incidence [19]. Another study suggested that elevated serum cholesterol may impair adaptive immunity to TB [20]. These findings indicate that host metabolism and TB immunology may intersect in clinically relevant but non-linear ways. NAFLD is a metabolic disease, and MAFLD has been proposed to describe fatty liver associated with metabolic dysfunction more directly [21]. NAFLD and MAFLD often coexist with insulin resistance, hypertension and other metabolic abnormalities linked to infection risk [22], [23]. In our cohort, hepatic steatosis was strongly associated with delayed sputum smear conversion. However, hepatic steatosis may be a direct contributor to immune or drug-metabolism changes, a marker of broader metabolic dysfunction, or both. Because this study was retrospective and observational, causality cannot be inferred.

T-SPOT is an interferon-gamma release assay endorsed by WHO for the diagnosis of TB infection. IFN-gamma responses can change during anti-TB treatment and may correlate with smear conversion in clinical specimens [24], [25]. In this study, T-SPOT positivity was inversely associated with delayed smear conversion. One explanation is that a detectable antigen-specific T-cell response reflects more effective cell-mediated immune control. Conversely, a negative or weaker response may indicate impaired host immunity or more severe disease. This interpretation requires confirmation in larger prospective cohorts with serial immune measurements.

The cytokine profile suggests that immune dysregulation may connect hepatic steatosis with delayed TB treatment response. IL-6 contributes to immune responses in TB and signals through the gp130/JAK-STAT pathway to regulate mediators such as MCP-1 and GM-CSF. Although previous studies often associate higher IL-6 with inflammation and disease severity, IL-6 was lower in the delayed-conversion group in our cohort [26]. This discrepancy may reflect sampling time, immune exhaustion, anti-inflammatory counter-regulation, hepatic metabolic status or limited sample size. IL-10 was higher in the delayed-conversion group and may impair antigen presentation or alter T-cell localisation during TB infection [27], [28], [29], [30]. IL-18, a member of the IL-1 cytokine family, can contribute to lung tissue injury during TB infection and was independently associated with delayed smear conversion in our model [31], [32].

Several plausible pathways could link hepatic steatosis, macrophage cytokine production and TB outcomes. NAFLD-related adipocyte death can induce liver injury and inflammation through CCR2-positive macrophages [33]. MAPK14, NOD2, RIPK2 and GADD45β pathways have been implicated in metabolic inflammation and innate immune regulation [34], [35]. These observations support a model in which hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction alter macrophage activity, cytokine profiles and host immune control of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. The effect of hepatic steatosis on anti-TB drug metabolism remains uncertain. CYP2E1 expression is associated with steatosis and oxidative stress, and rifampicin may influence CYP450 expression in steatotic hepatocytes [36], [37]. Thus, interactions among hepatic steatosis, rifampicin metabolism and drug-drug interactions should be treated as hypotheses for future mechanistic and pharmacokinetic studies, not as conclusions from the present data.

Overall, these findings suggest that hepatic steatosis may be linked to delayed sputum smear conversion through cytokine dysregulation, impaired anti-tuberculosis immune responses and, potentially, altered anti-TB drug handling. This hypothesized pathway is summarised in Fig. 3 and should be interpreted as a conceptual model based on the current clinical associations rather than evidence of a confirmed causal mechanism.

Hypothesized link between hepatic steatosis and delayed sputum smear conversion in PTB. Hepatic steatosis may be associated with cytokine dysregulation, including decreased IL-6 and increased IL-10 and IL-18, which may impair anti-TB immune responses and contribute to delayed sputum smear conversion. The potential effect of hepatic steatosis on anti-TB drug handling remains hypothetical and requires further investigation.

Limitations.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective single-center analysis, so residual confounding could not be excluded. Second, the sample size was relatively small, particularly for multivariable logistic regression with several candidate predictors. This resulted in wide confidence intervals and possible model instability. Third, excluding patients with MDR-TB and several comorbidities reduced clinical heterogeneity but limited generalizability. Fourth, liver ultrasonography and cytokine measurements were obtained from routine clinical data, and formal blinding and serial cytokine dynamics could not be fully assessed. Finally, hepatic steatosis may represent broader metabolic dysfunction rather than a direct causal factor. Larger prospective studies with standardized imaging, serial immune profiling and pharmacokinetic assessment are needed.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort, hepatic steatosis was independently associated with delayed smear conversion in patients with PTB. Serum cytokines, including IL-6 and IL-18, were also related to treatment response. These findings suggest that hepatic steatosis may help identify PTB patients who require closer monitoring during anti-tuberculosis therapy, including liver-function follow-up, assessment of metabolic comorbidities and careful evaluation of sputum conversion.
