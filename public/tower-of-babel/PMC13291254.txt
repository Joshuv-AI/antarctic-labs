Wearable ultrasound: a review of core technologies and clinical applications in cardiovascular monitoring
Authors: Mengxue Yan, Zirui Wang, Zhenfeng Li, Peng Wang, Pang Wu, Xianxiang Chen, Xiaoxia Chen, Lidong Du, Yong Wang, Junxian Song, Zhen Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wearable technology is breaking the confines of skin-surface monitoring, moving towards deep-tissue physiological insights. Within this evolution, wearable ultrasound is emerging as a transformative technology, offering a non-invasive and real-time window into deep physiological dynamics. This review systematically deconstructs the technological framework of a complete wearable ultrasound system. It provides an in-depth analysis of the system’s three core components: (1) conformal transducers, examining progress in materials, microfabrication, and the critical trade-off between acoustic performance and mechanical compliance; (2) miniaturized hardware systems, detailing design strategies to resolve the fundamental conflict among processing power, energy efficiency, and form factor; and (3) advanced algorithms, highlighting their role as essential compensatory systems for ensuring data integrity in dynamic environments. Finally, the clinical power of this integrated technology stack is demonstrated through a comprehensive survey of its cardiovascular applications. By systematically analyzing the core technologies and future challenges, this review offers a forward-looking perspective on the evolution of wearable ultrasound, charting its path toward becoming a cornerstone of continuous, personalized health management.

Introduction

The paradigm of modern healthcare is shifting from hospital-centric, intermittent diagnostics toward person-centric, continuous health management1. This transformation is primarily driven by the demand for effective chronic disease management and personalised precision medicine. Enabled by advancements in materials science, microsystems engineering, and electronics manufacturing, wearable technology has emerged as a key force in transforming personal health monitoring2. Currently, a range of skin-conformal sensors has been developed for continuous physiological data acquisition3. These systems include sensors for monitoring electrophysiological signals (e.g., electrocardiogram, ECG; electromyogram, EMG)4, optical signals (e.g., PPG, for heart rate and blood oxygen saturation)5, and biochemical markers in biofluids (e.g., glucose, lactate)6. Such continuous data are crucial for identifying longitudinal health patterns and capturing transient abnormal events7. However, existing mainstream wearable sensing technologies share a fundamental limitation: their capabilities are largely confined to the skin or shallow subcutaneous tissues8.

Overcoming this fundamental barrier of superficial sensing to enable continuous access to physiological information from deep tissues is a key challenge in the wearable sensing field9. This goal requires technologies that can continuously assess both the functional dynamics (e.g., hemodynamics) and anatomical structures of internal organs. Monitoring these deep physiological events holds immense clinical value, particularly in acute and critical care10. For example, real-time tracking of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular hemodynamic status is vital for patient monitoring in intensive care units (ICUs) and for risk stratification in chronic disease management10,11. While established medical imaging modalities like computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offer excellent deep-tissue visualization, factors such as bulky equipment, high cost, and, in some cases, ionizing radiation render them unsuitable for continuous, portable, and wearable applications12,13.

In contrast, ultrasound technology is an ideal candidate to address this challenge due to its unique combination of attributes. It is a non-ionising, cost-effective, real-time imaging modality with excellent spatial and temporal resolution14. Its versatility is further highlighted by multiple operational modes: A-mode for depth measurement, M-mode for motion tracking, B-mode for anatomical imaging, and Doppler mode for quantifying blood flow. This synergy enables ultrasound to provide a comprehensive assessment of deep-tissue physiology.

Enabled by recent breakthroughs in flexible materials, advanced manufacturing, and integrated circuits, the field of wearable ultrasound has developed rapidly8. A wearable ultrasound device is a body-conformal system that integrates the core functionalities of clinical ultrasound into a compact, autonomous form factor15. This design enables continuous and operator-independent data acquisition. However, building a stable and reliable wearable ultrasound system is a complex systems-engineering task. Its success relies on the synergistic integration of three core components: conformal transducers, miniaturized hardware systems, and advanced algorithms16. These three components have a tightly coupled and interdependent relationship, governed by mutual constraints17. As the sensing front-end, the evolution of transducers toward miniaturization, high density, and flexibility is the first step toward a viable wearable system. This evolution, however, poses significant challenges for the hardware system, which must support higher channel counts and maintain high signal sensitivity within stringent size and power constraints18. In this context, the role of algorithms becomes critical. Flexible transducers introduce motion artifacts and geometric deformations, requiring sophisticated signal processing (e.g., phase correction, shape estimation) to ensure data quality8. Concurrently, the resource limitations of miniaturized hardware demand computationally efficient algorithms. For instance, on-device data preprocessing can reduce the system’s reliance on wireless bandwidth and back-end computing power. Therefore, the co-evolution of these three technological domains collectively determines the ultimate performance and practicality of wearable ultrasound systems.

Most existing reviews on wearable ultrasound focus primarily on transducer materials and structures, while offering less in-depth discussion on the equally important domains of hardware systems, algorithms, and their intrinsic connections8,15,18–20. To systematically deconstruct this complex systems-engineering challenge, this review is organized according to a bottom-up technological architecture, as illustrated in Fig. 1. We begin at the foundation: the conformal transducer itself (“Wearable ultrasound transducers for conformal sensing”), exploring the innovations required to bridge the gap between acoustic performance and mechanical wearability. We then ascend the technology stack to the miniaturized hardware that powers it (“System-level integration for wearable ultrasound”), analyzing design trade-offs driven by the constant tension between data demand and resource constraints. Next, we examine the intelligent algorithms that interpret its data (“Advanced algorithms for signal processing and imaging”), which act as essential systems to restore signal integrity. Finally, we demonstrate the clinical power of this integrated system through a case study on cardiovascular hemodynamic monitoring (“Clinical applications in cardiovascular hemodynamic monitoring”). Wearable ultrasound transducers for conformal sensing

The performance of any wearable ultrasound system is fundamentally rooted in its core sensing unit—the transducer. The transducer design extends far beyond selecting a single functional material; it is a systematic engineering effort involving the synergistic operation of multiple functional modules. From a structural-functional perspective, a high-performance conformal transducer can be deconstructed into three interdependent subsystems: (1) the Piezoelectric Core, which acts as the system’s “engine”, responsible for the interconversion of electrical and acoustic energy; (2) the Acoustic Energy Transmission System, composed of matching and backing layers, which functions as the acoustic “lens and damper”, responsible for efficiently coupling acoustic energy into the target medium and suppressing noise; and (3) the Mechanical and Electrical Integration Framework, comprising the substrate and interconnecting electrodes, which serves as the system’s “skeleton and nervous system”, providing mechanical support, conformal capabilities, and stable signal pathways (see the illustration in Fig. 2a). Piezoelectric core: materials and technologies

The piezoelectric element is the heart of an ultrasound transducer, directly determining the energy conversion efficiency and signal quality of the entire system. An ideal piezoelectric material must strike a balance among several key performance indicators: a high electromechanical coupling coefficient (kt) to ensure efficient energy conversion, a high piezoelectric coefficient (d33) to obtain strong transmit/receive signals, and a low acoustic impedance (Z) that matches human tissue (~1.5~MRayl) to minimize interface reflection losses. However, conventional materials often struggle to meet all these criteria simultaneously, especially when it comes to the mechanical compliance required for wearable applications. Consequently, the development history of piezoelectric core materials clearly shows an evolutionary path from optimising single performance metrics to achieving multi-objective synergy. This section will systematically review the four mainstream technology platforms: piezoelectric ceramics, piezoelectric polymers, piezoelectric composites, and micromachined ultrasonic transducers (MUTs).

Piezoelectric ceramics, particularly materials represented by lead zirconate titanate (PZT) (Fig. 2b) and its variants (PZT-5A, PZT-5H), have long formed the technological cornerstone of high-performance ultrasound transducers21–23. Their exceptional piezoelectric effect originates from the perovskite crystal structure, exhibiting high piezoelectric coefficients, with d33 values often exceeding 600 pC/N for high-performance variants24, and kt approaching 0.525 near the morphotropic phase boundary (MPB). However, their high density and high stiffness lead to significant acoustic and mechanical mismatches with soft tissues (Z ≈ 30–35~MRayl, Young’s modulus >60~GPa)26.

To further push performance limits, researchers have turned to relaxor-ferroelectric single crystals, such as lead magnesium niobate-lead titanate (PMN-PT) (Fig. 2c). Recent studies have shown that unparalleled performance (d33 can exceed 2000~pC/N, which also enhances thermal and electrical stability, making it suitable for high-resolution deep-tissue imaging, as demonstrated in a conformal breast scanner27. To address the biotoxicity of lead, significant progress has been made in developing lead-free piezoelectric ceramics15. Among them, barium zirconate titanate–barium calcium titanate (BZT–BCT) systems have demonstrated excellent piezoelectric performance near their MPB, with reported values of d33 = 600~pC/N and kt = 0.41, and have been successfully integrated into flexible and wearable ultrasound patches28. However, despite their promising electromechanical properties, BZT–BCT ceramics exhibit a relatively low Curie temperature (typically 100–130 °C) and a narrow temperature-stability range, which can lead to performance degradation at elevated temperatures. In addition, precise densification and compositional control are required during fabrication, making large-scale processing more challenging29. More recently, potassium sodium niobate (KNN)-based systems have emerged as another major class of lead-free materials15. Through multi-element doping and entropy engineering strategies, KNN ceramics have achieved a high d33 of up to 680 pC/N, surpassing many commercial PZT variants while maintaining good thermal stability30. Nevertheless, their synthesis remains challenging because the volatility of alkali elements (K and Na) during high-temperature sintering often leads to compositional inhomogeneity and reduced reproducibility31. These limitations in Curie temperature, thermal stability, and processing control continue to restrict the large-scale commercialization of lead-free piezoelectric ceramics for ultrasound applications32. Despite continuous improvements in material properties, the intrinsic rigidity of bulk ceramics and single crystals necessitates that they be endowed with macroscopic flexibility for wearable applications through structural engineering, such as dicing, thinning, or integration into flexible frameworks33,34.

To address the issue of mechanical compliance at the intrinsic material level, research shifted to piezoelectric polymers. Among them, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) (Fig. 2g) and its copolymer with trifluoroethylene (PVDF-TrFE) (Fig. 2h) are the most prominent examples35. Their piezoelectricity arises from the alignment of C-F dipoles in the polymer chains. The main advantages of these polymers are their excellent mechanical flexibility (Young’s modulus ~2–3 GPa) and a low acoustic impedance ( ~ 4~MRayl) that closely matches biological tissues36. However, their piezoelectric performance (d33 ≈ 10–30~pC/N, kt < 0.237) is significantly lower than that of ceramics. To overcome this limitation, research strategies include stacking multiple layers of film to amplify the output signal, as seen in a bi-layer PVDF sensor for monitoring muscle contractions38,39. A breakthrough development is the advent of weavable, fibre-like polymer transducers that can be seamlessly integrated into textiles for unobtrusive and comfortable monitoring of carotid and cardiac blood flow, exhibiting a -6~dB bandwidth as high as 93%40. Similarly, large-area flexible arrays based on imprinted P(VDF-TrFE) structures have also been successfully fabricated, enabling high-quality 2D imaging and non-invasive blood pressure (BP) sensing of the carotid artery41.

As an engineered solution, piezoelectric composites aim to synergistically combine the high performance of piezoelectric ceramics with the mechanical and acoustic advantages of polymers. Among various configurations, the 1–3 type composite (Fig. 2d, e), consisting of a 1D array of piezoelectric ceramic pillars embedded in a 3D polymer matrix, has been widely adopted in medical ultrasound for its optimal overall performance42,43. The low-modulus polymer matrix mechanically decouples the ceramic pillars, allowing them to vibrate freely in a mode with a higher d33, often leading to superior effective kt ranging from 0.6 to over 0.844. This structure effectively reduces the overall acoustic impedance (typically to 10–20~MRayl44) and enhances flexibility. The ‘dice-and-fill’ method, which involves dicing a bulk piezoelectric ceramic into an array of pillars and then backfilling the gaps (kerfs) with a polymer, is the standard fabrication process45. This technology forms the basis for the vast majority of high-performance flexible and stretchable transducers, which are now used in applications ranging from deep-tissue hemodynamic monitoring to wearable cardiac imaging17,46–48. MUTs represent a paradigm shift from bulk materials to technologies based on semiconductor microfabrication (MEMS) (Fig. 2i). Instead of relying on thickness vibration, MUTs operate through the flexural vibration of suspended, micron-scale thin films. This principle brings three transformative advantages: (1) intrinsically low acoustic impedance, enabling excellent matching with biological tissue; (2) greater design freedom, as the resonant frequency is determined by the membrane’s geometry and material properties rather than its thickness; and (3) suitability for wafer-level batch manufacturing and potential for monolithic integration with CMOS driving circuits.

MUTs are mainly categorized as either capacitive (CMUTs) (Fig. 2j) or piezoelectric (PMUTs) (Fig. 2k). PMUTs, which integrate a piezoelectric thin film (e.g., AlN, AlScN, PZT) on a flexible membrane, have become a promising candidate for wearable applications due to their lower driving voltages and high energy efficiency49,50. The choice of piezoelectric film is critical: AlN is favored for its lead-free nature and excellent compatibility with CMOS manufacturing processes, making monolithic integration easier. However, its piezoelectric coefficient is modest. PZT thin films offer significantly higher performance, leading to greater sensitivity and acoustic output, but their integration with CMOS is more challenging. In contrast, CMUTs operate on an electrostatic principle. They consist of a suspended, conductive membrane positioned over a fixed bottom electrode, separated by a vacuum gap. An applied DC bias voltage pulls the membrane downwards, and an AC signal then modulates this electrostatic force, causing the membrane to vibrate and generate sound. This structure allows CMUTs to achieve extremely wide bandwidth, but their operation requires high DC bias voltages (tens to hundreds of volts)51, which poses a challenge for the power management units (PMUs) of low-power wearable electronics. Scandium-doped aluminum nitride (AlScN) has recently emerged as a balanced alternative, substantially enhancing the piezoelectric response of AlN by several times while preserving its CMOS process compatibility52. Despite these advantages, challenges such as maintaining uniform Sc incorporation and ensuring high film quality at increased doping levels remain active research topics53. Recent advancements have focused on enhancing PMUT performance for wearable use. For example, ultra-thin curved diaphragms have reduced the required driving voltage to as low as 2.5 V, simplifying the system electronics52. Structural innovations, such as “skeletal” membranes, have also been used to increase bandwidth for Doppler blood flow detection54. These developments have enabled AlN-based PMUT arrays for continuous, wearable BP monitoring by tracking arterial diameter waveforms49 or measuring local pulse wave velocity (PWV)55. These miniaturised PMUT systems demonstrate a high signal-to-noise ratio and good motion artifact rejection, making them well-suited for wearable ultrasound applications55,56.

Once the piezoelectric core converts electrical energy into acoustic energy, the efficiency of the entire transducer depends on how this energy is managed. This is the responsibility of the acoustic energy transmission system, composed of the matching and backing layers.

The backing layer absorbs acoustic energy propagating from the rear surface of the piezoelectric element. Without it, this energy would reflect within the housing, creating a prolonged “ringing” effect that severely degrades the axial resolution. Therefore, an effective backing layer must possess high acoustic attenuation and an acoustic impedance that closely matches the piezoelectric core. A common material is a mixture of epoxy, high-density tungsten powder, and sometimes microspheres27,33, which yields materials with a tunable acoustic impedance of 8–20 MRayl. Conductive silver epoxy is also often used, serving the dual role of acoustic damping and a common ground electrode42,60,63. The design of the backing layer involves a critical trade-off: a heavily damped backing provides excellent bandwidth and resolution but reduces sensitivity, while a lightly damped (or air-backed) design maximizes sensitivity at the cost of resolution. In wearable applications, minimizing the thickness of this layer is crucial for overall device flexibility and comfort. To this end, novel ultra-thin, high-attenuation backing materials are being developed. For instance, recent material innovations have led to ultra-thin backing layers with high acoustic attenuation, enabling the fabrication of highly compact wearable patches without compromising deep-tissue imaging capabilities64.

For wearable ultrasound, the long-term stability of the acoustic interface is crucial. Traditional liquid ultrasound gels are unsuitable for long-term use due to dehydration. This has led to extensive research into solid and semi-solid coupling media. A significant advancement is a bioadhesive hydrogel-elastomer hybrid that maintains robust adhesion and acoustic coupling on the skin for over 48 hours, even during motion (Fig. 3g)33,34. To further combat dehydration, encapsulated organohydrogels have been developed, demonstrating stable performance for over eight days by using glycerol as a humectant and an optimized pre-gel template to ensure a smooth interface with the encapsulating elastomer (Fig. 3h)65. For even longer durations, bioadhesive hydrogels for transcranial applications have achieved stable coupling for 28–35 days, enabling long-term, wearable neuromodulation (Fig. 3i)66. Other strategies include gels based on non-Newtonian fluids to improve contact for rigid probes67 and solid coupling films with micro-pillars for PMUTs to conform to the skin’s micro-topography68. Many flexible devices also utilize the low-modulus properties of their encapsulation materials (e.g., Ecoflex, PDMS) for direct skin coupling (Fig. 3f), eliminating the need for an additional medium55,62,69.

Mechanical design for conformability: substrates and interconnects

The final step is to assemble the functional acoustic stack into a device that can conform to the human body while maintaining stable electrical connections. This relies on the strategic design of substrate materials and electrical interconnects.

The substrate provides the physical foundation for the transducer array. While rigid substrates like FR-4 are used in some strap-on wearable prototypes22,70, true conformability requires flexible or stretchable materials. Flexible substrates, such as polyimide (PI)69 and PET60 (Fig. 3a, c), allow the device to bend around cylindrical limbs. To accommodate the complex, multi-axial deformations of skin, stretchable substrates are necessary. Elastomers like polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) (Fig. 3d)57 and Ecoflex (Fig. 3b)60,62 are preferred due to their low, skin-like Young’s modulus (0.05–3 MPa for PDMS/Ecoflex vs. 5–140 MPa for skin71,72), excellent biocompatibility, and acoustic transparency. For a more sustainable approach, biodegradable substrates based on natural polymers like chitin are emerging. These materials can achieve high mechanical strength and toughness while being fully degradable, addressing concerns about electronic waste73.

While the substrate provides the mechanical backbone for conformability, it is the electrical interconnects that serve as the device’s nervous system. These conductive pathways are responsible for delivering driving signals to each piezoelectric element and transmitting the received echo signals back to the hardware. In a flexible or stretchable device, these interconnects must endure the same mechanical deformations as the substrate without fracturing or losing conductivity. Maintaining electrical integrity under mechanical deformation is a critical challenge. Two primary strategies have emerged:

Structural design

This approach engineers stretchability by patterning conventional conductive metals like copper or gold into specific geometries. A dominant strategy is the island-bridge architecture (Fig. 4a). This design partitions the device into rigid functional “islands”, such as piezoelectric elements, and deformable “bridges” that serve as electrical interconnects (Fig. 4b). When the device is stretched, strain is concentrated in the bridges, leaving the sensitive island components largely unaffected. These bridges are typically realised as serpentine or “wavy” traces (Fig. 4c, d)17,69,74. During deformation, these serpentine structures can unfold, buckle, or twist to accommodate mechanical stress while maintaining electrical integrity. Intrinsically stretchable materials

This strategy uses inherently conductive and stretchable materials, typically realized as either conductive polymer composites or liquid metals. Conductive polymer composites embed fillers like silver nanowires (AgNWs) in a stretchable matrix (Fig. 4e)76. They conduct via a percolating network that is maintained during strain by the sliding and reorientation of fillers. Their primary drawback is potential conductivity loss and hysteresis after repeated stretching. Alternatively, liquid metals like EGaIn are encapsulated in soft microfluidic channels (Fig. 4f). They offer exceptionally stable, metallic-level conductivity at very high strains (>100%) by simply flowing with the deformation (Fig. 4g)47,77. Key challenges are preventing leakage and fabricating complex, high-density circuits.

Design paradigms for wearable transducers

The technological elements discussed in this section have given rise to three distinct but increasingly converging design paradigms for wearable ultrasound transducers, each making different trade-offs among acoustic performance, mechanical conformability, and integration complexity.

The performance-first paradigm: rigid transducers in wearable fixtures

This approach prioritizes signal quality by using conventional, high-performance rigid transducers and enabling long-term wear through external fixtures like straps (Fig. 5b, d, e, f)70, bioadhesive hydrogels (Fig. 5a, c)33,47, or soft, skin-conformable encapsulants (Fig. 5g). The form-first paradigm: intrinsically conformal transducers

This approach designs transducers that are “born to be worn”. It utilizes intrinsically flexible materials like piezoelectric polymers40 or, more commonly, employs the “island-bridge” architecture to build fully flexible (Fig. 5j) and stretchable arrays (Fig. 5h, i) from high-performance 1–3 composites17,46,75. These devices provide unparalleled comfort and stable acoustic coupling on complex and dynamic surfaces like the neck and joints. Although their acoustic performance is continually improving60,61, they often involve more complex fabrication processes, and their raw imaging capabilities may not yet match those of their rigid counterparts.

The integration-first paradigm: MUT-based systems

MUT-based systems, including both PMUTs (Fig. 5i) and CMUTs (Fig. 5k), hold significant promise for the future of miniaturized and integrated ultrasound systems. Their MEMS-based fabrication is inherently scalable and offers CMOS compatibility, paving the way for highly integrated systems analogous to an “ultrasound smart patch”14,78,79. Current approaches for achieving flexibility follow two main paths: one strategy involves assembling rigid silicon-based MUT chips into a flexible package (using hybrid and meta-rigid approaches)49,55, while another focuses on fabricating intrinsically flexible MUTs directly on polymer substrates80–82. Recent breakthroughs in this area have demonstrated the high potential of this approach, such as the development of long, monolithic flexible CMUT arrays for large-area conformal imaging83, and high-frequency flexible PMUT arrays based on PZT thin films for high-resolution wearable imaging84. A key advantage of this paradigm is embedding advanced functionality into the transducer to simplify the overall system. For example, recent skin-adaptive focusing MUTs achieve passive acoustic focusing through their structural design, enhancing signal quality while reducing reliance on complex driving electronics85. Beyond wearable imaging, the versatility of flexible MUTs is also enabling novel applications like implantable, biocompatible ultrasonic stimulators for neuromodulation86. Both integration paths ultimately converge on delivering low-power, cost-effective systems for continuous monitoring.

Ultimately, the choice of transducer technology is an application-driven decision, where each paradigm imposes distinct constraints on the subsequent system. To realize the full potential of these advanced transducers, a miniaturized and power-efficient electronic system is required to drive them and process their signals. Specifically, innovations such as higher element counts and flexible form factors create stringent requirements for the hardware design. This intricate relationship highlights the first layer of mutual constraints, where transducer innovations directly dictate the performance requirements and physical limitations of the electronic hardware, a topic we will explore in the next section. For example, a higher channel count must be supported within a limited physical footprint and power budget. The next section examines these system-level integration challenges and the corresponding solutions for creating functional wearable ultrasound platforms.

System-level integration for wearable ultrasound

After addressing the challenge of achieving stable and comfortable acoustic coupling between the transducer and human skin, the integration and miniaturization of the hardware electronics system becomes the pivotal step in transitioning wearable ultrasound from proof-of-concept to practical application. This endeavour is governed by a fundamental engineering conflict: the relentless demand for high-quality, high-throughput data to ensure clinical accuracy versus the severe resource constraints imposed by a wearable form factor—namely, limited power, size, and bandwidth. While traditional prototyping methods using large, tethered backend systems (e.g., the Verasonics Vantage system, (Fig. 6b)) have accelerated early algorithm development, their physical constraints preclude real-world deployment33,47,87. The core engineering challenge, therefore, is to construct a miniaturized, low-power, integrated electronics system that can drive the transducer, process echo signals, and enable wireless data transmission. Successfully navigating this requires a systematic design approach focused on resolving the critical data-resource trade-off. The generic architecture and evolution of wearable ultrasound hardware

Despite the diverse forms and applications of wearable ultrasound systems, their hardware core can be abstracted into a generic functional architecture. The evolution of this architecture clearly reflects the technological adaptations made to meet the demands of wearability. A typical wearable ultrasound hardware system is primarily composed of four distinct yet closely integrated components as demonstrated in Fig. 6a: the Transducer Interface and Analog Signal Path, which handles signal generation and conditioning; the Digital Processing Core for system control and data management; the Data Communication Module for data transmission; and the power management unit (PMU) for energy supply and thermal regulation.

The signal chain begins at the Analog Subsystem, which serves as the critical interface between the digital core and the ultrasound transducer. Its functionality is divided into the transmit (TX) and receive (RX) pathways. The RX chain, comprising the low-noise amplifier (LNA), time-gain compensation (TGC), programmable gain amplifier (PGA), and analog-to-digital converter (ADC), is often specifically referred to as the analog front-end (AFE). The TX path is responsible for generating and delivering acoustic energy. The process typically starts with a high-voltage (HV) pulser that generates high-voltage pulses to excite the transducer based on commands from the digital core. For instance, the STHV748 chip can produce (±)50 V pulses88, while systems prioritizing portability might use chips like the TX7332 to support output voltages up to 160 Vpp89. However, a competing paradigm is emerging based on continuous-wave excitation. Systems utilizing time delay spectrometry (TDS), for example, can achieve deep-tissue imaging (up to 6 cm) with low-voltage (5 V peak-to-peak) sinusoidal sweeps. This approach significantly reduces sampling rate requirements to the kHz range and mitigates the safety concerns associated with high-voltage pulses, thereby simplifying the entire analog front-end design90. During transmission, a crucial Transmit/Receive (T/R) switch directs these high-energy pulses to the transducer while isolating the sensitive receiving circuitry to prevent damage91,92. The RX path is designed to capture and process the faint returning echo signals. After transmission, the T/R switch quickly changes state to guide the incoming echoes to the receive circuitry. Here, an LNA first amplifies the weak signals, followed by a TGC circuit that dynamically adjusts the gain to compensate for signal attenuation with tissue depth. Many systems employ integrated AFE chips, such as the AFE585191, which consolidate functions like the LNA and a PGA. Finally, an ADC digitizes the conditioned analog signal. Its sampling rate, typically between 40 MSPS and 50 MSPS, and resolution of 12 bits directly influence the final image quality89,93,94. This digitized data stream is then passed to the digital processing core for further handling. The digitized data is processed by the Digital Processing Core, which serves as the system’s central controller. This module is responsible for system timing control, data processing, and overall management. Key processing tasks in imaging systems include real-time beamforming, envelope detection, and Doppler processing, which necessitate the parallel computing power of FPGAs or ASICs. Its implementation has evolved, clearly reflecting the trade-off between performance and power consumption. In application-specific, low-power systems, a Microcontroller (MCU) often serves as the core (Fig. 6c–e), such as in A-mode systems based on the STM32F788,95 or ultra-low-power devices like the MSP43092 and STM32L496. For imaging systems requiring real-time beamforming, the Field-Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) is widely adopted due to its powerful parallel processing capabilities, with devices ranging from the early Spartan series93,97 to modern Artix-789,91 and low-power Igloo298 FPGAs. The most effective path to achieving maximum energy efficiency and compactness is the ASIC, which, through custom design, can reduce power consumption and area by an order of magnitude or more compared to general-purpose solutions99.

The Data Communication Module is essential for enabling wireless operation, a fundamental requirement for truly wearable devices (Fig. 6f). The development of this module reflects a significant progression from wired to wireless connectivity, driven by the demand for greater user mobility. Early research platforms typically relied on wired interfaces, such as serial ports95, USB-C89,94, and even fiber optics91,100, to transfer the high-volume raw RF data streams. Modern wearable systems have transitioned to wireless technologies, where the choice of protocol is dictated by a critical trade-off between data bandwidth and power consumption. For applications that require long-term physiological monitoring over days or weeks, Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) is the ideal solution due to its exceptionally low power usage, as demonstrated by the WULPUS platform92. In contrast, for mobile imaging systems that must stream B-mode image data in real time, Wi-Fi is the common choice. It provides a much higher bandwidth in the tens of Mbps, sufficient for maintaining a stable video feed98,101.

The stable operation of all system components is supported by the PMU. A typical PMU consists of a battery along with a network of DC-DC converters and low-dropout (LDO) regulators. Its primary function is to generate and distribute multiple stable, low-noise voltage rails, such as ±50 V, 3.3 V, and 1.2 V, to power the entire system93,98. A key challenge for the PMU in ultrasound systems is efficiently generating the high-voltage rails required by the pulser alongside the low-voltage supplies for digital logic, all from a single, low-voltage battery source. The role of the PMU is of particular importance in wearable designs, where energy efficiency directly dictates the device’s usability and operational longevity. The significant variation in system endurance observed across different platforms, which can range from a few hours of operation (1.5 hours93,97) to continuous multi-day monitoring (over 2 days92 or even 7 days96), directly reflects the effectiveness of the underlying power management strategy.

These four modules are deeply interdependent: higher imaging performance demands greater processing power and higher data bandwidth, which typically leads to an increase in size and power consumption, thereby reducing battery life. Understanding this trade-off is fundamental to all wearable ultrasound hardware design.

The first pathway involves minimalist systems dedicated to long-term physiological monitoring. Such designs prioritize ultra-low power and extended operational periods (days or longer), focusing on extracting specific physiological parameters like heart rate or blood flow velocity from 1D A-mode or Doppler signals. The core of this approach lies in a simplified hardware architecture and efficient on-device computation. For example, the WULPUS platform92 utilizes an ultra-low-power MSP430 MCU with its integrated ultrasound subsystem, paired with BLE communication, to achieve a system power consumption below 25 mW and an endurance of over two days. The PuLsE wrist-worn device96 takes this approach further, employing an innovative analog-domain envelope detection technique to reduce the 10 MHz signal bandwidth by more than fivefold, enabling direct processing by a low-cost MCU. This results in an average power consumption of just 5.8 mW and an operational battery life of over seven days. These systems typically transmit only the final physiological parameters, greatly reducing the demand on wireless bandwidth.

The second pathway seeks a balance between imaging performance and portability for mobile imaging. These systems aim to provide real-time B-mode or color Doppler images while managing resource consumption. To support the large number of elements required for imaging, multiplexing (MUX) technology is widely used to drive a large transducer array (e.g., 128 or 256 elements) with a smaller number of backend electronic channels (e.g., 32)68,101. While this may sacrifice some temporal resolution, it significantly reduces hardware complexity, size, and power consumption. For instance, the 256-element wearable array system developed by Speicher et al. successfully achieves high-quality abdominal muscle imaging using just 32 backend channels, enabled by a custom MUX ASIC101. For data transmission, Wi-Fi is the mainstream choice. The TinyProbe98, a 32-channel wireless probe, uses Wi-Fi to stream data with a power consumption of ~970 mW in imaging mode, allowing for approximately two hours of continuous operation. This balanced approach is the dominant direction for current commercial handheld ultrasound devices and cutting-edge research.

The third pathway comprises high-performance research platforms designed for cutting-edge exploration. These designs prioritize performance above all else to provide the highest fidelity data for advanced algorithm research, such as ultrafast imaging, often at the cost of portability. These platforms typically feature full parallel channel processing, relying on the immense parallel computing power of FPGAs, and use high-speed wired interfaces like USB-C or fiber optics to overcome wireless bandwidth limitations. For example, the 64-channel LightProbe91,100 transmits raw RF data in real-time to a host for GPU processing via a fiber optic link with a bandwidth of up to 26.4 Gb/s. Similarly, the programmable platform designed by Xu et al. achieves 128-channel real-time multimodal imaging via a USB-C connection (> 3.5 Gb/s)89. Although these platforms are not intended as wearable endpoints themselves, they provide the foundational hardware necessary to develop and validate the next generation of ultrasound algorithms. They are, therefore, crucial enabling technologies that drive innovation, which can later be translated into practical wearable systems.

Enabling technologies for system miniaturization

The realization of compact, power-efficient wearable ultrasound systems hinges on the convergence of three key enabling technologies. These innovations at the chip, transducer, and package levels are not merely incremental improvements; they fundamentally alter the design space and directly enable the system archetypes discussed previously.

Chip-level integration: the path to ASICs

At the heart of miniaturization is the strategy of chip-level integration, culminating in the development of Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs) or System-on-Chip (SoC) solutions. While FPGAs offer the flexibility required for research platforms, ASICs provide the ultimate path to commercial viability by integrating the analog front-end, digital beamforming logic, and even processor cores onto a single silicon die. This co-integration drastically reduces the physical footprint and eliminates power-hungry off-chip data transfers, leading to an order-of-magnitude reduction in power consumption102. The trade-off, however, is a significant non-recurring engineering (NRE) cost, which explains why ASICs are characteristic of mature, high-volume products rather than early-stage prototypes. For instance, the miniaturized therapeutic ultrasound device by Jiang et al. exemplifies this approach, with an ASIC that consolidates the DC-DC boost converter and transducer driver, achieving a highly compact form factor99. For diagnostic systems, future ASICs are expected to integrate the entire signal path, from multi-channel LNAs to digital beamformers, enabling truly “smart patch” functionalities.

Transducer technology evolution: the impact of MEMS

The maturation of MEMS transducer technology, particularly PMUTs and CMUTs, represents a paradigm shift that reverberates throughout the entire system design. Unlike traditional PZT piezoceramics, which require high driving voltages (often >50 V), MEMS transducers operate at much lower voltages (<20 V) compatible with standard CMOS processes103. This has profound system-level implications: it dramatically simplifies the PMU by obviating the need for complex and bulky high-voltage boost converters, which are often a major source of power loss and electronic noise. Furthermore, their wafer-level fabrication allows for the potential of monolithic integration—building the transducer elements directly atop the CMOS circuitry. This vision of a single chip containing both sensors and electronics is a critical step toward ultra-low-cost, disposable ultrasound patches.

Advanced packaging and heterogeneous integration

Finally, advanced packaging technologies provide the physical foundation for assembling these disparate components into a cohesive, reliable, and body-conformable device. The core challenge lies in bridging the vast mechanical mismatch between rigid, brittle silicon chips and soft, flexible transducer arrays. This is achieved through Heterogeneous Integration strategies. Technologies like flexible PCBs (FPCs) act as compliant interconnects between rigid circuit boards or chips. System-in-Package (SiP) techniques enable the vertical stacking of multiple dies, minimizing the lateral footprint. The stacked PCB designs seen in platforms like TinyProbe98 and WULPUS92 are prime examples, where SiP and FPCs are used to create a highly dense electronic core. These packaging solutions are not just about making things smaller; they are essential for ensuring long-term reliability and biocompatibility in a dynamic, on-body environment.

The data-resource trade-off in system miniaturization

The diverse hardware systems discussed in this section, from minimalist MCU-based patches to high-channel-count FPGA platforms, should be seen as different answers to the same fundamental engineering question: how to balance the need for high-quality data against strict resource limitations. On one hand, clinical applications require high-fidelity data. This is characterized by high channel counts for better imaging resolution, fast sampling rates for temporal accuracy, and the transmission of raw RF data for maximum post-processing flexibility. On the other hand, the wearable form factor imposes severe resource constraints. These include the need for minimal power consumption to extend battery life, a small physical footprint for user comfort, and a limited thermal envelope for safety.

This inherent conflict shapes the entire design approach and explains why different types of systems exist. For example, minimalist systems like the WULPUS platform prioritize long battery life and small size. They achieve this by strategically sacrificing data richness, processing complex signals into single parameters directly on the device to achieve multi-day autonomy. In direct contrast, high-performance research platforms like LightProbe prioritize data fidelity above all else. They offload resource-intensive computation to powerful external processors using high-bandwidth wired tethers, which sacrifices true wearability. The mainstream “balanced” approach, seen in devices like TinyProbe, represents a carefully engineered compromise. It uses techniques such as channel MUX and wireless streaming of compressed data to deliver acceptable imaging performance within a practical power and size envelope.

Ultimately, this essential trade-off leads to a critical and unavoidable conclusion: any data acquired from a wearable system is an inherently imperfect reflection of the body’s physiology. The constant need to manage resources means that the hardware itself has limitations, and the dynamic on-body environment introduces further noise and motion artifacts. This creates a significant “data-to-insight” gap that cannot be closed by improving hardware alone. Therefore, the limitations of the physical system create a clear and direct need for computational compensation. This inescapable trade-off underscores hardware limitations and creates a critical need for computational compensation. This requirement is a logical bridge to the next section, which focuses on advanced algorithms. These algorithms serve as the third pillar of the technology stack and are designed to restore signal quality and extract meaning from imperfect data.

Advanced algorithms for signal processing and imaging

Wearable ultrasound devices, enabled by conformal transducers and portable hardware, can acquire a continuous stream of physiological data. However, the raw radiofrequency (RF) signals or A-mode data are not the final output; a significant gap exists between this raw data and clinically valuable information. This section focuses on the advanced algorithms essential for bridging this gap. These algorithms transform noisy, continuous raw data into stable, accurate, and meaningful physiological metrics or medical images. They also address the unique challenge of signal distortion caused by the deformation of flexible devices. By performing these functions, these algorithms ensure that wearable ultrasound devices are both usable and reliable. We will explore the specific algorithmic challenges in wearable ultrasound, including real-time processing and robustness in dynamic environments, and systematically review current mainstream solutions.

Non-imaging algorithms for physiological parameter extraction

The high temporal resolution and low computational demand of one-dimensional (1D) ultrasound signals, such as A-mode and M-mode, offer a distinct advantage for wearable devices in capturing deep tissue dynamics. This capability is particularly crucial in cardiovascular monitoring, where the dynamic waveforms of organs and tissues directly reflect physiological function. Among these, the arterial diameter waveform is a physiological signal of paramount importance, as it captures the pulsatile change in vessel diameter with each cardiac cycle. This waveform not only reflects the mechanical properties and compliance of the vessel wall but also serves as the foundation for deriving a suite of core hemodynamic indicators, including BP, heart rate (Fig. 7a), PWV (Fig. 7c), and arterial stiffness104. These algorithms are founded on displacement estimation, using inter-frame cross-correlation or phase tracking to compute a continuous arterial diameter waveform, (D(t)35,105. These methods are computationally lightweight, making them well-suited for real-time execution on low-power MCUs typically found in long-endurance wearable monitors. However, these fundamental methods are sensitive to signal quality, and their accuracy degrades significantly with low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) signals from flexible sensors35,106. To enhance robustness, signal purification techniques are employed to increase the salience of target echoes. These techniques include decomposing the signal based on its structural or statistical properties. Methods like matching pursuit (MP) or singular value decomposition (SVD) can separate vessel wall echoes from noise106–108, while filtering methods like wavelet transforms can suppress specific speckle noise35.

The evolution of these algorithms is moving towards full automation, which relies on automatic target identification and continuous tracking. The former is achieved by automatically locating the vessel wall using techniques from image processing (Canny operator), machine learning (k-means), or by exploiting signal dynamics (frame differencing, SWC)49,105,109. The latter introduces state-space models, such as the Kalman filter (Fig. 7d), to fuse predictive and measurement data for smooth, real-time tracking109,110.

The stable diameter waveform, (D(t), produced by these algorithms is the basis for deriving clinical metrics. Integrated with physiological models like the Bramwell-Hill equation, it allows for the calculation of PWV and arterial stiffness109,111. A more significant application is cuffless BP monitoring, where a one-time calibration establishes a pressure-diameter model to convert the continuous diameter waveform into a BP waveform17,49,112,113.

Recently, machine learning has introduced a data-driven paradigm to parameter extraction. For instance, modular neural network (NN) pipelines can perform fully automated extraction of the diameter waveform from A-mode signals (Fig. 7e) with minimal computational complexity114. More advanced works utilize convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to automatically select the optimal signal channel from an array (Fig. 7b), ensuring robust tracking during motion, and employ domain adaptation techniques to improve the model’s generalization across different users14.

The core of imaging is beamforming, which synthesizes an image by applying precise delays and summations (Delay-and-Sum, DAS) (Fig. 8a) to signals received by each element of an array. Conventional ultrasound systems typically rely on powerful back-end processors, but wearable devices must perform computations under strict power and size constraints. Therefore, the hardware implementation and optimization of algorithms are critical. For instance, a high-resolution, all-digital transmit beamformer ASIC was designed for a high-frequency wearable ultrasound system. It achieved an ultra-high delay resolution of 535 ps while minimizing hardware cost and power consumption through an innovative architecture115. On the receiving end, a low-power, ultra-high-speed beamforming solution based on an FPGA has also been demonstrated. By optimizing IQ signal processing and leveraging the parallel computing capabilities of FPGAs, it achieved ultra-high-speed imaging at over 4000 frames per second with a total power consumption of just 1.627 watts. This performance lays the groundwork for cutting-edge applications like wearable microvascular imaging116.

For wearable ultrasound using flexible or stretchable arrays, the primary technical hurdle is the dynamic change in the transducer’s geometry during wear and motion. Traditional DAS algorithms assume that element positions are fixed and known. Once this assumption is violated, the calculated delays become incorrect, leading to severely defocused, distorted, or even unusable images. While structural innovations like the “constant-pitch-preservation” design aim to mitigate this issue at the physical level117, in many highly compliant systems, some degree of geometric distortion is unavoidable. Therefore, real-time and accurate estimation of the flexible array’s shape has become a prerequisite for high-quality imaging. To address this core problem, several technical paths have been explored.

Extrinsic-sensing-assisted methods

A direct approach is to integrate additional sensors to measure the array’s shape in real time. Pioneering work involved embedding optical shape sensing (OSS) fibers into the backing layer of flexible transducers118–120. By measuring the strain on the fibers, the 3D contour of the array can be accurately reconstructed, providing the precise position of each element to correct beamforming delays. Beyond embedded fibers, a range of external tracking modalities have been explored to map the transducer’s geometry, as illustrated in Fig. 8d–g. For high-precision, static shape characterization, MRI has been used to guide beamforming (Fig. 8d)121. For more dynamic applications, optical methods are prevalent. These include using a laser scanning system to acquire the 3D surface geometry of the deformed array (Fig. 8e)122, or employing a 3D surface scanner to map the patient’s skin curvature for pre-emptive phase correction (Fig. 8f)47. Furthermore, hybrid approaches, such as the FLEX system, utilize external optical markers and infrared trackers to continuously monitor the probe’s geometry in real time (Fig. 8g)123,124. Although these methods offer high accuracy, they increase system complexity, size, and cost, limiting their application in highly compact wearable scenarios.

Intrinsic self-estimation algorithms

To eliminate the reliance on external sensors, “intrinsic” algorithms have been developed that deduce the array’s shape solely from the ultrasound echo signals. The fundamental idea is that the correct array shape produces the sharpest image, for instance, an image with minimum information entropy or maximum coherence. Several strategies based on this principle have been developed. One common approach is to use an iterative optimization algorithm that adjusts shape parameters to maximize an image sharpness metric, functioning like an autofocus system125. For example, an algorithm based on image entropy minimization was proposed to estimate the array’s morphology by searching for the geometry that minimizes the reconstructed image’s entropy126. A similar model-based iterative approach is shown in ref. 127. In another method, the phase coherence between receive channels was utilized as a metric, which was then optimized to determine the array’s shape128. Other intrinsic self-estimation algorithms have been developed based on different physical or mathematical principles, such as direct-path analysis129, differentiable beamforming130, and maximizing image gradient energy131. Mathematical modeling combined with a simulated annealing optimization algorithm has also been applied to achieve self-shape estimation for a flexible array in breast diagnostics127. These intrinsic methods are more streamlined and highly integrated, representing a key direction for achieving truly seamless wearable imaging.

The rise of deep learning offers a new and more powerful toolkit for addressing all the aforementioned challenges. It can not only enhance the performance of traditional algorithms but can also subvert conventional processing workflows in an “end-to-end” fashion.

In shape estimation, deep neural networks (DNNs) have demonstrated strong non-linear mapping capabilities (Fig. 8h). DNN models like FlexSANet and others based on DenseNet have been proposed132–134. These models can directly learn the complex relationship between raw or pre-processed RF data and the array’s geometric parameters. This enables fast and highly accurate shape estimation without iterative optimization, showing great potential for real-time applications.

In beamforming and subsequent image analysis, deep learning has emerged as a powerful tool, transforming the entire processing pipeline from raw RF data to clinical insights. Its application begins at the image reconstruction stage. While early DNNs were applied to beamforming primarily to suppress off-axis scattering135, more advanced models have since been developed. For instance, sophisticated architectures like the multiconstrained hybrid generative adversarial network (MC-HGAN) are now used for advanced beamforming, capable of producing images with significantly higher quality and fewer artifacts than traditional methods (Fig. 8b)136. These data-driven beamformers can also effectively correct for phase aberrations caused by tissue inhomogeneity, a task that is challenging for conventional algorithms137,138. Furthermore, the role of deep learning extends into the post-processing stage, where it automates the extraction of clinically relevant information from the reconstructed images. A prime example is the use of deep learning workflows for automated cardiac assessment (Fig. 8c)47. In this application, a neural network performs semantic segmentation of the left ventricle, enabling the automatic calculation of key functional indices such as ejection fraction and stroke volume. This progression from enhancing image quality to automating quantitative analysis highlights deep learning’s potential to create more intelligent and autonomous wearable ultrasound systems.

Notably, deep learning can even bypass the challenging intermediate step of shape estimation. An end-to-end beamforming network, such as a Pix2Pix GAN, was proposed to directly learn the mapping from RF channel data acquired by a flexible array with unknown geometry to a high-quality B-mode image139. This approach does not rely on any prior geometric information and merges the two steps of shape estimation and beamforming into one. It offers a transformative new paradigm for solving the flexible array imaging problem. However, these advanced deep learning models for tasks like shape estimation or end-to-end beamforming are computationally intensive. Unlike traditional algorithms, they demand more powerful processing units, such as on-device AI accelerators (Edge AI) or FPGAs, to achieve real-time performance within the stringent power budget of a wearable system.

The role of algorithms in data compensation, restoration, and fusion

This section synthesizes the core theme of synergistic integration, illustrating how algorithms function as an indispensable compensatory layer bridging the gap between the physical limitations of wearable transducers and the demand for high-fidelity clinical data. The collection of algorithms discussed here, from cross-correlation tracking to deep learning-based beamforming, shares a common objective: to computationally restore or enhance data integrity degraded by motion, deformation, or environmental perturbations. Conventional ultrasound assumes a stable operator, a stationary patient, and a rigid probe. Wearable ultrasound violates all these assumptions, producing inherently imperfect signals that require computational restoration. Algorithmic intelligence thus becomes the key enabler linking flexible hardware with reliable clinical outcomes.

Data compensation and restoration algorithms

Viewed from this perspective, the algorithms are not merely post-processing tools but active compensation and restoration systems. Signal purification and tracking algorithms for 1D data act to recover canonical physiological waveforms from noisy and intermittent signal streams, effectively filtering out “wearable noise.” Similarly, advanced beamforming algorithms for flexible arrays serve as shape-restoration techniques, computationally reconstructing the ideal undeformed array geometry to restore the spatial focus and clarity of the resulting image.

Deep learning models, in their end-to-end implementations, represent the pinnacle of this paradigm. They learn to transform the “distorted reality” of raw RF data from a bent or shifting array into the “idealized output” of a high-quality B-mode image. From a system design viewpoint, a mechanically robust sensor may require less computational correction, whereas a highly compliant, skin-conformal device depends heavily on algorithmic intelligence to ensure data fidelity. The establishment of this complete technology stack, comprising transducer, hardware, and algorithms, lays the foundation for reliable clinical deployment.

Multimodal data fusion for wearable ultrasound

To overcome the limitations of single-modal sensing and enhance data fidelity, wearable ultrasound systems are increasingly integrated with other sensors in a strategy known as multimodal data fusion. This approach leverages the complementary strengths of different sensors to create a more complete and robust physiological picture. A primary application is motion compensation140. For instance, combining A-mode ultrasound with inertial measurement units (IMUs) enables accurate joint torque estimation during dynamic activities70. In this synergy, the ultrasound provides muscle-specific biomechanical data while the IMU offers the global kinematic context, achieving a level of biomechanical insight that neither sensor could provide alone. This fusion logic extends to the simultaneous assessment of tissue structure and function. By integrating flexible ultrasound transducers with EMG electrodes, it is possible to synchronously measure a muscle’s morphological changes and its underlying electrical activation, leading to superior performance in neuromuscular diagnostics141. Such integration also drives device-level innovation, as seen with transparent ultrasound transducers that allow coaxial fusion with photoplethysmography (PPG) sensors. This design not only solves physical integration challenges for miniaturisation but also enhances cardiovascular monitoring by using robust ultrasound data to validate or correct the more motion-sensitive optical signals142. Despite these demonstrated performance enhancements, significant challenges remain in real-time data synchronisation, the computational efficiency of on-device fusion algorithms, and the fabrication of highly integrated yet comfortable systems, all of which are critical for achieving truly autonomous multimodal monitoring.

The establishment of this complete technology stack, comprising transducer, hardware, and algorithms, provides the necessary foundation for clinical implementation. Having detailed the technological underpinnings, we now turn to the clinical utility of this end-to-end system in the survey of cardiovascular applications that follows.

Clinical applications in cardiovascular hemodynamic monitoring

Building upon the technological foundations of conformal transducers (“Wearable ultrasound transducers for conformal”), miniaturized electronics (“System-level integration for wearable ultrasound”), and intelligent algorithms (“Advanced algorithms for signal processing and imaging”), this section systematically explores the current landscape and future potential of wearable ultrasound in cardiovascular monitoring. Monitoring cardiovascular health is paramount, driving a crucial paradigm shift from reactively treating acute events to proactively managing health risks through personalized care143. Adopting a “core-to-frontier” approach, we begin with established applications like continuous arterial hemodynamic monitoring, then progress to the direct assessment of central organs, specifically the heart and brain. Finally, we examine emerging applications in the venous system and peripheral circulation. This section’s structure mirrors the technological evolution of the field, moving from one-dimensional (1D) data streams to four-dimensional (4D) imaging. This progression underscores how wearable ultrasound is designed to overcome the limitations of existing clinical monitoring. The ultimate goal is to transform cardiovascular health management from an intermittent, operator-dependent model to a new paradigm of continuous, automated, and personalized care.

Core application: continuous arterial hemodynamic monitoring

The arterial system offers a high-fidelity window into cardiovascular health, yet its continuous monitoring remains a long-standing clinical challenge. Intermittent cuff-based sphygmomanometry misses crucial dynamic information, while invasive arterial catheters are reserved for critical care settings. Wearable ultrasound is engineered to bridge this gap, facilitating a paradigm shift from sporadic snapshots to a continuous, multi-parametric stream of arterial function. This section explores how the technology systematically enables the acquisition of key arterial parameters, from foundational BP and heart rate to advanced metrics of blood flow and vascular stiffness.

BP and heart rate monitoring

Achieving cuffless, continuous BP monitoring is a primary objective, poised to revolutionize hypertension management by revealing diurnal patterns and masked conditions144. The core principle of ultrasound-based BP sensing lies in its ability to directly measure the mechanical distension of the arterial wall. Using pulse-echo techniques (A-mode or M-mode), wearable sensors can track the beat-to-beat arterial diameter waveform with high temporal resolution (Fig. 9d)35,106,145. While early rigid probes validated this principle, their clinical utility was hampered by difficulties in maintaining precise alignment and stable acoustic coupling over time149–151. The advent of flexible and conformal electronics proved to be a critical turning point. For instance, Wang et al. developed a device that integrates the high-performance 1-3 piezocomposites with the stretchable “island-bridge” architecture, two key technologies detailed in “Wearable ultrasound transducers for conformal sensing”. This design enabled a flexible patch to maintain stable, long-term conformal contact with the neck. This breakthrough enabled the acquisition of central arterial BP waveforms that showed remarkable consistency with clinical gold standards, laying the groundwork for monitoring under dynamic conditions17. The robustness of this approach has since been validated in demanding clinical environments, such as a study by Zhou et al., where a wearable sensor provided continuous, accurate BP data from ICU patients for over 12 hours, remaining reliable during routine patient activities and clinical interventions63.

A significant hurdle for early models was their reliance on initial cuff calibration. To create truly autonomous systems, researchers have pioneered calibration-free methods. A key strategy involves using dual-probe PMUT systems to simultaneously measure diameter waveforms at two arterial sites. This allows for the beat-to-beat calculation of local PWV, which directly reflects instantaneous arterial stiffness and enables self-contained BP estimation55,152. This trend is powered by MEMS-based devices (PMUTs and CMUTs), whose miniaturization, low power consumption, and integration potential are driving the field toward compact, image-free sensing systems ideal for long-term, ambulatory use49,56.

While often derived from the BP waveform, dedicated heart rate (HR) monitoring is another area where ultrasound excels, offering a robust alternative to PPG, especially in challenging conditions. On the wrist, a low-power ultrasound system has demonstrated reduced sensitivity to motion artifacts, skin color variations, and ambient light compared to PPG, leading to superior accuracy96. More advanced wrist-worn systems even use sensor arrays to capture detailed pulse morphology for classifying physiological states153. Furthermore, a single, ultra-low-power chest ultrasound patch has shown the ability to monitor both HR and respiration rate (RR) simultaneously, highlighting the potential for holistic, unobtrusive cardiopulmonary assessment154.

Hemodynamics and arterial stiffness assessment

A comprehensive understanding of arterial health necessitates moving beyond pressure and rate to assess blood flow and vessel wall integrity. Wearable ultrasound provides powerful tools for both.

For hemodynamics, the primary challenge is accurately measuring blood flow velocity, a task complicated by the inherent Doppler angle dependency. To address this, distinct device- and algorithm-level strategies have emerged. A rigid, hands-free Doppler patch (Fig. 9b), pioneered by Kenny et al., utilizes an innovative wide-beam design to insonate the entire carotid artery. This mitigates operator dependency and enables robust tracking of relative changes in stroke volume surrogates, a critical capability in acute care155,156. For measuring absolute velocity, a complementary approach by Wang et al. employs a flexible, skin-conforming patch that embeds rigid transducer elements at precise angles. Combined with a dual-beam Doppler (DBUD) method (Fig. 9g), this allows for the algorithmic elimination of the unknown vessel angle, showcasing a sophisticated fusion of mechanics and signal processing69. These efforts, alongside integrated neckband systems for bilateral monitoring97, demonstrate that stable geometric control, achieved through either rigid platforms or hybrid flexible-rigid designs, is key to unlocking continuous, quantitative flow assessment.

For arterial stiffness, wearable ultrasound enables the monitoring of its gold-standard metric, PWV, with significantly enhanced local resolution. Flexible arrays laminated onto the skin can track PWV over short arterial segments, offering superior sensitivity to localized pathologies74. This local approach is being advanced across diverse platforms, from polymer-based arrays41 to miniaturized PMUTs for pulse wave imaging (PWI)106,157, with novel scanning methods enhancing robustness against misalignment158. In parallel, the technology targets structural markers of stiffness like Carotid Intima-Media Thickness (CIMT). Prototypes for automating B-mode imaging and CIMT analysis aim to bring this key atherosclerotic assessment into the home51,159. The feasibility is supported by high-frequency flexible arrays capable of long-term imaging (Fig. 9h)160 and PMUTs that can not only quantify biomechanical properties like distensibility161 but also provide direct imaging of the arterial wall and lumen for intuitive vascular health assessment162.

A more advanced frontier in stiffness assessment involves moving beyond simple PWV to characterize the complex, anisotropic mechanical properties of the arterial wall. Recent breakthroughs, although not yet fully wearable, demonstrate the principle of using programmed acoustic radiation force to excite and track bidirectional guided waves within the artery. By applying sophisticated inverse models, it is possible to dynamically quantify directional stiffness, wall stress, and even local BP within a single cardiac cycle. This provides an unprecedentedly rich view of vascular health, setting a clear future target for the next generation of intelligent wearable ultrasound systems163.

Emerging frontiers: direct monitoring of central organs

Early attempts to apply ultrasound patches for cardiac monitoring initiated a quest to maintain stable acoustic access to deep organs during motion. First-generation devices employed single-element piezoelectric polymers (e.g., PVDF) to record M-mode traces of heart wall motion along a single line, from which basic parameters like HR could be derived164. These sensors, however, faced two formidable challenges: a weak signal that struggled to penetrate the thoracic cage for clear cardiac echoes, and a narrow “acoustic window” that made the signal highly susceptible to loss from minor body movements or respiration. This rendered them more as proofs-of-concept than reliable monitoring tools. Subsequent efforts sought to overcome these bottlenecks through iterative improvements at both the device and algorithm levels. On the device front, designs like the double-layer PVDF composite structure significantly boosted signal strength and penetration depth, making it feasible to stably capture wall motion at a 30 mm depth38. At the algorithm level, an “intelligent gating” strategy has emerged, where machine learning is leveraged to automatically select the optimal tracking channel from a miniature array’s multi-channel data in real-time14. Despite these remarkable advances, they could not escape the fundamental limitation of one-dimensional measurement. They provided a temporal, cardiogram-like trace of cardiac motion, but not a true anatomical snapshot. A comprehensive assessment of ventricular volume, ejection fraction, and regional wall motion abnormalities remained out of reach.

Recently, the field achieved a breakthrough with two-dimensional (2D) imaging, transitioning from single-line measurements to the direct visualization of cardiac anatomy. Hu et al. presented a landmark wearable cardiac imager built upon an innovative orthogonal phased-array design (Fig. 9i)47. This cruciform structure is not merely a structural choice but a functional necessity: its two perpendicular linear arrays enable electronic steering of the ultrasound beam along two orthogonal planes without physically repositioning the patch. Consequently, the device can be affixed to the chest to continuously acquire pairs of standard clinical views, such as the apical four-chamber and two-chamber views, even while the subject is exercising. The system then employs the deep-learning-based analytical methods, a strategy reviewed in “Advanced algorithms for signal processing and imaging”, to provide real-time outputs of key functional parameters (including left ventricular volume and ejection fraction) with an accuracy comparable to clinical equipment, allowing for a direct assessment of cardiac performance under stress.

Furthermore, addressing the challenge of long-term acoustic coupling as detailed in “Acoustic Interface Layers: Matching and Backing”, Wang et al. utilized a novel bio-adhesive hydrogel (Fig. j)33. This material innovation enables a wearable patch to achieve stable, high-quality imaging of deep organs, including the heart, for an unprecedented duration of up to 48 hours. This capability extends the monitoring window far beyond short-term stress tests, making it possible to continuously track cardiac parameters throughout diverse daily activities and sleep cycles. These complementary technologies transform cardiac function assessment from a static, “before-and-after” snapshot model to a dynamic, continuous “video” model. This provides an unparalleled ability to capture transient abnormalities (such as myocardial ischemia) that may only manifest under specific physiological stress or during particular daily routines, which are often missed in conventional clinical settings.

Parallel to the heart, the brain stands as another vital central organ where continuous hemodynamic monitoring offers profound clinical insights. The cardiovascular and cerebrovascular systems are intrinsically linked, and assessing the brain’s blood supply is critical for understanding neurological function and risk. Currently, the standard method for non-invasively monitoring cerebral hemodynamics is transcranial doppler (TCD), but its operational complexity and reliance on skilled professionals limit its use in continuous monitoring. While robot-assisted systems have shown some improvement, the equipment remains cumbersome165,166.

Wearable TCD technology is dedicated to solving this problem. A breakthrough was achieved by Zhou et al. with a conformal matrix-array ultrasound patch. This device utilizes ultrafast diverging wave imaging to achieve, for the first time in a wearable context, transcranial three-dimensional (3D) volumetric blood flow imaging (Fig. 9a)167. It can construct a 3D vascular network map of the Circle of Willis and its main branches, providing a clear anatomical context for blood flow velocity measurements and thus overcoming the “blind search” limitation of conventional TCD. This technology supports continuous, hands-free monitoring for several hours, offering a powerful new tool for neurocritical care (e.g., vasospasm monitoring) and stroke risk assessment. However, the human skull remains a formidable acoustic barrier, causing severe attenuation and phase aberration. Addressing this skull attenuation and phase aberration remains a central challenge168. Emerging solutions include the development of soft acoustic metalenses, which use gradient-index soft materials to pre-compensate for skull-induced aberrations, enabling broadband ultrasonic focusing through the skull with significant intensity enhancement at the target169. In frontier explorations, high-frequency ultrafast ultrasound is being used for cerebral microcirculation imaging in animal models, aiming to resolve blood flow perfusion at the microvascular level. This may open new avenues for future research into the early pathophysiology of neurodegenerative diseases170. Furthermore, the concept of wearable neuromodulation is taking shape, with miniature, implantable, and frequency-tunable focused ultrasound devices being developed. These devices, based on high-performance lead-free materials, can be implanted on the skull for long-term, targeted stimulation of deep brain structures, opening new therapeutic avenues for cardiovascular conditions (e.g., post-infarction sympathetic hyperactivity) by modulating the brain-heart axis32.

Expanding applications: venous system and peripheral perfusion

The scope of wearable ultrasound applications is gradually expanding to the hemodynamic monitoring of the venous system and peripheral tissues. The venous system provides an important window into the body’s volume status and right heart function. A wearable ultrasound device tracking the pulsation of the internal jugular vein (IJV) can non-invasively quantify the jugular venous pulse (JVP) as a surrogate for central venous pressure (CVP) (Fig. 9b)171. Additionally, wearable Doppler can be used to analyze the IJV flow spectrum, whose waveform characteristics are closely related to right atrial pressure and can help diagnose conditions like pulmonary hypertension172. A multimodal monitoring strategy, by synchronously acquiring signals from the carotid artery (an indicator of cardiac output) and the IJV (an indicator of volume), enables the non-invasive, real-time construction of a Frank-Starling curve, providing a quantitative basis for fluid management in critically ill patients173.

In peripheral circulation monitoring, wearable ultrasound also demonstrates unique application value. For the prevention of deep vein thrombosis (DVT), researchers are developing wearable systems capable of automated compression ultrasound to enable long-term, unattended monitoring of high-risk populations174. For patients with peripheral artery disease (PAD), portable Doppler devices can be used to assess blood flow in the lower limbs (Fig. 9e)175. In terms of tissue perfusion, although quantitative monitoring of deep organs remains a challenge, preliminary progress has been made in local tissue perfusion monitoring. For example, after free flap transplantation surgery, a patch-type ultrasound probe can directly image and monitor blood flow in the microvasculature of the flap, providing a reliable means for the early detection of vascular compromise and ensuring flap survival176.

The trajectory of clinical translation and impact

The clinical applications surveyed in this section illustrate a clear and logical trajectory in the evolution of wearable ultrasound, a path defined by increasing information dimensionality and clinical depth. This progression is not arbitrary; it directly mirrors the maturation of the underlying technology stack and reflects a strategic expansion from addressing simple physiological questions to tackling complex, systemic clinical problems.

The journey begins with 1D physiological parameter monitoring. Applications like cuffless BP and heart rate monitoring rely on extracting high-fidelity temporal information from a single spatial point. This represents the most accessible entry point, requiring the simplest technological implementation but delivering profound value by making vital signs continuous. The next stage advances to 2D/3D organ-level functional imaging. The development of wearable cardiac and transcranial imagers signifies a major leap in complexity, demanding an array of transducers, parallel hardware, and sophisticated beamforming. The clinical reward is a shift from monitoring abstract parameters to visualising the dynamic anatomy and function of central organs, enabling the capture of transient events like stress-induced ischemia. The current frontier is emerging at the level of multi-site, systemic circulatory assessment. By integrating data from both arterial and venous systems, or by mapping perfusion across tissues, these applications aim to paint a holistic picture of a patient’s hemodynamic status, moving towards a comprehensive assessment of the entire cardiovascular loop.

This framework synthesizes the field’s progress. By navigating this trajectory, the field has established a powerful toolkit. However, the successful demonstration of these applications also highlights the systemic challenges that must be overcome for widespread clinical adoption. Making these tools ubiquitous and autonomous requires a more deeply integrated approach across the three core technological domains. Consolidating these overarching challenges, from materials to on-device computation, is essential for future progress. These topics form the subject of our concluding section.

Clinical translation pathways and commercialization outlook

The translation of wearable ultrasound from research prototypes to mainstream clinical tools involves overcoming multiple practical challenges, including device optimization, clinical validation, regulatory approval, and integration into healthcare workflows. A primary hurdle is rigorous clinical validation. Unlike conventional snapshot ultrasound, wearable devices produce continuous, high-volume data streams, requiring longitudinal studies in real-world settings to demonstrate both measurement accuracy and actionable clinical benefit.

Regulatory clearance is a critical milestone. Wearable platforms, particularly those incorporating machine-learning-based analysis, face complexities beyond traditional ultrasound systems. Frameworks such as the FDA’s guidance on Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) provide initial pathways, yet many devices lack clear predicate systems and may require De Novo classification or premarket approval (PMA). Compliance with standards for electrical safety, acoustic output, and biocompatibility remains essential.

The commercial trajectory of recent innovations highlights that targeting well-defined clinical needs is a key strategy. For instance, Flosonics Medical’s FloPatch® achieved FDA and Health Canada approval by focusing on a specific, high-value problem: assessing fluid responsiveness in critically ill patients155. This niche focus provided a clear path through regulatory and clinical validation. This contrasts with ventures like Softsonics Inc., which translates advanced research on bioadhesive patches into a broader platform for applications such as continuous cardiac imaging17. Both cases confirm that a clear clinical purpose is vital for transforming a technology into a market-ready product, reflecting an industry-wide convergence of wearable electronics and medical imaging.

Looking forward, the successful clinical adoption of wearable ultrasound will depend on coordinated advances across materials, electronics, algorithmic intelligence, and integration into healthcare systems. Establishing robust validation protocols, regulatory strategies, and reimbursement pathways will be essential to extend continuous, data-driven cardiovascular monitoring beyond hospital settings, supporting proactive and personalized patient care.

Challenges and future perspectives

Wearable ultrasound technology is at a critical juncture, transitioning from laboratory prototypes to widespread clinical application. To successfully navigate this transition, it is essential to systematically address the complex challenges that span the device, system, and algorithm levels. These challenges, however, also guide the direction of future technological innovation. Materials and devices: the bio-physical interface

Future development at the device level hinges on resolving the trade-offs among acoustic performance, long-term wearing comfort, and biosafety.

First, achieving high acoustic performance with assured biocompatibility is paramount. While PZT-based materials offer excellent signal quality, their lead content raises concerns for long-term wear. A key research direction is therefore the development of high-performance, lead-free piezoelectric materials, such as KNN-based ceramics, that can match the performance of their lead-based counterparts. For flexible arrays, maintaining acoustic integrity during dynamic deformation remains a central challenge. Geometric distortion of the array can lead to beam defocusing and image artifacts. Future work must explore robust solutions like adaptive array configurations and multi-subarray designs that can computationally or structurally compensate for deformation in real time.

Second, ensuring long-term comfort and safety is critical for user adoption and regulatory approval. Current encapsulation materials like PDMS are often not breathable, which can cause skin irritation. Future research should focus on novel substrates and encapsulation materials that combine excellent acoustic properties with breathability, such as functionalized textiles. Stable, long-term acoustic coupling also requires innovative solid or semi-solid media, like advanced hydrogels, which must demonstrate durable adhesion, water retention, and validated biocompatibility. Finally, thermal safety from prolonged operation is a significant concern. Future devices must integrate temperature monitoring and adaptive power control functions to prevent tissue overheating, a necessary condition for regulatory approval.

Systems and algorithms: the intelligent core

The future goal at the system level is to build an integrated, autonomous platform that operates reliably in real-world settings. This requires the co-evolution of hardware and algorithms to overcome the fundamental trade-off between high-throughput data demands and strict power constraints.

The core challenge for hardware is to manage the large volume of data generated by wearable applications. The technical path forward is toward higher integration, ultimately incorporating the analog front-end, digital beamforming, and processing units into ultra-low-power ASICs. These hardware advancements provide the physical foundation for migrating complex processing from the cloud to the device itself.

Concurrently, algorithms must ensure system robustness in dynamic environments. The primary task is to restore clear clinical data from signals inherently corrupted by motion and device deformation. On-device artificial intelligence (Edge AI) is key to achieving this goal. By deploying lightweight neural networks on-chip, a wearable system can perform real-time signal quality assessment, motion artifact suppression, and key parameter extraction. This enables advanced autonomous tasks, such as automated ejection fraction calculation, providing immediate health feedback while fundamentally protecting user privacy. Furthermore, multimodal data fusion, integrating ultrasound with sensors like IMUs and ECG, will be standard for building a comprehensive model of device motion and enhancing artifact compensation.

Clinical translation and application expansion

Ultimately, synergistic technological advancements will drive the expansion of clinical applications in both depth and breadth.

The next major frontier for wearable ultrasound is the continuous hemodynamic assessment of deep parenchymal organs, including the kidneys and liver. Success in this area could transform fields like acute kidney injury prediction and liver fibrosis management. Monitoring the microvascular perfusion of an organ or tumor could also enable real-time assessment of therapeutic efficacy, laying the foundation for future systems to evolve from a diagnostic tool to a closed-loop “theranostic” platform that supports therapeutic interventions.

Regarding clinical translation, the final barriers are both technical and logistical. This technology requires large-scale, multi-centre, long-term clinical trials to validate its accuracy, reliability, and clinical benefit in real-world settings. Academia, industry, and regulatory agencies must collaborate to establish standardized evaluation protocols and regulatory frameworks for these novel medical devices. Finally, factors such as data security, device privacy, and cost control must be comprehensively considered, as they are essential for achieving broad clinical and market acceptance.

Conclusion

This review follows the “transducer-hardware-algorithm” technical framework to systematically chart the evolution of the wearable ultrasound field. It deconstructs the complete technological chain, from conformal transducers and miniaturized hardware to the intelligent algorithms that ensure data fidelity. The analysis shows that the development trajectory of wearable ultrasound clearly points toward the continuous miniaturization and integration of clinical ultrasound’s diagnostic capabilities into devices that conform to the human body. The future direction will be guided by clinical application needs, aiming for a deep integration across four levels: materials, structure, hardware, and algorithms. Although significant challenges remain in biocompatibility, system autonomy, and clinical validation, ongoing innovations in materials science, edge computing, and multi-modal sensing are paving the way to overcome them. Wearable ultrasound is more than just an engineering achievement. It represents a paradigm shift in how we perceive and manage human health. It promises to transform medicine from intermittent, reactive, snapshot-based diagnostics to a proactive, personalized, and continuous form of health management. Ultimately, it will make the invisible physiological dynamics within our bodies visible, interpretable, and actionable at any time and any place.
