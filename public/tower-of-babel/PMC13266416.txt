Comparative Usability Evaluation of Two Hospital Information Systems: A Mixed‐Methods Study in an Iranian Teaching Hospital
Authors: Atefeh Sadat Mousavi, Ehsan Jalili Honarvar, Aida Ardebili Haghighi, Sedigheh Parveizi, Mouzhan Granfar, Seyyedeh Fatemeh Mousavi Baigi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsUsability of hospital information systems (HIS) is critical for workflow efficiency, user satisfaction, and safe clinical practice. Comparative evaluations of legacy desktop‐based HIS and newer web‐based platforms remain limited, particularly during the real‐world digital transition. This study aimed to compare the usability of the legacy Iranian Hospital Information System (IHIS) and the newly implemented web‐based Sepid system in the Inpatient Admission and Radiology modules of a teaching hospital in Mashhad, Iran.MethodsThis convergent parallel mixed‐methods study was conducted from July to September 2025 at Qaem Educational and Research Hospital. In the qualitative phase, eight expert evaluators conducted heuristic evaluations based on Nielsen's ten usability heuristics. In the quantitative phase, 34 end‐users with routine experience using both systems completed the System Usability Scale (SUS). Qualitative findings were thematically consolidated, and quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and paired comparative tests.ResultsHeuristic evaluation showed that Sepid's main usability problems were related to system performance, external database integration, and error messaging, whereas IHIS demonstrated more fundamental interface‐related limitations, including visual clutter, weak flexibility, and lack of undo/redo functionality. Quantitatively, Sepid achieved a higher mean SUS score than IHIS (66.25 vs. 51.99). For Sepid, 4 users (11.8%) rated usability as unacceptable, 17 (50.0%) as marginal, and 13 (38.2%) as acceptable. For IHIS, the corresponding values were 12 (35.3%), 19 (55.9%), and 3 (8.8%), respectively. The between‐system difference in SUS scores was statistically significant (p < 0.001).ConclusionAlthough Sepid outperformed IHIS, both systems remained within the marginal usability range. The findings indicate that HIS improvement should prioritize user‐centered interface redesign, stronger error prevention, better interoperability with external databases, and continuous user training. These results may guide developers, hospital managers, and policymakers in improving HIS design and implementation during the digital transition.Keywords: heuristic evaluation, hospital information system, sepid, usability, user interface design, user‐centered design

Background

In recent decades, the exponential advancement of information and communication technologies (ICT) has fundamentally transformed all aspects of human life, including the healthcare sector [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Hospitals, as complex social and clinical institutions, rely heavily on the timely collection and analysis of accurate medical data to support clinical decision‐making, education, research, and quality improvement in healthcare delivery [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Hospital Information Systems (HIS), therefore, play a pivotal role in managing patient‐related data, optimizing hospital operations, and ensuring effective decision‐making by administrators and clinicians [11].

Modern HIS are increasingly sophisticated, integrating multiple subsystems and enabling the collection, processing, and distribution of clinical and administrative data across departments [12, 13]. When effectively implemented, these systems can improve care quality, enhance patient safety, reduce clinical errors, lower costs, and increase provider efficiency [14, 15]. However, despite these advantages, the successful deployment of HIS often faces major usability challenges that hinder user adoption and system effectiveness [16, 17, 18, 19, 20].

Given the essential role of HIS in delivering high‐quality healthcare, usability is recognized as a critical attribute. The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) defines usability as the degree to which a system can be used by specific users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a given context [21, 22]. Nielsen further emphasizes five dimensions of usability: learnability, efficiency, memorability, error reduction, and user satisfaction [23]. Poor usability can lead to delayed task completion, user frustration, increased training needs, medical errors, and ultimately system abandonment [24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29].

A recent systematic review of the global literature highlights that user testing and heuristic evaluation (HE) are the most frequently applied methods for evaluating HIS usability internationally [30]. Standardized instruments such as the System Usability Scale (SUS) effectively capture subjective user satisfaction, while expert‐based heuristic evaluations systematically uncover latent design flaws against established principles [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. Recent international evidence underscores that relying on a single evaluation method is often insufficient; methodological triangulation—combining both expert‐based cognitive evaluations and user‐based perceptual assessments—provides the most robust understanding of system functionality and user experience [30].

Importantly, prior studies have demonstrated that usability problems in hospital information systems are not comprehensively captured when relying on a single evaluation method. While user‐based instruments such as the SUS effectively assess perceived satisfaction, they may fail to detect underlying interface design flaws. Conversely, expert‐based approaches, such as heuristic evaluation, can systematically identify structural usability issues but may not fully reflect real‐world clinical workflows and user experiences. This inherent methodological limitation further reinforces the necessity of adopting integrated, mixed‐method approaches to achieve a more comprehensive and valid usability assessment.

Despite a growing body of research evaluating HIS usability, a critical review of the literature reveals specific limitations. First, the vast majority of usability evaluations are conducted on single, established systems post‐implementation, lacking a comparative baseline to measure technological progress [30]. Second, while high‐income countries are increasingly focusing on the usability of interoperable, longitudinal Electronic Health Records (EHRs), research in resource‐constrained or developing settings still heavily focuses on isolated, legacy desktop modules [30]. Furthermore, comparative usability evaluations focusing specifically on the critical transitional phase—migrating from legacy, desktop‐based HIS to modern, cross‐device, web‐based platforms—remain exceedingly scarce, particularly in high‐traffic hospital modules such as Admission and Radiology.

The present study addresses these gaps by evaluating a real‐world digital transformation scenario in Iran. In the Iranian context, legacy systems and national electronic health records (e.g., SEPAS) have been shown to suffer from significant technical, identity, and clinical errors, underscoring the urgent need for modern, user‐centered systemic upgrades [36]. In 2002, Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (MUMS) introduced a customized desktop‐based HIS known as IHIS, deployed across numerous hospitals. While functional, its legacy interface has shown severe limitations in flexibility and modern clinical adaptability [16]. Recently, a new web‐based platform called Sepid has been introduced in teaching hospitals, offering cross‐device compatibility (desktop, tablet, mobile) to replace IHIS.

This study aimed to comparatively evaluate the usability of the legacy platform (IHIS) and the newly implemented web‐based platform (Sepid) in the Inpatient Admission and Radiology modules of Qaem Educational Hospital in Mashhad. By employing a triangulated mixed‐methods approach (integrating SUS and heuristic evaluation), this study provides a comparative, multi‐dimensional assessment of system performance. By focusing on a real‐world transition from a legacy desktop‐based system to a modern web‐based platform, this study provides rare empirical evidence on usability challenges during digital transformation—an area that remains underexplored in current literature. The findings will contribute to the global discourse on digital health transformation by providing evidence‐based insights for health IT managers and HIS vendors to minimize transition friction, prioritize user‐centered design, and enhance the adoption of next‐generation HIS platforms.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study employed a convergent parallel mixed‐methods design, integrating expert‐based qualitative heuristic evaluations with user‐based quantitative assessments to provide a comprehensive usability profile. In this design, qualitative and quantitative data were collected concurrently, analyzed separately, and integrated during the interpretation phase to enable triangulation and cross‐validation of findings. The study was conducted from July to September 2025 at Qaem Educational and Research Hospital, a major referral center affiliated with MUMS in northeastern Iran. The evaluation focused on the Inpatient Admission and Radiology Information System (RIS) modules of two platforms: the legacy desktop‐based system (IHIS) and the newly implemented web‐based system (Sepid).

Heuristic Evaluation (Expert‐Based Assessment)

System usability was assessed using Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics framework [23]. Eight evaluators participated, including five Health Information Technology (HIT) specialists and three senior radiology experts. The evaluation team comprised software engineers, health information managers, the hospital's HIS coordinator, and clinical radiologists. The panel demonstrated a high level of expertise, with a mean professional experience of 20.12 ± 0.52 years. All evaluators had extensive hands‐on experience with both IHIS and Sepid systems and had received formal training in heuristic evaluation.

Evaluators independently examined the system interfaces across three to four structured sessions to identify usability violations without peer influence. Subsequently, each identified issue was assigned a severity rating based on Nielsen's standardized scale (0 = not a usability problem to 4 = usability catastrophe). Prior to evaluation, calibration sessions were conducted to ensure consistency in interpreting heuristic criteria.

System Usability Scale (SUS – User‐Based Assessment)

End‐user usability was assessed using the SUS [33, 34, 35]. A census sampling approach was applied, resulting in 34 eligible participants from healthcare and HIT domains. Participants included professionals from HIT (n = 7), radiology (n = 7), nursing (n = 5), medical records (n = 5), biomedical engineering (n = 5), and health information management (n = 5). The mean professional experience was 8.50 ± 0.32 years, and all participants reported daily use of both systems.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) routine practical experience with both IHIS and Sepid, (2) basic familiarity with usability concepts, and (3) willingness to participate. The SUS consists of ten items rated on a five‐point Likert scale. Final scores were calculated by multiplying adjusted scores by 2.5, resulting in a range of 0–100, categorized as unacceptable ( < 50), marginal (50–70), or acceptable ( > 70).

Qualitative Analysis and Data Consolidation

A structured three‐phase process was used to synthesize qualitative findings. First, evaluators reviewed and merged their independently identified issues. Duplicate entries within each system were removed to determine the frequency of distinct usability violations. Second, overlapping issues across IHIS and Sepid were consolidated to facilitate comparative analysis. Third, qualitative findings were integrated with quantitative SUS results to provide a comprehensive usability assessment. All identified issues were categorized based on the ISO–Nielsen usability framework. Data visualization, including comparative frequency charts, was generated using Microsoft Excel.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27. All analyses reported in this study were prespecified comparative analyses of expert‐based and user‐based usability outcomes; no exploratory subgroup analyses were performed. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) were used to summarize participant characteristics and SUS scores. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To compare usability scores between IHIS and Sepid (paired observations), a Paired‐Samples t‐test was applied for normally distributed data, while the Wilcoxon Signed‐Rank test was used for non‐parametric data. All hypothesis tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was set a priori at α = 0.05.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.MUMS.FHMPM.REC.1403.080). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

Qualitative Usability Evaluation Based on Heuristic Principles

In the initial qualitative phase of the study, a panel of eight experts (comprising five HIT specialists and three radiologists) evaluated both the Sepid and IHIS systems using Nielsen's ten usability heuristics. The evaluation focused on identifying usability challenges within two critical modules—Inpatient Admission and Radiology. Each identified issue was assigned a severity rating ranging from 0 (not a usability problem) to 4 (usability catastrophe). The reported issues were subsequently coded and thematically categorized to enable a structured interpretation of system usability.

Thematic Analysis of Sepid Usability – Admission Module

The expert evaluation of the Sepid Admission module revealed several usability challenges related to interface design, system performance, and interoperability. The most prominent issues included slow system response, complex data entry processes, lack of contextual guidance, incomplete integration with external databases, and ineffective error messaging.

Comparative Usability Analysis of Sepid and IHIS Systems (Expert Perspective)

Following the module‐level analyses, a comparative assessment highlights distinct usability profiles for the two systems. Sepid demonstrated more prominent issues related to system performance and interoperability, particularly in relation to external database integration. In contrast, IHIS exhibited more fundamental design limitations, including interface complexity, lack of flexibility, and insufficient user control mechanisms.

Quantitative Usability Evaluation

Usability Evaluation of the Sepid System (End‐User Perspective)

Following the expert evaluation phase, a total of 34 end‐users participated in the quantitative usability assessment using the System Usability Scale (SUS). Participants represented diverse professional backgrounds, including radiology staff, nurses, health information managers, HIT specialists, and biomedical engineers, all with routine exposure to both systems.

The mean SUS score for the Sepid system was 66.25 (SD = 12.50, 95% CI: 62.05–70.45), placing it within the upper range of the “Marginal Usability” category. This suggests that while the system demonstrates acceptable functional performance, further optimization—particularly in system responsiveness and integration—is required to achieve broader user acceptance.

As shown in Figure 2, 4 users (11.8%) rated the system as Unacceptable, 17 users (50.0%) as Marginal, and 13 users (38.2%) as Acceptable.

Usability Evaluation of the Ihis System (End‐User Perspective)

The same cohort evaluated the legacy IHIS system. The mean SUS score was 51.99 (SD = 14.20, 95% CI: 47.20–56.78), placing it at the lower boundary of the “Marginal Usability” range.

This finding indicates substantial usability challenges, including increased cognitive load and reduced efficiency during system interaction.

As shown in Figure 3, 12 users (35.3%) rated the system as Unacceptable, 19 users (55.9%) as Marginal, and only 3 users (8.8%) as Acceptable.

Comparative Quantitative Analysis of Sepid and IHIS Systems

A direct comparative analysis demonstrated a clear usability advantage for the Sepid system over IHIS. The mean SUS score for Sepid (66.25) was significantly higher than that of IHIS (51.99), with a mean difference of 14.26 points, which was statistically significant (Paired‐Samples t‐test, p < 0.001).

Furthermore, the proportion of users reporting unacceptable usability (SUS < 50) was approximately three times higher for IHIS compared to Sepid (35.3% vs. 11.8%). Conversely, a substantially higher proportion of users rated Sepid as acceptable (38.2% vs. 8.8%).

Importantly, these quantitative findings are consistent with the qualitative expert evaluations. Together, they indicate that although both systems fall within the marginal usability range, Sepid provides a comparatively improved user experience, while IHIS requires more extensive structural and interface‐level redesign.

Discussion

This study compared the usability of two hospital information systems—Sepid and IHIS—from the perspectives of both end‐users and domain experts using a convergent mixed‐methods design. By integrating heuristic evaluation with the System Usability Scale (SUS), the study captured both structural interface problems and user‐perceived usability, consistent with recent evidence supporting multi‐method approaches in HIS usability research [30]. Overall, both systems fell within the “Marginal Usability” range; however, IHIS showed more substantial usability problems, particularly in relation to interface complexity, error handling, system flexibility, and access control. These challenges were especially pronounced in the Admission and Radiology modules, where users experienced system latency, visually dense interfaces, and limited control over corrective actions.

Critical Comparison of Usability Issues With Previous Studies

The qualitative findings identified several usability deficiencies that are consistent with prior national and international studies of hospital information systems. In Sepid's Admission module, system slowness, incomplete integration with civil registration and insurance databases, and insufficient user guidance were among the most prominent issues. These findings are consistent with earlier work on Admission, Discharge, and Transfer systems, which reported poor form design, high cognitive load, and workflow inefficiencies during data entry [36, 37]. Similarly, the integration failures observed in Sepid reflect broader infrastructural problems previously documented in national electronic health record environments, including SEPAS‐related fragmentation, inconsistency, and data exchange limitations [36, 38].

The Radiology‐related findings also align with previous research. In both systems, weaknesses in error prevention, authentication, and role‐based access were notable. These results are consistent with prior evaluations of radiology information systems, in which a substantial proportion of identified problems were classified as major or catastrophic because of inadequate error control, weak access management, and noncompliance with usability standards [39]. From a clinical perspective, such shortcomings are important because poor error management and weak interface safeguards can contribute to documentation errors and unsafe workflow conditions. This interpretation is also supported by previous research linking usability flaws to negative downstream outcomes, including increased documentation burden and patient safety risks [40, 41].

In contrast, IHIS was characterized more strongly by design‐related limitations, such as interface clutter, poor visual hierarchy, lack of reversibility, and structural rigidity. Similar deficiencies have been described in previous evaluations of laboratory and hospital information systems, where poor documentation, low flexibility, and inadequate error prevention were identified as recurring barriers to effective use [4, 7, 26, 42]. Taken together, these comparisons suggest that the usability problems observed in this study are not isolated findings, but rather reflect persistent design and workflow challenges that continue to affect HIS performance across multiple contexts.

Comparison of SUS Scores With Other Healthcare Systems

From a quantitative perspective, the SUS findings provide additional context for interpreting the relative performance of the two systems. Sepid achieved a mean SUS score of 66.25, whereas IHIS scored 51.99. Although Sepid performed better, both scores remained below the generally desirable threshold for clearly acceptable usability. Sepid's mean score is comparable to marginal usability scores reported in nursing information systems in the United States [43]. By contrast, the IHIS score approximates the low usability reported for an Emergency Department Information System [44]. Similar usability concerns have also been observed in laboratory information systems and electronic patient record systems evaluated in other countries [45, 46].

These comparisons indicate that usability limitations are not unique to the present setting. Rather, they reinforce the broader observation that healthcare information systems frequently remain difficult to use despite ongoing technological development [11, 47]. In this regard, the present study adds value by showing that a newer web‐based system may improve usability relative to a legacy desktop‐based platform, yet still fail to reach a clearly acceptable level. This pattern also supports previous arguments that expert‐based and user‐based methods should be combined when evaluating healthcare technologies, because each reveals different but complementary aspects of usability [48, 49].

Practical Implications for Stakeholders

The findings of this study have practical implications for multiple stakeholder groups. For system developers, the results highlight the need to move beyond feature‐oriented implementation and prioritize user‐centered design. Specific priorities include simplifying data‐entry workflows, improving visual hierarchy, strengthening feedback and error prevention mechanisms, and incorporating reversible actions such as undo/redo functionality. For Sepid in particular, improved interoperability with external databases and optimization of backend performance appear essential for reducing latency and workflow disruption.

For hospital administrators, the findings indicate that system deployment should be accompanied by structured training, continuous user support, and adequate technical infrastructure. Usability problems not only affect user satisfaction; they may also reduce efficiency and increase the burden of everyday clinical and administrative tasks. Therefore, successful implementation depends not only on software quality but also on local readiness, training strategies, and infrastructure support [50].

At the policy level, the results support the need for standardized usability requirements in HIS procurement and implementation decisions. Requiring formal usability assessment—using both expert‐based and user‐based methods—before large‐scale deployment may reduce the risk of adopting systems with substantial workflow and safety limitations. In addition, addressing integration bottlenecks will likely require broader national interoperability standards and coordinated registry frameworks to reduce fragmented and duplicate data entry [36, 51].

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is its module‐level, convergent mixed‐methods design, which enabled the integration of expert structural evaluation with real‐world user feedback. This design provided a more comprehensive usability profile than would have been possible using a single method alone [30, 48]. In addition, the comparative evaluation of a legacy desktop‐based system and a newer web‐based platform offers practical insight into usability challenges during digital transition.

However, the findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the study was conducted in a single teaching hospital, and the usability perceptions observed here may have been shaped by local workflow structures, organizational culture, infrastructure reliability, and prior user training. As previous research suggests, multidisciplinary documentation practices and local clinical environments can substantially influence how users perceive and interact with health information systems [52]. Second, although the mixed‐methods design strengthened the analysis, the user sample remained limited to professionals who had experience with both systems in one institutional context. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing the findings to other hospitals, particularly private or smaller centers with different technical and organizational conditions. Future multi‐center studies using complementary approaches such as contextual inquiry and simulation‐based usability assessment may provide a broader understanding of HIS usability across diverse care settings [53].

Conclusion

This study provided a comparative, multi‐method evaluation of two hospital information systems (IHIS and Sepid) during a real‐world digital transition, integrating expert‐based heuristic analysis with end‐user usability assessment. The findings demonstrated that although the web‐based Sepid system achieved relatively higher usability scores than the legacy IHIS platform, both systems remained within the “Marginal Usability” range, indicating substantial room for improvement. Importantly, the identified usability issues were not merely technical but structurally embedded within system design and workflow integration. In IHIS, the predominant challenges were related to interface complexity, lack of reversibility (undo/redo), and poor visual hierarchy, all of which increased cognitive load and reduced efficiency. In contrast, Sepid's limitations were primarily associated with system performance, weak interoperability with external databases (e.g., civil registry and insurance systems), and inadequate error feedback mechanisms. Based on these findings, several actionable recommendations can be proposed. From a system design perspective, developers should prioritize user‐centered design principles, including simplification of data entry forms, implementation of real‐time validation and error prevention mechanisms, and incorporation of undo/redo functionalities to enhance user control. For web‐based systems such as Sepid, optimizing backend architecture and improving interoperability with national databases are critical to reducing latency and workflow disruption. At the organizational level, hospital administrators should complement system deployment with structured and continuous user training programs, particularly targeting high‐complexity modules such as Admission and Radiology. Ensuring adequate IT infrastructure is also essential to support system responsiveness under high workload conditions. At the policy level, the findings highlight the necessity of mandating standardized usability evaluation frameworks (e.g., SUS and heuristic evaluation) as a prerequisite for HIS procurement and implementation. Establishing national interoperability standards and integrated data exchange frameworks is equally critical to overcoming current fragmentation challenges.

Overall, this study underscores that successful digital transformation in healthcare extends beyond system replacement; it requires a holistic alignment between technology, user needs, and clinical workflows. Future multi‐center studies are recommended to validate these findings across diverse healthcare settings and to further explore the relationship between usability, clinical performance, and patient safety outcomes.
