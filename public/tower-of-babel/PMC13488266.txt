Trypanosome circulation in hematophagous flies in livestock-rearing areas of southern Benin: prevalence, species diversity, and associations with vertebrate hosts
Authors: Zul-kifl Affolabi, Razaki A. Ossè, Prudenciène A. Agboho, Minassou Juvénal Ahouandjinou, Linda Towakinou, Sabbas Attindéhou, Rock Aïkpon, Ghislain Tepa-Yotto, Filémon Tokponnon, Martin Akogbeto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAfrican animal trypanosomosis (AAT) remains a major obstacle to livestock productivity in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in West Africa. In Benin, the epidemiological role of hematophagous flies in trypanosome circulation remains insufficiently documented. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of Trypanosoma infections in these potential vectors and to characterize their blood meal sources in livestock interface zones of southern Benin.MethodsHematophagous flies were trapped using Vavoua, biconical and Nzi traps in 6 communes during dry and rainy seasons. The sample size for molecular analyses was determined using Cochran’s formula, resulting in the selection of 384 individuals. These were analyzed by species-specific PCR targeting T. vivax, T. congolense (savannah and forest types) and T. brucei s.l. Blood meal identification was performed on engorged flies using multiplex PCR targeting cattle, humans, pigs, dogs and sheep.ResultsA total of 6550 hematophagous flies were collected across the 6 communes. The overall prevalence of trypanosomes was 13.8% (53/384; 95% CI 10.7–17.6%). T. vivax (6.8%) and T. congolense savannah type (6.5%) were the predominant species, while T. brucei s.l. was detected in only one specimen (0.3%); T. congolense forest was absent. The highest prevalences were recorded in Allada and Djidja (20.3% each). Dry season prevalence (16.1%) exceeded rainy season prevalence (11.8%), without statistically significant difference (p = 0.238). Blood meal analysis showed a predominance of cattle (44.4%), followed by humans (20.4%) and mixed sources. No significant association was observed between blood meal origin and infection status.ConclusionsThese results suggest that non-tsetse hematophagous flies may contribute to trypanosome circulation in livestock systems of southern Benin. Their inclusion in trypanosomosis surveillance and control programs is strongly recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-01014-5.

Introduction

African animal trypanosomosis (AAT), caused by protozoa of the genus Trypanosoma, constitutes one of the most constraining parasitic diseases for livestock in sub-Saharan Africa [1, 2]. It affects a wide range of domestic animals, notably cattle, sheep and goats, inducing clinical manifestations, such as anemia, weight loss, reduced fertility and decreased milk production. These effects result in a significant reduction in zootechnical performance and represent a major impediment to the development of animal production systems and food security of rural populations. At the continental scale, AAT is responsible for economic losses estimated at several billion dollars per year, owing both to production losses and costs associated with treatment and control measures [1–3]. The disease hinders agricultural development over approximately 10 million km2 of the continent, an area home to tens of millions of cattle and small ruminants exposed to risk [4].

The main etiological agents of AAT are Trypanosoma vivax, T. congolense, and T. brucei brucei. Each species presents a distinct geographical distribution and epidemiological profile. T. vivax is the most frequent species in cattle and small ruminants in West Africa and is distinguished by its unique capacity to be transmitted both cyclically by tsetse flies (Glossina spp.) and mechanically by hematophagous flies, such as Stomoxys and Tabanus [5, 6]. This dual mode of transmission allows maintenance of the disease even in the absence or low presence of tsetse flies, sustained by the mechanical activity of flies, such as Stomoxys, Tabanus, and Chrysops spp. [7–10].

Unlike cyclical transmission, which requires parasite development within the vector, mechanical transmission relies on passive transfer of trypanosomes from an infected host to a susceptible host during successive, often interrupted, blood meals. The efficiency of this transmission depends on several factors, including host parasitemia, the number of interrupted meals, vector density and the interval between 2 successive meals [11, 12]. These characteristics confer a particularly important epidemiological role on dense populations of generalist hematophagous flies, particularly Stomoxys spp., in agropastoral systems of West Africa [5, 10].

In Benin, AAT remains endemic and constitutes a major sanitary and economic constraint for livestock farms, where parasite pressure combines with high livestock mobility, complex agro-ecological interfaces and repeated contacts between herds, wetlands, fallows and cultivated mosaics [13, 14]. Control strategies have historically focused on tsetse fly management, considered the primary vectors of the disease. However, this approach may underestimate the importance of mechanical vectors, particularly in areas, where tsetse populations have been reduced or are naturally limited. In several comparable West African contexts, natural infections by Trypanosoma spp. have been detected in stomoxys and tabanids, suggesting that these insects may contribute to local maintenance of transmission, particularly for T. vivax [15, 16].

One of the major challenges in studying vectors lies in the difficulty of establishing a direct link between the presence of parasites in insects and their actual capacity to transmit infection. Molecular approaches, such as PCR, allow detection of parasite DNA with high sensitivity but do not necessarily provide information on parasite viability or infectious potential [11]. Nevertheless, when combined with other approaches, these methods offer valuable information on interactions between vectors, parasites and hosts.

Blood meal analysis constitutes a particularly relevant tool for understanding vector–host interactions. By enabling identification of host species on which flies have fed, it provides direct indications of host–vector contacts and potential transmission pathways. Molecular techniques based on amplification of mitochondrial genes, such as the COI gene, allow precise identification of blood meal origins even from small quantities of partially degraded DNA [17, 18]. This approach, combined with species-specific PCR detection of trypanosomes, offers a powerful framework for simultaneously characterizing vector trophic behavior and infection status, thus providing direct evidence of epidemiological bridges between host species [10, 19].

Despite these methodological advances, available data on the contribution of hematophagous flies to trypanosome circulation in Benin remain fragmentary. In particular, few studies have combined parasite identification and blood meal source identification within the same individuals, which limits understanding of complex vector–host interactions.

The present study aimed to fill this gap by adopting an integrated approach. The specific objectives were to: (i) estimate the prevalence of Trypanosoma infections in hematophagous flies; (ii) compare infection prevalences by fly species, commune and season; and (iii) identify blood meal sources and explore their relationship with infection status. By providing original data from multiple agro-ecological contexts, this study contributes to a better understanding of AAT epidemiology and to the identification of potential levers for improving control strategies.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in 6 communes of Benin: Allada and Dangbo (departments of Atlantique and Ouémé, respectively, humid coastal zone); Bopa (department of Mono, forest–savannah transition zone); Djidja and Dogbo (departments of Zou and Couffo, respectively, agropastoral savannah); and Kétou (department of Plateau, Sudano–Guinean zone) (Fig. 1). These localities were selected to cover a diversity of agro-ecological contexts and livestock systems. The climate is subequatorial and mean annual rainfall varies from 800 mm to 1400 mm. It is characterized by an alternation of 4 seasons, including a major rainy season (March–July), a minor dry season (August–September), a minor rainy season (September–October) and a major dry season (November–March) [20, 21].Fig. 1Map showing the study communes

Map showing the study communes

Fly collection and identification

Flies were collected during two seasons: the minor dry season (August–September 2025) and the minor rainy season (October–November 2025). In each commune, three trap types, namely, Vavoua, biconical and Nzi traps, were deployed and georeferenced across ecologically suitable habitats, including livestock farming areas, riverbanks, grazing lands and gallery forest margins. GPS coordinates of all collection sites are provided in Supplementary file 2: Table S1. The use of multiple trap types was intended to maximize the capture of the diverse hematophagous fly fauna present in the study area, as trap efficiency varies among vector taxa. The Vavoua trap is particularly effective for sampling Stomoxys spp. and tabanids [22], the biconical trap is the reference device for Glossina palpalis group species [23], while the Nzi trap efficiently captures tsetse flies, tabanids and stable flies [24]. Traps were deployed for 72 consecutive hours per commune and season, according to standardized protocols [22, 24]. Captured insects were collected every 24 h, transferred into 70% ethanol and transported at 4 °C for morphological identification using reference taxonomic keys [25–28]. Each fly was dissected under a stereomicroscope into two anatomical fractions: the head–thorax (HT), including mouthparts and salivary glands, and the abdomen (ABD), containing the midgut and blood content.

Sample size determination and sampling strategy

The representative sample size for estimation of trypanosome prevalence was determined using the Cochran formula (n0 = Z2 × p × (1 − p)/e2), considering a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a 5% margin of error (e = 0.05) and a conservative expected prevalence of 50% (p = 0.5). This led to a minimum size of 384 samples, considered sufficient to ensure adequate statistical precision of estimates [29]. A balanced allocation (n = 64 per commune) was adopted to allow spatial comparisons between sites. Specimen selection was performed by stratified random sampling, taking into account the specific composition of captures.

Molecular analyses

DNA extraction

DNA extraction was performed separately on each dissected fraction using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method [30]. Each fraction was mechanically homogenized in 200 µl of CTAB buffer using a Tissue Lyser III in the presence of a 3 mm stainless steel bead, then incubated at 65 °C for 5 min in a water bath for thermal lysis. A deproteinization step was performed by addition of 200 µl of chloroform, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The aqueous supernatant was recovered and DNA precipitated by addition of an equivalent volume of isopropanol, then collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The pellet was washed with 200 µl of 70% ethanol, dried at room temperature or under a Speed Vac, then resuspended in 20 µl of sterile water. DNA extracts were stored at − 20 °C until use. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) by measuring absorbances at 260 and 280 nm; the A260/A280 ratio should be between 1.8 and 2.0 to attest to satisfactory purity.

Trypanosome detection by species-specific PCR

Detection of trypanosome infections was performed independently on the head–thorax (HT) and abdomen (ABD) fractions by four species-specific PCRs targeting, respectively, Trypanosoma brucei s.l. (primers TBR1/TBR2), T. vivax (TVW1/TVW2), T. congolense savannah type (TCS1/TCS2) and forest-type T. congolense (TCF1/TCF2), according to protocols established by Moser et al. [31] and Masiga et al. [32] (Table 1). Each amplification reaction was performed in a final volume of 12.5 µL containing 10X buffer (Eurogentec, Germany), 5 mM dNTPs, 10 pmol of each primer, 5 U/µL Taq polymerase, 50 ng/µL template DNA, and sterile nuclease-free water to volume. Each run included a positive control (reference strain DNA) and a no-template negative control (sterile water substituted for DNA). Amplifications were carried out in a thermocycler under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 60 s, primer annealing at 60 °C for 2 min, and extension at 74 °C for 30 s, with a final extension step at 74 °C for 5 min. Amplified products were separated by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gel and visualized under UV transillumination (E-BOX, VILBER). Blood meal identification by multiplex PCR

The abdominal fraction of macroscopically engorged flies was used to determine the origin of the blood meal by a multiplex PCR simultaneously targeting five vertebrate host species, namely, cattle, humans, pigs, dogs and goat. This approach was based on the amplification of the mitochondrial COI gene region (Cytochrome Oxidase I) using a universal reverse primer (UnRev) combined with species-specific forward primers, following the protocol described by Kent [18] Each multiplex reaction was performed in a final volume of 12.5 µL comprising 6.25 µL of DreamTaq Hot Star 2X Master Mix (Thermo Scientific), 10 pmol of each species-specific primer, 2 µL of template DNA, and sterile nuclease-free water to volume. A positive control for each blood meal type and a no-template negative control were included in every run to ensure assay validity. Thermocycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, primer annealing at 50 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 7 min. Amplicons were separated by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gel in 1 × TBE buffer. The fragments obtained allowed differential identification by their characteristic size: 132 bp (goat), 334 bp (human), 453 bp (pig), 561 bp (cattle) and 680 bp (dog) (Table 1).

Statistical analyses

Infection prevalences were calculated as the proportion of PCR-positive flies (across all fractions) to the total number of flies analyzed, expressed as a percentage with exact 95% Wilson confidence intervals. Comparisons between categorical groups (season, commune, fly species, blood meal host) were performed using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test when sample sizes were small. Agreement between the head–thorax (TH) and abdomen (ABD) fractions was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient (κ). All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05. Figures were generated using the ggplot2 package.

Results

Diversity and distribution of fly species in the samples

Of the 6550 flies collected, 384 were selected for molecular analyses according to the predetermined sampling strategy (Cochran’s formula, n = 384), with 64 specimens per commune allocated by stratified random sampling. Seven species were identified, with a dominance of Stomoxys niger (n = 228; 59.4%), followed by Stomoxys calcitrans (n = 70; 18.2%), Chrysops distinctipennis (n = 36; 9.4%), Tabanus par (n = 32; 8.3%), Chrysops longicornis (n = 8; 2.1%), Atylotus agrestis (n = 6; 1.6%) and Glossina tachinoides (n = 4; 1.0%) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Relative composition of sampled fly species by commune

Relative composition of sampled fly species by commune

Overall prevalence of Trypanosoma infection

Of the 384 flies analyzed, 53 were positive for at least one Trypanosoma species, giving an overall prevalence of 13.8% (95% CI 10.7–17.6%). Infections were dominated by T. vivax (n = 26; 6.8%; 95% CI 4.5–9.8%) and T. congolense savannah type (n = 25; 6.5%; 95% CI 4.3–9.5%). A single detection of T. brucei s.l. was observed (0.3%), and no case of forest-type T. congolense was identified. One co-infection T. vivax/T. congolense savannah was detected (Fig. 3). Representative agarose gel electrophoresis images confirming PCR amplification for each detected species are provided in Supplementary files 2 (Figures S1–S3). Variation in prevalence by fly species, commune and season

The highest infection rates were observed in Tabanus par (21.9%; 95% CI 11.0–38.8%), followed by Stomoxys niger (17.1%; 95% CI 12.8–22.5%), Chrysops longicornis (12.5%; 95% CI 2.2–47.1%) and Stomoxys calcitrans (8.6%; 95% CI 4.0–17.5%). No infection was detected in Chrysops distinctipennis, Atylotus agrestis or Glossina tachinoides. Anatomical location of infections

Trypanosome detection differed according to the anatomical fractions analyzed (Table 4). For T. vivax, an equivalent number of cases was detected in the head–thorax fraction (HT) (n = 13; 3.4%; 95% CI 1.8–5.7%) and in the abdomen fraction (ABD) (n = 13; 3.4%; 95% CI 1.8–5.7%), without any overlap (κ = 0.0). Discussion

This study demonstrates significant trypanosome circulation in hematophagous flies in livestock areas of southern Benin.

The overall trypanosome infection prevalence of 13.8% recorded in hematophagous flies of southern Benin is noteworthy from an epidemiological standpoint. This level is comparable to values reported in other West African contexts, notably in Nigeria, where rates of 22.2% in Stomoxys and 33.3% in Tabanus have been documented [19]. Infections were primarily driven by Trypanosoma vivax (6.8%) and T. congolense savannah type (6.5%), with very low detection of T. brucei s.l. (0.3%). This dominance of T. vivax and T. congolense savannah is typical of AAT in the Sudano–Guinean zone [13, 33]. The statistically significant difference in prevalence between fly species, with high rates in Tabanus par (21.9%) and Stomoxys niger (17.1%), confirms the major role of mechanical vectors. The interrupted feeding behavior of tabanids and their large mouthparts facilitate mechanical transfer of infectious blood between hosts, which explains their particularly high vectorial efficiency for T. vivax [34, 35]. Recent experimental work has demonstrated that Stomoxys spp. are competent vectors for T. vivax and T. evansi, with transmission rates up to 30% [9, 10]. Detection of a single T. brucei s.l. specimen (0.3%) in S. niger at Dogbo is noteworthy given that Benin falls within the endemic range of T. b. gambiense [36]. However, the TBR1/TBR2 primers used target the 173-bp satellite repeat conserved across the entire T. brucei complex and do not permit subspecific discrimination [31]. As the positive fly carried a blood meal of bovine origin and no confirmatory sequencing was performed, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as indicative of T. brucei s.l. circulation in mechanical vectors in southern Benin, pending subspecies-level confirmation. The absence of T. congolense forest type is consistent with the savannah character of the vegetation in the surveyed communes [33].

At the spatial level, although not statistically significant (p = 0.130), the heterogeneous distribution of prevalences across communes likely reflects variations in livestock density, presence of water sources and habitat fragmentation [13, 16]. The trend toward numerically higher dry season prevalence is likely explained by the concentration of hosts and vectors around residual water resources [37].

Analysis of the anatomical location of infections provides important elements on parasite dynamics. For T. congolense savannah type, more frequent detection in the HT fraction (4.7%) than in the ABD (1.8%), without overlap (κ = 0.0), suggests possible parasite migration toward anterior parts of the insect [38]. For T. vivax, the balanced distribution between both fractions (3.4% each) is compatible with its capacity to survive in the proboscis of stomoxys and tabanids, facilitating transmission during interrupted meals [11]. The single case of T. brucei s.l. detected exclusively in the head–thorax highlights the potential residual role of tsetse flies in maintaining the cycle, despite their low abundance in the sample. This differential anatomical distribution has direct implications for distinguishing infected status from infectious status and deserves further investigation through microscopic examination and experimental transmission studies.

The study of blood meals reveals strong interaction with livestock (44.4% on cattle), reflecting the agropastoral structure of the study area. Flies fed on cattle presented the highest infection rate among common host categories (20.8%), establishing a direct epidemiological loop. Detection of human blood in 20.4% of identified flies raises a public health concern, given that T. brucei s.l. includes the humanly infectious subspecies T. b. gambiense. The high infection rate in flies that had ingested dog blood (40.0%), though non-significant due to the small sample size, is consistent with reported canine reservoir roles for T. vivax and T. congolense in Africa [39, 40]. The presence of mixed meals (pig/human and human/cattle) indicates trophic plasticity reinforcing the human–animal–vector interface, with potential zoonotic transmission risks [19]. This observation is notable from a One Health perspective: a fly feeding successively on humans and animals constitutes a potential vector for crossing inter-species barriers, with a zoonotic transmission risk not to be neglected.

These results fit within a regional context, where AAT remains a major impediment to livestock productivity. In Benin, parasitological prevalences in cattle of 15.7–19.7% have been recently reported, with clear dominance of T. vivax [13, 41]. The low tsetse fly abundance observed here contrasts with the high prevalence of mechanically transmissible trypanosomes, confirming the increasing weight of stomoxys and tabanids in areas, where Glossina populations have declined due to anthropization and control campaigns [16]. This underscores the need for an integrated approach combining mechanical vector control, rational trypanocide treatments and continuous molecular surveillance [42].

Several limitations should be noted. Species identification relied exclusively on morphological criteria using validated taxonomic keys. While this approach is standard in field entomological surveys, molecular barcoding (e.g., COI sequencing) could improve confidence in identification, particularly for morphologically similar species within the genera Chrysops and Stomoxys. Future studies should consider integrating molecular confirmation, especially for cryptic species The cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences or confirmation of effective vectorial competence, underlining the need for experimental studies. The restricted molecular targeting does not exclude the presence of other trypanosomes, such as T. evansi. Nevertheless, these limitations do not diminish the scope of the results, which provide solid original data on the potential role of mechanical vectors in a context of low tsetse fly density.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that non-tsetse hematophagous flies, particularly Stomoxys niger and Tabanus spp., are frequent carriers of Trypanosoma vivax and T. congolense (savannah type) in livestock areas of southern Benin. The combined approach associating species-specific PCR and multiplex blood meal analysis linked the trophic behavior of flies, predominantly oriented toward cattle, to trypanosome carriage, suggesting active epidemiological bridges in agropastoral systems. The predominance of mechanically transmitted T. vivax, as well as its co-circulation with T. congolense, highlights the need to strengthen AAT surveillance strategies beyond tsetse-focused approaches. Longitudinal studies integrating entomological surveillance, vector competence analyses and expanded reservoir host characterization are needed. These results advocate for the integration of mechanical vector control into AAT management strategies in Benin.
