A Card is worth a thousand questions: a clinical resource to support Canadian medical students and their preceptors
Authors: Susan L Bannister, Karen L Forbes, Hosanna Au, Marc E Zucker, Gabrielle Weiler, Angela Punnett, Mumtaz Virji, Michael K Paget
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveHaving developed a national paediatric undergraduate curriculum, canuc-paeds, our group identified a need for active learning resources aligned with it to support Canadian medical students and preceptors. Paediatricians and family physicians, practising and teaching in varied clinical settings, require Canadian-relevant curricular resources for their students. Having developed a national paediatric undergraduate curriculum, canuc-paeds (1), our group identified a need for active learning resources aligned with it to support Canadian medical students and preceptors. We developed a formative learning resource with content directly aligned with this curriculum, called Cards, that enables students to self-test, supplement their clinical experiences in paediatrics, and develop their clinical decision making (CDM) skills.

Multiple choice questions (MCQs) that test medical knowledge are frequently used in formative and summative assessments. Creation of high-quality MCQs is time and resource intensive (2); it is particularly challenging to create items that assess CDM. Automatic item generation (AIG) is a technology that uses models to efficiently generate many MCQ items; content specialists identify the structure and content for test items, and a computational process generates new test items by systematically combining the content (2). This has application for large-scale examinations, such as licensure examinations that require large banks of items. While AIG has been used to generate MCQs that test medical knowledge, its use for developing items that focus specifically on CDM or for the creation of formative learning resources is uncommon, particularly for paediatric content.

The medical education literature describes several concepts integral to CDM. The identification of salient features in a patient's presentation to create a mental abstraction or problem representation (3,4), the use of illness scripts (5), and theory about knowledge organization, storage, and retrieval (6) provide the theoretical foundation for Cards. When presented with a clinical vignette, clinicians first identify salient attributes and subsequently transform those into more abstract qualities, constructing a problem representation (3,4). This early problem representation plays a crucial role in problem solving and serves to construct a more abstract mental image, allowing the clinician to address the diagnostic possibilities (3,6,7). Script theory proposes that patient-oriented clinical knowledge is organized around the enabling conditions (patient factors), the fault (pathophysiologic process), and the consequences (patient signs and symptoms) that create an ‘illness script’ (5). These conceptual knowledge structures provide a framework for understanding the initial processes undertaken by clinicians in diagnostic reasoning (3).

Teaching strategies that incorporate the use of semantic abstraction and focus on eliciting and recognizing salient attributes would, therefore, be valuable for novice clinicians (3) and may serve as a starting point for self-assessment. Furthermore, in the clinical setting, patients typically do not use medical terminology that may ‘cue’ clinicians, particularly weaker clinicians, to the diagnosis (8). Thus, practice with vignettes that use lay language may help learners identify and translate salient attributes of a case.

Drawing from this literature and recognizing the gap in open-access formative self-assessment resources for Canadian medical students aligned with the national curriculum in paediatrics that focus on CDM in paediatrics, we developed Cards specific to paediatrics. The objectives of this descriptive educational innovation report are to (a) outline the process of card development and (b) provide basic metrics on usage to demonstrate the breadth and depth of its uptake, use, and adoption.

Methods

Card development

Our methods describe Cards and their development. Cards is an electronic resource that students use to test their paediatric knowledge and clinical decision making. A student selects a clinical presentation, such as Vomiting, depicted as a deck of cards. The student then plays individual cards, being provided a clinical vignette and an associated question about diagnosis, investigation, or management; feedback and additional resources are provided following their response.

Cards are developed following a step wise approach. The clinical presentation of vomiting in an infant will be used to illustrate this approach (Figure 1). Starting with a clinical presentation, authors identify the key conditions, or specific diagnoses, related to the clinical presentation. Key conditions for vomiting in an infant include pyloric stenosis, gastroesophageal reflux, volvulus, and meningitis.

A Card template and a generated Card. All variables are represented inside double curly braces. The defining feature of this case, pyloric stenosis, is non-bloody, non-bilious, projectile vomiting after every feed. These babies usually present between 4 and 12 weeks of age with significant failure to thrive and dehydration.

A short clinical vignette or prototypical case description is created for a given diagnosis which includes salient features of the case that are used in clinical decision making. In this example, the vignette is ‘A baby presents to medical care with vomiting’ (Figure 1). This is followed by case details, such as a description of the vomiting, its relation to feeds, current and birth weight, and number of diapers per day. The physical exam describes the baby's appearance, vital signs, and relevant findings such as hydration status and abdominal exam.

Next, variability is introduced into the case, through two types of variables—those considered critical because they represent defining and discriminating features of a case, and those that do not influence the diagnostic reasoning process. Alternate descriptions are created for the defining and discriminating features using lay language encountered in clinical practice. For instance, since the classic description of the nature of the emesis in pyloric stenosis is projectile vomiting, this is represented by different words in addition to projectile, such as goes across the room or is forceful.

The variables that do not influence CDM, such as image of the child, caregiver, and clinical setting, also vary within each Card. These elements provide variability as seen with real patients and require students to focus on the distinguishing features of each diagnosis for a given clinical presentation.

The original clinical vignette is then modified by changing the defining and discriminating variables to accurately describe other diagnoses that present similarly. For instance, a baby presenting with vomiting that has a diagnosis of volvulus, the variable describing the emesis becomes bilious or dark green. This process is repeated for each key condition; together, these clinical vignettes for different diagnoses with the same initial clinical presentation become a Deck of Cards. The creation of a matrix for each clinical presentation that reflects the collection of clinical vignettes and variables for specific diagnoses/conditions are referred to as a card template.

The student plays individual Cards based on clinical presentations; a clinical vignette and an associated question about diagnosis, investigation, or management is presented; feedback and additional resources are provided. (Figure 2)

Technology, developed by the Academic Technology team at the University of Calgary at the Cumming School of Medicine, turns the templates into Cards. Since both discriminating and non-discriminating variables are randomly generated in real time for each Card played, thousands of different Cards can be created from each Card.

Using data from Statistics Canada (9), every time a Card is drawn by a player, the system selects an image reflecting the diverse patient population the students are learning to serve (Figure 3).

Data from Statistics Canada is used to generate patients that are representative of Canada's population.

Peer-review, using a standardized rubric, is undertaken by one or two paediatric educators prior to publishing online. Cards are available as an open-access resource at cards.ucalgary.ca/institute/6 (10). With creation of a free account, the system tracks a user's progress and development of proficiency: when students draw a new Card, those Cards they have answered incorrectly or have not played for an extended period are favoured in the selection process (11). If learners identify an issue or discrepancy in a question, they can submit a comment. Edits are made based on learner feedback.

To demonstrate the extent to which this model creates clinical vignettes for learners, we collected additional data: the number of Card templates developed with the resultant number of unique cases that can be generated and the number of authors and reviewers of these Card templates.

Usage metrics

To estimate number of active users, defined as any individual who has registered and played a card, we identified the number of unique devices accessing cards and the location from which access took place. We present data based on country of access to represent worldwide usage. We specifically identified number of users from each Canadian medical school based on institutional email addresses of users. Number of unique plays per year since Cards launch in 2015 and usage data by clinical presentation have been tabulated.

Basic data to provide learner perspectives on Cards as a learning resource are provided. These data were obtained from a survey of graduating medical students at one Canadian institution (12) and an optional user survey on the Cards site in 2021.

At one Canadian institution, the effectiveness of various online-learning strategies was evaluated through a survey of graduating medical students (12). Cards were viewed as an effective teaching tool by 85% of students. Cards were viewed to be a more effective teaching tool than online lectures, case-based learning, flipped classroom, or self-directed modules (12). In April of 2021, an optional survey was made available on the Cards site, open for a period of 50 days to all users who visited the site during that period. Ninety-two of 758 users to the site during that period opted in to complete the survey for a response rate of 12.1%. Users were asked to ‘Please rank the effectiveness of each resource in contributing to your medical educational experience’; Cards had the highest level of effectiveness compared with multiple other online resources including Ankhi, Osmosis, MedEd Online, UptoDate, and YouTube.

Discussion

In this paper, we describe the development of Cards, a formative online resource created for Canadian medical students, aligned with the national curriculum in paediatrics, and used across the world. We ground Card development in educational theory related to clinical decision-making. As a description of an educational innovation, formal evaluation of the resource is beyond the scope of this manuscript, although basic usage metrics are provided to demonstrate breadth of adoption and use.

As learners transition to the clinical learning environment, they often struggle to integrate and apply their medical knowledge to patients. As a formative learning resource based on clinical vignettes that reflect clinical practice, Cards is a tool many learners have utilized to practice clinical decision-making skills. The ability to self-test, in a low-stakes fashion, with adaptation of cases presented based on past performance, and the flexibility of choosing topics based on personal learning needs, are all strengths of Cards. In addition, Cards can be used when students have short amounts of “down time” during their clinical rotations (13). The alignment with the canuc-paeds, the Canadian undergraduate curriculum in paediatrics, is an additional strength that makes Cards highly relevant for students at Canadian medical schools and their clinical preceptors.

While Cards were initially developed to focus on the CDM aspects of cases, we have identified other learning opportunities using this technology. For example, Cards have been created that require learners to rapidly interpret data in succinct and specific patient scenarios such as dehydration assessment and hemolytic anaemia blood group matching. Similarly, cards are being developed for interpretation of vital signs, assessment of developmental milestones, and identification of pathogens and antibiotic choices for specific paediatric infections. Further adaptations of the technology have enabled development of decks to conditions of the Well Child, such as injury prevention, discipline, sleep, and circumcision that lend themselves more to counselling than to problem representations and diagnosis. Vignettes and questions are structured around free responses that are compared with an expert's response. Use of ‘what if?’ follow-up questions change the clinical context and provide variation required to develop a student's expertise (10).

Limitations and future directions

Frequency of use data has been the primary outcome data source to date. Correlation of student performance on decks with clerkship assessments may provide validity evidence for Cards but is challenging with the open access nature of the platform and poses challenges with confidential student assessment data. The effectiveness of Cards is for improving knowledge in paediatrics for undergraduate medical students needs further study.

Currently, we are unable to report individual learner's progress and development of proficiency, given variability in learner usage patterns and inability to correlate with other assessments. Identifying means to track and report individual learner progress over time is an area for future development. The number of cards accessed by individual users, including specific topic areas most often accessed may serve as an area of future work, to help identify the types of presentations that may be considered higher yield by users. Feedback provided is based on rationale for correct responses, rather than rationale for incorrect response chosen; more individualized feedback may be a valuable area for future development.

Other future directions include surveying users for feedback about ease of use and learning value, reviewing psychometric data annually to identify and address problematic items, and providing users with individualized performance reports to guide their learning.

Conclusion

Cards is a valuable tool that may help learners develop clinical decision-making skills in paediatrics. Cards have demonstrated a high rate of return on authors' investment, as the technology generates thousands of unique vignettes from a single template. High-usage rates support Cards as a valuable open-access resource.
