Foliar serotonin and allantoin mitigate tellurium toxicity in Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) through antioxidant activation and nutrient homeostasis to enhance yield and grain quality
Authors: Rashid Iqbal, Hassan Mehmood, Sajjad Hyder, Dilfuza Jabborova, Ghulam Murtaza, Mohammed S. Alotaibi, Asqer S. Aloteeb, Sajid Ullah, Sajad Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTellurium toxicity severely impairs soil health, plant physiology and crop productivity, posing a critical threat to global food security, ecosystem sustainability and human health. The exogenous application of phytohormones and osmoprotectants offers a sustainable strategy for mitigating heavy metal-induced phytotoxicity and restoring agricultural viability. A comprehensive experiment was conducted to evaluate the synergistic efficacy of serotonin (5-HT) and allantoin (ALL) in ameliorating tellurite stress in quinoa plants. Plants subjected to 50 µM sodium tellurite soil drenching were treated with 50 µM 5-HT and 100 µM ALL via foliar spray under a randomized complete block design with a factorial arrangement and four replications. Tellurite stress alone drastically reduced morphological traits and overall yield, while simultaneously elevating oxidative damage. However, combined 5-HT and ALL application under stress increased grain yield by 90.41%, photosynthetic rate by 78.68%, stomatal conductance by 80.30%, relative water content by 33.52%, and antioxidant enzyme activities by up to 84.86%. Furthermore, it decreased malondialdehyde content by 58.20%, hydrogen peroxide by 61.16%, tellurium root accumulation by 52.48%, shoot translocation by 60.41%, and grain residue by 66.74% compared to stressed controls, while concurrently improving soil microbial biomass carbon by 63.71%, dehydrogenase activities by 88.49%, and available nitrogen by 39.48%. The integrated treatment significantly restored cellular ion homeostasis, enhanced complex root architectural traits, and effectively restricted heavy metal translocation to the edible harvested grains. Consequently, the co-application of 5-HT and ALL is highly recommended as a robust agronomic intervention to safeguard crop productivity, improve soil health, and ensure long-term food safety in severely Te-contaminated agricultural environments.

Introduction

The contamination of agroecosystems by increasing levels of heavy metal(loid)s worldwide is a serious environmental issue that jeopardizes the productivity and safety of food production. 1–3 Tellurium (Te) is an emerging contaminant of increasing dietary concern. It is released into agricultural soils through the disposal of electronic waste, photovoltaic cells, and industrial mining activities, and has been detected in plant-based foods at levels warranting dietary assessment. 4 TeO3 2 − (tellurite) toxicity is a poorly studied environmental risk in plant science, unlike other well-studied toxicants such as cadmium or lead. 5 Tellurite enters the root system, where it causes high levels of phytotoxicity and increases the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn damages the photosynthetic apparatus and inhibits nutrient uptake. 6 This oxidative burst leads to lipid peroxidation, damaging cellular membranes, impairing cellular metabolism, and resulting in reduced growth and significant yield losses.

Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) is now a pseudo-cereal that is important for global food security because of its outstanding nutritional, climatic, and economic value. 7 , 8 It is cultivated across diverse agroecological zones and increasingly sustains agricultural production on degraded lands. 9 However, despite its tolerance to different abiotic stresses, severe metalloid toxicity, such as tellurite, seriously affects its morphological growth, photosynthetic performance, and grain quality. 10 Protecting quinoa from such emerging contaminants is therefore essential to sustaining its yield and nutritional quality. 4

The exogenous use of plant-derived signaling molecules and metabolites has become an important sustainable agronomic approach to combat oxidative damage caused by metalloids. 11 , 12 The biogenic amine serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) is a potent antioxidant and has been found to be a signaling molecule which regulates root architecture, increases ROS-scavenging enzyme activities, and regulates ion homeostasis under environmental stress. 13 , 14 At the biochemical level, 5-HT upregulates the expression of superoxide dismutase and catalase, limiting the accumulation of hydrogen peroxide and superoxide radicals. 11 Similarly, another purine degradation product, allantoin, acts as a major osmoprotectant and significant nitrogen source, while also reducing oxidative stress through the stabilization of cellular membranes and enhancement of the ascorbate‒glutathione cycle. 15 , 16 These metabolites, in turn, affect the exudation profile of roots and cause them to excrete organic acids that bind toxic metal ions in the rhizosphere, thereby changing their bioavailability and uptake. 17 Studies suggest that both 5-HT and allantoin are effective in enhancing salinity and drought tolerance in plants, contributing to osmotic regulation, stabilization of photosystem II, and enhancement of antioxidant defenses. 18 , 19 However, more studies are needed on the transition from individual to synergistic combined application and a deeper study on the integrated biochemical mechanism of heavy metal toxicity mitigation.

Although the individual effects of 5-HT and allantoin are well documented, their combined physiological and biochemical interactions under tellurite stress remain unexplored, particularly their joint influence on soil microbial dynamics, root exudation, Te translocation, and the soil enzyme activities central to nutrient cycling (Kaur et al., 16 ). The mechanism by which a 50 µM serotonin plus 100 µM allantoin combination might enhance Te sequestration and grain-nutrient homeostasis is likewise unknown. This study addresses that gap through the first evaluation of the synergistic efficacy of foliar-applied 5-HT and allantoin against tellurite toxicity, integrating soil health indicators, root architecture and biochemical profiling into a single plant-microbe-metalloid framework. Accordingly, the primary objective was to examine the combined effect of foliar-applied 50 µM serotonin and 100 µM allantoin on soil health, physiological and biochemical responses, Te accumulation, and yield attributes of quinoa under 50 µM tellurite stress.

Materials and methods

Experimental site, duration, and growing season

A pot experiment was conducted during the Rabi (cool, winter) crop season of 2024–2025 under natural environmental conditions in a protected wirehouse in the research area of the Department of Agronomy, Faculty of Agriculture and Environment, The Islamia University of Bahawalpur, Punjab, Pakistan (29.39° N, 71.66° E). The wirehouse was covered with a stainless-steel mesh to keep birds and reptiles out and to allow normal solar radiation, temperature, and photoperiod to affect the crop. The seeds were sown on 16 October 2024 and the crop was harvested at the PM stage in late March 2025, providing a total study period of approximately 160 d from sowing to final harvest. Tellurite stress was applied at the 4-leaf stage (25 d after sowing; 10 November 2024), and foliar amendments were made at the vegetative stage (35 d after sowing; late November 2024) and panicle initiation stage (65 d after sowing; late December 2024). During the growing period, the air temperature varied from 8.5 °C (mean minimum) in December–January to 27.4 °C (mean maximum) in October and March, and the mean relative humidity ranged from 45% to 72%.

Soil sampling and physicochemical characterization

Bulk surface soil samples from the experimental field were collected from two depths (0–15 and 15–30 cm) before potting, air-dried in the shade, ground and sieved at 2 mm to homogenize the sample for analysis. The soil was classified as sandy loam (56% sand, 27% silt and 17% clay). Soil pH and electrical conductivity (EC) were determined in a 1:2.5 (w/v) soil:water suspension using a calibrated pH meter and conductivity meter, respectively. 20 Organic matter was estimated using the wet-oxidation method of Walkley and Black, 21 and the cation exchange capacity (CEC) was estimated using the ammonium-acetate (1 N NH4OAc pH 7.0) saturation method. Available phosphorus was extracted according to 22 and measured colorimetrically, while available potassium was extracted from the soil with neutral 1 N ammonium acetate and determined using a flame photometer. Total nitrogen was determined using the Kjeldahl digestion-distillation procedure. 23 To ensure that tellurium remained the sole limiting stressor, the soil was uniformly amended with a basal dose of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium at 100:60:40 kg ha−1 using urea, single superphosphate and sulfate of potash, respectively.

Both total and bioavailable tellurium fractions were quantified in pre- and post-harvest soil (0–15 cm). Total Te was extracted by microwave-assisted aqua-regia digestion (HNO3:HCl, 1:3 v/v) according to USEPA Method 3051 A. 24 Bioavailable Te was extracted with a diethylenetriaminepentaacetic-acid (DTPA) solution (0.005 M DTPA + 0.01 M CaCl2 + 0.1 M triethanolamine, buffered at pH 7.3) at a 1:2 (w/v) soil-to-solution ratio, shaken for 2 h, following Lindsay and Norvell. 25 All digests and extracts were filtered through Whatman No. 42 paper, and the elemental Te concentration was determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; Agilent 7900, Agilent Technologies, USA). The same soil samples were used to determine soil microbial biomass carbon using the chloroform fumigation-extraction method. 26

Plant material, experimental design and treatment plan

Certified seeds of quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) cultivar UAF-Q7 were surface-sterilized with 2% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, rinsed three times with deionized water and air-dried in the shade. Plastic pots (30 cm diameter × 35 cm height) were filled with 5 kg of prepared soil. Ten seeds were sown per pot at a uniform depth of 1.5 cm, and seedlings were thinned to four uniform plants per pot at the 3-leaf stage to standardize the plant density. The experiment was conducted in a randomized complete block design with a two-factor factorial arrangement and four replications (32 experimental units). Factor A included two tellurite concentrations (0 and 50 µM Na2TeO3), while Factor B included four amendment treatments: control, serotonin, allantoin, and serotonin + allantoin. The eight treatments and application procedures are presented in Table 1.

Sodium tellurite (Na2TeO3), serotonin hydrochloride and allantoin were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and dissolved in deionized water of analytical grade. Tellurite stress was imposed as a uniform soil drench of 50 µM Na2TeO3 (100 mL per pot) at the 4-leaf stage, whereas control pots received an equivalent volume of deionized water (Table 1). Foliar sprays of the amendments were applied at the vegetative and panicle initiation stages until run-off (approximately 15 mL per pot) and supplemented with 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20 as a non-ionic surfactant to improve leaf-surface adhesion and uptake. The experimental design did not include a surfactant-only (Tween-20 without 5-HT or ALL) treatment; 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20 was used strictly as a wetting agent at a concentration that is widely reported to be physiologically inert in foliar-spray studies, but its independent effect on quinoa was not isolated in the present factorial arrangement and is acknowledged as a limitation (Section 4.2).

Crop husbandry and agronomic management

Pots were arranged on raised benches within the wirehouse and were re-randomized weekly to neutralize any positional micro-environmental gradients. Irrigation was applied with deionized water to maintain the substrate at approximately 70% of field capacity, and pot weights were monitored gravimetrically every 48 hours to schedule water replenishment. Weeds were removed manually as they emerged, and no synthetic pesticide or herbicide was applied during the growing period to preserve the integrity of the rhizosphere microbial community. Plants were allowed to complete their phenological cycle, and destructive sampling was performed at physiological maturity, when panicles had attained physiological ripeness and the lower leaves had begun to senesce.

Plant sampling and sample preparation

At physiological maturity, the plants were harvested and partitioned into roots, shoots, leaves, panicles, and grains. Root systems were carefully excavated, and adhering soil was removed by gentle washing under a slow stream of deionized water over a fine sieve to minimize the loss of fine roots and root-tips. Rhizosphere soil was collected from the root systems by gently shaking and brushing away soil particles (less than 5 mm) that adhered to the root system into sterile containers and saved for soil analysis at post-harvest. Immediately after sampling, tissue subsamples were frozen in liquid N2 and stored at −80 °C for biochemical and enzymatic analyzes, while other subsamples were oven-dried (70 °C) to a constant weight and then ground to pass through a 0.5-mm sieve for elemental and proximate analyzes.

Soil enzyme activities and root exudate collection

Dehydrogenase, urease, phosphatase, and β-glucosidase enzyme activities were determined colorimetrically using standard procedures as described by Tabatabai. 27 Dehydrogenase activity was measured by the reduction of triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) to triphenylformazan (TPF). Urease activity was determined by quantifying the amount of ammonium released after incubation with urea. Phosphatase and β-glucosidase activities were measured using p-nitrophenyl phosphate and p-nitrophenyl-β-D-glucopyranoside as substrates, respectively, and the released p-nitrophenol was measured spectrophotometrically. Organic acids from root exudates were extracted after washing the roots with deionized water at 40 DAS, followed by incubation of the roots in a 0.5 mM CaCl2 solution for 2 hours. The obtained exudates were then subjected to centrifugation, filtration, and HPLC analysis. 28

Morphological, root-architecture and yield measurements

Plant height and root length were measured using a graduated meter scale, and the fresh weights of roots and shoots were recorded immediately after harvest using an electronic balance. Dry weights were obtained after oven-drying the corresponding tissues at 70 °C to a constant mass, and the root-to-shoot dry weight ratio was computed from these values. 29 Leaf area was measured using a computerized leaf-area scanner (WinFOLIA Pro, T221B, 2016; Régent Instruments, Netherlands). 30 Root morphological attributes, namely root volume, average root diameter, root surface area and number of root tips, were determined by scanning the washed root systems at a resolution of 800 dpi and analyzing the images with the WinRHIZO Pro 2017 root-scanning system (Régent Instruments Inc., Canada). Root density was calculated as the root dry mass per unit root volume. 31 At maturity, yield and yield components, including panicle length, panicle fresh and dry weights, number of seeds per panicle, 1000-seed weight, biological yield and grain yield per plant, were recorded. The economic grain yield was extrapolated to a per-hectare basis (kg ha−1) assuming a standard plant population density, and the harvest index was calculated as the ratio of grain yield to the total above-ground biological yield, expressed as a percentage.

Physiological and gas-exchange attributes

Relative water content (RWC) was determined using fully expanded young leaves, following Barrs and Weatherley. 32 Leaf discs were weighed to obtain the fresh weight (FW), floated on deionized water for 4 h to record the turgid weight (TW), and oven-dried at 70 °C to obtain a constant dry weight (DW). Leaf discs were held in deionized water at 40 °C for 30 min, and the first conductivity reading (EC1) was recorded. The second reading (EC2) was obtained after autoclaving the same samples at 121 °C for 15 min. MSI and EL were evaluated according to the following formulas: MSI(%)=[1−EC1EC2]×100

Carotenoid content was calculated from the absorbance at 470 nm using the Lichtenthaler 36 equation: Carotenoids (mg g−1 FW) = [1000(A470) − 1.82(Chlorophyll a) − 85.02(Chlorophyll b)]/198 × V/(1000 × W), where A is the absorbance at the indicated wavelength, V is the total extract volume (mL) and W is the fresh tissue weight (g).

Oxidative-stress markers and antioxidant defense

Lipid peroxidation was estimated as the malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances assay of Heath and Packer, 41 using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM−1 cm−1. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content was measured using the potassium-iodide method of Velikova, Yordanov, 42 and the superoxide radical (O2 −) generation rate was determined spectrophotometrically following Elstner and Heupel. 43 For enzymatic assays, frozen leaf tissue was homogenized in chilled 50 mM potassium-phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) containing 1 mM EDTA and 1% polyvinylpyrrolidone, and the supernatant obtained after centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C served as the crude enzyme extract. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assayed based on its capacity to inhibit the photochemical reduction of nitro-blue tetrazolium, following Giannopolitis and Ries. 44 Peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT) activities were determined according to the guaiacol oxidation and hydrogen peroxide decomposition methods of Chance and Maehly 45 and Aebi, 46 respectively. Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was measured from the rate of ascorbate oxidation following Nakano and Asada, 47 glutathione reductase (GR) activity following Carlberg and Mannervik 48 and guaiacol peroxidase (GPX) activity from the same crude extract, using standard kinetic protocols. 49 The total soluble protein in the extracts was determined using the dye-binding method described by Bradford 50 to determine the expression of specific enzyme activities.

Osmolytes, non-enzymatic antioxidants, and glutathione redox homeostasis

The free proline content was determined colorimetrically using the ninhydrin method described by Bates, Waldren, 51 and glycine betaine was quantified following Grieve and Grattan. 52 Total ascorbic acid was assayed using the 2,6-dichlorophenol-indophenol titration method, 53 while total phenolics and flavonoids were determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu and aluminum-chloride colorimetric procedures, respectively, and expressed as gallic acid and quercetin equivalents. 54 Reduced (GSH) and oxidized (GSSG) glutathione concentrations were measured using an enzymatic recycling assay, 55 and the glutathione redox ratio was calculated as the quotient of GSH to GSSG. 56 , 57

Shoot pigments, carbohydrates and biochemical composition

Shoot pigments (anthocyanins, betacyanin and betaxanthin) were extracted and spectrophotometrically quantified. 58 , 59 Soluble sugars (fructose, sucrose, reducing, non-reducing and total soluble sugars) were measured using standard colorimetric methods, and total soluble sugar was calculated as a combination of reducing and non-reducing sugars. 60 The Bradford 50 assay was used to determine the protein content in the shoots. The amount of dietary fiber was estimated using the enzymatic-gravimetric method, and the amount of ash was determined by dry-ashing at 550 °C using a muffle furnace. 61

Endogenous serotonin and allantoin quantification

The endogenous levels of serotonin and allantoin were measured in the roots, shoots and leaves using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The freeze-dried tissue was wet-extracted in acidified methanol, centrifuged and filtered using a 0.22-µm membrane before injection. Allantoin and serotonin concentrations were determined using authentic external standards and expressed on a dry-weight basis (µg g−1 DW) using a UV detector for allantoin and a fluorescence detector for serotonin. 62

Tellurium accumulation and mineral-nutrient analysis

Di-acid digestion of the oven-dried roots, shoots, leaves and grains (HNO3:H2O2, 3:1 v/v) followed by ICP–MS analysis was used to determine the concentration of Te in the roots, shoots, leaves and grains. 63 To assess Te mobility and uptake, the translocation factor (TF) and bioconcentration factor (BCF) were calculated as the ratio of shoot to root Te concentration and the ratio of tissue Te concentration to the bioavailable Te concentration in soil, respectively. 64 The same digests were used to determine the leaf and grain concentrations of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, copper, and manganese. Nitrogen was determined using the Kjeldahl method, phosphorus colorimetrically using the vanadate-molybdate method, and the remaining elements using ICP optical-emission spectrometry. 65 The K+/Na+ ratios of leaves and grains were calculated from the corresponding elemental concentrations to evaluate ionic homeostasis under tellurite stress. Analytical quality control included concurrent digestion and analysis of a certified reference material, procedural reagent blanks, and duplicate samples within each analytical batch. The instrument response was calibrated against certified multi-element standards, and the limits of detection and quantification were established from the standard deviation of the reagent-blank replicates.

Grain quality and food-safety attributes

Grain quality was characterized using oven-dried, grinded seeds. 66 Crude protein was determined by multiplying Kjeldahl nitrogen by the appropriate conversion factor, crude fat by Soxhlet extraction, crude fiber by the acid–alkali digestion method and total ash by dry-ashing in a muffle furnace at 550 °C. 61 Grain reducing, non-reducing, and total soluble sugars, as well as total starch, were determined using standard colorimetric procedures. The total saponin content was quantified using the vanillin-sulfuric acid method. 67 The grain tellurium concentration was determined by ICP–MS as described in Section 2.13 and compared with internationally recognized permissible limits to assess the food-safety status of the harvested grain.

Statistical analysis

All data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) appropriate for a two-factor factorial arrangement (tellurite level × amendment) laid out in a randomized complete block design, 68 using Statistix 8.1 software. 69 The statistical model partitioned the total variation into replication (block), the main effects of tellurite and amendment, their two-way interaction, and residual error. Statements describing the co-application as producing a “synergistic” response are grounded in this two-way Te × Amendment interaction term rather than in a simple visual comparison of means: a trait was described as responding synergistically only where the combined-treatment effect exceeded the arithmetic sum of the two single-amendment effects and the corresponding Te × Amendment interaction was statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05); where the interaction term was nonsignificant for a given trait, the combined effect is instead described as additive or as the numerically superior treatment, without invoking synergism. Interaction ANOVA statistics for all 126 measured variables are reported in Table 10 (Section 3.20). Before analysis, the assumption of normality of the model residuals was verified using the Shapiro‒Wilk test, and the homogeneity of error variances among treatment cells was verified using Levene's test; in both cases, departures were considered significant at p ≤ 0.05. Treatment means were separated using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test at a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05, employing the studentized range distribution, and a compact letter display was used to denote statistically distinct groups. 70 In addition, multivariate relationships among the measured traits were explored using principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson's correlation analysis using OriginPro 2021 (Origin Lab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). The relative changes reported in the results are expressed on a percentage basis, computed for the nonstress amendment treatments relative to the absolute control and for the tellurite-stress amendment treatments relative to the tellurite-stress-alone control unless otherwise stated.

Results

Soil physicochemical properties and microbial biomass carbon

Tellurite stress fundamentally destabilized the soil chemical and microbial environment, whereas foliar serotonin (5-HT) and allantoin (ALL) progressively restored rhizosphere fertility, with their combination being the most effective (Table 2). Soil drenching with 50 µM sodium tellurite (Na2TeO3) drove a marked accumulation of bioavailable tellurium (Te), which increased by approximately 37-fold relative to the absolute control, whereas total soil Te increased by approximately 14-fold. This metalloid loading coincided with a 22%–31% depletion of plant-available nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K+), a 19% decline in organic matter (OM), a 12% contraction of cation exchange capacity (CEC), and a 38% collapse of microbial biomass carbon (MBC), along with elevated electrical conductivity (EC) and pH. These changes reflect that Te toxicity reduced soil microbial growth and mineralization to levels below what is needed for soil functionality. Under nonstress conditions, foliar 5-HT and ALL provided modest but consistent increases in MBC (11%–22%) and slightly improved macronutrient availability, with ALL outperforming 5-HT in terms of available P and CEC. The most significant recovery occurred under stress, with the combined 5-HT + ALL treatment reducing bioavailable Te by 35.6%, increasing available N, P and K+ by 32%–39% and recovering OM, CEC and MBC by 30.5%, 14.1%, and 63.7%, respectively, while reducing EC by 15.6% relative to the tellurite-stressed control. The single amendments exhibited the same trend but only partially restored these soil health indices (5-HT by 29.7% and ALL alone by 40.4%). Of these soil variables, however, only bioavailable Te showed a statistically significant Te × Amendment interaction (F3,21 = 7.33, p = 0.002; Table 10), meeting the formal criterion for synergism; available N, P, and K+, EC, pH, OM, CEC, and MBC all showed nonsignificant interactions (p = 0.081–0.950), indicating that their recovery under co-application, while numerically the largest, was statistically additive rather than synergistic.

Soil enzyme activities

Extracellular enzyme activities in the soil, which are important indicators of soil microbial metabolic potential and nutrient cycling, were similarly affected by tellurite stress, with a strong response to the amendment (Table 3). All four assayed enzymes, dehydrogenase, urease, phosphatase and β-glucosidase, were inhibited by the same amount (42%–44%) under stress of tellurite alone as compared to the absolute control, showing a general decrease in the oxidative and hydrolytic activities of the microorganisms and not a differential effect on the substrate. The foliar amendments in the no-stress treatments slightly increased enzyme activities (3%–23%), but the 5-HT + ALL application was the most effective, and ALL provided a slight advantage over 5-HT. The dehydrogenase activity increased by almost 88.5% over the stressed control upon co-application under stress, while 32%–43% recovery was obtained with the single application of each amendment alone. Urease, phosphatase, and β-glucosidase activities were recovered by 73%–76% with co-application under stress and 40%–65% with a single application. This disproportionate benefit of the combination (which restored enzyme activity to nearly or above nonstressed levels) suggests that 5-HT and ALL worked together to restore the functional capacity of the microbial community that was tellurite-limited.

Growth and biomass characteristics

Co-application resulted in the greatest recovery, and foliar amendment significantly alleviated the severe and coordinated vegetative growth contraction imposed by tellurite stress (Table 4). Compared to the absolute control, tellurite stress alone decreased root length (RL), plant height (PH), root and shoot fresh weight (RFW, SFW), root and shoot dry weight (RDW, SDW) and leaf area (LA) by 32%–45%, with the most severe effects on biomass accumulation (RFW, RDW and SDW), while the root-to-shoot dry weight ratio (RSDW) did not change significantly (−2.6%), suggesting that tellurite suppressed root and shoot growth proportionately rather than selectively. Under nonstress conditions, 5-HT and ALL increased these traits by 3%–16% and 17%–28%, respectively, with ALL being slightly more effective as a single treatment than 5-HT. The greatest treatment differences occurred under stress, where 5-HT + ALL recovered 48%–80% relative to the stressed control (RFW + 80.1%, LA + 78.3%, RDW + 77.6%) and approximately twice as much as the individual amendments (5-HT + 31.3%, ALL + 46.3%). This graded recovery pattern from the stressed control through the single amendments to the combination shows a consistent recovery trajectory, with several fresh-weight traits exceeding non-stressed baselines, confirming the synergism of the recovery of growth machinery.

Root architecture and exudation

Tellurite stress reshaped root system architecture and organic acid exudation in opposing directions, collapsing structural root development, while triggering a pronounced efflux of chelating exudates. Foliar amendment reversed these responses (Table 5). Under stress alone, root volume (RV), root surface area (RSA), average root diameter (ARD) and the number of root tips (NRT) fell by 38%–42% and root density (RD) by 11% relative to the absolute control, whereas citrate, malate and oxalate exudation more than doubled (+127%–137%), a characteristic organic-acid chelation response to metalloid loading at the root surface. Under nonstress conditions, foliar 5-HT and ALL expanded root architecture modestly (RV, RSA and NRT gains of 8%–26%). Critically, under stress, the amendments simultaneously restored architecture and suppressed the exudation burst: co-applied 5-HT + ALL increased RSA and NRT by approximately 71% and RV by 61% over the stressed control while lowering citrate, malate and oxalate efflux by 35%–37%, with single amendments producing intermediate effects (33%–45% architectural recovery, 20%–25% exudate suppression). This inverse association, greater structural growth and less chelation demand, suggests that 5-HT and ALL were able to limit Te contact at the rhizoplane, thereby reducing the metabolic cost of exudation under stress and freeing resources for root growth.

Physiological attributes and photosynthetic pigments

Tellurite stress precipitated a broad deterioration of leaf water relations, membrane integrity and photosynthetic pigmentation, which was reversed toward or beyond the control levels by foliar amendment, most powerfully in the combination (Figure 1). Under stress alone, chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll and carotenoids declined most steeply (43%–45% below the absolute control), followed by SPAD greenness, membrane stability index (MSI; 32%–33%) and relative water content (RWC; 24%), whereas the maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (Fv/Fm) was comparatively resilient (−18%), indicating that pigment loss and membrane damage preceded any collapse of photochemical capacity. There were slight pigment improvements (increase in chlorophyll and carotenoid of 8%–25%) with non-stress amendment. Recovery under stress clearly scaled with treatments: total chlorophyll increased by 74% with 5-HT + ALL, carotenoids increased by 66%, MSI by 49%, RWC by 34%, and Fv/Fm by 22% compared to the stressed control, which was approximately twice the increases obtained from either amendment alone (37%–44% for chlorophyll). These attributes were restored to near non-stressed conditions when the combination was applied, but only partially by the single amendments. This pattern corresponds to a significant Te × Amendment interaction for total chlorophyll, chlorophyll b, carotenoids and SPAD greenness (p ≤ 0.034; Table 10), consistent with true synergistic protection of the photosynthetic pigment apparatus, whereas relative water content, Fv/Fm, and chlorophyll a showed nonsignificant interactions and are better described as additively, rather than synergistically, restored.

Gaseous exchange attributes

Tellurite stress severely constrained leaf gas exchange, and the accompanying rise in intercellular CO2 pointed to a biochemical rather than purely stomatal limitation that foliar amendment relieved (Figure 2). Stress alone depressed the net photosynthetic rate (A), transpiration rate (E), and stomatal conductance (gs) by 44%–47% relative to the absolute control, while raising the sub-stomatal CO2 concentration (Ci) by 18%. The concurrence of decreasing A and increasing Ci indicates that reduced carboxylation capacity, not stomatal closure alone, limits assimilation. Nonstress amendment improved A, E, and gs modestly (7%–26%). Under stress, recovery increased consistently from single amendments (A, E, and gs gains of 41%–54%) to co-application, which raised A by 78.7%, E by 79.4%, and gs by 80.3% and lowered Ci by 13.2% relative to the stressed control. The parallel restoration of conductance and the reversal of the elevated Ci signature under 5-HT + ALL indicate that the combination re-established both the stomatal and mesophyll components of photosynthetic function.

Oxidative stress markers

Tellurite stress provoked a pronounced oxidative burst and membrane injury, which was strongly attenuated by foliar amendment, most effectively under co-application (Figure 3). Relative to the absolute control, stress alone elevated malondialdehyde (MDA), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide anion (O2 −), and electrolyte leakage (EL) by 184%–236%, with H2O2 and EL rising most steeply, indicating that reactive oxygen species accumulation had overwhelmed endogenous scavenging and compromised membrane integrity. Without stress, the amendments reduced these markers to a small extent (10%–24%). The decrease in oxidative damage under stress ranged from 30%–40% with single amendments, with up to a 58.2% decrease in MDA, 61.2% decrease in H2O2, 53.4% decrease in O2 − and 57.4% decrease in EL when compared with the stressed control. The greater than 50% decrease in each oxidative parameter under 5-HT + ALL and the highest increase in membrane stability coincided with statistically significant Te × Amendment interactions for all five markers (MDA, H2O2, O2 −, EL, and MSI; all p ≤ 0.005; Table 10), confirming a true synergistic effect in restoring the redox balance.

Antioxidant enzyme activities

The antioxidant enzyme system was strongly induced in response to tellurite stress, and foliar amendment further induced this response, suggesting that antioxidant capacity and/or repair was insufficient without supplementation (Figure 4). The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), glutathione reductase (GR) and glutathione peroxidase (GPX) were up-regulated in response to stress alone, increasing by 57%–94% over the absolute control, but still did not prevent the oxidative damage described above. There was moderate enzyme stimulation (11%–41%) with non-stress amendment. When 5-HT + ALL were co-applied, the highest additional increases were observed, with GR and GPX increased by 82.6% and 84.9%, respectively, and the other enzymes by 65%–74% over the stressed control, which is approximately twice the effect of either amendment alone (37%–47%). The fact that the combination further enhanced an already primed defense system, while the oxidative markers decreased most dramatically, suggests that this increased enzyme activity, and not just the exclusion of metal, led to a better redox outcome.

Osmoprotectants, non-enzymatic antioxidants and glutathione redox homeostasis

Tellurite stress reconfigured the osmotic and nonenzymatic antioxidant pools in two opposing directions: hyper-accumulating osmolytes while collapsing the glutathione redox pool, and foliar amendment normalized both (Figure 5). Stress alone triggered massive proline (+483%) and glycine betaine (+200%) accumulation, together with elevated phenolics, flavonoids, and oxidized glutathione (GSSG), yet depleted ascorbic acid, reduced glutathione (GSH), and the glutathione redox ratio (GSH/GSSG) by 32%–66%. The steep decline in the GSH/GSSG ratio indicates a critical loss of cellular redox buffering. Coordinated, moderate improvements were achieved in all these pools with nonstress amendments (9%–47%). The amendments changed the stress signature under stress; when co-applied, 5-HT + ALL reduced excess proline, glycine betaine, and GSSG by 34%–44%, increased ascorbic acid and GSH by ~60%–63%, and restored GSH/GSSG by 150% compared to the stressed control, far exceeding the effect of either amendment alone. It is important to note that the combined treatment led to relief of the osmotic strain and restoration of the ascorbate-glutathione redox couple, and not to sustained osmolyte accumulation, as an indicator of restored redox homeostasis.

Shoot pigments, carbohydrates, and biochemical composition

Tellurite stress caused shoot metabolism to switch to the synthesis of defense pigments at the cost of primary carbon and protein pools, a switch that foliar amendment reversed (Figure 6). Endogenous phytochemical accumulation

Increased exogenous supply of each compound resulted in an increase in the endogenous pool of each compound, generally in a compound-specific manner, and tellurite stress alone caused a small constitutive upregulation, in line with these compounds being involved in the plant's stress-signaling network (Figure 7). When applied alone, 5-HT increased endogenous 5-HT by 187%–221% in roots, shoots, and leaves, and ALL alone increased endogenous ALL by approximately 200% without much effect on serotonin, demonstrating that both compounds were taken up and increased endogenous levels of their respective compounds when sprayed alone. The endogenous compounds were up-regulated by 25%–40% under tellurite stress alone, which is a hallmark of their induction into the intrinsic stress response. The combination was significantly synergistic under stress in roots and shoots: co-applied 5-HT + ALL increased endogenous serotonin by 266%–278% and endogenous allantoin by 221%–228% in these tissues, compared to the stressed control, with significant Te × Amendment interactions for both compounds in roots and shoots and for allantoin in leaves (p ≤ 0.001; Table 10); the leaf serotonin interaction was not significant (p = 0.808), indicating additive rather than synergistic behavior for that specific pool. Mutual amplification suggests positive feedback between exogenously applied and endogenously synthesized compounds, strengthening plant signaling and antioxidant systems.

Tellurium accumulation and bioconcentration factors

Tellurite stress produced extreme tissue tellurium loading with preferential root retention, and foliar amendment curtailed the uptake and translocation most decisively in the edible grain (Figure 8). Under stress alone, Te concentrations rose more than 40-fold across roots, shoots, leaves, and grains relative to the absolute control, yet the translocation factor (TF) fell by 26%, indicating that roots sequestered a disproportionate share and limited shoot loading, even as absolute tissue burdens soared. Non-stress amendment slightly lowered the baseline Te uptake (4%–21%). Under stress, the restriction of Te accumulation increased from single amendments (tissue reductions of 34%–44%) to the combination, which lowered root, shoot and leaf Te by 52%–62%, reduced grain Te residue by 66.7% and further depressed TF by 16.9% and the tissue bioconcentration factors (BCF) by 26%–48% relative to the stressed control. The disproportionate 66.7% reduction in grain residue, the largest of any tissue, demonstrates that 5-HT + ALL preferentially restricted phloem-mediated Te loading into the grain, which is the most consequential outcome for food safety.

Yield and yield-related parameters

Tellurite stress inflicted the most severe penalties of any measured trait category on reproductive development and grain yield, and foliar amendment delivered a correspondingly large recovery that peaked under the co-application (Table 6). Stress alone reduced panicle length (PL), panicle fresh and dry weight (PFW and PDW), total number of seeds per plant (TNSP), biological yield (BY), grain yield per plant (GYP), 1000-seed weight (TSW) and extrapolated grain yield (EGY) by 30%–50% relative to the absolute control, with the reproductive sink traits (GYP and EGY) being the most affected. The comparatively small decline in the harvest index (HI, −10.7%) shows that the source (biomass) and sink (grain) were impaired in near-parallel. Non-stress amendment raised yield components by 8%–30%, with ALL exceeding 5-HT and the combination exceeding both. Under stress, recovery scaled steeply from single amendments (GYP gains of 47%–51%) to the combination, which increased GYP by 90.4%, EGY by 94.8%, BY by 72.2% and the remaining components by 43%–69% over the stressed control while restoring the HI. The co-application nearly doubled the grain yield relative to the stressed control, recovering it close to non-stressed levels, and identified the preservation of reproductive sink capacity as the agronomic culmination of the physiological protection documented above.

Grain quality attributes

Tellurite stress degrades grain nutritional quality by depressing protein, lipid, and sugar reserves while concentrating fiber, ash, and the antinutritional saponin fraction. Foliar amendment reversed this compositional shift (Table 7). Stress alone lowered crude protein content (CPC), crude fat content (CFC), grain reducing, non-reducing and total soluble sugars (GRS, GNRS, GTSS) and grain total starch (GTS) by 8%–34% relative to the absolute control, with soluble sugars being the most severely affected, while it increased crude fiber content (CFib) by 24.5%, grain ash content (GAC) by 19.2% and grain total saponin content (GTSC) by 57.6%. The latter reflects a marked accumulation of bitter antinutrients that compromise palatability. Non-stress amendment improved the nutritional fractions modestly (2%–9%) and lowered saponin levels (9%–11%). When applied together, 5-HT + ALL showed the highest correction under stress, with an increase of 29%–47% in CPC, CFC, and soluble sugars and a decrease of 32.7% in GTSC and 22.8% in CFib, which is approximately double the effect of the single amendments. This two-way normalization, that is, replenished storage reserves and a significant reduction in saponins, shows that the combination not only restored grain yield but also improved the edible quality and food-safety profile.

Grain mineral nutrient composition

Tellurite stress precipitated a pervasive nutritional imbalance in quinoa grain, depleting every essential macro- and micronutrient while doubling sodium, and foliar amendment restored mineral homeostasis, most completely under co-application (Table 8). Stress alone reduced grain nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium (K), calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, copper, and manganese by 21%–29% relative to the absolute control, while increasing sodium (Na) by 102%, collapsing the grain K+/Na+ ratio by 60.9% and signaling a breakdown of ionic selectivity under metalloid stress. Non-stress amendment improved mineral acquisition modestly (1%–17%) and increased the K+/Na+ ratio by 15%–17%. Under stress, recovery increased from single amendments (K+/Na+ gains of 48%–58%; micronutrient gains of 14%–25%) to the combination, which restored the K+/Na+ ratio by 132% over the stressed control through a 43% reduction in Na and a 32% rise in K and increased phosphorus, zinc, and the remaining minerals by 30%–49%. The disproportionate recovery of the K+/Na+ ratio identifies re-established ion homeostasis as the mechanism underlying the broad nutritional rescue, consistent with the protection of membrane transporter selectivity by the combined treatment.

Leaf nutrient composition and ion homeostasis

Tellurite stress disrupted leaf ionic balance in the same pattern as observed in grains, suppressing essential nutrients while nearly doubling sodium. Foliar amendment most strongly re-established homeostasis under co-application (Table 9). Stress alone lowered leaf nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium (K), calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, copper, and manganese by 26%–34% relative to the absolute control, while raising sodium (Na) by 97.8%, driving a 65.1% collapse of the leaf K+/Na+ ratio that marks the loss of membrane ion selectivity central to Te toxicity. Non-stress amendment had a small positive effect on nutrient concentrations (5%–20%), and the K+/Na+ ratio increased by 17%–24%. When subjected to stress, restoration increased from single amendments (K+/Na+ gains of 61%–72%; nutrient gains of 17%–32%) to the combination, which increased the leaf K+/Na+ ratio by 163% (42% decrease in Na and 53% increase in K) and the remaining nutrients by 38%–52%. The parallel recovery of leaf and grain K+/Na+ ratios confirms the systemic protection of the ion homeostasis system by the combined treatment, which resulted in the recovery of nutrient acquisition that underlies the physiological and yield responses reported above.

Correlation analysis of physiological, biochemical, and yield parameters in quinoa under tellurite stress with serotonin and allantoin application

Pearson correlation analysis identified a coherent two-pole structure that correlated soil conditions, plant function, and Te fate for all treatments (Figure 9). The negative pole consisted of bioavailable soil tellurium and the tellurium translocation factor, which were negatively correlated with plant height, root length, shoot dry weight, photosynthetic rate and grain yield, and positively correlated with the oxidative markers MDA, H2O2 and electrolyte leakage, thereby quantifying the causal chain from metalloid loading to oxidative injury to yield loss. In contrast, soil microbial biomass carbon and dehydrogenase activity were positively correlated with nutrient availability, growth and yield, and the antioxidant enzymes SOD, CAT, and APX were correlated with proline, glycine betaine, ascorbic acid, phenolics, and reduced glutathione, suggesting co-regulated enzymatic and non-enzymatic defense. Root exudate citrate and malate were negatively associated with tissue Te and positively associated with nutrient uptake and soil health indicators, whereas the leaf K+/Na+ ratio was positively associated with the membrane stability index, relative water content, and yield but negatively associated with sodium accumulation and oxidative damage. The negative relationship between the translocation factor and grain yield and crude protein further confirmed that the restriction of Te transfer to the reproductive tissue maintained grain quality. The combined 5-HT + ALL treatment consistently enhanced positive relationships and reduced negative relationships, thus integrating the soil-plant system toward productivity.

Multivariate analysis of physiological, biochemical, and yield responses to serotonin and allantoin application under tellurite stress

The radar profiles separated the eight treatments into three functionally distinct response envelopes (Figure 10). Tellurite stress alone traced an inverted profile that expanded along the tellurium-accumulation and translocation axes and the oxidative and osmolyte markers (MDA, H2O2, electrolyte leakage, proline, and glycine betaine) but contracted across the growth, photosynthetic, and yield axes, capturing the diversion of metabolism from productivity to defense. The non-stress foliar treatments occupied an enhanced envelope, exceeding the absolute control in terms of growth, photosynthesis, and yield. The combined 5-HT + ALL treatment was the most complete outwards expansion under stress along the growth, antioxidant enzyme, membrane stability, water relations, ion homeostasis, and yield axes, and the most complete inward contraction along the tellurium and oxidative damage axes, approaching the non-stressed envelope. The same treatment ranking was observed for the soil health and root architecture axes, indicating that the combination shifted the entire soil-plant system and not just individual traits.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis condensed the multivariate response onto a single dominant axis, with PC1 alone explaining 88.97% of the total variance (PC2 and PC3 adding 9.51% and 1.25%, respectively), such that treatment separation collapsed essentially onto one stress-recovery gradient (Figure 11). Growth and physiological performance (PH, RDW, A, TChl, gs), water relations (RWC, MSI), ion homeostasis (K+, K+/Na+), the antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, APX) and ascorbate (AsA) were the positive PC1 pole, while oxidative markers (MDA, H2O2), proline, root-exudate citrate and Te accumulation (TeR, TeS and bioavailable Te) were the negative pole. The absolute control and the 5-HT + ALL stress treatment groups were positioned together at the positive end of the pole, suggesting that the co-application of 5-HT and ALL returned quinoa to a physiological state statistically similar to the unstressed optimum, whereas the tellurite stress alone was at the far negative end of the pole. The single-amendment stressed treatments fell in intermediate positions, and the near-monopolization of variance by PC1 showed that a single physiological axis (and not independent clusters of traits) controlled the response, with the combined treatment being the most displaced toward the healthy end of the axis.

Two-way (tellurite × amendment) interaction effects

To formally test whether the co-application of 5-HT and ALL produced a true statistical synergism rather than simply the numerically larger of two additive effects, the two-way Tellurite × Amendment interaction term from the factorial ANOVA (Section 2.15) was examined for all 126 measured variables (Table 10). A significant interaction (p ≤ 0.05) indicates that the combined-treatment response deviated from the sum of the individual 5-HT and ALL effects, i.e., true synergism; a non-significant interaction indicates an additive pattern in which the combination was superior in magnitude but not statistically distinct in its interactive behavior. The interaction was significant for 62 of the 126 variables (49.2%), including all oxidative-stress markers (MDA, H2O2, O2 −, electrolyte leakage), all six antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, CAT, APX, GR, GPX), all three root-exudate organic acids (citrate, malate, oxalate), tissue tellurium concentration in every organ (root, shoot, leaves and, most notably, grain residue, p < 0.001), endogenous serotonin and allantoin in roots and shoots, and grain K+/Na+ ratio. The interaction was not significant for most primary soil physicochemical properties (available N, P, K+, EC, pH, organic matter, cation exchange capacity, microbial biomass carbon; only bioavailable Te showed a significant interaction), for most morphological and root-architectural traits, for relative water content and net photosynthetic rate, and for the tellurium translocation factor. Consequently, the term “synergistic” in this manuscript has been reserved for variables with a statistically significant Te × Amendment interaction; where the interaction was non-significant, the combined treatment is instead described as additive or as numerically superior to either single amendment, and the relevant sentences in Sections 3.1 and 4 have been revised accordingly.

Discussion

The main challenge is not only to reduce tellurite uptake but also to prevent its toxicity, since tellurite is the most soluble and bioavailable Te species and its damage stems chiefly from oxidative stress to the plant's cellular machinery and to rhizosphere microbial function. The present study addressed this double challenge, and the results suggest that the ameliorative effect of serotonin (5-HT) and allantoin (ALL) can be viewed as a concerted soil-plant response rather than a series of independent effects.

The unique aspect of this study is that two metabolites with complementary mechanisms of action are shown to synergistically protect against metalloid stress for the majority of oxidative-defense, root-exudation, endogenous-metabolite and tissue-tellurium variables (62 of 126 traits with a significant Te × Amendment interaction; Table 10), whereas primary soil physicochemical properties, root morphology and water status responded additively rather than synergistically. Indoleamine serotonin acts as a direct radical scavenger and regulates root growth and ion transport, whereas the purine-catabolism ureide allantoin primes antioxidant and abscisic-acid-linked stress responses and recycles nitrogen. These two metabolites therefore target different nodes of the stress-response network, and their co-application engages rhizosphere detoxification, systemic antioxidant induction and endogenous metabolite signaling. This is an important way to interpret the entire dataset, as the combination always performed better than either compound alone in soil, physiological and grain quality aspects, and it provides a perspective for the mechanistic discussion that follows.

The soil data suggest that tellurite toxicity is more of a microbial than a chemical phenomenon and that tellurite toxicity was reversed by 5-HT and ALL primarily through the restoration of the biological function of the rhizosphere. Tellurite loading reduced microbial biomass carbon and all soil enzymes in near-uniform proportions, increased electrical conductivity and pH, and decreased organic matter and exchangeable nutrients, a pattern indicative of a loss of microbial metabolism but not a specific enzyme lesion. This effect was most completely reversed by the combined amendment, and two complementary mechanisms explain this effect. First, 5-HT and ALL provide readily assimilable carbon and, in the case of the nitrogen-rich ureide ALL, mineralizable nitrogen that supports microbial growth and enzyme production, as is known for allantoin, which serves as a nitrogen reservoir in stressful situations. 15 , 16 Second, the simultaneous decrease in DTPA-extractable Te with the amendment suggests that the resurgent microbial community and the changes in root exudation caused Te to be more bound in less bioavailable fractions. This interpretation is supported by the exudation data: the release of organic acids, which usually increases with metal stress and decreases with improved soil organic status, was reduced by the amendments, 17 suggesting that the recovery of soil health was not only due to increased microbial biomass but also to reduced bioavailability of the metals.

The growth response indicated that the architecture of the root system and the exudation of organic acids were reciprocally regulated, and that the release of the metal at the rhizoplane was the key event. Under tellurite stress, quinoa reduced the size of its root and shoot systems and increased the efflux of citrate, malate, and oxalate, the typical chelation response of roots to bind a metalloid to the root surface, but at a high carbon and energy cost. 17 The combined 5-HT and ALL treatment reversed this trade-off, increasing the number of root tips and the surface area of the roots while reducing the exudation burst. The simplest explanation is that the amendments reduced the bioavailable soil Te and the accumulation of Te in the tissues, thereby reducing the metabolic drive to invest in the production of organic acids and allowing the reallocation of resources to structural root proliferation. Physiological data show that a larger root system is more absorptive, which is directly linked to the enhanced root architecture, 11 which is known to be stimulated by serotonin, which in turn regulates auxin-linked developmental programs.

Photosynthetic data indicate that the major site of tellurite toxicity is downstream of the stomata in the pigment and carboxylation apparatus. The simultaneous reduction in chlorophyll, carotenoids, and Fv/Fm and increase in sub-stomatal CO2 concentration are diagnostic of a non-stomatal, biochemical limitation: assimilation decreased even though intercellular CO2 was available, which means that the damage to the chloroplast pigments and photosystem II was not only due to stomatal closure. 10 The greater loss of carotenoids and total chlorophyll than of membrane stability and relative water content suggests that pigment photo-oxidation occurred before the general loss of cellular integrity. This apparatus was protected on two fronts by the combined 5-HT and ALL treatment which restored gas exchange, pigments, and photochemical efficiency toward control values. Serotonin is a lipophilic radical scavenger that protects thylakoid pigments, stabilizes chloroplast function and regulates stomatal behavior, 11 whereas allantoin strengthens the antioxidant and glyoxalase systems, which helps reduce lipid peroxidation of photosynthetic membranes. 19 The parallel recovery of relative water content and membrane stability under this combination indicates that the recovery of water relations and preservation of carboxylation capacity were not independent.

The oxidative stress results explain why tellurite was so harmful despite the activation of plant defense: the metalloid caused a quantitative induction of antioxidant enzymes, which was not enough to compensate for the oxidative stress. Under stress, all the above-mentioned enzymes, namely superoxide dismutase, catalase, and ascorbate-glutathione-cycle enzymes, increased, while malondialdehyde, hydrogen peroxide, superoxide, and electrolyte leakage increased in parallel, indicating that the scavenging of ROS was not sufficient to cope with the production of ROS during tellurite reduction. 10 Exogenous metabolites correct this imbalance by enhancing the same defense mechanisms above their stress-induced maximum. Serotonin is a direct radical scavenger and inducer of superoxide dismutase and catalase expression. 11 Allantoin reduces the accumulation of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide and increases the activity of superoxide dismutase and ascorbate peroxidase, as shown in allantoin-accumulating and allantoin-supplemented plants under salt, drought, and Cd stress. 18 , 72 The fact that the combination resulted in the greatest decrease in all oxidative markers and the greatest enzyme activities, and not metal exclusion alone, suggests that the restored redox balance is directly related to the preserved membranes and pigments mentioned above and that the combination was responsible for the restored antioxidant capacity.

The osmolyte and non-enzymatic antioxidant data indicate that there are two responses that are frequently confused: osmotic adjustment and redox buffering, and only the latter was correlated with recovery. Massive accumulation of proline and glycine-betaine, the characteristic osmotic-adjustment and chaperone response, which stabilizes proteins and scavenges radicals, 73 was observed as a result of tellurite stress, but the ascorbate pool and glutathione redox ratio collapsed, indicating failure of the cell's central redox buffer. The combined treatment not only increased the osmolyte levels but also reduced the surplus of proline and glycine-betaine, which were no longer necessary, and restored ascorbate and the reduced glutathione pool. This trend suggests that after the uptake of Te and the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) were reduced, the need for emergency osmotic adjustment was reduced, and the metabolic priority shifted toward the regeneration of the ascorbate-glutathione cycle, which is the central hub that recycles oxidized antioxidants and maintains redox homeostasis under metal stress. 74 , 75 As mentioned in the previous paragraph, enzymatic defense is linked to the reconstituted redox couple, as allantoin supplementation has been shown to increase ascorbate and glutathione levels and decrease their oxidized forms, 19 providing a direct mechanistic link.

The shoot metabolite data revealed a carbon-allocation trade-off in the center of the tellurite response. Quinoa increased the levels of anthocyanin and betalain (betacyanin and betaxanthin) pigments, which serve as auxiliary antioxidants, by about 2-fold under stress, and reduced the soluble sugar and protein levels, suggesting that photoassimilate and carbon skeletons were being diverted from primary growth metabolism to defensive pigment production. This diversion is energetically expensive and would exacerbate the yield penalty if it persisted, as it would compete with growth. The combined 5-HT and ALL treatment solved the conundrum by normalizing the pigment overabundance and restoring fructose, sucrose, and protein, which is a direct consequence of the restored photosynthetic capacity reported above: when assimilation is restored and oxidative stress is alleviated, the cell no longer has to over-invest in secondary antioxidants and can replenish primary carbon pools. The endogenous metabolite data support this interpretation, as exogenous application resulted in an increase in tissue serotonin and allantoin that was much higher than the increase seen in response to stress alone and was compound-specific. This suggests that the compounds applied were taken up and enhanced the plant's own biosynthetic pools, increasing the plant's natural signaling ability instead of being external transient compounds. 11 , 15

The mineral-nutrient data suggest that disruption of membrane ion selectivity is a fundamental mechanism of tellurite toxicity, and protection from tellurite is a fundamental benefit of the amendments. Tellurite stress resulted in the depletion of all essential macro- and micronutrients in leaves and grains and almost doubled the sodium content, leading to a collapse of the K+/Na+ ratio in both tissues, which is a characteristic of impaired transporter function, where selective K+ uptake is impaired, and Na+ leakage is increased. 76 Ionic imbalance is more of a downstream effect of the oxidative burst, as ROS directly affect the plasma membrane channels and H+-ATPases responsible for this selectivity. 10 The combined treatment restored the grain K+/Na+ ratio by 132% relative to the tellurite-stressed control, through a 43% reduction in tissue Na+ and a 32% increase in K+, while phosphorus, zinc and the remaining essential minerals rose by 30–49%; this disproportionate, ion-specific recovery is consistent with the integrity of the transporters being maintained once the oxidative pressure was lifted. 77 This restored selectivity, combined with the expanded root system, accounts for the ability of the amendments to simultaneously increase nutrient uptake and limit Te uptake. The decreased bioavailable Te and restored membrane function reduced the amount of Te taken up by roots, shoots and, most importantly, grains: the combined treatment lowered the translocation factor by 16.9% (a non-significant Te × Amendment interaction, p = 0.695) and grain Te residue by 66.7% relative to the stressed control; the grain Te × Amendment interaction was the strongest of any variable measured in this study (F3,21 = 177.6, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.962; Table 10), confirming that foliar 5-HT + ALL synergistically restricted Te translocation into the edible grain rather than merely enabling the plant to tolerate a higher internal Te burden.

Yield and grain quality are the culmination of all the agronomic aspects of the physiological processes mentioned above. Tellurite stress had the greatest relative impact on the reproductive sink (panicle development, seed number and grain yield) and reduced grain composition (protein and starch), with a significant increase in bitter saponins. The almost parallel decrease in the harvest index indicates that both source capacity (biomass and photosynthesis) and sink activity (grain filling) were affected, and that the yield reduction was the result of reduced assimilation, oxidative damage, and reduced nutrient supply during the reproductive phase. 78 This is why the combined application of 5-HT + ALL was able to restore the yield close to that of the control, preserve photosynthetic and water relations which maintained the source strength, relieved the oxidative and ionic stress which protected the reproductive development, and also reduced the accumulation of saponins which led to better palatability. Particularly, for practical deployment, the combination reduced grain tellurium residue more than in the other tissues, implying a restriction on phloem-mediated Te loading into the seed and hence a food-safety advantage, since Te loaded into foods of plant origin is subject to scrutiny in the diet. 4

Economic feasibility, scalability, and regulatory pathways

To translate this biostimulant approach from pot to field, it is important to consider the cost and regulatory status of this approach compared to conventional amendments. Synthetic chelators, such as EDTA, are effective at mobilizing metals, but they are not biodegradable and have been shown to leach metal-chelates into groundwater, a well-known secondary risk that has limited their use in the field. 79 Bulk organic amendments, such as biochar and compost, are expensive to transport and apply. Serotonin and allantoin are in a different position: both are natural metabolites of plants, biodegradable, and the extremely low doses used here (50 and 100 µM) would make the per-hectare input cost competitive with bulk fertilizers, even though the unit cost of analytical grade is currently higher. Both compounds are naturally occurring in plants and are already produced on a large scale for pharmaceutical and cosmetic applications, indicating that their toxicological profiles are known, which should facilitate the regulatory process for agricultural registration compared to new synthetic compounds. However, a formal field-scale cost-benefit and life-cycle assessment is still required before these economic benefits can be generalized.

Limitations and future perspectives

These results have several restrictions, and they consider priorities for further work. First, the experiment was performed in pots in a protected wirehouse, which isolates Te as the only variable, but cannot fully account for the varying climatic, soil, and natural microbiological conditions of open fields, and multi-season field tests are required in different agro-ecological areas to determine scalability and durability. Second, although the study clarified the physiological and biochemical basis of the response, it did not clarify the molecular aspects of the modulation of Te handling or the induction of antioxidant levels through the action of serotonin and allantoin; to this end, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic approaches are required. Similarly, the proposed mechanism by which foliar 5-HT and ALL reduced Te accumulation, via modified root exudation and restored ion homeostasis, was inferred from indirect physiological, elemental, and soil-chemistry endpoints rather than measured directly; no root transporter activity assays, plasma-membrane transport measurements, or direct rhizosphere Te speciation analyzes were performed, and this mechanism should therefore be regarded as hypothetical pending direct experimental validation. Third, the rhizosphere recovery suggested in this study, based on microbial biomass and enzyme activities, should be taxonomically and functionally resolved in the future using metagenomic sequencing of microbial communities to identify microbial taxa that mediate Te immobilization and growth promotion. Fourth, the dose-response surface was only determined for one concentration of each compound; optimal rates, timing of application, and longevity of Te-restriction in successive cropping cycles will need to be determined before field recommendations can be finalized. Finally, because the experimental design did not include a Tween-20-only treatment, the surfactant's own contribution to the observed responses, although expected to be minimal at the 0.1% (v/v) concentration used, cannot be formally excluded and should be isolated in future work. Closing these gaps would transform the mechanistic proof of concept developed herein into an agroecosystem management practice that is validated and deployable for tellurium-contaminated agroecosystems.

Conclusions

The application of a combination of 50 µM serotonin (5-HT) and 100 µM allantoin (ALL) as foliar sprays is an effective and mechanistically coherent strategy for protecting quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) from tellurite toxicity. The combined action of the two metabolites resulted in the restoration of rhizosphere microbial biomass and enzyme activity, sequestration of Te in less bioavailable soil fractions, and strengthening of the antioxidant and ascorbate-glutathione systems, which prevented oxidative and ionic damage to photosynthetic pigments, membrane integrity, and K+/Na+ homeostasis. These concerted soil and plant responses culminated in almost complete recovery of grain yield and nutritional quality, and importantly, a significant decrease in Te residue in the harvested grain. This combination has proven to be superior, creating a true synergy at the soil biology, plant physiology, and grain safety levels. Thus, the co-application of serotonin and allantoin is a sustainable low-input biostimulant approach to grow safe and high-yielding quinoa on Te-contaminated soils, which needs to be validated in multi-season field trials.
