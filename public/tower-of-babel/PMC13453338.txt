Life-course pathways from childhood starvation to late-life depressive symptoms: a double machine learning approach
Authors: Chaoxin Jiang, Hao Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesAlthough previous research has documented the adverse effects of childhood starvation as a profound early-life adversity, most studies have focused on physical health outcomes or mortality, with limited integration of life-course theory and Bourdieu’s capital-based framework. This study examined whether childhood starvation predicted depressive symptoms in later life and further investigated whether these associations were mediated by economic, social, and cultural capital.Research Design and MethodsData were drawn from the 2018, 2020, and 2023 waves of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey. An unbalanced panel dataset comprising 25,852 observations was constructed, with participants being older adults in China. The double machine learning approach was applied to estimate the causal impact of childhood starvation on late-life depressive symptoms and analyze the mediating roles of the 3 types of capital.ResultsThe findings indicated that (a) individuals who experienced frequent hunger in childhood reported significantly higher levels of depressive symptoms in old age and (b) economic, social, and cultural capital each partially mediated the association between childhood starvation and late-life depressive symptoms.Discussion and ImplicationsThis study advanced life-course scholarship by integrating a capital-based framework to understand the enduring psychological consequences of childhood starvation, linking early adversity to late-life mental health through cumulative depletion of key resources. It identifies capital erosion as a key mechanism and offers implications for policies aimed at reducing childhood food insecurity, strengthening life-course resources, and expanding mental health services.

This study demonstrates that childhood experiences of hunger may exert enduring consequences for mental health in later life. The findings underscore the importance of adopting a life-course perspective in promoting psychological well-being, with particular attention to the economic, social, and cultural resources that shape resilience across adulthood. Although early-life deprivation cannot be reversed, its long-term psychological consequences may be mitigated through policies and interventions that reduce cumulative disadvantage and strengthen later-life resources.

Background

Hunger during childhood represents one of the most profound forms of early-life adversity, exerting far-reaching effects on health and well-being across the life course (Li et al., 2025; Pickett et al., 2015). Early nutritional deprivation not only disrupts physical growth and cognitive development but also leaves lasting imprints on psychological functioning and social trajectories that may persist into older adulthood (Alaimo et al., 2001; Spettigue et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2017). A substantial body of research has demonstrated that inadequate nutrition in early life is associated with a wide range of adverse outcomes later in the life course, including increased risks of chronic diseases, functional decline, and premature mortality (Chen & Zhou, 2007; Kim et al., 2017). Nevertheless, as scholars have noted, most existing studies have predominantly focused on physical health outcomes or mortality (Wang & Wang, 2022), whereas the psychological sequelae of early-life starvation have received comparatively limited attention. This gap in the literature is particularly salient in rapidly aging societies such as China, where examining the long-term mental health consequences of early-life hunger is both timely and necessary.

As the world’s largest aging society, China is home to more than 310 million adults aged 60 and above, many of whom experienced widespread food scarcity during their formative years. Historical events such as the Great Chinese Famine (1959–1961) subjected large segments of the population to severe nutritional deprivation in early childhood, thereby creating a distinct cohort with shared experiences of hunger (Cui et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2024). Understanding how these early-life exposures influence mental health outcomes in later life is therefore not only of theoretical significance but also of pressing policy relevance. Generating empirical evidence on this topic can provide critical insights for the development of targeted public health interventions, social policies, and mental health services tailored to older adults whose life courses have been profoundly shaped by early-life starvation.

This study makes several important contributions to the existing literature. First, it advances life-course research on early adversity by examining childhood starvation as a critical determinant of depressive symptoms in later life, a mental health outcome that has received comparatively limited scholarly attention. Second, it leverages nationally representative data from the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS) and applies a double machine learning (DML) approach to generate robust empirical evidence from a population that experienced substantial historical variation in early-life nutritional conditions. Third, by situating the analysis within a life-course framework, the study offers novel insights into the pathways through which early-life experiences exert long-term effects on mental health in old age. Taken together, these contributions deepen our understanding of the enduring consequences of childhood hunger and highlight the importance of addressing early-life nutritional deprivation as part of broader public health strategies to promote healthy and active aging.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. The Literature review section reviews the relevant literature on childhood adversity, life-course processes, and mental health in later life. The Method section describes the data, measures, and analytical strategies employed in the analysis. The Result section presents the empirical findings, and The Discussion section discusses their implications for theory, policy, and future research.

Literature review

Childhood starvation and mental health in late-life

Childhood starvation is widely recognized as a profound early-life stressor with enduring consequences for both physical and psychological health across the life course. Life-course theory emphasizes that exposures during sensitive developmental windows can exert disproportionate and long-lasting effects on individual trajectories (Jiang & Jiang, 2022; Ramirez & Haas, 2022). From a biological perspective, early nutritional deprivation may induce permanent changes in gene expression, neurodevelopment, and stress-regulation systems, thereby heightening vulnerability to mental disorders in adulthood (Guo et al., 2024; Neelsen & Stratmann, 2011). Undernutrition can also impair the formation of key brain structures and disrupt neurotransmitter systems, both of which play crucial roles in mood regulation (Brown et al., 2000; Lumey et al., 2011). From a social perspective, childhood hunger frequently co-occurs with broader socioeconomic disadvantages, which may intensify feelings of fear and insecurity, undermine a sense of control and self-esteem, and initiate cumulative disadvantages that shape mental health across the life span (Li et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2021). Consistent with these theoretical accounts, a growing body of empirical research has shown that individuals who experience food insecurity or starvation in childhood face heightened risks of mental health problems in adulthood and later life, including depressive symptoms, schizophrenia, and diminished psychological well-being (Guo et al., 2024; Jiang & Jiang, 2022; Li & Lumey, 2017).

Despite substantial advances, existing research on childhood starvation and its long-term consequences remains limited in several important respects. First, a key limitation is the frequent conflation of famine exposure with individual experiences of hunger. The Great Chinese Famine of 1959–1961 provides an important historical context for interpreting the high prevalence of childhood hunger among older Chinese adults. As one of the most severe famines in modern history, it caused tens of millions of excess deaths, with commonly cited estimates ranging from approximately 16.5 to 30 million, as well as substantial numbers of lost or postponed births (Guo et al., 2024; Lay & Norling, 2020; Ren et al., 2025). Existing studies have often examined its long-term consequences using difference-in-differences strategies that compare cohorts born before, during, and after the famine, with provincial mortality rates or reductions in cohort size serving as proxies for famine severity (Chen & Zhou, 2007; Kim et al., 2017). Although informative, these macro-level indicators may measure individual exposure imprecisely and may be confounded by unobserved cohort- or region-specific characteristics. More importantly, famine exposure does not necessarily equate to individual-level hunger. Some children exposed to the famine period may not have experienced frequent hunger, whereas socioeconomically disadvantaged children may have faced food insecurity outside the formal famine years. Aggregate mortality or cohort-size measures may therefore fail to capture the actual distribution and intensity of childhood hunger (Cui et al., 2020). To address this limitation, our study uses an individual-level retrospective measure of childhood starvation, based on whether respondents reported often going to bed hungry during childhood, rather than inferring exposure from birth cohorts, famine years, or province-level mortality rates. Second, much of the existing literature relies on cross-sectional data or relatively outdated follow-up surveys and fails to link early-life nutritional conditions with long-term psychological outcomes, thereby limiting the strength of causal inference (Liu et al., 2021; Pickett et al., 2015). Without longitudinal evidence that traces individuals from early life into older adulthood, disentangling the lasting impacts of childhood starvation from subsequent life-course influences remains challenging. Third, there has been insufficient exploration of the mechanisms through which early-life starvation accumulates to shape mental health outcomes in later life. Cumulative disadvantage theory highlights the critical role of early adversity in shaping cohort differentiation over time, as risk factors compound throughout the life course and contribute to increasing heterogeneity in older age (Dannefer, 2003). Yet, most studies stop short of examining how this cumulative process unfolds and interacts with different pathways. As a result, our understanding of how childhood starvation translates into divergent mental health trajectories in later life remains incomplete.

The underlying mechanisms: Bourdieu’s capital-based perspective

Viewed through a life-course lens, early-life shocks seldom dissipate over time; rather, they accumulate and progressively shape divergent trajectories as cohorts age. Understanding how childhood starvation leads to adverse psychological outcomes in later life requires careful attention to the underlying processes through which these long-term effects unfold. Bourdieu’s theoretical framework provides a valuable lens for this inquiry by positioning individuals within social space according to the volume, composition, and convertibility of their economic, social, and cultural capital (Bourdieu, 1986; Veenstra & Abel, 2019). Each form of capital functions as a crucial health-related resource and plays a central role in the production and reproduction of health inequalities (Pinxten & Lievens, 2014; Qi et al., 2023). Building on this perspective, the following sections delineate the pathways through which childhood starvation may gradually erode these forms of capital, thereby heightening vulnerability to depressive symptoms in later life.

Economic capital encompasses the material resources and financial means that shape individuals’ access to health-promoting environments and services (Paccoud et al., 2020). Childhood starvation is often embedded within broader patterns of material deprivation and socioeconomic disadvantage, which exert enduring effects on economic outcomes across the life course (Schurer et al., 2019). Early malnutrition is strongly linked to impaired cognitive development, lower educational attainment, and reduced labor productivity, all of which constrain income generation and wealth accumulation in adulthood (Guo et al., 2024). These disadvantages compound over time, as limited financial resources in later life heighten psychological vulnerability, exacerbate chronic stress, restrict access to healthcare, and diminish individuals’ capacity to cope with adversity, thereby elevating the risk of depressive symptoms and other mental health problems (Gu et al., 2021). Empirical evidence consistently documents earnings and employment penalties associated with early-life adversity, including fetal or childhood exposure to famine, and shows that persistent financial hardship is closely linked to poorer mental health and higher levels of depressive symptoms (Ahnquist et al., 2012; Scholte et al., 2015; Schurer et al., 2019). In this sense, economic capital functions as a key indirect pathway through which childhood starvation shapes mental health trajectories in later life.

Social capital refers to the resources embedded in social networks that individuals can mobilize for support and information (Qi et al., 2023; Stephens, 2008). Early experiences of hunger often occur alongside family economic strain, residential instability, and diminished parental capacity to foster nurturing relationships, all of which can weaken children’s early socialization and constrain the development of social skills (Fan et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2024). The internalization of fear, insecurity, and social withdrawal during childhood may persist into adulthood, shaping patterns of social engagement and network formation across the life course (Spettigue et al., 2025). In later life, low levels of social capital are consistently linked to greater psychological distress and elevated risks of depressive symptoms (Li et al., 2021). Weakened social networks reduce access to emotional and instrumental support, limit opportunities for positive social roles, and restrict exposure to information about health services and coping strategies (Paccoud et al., 2020; Pinxten & Lievens, 2014). Empirical research consistently documents strong associations between low social capital and adverse health outcomes (Ahnquist et al., 2012; Dean & Sharkey, 2011). These findings underscore the pivotal mediating role of social capital in the pathway linking childhood starvation to mental health outcomes in older adulthood.

Cultural capital, most commonly operationalized as institutionalized educational attainment, has emerged as a promising concept for understanding health inequalities (Abel, 2008; Pinxten & Lievens, 2014). Childhood starvation can disrupt the accumulation of cultural capital through multiple pathways. Nutritional deprivation during critical developmental periods impairs cognitive functioning, hinders school readiness, and undermines academic performance, thereby constraining educational attainment and limiting the acquisition of knowledge and skills necessary to navigate complex social and institutional environments (Lay & Norling, 2020; Ramirez & Haas, 2022). Adolescents who experience chronic food insufficiency are more likely to exhibit lower cognitive ability, perform significantly worse on academic achievement tests, face higher risks of suspension or school dropout, and ultimately attain fewer years of schooling (Alaimo et al., 2001; Florence et al., 2008). These educational disadvantages accumulate over the life course, shaping health-related knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors in adulthood. In later life, lower levels of cultural capital are associated with less effective help-seeking, poorer navigation of health and social service systems, and the adoption of maladaptive health practices, all of which heighten vulnerability to depressive symptoms (Abel, 2008; Ohashi et al., 2017). In this way, cultural capital constitutes a critical mechanism linking childhood starvation to late-life mental health disparities.

Taken together, these three forms of capital are deeply interwoven, and their erosion over the life course represents a central mechanism through which childhood starvation leaves lasting imprints on mental health. Economic capital shapes access to material resources and coping capacity, social capital embeds individuals in networks that provide support and information, and cultural capital equips them with the knowledge and competencies needed to navigate health systems and shape health-related behaviors. Understanding how these resources interact and accumulate across the life course offers valuable insights into the persistent psychological consequences of early-life starvation.

The current study

Building on the theoretical and empirical foundations outlined above, this study aims to deepen our understanding of the long-term mental health consequences of childhood starvation by integrating a life-course perspective with Bourdieu’s capital-based framework. Although prior research has established links between early-life famine exposure and various adult outcomes, few studies have systematically explored how nutritional deprivation in childhood shapes psychological well-being in later life through the erosion of economic, social, and cultural capital. Furthermore, much of the existing literature relies on macro-level proxies of famine severity, neglects individual-level experiences of hunger, and offers limited insight into the distinct mechanisms through which early adversity becomes biologically and socially embedded. To address these gaps, this study draws on nationally representative data from the CLASS to investigate the long-term psychological consequences of childhood starvation. Specifically, we examine whether early-life hunger is associated with depressive symptoms in later adulthood and assess the mediating roles of economic, social, and cultural capital in this relationship. By doing so, our study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how childhood starvation shapes mental health trajectories across the life course and highlights the cumulative pathways through which early disadvantage is reproduced in later life. We propose the following hypotheses:H1: Childhood starvation is positively associated with depressive symptoms in later life.H2: Economic capital in later life mediates the relationship between childhood starvation and depressive symptoms in later life.H3: Social capital in later life mediates the relationship between childhood starvation and depressive symptoms in later life.H4: Cultural capital in later life mediates the relationship between childhood starvation and depressive symptoms in later life.

Method

Participants

This study draws on data from the CLASS, a nationally representative survey administered by the National Survey Research Center at Renmin University of China. Utilizing a stratified multi-stage probability sampling design, the survey randomly selects 134 districts and counties across 28 provinces as sampling sites. CLASS collects rich and comprehensive data on older adults, including basic demographic information, health status and disease history, socioeconomic conditions, daily activities, family structure and intergenerational relationships. Data were collected primarily through face-to-face personal interviewing by trained investigators. The baseline survey was launched in 2014, followed by four subsequent waves in 2016, 2018, 2020, and 2023. As information on childhood starvation was first collected in the 2018 wave, this study integrates data from the 2018, 2020, and 2023 waves. Initially, 26,778 observations contained valid depressive symptoms scores across these three waves. After excluding 926 observations with missing values on control or mediating variables, we constructed a final unbalanced panel dataset of 25,852 valid observations (7,748, 8,988, and 9,116 for 2018, 2020, and 2023, respectively). The missing data rate is 3.5% (926/26,778), minimizing concerns regarding sample selection bias. Descriptive statistics for the analytical sample are presented in Table 1. The mean age of respondents was 71.2 years.

Measurement

Childhood starvation was operationalized as a binary variable based on the CLASS survey item, “During your childhood, did you often go to bed feeling hungry?” Respondents who answered affirmatively were assigned a value of 1, indicating the experience of childhood starvation, whereas those who answered negatively were assigned a value of 0. Within the analytical sample, 65.01% of older adults reported having gone to bed hungry during their childhood, reflecting a relatively high prevalence of early-life food deprivation in this population.

The dependent variable, depressive symptoms, was measured using the nine-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D 9), a widely validated instrument for assessing mental health in Chinese older adults (Sun et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). Among these items, six capture negative affective states, including feeling lonely, feeling sad, loss of appetite, poor sleep, feeling worthless, and feeling that life has no purpose. The remaining three items reflect positive emotional experiences, namely feeling in a good mood, feeling that life is going well, and finding joy in life. Each item was rated on a 3-point Likert scale (1 = never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often). To ensure conceptual consistency in scoring, the three positively worded items were reverse coded before aggregation. A composite depressive symptom score was then calculated by summing all item responses, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms among older adults. The internal consistency of this scale was robust across our analytical sample, yielding Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.753, 0.671, and 0.703 for the 2018, 2020, and 2023 waves, respectively.

Three mediating variables are included in this study: economic capital, social capital, and cultural capital. First, economic capital was measured by average monthly household expenditure, which captures material resources and consumption capacity in later life (Li et al., 2024). This measure is particularly suitable for older adults, whose income may be unstable, incomplete, or less reflective of actual living standards after retirement. Observations reporting an average monthly expenditure above 100,000 yuan are excluded from the analysis; as such, values are flagged as statistical outliers in the CLASS dataset. Second, social capital was measured through current social support networks, including (a) the number of family members or relatives the respondent meets or contacts at least once a month; (b) the number of family members or relatives with whom the respondent can share personal matters; and (c) the number of family members or relatives who can provide help when needed. This approach is consistent with the network- and support-based understanding of social capital (Nyqvist et al., 2013; Tengku Mohd et al., 2019). Each item is coded on a scale from 0 to 5. Higher total scores indicate greater social capital. Finally, cultural capital was operationalized as educational attainment, which corresponds to the institutionalized form of cultural capital and has been widely used in empirical research on social inequalities in health (Paccoud et al., 2020; Pinxten & Lievens, 2014). Cultural capital was coded as follows: 0 = illiterate, 1 = primary school, 2 = junior high school, 3 = senior high school, and 4 = college or above. The average educational level in the analytical sample is 1.279, a figure broadly consistent with China’s national context and with findings reported in prior research (Chen et al., 2024).

Covariates

To minimize potential bias arising from omitted variable confounding in estimating the effect of childhood starvation on depressive symptoms in later life, we included two sets of control variables at the demographic and household levels. Covariates were selected based on prior literature on childhood adversity, late-life depressive symptoms, and health inequalities, and were distinguished from the mediating capital variables on conceptual and measurement grounds. Although these variables may be associated with the distribution or accumulation of capital, they do not directly measure the volume, intensity, or mobilizable resources of economic, social, or cultural capital. At the demographic level, we controlled for gender (1 = male, 0 = female), age, ethnicity (1 = ethnic minority, 0 = non-ethnic minority), marital status (1 = married, 0 = unmarried, divorced, widowed, or other), religious belief (1 = with religious belief, 0 = without religious belief), place of residence (1 = rural areas, 0 = urban areas), and retirement status (1 = retired, 0 = not retired). At the household level, we included indicators of living conditions, measured by the presence of six types of household facilities: bathroom, indoor toilet, toilet bowl, emergency call device, tap water supply, and cooking fuel equipment. Each facility was coded as a dummy variable, taking the value of 1 if the household possessed the facility and 0 otherwise. These variables were retained as covariates rather than incorporated into the mediating variables because they are primarily binary or categorical indicators of social status or living conditions. Incorporating them into the capital measures would require arbitrary scoring or weighting decisions that are not clearly supported by existing theory or prior empirical practice. Including them as covariates therefore allows us to adjust for relevant background differences while maintaining the conceptual coherence of the three capital-based mediators.

In the subsequent analyses, all variables are standardized using Z-scores to ensure comparability across measures and to eliminate potential distortions in coefficient interpretation arising from differences in scales and units. Analytical strategy

In estimating the causal effect of childhood starvation on later-life depressive symptoms, ordinary least squares regression is limited by its reliance on linearity assumptions and its vulnerability to omitted variable bias, particularly when high-dimensional controls and nonlinear interactions are present. These limitations risk producing biased or inconsistent estimates in the presence of complex confounding structures that characterize socioeconomic and health processes. By contrast, the DML framework (Chernozhukov et al., 2017, 2018) provides a more robust approach by integrating machine learning algorithms with orthogonalization and cross-fitting procedures, which effectively mitigate overfitting and reduce bias arising from high-dimensional confounders. Importantly, DML accommodates nonlinearities in cultural, social, and economic capital, as well as heterogeneous effects across demographic and household conditions, thereby offering a more accurate isolation of the net causal pathway from childhood starvation to older adults’ depressive symptoms. Consequently, compared to OLS, DML provides a more credible framework for disentangling the long-run health consequences of early-life deprivation within high-dimensional, confounded environments typical of aging populations. Mediation model results

We next examine the potential mechanisms through which childhood starvation influences depressive symptoms, focusing on the mediating roles of cultural, social, and economic capital. Results from the DML-based mediation models are reported in Table 4. In the first stage, childhood starvation significantly reduces cultural capital (β = –0.0384, p < .001), social capital (β = –0.0461, p < .001), and economic capital (β = –0.0187, p < .01), suggesting that early-life deprivation constrains the accumulation of human, relational, and financial resources later in life. In the second stage, each of these forms of capital is negatively and significantly associated with depressive symptoms, with effect sizes of –0.0541 (cultural capital, p < .001), –0.0324 (social capital, p < .001), and –0.0664 (economic capital, p < .001). The Sobel tests confirm the statistical significance of these indirect pathways, indicating that all three domains of capital partially mediate the impact of childhood starvation on late-life depressive symptoms. Taken together, the findings reveal that the enduring psychological costs of early-life food insecurity are transmitted not only directly but also indirectly through reduced opportunities for educational attainment, diminished social support, and constrained economic resources across the life course.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a series of sensitivity analyses with two objectives. The first objective was to address the concern that the estimated association between childhood starvation and depressive symptoms might be driven by the somatic components of the CES-D 9. Because childhood starvation is closely related to physical health, the two somatic items in the CES-D 9—loss of appetite and poor sleep—may partly capture physical health conditions rather than depressive symptomatology. To examine this possibility, we reconstructed the dependent variable as a seven-item CES-D measure by excluding these two items and re-estimated the full DML mediation model. As reported in Supplementary Table 1, the main results remain substantively unchanged, suggesting that our conclusions are not driven by the somatic items in the CES-D 9.

The second objective was to assess whether the benchmark and mediation results are robust to alternative model specifications and additional procedures aimed at mitigating endogeneity concerns. As shown in Figure 1 and Supplementary Tables 2–7, the results remain stable when using heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors, alternative cross-validation folds, a Random Forest algorithm within the DML framework, and models including additional pairwise interaction terms for control variables. To further address potential sample selection and observed-confounding concerns, propensity score matching with a 0.01 caliper was implemented prior to DML estimation. The covariate balance tests shown in Figure 2 indicate that matching substantially reduced observed covariate imbalance, and the estimates from the matched sample remain close to the baseline estimates. Overall, these sensitivity analyses indicate that the direct effect of childhood starvation on late-life depressive symptoms and the mediating roles of cultural, social, and economic capital are robust to alternative outcome measurement, alternative model specifications, alternative machine-learning algorithms, and additional procedures addressing potential endogeneity.

Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence that childhood starvation exerts a persistent and multifaceted influence on mental health well into older adulthood. Drawing on nationally representative longitudinal data and employing a DML approach, we show that individuals who experienced frequent hunger in childhood report significantly higher levels of depressive symptoms later in life. Although the estimated effect is modest at the individual level, its public health significance should be considered in relation to the high prevalence of childhood starvation in this cohort. When exposure is widespread, even small increases in depressive symptom scores may translate into a meaningful population-level burden. These findings therefore suggest that childhood starvation is associated with a small but persistent elevation in depressive symptoms in later life, with potential implications for population mental health among cohorts extensively exposed to early-life hunger. This finding underscores the enduring imprint of childhood starvation on psychological well-being and extends prior research that has predominantly focused on physical health or mortality outcomes (Chen & Zhou, 2007; Kim et al., 2017; Pickett et al., 2015). By shifting the analytical lens toward mental health, our study enriches life-course scholarship on early adversity and highlights the importance of addressing childhood hunger as a critical determinant of psychological well-being across the lifespan.

Our analysis further elucidates the mechanisms through which early-life hunger shapes mental health in later life. Consistent with Bourdieu’s capital-based framework, we demonstrate that economic, social, and cultural capital each partially mediates the association between childhood starvation and depressive symptoms. These findings offer a more nuanced understanding of the cumulative processes linking early adversity to later outcomes and point to the erosion of key forms of capital as a central mechanism through which childhood starvation becomes socially and biologically embedded (Pinxten & Lievens, 2014; Qi et al., 2023).

First, our results highlight the pivotal role of economic capital as a mediator across the life course. Early malnutrition constrains cognitive development and educational attainment, which in turn limit labor productivity and income-generating capacity in adulthood (Guo et al., 2024; Schurer et al., 2019). These disadvantages accumulate over time, resulting in reduced financial resources in later life. As our findings indicate, limited economic capital is strongly associated with heightened depressive symptoms, likely due to increased exposure to chronic stress, restricted access to health services, and diminished coping capacity (Gu et al., 2021). These patterns align with cumulative disadvantage theory, which posits that early inequalities compound over time, producing widening disparities in health and well-being in later life (Dannefer, 2003).

Second, social capital emerged as another significant mediating pathway. Childhood hunger is often accompanied by family instability and weakened socialization environments, which foster long-term patterns of social withdrawal and inhibit the formation of robust social networks (Fan et al., 2021; Spettigue et al., 2025). Our findings demonstrate that lower levels of social capital are strongly associated with greater psychological distress among older adults (Ahnquist et al., 2012; Dean & Sharkey, 2011). This underscores the importance of relational resources for mental health and suggests that childhood starvation has enduring social consequences that shape individuals’ access to emotional support, informational resources, and social participation opportunities throughout the life course. It should also be noted that the association between social capital and depressive symptoms in later life may involve complex temporal dynamics, although our analysis focuses on the theoretically specified pathway from childhood starvation to reduced social capital and subsequent depressive symptoms.

Third, cultural capital, operationalized through educational attainment, also mediates the relationship between childhood starvation and late-life depressive symptoms. Nutritional deprivation during critical developmental periods impairs cognitive functioning and school performance, leading to lower educational attainment and limited acquisition of skills necessary to navigate complex social and institutional systems (Lay & Norling, 2020; Ramirez & Haas, 2022). Lower levels of cultural capital in later life are associated with less effective help-seeking, poorer utilization of health services, and maladaptive health behaviors, all of which exacerbate vulnerability to depressive symptoms (Abel, 2008; Ohashi et al., 2017). These findings underscore the intergenerational significance of educational inequalities rooted in early-life conditions and their far-reaching implications for mental health.

Taken together, the theoretical significance of these findings lies in their integration of life-course theory, cumulative disadvantage theory, and Bourdieu’s capital-based framework. Childhood starvation should not be understood merely as a temporary episode of nutritional deprivation but as an early-life shock that may initiate long-term processes of resource depletion and social stratification. The observed mediating roles of economic, social, and cultural capital suggest that early hunger becomes embedded in later-life mental health not only through biological scarring, but also through the gradual erosion of material resources, relational support, and institutionalized cultural resources. In this sense, our findings extend prior studies on famine exposure and early-life adversity by specifying how early nutritional deprivation is converted into durable disadvantages across the life course. They also enrich the application of Bourdieu’s framework in health research by showing that different forms of capital are not merely correlates of mental health but may constitute key pathways through which early structural deprivation is reproduced in later life.

Implications

The findings of this study have important implications for policies and interventions aimed at mitigating the long-term mental health consequences of childhood starvation and reducing the intergenerational transmission of disadvantage. They suggest that policy responses should not be limited to nutritional interventions, but should adopt an integrated, multi-level, and life-course approach that strengthens material, social, and cultural resources at different stages of life. Such an approach is essential not only for alleviating the psychological burden associated with early-life hunger but also for promoting healthier and more equitable aging trajectories among cohorts exposed to early-life deprivation.

First, policies should prioritize early prevention and structural interventions to reduce childhood food insecurity and its cascading consequences. Expanding investment in child nutrition programs, particularly in economically disadvantaged and rural regions, remains fundamental. Universal school meal provision, nutritional supplementation during critical developmental periods, and targeted support for pregnant women and young children may help reduce early-life malnutrition and its long-term psychological effects. In addition, broader anti-poverty strategies, including income redistribution, strengthened social safety nets, and rural development initiatives, are necessary to address the socioeconomic vulnerabilities that increase the risk of childhood hunger. These upstream interventions are important not only for improving immediate health and developmental outcomes but also for reshaping life-course trajectories that might otherwise lead to cumulative disadvantage in later life.

Second, interventions should extend beyond childhood and continue into older adulthood. Because the consequences of childhood starvation may be transmitted through the erosion of economic, social, and cultural capital, later-life resource compensation remains meaningful. Integrating a life-course perspective into geriatric care may improve the identification of older adults who are vulnerable to depressive symptoms as a result of early-life deprivation. Expanding access to community-based mental health services, promoting social participation, strengthening family and neighborhood support networks, and improving health literacy may help buffer the enduring psychological consequences of childhood hunger. In parallel, policies that enhance financial security in later life, such as targeted pensions or income support for historically disadvantaged cohorts, may reduce psychological stress and improve mental health outcomes among those most affected by early nutritional deprivation.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, although we move beyond macro-level famine indicators by incorporating individual-level retrospective reports of childhood hunger, these self-reported measures may still be vulnerable to recall bias and measurement error. Future research could enhance measurement validity by integrating multiple sources of evidence, such as historical records, nutritional data, and biological markers, to better capture exposure to early-life nutritional deprivation. Second, despite the longitudinal nature of the CLASS dataset, it does not follow respondents from childhood into late adulthood. The absence of prospective life-course data constrains our ability to fully capture the timing, duration, and cumulative nature of early-life hunger exposure, thereby limiting the strength of causal inference. Future studies would benefit from the use of birth cohort designs or linked administrative records that trace individuals across the entire life span. Third, although our measures of economic, social, and cultural capital were informed by established theoretical frameworks and prior empirical research, they remain unable to capture the full complexity of capital composition, convertibility, and accumulation across the life course. Certain covariates, including marital status, religious belief, ethnicity, retirement status, and household living conditions, may also be associated with broader processes through which capital is distributed and accumulated. However, in this study, these variables were treated as covariates rather than as components of the mediating variables because they primarily represent categorical indicators of social status, institutional position, or living conditions, rather than direct measures of the volume, intensity, or mobilizable resources of economic, social, and cultural capital. Future studies with richer longitudinal data should develop more comprehensive and multidimensional measures of capital and further examine how different forms of capital are accumulated, converted, and mutually reinforced over time.
