Ambiguous dietary behaviors of female school students: a qualitative content analysis via social cognitive theory
Authors: Nasrin Mehrjoyan, Fatemeh Zarei, Fazlollah Ghofranipour, Fazlollah Ahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adolescence is a critical period for the development of dietary patterns that significantly impact long-term health. This study explores the perspectives of adolescent girls, their parents, and educational staff regarding the factors shaping dietary behaviors in female school students. A qualitative content analysis was conducted between February and August 2024 in various regions of Khuzestan, Iran. Data were collected through semistructured interviews guided by social cognitive theory (SCT) and analyzed via a directed content analysis approach. The study took place in school and home environments in Khuzestan, Iran. Twenty-eight participants, including 17 female school students (aged 13–17 years), 8 parents, and 3 health educational stakeholders, were interviewed for 28–55 min (average: 40 min). The analysis identified 208 thematic codes categorized into six main categories and 15 subcategories. The key categories include (1) self-efficacy, which addresses challenges in nutrition management during stress and positive eating habits; (2) outcome expectations, which highlight the role of physical consequences and social approval/disapproval in food choices; (3) environmental factors, which reflect the influence of physical and social contexts; (4) self-regulation, which emphasizes goal-setting and self-monitoring; (5) knowledge, which reveals gaps in general and specialized nutritional understanding; and (6) attitudes, which highlight various perceptions of local foods. The dietary behaviors of female school students are shaped by a dynamic interplay of individual, social, and environmental factors. Promoting healthy eating requires a multifaceted approach, incorporating education, environmental adjustments, and social support to create sustainable changes.

Background

Adolescence is a period of rapid physical, psychological, and social growth[1,2, making the optimal intake of nutrients essential during this critical stage of life to support development and help adolescents reach their full potential3. Proper nutrition is widely recognized as a key factor for both current4 and future4 health. The evidence suggests that dietary behaviors established during adolescence carry over into adulthood4,5. Inadequate nutrient intake during adolescence is associated with increased risks of immediate health outcomes, such as weight gain, reduced academic performance, and decreased bone mineralization6. In the long term, poor nutrition can contribute to the development of risk factors for chronic diseases later in life5.

Over a 15-year period, approximately 51% of female adolescents and 34% of male adolescents exhibited at least one disordered eating behavior7. Furthermore, malnutrition continues to be a significant concern in Iran, with a 2019 meta-analysis indicating a relatively high prevalence8. Similarly, a 2019 meta-analysis on the prevalence of obesity and overweight among Iranian students revealed that 11% of the students were obese and that 12% were overweight. Overweight was more prevalent among girls than boys, particularly among middle school girls compared with high school girls9.

Nutrition is intertwined with the culture and socioeconomic factors of a society and is significantly influenced by governance quality, food policy, production capacity, and economic factors5. Governance quality plays a crucial role in ensuring high food production, which improves the dietary energy supply10. Changes in lifestyle and socioeconomic needs, particularly with shifts in food consumption patterns, have further complicated this issue1. Over time, dietary habits are shaped by a complex interplay of various factors, including income, prices, personal preferences and beliefs, cultural traditions, and geographic, environmental, social, and economic factors12. Therefore, health policymakers must adopt a multifaceted approach to improve dietary behaviors. Focusing solely on diseases directly linked to nutrition and daily dietary intake is insufficient from the broader global health perspective13.

Previous studies have often explored dietary behaviors broadly, paying less attention to the factors influencing these behaviors and the interplay of social, psychological, and cultural elements. This study was guided by social cognitive theory (SCT). The consumption of food is affected by self-efficacy, cognitive functions, one’s behavior, and other self-determined elements operating within interlinked networks. An example is self-efficacy, which is defined as an individual’s belief in healthy diet choices and is expressed in four forms: task specific (confidence in choosing healthy food), situational (refraining from consuming unhealthy foods in difficult settings), maintenance (perpetuation of healthy eating), and recovery (shifting to healthy habits after inactivity)14,15. Expectations of outcomes shape behavior and include physical (improved health or control over weight), social (favorable recognition from significant others), and self-evaluative (witnessed pride or blame) results.16–18 Environmental factors include both the presence of healthy food and social norms, especially those shaped by family and peers. Self-regulation through goal setting and self-monitoring aids in intentional dietary behavior by influencing how a person plans, reviews, and assesses their eating habits. Decision making is further enabled through attitudes toward nutrition19—factual (what should be eaten) and procedural (how to eat healthily)—by empowering an individual’s rational decision making.20 Through in-depth interviews with adolescent girls, their parents, and relevant stakeholders, this study explores the multidimensional experiences of these groups with respect to dietary behaviors. Thus, the significance of this research extends beyond individual health and aligns with broader social goals of fostering a healthier and more resilient population.

Sampling strategy and county selection within khuzestan province

To capture the diverse sociocultural and ecological landscape of Khuzestan Province, a purposeful sampling strategy was employed. This approach was grounded in the principles of maximum variation sampling to ensure representation across age, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, which is consistent with the qualitative methodology’s aim to explore rich, context-specific insights. Khuzestan, located in southwestern Iran, is characterized by significant geographical and cultural heterogeneity, spanning from the hot lowland plains near the Persian Gulf to the temperate highlands of the Zagros Mountains. Reflecting this variation, 12 counties were purposively selected out of 30, namely, Ahvaz (Zones 1–4), Abadan, Andimeshk, Bhabhan, Dasht-Azadegan, Dezful, Izeh, Shoshtar, and Tropical Alvar (Fig. 1: Sampling area). These counties were chosen to ensure the inclusion of regions differing in climate, ethnic composition (including Arab, Lur, and Persian communities), and urban‒rural dynamics. Sampling continued until thematic saturation was achieved—when no new codes emerged from the interviews—affirming the adequacy of both participant diversity and county coverage in illuminating adolescent girls’ dietary behaviors within this complex province.

Materials and methods

Design

This qualitative study employed a deductive content analysis approach21,22 which was chosen to explore adolescent girls’ experiences with nutritional behaviors. The method was carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, such as the COREQ (COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research) Checklist23.

Philosophical stance of the study

The research adopted a deductive approach, beginning with four constructs from social cognitive theory (self-efficacy, outcome expectations, the environment, and self-regulation) and proceeding with specific observations. As elaborated later, the data analysis aligned with this philosophical stance.

Conceptual framework

Among the various behavioral theories in health education, social cognitive theory (SCT) serves as a useful framework for interpreting and assessing dietary behaviors among school-aged adolescents. SCT integrates psychosocial factors and strategies for facilitating behavior change 2425. The theory operates within an interactive model, positing that individuals are influenced by, and reciprocally influence, their social environment. This includes interactions with family, peers, educators, and others, reflecting how social contexts shape thoughts, beliefs, and behaviors24,25. SCT also considers the interplay between individuals and their environment, alongside the psychosocial factors that influence health behaviors.

An extensive literature review involving eighty-two studies related to SCT revealed that the constructs with the highest predictive power in the context of nutritional behaviors and dietary habits were self-efficacy, outcome expectations, the environment, and self-regulation. These constructs play a significant role in explaining and predicting individuals’ nutritional behaviors26–28. These four constructs were used as the initial theoretical framework to design the semistructured interview questions, ensuring a comprehensive exploration of both individual and environmental aspects of adolescent girls’ nutritional behaviors. However, during the interview process and after the data were coded, it became apparent that two additional constructs—knowledge and attitude—also played crucial roles in explaining the participants’ nutritional behaviors. Shift reflects the flexibility and dynamism of qualitative research29, which allows for a deeper understanding of the factors influencing health-related behaviors as data collection and analysis unfold.

Participants and setting

The study was conducted in 12 counties within Khuzestan Province, southern Iran, with a total of 28 participants. This included 17 adolescent girls aged 13–17 years, 8 parents of adolescent girls, and 3 stakeholders. The inclusion criteria required participants to reside in Khuzestan, with girls aged 13–17 years and parents of girls in that age range. The participants had to be willing to share their experiences. Purposeful sampling aimed to capture maximum diversity in age, ethnicity, education level, number of children, occupation, and socioeconomic status (Tables 1, 2 and 3).

Data collection

Semistructured interview guides were developed on the basis of four social cognitive theory constructs: self-efficacy, outcome expectations, the environment, and self-regulation. The interview questions for the students, parents, and stakeholders are presented in Table 4. Face‒to-face interviews were conducted from February to August 2024. Probing questions were used to explore participants’ experiences in greater depth. All interviews, conducted by the first author (NM), took place at the participants’ chosen locations, usually in schools. The interviews ranged from 28 to 55 min, with an average duration of 40 min. Each session was recorded with participants’ consent, and analysis was conducted after each interview. Data collection continued until saturation was reached, with no new codes emerging. Data analysis

Directed (deductive) qualitative content analysis was performed following the method outlined by Hsieh and Shannon22. Each interview was transcribed verbatim immediately after the session. Concrete examples of students’ nutritional behaviors were identified and coded. The codes were then organized into a predefined matrix, which was categorized according to relevant themes. Codes that did not fit into the initial categories were grouped inductively to form new subcategories30. Throughout the analysis, the coding process was monitored and verified by coauthors and external qualitative research experts to ensure accuracy and manage overlap between similar concepts.

Accuracy of the findings (Rigor)

To ensure the rigor of the findings, we applied five criteria based on Johnson and Rasulova’s framework29: credibility, confirmability, dependability, transferability, and authenticity (Table 4).

Criteria Definitions29:

Credibility

Ensuring that findings reflect the true experiences and perspectives of the participants.

Confirmability

This demonstrates that the results are grounded in the data and free from researcher bias.

Dependability: Providing sufficient methodological detail to allow the study to be replicated.

Transferability

Offering detailed contextual information to help readers assess the applicability of findings to other settings.

Authenticity

Ensuring that the study captures the genuine experiences and realities of the participants.

Ethical consideration

Ethics approval and consent to participate The study obtained ethical approval from the Ethics Sub-committee at IR.MODARES.REC.1402.247 and conducted the study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2010); written informed consent was obtained from the participant(s) and their legal guardian(s).

Finding

Data collection for this study began in February 2024 and concluded in August 2024, encompassing a total of 28 interviews. These interviews were conducted in three distinct groups: adolescent girls aged 13–17 years (17 interviewees), parents of adolescent girls (8 interviewees), and stakeholders responsible for adolescent girls aged 13–17 years (3 interviewees). The interviews lasted between 28 and 55 min (with an average of 40 min). During the data analysis, 208 contextual codes emerged according to SCT, which were meticulously reviewed and categorized into six main categories and 15 subcategories (Table 5). Self-efficacy in nutritional behaviors

Self-efficacy in nutritional behaviors refers to adolescent girls’ belief in their ability to make and adhere to healthy dietary choices. This concept encompasses several aspects, which include the following:

Managing nutrition during stress

Stress can lead to changes in eating patterns, increasing the consumption of unhealthy foods or causing a lack of appetite, both of which directly impact physical and mental health.

“When I’m stressed, I just eat whatever is in front of me. I overeat—anything, whether it is healthy or unhealthy, it does not matter to me.” (P13, aged 15).

During a field observation conducted at one of the schools under study (FN3), it was noted:

“The students said, before an exam, eating something sweet feels truly good, as if it takes away the stress and opens up our minds.”

In contrast, some individuals lose their appetite during periods of stress and have no desire to eat.

“When I’m stressed, I cannot eat anything, not even breakfast. I don’t feel hungry at all to want to eat something.” (P2, aged 15).

Establishing positive dietary habits

Establishing positive dietary habits, such as eliminating fast foods that contain unhealthy fats and salt, reflects an individual’s ability to permanently and sustainably adhere to these patterns.

“I have managed to completely stop eating fast food for a while now, and I do not eat it anymore.” (P2, aged 15).

For example, one parent expressed a strategy for controlling overeating: “To control my daughter’s overeating, I use vegetables and salads to fill her stomach so she eats less.” (P18, parent).

Encouragement, commitment, and barriers to healthy eating

In regard to unhealthy food promotions, advertisements for unhealthy foods use psychological techniques such as appealing colors and emotional messaging to encourage people, especially children and adolescents, to consume these foods. “I try not to go out too much, so I do not eat those unhealthy snacks, but if I do go out and see them in stores, I’ll definitely buy them because they’re so attractive and tempting. It is all the advertisements’ fault—they make you feel hungry and drive you toward these unhealthy foods.” (P4, aged 16).

Tempting and consuming unhealthy snacks have profound effects on motivation, commitment, and barriers to healthy eating. These temptations can reduce the desire for healthy eating, weaken the commitment to maintaining a healthy diet, and increase the degree of psychological and environmental obstacles to healthy eating.

“Many times, I have tried not to eat unhealthy things, but I couldn’t resist. My strong liking for them and the temptation to eat them pull me toward them.” (P16, aged 15).

Another strategy to increase motivation and commitment to healthy eating is to create easy access to healthy foods at school, which can reduce practical barriers to healthy eating. One stakeholder noted:

“One of the strategies we implement at school is organizing a healthy eating festival. For instance, on certain days of the week, like Mondays and Wednesdays, we dedicate the first 15 minutes of the first class to a healthy breakfast or a unity lunch.” (P26, stakeholder).

Practicing and reproducing dietary habits

An individual’s ability to establish and maintain healthy eating patterns comes through consistent practice and repetition. With continuous practice, these behaviors become long-lasting habits and are automatically integrated into daily life. “For some time now, I have been trying to use foods with natural sugars, like dates, and I have cut out sweets and artificial sugars from my diet. It is become a habit for me.” (P11, aged 15).

On the other hand, the continuous consumption of unhealthy snacks has led to the development of unhealthy and persistent dietary patterns. A participant in the study (P28, stakeholder) expressed, “Unfortunately, in secondary schools, students are quite unruly. Not having breakfast and eating unhealthy snacks during breakfast time have become habits for them.”

Outcome expectations in nutritional behaviors

Physical outcomes (positive and negative consequences of behavior)

Physical outcomes in outcome expectations refer to the positive and negative impacts of nutritional behaviors on physical health. For example, consuming fruits and vegetables can increase energy levels, improve digestion, increase focus, and enhance mental performance. Collectively, these effects make a person feel lighter, more energetic, and productive in daily tasks:

“When I eat vegetables, I feel like it makes me feel good and more energetic” (P9, aged 13).

Enjoying the taste and experience of healthy foods can motivate individuals to choose healthier options. By trying new, delicious recipes, healthy eating becomes an enjoyable experience:

“To motivate my kids, I try to make the table more beautiful with colorful salads and vegetables and serve yogurt alongside their meals. That way, I encourage them to eat healthily” (P23, parent).

On the other hand, fast foods often contain high levels of unhealthy fats, which can lead to stomach discomfort and increased stomach acid, sometimes causing nausea:

“There was a time when I used to eat falafel, and I experienced digestive problems such as stomachaches and nausea. Once I stopped eating falafel, these symptoms disappeared. I realized it was the falafel since I used to buy and eat it every day” (P7, aged 17).

Furthermore, in a field observation conducted at a school involved in the study (FN1), the school health monitor mentioned that several students had experienced digestive issues after consuming falafel from local vendors.

Social approval and disapproval outcomes

This concept explores how opinions, expectations, and support from others can influence an individual’s nutritional behaviors. One such outcome is the influence of peers’ dietary habits on nutritional behavior. Adolescents tend to model their behaviors after their friends:

“Most of my friends who eat unhealthy foods have influenced me, and now I behave like them, meaning my diet is just like theirs” (P17, aged 14).

Social media platforms can act as a broader social environment, impacting nutritional patterns both positively and negatively. Exposure to advertisements for unhealthy food products and appealing content on these platforms can encourage poor food choices:

“I use social media a lot, and I’ve seen many advertisements for fast foods and ice creams, which have influenced me to choose those foods” (P15, aged 17).

Positive and negative self-evaluation

One positive form of self-evaluation in nutritional behaviors is successfully changing one’s dietary habits. The lack of negative experiences in making these changes reflects success in maintaining positive dietary habits: “Thus far, I have not had any unsuccessful experiences in changing my eating habits” (P8, aged 14).

Environmental factors in nutritional behaviors

Physical environment

The physical environment includes all material and physical factors in one’s living environment that can influence dietary behaviors and food choices. A lack of access to healthy foods at school pushes students to purchase and consume unhealthy snacks from nearby stores:

“My friend and I go to the store outside the school and buy chips, cake, puffs, and ice cream… we bring them back and eat them together” (P4, aged 16).

In contrast, having a healthy food kiosk at school that offers homemade, nutritious meals can promote healthy eating behaviors and improve students’ dietary quality by providing better access to fresh, nutritious options:

“Our school’s healthy food kiosk sold all kinds of healthy foods. Most of the students bought from it, and the homemade foods they prepared were preferred over chips and puffs” (P12, aged 17).

Another participant shared their experience: “When we sold homemade soup at the school kiosk, students loved it and said it helped them overcome the morning sluggishness and drowsiness” (P27, stakeholder).

A lack of health monitoring at school reduces students’ access to essential health and nutritional information. This situation can hinder the promotion of healthy behaviors and the ability to meet students’ health needs:

“I haven’t had many conversations with our health monitor because they’re only here one day a week, or they’re too busy and not around much” (P5, aged 17).

Social environment

The social environment encompasses the influences of family, school, friends, teachers, and social groups on an individual’s dietary behaviors. On the basis of participants’ experiences, adolescents, influenced by their peers and the desire for social acceptance, often align their dietary behaviors with those of their friends:

“I overeat, and I’m overweight, but one of my friends eats less, and her body is well shaped and fit. I was influenced by her and tried to reduce the amount of food I eat” (P3, aged 13).

Schools play a key role in shaping adolescents’ dietary behaviors by providing formal and informal education that helps develop healthy eating habits and increases nutritional awareness:

“I got nutritional information through the school, from my teachers, like my chemistry teacher, and once an outside expert came to explain things to us, and it raised our awareness” (P6, aged 17).

The social environment, through virtual networks, provides teenagers with diverse nutritional information that can influence their eating patterns:

“Before asking me about the nutritional properties of foods, my daughters look it up on Google and quickly find the information online” (P24, parent).

Self-regulation in nutritional behaviors

Goal setting in nutritional behaviors

Setting nutritional goals helps individuals enhance their ability to establish and achieve healthy eating targets. People with experience in this area are better able to define and pursue their goals. A parent shared how they set measurable goals for their children’s diet:

“I told my kids to stop eating unhealthy snacks and soda, and for example, only eat a handful of bread or eight spoons of rice. That’s how I set goals for them” (P18, parent).

One adolescent shared their experience:

“I’ve been on a diet for three months. For breakfast, I eat a matchbox-sized portion of cheese with a small piece of bread and a date. For lunch, I have a salad with boiled chicken breast, and for dinner, I eat one boiled egg without the yolk. This has helped me lose weight” (P10, aged 15).

These experiences indicate that goal setting in nutritional behaviors can be shaped through self-regulation, personal planning, or expert guidance. However, academic pressures and daily responsibilities can sometimes hinder adherence to dietary plans: “I’ve been truly busy, and that’s caused me to not always follow my diet plan, like with schoolwork and daily tasks” (P10, aged 15).

Self-control and self-monitoring in nutritional behaviors

Self-control in food choices, including reviewing the nutritional content of items, helps individuals reduce their intake of harmful substances and move closer to their dietary goals. This skill aids in avoiding impulsive purchases of unhealthy foods and improves dietary management:

“When I’m truly hungry, I try to buy the healthiest biscuit instead of fast food. For example, I choose biscuits without chocolate and with less sugar. I check the nutrition label and pick those with less salt and sugar” (P1, aged 16).

Parents can also foster self-control and self-monitoring skills in their teenagers by creating a supportive environment and providing necessary education to maintain physical well-being:

“To make sure my kids are physically healthy, I cook more homemade meals for them, wake them up earlier so they don’t miss meals, sign them up for gym classes, take them walking, and cut down on oil and salt in our food” (P25, parent).

Knowledge of nutritional behaviors

General nutritional knowledge

According to the participants, general nutritional knowledge refers to the information that most people have about healthy eating, which they gain from sources such as family, school, or media. A lack of familiarity with local foods indicates a gap in nutritional and cultural knowledge, leading people to prefer modern foods over traditional foods: “I’m not very familiar with local foods; we mostly use modern, ready-made foods” (P9, aged 13).

This lack of awareness can prevent people from enjoying the nutritional benefits of traditional foods, which might be healthier.

Specialized nutritional knowledge

Inadequate knowledge of food sources containing essential vitamins and minerals highlights the lack of information needed to make healthy food choices. While vitamin D and iron supplements are beneficial, not knowing about food sources rich in these nutrients can hinder efforts to create a balanced diet:

“I take vitamin D pills for my deficiency, but I’m not familiar with foods that contain vitamin D” (P9, aged 13).

Attitudes toward dietary behaviors

The concept of attitudes toward dietary behaviors refers to a set of perspectives, beliefs, feelings, and evaluations that individuals have regarding food and healthy eating.

Positive attitudes toward food

The participants expressed various perspectives on food attitudes, with the majority having a positive attitude toward the local foods of their region. When individuals find these foods tasty and culturally or traditionally valuable, they are more likely to consume them regularly. As one parent stated,

“I make a lot of our local foods, mostly seafood, for my children. I prepare them once or twice a week.” (P20, parents).

Mofatah, as a traditional local dish, represents the significance and diversity of local foods in a region. Owing to its special characteristics and cultural connections, it is very popular at special occasions and ceremonies and is considered a banquet dish. One parent commented:

“One of our local dishes is Mofatah (one of the traditional and popular Arabic dishes, especially prepared in Gulf countries such as Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Bahrain. This dish is typically served at large ceremonies and celebrations and consists of lamb or goat or fish, rice, and a variety of spices), which are considered banquet dishes. My children love it, and they are very happy when I make it.” (P21, parents).

Negative attitudes toward food

In terms of negative attitudes, individuals may find healthy foods unappetizing, which results in them avoiding their consumption. For example, the strong or distinctive smells of fish and shrimp can be unpleasant for some, leading to reluctance to consume such foods. As one participant explained, “Our local foods are mostly made with fish and shrimp. I don’t eat them at all because I hate the smell of fish. I just can’t eat it.” (P14, aged 17).

People often make their food choices on the basis of personal preferences and tastes, and even when they feel weak or hungry, they may refuse to eat if the food is not liked. One young participant mentioned, “If I don’t like a food, I won’t eat it, even if I’m starving.” (P2, aged 15).

Discussion

This study explored the nutritional behaviors of adolescent girls (aged 13–17), their parents, and school staff, identifying six key factors influencing adolescent girls’ dietary habits: self-efficacy, outcome expectations, the environment, self-regulation, knowledge, and attitudes about nutritional behaviors. Overall, the findings emphasize the importance of social, cultural, and cognitive factors in shaping healthy eating behaviors in adolescent girls. These behaviors are not only influenced by individual factors but also shaped by the physical and social environments of schools, families, and communities. To improve these behaviors, future interventions should be designed across these domains to ensure both acceptability and feasibility within the target community. According to the participants in this study, exam periods were identified as particularly stressful, primarily due to academic demands and heightened self-imposed expectations. This stress was commonly associated with disrupted eating patterns, particularly an increased tendency toward consuming high-calorie, low-nutrient foods. These findings align with previous research indicating that individuals exhibit varied responses to stress in terms of food intake—some increasing consumption, others decreasing, and a smaller proportion showing no change31,32. Consistent with our observations, prior studies have shown that stress often prompts a shift toward the consumption of energy-dense “comfort” foods, which are typically rich in fat and sugar33. Our participants similarly reported turning to snacks and fast food during periods of stress, even when not physically hungry. This behavioral pattern is supported by evidence from neurobiological research showing that such foods activate the brain’s reward circuitry, offering short-term emotional relief and pleasure, which may temporarily mitigate stress and negative mood states34. Together, these findings and the supporting literature underscore a well-documented connection between academic stress and maladaptive eating behaviors among adolescents.

In our study, self-efficacy in nutritional behavior emerged as a key factor influencing healthy dietary behaviors among adolescents. The participants who expressed confidence in their ability to make healthy food choices reported positive behaviors, such as reducing or eliminating the consumption of fast foods high in unhealthy fats and salt. This finding aligns with prior research demonstrating that higher self-efficacy is associated with healthier eating patterns and greater resistance to dietary temptations35–37. For example, Efthymiou et al.38. (2022) reported that Greek adolescents with stronger dietary self-efficacy reported better nutritional habits, supporting the view that belief in one’s ability significantly influences food-related decision-making. Moreover, our participants frequently cited family support as instrumental in fostering these healthy habits—a finding that is consistent with studies highlighting the role of the family environment in reinforcing adolescents’ self-efficacy and guiding long-term behavioral choices39,40. These findings underscore self-efficacy not only as an internal motivator but also as a construct shaped and sustained by external support systems, particularly the family.

Another key finding of this study was the institutionalization of unhealthy eating habits among adolescents. One participant noted that such behaviors had become deeply embedded in daily routines, reflecting a broader trend observed in school-based research. For example, Kshatri et al.41. (2022) reported that more than 70% of adolescents lacked healthy dietary habits, indicating the widespread nature of this issue. Our findings also highlighted the influence of outcome expectations—individuals’ beliefs about the potential results of their dietary behaviors—on nutritional choices. Adolescents who perceived tangible benefits from healthy eating, such as improved physical and mental well-being, were more likely to adopt and maintain healthier eating patterns. In contrast, those who held negative or unrealistic expectations were less motivated to pursue such behaviors. The participants in this study cited personal experiences, noting that the perceived benefits of consuming fruits and vegetables (e.g., increased energy and improved digestion) motivated them to increase their intake. Conversely, awareness of the adverse effects of fast food—such as digestive discomfort and the risk of chronic illness—discourages consumption. These insights align with findings from prior studies, which have demonstrated that positive outcome expectations are significant predictors of healthy dietary behaviors, whereas negative or distorted expectations can contribute to poor eating habits42,43,4. These findings suggest that shaping realistic and positive expectations may be a vital strategy for improving adolescent nutrition and reversing the normalization of unhealthy eating behaviors.

The environmental construct in this study highlights the complex and multifactorial influences on adolescent dietary behaviors, particularly within school and home settings. The participants frequently identified the physical food environment—such as the availability of healthy options within schools—and the surrounding foodscape, including the proximity of shops offering processed and low-nutrient foods, as major determinants of their eating habits. This aligns with previous research demonstrating that limited access to nutritious foods within educational settings often leads students to make less healthy dietary choices (Pooler et al., 2017)44. The participants in this study expressed concern about the widespread consumption of street foods (physical environment) and advertisements (social environment), particularly unhealthy options such as falafel, which are popular among adolescents in Iran. These foods are often prepared in unhygienic conditions, raising serious public health concerns. This perception is supported by existing research showing that street foods, while convenient and culturally significant, can pose significant health risks, including gastroenteritis and other foodborne illnesses, due to improper handling and poor sanitation (Oscar, 2020)45. In the Iranian context, items such as Sambouseh, sandwiches, and especially falafel are commonly sold by street vendors and have become staple choices in many urban areas. A study by Zadeh-Dabbagh et al. (2022)46. emphasized that such foods are not only widely consumed but also attract tourists to regions such as Khuzestan, highlighting their cultural and economic importance. However, the same study also raised concerns about inadequate hygiene standards in the preparation of these foods, which can expose consumers—particularly adolescents with more frequent and less discerning eating habits—to potential health risks. In terms of the social environment, our findings highlight the significant influence of food promotion on adolescents’ dietary behaviors, particularly through the pervasive marketing of unhealthy food products. The participants reported that exposure to advertisements—especially on social media—often created temptations and weakened their ability to maintain healthy eating habits. This observation is consistent with findings from a systematic review by McCarthy, de Vries, and Mackenbach (2022)47, which demonstrated that digital marketing strategies effectively drive the consumption and purchase of unhealthy foods and beverages among young audiences. Such promotions contribute to the normalization of poor dietary choices and increase both psychological and environmental barriers to healthy eating. To counteract these influences, the participants in our study suggested school-level initiatives such as healthy food festivals, breakfast events, and shared lunches, which they believed enhanced access to nutritious options and encouraged healthier eating norms. These strategies align with those of Jayasinghe and Hills (2023)48, who emphasize the importance of improving the availability of healthy foods in school settings as a practical way to reduce structural barriers. Moreover, the emphasis on inclusive school-based nutrition programs resonates with the broader discourse on nutrition equity. As argued by Strieter et al. (2022)49, achieving social justice in adolescent nutrition requires ensuring that all students, regardless of socioeconomic status, have equitable access to healthy, affordable food. By incorporating participatory and inclusive approaches, as observed in our study, schools can play a pivotal role in promoting both healthy eating behaviors and nutritional equity, thereby supporting long-term public health and social justice goals. The participants’ insights align with existing evidence, highlighting the need for public health interventions that respect cultural food practices while promoting food safety and healthier alternatives. This study contributes to the understanding of how environmental food exposures—such as the availability and quality of street food—influence adolescent dietary behaviors and health outcomes. It emphasizes the importance of comprehensive, multilevel strategies that address both individual and environmental determinants of healthy eating. By examining physical and social contexts, the findings support the call for structural reforms in school and community food systems to foster healthier eating habits among adolescents.

The findings of this study regarding self-regulation in nutritional behaviors—particularly in the domains of goal setting, self-control, and self-monitoring—reinforce and extend the literature emphasizing the critical role of individual agency in shaping healthy eating patterns. Goal setting emerged as a central mechanism, enabling adolescents and parents to structure and pursue specific dietary objectives, whether through self-devised plans or parental guidance. This is consistent with Bandura’s social cognitive theory, which identifies goal setting as a key self-regulatory process that facilitates behavioral change by enhancing personal agency and outcome expectations. Similar findings were reported by50, who noted that adolescents with clearly defined and personally relevant dietary goals demonstrated greater adherence to healthy eating behaviors. Similarly, a study by Silva et al. (2025)51 revealed that parental involvement in setting realistic and measurable dietary goals significantly improved children’s eating habits. However, our findings also highlight how external stressors—particularly academic demands—can undermine adolescents’ capacity to maintain their dietary routines, echoing the conclusions of Aynehchi et al. (2023), who emphasized the importance of integrating stress-coping strategies into nutritional interventions52. Furthermore, the emphasis on self-control and self-monitoring aligns with prior research suggesting that adolescents who engage in deliberate food choices, such as reading nutrition labels and avoiding high-fat or high-sugar foods, are more likely to meet their dietary goals53. Our study adds to this body of knowledge by showcasing how self-regulatory skills are not only individually driven but also supported by parental strategies that promote a health-conscious environment. For example, parental efforts to modify the home food environment and establish routines—such as preparing home-cooked meals and enrolling children in physical activity—echo the findings of Black et al. (2023), who highlighted the efficacy of family-based interventions in promoting sustained dietary improvements among youth54. Collectively, these insights underscore the multifaceted nature of self-regulation in nutritional behavior, pointing to the need for comprehensive interventions that target both individual and environmental determinants to support healthy eating among adolescents.

The finding that participants lacked knowledge about food sources containing essential vitamins and minerals, such as vitamin D and iron (e.g., “I take vitamin D pills for my deficiency, but I’m not familiar with foods that contain vitamin D”), reveals a critical gap in specialized nutritional knowledge. This deficiency limits the ability to make informed dietary choices, a key behavioral component of SCT55. Adequate knowledge of nutrient-rich food sources is essential for preventing deficiencies, particularly during adolescence, a period of rapid growth and development56. For example, vitamin D and iron deficiencies are prevalent among adolescent girls globally and are associated with adverse health outcomes, including impaired bone health and anemia57,58. Similarly, a study in Ghana revealed that despite some adolescents having acceptable knowledge levels about anemia, there was a general deficit in understanding the causes, consequences, and management of iron deficiency59. These findings underscore the critical need for targeted nutrition education to bridge knowledge gaps and promote informed dietary choices among adolescent girls.

The positive attitudes toward local foods, particularly culturally significant dishes such as Mofatah (e.g., “My children love it, and they are very happy when I make it” [P21, parent]), demonstrate how affective and cultural factors shape dietary behaviors within SCT’s personal domain55. Positive attitudes, rooted in taste preferences and cultural value, increase the likelihood of consuming traditional foods, which are often nutrient dense and aligned with regional dietary patterns60. This finding aligns with research indicating that positive food attitudes, reinforced by family traditions and cultural practices, promote healthier eating behaviors among adolescents61. The popularity of Mofatah at ceremonies further suggests that social and cultural reinforcement can strengthen positive attitudes, an environmental influence in SCT. Public health interventions should leverage these cultural assets by promoting traditional foods through community events or school programs, fostering positive attitudes and enhancing dietary adherence.

Conclusion

This study offers valuable insights into the complex interplay of individual, social, and environmental factors that shape the dietary behaviors of female adolescents. This study highlights how challenges such as exam-related stress, limited access to healthy foods, and influential social environments hinder the maintenance of healthy eating habits. Core constructs such as self-efficacy, parental involvement, and supportive school settings emerged as critical in promoting healthier choices. While the findings provide rich, theory-informed perspectives, the study’s qualitative design and purposive sampling of 28 participants from 12 counties within a single Iranian province may limit the transferability of findings to other settings or populations. Nonetheless, these insights carry important practical implications: they can guide the design of school- and community-based nutritional interventions, inform policymakers aiming to create supportive food environments, and help educators and parents strengthen adolescents’ knowledge, attitudes, and confidence in making healthier dietary choices. By addressing contextual and behavioral dimensions together, stakeholders can develop multifaceted strategies to foster sustainable dietary behavior change among adolescent girls.
