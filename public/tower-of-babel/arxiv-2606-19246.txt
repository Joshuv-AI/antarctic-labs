Markovian actions are Anosov-like
Ioannis Iakovoglou

Abstract
. Motivated by the introduction of Anosov-like actions by Thomas Barthelmé, Steven Frankel, and Kathryn Mann, in this paper we study group actions on the plane that preserve a pair of transverse singular foliations, as well as a strong Markovian family—namely, a collection of rectangles serving as an analogue of a Markov partition for group actions. We prove that any such action is Anosov-like. In particular, the existence of a strong Markovian family constrains the behavior of the invariant bifoliation and endows the action with characteristic features of hyperbolic dynamics, such as the existence of infinitely many elements with fixed points.

The main result in this paper shows that:

Theorem 1.5
.

Every orientation preserving strong Markovian action on the plane is Anosov-like.

Our motivation for introducing the class of strong Markovian actions is twofold. First, in their recent work (see
[
BaFeMa
]
), T.Barthelmé, S.Fenley and K.Mann answer Question

in the case of transversally orientable pseudo-Anosov flows. More specifically, they provide a necessary and sufficient condition for an Anosov-like action to arise from a transversally orientable pseudo-Anosov flow and they also develop techniques allowing to reconstruct transversally orientable pseudo-Anosov flows from Anosov-like actions. Building on the previous techniques, together with F.Béguin, we generalize the previous results and we show in forthcoming work, that an orientation-preserving action on the plane arises from a pseudo-Anosov flow if and only if it is a strong Markovian action. Hence, preserving a strong Markovian family is the essential property separating general group actions on the plane preserving some bifoliation from actions that arise from pseudo-Anosov flows in dimension 3.

Second, because of the combinatorial nature of their definition, strong Markovian actions on the plane form a subclass of Anosov-like actions that is more amenable to a complete classification than general Anosov-like actions. In fact, in our previous work in

By construction, each

. By Item (5) of Proposition

-leaf of
R_{i}
. Moreover, thanks to the Markovian intersection axiom, we have that

. Consider

.

Case 1:

Assume that there exists a first element

that does not belong in

. Consider

containing

, intersecting non-trivially

and satisfying

(such a rectangle exists thanks to the strong finite return time axiom). By the definition of

is sufficiently big, the rectangle

is very thin in the

and

Case 2:

and

is a closed subset of

Denote by

the extremity of

which is not

. In this case, we have that there exists

such that

for every

. Thanks to the finiteness axiom, after possibly passing to a subsequence, assume that all the

are contained in the same
\rho
-orbit of rectangles. Take

. Passing to a further subsequence if necessary, we can assume that

is not contained in the
\mathcal{F}^{u}
-boundary of

; therefore,

.

Similarly to Case 1, we have that

lies in the interior of
\rho(g_{0})([w_{0},c_{0}^{\prime}]^{s})
. Therefore, thanks to Claims 1 and 2, the negative
\mathcal{F}^{u}
-separatrix of

intersects

and

intersects the interior of

. Since

is trivially bifoliated and

\mathcal{F}^{u}(p_{0})=\mathcal{F}^{u}(w_{1})

.

Our previous arguments imply that

and thus that
\rho(g_{0})
preserves
\mathcal{F}^{u}(p_{0})
, which finishes the proof of Claim 3. ∎

We are now ready to show that

is periodic, thereby completing the argument for Lemma
3.14
. Take
g\in\text{Stab}_{\rho}(\mathcal{F}^{u}(p_{0}))-\{e\}
such that
\rho(g)(\mathcal{F}_{-}^{u}(p_{0}))\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{-}^{u}(p_{0})
. Such a

exists, thanks to Claim 2 and Lemma
3.11
. By the definition of
g
, we have that

\underset{y\in\rho(g)(\mathcal{F}_{-}^{u}(p_{0}))}{\bigcup}\mathcal{F}^{s}(y)\subset\underset{y\in\mathcal{F}_{-}^{u}(p_{0})}{\bigcup}\mathcal{F}^{s}(y)

This implies that the
\mathcal{F}^{s}
-saturated neighborhoods of

\rho(g)(l)
always intersect along some leaf in
\underset{y\in\mathcal{F}_{-}^{u}(p_{0})}{\bigcup}\mathcal{F}^{s}(y)
; thus
\rho(g)(l)=l
or
\rho(g)(l)
is non separated from
l
.

Next, observe that the facts that
\rho(g)
is orientation preserving and that

is positively oriented imply that the segment
[\rho(g)(x_{0}),\rho(g)(p_{0})]^{s}
is also positively oriented. Consequently, for every point

sufficiently close to

, the following hold:

(1)

by Claim 1,
\mathcal{F}^{u}(z)\cap l\neq\emptyset
, and therefore
\mathcal{F}^{u}(\rho(g)(z))\cap\rho(g)(l)\neq\emptyset

(2)

since
\rho(g)(p_{0})\in\mathcal{F}^{u}_{-}(p_{0})\subset\mathfrak{R}

\mathcal{F}^{u}(\rho(g)(z))\cap[x_{0},p_{0})^{s}\neq\emptyset

(3)

since
\mathcal{F}^{u}(\rho(g)(z))\cap[x_{0},p_{0})^{s}\neq\emptyset

\mathcal{F}^{u}(\rho(g)(z))\cap l\neq\emptyset

The fact that
\mathcal{F}^{u}(\rho(g)(z))

implies, by Item (4) of Lemma

\rho(g)(l)
cannot be distinct from

, which finishes the proof of the lemma.

In this final section of this paper, we prove that

Lemma 4.1
.

There does not exist an embedding
\phi:(0,1)^{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}
such that:

(1)

\phi((0,1)\times\{t\})

\mathcal{F}^{s}
-segment in
\mathcal{P}

(2)

-segment in

for every

(3)

-leaf

(4)

as

\mathcal{F}^{u}
-segments

-leaves

Naturally, the same result holds if we change the roles of

\mathcal{F}^{u}

.

By our definition of

\mathcal{F}^{s}
and

Thanks to Lemma

p_{\infty}\in\underset{k>L_{0}}{\cap}R_{k}

Moreover, by the expansivity axiom and our definition of the sequence
(R_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,

\underset{k>L_{0}}{\cap}R_{k}\subset l^{s}_{-}

which would imply that
p_{\infty}\in l^{s}_{-}
and thus that
l^{s}_{-}
is periodic, contradicting the first part of our proof. We can therefore assume without any loss of generality that
p_{K,L}\neq p_{\infty}
.

Finally, since both
p_{K,L}
and
p_{\infty}
are fixed by
\rho(g_{K,L})
and are contained in the trivially bifoliated region
\rho(g_{L})(U)
, we have that
\rho(g_{K,L})
also fixes the unique point of intersection of
\mathcal{F}^{u}(p_{\infty})
and
\mathcal{F}^{s}(p_{K,L})
inside
\rho(g_{L})(U)
. This contradicts Lemma
3.10
and finishes the proof of Claim 5.
