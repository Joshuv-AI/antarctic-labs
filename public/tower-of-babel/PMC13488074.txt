Molecular mechanisms and recent advances in cellular senescence
Authors: Ashaq Hussain, Marc Tatar, Rujun Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSenescence is an adaptive cellular mechanism initiated in response to diversified intrinsic and extrinsic stress stimuli, that lead to altered cellular and pathophysiological state. In this article we will review the comprehensive role of variable factors like oxidative stress, telomere attrition and oncogene activation in the initiation of senescence. Cells have a robust Redox system in place to counteract the effects of ROS/RNS mediated cell organelles and macromolecular damage to prevent senescence. The morphological, physiological and metabolic changes associated with progression of senescence and their molecular regulation at transcriptional and posttranscriptional levels has also been highlighted. The signaling pathways like p53/p21 and p16/pRB governing the senescence associated proliferative arrest and other complex cellular mechanisms that modulate the development and regulation of senescence associated secretory phenotype has been discussed in detail.Keywords: Senescence, Cell cycle arrest, DNA damage, Transcription, Lysosome, ROS, Inflammation, SASP

Introduction

Senescence was originally recognized as irreversible and nonresponsive arrest of cell cycle in fibroblasts due to limited in vitro proliferative capacity commonly referred to as Hayflick limit [1, 2]. Senescence is a dynamic and multidimensional cellular phenomenon, during which the characteristics of the affected cell continually change and evolve, as shaped by extrinsic and intrinsic signals in a context-dependent manner [3, 4]. Senescence initiates as non-responsive cell cycle arrest, progresses through diverse functional, structural and metabolic changes that develop into stable senescent phenotype. Senescent cells have distinct morphological, unique functional characteristics and molecular signatures that differentiate them from other non-dividing or terminally differentiated/developed cells [5–10]. Senescent cells are viable and their unique attributes include irreversibly arrested cell cycle, chromatin remodeling, resistance to apoptosis, damaged cellular organelles, dysregulated metabolism, enhanced lysosomal activity, and global changes in gene expression that play a crucial role in development of senescence associated secretory phenotype [11–18].

Stress induced senescence is triggered by a complex array of stresses and developmental signals that may be intrinsic or extrinsic in nature [4]. The factors that induce senescence, but not limited to, include oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species, DNA damage, telomere shortening/uncapping or damage, exposure to radiation, chemotherapeutic intervention, nutrient deficiency, oncogene activation, epigenetic modification, chromatin reorganization, loss of pro-apoptotic genes, mitochondrial damage and dysfunction [19–26]. Different forms of senescence are named after the inducing stimuli like, ROS or oxidative stress induced senescence, Oncogene induced senescence (OIS), mitochondrial damage induced senescence and therapeutic intervention related senescence [4, 20, 22, 27, 28].

This article reviews in detail, the main factors that provide stimulus for initiation of senescence and different cellular processes that are key to changes in senescent cells at morphological, functional and metabolic levels. We will also discuss in detail the complex molecular mechanisms that govern the cell cycle arrest, transcriptional and posttranscriptional control of senescence and also highlight the signaling pathways that regulate the development of senescence associated secretory phenotype.

Senescence in patho-physiology and homeostasis

Disparate stimuli induce senescence via different means. The cellular response to senescence inducing stimuli depends on cell type, duration, nature and intensity of the stimuli [4, 29]. Based on the nature of the senescence inducing stimuli, progression of senescence and attainment of a stabilized senescent phenotype, senescence has been classified into acute and chronic senescence. Stress mediated organelle damage and molecular dysfunction are hall marks of both acute and chronic senescence. Acute senescence is mediated by short term stress that originates from well-coordinated, and programmed biological process, often targeting a particular type of cell/s to ensure physiological homeostasis, modulated immune activity, wound healing or tissue remodeling. Acute senescence may also originate from injury or infection and results in transient expression of senescence related biomarkers or temporary change in cell function. Chronic senescence is induced due to persistent stress, that leads to sustained alterations in cell function, persistent biomarker display and accumulation of senescent cells, that have a detrimental effect on the ageing, age associated metabolic dysfunctions and pathologies [3, 9, 30–33] (Fig. 1).

Senescent cells generally communicate with their neighboring cells through the secretion of inflammatory and other biologically active components called senescence associated secretory phenotype. In acute form or beneficial mode, the SASP is part of precisely regulated cellular process induced in response to stress like oncogene activation or DNA damage. The SASP components play a key role in recruitment of NK cells and macrophages, that facilitate in eliminating the deleterious senescent or precancerous cells. These bioactive components also promote tissue regeneration, suppress tumour initiation by promoting growth arrest and enhancing the immune surveillance. In chronic form, the SASP signaling becomes persistent. In aged tissues or individuals, senescence of immune system results in reduced clearance of senescent cells and subsequent accumulation of senescent cells. The SASP shifts into self-inducing or feed forward loop to drive persistent inflammaging, disruption of tissue structure, tumor initiation and spread of senescence to nearby tissues refered to as bystander effect [34–37].

Signals that differ in nature and origin induce different forms of senescence, reflecting the crucial role of this biological phenomenon in various physiological functions and pathological manifestations. Both replicative and developmentally programmed senescence occur during ageing and embryogenesis, respectively, as part of cellular homeostasis [3, 38]. Pathological stress that arises from diabetes, cancer, anti-neoplastic therapeutic intervention and radiation exposure also lead to different forms of senescence [39–41]. Senescence plays a crucial role in different physiological processes that include embryogenesis [41, 42], tissue repair, wound healing [43], pancreatic βeta cell function [44, 45], tissue remodeling [46], maintenance of tissue microenvironment and homeostasis [47]. Senescence also makes important contributions towards ageing and development of cancer as both the processes are deeply impacted by accumulation of senescent cells. Biomarkers of senescence are detectable in precancerous lesions; however, they are hardly detectable once the cell progresses to a malignant state. Senescence associated irreversible proliferative arrest in cells with “damaged DNA, activated oncogenes or mutated tumor suppressor genes” acts as barrier to initiation of cancer by preventing these precancerous cells with deleterious DNA mutations from progressing to a transformed state. Senescent cells through their SASP, attract the immune cells to facilitate their clearance, however, the SASP mediated inflammatory microenvironment can also facilitate tumorigenesis. Studies with role of biologically relevant small molecules in senescence have confirmed that like normal cells, transformed cells also enter into a senescent state and there are no separate biological markers of senescence in normal and cancer cells [48, 49].

Chronological age leads to accumulation of senescent cells in different tissues, similarly different stress signals lead to increased number of senescent cells in various tissues that accelerate the biological ageing of the organism [1, 2, 4, 50]. Senescent cells release inflammatory cytokines that lead to inflammation and age related metabolic/ physiological dysfunction. Age related senescence of stem cells negatively impacts tissue regeneration, repair, and homeostasis [51]. Convincing evidence about the contribution of senescence towards ageing and age-related disease came from investigations with transgenic animal models that made it possible to detect and identify the senescent cells in age related tissue dysfunction and disease [9, 50, 52, 53]. Similarly, the clearance of senescent cells by therapeutic intervention improved the physiological parameters and extended the life span in mice models of senescence [9, 53–55]. The important domains of senescence research are identification of senescent cells through biomarkers and elimination of these cells in a highly specific manner to improve health and longevity. Normal Cell cultures (2D) offer a simple platform for induction of stress, validation of biomarkers and screening of anti-senescence drugs [30]. Similarly, 3D cell cultures mimic the in vivo three-dimensional arrangement of cells, displays improved interaction between cells and better distribution of nutrients, however they also lack the complexity and diversity of the in vivo conditions. A leap in the development of advanced in vitro systems was the stem cell and iPSC derived organoids. The development of organoids provided a robust platform for investigating the senescence or age-related degenerative disorders. Brain organoids were valuable in studying the blood brain barrier and deciphering the molecular mechanisms that govern the initiation and progression of Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases. Experiments with cell cultures provided deep insights about the morphological changes in senescent cells, identification/validation of biomarkers and screening of anti-senescence drugs [56–58]. The breakthrough in senescence research came from the in vivo studies with mouse models of ageing and other transgenic mice generated for investigating the senescence, ageing and ageing related pathologies [59]. In vivo detection of senescent cells and senolytic drug mediated elimination of senescent cells were two important accomplishments in senescent research. Accelerated ageing mice models, that include progeroid mice, non progeroid mice like Klotho deficient and fibroblast growth factor null mice have been desirable for studying ageing associated pathologies. Mice with accelerated ageing display early onset of old age related organ dysfunction, osteoporosis, cardio-vascular complications, muscle wasting, decline in cognitive functions, kyphosis and calcifications [60]. Similarly, investigations with slow ageing mice models (Ames dwarf mice and Snell dwarf mice) have provided valuable insights into diverse ageing related signaling pathways, pathologies, validation of biomarkers and development of therapeutic interventions [61–63]. Experiments with transgenic mice models like p16, p21 or p19 were crucial for in vivo detection of senescent cells, progression of senescence in different tissues and more importantly the ablation or elimination of these senescent cells. Cloning of luciferase protein and toxins like diphtheria toxin under p16 or p21 protein, aids both in detection and elimination of senescent cells ([53, 64–66].

In vitro and in vivo screening of senolytic compounds provided a wealth of information regarding their potential as therapeutic agents, subsequently forming the corner stone of pre-clinical senescence research. Some examples regarding the therapeutic effects of senolytic drugs are as (a) Curcumin is a naturally occurring compound with anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. Administration of curcumin in mice on high fat diet, leads to improvement in age associated vascular changes [67]. Curcumin blocks binding of the transcription factors like NF-κB to promoters of IL-6, IL-1α, IL-1β and TNF-α, thereby reducing the systemic inflammatory milieu [68]. Treatment of human origin inter-vertebral disc senescent cells with curcumin results in reduced inflammation through suppressed expression of SASP components, increased number of apoptotic cells and improved matrix synthesis. and their expedited elimination through apoptosis [69]. (b). Dasatinib, is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor exerts its senolytic effects against human origin senescent preadipocytes and HUVECs by suppressing the expression of anti-apoptotic protein PAI-2 [70]. Administration of Dasatinib to old mice results in mitigation of ageing associated metabolic dysfunction like bone loss and loss of physical activity. Besides complementing each other’s activity, intermittent co-administration of dasatinib and quercetin into senescent cell transplanted young mice and naturally aged mice resulted in mitigation of physical dysfunction, increased longevity and post-operative survival [55]. D+Q treatment of old donor mice results in elimination of senescent cells and reduction in cell free mt-DNA, a key factor for inflammaging and dysregulated activation of CD4+ T-cell subsets, that play an essential role in development of organ rejection [71, 72]. (c). Among the Bcl-2 family inhibitors, Navitoclax exhibits its senolytic effects by binding to the inhibitory domain of the Bcl-2, Bcl-xl and Bcl-w, thus inducing the apoptosis [71–73]. Besides being capable of inducing the senolysis of diverse senescent cells (HUVEC, IMR-90) administration of Navitoclax to irradiated mice results in ablation of senescent bone marrow cells and senescent hematopoietic stem cells [347]. Similarly other senolytic compounds like Hsp90 inhibitors, inhibitors of MDM2/P53 interaction, p53 binding inhibitors and other naturally occurring alkaloids or flavonoids have displayed potent senolytic activity. Many of the senolytic compounds are in different stages of clinical trials. These include trials with D+Q senolytic cocktail in patients with osteoporosis, Diabetes with chronic kidney dysfunction, whereas fisetin was evaluated in patients suffering from osteoarthritis [67, 74–76].

Molecular insights into origin of senescence

Diverse factors exert effects of variable extents on the initiation and progression of senescence. The notable factors include oxidative stress, telomere shortening or damage, oncogene activatio, DNA damage and SARS CoV-2 infection. These factors exert their effects in a diversified form by engaging distinct signaling pathways to induce senescence.

Oxidative stress and senescence

A cell experiences oxidative stress, when it is exposed to excessive amounts of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS and NOS), that overwhelm the antioxidant capacity of the cell, resulting in damage to cell organelles and macromolecules, ultimately contributing to ageing and associated pathologies (Fig. 2). Most of these oxidants originate from metabolic processes such as oxidative phosphorylation, lipid oxidation and protein metabolism [77]. Recent studies with bone marrow derived mesenchymal cells have revealed that treatment with D-Galactose induces oxidative stress in BDSCs through generation of ROS especially hydrogen peroxide. Treatment of BMSCs with exogenous NAD+ increases the intracellular levels of NAD+ and protects the cell from D-Gal induced senescence by reducing the ROS levels. NAD+ reduces senescence in BMSCs by enhancing expression of Sirtuin 1, whereas RNA interference mediated downregulation of Sirtuin 1 abolishes the beneficial effects of exogenous NAD+. Exogenous NAD+ also reduces expression of both poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 and β-Gal activity [78, 79]. Treatment of human dental pulp cells (hDPCs) with FK866 reduces both hydrogen peroxide mediated senescence and telomeric damage. FK866 is a non-competitive inhibitor of Visfatin (pre-B cell colony enhancement factor) and acts by inhibiting NADH depletion, reduces generation of ROS and also suppresses expression of SASP factors by blocking NF-κB signaling pathways [80, 81].

To study the role of bicyclic mono terpenoid camphorquinone (2,3-bornanedione) in oxidative stress mediated senescence, experiments with hydrogen peroxide treated bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells and D-Gal treated mice revealed that CQ alleviates senescence markers (p21, p53 and p16) in both hBMMSCs and heart tissue harvested from D-Gal treated mice. The CQ achieves all these effects by activating the autophagy via upregulation of AMPK/SIRT1 signaling pathway [82]. Studies with fibroblasts have revealed that overexpression of Arginase 2 promoted the hydrogen peroxide induced senescence in fibroblasts, whereas knockdown of the Arginase 2 suppresses the same. Heterologous expression of miR-1299 silences arginase by targeting its 3' UTR mRNA and mitigates the hydrogen peroxide mediated fibroblast senescence via activation of autophagy [83]. Overall, the process of autophagy reduces the effects of oxidative stress by removing the damaged organelles and macromolecules [79]. Dysregulated autophagy in keratinocytes causes excessive production of ROS, DNA damage, aberrated lipid metabolism and consequent induction of senescence [96]. Experiments with fibroblasts (MRC-5) have revealed that lactose is capable of inducing senescence, however enhanced cellular levels of ROS and concomitantly decreased levels of GSH and SOD were indictive of the intense oxidative stress inside cells. Subsequent experiments revealed the involvement of Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway in lactose induced senescence, whereas the antioxidant N-Acetyl-L-cysteine was capable of successfully alleviating the lactose induced senescence [97]. Studies with mouse kidney cells confirmed that, accumulation of fumarate due to loss of fumarate hydratase leads to oxidative stress mediated senescence by formation of succinic GSH. Succination of GSH due to inactive FH or exogenous fumarate leads to production of ROS, reduced GSH/GSSG ratio and induction of oxidative stress mediated senescence [98].

Redox state of a cell is governed by balance between the production of oxidates and anti-oxidant system (Fig. 2). Mammalian cells have highly developed and evolved anti-oxidant system that is robust enough to restore the redox balance of the cell under challenging conditions to prevent damage to cellular organelles and biomolecules. The membrane protein Caveolin 1 suppresses the functioning of antioxidant enzyme thioredoxin reductase, thus enhancing generation of ROS and induction of senescence. Thioredoxin is a redox regulatory protein that modulates the cellular redox state by reducing the oxidized proteins. The protein acts directly as anti-oxidant and is also cofactor for various enzymes and transcription factors. Thioredoxin activates transcription factors like p53 and NF-κB by reducing their active site cysteine residues. Similarly, Thioredoxin inhibits pro apoptotic effectors like Caspase 3, 9 and also influences protein oligomerization by reducing their active site cysteine residues [45, 99, 100]. Microbiome derived hydrogen and other metabolites like short chain to long chain fatty acids, lipopolysaccharides, tryptophan metabolites and polyphenolic compounds alleviate the oxidative stress induced senescence by down regulating the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, reducing production of ROS and increasing antioxidant enzyme activity [101, 102]. Studies with senescent endothelial cells have revealed that loss of anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 results in disruption of redox homeostasis, however the anti-oxidant regulator protein thioredoxin upregulates the expression of Bcl-2 to restore redox homeostasis and prolong cell survival under stress [103]. Redox balance in cell is also crucial to maintaining the integrity of telomers, as telomere shortening and attrition interferes with faithful DNA replication and consequently result in induction of replicative senescence [104]. Thioredoxin also impedes the activation of Ras oncogenes by suppressing its farnesylation and membrane localization to maintain cellular homeostasis [99, 105].

Recent studies have confirmed a complex relationship between oxidative stress and autophagy, however the interaction is poorly understood and looks less dogmatic. Autophagy acts as a secondary protective mechanism responsible for removing damaged cell organelles and worn-out macromolecules to mitigate the intensity of trigger and restore homeostasis [1, 2]. The interplay between the ROS and autophagy is mediated by diverse signaling pathways and molecules that are sensitive to redox status of the cell. Elevated levels of ROS stimulate autophagy by suppressing the PI3K-Akt-mTOR signaling pathway, which modulates the function of mTORC1, a potent inhibitor of autophagy [3, 4]. mTORC1 plays a central role in regulation of cellular metabolism, proliferation, autophagy and apoptosis. The protein plays a key role in regulation of autophagy, and its activation results in inhibition of the autophagic process [84]. AMPK modulates the energy status of cell by regulating diverse metabolic pathways [85]. Under hypoxic conditions HIF is crucial for inducing the AMPK [86], AMPK activates autophagy by suppressing mTORC1 particularly under nutrient starvation. AMPK rapidly suppresses the mTORC1 signaling by phosphorylating tuberous sclerosis protein 2 (TSC2) and also by phosphorylating mTOR binding Raptor protein at two important serine residues (Ser722 and Ser792) [87]. The protein plays a key role in regulation of autophagy where its activation results in inhibition of the autophagy [84]. Fibroblast growth factor, insulin, VEGF and angiopoietin initiate the activation of P13K, that in turn activates Akt, a potent upstream activator of mTORC1, that ultimately results in downregulation of autophagy. Availability of amino acids, oxygen, growth factors and favorable environmental cues positively enhance the activity of mTORC1 to maintain cellular homeostasis and represses autophagy, whereas starving conditions, presence of ROS and damaged cell organelles activate the autophagy and inhibit the activity of mTORC1 [3, 4, 84, 88]. Beclin-1, Bcl-2, and PI3K signaling pathways play a crucial role in regulation of autophagy. Production of ROS leads to dissociation of Beclin-1-Bcl-2 complex, allowing the binding of Beclin-1 to P13K to enhance its activity that in-turn leads to activation of autophagy. Beclin-1 also positively interacts with Death associated Protein kinase (DAPK), to promote autophagy, similarly localization of DRP-1to autophagic vesicles suggests their involvement in process of autophagy [5]. Beclin 1 also interacts with diverse cofactors to regulate the function of Vps-34 and drive the formation of autophagy inducing complex Beclin 1-Vps34-Vps15 [6].

ROS upregulates autophagy through MAPK signaling pathway. Diverse components of MAPK pathway include P38, ERK and c-Jun, are activated and exert profound effect on the autophagy. The MAPK pathway regulates the expression of different transcription factors like FoxO and NF-κB, that regulate the expression of autophagy associated genes [89, 90]. Studies with mouse mesenchymal stem cells have revealed that, ROS transiently activates JNK signaling cascade that cross talks with Beclin 1 pathway to counter apoptosis and induce autophagy, however high levels of ROS lead to constant stimulation of JNK pathway that ultimately results into induction of apoptosis [91–93].

Telomere shortening and DNA damage response

Telomers are regions of repetitive TTAGGG nucleotide sequences at terminals of chromosome, that are shielded by binding of sequence specific proteins to form a complex called Shelterin that protects the ends of the chromosome from being recognized as damaged DNA by surveillance mechanisms of the cell. Sheltrin complex is composed of three subunits TRF1, TRF2 and POT1 that recognize and bind to the telomere specific repetitive sequences aided by three interconnecting proteins TIN2, TPP1, and Rap1 [94]. Shortening of telomers due to loss of telomerase activity acts as warning signal that culminates in proliferative arrest and senescence. Similarly telomeric damage due to oxidative radicles, nuclease activity or exonucleolytic processing or loss of telomere structure also leads to short telomers that initiate the senescent response in a cell [9, 95, 96]. Studies have confirmed that the length of telomers may not be the sole factor for a cell to initiate the entry into senescence as many senescent cells have telomers of 5–10 kb in size, whereas many actively dividing cancer cells have ultra short telomers [97, 98]. On the other hand overexpression of telomerase suppresses the erosion of telomers and allows a cell to divide indefinitely, on the opposite side expression of telomeric structural protein TRF2 results in shortening of telomere length without producing any noticeable effect on the initiation of senescence [99]. Short telomers result in activation of ATM (Ataxia Telangiectasia Mutated) mediated DNA damage response and initiation of senescence [100]. Studies with senescent fibroblasts have confirmed that discrete nuclear foci of phosphorylated histone H2AX that act as markers of double strand breaks tend to accumulate at the sites of telomeric lesions. These H2AX sites recruit and act as scaffold for the assembly of DNA repair proteins like NBS1, 53BP1 and MDC1 [100].

Studies with senescent fibroblasts have demonstrated that loss of Sheltrin association with telomers is also in close agreement with persistent displacement of the Sheltrin complexes from short telomers. Fibroblasts devoid of telomerase have elongated G2 phase of cell cycle and absence of telomerase is also associated with the activation of ATR/ATM dependent DNA damage signaling. Similarly, deletion of TRF1 or TRF2 or overexpression of nonfunctional mutants confirmed that telomer uncapping also results in activation of ATM dependent DNA damage signaling, NHEJ-dependent fusion of telomere extremities and cell cycle arrest in G2 phase [101]. Exposure of telomeric ends to NHEJ pathway results in enhanced end to end fusion in sister chromatids rather than inter chromosomal end to end fusion. Short telomers have length and TRF2 protein sufficient to prevent end to end fusion of chromosomes, but not sufficient to prevent telomeric activation of ATM pathway [9, 101–103]. Extremely shortened telomers are unable to fold into looped conformation after replication to ensure that telomere ends are not mistakenly recognized as breaks in DNA. Below a threshold telomere length, short telomers do not have enough length to get organized into and form stabilized t-loops. Similarly, loss of guanine rich single stranded overhangs or G-Tails in senescent cells represses the formation of t-loops [104]. Overexpression of telomerase prevents loss of overhangs; however, a continuous loss of overhangs is observed in cells that avoid entry into senescence by suppressing the p53 and RB signaling pathways, reflecting that overhangs are lost due to progressive cell divisions. The loss of telomere structure rather than telomere length is the reason for the cells to undergo senescence [105]. Similarly, over-expression of telomeric structural protein TRF2 promotes shortening of telomers without inducing senescence and also prevents end to end fusion of chromosomes, thereby confirming that replicative senescence is mainly induced by loss of telomere structure rather than the loss of telomeric DNA [99]. Progressive shortening of telomers leads to decreased number of t-loops due to diminished binding of TRF2 that is important for organization of stable and functional t-loops after DNA replication. As a result, the cell cycle stalls at G2 phase to give sufficient time to cellular machinery for re-establishment of stable t-loops post replication, so that the cells may proceed with cell division after achieving proper telomere protective state to prevent telomere end or chromatid fusion [9]. A cell must acquire the threshold number of dysfunctional telomers to generate a signal strong enough to activate DDR signaling and p53 mediated cell cycle exit in G2. Recent studies have suggested that a cell may proceed to mitosis despite having H2AX marked [4, 5] dysfunctional telomeric structures. During cell cycle, G2/M transition checkpoint recognizes 10-20 breaks in DNA, so the cells with low number of DNA breaks can enter into mitosis. Progression of cells with damaged DNA and fused chromosomes into mitosis results in generation of micronuclei during cytokinesis, however, additional DNA breaks and accumulation of dysfunctional telomeric structures leads to proliferative arrest of daughter cells in G1 phase of cell cycle. [9, 106–109].

Oxidative stress and telomeric attrition

In vivo studies with skin fibroblasts, mouse liver and gut, have revealed that association of DDR proteins with telomers is indictive of age associated telomere dysfunction [56, 57]. Accumulation of H2AX foci in cultured cells and also in cells derived from aging individuals reflects the presence of unrepaired DNA breaks that may not have occurred due to short telomers. Since age associated foci appear despite the presence of telomerase enzyme and long telomers, the telomere damage must be the result of oxidative stress that also increases with advancing age [58, 59]. Despite having long telomers and telomerase enzyme, mouse embryonic fibroblasts undergo senescence due to high ambient oxygen (20%) associated oxidative stress and damage. Similarly, culture of human and mouse fibroblasts at low oxygen concentrations suppresses senescence even after large number of passages or after activation of ras oncogene [60]. Overexpression of telomerase in human fibroblasts does not protect the cells from oxidative stress mediated senescence, however it suppresses the DNA damage response signals that originate from the stalled replication forks in telomers caused by stabilized G4 structures, R loops and Sheltrin complexes [9, 61, 62].

Large scale accumulation of unrepaired telomeric damage might be due to inability of cell to repair DNA damage at specific sites. Presence of TRF2 near telomeric lesions makes their repair and resolution difficult. TRF2 binds to telomeric DNA and makes it extremely difficult for ligases to act and repair any double strand DNA breaks. Repair resistant telomers drive persistent DDR signals to ensure cell cycle arrest. DNA damages in different parts of genome are efficiently resolved so that the cell experiences the DDR signal activation and cell cycle arrest only in a transient manner. TRF2 binding of telomers renders a cell incapable of efficiently repairing the DSBs, as a result oxidative stress or exogenous agent mediated damage to telomers results in unresolved DDR signal activation that culminates in stabilization of cell cycle arrest and induction of senescence [9, 63, 64].

Oncogene activation

Studies with fibroblasts have revealed that expression of oncogenes like ras or raf results in onset of mitotic arrest and induction of senescence likely by large scale accumulation of mutations. Activation of oncogenes results in enhanced activity of CDKs and dysregulated cell cycle entry, increased replication errors, aberrated use of replication origins, DNA breaks and ultimately initiation of DDR response and associated signaling pathways [20, 110]. Activation of oncogenes may also induce DNA damage by downregulating the activity of ribonucleotide reductase M2 (RRM2) that ultimately results in suppressed dNTP pools [111]. Blocking ATM/CHK2 signaling pathways results in suppression of oncogene induced DNA damage, cell cycle arrest and consequent induction of senescence [20, 112].

Recent studies have confirmed upregulation of NOTCH1 activity in oncogene induced senescent cells followed by the enhanced expression of TGFβ [113, 114]. Subsequent experiments revealed that the upregulation of NOTCH1 is transient during early stages of OIS, however during the later stages of the OIS, the expression of NOTCH1 and NICD return to basal levels. Ectopic expression of NICD in human fibroblasts leads to NOTCH induced senescence with a distinct secretory phenotype and chromatin structure from the OIS mediated by ras oncogene activation. Notch induced senescent cells display reduced expression of inflammatory cytokine IL-8, but increased expression of NOTCH ligand JAGI and TGFβ1, However, these cells display poor formation of SAHF [115–117]. Oncogene induced senescence occurs independent of telomere attrition and acts via p16/RB and p53/CDKN1A signaling cascades [118, 119]. Studies with mice have confirmed that loss of pP53 or p19 leads to aggressive Ras induced tumor cell invasion, whereas rescue of p53 function leads to suppression of tumor growth via decreased invasion and also enhances expression of senescent markers. Accumulation of high RB levels is key to establishment of oncogene induced senescent phenotype. RB acts downstream of p16 by repressing the transcription of E2F target genes linked to DNA replication specifically during the senescence. The role of p53 and RB is well documented as far as OIS is concerned, however many cell types including human mammary epithelial cells (HMECs) undergo OIS in response to oncogenic Ras without requiring p53 or p16/RB for induction or establishment of senescent phenotype [113, 120–123].

Oncogene induced senescence as barrier to tumorigenesis

Among the diverse types of DNA damage, double strand breaks are most challenging to cell, as sizable number of these breaks are incompatible with normal replication and cell proliferation. Continuous generation of double strand breaks leads to genomic instability, a crucial factor for initiation of cancer. Existence of highly evolved tumorigenesis barriers inhibit the initiation and progression of cancers. In response to replication stress, the cell ensures activation of DNA damage check points, that subsequently trigger cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. Oncogene induced cell senescence also acts as barrier to cancer initiation. Activated oncogenes induce pre-mature stalling and termination of replication forks, augmented number of replicons that in turn lead to generation of DNA DSBs. The DNA DSBs led to activation of tumor suppressor p53, or check point kinases like ATM that enforce cell cycle arrest and oncogene induced senescence. Breach of these protective barriers by diverse mechanisms that impair DNA damage repair like mutating the p53 or inhibiting the DNA double strand break response kinase ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM) abrogates senescence, and enhances cellular transformation. Oncogene activation does not lead to DDR in absence of replication, indicating that OIS results from DDR induced by oncogene associated DNA hyper-replication. Evidence from mice models and human origin precancerous lesions confirmed the co-occurrence of senescence and DDR markers, a strong indictive that oncogene induced senescence along with apoptosis constitute a formidable barrier against the development of neoplastic lesions [20, 124, 125].

SARS-CoV-2 infection and senescence

SARS-CoV-2 elicits cellular senescence as a primary stress response in infected cells. Besides being a cellular insult, the virus entry into the cell result in overwhelming and inappropriate immune response that include massive cytokine release, complement lysis, macrophage activation and thrombosis in lung tissue [126]. Transcriptomic analysis of pulmonary ECs from SARS-CoV-2 infected aged mice had higher viral load than ECs from younger mice. Similarly, in vitro studies with irradiation induced senescent ECs had higher viral genomic RNA than young ECs [127]. Florescent microscopic studies have confirmed that senescent ECs have high endocytic capacity than young ECs, whereas knockout of the BSG reduces the endocytic capacity or viral uptake of endothelial senescent cells. The high endocytic capacity, enhanced viral uptake by senescent ECs accompanied by resistance to apoptosis plays an important role in development of SASP, bystander effect and host mediated mutations in viral genome [127–129]. Comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis of senescence markers in nasopharyngeal and lung tissue samples revealed that, p16, p21, H3K9me3 and interleukin-8 displayed vigorous reactivity in samples from patients with fatal SARS-CoV-2 infection. Increased levels of p16 and SASP components were not only observed in ciliated epithelial cells but also in SASP attracted macrophages, strongly indicating the spread of senescence to macrophages and p16 elevated resistant mucosal epithelial cells by paracrine signaling [126, 130].

Experiments with mouse models of SARS-CoV-2 infection revealed that the virus induces senescence like alterations in olfactory fibroblasts that result in persistence of olfactory dysfunction. These senescent like fibroblasts release proinflammatory SASP components including, CXCL9, CXCL11 and IFNγ, that subsequently lead to chemotaxis of γδ T cells to the infected site. The γδ T cells release IL-17A and undermine the functioning of the olfactory neurons via disruption of their IL-17A receptor [131]. Disruptions in p16/p21 or ablation of senescent cells by administration of senolytics or genetic disruption in IL-17A receptor result in considerable mitigation of olfactory dysfunction. Liuliu and co-workers while investigating the central and peripheral nervous system dysfunctions in Covid-19 patients, reported that human pluripotent stem cell derived dopamine neurons develop an inflammatory and senescent response on being infected with SARS-CoV-2. Besides identifying the inflammatory and senescent signatures in substantia nigra samples of Covid-19 patients, comprehensive analysis of tissue samples yielded reduced numbers of neuromelanin + and tyrosine hydroxylase + DA neurons [132].

Cell cycle arrest in senescent cells

Cell cycle is a complex and tightly regulated phenomenon that culminates in division of parent cell into two daughter cells. Cell cycle arrest is most commonly triggered by severe DNA damage or substantial oxidative stress, serving as safe guard to block the transmission of genetic defects or division of defective cells. Non responsive cell cycle arrest is a defining feature of senescent cells, that is key to progression and establishment of a dynamic senescent phenotype [4, 9]. Senescent cells are arrested at G1or G2 phase of the cell cycle and mitotic arrest is quite distinct from the quiescence in which cells are arrested at G0 phase of cell cycle [133, 134]. The decision of a cell to enter into quiescent or senescent phenotype depends on the status of mTOR, as active mTOR encourages a cell to enter into senescence, whereas cells with inhibited mTOR undergo quiescence [135]. As opposed to senescent cells, quiescent cells can reenter into cell cycle on receiving appropriate signals from growth factors, however recent studies have confirmed that senescent cells do reenter into proliferative cycle during cancer development or can be genetically reprogrammed [136–138]. It will be informative to highlight terminal differentiation as an alternate but important cellular process where cells mature into specialized form and permanently lose their ability to divide. Terminal differentiation is ensured by a comprehensive network of signaling cascades like Notch, TGF beta and Wnt signaling. Transcription factors like p53, GATA1, RUNX2, MyoD and cell cycle inhibitors like p21, p27 and retinoblastoma, along with some histone modifying proteins play a key role in maintaining the non-dividing and specialized form of the differentiated cells [103]. Senescence associated mitotic arrest is mediated by p53/p21 and p16/RB signaling pathways. p21 and p16 are the key players of p53 and RB signaling pathways, that stop cell division and enforce senescent phenotype by carrying out inhibitory phosphorylation of cyclin dependent kinases [139, 140].

P53/P21 pathway

Intense oxidative stress, oncogene activation, and telomere attrition lead to extensive DNA damage and DDR signaling that in turn activates the p53/p21 pathway (Fig. 3). The activation of p53 directly corresponds to inhibition of cell cycle and initiation of senescence, whereas the inhibition of p53 activity disrupts the initiation or progression of senescence [141–143]. Both p53 and FOXO4 are transcription factors that upregulate expression of p21 by directly binding to or facilitating the binding of transcriptional co-activators to p21 promoter to drive mitotic arrest and senescence [144]. Increased expression of p21 is important only for induction of senescence, however persistently enhanced expression of p16 is required for successful implementation of senescent phenotype [145]. Post-translational modification of p53 activity is achieved through phosphorylation, methylation, acetylation and proteasomal degradation [146]. ATM kinase mediated phosphorylation of p53 (Ser-15) results in enhanced stability and activity of p53 and consequent upregulation of anti-proliferative genes [147, 148]. MDM2 mediated ubiquitylation of multiple lysine residues on p53 results in its proteasomal degradation and repression of p53 mediated transcription. The rapid degradation of MDM2 or related MDM4 results in accumulation of p53 and increase in p53 mediated transcription [149–151]. Post-translationally p21 expression is controlled by proteasome. A tetratricopeptide protein WISp39 binds to p21 and recruits Hsp90 to positively regulate the stability of p21 and prevents its proteasome mediated degradation [152]. Phosphorylation of p21 may alter the stability, binding of other adapter proteins to p21, or change the subcellular localization of p21 that may altogether change the function of p21 [153, 154]. p21 regulates distinct cellular processes, like nuclear p21 blocks cell division by inhibiting CDKs, whereas the cytosolic and phosphorylated form of p21 acts as potent anti-apoptotic effector protein. P21 also plays a critical role in DNA repair by interacting with proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) [155–157].

p21 is a member of Cip/Kip family of cyclin dependent kinase inhibitors, that is capable of inhibiting cyclin A, D and E, thereby preventing the progression of cell cycle [158, 159]. p21 inhibits different Cdk/Cyclin complexes to arrest cell cycle at any stage, however members of the INK4 family of cyclin dependent kinase inhibitors (p16, p15, p18 and p19) bind to Cdk4, Cdk6 to inhibit Cyc D and arrest cell cycle in G0 or G1 stage [160–162]. Two cyclin binding motifs in p21 protein (Cy1 and Cy2) facilitate the interaction of p21 with cyclins in a CDK independent manner, to exert inhibition of cyclin-Cdk kinases. Proteins containing only one Cy1 site lead to partial inhibition of cyclin-Cdk kinase activity, reflecting the essential role of Cyclin binding motifs in p21 mediated kinase inhibition and organization of P21-Cyclin-Cdk complexes [163]. This p21 mediated inhibition of phosphorylation of RB allows it to form complex with E2F family of transcription factors to downregulate many of the genes associated with cell cycle regulation. Since p21/CDKN1A activation is regulated by p53, taken together these effector proteins organize themselves into p53-p21-RB signaling cascade, and resultant RB-E2F or p53-p21-DREAM complex, that represses expression of cell cycle associated genes and consequent cell cycle arrest [139, 164, 165]. p21 can be also be activated by vitamin D, Androgens, retinoid receptors and also by kruppel like transcription factors in a p53 independent manner [166–168]. Depending on the expression level, p21 displays an anomalous role in cell, as high levels of p21 inhibit kinase activity of Cyclin D/Cdk4,6 complexes that results in mitotic arrest, whereas low levels of the p21 facilitate the assembly of Cyclin D/Cdk4,6 and cell cycle progression [4, 169, 170].

p16/pRB pathway

P16 acts by binding with CDK4/6 to inhibit the formation of Cyclin D-CDK4/6 complex thereby blocking the phosphorylation of RB and a consequent downregulation of E2F target genes (Fig. 3). Hypo-phosphorylated RB binds with E2F transcription factors to prevent the expression of genes required for DNA synthesis, thereby inducing a proliferation arrest [171, 172]. Recent studies have revealed two different E2F independent mechanisms for RB mediated cell cycle arrest. In one of the mechanisms, RB directly binds to SKP2 and targets it for ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Absence of SKP2 leads to stabilization of p27 and consequent CDK inhibition and cell cycle arrest. In second mechanism RB binds simultaneously to SKP2 and anaphase promoting complex that results in proteasome mediated degradation of SKP2 and stabilization of p27. The stabilized p27 causes inhibition of CDKs and proliferation arrest [173–175]. RB is targeted by Cycd-CDK4/CDK6 complexes to ensure the progression of cell cycle. Dephosphorylated RB binds with E2F to form a complex RB-E2F that results into transcriptional repression of genes associated with cell cycle progression [176–178]. The E2F-RB complex also recruits histone demethylase, chromatin modifying enzymes and histone deacetylases to maintain repressive state of E2F [179–181]. Studies with fibroblasts have revealed that cross talk between RB and AKT signaling cascades modulate the function of fork head transcription factors FOXO3a and FOXM1, that play a crucial role in initiation of senescence [182].

Studies with mammalian cell cultures have revealed that epigenetically induced senescence acts by upregulating the expression of p16 via transcriptional activation of repressed INK4A locus that codes for p16, whereas replicative senescence exerts its effects by inducing p21. Since epigenetic modifiers establish a senescent phenotype without inducing any stress, the epigenetic modification induced senescence is considered similar to developmental senescence or age-related senescence [48, 183]. Replicative senescence progresses via de-repression of CDKN2A that codes for p16 or p19. Transgenic expression of p16 in mice leads to inhibition of cell cycle progression and induces premature ageing, however the effects are reversed with the loss of p16 expression. Similarly, clearance of p16 positive cells leads to increased life span and mitigates senescence related organ damage [184–187]. The de-repression of CDKN2A has been largely attributed to loss of polycomb group of proteins (PcG) [188]. Polycomb group of proteins inhibit expression of target genes by histone modification on chromatin. While acting on chromatin, PcG proteins organize themselves into two distinct functional complexes that include polycomb repressive complex 1, a histone ubiquitin ligase and PRC2 a histone methyl transferase [189, 190]. Polycomb protein complexes PRC1 and PRC2 repress the INK4/ARF locus, so the disruption of PRC1 and PRC2 complexes and consequent decrease in methylation is essential for p16 induced senescence. [191, 192]. Long non coding RNA ANRIL facilitates the recruitment of PRC1 and PRC2 complexes to INK4/ARF locus. The ANRIL binds to SUZ12 subunit of PRC2 complex to promote Ezh2 mediated histone methylation and consequent repression of INK4/ARF locus [193, 194]. It will be worthwhile to mention that INK4A/ARF locus codes for three repressors namely p16, p15 and p14. Both p15 and p16 are cyclin dependent kinase inhibitors that mediate the arrest the cell cycle by repressing activity of CDK4/6 [195]. p14 plays an important role in stabilizing the p53 by reducing the proteasome mediated degradation of p53 in MDM2 (Ubiquitin E3 ligase) dependent manner [196–198]. Polycomb group of proteins are downregulated by microRNAs like miR-424, miR-26b and miR-181a that leads to enhanced p16 INK4 expression and successful induction of senescence [4, 199].

Escape from senescence and cell cycle reentry

Senescence mediated cell cycle arrest is controlled by diverse signaling pathways that include, p16, p21, p53 and methylation of lysine 9 at histone H3 (H3K9me3). Recent studies have established that cells can escape from the senescence associated cell cycle arrest and resume proliferation [138]. Experiments with human origin bronchial epithelial cells with CDC6 inducible oncogene expression cassette, revealed that induction of oncogene leads to chromosomal inversion and activation of BHLHE40 transcription factor, that subsequently drives the senescent cells re-entry into cell cycle [200]. Oncogene induced senescence enforces inhibition of proliferation associated genes via methylation of Histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9). H3K9 demethylases and Jumonji C domain containing demethylase that specifically target trimethylated lysine residues allows the Ras/Braf mediated cell cycle re-entry and subsequent cellular transformation. Experiments with diverse melanoma cell lines revealed the high expression of demethylases, whereas the inhibition of these demethylases restores cell senescence [201]. Convincing evidence regarding the reversible nature of senescence particularly in case of cancer cells, strongly indicates that senescent like arrest, may be the cellular state explored by cancer cells to evade the toxicity of anti-cancer treatments to prolong their survival in dormant state [202]. p21 is a p53 regulated cyclin dependent kinase inhibitor that plays a key role in induction of senescence. Experimental evidence has confirmed that p53 mutated subset of cancer cells display high expression of p21, indicating the crucial role of p21 in cancer progression. Genomic analysis of these p21 inducible cells revealed that after an initial senescence like phase, a subpopulation of highly expressing cells emerge that display drug resistance and high aggression. Persistently high expression of p21 results in suppression of CRL4-CDT2 ubiquitin ligase and subsequent dysregulated replication stress [203]. Reversibility of senescence mediated cell cycle arrest suggests that, senescence may be a precisely regulated stress response with strong implications for tissue regenerations, wound healing and a concern for cancer relapse and ageing related pathologies.

Molecular aspects of cellular alterations in senescence

Morphological alterations

Studies with cell culture have confirmed that senescent cells undergo an unexplained change in morphology like, increased and irregular size, flattened shape and increased volume of cytoplasm, however in vivo, the shape attained by senescent cells is dictated by the tissue architecture [32, 204]. Specialized senescent cells may attain some distinct morphological traits like senescent microglia have decreased number of processes, senescent auditory cells under oxidative stress attain a ramified shape, whereas bone marrow derived mesenchymal stem cells display polygonal phenotype when subjected to proliferative stress [205–207]. Some of these morphological changes may arise due to accumulation of macromolecules in nucleus and cytosol, that were synthesized before the irreversible mitotic arrest at G1/G2 phase of cell cycle [32, 39]. At molecular level the aberrated cellular phenotype is associated with scaffolding protein Caveolin 1, Rho GTPases Rac1 and CDC42 [208, 209]. These cells also display enhanced number of focal adhesions due to overexpression and activation of cytoskeleton protein ezrin [210]. The change in shape of nucleus in senescent cells is affected by upregulation of Cofilin that in turn leads to increased formation of stress fiber network and remodeling of cytoskeleton [211–213].

Senescent cells also undergo permanent changes and modifications at organelle level. These changes include presence of large sized nucleus or more than one nucleus. Multinucleated senescent cells arise from the fusion of mononucleated cells; however, the fusion may be homogenic or heterogenic depending if the fusion occurred between cells that are similar or dissimilar in origin. Since macrophages are the main fusogenic cells, most of the giant sized multinucleated senescent cells arise from fusion of macrophages among themselves or with other types of cells [214] [215–217]. Senescent cells are accompanied by changes in number, activity and mass of organelles like lysosomes, mitochondria, vacuoles and also expansion in endoplasmic reticulum; however, these organelles are functionally defective and display aberrant communications (Fig. 4). To mitigate the deleterious effects of dysfunctional organelles and maintenance of cellular functions at acceptable level, new organelles are synthesized. Persistent stress stimuli and defective autophagy leads to accumulation and buildup of defective organelles that in turn compound the already existing senescent phenotype [9, 218–220].

Senescent cell morphology. Besides change in morphology like enlarged size and flattened cell body, senescent cells display large vacuoles, enlarged Endoplasmic reticulum, rearranged cytoskeleton, damaged mitochondria and increased number of active lysosomes. Similarly, the nucleus of senescent cells also undergoes profound changes like loss of Lamin B1 protein, nuclear membrane budding that generates cytoplasmic chromatin fragments and other important changes like chromatin reorganization, epigenetic modifications, formation of heterochromatic foci and development of SASP. A proper cellular redox state is crucial to the formation of disulfide interactions and proper folding of functional proteins. Senescent cells have extended ER to maintain high rates of synthesis and secretion of proteins involved with SASP [221]. High demand for protein synthesis and folding overwhelms the functional capacity of ER, leading to protein misfolding, production of ROS, disturbance in cellular redox state and reduced expression of chaperones [222]. The accumulation of unfolded proteins and enhanced synthesis of SASP leads to poor protein quality control and finally the activation of unfolded protein response in senescent cells. UPR signaling cascade culminates in reducing the ER stress via cessation of translation, rescue of protein folding, decreased production of ROS, restoring the chaperone expression and proteasome mediated degradation of aggregated or misfolded proteins [223–228]. In senescent fibroblasts, unfolded protein response leads to expansion/dysfunction of endoplasmic reticulum and cell shape modification, a process governed at molecular level by transcription factor ATF6α [32, 39, 122, 229]. Lysosomes are store houses of hydrolytic enzymes that play an important role in terminal degradation of cellular debris [230] and recycling of nutrients for efficient use of resources [231]. Senescent cells have enlarged lysosomes accompanied by enhanced activity of β-galactosidase, which serves as an important biomarker of senescence [232–234]. Lipofuscin is non degradable, heterogenous lysosomal aggregate of oxidized protein, lipid, carbohydrate and metal. Lipofuscins are useful biomarkers of senescence and strong indicators of reduced lysosomal activity and dysfunctional autophagy [235, 236]. Recent studies have explored the accumulation of lipofuscin in senescent cells to develop a highly specific senolytic platform. The Platform incorporates a lipofuscin binding scaffold covalently conjugated to Dasatinib through an ester link. Delivery of the complex senolytic drug in encapsulated form has allowed the removal of senescent cells, both In Vitro and In Vivo with high specificity and reduced off target effects [237]. In a similar study, a hydrophilic fluorophore was conjugated to analog of sudan black-B, that possesses high affinity for lipofuscin. Delivery of the fluorophore conjugated SBB enabled the easy, precise and rapid tracking of senescent cells both In vitro and In vivo [238]. Designing of lipophilic biotin conjugated SBB has enabled the antibody mediated detection of senescent cells in diverse biological samples (Table 1) [239].

Senescent cells display special alterations in structure of mitochondria [240]. Large, heterogenous and interconnected mitochondria have been observed in aged cells [241, 242]. Also, giant and elongated mitochondria have been spotted in senescent cells often with defects in OXPHOS and complex II. The defective mitochondria have downregulated Fission modulator protein Fis1 and are resistant to autophagic degradation [243, 244]. These cells also undergo chromatin reorganization that include enhanced formation of heterochromatin regions, condensation of chromosomes, histone modification and changes in nuclear membrane due to reduced expression/loss of Lamins. Recent studies have reported the differential expression of diverse genes, that are potentially associated with regulation of epigenetic modifications in senescent cells. These genes include transcription factors (TP53, SIN3B), DNA methylation proteins (DNMT3L), chromatin remodeling enzymes (CHD3, SMARCB1), histone chaperones (HIRA) and histone modifying enzymes (HDAC6, SIRT6, KAT8) [245]. In comparison to senescent cells, normal proliferating cell possesses structured chromatin generally deposited at the periphery of nucleus [18, 246]. Heterochromatin is tethered to the nuclear lamina via lamin associated domains, that mostly contain repressed genes and heterochromatic histone marks. Senescence leads to loss of Lamin B protein, that leads to rearrangement of heterochromatin and its relocation to interior of nucleus. This rearrangement of heterochromatin leads to chromatin reorganization and changes in nuclear morphology [247–250]. Senescence is associated with changes in histone composition. Telomere shortening leads to reduced H3 and H4 levels, whereas loss of linker H1 and downregulated expression of histone chaperones result in large scale alteration in histone landscape. Senescence leads to accumulation of histone variant H3.3, resulting in replacement of histone variants H3.1 and H3.2. Histone H3.3 plays a significant role in suppression of genes related to cell division and enforcement of cell cycle arrest [251–254]. Senescence is characterized by formation of super enhancers near promoters of cell division associated genes. Transcription of SASP components is mediated by enrichment of H3K9ac and H4K16ac at their promoters. A loop-based mechanism ensures the exclusion of SASP associated genes from heterochromatic region of SAHF, thus allowing their transcription and expression. Under normal circumstances, sirtuin blocks the expression of SASP components by binding to their promoters, however, as senescence sets in sirtuin falls from the promoters of SASP genes to ensure their expression [255–257].

Physiological and functional alterations

The formation of senescent cells due to diverse stress stimuli, together with diminished clearance due to reduced immune surveillance combined with other important factors like resistance to apoptosis and pro-survival mechanisms lead to accelerated accumulation of senescent cells in different tissues during ageing. Studies with heterochronic parabiosis (Parabiosis between young and old mice) have provided valuable insights into role of age in senescence, as the number of senescent cells increase in young mice, whereas number of senescent cells decrease in older mice [39, 258]. These senescent cells cause tissue degeneration, premature ageing and inflammation commonly referred to as inflammaging. Through the release of inflammatory cytokines (SASP), the senescent cells create inflammatory niche in tissues that results in age/inflammation related disorders and spread of senescence to the healthy tissues. Transplantation of senescent cells into young mice causes tissue dysfunction, spread of senescence to healthy tissues and reduced life span. In contrast, selective removal of senescent cells by senolytic cocktail mitigates the senescent cell associated inflammation, rescue of tissue function and increase in survival rate [55, 259–261].

Presence of senescent cells are a huge risk for accelerated ageing and ageing related pathologies. Senotherapeutics are a group of synthetic or naturally occurring compounds, that are grouped into two main categories named senolytics and senomorphics. Senolytic drugs act by eliminating a senescent cell, whereas senomorphic drugs directly neutralize the effects of SASP components. Both the drugs function to mitigate the adverse effects of senesce and senescence associated pathologies [67]. Different compounds that have been identified as senolytic include, Quercetin, Fisetin, Piperlongumine, Curcumin, Dasatinib, Bcl-2 family inhibitors, HSP90 inhibitors, p53 binding inhibitors and inhibitors of MDM2/p53 interaction.

To reduce the off target deleterious effect of senolytics, diverse strategies are being developed to eliminate the senescent cells with greater precision and improved clinical outcome. The new approach is more focused on the use of antibody drug conjugates, senolytic vaccines, CAR-T cell therapy, use of nano drugs, galactose-modified prodrugs and immune based therapies. Galactose-modified duocarmycin induces apoptosis in cells induced to senescence by replicative exhaustion, radiation exposure or oncogene activation. Besides reducing senescence in cell cultures derived from mouse and human cell lines, gemcitabine displayed potent senolytic activity against senescent cells induced by radiation, oncogene exposure or replicative exhaustion. Senescent cells express unique surface biomarkers that have been explored for identification as well as development of therapeutic intervention. uPAR and NKG2A targeted CAR-T cells selectively eliminated senescent cells from in vivo mice models, that resulted in improvement of biological measures like physical wellbeing and metabolic functions. Macrophages, T-cells and NK cells have been associated with elimination of senescent cells. Senescent cells exhibit high expression of surface molecules PD-1 and CD-47, which act as potent immune suppressors and prevent their elimination. Antibody mediated inhibition of both CD-47 and PD-1 results in enhanced elimination of senescent cells and reversal of ageing phenotypes. To over-come the necessity of repeated administration and dose related limitations, senolytic vaccines were introduced as a viable option to delay ageing and related pathologies [262–264]. Selective targeting of CD153high cells, resulted in reduced accumulation of senescent T cells in adipose tissues and improvement of physiological functions like glucose tolerance [265]. Similarly targeting of GPNMBhigh cells resulted in high glucose tolerance and reduced size of atherosclerotic plaques [266].

Senomorphic drugs act by inhibiting the components of SASP, thereby mitigating the effects of senescence, ageing and related pathologies. Senomorphics act by downregulating the signaling pathways like P13/Akt signaling cascade, JAK/STAT pathway, mTOR signaling, or activity of transcription factors like NF-ΚB, STAT, or C/EBP β, that are crucial to the development of SASP. Senomorphics may also act by direct inhibition or neutralization of inflammatory components like IL-8, IL-1α and IL-6 that may be achieved through enhanced surveillance of immune activity [76]. The notable senomorphics include Rapamycin, Metformin, Ruxolitinib, Apigenin, statins, JAK/STAT inhibitors, NBD peptide and glucocorticoids [67, 267].

The complement fragments augment the inflammaging via secretion of pro senescence and pro inflammatory molecules like IL-6, ROS and TNF from attracted macrophages [268–271]. Senescent cells, that are mitotically arrested at G1/G2 phase of cell cycle, are unable to function properly, thereby rendering the tissue devoid of robust cells that are crucial for maintenance of tissue and physiological homeostasis [272]. Senescent cells secrete variety of molecules that have significant biological activity and clinical relevance. These bio active molecules commonly referred to as senescence associated secretory phenotype (SASP) include, metalloproteases, serine proteases, chemokines, cytokines, plasminogen activators, growth factors and various regulatory molecules [273–277]. Transcriptional activation coupled to translation and under the regulatory control of inflammasomes, the components of SASP are secreted and cause inflammation, detrimental effects on tissue microenvironment and senescence associated pathologies [273, 275, 276, 278–280]. Recent studies with senescent cells and bone marrow derived stem cells have confirmed that the extracellular vesicles released by these cells have special characteristics and are capable of modulating the functioning and behavior of target cells. The EV’s from these cells are highly enriched in miRNA and DNA that modulate the gene expression or signaling in a target cell. The content of the EV’s released from the senescent cells work similarly like the soluble SASP components as important mediators of intercellular communication, and thus qualify as an emerging component of secretory phenotype [274, 281–283].

Senescence as a biological process may have evolved to facilitate and fine tune embryonic development, tissue remodeling and regeneration. The senescent cells communicate with each other or other cells in their vicinity via the biologically active components of secretory phenotype, intercellular protein bridges and release of extracellular vesicles [284, 285]. These biologically active components perform two functions, first is to facilitate the clearance of senescent cells by chemokine mediated recruitment of macrophages and second is to induce the senescence in nearby cells via paracrine signaling and establishment of inflammatory niche in tissues [274, 276, 286–288]. Senescent cells release chemokines that attract macrophages and result in establishment of chronic inflammatory niche. Immune cell senescence is characterized by decline in the number of immune cells, decrease in the robustness of their activity and diminished response to infections and self-altered cells [289]. Tumor microenvironment is characterized by presence of dysfunctional senescent T cells lacking co-stimulatory signaling molecule CD28, that results in immune cell impairment, compromised anti-tumor response and subsequent immune evasion by transformed cells [290]. Senescent immune cells fail to clear cancerous cells and may promote the cancer progression by secreting immune-suppressive components [291]. T cells are most important players in anti-cancer immune responses, however persistent exposure to antigen, accompanied by elevated expression of inhibitory receptor molecules like CTLA-4 and PD-1 impairs their function [292, 293]. T-regulatory cells suppress the effector T cells and promote tumor tolerance, whereas B cells produce pro tumor anti-bodies. Immune senescence causes impaired antigen presentation in dendritic cells, remodeling of CD8 T cell function, reduced clonal expansion and aged B cell populations [269, 270]. The functioning of different immune cells is determined by inter-cellular communication, competition in tumor microenvironment and other immune regulatory networks modulated by cytokines and other biologically relevant components [294–297]. Immune senescence also has a profound effect on the outcome of cancer immune therapy especially in case of melanoma. Pavlos and coworkers, while studying the outcome of immunotherapy in melanoma patients that received immunotherapy (PD-1 or CTLA-4 or both), reported, ex vivo analysis of clinical samples especially PMBCs, from melanoma patients revealed that PMBCs from non-respondents displayed high degree of senescence especially in CD4+ T cell, CD8+T cell, B cell and NK cells. High degree of senescence in CD4+ T cell, CD8+T cell, B cell (CD19+ and CD20+) and NK cells was also confirmed by tissue biopsies of the non-respondents, that is strongly indictive of increased immune senescence in non-respondents. Analysis of PMBCs for senescence also provided useful insights about the tumor infiltrating immune cells and tumor microenvironment. Analysis of intercellular communications revealed that diverse alterations in ligand-receptor communications among CD4+ T cell, CD8+T cell, B cell (CD19+ and CD20+) and NK cells. Interaction between cells that enhanced a robust immune response were active in respondents, whereas cell-cell communications that suppress the immune responses were active in non-respondents. Robust immune cell populations, NK cell penetrations and productive antigen presentation by dendritic cells positively enhance the outcome of anti-melanoma therapy, whereas Treg accumulation, B cell suppression, T cell dysfunction and presence of M2 macrophages negatively impact the outcome of immunotherapy in non-respondents [298]. The SASP functions predominantly as tumorigenic by facilitating the epithelial to mesenchymal transition and enhancing the vascularization of malignant tumors. SASP from preneoplastic hepatocytes suppress natural killer cell activity and subsequently promote development of cancer [51, 299]. Age associated chronic inflammation, collectively referred to as inflammaging is a critical factor that drives pathophysiological disorders through dysregulation of immune system and metabolism [299].

On the positive side the SASP released by senescent cell, attracts the immune cells, that results in enhanced immune surveillance and clearing of senescent cells. SASP also plays an important role in wound healing, cellular reprogramming, regeneration, differentiation of different cell types during embryonic development and organogenesis [35, 41, 288, 300, 301].

Metabolic changes in senescent cells

Senescent cells are mitotically arrested but are metabolically active with high glycolytic flux and activated mTOR [302–304]. Enlarged size, high expression of SASP associated proteins and ER stress, whip up the energy demands that enforce compensatory changes in metabolism to meet the increasing energy and biosynthetic requirements [305]. These cells increase the rate of glycolysis, but due to disbalanced activity of glycolytic enzymes and impaired mitochondrial activity, an energy deficient chaotic state is created in the cell which is compensated by accelerated ATP production via aerobic glycolysis [9, 306, 307]. In senescent cells mitochondria have low inner membrane potential and defective oxidative phosphorylation that results into production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)[308, 309]. To compensate for the defective mitochondrial function and to maintain cellular energy homeostasis, the cell initiates biogenesis of new mitochondria. The process depends on the Akt/mTORC1 phosphorylation cascade, that leads to activation of the peroxisome proliferator activated receptor gamma coactivator α (PGC1α), a key regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis [310–312]. Sensitivity to oxidative stress and diffusion of peroxides into lysosomal compartments leads to formation of heterogenous lipofuscin aggregates in senescent cells. Formation and accumulation of lipofuscin results in misdirection of hydrolytic enzymes away from autophagosome. The process causes aberrant mitophagy and accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, enhanced ROS mediated oxidative stress and dysregulated cellular functions [9, 313]. Removal of defective mitochondria from senescent cells results in reduced formation of ROS, diminished ROS mediated oxidative stress and inhibition of DDR response.

Senescent cells have high rates of glycolytic activity and ATP production to sustain demanding energy requirements. The development of senescence and SASP are more associated with defective oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria and resultant ROS production. Recent studies have confirmed that transition from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation, mediated through the activation of mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase is essential for initiating the oncogene induced proliferative arrest and establishment of senescent phenotype [314–318]. The dysfunctional oxidative phosphorylation leads to production of ROS, efflux of calcium into cytosol, high ADP/ATP and NAD/NADH ratios, which in turn lead to activation of cytosolic transducers that are important mediators of retrograde signaling or nucleus mitochondria cross talk. These transducers fine tune the activities of different transcription factors like VDAC1, NFKβ, Sirt1, CREB, mTOR1, PGC-1α that play an essential role in transcriptional modifications and metabolic reprogramming [319, 320]. The reprogramming achieved through retrograde signaling may change cellular function, integrate different energy producing pathways or stabilize the redox state of the cell for establishment of senescent phenotype or change the cell fate to death or division (Fig. 5) [240, 318, 320–324].

Recent advances in understanding of cellular senescence

Metabolic regulation in senescent cells

Senescent cells are mitotically arrested but are metabolically active with high glycolytic flux and activated mTOR [302–304]. Enlarged size, high expression of SASP associated proteins and ER stress, whip up the energy demands that enforce compensatory changes in metabolism to meet the increasing energy and biosynthetic requirements [305]. Since senescent cells have high rates of glycolytic activity and ATP production to sustain demanding energy requirements, the development of senescence and SASP are more associated with defective oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria and resultant ROS production. Recent studies have confirmed that transition from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation, mediated through the activation of mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase is essential for initiating the oncogene induced proliferative arrest and establishment of senescent phenotype [314–318]. The dysfunctional oxidative phosphorylation leads to production of ROS, efflux of calcium into cytosol, high ADP/ATP and NAD/NADH ratios, which in turn lead to activation of cytosolic transducers that are important mediators of retrograde signaling or nucleus mitochondria cross talk. These transducers fine tune the activities of different transcription factors like VDAC1, NFKβ, Sirt1, CREB, mTOR1, PGC-1α that play an essential role in transcriptional modifications and metabolic reprogramming [319, 320]. The reprogramming achieved through retrograde signaling may change cellular function, integrate different energy producing pathways or stabilize the redox state of the cell for establishment of senescent phenotype or change the cell fate to death or division (Fig. 5) [240, 318, 320–324].

Transcriptional control of senescence

Senescence is a precisely regulated cellular response to stress, that leads to cell cycle arrest. Diverse components that constitute the core of senescence signature include, p16 and p21, that associated with cell cycle arrest or induction of early senescence respectively, whereas SASP components like IL-8, IL-6, IL-1β, CXCL, CCL2, EGF and matrix metalloproteases also form integral part of senescent cell signature. Classical markers of senescence include SA-β gal, loss of Lamin B and other morphological changes. The exploration of advanced techniques like single cell RNA sequence, transcriptomics, and metagenomics to identify and validate senescence markers for development of therapeutics, has shifted the senescence research into a new paradigm (Table 1).

RNA sequencing studies have confirmed that senescent cells are heterogenous in nature and each cell in a population has a unique transcriptomic signature. Since senescence is dynamic and evolving adaptive cellular response, single cell transcriptomic analysis provided deeper insights into the transcriptomic foot print of a particular cell during different stages of senescence development [339, 340]. Expression of many genes have been studied and validated, especially those that are associated with biomarker expression. A unique transcriptomic signature can also be associated with specific senescence inducing stress or a particular cell/group to identify the commonly or differentially expressed genes [340]. Studies with human fibroblasts have confirmed that individual senescent cells present a marked difference in transcription and this cell-to-cell variability results in poor correlation between senescence associated genes. Similarly, genes encoding for SASP components also showed high degree of variability, however a subset of SASP linked genes displayed a robust induction at population level. Inflammatory genes in clustered loci show high correlation as compared to genes in non-clustered loci, indicating that these genes may be secondarily regulated by genomic location [341]. Expression profile studies have provided detailed insights into function of genes that are differentially expressed in different stages of senescence like initial, middle and advanced (Fig. 6). In early stages the genes associated with cell cycle, DNA repair, and cytoskeleton remodeling are expressed in abundance, indicating the circumstances where cells are preparing to divide, however it might be the stage at which stress signals become overwhelming and cell changes the course of direction and decides to enter into proliferative arrest. In mid senescent stages the genes associated with RNA processing, amino acid metabolism and ribosome biogenesis were upregulated indicating increase in growth and organelle biogenesis immediately after mitotic arrest. In advanced stages of senescence, the genes that demonstrate upregulation include inflammatory response genes, cytokine signaling and antigen presentation reflecting establishment of a senescent phenotype [9, 342–344]. Genes involved in cell signaling like, adhesion molecules, membrane receptors and also the genes associated with vesicle trafficking are upregulated [340]. Altered morphology and increased adhesion of senescent cells to extracellular matrix demands the upregulation of relevant membrane receptors. Downregulation of apoptotic factors like caspase-3 and upregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins like BCL-2, BCL-W and BCL-XL are important players that prevent a senescent cell from undergoing programmed cell death and apoptosis [12, 13, 345]. Removal of anti-apoptotic proteins from senescent cells by pharmacological intervention induces apoptosis in senescent cells, thereby validating the key role of these anti-apoptotic proteins in implementation of the senescent phenotype [346]. In senescent cells, the genes related to DNA replication, spindle formation, chromosome segregation, cell cycle and DNA synthesis are down regulated, and also the transcription profile of senescent cells has been observed to be unique and distinguished from quiescent cells [340, 342, 347, 348]. RB plays an essential role in establishment of senescent phenotype by inhibiting the synthesis of DNA and also by keeping the transcription of replication associated genes under repression. Similarly, RB represses the transcription of genes linked with synthesis and replication of DNA in quiescent cells [349]. The downregulation of genes associated with cell cycle and upregulation of genes associated with progression and establishment of senescent phenotype play an essential role by preventing the transfer of deleterious mutations and defective organelles to future generations [9].

Global transcriptomic changes in senescent cells are accompanied by changes in cellular signaling cascades and biochemical characteristics. Genes associated with synthesis of nucleic acids, proteins and other biosynthetic pathways are repressed. To mitigate the effect of ROS mediated oxidative stress, the genes that code for components of mitochondrial electron transport chain are downregulated to reduce the oxidative phosphorylation and generation of superoxides. The diminished mitochondrial activity and shutting down of translation machinery forces the cell to enter into mitotic arrest and senescence [9, 340, 350, 351].

Post-transcriptional control of senescence

Senescence is post transcriptionally regulated through mediation of micro RNAs, non-coding RNAs, and RNA binding proteins. The fluctuating levels of diverse effector proteins in the cell make remarkable contribution towards progress and establishment of stabilized senescent phenotype [352, 353]. Non coding regulatory RNAs, that include lncRNA and Micro RNAs (miRNAs) play an important role in gene expression at post-transcriptional level. The miRNA molecules, vary in length from 18 to 25 nucleotides, repress the translation of target mRNA molecules by binding to the complementary regions at their 3' UTR, thus expediting the removal of destabilized RNA structures through degradation. Control of gene expression by diverse miRNAs is inherently variable, as one miRNA can simultaneously regulate many target mRNA molecules, whereas many miRNA molecules can regulate a single mRNA molecule thereby modulating gene expression as per requirements of cell [354–356]. Different miRNAs regulate senescence by directly binding to the p53 and p16 specific mRNAs and also to mRNAs specific for SASP factors. The miRNAs also play an essential role in regulation of senescent phenotype by affecting intracellular calcium levels [357]. Studies with fibroblasts have validated the role of miR-24 in suppression of p16 mRNA expression, whereas similar studies with skin fibroblasts have confirmed that miR-217 indirectly elevates the levels of p16 mRNA and protein expression, by repressing DNA methyl transferase 1 that results in implementation of senescent phenotype [358, 359]. miR-26b and miR-181a mediated transcriptional activation of p16 results in induction of senescence through chromatin remodeling [360]. Similarly, miR-25 and miR-30d, directly target the 3' region of TP53 mRNA to reduce expression of p53 protein and thus negatively regulate the cell cycle, apoptosis and senescence [361]. mir-34a plays a key role in stabilization of p53 by decreasing the expression of the deacetylase gene sirtuin that ultimately results in positive effect on progression of senescence [362]. Studies with aging mice have implicated the role of upregulated micro RNAs like miR-34a, miR-29a/b/c and miR-124 in promotion of cellular senescence via downregulation of p53 responsive microRNA molecules [363]. Premature senescence is induced in HUVEC’s by mir-216a mediated inhibition of Smad3 activity and consequent activation of IκBα [364]. Studies with dermal fibroblasts revealed that extracellular vesicles secreted as a part of SASP from senescent cells are rich in miRNAs, and highly specific for targeting mRNAs that code for anti-apoptotic proteins thereby playing an essential part in establishment of senescent phenotype [365]. miRNAs also regulate calcium levels in cell and different organelles to maintain homeostasis. Influx of calcium into cytoplasm, or release from ER leads to increased intracellular calcium levels and influx of same into mitochondria, where it causes DNA damage and generation of ROS and consequent implementation of senescent phenotype [357].

Post transcriptional regulation of gene expression during senescence is also achieved by alternate splicing, the mechanism through which a primary transcript can generate multiple translatable mRNA molecules and resultant protein isoforms. Alternate splicing enhances coding capacity of genome and qualifies as the main source of complexity and diversity of mRNA pool in cells, that eventually gives rise to variable proteins with modified functions. Dysregulation of splicing becomes more frequent in aging or senescence and is generally attributed to exon skipping, pseudo exons or cryptic exons, retention of introns or use of pseudo splice sites that eventually result in synthesis of protein with altered structure, specificity or function [357, 366–368]. Some of the genes that undergo changes in expression due to splicing are astroglia glutamate transporter EAAT2, nuclear Lamin A, p53 and endoglin that codes for transmembrane co receptor. p53 β isoform is produced as a result of cryptic exon activation, however the decreasing levels of splicing factor SRSF3 have been attributed for this dysregulated splicing event that ultimately leads to promotion of senescence [309, 369, 370]. Similarly cryptic exon activation in LMNA gene leads to aberrant alternate splicing and a corresponding frameshift leading to formation of a truncated isoform called Progerin that has a detrimental effect on chromatin organization [371, 372]. In addition to alternate splicing, splicing factors also paly an appreciable role in governing the diversity of cellular transcriptome and metabolic reprogramming [373]. Recent studies with different models of study observed a variable expression of different splicing factors in different organs and tissues [373, 374]. in vitro studies with senescent human cells have revealed an important role of ATM gene in regulation of splice factor levels, as deletion of ATM genes leads to upregulation and high expression of TRA2B, hnRNPA1, hnRNPD, RSF1, SRSF2, SRSF3 and SRSF7 [375]. Splicing factors SRSF1, SRSF5 and SRSF6 promote donor sites specific for Lamin A while repressing the sites for generation of Progerin [376, 377]. Mutations in spliceosome genes like U2AF1, SF3B1 and SRSF2 are generally found in older subjects, thus implicating role of these effector proteins in age related disorders. Similarly splicing factors that show consistently variable expression in different tissues like SF3B1, SRSF1, SRSF2 can qualify as potential targets for therapeutic intervention [373]. Studies with hematopoietic stem cells revealed that inhibition of mRNA splicing by pharmacological agent (Spliceostatin A) results in induction of senescence in HPSCs [378]. Investigating the role of splice factors in cryptic exon activation will provide deeper insights into molecular aspects that govern senescence and related clinical implications.

Autophagic control of senescence

Senescence is a multifactorial cellular process that originates from complex interactions between genetic, metabolic and environmental factors. Autophagy refers to lysosome mediated renewal process in which a cell degrades defective organelles and damaged macromolecules to maintain quality control, recycle the components for biosynthetic purposes and to promote survival especially during prolonged nutrient starvation [379, 380]. During senescence, activation of autophagy is mediated by negative feedback in mTOR-P13K pathway. mTOR regulated protein synthesis and autophagy act in opposite manner. Similarly, upregulation of ULK3 is crucial for regulation of autophagy and induction of senescence as inhibition of ULK3 delays execution of proliferative arrest, however the autophagy is highly active during late developmental stages of senescence to increase the synthesis of SASP components [9, 381].

In oncogene induced senescence, the nuclear Lamin protein B1 is transported into cytosol and undergoes lysosome mediated degradation. The autophagy-related ATG8/LC3 works as an adaptor protein to recruit designated cargo to the autophagosome via interaction with cargo specific receptors [382]. Besides being associated with elongation and maturation of autophagosomes ATG8/LC3 protein has presence in nucleus, where it binds to Lamin B1 and Lamin associated domains that are essentially the transcriptionally silent areas of heterochromatin [250, 383]. Under normal conditions, the mere interaction of these proteins has hardly any downregulatory effect on Lamins, however on oncogenic activation the Lamin associated domains are delivered into cytosol through nuclear budding, resulting into cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs)c that are degraded by lysosomes [109]. Studies with primary human cell lines have established that inhibition of autophagy or Lamin-LC3 interactions prevent oncogene ras mediated loss of nuclear Lamins or membrane [109, 248]. The presence of CCFs acts as a strong reservoir for activation of inflammatory responses. Cytoplasmic DNA activates the cytosolic DNA sensor cGAS-STING pathway that plays an important role in activation of NF-κB and IFN regulatory factor 3, that play a key role in development of inflammatory responses and promotion of SASP during oncogene induced senescence [384, 385].

One of the hallmarks of the senescence is the development of proinflammatory secretome commonly referred to as SASP. Recent studies have implicated the important role of transcription factor GATA4 for initiation of senescence and SASP. Activation of GATA4 is highly dependent on DNA damage response regulators ATM or ATR, but not on p53 or p16. Under normal conditions GATA4 undergoes p62 dependent degradation via autophagy, however in senescent cells this degradation is inhibited resulting in stabilization of GATA4. The stabilized GATA4 activates transcription factor NF-κB that plays a key role in establishment of SASP [9, 386, 387]. In ras oncogene induced senescence a distinct cellular compartment TOR-autophagy spatial coupling compartment (TASCC) where mTOR and lysosomes accumulate to simultaneously regulate the enhanced synthesis of secretory proteins and autophagy [387]. In TASCC, mTOR remains in close vicinity of lysosomes that generate the enormous amount of recycled amino acids, which in turn are utilized for mTOR regulated protein synthesis [387].

Senescence associated secretory phenotype

Senescent cells secrete a cocktail of biologically active heterogenous molecules commonly referred to as secretome or senescence associate secretory phenotype (Table 2). These secretome based effector molecules act on nearby cells by binding to the cell surface receptors and activate their signal transduction pathway to induce senescent behavior in them, a phenomenon called bystander effect Fig. 1. Most of the changes in senescent cells involve the genes related to cell cycle, metabolism and genes that code for the components of SASP.

IL-6 is one of the key factors of SASP, that interacts with target cells through CD126 (IL-6Rα) and CD130 (IL-6Rβ) cytokine receptors [388]. Experiments with endothelial and epithelial cells have revealed that, IL-6 activates downstream signaling pathways like Jak/STAT, Mitogen activated protein kinase and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase through homodimerization of gp130 [388]. IL-6 has been linked with chronic DNA damage and oncogene activated senescence, whereas secretion of IL-6 as component of SASP is associated with stress signals that originate from persistent DNA damage [276, 389]. Similarly, proinflammatory cytokine IL-1 (α and β forms) is secreted by senescent cells and acts on neighboring cells via IL-1 receptor or Toll like receptor to upregulate the NF-κB transcription factor and associated signaling cascades [390–392].

Chemokines constitute another group of proinflammatory effectors that act through interaction with G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). The chemokines play a crucial role in aging and age related pathophysiology through enhanced inflammatory responses, a process referred to as inflammaging. Different components of the chemokine repertoire exert diverse effects like inflammation, chemo-attraction of different immune effector cells and extracellular matrix remodeling [393, 394]. CXCL-8 is one of the overexpressed chemokines released from the senescent cells. Other chemokines that experience upregulation and release from senescent cells to achieve the desired physiological or pathophysiological effect include macrophage inflammatory protein, CCL-8, CCL-13, CCL-2, CCL-16, CCL-26 and MCP-3. Studies with fibroblasts observed that RAS oncogene induced senescent cells display high levels of MCP-3 expression, whereas high expression of CXCL-5,6 is observed both in senescent cells induced by ras oncogene activation or in injury induced senescent hepatic stellate cells [51]. Comprehensive studies with primary human fibroblasts have confirmed that, besides mitigating the DNA damage effects, downregulation of chemokine receptor CXCR2 (IL-8) diminishes both the replicative and oncogene activated senescence. Heterologous expression of CXCR2 results in induction of p53 dependent premature senescence [287, 395, 396].

Matrix metalloproteases form an important part of secretome. Fibroblasts experiencing the replicative or stress induced senescence display consistently higher expression of Stromelysin-1, 2 and collagenase 1 [397–399]. Many of the MMPs modulate the functions of other soluble components of the SASP. Different inflammatory proteases like IL-8 and MCP 1,2,4 undergo MMP mediated cleavage/processing that results in their inactivation [400]. Similarly, matrixmetalloproteases perform processive cleavage of CXCL/CCL members of the chemokine group, that are essential components of SASP [401, 402]. Besides MMPs, Serine proteases and their regulators like, urokinase, plasminogen activators, uPA receptor and serine protease inhibitors form an important part of the SASP. Experiments with senescent fibroblasts and endothelial cells have recorded a 50-fold increase in the activity of plasminogen activator. Studies with embryonic fibroblasts have revealed that knockdown of PAI-1 leads to persistent activation of PIK-PKB-GSK3β pathway and nuclear retention of Cyclin D that culminates in escape from mitotic arrest, whereas upregulation of PAI is observed in aged fibroblasts where it plays a key role in senescence associated cell cycle arrest [371, 403–406]. In addition to releasing bioactive molecules as part of SASP into surroundings, senescent cells also release reactive oxygen intermediates, nitric oxide and different ions due to changes in nitric oxide synthase and superoxide dismutase activities. These hyper reactive molecules promote vascular disorders, aging and tissue degeneration [51, 406–409].

Senescent cells secrete eicosanoids and other bioactive lipids that mediate autocrine and paracrine signaling to exert diversified physiological effects like inflammation, muscle contraction and vasodilation [410]. 5-Lipoxygenase is associated with production of Leukotrienes and ROS from arachidonic acid, and has been implicated in promotion of senescence like growth inhibition via p53/p21 dependent pathway [411]. Leukotriene C4 is an essential mediator of ER stress and chemotherapy induced oxidative damage [412]. Most of the SASP components are released from the senescent cell, however many are retained and accumulated in the cells and play a key role in establishment of senescent phenotype. These intracellular components act via intracrine signaling and include PGE2, IL-6, Leukotriene C4 and PAI-2. PAI-2 acts downstream of p53, co-immunoprecipitates with p21 and is key to senescence induction. Loss of p21 leads to loss of PAI-2 induced senescence, however loss of p53 has no effect on the PAI-2 mediated induction of senescent phenotype [413–415].

Regulation of SASP

Senescence associated secretory phenotype is hall mark of senescent cells and is regulated at different levels like transcription, translation, RNA stability, and epigenetic changes. Different signaling cascades such as JAK2/STAT3, PI3K, p38MAPK and other molecules like mTOR, chaperones and non-coding RNA molecules play a crucial role in development and control of SASP [4].

Persistent stress signals from exterior or interior are processed and often result in arrest of cell cycle. The decision of the cell to enter into quiescence or senescence depends on the status of mTOR, as activated mTOR favors the initiation of senescent phenotype. Active mTOR is also key to metabolic reprogramming and key to establishment of senescence associated secretory phenotype [416]. Inhibition of mTOR or downstream S6K1 increases the life span of transgenic mice, reflecting the exceptional role of mTOR in longevity and as an antiaging regulatory effector [417, 418]. mTOR plays a key role in development of SASP, as rapamycin blocks the progression of senescence by suppressing the expression of cytokine IL-1A, that in turn reduces the expression of NF-κB regulated inflammatory genes [419]. mTOR indirectly phosphorylates RNA-binding protein ZFP36L1 through MK2 (Also known as MAPKAPK2), thereby suppressing the ability of ZFP36L1 to degrade the transcripts that code for SASP components (Fig. 7). Senescent cells display persistently active mTOR, often non responsive to amino acid starvation, a condition that is complemented by defective growth factor signaling due to depolarized plasma membrane [420, 421]. Activated mTOR mediates establishment of senescent phenotype by constitutively upregulating SASP component expression, and also by suppressing the expression of DNA damage repairing proteins MGMT and NDRG1 via a downstream CCR4-NOT complex [335, 420, 422].

Novel role of NAD+ metabolism and HMGA-NAMPT-NAD+ signaling cascade in regulation of senescent cell secretome has recently been identified. In senescent cells high mobility group A proteins positively regulate the expression of Nicotinamide phosphoribosyl transferase (NAMPT) through modification of chromatin architecture. Since NAMPT has a key role in control of proinflammatory SASP, the HMGA-NAMPT-NAD+ signaling pathway promotes the SASP mechanistically by increasing the glycolytic flux and oxidative phosphorylation. Both NAMPT and HMGA proteins increase the SASP production at transcription level through the mediation of NAD+. NAD+ mediated inhibition of AMPK results in p53 dependent suppression of p38MAPK and ultimately the upregulated NF-κB activity [423, 424]. Both glycolysis and oxidative metabolism lead to formation of NAD+ and implement a robust and proinflammatory SASP. NAMPT governs the ratio of NAD+/NADH and has a profound effect on the quality and composition of SASP. Depending on the concentration of NAD+ in a senescent cell, the resultant SASP may be less or more inflammatory [4].

SASP associated gene expression is also regulated at epigenetic level. Response from both DNA damage and epigenetic changes are integrated to fine tune the expression of genes linked to the development of SASP. Long term DNA damage response culminates in ubiquitin ligase APC/C-Cdh1 (ubiquitin ligase) mediated degradation of histone H3K9 mono and dimethyl transferases (G9a and GLP) that results in systemic reduction in H3K9 demethylation, an epigenetic marker for robust gene silencing [425, 426]. Reduced methylation imprint at promoters of proinflammatory cytokines, IL-6 and IL-8 corresponds to their enhanced transcription and expression. Loss of Cdh1 results in reduced expression of IL-6, IL-8 reflecting the critical role of anaphase promoting complex C/C(Cdh1)-G9a/GLP signaling axis in establishment of senescent phenotype [426]. High mobility group protein B2 (HMGPB2) binds to SASP gene loci in senescent cells to prevent their incorporation into SAHF that results in their activated transcription [427]. In senescent cells, changes in chromatin structure leads to formation of super enhancers that enhance transcription at SASP gene loci. Increase in H3K27 acetylation at these super-enhancer sites and recruitment of BRD4 plays a key role in upregulated expression of SASP genes [428]. Studies with fibroblasts and human melanocytes confirm that, H3K4 methyl transferase (MLL1) is important for SASP development, as silencing of the same with RNA interference results in reduced expression of SASP components [429]. Similarly, Histone methyl transferase DOT1L methylates H3K79 on IL1A locus to enhance its transcription [430]. JMJD3, an epigenetic regulator and histone demethylase acts by removing H3K27me3 mediated gene repression and enhances expression of SASP factors [431]. In senescent cells downregulation of NAD dependent deacetylase, sirtuin 1 (SIRT-1) results in increased acetylation of H3K9, H4K16 and corresponding enhancement in transcription of pro-inflammatory SASP components [256].

In early senescent cells, DNA appears in the cytoplasm due to formation of micronuclei, which result from abnormal segregation of chromosomes during cell division. The unstable membranes of these micronuclei rupture to release the micronuclear DNA into cytosol and activate the cGAS mediated signaling pathways. Similarly in cells with fully developed senescent phenotype, loss of Lamin B1 leads to compromised integrity of nuclear membrane that results in budding/blebbing and release of cytoplasmic chromatin fragments [109]. Presence of extrachromosomal DNA in cytoplasm recruits the cytoplasmic DNA sensor cGAS (cyclic GMP-AMP synthase), to generate cGMP that binds to regulatory adaptor protein STING (Stimulator of interferon genes). This results in phosphorylation of NF-κB transcription factor, interferon regulatory factor 3 and consequent production of inflammatory cytokines and type 1 interferons [9, 386, 432, 433]. Studies with human primary cell lines and mouse models of DNA damage induced senescence have confirmed that loss of cGAS or STING results in reduction of SASP component synthesis [386]. During senescence, enhanced transcription of human retro transposable element LINE-1 leads to accumulation of cytosolic DNA fragments. Since L1 displays a high-level reverse transcriptase activity, the activation of the same results in increased cytosolic DNA content and concomitant increase in SASP component synthesis and inflammation due to cGAS signaling activation [434]. Presence of any type of DNA in cytosol like mitochondrial, cDNA, or nuclear origin results in activation of cGAS-STING signaling pathway to upregulate the synthesis of SASP components.

In senescent cells, regulators of SASP exert their effect through NF-κB and C/EBP-β transcription factors. Persistent DDR signaling is pre-requisite for activation and establishment of SASP, however the cellular process is independent of p53, p16 and p21 effectors that mediate the mitotic arrest [9, 435, 436]. ATM kinase phosphorylates the NEMO (NF-κB essential modulator) that in turn facilitates the nuclear export of ATM/NEMO complex. Once in cytosol the ATM/NEMO complex phosphorylates the IκB kinase that in turn phosphorylates the inhibitory IκB protein and enhances their proteasome mediated degradation. Proteasomal degradation of IκB allows the translocation of NF-κB into nucleus to activate the transcription of SASP components [435, 437]. Similarly, inhibition of NF-κB downregulates transcription of many essential genes that code for components of the SASP. Loss of IL-1α reduces the DNA binding capacity of C/EBPβ and NF-κB, that in turn downregulates the transcription of IL-6 and IL-8 cytokines. Interestingly, experiments with senescent cells have confirmed that media from IL-1α depleted cells convincingly reduced the IL-6 and IL-8 mediated invasiveness of cancer cells. Cell surface IL-1α plays a key role in activation of signaling cascades essential for secretion of IL-6, IL-8 cytokines and establishment of senescent phenotype. Blocking of IL-1 receptor with antibodies or depletion of mRNA that codes for IL-1 receptor results in repressed secretion of IL-6 and IL-8 cytokines, reflecting the importance of IL-1α in senescence and establishment of senescence associated secretory phenotype [9, 438].

Induction of mitochondrial dysfunction by different agents that disrupt the NAD/NADH ratios lead to mitotic arrest and development of SASP with a distinct composition. Cells with dysregulated mitochondrial function experience low ratio of NAD/NADH that leads to activation of AMPK, p53 and mitochondrial dysfunction associated senescence. The remarkable feature of miDAS is the distinct SASP composition that lacks NF-κB mediated inflammatory components like IL6, IL-8, IL-1, but is rich in IL-10, TNF-α and chemokines (CCL27) [439]. Recent studies have indicated the importance of transmembrane signaling protein NOTCH in regulating the composition of SASP. Activated NOTCH and associated signaling cascades mediate the transition from pro-inflammatory to immunosuppressive secretome. Investigations with oncogene induced senescence revealed that activated NOTCH1 activity upregulates the expression of TGFβ1 and TGFβ3 factors that play a key role in induction of senescence via NOTCH-JAG1 signaling pathway. NOTCH exerts its repressive effect on the proinflammatory cytokine expression by inhibiting the C/EBPβ, whereas inhibition of NOTCH activity leads to enhanced expression of proinflammatory cytokines that enhance the immune cell recruitment and monitoring [115].

SASP develops 5-10 days after the upregulated expression and detection of senescent markers. Different signaling pathways like P13K/Akt/mTOR, or p13MAPK play a key role in the progression and development of viable senescent phenotype. Numerous changes in cell are sensed by different effectors that activate a specific signaling pathway and play key role in development of senescent phenotype and also modulate the heterogeneity of SASP. Senescent cells communicate with the neighboring cells through protein bridges, extracellular vesicle, cytoplasmic bridges and also by NOTCH/AG1 signaling [4, 440, 441]. The secretory phenotype is essential for senescent cells to create a feed forward loop via autocrine signaling for generating the persistent stress signals and activation of response pathways to establish a robust senesce associated secretory phenotype.

Functions of SASP

One of the important functions of SASP components is attraction of immune cells like macrophages, T-cells and NK cells for effective removal of senescent cells and reducing the incidence of tumor initiation, however recent studies have confirmed that preneoplastic hepatocytes recruit immature myeloid cells that inhibit NK cell activity resulting in immune evasion and progression of hepatic cell cancer [299, 442]. Components of SASP like IL-6, IL-8 and PAI-1 cause cell cycle arrest In vitro, whereas in liver cancer, components of SASP also modify the tumor micro environment to enhance the development of macrophages towards tumor suppressing M1 subtype [287, 443]. Studies with xenograft models have revealed that SASP components promote cancerous growth, and also induce epithelial to mesenchymal cell transition to enhance vascularization, thereby confirming the pro-tumorigenic potential of SASP components. In obese mice, dysregulation of gut microbiome causes generation of deoxycholic acid, a DNA damaging metabolite that induces release of SASP components and promotion of hepatic cancer, whereas blocking the generation of deoxycholic acid prevents development of hepatic cancers, thereby confirming the essential role of DCA-SASP axis in development of tumors [288, 444]. In atherosclerosis, foamy macrophages with decent expression of senescence markers drive pathogenesis by expressing inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in subendothelial spaces [445].

SASP plays a prominent role in age associated inflammation referred to as inflammaging. Aged cells release pro inflammatory cytokines, as removal of senescent cells mitigates the inflammatory conditions, whereas implanting of senescent cells actively promotes inflammation and tissue dysfunction, reflecting the positive role of SASP in inflammaging [55, 184, 446]. SASP has also important contribution in disruption of tissue development and function. It may either suppress or enhance the differentiation and accumulation of senescent keratinocytes, resulting in lipodystrophy and altered phenotype associated with old mice [330]. Paracrine signaling is an essential phenomenon exerted effectively by senescent cells to modulate the functioning of neighboring cells to achieve desired effect. TGF-β, chemokines (CCL2, CCL20) and VEGFs are crucial in exerting paracrine effects, whereas ROS signaling through GAP junctions induces senescence in nearby cells commonly referred to as bystander senescence [53, 286, 447]. TGF-β acts by regulating p21 and p15, whereas expression of SASP components is upregulated by activated inflammasome-mediated IL-1 signaling [286].

Conclusion

Senescence is a complex and highly regulated cellular process that initiates in response to diversified stimuli, that vary in nature, origin and intensity. These factors include oxidative stress, oncogene activation, loss of telomere structure and DNA damage. Depending on the nature of the stimulus, cells under stress launch a measured response by activating the relevant signaling cascades and effector proteins. Cells possess a robust and highly evolved redox system that mitigates the oxidative stress by neutralizing broad spectrum of reactive intermediates to maintain metabolic homeostasis, thereby acting as primary line of defense against the highly reactive oxidative radicles and accompanying cellular damage. Once the stress and stress induced damage becomes overwhelming and difficult to manage, the cell activates the signaling pathways that culminate in non-responsive cell cycle arrest. While the cell is experiencing high stress accompanied by cellular damage, the arrest of the cell cycle is highly significant as it prevents the transfer of damaged organelles and deleterious mutations from being transferred to daughter cells and may help the cell in diverting all its available resources towards the repair of damaged cellular components. During senescence the cell undergoes global changes in transcription, translation and other cellular processes to maintain proper energy status and divert the metabolic machinery towards the development of senescence associated secretory phenotype. The SASP consists of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines and other biologically active components that play a key role in attraction of immune cells and enhanced immune surveillance. One of the crucial effects of the senescent cells is SASP mediated bystander effect, where healthy cells adjacent to a senescent cell turn senescent, that in turn leads to loss of healthy cells, widespread tissue remodeling and dysfunction. The bystander effect too may have evolutionary significance, as the increased number of senescent cells release more immune modulating intermediates to generate a more robust signal for immune cell attraction that will be key for removal of the maladapted senescent cells. The SASP mediated attraction of immune cells results in removal of senescent cells and consequent reduction in inflammation. Besides the reduction in inflammation, the removal of senescent cells from the tissues protects the neighboring cells from inflammatory damage and relieves the senescent cell from receiving the persistent DDR stimuli via autocrine signaling. The SASP-mediated enhanced immune surveillance may be the highly evolved protective mechanism through which a senescent cell arranges or initiates its own removal to protect the other cells from damage.
