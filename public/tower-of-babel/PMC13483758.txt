Activation of c-Myc confers resistance to venetoclax via inhibition of Bim in t(8;21)-positive acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Zhao Yin, Zurong Yao, DanDan Chen, Dan Xu, Li Xuan, Qifa Liu, Baohong Ping, Guopan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeVenetoclax (VEN), a selective BCL-2 inhibitor, has improved outcomes in acute myeloid leukemia (AML). However, patients carrying the t(8;21)(q22;q22) translocation often show limited sensitivity to VEN-based therapy. This study aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanism of VEN resistance and explore rational combination strategies.Experimental approachMolecular and functional analyses were performed in t(8;21) AML models to examine the role of c-Myc activation in VEN response. Pharmacologic inhibition, genetic knockdown, and xenograft studies were used to assess the effects of c-Myc and its downstream effector Bim. The efficacy of VEN combined with homoharringtonine (HHT) was further evaluated.Key resultsVEN induced c-Myc expression in a dose-dependent manner, which was not suppressed by azacitidine. Inhibition or knockdown of c-Myc enhanced VEN-induced apoptosis. Mechanistically, c-Myc repressed the pro-apoptotic BH3-only protein Bim, reducing mitochondrial priming and conferring VEN resistance. HHT downregulated c-Myc and restored Bim expression, leading to synergistic anti-leukemic activity with VEN in vitro and in vivo.Conclusions and implicationsc-Myc activation is a key driver of VEN resistance in t(8;21) AML. HHT acts as a mechanistically complementary agent, restoring VEN sensitivity. These results provide a preclinical rationale for clinical evaluation of VEN–HHT combination therapy in genetically defined AML subsets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02994-x.

Introduction

The BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax (VEN) has considerably broadened the therapeutic repertoire for AML and improved patient outcome. Nonetheless, responses to VEN-based regimens remain heterogeneous, with genetic alterations emerging as a critical determinant of drug sensitivity or resistance. Molecular subgroups harboring IDH1/2, NPM1 mutations typically exhibit favorable responses, whereas those with FLT3-ITD, TP53, N/KRAS, PTPN11 often demonstrate limited response [1–7]. This variability may stem from compensatory reliance on alternative antiapoptotic proteins such as MCL-1 or BCL-xL [8], activation of alternative energy sources beyond OXPHOS, or the expansion of resistant subclones with distinct molecular profiles [9, 10]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms underlying the heterogeneity in VEN sensitivity across different AML subtypes remain incompletely understood. Thus, defining the genetic determinants of VEN resistance and their functional contributions is essential to optimize therapeutic strategies.

Recent studies, including our own observations in a limited cohort, have indicated that patients with t(8;21)(q22;q22)/AML1-ETO-positive AML (abbreviated as t(8;21) AML), a subtype classified as favorable-risk by current guideline [11], exhibit poor response to the treatment of VEN combined with azacitidine (AZA) (VA) [3, 12–15]. Notably, the addition of homoharringtonine (HHT) to VA (VAH) appears to improve outcomes in these patients [16], suggesting a unique biological vulnerability in this subset. c-Myc is known to be hyperactivated in t(8;21) AML [17–19], and its overexpression has been associated with chemoresistance and adverse prognosis [20, 21]. Emerging evidence also reported that targeting c-Myc expression to may enhance the pro-apoptotic effect of VEN [22–24], raising the possibility that MYC-driven signaling pathways contribute to the poor response to VA therapy in t(8;21) AML.

HHT is a clinically approved ribosome inhibitor that exerts anti-leukemic effects by targeting short-lived oncoproteins such as c-Myc and MCL-1 in AML cells [25]. Previous studies, including our own, have demonstrated that HHT potently suppresses MCL-1 expression, thereby synergizing with VEN to enhance the anti-leukemia activity [16, 26–28]. In t(8;21) AML, HHT-mediated MYC suppression occurs through dual mechanisms involving both transcriptional repression and protein degradation [29], suggesting that the addition of HHT to VA may provide a unique therapeutic advantage in this subtype. These observations underscore the need for further investigation into the role of c-Myc in VEN resistance and the mechanisms by which HHT overcomes it in t(8;21) AML.

In this study, we uncover a previously unrecognActivation of c-Myc confers resistance to venetoclax via inhibition of Bim in t(8;21)-positive acute myeloid leukemiaized mechanism by which c-Myc activation confers VEN resistance in t(8;21) AML cells through transcriptional repression of Bim, a critical mediator of VEN-induced apoptosis. Significantly, we demonstrate that HHT inclusion effectively counteracts this resistance by specifically targeting c-Myc. Our findings therefore reveal a novel pathway of VEN resistance in t(8;21) AML and, importantly, present a clinically actionable strategy to overcome it through rational combination therapy.

Methods

Cell lines and reagents

The following AML cell lines were used in this study: Kasumi-1 (CRL-2724, ATCC), SKNO-1(CTCC-004-0075, Meisen CTCC), THP-1 (TIB-202, ATCC), MOLM13 (C6600, Beyotime), MV4-11(CRL-9591, ATCC), U937 (CRL-1593.2, ATCC), K562(CCL-243). All cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (KGL1505, KeyGEN) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FSP500, ExCell) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (KGL1206, KeyGEN). The adriamycin-resistant HL60 cell line (HL60-ADR) was kindly provided by Prof. Hui Zeng [30] and cultured under the same conditions. For SKNO-1 cells, the medium was additionally supplemented with 10 ng/mL recombinant human G-CSF (abs04131, Absin). HEK293T cells (CRL-3216, ATCC) were cultured in DMEM medium (KGL1206, KeyGEN) instead of RPMI-1640, under otherwise identical supplementation conditions. All cell lines were authenticated by STR profiling and confirmed mycoplasma-free prior to use.VEN was purchased from Selleck (S8048). HHT (HY-14944), 10,058-F4 (HY-12702), A-1,331,852 (HY-19741) and S63845 (HY-100741) were purchased from MedChemExpress. Stock solutions for in vitro studies were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored at − 20℃ until further use.

Patients’ data and material

The bone marrow samples consisted of five from patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) t(8;21) AML and eight paired diagnostic-relapse samples. Mononuclear cells were isolated from these bone marrow samples using Ficoll-Paque Plus reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients/guardians. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Details of patients’ characteristics are listed in Table S1.

Assessment of apoptosis

The cells were seeded in 24-well plates with different concentrations of HHT, VEN, or the combination for 48 h. An identical volume of DMSO was used as a control and the final DMSO% was kept at ≤ 0.1%. Apoptosis was determined using the Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis Kit (556547, BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Synergy scores were obtained through SynergyFinder 3.0 [31], and the ZIP (zero interaction potency) score [32] was utilized to evaluate the performance of the combination treatment (a synergy score > 10 is considered as synergistic).

RNA transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Plus RNA Purification Kit (12183555CN, Invitrogen) and reverse transcribed into cDNA with random primers. A cDNA library was processed with NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (E7530). Samples were then analyzed on Illumina HiSeq2500 platform by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. Three independent samples per group were analyzed. Gene differential expression analysis was performed by DEseq2 between two different groups. The GSEA analysis was performed using GSEA software. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using STRING and visualized in Cytoscape. Hub genes were identified and ranked by degree score using CytoHubba.

Analysis of public gene expression datasets

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession numbers GSE13159 [33] and GSE6891 [34]. The following preprocessing steps were applied: probes corresponding to the gene were identified, and empty probes were eliminated. For the GSE6891 dataset, which comprises 461 AML samples, MYC expression levels were extracted after normalization and log2 transformation, and then compared between t(8;21)-positive (n = 35) and non‑t(8;21) AML(n = 426) samples. For GSE13159 dataset, we specifically selected patients annotated as de novo AML/ETO-positive for analysis. The correlation between MYC and BCL2L11 expression was evaluated statistically in R, with significance defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05, and was visually presented as a scatter plot generated using the ggplot2 package.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT–qPCR)

Total cellular RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol (15596018CN, Invitrogen). RT–qPCR was performed as previously described [35]. The qPCR primers used to amplify the indicated genes are listed in Table S2.

Western blots and co-immunoprecipitation

Immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed using an IP/Co-IP Kit (abs955, Absin) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, cells were washed and lysed in IP lysis buffer. Protein concentration was determined by BCA Protein Assay Kit (FD2001, Fudebio-tech). A total of 500 µg of each cleared lysate was incubated with 5ug of antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Protein A/G agarose beads were added to each tube and the samples were incubated again for 2–3 h at 4 °C. The bead-protein complexes were washed with cold wash buffer. The antibody-selected proteins were later eluted from the agarose beads and analyzed by western blot. An immunoprecipitation reaction was performed with isotype-specific non-related IgG as a negative control to check the nonspecific immunoprecipitation.

The primary antibodies used in these studies were: Bcl-2 (mouse, 1:1000, 15071), Bcl-xL (rabbit, 1:1000, 2764), Mcl-1 (rabbit, 1:1000, 39224), BIM (rabbit, 1:1000, 2933), BAX (rabbit, 1:1000, 5023), c-Myc (rabbit, 1:1000, 5605), caspase-3 (rabbit, 1:1000, 14220), Rabbit IgG Isotype Control (3900); (all from Cell Signaling Technology); BID (mouse, 1:100, sc514622), PUMA (mouse, 1:100, sc374223) (all from Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Plasmids and cell transfection

The plasmids shRNA targeting human c-Myc (shMYC-#1: 5’-CCCAAGGTAGTTA TCCTTAAA-3’; shMYC-#2: 5’-GCTTCACCAACAGGAACTATG-3’; shMYC-#3: 5’-GGAAACGACGAGAACAGTTGA-3’), human BCL2L11 (shBim-#1: 5’-GGCG TATTGGAGACGAGTTTA-3’, shBim-#2: 5’-CGATCCTCCAGTGGGTATTTC-3’, shBim-#3: 5’-GACCACCCACGAATGGTTATC-3’), as well as the non-targeting control shRNA, were purchased from Kidan Biosciences. Human c-Myc cDNA was purchased from Addgene and subcloned into the pCDH vector. To produce virus, the lentiviral expression vector and packaging vectors (pMD2.G and psPAX2) were transfected into HEK293T cells using PEI reagent (40816ES02, Yeasen). After collecting the viral supernatant fractions at 48 h and 72 h, cells were transduced overnight using 1 mL of virus supernatant and 5ug of polybrene (40804ES86, Yeasen). The transfected cells were then cultured for additional 48 h prior to selection with puromycin (60210ES25, Yeasen). The knockdown and overexpression efficiencies were validated by RT–qPCR or western blotting.

Cleavage under targets and release using nuclease (CUT&RUN)

The CUT&RUN Fast Kit (P-2028-24, Epigenteck) was used to perform CUT&RUN assays according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, approximately 1 million cells were incubated with anti-c-Myc antibodies (sc-40X, Santacruz, 2ug per assay) or anti-IgG (sc2025, Santa Cruz, 2ug per assay) at room temperature for 90 min. The enriched DNA was quantified by qPCR. 5% input was used as the control. The primers for the Bim promoter are listed in Table S2.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

293T cells were seeded into 24-well plates at 5 × 104 cells/well and co-transfected with overexpression plasmids for c-Myc and plasmids containing human Bim promoter fragments (pGL3-Bim-Luc) using Liposomal Transfection Reagent (40802ES, Yeasen), the TK-Renilla reporter (Public Protein/Plasmid Library) was co-transfected to each sample as an internal control. After 48 h transfection, cells were collected and the activities of Firefly and Renilla luciferase were measured by Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (SpectraMax i3X) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (11402ES60, Yeasen). The firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase for each well.

Animal studies

Male immunodeficient NCG mice (BOD-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/NjuCrl) with the age of six weeks were purchased from Guangzhou Yaokang Biological Co. Ltd. (body weights upon arrival 18–20 g) and raised in an SPF animal laboratory. After one week of acclimatization, the mice underwent sublethal total body irradiation (2 Gy) at an interval of 6 h prior to the intravenous injection of 5 × 106 Kasumi-1GFP cells via the tail vein. Engraftment of AML cells in the bone marrow was confirmed in at least three randomly selected mice before the remaining animals were randomly assigned to treatment groups (n = 5 per group): vehicle control, HHT (i.p. 1 mg/kg qd for 1 week), ABT-199 (p.o. 50 mg/kg qd for 2 weeks) or their combination. HHT was dissolved in normal saline, and ABT-199 was suspended with 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na). Mice were euthanized on the day after the final dose (day 15). Overall survival, histological parameters and the proportion of. GFP+ leukaemic cells in the bone marrow (as determined by flow cytometry) were monitored. Animal experiments were conducted according to the National Guidelines for Animal Usage in Research (China) with approval from the ethics committee of the Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University.

Statistical analyses

Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) as indicated in figure legends. Statistical differences between groups were assessed using two-tailed Student’s t-test for normally distributed data, or the Mann‑Whitney U test (two‑tailed) for non‑normally distributed data. for comparisons between two groups. One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s (vs. control) or Bonferroni’s (for inter-group comparisons) correction was used for multiple groups. Two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test was applied as indicated in the figure legends for multifactorial or repeated-measures designs. Correlation analyses were conducted using Pearson’s test, and survival analyses were performed with the log-rank test. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For DEG analysis, the significant DEGs were selected with the cut-off criteria P value < 0.05 and |logFC| ≥ 2. For GSEA analysis, FDR values < 0.25 were considered statistically significant.

Results

HHT enhanced the anti-leukemia effect of VEN in t(8;21) AML cells

Given the potential synergy between HHT and VEN suggested by our previous studies [36], we aimed to further validate their combined therapeutic effect in t(8;21) AML cells. The synergistic effect was assessed in Kasumi-1 and SKNO-1 cell lines (Fig. 1A-B) as well as in t(8;21)-positive primary cells (Fig. 1C). The combination of VEN plus HHT(VH) induced significantly greater apoptosis than either agent alone. Consistent with previous studies, we observed that AML1/ETO-positive patients who relapsed after VA exhibited significantly elevated expression levels of MCL1 and BCL-xL compared to their initial diagnosis, whereas BCL2 expression was not increased in most patients and was even downregulated in some cases (Fig. 1D). These findings align with existing literature, suggesting that HHT may enhance the anti-leukemic efficacy of VEN in part through downregulation the expression of BCL-xL and MCL1 [26–28, 36]. However, the synergy of VEN and HHT exceeded that of VEN combined with either MCL1 inhibitors (S63845) or BCL-xL inhibitors (A-1331852) in t(8,21) AML cells (Fig. 1E-G), indicating that the underlying mechanism may involve additional key factors beyond BCL-xL and MCL1 in this specific subtype. This prompted us to further investigate the molecular basis of this enhanced synergy.

Activation of c-Myc might contribute to VEN-resistance in t(8;21) AML and could be inhibited by HHT but not AZA

We delved deeper into investigating the mechanistic foundation of synergy between VEN and HHT, as well as the key factor mediating VEN resistance in t(8;21) AML. Initially, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on Kasumi-1 cells treated with control, VEN, HHT, and a combination treatment. Comparative transcriptomic analysis across the four experimental groups revealed significant transcriptional changes induced by the treatment combination versus control (Fig. 2A). The expression patterns of these differentially expressed genes are visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, CytoHubba network analysis was employed to identify the top 10 hub genes from the constructed protein-protein interaction network (Fig. 2C) the distribution of which is further illustrated in a volcano plot (Fig. 2D). Among these top hub genes, MYC was prioritized for further experimental validation and RT-qPCR analysis confirmed its particularly pivotal role in the underlying molecular mechanism (Figure S1A). In accordance with this, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) demonstrated a negative normalized enrichment score (NES) for MYC target genes (Fig. 2E and Table S3). Consistently, ETO-positive AML cells exhibited elevated c-Myc expression compared to non-ETO-positive AML cells (Fig. 2F and S2A). Notably, increased c-Myc levels were also observed in several non-ETO chemoresistant cell lines and, importantly, in patients with AML/ETO-positive relapsed disease (Fig. 2G). This observation was further validated by analysis of a public AML expression dataset (GSE6891) [34], in which t(8;21)-positive cases showed significantly higher MYC expression than non-t(8;21) AML cases (P = 0.0004; Fig. 2H). Collectively, these findings suggest that VH treatment exerts therapeutic effects, at least in part, through downregulation of MYC, which may serve as a critical survival driver in t(8;21) AML [17, 18].

Consistent with the transcriptomic findings, VH treatment significantly suppressed c-Myc expression at both the mRNA and protein levels in vitro (Fig. 3A-B). To exclude potential confounding effects from apoptosis, cells were co-treated with the pan-caspase inhibitor Q-VD-OPh (QVD) in combination with the indicated treatments (Fig. 3C). Under these conditions, the VH-induced downregulation of c-Myc remained evident, indicating that the reduction was not merely a consequence of apoptotic cell death.

To dissect the underlying mechanisms, we next examined whether HHT modulates c-Myc protein stability. Kasumi-1 cells were treated with HHT in combination with cycloheximide (CHX), a protein synthesis inhibitor, and c-Myc levels were assessed over time. We found that CHX further shortened the half-life of c-Myc in the presence of HHT. Notably, this destabilizing effect was partially reversed by the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 3D), suggesting that HHT promotes proteasomal degradation of c-Myc. Consistently, MG132 treatment also attenuated the effects of CHX in HHT-treated cells, further supporting the role of the proteasome in HHT-mediated c-Myc downregulation (Fig. 3E). In contrast, AVA alone or VA treatment failed to exert a comparable inhibitory effect on c-Myc expression (Fig. 3F). Intriguingly, VEN monotherapy induced a dose-dependent upregulation of c-Myc (Fig. 3G-H), suggesting a potential compensatory survival mechanism that may limit therapeutic efficacy. To functionally validate the role of c-Myc in VEN resistance, we employed pharmacological inhibition (10058-F4, a disruptor of c-Myc/Max dimerization) and genetic knockdown. Both strategies markedly sensitized t(8;21) AML cells to VEN-induced apoptosis, and c-Myc knockdown further restored chemosensitivity (Fig. 3I-L). Conversely, c-Myc overexpression conferred resistance to VEN (Fig. 3M-N). Collectively, these data demonstrate that c-Myc upregulation attenuates the anti-leukemic effects of VEN in t(8;21) AML. Importantly, this resistance mechanism was effectively counteracted by HHT, whereas AZA alone showed no such effect.

c-Myc might directly regulate Bim to decrease VEN-induced cell death in t(8;21) AML

The intrinsic apoptotic pathway, governed by BCL-2 family proteins, represents the primary mechanism of cell death induced by VEN-based therapy. Disruption of specific BH3-only proteins has been linked to reduce the anti-leukemic efficacy of VEN [37]. To investigate how individual BH3-only proteins contribute to apoptosis triggered by VEN alone or in combination with HHT, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays to assess their binding interactions with anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members in AML1/ETO-positive cell lines.

As shown in Fig. 4A, VEN monotherapy reduced Bim binding to BCL-2 while enhancing its association with MCL-1 or BCL-xL. In contrast, combination treatment with HHT decreased Bim binding to all three anti-apoptotic proteins (BCL-2, MCL-1 and BCL-xL), leading to elevated levels of free Bim and increased interaction with the pro-apoptotic effector BAX. Meanwhile, the binding profiles of two other BH3-only proteins, BID and PUMA, remained unchanged under either condition (Fig. 4B). To functionally assess the role of Bim, we performed genetic knockdown experiments and observed that Bim silencing significantly attenuated the sensitivity to VEN (Fig. 4C-E), confirming its critical role in mediating VEN-induced apoptotic effects in t(8;21) AML cells [38, 39]. Collectively, these findings suggest that enhanced binding of Bim to MCL-1 and BCL-xL may contribute to VEN resistance, a vulnerability that can be effectively targeted by the addition of HHT [16, 26, 27].

To further investigate whether c-Myc is involved in Bim-mediated cell death triggered by VEN in AML, we analyzed an external RNA-seq dataset comprising 68 treatment-naïve AML patients with AML1/ETO rearrangement. A significant inverse correlation was observed between c-Myc and Bim transcript levels (Pearson’s r = -0.51, p < 0.0001; Fig. 5A), suggesting that c-Myc may participate in the transcriptional regulation of Bim. We therefore aimed to determine whether c-Myc directly regulates BIM transcription in the context of AML1/ETO-positive AML. Initial experiments demonstrated that both pharmacological inhibition and genetic knockdown of MYC consistently led to a marked elevation in Bim expression, suggesting a negative regulatory relationship between MYC and Bim. (Fig. 5B-C). To assess the specificity of this regulatory relationship, we analyzed additional VEN-resistant cell lines with distinct genetic backgrounds, including MV4-11 cells harbor FLT3-ITD mutations (Figure S2B). While these models consistently exhibited MYC upregulation, they did not show the concomitant downregulation of Bim observed in AML1/ETO-positive cells (Fig. 5D). This dissociation between MYC induction and Bim suppression implies that the reliance on c-Myc mediated transcriptional control of Bim may be a unique feature of t(8;21) AML, likely attributable to its AML1/ETO-driven oncogenic program. Based on these findings, we next employed JASPAR to predict c-Myc-binding sites within the Bim promoter region. Subsequent CUT&RUN assays identified two functional binding sites where c-Myc enrichment significantly exceeded that of the IgG background control (Fig. 5E-F). Agarose gel electrophoresis (AGE) further indicated that the region between − 349 and − 360 bp in the Bim promoter is critical for c-Myc-mediated regulation in AML cells (Fig. 5G).

To determine whether c-Myc directly represses the Bim promoter, a full length of 2k bp (containing sites 1, 2) relative to the Bim transcription start site was selected cloned in the upstream of the firefly luciferase coding region in the vector pGL3 Basic. The resultant plasmid (Bim-Promoter) and c-Myc expression plasmid were co-transfected into 293T cells, and luciferase activity was measured by a luminometer to reflect Bim promoter activity. As shown in Fig. 5H, c-Myc overexpression substantially decreased the luciferase activity compared with the controls, indicating that c-Myc transcriptionally represses the Bim promoter.

We next established a double-knockdown model to investigate whether the enhanced venetoclax sensitivity induced by c-Myc knockdown depends on the consequent upregulation of Bim. By depleting Bim in Kasumi-1 cells with stable c-Myc knockdown (Fig. 5I), we found that Bim knockdown abolished the sensitizing effect of c-Myc knockdown and reduced venetoclax sensitivity (Fig. 5J). These findings suggest Bim serves as a potential mediator of c-Myc-driven venetoclax resistance in t(8,21)AML cells, which occurs through transcriptional repression of Bim.

The combination therapy exhibits limited toxicity to normal hematopoietic cells in vitro and potent anti-leukemic activity in vivo

To assess the impact of VEN and HHT combination on normal hematopoiesis, we performed in vitro colony formation assays using CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) isolated from mobilized peripheral blood of three healthy donors (Fig. 6A). Following treatment with VEN and HHT, progenitor activity was evaluated based on colony-forming capacity. The combination treatment showed minimal inhibition of colony formation compared to untreated controls, indicating a favorable safety profile toward normal hematopoietic progenitors (Fig. 6B). Consistently, the combination exerted significantly weaker cytotoxic effects on healthy human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) than on leukemia cells (Figs. 1C and 6C), underscoring its selective anti-leukemic activity. Given its selective cytotoxicity and potent in vitro efficacy against leukemia cells, we next evaluated the therapeutic potential of the VEN/HHT combination in vivo using a cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) model. Immunodeficient NCG mice engrafted with Kasumi-1 cells were randomized to receive vehicle control, HHT alone, VEN alone, or the combination regimen (Fig. 6D). Notably, combination therapy significantly reduced the leukemic burden in the bone marrow (Fig. 6E-F), attenuated tumor infiltration in the liver and spleen (Fig. 6G–I), and extended overall survival compared to either monotherapy or vehicle control (Fig. 6J). Importantly, the combination was well tolerated, with no overt signs of toxicity observed. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the combination of HHT and VEN exerts a strong synergistic anti-leukemic effect in vivo, supporting its potential as a therapeutic strategy for AML.

Discussion

A growing body of evidence underscores the critical impact of genetic profiles on the response to VEN-based regimens in AML. However, the mechanistic pathways through which specific genetic abnormalities drive clinical resistance to VEN are still not fully elucidated. We previously reported that patients with t(8;21) AML exhibit poor responses to the VA regimen but derive significant benefit from the addition of HHT [15]. This specific subtype offers a unique opportunity to investigate the determinants of VEN sensitivity. Our subsequent investigation identified the aberrant activation of the c-Myc/Bim axis as a pivotal mechanism of VEN resistance in this genetic context, which can be effectively counteracted by HHT.

The AML1/ETO fusion protein is known to dysregulate key transcriptional networks, including aberrant activation of c-Myc [17, 18, 40]. While separate lines of evidence have implicated c-Myc inhibition as a therapeutic strategy to restore sensitivity to VEN in AML [22–24], a direct mechanistic link within a defined genetic subtype like t(8;21) AML has been lacking. In the present study, we demonstrated that c-Myc activation is a pivotal driver of VEN resistance in t(8;21) AML, pharmacological inhibition of c-Myc potently restored VEN sensitivity. This observation led us to investigate the mechanism by which c-Myc mediates resistance in this setting.

Mechanistically, our data revealed that VEN displaced Bim from BCL-2 which was accompanied by increased binding of Bim to BCL-xL and MCL-1 This observation underscores the central role of Bim in VEN-induced apoptosis, as a high-affinity binding partner to all anti-apoptotic proteins [38, 39]. Bim serves as a critical mediator of mitochondrial apoptosis, and its sequestration by compensatory upregulated anti-apoptotic proteins may limit the efficacy of VEN monotherapy. Given the pivotal function of Bim in this process, we questioned whether its expression or availability is dysregulated in the context of t(8;21) AML. Accumulating evidence from other tumor contexts suggests that c-Myc can suppress Bim expression, thereby promoting tumor cell survival [41, 42]. We therefore hypothesized that c-Myc may drive VEN resistance in t(8;21) AML through transcriptional repression of Bim.

To test this, we performed genetic rescue experiments. While c-Myc knockdown sensitized cells to VEN, subsequent knockdown of Bim in this context significantly restored resistance, confirming that this resistance is mediated, at least in part, through the c-Myc/Bim axis. The specificity of this mechanism was further supported by observations in a distinct genetic context: in FLT3-ITD-mutated AML cells with acquired resistance to VEN, elevated c-Myc expression was not accompanied by Bim suppression. This dissociation indicates that while c-Myc upregulation may represent a convergent stress response across different AML subtypes, its engagement of Bim repression as a dominant resistance mechanism is likely specific to the AML1/ETO-driven oncogenic program in t(8;21) AML.

Consistent with the sequestration effect observed in our Co-IP studies, we found that combined inhibition of BCL-2 with MCL-1 or BCL-xL was insufficient to fully overcome resistance in t(8;21) cells. This aligns with our proposed mechanism: in this genetic context, c-Myc-driven Bim repression limits the pool of available activator protein, thereby blunting the apoptotic response even when other anti-apoptotic proteins are inhibited. In contrast, we identified HHT as a clinically applicable agent that effectively targets this vulnerability through a dual mechanism. HHT not only inhibits MCL-1 and BCL-xL but also suppresses c-Myc expression, thereby relieving Bim repression, increasing free Bim to engage BAX, and restoring mitochondrial apoptosis. These mechanistic insights explain our observation that the VH combination exhibited superior synergy compared to combinations with selective MCL-1 or BCL-xL inhibitors (Fig. 6K), and they provide a rationale for the enhanced efficacy of the VAH regimen over VA or AZA-containing regimens in this patient population.

Importantly, the therapeutic potential of this approach is supported by its favorable safety profile. In our studies, the VEN + HHT combination demonstrated selective anti-leukemic activity, with minimal impact on normal hematopoiesis and weaker cytotoxic effect on healthy donor PBMCs. In vivo, the combination was well tolerated, with no histopathological evidence of liver injury. More importantly, in our phase 2 [16] and real-world studies [15] of VAH regimen in patients with R/R-AML, preliminary safety data indicate that treatment-related myelosuppression of HHT combined with VA was manageable with standard supportive care, and the overall toxicity profile was comparable to that of VA alone. A completed randomized controlled trial (NCT05457361) will provide additional safety data in the future. Collectively, these findings suggest that the combination of VEN with HHT has an acceptable safety profile and warrants further clinical development.

While our study highlights a novel mechanism supporting the association between t(8;21) and c-Myc-mediated BIM suppression, further work is required to clarify the direct causal role of RUNX1-RUNX1T1. Specifically, loss- or gain- of function experiments targeting the fusion oncoprotein, as well as chromatin analyses to assess direct promoter binding, are needed. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and future perturbation studies are needed to validate the role of RUNX1-RUNX1T1 in this resistance mechanism.

In summary, our findings indicate that c-Myc-associated BIM suppression may contribute to venetoclax resistance in t(8;21) AML. Targeting this pathway with HHT represents a promising strategy to overcome resistance and improve outcomes for this molecularly defined patient subset.
