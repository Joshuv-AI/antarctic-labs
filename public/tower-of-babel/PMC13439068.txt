Direction-resolved nanoscale optical imaging with near-nanometer resolution by emerging infrared torsional force microscopy
Authors: Yonatan Gazit, Son T. Le, Aubrey T. Hanbicki, Adam L. Friedman, Min Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Resolving nanoscale light-matter interactions requires both high spatial resolution and anisotropic sensitivity to in-plane and out-of-plane optical responses. We introduce torsional force microscopy-infrared microscopy, a new optical imaging technique that combines cantilever torsional dynamics with a nonlinear frequency-mixing scheme to map both in-plane and out-of-plane photothermal signals. Using birefringent mica as a model system, we resolve distinct in-plane and out-of-plane vibrational responses and reconstruct the anisotropic strain distribution of nanobubbles, in excellent agreement with simulation. Furthermore, we demonstrate near-nanometer ( ~ 1 nm) spatial resolution in optical imaging of twisted bilayer graphene, enabling site-resolved spectroscopy within individual moiré cells. Energy-dependent imaging further reveals intra-unit cell optical features, highlighting the role of competing physical processes in a moiré lattice modulating its optical properties. By providing a direct, anisotropy-resolved view of nanoscale optical heterogeneities, this emerging infrared torsional force microscopy establishes a powerful platform for probing, understanding, and ultimately engineering light-matter interactions in complex quantum materials.

Introduction

Nanoscale optical imaging is a powerful tool for probing light-matter interactions, resolving spatially localized optical responses that cannot be accessed with conventional far-field methods. Integrating atomic force microscopy (AFM) with optics, such as scattering-type scanning near-field optical microscopy (s-SNOM) and infrared nanospectroscopy (AFM-IR), enables exploration of phenomena including phonon polaritons, excitonic resonances, and nanoscale absorption heterogeneity, with broad applications spanning chemical reactions, biology, and materials science1–7. However, existing AFM-based optical techniques are primarily sensitive to a sample’s out-of-plane response, leaving distinct and complementary in-plane properties largely inaccessible. Additionally, their spatial resolution remains constrained by the AFM tip apex. There is thus a pressing need for ambient-condition methods that combine high spatial resolution, directional sensitivity, and broadband optical access. Previous attempts to address these challenges often required specialized environments, complex instrumentation, or sacrifice optical contrast, thereby limiting insight into active light-matter interactions8–10.

Herein, we introduce infrared torsional force microscopy (TFM-IR), a new optical imaging technique that extends TFM11,12 to optical measurements by exploiting the cantilever’s torsional resonance under optical excitation, enabling nanometer-scale optical imaging resolution and selective detection of both lateral (in-plane) and vertical (out-of-plane) components of optically induced forces. By harnessing the inherent anisotropy of tip-sample torsional interactions, directional sensitivity can be selectively enhanced through nonlinear frequency mixing between the cantilever’s torsional resonance modes and the laser repetition rate. This capability enables the first ambient-condition nanoscale direction-resolved photothermal mapping without compromising the broadband spectral access and quantitative detection advantages of standard AFM-IR. We demonstrate reconstruction of in-plane and out-of-plane strain fields in a mica nanobubble with excellent agreement with finite element method (FEM) simulation. Energy-dependent nanobubble imaging reveals variation of anisotropic strain distributions. Furthermore, TFM-IR achieves near-nanometer spatial resolution, enabling spatially resolved spectroscopy within a single moiré unit cell and energy-dependent imaging of twisted bilayer graphene for the first time. Together, these results establish TFM-IR as a broadly applicable technique for nanoscale anisotropic optical imaging under ambient conditions. It offers a new dimension of insight into photothermal and optomechanical interactions with unprecedented spatial resolution, opening a new horizon of nanoscale optical study for diverse areas ranging from two-dimensional materials and nanophotonics to strain engineering and quantum materials13.

Results

Principles of TFM-IR and direction-resolved nanoscale optical imaging

Figure 1a illustrates the schematic setup of TFM-IR. Briefly, a pulsed laser source with repetition rate flaser is coupled to the AFM tip in either top- or bottom- illumination configurations. The tip is mounted on a holder equipped with two piezoelectric actuators at the frequency fm in an out-of-phase configuration, enabling controlled torsional excitation of the cantilever with a tunable driving amplitude. The cantilever motion is monitored by a position sensitive quadrant detector (PSD), whose vertical DC deflection provides tip-sample feedback while the horizontal AC deflection is demodulated by employing multiple lock-in amplifiers for monitoring the driven torsional oscillation amplitude and phase as well as for detecting TFM-IR signals arising from different frequency intermodulation schemes, as exemplified in Fig. 1b. Importantly, in TFM-IR the tip-sample feedback and the driven torsional motion are independently controlled through orthogonal mechanical modes. This decoupling allows the optical signal intensity to be tuned and optimized via the torsional drive amplitude without perturbing the tip-sample feedback. It is worth noting that in conventional AFM-IR and sSNOM techniques based on flexural cantilever motion, the tapping amplitude can influence both the feedback and the optical signal readout. To demonstrate the feasibility of TFM-IR and compare its performance with existing AFM based optical imaging techniques, Fig. 1c presents a typical TFM-IR image of a 64 nm- thick hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) flake with natural boron abundance exfoliated on a ZnSe substrate. hBN is a polar dielectric material that support surface phonon polaritons (PhPs) through strong photon-phonon coupling within the lower (760–820 cm–1) and upper (1365–1610 cm–1) Reststrahlen bands15,16. Prior studies using either sSNOM or AFM-IR have established that PhPs can be launched by either metallic AFM tips or hBN edges, creating oscillatory optical contrasts across the flake surface17–20. The TFM-IR image in Fig. 1c, acquired at 1455 cm−1 within the upper Reststrahlen band, exhibits oscillatory fringes running parallel to the crystal edges. Line profiles taken perpendicular to the edge (Fig. 1c) show that the fringes are strongest near the flake boundary and gradually decay with increasing distance.

A distinctive feature consistently observed in our TFM-IR measurements is the presence of beating patterns in the oscillatory signal, with two distinct periodicities. The shorter-wavelength component decays more rapidly from the edge. Fits to the line profiles (Supplementary Note 1) confirms that the periodicities differ by roughly a factor of two, corresponding to the full and half wavelengths of the hBN PhPs. This beating is caused by the interference of edge-launched and tip-launched, edge-reflected PhPs17–22. For comparison, conventional tapping AFM-IR only revealed edge-launched PhPs, corresponding to the full periodicity of PhPs (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, we systematically investigated the effects of torsional driving amplitude and feedback conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2). The TFM-IR signal scales strongly with the torsional drive, but is nearly independent of feedback settings, further supporting the working principle outlined in Fig. 1a and 1b.

Resolving anisotropic strain at the nanoscale: experiment and simulation

To highlight the unique capability of TFM-IR in probing nanoscale anisotropic properties, we selected natural muscovite mica as an example, which is a well-known birefringent material that exhibits characteristic in-plane and out-of-plane refractive indices in the spectra range of 900−1100 cm–1 (ref. 24,25). Conventional methods for studying birefringence or optical anisotropy, such as ellipsometry and polarization-dependent Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, are fundamentally limited in spatial resolution by far-field diffraction. More recently, techniques that combine polarized light with scanning probes, including sSNOM and AFM-IR, have been developed to overcome this limitation, yet often require careful consideration of unavoidable tip-induced perturbations to the incident polarization state or specialized instrumentation adapted to particular materials8,9,26–28. Importantly, this variability does not alter the relative ordering of out-of-plane versus in-plane modes, which is reproducible across studies. Taken together, these results provide strong experimental validation of our TFM-IR technique and demonstrate excellent agreement with theoretical predictions, highlighting its potential to enable new applications in diverse materials systems through tailored nonlinear frequency mixing schemes.

The in-plane and out-of-plane TFM-IR images in Fig. 2 reveal the intrinsic anisotropic strain distribution of a nanobubble, establishing TFM-IR as a fingerprinting tool for a sample’s anisotropic thermo-mechanical response. Qualitatively, this behavior arises because wavelength-dependent absorption and subsequent thermalization generates both vertical and lateral expansion fields (Supplementary Note 3). Changing the optical excitation wavelength alters their relative contributions, leading to a wavelength-dependent redistribution of strain. Figure 3 shows the evolution of reconstructed in-plane and out-of-plane TFM-IR maps as a function of laser wavelength. Across the probed spectral window, both reconstructions consistently display annulus strain features centered on the nanobubble, confirming that the underlying mechanical strain distribution is robust and primarily geometry-driven rather than tied to a single vibrational resonance. Nevertheless, finer details, such as spatial extent, symmetry and intensity distribution, depend strongly on excitation frequency. For example, the absolute amplitude of both in-plane and out-of-plane maps varies with wavenumber, likely reflecting variation in IR absorption strength and photoelastic coupling to distinct vibrational modes (Supplementary Note 3). At certain wavenumbers (e.g., 990 cm−1 and 1020 cm−1), the in-plane annulus appears sharper and narrower, whereas at others the response is broader and less defined. This suggests that the effective interaction volume and strain readout are modulated by phonon-mode dependent resonant enhancement. Interestingly, the out-of-plane channel occasionally exhibits strong azimuthal asymmetry across the nanobubble (e.g., at 995 cm−1), particularly in the region adjacent to the positive annulus rather than as a direct modulation of the positive band itself. This points to local polarization sensitivity tied to specific out-of-plane resonance. Overall, observed spectral variations in intensity, sharpness and symmetry underscore how strain couples differently to distinct vibrational modes. We note that, although the mica nanobubble in Fig. 2a is employed as an example to demonstrate the unique capability of TFM-IR to resolve nanoscale anisotropy, our conclusions are not specific to a single structure. Supplementary Fig. 6b, acquired simultaneously with Supplementary Fig. 6a at 1045 cm−1, shows that all the mica nanobubbles exhibit similar optical features, including a reduction of optical intensity at the nanobubble center, confirming the underlying mechanical strain mechanism for a nanobubble structure. Furthermore, Supplementary Fig. 9 provides data from another nanobubble with different height and width, obtained using the same in-plane and out-of-plane imaging and reconstruction methods. The corresponding results reveal consistent fundamental in-plane and out-of-plane radial distributions, as in Figs. 2 and 3, with additional fine-scale asymmetries and inhomogeneous features attributed to the specific geometry of each nanobubble. The observation of reproducible features across different nanobubbles both validates the underlying physics and our data analysis approach and highlights the generality of TFM-IR as a sensitive tool for probing nanoscale anisotropy. By combining spectral tunability with anisotropic spatial resolution TFM-IR is uniquely suited to capture robust, geometry-induced strain morphology while simultaneously revealing phonon resonance dependent enhancements or suppressions of specific strain components. Although more comprehensive simulation combining optical absorption and strain-field calculations will be needed to fully elucidate the mechanisms behind the observed spectral evolution, Figs. 2 and 3 together establish that TFM-IR offers a powerful new route to map strain fields and probe anisotropic coupling pathways at the nanoscale.

Near-nanometer spatial resolution in TFM-IR imaging

In addition to enabling nanoscale anisotropy-sensitive imaging, TFM-IR builds on the intrinsic high-resolution capability of TFM, which has achieved atomic lattice resolution under ambient conditions11. However, extending this level of resolution to optical response measurements has not yet been achieved35. Moiré lattices, which are formed when atomically thin crystals are stacked with a slight twist or lattice mismatch, are a class of potentially useful quantum material interactions36–39. These long-wavelength superlattices dramatically modify electronic bands, phonons, and exciton states, creating varied phenomena such as correlated insulating states, unconventional superconductivity, and tunable optical responses40–46. It is worth noting that, although most vibration modes in the mid-IR regime are IR inactive in far-field optical spectroscopy of pristine monolayer graphene due to symmetry constraints and strict optical selection rules, both experimental and theoretical studies have shown that under symmetry-breaking conditions such as twisting, stacking, straining or electrostatic gating in bilayer and multilayer graphene, phonon dispersion can be modified and enhanced with manifestation of folded phonons47–53. Moreover, tip-based near-field experimental techniques can further relax the selection rules for phonons originally at finite momentum, thereby enabling mid-IR optical activity in bilayer and multilayer graphene systems, even for modes that are otherwise forbidden in pure monolayer graphene3,54,55. Resolving these moiré patterns with optical information at atomic or near-nanometer resolution is therefore impactful, as it facilitates direct mapping of spatially varying optical absorption, exciton localization, and strain-modulated interactions within a single moiré cell56. Experimentally, achieving this has been highly challenging. For example, sSNOM studies of twisted bilayer graphene typically achieve spatial resolutions of a few tens to hundreds of nanometers, with the tip apex size imposing a fundamental limitation54–59. We performed a control experiment by tuning the optical excitation to an off-resonance wavelength while keeping all other imaging conditions identical. In this case, the previously observed optical features disappeared (Supplementary Fig. 10), confirming that the TFM-IR signals in Fig. 4a are not caused by mechanical crosstalk from TFM, but instead originate from a genuine optical response. Notably, without applying any image filters or processing, the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the smallest features in the line profile of Fig. 4a is ~ 1 nm, which is an order of magnitude smaller than the typical radius of tip apex ( ~ 25 nm) that was utilized for acquiring the data. The underlying mechanism of the observed super-resolution in TFM-IR imaging remains to be fully elucidated and will require systematic studies and modeling, but it is likely linked to the unique tip-sample interactions that govern the intrinsic resolution of TFM11. The spatial resolution in TFM-IR is qualitatively determined by the detection mechanism, which transduces localized optical responses into torsional motion of the cantilever. Unlike conventional sSNOM and AFM-IR techniques, where the measured optical signal is effectively averaged over a finite tip-sample interaction volume and is thus limited by the tip apex size and thermal diffusion, the TFM-IR signal originates from cantilever’s torsional motion driven by lateral force asymmetries (i.e., torque) within the tip-sample contact region (see Supplementary Note 2). This detection mechanism is inherently sensitive to spatial variations and gradients in the photothermal response, rather than its absolute magnitude. Such gradient-sensitive detection suppresses slowly varying background contributions while enhancing localized variations, thereby increasing sensitivity to high-spatial frequency components and enabling a spatial resolution much smaller than the nominal tip-sample interaction size.

The exceptional spatial resolution in TFM-IR imaging as demonstrated in Fig. 4a immediately opens new opportunities for spatially resolved optical studies. To explore the intra-cell variation in greater detail, we performed spatially resolved spectroscopy at 15 different positions spanning from the edges to the center of a single moiré unit cell (Fig. 4b). Remarkably, the spectra segregate cleanly into three distinct clusters according to their spectral amplitude. Specifically, the six edges of the moiré honeycomb lattice group into three pairs of neighboring edges, with each pair exhibiting similar amplitude, while the cell center also shows the largest overall amplitude. This clear grouping highlights a pronounced site dependence of optical response within a single moiré unit. Qualitatively, such spatial variation likely arises from differences in local stacking configurations (e.g., AA vs. AB/BA) across the moiré pattern, which are known to modulate interlayer coupling, electronic density of states, and light-matter interactions41,61. The emergence of distinct amplitude groups within a single cell may reflect the discrete set of symmetry-related local stacking environments, where paired edges correspond to equivalent regions of the moiré potential landscape, and the center corresponds to a high-symmetry stacking that maximizes optical response. Our observation of inhomogeneous optical response within a single moiré cell is significant. It demonstrates that the optical properties of moiré materials can vary dramatically even at the sub-unit cell scale, underscoring the importance of nanoscale optical probes in uncovering hidden intra-cell heterogeneity.

Beyond the super-resolution capability of TFM-IR imaging, which enables fine optical details within a single moiré cell to be resolved, energy-dependent high-resolution imaging can also be performed. Several mechanisms have been theoretically proposed to account for energy-dependent nanoscale optical heterogeneity, arising from the interplay of stacking configuration, strain and interlayer electronic coupling. The aforementioned local stacking configuration can lead to spatial variations in interlayer hybridization, symmetry, and local electronic structure, which in turn modulate local optical absorption and electron-phonon coupling, with supporting evidence from the site-resolved spectroscopy in Fig. 4b. Importantly, such variations can alter both the spectral weight and selection rules of optical transitions, resulting in spatially varying infrared responses that evolve with excitation energy62,63. In addition, strain fields induced by lattice relaxation and interlayer interactions are non-uniformly distributed across the moiré unit cell, often concentrating near domain boundaries or specific stacking regions. These strain variations can locally shift phonon frequencies and modify electron-phonon coupling strength, thereby changing the resonance conditions for infrared absorption64,65. As a result, different regions within a moiré unit cell can exhibit distinct spectral responses, giving rise to energy-dependent contrast in TFM-IR imaging. Furthermore, the interlayer electronic coupling varies spatially across the moiré lattice, leading to direct modulation of the local band structure, including the emergence of flat bands and spatially localized electronic states. These variations can impact optical transitions, even in the mid-infrared regime, where dipole-allowed transitions are typically weak. In particular, symmetry breaking and interlayer hybridization can activate otherwise weak or forbidden transition through indirect processes such as phonon-assisted absorption or coupling to localized electronic states, contributing to spatially heterogeneous optical responses, which are inherently energy dependent63,66. Importantly, these mechanisms are strongly intertwined at the microscopic level and collectively govern a spatially and spectrally varying optical response, providing the basis for the wavelength-dependent phenomena observed in Fig. 5. The preservation of moiré periodicity across all excitation wavelengths in the TFM-IR images further suggests that the observed responses arise from competing processes that vary within a single unit cell, while the emergency of energy-dependent local optical motifs and symmetries reflect domain-specific responses. For example, dot-like hotspots with two-fold symmetry in Fig. 5a and 5c may be associated with domain junctions where three stacking registries meet in a honeycomb lattice, whereas triangular patches in Fig. 5b and 5d may indicate an extended sub-domain response arising from competition between two inequivalent sub-domains (AB vs. BA) with distinct complex susceptibilities65,67–70. A comprehensive theoretical framework that integrates strain-field distributions, stacking-dependent electronic structure, and optical response will be necessary to fully elucidate the sub-moiré-scale optical features and their energy dependence observed in Figs. 4 and 5. In particular, capturing the interplay of interlayer hybridization, symmetry breaking, and electron-phonon coupling across the moiré unit cell likely calls for multi-scale approaches that bridge continuum descriptions with atomistic modeling. The experimental capabilities demonstrated here, combining ultra-high spatial resolution with spectral sensitivity in TFM-IR imaging, provide a new opportunity to directly probe these coupled effects, thereby offering a platform to guide and benchmark future theoretical developments.

Discussion

We have established TFM-IR imaging as a versatile, ambient condition platform for nanoscale optical characterization. By harnessing a cantilever’s torsional driving and detection in a nonlinear frequency mixing scheme, this approach enables mapping of in-plane and out-of-plane photothermal response, granting direct access to nanoscale anisotropies (e.g., local strain), a capability largely inaccessible to conventional sSNOM and AFM-IR methods1. Furthermore, achieving near-nanometer ( ~ 1 nm) spatial resolution, we demonstrate in bilayer graphene moiré lattices that site-resolved spectroscopy within a single moiré unit cell reveals distinct, spatially dependent optical responses. Moreover, energy-dependent imaging coupled with ultra-high spatial resolution uncovers sub-unit cell optical variations, shedding light on the complex interplay of different physics including local stacking, strain, and electronic structure in moiré lattice. Looking forward, TFM-IR offers an unprecedented window into site-specific and anisotropic light-matter interactions across a broad range of quantum and van der Waals materials71,72. The ability to directly correlate nanoscale optical response with local structural and electronic heterogeneity at near-atomic scales provides a pathway to not only map but also ultimately engineer site-specific functionalities73,74. Beyond fundamental insights, the combination of anisotropy-resolved detection, quantitative spectral fingerprinting, and nanometer spatial precision positions TFM-IR as a promising characterization platform for advanced nanomanufacturing and semiconductor metrology, where accurate monitoring of strain, interface chemistry, and materials anisotropy is critical for device reliability and performance75. While the present implementation of TFM-IR operates in contact mode, the generic decoupling between tip-sample feedback and torsional-based optical detection through orthogonal mechanical modes immediately suggests a natural extension to tapping or intermittent-contact imaging12. Such developments could further broaden their applicability across diverse materials systems. By providing direction-resolved, broadband optical information under ambient conditions, TFM-IR bridges the gap between laboratory-scale nanoscience and industrial-scale process control, offering powerful framework for guiding the design, optimization, and quality assurance of next generation nanoelectronic, nanophotonic and quantum devices, where local optical properties and functionalities are dictated by sub- unit cell variations37,76.

Methods

Materials

The hBN flakes (PT110A) used for PhPs imaging were obtained from Momentive Performance Materials. The mica used in this study is natural muscovite mica, iron- and copper-stained with exceptional density, purchased from Asheville-Schoonmaker Mica Co. Both hBN and mica were mechanically exfoliated 3–5 times using Nitto SPV224 PCV vinyl surface protection tape and subsequently transferred onto optically polished, uncoated ZnSe substrates, which were cleaned using First-Contact Polymer Solution.

The twisted bilayer graphene sample was prepared using a modified tear-and-stack method (Supplementary Fig. 11)77–79. During fabrication, a PDMS/PPC stamp mounted on a glass slide was first used to pick up a ~ 100 nm thick hBN flake, followed by the first graphene layer. The second graphene layer was then picked up after a controlled rotation of approximately 2.4°, thereby defining the twist angle between the two graphene layers. The hBN flake was intentionally misaligned relative to the graphene layers to minimize substrate-induced moiré effects. The assembled stack was then manually delaminated from the PDMS, flipped so that the bilayer graphene faced upward, and transferred onto a polished, uncoated ZnSe substrate, which were cleaned using First-Contact Polymer Solution. Finally, the sample was annealed at 250 °C for 2 h to remove polymer residues.

All samples were mounted on a home-built sample stage using double-sided carbon tape. Moiré imaging was performed with PPP-NCHPt tips (from NanoAndMore USA Corp). Mica and hBN imaging were done with PPP-CONTSCAu tips (from NanoAndMore USA Corp). Cantilever contact force was calculated by engaging the tip on a clean Si wafer, performing a single height ramp, and fitting the contact curve’s slope to get the deflection sensitivity in mV per nanometer moved vertically (mV/nm). PPP tips come with the manufacturer’s measured spring-constant (N/m) for each tip. Dividing the second by the first gives a direct conversion between the deflection setpoint and the tip-sample force.

TFM-IR measurements

TFM-IR measurements were performed on a Bruker Dimension Icon AFM equipped with custom-designed optical path and a 3D-printed sample stage that enables optical excitation from either above or below the sample. For moiré imaging, once the tip was engaged with the sample, the driving amplitude and deflection setpoint were incrementally optimized to achieve the desired contrast. Optimal imaging conditions were typically obtained with contact forces in the range of 150–250 nN and driving amplitudes of 20–30 mV. For hBN and mica measurements, the driving amplitude and contact force were set to 20 mV and 50 nN, respectively, and subsequently adjusted to optimize the TFM-IR signal. The cantilever’s driving frequency was controlled via an external program interfaced with a RIGOL DG1022 dual-output function generator. One output channel provided a sinusoidal signal at the specified lock-in reference frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{{lock}-{in}}$$\end{document}flock−in, corresponding to a torsional resonance distinct from that used for TFM imaging, and was sent to a Stanford Research Systems’ SR844 RF Lock-In amplifier. The second output channel generated TTL pulses at frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{{laser}}$$\end{document}flaser to trigger the IR source. The horizontal signal from the PSD was routed through the Bruker’s Signal Access Module (SAM) to the SR844, and the demodulated output was fed into the NanoScope 6 controller to generate TFM-IR images simultaneously with other imaging channels in real-time. During systematic studies involving variation of optical parameters (e.g., excitation wavelength or power), the simultaneously acquired TFM and height images were continuously monitored to ensure consistency and to exclude artifacts arising from changes in imaging conditions.

Three tunable pulsed IR sources were used. Two were complimentary Daylight Solutions’ MIRCat quantum cascade lasers (QCLs). The first covered 860−1479 cm−1 (6.9–11.6 µm), and the second spanned 1480–2222 cm−1 (4.5–6.6 µm), both with linewidths <1 cm−1. These two QCLs have a maximum repetition rate of 2 MHz and a maximum duty cycle of 20%. The third source was a Stuttgart Instruments’ optical parametric oscillator (OPO)-based 40 MHz laser, tunable from 700–18,000 nm. The OPO output was modulated in burst mode using an acoustic-optical modulator, enabling customization of waveform, duty cycle, and repetition rate up to 3 MHz. Typical laser output power for TFM-IR measurements in current study was 5–20 mW.

To maintain consistent beam alignment during wavelength tuning, broadband optical components were used throughout the optical path. Specifically, the laser beam was collimated using a pair of 50 mm focal-length, 3−12 µm antireflection-coated lenses (Edmund Optics #87-947), and directed to the AFM tip via a series of broadband silver mirrors (Newport 10D20ER.2). A broadband gold-coated fast steering mirror (Optics In Motion OIM5001) was used for fine alignment, followed by focusing with a 15 mm focal-length gold-coated OAP (Thorlabs MPD00M9-M01), ensuring stable and consistent alignment across all wavelengths.

FEM simulations

Nanobubble simulations were performed with COMSOL Multiphysics 5.0 with the stationary Structural Mechanics module. COMSOL’s built-in parameters for mica, including Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and density were utilized in all calculations. The nanobubble geometry was defined by importing an experimentally measured height profile (Supplementary Fig. 8) as a preformation on a flat disk of mica, with a constant internal pressure applied within the nanobubble. An extra fine mesh was employed to ensure numerical accuracy for all the computations. Strain maps were obtained by calculating the resulting in-plane and out-of-plane displacement fields at the nanobubble surface.
