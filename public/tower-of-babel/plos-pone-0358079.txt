Source node management in complex water network based on cascading failure and bidirectional risk transmission: The case of Henan Province

Junhua Zhang, Ran Jing, Jing Dai, Yaohong Yang, Runpeng Wei, Junyan Gao

ABSTRACT
Water source nodes within water network are subject to dual pressures: the propagation of failure risks and reverse feedback from fluctuations in user demand. As the connectivity of water network and the relationship between water supply and consumption become increasingly complex, there is an urgent need to elucidate the patterns of bidirectional risk transmission in complex water network and to effectively manage water source nodes. Taking the water network of Henan Province as a case study, improved forward and reverse cascade failure models were established respectively. The criticality of water source nodes in forward risk propagation and their sensitivity to pressure in reverse feedback were comprehensively identified. Spearman correlation analysis and K-means clustering were employed to classify water source nodes into ‘criticality+sensitivity’ two-dimensional clusters. The results indicate that forward risk transmission exhibits distinct head-concentration characteristics, with a small number of critical reservoir nodes dominating cascade failure. Spatially, risk first propagates through the structural layer before permeating to the functional endpoints.Temporally, it follows a non-linear evolution pattern, initially intensifying and subsequently attenuating. Reverse risk propagation exhibits characteristics of ‘source-proximity priority’ and hierarchical feedback: as the degree of demand deviation and the number of affected users increase, reservoirs closest to the source are the first to reach their pressure limits and transmit pressure to secondary reservoirs. The two-dimensional classification criteria combining the criticality and sensitivity of reservoir nodes more comprehensively reflect the characteristics of reservoir nodes within the water network, enabling more precise, differentiated management of these nodes. This study broadens the research perspective on risk propagation in complex water network and provides theoretical support and decision-making guidance for the precise management of water source nodes to enhance the water supply security of water network.

Introduction With the successive completion and commissioning of cross-regional and cross-basin water transfer projects such as the South-to-North Water Diversion Project, the Yangtze River to Huai River Water Diversion Project, and the Han River to Wei River Water Diversion Project, the main framework of China’s national water network is gradually taking shape. The pattern of water resource allocation continues to be optimised, and the overall capacity to ensure water security has been significantly enhanced [ 1 ]. Located in central-eastern China, Henan Province is a vital region connecting the east, centre and west of the country and linking the north and south. It is also a key area for implementing national strategies such as ecological protection and high-quality development in the Yellow River Basin, as well as the high-quality development of follow-up projects for the South-to-North Water Diversion Project. Consequently, water supply security in Henan Province is of major strategic significance, as it not only concerns regional socio-economic stability but also directly impacts national food security, the stability of key industrial chains, and the security of cross-regional water resource allocation. Henan Province spans the four major river basins of the Yellow River, the Huai River, the Hai River and the Yangtze River. The natural water systems of these four basins, together with artificial water diversion projects such as the Central Route of the South-to-North Water Diversion Project and the Yellow River Diversion Project, as well as regional reservoirs and urban and rural water supply networks, collectively form a complex water network. Numerous practical factors, including increasingly severe climate change, frequent extreme hydrological events, rapid urbanisation and the growing connectivity of water systems, have increased the probability of risks occurring within the water network system and amplified the severity of their consequences. When local critical nodes are affected by extreme events or operational disturbances, risks are transmitted and propagated throughout the network via the interconnections within the water system. The heterogeneous nature of water network means that risk propagation exhibits complex characteristics, such as amplification at critical nodes and local failures triggering large scale impacts. Therefore, analyzing the patterns of risk propagation in water network to provide a scientific basis for their management holds significant theoretical and practical value. Risks exhibit bidirectional propagation within water network. They can propagate from the water source to the water user end along the connections within the water system, and they can also propagate in the opposite direction.Accordingly, risks within water network systems are categorized into two main types: forward transmitted risks and reverse-transmitted risks. Forward-transmitted risks include: water quantity risks such as insufficient water volume, interruptions in water supply and reduced water supply capacity; water quality risks triggered by sudden point source pollution; the downstream accumulation of pollutants and the resuspension of pollutants from bottom sediments; flood risks caused by the downstream discharge of flood peaks and reservoir overfilling; and operational risks arising from failures in reservoirs, sluice gates, pumping stations and water conveyance channels. Reverse transmission risks primarily manifest as supply pressures on upstream water source nodes caused by fluctuations in water demand. Such significant fluctuations in water demand stem from various factors: the concentration of population inflows into cities or high temperatures leading to increased domestic water use for bathing, cleaning and cooling; crops entering critical water-demand periods, with high temperatures and droughts causing increased irrigation water use; the expansion of urban industrial capacity, the growth of water-intensive industries, and the addition of new water intensive enterprises in industrial parks. Conversely, increased rainfall leading to a reduction in the three categories of water use, can all cause significant fluctuations in demand at the user end. Here, this impact arising from demand side disturbances and transmitted to the water source side is characterized as pressure feedback [ 2 ]. Given that water supply reliability risks may stem either from impaired supply capacity at the water source end or from fluctuations in demand at the user end, and that this approach effectively links the two processes of forward propagation and reverse feedback, we have selected differential water volume risk to characterize both water supply capacity failure and fluctuations in user demand, thereby analyzing the bidirectional propagation patterns of risks within the water network. When a risk event occurs at a source side node, the risk load released by that node propagates downstream along the water flow, through network connections and the redistribution of risk loads, this results in continuous propagation and amplification. The cascade failure model is capable of characterizing the dynamic evolution of the network following a local failure [ 3 ]. Consequently, it is reasonable to introduce the cascade failure model to simulate and analyse the patterns of risk propagation. However, when risk source nodes transform into water use end nodes and the direction of risk transmission changes, the network edges become pressure edges reflecting pressure relationships, and the attributes of the edges change. Therefore it is necessary to redefine the network edges based on the water network topology to generate a new pressure feedback network. In this pressure feedback network, pressure is also transmitted via network connections, and the cascade failure model remains applicable. Accordingly, this paper constructs a directed weighted water network topological model using the Henan Province water network as an example, and analyses the patterns of forward risk propagation based on simulation results from the cascade failure model. Furthermore, based on the pressure feedback network, the patterns of reverse risk propagation are analyzed using results from a modified reverse cascade failure model. Given that the heterogeneity of water network may cause the same reservoir node to play different roles in the two processes, this study further establishes key indicators for forward risk propagation and reverse pressure sensitivity for reservoir nodes. Spearman correlation analysis is employed to identify the relationship between the two sets of results, and K-means clustering is used to classify reservoir nodes into six categories based on the dual dimensions of ‘criticality’ and ‘sensitivity’. On this basis, differentiated management strategies for water source nodes are proposed. The main contributions of this study are threefold. First, from a supply—demand perspective, the nodes are classified into three categories—reservoir, confluence, and user nodes—and a directed and weighted complex water network is constructed by simultaneously incorporating the structural and functional attributes of nodes as well as edge weights. Second, the risk assessment of water-source nodes incorporates the reverse pressure induced by fluctuations in user demand, thereby extending the conventional one-dimensional assessment of forward node criticality into a two-dimensional criticality—sensitivity framework. Third, reservoir nodes are divided into different management zones based on the two indicators of criticality and sensitivity, and differentiated management strategies are proposed for each zone. Literature review Existing research on water network risks has covered numerous dimensions, including economic, social, managerial, engineering {Guo Yongyong, 2022 #80} and ecological. Research on risk propagation has gradually expanded from the analysis of a single risk in a single project to the study of risk propagation involving the coupling of multiple risks. Focusing on a single risk within a specific project, risk research in the field of water resources systems has primarily centred on the management of man-made engineering structures [ 4 – 6 ] and risk assessment in the field of ecological risks [ 7 , 8 ]. Research subjects have largely focused on key water projects such as the South-to-North Water Diversion Project [ 9 ] and the Three Gorges Reservoir [ 10 , 11 ]. Research has expanded from single risk studies to coupled analyses of multiple risk types. Addressing the issue of numerous risk sources and multi-factor coupling leading to disasters in high slope construction, Jiang et al. utilized systems theory to analyse the coupled mechanisms and conduct risk management research involving multiple risks namely, human, physical, environmental and management factors [ 12 ]. Focusing on the catchment scale [ 13 ], Feng et al. constructed a multi-linkage network of risks in complex river basin water resource systems based on complex network theory, investigated the propagation pathways of different interrelated elements within the network, and, on this basis, developed a risk propagation model, designed risk propagation indicators and risk management strategies [ 14 ]. Existing research lacks studies on risk propagation from the perspective of water network systems, and there is an even greater lack of research into the patterns of risk propagation from a reverse perspective. Taking into account the differences in water network types, distinct topological network structure models have been constructed: one category comprises inter-basin water network [ 15 ], and Fan et al., which, based on complex network theory, generalize water network into a ‘spine-branch-node’ topological structure, where the spine and branches correspond to river systems and water diversion projects, the nodes correspond to water storage and regulation projects and key cross sections [ 16 ]. One category comprises regional water network: Zhang et al. constructed undirected topological networks (with reservoirs and lakes as nodes, and rivers and canals as edges) [ 17 ]. Li et al. abstracted the nodes (water conservancy hubs) of the water network as nodes and the ‘backbone-branch’ (water conveyance channels) as edges to construct a topological network [ 18 ]. Existing studies that use complex network models to construct topological network structures for water network have only analyzed the resilience and vulnerability of the network under specific risk scenarios, namely when certain network characteristic indicators are compromised [ 19 ]. Furthermore, existing water network models do not take into account the attributes of nodes and edges within the topological network, resulting in a certain gap of discrepancy between the constructed topological network and the actual network. Research into the patterns of risk propagation has established a comprehensive logic of ‘risk source identification analysis of propagation mechanisms assessment of propagation effects’ across multiple fields, including energy systems [ 20 , 21 ], ecosystems [ 22 – 25 ], transport networks [ 26 – 28 ], and shipping networks [ 29 ]. In the field of energy systems, Fu et al. took China’s carbon neutral energy transition system as their subject of study, dividing systemic risk into four heterogeneous subsystems economic, social, natural and digital and utilized a system dynamics model to simulate the risk propagation process [ 20 ]. Taking cascading effects into account, Liu et al., against the backdrop of the Ukraine crisis, constructed a two stage model of oil supply risk propagation and recovery to analyse the cascading effects of risk within the global oil trade network. Their research overcame the limitations of risk analysis based solely on bilateral trade between individual countries and revealed the role of indirect risk transmission via non-direct partners within energy trade networks [ 21 ]. In the field of ecosystems, Deng et al. focused on three types of soil ecosystems in China agricultural, natural and urban and quantified the cascading effects of microplastic risk transmission at a national scale [ 24 ]. Jing et al. focused on drought risk in the Xilingol temperate grasslands of Inner Mongolia, revealing a three tiered cascading mechanism of risk transmission involving meteorological drought, soil drought and eco-hydrological drought [ 25 ]. In the field of transport networks, Hu et al. constructed a theoretical framework and simulation model for risk transmission cascade effects from the perspective of complex system process safety. They revealed the chain reaction transmission pattern of ‘initial accident secondary disaster’ in LNG-powered vessels, clarifying the mechanisms by which multi-factor coupling drives cascades and identifying key control nodes [ 28 ]. In the field of shipping networks, Xu et al. combined the Motter-Lai load capacity model with a dynamic shipping traffic reallocation mechanism to investigate the cascading propagation of risk within the global container shipping network following the disruption of a single port. The widespread application of theories such as cascading failure [ 26 , 30 ], complex networks [ 31 , 32 ], and infectious disease models [ 33 ] The widespread application of these theories in research on risk propagation provides methodological insights for studies on risk transmission in water network. Traditional research has largely assessed the importance of water source nodes based on unidirectional water supply processes, making it difficult to answer two key questions: firstly, what is the extent and severity of the cascading effects on the water network following risks at different reservoir nodes. Secondly, how does the sensitivity to pressure vary across different reservoir nodes in response to feedback from fluctuations in downstream demand. Consequently, determining how to comprehensively assess the forward risk propagation capacity and reverse pressure resilience of reservoir nodes, identify their characteristics and implement zoned management, is an urgent issue in the risk management of water source nodes within complex water network.The contributions of this study are as follows: First, it provides a new approach for constructing a more realistic water network model by considering the structural roles and functional differences of different types of nodes in risk transmission, characterizing betweenness centrality as a structural attribute of nodes, characterizing reservoir storage capacity and designed water supply scale at the user end as functional attributes, setting edge weights according to the resistance to risk propagation in different water conveyance channels. Second, it incorporates the reverse feedback of demand pressure from the water use end to the water source end into water network risk transmission analysis, and constructs indicators of forward transmission criticality and reverse pressure-bearing sensitivity to reveal bidirectional risk transmission patterns from the dimensions of risk output and pressure bearing. Third, it conducts a two-dimensional comprehensive evaluation of reservoir nodes to more accurately identify the characteristics of different reservoir nodes and achieve refined classification, thereby providing a decision-making basis for differentiated control and management of reservoir nodes, determination of management priorities, and allocation of risk prevention and control resources. Methodology Based on the complex network characteristics of water network and the bidirectional transmission of water network risks, this paper constructs a water network model and employs a cascade failure model to analyse the patterns of bidirectional risk transmission. For the forward analysis, a directed weighted water network topological model was constructed. An improved forward cascading failure model is used to analyse the patterns of forward risk transmission. Reverse analysis retains the network node configuration, converting the watercourse edges into pressure relationship edges, redefining edge weights, and constructing a pressure feedback network. An improved cascading failure model is then employed to analyse the patterns of reverse risk propagation. The research framework is shown in Fig 1 . The improvements to the traditional cascade failure model in this model are primarily reflected in the load redistribution rules. The traditional model relies on direct topological connections between nodes to distribute the load released by a failed node to neighbouring nodes according to transmission probabilities. The improved cascade failure model first identifies several reachable connection paths between the risk node and potential load bearing nodes, then retains the path with the lowest connection resistance as the effective transmission path for the stress load, and finally distributes the load according to the transmission probabilities between nodes. In reality, as the water demand at the user end increases, the user end will first look for water supply from nearby reservoir nodes. If the nearby reservoir nodes cannot meet their demands, they will then transfer the water supply to distant reservoir nodes. This is the actual situation of water resource management in the water network system. In addition, the risk back propagation considered in this study is a simplified management abstraction and does not specifically account for the effects of changes in pressure, flow, hydraulic resistance, or equipment operating modes on this process. Based on this, this paper designs an improved cascading failure model to simulate this process. The deviation of the user node’s demand leads to the generation of the initial risk source, which then triggers the cascading failure; after the failure, the pressure load of the nodes is first transferred to nearby reservoir nodes. A higher value indicates that the reservoir node is more sensitive and more susceptible to stress. Analysis of model simulation results Based on the aforementioned topological network model of Henan Province’s water network, a simulation of bidirectional risk propagation and cascading failure was conducted. The parameter values were determined according to the attribute differences among node types, practical engineering operating characteristics, and related cascading failure models [ 3 , 34 , 37 ]. The risk-threshold redundancy coefficients α 1 , α 2 , α 3 correspond to junction nodes, reservoir nodes, and user nodes, respectively. Under actual engineering operation, beneficial storage generally accounts for approximately 30%–50% of total reservoir storage, whereas the reservoir storage attribute used in this study is the total storage capacity. Therefore, α 1 = 0 . 1 , α 2 = 0 . 4 , α 3 = 0 . 3 were adopted. The external impact coefficient was set to ρ = 1 . 5 . When α 2 = 0 . 4 , the risk threshold of the initial reservoir risk source is 1 . 4 L s ( 0 ) , while its disturbed risk load is 1 . 5 L s ( 0 ) . This setting ensures the failure of the initial risk source and initiates cascading propagation while avoiding an excessively large impact load. The parameters λ = 0 . 8 and μ = 0 . 7 represent the weights of the functional attributes in the initial risk loads of reservoir nodes and user nodes, respectively, indicating that reservoir storage capacity and design capacity have greater effects on the initial risk loads than structural attributes. In the forward model, | β i | represents the demand deviation and was varied within ( 0 , 0 . 25 ] . The conversion coefficient from demand fluctuations to pressure risk loads was set to γ = 1 , indicating that the differential water volume caused by demand fluctuations is fully converted into a pressure risk load. The conversion coefficient from normalized reservoir storage capacity to the pressure threshold was set to σ = 1 , such that the normalized reservoir storage capacity directly represents the relative pressure-bearing capacity of reservoir nodes. As shown in Fig 3 , the risk propagation triggered by reservoir nodes exhibits a head-concentration phenomenon, with a small number of nodes experiencing large scale failures while the majority suffer smaller scale failures. The majority of both the failed nodes and the affected but non-failed nodes are watercourse junction nodes. There are variations in the composition of affected nodes following the failure of a reservoir node. Most of the risk is first absorbed and buffered at the confluence nodes before being transmitted to end users. Cascading failures of reservoir nodes exhibit the characteristic of long distance propagation along the backbone network. As shown in Fig 4 , which ranks the top 20 reservoir nodes by spatial propagation range, when nodes such as Sanmenxia Reservoir, Xixia Yuan Reservoir, Danjiangkou Reservoir and Luhun Reservoir act as initial risk sources, the spatial coverage of the failure is relatively extensive. As large reservoirs in Henan Province, these nodes occupy key positions in regional water supply or inter-basin water transfer systems and are connected to multiple downstream nodes. Once a node fails, the risk load propagates over long distances along the main water conveyance channels and trunk and branch lines, thereby exhibiting a large spatial coverage. Judging from the ranking results in Figs 3 and 4 , risk source nodes with a high number of failed nodes are not necessarily the same as those with a large spatial diffusion range. The former are concentrated at nodes with a higher density of adjacent nodes, and their indicators focus on the structural characteristics of the network. The latter are concentrated at nodes in the backbone network, at upstream control points, and at nodes responsible for cross regional water conveyance and distribution, with their indicators covering both structural and functional characteristics of the water network. Judging from the results on network efficiency loss ( Fig 5 ), the impact of different reservoir risk sources on the overall connectivity of the water network varies, and also exhibits a head heavy distribution, with a maximum efficiency loss of approximately 7.41%. The relatively limited loss of network efficiency does not contradict the large number of failed nodes or the extensive spatial propagation observed in some scenarios, because these indicators reflect different dimensions of cascading failure. A large spatial propagation range indicates that risk can travel over a long geographical distance, but does not mean that a large number of global shortest paths are disrupted. Similarly, a large number of failed nodes does not necessarily cause a substantial efficiency loss, because nodes at different topological positions make unequal contributions to global connectivity. Therefore, the results indicate that the cascading failures caused by individual reservoir nodes have a relatively limited impact on the shortest-path structure of the water network. Judging from the risk propagation process and the composition of affected nodes ( Fig 7 ), risk propagation exhibits a temporal evolution characterized by an initial intensification followed by a subsequent weakening. From the cumulative curve of the propagation process, the cumulative number of failed nodes continues to rise but gradually stabilizes in the later stages. In the later stages of risk propagation, the system exhibits a situation where the majority of nodes are affected but do not fail. When the Sanmenxia Reservoir node acts as the initial risk source, the loss in network efficiency is not significant, but the number of failed nodes and the spatial extent of failure are substantial. The reason is that, following the failure of this reservoir node, a large amount of risk load is released. Even with other reservoir nodes acting as buffers, a significant number of junction nodes and user nodes are still affected. Secondly, as the connections between the reservoir node and other nodes are characterized by long distance backbone links, the propagation pattern when the reservoir node acts as the initial risk source tends to form ‘long chains’, facilitating long distance spatial propagation and consequently resulting in a wider spatial spread. (2) Comparison with the Traditional Cascading Failure Model The traditional model uses only normalized betweenness centrality as the initial node load and therefore assumes that structurally similar nodes have similar initial loads. However, reservoirs are not only topological nodes in a water network but also perform water storage, regulation, and supply functions, and reservoir storage capacity can represent their functional scale. Therefore, the improved model incorporates both betweenness centrality and reservoir storage capacity into the initial-load setting, enabling the initial load to reflect both the structural and functional attributes of nodes and better represent the actual characteristics of reservoir nodes in water-network systems. Second, node failure indicates that the risk load of a node exceeds its load-bearing threshold and does not mean that all of its water storage and regulation capacity disappears. Therefore, compared with the traditional model, which releases the entire load after node failure, the improved model redistributes only the load exceeding the load-bearing threshold, which is more consistent with actual conditions. Except for the initial-load setting and the load-release rule for failed nodes, all other settings of the traditional model remained unchanged. Discussion This study focuses on the identification of risk propagation patterns, as well as the classification and differentiated management of water source nodes within complex water supply networks. A topological network model reflecting actual water supply relationships was constructed, and reservoir nodes were evaluated from two dimensions: forward risk propagation and reverse pressure feedback. Firstly, forward cascade failure simulations were conducted using reservoir nodes as the initial risk sources. The consequences of risk were characterized in terms of the number of failed nodes, propagation speed, spatial spread, and loss of network efficiency, thereby establishing a comprehensive evaluation index for the criticality of reservoir nodes. Secondly, network edges and their attributes were redefined to construct a pressure feedback network. Reverse cascade failure simulations were conducted with user nodes as the initial risk sources. The patterns of reverse transmission of demand fluctuations were revealed through changes in the pressure index of reservoir nodes as the number of propagation steps increased, and a comprehensive indicator of pressure sensitivity for reservoir nodes was derived through sensitivity analysis. Finally, by integrating the forward criticality and reverse pressure sensitivity of reservoir nodes, a two-dimensional classification is performed using the K-means clustering method, providing a basis for formulating differentiated risk management strategies for different types of reservoir nodes. In this paper, within the framework of the forward-improved cascading failure model setting, both the structural indicators used in the traditional network structure feature analysis as the structural attributes of the nodes and the functional attributes of the nodes are taken into account. Then, Compared with degree centrality and betweenness centrality, which characterize the static topological importance of nodes, the composite criticality indicator used in this study reflects the dynamic consequences of reservoir failure in terms of failure scale, propagation speed, spatial influence, and network-efficiency loss. Furthermore, existing studies identify critical reservoir nodes based on the consequences of forward risk propagation, this study introduces reverse pressure feedback caused by demand disturbances at the user end, thereby expanding the analytical dimension of reservoir nodes from a single criterion of criticality to a dual dimension of criticality and sensitivity. This method not only identifies the impact of reservoir node failure on the water supply network but also reflects the extent to which reservoir nodes are affected by demand disturbances, thereby providing a more comprehensive characterization of their risk characteristics. The results of the Spearman correlation analysis between the two composite indicators indicate a weak correlation between the composite indicator of reservoir node criticality and that of pressure sensitivity. This is because the two indicators reflect risk propagation characteristics in different directions: the criticality composite indicator focuses on the potential consequences of cascading failures when a reservoir node acts as an initial risk source, while the pressure sensitivity composite indicator focuses on the extent to which a reservoir node is affected by demand disturbances at the user end. The weak correlation between the two further illustrates that identifying critical nodes based on a single dimension alone makes it difficult to fully characterize the risk attributes of reservoir nodes, and that it is necessary to conduct a comprehensive analysis of bidirectional propagation. The “criticality+sensitivity” two-dimensional classification provides a practical basis for the differentiated management of reservoir nodes. Criticality reflects the cascading failure consequences caused by a reservoir node acting as an initial risk source. It can therefore be used to determine the priority of structural safety monitoring, preventive maintenance, protection of key water-conveyance routes, emergency resource allocation and infrastructure modernization. Pressure sensitivity reflects the susceptibility of a reservoir node to demand fluctuations at user nodes. It can therefore be used to determine the intensity of inflow and water-demand monitoring, pressure-index warning and operational regulation. For nodes with high pressure sensitivity, joint dispatch, coordinated supply from adjacent reservoirs or backup water sources should be activated when the pressure index approaches its threshold. Accordingly, the management intensity and priority of each zone are jointly determined by its levels of criticality and pressure sensitivity. Nodes in Zones (I), (II), and (III) all have high criticality and should be regarded as priority targets for engineering safety protection and infrastructure modernization. Specifically, priority monitoring should cover dam deformation, seepage, cracks, gates and gate-operating equipment, water-conveyance structures, and power-supply and communication facilities. Preventive inspections and equipment maintenance should also be strengthened, and emergency repair teams, equipment, and materials should be allocated in advance. In addition, the availability of alternative water-conveyance routes should be assessed, and water-network interconnection projects, backup water-conveyance routes, or emergency water sources should be developed according to actual needs to reduce the risk of cascading failures caused by the failure of critical nodes.The difference among nodes in Zones (I), (II), and (III) lies in their successively decreasing pressure sensitivity. Nodes in Zones (I) and (II) have high and medium pressure sensitivity, respectively. Therefore, in addition to the aforementioned engineering safety protection measures, changes in agricultural, domestic, industrial, and ecological water demand should be monitored by sector and region. Inflow, reservoir storage, water supply, downstream water demand, supply–demand deficits, and the pressure index should also be monitored simultaneously. When the pressure index continuously increases or approaches its threshold, joint operation of adjacent reservoirs, interregional water allocation, or backup water-source supply should be activated. If the existing water-supply capacity remains insufficient to meet demand, graded water-supply restrictions, off-peak water supply, or rotational irrigation can be further implemented. The monitoring frequency and warning-response intensity for Zone (I) nodes should be higher than those for Zone (II) nodes.Nodes in Zones (IV), (V), and (VI) all have relatively low criticality and can be managed through routine structural safety inspections and equipment maintenance. The pressure sensitivity of nodes in Zones (IV), (V), and (VI) also decreases successively. Nodes in Zones (IV) and (V) have high and medium pressure sensitivity, respectively, and should therefore adopt supply—demand monitoring, pressure warning, and operational regulation measures corresponding to those applied to Zones (I) and (II). The monitoring and regulation intensity for Zone (IV) nodes should be higher than that for Zone (V) nodes. Nodes in Zones (III) and (VI) both have low pressure sensitivity, and therefore do not require excessive resource input for water-demand monitoring. The generality of the proposed model is primarily reflected in its modelling framework and analytical methods. The following components can be directly applied to the analysis of bidirectional risk transmission in other regional water networks without modifying the model: the analytical framework for bidirectional risk transmission, the bidirectional application of the cascading failure model, the method for determining the initial risk loads of different node types, and the setting of node risk thresholds. During model transfer, the nodes, edges, and their connections need to be reconstructed according to the local water network, and the model parameters need to be assigned using local data. For example, the storage capacities of reservoir nodes, the designed scales of user nodes, water-conveyance-route lengths, roughness coefficients, initial node risk loads need to be redetermined using local data. These adjustments constitute updates to model input data and parameters rather than modifications to the model structure. When new types of water network nodes appear in other water networks—such as when the water source node is a seawater desalination plant—it is necessary to add node attributes and define the relationships between this node and other nodes within the network. This study generalizes the changes in water supply and water consumption caused by different water volume risks and is based on a static network topology, without considering seasonal variability in hydrological conditions, changes in management regimes or operators’ operational decisions. Future research could further refine the relationships between specific risks and differential water volumes and improve the model by incorporating dynamic networks, hydraulic simulations, seasonal hydrological conditions, changes in management regimes, operators’ operational decisions and actual dispatch data. This will enhance its ability to characterize bidirectional risk transmission under different risk and operating scenarios and thereby provide more comprehensive and in-depth suggestions for water network management. Conclusions Based on the management requirements for water source nodes in complex water network, this study has established a classification and management framework for water source nodes that takes into account both the criticality of forward risk propagation and the sensitivity to reverse pressure. The conclusions of the study are as follows: (1) The forward transmission of risks at reservoir nodes exhibits significant non-equilibrium, phased and hierarchical characteristics. A small number of reservoir nodes can trigger large scale cascading failures, and the number of ultimately failing nodes displays a pronounced ‘head heavy’ distribution, indicating that water network risks are not determined by an even distribution of nodes, but are instead concentrated and controlled by key reservoir nodes. The propagation path is not a simple single-chain diffusion, but rather first expands along the backbone connections of the water network towards structural levels, and subsequently permeates towards functional endpoints such as users, forming a transmission pattern of ‘structural diffusion functional permeation’. The propagation speed exhibits a phased change, accelerating initially and then slowing down, indicating that in the early stages, risk is primarily driven by network connectivity, while in later stages, as the load bearing status of nodes changes, risk propagation gradually attenuates. (2) The reverse transmission of risk at user nodes exhibits distinct spatial proximity and hierarchical progression. Pressure feedback first acts upon upstream nodes closely linked to the risk source, exhibiting the characteristic of priority response by nodes near the source. It then feeds back layer by layer to upstream reservoirs along water supply relationships, forming a reverse propagation process characterized by local pressure disturbances, stepwise upstream feedback and cumulative pressure on reservoirs. Consequently, the scale of user nodes and their topological connections with reservoirs jointly determine the intensity and scope of pressure feedback. (3) Forward water volume risk propagation and reverse pressure feedback differ markedly in terms of propagation direction, affected entities and mechanisms of action. However, both exhibit the characteristic of risk impacts concentrating on a small number of key reservoirs. The results of forward propagation reflect the capacity of a reservoir failure to impact the downstream water supply system, while the results of reverse propagation reflect the extent to which a reservoir can withstand downstream demand disturbances and pressure feedback. A comparison of the two indicates that a reservoir’s risk emitting capacity is inconsistent with its risk bearing capacity. Consequently, relying solely on a unidirectional assessment may lead to a misinterpretation of reservoir characteristics, thereby affecting the precise management of reservoir nodes. (4) Based on the results of K-means clustering using comprehensive indicators of reservoir node criticality and sensitivity, reservoirs were classified into six categories. High criticality nodes should prioritize preventing themselves from becoming initial risk sources and mitigating the consequences of cascading failures: among these, high sensitivity nodes must balance facility safety, demand early warning, capacity redundancy and the safeguarding of backup water sources; medium sensitivity nodes should strengthen preventive maintenance of critical facilities and early warning systems for pressure related issues; low sensitivity nodes do not require specific measures regarding sensitivity, but should prioritize preventing themselves from becoming an initial risk source. As the cascading impact of low criticality nodes is relatively limited, management priorities should be adjusted progressively in line with decreasing sensitivity: high sensitivity nodes should focus on early warning of demand fluctuations, dynamic scheduling and the development of alternative supply routes; medium sensitivity nodes should prioritize periodic monitoring, initiating coordinated water supply and regional redistribution when stress levels approach thresholds; low sensitivity nodes require only routine inspections and basic maintenance. This classification more accurately identifies the characteristics of reservoir nodes and provides a basis for differentiated management of water source nodes within complex water network. This paper constructs the topological network as a directed weighted network that takes into account the structural and functional attributes of nodes as well as the weight attributes of edges, thereby making the topological network more closely aligned with the reality of water network. It analyses the bidirectional transmission patterns of risk within water network, examines the characteristics of reservoir nodes from the dual dimensions of criticality and sensitivity, and proposes a differentiated management strategy based on classification.The limitations of this study lie in the fact that it generalizes the changes in water supply and water consumption caused by various risks using differential water volumes, which is not sufficiently detailed. Subsequent research could further refine the relationship between specific risks and changes in water volume. Furthermore, as this study has not yet incorporated dynamic hydraulic processes, future work could extend and refine the model by integrating dynamic networks, hydraulic simulations and actual dispatch data.
