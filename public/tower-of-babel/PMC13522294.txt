CXCL9/SPP1-polarized tumor-associated macrophages exert dual roles in regulating anti-tumor immunity in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Haixiao Liu, Lingyun Wang, Changlin Li, Dongtao Li, Linghan Meng, Guangda Zheng, Juanxia Ren, Lu Shang, Yanju Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCS polarity, the mutually exclusive expression of CXCL9 and SPP1, is a key feature of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in the lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) microenvironment. The mechanisms and impact of CS polarity on tumor progression remain incompletely understood. The study aims to define the molecular basis of CS polarity and its role in LUAD progression to inform new immunotherapies.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing was used to characterize TAM subsets within the LUAD microenvironment, with survival analysis assessing their prognostic values and CellChat mapping cell–cell communication. In the functional validation section, a CS polarization regulation model of THP-1 cells was established. The Transwell co-culture system was utilized to evaluate its effects on the malignant behaviors of LUAD cells. CCK-8 assay, colony formation assay and cell migration assay were performed to detect cell proliferation and metastatic capacities. Flow cytometry was applied to determine the polarized phenotypes of TAMs (CD86⁺/CD163⁺). Dual-luciferase reporter assay was conducted to verify the direct transcriptional regulation of HPGDS promoter by MEF2C. Western blot, immunocytochemistry and qRT-PCR were adopted to analyze the activity of key signaling pathways. Furthermore, the above findings were further validated in vivo using a subcutaneous transplantation tumor model in nude mice.ResultsFive functionally distinct TAM subsets were identified. SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs were enriched in immune-activation pathways and strongly associated with favorable LUAD prognosis, whereas SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs participated in immune suppression and tumor-promoting processes, correlating with poor outcome. In vitro, increasing the CXCL9/SPP1 ratio synergistically promoted macrophages toward an M1 phenotype and suppressed LUAD cell proliferation, migration and invasion. In addition, CS polarity reversal further promoted MEF2C nuclear translocation and HPGDS transcription by activating the p38/MAPK signaling axis. Dual-luciferase reporter assays directly confirmed that MEF2C activated HPGDS transcription via specifically binding to its promoter, which served as a critical molecular event that induced phenotypic switching of TAMs and triggered their antitumor functions. In vivo experiments using the subcutaneous transplantation tumor model in nude mice further verified that combined treatment with KO-SPP1 and OE-CXCL9 markedly inhibited tumor growth and facilitated the polarization of intratumoral macrophages toward an antitumor phenotype, which was highly consistent with the in vitro findings.ConclusionCS polarity modulates TAM polarization and their anti-tumor function through the p38/MAPK-MEF2C-HPGDS axis, offering theoretical foundation and potential targets for macrophage reprogramming-based LUAD immunotherapy.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the malignancy with the highest incidence and mortality worldwide [1]. Among its histological subtypes, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) stands out as the most common and lethal type, characterized by profound heterogeneity at the histological, cellular and molecular levels [2, 3]. Within a single tumor, multiple histological subsets of LUAD often coexist, including micro-papillary, solid and complex glandular patterns, which are typically highly invasive, and the lepidic subtype that exhibits indolent growth [4]. However, due to limited early-detection tools and nonspecific clinical symptoms, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced or metastatic stage [5, 6]. Current therapies are still inadequate, resulting in poor overall prognosis. Although extensive genetic and epigenetic diversity has been documented, the key molecular mechanisms and biological bases that drive the transition among histologic subtypes during tumor evolution remain obscure.

Over the years, the tumor microenvironment (TME) has emerged as a critical determinant of therapeutic outcome across multiple cancers [7, 8]. TME comprises multiple cell types and cytokines, with tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) being a critical component, which participate in the wide processes of tissue homeostasis and disease progression [9, 10]. Macrophages perform multiple functions including phagocytosis, antigen presentation and cytokine secretion, acting as immunologic “firewalls” that preserve internal stability [11]. Moreover, macrophages exhibit highly dynamic properties: upon tissue injury, inflammation or malignant tumors, infiltrating monocytes can rapidly expand the resident macrophage compartment in the tissue [12]. Conventionally, environmental cues polarize TAMs into classically activated (M1) or alternatively activated (M2) states. M1 TAMs exert pro-inflammatory and anti-tumor immune functions, whereas M2 TAMs support tumor progression by suppressing adaptive immunity and promoting inflammation [13–15]. However, many TAM subsets display non-canonical or hybrid M1/M2 signatures, indicating that the traditional dichotomy insufficiently captures their functional complexity.

In 2023, Bill et al. [16] identified a mutually exclusive expression pattern of CXCL9 and SPP1 (CS polarity) in TAMs and showed that this polarity stratifies prognosis in head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma. Subsequent studies have corroborated CS polarity as a prognostic indicator of immune activity in the TME of multiple tumor types [17, 18]. Nevertheless, whether and how CS polarity operates in LUAD and its clinical significance remain incompletely understood. Elucidating TAM heterogeneity and its regulatory role within the LUAD immune microenvironment is therefore imperative for the development of novel immunotherapies.

Here, we integrate single-cell transcriptomics with molecular biology experimental methods to systematically characterize CS-polarized TAMs in LUAD immune regulation, delineate their cell–cell communication circuits, and evaluate their impact on therapeutic response, providing both theoretical and experimental foundations for TAM-targeted novel immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Data source

mRNA expression profiles and matched clinical information (age, sex, tumor grade, TNM stage) for 539 tumors and 59 normal samples of LUAD were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA-LUAD) [19]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from 10 LUAD tumors were obtained from Code Ocean [20] for downstream analyses.

Quality control and preprocessing of scRNA-seq data

A Seurat object was created using the “CreateSeuratObject” function in the “Seurat” R package (v4) [21] (min.cells = 3, min.features = 200). Filtering criteria: (i) genes detected in ≥3 cells; (ii) nFeature_RNA per cell 500–6000; (iii) mitochondrial content <30%. Data were normalized with the “NormalizeData” function, scaled with the “ScaleData” function, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were identified with the “FindVariableFeatures” function. Dimensionality reduction was performed with principal component analysis (PCA) (first 15 PCs); batch effects were removed with the “Harmony” algorithm. Finally, the “FindNeighbors” function was used to construct a k-nearest-neighbor graph, the “FindClusters” function was applied to perform cell clustering, and the “RunUMAP” function was executed to generate a two-dimensional embedding for visualizing the clusters.

Additional processes on single-cell data and downstream analyses were performed with the “SCP” R package [22]. Based on the provided multiple visualization functions, we used the “CellDimPlot” function to generate cell clustering UMAP plots, the “FeatureStatPlot” function for gene-expression violin plots, the “CellStatPlot” function for stacked bars showing cell-type composition or status, and the “FeatureDimPlot” function for the distribution of signature genes.

Cell annotation

Cells were systematically annotated based on reference studies [16, 23], CellMarker 2.0 [24] and PanglaoDB [25]. First, cells were broadly classified as epithelial, immune or stromal. Immune cells were then sub-annotated into T cells, macrophages, monocytes, B cells, myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs), mast cells and plasma cells. Finally, macrophages were re-clustered and refined using established marker genes to obtain high-resolution sub-sets.

Cell-type deconvolution

We performed cell-type deconvolution on the TCGA-LUAD dataset using the “BayesPrism” package [26]. First, a reference profile was built from our scRNA-seq data after removing ribosomal and mitochondrial genes to minimize high-expression noise. Then, the filtered reference matrix and the bulk TCGA-LUAD expression matrix were supplied to the BayesPrism model, yielding sample-level proportions of each cell type.

Scissor analysis

Using the “Scissor” package [27], we coupled TCGA-LUAD survival information with the single-cell data to identify TAM sub-sets significantly associated with patient prognosis.

Multi-dataset validation analysis

To verify the universality of the mutually exclusive expression pattern of SPP1 and CXCL9 (CS polarity) in independent datasets, three additional public single-cell RNA-seq datasets of LUAD/NSCLC were downloaded from the NCBI GEO database: GSE131907 (Kim et al. [28], containing pre-annotated macrophage subtypes mo-Mac and Alveolar Mac), GSE127465 (Zilionis et al. [29], including pre-annotated tMac1–tMac9 subtypes), and GSE189357 (Qian et al. [30], in which the top 15% of cells scored by marker genes including CD68, CD163, MRC1, MARCO, CSF1R and ITGAM were extracted as macrophages). The raw count matrices were normalized to 10,000 counts per cell followed by log1p transformation. For GSE127465 with pre-normalized counts, only log1p transformation was performed. Dimensionality reduction and clustering analyses were conducted using Scanpy (v1.9). A total of 2000 highly variable genes were selected, followed by principal component analysis with 30 principal components, k-nearest neighbor graph construction (k = 15, using the top 20 principal components), Leiden clustering (resolution = 0.4), and UMAP embedding (min_dist = 0.4, spread = 1.2). DBSCAN (eps = 0.6, min_samples = 15) was applied to remove outlier cells in the UMAP space, and clusters accounting for less than 3% of the total cell number were excluded. To evaluate the mutual exclusivity between SPP1 and CXCL9, the Spearman rank correlation coefficient was calculated in the cell subset expressing at least one of the two genes (SPP1 > 0 or CXCL9 > 0). A fitted linear regression line in the scatter plot was used for visual presentation.

Cell–cell communication

Cell–cell communication within the LUAD TME was systematically analyzed with the “CellChat” package [31]. We focused on the two functionally distinct TAM subsets, SPP1⁺CXCL9low and SPP1⁻CXCL9high, and quantified their interaction strength and signaling modes with all other cell types. Ligand-receptor networks were constructed, statistically compared across different cell types, and the top differential pairs were visualized with bubble plots.

Subsequently, to further assess the involvement of non-immune cells (epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells) within the TME, the analytical scope was expanded to cover all 14 cell types. The identical workflow was applied to construct the global cell communication network, and ligand-receptor interactions between the two SPP1-related TAM subsets and the newly incorporated cell types were further compared.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

To explore the cellular origin and differentiation trajectories of TAM subpopulations, pseudotime analysis was performed on five TAM subsets using the Monocle2 package [32]. Firstly, ordering genes for trajectory construction were screened based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of each subset. The DDRTree algorithm was adopted for dimensionality reduction to establish the cellular differentiation trajectory. Pseudotime values were calculated for individual cells, the distribution of each subset along the differentiation trajectory was visualized via trajectory plots, and density plots were utilized to illustrate the distribution patterns of each TAM subset on the pseudotime axis. Furthermore, the dynamic expression patterns of the pivotal genes SPP1 and CXCL9 along the pseudotime axis were visualized to reveal their expression alterations during TAM differentiation.

High-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA)

hdWGCNA [33] was employed to extract key genes tightly linked to the SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAM subset. First, an expression correlation matrix was built based on the subset’s scRNA-seq data, and a co-expression network was constructed using the optimal soft-threshold β = 6 to identify co-expression modules. Through differential analysis, modules significantly associated with the SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAM subset were identified. Hub genes were defined by intramodular connectivity.

Transcription-factor (TF) regulation was subsequently inferred within the hdWGCNA framework. To preliminarily establish the modulation network between TFs and target genes, all gene promoter regions were scanned based on TF motifs from JASPAR 2020 [34] and their position-weight matrices, using the “motifmatchr” package [35]. To increase the reliability of our prediction, an XGBoost machine learning model [36] was trained for each gene using fivefold cross-validation. The expression levels of TFs with matched promoter binding sites were used as input features to calculate regulatory weights of each TF, thereby quantitatively assessing their predictive contribution to target gene expression. For every target gene, the top 10 TFs were retained. Activating vs. repressive relationships were assigned based on the sign of the Pearson correlation coefficients between TF and target expression, thereby constructing a context-specific TF regulatory network.

Gene-signature scoring and phenotype association

Using the TCGA-LUAD cohort, we first calculated scores for 281 validated gene signatures with the “IOBR” package [37]. Overall survival (OS) analysis was then performed to retain signatures significantly linked to prognosis. To further assess the prognostic values of these signatures, the “surv_cutpoint” function in the “survminer” package [38] was applied for risk stratification. The top 20 molecular signatures were visualized with a heat map to depict their expression patterns across risk strata.

Immune-infiltration analysis

The CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm was used to quantify relative abundances of immune cells in TCGA-LUAD samples, enabling comparison of immune composition between risk groups. Meanwhile, expression profiles of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) family genes and key immune-checkpoint molecules were also analyzed. All results are presented as box plots. Inter-group differences were evaluated with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Cell culture

Human LUAD A549 (CL-0016) and human monocytic leukemia THP-1 (CL-0233) cells were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology (China). A549 cells were maintained in DMEM (11965118, Thermo Fisher, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10099141C, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (PS, C0222, Beyotime, China). THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (11875119, Thermo Fisher, USA) containing 10% FBS and 1% PS. THP-1 cells were differentiated into macrophages by 48-h treatment with 50 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, HY-18739, MedChemExpress, USA). All cells were cultured in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Cell transfection

Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (L3000015, Thermo Fisher, USA) was used for siRNA knockdown, overexpression and combined transfection. CRISPR/Cas9 knockout plasmid targeting SPP1 (KO-SPP1) and its corresponding negative control plasmid (KO-NC) were designed, while the CXCL9 overexpression plasmid (OE-CXCL9) and empty vector control plasmid (OE-NC) were constructed simultaneously. All plasmids were synthesized and purchased from ELEM Biotech (Wuhan, China). Additionally, a co-transfection group (KO-SPP1 + OE-CXCL9) was established to explore the synergistic effect. When the confluence of THP-1 cells reached 60–70%, the control group (NC), KO-SPP1, OE-CXCL9 and co-transfection complex were separately mixed with transfection reagents following the manufacturer’s instructions. After 6 h of transfection, the complete medium was replaced, and cell culture was continued for another 48 h.

Small molecule inhibitor treatment

To verify the role of the p38/MAPK signaling pathway in the regulation of TAM polarization via CS polarity, the specific p38/MAPK inhibitor SB203580 (#5633, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) was used for blockade experiments. Based on previous research [39] and the recommended conditions in the product manual, SB203580 was applied at a final concentration of 10 µM to effectively suppress the activity of the p38/MAPK signaling pathway. After THP-1 cells completed transfection (see Sect. 2.13 for details) and were cultured for an additional 24 h, the original medium was discarded, and fresh RPMI-1640 complete medium containing SB203580 was added, while an equal volume of DMSO was supplemented in the control group. Upon completion of treatment, cells were collected for subsequent Western blot (WB), immunocytochemistry (ICC), and co-culture experiments with A549 cells.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (15596018CN, Thermo Fisher, USA). RNA concentration and purity were determined on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized with HiScript III RT SuperMix (R323-01, Vazyme, China). qRT-PCR was performed on a StepOne Plus system with SYBR Green Mix (CL132-01, Vazyme, China). The sequences of the primers used are as follows: CD86 (Forward Primer: 5′-CCTTCCTGCTCTCTGCTAACTT-3′, Reverse Primer: 5′-ACAAGCTGATGGAAACGTCG-3′), CD163 (Forward Primer: 5′-GAAGACAGAGACAGCGGCTT-3′, Reverse Primer: 5′-AGGTATCTTAAAGGCTCACTGG-3′), SPP1 (Forward Primer: 5′-CCCTGACCCATCTCAGAAGC-3′, Reverse Primer: 5′-CTTGGAAGGGTCTGTGGGG-3′), CXCL9 (Forward Primer: 5′-TTGGAGTGCAAGGAACCCC-3′, Reverse Primer: 5′-TAGTCCCTTGGTTGGTGCTG-3′), GAPDH (Forward Primer: 5′-AATGGGCAGCCGTTAGGAAA-3′, Reverse Primer: 5′-GCCCAATACGACCAAATCAGAG-3′).

WB

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) and protein concentration was determined with a BCA kit (P0009, Beyotime, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h at room temperature, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against: p-p38 (1:1000, rabbit, 9211, CST, USA), p38 (1:1000, rabbit, 9212, CST, USA), MEF2C (1:1000, rabbit, 5030, CST, USA), HPGDS (1:1000, mouse, 22522-1-AP, Proteintech, USA) and GAPDH (1:8000, mouse, 60004-1, Proteintech, USA). After three TBST washes, membranes were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), 1:8000, 111-035-003, Jackson, USA; goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L), 1:8000, 115-035-003, Jackson, USA). Signals were developed with an ECL kit (34577, Thermo Fisher, USA) and protein expression was quantified.

Colony-formation assay

Log-phase A549 cells were trypsinized, resuspended to a single-cell suspension, counted, and seeded at 800 cells per well in 6-well plates with 2 mL conditioned medium derived from THP-1 cells in different experimental groups. Cultures were maintained in an incubator at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ with medium renewal every 3 days. When macroscopic colonies appeared, media were discarded and cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, rinsed gently under running water, and air-dried at room temperature. Colonies containing >50 cells were counted.

CCK-8 cell viability assay

Log-phase A549 cells were seeded at 2 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates (three replicates) with blank wells (medium only) as controls. Each well was cultured with 200 μL of THP-1 conditioned medium from the corresponding experimental group. After 12 h of incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (C0038, Beyotime, China) was added to each well, followed by light-free incubation at 37 °C in a cell incubator for 1 h. Optical density (OD) values at 450 nm were measured with a Multiskan SkyHigh microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA). These recorded data were defined as the day 0 measurements. Subsequent data collection was performed once daily for a total of three consecutive days.

Transwell migration/invasion assay

THP-1 cells were plated in the lower chamber of Transwell inserts filled with RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% PS. A549 cells suspended in 200 µL DMEM were seeded onto Matrigel-coated (354230, Corning, USA; invasion) or uncoated (migration) inserts at 2 × 104 cells per well. After 24 h co-culture at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, cells on the underside were fixed with 4% PFA, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, and counted in five random fields under an inverted microscope.

Flow cytometry

THP-1 cells in the co-culture system were washed twice with PBS to prepare single-cell suspension, and incubated with PE Mouse anti-Human CD163 (556018, BD Biosciences, USA) or Mouse Anti-Human CD86 antibody (Thermo Fisher, PA5-88284, USA) for 60 min at 4 °C. After two additional washes, cells were resuspended in 200 µL FACS buffer and analyzed on a FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) to determine the percentage of CD163⁺ cells and CD86+ cells.

ICC

In vitro cellular ICC: After discarding the medium, THP-1 cells on coverslips were gently rinsed with PBS (10 min each), fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, blocked with immunostaining blocking buffer (P0102, Beyotime, China) for 2 h at room temperature, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-MEF2C (1:400, rabbit, 5030, CST, USA). Sections were then incubated with Donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor®488 (1:1000, 711-545-152, Jackson, USA) for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, the sections were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI (P0131, Beyotime, China), and a Leica fluorescence microscope was used for photographic recording.

ICC of mouse tumor tissues: After isolation, tumor tissues from subcutaneous transplantation tumor model in nude mice were immediately fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h. Following gradient dehydration and transparency treatment, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and cut into 4 μm-thick sections for subsequent use. The sections were dewaxed, rehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and rinsed thoroughly. Antigen retrieval was then performed via heat induction, followed by natural cooling to room temperature and another round of rinsing. The sections were blocked with immunofluorescence blocking solution (P0102, Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 2 h. After removal of the blocking solution, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with primary antibody against MEF2C (1:400, Rabbit, 5030, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) under light protection. On the next day, the sections were washed with PBS, then incubated with Donkey Anti-Rabbit 488 secondary antibody (1:1000, 711-545-152, Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA) at room temperature for 2 h in the dark. Upon completion of staining, the sections were mounted with DAPI-containing anti-fade mounting medium (P0131, Beyotime, China), and images were captured using a Leica fluorescence microscope.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

According to predictions from the JASPAR database, potential MEF2C binding sites exist within the HPGDS promoter region. The target fragment was amplified and cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector to construct the HPGDS-WT luciferase reporter vector. Meanwhile, site-directed mutagenesis was performed to mutate the core MEF2C binding sequence, generating the HPGDS-MUT promoter reporter vector. All constructed plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing. Cells were seeded and transient transfection was performed when cell density reached approximately 70%–80%. The experimental groups were set as follows: WT + NC, co-transfection with HPGDS-WT plasmid and empty vector; WT + OE, co-transfection with HPGDS-WT and MEF2C overexpression plasmid; MUT + NC, co-transfection with HPGDS-MUT and empty vector; MUT + OE, co-transfection with HPGDS-MUT and MEF2C overexpression plasmid. The pRL-TK Renilla luciferase plasmid was co-transfected simultaneously as an internal reference to correct for transfection efficiency. Each group was set up with three replicate wells, and all experiments were repeated at least three times. At 48 h after transfection, detection was conducted in accordance with the instructions of the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Beyotime, RG027, China). The activities of firefly luciferase and Renilla luciferase were sequentially measured using a microplate luminometer. The relative luciferase activity was finally presented as the Firefly/Renilla ratio.

Animal ethical statement

All mice used in this study were BALB/c-nu. Six to eight-week-old male wild-type BALB/c-nu mice were purchased from Charles River (Beijing, China). All experimental procedures complied with the Regulations on the Administration of Laboratory Animals of China and were reviewed and approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Luoxi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China (Approval No.: LX4825040703), with full adherence to laboratory animal welfare and ethical requirements.

Establishment of mouse model

The human lung cancer cell line A549 was cultured in 10 cm tissue culture dishes, and cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected. Macrophages were isolated and activated simultaneously. Referring to the proportion described in published literature [40], A549 cells and THP-1 cells were mixed at a ratio of 2.5:1 and subcutaneously injected into nude mice. The animals were divided into four groups: NC group, THP-1 cells co-transfected with two empty plasmids; KO-Spp1 group, THP-1 cells co-transfected with SPP1 knockout plasmid and CXCL9 empty plasmid; OE-CXCL9 group, THP-1 cells co-transfected with SPP1 empty plasmid and CXCL9 overexpression plasmid; KO-Spp1 + OE-CXCL9 group, THP-1 cells co-transfected with SPP1 knockout plasmid and CXCL9 overexpression plasmid. All experimental operations strictly followed animal ethical guidelines. Mice were raised in an SPF environment in accordance with the relevant protocols of the Animal Care and Use Committee.

Monitoring of tumor volume

Starting from Day 0 after mixed cell inoculation, the longitudinal diameter (L) and transverse diameter (W) of tumors were measured every five days using a vernier caliper. Tumor volume was calculated according to the formula: Tumor volume (mm3) = L × W2/2, and continuous monitoring was performed for 25 consecutive days.

Isolation of primary cells

Freshly resected mouse tumor tissues were rinsed with pre-cooled PBS under sterile conditions to remove residual blood, and then cut into tissue fragments of approximately 1–2 mm3. Enzymatic digestion was applied for tissue dissociation: tissue fragments were immersed in RPMI-1640 digestion medium containing Collagenase IV (Sigma-Aldrich, C5138, USA, final concentration 1 mg/mL) and DNase I (Roche, 10104159001, Switzerland, final concentration 50 U/mL), followed by shaking incubation at 37 °C for 30–45 min. Gentle pipetting was performed every 10 min to facilitate tissue dissociation. After digestion, the cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer (BD Falcon, USA) to remove undigested tissue debris, centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded before resuspension with PBS. To enrich tumor-infiltrating immune cells, CD45⁺ cells were first isolated via magnetic bead sorting. The cell suspension was mixed with CD45 MicroBeads, mouse (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-052-301, Germany) at the ratio specified in the product manual, incubated at 4 °C for 15 min, and subjected to positive selection using the MACS magnetic column system to collect CD45⁺ tumor immune cells. Macrophages were further enriched from CD45⁺ cells using F4/80 MicroBeads, mouse (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-110-443, Germany) for magnetic sorting to obtain F4/80⁺ macrophages. Sorted cells were washed and resuspended in PBS containing 2% FBS, followed by cell counting and viability assessment. The obtained single-cell suspension was subsequently used for flow cytometry analysis, and the proportional distribution of different macrophage subsets in tumor tissues was evaluated by detecting combined macrophage biomarkers.

Data analysis and statistics

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical significance was determined by Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA where appropriate. Data analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel, GraphPad Prism 8.0 and ImageJ. P-values: ns, P > 0.05; *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; ****, P < 0.0001.

Results

Composition of the LUAD immune microenvironment and identification of TAM functional subsets

To dissect the cellular heterogeneity of the LUAD TME, we first classified cells into three major compartments using canonical markers: PTPRC for immune cells, EPCAM for epithelial cells, and PECAM1 for stromal cells (Fig. 1A, B). Comparison across different LUAD samples revealed that immune cells dominated in every patient, accounting for the largest fraction across all samples (Fig. 1C). Subsequent high-resolution annotation of immune cells identified seven distinct cell types: T cells, macrophages, monocytes, B cells, mDCs, mast cells and plasma cells (Fig. 1D, E). We therefore sub-clustered macrophages into nine clusters that were consolidated into five functionally defined subsets: SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs, SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs, MARCO⁺ TAMs, FOLR2⁺ tissue-resident macrophages (TRMs) and EPCAM⁺ TAMs (Fig. 1G–I). To dissect the functional differences among TAM subsets, we performed differential expression analysis to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 1J), and GO-Biological Process and KEGG enrichment analyses on each subset’s DEGs. Results revealed that SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs were enriched for chemokine-mediated signaling, response to chemokine, neutrophil migration and chemotaxis, IL-17 and TNF pathways; SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs displayed strong antigen processing and presentation via MHC class II; MARCO⁺ TAMs were enriched for lipid-metabolic processes; FOLR2⁺ TRMs mainly participated in complement activation and humoral immune response; EPCAM⁺ TAMs were enriched for oxidative stress, TNF and IL-17 signaling (Fig. 1K). Collectively, LUAD macrophages display marked functional heterogeneity, with each subset potentially influencing tumor immunity responses and disease progression through distinct molecular programs.

SPP1⁺ and CXCL9⁺ TAMs predict opposite LUAD prognoses

Given that functional polarization dictates the dual roles of TAMs, we speculated that the abundance of individual TAM subsets might correlate with clinical prognosis in LUAD. To test this, we performed survival analysis in the TCGA-LUAD cohort. Results revealed that high proportions of SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs, MARCO⁺ TAMs and EPCAM⁺ TAMs were each associated with markedly shorter OS (P < 0.0001), whereas enrichment of SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs or FOLR2⁺ TRMs predicted significantly better prognosis (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2A). These findings indicate that SPP1 and CXCL9 expression patterns are not only mutually exclusive but also exert opposing influences on patient outcome. To further validate this mutually exclusive relationship, we visualized the expression distributions of SPP1 and CXCL9 on the UMAP plot, which showed mutually exclusive spatial distribution (Fig. 2B). To further validate the generality of CS polarity, we systematically verified the mutually exclusive expression pattern of SPP1 and CXCL9 across three independent public single-cell datasets of LUAD/NSCLC (GSE131907, GSE127465, and GSE189357) (Fig. 2C–E). In TAMs from all three datasets, the expression of SPP1 and CXCL9 exhibited a significant negative correlation (Spearman r = −0.558, −0.517, and −0.395, with all P values lower than 1.7 × 10⁻81) (Fig. 2C). UMAP visualization further confirmed that these two genes displayed distinctly segregated distribution within the macrophage landscape (Fig. 2D). Quantitative analysis revealed that SPP1⁺CXCL9⁻ and SPP1⁻CXCL9⁺ cells constituted the two predominant polarized populations in each dataset, whereas cells co-expressing SPP1 and CXCL9 accounted for an extremely low proportion across the three cohorts (0.7%–4.3%), indicating that the vast majority of TAMs reside in a monophenotypically polarized state (Fig. 2E). The highly consistent results obtained from three independent cohorts confirm that the mutually exclusive expression pattern of CS polarity represents a prevalent biological characteristic of TAMs in LUAD/NSCLC, rather than a sporadic observation limited to a single dataset.

To exclude potential confounding effects derived from non-macrophage populations on the above analyses, we further systematically evaluated the expression distribution of SPP1 and CXCL9 at the pan-cell-type level (Fig. 2F, G). Dot plot analysis demonstrated that both the average expression level and the proportion of positive cells for SPP1 and CXCL9 in TAMs were markedly higher than those in all other cell types (Fig. 2F). Scatter plot results further indicated that CXCL9 and SPP1 were predominantly expressed in TAMs; although partial expression was detected in other non-macrophage cell types, their expression levels remained low or extremely marginal (Fig. 2G). These results reveal that the expression of SPP1 and CXCL9 in this study exhibits high TAM-enriched specificity, and confounding factors originating from non-macrophage cells exert a limited impact on the conclusions regarding CS polarity analysis.

Furthermore, we explored the functional characteristics of the two TAM subpopulations, namely SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs and SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs. Gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs were enriched for cell adhesion and vasculature development pathways, while SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs were primarily associated with adaptive immune responses, such as activation of lymphocytes and leukocytes (Fig. 2H, I). Next, to systematically compare the functional characteristics of these subsets, DEGs were analyzed and normalized enrichment scores (NES) of these genes across various signaling pathways were calculated. Results showed consistent differences of activation in key biological processes such as epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis and interferon (IFN)-α/γ responses (Fig. 2J). Specifically, SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs exhibited significantly higher EMT and angiogenic activity than SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs (P < 0.0001), whereas SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs displayed stronger IFN-α/γ response (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2K). These findings indicate that SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs may foster tumor invasion and metastasis through vascular development and EMT, whereas the SPP1⁻CXCL9high subset could exert anti-tumor effects through type I IFN responses and adaptive immunity. Nevertheless, these functional differences remain inferred solely at the transcriptomic level, and their specific underlying mechanisms require further experimental validation.

To further identify TAM subsets linked to survival outcomes in LUAD patients, we applied Scissor analysis and found 613 Scissor⁺ TAMs significantly associated with poor prognosis, whereas 905 Scissor⁻ TAMs correlated with better survival (Fig. 2L). Further analysis of the proportion of Scissor⁻ cells within each TAM subset revealed that SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs and FOLR2⁺ TRMs contained a higher fraction of Scissor⁻ TAMs (Fig. 2M), reinforcing their association with favorable survival outcomes.

Collectively, the above results demonstrate that the mutually exclusive polarized expression pattern of CXCL9 and SPP1 not only helps distinguish TAM subpopulations with distinct functional states, but may also be correlated with the immune microenvironmental characteristics and patient prognosis of LUAD. Nevertheless, the specific regulatory mechanisms and causal relationships remain to be elucidated by further experimental investigations.

The trajectory originated predominantly from the upper-right branch, and cells gradually differentiated and extended toward the upper-left and lower-left directions along pseudotime progression. In terms of trajectory distribution, SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs were mainly enriched in the right branch and the early pseudotime stage, accounting for a large proportion at the initial pseudotime region. TAM subsets exert opposing immunomodulatory effects through differential CXCL9/SPP1 expression

To clarify how CS polarity influences LUAD outcome, we analyzed the associated signaling pathways and immune microenvironment. The CXCL9/SPP1 expression ratio was markedly higher in normal lung tissues than in tumors (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3D), indicating that imbalanced CS polarity may be closely related to the onset and progression of LUAD. Survival analysis revealed that the SPP1⁻CXCL9high phenotype was associated with better clinical prognoses, evidenced as longer OS, disease-specific survival and progression-free interval relative to the SPP1⁺CXCL9low subset (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3E). Through analyses of DEGs and pathway enrichment, it was revealed that the SPP1⁻CXCL9high group displayed robust activation of type I and type II-IFN responses, whereas hypoxia- and EMT-related pathways were suppressed (Fig. 3F). Correlation analysis of the key molecular signatures demonstrated negative associations of the DEGs with chemokine receptors, mast cells, B cells, MHC-II and other immune signatures (Fig. 3G). The heatmap displayed the top 20 molecular signature gene sets with the most significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 3H). The results showed that these 20 signature gene sets could be clearly divided into two clusters with completely opposite functional orientations. Signatures markedly enriched in the CXCL9high SPP1low group included MHC_Class_II (MHC class II antigen presentation), TLS_Nature (tertiary lymphoid structures), B_cells_Danaher_et_al and B_cells_Bindea_et_al (B cell infiltration), TFH_Bindea_et_al (follicular helper T cells), Tem_Bindea_et_al (effector memory T cells), and Mast_cells_Bindea_et_al (mast cells), suggesting that patients in this group possessed more active adaptive immune responses and more intact antitumor immune surveillance functions. By contrast, signatures significantly enriched in the CXCL9low SPP1high group comprised Nature_metabolism_Hypoxia and Hu_hypoxia_signature (hypoxic metabolism), MDSC_Peng_et_al (myeloid-derived suppressor cells), PPAGs (pro-tumor associated genes), and MT_exosome (exosome secretion), indicating a more prominent immunosuppressive microenvironment in patients of this group. Further quantitative comparisons confirmed that MHC-II molecules were significantly upregulated (P < 0.0001) and hypoxia-metabolism signatures were down-regulated (P < 0.0001) in the SPP1⁻CXCL9high subset (Fig. 3I). Immune-infiltration profiling indicated higher abundances of anti-tumor cells, such as activated NK cells and B cells, in the SPP1⁻CXCL9high subset than the SPP1⁺CXCL9low subset (P < 0.05, Fig. 3J). Moreover, expression of multiple immune-checkpoint molecules, including CD27, CTLA-4, PDCD1 and LAG3, was significantly elevated in this group (P < 0.001, Fig. 3K), suggesting that the SPP1⁻CXCL9high phenotype may be more responsive to immunotherapy.

SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs exert their regulatory effects through the MEF2C-HPGDS signaling

To dissect the transcriptional regulatory mechanism of MEF2C in depth, we mapped a TF-target transcriptional network with MEF2C participation. Results showed that among SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs, MEF2C may directly or indirectly exert its biological function on downstream target genes such as SMAD5, NFIC and CEBPD (Fig. 5G). Further target-prediction revealed that MEF2C positively regulated the expression of ATP8B4, ALX3, RNPC3, ESRRB and HPGDS, and suppressed the expression of CD44, TIMM8B, ARF1 and DYNLT1 (Fig. 5H). Notably, MEF2C and HPGDS expression correlated positively across most cancer types in the TCGA pan-cancer cohort (Fig. 5I), and this correlation remained within the LUAD cohort (P < 0.0001, Fig. 5J). By analyzing the sequence of the regulatory region of HPGDS, we identified the presence of a MEF2C binding motif, suggesting that MEF2C may directly activate HPGDS transcription by specifically recognizing and binding to this cis-regulatory element (Fig. 5K). To further verify this hypothesis, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was designed to validate the regulatory mechanism of MEF2C on HPGDS. Compared with the WT + NC group, MEF2C overexpression markedly enhanced the luciferase activity of the HPGDS promoter. In contrast, for the HPGDS promoter with mutated MEF2C binding sites, no significant alteration in luciferase activity was observed regardless of MEF2C overexpression (Fig. 5L). These results indicate that MEF2C directly activates the transcriptional activity of the HPGDS promoter by binding to its specific binding sites.

CS polarity modulates the malignant progression of LUAD cells

To further investigate the impact of CS polarity on the malignant phenotype of LUAD cells, this study established an in vitro co-culture model of THP-1 and A549 cells using a Transwell co-culture system. First, the transfection efficiency was verified by qRT-PCR. CS polarity modulates TAM polarization

To further verify how CS polarity shapes TAM polarization, we performed a comprehensive phenotypic profiling of TAMs by qRT-PCR and flow cytometry. Under LUAD co-culture conditions, either KO-SPP1 or OE-CXCL9 treatment individually could significantly promote M1 polarization of TAMs (P < 0.0001). The combined treatment of KO-SPP1 + OE-CXCL9 produced the strongest effect, yielding the highest CD86 expression level and the lowest proportion of CD163⁺ cells (Fig. 6G–J). These results showed that concurrently inducing high CXCL9 expression and suppressing SPP1 could synergistically drive TAMs polarization toward the anti-tumor M1 phenotype.

Prior studies have established that type I/II IFN pathways are central regulators of macrophage polarization, and the generation as well as the responsiveness of IFN signals are frequently modulated by the p38/MAPK pathway [41–43]. In addition, p38/MAPK, an essential stress and differentiation regulatory pathway, governs the differentiation of immune cells and their functional fate by targeting the downstream TF MEF2C [44–46]. Based on this background, we further investigated the role of the p38-MEF2C axis in CS polarity-mediated TAM polarization. WB showed that either KO-SPP1 or OE-CXCL9 alone activated the p38/MAPK pathway and upregulated the expression of MEF2C. The combined intervention (KO-SPP1 + OE-CXCL9) produced the strongest activation and simultaneously upregulated HPGDS, the downstream target of MEF2C, and this effect could be reversed by the p38/MAPK pathway inhibitor SB203580 (Fig. 6K). ICC confirmed that MEF2C nuclear translocation was most prominent in the KO-SPP1 + OE-CXCL9 group while the application of SB203580 could partially inhibit this nuclear translocation phenomenon (Fig. 6L). Collectively, these data indicated that reversal of CS polarity might drive TAM polarization to the M1 phenotype through the activation of the p38/MAPK-MEF2C signaling axis, modulating TAMs’ immunoregulatory function.

Discussion

LUAD, the most frequent histological subtype of non-small-cell lung cancer, is driven by a TME whose extraordinary heterogeneity affects both disease progression and therapeutic responses [47, 48]. Among the diverse immune cells in the LUAD TME, TAMs constitute the dominant myeloid compartment and modulate the tumorigenesis by modulating immunosuppression, angiogenesis, and metastasis [49–51]. Yet the conventional M1/M2 dichotomy fails to capture the functional diversity of TAMs. In reality, within the TME, multiple functionally heterogeneous TAM subsets coexist, and their polarization status is closely linked to patient prognosis [23, 52, 53]. Therefore, there is an urgent biological imperative to integrate multi-omics datasets with functional assays, delineate the multi-subset landscape of TAM polarity in LUAD, clarify its molecular underpinnings and immune-regulatory networks, and identify novel therapeutic targets. By combining single-cell transcriptomics, in vitro modeling, and clinical-cohort validation, the present study elucidates the pivotal role of the CXCL9/SPP1 expression polarity in TAM functional differentiation and LUAD prognosis, uncovers its upstream signaling control, and provides a theoretical framework for refined immune classification and precision immunotherapy of LUAD.

We first performed a systematic annotation of the TME based on classical marker genes and found that immune cells dominate the LUAD microenvironment, with T cells and macrophages representing the two largest compartments. Notably, macrophage abundance varied markedly among patients, underscoring their phenotypic plasticity and functional diversity as potential drivers of LUAD immune regulation. Sub-clustering further resolved five functionally distinct TAM subsets: SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs, SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs, MARCO⁺ TAMs, FOLR2⁺ TRMs and EPCAM⁺ TAMs. GSEA on their DEGs revealed different biological processes enriched by these subsets: SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs correlated with chemotaxis signaling, neutrophil migration, and IL-17/TNF pathways; SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs had upregulated MHC-II molecules and were enriched for antigen presentation pathways, indicating their greater potential for immune activation [54, 55]. The prognostic significance of this classification was validated: SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs, MARCO⁺ TAMs, and EPCAM⁺ TAMs were associated with poor prognosis, whereas SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs and FOLR2⁺ TRMs indicated better clinical outcomes, underscoring that the functional polarization of TAMs is closely linked to LUAD progression. More importantly, SPP1 and CXCL9 exhibited a mutually exclusive expression pattern, with opposite prognostic impacts, suggesting that the SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAM subset may define an anti-tumor population. This notion is supported by Su et al.[56], who showed that Denosumab downregulates SPP1 and promotes CS polarity to enhance cytotoxic T cell activity and anti-tumor immune responses. Consistently, Scissor analysis revealed that prognostically favorable TAMs (Scissor⁻) were enriched in SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs and FOLR2⁺ TRMs, further linking increased CS polarity to better clinical outcomes in LUAD.

Through cell–cell communication analysis, we found that distinct TAM subsets play diametrically opposed signaling roles in the TME. SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs acted as the principal senders in MHC-II associated signaling pathways, probably promoting T cell activation through ligand-receptor pairs such as HLA-DRA-CD4 [57, 58]. Conversely, SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs preferentially engaged the microenvironmental modulation through the SPP1-CD44 and FN1-CD44 axes, serving mainly as receivers or intermediaries within FN1 and SPP1 pathways. This indicates that this subset may primarily participate in stromal remodeling and immunosuppressive processes [59–62].

To uncover the regulatory mechanism underlying the SPP1⁻CXCL9high phenotype, we constructed a co-expression network with hdWGCNA and identified module M5 as the core functional module. Within M5, MEF2C emerged as the key TF and was selectively highly expressed in SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs. Meanwhile, MEF2C bound directly to regulatory elements of HPGDS and robustly activated its transcription; across multiple cancer cohorts, MEF2C and HPGDS expression were positively correlated. Notably, Liu et al. [63] reported that mast cell derived PGD2 upregulates HPGDS to exert tumor-suppressive and anti-inflammatory effects in LUAD. Integrating literature and our findings, we speculate that the MEF2C-HPGDS axis may launch a similar anti-tumor immune program in SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs, enhancing antigen presentation and sculpting a beneficial TME. Our future work will focus on the specific mechanism of this pathway governing macrophage polarization and LUAD progression, aiming to offer new immunotherapeutic strategies that target TAMs.

Building on the above findings, we used an in vitro co-culture system to define the functional impact of CS polarity on LUAD malignant progression. Inducing high CXCL9 expression while silencing SPP1 synergistically skewed macrophages toward an M1 phenotype, as evidenced by upregulated CD86, reduced CD163, and a marked suppression of LUAD-cell proliferation, migration and invasion. WB and ICC showed that this reprogramming coincided with increased p38 phosphorylation, enhanced nuclear translocation of MEF2C and upregulation of its downstream target HPGDS, implicating activation of the p38/MAPK-MEF2C axis as a core mechanism through which CS polarity modulates TAM polarization. To further verify the direct transcriptional regulation of HPGDS by MEF2C, we performed a dual-luciferase reporter assay. The results showed that MEF2C overexpression significantly enhanced the luciferase activity of the wild-type HPGDS promoter, whereas this effect was abolished after mutation of the MEF2C binding sites. These findings directly confirm at the molecular level that MEF2C activates HPGDS transcription by specifically binding to its promoter. In addition, the p38/MAPK pathway inhibitor SB203580 reversed the above polarization effects, further validating the causal role of this pathway in CS polarity-mediated functional conversion of TAMs. The p38/MAPK pathway is a canonical regulator of M1 polarization that cross-talks with type I/II IFN and other immune signals [41, 64, 65], whereas MEF2C, as its key TF, governs cell differentiation, immune responsiveness and tumor suppression [66–68]. To further validate the above findings in a physiological microenvironment, we established a subcutaneous transplantation tumor model in nude mice. The results demonstrated that tumor growth was markedly inhibited in the KO-SPP1 + OE-CXCL9 combined treatment group, which also presented the highest proportion of intratumoral CD86⁺ macrophages and the lowest proportion of CD163⁺ cells. The in vivo experimental results were highly consistent with the in vitro conclusions. These findings not only functionally confirm the critical role of CS polarity in regulating TAM polarization and the malignant phenotype of LUAD, but also systematically reveal the pivotal function of the p38/MAPK-MEF2C-HPGDS signaling axis in this process, providing experimental evidence for subsequent mechanistic research and therapeutic targeting of this pathway.

In summary, this study has systematically mapped the heterogeneity of TAMs in LUAD, established the CS polarity as a key molecular signature governing TAM differentiation and prognosis prediction, and uncovered a p38/MAPK-MEF2C axis that modulates TAM polarization and malignant tumor phenotype, offering potential targets for immunotherapy targeting TAMs. Nevertheless, several limitations remain. First, at the bioinformatics analytical level, the single-cell transcriptomic data adopted in this study were mainly derived from public databases. Heterogeneity may exist among different cohorts in terms of sample origin, sequencing platform, cell capture efficiency, and data preprocessing procedures. Although the mutually exclusive expression pattern of SPP1/CXCL9 was cross-validated across multiple independent LUAD/NSCLC datasets, potential batch effects and sample selection bias may still interfere with certain results. Second, several key conclusions of this study were primarily based on computational inference approaches, including Monocle pseudotime analysis, CellChat cell communication analysis, Scissors prognosis correlation analysis, and hdWGCNA co-expression network analysis. Such analyses mainly reflect correlations among cellular states rather than strict biological causality. Accordingly, the dynamic transformation relationship between SPP1⁺CXCL9low TAMs and SPP1⁻CXCL9high TAMs, the directionality of cell communication, and the authenticity of the MEF2C regulatory network still require further validation by combining lineage tracing, spatial transcriptomics, and gain- or loss-of-function assays. Moreover, this study mainly focused on macrophage subsets and did not fully explore the interplay between CS polarity and other immune cells such as T cells and neutrophils. In addition, the in vivo model applied herein was a subcutaneous transplantation tumor model in nude mice, which lacks a complete immune system. Further verification in immunocompetent mouse models such as the C57BL/6 LLC model is warranted to clarify the influence of CS polarity on systemic immune responses. Finally, although dual-luciferase reporter assays have functionally verified the transcriptional activation of the HPGDS promoter by MEF2C, ChIP-qPCR experiments are still lacking to directly confirm the physical binding of MEF2C to the HPGDS promoter region at the chromatin level. Follow-up studies could further refine the mechanistic interpretation of the MEF2C-HPGDS transcriptional regulatory axis. Future investigations may integrate gene-edited animal models, multi-omics integrated analysis, and clinical sample validation to comprehensively dissect the mechanisms by which CS polarity regulates the immune microenvironment and explore its translational potential as a novel target for LUAD immunotherapy.
