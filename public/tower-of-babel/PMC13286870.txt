Investigating the Effect of Transdiagnostic Group Intervention on Depression, Anxiety, and Postpartum Stress in Women With Preeclampsia: A Quasi‐Experimental Study
Authors: Zahra Hatami Asl, Nasrin Jaberghaderi, Mansor Rezaei, Negin Rezavand, Mastaneh Kamravamanesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectiveWomen with preeclampsia are at increased risk of postpartum mood and psychological disorders. This study examined the effect of a group‐based transdiagnostic intervention on depression, anxiety, and stress in this population.MethodsIn this semi‐experimental, single‐blind study using a pretest–posttest control design, 95 postpartum women with a history of preeclampsia were recruited from Imam Reza (AS) Hospital in Kermanshah. Participants were randomly assigned via block randomization to either the intervention group (n = 48) or the control group (n = 47). The intervention comprised eight 45–60‐min group sessions delivered using a transdiagnostic protocol, whereas the control group received routine postpartum care. Outcomes were assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale and the DASS‐21 at baseline, 1 week, and 2 months after the intervention. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.ResultsThe intervention group showed a significant decrease in depression (1.54 ± 0.43), anxiety (2.13 ± 0.40), and stress (1.5 ± 0.54) in both follow‐up periods compared to baseline (p < 0.05), while no change was observed in the control group.ConclusionGroup‐based transdiagnostic training effectively improves postpartum depression, anxiety, and stress in women with preeclampsia and may be integrated into postpartum care protocols for high‐risk pregnancies.Keywords: anxiety, cognitive‐behavioral therapy, depression, perinatal mental health, postpartum, preeclampsia, stress, transdiagnostic

Introduction

Preeclampsia, whether occurring on its own or superimposed on chronic hypertension, is among the most severe hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and a pregnancy‐specific syndrome that may impact nearly every organ system in the body [1]. Preeclampsia is usually characterized by elevated blood pressure and proteinuria [2]. Preeclampsia represents a severe hypertensive syndrome specific to gestation, with the potential to cause multi‐organ damage. It is recognized as a primary contributor to worldwide maternal and perinatal mortality. Recent data suggest a global prevalence of approximately 4.4% (within a 2%–8% range), though substantial regional variation exists. The higher impact observed in LMICs is largely linked to inadequate prenatal services and systematic healthcare challenges [3].

Although the physical symptoms of preeclampsia usually resolve after childbirth, women who experience this condition are at increased risk for postpartum mental health disorders compared with women who have normotensive pregnancies [4]. Research has demonstrated significant associations between maternal psychological conditions—such as anxiety and depression—and preeclampsia [5]. In addition, PTSD occurs at a notably high rate following preeclampsia, with cesarean delivery, limited social support, and postpartum depression identified as major risk factors [6].

Another common psychiatric condition among women with preeclampsia is postpartum depression (PPD) [5]. Postpartum depression is a mood disorder that typically emerges between 2 weeks and 1 year after delivery [7]. Its global prevalence ranges from 3% to 38%, and studies indicate that gestational hypertension is a significant risk factor, with depression rates reported between 20% and 30% in this population [8]. When left untreated, postpartum depression can continue for months or years, resulting in weakened mother–infant bonding, strained family relationships, reduced maternal functioning, and, in severe cases, maternal suicide or infanticide [7].

Even with these risks, postpartum care for women who have had preeclampsia typically prioritizes physical healing over mental health screening and intervention. Implementing targeted mental health screenings for this high‐risk population could help ensure early detection, appropriate referrals, and prompt treatment for psychological conditions [9].

In addition to pharmacological treatment, psychological interventions—especially cognitive‐behavioral therapy (CBT)—have received increasing attention in the management of postpartum mental health disorders. CBT combines cognitive and behavioral methods, including behavior modification, cognitive restructuring, problem‐solving, relaxation, and interpersonal skills training, to target maladaptive thoughts, emotions, and behaviors [10]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of CBT in reducing symptoms of anxiety, stress, and depression [11].

One innovative approach within the CBT framework is integrative transdiagnostic therapy [12]. This treatment model aims to equip individuals with skills to effectively regulate negative emotions across a range of psychological disorders [13]. The protocol is designed to address five key areas: developing awareness of emotions in the moment, enhancing cognitive flexibility, decreasing behaviors that involve avoiding emotions, increasing tolerance for physical sensations, and engaging in exposure to situations that trigger emotions [14].

Grounded in the three‐component model of emotion (thoughts, feelings, and behaviors), transdiagnostic therapy emphasizes present‐moment awareness and adaptive emotional regulation, allowing individuals to recognize and modify ineffective coping strategies [15].

Research indicates that transdiagnostic interventions are effective for perinatal populations. For instance, a clinical trial in Iran during 1400 showed that integrating online transdiagnostic therapy led to significant reductions in emotional distress and increased psychological empowerment among pregnant women [16]. Similarly, a quasi‐experimental study in Egypt (2020) found that CBT effectively reduced anxiety, stress, and pregnancy‐specific depression in women with preeclampsia while improving coping strategies in high‐risk pregnancies [17].

Notwithstanding this increasing body of evidence, research specifically investigating the effectiveness of transdiagnostic therapy for postpartum mental health disorders in women with preeclampsia has not yet been conducted. This highlights a critical need for psychological support during the postpartum period [18]. Midwives, as key members of the maternal healthcare team, play a vital role in identifying postpartum depression, providing counseling, and facilitating referrals for psychological treatment when necessary [19].

Accordingly, the present study aimed to determine the effect of a group‐based transdiagnostic intervention on depression, anxiety, and postpartum stress among women with preeclampsia referred to Imam Reza Hospital in Kermanshah.

Materials and Methods

This study employed a quasi‐experimental design with a pretest–posttest control group. The statistical population consisted of women with a history of preeclampsia in Kermanshah who had given birth between 2 months and 1 year prior to the study. Participants were selected using convenience sampling.

The inclusion criteria were: age between 15 and 49 years; absence of severe mental disorders; no use of mood‐altering medications (including antidepressants); a score of 12 or higher on the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS); absence of suicidal ideation or intent to self‐harm; fulfillment of at least three DSM‐5 diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder; delivery resulting in a healthy live infant; and a confirmed diagnosis of preeclampsia of varying severity during pregnancy or delivery based on clinical and paraclinical evidence and confirmed by a gynecologist. Cases with eclampsia were excluded.

Preeclampsia severity was classified according to established clinical guidelines as follows:

1.Mild preeclampsia: Elevated blood pressure (systolic 140–159 mmHg and/or diastolic 90–109 mmHg) occurring after 20 weeks of gestation in women with previously normal blood pressure, accompanied by proteinuria ≥ 300 mg in a 24‐h urine sample, and without evidence of vital organ involvement.

Mild preeclampsia: Elevated blood pressure (systolic 140–159 mmHg and/or diastolic 90–109 mmHg) occurring after 20 weeks of gestation in women with previously normal blood pressure, accompanied by proteinuria ≥ 300 mg in a 24‐h urine sample, and without evidence of vital organ involvement.

2.Severe preeclampsia: Presence of at least one of the following: systolic blood pressure ≥ 160 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 110 mmHg, severe proteinuria (≥ 5 g in a 24‐h urine sample), or signs and symptoms indicative of involvement of other organ systems.

Severe preeclampsia: Presence of at least one of the following: systolic blood pressure ≥ 160 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 110 mmHg, severe proteinuria (≥ 5 g in a 24‐h urine sample), or signs and symptoms indicative of involvement of other organ systems.

The exclusion criteria included severe complications of preeclampsia such as nephropathy, current use of psychiatric medications, absence from more than two intervention sessions, incomplete questionnaire responses, unwillingness to continue participation, and cases requiring referral to a psychiatrist.

Following approval of the research protocol by Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences and ethical clearance from the Ethics Committee (IR.KUMS.REC.1402.386), eligible women with a documented history of preeclampsia were identified through medical records. After receiving a full explanation of the study objectives, participants completed a demographic information form, the EPDS, and the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS‐21).

Initial screening data were collected from medical records, and eligible individuals were contacted using the addresses listed in their files. After obtaining verbal agreement, a meeting time was scheduled, during which participants completed the EPDS. Women who scored ≥ 12 on the EPDS and reported no suicidal ideation subsequently underwent a semi‐structured telephone interview based on DSM‐5 criteria for major depressive disorder, conducted in collaboration with a trained clinical psychologist. Inter‐rater reliability for DSM‐5 diagnoses was evaluated using Cohen's kappa coefficient, which indicated good agreement between raters (κ = 0.80).

After confirmation of eligibility, participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group using block randomization with blocks of four (e.g., AABB, ABAB, BAAB) to ensure equal group sizes. Randomization was carried out by an independent research team member using random number generation software, with no involvement in assessment or intervention procedures.

During the first session, the researcher explained the objectives of the study and obtained written informed consent from all participants. Baseline demographic and obstetric data, along with the DASS‐21, were completed by both groups. Due to the face‐to‐face nature of the educational intervention, blinding of participants and facilitators was not feasible; therefore, the study was conducted as a single‐blind (outcome‐assessor) trial.

The intervention group participated in 8 weekly in‐person transdiagnostic group intervention sessions, each lasting 45–60 min, conducted by a researcher trained in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and the integrated transdiagnostic treatment protocol for emotional disorders, under the supervision of a licensed psychological counselor. The intervention group was divided into four subgroups, each consisting of 12 participants. The control group received routine postpartum care per Iranian guidelines and did not receive any psychological intervention.

Assessments were conducted at three time points: baseline (pre‐intervention), 1 week post‐intervention, and 2 months post‐intervention. Depression, anxiety, and stress scores were compared between the two groups across these time points. Baseline assessments were administered by the researcher, while follow‐up assessments at 1 week and 2 months post‐intervention were conducted by a trained health worker.

The intervention content was derived from the 15‐session Unified Protocol for Transdiagnostic Treatment of Emotional Disorders developed by Barlow and colleagues. Based on the Persian translation by Dr. Ali Mehrabi and Dr. Abolfazl Mohammadi, and following expert review by faculty members of the Departments of Psychology and Psychiatry at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences, an 8‐session version of the protocol was approved and implemented (Table 1).

To ensure treatment fidelity, the intervention followed a standardized manual based on the adapted Unified Protocol. Session content was monitored using structured checklists, and the facilitator received ongoing supervision from a licensed psychological counselor to ensure adherence to the protocol.

This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and suitability for inclusion in systematic reviews.

Sample Size and How to Determine It

According to the studies by Hassan et al. [17] and Asghari et al. [20], and considering the depression, anxiety, and stress scores in these two studies after the intervention, calculations were made in the two intervention and control groups. According to the mean and standard deviation of the three variables above in the two groups and in both studies (6 cases), the sample size calculation formula was used to compare the means of the two quantitative variables. With a confidence level of 95% and a test power of 90%, at least 24, 16, 31, 12, and 47 people were considered in each group. Finally, with a confidence of 95% and a test power of 90%, the largest number of samples among the above 6 cases was at least 47 people in each group and 94 people in total. Data Collection Tool.

Data Analysis Methods

In the descriptive statistics section, the mean and standard deviation were used to describe and report quantitative variables with a normal distribution. The χ 2 test, Mann–Whitney test, and independent t‐test were used to examine the similarity of demographic variables between the experimental and control groups. The independent t‐test was used to examine the changes in the mean of the aforementioned scale in different time intervals, and the analysis of variance was used between three times in each group. The paired t‐test was used to compare both times in each group, and the Bonferroni post hoc test was used to compare both times in the analysis of variance with repeated values. Ratios and percentages were also expressed for qualitative variables. Paired t‐test or Wilcoxon was used for comparison before and after. The appropriate test for repeated data was also used. SPSS statistical software version 25 was used to analyze the data. The statistical significance level was considered to be 0.05.

Findings

A total of 95 postpartum women participated in the study, of whom 47 (49.5%) were allocated to the control group and 48 (50.5%) to the intervention group. Most participants had secondary or higher education, and the majority were homemakers. In terms of economic status, the largest proportion reported a monthly income exceeding 15 million tomans.

The mean age of participants was 32.47 ± 6.63 years (range: 16–48 years), and the mean gestational age was 35.16 ± 3.09 weeks (range: 26–40 weeks). More than two‐thirds of the participants were experiencing their first or second pregnancy. The majority had preterm deliveries and underwent cesarean section. Regarding the severity of preeclampsia, participants were almost equally distributed between mild and severe categories.

No statistically significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups with respect to mode of delivery or severity of preeclampsia (p > 0.05).

To compare the two groups at each time point, the t‐test was used, and to compare the means of each group at three times, the repeated measures design test was used. Accordingly, in the intervention group, the mean postpartum depression, anxiety, and stress scores of women with preeclampsia decreased significantly in 1 week and 2 months after the intervention, respectively, compared to the pre‐test, which was not observed in the control group. Also, based on the results, the mean depression, anxiety, and stress scores of pregnant women were lower than the pre‐test in 1 week and 2 months after the intervention. Analysis of the findings from the independent t‐test to compare the mean scores of depression, anxiety, and postpartum stress in women with preeclampsia showed that there was a significant difference between the mean scores of depression, anxiety, and postpartum stress in women with preeclampsia 2 months after the intervention in the study groups (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

The results obtained in this study showed that before the intervention, there was no statistically significant difference between the mean depression score in the experimental and control groups, while 1 week after the intervention and at the follow‐up time 2 months after the intervention, a statistically significant difference was observed between the mean depression score in the aforementioned groups. Goudarzi et al. concluded in a clinical trial with a pre‐test, post‐test, and follow‐up design that the transdiagnostic intervention created a significant difference in the mean depression score in the intervention and control groups, and these two groups had a significant difference [16]. This was consistent with the results of the present study.

The results of repeated measures analysis in the present study showed that the intervention on postpartum depression in women with preeclampsia caused a significant decrease in the level of depression in these women over time, and there was a significant difference between the two groups, which was consistent with the results of the study by Hassan et al. and Ye et al. [8, 17]. The results of the study by Hassan et al. (2020) entitled “The Effect of Cognitive‐Behavioral Therapy on Anxiety, Stress, Depression, and Coping Pattern in Pregnant Women with Preeclampsia” showed that after the intervention (12 sessions of CBT), there was a statistically significant difference in depression, anxiety, and stress scores compared to before cognitive‐behavioral therapy in the intervention group [17]. Also, Carta et al. agreed with this issue and reported that mothers had a significant improvement in postpartum depression after receiving CBT [24]. In other interventions where people received mindfulness‐based treatments, they had greater reductions in symptoms of depression and stress than the control group, suggesting that mindfulness‐based interventions can improve depressive symptoms [25]. Studies have shown that transdiagnostic interventions can also be effective in treating postpartum depression in women with preeclampsia.

The results of the present study showed that after the intervention, there was a statistically significant difference between the mean anxiety scores in the experimental and control groups. The results of the present study were consistent with the results of studies by Ellard et al. [26] and Farchione et al. [27] which examined the effectiveness of transdiagnostic treatment in depressed and anxious samples, and provided further empirical support for the effectiveness of the transdiagnostic treatment approach [26, 27].

The results of the present study showed that the intervention significantly reduced postpartum anxiety in women with preeclampsia over time. This study is consistent with the studies of Zemestani and Imani, Kamali et al. and Li et al. [28, 29, 30]. In the Zemestani and Imani study, the findings of the study showed that transdiagnostic treatment is effective in reducing symptoms of depression, anxiety, and also in modifying emotion regulation strategies [29]. In explaining this hypothesis, it can be stated that one of the fundamental skills in the transdiagnostic protocol is the skill of emotional awareness, which emphasizes non‐judgmental and present‐focused awareness of emotional experience. This skill is achieved by understanding the adaptive nature of emotions and increasing emotional awareness using mindfulness techniques. The result of transdiagnostic therapy is that patients have a better understanding of the interaction of the three components (thoughts, feelings, and behavior) in creating internal emotional experiences.

The results of this study showed that after the intervention, there was a statistically significant difference between the mean stress scores in the experimental and control groups. These results are consistent with the study by Ito et al.(2016), who showed in their study that integrated transdiagnostic therapy reduced anxiety among women [31]. The results of the study by Holakouee et al.(2022) also showed that integrative transdiagnostic therapy is not only effective in reducing stress and anxiety, but also effective in reducing depression and increasing positive interactions between adolescents and their parents [32]. In explaining the above results, it can be said that one of the techniques of integrative transdiagnostic treatment is confronting the individual with anxiety and stress‐inducing symptoms. In this way, the individual is confronted hierarchically with anxiety‐inducing events and physical and psychological factors that cause anxiety and stress, and this confrontation continues to the extent that the individual's sensitivity to the stressful event and the individual's misinterpretation of anxiety symptoms that are experienced physically and psychologically are reduced. In fact, integrative transdiagnostic treatment leads to a reduction in symptoms by using acceptance of emotions, giving validity to the patient's emotions, as well as changing false metacognitive beliefs and teaching patients not to judge emotions and simply observe them [33].

The results showed that transdiagnostic therapy improved stress symptoms in the intervention group for women with a history of preeclampsia. This result is also consistent with the findings of Green et al., Netsi et al. [34, 35]. However, we believe that cognitive behavioral therapy has significant effects on reducing stress symptoms, which may be related to the type of method used. Therefore, the most influential factor in causing anxiety and stress may be related to dysfunctional thoughts and false beliefs about pregnancy and childbirth. As with many applications of meditation techniques related to CBT, there is value in framing CBT in the context of relaxation or stress management. This is notable in relation to preeclampsia, as stress is a common trigger of complications in pregnancy [36]. CBT provides individuals with a high level of ability to simply observe feelings, behaviors, and experiences, eliminate automatic and often dysfunctional reactions, and allow themselves to function and develop more rational and balanced relationships. In women with preeclampsia, psychotherapy may regulate emotions and feelings and psychosocial modifications. Thus, problematic health behavior is reduced. The collaboration of midwives, obstetricians, and psychiatrists as a team in the treatment of psychological disorders (anxiety, depression, and pregnancy‐specific stress) can help improve women's adverse conditions during pregnancy and postpartum. In addition, the effectiveness of other psychological treatments in the treatment of mental disorders in women with preeclampsia is essential.

The results of a study conducted by Asghari et al. are closely related to the results of the present study, which showed that the mean total score of the Pregnancy Stress and Worries Questionnaire was 21.12 ± 15.06 in the intervention group and 39.09 ± 19.55 in the control group [20]. Similarly, Jabari et al. reported a significant improvement in stress and anxiety levels in the intervention group compared to the control group [37].

Furthermore, CBT reduces stress and psychological symptoms, which is consistent with the results of Stewart, who explained that stress can stimulate the sympathetic nervous system and adrenal glands. Subsequently, it can increase heart rate, respiratory rate, and cause high blood pressure [37]. Significant reductions in anxiety and depression levels have been reported after CBT [34]. While Netsi et al. showed that women with depression had better night's sleep after CBT than the control group [35]. Also, Mahmoudjanlou et al.(2016) stated that CBT reduces stress levels in pregnant women and that CBT is safer than medication for anxiety disorders during pregnancy [38].

In a transdiagnostic intervention with factors that lead to depression, anxiety, stress, and lack of emotional control in women with a history of pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia, it teaches how to reduce these emotions. Also, by training mindfulness in people with depression, anxiety, and stress, it is possible to teach ways to deal with those emotions by examining pleasant daily activities versus unpleasant activities and planning for pleasant activities. In fact, in the mindfulness sessions held in this study, women who have given birth with a history of preeclampsia were able to discuss effective coping strategies with depression, anxiety, and stress, and learn more practical ways. In fact, all of this causes women who have given birth and have a history of preeclampsia to be in better psychological and emotional conditions, and due to their goal‐setting skills, understanding emotions and mindfulness, cognitive flexibility, and greater levels of emotional well‐being, they have a greater ability to use emotionally adaptive strategies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that group‐based transdiagnostic interventions can improve postpartum depression, anxiety, and stress in women with preeclampsia. The findings underscore the importance of integrating mental health services into midwifery counseling. Given that postpartum mental health in women with a history of preeclampsia has largely been neglected, it is recommended that maternal health policymakers, including those responsible for developing Iran's postpartum care protocols, prioritize timely identification of this high‐risk group and provide appropriate psychological support, including transdiagnostic interventions, to enhance long‐term mental health and quality of life. Furthermore, it is suggested that theoretical training on transdiagnostic interventions and their mechanisms in emotional disorders be incorporated into midwifery counseling education programs.

Future Research

It is suggested that future studies be conducted with randomized controlled trial designs and blinding of assessors to reduce potential biases and increase the internal validity of the findings. Also, the simultaneous use of objective indicators and biomarkers of stress and emotion regulation, such as cortisol measurements, along with self‐report instruments, can help to more accurately understand the mechanisms of effect of psychological interventions and provide stronger evidence for the effectiveness of these interventions.

We suggest that future studies directly compare this intervention with standard treatments using an active control group.
