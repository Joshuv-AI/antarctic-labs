On covers of quasivarieties of p-algebras
Zalán Gyenis

Abstract
. This paper characterizes the covers of varieties of p-algebras in the lattice of quasivarieties of p-algebras. In particular, it is shown that every such variety has exactly one cover in the lattice of subquasivarieties. This answers a problem of Kowalski and Słomczyńska [ 6 ] .

Overview

This article characterizes covers of subvarieties
in the lattice of subquasivarieties of p-algebras, settling Question 3 of
Kowalski and Słomczyńska
[
6

. I follow the notation and terminology
of
[
6
]
, but to make the paper self-contained, I recall below the
most important facts from
[
6
]
that will be needed. Fundamentals
of universal algebra (say, quasivarieties, subalgebras, etc.) are not recalled,
but the readers might consult
[

.

Recall that p-algebras are bounded distributive lattices endowed with
a unary operation
*
satisfying the condition
x\wedge y=0
iff
x\leq y^{*}
.
It is known that p-algebras form a variety
\mathsf{Pa}
, and by a result of
Lee
[
7
]
the lattice
of subvarieties of
\mathsf{Pa}
is a chain

\mathsf{Pa}_{-1}\subsetneq\mathsf{Pa}_{0}\subsetneq\mathsf{Pa}_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathsf{Pa}

of order type
\omega+1
, where
\mathsf{Pa}_{-1}
is the trivial variety, and

\mathsf{Pa}_{k}=\mathbf{HSP}(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{k})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Here

\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{k}
is the
k
-atom Boolean algebra with an extra cover of the top
element. In particular,
\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{0}
is the two-element Boolean algebra, and
\mathsf{Pa}_{0}

is the variety of Boolean algebras.

\mathsf{Pa}
is locally finite (finitely generated p-algebras are finite,
see
[
3
]
), and for any
m>0
the variety
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
is
axiomatized by a single identity

\bigvee_{i=1}^{m+1}(x_{i}\wedge\bigwedge_{j\neq i}x_{j}^{*})^{*}=1.

(see
[
5
]
and Lemma 1.3 in
[
6
]
).

The main tool that is used in this paper is Priestley-duality
for finite p-algebras. Objects dual to finite p-algebras are finite
posets, and morphisms are order-preserving maps commuting with the
maximal elements of the poset. Following
[
6
]
, these morphisms
are called pp-morphisms. Let
(P,\leq)
be a finite poset, and for
x\in P

write
M(x)
for the set of maximal elements in
P
above
x
.
(By the same token,
M(P)
is the set of all maximal elements of
P
).
Then
h:(P,\leq)\to(Q,\leq)
is a pp-morphism, if it preserves the ordering,
and
M(h(x))=h[M(x)]
for every
x\in P
.

For a finite p-algebra
\mathbf{A}\in\mathsf{Pa}
we write
\delta(\mathbf{A})
for its dual poset;
and for a finite poset
P
we write
\varepsilon(P)
for its dual p-algebra. Elements
of
\delta(\mathbf{A})
are the join-irreducible elements of
\mathbf{A}
ordered by the
converse of the order inherited from
\mathbf{A}
; while elements of
\varepsilon(P)

are the upsets of the poset. For finite
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathsf{Pa}

we have
\mathbf{A}\subseteq\mathbf{B}
if and only if there is a surjective pp-morphism

\delta(\mathbf{A})\twoheadrightarrow\delta(\mathbf{B})
. Conversely, for finite posets

P
and
Q
, there is a surjective pp-morphism
P\twoheadrightarrow Q

if and only if
\varepsilon(Q)\subseteq\varepsilon(P)
. (Here and later on
\subseteq

between algebras means subalgebra). Recall (see
[
5
]
) what
the dual posets of
\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m}
look like:

\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{0})
\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{1})
\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{2})
\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
\ldots
m
points

Figure 1.
The dual posets
\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
of the p-algebras

\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m}
generating
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
.

Let

there is a surjective pp-morphism

P_{1}\uplus\cdots\uplus P_{n}\twoheadrightarrow X

2.
Subvariety covers of
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
for
m\geq 2

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\prec\mathsf{K}
is a cover, then
\mathsf{K}=\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{A})
for a finite
\mathbf{A}\in\mathsf{Pa}
.

Proof .

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathsf{Pa}
be arbitrary non-trivial p-algebras.

Claim 1: There are homomorphisms
h:\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}
and
k:\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{A}
. To see this
consider the two-element
\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{0}\in\mathsf{Pa}
(which is the two-element Boolean algebra).
By Glivenko’s theorem (see
[
1
]
) there is a homomorphism
from
\mathbf{A}
into a non-trivial Boolean algebra, and from any non-trivial
Boolean algebra there is a
homomorphism into the two-element Boolean algebra. Composition of homomorphisms
gives a
homomorphism
\mathbf{A}\to\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{0}
. Also,
\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{0}
is a subalgebra of
\mathbf{B}
, therefore
the composition

\mathbf{A}\to\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{0}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{B}
gives
h:\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}
. The other case is analogous.

Claim 2:
\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})
. That
\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}\in\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})

and thus

\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
is straightforward. For the converse inclusion
define

f:\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}
by
f(a)=(a,h(a))
. Then
f
is a homomorphism and as it is
the identity
on the first coordinate,
f
is injective. Therefore,

\mathbf{A}\in\mathbf{IS}(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})
.
Similarly,
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbf{IS}(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})
. Consequently,

\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})
.

To complete the proof, let
\mathsf{K}=\mathbf{Q}(\{\mathbf{A}_{i}:i\in I\})
be a cover of
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
.
Then there is

\mathbf{A}_{i}\notin\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
. As
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
is axiomatized by a single
identity
\mathbf{ib}_{m}
,
it follows that
\mathbf{A}_{i}\nvDash\mathbf{ib}_{m}
, and there are finitely
many elements

a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbf{A}_{i}
such that in the generated subalgebra

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Sg}^{\mathbf{A}_{i}}(\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\})
we also have
\mathbf{A}\nvDash\mathbf{ib}_{m}
,
that is,
\mathbf{A}\notin\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
. The class
\mathsf{Pa}
is locally finite, ensuring
\mathbf{A}

is finite. As
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
is generated by
\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m}
, it follows that

\mathsf{Pa}_{m}=\mathbf{Q}(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m},\mathbf{A})\subseteq\mathsf{K}\,.

As
\mathsf{K}
is a cover,
\mathsf{K}=\mathbf{Q}(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m},\mathbf{A})
. By claim 2 above,

\mathbf{Q}(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m},\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{Q}(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m}\times\mathbf{A})
. But
\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m}\times\mathbf{A}
is finite,
concluding the proof.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.2
.

For a finite poset
X
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\varepsilon(X)\in\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
;

(2)

every
x\in X
satisfies
|M(x)|\leq m
.

Proof .

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
Since
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}=\mathbf{Q}(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})=\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(B_{m}))
, where
B_{m}
is the
poset with one bottom element and
m
maximal elements, Lemma
1.1
yields a
surjective pp-morphism from a finite disjoint union of copies of
B_{m}
onto
X
.
Every point of
B_{m}
has at most
m
maximal elements above it,
so by preservation of maxima the same holds in
X
.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
For each
x\in X
, choose a surjection from the set of
maxima of
B_{m}
onto
M(x)
, and map the bottom of
B_{m}
to
x
.
This gives a pp-morphism
f_{x}:B_{m}\to X
.
Taking the disjoint union of all these maps yields a surjective pp-morphism

\biguplus_{x\in X}B_{m}\twoheadrightarrow X
.
By Lemma
1.1
,
\varepsilon(X)\in\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(B_{m}))=\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.3
.

For a finite poset
X
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(X))
;

(2)

there is a surjective pp-morphism
g:X\twoheadrightarrow\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
.

Proof .

By Lemma
1.1
,
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(X))
if and only if
there is a surjective pp-morphism

f:\biguplus_{i}X\twoheadrightarrow\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})

from finitely many copies of
X
onto
\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
.
From this
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
is immediate. For
(1)\Rightarrow(2)

Write
\bot
for the bottom element of
\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
, and let

x\in\biguplus_{i}X
such that
f(x)=\bot
.
Then
f
restricted to the component to which
x
belongs is already
a surjective pp-morphism
g:X\twoheadrightarrow\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
.

\blacksquare

Definition 2.4
.

Let
M
be a non-empty set and
\mathcal{F}
a possibly empty family of
non-empty subsets of
M
. Let

P(M,\mathcal{F})=\bigg\{M,\{m\}:m\in M\bigg\}\cup\mathcal{F},

ordered by reverse inclusion: for
A,B\in P(M,\mathcal{F})
put

A\leq B
iff
B\subseteq A
.

P(M,\mathcal{F})
thus has the set
M
as a smallest element, singletons

\{m\}
for
m\in M
as maximal elements, and possibly some other
subsets of
M
that are listed in
\mathcal{F}
. We call posets of this form

reduced
posets, and
M
is the base of
P
.
If
\mathcal{F}=\emptyset
, then we might simply write

P(M)
in place of
P(M,\emptyset)
.

\square

If
P=P(M,\mathcal{F})
is reduced, then the maximal elements
of
P
are the elements of the set

M(P)=\big\{\{m\}:m\in M\big\}
. Further, for any
a,b\in P

we have
a=b
iff
M(a)=M(b)
.

Definition 2.5
.

For a finite poset
P
we define the poset
P^{\sharp}
by

P^{\sharp}=\bigg\{M(x):x\in P\bigg\},

ordered by reverse inclusion: for
A,B\in P^{\sharp}
put

A\leq B
iff
B\subseteq A
.

P^{\sharp}
is a reduced poset with base
M(P)
, and we call
P^{\sharp}
the
reduction
of
P
.

\square

Lemma 2.6
.

For a finite poset
P
,

\text{if }\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\subsetneq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P)),\text{ then }\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\subsetneq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P^{\sharp}))\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P)).

In particular, if
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\prec\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))
is a cover,
then

\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))=\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P^{\sharp})).

Proof .

For any
P
the mapping
x\mapsto M(x)
is a
surjective pp-morphism
P\twoheadrightarrow P^{\sharp}
. Surjectivity
is immediate, and order preservation is simply because
x\leq y

implies

M(y)\subseteq M(x)
. The mapping preserves maxima, because maximal elements
of
P^{\sharp}
are singletons
\{m\}
, where
m
is maximal in
P
.
By duality it follows that
\varepsilon(P^{\sharp})\subseteq\varepsilon(P)
and thus

\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P^{\sharp}))\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))
.

As for the converse inclusion: As

\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))
properly contains
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
, we have

\varepsilon(P)\notin\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
.
By Lemma
2.2
there is
x\in P
with
|M(x)|\geq m+1
.
For each
m\in M(x)
,
M(m)=\{m\}
, and so
\{m\}
is
a maximal element in
P^{\sharp}
.
M(x)
is thus an element of
P^{\sharp}

which has at least
m+1
maximal elements above it. By Lemma
2.2

\varepsilon(P^{\sharp})\notin\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
and thus
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\neq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P^{\sharp}))
. By Lemma
2.3
there is a surjective pp-morphism

g:P\twoheadrightarrow\delta(\mathbf{B}_{m})
. This
g

induces a surjective pp-morphism
h:P^{\sharp}\twoheadrightarrow\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})

in a natural way:
h(M(x))=g(x)
. This indeed is a pp-morphism: if
M(x)=M(y)
,
then since
g
is a pp-morphism we have
M(g(x))=g[M(x)]=g[M(y)]=M(g(y))
,
and in
\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
two elements are equal iff they have the same set of
maximal elements.
By Lemma
2.3

again,
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P^{\sharp}))
.

Now, we have
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\subsetneq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P^{\sharp}))\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))
.
If
\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))
is a cover,
\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))=\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P^{\sharp}))
follows.

\blacksquare

To characterize covers it is enough to focus on posets of the form
P^{\sharp}
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
P
be a finite poset and assume
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\subsetneq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))
. Then there is
a finite reduced poset
R(N,\mathcal{F})
such that

\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\subsetneq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(R(N,\mathcal{F})))\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P)),

and
|N|=m+1
. In particular, if
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\prec\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))
is
a cover. Then

\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))=\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(R(N,\mathcal{F}))).

Proof .

By Lemma
2.6
without loss of generality
we can assume that
P=P^{\sharp}
. By definition

P^{\sharp}
is of the form
P^{\sharp}=P(M,\mathcal{F})
.
Thus, we assume that
elements of
P
are certain subsets of
M
, with

M\in P
, and maximal elements are
\{m\}\in P
for
m\in M
.

Pick a non-empty set
N
and suppose that

\pi:M\twoheadrightarrow N
be a surjective function.
Define the poset
P^{\pi}
by

P^{\pi}=\bigg\{\pi[X]:X\in P\bigg\},

ordered by reverse inclusion.
P^{\pi}
is a
reduced poset with base
N
. Define

g:P\to P^{\pi},\qquad g(X)=\pi[X]\,.

Claim 2.8
.

g
is a surjective pp-morphism.

Proof .

Surjectivity is clear. Take
X,Y\in P
. Order preservation
follows from

X\leq Y\ \Leftrightarrow\ Y\subseteq X\ \Leftrightarrow\ \pi[Y]\subseteq\pi[X]\ \Leftrightarrow\ g(X)\leq g(Y).

Let us calculate images of maxima:

\displaystyle M(g(X))

\displaystyle=\big\{\{y\}:y\in\pi[X]\big\}=\big\{\{\pi(x)\}:x\in X\big\},

\displaystyle g[M(X)]

\displaystyle=\big\{g(\{x\}):x\in X\big\}=\big\{\pi[\{x\}]:x\in X\big\}=\big\{\{\pi(x)\}:x\in X\big\}\,.

Therefore
M(g(X))=g[M(X)]
and thus
g
preserves maxima.

\blacksquare

Since
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))
, by
Lemma
2.3

there is a surjective pp-morphism

f:P\twoheadrightarrow\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
.

Claim 2.9
.

Assume that
\pi:M\twoheadrightarrow N
is
such that

\pi(a)=\pi(b)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad f(\{a\})=f(\{b\}).

Then there is a surjective pp-morphism

\bar{f}:P^{\pi}\twoheadrightarrow\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
.

Proof .

For
Z\in P^{\pi}
choose
X\in P
such that

g(X)=Z
, that is,
\pi[X]=Z
. Define

\bar{f}(Z)=\bar{f}(\pi[X])=f(X)\,.

We claim that this is a well-defined mapping
(the value
\bar{f}(Z)
does not depend on which
X

is chosen) and
a surjective pp-morphism. The key observation
is that in
\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
(and generally, in every
reduced poset) the following holds

\displaystyle s<t\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad M(t)\subseteq M(s)\,.

(2.1)

We show first that for
X,Y\in P
we have

\displaystyle\pi[X]\leq\pi[Y]\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad f(X)\leq f(Y)\,.

(2.2)

Suppose
\pi[X]\leq\pi[Y]
, i.e.,
\pi[Y]\subseteq\pi[X]
.
By (
2.1
) for
f(X)\leq f(Y)
it is enough
to show
M(f(Y))\subseteq M(f(X))
. Now,

\displaystyle M(f(X))

\displaystyle=f[M(X)]=\big\{f(\{a\}):a\in X\big\},

\displaystyle M(f(Y))

\displaystyle=f[M(Y)]=\big\{f(\{b\}):b\in Y\big\}.

Take
f(\{b\})\in M(f(Y))
for
b\in Y
. Then

\pi(b)\in\pi[Y]
and so
\pi(b)\in\pi[X]
.
Thus there is
a\in X
such that
\pi(b)=\pi(a)
.
But then
f(\{a\})=f(\{b\})
and so
f(\{b\})\in M(f(X))
.
Consequently, (
2.2
) is established.

Now, using (
2.2
) if
\pi[X]=\pi[Y]
,
then
f(X)=f(Y)
, which ensures
\bar{f}
is well-defined.
That
\bar{f}
is order-preserving is exactly
(
2.2
). It remains to prove that
\bar{f}

preserves maxima.

Take
Y\in P^{\pi}
, and
A\in P
such that
Y=\pi[A]
.
Maximal elements above
\pi[A]
is the set

M(\pi[A])=\big\{\{y\}:y\in\pi[A]\big\}=\big\{\{\pi(a)\}:a\in A\big\}\,.

Observe that
g(\{a\})=\pi[\{a\}]=\{\pi(a)\}
.
Thus

\bar{f}(\{\pi(a)\})=\bar{f}(g(\{a\}))=f(\{a\})\,.

Therefore

\bar{f}[M(\pi[A])]=\big\{f(\{a\}):a\in A\big\}\,.

On the other hand,

M(\bar{f}(\pi[A]))\overset{\text{def. of }\bar{f}}{=}M(f(A))\overset{\text{pp of }f}{=}f[M(A)]=\big\{f(\{a\}):a\in A\big\}\,.

This shows that

\bar{f}:P^{\pi}\twoheadrightarrow\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})

is a pp-morphism. Finally, surjectivity of
\bar{f}

follows from surjectivity of
f
: for any

s\in\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
there is
X\in P
such that

f(X)=s
. But then
\bar{f}(\pi[X])=f(X)=s
.

\blacksquare

Take the surjective pp-morphism

f:P\twoheadrightarrow\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
.
Since
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\subsetneq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))
, we have

\varepsilon(P)\notin\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
, and by Lemma
2.2
there
is
x\in P
with
|M(x)|\geq m+1
. Then the base
M

of
P
must have at least
m+1
elements. Take any set
N

with exactly
m+1
elements, and let

\pi:M\to N
be a surjection such that if
\pi(a)=\pi(b)
,
then
f(\{a\})=f(\{b\})
. Such a
\pi
exists because

|N|>|M(\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m}))|=m
, and
f
maps maximal
elements into maximal elements surjectively.

By Claim
2.8
there
is
g:P\twoheadrightarrow P^{\pi}
,
and by Claim
2.9
there is

\bar{f}:P^{\pi}\twoheadrightarrow\delta(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{m})
.
Then Lemma
1.1
implies

\mathsf{Pa}_{m}\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P^{\pi}))\subseteq\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))\,.

But in
P^{\pi}
there is an element
x
with
|M(x)|=m+1
, so
by Lemma
2.2
,
\varepsilon(P^{\pi})\notin\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
.
If
\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))
is a cover, we must have then

\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))=\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P^{\pi}))
.

Finally, the base set

has exactly

elements,
therefore

is the poset

Corollary 2.10
.

Covers of
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
generated by a finite poset
must be of the form
\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P(M,\mathcal{F})))

for a reduced poset
P(M,\mathcal{F})
, where
|M|=m+1

and
\mathcal{F}
is a family of non-empty subsets of
M
.
(We do not claim here that posets of this form are necessarily
covers).

Proof .

Immediate from Theorem
2.7

\blacksquare

To reiterate: the reduced poset
P(M,\mathcal{F})

has a bottom element
M
which has all
m+1
maximal elements

\{m\}
for
m\in M
above it. And there might be certain
other subsets of
M
inside
P
listed by
\mathcal{F}
. The question
now is this: which other subsets are allowed, that is, what
\mathcal{F}

looks like?

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
m\geq 2
,
|M|=m+1
and
P=P(M,\mathcal{F})
be a reduced poset.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
m\geq 2
,
|M|=m+1
and
P=P(M,\mathcal{F})
be a reduced poset.
Suppose there are distinct
a,b\in M
such that

\mathcal{F}\subsetneq\bigg\{\{a,b\},M-\{a\},M-\{b\}\bigg\}\,.

Then
\mathbf{Q}(\varepsilon(P))
is not a cover of
\mathsf{Pa}_{m}
.

Proof .

Write
p=\{a,b\}
,
q_{1}=M-\{a\}
and
q_{2}=M-\{b\}
.
Take
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\subseteq\{p,q_{1},q_{2}\}

,
P_{\mathcal{G}}=P(M,\mathcal{G})

Claim 2.13
.

There is a surjective pp-morphism from a finite disjoint
union of copies of
P_{\mathcal{F}}
onto
P_{\mathcal{G}}
iff

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
after possibly exchanging
elements of
\{p,q_{1},q_{2}\}
of the same size.

2

2

If

m=2

\{p,q_{1},q_{2}\}
as in this case

|p|=|q_{1}|=|q_{2}|=2

Proof .

Note first that a pp-morphism

is determined
by its action on the maximal elements, that is, on the singletons. If

f(\{x\})=\{\varphi(x)\}
for
x\in M
,
then for every element

,

M(f(X))=f[M(X)]=\bigl\{\{\varphi(x)\}:x\in X\bigr\},

so

is uniquely determined by the set
\varphi[X]
.

(
\Leftarrow
) Assume, after perhaps the exchanges,
that
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
.
We construct a surjective pp-morphism
from finitely many copies of
P_{\mathcal{F}}
onto
P_{\mathcal{G}}
.
First copy: cover the bottom, maxima and all of
{\mathcal{F}}
. Take
one copy of
P_{\mathcal{F}}
and map it identically onto

.
Extra copies: For each

such that the bottom of

.
It is easy to see that this can always be done.
Taking the disjoint unions of the pp-morphisms of the copies gives

\Rightarrow
) Assume there is a surjective pp-morphism

Because

M(M)=M(g(M))=g[M(M)]

g
maps maximal elements onto maximal elements.
Since there are exactly
m+1
