Gingival expression of MMP-1, MMP-9, and HBD-3 in healthy individuals and patients with moderate to severe chronic periodontitis
Authors: Mahya Ramezani, Mahsa Bazargan, Mandana Sattari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriodontitis arises from dysregulated host-microbe interactions, driving progressive tissue degradation. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and antimicrobial peptides like human β-defensin-3 (HBD-3) serve as pivotal biomarkers in periodontal pathogenesis. This study quantified gingival mRNA expression of MMP-1, MMP-9, and HBD-3 across health, gingivitis, and chronic periodontitis to assess their diagnostic and therapeutic relevance. Gingival biopsies were collected from 23 healthy controls (probing depth [PD] < 3 mm), 18 gingivitis patients, and 19 moderate-to-severe chronic periodontitis patients (PD > 5 mm). RNA extraction utilized FavorPrep™ kits, followed by cDNA synthesis and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) with GAPDH normalization. Gene expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method. Statistical analyses included Kruskal-Wallis, Games-Howell post-hoc, and Spearman’s correlation (SPSS v20.0; α = 0.05). Chronic periodontitis exhibited significantly elevated MMP-1 expression (31.00 ± 7.1) versus healthy controls (21.26 ± 4.3; p = 0.001), with overall intergroup differences (p = 0.001). HBD-3 expression demonstrated significant variation across groups (p = 0.045), peaking in periodontitis (38.22 ± 9.6), though pairwise comparisons were non-significant. MMP-9 expression showed no intergroup differences (p = 0.688). Critically, HBD-3 expression correlated positively with clinical attachment loss (CAL) in periodontitis (ρ = 0.497; p = 0.030). MMP-1 overexpression in chronic periodontitis underscores its role as a primary mediator of tissue destruction and potential diagnostic biomarker. The HBD-3/CAL correlation suggests compensatory antimicrobial responses during disease progression. These findings nominate MMP-1 as a promising therapeutic target for mitigating periodontal breakdown. Introduction

Periodontitis, a chronic inflammatory disease initiated by dysbiotic microbial biofilms, results in progressive destruction of tooth-supporting tissues, affecting nearly 50% of adults globally1,2. The 2017 World Workshop classification redefined periodontitis as a single disease entity graded by biological complexity and staged by severity3. This multifactorial disease stems from aberrant host-microbe interactions wherein bacterial pathogens activate immune cascades that drive extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and alveolar bone resorption4. Conventional diagnostics relying on clinical attachment loss (CAL), probing depth (PD), and radiographic analysis fail to predict individual susceptibility or early subclinical progression5,6. Consequently, molecular biomarkers reflecting active host-pathogen dynamics are critical for early diagnosis and targeted therapeutic interventions.

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are zinc-dependent endopeptidases that play pivotal roles in both physiological tissue remodeling and pathological destruction in periodontitis7,8. Among these, MMP-8 is widely recognized as a key neutrophil-derived collagenase in periodontal inflammation9–11. In parallel, MMP-1 (interstitial collagenase) specifically targets type I collagen, the primary structural component of periodontal ligaments, while MMP-9 (gelatinase B) degrades denatured collagens and basement membrane components12. Under healthy conditions, MMP activity is tightly regulated by endogenous tissue inhibitors (TIMPs). However, during chronic inflammation, this regulatory balance is disrupted, leading to excessive connective tissue degradation13. Clinically, elevated levels of MMP-1 and MMP-9 in gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) have been associated with periodontal disease severity, and genetic polymorphisms in MMP genes may further influence individual susceptibility to periodontitis13,14.

Concurrently, antimicrobial peptides constitute essential components of innate immunity. Human β-defensin-3 (HBD-3) exhibits broad-spectrum activity against periodontal pathogens and immunomodulatory functions15. Unlike constitutively expressed defensins, HBD-3 is inducible by inflammatory stimuli and microbial challenge, positioning it as a dynamic responder in periodontal defense16. Paradoxically, studies report both elevated and suppressed HBD-3 expression in periodontitis, reflecting complex host-microbe adaptations and methodological variations17–19.

Despite extensive investigation of individual biomarkers, simultaneous analysis of proteolytic enzymes and antimicrobial effectors in gingival tissues remains underexplored. This study quantifies mRNA expression of MMP-1, MMP-9, and HBD-3 in gingival biopsies from healthy controls, gingivitis patients, and chronic periodontitis patients. We hypothesize that dysregulated expression of these mediators correlates with clinical parameters of disease severity, offering insights into their synergistic roles in periodontal pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

Gingival tissue specimens were obtained from 18 gingivitis patients, 19 individuals with moderate to severe chronic periodontitis (probing depth > 5 mm at sampling sites), and 23 periodontally healthy controls (probing depth < 3 mm without radiographic bone loss). Comprehensive periodontal examinations were performed by a calibrated periodontist at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, with clinical attachment loss (CAL) and probing depth (PD) recorded using a Williams probe. Control tissues were acquired during crown-lengthening procedures, while diseased tissues were collected during periodontal surgery. Exclusion criteria encompassed systemic diseases (e.g., diabetes, immunodeficiencies), tobacco use, orthodontic treatment, pharmacological interventions within three months, and allergic conditions. Ethical approval was secured from the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1398.315) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

RNA extraction and quality assessment

Tissue samples were immediately immersed in RNAlater™ Stabilization Solution (YT9085) and stored at -20 °C. Total RNA was isolated using the FavorPrep™ Blood/Cultured Cell Total RNA Mini Kit (Favorgen, Taiwan) following homogenization with YTzol Pure RNA reagent (YT9064). RNA integrity was verified through 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, while concentration and purity (A260/A280 ratio) were quantified using a NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Samples exhibiting A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 were processed for downstream analysis.

cDNA synthesis and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the TaqMan™ MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (M-MLV system) according to manufacturer specifications. Quantitative real-time PCR amplification was performed in duplicate with RealQ™ Plus Master Mix Green (Ampliqon, A323402) on a LightCycler® 96 System (Roche), using the following cycling protocol: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at gene-specific temperatures (58 °C for MMP-1/HBD-3; 60 °C for MMP-9) for 60 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Quantification and analytical validation

Amplification efficiency (90–110%) was confirmed through standard curves generated from serial 10-fold dilutions of pooled cDNA. Target gene expression (MMP-1, MMP-9, HBD-3) was normalized to the endogenous control GAPDH using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Inter-run calibration controls were implemented to ensure technical reproducibility across all qRT-PCR batches.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using SPSS® v20.0 (IBM Corp.) with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Demographic variables were compared via Chi-square tests (gender distribution) and ANOVA (age). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test confirmed non-normal data distribution. Non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis tests were employed for intergroup gene expression comparisons, supplemented by Games-Howell post-hoc pairwise analyses. Spearman’s rank correlation assessed relationships between gene expression levels and clinical attachment loss (CAL). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

Participant demographics

A total of 60 participants were enrolled, comprising 23 healthy controls, 18 gingivitis patients, and 19 patients with moderate to severe chronic periodontitis. The cohort consisted of 39 females (65%) and 21 males (35%), with no significant differences in gender distribution across groups (χ² = 0.284, p = 0.868). The mean age was 37.70 ± 8.2 years in healthy controls, 43.67 ± 9.1 years in gingivitis patients, and 37.95 ± 7.5 years in periodontitis patients. Despite the higher mean age in the gingivitis group, no significant intergroup age differences were observed (F = 2.981, p = 0.058). Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 2.

Differential gene expression analysis

Significant differences in MMP-1 expression were detected across groups (Kruskal-Wallis χ² = 14.043, p = 0.001), with highest levels in moderate to severe chronic periodontitis (31.00 ± 7.1) compared to healthy controls (21.26 ± 4.3). HBD-3 expression also varied significantly (χ² = 6.221, p = 0.045), demonstrating a progressive increase from health (24.52 ± 6.4) to periodontitis (38.22 ± 9.6). In contrast, MMP-9 expression showed no intergroup differences (χ² = 0.749, p = 0.688). Discussion

The present study aimed to quantify mRNA levels of MMP-1, MMP-9 and hBD-3 in gingival tissue samples from periodontally healthy controls, patients with gingivitis, and patients with moderate-to-severe chronic periodontitis. We hypothesized that dysregulated expression of these markers would correlate with clinical severity. Our analysis revealed that MMP-1 transcript levels were significantly elevated in chronic periodontitis compared to healthy tissue, whereas MMP-9 mRNA did not differ significantly among the groups. Human β-defensin-3 expression showed overall group differences with highest mean levels in periodontitis, but pairwise comparisons were not significant. Notably, hBD-3 expression correlated positively with clinical attachment loss in the periodontitis group, suggesting an association with disease severity. These findings provide new insights into host mediator profiles in periodontal pathology.

The prominent upregulation of MMP-1 mRNA in periodontitis aligns with its established role as a primary collagenase in periodontal breakdown8,20. MMP-1 (collagenase-1) is known to cleave interstitial collagens and facilitate connective tissue degradation during inflammation. In agreement with our results, Aiba et al. demonstrated significantly higher MMP-1 mRNA in inflamed gingival biopsies than in healthy gingiva20. Similarly, Kubota et al. (2008) reported that periodontitis-affected gingival tissues exhibit significantly elevated MMP-1 transcripts versus controls21. These concordant findings underscore that increased MMP-1 gene expression is a hallmark of active periodontal lesions. Moreover, a recent systematic review notes that increased MMP-1 levels in tissues and crevicular fluid are consistently observed in periodontitis22.

In contrast, MMP-9 (gelatinase B) did not differ significantly between health, gingivitis and periodontitis in our study. This finding concurs with several prior reports indicating modest or non-significant changes in gingival MMP-9 expression with disease. Kubota et al. similarly found that although MMP-9 mRNA tended to be higher in diseased gingiva, the increase was not statistically significant21. More recently, Razavi et al. (2023) reported a trend toward higher MMP-9 in periodontitis tissues but no significant difference from controls23. The lack of a robust MMP-9 transcriptional signal may reflect the biology of this enzyme: much of the active MMP-9 in periodontal lesions is derived from neutrophils and may be regulated post-transcriptionally. Indeed, Radzki et al. noted that gingivitis and aggressive periodontitis often show abundant MMP-9 protein even when transcript levels are unchanged, likely due to release of pre-formed MMP-9 from leukocyte granules8. Thus, the stable mRNA levels we observed do not exclude increased MMP-9 activity in disease, but suggest that changes in MMP-9 are less driven by new gene expression and more by enzyme activation.

The pattern of hBD-3 expression in our study was more complex. Although the highest mean hBD-3 mRNA was seen in periodontitis samples, the difference did not reach significance in pairwise comparisons. This ambiguous result reflects the varied findings in the literature on hBD-3 regulation. Human β-defensin-3 is constitutively expressed in gingival epithelia and is known to be inducible by inflammatory stimuli24. It is a potent antimicrobial peptide with broad-spectrum activity against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. One interpretation of our trend toward higher hBD-3 in disease is that the inflamed gingiva ramps up defensin production in response to increased microbial load. Indeed, hBD-3 peptide is commonly detected in healthy gingival epithelium, as Lu et al. observed hBD-3 in 88% of biopsies, confined to epithelial layers24. In pathological conditions, altered expression may occur. Notably, Bissell et al. found that healthy gingival tissues had significantly higher HBD-3 mRNA than periodontitis sites, suggesting that hBD-3 is depleted or degraded in chronic lesions25. Similarly, Brancatisano et al. reported that hBD-3 peptide levels in gingival crevicular fluid were drastically reduced in periodontitis patients, inversely correlating with disease severity26. These results contrast with our observation of higher hBD-3 in deeper lesions; it may be that local factors in diseased pockets, such as cytokine milieu or cellular infiltration, influence defensin production. Interestingly, Liu et al. showed that gingival keratinocytes from periodontitis patients have elevated basal hBD-3 mRNA, indicating a heightened ‘primed’ state, even though overall tissue expression was lower in disease27. In our data, the positive correlation between hBD-3 mRNA and attachment loss suggests that pockets with more severe destruction express more hBD-3, possibly as a compensatory host response.

The present study has several limitations that merit consideration. First, its cross-sectional design precludes inferences about causality; longitudinal studies would be needed to determine whether changes in MMP or hBD-3 levels predict disease progression or respond to therapy. Second, mRNA levels were measured in biopsies obtained at a single time point; these may not fully reflect protein abundance or enzymatic activity, especially for MMPs that are secreted as zymogens. Future work should measure protein levels and activity (e.g., via immunoassays) and correlate them with mRNA. Third, the sample size, while adequate to detect differences in MMP-1, may have been underpowered to resolve subtler differences in hBD-3 or MMP-9; studies with larger cohorts could clarify these trends. Additionally, we studied only moderate-to-severe chronic periodontitis; inclusion of mild/moderate cases and aggressive periodontitis in future studies could determine whether marker expression scales with disease grade. Finally, because host mediators can be influenced by factors such as age, smoking, and systemic health, stratification or multivariate analysis in larger populations would be important to generalize these findings. Despite these limitations, our data point to clear patterns deserving further investigation.

We acknowledge that MMP-8, a key neutrophil-derived collagenase, was not assessed in the present study. MMP-8 is widely recognized as a major biomarker of periodontitis activity and progression, with elevated levels in gingival crevicular fluid and tissues correlating strongly with disease severity9–11. Indeed, MMP-8 has been proposed as a diagnostic and prognostic marker for periodontitis, with active forms reflecting ongoing tissue destruction28–30. Our focus on MMP-1, MMP-9, and HBD-3 was driven by interest in stromal and epithelial contributions to tissue remodeling and host defense. However, we recognize that integrating MMP-8 analysis in future studies would provide a more comprehensive view of the proteolytic networks in periodontal disease. The present findings should therefore be interpreted as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, MMP-8-based investigations.

Conclusion

This study provides a detailed analysis of gingival mRNA expression for MMP-1, MMP-9 and hBD-3 across periodontal health and disease. We demonstrate that chronic periodontitis is characterized by a significant upregulation of MMP-1, consistent with its role in collagen degradation, whereas MMP-9 showed no significant change. Human β-defensin-3 exhibited a variable pattern, with its expression correlating with attachment loss, underscoring a complex defensive response in advanced disease.
