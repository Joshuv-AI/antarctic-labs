A concept analysis of artificial intelligence anxiety among nurses based on Walker and Avant’s method
Authors: Xin Luo, Chunxiu Zhang, Jiajia Xia, Fangmin Li, Qi Ao, Peili Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe growing integration of artificial intelligence in nursing has been accompanied by increased anxiety among nurses, even as they aim to enhance the quality and efficiency of care. Artificial intelligence anxiety (AI anxiety) constitutes another significant factor influencing the decline in nursing work quality and the deterioration of nurses’ physical and mental health. However, AI anxiety among nurses is still not clearly conceptualized and has received limited empirical examination.ObjectiveThis study clarifies the concept of AI anxiety among nurses, offering a comprehensive understanding for nursing managers and researchers to support relevant measurements and interventions.Data sourcesThis study retrieved studies from inception to July 2026 across PubMed, CINAHL (via EBSCO), ELSEVIER ScienceDirect, ProQuest, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), China Wanfang Database, and China VIP Database. Relevant references were tracked. This systematic database search aimed to comprehensively collect various research and academic literature to provide evidence and support for conceptual analysis. A total of 31 articles were included in the review.MethodsThis study employed Walker and Avant’s concept analysis method.ResultsA total of 31 papers were included in the study. The four primary characteristics of nurse AI anxiety are: Technical concerns, patient safety concerns, perceived ethical burden, and sense of occupational devaluation. The antecedents are categorized into individual, environmental and related to artificial intelligence. The consequences of AI anxiety are distinguished into individual-level and hospital-level impacts.ConclusionThis study provides a comprehensive understanding of the concept of AI anxiety among nurses by outlining its antecedents, attributes, and consequences. The conceptualization of AI anxiety will facilitate future research aimed at establishing effective prevention strategies.

Introduction

Technological advancements and innovations have become deeply embedded across all sectors of society. Among these, artificial intelligence (AI) serves as a pivotal driver of technological transformation, playing an especially prominent role [1]. Healthcare, which is fundamental to people’s well-being, has unavoidably been influenced by AI. Its effects extend across diagnosis, treatment, and management, and it may drive substantial changes in patient care domains such as nursing [2, 3]. As both potential users of AI applications and professional caregivers, nurses occupy a crucial position to shape and guide the transformation of AI within the healthcare sector [4]. Recently, technologies such as smart monitoring devices [5, 6], predictive and early-warning models [7], artificial intelligence algorithms [8], and automated nursing documentation systems [9, 10] have been progressively incorporated into frontline clinical practice to mitigate nurses’ workload. However, overdependence on artificial intelligence compromises nurses’ professional judgement and may endanger patient safety [11]. Therefore, it is essential to examine nurses’ emotional reactions to the implementation of artificial intelligence.

The use of artificial intelligence in healthcare brings clear advantages. However, it can also create a psychological hurdle known as AI anxiety. Recent studies show that many nurses experience this anxiety, mainly due to concerns about job security, ethical challenges, patient safety, and their own ability to use AI tools [12–15]. Qualitative studies highlight that while nurses value AI for its potential to enhance efficiency and reduce workloads, they frequently experience uncertainty in its practical use [16–18]. Quantitative data indicate that anxiety levels differ across departments, including paediatrics, surgery, oncology, and intensive care [13, 19–21]. High AI anxiety correlates with reduced readiness for adoption, limited innovation, and lower professional confidence, ultimately influencing workflows and patient outcomes [22–24]. Additionally, AI anxiety can adversely affect nurses’ mental health, causing stress and doubts about their professional competence [13, 20, 25].

Anxiety is a common negative psychological state. It affects the work efficiency of nurses, as well as their physical and mental health [26]. Different types of anxiety stem from specific causes and have distinct effects. For example, fertility anxiety arises from the internal conflict between an individual’s desire to have children and their objective reproductive capacity, leading to intense emotional distress [27]. Anxiety about aging arises from concerns and worries about growing older, reflecting one’s fear of anticipated threats and losses that may be experienced during the aging process [28]. Death anxiety stems from contemplating death or end-of-life issues, causing individuals to become trapped in a state of negative emotions such as unease, worry, or fear [29, 30]. AI anxiety specifically refers to the worries and fears employees feel about the changes and issues brought about by artificial intelligence technology in their personal lives and workplaces [31]. However, unlike other types of anxiety, AI anxiety primarily stems from concerns about the potential impact of artificial intelligence in areas such as employment, security, privacy, and interpersonal interactions [32].

Artificial intelligence anxiety has become an increasingly recognized concern among nurses. Although previous research has explored negative emotional responses to technology through concepts such as computer anxiety, digital stress, occupational anxiety, and resistance to innovation, these constructs do not adequately reflect the unique characteristics of AI-related anxiety in nursing practice. Computer anxiety primarily refers to apprehension associated with operating computer systems, whereas digital stress emphasizes the cognitive burden arising from extensive digital information processing. Occupational anxiety encompasses a broad range of work-related concerns but lacks specificity regarding AI-driven clinical environments. As frontline healthcare professionals, nurses encounter AI within a distinctive clinical and professional context. They are required to integrate AI-assisted decision support with independent clinical judgment while simultaneously addressing uncertainties surrounding AI use alongside responsibilities for patient safety, ethical decision-making, and the delivery of compassionate, person-centred care. Additionally, exploring the concept of AI anxiety is not only crucial for its theoretical development but also helps guide the optimization of nursing practice, aiming to reduce the workload of nurses and enhance the quality of care. Consequently, examining this concept is essential for improving researchers’ understanding of nurses’ AI anxiety and addressing the ongoing challenges posed by artificial intelligence.

Methods

Concept analysis method

Walker and Avant’s concept analysis method is widely used in research to address concepts that suffer from ambiguity and lack clear definitions [33]. The specific steps include: (1) selecting a concept; (2) determining the purpose of the analysis; (3) identifying all uses of the concept; (4) determining the defining attributes; (5) constructing a model case; (6) constructing other cases; (7) identifying antecedents and consequences; and (8) identifying empirical references [33].

Data source

Alongside dictionary resources for common definitions, we also searched empirical literature to further understand the concept of AI anxiety among nurses. The literature review identified “AI anxiety” OR “artificial intelligence anxiety” OR “fear of artificial intelligence” OR “algorithm anxiety” OR “technology anxiety” OR “digital stress” OR “automation anxiety” OR “artificial intelligence readiness” OR “artificial intelligence acceptance” OR “professional autonomy” OR “algorithmic accountability” OR “nursing informatics anxiety” AND “nurs*” among other keywords. A Boolean search strategy combining “and” and “or” was employed to search PubMed, CINAHL (via EBSCO), ELSEVIER ScienceDirect, ProQuest, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), China Wanfang Database, and China VIP Database. Studies from inception to July 2026 were retrieved, and relevant references were tracked (Table 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The primary aim of this study is to examine the literature on AI anxiety in nursing and provide a comprehensive analysis of its numerous dimensions. The studies included must meet the following criteria: (1) The research context of the included studies must be explicitly related to AI anxiety among nurses; (2) Studies that explicitly used and defined the precise term “AI anxiety”; and (3) Studies investigating closely related alternative terms or parallel constructs (e.g., fear of artificial intelligence, algorithm anxiety, technology anxiety, digital stress, automation anxiety, and artificial intelligence readiness), provided that they contributed empirical or theoretical evidence directly relevant to the definition, defining attributes, antecedents, or consequences of the phenomenon; (4) Be written in Chinese or English. In addition, studies were excluded if they did not primarily focus on the definitions, core features, or nursing-specific contexts of AI anxiety or its predefined surrogate terms. General technophobia, basic computer literacy anxiety, or stress unrelated to advanced AI technologies (such as algorithmic decision-making or automation) was excluded. Furthermore, there are no restrictions on research design; qualitative, quantitative, mixed-methods studies, as well as reviews and commentaries, are permitted.

Quality assessment

Considering that this study is a conceptual analysis rather than a systematic review, no standardized methodological quality score was given to the included literature. However, in order to enhance the transparency and credibility of evidence integration, this study comprehensively considered the source type of the literature and its potential methodological limitations during the analysis process. The included studies exhibited certain heterogeneity in terms of research design, sample size, and measurement tools. In the process of conceptual synthesis, this study mainly extracted the core concept attributes based on the frequency of repetition and cross-study consistency, and prioritized the reference to peer-reviewed empirical research results. For studies with weak methodology or indirect evidence, they were only used to assist in interpretation or supplement the conceptual boundaries, rather than serving as the main basis for defining the core concepts. Thus, to reduce the potential impact of low-quality evidence on the core concepts themselves.

Screening method

Based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, two researchers independently screened the retrieved studies. Duplicate studies, non-English or non-Chinese publications, and irrelevant materials were excluded based on titles or abstracts. All studies contributing to understanding the concept of AI anxiety among nurses were included, regardless of methodological quality. We also included studies examining at least one component of nurses’ AI anxiety: definition, attributes, antecedents, consequences, or empirical evidence.

Data extraction

To address the specific research questions and analytical objectives of this study, we systematically and uniformly extracted information from the included studies. The extracted data included: basic characteristics of each study (title, authors, year of publication, and country or region); study type and design (e.g., qualitative, quantitative, mixed-methods, or review); characteristics of study participants (nurses, nursing students); conceptual definitions of AI-related anxiety in nursing practice, relevant descriptive texts, antecedents, and consequences. Data extraction was performed independently by two researchers using a standardized template, followed by comparison and cross-checking; any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and re-examination of the original texts until consensus was reached. Throughout the process, we consistently emphasized consistency in standards and standardization of documentation to ensure the reliability and consistency of the extracted results.

Data synthesis and analysis

Data analysis employs the inductive thematic synthesis method. Firstly, relevant research results are extracted word-by-word from each included study. Secondly, these data extracts are coded line by line. Thirdly, through continuous cross-study comparisons, similar codes are grouped into preliminary categories. Fourthly, based on conceptual similarity and uniqueness, the categories are merged or split. Finally, higher-level conceptual attributes are generated by refining the stable and repeatedly occurring categories across studies. When there is conceptual overlap between categories, distinctions are made according to the main focus of each construct. When content-similar categories reflect the same underlying phenomenon, they are merged; while dimensions that are conceptually distinct are retained as independent attributes.

Results

This search yielded a total of 7,689 articles and identified 15 relevant references. After removing 3,784 duplicate records, 3,920 records remained. Following preliminary screening of titles and abstracts, 95 articles were retained. Ultimately, 31 studies were included and analyzed (Fig. 1). Among these, 1 study was conducted in USA [34], and 21 in Turkey [13–19, 21, 24, 25, 35–45]. The remaining 9 studies were conducted in Asia and the Middle East, including Korea [46, 47], China [12, 22, 48], Jordan [49] and Palestine [20, 50, 51]. In terms of study design, 26 were quantitative studies [12, 13, 17, 19–25, 34–38, 40, 42–51], 2 was a qualitative study [16, 18], and 3 were mixed-methods studies [14, 15, 41]. Specifically, 20 of the studies focused exclusively on nurses, while 11 involved nursing students. Studies involving nursing students were also included to provide supplementary perspectives on the conceptual development of AI anxiety along the nursing professional continuum. All of the included papers examined anxiety or fears related to AI.

Uses of concept

Artificial intelligence anxiety encompasses the terms “artificial intelligence” and “anxiety”. According to the Cambridge English Dictionary, “artificial intelligence” is defined as “the study of how to produce machines that have some of the qualities that the human mind has, such as the ability to understand language, recognize pictures, solve problems, and learn.” Within the nursing field, a scoping review highlighted that nurses’ references to artificial intelligence encompass documentation, nursing diagnoses, nursing plans, patient monitoring, and nursing predictions [52]. According to the Cambridge English Dictionary and Merriam Webster’s English Dictionary, “anxiety” is defined as “an uncomfortable feeling of nervousness or worry about something that is happening or might happen in the future.” It often marked by physical signs (such as tension, sweating, and increased pulse rate), by doubt concerning the reality and nature of the threat, and by self-doubt about one’s capacity to cope with it. Studies have shown that AI anxiety is recognized as a potential influencing factor that can affect the safety and practice of care [53].

Early research on artificial intelligence anxiety originated from the first generation of computers, termed computer anxiety [54]. This anxiety primarily stemmed from feelings of loss of control, fear of negative evaluation, and unfamiliarity with computer usage [55]. Subsequently, with the emergence of artificial intelligence technologies and products, the concept of computer anxiety became obsolete, lagging far behind the anxieties generated by current technological advancements. Consequently, the concept of artificial intelligence anxiety was introduced. As an interdisciplinary concept, AI anxiety originated in the fields of psychology and management, and its core focus revolves around employees’ emotional reactions to the uncertainty and career risks posed by artificial intelligence.

Compared to other professions, nurses face a series of profession-specific challenges in the context of AI applications. For example, nursing relies heavily on clinical judgment and observational skills, making it a highly specialized field. The widespread adoption of AI technologies, particularly their deep integration into clinical decision-making, may alter nurses’ decision-making processes and, to some extent, influence their patient care practices, thereby raising concerns among nurses about the erosion of their professional autonomy [32, 56]. Furthermore, as key users of AI, nurses must clinically interpret system-generated alerts and risk predictions and adjust their clinical judgments and procedures accordingly. When AI outputs conflict with nurses’ clinical experience-based judgments, this can blur lines of responsibility, leading to ethical pressures and professional uncertainty [56]. It is worth noting that emotional communication and relationship-building are integral components of nursing practice. As the clinical application of AI tools increases, opportunities for direct interaction between nurses and patients may decrease, thereby affecting the humanistic nature of nursing and patients’ subjective perceptions of care quality to some extent [48, 57]. Furthermore, Fan and colleagues point out that AI anxiety is accompanied by a decline in an individual’s sense of value within healthcare organizations and a loss of self-esteem [22].

Analysis of related concepts

Occupational anxiety: Occupational anxiety refers to a form of anxiety tied to the job and specifically elicited by work-related stimuli. Such stimuli are highly diverse and can range from an overflowing inbox or upcoming performance evaluations to potentially more intense triggers, such as workplace bullying, public humiliation during an executive presentation, or, in high-risk occupations, ongoing concern about physical harm or being fatally injured while on duty [58]. Occupational anxiety refers to negative emotional reactions triggered by work-related factors, and its scope extends beyond AI-related issues.

Digital stress: digital stress, defined as a stress response associated with perceptions, emotions, and thoughts surrounding the implementation and use of information communication technologies [59, 60]. It is essentially an adjustment disorder characterized by technical demands that exceed an individual’s capacity to cope, and it forms the basis of AI anxiety.

Innovation resistance: Innovation resistance stems from the theory of innovation resistance, which was first proposed by Ram (1987) and later further developed by Ram and Sheth (1989) [61]. At its core, this theory seeks to explain why individuals exhibit hesitation or resistance to adopting innovations even when those innovations offer potential benefits.

Defining attributes

The core of concept analysis lies in identifying the defining attributes of a concept, which distinguish the target concept from similar or related constructs. Our analysis identified four defining characteristics of AI anxiety: Technical concerns, patient safety concerns, perceived ethical burden, and sense of occupational devaluation.

Technology-related concerns are a key characteristic of nurses’ anxiety regarding artificial intelligence and represent their most immediate experience when encountering new technologies. In an environment where AI technology evolves rapidly, nurses generally face pressure stemming from technical operations due to their lack of understanding of complex algorithms [17, 19]. Multiple studies support this view, reporting that nurses experience a strong sense of uncertainty and reluctance when faced with intelligent devices [14, 15]. Furthermore, this concern is viewed as a form of anxiety that extends beyond the boundaries of individual cognition, creating a psychologically overwhelming burden for nurses when processing AI system outputs [40, 48]. A lack of digital leadership or insufficient technical training leaves nurses feeling overwhelmed, necessitating targeted educational support to alleviate this technological stress [34, 35].

Patient safety concerns are a central feature of nurses’ anxiety regarding artificial intelligence. The core of the nursing profession is ensuring patient safety, but as AI becomes involved in clinical decision-making, the medical risks posed by algorithmic bias or system failures have become a major source of anxiety. Studies have found that in high-risk clinical settings such as operating rooms and intensive care units (ICUs), nurses maintain a cautious or even skeptical attitude toward the reliability of AI systems [13, 50]. Frequent technological updates have a comprehensive impact on nurses’ monitoring responsibilities and risk prevention, forcing them to constantly balance reliance on technology with maintaining vigilance, thereby increasing occupational anxiety [20, 36]. These concerns mainly involve two aspects: the first is the fear of clinical risks caused by AI misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis; Second, concerns about the inability of technology to respond quickly in emergency situations may affect the quality and efficiency of nursing care.

The perception of ethical responsibility is another key characteristic of nurses’ anxiety regarding AI. Unlike operational difficulties, this aspect tends to focus more on concerns about potential ethical conflicts. Nurses experiencing AI anxiety are often unsettled by the question of accountability behind algorithmic decisions, questioning whether they would still bear moral and legal responsibility if the AI makes a mistake [47, 49]. They may question the legitimacy of AI applications regarding data privacy protection and informed consent, demonstrating sensitivity and anxiety regarding the ethical boundaries of technology [16, 45]. Due to a lack of voice in the development and ethical review of AI systems, nurses often feel they have passively accepted a heavy moral burden [44, 51]. This perception of ethical responsibility not only affects nurses’ trust in AI but also serves as an invisible barrier hindering their continued adoption of intelligent technologies.

Sense of occupational devaluation is a key characteristic of nurses’ anxiety regarding artificial intelligence. Studies have found that some nurses believe the introduction of AI will mechanize nursing work, thereby undermining the core values of “humanistic care” and “emotional labor” in nursing [16, 18]. In addition to a decline in intrinsic sense of value, there is a heightened perception of external job displacement. Research indicates that nurses worry AI technology will replace their clinical judgment functions, leading them to feel that their decision-making autonomy is compromised in the workplace [22, 37]. Furthermore, nurses often feel powerless when collaborating with AI, perceiving their professional expertise as overshadowed by algorithmic logic [23]. When nurses perceive their role in the clinical process as marginalized, their sense of identification with and willingness to engage with new technologies decrease significantly [24, 46]. Prolonged exposure to such pressures may lead nurses to adopt resistance or avoidance strategies when confronting innovation initiatives [22, 62].

A model case

Model cases embody the concept by encompassing all defining attributes of that concept, thereby clarifying its meaning [33].

Ms. A is an experienced registered nurse using a newly implemented AI-powered wound assessment system. Shortly after its introduction, she notices discrepancies between the algorithm’s output and her own clinical judgment. She begins to worry persistently about the algorithm’s accuracy and its vulnerability to environmental factors like lighting [Technical concerns]. Fearing that the system might misclassify severe wounds and delay critical treatments, she becomes highly stressed and anxiously double-checks every AI-generated result [Patient safety concerns]. Although the AI serves as an aid, Ms. A is required to sign off on the assessments. She constantly worries about unclear liability in the event of an AI error, feeling the heavy, distressing responsibility of “being the fallback for the machine” [Perceived ethical burden]. Furthermore, she feels that the technology interrupts her physical examinations and face-to-face patient interactions, causing her to feel that her traditional humanistic nursing skills are being marginalized [Sense of occupational devaluation].

Analysis: This model case clearly demonstrates all four defining attributes of nurses’ AI anxiety. The presence of these specific cognitive and emotional distresses confirms the occurrence of the concept.

A contrary case

A Contrary Case does not contain any conceptual attributes [33].

Ms. C, 32, is a registered nurse with seven years of hospital experience. Recently, her department introduced an AI-based IV infusion monitoring system. She has recently noticed that the smart infusion pump occasionally triggers false alarms, and sometimes the system’s calculated completion time does not match the estimated time. Although she is aware of these technical limitations, she does not experience psychological stress such as worry or anxiety. Instead, she proactively addresses these alerts. Nurse C follows the established procedures to conduct the necessary verifications; for her, this process is simply part of her standard workflow. It does not cause confusion regarding accountability nor does it create an ethical burden. She neither relies heavily on the system nor resists it; she views it with a neutral attitude as an auxiliary tool.

Analysis: Although Nurse C was aware of the limitations of AI (in terms of cognitive awareness), she did not experience the “AI anxiety” characterized by anxiety, worry, burden, or threat to her professional identity. The only technical awareness, if not accompanied by emotional and psychological distress, does not constitute AI anxiety.

Antecedents

Antecedents refer to an event or circumstance that must have existed prior to the occurrence of a certain concept [33]. We reviewed the literature and classified the antecedent factors into individual, environmental and related to artificial intelligence.

Individual factors include gender, age and education status. A study by Ahmad et al. indicates that female gender is a predictor of AI anxiety [51]. Additionally, Subasi et al. found that age shows a significant positive correlation with AI anxiety [19]. Educational attainment also influences AI anxiety: higher levels of education are associated with lower anxiety [36].

Nurses’ exposure to AI environments is a trigger for AI-related anxiety. In clinical settings, observable AI implementations that threaten role displacement or career advancement often signify challenges to professional competence and clinical authority [63]. Such challenge triggers a crisis in nurses’ perception of their professional value and career prospects, thereby becoming a significant catalyst for AI anxiety. In healthcare settings with limited resources, critical priorities such as inadequate equipment configuration, nursing staff shortages, and fundamental patient safety safeguards often dominate clinical decision-making and resource allocation. In contrast, issues related to the adaptability of emerging technologies like artificial intelligence as clinical application standards and nurse competency development are often viewed as low priority by healthcare institutions and clinical nursing managers because they do not directly relate to immediate patient safety incidents [20]. This cognitive bias not only undermines healthcare institutions’ systematic training and clinical support for nurses’ AI application capabilities, but also further amplifies nurses’ professional competence anxieties and career development concerns when implementing AI.

Factors related to artificial intelligence include negative pre-existing attitude toward AI, perceived substitution of AI technology and inadequate AI preparedness.

Negative pre-existing attitude toward AI is a significant precursor to AI anxiety among nursing staff. Negative attitudes toward AI show a significant positive correlation with anxiety levels [19]. Negative attitudes toward AI technology, such as resistance and skepticism, cause nurses to focus on potential risks like job displacement and diminished professional value, manifesting as AI anxiety during work [53]. Conversely, nurses with positive attitudes toward AI proactively learn AI-related knowledge during their free time, enhancing their AI literacy and thereby reducing anxiety [64]. Furthermore, digital leadership serves as a key strategy for integrating artificial intelligence into the nursing profession. Research indicates that digital leadership can positively influence AI anxiety and attitudes, positioning it as a practical strategy for AI implementation [35].

Artificial intelligence technology is perceived as a threat to nurses, heightening anxiety surrounding AI [22]. On the one hand, the perception that artificial intelligence technology is replacing human nurses leads to uncertainty about future work conditions and career prospects, exposing them to the risk of layoffs and fueling concerns about professional advancement [65, 66]. When career needs remain unmet, this often triggers AI anxiety. On the other hand, the perception of AI substitution also implies that AI technology can operate independently and learn autonomously, causing nurses to feel undervalued and overlooked by their organizations [66]. This diminishes their professional self-esteem and exacerbates their lack of job security [67].

Inadequate AI preparedness can also lead to anxiety among nurses [48]. Nurses well-prepared for AI possess more extensive AI knowledge reserves and are more likely to accept AI [36]. Conversely, nurses with insufficient AI preparedness develop cognitive biases toward AI, causing them to feel a sense of loss of control during AI technology applications in nursing scenarios, which in turn triggers anxiety about AI [14].

Consequences

The consequences of a concept arise from its existence [33]. The consequences of nurse AI anxiety encompass both individual dimension consequences and hospital organizational dimension consequences.

Individual dimension consequences

These consequences include nurses’ psychological adjustment and work status. When adapting to the changes in work patterns brought about by AI technology, nurses may experience a certain degree of psychological stress and physical and mental fatigue if they lack effective organizational support [68]. At the same time, when AI systems fail to integrate well with existing workflows or generate false-positive alerts, this not only leads to alert fatigue but also forces nurses to perform frequent manual verifications and double-checks, which undoubtedly adds to their workload [69]. Furthermore, as AI is increasingly applied in clinical decision-making, nurses may face the risk of reduced autonomy due to overreliance on algorithmic recommendations or being constrained by system directives. This sense of algorithmic control may influence nurses’ professional judgment and, to some extent, undermine their professional value [70].

Another consequence involves changes in individual behavioral intentions. Negative expectations that “AI may replace the nursing profession” may affect nurses’ acceptance of new technologies. To some extent, this weakens nurses’ motivation to proactively learn new skills, negatively impacting their innovative capabilities [22]. Over time, this may have adverse effects on their career development and the enhancement of their professional competence.

Hospital organizational dimension consequences

Failure to adequately address healthcare workers’ anxiety regarding artificial intelligence may, to some extent, undermine the collaborative atmosphere within the team and increase the potential risk of staff turnover [71]. At the same time, negative behaviors stemming from this anxiety may slow the adoption of intelligent nursing technologies in clinical settings [72], thereby affecting the overall efficiency and expected outcomes of hospitals’ digital transformation [73].

Empirical referents

The final step in conceptual analysis is to identify empirical references that support the conceptual interpretation. The General AI Anxiety Scale developed by Wang et al. was selected as the primary valid instrument for studying AI anxiety [54]. It encompasses four dimensions: (1) learning (referring to the fear of learning AI technologies), (2) job replacement (referring to the fear of negative consequences of AI on working life), (3) sociotechnical blindness (referring to the fear resulting from a limited understanding of how AI depends on humans; expressing fear that humans will be sidelined by the introduction of AI), and (4) AI configuration (referring to the fear of humanoid AI). This scale is a 7-point Likert type. A minimum score of 21 and a maximum of 147 points can be obtained from the scale. The higher the score, the greater the level of anxiety. This scale demonstrated excellent reliability and internal consistency. Through an online survey conducted in Taiwan, 301 valid questionnaires were collected, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.964. It has since been progressively applied to nursing professionals and nursing students.

Definition of AI anxiety among nurses

Nurses’ AI-related anxiety is defined as a range of negative emotional reactions-such as anxiety and worry-triggered by the increasing integration of artificial intelligence into clinical practice. At its core, this anxiety encompasses concerns about AI technology, worries about potential risks to patient safety, a perceived burden of ethical responsibility, and fears that the value of the nursing profession may be diminished.

Discussion

Increasing complexity in healthcare has spurred the exploration of emerging technologies to enhance care management capabilities. AI demonstrates significant potential to optimize decision-making, improve workflow efficiency, and strengthen resource allocation by nursing leadership. However, its implementation presents both opportunities and challenges [73]. This concept analysis aimed to systematically delineate AI anxiety among nurses, benefiting both research and practice while drawing attention to its potential implications. This study can provide a reference for the follow-up research on AI anxiety among nurses. According to Walker and Avant’s approach [33], comprehending nurse AI anxiety requires identifying its attributes, antecedents, and consequences. This concept encompasses four defining characteristics: Technical concerns, patient safety concerns, perceived ethical burden, and sense of occupational devaluation (Fig. 2).

The antecedent analysis of this study reveals that AI anxiety among nurses is influenced by individual, environmental, and AI-specific factors. This study reveals that gender, age, and educational attainment are core individual factors influencing nurses’ AI anxiety. Identifying these antecedents may contribute to enriching the theoretical understanding of the sources of nurses’ AI-related anxiety in the academic community, and may provide a preliminary conceptual reference for future empirical research to further explore potential causal mechanisms. Furthermore, the systematic analysis of AI-related influencing factors may offer a conceptual-level reference for hospitals in developing management strategies when introducing AI technology, thereby providing direction for future empirical research and practical exploration. This study also identified environmental antecedents, such as nurses’ perceived levels of leadership, suggesting a potential association between organizational support systems and the development of AI-related anxiety among nurses. This finding aligns with previous research on nursing students’ intentions to use AI-based medical technologies [74]. In summary, this study systematically identified the antecedents of nurses’ AI anxiety, which may provide a conceptual framework for the future development of targeted AI anxiety assessment tools, the design of educational intervention programs, and the exploration of management strategies. The practical applications of these findings await further support from future empirical research.

This study analyzed the consequences of AI anxiety among nurses and found that implementing scientific interventions and management offers significant practical value. As a core component of healthcare teams, nurses directly undertake critical tasks such as clinical patient care and clinician-patient communication. Their psychological state and professional behavior impact healthcare service quality and team collaboration efficiency [75]. Research has found that AI-related anxiety not only has a negative impact on nurses’ physical and mental health but may also pose multidimensional ethical risks [11, 56]. At the level of data and algorithms, the “black box” nature (opacity) of AI systems not only undermines clinical trust but also places nurses in an ethically passive position when obtaining informed consent from patients, thereby triggering profound anxiety over the erosion of their professional autonomy [56]. Furthermore, conceptual analysis reveals that AI intervention profoundly impacts the boundaries of professional autonomy in nursing. Although AI can alleviate nurses’ cognitive load to some extent, its “black box” nature may lead to a lack of understanding of clinical prediction mechanisms, thereby negatively impacting the autonomy of the nursing profession [76]. Furthermore, overreliance on AI systems may, on the one hand, weaken nurses’ critical thinking and independent judgment, leading to the erosion of core nursing skills. On the other hand, it may hinder their professional innovation, thereby affecting the innovative development of nursing teams [22]. The findings reveal that when predictive outcomes are inaccurate, the attribution of “accountability” among nurses, healthcare institutions, and technology providers becomes ambiguous, compelling nurses to serve as the “liability absorbers” for technological deficiencies. This heavy ethical burden represents a core driving force underlying nurses’ AI anxiety [56]. Finally, research has found that if nurses focus excessively on algorithm-driven data and alerts, they may neglect patients’ individualized emotional needs [14]. At the same time, continuous real-time data collection makes informed consent difficult to obtain; patients’ understanding is limited when faced with complex algorithms, leading to an implicit erosion of their autonomy in clinical decision-making [14].

Limitations

Although this study provides valuable insights into clarifying the concept of AI anxiety among nurses, several limitations must be considered. First, the concept development itself is constrained by the current lack of empirical evidence in the nursing field, and the literature review was limited to Chinese and English publications, which may have resulted in the omission of other perspectives. Second, since conceptual analysis inherently relies on researchers’ qualitative interpretation of textual data, it is difficult to completely avoid a certain degree of subjectivity. This potential bias may explain why definitions of AI anxiety vary across different studies, which could hinder the consistent application of this concept in clinical practice. Furthermore, the existing literature primarily relies on cross-sectional study designs and quantitative indicators, lacking the necessary longitudinal tracking to capture the dynamic evolution of nurses’ AI anxiety over time. To address these shortcomings, future research should conduct large-scale, long-term longitudinal studies to strengthen the empirical foundation of this concept.

Implications for nursing management

Through the concept analysis of nurses’ anxiety about artificial intelligence, a preliminary framework is proposed that can guide future empirical research and theoretical development in the field of nursing artificial intelligence. Firstly, clarifying conceptual attributes may inform future measurement development tailored to the nursing field. Second, through the systematic identification of antecedents and consequences, this study may provide a conceptual foundation for intervention design. Finally, a deeper understanding of this concept may help expand our understanding of the psychological state of nurses when using AI, potentially fostering the development of rational and prudent attitudes of nurses towards AI technology, and providing conceptual guidance for the integration of AI and clinical nursing.
