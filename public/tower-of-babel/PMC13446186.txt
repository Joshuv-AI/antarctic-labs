Epigenetic memory of inflammation as a driver of cancer risk: insights from Colitis
Authors: Alok K. Mishra, Umar Khalid Khan, Bushra Ateeq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic inflammation increases cancer risk, yet the mechanisms linking transient injury to long-term susceptibility remain elusive. Nagaraja et al [1] demonstrate that intestinal stem cells retain durable epigenetic memory of colitis, thereby priming tumorigenesis. This discovery reframes cancer initiation as a consequence of heritable chromatin states, introducing an additional regulatory dimension that operates alongside, and beyond, genetic aberrations. By positioning inflammation induced epigenetic memory as a novel axis for cancer predisposition, this study open avenues for biomarker development and preventive therapeutic strategies.

Chronic inflammation has long been recognized as a major risk factor for cancer pathogenesis, but the mechanistic bridge connecting transient inflammatory insults to long-term tumor susceptibility has remained poorly defined [1]. In this context, the recent study by Nagaraja et al [2]. represents a conceptual advance by demonstrating that inflammation can leave a persistent epigenetic mark(s) embedded in tissue-resident stem cells that actively shapes future tumorigenic potential.

The prevailing paradigm in inflammation-associated cancer has emphasized DNA damage, increased mutational burden, and selective expansion of pre-malignant clones [3]. While these processes undoubtedly contribute, they fail to fully explain why tissues that appear histologically normal after recovery from inflammation remain predisposed to tumorigenesis. The present study addresses this longstanding gap by showing that colonic stem cells retain long-lived changes in chromatin accessibility following colitis, persisting for >100 days, well beyond the normal turnover time of the intestinal epithelium. This finding is striking because it establishes that inflammatory exposure can be durably encoded at the epigenomic level, even after signs of inflammation have resolved.

A particularly important insight is the observed decoupling between chromatin dynamics from transcriptional consequences. Although gene expression profiles largely return to baseline after recovery, the underlying chromatin landscape remains persistently altered. This disconnect suggests that epigenetic memory can exist in a latent or poised state, invisible to conventional transcriptomic analyses. Functionally, however, these changes are far from inert. When cells harboring such epigenetic scars encounter oncogenic mutations, they respond in an exaggerated manner, activating pro-tumorigenic programs more efficiently than naive cells. This distinction exposes a critical limitation in current approaches to tissue characterization, which underestimate disease risk susceptibility by relying primarily on steady-state gene expression profiles.

This study identifies AP-1 transcription factors, particularly FOS and JUN, as central drivers of inflammation-induced epigenetic memory linking chronic inflammation to cancer. Although AP-1 has been linked to chronic inflammation induced cancer progression [4], and epigenetic memory in skin stem cells, those earlier findings were mostly derived from bulk cell populations [5,6].By contrast, Nagaraja et al., the authors employed single-cell analysis to show that this memory is not uniform across all the cells. Instead, there is clear variability, with a subset of stem cells showing particularly high AP-1 activity even after recovery from colitis. This provides compelling evidence that epigenetic memory is not uniformly distributed, across the cellular population, but instead concentrated within specific clonal subsets with heightened functional potential.

The authors show that AP-1 binding motifs exhibit sustained increases in chromatin accessibility following inflammatory injury, representing one of the most stable features of the memory state. Notably, this persistence is heterogeneously distributed and not observed uniformly across all cells. Instead, a subset of stem cell clones displays particularly strong AP-1-associated accessibility, introducing a layer of functional heterogeneity within the tissue, effectively creating subpopulations uniquely poised for neoplastic transformation. This observation aligns with prior studies that have characterized AP-1 as a stress-responsive pioneer factor capable of remodeling chromatin landscapes. However, the current work extends this role beyond by implicating AP-1 in the long-term maintenance of epigenetic memory, rather than merely transient responses to stimuli. The data suggest that AP-1 not only responds to inflammatory cues but also as a molecular encoder of their history within the genome, thereby shaping subsequent cellular trajectories. This temporal extension of AP-1 function represents an important conceptual advance in our understanding of transcription factor biology, revealing its role not only in acute signal response but also in the long‑term encoding of cellular memory.

The introduction of the SHARE-TRACE methodology represents a key strength of the study. By refining existing barcoding strategies to enhance nuclear retention and ensure compatibility with SHARE-seq [7], the authors developed a platform that simultaneously captures chromatin accessibility, gene expression, and clonal lineage information at a single-cell resolution. This integrated approach enables a detailed and dynamic view of how epigenetic states are established, maintained, and heritably propagated across stem cell divisions. Crucially, it allows the authors to demonstrate that epigenetic memory is clonally propagated, supporting the idea that these chromatin states are intrinsic properties of individual stem cells rather than transient effects imposed by the surrounding microenvironment. This finding reinforces the concept that epigenetic information can function as a stable and heritable dimension of cellular identity.

The clonal nature of epigenetic memory also provides a framework for understanding the observed heterogeneity in tumorigenic potential. Only a subset of stem cell clones exhibits strong memory signatures, yet these clones disproportionately contribute to tumor formation. This uneven distribution suggests that tissues recovering from inflammation may retain localized “hotspots” of heightened susceptibility, even in the absence of genetic alterations. Such a model evokes the paradigm of field cancerization, which has traditionally been attributed to the clonal expansion and dissemination of genetically altered cells [8,9]. Here, however, the field effect appears to be driven by epigenetic rather than genetic changes, expanding the scope of this classical idea. Mechanistically, the study further implicates cooperation between AP-1 and FOX family transcription factors in stabilizing the memory state. Composite binding motifs for these factors show enhanced accessibility, and FOX proteins, particularly FOXP1, appear to facilitate sustained AP-1 engagement with chromatin. This cooperative interaction underscores the importance of combinatorial transcription factor networks in encoding epigenetic information. Rather than acting in isolation, factors such as AP-1 operate within broader regulatory circuits that collectively shape chromatin architecture and determine cellular responsiveness.

Despite its strengths, the study also prompts several critical questions. The extent to which these findings generalize to human disease remains to be fully established, as much of the experimental work relies on mouse models and organoid systems. Additionally, while chromatin accessibility changes are well characterized, the molecular mechanisms responsible for their long-term maintenance, such as the role of DNA methylation or histone modifications require further investigation. Another open question is whether therapeutic interventions can erase or reprogram these epigenetic memories to reduce cancer risk. Nevertheless, the implications of this work are far-reaching. By demonstrating that past inflammatory experiences can be durably encoded in stem cells, the study suggests that cancer risk may be shaped not only by genetic mutations but also by the epigenetic legacy of environmental exposures. This perspective carries important clinical ramifications, particularly for patients with chronic inflammatory conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease. It raises the possibility that interventions targeting epigenetic regulators or memory-associated transcription factors could mitigate long-term cancer risk, even after inflammation has been controlled.

More broadly, the concept of inflammatory epigenetic memory introduces a new framework for understanding how transient environmental exposures generate long-lasting biological consequences. Cells may not merely respond passively to inflammatory stimuli but instead actively record prior experiences within their chromatin architecture. In this view, tumorigenesis may be shaped not only by genetic alterations but also by the cumulative epigenetic history of a tissue. Such a model has important implications for chronic inflammatory diseases including inflammatory bowel disease, where cancer risk may persist long after apparent clinical resolution of inflammation.

Despite the growing recognition of inflammatory epigenetic memory, the broader regulatory circuitry governing these persistent chromatin states remains poorly defined. Most mediators implicated thus far, including AP-1 family transcription factors and associated chromatin regulators, have largely emerged through hypothesis-driven approaches. Whether these represent central memory regulators or merely a subset of a substantially larger interconnected network remains unknown. Given the marked heterogeneity observed across stem cell populations, it is likely that additional chromatin remodelers, histone modifiers, pioneer transcription factors, non-coding regulatory elements, and epigenetic maintenance machinery contribute to the establishment, maintenance, and recall of inflammatory memory states. A major challenge moving forward will therefore be the systematic identification of causal regulators that functionally encode persistent epigenetic susceptibility to tumorigenesis.

Emerging CRISPR functional genomic technologies integrated with single-cell multiomic profiling provides an unprecedented opportunity to mechanistically decode inflammatory epigenetic memory through unbiased approaches. Genome-wide CRISPR screens coupled with chromatin accessibility-based readouts could enable systematic identification of factors required for memory establishment, propagation, and maintenance following inflammatory injury. Beyond discovery screens, advances in CRISPR-based epigenome engineering have demonstrated that epigenetic states can be directly manipulated and stably inherited. The development of programmable epigenetic editors, such as CRISPRoff, has shown that targeted deposition of repressive chromatin modifications can induce durable and heritable gene silencing, establishing long-lasting epigenetic memory programs in mammalian cells [10]. These findings underscore the concept that inflammatory memory may represent a functionally encoded and experimentally tractable chromatin state.

Integrating perturbation platforms with lineage tracing and single-cell epigenomic approaches, including Perturb-seq, SHARE-seq, and multimodal chromatin profiling, may further enable direct mapping of how specific genetic perturbations reshape clonal memory states and influence subsequent oncogenic transformation. Such strategies could establish a functional framework for identifying epigenetic memory dependencies that may be therapeutically targeted to prevent inflammation-associated tumor initiation or progression. Systematic CRISPR-based interrogation of inflammatory memory may also uncover actionable vulnerabilities within memory-primed stem cell populations. These persistent memory states may depend on specific chromatin remodelers, transcription factor networks, or metabolic adaptations that can be selectively disrupted without compromising normal tissue homeostasis. In addition to advancing mechanistic understanding, these approaches provide a conceptual basis for developing epigenetic memory erasing therapies aimed at reducing long-term cancer susceptibility in patients with chronic inflammatory diseases. Collectively, these technologies may redefine epigenetic memory not merely as a descriptive biological phenomenon, but as a functionally tractable and potentially druggable vulnerability in cancer evolution.

In summary, the study by Nagaraja et al.[2] advances our understanding of tumorigenesis beyond mutation-centric models by identifying inflammatory epigenetic memory as an additional layer of heritable information that shapes long-term cancer susceptibility. These findings open new avenues to investigate how inflammatory memory is established, maintained, and reversed across tissues and disease contexts. The integration of CRISPR functional genomics with single cell multiomic technologies may enable systematic dissection of the molecular circuitry underlying inflammatory memory and uncover therapeutically actionable epigenetic dependencies. Ultimately, these advances could inform the development of epigenetic memory erasing strategies for cancer prevention, risk stratification, and precision intervention in inflammation-associated malignancies.
