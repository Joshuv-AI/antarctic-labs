Optimization of Fluorescent Nanoparticles Architecture for Efficient Brightness, FRET Efficiency and Biosensing
Authors: Dalel El Marrouki, Clara Catros, Julien Sanchez, Paul Marque, Chloé Grazon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrecise control over fluorophore localization within polymer nanoparticles remains a major challenge, limiting the rational optimization of brightness, Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET), and biosensing performance. Here, we report architecturally controlled donor core‐shell‐crown fluorescent polymer nanoparticles synthesized via a controlled radical polymerization in miniemulsion through a one‐pot process performed entirely in water at high solid content. The polymerizable BODIPY monomers are covalently incorporated into the polymer backbone, preventing dye leakage while enabling spatial confinement within defined compartments. By selectively positioning the dye in the core, the shell, or both, we decouple brightness and donor‐acceptor distance effects. Core‐confined dyes maximize brightness by reducing water‐induced quenching, whereas shell‐localized dyes enhance FRET efficiency (up to 75%) by minimizing donor‐acceptor separation, revealing a brightness/distance trade‐off analogous to that observed in inorganic nanomaterials. Exciton migration and multivalent surface functionalization further amplify energy transfer within each nano‐object. In a competitive biotin‐streptavidin proof of concept assay, nanoparticle brightness emerges as the dominant parameter controlling analytical sensitivity. The optimized sensor achieves a limit of detection of ∼4–8 nM in different buffers or artificial fluids, and using both a spectrofluorometer and a low‐cost LED‐based device, highlighting the potential of these scalable, metal‐free nanotransducers for point‐of‐care biosensing.Keywords: amplification, biosensor, device, energy transfer, polymer

Introduction

Luminescent nanoparticles have been widely employed for more than two decades as efficient transducers in ex vivo and in vivo biosensing platforms [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], with semiconductor quantum dots (QDs) [6] and lanthanide‐doped upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) [7] among the most developed systems. However, the presence of heavy metals in their core raises concerns regarding long‐term toxicity and environmental impact, which may limit their translation toward point‐of‐care applications.

Beyond these considerations, a more fundamental issue remains insufficiently addressed: the rational optimization of nanoparticle architecture to simultaneously maximize intrinsic brightness, Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) efficiency, and ultimately biosensing performance. In QD‐based FRET systems, increasing shell thickness enhances brightness by improving exciton confinement and surface passivation, but it also increases the donor‐acceptor distance, which reduces FRET efficiency [8]. A similar dilemma exists for UCNP‐FRET systems, where the overall upconversion luminescence arises from activator ions distributed throughout the nanoparticle [9, 10]. Such studies demonstrate that brightness alone is not the relevant figure of merit, rather, the spatial distribution of emitters within the nanoparticle critically governs biosensing sensitivity.

In contrast, within the field of fully organic fluorescent nanoparticles, comparable architectural optimization remains largely unexplored. Most reported systems rely either on conjugated polymer particles (Pdots) or on dye‐loaded latex nanoparticles [11, 12] prepared by swelling or nanoprecipitation methods. While enabling efficient cascade quenching and amplified sensing responses [13] based on collective energy migration mechanisms originally introduced by Swager and coworkers [14, 15], such systems suffer from limited architectural control, especially dye distribution in the hydrophobic part, and chromophores doping ratio. As a result, no systematic study has yet clarified how these parameters collectively govern brightness, FRET efficiency, water‐induced quenching, and ultimately biosensor performance.

Here, we address this gap through the synthesis of a library of nine core–shell–crown fluorescent polymer nanoparticles prepared by RAFT polymerization in miniemulsion [16], a strategy that uniquely enables precise spatial confinement of covalently incorporated BODIPY monomers within distinct nanoparticle compartments. To investigate the interplay between emitter localization and FRET behavior in a biosensing context, we engineered three well‐defined nanoparticle architectures: (i) dye confined exclusively within a protected core (NPC ), (ii) dye localized only in the shell (NPS ), and (iii) dye distributed in both core and shell (NPCS ). The outer crown was further functionalized with biotin to implement a competitive biotin–streptavidin FRET assay.

By systematically comparing these architectures across different doping ratios, we establish direct structure–property–function relationships linking dye spatial distribution to intrinsic brightness, donor–acceptor distance, FRET efficiency, and analytical sensitivity. This work identifies the optimal compromise between brightness and energy‐transfer performance and provides rational design rules for fully organic fluorescent nanoparticles as structurally engineered FRET nanotransducers. More broadly, it opens new perspectives for sustainable, metal‐free, high‐performance biosensors compatible with point‐of‐care applications.

Results

Synthesis of Core‐Shell Nanoparticles

The synthesis of the different nanoparticles is built upon our previously reported core‐shell‐crown fluorescent polymer nanoparticles synthesized by RAFT polymerization in miniemulsion [16]. This one‐pot, two‐steps process is performed entirely in water at high solid content (15 wt.%), ensures quantitative incorporation of all reactants without purification or organic solvents, and covalently embeds the dye within the polymer backbone, thereby preventing leakage even in complex biological environments. This polymerization in dispersed media is a substantial improvement over conventional nanoprecipitation methods to obtain fluorescent nanoparticles, typically operating at much lower concentrations (0.001–0.2 wt.%) [17, 18]. A major limitation of such nanoparticles prepared by nanoprecipitation is the lack of architectural control. Because nanoprecipitation relies on rapid solvent exchange and kinetic trapping, the resulting internal structures are often heterogeneous, and the spatial distribution of dyes cannot be precisely controlled. Attempts to construct multilayered nanoparticles by sequential polymer deposition may additionally generate secondary nucleation events in water and inhomogeneous shell growth. Consequently, the exact positioning of fluorophores relative to the nanoparticle surface—and thus their degree of protection from water‐induced quenching [19] or their distance to FRET acceptors—remains largely uncontrolled.

In more details, the BDπ monomer (Scheme S2), which features a polymerizable phenyl acrylate function, was synthesized according to a previously reported procedure [20]. This monomer was selected for its compatibility with radical polymerizations, particularly RAFT in miniemulsion, its ease of synthesis (only two steps), and its ability to copolymerize with styrene. The three types of nanoparticles (NPC , NPS , NPCS ) were synthesized via controlled RAFT polymerization in miniemulsion in water [16]. The hydrophilic crown consists of a poly(acrylic acid‐co‐poly(ethylene glycol) acrylate) (P(AA‐co‐APEG), M n ∼ 6.2 kg/mol), while the hydrophobic cores or shells are composed of copolymers of styrene and BDπ (P(S‐co‐BDπ), M n ∼ 16 kg/mol). We also varied the molar percentage of BDπ relative to styrene from 0.2% to 1% (Figure 1). Therefore, nanoparticles with a fluorescent core (BDπ localized only in the core) are denoted as NPC,0.2‐1 , those with a fluorescent shell (BDπ localized only in the shell) as NPS,0.2‐1 , and nanoparticles with both a fluorescent core and shell (containing BDπ in both the core and shell) are denoted as NPCS,0.2‐1 . All nanoparticles share the same hydrophilic corona, resulting in identical surface chemistry.

(A) core (NPC): P(AA11‐co‐APEG11)22‐b‐PS50‐b‐P(Sx‐co‐BDπy)150‐TTC‐C12H25. (B) shell (NPS): P(AA11‐co‐APEG11)22‐b‐P(Sx‐co‐BDπy)50‐b‐PS150‐TTC‐C12H25. (C) core‐shell (NPCS): P(AA11‐co‐APEG11)22‐b‐P(Sx‐co‐BDπy)200‐TTC‐C12H25.

The precise control of BDπ incorporation is achieved through the unique combination of RAFT living controlled radical polymerization [21] and the one‐pot, two steps miniemulsion process. Briefly, a bulk polymerization of styrene with y moles % of BDπ (for NPS or NPCS ) or without BDπ (NPC ) is initiated by a radical source in the presence of a macro‐RAFT agent P(AA‐co‐APEG)‐TTC‐C12H25, under an argon atmosphere. After approximately 120 min, the polymerization is interrupted and amphiphilic copolymers of P(AA‐co‐APEG)22‐b‐P(S x ‐co‐BDπ y )50‐TTC‐C12H25 are obtained (in the case of NPC , y = 0). Then, for (i) NPC , y mol% of BDπ is added to the mixture, as there was no BDπ in the first step; for (ii) NPS , since all monomers are consumed, styrene is added to the mixture; and for (iii) NPCS , the mixture remains unchanged. Finally, basic water is added to this mixture and sonicated to form the miniemulsion, that is, droplets of styrene with free BDπ (NPC and NPCS ) or without free BDπ (NPS ), stabilized by amphiphilic P(AA‐co‐APEG)22‐b‐P(S x ‐co‐BDπ y )50‐TTC‐C12H25. Oxygen is removed from the dispersion by argon purging, and after heating overnight at 80°C, the polymerization is complete (∼97% conversion for styrene and ∼98% for BDπ, Table 1). The resulting nanoparticles have a dry diameter of 26 ± 3 nm (Figures S7 and S8, Table 2), consisting of a ∼23 nm diameter core surrounded by a ∼1.6 nm thick shell (calculated using the molar ratio of PS contributing to the core or shell). These nanoparticles consist of amphiphilic block copolymers, specifically P(AA‐co‐APEG)22‐b‐PS50‐b‐P(S x ‐co‐BDπ y )150‐TTC‐C12H25 for the NPC , P(AA‐co‐APEG)22‐b‐P(S x ‐co‐BDπ y )50‐b‐PS150‐TTC‐C12H25 for the NPS , and P(AA‐co‐APEG)22‐b‐P(S x ‐co‐BDπ y )200‐TTC‐C12H25 for the NPCS , where P(AA‐co‐APEG) forms the hydrophilic crown and P(S x ‐co‐BDπ y ) constitutes the hydrophobic core or shell. The polymerization process is well‐controlled, as indicated by a narrow molecular weight distribution (M w/M n ≈ 1.6, Table 1), ensuring uniformity and precision in the polymer chains. All nanoparticles were used in the subsequent experiments without routine post‐synthesis purification, owing to the near‐quantitative incorporation of BDπ during polymerization. Due to its highly hydrophobic character, residual unreacted BDπ is expected to remain confined within the nanoparticle core rather than partitioning into the aqueous phase. To evaluate the possible contribution of residual free BDπ, nanoparticles containing 1% BDπ were washed three times using 100 kDa ultrafiltration membranes with ultrapure water. Absorption, emission, and time‐resolved fluorescence spectra remained unchanged before and after washing, demonstrating that residual free BDπ does not measurably affect the photophysical response of the nanoparticles (Figure S6). Additional details on the synthesis steps and the control of BDπ incorporation are provided in the Supporting Information.

Multi‐Angle Light scattering (MALS) (Figure S1) was performed on NPCS, 0 (0% BDπ) of 25 nm dry diameter in TEM (DTEM ) and hydrodynamic radius (Rh ) of 25 nm, and allowed to determine the weight average molecular weight (M w, NP = 2.3 × 107 g/mol) and gyration radius (Rg = 20 nm) of the nanoparticle. The Rg/Rh ratio of 0.8 confirm the solid sphere morphology of the NP. From M w, NP, and given the M w, chain of a single copolymer chain, the aggregation number (Nagg ), ie the number of amphiphilic copolymer chains constituting the nanoparticles can be determined (Equation S7), and calculated as Nagg = 750±150. In addition, Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) measurements performed on all nanoparticles show a consistent average 58 nm hydrodynamic diameter (Table 2). Given that all fluorescent nanoparticles in this study share the same diameter and TEM confirms a spherical morphology, we approximate that all nanoparticles have the same aggregation number (Nagg = 750±150). This means that, on average, the nanoparticles doped with 0.2% BDπ contain ca 300 BDπ monomers per particle, with a nanoparticle molar absorption coefficient εNP = 2.2 × 107 M−1.cm−1, those doped with 0.5% BDπ contain ca 750 BDπ monomers per nanoparticle with εNP = 5.4 × 107 M−1.cm−1, and those doped with 1% BDπ contain ca 1500 BDπ monomers per nanoparticle, with εNP = 1.1 × 108 M−1.cm−1 Equation (1). In addition, due to the dye partitioning in the core/shell or core‐shell polystyrene parts, the local BDπ concentration (Table 1) is strongly influenced by the nanoparticle architecture and varies between 20 and 330 mM.

Overall, this one‐pot two steps miniemulsion approach uniquely enables precise architectural control over dye localization and concentration within core‐shell nanoparticles.

Spectroscopic Characterization of the Different Fluorescent Nanoparticles

The nine nanoparticles were then deeply characterized by steady‐state or time‐resolved spectroscopy (Table 2). Some general trends can be extracted. First, whatever the type of nanoparticle (core, shell, or core‐shell), all nanoparticles absorb around 529 nm and emit at 543 nm (Figure 2). Thus, at the particle level, collective absorption dominates over individual quantum yield loss and all the 1% loaded nanoparticles are brighter than the 0.2% (Figure 2). Then, for a given BDπ concentration, NPC always have a higher ΦF and therefore are brighter than NPCS and NPS , which slightly have the same ΦF . Overall, the knr of NPCS and NPS is higher than that of NPC . We attribute this to a protective effect of the non‐fluorescent polystyrene shell in the NPC , avoiding fluorescence quenching by high‐energy vibrations of the water [19]. In contrast, NPCS and NPS lack this protective barrier, allowing energy transfer from surface BDπ dyes. In our system, the BDπ concentration in the nanoparticles polystyrene part is ∼20–330 mM (Table 1), which places the nanoparticles in a high‐density regime where efficient intermolecular energy transfers (Dexter or FRET type) [11, 23] are expected due to short dye–dye distances. According to Gharbi et al. [24] and Fennel et al. [25], the exciton diffusion coefficient should span from ∼10 to more than 370 nm2/ns, corresponding to a diffusion length of 5 – 50 nm, comparable to the 26 nm nanoparticles hydrophobic core. The observed decrease in fluorescence lifetime (6 to 4 ns across 0.2%–1%, i.e., 20–330 mM of BDπ) further supports this mechanism, as it reflects enhanced coupling between confined dyes, consistent with increased exciton mobility at higher loading. In such concentrated dye‐loaded nanoparticles, quenching can thus propagate in cascade throughout the entire nanoparticle via energy transfers, with efficiency increasing at higher dye concentrations [22].

As a comparison with the state‐of‐the art, the maximum nanoparticle brightness achieved in this study (B = 6.9 × 107 M−1.cm−1 for NPC,1 ) surpasses previously reported bright green‐emitting organic nanoparticles such as Pdots (B = 3 × 106 M−1.cm−1) [26], aggregation‐induced emission nanoparticles (B = 3.5 × 107 M−1.cm−1) [27], BODIPY‐dopped polystyrene nanoparticles (B = 5.1 × 106 M−1.cm−1), but is lower than nanoparticles based on charged dyes with bulky counterions (B = 1.1 × 108 M−1.cm−1) [28].

Biotinylated NP and Optimized FRET Efficiency

To construct a biosensor, biotinylation of the nanoparticles was performed as a proof of concept. 1000 equivalents of biotinamine (biotin‐PEG7‐NH2, M w = 595 g/mol) per nanoparticle were reacted directly in water with nanoparticles bearing carboxylic acid groups on their hydrophilic crown, in the presence of 1000 equivalents of 4‐(4,6‐Dimethoxy‐1,3,5‐triazin‐2‐yl)‐4‐methylmorpholin‐4‐ium chloride (DMTMM) per nanoparticle as a coupling agent, at room temperature overnight. The biotinylated nanoparticles (bt‐NP) were purified by dialysis in ultrapure water, and dispersed in HEPES 1× buffer (25 mM HEPES with 150 mM NaCl at pH = 7.4) for FRET assays. The biotinylation efficiency (ie the presence of biotin on the nanoparticles surface) was confirmed by the specific recognition of bt‐NP using streptavidin‐coated agarose beads (Figure 3; Figure S2).

Texas Red‐labeled streptavidin (SA‐TR) was selected as the FRET acceptor due to its spectral compatibility with the BDπ donor in the nanoparticles (Figure 4B). The commercial SA‐TR, labeled with two Texas Red molecules per protein, was used for all assays (ΦF, SA‐TR = 0.34). The ultra‐high biotin‐streptavidin affinity (Kd = 10−13 – 10−15 M [29] << [NP] ∼ 10−10 M) ensures robust conjugation of SA‐TR to the bt‐NP surface, which should enable efficient FRET from the BDπ loaded fluorescent core and/or shell to the Texas Red dyes on the streptavidin. To determine the maximum FRET efficiency for each nanoparticle type, increasing quantities of SA‐TR were titrated into a fixed quantity of bt‐NP until saturation (∼100±20 SA per nanoparticle). As a simple geometrical estimation, considering nanoparticles with an average hydrophobic core‐shell diameter of 25 nm surrounded by a ∼5 nm hydrophilic crown [30], the resulting nanoparticle surface could theoretically accommodate approximately 150–200 SA proteins (assuming an average SA size of ∼5 nm) under close‐packing conditions. Given the uncertainty associated with these approximations and the experimental error on the saturation value, the observed plateau remains broadly consistent with steric crowding effects at the nanoparticle surface.

The resulting FRET efficiencies are in line with the state‐of‐the‐art values reported for dye‐doped nanoparticles (E = 60%) [34], Pdots (E = ∼80%) [35], QDs (E = 67%) [8], or UCNP (E = 49%) [9] as donors with dyes as acceptors.

Interestingly, the highest FRET efficiencies are systematically observed for NPS architectures, despite NPC exhibit the largest R0 . This indicates that donor‐acceptor spatial positioning plays a more dominant role than moderate variations in R0 in determining the overall FRET response. Indeed, although NPC possesses a larger R0 , the BDπ dyes confined within the nanoparticle core remain, on average, farther from the acceptors located at the particle periphery. In contrast, shell‐localized BDπ dyes in NPS are positioned closer to SA‐TR, resulting in more efficient energy transfer. Consistently, the estimated donor‐acceptor distances (RDA , Equation S14) are approximately 6.5 nm for NPC and 5.6 nm for NPS , in good agreement with the expected nanoparticle structure composed of a ∼5 nm hydrophilic crown and a ∼1.6 nm PS shell.

Analysis of the FRET rate constants kET (Equation S13) further supports this interpretation. For a given nanoparticle architecture, kET remains globally constant regardless of the dye loading, indicating that the local BDπ concentration does not significantly influence the energy transfer rate to surface‐bound acceptors (Texas Red). In contrast, clear differences are observed between nanoparticle architectures. NPS displays the highest kET values (kET ∼5 × 108 s−1), consistent with shorter donor‐acceptor distances, whereas NPC exhibits the lowest values (kET ∼2 × 108 s−1). NPCS nanoparticles show intermediate unexpected behavior (kET ∼2.7 × 108 s−1). Given their R0 values and the partial localization of BDπ within the shell, one could anticipate FRET efficiencies closer to those of NPS . A plausible hypothesis is that the behavior of NPCS arises from the larger nonradiative decay rates (knr ) for NPCS compared to NPS . Because knr competes directly with kET , enhanced nonradiative deactivation decreases the probability of energy transfer and thereby limits the overall FRET efficiency.

Altogether, these photophysics results confirm that the spatial localization of the emitters within the nanoparticle is the main parameter governing energy transfer process.

FRET‐Based Competitive Biotin Biosensor

In light of the exceptionally high FRET efficiencies observed for these systems, we next exploited the bt‐NP to design a FRET‐based biotin sensor using SA‐TR. The sensor operates on a competitive binding mechanism between biotin molecules grafted onto the nanoparticle surface and free biotin present in solution (Figure 6). At low concentrations of free biotin, SA‐TR preferentially binds to the bt‐NP, resulting in increased proximity between donor and acceptor and thus enhanced FRET. Conversely, increasing the concentration of free biotin promotes the binding of SA‐TR to biotin in solution rather than to bt‐NP, leading to a decrease in FRET efficiency.

(A) Schematic illustration of the biotin sensor design. Without free biotin, SA‐TR associates with bt‐NP, enabling FRET to occur from bt‐NP to TR. With free biotin, SA‐TR preferentially binds to it, inhibiting FRET. The FRET efficiency is proportional to the free biotin concentration within the sensor's dynamic range. (B) Apparatus used for the sensor readout. Left: copyright Edinburgh Instruments.

Experimentally, bt‐NP were mixed with free biotin and buffer and gently agitated. SA–TR was then added, followed by further agitation prior to analysis. All measurements were performed in triplicate (N = 3) using identical starting nanoparticle batches. For all sensor systems, the fluorescence intensity was carefully adjusted to remain constant by adapting the nanoparticles concentration. As a result, less bright nanoparticles (NPCS,0.2 and NPS,0.2 ) were used at higher concentrations (450 and 366 pM respectively), whereas moderately emissive (NPC,0.5 ) and brighter nanoparticles (NPC,1 ) were correspondingly diluted (respectively 150 and 86 pM). Since the amount of SA‐TR bound to the bt‐NP scales with the nanoparticle concentration, higher SA–TR concentrations were therefore required for the less emissive systems.

To evaluate the potential of this sensing strategy for point‐of‐care applications, we compared its performance using a standard spectrofluorometer and a low‐cost, home‐built LED‐based device (“POC device”). Whatever the device used, the sensor output is the ratio of the fluorescence intensity of the acceptor (I(A), at 613 nm) over the donor (I(D), at 543 nm).

Sensors 1 and 2 were first compared to assess the effect of decreasing the number of SA‐TR molecules per nanoparticle at constant brightness, using the brightest nanoparticle system (NPC,1 ). Reducing the number of SA‐TR equivalents from 100 (Sensor 1, EFRET = 40%) to 50 (Sensor 2, EFRET = 25%) led to a decrease in FRET efficiency but a clear improvement in the LOD from 9.9 to 3.5 nM, consistent with a shift in the competitive binding equilibrium. Based on this result, the number of SA‐TR molecules was fixed at 50 equivalents per nanoparticle for subsequent studies.

Sensors 2, 3 and 4 were then compared to isolate the effect of nanoparticle brightness at nearly constant FRET efficiency. Switching from bright NPC,1 nanoparticles (Sensor 2, B = 6.9 × 107 M− 1.cm− 1, [NP] = 86 pM, EFRET = 25%) to less emissive NPC,0.5 nanoparticles (Sensor 3, B = 3.9 × 107 M− 1.cm− 1, [NP] = 150 pM, EFRET = 20%) to even less emissive NPCS,0.2 nanoparticles (Sensor 4, B = 1.3 × 107 M−1.cm−1, [NP] = 450 pM, EFRET = 21%) resulted in an increased LOD. In addition, the signal measured from the low‐cost device shows significantly larger error bars, especially for the most concentrated nanoparticles, ie the less bright. This behavior is tentatively attributed to nanoparticle aggregation mediated by the tetravalent SA‐TR, as observed under confocal microscopy conditions (data not shown), which becomes more pronounced at higher nanoparticle concentrations required for less bright systems. Such aggregation can perturb the excitation field of the LED, increasing stray excitation that partially overlaps with donor emission and thus affects the ratiometric sensor output (I(A)/I(D)). This effect is not observed for the brightest nanoparticles, for which lower concentrations slow down aggregation kinetics and preserve signal stability, nor on the standard spectrofluorometer, where the use of two excitation monochromators and one emission monochromator minimize stray excitation leakage into the donor emission channel.

Finally, Sensors 4 and 5 were compared to evaluate the impact of FRET efficiency at nearly constant brightness. Replacing NPCS,0.2 (Sensor 4, B = 1.3 × 107 M− 1.cm− 1, EFRET = 21%) with NPS,0.2 (Sensor 5, B = 1.6 × 107 M− 1.cm− 1, EFRET = 46%) led to a modest decrease in the LOD, although the difference remains limited and may partially originate from the slightly higher brightness of NPS,0.2 . A somewhat broader sensing range is also observed for lower FRET efficiencies.

To rationalize the respective roles of nanoparticle brightness, nanoparticle concentration, and FRET efficiency on sensing performance, an extended comparative analysis was introduced (Sensors 2–5, Figure 8). The LOD shows a clear inverse dependence on nanoparticle brightness (Figure 8A). In contrast, no systematic trend is observed between LOD and FRET efficiency (data not shown), indicating that variations in energy transfer efficiency do not directly translate into improved analytical sensitivity in this system.

(A) LOD as a function of the brightness of the NP, using SA‐TR/NP = 50 (sensors 2–5). (B) LOD as a function of the NP concentration in the sensors assay, using SA‐TR/NP = 50 (sensors 2–5). Error bars are standard errors over N = 3 experiments.

Conversely, increasing nanoparticle concentration leads to a systematic increase in LOD due to the higher number of biotin binding sites available for SA–TR (Figure 8B).

Overall, these results identify nanoparticle brightness as the key governing parameter of analytical sensitivity. Since brightness is primarily governed by dyes located in the nanoparticle core, which are better protected from water‐induced quenching, this further indicates that core‐localized emitters dominate the sensing performance, whereas shell‐localized dyes contribute less effectively despite their higher FRET efficiency.

Finally, to further evaluate the robustness and applicability of our sensing platform in biologically relevant environments, we investigated the performance of the best‐performing sensor (Sensor 2) in artificial urine and artificial saliva (Figure 9 and Table 3). In both media, the normalized dose–response curves closely overlap with those obtained in the reference HEPES 1× buffer, indicating that the sensing mechanism is not significantly affected by matrix composition. The corresponding IC50 values remain of the same order of magnitude across all conditions. Although a slight increase in the LOD is observed (∼7 nM compared to ∼4 nM in HEPES 1× buffer), the analytical signal remains well resolved and measurable using the low‐cost POC device. These results demonstrate the robustness of the FRET response in complex biological fluids and support the potential applicability of this platform for point‐of‐care biosensing in realistic sample environments.

Discussion

Most dye‐loaded latex nanoparticles and semiconducting polymer dots rely on nanoprecipitation [17, 23, 35] or swelling‐based processes [11], where rapid solvent exchange and kinetic trapping lead to heterogeneous internal structures and limited control over fluorophore localization. In addition, Pdots can present an oxidized surface layer which can create exciton‐trapping sites, potentially detrimental to FRET processes [35, 36].

In contrast, the RAFT miniemulsion strategy used here enables covalent incorporation of dyes at different concentrations and their spatial confinement within well‐defined core, shell, or core–shell configurations, without modifying particle size or surface chemistry. A central outcome of this work is therefore the establishment of architecture‐photophysics‐sensing relationships in fully organic fluorescent nanoparticles with controlled internal architecture. This control directly reveals a brightness‐distance trade‐off by decoupling dye localization from composition. Core‐localized fluorophores maximize brightness due to reduced water‐induced quenching, while shell‐localized dyes benefit from shorter donor‐acceptor distances and therefore higher FRET efficiency. FRET analysis confirms that donor‐acceptor distance is the main parameter governing energy transfer efficiency, with shell‐localized nanoparticles systematically showing higher FRET efficiencies and core‐localized systems remaining lower due to larger donor‐acceptor distance despite higher intrinsic brightness and higher Förster radius. Nanoparticles combining both regions show intermediate behavior, indicating a competition between improved brightness and less favorable spatial distribution of emitters. This behavior follows general trends observed in QDs [8] and UCNPs [9, 10], where emitters localization govern the balance between emission intensity and energy transfer, but is here demonstrated in a fully organic system with controlled architectural access.

When translated into biosensing, a different hierarchy emerges. Brightness becomes the dominant parameter controlling analytical sensitivity, while FRET efficiency plays a secondary role. At comparable transfer efficiencies, brighter nanoparticles consistently provide lower limits of detection, whereas increasing FRET efficiency alone leads to only marginal improvements. This behavior reflects the fact that brightness directly defines the effective concentration of emitting nanoparticles and therefore the number of active binding sites in the competitive assay, and as such the LOD [37, 38].

Finally, the strong biotin‐streptavidin affinity ensures essentially quantitative binding under the experimental conditions used here ([NP] ∼ 10−10 M), and does not limit the sensing response in the concentration regime explored. This parameter may however become relevant in alternative competitive or non‐equilibrium sensing formats involving weaker bioreceptor interactions.

The robustness of the platform is confirmed by similar performance obtained with both a standard spectrofluorometer and a low‐cost LED‐based device. Despite the large difference in instrumental complexity, both configurations provide comparable dose‐response curves, IC50 values and limits of detection, showing that the analytical response is largely independent of the detection setup when a ratiometric readout (I(A)/I(D)) is used, although even better LOD can be achieved with expensive time‐resolved detection systems [39, 40]. Overall, it clearly demonstrates that competitive biosensing strategies can be implemented with minimal instrumentation when combined with highly emissive nano‐objects. The same behavior is observed in complex biological media such as artificial urine and saliva, with only minor variations in LOD, further supporting the suitability of the system for point‐of‐care applications.

Conclusion

This work establishes structure‐property‐function relationships in fully organic fluorescent polymer nanoparticles with controlled internal architecture, obtained through a scalable RAFT miniemulsion process in water. Covalent incorporation of dyes combined with spatial confinement within defined nanoparticle compartments enables independent control of optical output and energy‐transfer behavior, while preventing dye leakage.

Tuning dye localization within the nanoparticles leads to distinct photophysical regimes, where confinement in the particle core favors higher fluorescence emission due to reduced interaction with the surrounding medium, whereas shell localization promotes more efficient energy transfer by shortening donor‐acceptor distances. These observations confirm that internal organization, rather than overall composition alone, is a key parameter governing performance in organic nanotransducers.

In a competitive biotin‐streptavidin sensing assay, the best performing system reaches a LOD of ∼4 nM in a 50 µL volume, corresponding to approximately 200 femtomoles of biotin. The same analytical performance is obtained using both a laboratory spectrofluorometer and a low‐cost LED‐based detection setup, demonstrating that sensitivity is preserved even under simplified instrumentation. Comparable responses are also observed in complex biological media, including artificial urine and saliva, with only minor variations in detection limits.

Importantly, sensing performance could be further tuned through the choice of bioreceptor, since binding affinity directly affects the equilibrium of competitive displacement assays [37, 38, 39]. This provides a general route to adapt sensitivity across different targets by selecting appropriate recognition elements such as antibodies or aptamers. Additional improvements are expected from brighter nanoparticles and more sensitive detectors.

Overall, this metal‐free and scalable platform enables competitive biosensing with fluorescent organic nanoparticles and opens perspectives for displacement assays, lateral flow diagnostics [41], and smartphone point‐of‐care devices [42, 43].
