Quality and reliability of short videos about long COVID on bilibili and tiktok: cross-sectional study under health community construction background in China
Authors: Xianyu Xie, Zijun Zhao, Qinde Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong COVID, characterized by persistent symptoms such as fatigue and cognitive impairment, continues to pose a global public health burden. Bilibili and TikTok are major platforms for public health information; however, the lack of systematic evaluation contributes to low-quality information and may hinder effective health management. We aimed to evaluate the quality and reliability of long COVID-related videos on these platforms.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed long COVID-related videos from Bilibili and TikTok. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Quality and reliability were evaluated using the Global Quality Score (GQS) and modified DISCERN (mDISCERN). Multivariate linear regression was performed to identify the independent predictors of video quality.ResultsThe median GQS and mDISCERN scores were both significantly higher for Bilibili than for TikTok (P = 0.016 and P = 0.039, respectively). Professional-source videos showed significantly higher quality than non-professional ones (P < 0.001). Regression analyses revealed that a professional source was the strongest independent predictor of both GQS and mDISCERN scores (all P < 0.001). Video duration and shares were significantly, albeit weakly, associated with the GQS, whereas other engagement metrics were not.ConclusionsThe overall quality of long COVID videos was suboptimal. Professional source was the primary independent predictor of higher quality, while engagement and duration showed limited influence. Background

Long COVID has emerged as a major public health concern with widespread impact. It is characterized by persistent symptoms, such as fatigue, cognitive impairment, and respiratory difficulties, following COVID-19 infection, affecting the daily functioning and quality of life of millions of people worldwide [1, 2]. Beyond its physical health burden, long COVID poses significant challenges to healthcare systems. Its pathogenesis remains unclear, and the high variability in symptom trajectories among individuals complicates the development of standardized diagnostic and treatment protocols [3, 4]. A critical barrier to addressing this issue is the uneven quality of publicly accessible health information on Long COVID.

“Misconceptions about long COVID are prevalent among platform users, such as equating general post-COVID fatigue with long COVID or believing in unproven ‘cure’ methods, which can delay care and contribute to poor health outcomes. Differences in health literacy further exacerbate inequalities in information reception—users with low health literacy are more likely to be misled by exaggerated and unscientific content, while those with high health literacy tend to filter information from professional sources.” [5, 6].

In China, access to standardized medical information remains inconsistent across regions, with notable disparities between urban and rural or underdeveloped areas. Amid this landscape, the rapid expansion of digital health technologies has reshaped the way the public acquires medical knowledge [7]. Short-video platforms, such as Bilibili and TikTok, which cater to a global user base of over 2.5 billion active users monthly, have become significant channels for disseminating long COVID-related health information to diverse audiences [8]. Bilibili, originally dedicated to animation, comics, and games (ACG) content, has gradually evolved into a platform that integrates both entertainment and educational resources, attracting user groups who actively seek comprehensive information. In contrast, TikTok emphasizes short-form video entertainment and uses an algorithm-driven recommendation model that enables users to discover a wider range of content passively. Bilibili’s knowledge community attributes facilitate the accumulation and in-depth discussion of professional health content, while TikTok’s extensive user coverage enables the rapid popularization of health information. These platforms offer distinct advantages for health communication: concise visual content can simplify complex medical concepts, such as demonstrating rehabilitation exercises or symptom self-monitoring, making them more accessible to non-professional audiences [9]. In addition, interactive features, including comments, sharing, and real-time engagement with creators, facilitate direct communication between healthcare professionals and the public, help overcome temporal and spatial barriers, and may improve adherence to treatment recommendations among patients with long COVID who seek information through these platforms [10]. Furthermore, algorithm-driven content distribution enables targeted delivery of long COVID-related information to specific audiences [11].

Despite these advantages, several pressing challenges remain. Long COVID–related videos produced by non-professional creators frequently exhibit shortcomings, including oversimplified symptom descriptions, non-evidence-based interventions, or misleading guidance [12, 13]. Moreover, high engagement metrics, such as likes, shares, and comments, do not necessarily reflect scientific accuracy or informational quality, potentially generating confusion among viewers seeking reliable long COVID information [14, 15]. Although short-video platforms are widely used for health information, few studies have systematically evaluated the quality and reliability of long COVID–related videos.

The included videos are designed for adults aged 18–75 years with basic digital literacy and specifically target patients with suspected or recovered long COVID and their families. To ensure accessibility, the content is tailored to individuals with a high-school level of understanding. The primary motivations for users include acquiring information about the disease, obtaining rehabilitation guidance, and alleviating health-related anxiety, with the principal benefit being access to practical self-management strategies. The core behavioral objectives promoted by these long COVID-focused videos are seeking professional medical diagnosis when experiencing suspected symptoms, adhering to evidence-based rehabilitation practices, and avoiding inappropriate self-treatment.

Given this context, this study seeks to answer the following key research questions: (1) Are there significant differences in the quality and reliability of long COVID-related videos between TikTok and Bilibili? (2) Are video characteristics—such as source, duration, and engagement metrics—associated with content quality (measured by the Global Quality Scale [GQS] score) and reliability (measured by the modified DISCERN [mDISCERN] score)? (3) Are there differences between platforms and sources in the comprehensiveness of content coverage (e.g., etiology, symptoms, diagnosis) regarding long COVID-related videos on the two platforms? It should be noted that this study exclusively addresses long COVID management behaviors, distinguishing them from general COVID-19 preventive measures such as vaccination.

Methods

Search strategy and data extraction

This cross-sectional study was conducted in strict compliance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. We aimed to gather brief videos concerning long COVID from Bilibili (https://www.bilibili.com) and TikTok (https://www.douyin.com). To reduce potential bias from personalized recommendation systems, all searches were conducted using newly created accounts in private browsing mode, thereby preventing the influence of user history or algorithmic tailoring on search results. The initial data collection involved retrieving the first 100 videos from each platform using the platform’s default sorting method. On Bilibili, videos are sorted based on four dimensions: “relevance,” “play volume” (total video views), “engagement rate” (a weighted measure of likes, collections, and comments), and “release time” (video upload date) with newer content slightly prioritized. TikTok uses an “algorithmic recommendation index” that factors in elements such as video completion rate, engagement rate, user viewing time, and account influence. Only videos focusing specifically on the epidemiology, etiology, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, or dermatological manifestations of long COVID—and that were publicly accessible—were included in this study.

The decision to review 100 videos per platform was based on design principles from previous research on the quality of short health information videos. With a combined sample of 200 videos, the study met the requirements for statistical analyses, including nonparametric tests and correlation analyses. The following types of content were excluded: (1) advertisements or promotional clips not substantially related to long COVID or only briefly referencing the term; (2) videos published within the 7 days preceding data extraction (to maintain data consistency and reliability); and (3) repeated or re-uploaded materials already captured in the dataset.

For each eligible video, the following parameters were documented: source platform (Bilibili or TikTok), date of upload, video length (in seconds), and interaction statistics, including counts of likes, comments, shares, and favorites. All data acquisition was performed using official, publicly accessible APIs from the respective platforms to ensure precision and consistency. These collected metrics formed the basis for the subsequent content evaluation and quality assessment (Fig. 1).

Video quality assessment

Before conducting video assessments, clear definitions for professional and non-professional sources was established: (1) professional individuals referred to physicians, nurses, and public health physicians; (2) professional institutions were defined as officially verified accounts of healthcare entities such as hospitals, Centers for Disease Control (CDCs), medical societies, or accredited medical education institutions; (3) non-professional individuals included those who lacked medical qualifications, presented no evidence of professional medical background on their accounts, and primarily shared personal experiences or information obtained online; (4) non-professional institutions encompassed businesses, social organizations, or media outlets unaffiliated with the medical field and without recognized medical endorsements. To appraise the reliability and quality of the collected short videos, two validated instruments were employed: the mDISCERN tool and the GQS. GQS evaluates videos based on overall quality, considering factors such as production professionalism, information thoroughness, presentation clarity, and audience comprehension to reflect their “communication applicability.” By contrast, mDISCERN, which was adapted from the DISCERN instrument originally designed for written health information, assesses the reliability of video content. It evaluates information across five dimensions, namely, clarity, relevance, traceability, rigor, and fairness, which align closely with the primary requirement for “scientific accuracy” in health information [16, 17]. Using multiple scales mitigates the limitations of relying on a single measure. Fairness was evaluated by assessing whether video content avoided demographic bias (e.g., age, gender) and addressed the needs of diverse populations, including patients across different age groups. Clarity was assessed based on the logical structure of the material, the use of accessible language, and the clarity of visual elements. Each item was scored as either “yes” (1 point) or “no” (0 points), yielding a total score of 0–5. For quality assessment, two critical dimensions—trustworthiness and misinformation detection—were included. Trust was evaluated based on explicit disclosure of the source’s professional qualifications (e.g., practicing licenses or institutional verification) and citation of authoritative references (e.g., WHO guidelines). Detection of false or misinformation involved identifying contradictory medical statements, unsubstantiated “cure” claims, and discrepancies with widely accepted medical consensus.

In addition, content completeness was assessed by determining whether each video included information on epidemiology, etiology, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of long COVID. All assessments were performed independently by two evaluators with medical backgrounds. Prior to scoring, both assessors underwent standardized training to enhance interrater agreement and minimize potential bias. In this study, two physicians independently evaluated video quality using the GQS and mDISCERN criteria (see Additional file 1), while resolving discrepancies through discussion. Any disagreements between the primary evaluators were adjudicated by a physician with a senior professional title. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, indicating good agreement (κ = 0.82 for mDISCERN; κ = 0.85 for GQS; κ = 0.83 for video source coding) and supporting the reliability of the assessment and coding outcomes. Additionally, relevant guidelines served as the gold standard for verifying the accuracy of the medical perspectives and intervention measures presented in the videos.

Statistical analysis

The normality of video-derived variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. All engagement metrics (likes, collections, shares, comments) exhibited right-skewed distributions (P < 0.001); therefore, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were used to describe central tendency and dispersion, and nonparametric tests were used for group comparisons. For nonparametric comparisons, the Mann–Whitney U-test was used for two independent groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied for three or more groups. Relationships between general video metrics (likes, collections, shares, comments, and duration) and quality or reliability scores were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). Correlation strengths were interpreted as negligible (∣ρ∣ ≈ 0.2), weak (0.2–0.4), moderate (0.4–0.6), strong (0.6–0.8), and very strong (> 0.8). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Distribution of videos by source type

Engagement metrics for the videos were as follows: the median duration was 197.0 s (IQR: 73.75–419.50), median likes totaled 490.0 (IQR: 61.50–2141.00), median collections reached 137.0 (IQR: 30.75–584.25), median shares were 132.5 (IQR: 16.00–819.50), and median comments numbered 133.5 (IQR: 16.00–519.00). Regarding quality assessment, the median GQS score was 3.0 (IQR: 2.00–3.00), and the median mDISCERN score was 2.0 (IQR: 2.00–3.00).

Video content

For long COVID-related videos, clinical manifestations were the most frequently discussed topics. On TikTok, 86% of the videos addressed clinical manifestations, compared with 83% on Bilibili. Diagnosis was covered in 65% of videos on both platforms. Other content categories on TikTok included dermatology (47%), treatment (34%), etiology (24%), and prevention (19%). On Bilibili, treatment appeared in 39% of videos, etiology in 33%, dermatology in 33%, and prevention in 12% (Fig. 3).

Completeness of video content by platforms

Content distribution varied by video source. Non-professional individuals covered clinical manifestations in 83% of videos, diagnosis in 57%, treatment in 35%, dermatology in 26%, etiology in 19%, and prevention in 13%. Non-professional institutions addressed clinical manifestations in 89%, diagnosis in 68%, dermatology in 46%, treatment in 30%, etiology in 20%, and prevention in 14% of their videos. Professional individuals covered clinical manifestations in 98% of videos, diagnosis (69%), treatment (50%), etiology (40%), dermatology (37%), and prevention (23%). Professional institutions emphasized clinical manifestations (90%), epidemiology (80%), diagnosis (65%), etiology (45%), treatment (25%), and prevention (10%) in their videos, respectively (Fig. 4).

Table 2; Fig. 5 compare video characteristics and quality between Bilibili and TikTok. Significant differences were observed across several metrics. The median video length on Bilibili was 408.50 s (IQR: 263.50–639.75), significantly longer than TikTok videos, which had a median length of 77.50 s (IQR: 16.25–154.00; Z = − 9.75, P < 0.001). Regarding engagement metrics, TikTok videos received significantly more likes (median: 660.50 vs. 179.00; Z = − 3.88, P < 0.001), shares (median: 202.50 vs. 41.00; Z = − 3.87, P < 0.001), and comments (median: 277.50 vs. 61.00; Z = − 4.06, P < 0.001) compared with Bilibili videos. In terms of video quality, Bilibili videos had a significantly higher median mDISCERN score (3.00 vs. 2.00, Z = − 2.07, P = 0.039) and a significantly different distribution of GQS scores (median 3.00 vs. 3.00, Z = − 2.41, P = 0.016) compared with TikTok videos.

GQS demonstrated a weak positive correlation with video length (r = 0.20, P < 0.001), whereas mDISCERN exhibited an even weaker association with video length (r = 0.13) and negligible relationships with all engagement metrics. Discussion

The findings indicate that videos produced by professional sources, both individual creators and institutions, consistently achieved higher scores on both GQS and mDISCERN scales, underscoring the significant influence of uploader expertise on content quality and credibility. This observation aligns with prior studies confirming that health information authored by medical professionals generally exhibits greater rigor, standardization, and scientific validity. The strong positive correlation identified between GQS and mDISCERN scores across the sample further validates the reliability of these multidimensional assessment tools in evaluating video quality [18, 19]. While engagement metrics, likes, collections, shares, and comments, showed strong intercorrelation, indicating cohesive user interaction patterns, none of these indicators demonstrated meaningful association with either GQS or mDISCERN scores. User engagement does not necessarily reflect information quality. The analysis indicates that non-professional videos (64%) often demonstrate lower quality yet achieve high engagement, likely driven by emotional or sensationalized content, attention-grabbing titles, short durations suited to fragmented viewing, and algorithm-driven content promotion on platforms. In contrast, the number of collections more accurately reflects users’ long-term valuation of content, which is particularly relevant for chronic conditions like long COVID. Collections function as a unique indicator of content’s “future utility,” highlighting users’ assessments of its practical applicability for ongoing management—an insight valuable for platforms and institutions seeking to optimize content strategies. Furthermore, the observed weak positive correlation between video length and GQS implies that longer videos may facilitate the delivery of more comprehensive health information and are associated with higher perceived quality.

TikTok demonstrated significantly higher user engagement, likely attributable to three key factors: a broader user base, short-video formats suited to fragmented viewing, and more accessible interactive features. Bilibili, with its knowledge-focused infrastructure and active user search behavior, is better positioned to disseminate specialized information on long COVID. Conversely, TikTok’s algorithm-driven distribution and shorter video format are better suited to popular science and alert messaging than to in-depth clinical guidelines.

Regarding the scientific accuracy of long COVID-related content on short-video platforms, several issues were identified in public understanding and dissemination. Some videos exaggerated the effectiveness of unproven interventions, such as unsupported nutritional supplements or unconventional rehabilitation therapies, potentially misleading viewers and diverting them from evidence-based medical care. Other videos presented overly narrow or simplified descriptions of key long COVID symptoms, such as fatigue and cognitive impairment, or emphasized prevention strategies, such as vaccination and general health management, without acknowledging individual variability or the multisystem nature of the condition. Additionally, many videos described manifestations such as persistent cough or post-exertional dyspnea but failed to outline essential clinical processes, including medical history assessment, laboratory testing, and differential diagnoses. Such omissions may lead viewers to misclassify common post-COVID symptoms or unrelated chronic conditions as long COVID, potentially increasing unnecessary anxiety. These issues not only distort public understanding but may also contribute to delays in appropriate diagnosis and treatment or encourage ineffective self-directed interventions. Therefore, strengthening scientific review and oversight of long COVID-related content on short-video platforms is essential to ensure the dissemination of accurate, evidence-based information [20].

Given the extensive adoption of short video platforms, these digital channels have evolved into significant sources for the public acquisition of health information, particularly concerning long COVID, an emerging condition that continues to attract worldwide attention [21, 22]. While prior investigations have assessed health information quality on social media, studies focusing specifically on long COVID-related short videos remain scarce, highlighting the relevance of this research. Our results align with the established literature on health communication. For instance, a study evaluating other diseases short videos on mainstream platforms indicated that, compared with content produced by non-medical practitioners, videos created by medical professionals received significantly higher scores for quality and reliability assessments [23]. Similar patterns were observed in another study, in which Bilibili videos generated by medical professionals achieved the highest scores across multiple evaluation tools, including GQS and mDISCERN [24]. According to the results of the current study, a long COVID health communication strategy should emphasize the targeted delivery of professional content. For knowledge-oriented audiences on Bilibili, it is recommended that professional organizations produce comprehensive, in-depth video analyses. Conversely, for TikTok’s general public audience, succinct videos featuring visually presented core information—such as symptom identification flowcharts—are preferable. Public health education initiatives should also enhance guidance on screening information sources, thereby improving users’ ability to recognize professional account credentials and evidence-based statements.

Practical recommendations include the following hierarchical actions: platforms should prioritize professional content within their algorithms, establish dedicated pages for long COVID, and implement credibility labels; content creators should tailor their content to platform styles, with in-depth series for Bilibili and concise, visually driven content for TikTok; and health authorities should provide training for creators, publish reports on information dissemination, and standardize health communications.

Moreover, longer videos may result in lower completion rates, particularly among individuals with fragmented viewing habits or limited health literacy. The timing of video consumption may also influence information uptake; users seeking immediate health guidance—such as post-COVID symptom management—have a greater need for brief, timely content. This should inform future content optimization efforts.

The present study found that non-professional individuals and institutions contributed 64% of long COVID-related videos, and these videos demonstrated notably lower quality scores, often exhibiting simplified symptom descriptions, interventions lacking evidence support, or inadequate diagnostic guidance. Additionally, a separate study examining chronic obstructive pulmonary disease-related content on TikTok showed significant quality disparities between individual and organizational users [24]. This study offers guidance on disseminating long COVID health information via short-video platforms. The proposed assessment framework—which evaluates source qualification, accuracy, and understandability—could be adapted to include other chronic diseases (such as type 2 diabetes and obesity) with minor disease-specific modifications and further validation.

Overall, findings from this study, together with evidence from previous research, highlight a consistent challenge: although short-video platforms can enable rapid dissemination of health information, the scientific rigor of such content is often insufficient, a limitation particularly pronounced for complex conditions, such as long COVID. The alignment between our results and those reported in existing literature reinforces the need for stronger regulation of health-related content on these platforms. Measures such as implementing professional review mechanisms and promoting the dissemination of evidence-based information created by qualified health professionals may help ensure that the public receives reliable and clinically meaningful guidance.

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the quality of the health information across major short-video platforms, with a focus on long COVID. By investigating video sources, platform characteristics, and the relationships between interaction metrics and quality scores, this study identifies the key factors influencing the reliability of health information dissemination. Furthermore, the integrated evaluation toolset (GQS + mDISCERN) provides a practical framework for content assessment and warrants further validation across other diseases and platforms [25]. The findings confirm that the uploader’s professional background is a decisive factor in determining content quality, whereas high user engagement does not necessarily reflect scientific rigor. These conclusions offer valuable empirical evidence for optimizing health information dissemination strategies on short-video platforms [26].

The quality assessment approach adopted in this study—focused on accuracy, completeness, and clarity—can also be extended to evaluating medical documents, such as discharge summaries, treatment records, thus reflecting clinical standardization and warranting further investigation.

While this study provides important empirical evidence on the quality of health information disseminated through short-video platforms, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis focused on mainstream Chinese platforms (Bilibili and TikTok) owing to their large user bases and substantial market influence; however, global platforms, such as YouTube, were not included [27, 28]. Future studies should expand to cross-platform comparisons to identify broader patterns and regional differences in health information dissemination. Second, because short-video content is rapidly updated, the cross-sectional design of this study may not reflect emerging trends in video quality and evolving user behaviors. Third, although rigorous consistency testing was conducted to ensure interrater reliability, subjective interpretations of assessment tools may still introduce potential bias [29, 30]. Additionally, the study primarily analyzed Chinese-language videos; future research should incorporate cross-linguistic and cross-cultural comparison analyses to investigate how cultural contexts influence the presentation, interpretation, and reception of health information. Finally, integrating AI-driven content analysis tools into future research could enhance the objectivity, reproducibility, and scalability of video evaluations, enabling large-scale assessments that better capture the dynamic nature of short-video content. Implementing a unified credibility certification similar to the Good Housekeeping seal could help users, especially non-professional users with limited health literacy, identify reliable content quickly and avoid misinformation.

In this study, we evaluated the quality of health-related videos on long COVID across two major short-video platforms, Bilibili and TikTok, using the GQS and mDISCERN instruments. Significant variations in video quality and reliability were observed. Content produced by medical professionals consistently achieved higher scores across all evaluation tools, whereas 64% of long COVID-related videos from non-professional sources exhibited lower quality, often featuring oversimplified symptom descriptions and interventions lacking scientific support. Between platforms, Bilibili generally outperformed TikTok, particularly for professionally generated content; however, neither platform consistently ensured satisfactory scientific rigor. Given the increasing public reliance on short-video platforms for health information, healthcare professionals and institutions should prioritize producing accurate, evidence-based content. Simultaneously, platform operators should enhance monitoring and review mechanisms to reduce the dissemination of misleading or scientifically unsubstantiated information. Finally, this study provides insights regarding how platform features, content attributes, and user engagement interact, moving beyond descriptive analysis to offer preliminary explanations and strategies for platform management and public health communication.

One limitation of this study is that it did not measure the effectiveness of videos in driving behavioral changes. Future research can use follow-up surveys or experimental methods to determine if high-quality videos prompt positive actions, such as seeking medical care or undergoing recommended rehabilitation. Moreover, we relied solely on publicly available video data and did not include empirical user-level data, such as user motivation, satisfaction, or knowledge acquisition. The sample size of this study constitutes an additional limitation. Expanding the sample size and accounting for platform differences would also improve generalizability. Nevertheless, these constraints do not undermine the reliability of the current study’s findings.

Conclusions

The overall quality of long COVID–related short videos on Bilibili and TikTok remains suboptimal. Video source (professional vs. non-professional) is the strongest independent predictor of information quality and reliability, whereas user engagement and video duration show limited association with scientific accuracy. These findings highlight the need for strengthened platform oversight and greater involvement of medical professionals to improve the reliability of public health information on short-video platforms.
