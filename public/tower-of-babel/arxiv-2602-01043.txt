A Deflationary Account of Quantum Theory and its Implications for the Complex Numbers
Jacob A. Barandes

Abstract
Why does quantum theory need the complex numbers? With a view toward answering this question, this paper argues that the usual Hilbert-space formalism is a special case of the general method of Markovian embeddings. This paper then describes the ‘indivisible interpretation’ of quantum theory, according to which a quantum system can be regarded as an ‘indivisible’ stochastic process unfolding in an old-fashioned configuration space, with wave functions and other exotic Hilbert-space ingredients demoted from having an ontological status. The complex numbers end up being necessary to ensure that the Hilbert-space formalism is indeed a Markovian embedding.



1
Introduction

One of the most mysterious features of quantum theory is its apparent
reliance on the complex numbers, which are included explicitly in
the textbook theory’s Dirac-von Neumann axioms (Dirac 1930, von Neumann
1932). There are various
arguments in the research literature that aim to establish the necessity
of the complex numbers in quantum theory through various measurement
scenarios (Renou et al. 2021).
This paper takes a different and more foundational approach.

After reviewing the complex numbers in Section
2
,
this paper turns to an investigation of Markovian embeddings of non-Markovian
processes in Section
3
. Recall that
a process is called non-Markovian if its dynamical laws depend not
only on the system’s configuration at the present moment in time,
but also on past configurations. A Markovian embedding of a non-Markovian
process is a technique for changing mathematical representations so
that the process looks like it only depends on a suitably redefined
notion of a ‘state’ at just the present
time. Section
4
reviews
the Strocchi-Heslot formulation of a quantum system as a collection
of classical harmonic oscillators (Strocchi 1966, Heslot 1985),
and argues that the infinite size of the resulting Strocchi-Heslot
state space gives a good motivation for thinking that the Hilbert-space
formalism of quantum theory is actually the Markovian embedding of
extremely non-Markovian processes. Section
5

argues that these extremely non-Markovian processes should be understood
as ‘indivisible’ stochastic processes,
each of which represents a whole equivalence class of non-Markovian
processes that agree on a sparse set of dynamical laws consisting
of first-order conditional probabilities for certain pairs of times
(Barandes 2025a, 2025b, 2023).
This paper then reviews the stochastic-quantum correspondence, which
asserts an equivalence between indivisible stochastic processes and
quantum systems (Ibid.), showing along the way that the complex numbers
are needed to ensure that the Hilbert-space formalism indeed provides
a Markovian embedding. Section
6
concludes with
a summary and directions for future research.

The Complex Numbers

Roughly speaking, an algebra is a vector space, with its usual rules
for addition and multiplication by scalars as well as an additive
identity
0
, together with a multiplication rule that takes two
elements of the algebra and returns another element of the algebra.
A normed division algebra has a norm
|q|\geq 0
and includes
for every nonzero element
q\neq 0
a multiplicative reciprocal
1/q
,
so that division is defined for all nonzero divisors.

A key example is the set of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
, which form
a one-dimensional normed division algebra, with norm
|x|\equiv\sqrt{x^{2}}
.

As follows from the Cayley-Dickson construction (Dickson 1919)
together with the Hurwitz theorem (Hurwitz 1922),
there exists a unique two-dimensional normed division algebra, called
the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
. Elements of
\mathbb{C}
can be
identified with ordered pairs
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)
of real numbers,

a
and
b
, together with the following arithmetic rules:

•

Addition:
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c,d}}\right)\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a+c,b+d}}\right)
.

•

Multiplication by scalars:
c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(ca,cb}}\right)

for
c
any real number.

•

Multiplication:
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c,d}}\right)\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(ac-bd,ad+bc}}\right)
.

Notice that addition and multiplication by scalars are carried out
entry-wise, whereas multiplication of complex numbers with each other
takes a much less obvious form. To see why entry-wise multiplication
fails to give a division algebra, notice that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,0}}\right)

is not the additive identity
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,0}}\right)
, and yet, under entry-wise
multiplication, it lacks a multiplicative reciprocal, because then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,1}}\right)
is the multiplicative identity, and there are
no ordered pairs
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c,d}}\right)
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,0}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c,d}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,1}}\right)

under entry-wise multiplication.

The complex numbers also come with several other important operations:

•

Complex conjugation:
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)^{\ast}\equiv\overline{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)}\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,-b}}\right)
.

•

Norm or modulus:
|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)|\equiv\sqrt{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)}=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
.

•

Real part:
\mathrm{Re\,}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)\equiv a
.

•

Imaginary part:
\mathrm{Im\,}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)\equiv b
.

Any set of mathematical symbols that are in a one-to-one correspondence
with these ordered pairs and exhibit the same properties give a representation
of the complex numbers. To construct the most familiar representation,
denote:

``1"\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right),\quad``i"\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right).

(1)

Then:

z\equiv x+iy\equiv 1x+iy=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0}}\right)x+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)y=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right).

(2)

One has

i^{2}=-1,

(3)

so one can formally write down the mystical-looking formula

``i=\sqrt{-1}."

(4)

However, there is nothing particularly mysterious going on here—under
the hood, there are just ordered pairs of real numbers with a special
multiplication rule.

From the Taylor series for the exponential function
e^{x}
, applied
for
x\mapsto i\theta
, one sees that

\displaystyle e^{i\theta}

\displaystyle=1+i\theta+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i\theta}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i\theta}}\right)^{3}+\cdots

\displaystyle=1+i\theta-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}-i\frac{1}{6}\theta^{3}\pm\cdots

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}\pm\cdots}}\right)+i\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta-\frac{1}{6}\theta^{3}\pm\cdots}}\right).

Identifying the two quantities in parentheses with the respective
Taylor series for the cosine and sine functions, one ends up with
the Euler formula:

e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta.

(5)

As a special case, for
\theta=\pi
, this result leads to the famous
Euler identity,

e^{i\pi}+1=0,

(6)

which unites the fundamental constants
0
,
1
,
e
,
i
, and

\pi
together with the basic mathematical operations of addition

+
and equality
=
.

From trigonometry, for a right triangle with opening angle
\theta
,
adjacent side of length
x
, opposite side of length
y
, and hypotenuse
of length
r
, one has the basic relationships

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=r\cos\theta,

(7)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=r\sin\theta,

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}.

These formulas give the polar form of a complex number
z
:

z=x+iy=r\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta}}\right)=re^{i\theta}.

(8)

Here
r=|z|
is the norm or modulus of the complex number,
and
e^{i\theta}
is called its phase factor, or phase for short.
Phases have unit modulus:

|e^{i\theta}|=1.

(9)

Taking the modulus of a general complex number
z=re^{i\theta}

erases its phase information
e^{i\theta}
:

|z|=|re^{i\theta}|=r.

(10)

There exist other representations of the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
.
One interesting example is the ‘real’

2\times 2
representation (see, for example, Myrheim 1999),
in which one introduces the following pair of
2\times 2
matrices,
both of which have only real-valued entries:

\mathbf{1}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{I}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

These matrices satisfy

\mathbf{I}^{2}=-\mathbf{1}.

(12)

It follows that any linear combination of the two matrices
\mathbf{1}

and
\mathbf{I}
with real-valued coefficients
x
and
y
defines
a
2\times 2
matrix of the specific form

\mathbf{Z}\equiv\mathbf{1}x+\mathbf{I}y=\begin{pmatrix}x&-y\\
y&x\end{pmatrix}.

(13)

One can check that matrices of this form have precisely the same
arithmetic behavior as complex numbers, so they indeed provide an
alternative mathematical representation of
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover,
taking the transpose of
\mathbf{Z}
precisely implements complex conjugation,

\mathbf{Z}^{\mathrm{T}}=\mathbf{1}x-\mathbf{I}y,

(14)

taking the trace gives twice the real part,

\mathrm{tr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{Z}}}\right)=2x,

(15)

and taking the determinant yields the modulus-squared,

\det\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{Z}}}\right)=x^{2}+y^{2}.

(16)

Additionally, recall that a
2\times 2
matrix that implements rotations
by an angle
\theta
is given by

\operatorname{Rot}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta}}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},

(17)

and observe that one can decompose the right-hand side as

\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\cos\theta+\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\sin\theta.

That is,

\operatorname{Rot}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta}}\right)=\mathbf{1}\cos\theta+\mathbf{I}\sin\theta=e^{\mathbf{I}\theta},

(18)

where the exponential of a matrix is formally defined by its Taylor
expansion. This result is just the ‘real’

2\times 2
representation of the Euler formula (
5
).

The 2 × 2 matrix
\mathbf{I}
here is called a linear complex
structure. Notice that it does not involve any appearances of a ‘literal’

i
, but only real numbers. However, the complex numbers, as an algebraic
structure, are still present. The complex numbers are fundamentally
an algebraic structure, so any algebraic structure isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}

is really just
\mathbb{C}
itself.
1
1

1

By contrast, the authors of Renou et al. (2021)
argue that replacing
i
with the
2\times 2
matrix
\mathbf{I}
qualifies
as removing the complex numbers from quantum theory and results in
a truly real theory. This argument is not correct—their
theory still involves the complex numbers, because their theory still
involves factors of
\mathbf{I}
, with all its usual algebraic properties.
Moreover, when replacing factors of
i
with
\mathbf{I}
, it follows
from straightforward mathematical identities that one must give up
certain assumptions about tensor-factorizability in order to obtain
an equivalent formulation of standard quantum theory. The authors
of Renou et al. (2021) decide not to give up those assumptions, and,
rather unsurprisingly, they ultimately find that their still-complex
theory disagrees with some of the predictions of standard quantum
theory. It is not clear how significant it is to demonstrate the inequivalence
between standard quantum theory and an arbitrarily defined new theory
that still involves the complex numbers.

Returning to a more abstract representation of complex numbers as
expressions of the form
z=x+iy
, it will turn out to be useful to
introduce an operator
K
that implements complex conjugation:

Kz\equiv\overline{z}K.

(19)

By construction,
K
anticommutes with
i
:

Ki=-iK.

(20)

It follows that the symbols
i
,
K
, and
iK
generate a Clifford
algebra known as the pseudo-quaternions (Stueckelberg 1960):

-i^{2}=K^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(iK}}\right)^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(iK}}\right)=1.

(21)

In the ‘real’
2\times 2
matrix representation
(
13
),
K
is represented by
a
2\times 2
matrix with real-valued entries that happens to coincide
with the first Pauli sigma matrix
\sigma_{x}
:

\mathbf{K}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=\sigma_{x}.

(22)

3
Markovian Embeddings

A Markov process is a dynamical system for which the laws that pick
out future configurations, either deterministically or probabilistically,
depend only on the system’s present configuration, and not on prior
configurations in the past. It will be fruitful to examine the ways
in which a non-Markovian system can be made to look Markovian by a
suitable change of mathematical representation.

As a simple first example, consider a
discrete
system, whose
kinematics consists of configurations

x=1,2,3,\dots,N

(23)

that make up a configuration space
\mathcal{C}
. At the level
of dynamics, suppose that the system has a second-order deterministic
law

x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t+1}}\right)=F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t-1}}\right)}}\right),

(24)

where
F
is some given function with two arguments. This dynamical
law is said to be non-Markovian of second order because picking out
the system’s configuration at a later time
t+1
requires specifying
the system’s configuration not merely at the present time
t
, but
also at the previous time
t-1
. This model as a whole can be understood
as a discrete version of a simple system of one degree of freedom
in Newtonian mechanics.

One can then always make the following nomology-ontology trade-off:
simplify the model’s dynamical law, or nomology, to be first-order,
or Markovian, at the cost of complicating the model’s configuration
space, meaning its ontology. According to the new kinematics, the
‘states’ are now ordered pairs
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)

making up a ‘state space’
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
,
and the new dynamics is given by the
pair
of
first-order

equations

\displaystyle x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right),

(25)

\displaystyle y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right).

One therefore ends up with a Markovian embedding of the originally
second-order, non-Markovian model.

This Markovian embedding also features an emergent set of transformations
that are allowed to mix up the variables
x
and
y
for convenience
as long as the transformations do not entail losing any information.
For example, one can define
X\equiv x+y
and
Y\equiv x-y
, which
would then satisfy the equations

\displaystyle X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)+Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}{2},\frac{X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)-Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}{2}}}\right)+\frac{X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)+Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}{2},

(26)

\displaystyle Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)+Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}{2},\frac{X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)-Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}{2}}}\right)-\frac{X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)+Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}{2}.

After solving these alternative first-order equations, one could
then immediately write down the solutions to the original equations
as
x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)+Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right)/2

and
y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)-Y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right)/2
.

As a second example, consider a
continuous
system whose kinematics
consists of configurations
x
making up a configuration space
\mathcal{C}
,
with dynamics given by a second-order deterministic law

\ddot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),\dot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right),

(27)

using Newton’s fluxion notation in which dots denote time derivatives.
Again, one can make a nomology-ontology trade-off, with the new kinematics
consisting of ‘states’
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)

making up a ‘state space’
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
,
and the new dynamics consisting of a Markovian embedding given by
the pair of first-order equations

\displaystyle\dot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle=y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),

(28)

\displaystyle\dot{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle=F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right).

As before, this Markovian embedding features an emergent set of transformations
that can freely mix up
x
and
y
as long as they do not lose track
of any information. This example is a template for the Hamiltonian
formulation of Newtonian mechanics, with
x
playing the role of
a canonical coordinate,
y
playing the role of a canonical momentum,
and the set of transformations mixing up
x
and
y
containing
the set of canonical transformations.

For
t_{0}
any choice of origin for the time coordinate, one can
define a time-reversal transformation by the replacement

t_{0}+t\mapsto t_{0}-t,

(29)

so that translations forward in time from
t_{0}
become translations
backward in time from
t_{0}
, and vice versa. For simplicity, the
origin for the time coordinate will be taken to be
t_{0}=0
. Then
it follows from the chain rule that for the original second-order
formulation of the continuous model, time-reversal transformations
act on the degree of freedom
x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
and its time derivatives
according to the rules

\displaystyle x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto+x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right),

\displaystyle\dot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto-\dot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right),

(30)

\displaystyle\ddot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto+\ddot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right).

The original second-order law (
27
)
is therefore time-reversal invariant precisely if

F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,-\dot{x}}}\right)=F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,\dot{x}}}\right).

(31)

For the equivalent Markovian embedding, a time-reversal transformation
must instead be required to take the form

\displaystyle x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto+x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right),

\displaystyle\dot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto-\dot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right),

(32)

\displaystyle y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto-y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right),

\displaystyle\dot{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto+\dot{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right),

where
y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
and
\dot{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
transform with
opposite signs as compared with
x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
and
\dot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
.
The first-order Markovian laws then transform as

\displaystyle\dot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto-\dot{x}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=-y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),

\displaystyle\dot{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\dot{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),-y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right).

(33)

The dynamical laws are time-reversal-invariant if

F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,-y}}\right)=F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right).

(34)

Many Markovian embeddings are naturally expressible using complex
numbers. For the present example, one can define

z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\equiv x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)+iy\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

(35)

and

\mathcal{F}\big(z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),z^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\big)\equiv y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)+iF\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),y\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right).

(36)

Then one ends up with a
single
first-order Markovian law:

\dot{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\mathcal{F}\big(z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),z^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\big).

(37)

So one has made the dynamical law look even simpler, at the cost
of making the ontology murkier and introducing the purportedly exotic
complex numbers into the story.

This complex form of the dynamics makes it particularly easy to implement
time-reversal transformations:

\displaystyle z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto Kz\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right),

(38)

\displaystyle\dot{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto-K\dot{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right).

Notice, in particular, how the transformation law for
z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

entails
both
a change in its argument from
t
to
-t
,
together

with an application of the complex-conjugation operator
K
, as originally
introduced in (
19
). Meanwhile, the first-order
Markovian law (
37
)
transforms according to:

-K\dot{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right)=\mathcal{F}\big(z^{\ast}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right),z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right)\big).

(39)

The dynamical law is therefore time-reversal-invariant if

-K\mathcal{F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z^{\ast},z}}\right)=\mathcal{F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z,z^{\ast}}}\right).

(40)

This trick of embedding a non-Markovian model into a Markovian model
with a more complicated state space generalizes to systems with

•

arbitrarily many degrees of freedom,

•

non-Markovian dynamics of arbitrarily high order,

•

and stochastic dynamical laws.

The embedded-Markovian formulation may have a murky ontology, a large
class of strange-looking transformations of its state-space variables,
and exotic mathematical structures, such as the complex numbers. If
one were handed such an embedded-Markovian formulation at the outset,
one could easily imagine endless debates over its correct physical
interpretation.

4
The Strocchi-Heslot Formulation

A closed quantum system with state vector
|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle

evolving according to the
Schrödinger equation

i\frac{\partial|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle}{\partial t}=H|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle

(41)

(conventionally written with a partial time derivative
\partial/\partial t
)
is a Markov process with a murky ontology, features a large class
of strange (unitary change-of-basis) transformations, and involves
the complex numbers. Reversing the logic of the previous section,
one might then naturally ask if there is some non-Markovian system
with a more transparent ontology for which this Hilbert-space formulation
is merely its Markovian embedding. Remarkably, the answer turns out
to be in the affirmative. One can find hints in this direction from
works like that of Glick and Adami (2020)
that demonstrate clear instances of non-Markovian behavior for reasonably
straightforward,
closed
quantum systems. (For
open
systems,
non-Markovianity has long been known to be ubiquitous, in both the
classical and quantum cases, due to prosaic feedback effects from
the environment.) Another important hint comes from replacing the
Hilbert-space formulation with an alternative.

There exists an intriguing but little-known reformulation of quantum
theory as a
classical
Hamiltonian system of coupled harmonic
oscillators. This reformulation comes from two beautiful papers, the
first by Franco Strocchi (1966), and the
second by André Heslot (1985).
2
2

2

For the generalization to infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, see
the work of Ashtekar and Schilling (1999).

To start, consider a quantum system with an
N
-dimensional Hilbert
space
\mathcal{H}
, a state vector
|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle
, and
a Hamiltonian
H=H^{\dagger}
. Again, the Schrödinger equation reads

i\frac{\partial|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle}{\partial t}=H|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle.

Next, pick an arbitrary orthonormal basis—a choice to
be revisited shortly—so that the state vector
|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle

is represented by the column matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\\
\vdots\\
\Psi_{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\end{pmatrix},

(42)

and the Hamiltonian
H
is represented by the square matrix

\begin{pmatrix}H_{11}&H_{12}\\
H_{21}&\ddots\\
&&H_{NN}\end{pmatrix}.

(43)

Writing out the Schrödinger equation in terms of individual entries,
one has

i\frac{d\Psi_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}{dt}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}H_{ij}\Psi_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right).

(44)

Then decompose all the individual entries of
|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle

and
H
into their real and imaginary parts:

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)+ip_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right),

(45)

\displaystyle H_{ij}

\displaystyle=A_{ij}+iB_{ij},

implies the symmetry conditions

\displaystyle A_{ij}

(46)

\displaystyle B_{ij}

The Schrödinger equation then becomes

i\frac{d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)+ip_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right)}{dt}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{ij}+iB_{ij}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)+ip_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right).

(47)

Breaking up this equation into its real and imaginary parts, and
simplifying fully, one ends up with a system of
2N
classical Hamiltonian
equations of motion:

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial H_{\textrm{SH}}}{\partial p_{i}},

(48)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial H_{\textrm{SH}}}{\partial q_{i}}.

Here
H_{\textrm{SH}}
is the Strocchi-Heslot Hamiltonian, which
is just a classical Hamiltonian that ends up with a manifestly bilinear
form, due to the linearity of the Schrödinger equation:

H_{\textrm{SH}}\equiv\sum_{k,l}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2}A_{kl}p_{k}p_{l}+B_{kl}q_{k}p_{l}+\frac{1}{2}A_{kl}q_{k}q_{l}}}\right).

(49)

Schrödinger time evolution is therefore isomorphic, in the Strocchi-Heslot
formulation, to the dynamics of a system of
classical
coupled
harmonic oscillators. Changing orthonormal basis on the Hilbert-space
side is then equivalent to carrying out a linear canonical transformation
on the Strocchi-Heslot side.

Moreover, under the most straightforward notion of a time-reversal
transformation, one has
q_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\mapsto q_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right)

and
p_{i}\mapsto-p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right)
, with an extra minus sign
for
p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
, precisely as in (

).
Hence, when
q_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
and
p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
are

\Psi_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=(1/\sqrt{2})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)+ip_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right)

of the system’s state vector, one sees in analogy with the transformation

z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
in (

)
that
\Psi_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\Psi_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\mapsto K\Psi_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right).

(50)

That is,
\Psi_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
experiences
both
a reversal
of the time argument from
t
to
-t
,
together
with an application
of the complex-conjugation operator
K
. The Strocchi-Heslot formulation
therefore gives a nice way to see why state vectors obey this odd-looking
transformation rule.

4

4

If one were to allow for a linear canonical transformation as part

\Psi_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\Psi_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\mapsto VK\Psi_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t}}\right)
,
where

law is, in fact, the most general textbook rule for the time-reversal
of a state vector.

As a particularly interesting choice of orthonormal basis, picking
the energy eigenbasis corresponds to carrying out a linear canonical
transformation to the set of normal modes, for which the oscillators
are uncoupled and independent, with individual frequencies proportional
to the eigenvalues of the original quantum Hamiltonian
H
. Mathematically
speaking, this canonical frame is arguably the simplest description
of the evolution for any quantum system—after all, there
are few systems simpler than a set of uncoupled, classical harmonic
oscillators.

Some interpretations of quantum theory attempt to reify its usual
Markovian formulation. For such interpretations, it is not immediately
clear why the Hilbert-space formulation should be favored, metaphysically
speaking, over the Strocchi-Heslot formulation. If, however, the Strocchi-Heslot
formulation is reified, then is the implication that the universe
is just a collection of harmonic oscillators? In that case, why should
having zero amplitude—say, for a ‘branch’
or ‘world’ in some versions of Everettian
quantum theory—be taken to mean a lack of physical existence?
After all, a harmonic oscillator that is not oscillating still physically
exists. One therefore sees the dangers of trying to build an ontology
directly out of a purely mathematical structure, simply because mathematical
structures have many different-looking formulations, and what fixes
the formulation to be one over another?

Notice that for a quantum system with an
N
-dimensional Hilbert
space, for
N
finite, the corresponding Strocchi-Heslot formulation
features a continuously infinite state space of dimension
2N
. In
particular, the Strocchi-Heslot state space is continuously infinite
even for the simplest possible quantum system: a qubit, which has

N=2
. Hence, if the Strocchi-Heslot state space can be understood
as the state space of a Markovian embedding of some underlying non-Markovian
system with a discrete configuration space consisting of just
N

elements, then the dynamics of that underlying system is presumably
going to be extremely non-Markovian, far more so than second order.

5
Indivisible Stochastic Processes

An indivisible stochastic process is a simple generalization of a
non-Markovian stochastic process. The new axioms, which are considerably
simpler than the Dirac-von Neumann axioms and do not involve Hilbert
spaces or even the complex numbers, are a combination of fixed and
contingent ingredients. The fixed ingredients, which are the same
in every real-world instantiation or run of the given indivisible
model, are:

•

a kinematics consisting of a configuration space
\mathcal{C}
,
and

•

a dynamics consisting of transition maps
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow t_{0}}}\right):\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]

for pairs of times
t,t_{0}
taken from respective index sets
\mathcal{T}
,
called the target times, and
\mathcal{T}_{0}\subset\mathcal{T}
,
called the conditioning times, where each transition map
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow t_{0}}}\right)

encodes first-order conditional transition probabilities connecting

t_{0}
to
t
.

The contingent ingredient, which will generically differ between real-world
instantiations or runs of the model, is:

•

a standalone probability distribution
p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right):\mathcal{C}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]

for times
t
in the set of target times
\mathcal{T}
.

Labeling distinct configurations in
\mathcal{C}
with an index

i
or
j
, the standard rules of ordinary probability theory include
the usual law of total probability, which implies that there is a

linear
marginalization rule connecting standalone probabilities

p_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{0}}}\right)
at conditioning times
t_{0}
and standalone
probabilities
p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
at target times
t
:

p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\sum_{j}\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow t_{0}}}\right)p_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{0}}}\right).

(51)

Here the two-indexed quantities
\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow t_{0}}}\right)

define a transition matrix, which is then a (column) stochastic matrix,
meaning a matrix whose entries are non-negative real numbers and each
of whose columns sum to
1
.

Indivisibility is a simple and remarkably new idea. One can think
of it as ‘a failure of iterativeness.’
The conceptual foundations of indivisibility originated in the theory
of quantum channels (Wolf, Cirac 2008),
but were only first applied to ordinary probability theory and stochastic
processes within the last few years (Milz, Modi 2021).

In short, for an indivisible stochastic process, not all target times
are valid conditioning times. That is, even if the transition matrices

\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow t_{0}}}\right)
and
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t^{\prime}\leftarrow t_{0}}}\right)

exist for times
t>t^{\prime}>t_{0}
, where
t^{\prime}
is intermediate
between
t_{0}
and
t
, it may be the case that

\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow t_{0}}}\right)\neq\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow t^{\prime}}}\right)\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t^{\prime}\leftarrow t_{0}}}\right)

(52)

for all matrices
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow t^{\prime}}}\right)

that satisfy the conditions of being stochastic. The inequality (
52
)
is called indivisibility.

As emphasized, for instance, in work by Gillespie (1998, 2000),
a traditional non-Markovian stochastic process features an infinite
hierarchy or Kolmogorov tower of conditional probabilities of first
order, second order, third order, and higher orders:

\displaystyle p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j_{1},t_{1}}}\right),

(53)

\displaystyle p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j_{1},t_{1};j_{2},t_{2}}}\right),

\displaystyle p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j_{1},t_{1};j_{2},t_{2};j_{3},t_{3}}}\right).

From this Kolmogorov tower of conditional probabilities, together
with standalone probabilities
p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t}}\right)
, one can go on
to construct all possible two-way joint probabilities, three-way joint
probabilities, and so on.
5
5

5

For pedagogical treatments of the theory of stochastic processes,
see Rosenblatt (1962), Parzen (1962), Doob (1990), or Ross (1995).

An indivisible stochastic process represents an entire
equivalence
class
of non-Markovian stochastic processes of arbitrary order that
agree on the sparse set of first-order conditional probabilities encoded
in the transition maps
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow t_{0}}}\right)
.
Each such non-Markovian stochastic process in this equivalence class
is compatible with the laws of the given indivisible stochastic process,
and is called a non-Markovian realizer.
6
6

6

The author thanks Alexander Meehan for suggesting this terminology.

By contrast, a Markov process entails making the approximation that
all second- and higher-order conditional probabilities
p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j_{1},t_{1};j_{2},t_{2};\dots}}\right)

are fixed and numerically equal to first-order conditional probabilities,
conditioned on the time closest to the target time
t
.

To establish the equivalence between indivisible stochastic processes
and quantum systems, called the stochastic-quantum correspondence,
one needs to define the stochastic-to-quantum direction of the equivalence,
and then the quantum-to-stochastic direction.

For the stochastic-to-quantum direction, one starts by considering
any indivisible stochastic process with
N
configurations, and then
picking a conditioning time, taken to be
0
without loss of generality,
as well as a target time
t
. Then given the transition matrix
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)
,
one proceeds to ‘solve’ the non-negativity
condition
\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)\geq 0
by introducing
a non-unique ‘potential’ matrix
\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)

whose entries are allowed to be complex-valued, according to the entry-by-entry
equality

\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)=|\Theta_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)|^{2}.

(54)

From the fact that
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)
is a
stochastic matrix, so that its columns each sum to
1
, it follows
that the ‘potential’ matrix
\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)

satisfies the following sum rule:

\sum_{i}|\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)|^{2}=1.

(55)

If it happens that the transition matrix
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)

is unistochastic (Horn 1954; Thompson 1989; Nylen, Tam 1993),
then
\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)
can be assumed to be a unitary
matrix
U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)
, satisfying

U^{\dagger}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)=U^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right),

(56)

and called the system’s time-evolution operator.

Otherwise, one can define a set of
N\times N
matrices
K_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)
,
with
\beta=1,\dots,N
, that each take one column from
\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)

and are otherwise filled with
0
s. It is a straightforward exercise
to show that the matrices
K_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)

are Kraus operators, meaning that they satisfy the Kraus identity

\sum_{\beta}K_{\beta}^{\dagger}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)K_{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)=\mymathbb{1},

(57)

where
\mymathbb{1}
is the identity matrix, and also that the transition
matrix
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)
has the following
Kraus decomposition:

\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)=\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}|K_{\beta,ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)|^{2}.

(58)

It follows from an application of the Stinespring dilation theorem
(Stinespring 1955; Barandes 2025a, 2025b, 2023)
that by increasing
N
to
N^{\prime}
, which is bounded from above
by
N^{3}
, one can replace the
N\times N
matrix
\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)

with an
N^{\prime}\times N^{\prime}
unitary matrix
U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)

that can serve as the system’s time-evolution operator. This argument
gives a novel and compelling first-principles explanation of why unitary
time evolution plays such a special role in quantum theory.

Crucially, for
N>2
, an
N\times N
unistochastic matrix will not
generally be orthostochastic. An orthostochastic matrix is a unistochastic
matrix for which the underlying unitary matrix can be taken to be
real-valued and orthogonal. Hence, to exploit the stochastic-to-quantum
side of the correspondence, with unitary time evolution, it will be
necessary to introduce the complex numbers, or an algebraic construct
isomorphic to them. Of course, from the standpoint of the stochastic-quantum
correspondence, Hilbert spaces are mere mathematical fictions anyway,
so introducing the complex numbers to define the Hilbert-space picture
is a harmless move. The complex numbers also provide important benefits
and resources, like the spectral theorem, symmetry generators, Hamiltonians,
energy eigenvalues, stationary states, the Schrödinger equation, the
uncertainty principle, spinors, and more.

As it turns out, to implement time-reversal transformations, one again
needs to introduce the complex-conjugation operator
K
, as defined
in (
19
), which combines with
i
and

iK
to form the pseudo-quaternions, as in (
21
).
In a sense, then, the Hilbert spaces of quantum systems are actually
defined over the pseudo-quaternions, rather than just over the complex
numbers, although observables are typically constructed out of only
the complex numbers.

After encoding the initial probabilities
p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)
as
the diagonal entries of an initial density matrix
\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)

that is otherwise filled with
0
s, one can go on to define a time-evolving
density matrix
\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\equiv U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)U^{\dagger}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)
,
as well as the rest of the Hilbert-space picture, as detailed in other
work (Barandes 2025). If the density matrix
is rank-one, then there exists an
N\times 1
state vector or wave
function
|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle=U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)\rangle

that gives a simple outer-product factorization

\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle\langle\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)|,

(59)

where
\langle\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)|\equiv|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle^{\dagger}
.
Assuming that the unitary time evolution is sufficiently smooth as
a function of
t
, the wave function then satisfies the Schrödinger
equation (
41
),

i\frac{\partial|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle}{\partial t}=H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)|\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\rangle,

for a self-adjoint Hamiltonian
H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=H^{\dagger}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

defined in keeping with Stone’s theorem (Stone 1930)
in terms of the time-evolution operator
U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)

according to

H\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\equiv i\frac{\partial U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)}{\partial t}U^{\dagger}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right).

(60)

The unitarily-time-evolving Hilbert-space formalism therefore yields
a ‘divisible’ Markovian embedding of
the original indivisible stochastic process, with a first-order differential
equation for the time evolution. Moreover, the linearity of the Schrödinger
equation now has a clear explanation: as one can check, it ultimately
descends from the linearity of the law of total probability (
51
)
connecting standalone probability distributions via the transition
matrix.

From the perspective of this formulation, one sees that wave functions
and the Schrödinger equation are secondary pieces of derived mathematics,
and not the primary ontological furniture of quantum systems. In particular,
wave functions, like the luminiferous aether or Magritte’s famous
pipe (Magritte 1929) are not physical
or ontic. Wave functions are not even entirely epistemic, but encode
a blend of epistemic and nomological information, so they are not
well-classified according to the Harrigan-Spekkens ‘ontological
models’ framework (Harrigan, Spekkens 2010).

To establish the quantum-to-stochastic direction of the stochastic-quantum
correspondence, one begins with a unitarily evolving quantum system,
with an
N
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. After picking
a convenient orthonormal basis (which is
also
a step used to
define the path-integral formulation), one proceeds to define an indivisible
stochastic process via introducing a manifestly unistochastic transition
matrix
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)
according to the rule

\Gamma_{ij}\equiv|U_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)|^{2}.

(61)

One might initially be concerned that this formula erases phase information
from
U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)
, as in (
10
).
However, this phase information does not matter as long as one remembers
that all empirical results come from measurement processes, and one
is careful to include measuring devices and environments in models
of quantum systems. If, say, a measuring device is properly regarded
as a subsystem of the overall indivisible stochastic process, and
the chosen orthonormal basis for the overall system’s Hilbert space
properly captures the measuring device’s pointer variables, then,
by construction, the dynamics of the overarching indivisible stochastic
process will deliver the measuring device into its correct read-out
configurations with the correct measurement-outcome probabilities,
in accord with the Born rule. Keeping track of phase information in

U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t\leftarrow 0}}\right)
is only necessary if one wishes to

exclude
the measuring device from the system, for reasons of
mathematical simplicity and convenience, in keeping with the usual
Dirac-von Neumann axioms. A detailed treatment of the measurement
process within this framework is covered in other work (Barandes 2025).

One therefore arrives at a comprehensive stochastic-quantum correspondence,
according to which the Hilbert-space formalism is a kind of a Markovian
embedding, serving as a form of ‘analytical mechanics’
for highly non-Markovian stochastic systems, giving rise to an effective
first-order, ‘divisible’ differential
equation. It is important to note that this correspondence is many-to-one
in both directions, much as classical-mechanical systems based on
second-order differential equations have a many-to-one correspondence
with the first-order Hamiltonian phase-space formalism. Like any form
of analytical mechanics, the Hilbert-space formalism provides a powerful
set of mathematical tools for specifying microphysical laws in a systematic
manner, for studying dynamical symmetries, and for calculating predictions.

6
Conclusion

To summarize the arguments in this paper, one can regard a Hilbert-space
quantum system as a Markovian embedding for an indivisible stochastic
process, which represents an equivalence class of non-Markovian stochastic
processes of arbitrarily high order, each of which is called a non-Markovian
realizer. An indivisible stochastic process has a classical-like ontology
and is described using ordinary probability theory, with no fundamental
appearance of the complex numbers. The complex numbers are only necessary
as part of reformulating an indivisible stochastic process in the
Hilbert-space picture with unitary time evolution.

The claim that every quantum system is really an indivisible stochastic
process in disguise leads to a new interpretation of quantum theory,
naturally called the ‘indivisible interpretation,’
or ‘indivisible quantum theory.’ Indivisible
quantum theory is based on simpler axioms than textbook quantum theory,
does away with treating superposition as a literal fact of physical
objects being in multiple configurations at once, arguably does not
suffer from a measurement problem, demotes wave functions from having
a directly physical or ontological status, and deflates a lot of the
exotic talk about quantum phenomena more generally. In a sense, indivisible
quantum theory is a ‘hidden-variables theory,’
with configurations playing the role of the hidden variables, although,
obviously, these configurations are the things actually seen in experiments,
and they
replace
rather than
augment
wave functions
as the ingredients of the ontology. Finally, as argued elsewhere (Barandes 2024),
one can introduce a plausible microphysical theory of causal influences
according to which indivisible quantum theory can be regarded as a
causally local theory.

There are many prospects for future research directions, including
applications to dynamical systems and real-world stochastic models,
the development of new algorithms for quantum systems, new ways of
thinking about quantum causal models, implications for old problems
in statistical mechanics, and ramifications for so-called algebra-first
formulations of quantum theory. Particularly exciting are potentially
novel, self-consistent ways of generalizing quantum theory that start
from the axioms of indivisible stochastic processes, which are grounded
in ordinary probability theory, as well as possible applications to
quantum gravity.
