A needs assessment survey for improving library support for chiropractic faculty research
Authors: Jennifer A Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to investigate the services and resources health sciences libraries and librarians can provide to support chiropractic faculty in conducting research and increase institutional research capacity.MethodsA one-time, anonymous, online survey was administered to a convenience sample of 120 faculty at a North American chiropractic college in December 2024. The 11-item questionnaire was adapted from prior research in academic health sciences libraries and included both quantitative and qualitative questions about respondents’ research interests, experience, needs and library use. Descriptive data analysis was completed.ResultsThe survey was completed by 39 faculty (response rate = 32.5%). Seventy-four percent (n = 29) of respondents had sought research assistance from the library. The areas of greatest interest in library research support were literature searching (56%, n = 20), citation management (53%, n = 19), data analysis (53%, n = 19), research design (39%, n = 14), and journal selection (39%, n = 14). Fifty-six percent (n = 22) of respondents were interested in library research workshops, particularly asynchronous video training. Respondents reported the following research interests: educational research (62%, n = 24), case reports (46%, n = 18), clinical research (44%, n = 17), evidence synthesis (38%, n = 15), historical research (23%, n = 9), and other (5%, n = 2).ConclusionMost respondents had sought research assistance from the library in the past, and more than half were interested in future research training from the library which suggests that the library supports research capacity building within the organization.Keywords: Library Services, Chiropractic, Education, Research, Faculty

INTRODUCTION

Many studies have demonstrated the need for formal research training for chiropractic faculty in order to build research capacity.1–6 The Council on Chiropractic Education addresses the role Doctor of Chiropractic programs must play, stating “Faculty members are provided opportunities for professional development to improve their content expertise and competence, their instructional skills, and their capabilities in research and scholarship.”7 Proposed solutions include training opportunities, mentoring, and academies of educators.1,3,8

Academic libraries support the teaching, learning, and scholarship needs of institutions of higher education through the information resources and services they provide.9 Traditionally, academic libraries were known for their physical spaces and the collections they housed. As electronic resources became a prominent part of library collections, new service opportunities arose separate from traditional reference assistance.10 Where once there was the ubiquitous reference desk, staffed by librarians answering library user questions with a collection of print resources close at hand, now many libraries have eliminated, renamed or combined the reference desk with other service points. Today, reference librarians frequently handle information requests virtually through email and online chat services which may include the use of generative AI tools and are frequently called upon to provide technology assistance to library users.11

Academic libraries have a history of providing research support services, with rapid growth in scope over the last 2 decades.12,13 As technological advancements have impacted how research is conducted, libraries have taken on larger roles in scholarly communications, open access, institutional repositories, bibliometrics, and systematic reviews, to name just a few new service opportunities.13–18 Health sciences librarians also have a record of collaborating with faculty as co-authors on published research.19–22 The literature indicates that academic libraries are positively situated to provide valuable support throughout the research life cycle but due to the complex nature of university organizations, faculty may be unaware of the wide variety of services libraries provide.23,24

The purpose of this study was to determine the services and resources that health sciences libraries and librarians can provide to support chiropractic faculty in conducting research and enhancing their research competencies.

METHODS

Study Design

A voluntary, one-time, anonymous, online cross-sectional survey was distributed electronically in December 2024 to a convenience sample of all full-time and part-time faculty (n = 120) at a North American chiropractic college. The 11-item questionnaire was adapted from prior research in academic health sciences libraries.25,26 Questions were pretested by 5 librarians. All survey items were optional and included both quantitative and qualitative questions about respondents’ research interests, experience and library use (Supplementary File; available online accompanying this article). No personally identifiable information was collected. There was no financial compensation for completing the survey. Reporting follows the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting Survey Studies (CROSS) guidelines.

Participants were recruited by an invitation to the study via the faculty email distribution list on December 2, 2024, and it included a link to the survey hosted by SurveyMonkey (Symphony Technology Group). The online survey was designed to prevent participants from submitting multiple responses. Weekly email reminders were sent, and the survey was closed on December 20, 2024.

Ethics

This study was deemed exempt by the Palmer College of Chiropractic Institutional Review Board, IRB Assurance # 2024-008 on November 21, 2024.

RESULTS

There were a total of 39 responses to the survey, representing a 32.5% response rate. Skipped items ranged from 0–7 respondents. Statistics were summarized using SurveyMonkey, including the number and percentages of responses.

Participant Research History

Table 1 presents information from respondents regarding their years of academic research experience, number of published peer-reviewed articles, and number of conference presentations to create an understanding of research accomplishments across the participant population. Thirty-three percent (n = 13) reported 0–5 years of active involvement in academic research. Over half of respondents (n = 20) reported having published 1–5 peer-reviewed articles and the same number (n = 20) had presented 1–5 conference platform or poster presentations.

Participant Research Interests and Needs

The topic of greatest research interest was conducting educational research (62%, n = 24), followed by case reports (46%, n = 18), clinical research (44%, n = 17), evidence synthesis (38%, n = 15), historical research (23%, n = 9), and other (5%, n = 2). The 2 write-in responses for “other” research interests were “observational research” and “health services research.”

Respondents reported their perceived need for training from the library and/or other campus partners for a variety of research related tasks and activities (Fig. 1). More than half of respondents reported that they would benefit from training in literature searching (56%, n = 20) and citation management and data analysis (both 53%, n = 19). Other topics of high interest included research design and assistance selecting journals for publication (both 39%, n = 14) and writing (36%, n = 13).

Research training needs by years of academic research experience.

Respondents were asked who they sought research assistance from, on and off campus. Eighty-two percent (n = 32) sought help from colleagues/mentors in their department. Seventy-four percent (n = 29) had previously sought assistance from the library. Sixty-nine percent (n = 27) had received guidance from colleagues/mentors outside their department and 36% (n = 14) had contacted colleagues/mentors at other institutions for research support. Zero respondents reported not seeking help.

Specific to the library, in-person and email tied as the most common methods of contact for support—both 58% (n = 21), followed by using a resource on the library website (44%, n = 16), phone contact and “did not seek help” (both 17%, n = 6) and used the library’s online chat service (6%, n = 2).

More than half of respondents (56%, n = 22) reported interest in attending library workshops for research upskilling. Training format preference was greatest for asynchronous videos (63%, n = 20), followed by in-person (47%, n = 15), synchronous webinar (34%, n = 11), and one-on-one training (19%, n = 6).

DISCUSSION

This is the first study to investigate the needs of chiropractic faculty for research support and training from academic health sciences libraries and librarians. Survey questions on training needs were grouped according to Ragon’s27 research lifecycle model: planning, conducting, disseminating and assessing research impact (Fig. 2). Respondents expressed interest in training in all phases of the research lifecycle, with the greatest interest in training activities that are categorized within the planning stage, in particular literature searching, citation management, and research design. Given that research methods training is minimal within the Doctor of Chiropractic education program, it may not be surprising that these foundational skills are priorities for faculty undertaking scholarship.3,28,29

Research training topics arranged by Ragon’s research lifecycle model.

All respondents to this survey reported seeking research support from colleagues, mentors, or institutional offices, highlighting a general need for research support and the benefit of the availability of such at this institution. Nearly three-quarters of survey respondents reported contacting library staff or using library services or resources previously for their research needs which aligns with findings recognizing academic libraries and librarians for their contributions to institutional research output and their support throughout all stages of the research process.30 Health sciences librarians in chiropractic educational institutions have demonstrated experience teaching information literacy and research literacy courses indicating they are good candidates for providing faculty research assistance.20,31

Looking ahead, next steps include outreach to the 60% of faculty who did not participate in this study to understand their research support needs. This might be achieved by presentations at departmental meetings, new faculty onboarding, and marketing via faculty email distribution lists as the literature shows that faculty are unfamiliar with the scope of research support offered by librarians.32

These results can be used at this institution to promote existing materials and services and create additional training resources like online library guides, videos, workshops and one-on-one instruction and may be applicable to other chiropractic libraries and institutions. Because developing these materials is time-intensive and most chiropractic libraries have small staff, this might be an opportunity for collaboration between the library and other campus departments or among chiropractic librarians at different institutions.33

Future Studies

Since the development of this survey there has been an explosion in artificial intelligence (AI) technologies supporting a wide range of research tasks across the research lifecycle.34,35 Future studies exploring chiropractic faculty needs regarding AI-based research tools are warranted.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. Nonresponse bias may be a factor and these results might reflect the opinions of faculty with an existing interest in research and/or libraries.36 Since survey questions were based on studies conducted by librarians, they may reflect the priorities and interests of librarians and miss some faculty needs. Because the findings are from just 1 chiropractic higher education institution there is limited generalizability.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the self-reported research support needs of faculty at 1 chiropractic college. Most respondents had previously sought research assistance from the library and over half were interested in future research training from the library. More than half of respondents expressed interest in support from librarians for literature searching, citation management and data analysis. This suggests that the library has a role to play in research capacity building within the organization.
