Area-adjusted influence of built-up pattern on urban land surface temperature in an arid landscape
Authors: Ali Asgarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban heat intensification threatens environmental quality and human health, particularly in arid urban areas where cooling resources are scarce. While impervious surface area is a well-established driver of urban land surface temperature (LST), the influence of built-up spatial patterns beyond their total area remains poorly understood. This study aimed to disentangle the effect of built-up pattern after accounting for built-up area on LST using a dual-scale analysis in an arid urban–barren mosaic. The spatial pattern of Sentinel-2 10 m–derived built-up areas, measured as Area, Mean Patch Size (MPS), Shape Index (SHI), Nearest Neighbor Distance (ENN), and Number of Patches (NP), was extracted within each 100 m Landsat thermal pixel, creating a dataset of 8,130 observations. Landsat-derived summer LST served as the dependent variable in a set of flexible regression models designed to evaluate both the total extent of built-up land and the spatial structure of urban patches. Results showed that adding configuration metrics to Area-only models improves LST prediction accuracy (R² = 0.589 to 0.693) and reduces RMSE (2.792 °C to 2.409 °C). Additional models examined how different spatial metrics interact, allowing the influence of built-up patterns to be evaluated independently of total built-up area. Unsupervised clustering (average silhouette width = 0.295) revealed that, when controlling for area, Compact configurations, low NP with high MPS, SHI, and ENN, were on average 2.7 °C warmer than Fragmented or Balanced types. These findings demonstrate that, after adjusting for built-up extent, built-up pattern exerts a measurable and spatially distinct influence on LST, underscoring its importance for climate-responsive urban design in arid regions.Keywords: Spatial configuration, Built-up pattern, Non-linear regression, Urban heat island, Arid landscapeSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Urban heat accumulation is a prominent and growing environmental concern, especially in arid and semi-arid regions where limited vegetation and persistent solar radiation reduce the landscape’s ability to self-regulate thermal conditions1. The rise in urban temperatures is closely tied to the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect2, which reflects the air temperature differential between built-up and surrounding rural areas. Central to understanding the UHI phenomenon is the analysis of Land Surface Temperature (LST): a physical variable that quantifies the radiative temperature of the Earth’s surface and serves as a proxy for surface heat retention3. Due to its spatial and temporal sensitivity, satellite-derived LST, particularly from sensors like Landsat, has proven essential for detecting thermal patterns and their spatial variation within urban areas4, allowing researchers to capture fine-scale variability in urban heat profiles.

Land surface temperature has traditionally been explained by landscape composition (e.g., proportion of built-up land), but a growing body of evidence indicates that landscape configuration (the spatial arrangement, shape, and connectivity of land cover) also modulates LST5,6. Large, contiguous built-up patches exhibit higher LST than fragmented patterns of equivalent area6, and integrating configurational metrics improves LST prediction7,8. LST also exhibits pronounced scale-dependency: composition dominates at broader regional scales, while configuration becomes more influential at finer scales9,10. Beyond classification, diverse modeling approaches have recently been applied to interpret urban heat relationships such as machine learning combined with partial dependence analysis11, multi-year frameworks incorporating urban hot spots12, and pixel-level cooling exposure metrics integrated with spatial association analysis11. In arid environments, where vegetation is sparse, the structural attributes of the built environment primarily govern thermal behavior, and spatial configuration exerts a disproportionately strong influence on LST10,13, underscoring the need for context-specific analytical frameworks.

Despite these advances, several research gaps remain. Few studies have clearly isolated the influence of configuration from that of composition, making it difficult to evaluate the independent role of spatial structure in driving thermal variation. Traditional regression-based models may miss non-linear interactions among variables, and limited research has employed dual-scale or pixel-wise approaches to bridge the mismatch between high-resolution land cover and coarser thermal data, particularly in drylands. This study directly addresses these gaps by investigating the influence of built-up spatial configuration on LST in an arid urban environment using a dual-scale modeling framework. The key innovation lies in integrating built-up patch metrics derived from Sentinel-2 imagery into the coarser 100 m Landsat LST grid, effectively isolating each Sentinel-2 configuration within the corresponding Landsat pixels. This approach generates a robust dataset for spatial analysis while preserving the integrity of thermal measurements. This study tests the following two hypotheses:

Materials and methods

Study area description

This investigation was conducted within an urbanized landscape situated southwest of Isfahan City, on Iran’s central arid plateau, encompassing coordinates from 51°20′ to 51°32′ E longitude and 32°29′ to 32°40′ N latitude, with a total expanse of 271.25 km² (Fig. 1). The study area comprises 40 distinct urban settlements, each with an average size of 105.45 ± 153.88 ha. Predominantly characterized by low-rise structures, typically fewer than two stories, these settlements exhibit a marked scarcity of green infrastructure, such as parks or tree-lined avenues. Instead, a heterogeneous mosaic of built-up areas interspersed with barren open spaces defines the landscape. The region, subjected to an arid climate with a long-term mean annual precipitation of less than 150 mm, has consistently faced water deficit conditions14. Between 1972 and 2010, a notable rise in mean annual temperature over impervious surfaces was recorded, increasing from 14.2 °C to over 18 °C15.Consequently, approximately all agricultural lands are left fallow during summer months due to insufficient water availability, with the Zayandehrood River often desiccating completely during this period5. The increasing abandonment of croplands, as documented by Lotfi, Nadoushan16, results in a summer landscape characterized as a mixed barren–urban mosaic, a configuration likely exacerbating local land surface temperature patterns.

Data sources

This study employed remote sensing datasets derived primarily from Landsat 8 and Landsat 9 thermal imagery to estimate LST. Specifically, Band 10 from both satellites was used, as it is optimized for land surface temperature analysis due to its thermal infrared sensitivity and minimal atmospheric interference17. The raw data were accessed through Google Earth Engine (GEE) using the image collections LANDSAT/LC08/C02/T1 and LANDSAT/LC09/C02/T1. A total of 22 cloud-free (less than 1% cloud cover) images from Path 164 and Row 37, acquired between June 1 and September 30, 2024, were selected. This period represents the hot, dry season, when vegetation is sparse or absent and rainfall is negligible. The dataset included 9 Landsat 8 and 13 Landsat 9 images. Since Band 10 in the Landsat Collection 2 products has a native spatial resolution of 100 m, it was used directly without further downscaling. To generate a consistent representation of thermal conditions, a mean filter was applied to combine all cloud-free scenes into a composite image, reducing anomalies and capturing the average LST across the summer months.

Built-up areas were extracted using Sentinel-2 Level-2 A optical imagery from the same dry-season period, accessed through GEE using the COPERNICUS/S2_SR collection. Only bands with a 10-m resolution (B2, B3, B4, and B8) were used in the classification process to ensure high spatial accuracy. As each 100-m thermal pixel corresponds to 100 Sentinel-2 pixels, this enabled detailed identification of built-up features at sub-pixel scale relative to the thermal data. To isolate true barren-built-up areas, asphalted roads (known to have distinct thermal characteristics18 were masked using a vector dataset of the regional road network. The study area lies within a flat alluvial plain, and due to the absence of significant elevation variation, no topographic correction was applied, as topography was unlikely to influence the observed LST patterns.

LST retrieval and built-up area extraction

LST was derived using the standard radiative transfer approach19 that incorporates physical constants and sensor metadata. This approach together with the NDVI-based emissivity correction20 represent the current best-available approach for this region, where field LST data are extremely scarce. This approach has been extensively validated in studies performed in the region5,6,21 and showed reliable surface temperature estimates when applied to Landsat thermal imagery under clear-sky conditions. Initially, the digital numbers (DN) from the thermal band were converted to top-of-atmosphere radiance using the radiometric rescaling coefficients provided in the image metadata. This radiance was then transformed into brightness temperature (BT) in Kelvin using the inverse Planck function, which includes the K1 and K2 thermal calibration constants. To retrieve actual LST values, the brightness temperature was corrected for surface emissivity based on land cover types. The correction employed Planck’s constant, Boltzmann constant, and the central wavelength of the thermal band to adjust the temperature, accounting for emissivity variability among different surfaces. The NDVI-based threshold method20 was used to estimate emissivity corrections, ensuring accurate adjustments for different surface types.

For the extraction of built-up areas, supervised classification using the maximum likelihood algorithm22 was applied to Sentinel-2 imagery. Representative training samples were manually collected for three primary land cover types: built-up, vegetation, and bare land. The classification process was further refined through a post-classification correction step, which involved manual inspection and logical filtering to rectify misclassifications, particularly in areas with complex reflectance patterns, mostly including bright surfaces which are common in arid regions. Moreover, asphalt roads were masked using an auxiliary vector dataset to reduce confusion with urban structures. Classification accuracy was evaluated using an independent set of visually interpreted reference samples derived from Google Earth Pro high-resolution (0.5 m) imagery (2000 points) and field checks (500 points). The resulting confusion matrix produced an overall Kappa coefficient of 96.67%, indicating a very high level of agreement between the classified map and the validation samples.

Statistical modeling framework

Variable definition and data filtering process

To conduct spatial statistical modeling, the 100-m LST raster was converted to a grid of vector polygons, where each pixel represented an individual spatial analysis unit or “net.” In order to ensure that the effects of built-up structures on LST were isolated, a filtering process was applied. First, all grids that either entirely lacked built-up areas or contained only portions of a patch were excluded. Next, to minimize the confounding influence of cooling vegetation, although limited in the study area, any grid cell containing vegetation or located adjacent to vegetation patches was also removed. For this purpose, the Landsat-derived NDVI produced during emissivity correction (Sect. 3.2) was used to identify traces of vegetation as a potential confounding factor. Pixels with NDVI values greater than 0.05 were excluded. Furthermore, pixels representing known low-albedo but non-urban features, such as athletic fields or other dark-colored surfaces, were visually identified and removed. For these exclusions, a 3 × 3 majority filter was applied to extend the exclusion to adjacent pixels and prevent spurious LST values caused by external influences unrelated to built-up morphology. After all filters were applied, a final dataset of 8,130 spatial units remained, each consisting of a relatively isolated and complete built-up patch. This refined set of analysis units represents a high-quality and focused dataset suitable for investigating thermal behavior in relation to urban form.

For each of the remaining spatial units, five built-up patch metrics were calculated to serve as independent variables: patch area (Area), the total size of built-up land within a grid; mean patch size (MPS), the average size of individual built-up patches; shape index (SHI), a measure of patch shape complexity relative to a standard shape; Euclidean nearest-neighbor distance (ENN), the shortest straight-line distance between the centroids of adjacent patches; and number of patches (NP), the count of discrete built-up patches within the grid (see detail definitions and computations in McGarigal23. All metrics were computed using Fragstats v 4.2.1 and were designed to reflect both the internal structure and spatial configuration of built-up areas. The mean LST within each grid, derived from the filtered LST layer, served as the dependent variable in all models.

Modeling strategy: simple, global, and interaction GAMs

To evaluate the statistical relationship between built-up spatial metrics and land surface temperature (LST), three types of generalized additive models (GAMs) were constructed, each addressing a specific analytical objective. The Simple model aimed to test the individual explanatory power of each built-up metric in a univariate setting, serving as a baseline for comparison. Residuals from this model were specifically collected for further analysis, particularly to examine the spatial distribution of unexplained variance and inform subsequent modeling strategies. The Global model assessed the joint contribution of all five spatial metrics simultaneously, identifying which variables retained predictive significance when potential inter-variable competition was accounted for. Improvement in model performance relative to the Simple model was interpreted as an indication of the added value of considering multiple spatial metrics together, thereby reflecting the increasing contribution of built-up structure in improving the prediction of LST. The Interaction model was constructed to evaluate whether the effect of one spatial metric on LST was influenced by the presence or magnitude of another, thereby capturing conditional or synergistic relationships. This approach enabled the assessment of pairwise interactions between spatial metrics, such as how the influence of patch size might vary depending on patch density or spatial proximity, offering deeper insight into the combined roles of urban form characteristics in modulating surface temperature.

GAMs were selected due to their ability to model nonlinear relationships between the predictor variables and LST without requiring a strict parametric form. In our data, all smooth terms subsequently showed effective degrees of freedom greater than 1 (edf > 1; see Table 3), providing direct empirical evidence that the relationships between landscape metrics and LST are nonlinear. Linear regression assumes edf = 1 for each predictor, which would misrepresent the curvature of these relationships. Each smooth term was specified with a basis dimension (k = 10) after confirming that the effective degrees of freedom did not approach the upper limit, indicating adequate flexibility. Thin plate regression splines were used as the smoothing basis. Model fitting employed the gam() function from the mgcv package in R24 with the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method for automatic smoothing parameter selection. The convergence criterion for the REML score was set to the default value of 1e-6. No cross-validation or additional tuning was performed, as the sample size (n = 8,130) was sufficiently large to rely on REML-based inference. Model performance was compared using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), adjusted R², and root mean square error (RMSE). For the interaction models, tensor product smooths (te())25 were used to model pairwise interactions between spatial metrics. All models were checked for concurvity and residual independence prior to interpretation.

Spatial clustering of built-up patterns and residual comparison

To explore whether distinct configurations of built-up patterns were associated with characteristic thermal behaviors, a K-means clustering analysis was applied to the standardized values of the five spatial metrics. All metrics were normalized to zero mean and unit variance prior to clustering to ensure comparability and prevent scale-driven bias.

Clustering was performed using the kmeans() function in R (base package) with the following fixed parameters: 25 random starts (nstart = 25), a maximum of 1000 iterations (iter.max = 1000), and a random seed (set.seed(123)) to ensure reproducibility. Silhouette widths were computed using the silhouette() function from the cluster package26, and WSS values were derived using the factoextra package27. The optimal number of clusters (k = 3) was selected based on inspection of the within-cluster sum of squares (WSS) across increasing values of k, using the elbow method. A marked decrease in WSS from k = 2 to k = 3 was considered an appropriate trade-off between model complexity and cohesion. Moreover, silhouette width values28 were computed to assess the internal validity of the clusters and ensure sufficient separation among them. Following clustering, each observation was assigned to one of three cluster types: Fragmented, Compact, and Balanced, based on their centroid characteristics in the standardized spatial metric space.

Cluster centroids were later used to interpret the structural identity of each group, reflecting differences in patch number, size, shape complexity, and spatial connectivity. To examine whether these spatially derived clusters were associated with consistent patterns in local land surface temperatures, we compared the residuals from the previously fitted Simple GAM model (LST ~ s(Area)) across the clusters. This model was chosen because it adjusted LST solely for total built-up area, allowing residuals to reflect variation not explained by area alone. A one-way ANOVA was used to assess differences in the residuals among the three clusters, followed by pairwise t-tests with Bonferroni correction to identify statistically significant contrasts between cluster types and determine whether the form and structure of urban patches, beyond their total area, exerted distinguishable effects on surface temperature behavior. All analyses were performed in R using the kmeans() function with 25 random starts and a maximum of 1000 iterations to ensure convergence and robustness. Silhouette widths and WSS values were derived using the cluster and factoextra packages for cluster quality assessment29. Results

The LST for the selected pixel in this study exhibited a mean of 37.08 ± 4.36 °C, reflecting moderate variability across the study area (Fig. 3; Table 1). The LST range spanned from a minimum of 27.67 °C to a maximum of 57.80 °C, indicating significant thermal heterogeneity. The LST skewness (0.626) and kurtosis (0.220) values suggest a slightly right-skewed distribution with a relatively flat peak, consistent with the presence of both cooler and warmer extremes within the region. Spatial metrics of built-up patch patterns exhibited substantial variation across the landscape, with Area and ENN showing the broadest ranges (0.03–0.69 ha and 20.00–120.42 m, respectively). The mean values ranged from 0.056 ha for MPS to 32.008 m for ENN. Skewness and kurtosis values indicate that MPS, ENN, and SHI were notably right-skewed with high kurtosis, suggesting the presence of extreme values or outliers in some locations. Among metrics, MPS and ENN displayed the highest skewness (2.298 and 2.241, respectively) and kurtosis (6.798 and 5.700), indicating that these variables were concentrated around lower values with few large outliers. The VIF values (Fig. 4) remained below the conservative threshold of 5 for all variables, suggesting the absence of problematic multicollinearity. However, MPS had the highest VIF (3.537), followed by SHI (2.478) and Area (2.156). The scatterplots of each metric against LST is shown in Fig. 4. The positive association of Area, MPS, and SHI with LST is evident from their upward trend lines, suggesting that larger, more complex-shaped patches tend to coincide with elevated surface temperatures. In contrast, NP and ENN were negatively associated with LST, indicating that fragmented or more dispersed built-up patterns may contribute to reduced surface temperatures.

To further investigate the combined effects of Area and individual landscape metrics on LST, interaction term GAMs were fitted using scaled variables (Area paired with spatial metrics) with summarized in Tables 2 and 3. The Area + MPS model exhibited the best performance, with the lowest RMSE of 2.547 °C, the highest coefficient of determination (R² = 0.657), and the lowest AIC of 22262.26, indicating superior predictive accuracy compared to other interaction models. The Area + SHI model performed similarly (RMSE = 2.57 °C, R² = 0.651, AIC = 22353.48), followed by the Area + ENN model (RMSE = 2.678 °C, R² = 0.621, AIC = 22720.35). Residual distributions were also comparable across models, with median residuals ranging from − 0.13 to −0.08 and interquartile ranges of approximately − 1.76 to 1.64 (Table 2). The Area + ENN model showed the widest residual range (−20.88 to 15.84), while the Area + SHI model had the narrowest (−19.48 to 13.8), suggesting slightly less extreme deviations in the latter.

All models had intercepts around 37 °C (ranging from 36.993 to 37.079), representing the baseline LST after variable scaling. The smooth terms for Area were highly significant across all models (p = 0.001), with effective edf ranging from 3.187 to 3.501 (Ref.df = 3.958 to 4.137), confirming non-linear relationships between Area and LST. The smooth terms for the landscape metrics were also significant, with p-values ranging from 0.002 to 0.007 and edf values from 2.098 to 2.751, indicating non-linear effects of NP, MPS, SHI, and ENN on LST. The tensor product interaction terms (ti) were significant in all models (p-values from 0.011 to 0.045), with edf values between 1.768 and 2.012, suggesting non-linear interactions between Area and each landscape metric. The Area + NP interaction was the most significant (p = 0.011), while the Area + MPS interaction was the least significant (p = 0.045), though still notable. The interaction effects are visualized using contour plots (Fig. 6), which depict the combined influence of scaled Area and each spatial metric on LST. In the Area + NP model, higher LST values were observed for high Area and low NP values, indicating that smaller, highly fragmented patches are associated with cooler landscapes. The Area + MPS model showed that LST increases with larger patch sizes in larger areas. In the Area + SHI model, complex patch shapes (high SHI) combined with larger areas were linked to elevated LST, while simpler shapes had a reduced impact. The Area + ENN model indicated that lower patch isolation (lower ENN) in smaller areas corresponded to higher LST, with effects diminishing as area increased.

The K-means clustering analysis, utilizing standardized spatial metrics, identified three distinct clusters of Fragmented, Compact, and Balanced. Cluster sizes varied significantly, with the Fragmented cluster comprising the largest group (2908 observations), followed by the Compact cluster (1245 observations), and the Balanced cluster (567 observations). The optimal number of clusters (k = 3) was determined based on the within-cluster sum of squares (WSS), which decreased from 14,245.357 at k = 2 to 6,559.506 at k = 6, with a notable reduction observed between k = 2 and k = 3 (from 14,245.357 to 11,387.210) (Fig. 7), suggesting a reasonable trade-off between cluster cohesion and model complexity.

The standardized cluster centers highlighted distinct landscape characteristics (Table 4). The Fragmented cluster was characterized by relatively neutral NP (−0.175), low MPS (−0.361), moderate SHI (−0.332), and low ENN (−0.235), suggesting a landscape with a moderate number of small, moderately shaped, and closely spaced patches. The Compact cluster exhibited the lowest values for NP (−0.456), MPS (−1.065), SHI (−1.052), and ENN (−0.475), indicating fewer, larger, simpler-shaped, and more isolated patches. The Balanced cluster showed moderate NP (−0.103) and MPS (−0.487), low SHI (−0.609), and an exceptionally low ENN (−2.246), reflecting a landscape with average patch numbers and sizes, simpler shapes, and highly connected patches.

The Compact cluster had the highest mean residual (1.515, SD = 2.845), indicating it was the warmest cluster, with LST values significantly above the expected levels. The Fragmented cluster exhibited a mean residual of −0.351 (SD = 2.515), suggesting cooler-than-expected LSTs, while the Balanced cluster had the lowest mean residual (−1.528, SD = 2.591), identifying it as the coolest cluster. An ANOVA confirmed significant differences in residuals across clusters (F = 331.9, p < 2e-16), supporting the hypothesis of distinct thermal profiles. Pairwise t-tests with Bonferroni correction further validated these differences, with all pairwise comparisons (Fragmented vs. Compact, Fragmented vs. Balanced, Compact vs. Balanced) yielding p-values of 0.000, indicating highly significant differences in LST residuals across all clusters (Table 4).

Discussion

Dominant role of landscape composition in driving LST

Consistent with the first hypothesis, models that incorporated both compositional and configurational metrics outperformed the area-only model. Urban landscape composition, particularly the extent and size of impervious surfaces, strongly influences LST through heat absorption and storage. Our results showed that Area and MPS were positively correlated with LST, with the Simple GAM (Area only) explaining 58.9%. The magnitude of this relationship aligns with earlier findings where impervious surface metrics such as the percentage of built-up lands accounted for a large share of LST variability8,30 and slightly exceeds the 56% variance reported by Chen, Yao30. Consistent with our results, Mokhtari, Amani-Beni17 reported that not only mean LST levels but also their inter-annual variability are sensitive to the area of built-up patches, suggesting that larger impervious expanses exert a sustained thermal influence over time. In arid regions, Afrakhteh, Asgarian7 found a logarithmic relationship between built-up patch area and LST, with the slope becoming nearly constant beyond 50 ha, indicating diminishing incremental warming from further expansion once this threshold is exceeded. In our study area, where many buildings are detached, predominantly two stories or less, and policy permits fragmented growth, large impervious patches still retain substantial heat due to high solar radiation, indicating that surface material likely plays a dominant role in determining LST. It is still valid in the region where the structural openness and dispersed pattern could facilitate airflow and the integration of cooler open spaces, potentially moderating peak heating relative to the denser, high-rise contexts studied by Afrakhteh, Asgarian7 and Mokhtari, Amani-Beni17. In total, the strong positive relationship between composition metrics and LST in this arid, low-rise urban context underscores the persistent influence of impervious extent, while local morphological and policy factors may temper, but not eliminate, its warming impact.

Modulating effect of landscape configuration on LST

Landscape configuration shapes LST patterns by influencing not only how built surfaces interact with air movement and vegetated cooling areas5,31, but also how neighboring land cover types exchange thermal effects. Recent research also emphasizes that these effects are not confined to the immediate patch or zone, but extend through spatial interactions with surrounding urban zones, where neighborhood-scale context can exert comparable or greater influence on LST than internal morphological features32. In our Global GAM, the negative association of NP with LST suggests that greater fragmentation disperses built-up surfaces into smaller, more numerous patches, increasing their proximity to open areas that are cooler with lower LST values. Similarly, higher ENN values indicate increased isolation between built-up patches, allowing stronger cooling influence from nearby non-built areas. This configurational impacts align with Asgarian, Amiri5, who showed that urban areas adjacent to large vegetated zones experience lower LST, demonstrating the importance of neighboring effects.

Our Interaction model further clarified these dynamics. When fragmentation (high NP) coincided with low ENN, cooling effects were amplified, which found that strategically identified ventilation corridors significantly overlapped with areas of lower land surface temperature in Shanghai33 and Shenyang34. Conversely, compact urban forms, where NP and ENN are low, create extensive, contiguous impervious surfaces with large “core” areas of high LST, limiting the penetration of neighboring cooling influences. Chen, Shu6 support this pattern, finding that urban expansion within already built-up areas tends to exhibit higher LST than outlying patches, reflecting accumulated heat from neighboring impervious surfaces rather than cooling influence from vegetated or open surroundings. The positive relationship between SHI and LST indicates that more irregular patch geometries can retain heat. In Delhi, Jha, Yadav35 found a similar pattern suggesting that patch‑level structural attributes, including size and configuration, significantly influence surface temperature. As shown by Yu, Chen33, this may be due to the creation of micro-scale turbulence zones that reduce ventilation efficiency among these structures. However, the magnitude of this effect in our study area was weaker than expected, likely because building footprints and block layouts are relatively uniform, constraining variation in shape complexity. In addition, the policy context permitting fragmented development results in many built-up patches being bordered by open land, which may dilute the warming potential typically associated with high SHI in denser contexts. Overall, the configuration metrics in both models suggest that fragmentation and reduced isolation of built-up patches act as moderating influences on LST in arid environments, mainly by enabling neighboring cooling effects from adjacent open space. At the same time, the literature6 and our results indicate that when urban expansion fills in existing built-up areas, these moderating effects are diminished, reinforcing the role of configuration as a spatial filter for thermal interactions.

Thermal differentiation across landscape clusters

The overall average silhouette width of 0.295 falls below the conventional threshold of 0.5 for well-separated clusters, indicating that the three identified clusters are not strongly distinct. This relatively weak separation likely arises from the continuous nature of the underlying landscape metrics and the inherent gradation between patch types. Consequently, the cluster assignments should be interpreted as a heuristic simplification of a continuous gradient, and the thermal differences between clusters, while statistically significant, may represent gradual transitions rather than sharp categorical boundaries. Consistent with the second research hypothesis, that distinct structural configurations exhibit different thermal responses. After accounting for patch area, our results showed that the three clusters had markedly different mean LST residuals. Specifically, after controlling for patch area, the Compact cluster showed the highest mean residual LST (1.515), indicating that these areas are substantially warmer than expected based on patch area alone. The elevated LST in the Compact cluster reflects the combined influence of large, aggregated nearby patches, probably due to heat received from neighboring impervious surfaces. The large MPS values in these compact areas echo the sustained thermal influence noted by Afrakhteh, Asgarian7 and Mokhtari, Amani-Beni17, suggesting that large patches may have already reached a plateau in incremental warming. This aligns with studies that find compact, monocentric patterns concentrate thermal loads not only internally but also via reduced opportunities for beneficial neighborhood-scale interactions, a mechanism increasingly recognized in urban climate research32. By contrast, the Balanced cluster’s markedly lower LST aligns with its high ENN, indicating strong connectivity with surrounding open patches. This is consistent with the fragmentation–cooling link reported by Rahimi, Barghjelveh10 and Nasar-u-Minallah, Haase36, where increased patch division exposes more vegetated edges to built-up surfaces, enhancing convective and evaporative cooling.

In our case, the Balanced pattern appears to facilitate both neighboring cooling from open areas and airflow corridors between detached or semi-detached low-rise structures which is a microclimatic benefit amplified in arid climates with strong diurnal winds. Policy context plays a decisive role in enabling these configurations. The allowance for fragmented growth in our study area (moderate NP, low MPS) permits open spaces to persist between built patches, creating opportunities for ventilation and vegetation integration. However, as seen in the Fragmented cluster, excessive dispersion without coordinated green-space planning risks sprawl, which can increase impervious cover overall and erode cooling gains. The implication for planning in arid regions is that cooler urban landscapes can be achieved not by strict compaction, which risks producing large, isolated heat cores, but through strategically fragmented patterns that maintain low ENN, integrate vegetated buffers, and avoid excessive MPS growth. Such designs harness the beneficial neighboring effects highlighted in the literature while preventing the heat concentration associated with compact, isolated impervious expanses.

Limitations and future research directions

This study is limited by its use of daytime LST data averaged over spring and summer, which does not capture diurnal or full seasonal variability. Incorporating higher-frequency sources like MODIS or ground measurements could improve temporal coverage. Moreover, no independent ground-based LST measurements (such as field campaigns or cross-validation with alternative remote sensing products) were available to validate the satellite-derived thermal data, which should be addressed in future studies. The findings are also specific to an arid urban context, where sparse vegetation and high radiation dominate. Extending the study to cities in other climatic zones would help assess the broader applicability of the results. The spatial resolution of Landsat imagery may miss finer-scale urban features such as narrow streets or small structures that affect microclimate. Higher-resolution data and object-based methods could enhance detection of these elements. Non-spatial factors such as anthropogenic heat, traffic, and material properties were not included but may significantly influence surface temperature. Moreover, the relationships observed are correlative and do not confirm causality. Mechanistic modeling or simulation tools could help test the physical processes behind these patterns. Finally, while patch metrics like shape complexity are useful analytically, they are not easily adopted by urban planners. Translating these into practical planning tools or design guidelines would enhance their relevance for policy and implementation.

Conclusion

This study sought to investigate the influence of spatial configurations of built-up areas on LST in arid urban environments, employing landscape metrics and clustering methodologies. Models that combined compositional and configurational metrics outperformed the area-only model, and the three structural clusters exhibited distinct LST residuals after area adjustment. This supports both research hypotheses that configurational metrics improve predictive performance, and that different patch configurations are associated with different thermal responses. Compact urban forms, distinguished by large, aggregated patches, were consistently correlated with elevated LST. In contrast, balanced configurations, characterized by moderate patch sizes and enhanced connectivity, demonstrated reduced thermal intensity. Fragmented patterns, shaped by intricate edge interactions, exhibited intermediate LST values. These results underscore that, beyond the extent of built-up areas, the spatial arrangement of urban development significantly influences urban heat dynamics. From an urban planning perspective, these insights emphasize the necessity of fostering spatial patterns that mitigate thermal accumulation. Promoting moderately dispersed configurations with irregular patch shapes, while avoiding excessively compact development, is recommended to enhance thermal resilience in arid urban regions.
