Moving Toward Universal Risk Stratification: Globally Standardized Lipoprotein(a) in Molar Units
Authors: Takashi Miida
Published: 2026

See article vol. 33: 1049-1059

The management of dyslipidemia plays a crucial role in the primary and secondary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) 1) . Although recent drug therapies can reduce low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels to less than 70 mg/dL, a significant residual risk of ASCVD remains 2) . Numerous studies have consistently shown that lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is closely associated with ASCVD, and aortic calcification,/stenosis 3) . Several drugs currently under development can reduce the Lp(a) concentrations by up to 90% 4) . Therefore, they are expected to decrease residual risk even in patients whose LDL-C is maintained below the target values set by the dyslipidemia guidelines.

Lp(a) is a complex of LDL and apolipoprotein(a) [apo(a)]. Apolipoprotein B of LDL is covalently bound to apo(a) via a disulfide bond. Although Lp(a) belongs to the family of apolipoprotein B-containing lipoproteins, its synthesis and catabolism differ from those of LDL 5) . Immunoassays for Lp(a) detect apo(a), which exhibits several isoforms with different molecular weights owing to genetically determined repeats of Kringle IV type 2 6) . For over 30 years, the Lp(a) values have been inconsistent among immunoassays because of variable antibody reactivity against apo(a) 6 , 7) . Currently, both physicians and the guidelines rely on unstandardized Lp(a) values, which often leads to confusion in clinical practice. To address this, the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (IFCC) endorsed a mass spectrometry-based reference measurement procedure (RMP) for Lp(a) in 2023 8) and launched a certification program in 2026.

In this issue, Yamada et al. report a retrospective multicenter study enrolling more than 6,000 Japanese patients. To harmonize the Lp(a) values measured using different immunoassays, all values were expressed as standardized values in molar units 9) . High Lp(a) concentrations were associated with a higher prevalence of coronary artery disease, ASCVD, chronic kidney disease, and familial hypercholesterolemia. Based on their receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, the authors proposed two clinical decision limits for Lp(a) (25 and 125 nmol/L) to stratify patients into low-, intermediate-, and high-ASCVD risk groups. This is the first study to determine clinical decision limits using globally standardized Lp(a) values. Further accumulation of epidemiological and clinical data using standardized values is essential.

Several reasons make this the optimal time for the global standardization of Lp(a). First, the number of immunoassays available in the market is at its lowest in 30 years, simplifying the transition. Second, Lp(a) is significantly underutilized in daily clinical practice in Japan; according to the Japanese NDB Open Data, the frequency of testing is 1,000-fold lower than that of triglycerides in outpatient settings and 200-fold lower in inpatient settings. Third, interest in Lp(a) is surging as potent Lp(a)-lowering agents are expected to be used clinically within a few years. Therefore, the standardization of Lp(a) and the transition from mass to molar units are unlikely to cause significant confusion at this stage of the transition. I therefore hope that Japan will spearhead the global movement toward Lp(a) standardization.
