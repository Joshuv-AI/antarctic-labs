Development and validation of a deep learning model for automatic detection of depressed skull fractures from CT scans
Authors: Salita Angkurawaranon, Sarawadee Chatchavan, Teeraporn Iangkoonchorn, Taned Singlor, Natthawut Jarunnarumol, Papangkorn Inkeaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepressed skull fractures with bone depression greater than in one cortex might cause major consequences and require surgery in traumatic head injury patients. Therefore, skull fractures with depression in more than one cortex must be identified quickly and accurately.MethodsThis study proposes using a deep learning model to deal with the task. Cranial CT scans of traumatic head injury patients with and without depressed skull fractures were collected for this retrospective investigation. A real-time object detection model, You Only Look Once (YOLO), was adopted to detect depressed skull fractures in more than one cortex. We proposed a two-phase training strategy for training the model. The model was evaluated using internal and external test datasets. The detection performance was reported in terms of accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, negative predictive value, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve.ResultsThe deep learning model demonstrated strong performance on an internal test dataset (accuracy = 0.957); however, its performance declined on two external test datasets (accuracy = 0.884 and 0.857).ConclusionThis model enables automated detection of depressed skull fractures, streamlining the clinical workflow by flagging high-priority cases for expedited radiologist review.Keywords: Traumatic brain injury, Depressed skull fracture, Deep learning

Background

Head trauma is a prevalent condition frequently encountered in emergency departments. In Thailand, the age-standardized prevalence rate for head trauma is 812 per 100,000 population [1]. Instances of head trauma resulting from significant force, such as motor vehicle accidents, pedestrian injuries, falls, and assault, frequently manifest as skull fractures [2]. Skull fractures can be closed or open, simple or compound, linear, depressed, or diastatic, as shown in Fig. 1 [3]. Depressed skull fractures are observed in 9.8% of severe head injury cases in Thailand [4]. Depressed skull fractures occur when bone fragments are driven below the level of the adjacent skull bones [5]. These fractures are more likely to involve injury to the brain parenchyma and carry a higher risk of infection, seizure, intracranial hemorrhage, neurological deficits, and death if not diagnosed early and managed appropriately. Sharp bone fragments driven into the brain parenchyma can damage the dura mater, leading to a risk of leakage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and an increased risk of infection of the central nervous system (CNS) [5–7]. Indications for surgical intervention in depressed skull fractures include depression exceeding the inner table of adjacent non-depressed bone, complex depressed fractures, CSF leakage, local neurological deficits caused by pressure from a single or multiple depressed fragments, associated lesions, and the presence of a depressed fracture for cosmetic considerations [8, 9]. CT scans can identify a depressed skull fracture with a bone depression below the inner table of non-depressed bone or depressed more than 1 cortex as one indicator necessitating surgical treatment to elevate the skull. Timely surgical management gives excellent outcomes, decreasing both morbidity and mortality [10]. For that reason, early detection of traumatic depressed skull fractures meeting these surgical criteria is crucial. However, in current practice, a large number of traumatic head injury patients requiring diagnostic cranial CT scans experience a prolonged door-to-CT result time due to the overwhelming workload of the radiologists. Developing a deep learning model to detect a depressed skull fracture automatically could be potentially very useful, reducing the diagnostic time and assisting in the prioritization of patients for radiological assessments and further well-run management [8, 9].

CT is the primary diagnostic tool for traumatic skull injuries, not only providing excellent visualization of cortical bones but also having a very short imaging time. It could facilitate early intervention for brain injury to prevent further morbidity or even death [11]. Currently, artificial intelligence is becoming increasingly utilized in the development of computer-aided diagnosis systems, particularly in an emergency setting, for example, in the case of traumatic brain injury [12–14]. Many previous studies have used various algorithms to automate the process of detecting skull fractures from CT scans [8, 9, 15, 16]. However, only one study used a deep learning model to categorize skull fractures into five subtypes [17, 18]. One of the key challenges of current systems is the limited availability of models that can precisely localize skull fractures, as most approaches primarily focus on fracture classification, and only a few studies [16, 19] have reported localization or segmentation methods. This shortcoming necessitates additional time and effort from radiologists to manually search for the exact fracture site on imaging studies, which can be time-consuming in a busy clinical setting.

Therefore, this study aims to develop a deep learning model for automatically identifying the locations of depressed skull fractures that require surgical management from cranial CT scans. In addition, a second objective is to externally validate the diagnostic performance of this deep learning model.

Materials and methods

The workflow of this study is given in Fig. 2. It includes the study cohort, deep learning model development, and statistical analysis. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the committees of the Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University (CoA No. 081/2023), and the Lampang Hospital (CoA No. 058/67). Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the analysis used anonymous clinical data.

The workflow of our proposed method

Internal Data

Cranial CT scans of 1.5-mm slice thickness performed at Maharaj Nakorn Chiang Mai hospital from October 2013 to October 2022 were collected. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had a documented history of traumatic head injury and a complete, technically adequate non-contrast cranial computed tomography (CT) scan suitable for full diagnostic evaluation. Conversely, patients younger than 15 years of age were excluded from the study. Further exclusion criteria included evidence of prior surgical interventions, such as craniectomy or cranioplasty, to avoid confounding postoperative changes. Additionally, examinations with suboptimal image quality specifically those affected by severe motion or metallic artifacts that obscure osseous structures were excluded to ensure the integrity of the final analysis.

No traceable data allowing identification of the patients were collected. The CT images were acquired using three CT scanners (SOMATOM Definition, Siemens; SOMATOM Force, Siemens; and IQon Spectral CT, Philips). Axial images with 1.5-mm slice thickness were used.

Data from a total of 325 traumatic head injury patients were included. The data was randomly divided into a training dataset (n = 231, 70%) and an internal test dataset (n = 94, 30%). The training dataset was used to develop the model, while the internal test dataset was strictly reserved for independent performance evaluation and was not involved in any stage of model training. Not only the data from patients with depressed skull fractures, but also CT imaging data from patients with no skull fracture and other types of skull fractures were also included in both datasets. Out of the 231 cases in the training dataset, 117 cases were identified as depressed skull fractures in more than one cortex; 102 cases had other types of skull fractures, and 12 cases had no skull fractures. Of the 94 cases in the test dataset, there were 45 cases with a depressed skull fracture in more than one cortex, 32 cases with other types of skull fractures, and 17 cases with no skull fracture.

For preparing the training dataset, the images from patients with a mention of a depressed skull fracture in the electronic health records were reviewed, and the patients with depressed skull fractures in more than one cortex were selected. Because the deep learning model used requires bounding box annotations for training, the locations of depressed skull fractures in more than one cortex were manually marked with bounding boxes by radiology residents and medical students who were trained by a board-certified diagnostic neuroradiologist using the Fujifilm Synapse 3D software. A bounding box is specified by the coordinates of its center, along with its width and height. Then, all the annotations were reviewed by neuroradiologists. Any discrepancies in fracture location or bounding box accuracy were resolved through final adjudication by these experts. This consensus was used as the reference standard for all subsequent model training.

External data

To demonstrate the usability of the proposed model, external CT brain scans were collected. These scans were obtained from patients assessed for traumatic brain injury at Lampang Hospital, Thailand (institution 2), between October 2019 and October 2022. The same inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. A total of 147 patients were retrieved, with 64 patients identified as having depressed skull fractures in more than one cortex, 60 patients with other types of skull fractures, and 23 patients with no skull fracture. CT scans were acquired using 2 CT scanners, specifically Philips Incisive CT 128 slices (SN 55000) and Philips Ingenuity Core CT 128 slices. Two external test datasets were created using the same group of patients but with different reconstruction settings and slice thicknesses. Different CT acquisition parameters were intentionally applied to evaluate the impact of acquisition variability on model performance and to assess its generalizability. The use of heterogeneous CT settings was intended to simulate real-world clinical conditions, where acquisition parameters often vary across institutions.

In external test dataset 1, CT scans were generated with a thickness of 1.5 mm. These scans from a Philips Incisive CT 128-slice (SN 55000) were reconstructed using the YB filter with an enhancement of 0.00 and a bone window preset (window width = 1600, window level = 500). Meanwhile, CT scans from a Philips Ingenuity Core 128-slice CT were reconstructed using the Y-Detail (YB) filter with a -1.00 enhancement and a bone window preset (window width = 1500, window level = 500). For the external test dataset 2, CT scans were reconstructed with a slice thickness of 3 mm. There was no filter applied to this dataset. The images were then adjusted to the bone window in a preprocessing step.

Deep learning model development

Head CT images were first preprocessed by conversion from DICOM files to JPEG files. In this step, the CT scans were automatically adjusted to the bone window (window width = 2,800 and window level = 600) while the original image size was kept in JPEG conversion. Since the raw DICOM files represent imaging data in Hounsfield units, the adjustment to the bone window can be uniformly applied across all datasets. This process ensured consistent intensity scaling and visualization parameters, thereby minimizing potential bias arising from inter-scanner or inter-institutional variability.

As our objective is the identification of the location of the depressed skull fractures across more than one cortex, we adopted a You Only Look Once (YOLO) version 8 model [20], which is a powerful object detection model. The main advantage of YOLO is its exceptional speed and accuracy in real-time object detection. Compared with two-stage detectors, e.g., Faster R-CNN and RetinaNet, the YOLO version 8 offers faster inference with competitive accuracy. Additionally, skull fractures typically present as thin, irregular, and elongated structures with subtle contrast differences relative to surrounding bone tissue. Accurate localization of such fine-grained patterns requires a model capable of robust multi-scale feature extraction and precise bounding box regression. The anchor-free design of YOLO version 8 is particularly advantageous for detecting irregular fracture geometries that do not conform to predefined anchor box shapes. Furthermore, YOLO version 8 supports efficient training and inference, which aligns with the practical clinical deployment objective of this study.

The architecture of YOLO is based on a multi-layered deep convolutional neural network. It can be divided into two main parts, namely the backbone and head sections [21]. The backbone part extracts meaningful features from multiple scales of the input. The feature maps are then collected and aggregated in the head part. Finally, the model outputs (i.e., bounding boxes and confidence scores for detected objects) are generated. A YOLO version 8 model of size M comprising 25.9 million parameters was used. No architectural modifications were applied. The model was initialized with pretrained weights from the COCO dataset and subsequently fine-tuned on the training dataset.

Fracture types were first extracted from the electronic health records and then rechecked and confirmed by a board-certified radiologist during the annotation process. In this work, we define the regions of depressed skull fractures as foreground objects and all other regions as background. The detection task is therefore formulated as distinguishing between foreground (fracture) and background regions. A CT slice without a depressed skull fracture is considered a background image, whereas a CT slice with a depressed skull fracture is considered a foreground image. Since fractures typically appear in only a few slices per scan, the number of background images greatly exceeds that of foreground images, leading to severe class imbalance. Training a model directly on all slices may therefore introduce bias. To address this, we designed a two-phase training strategy as summarized in Fig. 3. In the first phase, the model was trained exclusively on foreground images, enabling it to learn both fracture patterns (from fracture regions) and normal patterns (from non-fracture regions within the same slices). Afterward, all images in the training dataset were fed to the model for detecting depressed skull fractures, and images misclassified as positive (false positives) were collected. The false-positive images might contain some patterns that are similar to the pattern of a depressed skull fracture (e.g., non-depressed skull fracture and skull sutures). And the model encountered the patterns from the foreground images in the first training phase. Consequently, the model was confused and produced incorrect detections. So, we introduced the second training phase. A new model was trained using images containing skull fractures and the collected false-positive images. This approach avoids continued parameter adaptation from the first-phase model and reduces the risk of cumulative overfitting. Incorporating these false positives provided the model with additional informative patterns that were absent in the initial training data. In summary, the first phase served to identify informative background data, while the second phase refined the model to achieve more robust depressed skull fracture detection.

Training strategy for optimizing a YOLO detection model

For training the YOLO model, we used the stochastic gradient descent algorithm as an optimizer. A learning rate was set to be 0.01. The cost function was an incorporation of bounding-box regression loss, classification loss, and distributed focal loss with weights of 7.5, 0.5, and 1.5, respectively. As we have a small amount of data that is insufficient to create a validation dataset, the model was trained for over 1000 epochs with a batch size of 32 until the training loss became stable. This comprehensive configuration aimed to strike a balance between model complexity and training efficiency, ensuring robust performance across a diverse range of scenarios. In addition, the data augmentation techniques played a crucial role in diversifying the training dataset. The images were randomly flipped vertically with a probability of 0.5 and then horizontally with a probability of 0.5. To introduce additional variability, the images were randomly scaled with a scaling factor of 0.5 and underwent rotation with a maximum angle of 20 degrees. These transformations reduce the risk of learning dataset-specific spatial patterns and improve generalization. The trained model was publicly available via https://github.com/radiomicsaicmu/YOLO-for-skull-fracture-detection.

In prediction, the optimized model detected the presence of a skull fracture on each CT slice individually for a patient. It produced objects with bounding boxes and confidence scores. The objects that had a confidence score equal to or more than 0.5 were accepted as evidence of a depressed skull fracture in more than one cortex. For a patient, if a skull fracture is detected on at least one slice, the presence of depressed skull fractures across more than one cortex will be reported.

Statistical analysis

Demographic data were compared between the training dataset, internal test dataset, and external test datasets. Age differences across the groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test. The proportions of gender and target classes (i.e., depressed skull fracture, other types of skull fracture, and no skull fracture) were compared using the chi-square test. Data were considered to show a significant difference when p < 0.05. To be considered as a true positive, the model needs to correctly localize the location of the fracture. If a fracture was identified but in the wrong location, it was considered a false positive. Since the samples in all test datasets were not annotated with ground-truth bounding boxes, the predictions generated by the deep-learning model, including predicted bounding boxes and confidence scores, were reviewed slice-by-slice by a consensus of neuroradiologists to carefully assess the localization results while minimizing individual interpretation bias. Based on the neuroradiologists’ consensus review, true positive, true negative, false positive, and false negative cases were identified. Cases initially classified as true positives but with incorrect localization of the skull fracture were reclassified as false positives. Subsequently, the performance metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision (positive predictive value; PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), were calculated accordingly.

Results on the internal test dataset

Following the training of the deep learning model for identifying the location of a depressed skull fracture, the performance of the model was tested on the internal test dataset containing the CT scans of 94 patients. All slices from each CT scan were used to assess the model performance.

The model performance metrics are shown in Table 2. Before the second-phase training, the model reached an accuracy of 0.673, sensitivity of 0.980, specificity of 0.360, precision of 0.610, NPV of 0.947, F1-score of 0.752, and AUC-ROC of 0.894. However, after the second-phase training, it was able to identify at least one positive lesion in the patient in more than one cortex, achieving an accuracy of 0.957, sensitivity of 0.933, specificity of 0.980, precision of 0.977, NPV of 0.941, F1-score of 0.955, and AUC-ROC of 0.925. Three out of 45 cases with depressed skull fractures in more than one cortex were misclassified as negative. Meanwhile, only one out of 49 cases without such fractures were misclassified as having a depressed skull fracture in more than one cortex.

Result on the external test dataset 1

The proposed deep learning model was evaluated on external test dataset 1. Before the second-phase training, the model achieved 0.452 accuracy, 1.000 sensitivity, 0.024 specificity, 0.444 precision, 1.000 NPV, 0.615 F1-score, and 0.827 AUC-ROC in identifying patients with depressed skull fractures involving more than one cortex. After the second-phase training, the performance of the proposed deep learning model improved noticeably. The accuracy, specificity, precision, F1-score, and AUC–ROC increased to 0.884, 0.843, 0.822, 0.876, and 0.901, respectively. Meanwhile, sensitivity and NPV showed a slight decrease to 0.938 and 0.946, respectively. When analyzing the confusion matrix shown in Fig. 4(a), a total of 17 misclassified cases were identified. Specifically, 4 actual depressed skull fractures were not detected, while 13 cases of actual non-depressed skull fractures (i.e., other types of fractures or no fractures) were misclassified as depressed skull fractures.

Results on the external test dataset 2

Evaluation on the external test dataset 2 showed that the first-phase trained model achieved an accuracy of 0.607, sensitivity of 0.952, precision of 0.526, specificity of 0.341, NPV of 0.903, F1-score of 0.678, and AUC–ROC of 0.842 for identifying patients with depressed skull fractures involving more than one cortex. Following the second-phase training, substantial performance improvement was observed. The proposed model achieved an accuracy of 0.857, a sensitivity of 0.766, a precision of 0.891, a specificity of 0.928, NPV of 0.837, F1-score of 0.824, and AUC–ROC of 0.856. The confusion matrix in Fig. 4(b) revealed 21 misclassifications. Of these, 15 cases of actual depressed skull fractures were incorrectly classified as non-depressed, while 6 non-depressed cases were misclassified as depressed skull fractures.

Discussion

This model is designed to identify a subtype of a depressed skull fracture in more than one cortex, utilizing external test datasets to assess its performance. This type of skull fracture needs a neurosurgeon to elevate the skull. Based on the experimental results, the two-phase training strategy effectively improved the model’s performance, particularly in reducing false positive predictions. Before the second-phase training, the model tended to over-detect small fractures, resulting in very high sensitivities but markedly low specificities. Consequently, many non–depressed skull fracture cases were incorrectly classified as depressed skull fractures, as reflected by the low precision. After the second-phase training, the number of non–depressed skull fractures incorrectly classified as depressed skull fractures was reduced. Consequently, specificities and precision improved significantly. Although the sensitivities decreased, the results indicate a more balanced trade-off between false positives and false negatives, as reflected by the improved AUC–ROCs. With the internal test dataset, the trained model performed excellently in the detection of this specific type of skull fracture, achieving an accuracy of 0.957, sensitivity of 0.933, specificity of 0.980, precision of 0.977, NPV of 0.941, F1-score of 0.955, and AUC-ROC of 0.925. Although the model dealt with unseen data in the internal test dataset, it registered as having high performance. The model demonstrated strong ability in detecting depressed skull fractures, supported by its high sensitivity and precision. Additionally, its reliability in recognizing non-fracture cases remained robust, as indicated by the high specificity and NPV. Furthermore, the high F1-score and AUC-ROC values indicate a well-balanced performance between sensitivity and precision, as well as a strong overall discriminative capability. It seems that the model could capture the general patterns of depressed skull fractures as a consequence of the mechanism of multi-scale feature extraction of the YOLO model and data augmentation. In addition, the training strategy helped the model learn to discriminate between depressed and non-depressed skull fractures by presenting difficult samples to the model in the training process. With the external test datasets, the performance of the model falls behind that of the internal test dataset. The evaluation of the external test dataset 1 resulted in an accuracy of 0.884, sensitivity of 0.938, specificity of 0.843, precision of 0.822, NPV of 0.946, F1-score of 0.876, and AUC-ROC of 0.901. External test dataset 2 resulted in an accuracy of 0.857, sensitivity of 0.776, specificity of 0.928, precision of 0.891, NPV of 0.837, F1-score of 0.824, and AUC-ROC of 0.856. Although the model’s performance slightly decreased when applied to the external test datasets, it maintained a well-balanced performance in discriminating between depressed and non-depressed skull fractures. These results show that though the model is generalized for depressed skull fracture patterns, it still requires input images generated from the same setting as the data it had learned. Notably, slice thickness does not directly affect the YOLO model itself. However, it affects the quality of the input CT scans. When thicker slices (e.g., 3.0 mm) are used, some fine details of depressed skull fractures may be missing compared with thinner slices (e.g., 1.5 mm). As a result, the model may not detect some fractures because important information is not clearly visible in the CT scans. Therefore, the lower performance in the external dataset 2 is likely caused by the lower image detail of the CT scans rather than a limitation of the YOLO model itself. This suggests that, especially when using the same CT image protocol (slice thickness of 1.5 mm) utilized for model training, the model allows for outstanding automatic detection and diagnosis of depressed skull fractures that necessitate skull elevation. Previous studies that focused solely on the detection of linear skull fractures reported sensitivities of approximately 86% [9], whereas models trained to detect skull fractures demonstrated sensitivities ranging from 80.64% to 91.4% and specificities of around 85.92% to 88.73% [8, 16, 19]. An interesting model capable of detecting and classifying skull fractures into five subtypes achieved sensitivities and specificities of 92% and 95%, respectively, for detecting depressed skull fractures [17]. In comparison, our model, which specifically detects depressed skull fractures involving more than one cortical surface, achieved a sensitivity of 93% and specificity of 98% in the internal test set. In external validation, the model achieved sensitivities of 93% and 76% and specificities of 84% and 92% in datasets 1 and 2, respectively.

However, a direct comparison between studies may not be appropriate because the target fracture types differ. In particular, linear fractures are often subtle and may be more challenging to identify, whereas displaced or depressed skull fractures generally present with more conspicuous imaging features, potentially contributing to higher detection performance in our model. However, the accuracy and sensitivity of our model are slightly lower than those reported by Yuchen Guo et al., but its precision and specificity are better [22]. Yuchen Guo et al.’s model was built from about twenty thousand CT scans, but our model was trained from fewer than four hundred CT scans. The image augmentation we employed could increase the variation of the training data.

However, there are some erroneous predictions made by the model with the internal test. All three false-negative cases involved depressed skull fractures located near the vertex, indicating that fractures in this region may be more challenging for the model to detect (Fig. 5c and d). In real-life scenarios, skull fractures that are in parallel alignment to the acquisition plane can go undetected on axial images [23]. Coronal reconstructions may be necessary in these cases to clearly define depressed fractures [24]. Only one false-positive case was observed, which was related to normal skull anatomy (Fig. 5f). Examples of true-positive and true-negative cases from the internal test dataset are illustrated in Fig. 5a-b and e, respectively.

In the case of the external test datasets. The external test dataset 1, which uses CT images applied to the YB filter or algorithm to identify the image sharpness with a bone window preset, illustrates the high number of false positive cases. Most of these false positive cases (12 from 13 cases, 92%) came from the normal anatomy of the skull that mimics the gaping areas between the bones, such as in the case of the hypoglossal canal (Fig. 6c) or superior orbital fissure. Only one (8%) false-positive case was related to the partial volume effect of the CT scan. The YB filter, also known as a mathematical algorithm, modifies the image during image reconstruction in a CT scanner. The tendency of the model to overread normal anatomical structures of the skull as depressed fractures may be attributed to the use of filtering techniques designed to increase sharpness and reduce blur [25], which can inadvertently enhance normal structures and mimic fracture lines. All four false-negative cases (100%) from the external test dataset 1 occurred when a small, depressed fracture fragment was located beneath the skull surface (Fig. 6d). In addition, external test dataset 1 shows the true positive as Fig. 6a and the true negative as Fig. 6b. External test dataset 2 exhibits the lowest sensitivity while simultaneously improving specificity as compared to external test dataset 1, as evidenced. Figure 6e illustrates a true-positive case, while Fig. 6f shows a true-negative case. The low sensitivity of the model for this dataset was related to the protocol of CT scans with the greatest slice thickness. Small fracture lesions were observed in many false-negative cases, accounting for 9 of 15 cases (60%) (Fig. 6h). Previous studies have also found fracture-missed cases in CT scan protocols that solely use the axial plane and relatively thick slices [26, 27]. The small fractures could go unnoticed due to the thick CT scan slices. The remaining false-negative cases (6 from 15 cases, 40%) were associated with depressed fractures that maintained cortical continuity, making them challenging for the model to detect. In external test dataset 2, four of six false-positive cases (66.6%) were attributed to CT slice thickness, which caused a partial volume effect mimicking a skull fracture when both bone and soft tissue were present in the same voxel (Fig. 6g). The partial volume effect occurs when a CT voxel contains tissues of different densities, and because CT measures the average attenuation coefficient within that voxel, the resulting Hounsfield unit (HU) represents a weighted average of all the tissues inside it [28]. The remaining two false-positive cases (33.3%) were related to normal anatomical structures that mimicked fracture lines.

In our study, the number of surgically relevant depressed skull fractures missed by the model varied across datasets. In the internal test dataset, 2 of 3 cases (67%) were missed, while no relevant cases were missed in the external test dataset 1 (0 of 4 cases). In external test dataset 2, 6 of 15 cases (40%) were not detected. These findings highlight that while the model performs well overall, attention is needed for small or subtle fractures that may have surgical significance. The potential for false-negative results poses a significant challenge in the automated detection of depressed skull fractures. A missed fracture could lead to delayed surgical intervention and adverse patient outcomes. Consequently, we advocate the model’s role as a supplementary diagnostic aid designed to reduce human fatigue and perceptual errors. This human-in-the-loop approach ensures that the radiologist’s clinical expertise complements the AI’s detection capabilities, thereby mitigating the risk of missed diagnoses and prioritizing patient safety.

The external validation cohort was significantly older than the training set, which may introduce confounding due to age-related anatomical changes. While older patients may exhibit cortical thinning or early osteoporosis, the skull generally undergoes relatively slow age-related changes compared to other bones [29]. Therefore, these differences may have a modest effect on model performance. However, future research using external validation cohorts with age distributions more closely matched to the training set may help to further minimize the impact of age-related anatomical differences.

The deep learning model in this study has been shown to be accurate in detecting the presence of depressed skull fractures in more than 1 cortex in the cranial CT images. External validation of any AI model is essential for assessing model performance and generalizability. In this case, just 6% of published deep learning research in the diagnostic domain conducted external validation [30]. Most externally validated research indicates reduced algorithm performance [31], which is also the case in our study. The stability of the model during internal test (95.7% accuracy) indicates that the core algorithm is robust and not significantly overfitted to the training samples. However, the external validation revealed a vulnerability to domain shift. Specifically, the use of 3.0 mm slices in the external test dataset 2 likely led to a loss of fine structural detail compared to the 1.5 mm training standard. Furthermore, the application of the YB reconstruction filter in the external test dataset 1 introduced unique edge characteristics that differed from the internal domain. These findings underscore that institutional protocol variations, rather than model overfitting, are the primary challenges for clinical deployment.

One of the potential contributing factors for our study is the variation in the detailed protocols for brain CT scans across different institutions. The thicker the CT scan slice, the lower the sensitivity of the model. The filter will sharpen the anatomy of the skull, resulting in the false detection of a depressed skull fracture. This model could assist in the rapid identification of surgically relevant depressed skull fractures in trauma cases, improving initial screening to prioritize cases for radiologist review and prompt neurosurgical notification. The integration of real-time detection shifts the paradigm from a passive diagnostic tool to an active triage system. While traditional workflows rely on chronological review, our model enables a ‘Fast-Track’ protocol. Faster detection may lead to quicker diagnosis, earlier treatment, and potentially improved patient outcomes.

Limitations of this study include the sample size of our study being relatively small. The small sample size could limit the improvement of the performance of the model in the training and testing procedures. This model’s use of only axial CT images is another limitation. Applying coronal and/or sagittal views may improve the performance of the model. However, many centers worldwide do not routinely perform and curate reconstructions in coronal and/or sagittal views for every CT study of the head. Future studies incorporating multiplanar reconstructions could provide more robust and universally applicable models, but the lack of routine availability of these views remains a practical constraint in many settings. Another key limitation of this study is that external validation was restricted to CT scans obtained from a single manufacturer, which may limit the generalizability of the model to images acquired from different vendors or with varying acquisition protocols. These findings may not yet demonstrate robust generalizability across diverse clinical settings that utilize different CT vendors and different data. The model should be treated as a triage tool that requires local calibration when introduced to imaging environments that differ significantly from the training domain.

Future work should validate the model on datasets including multiple manufacturers and institutions. Additionally, a comprehensive comparison of various deep-learning models might provide deeper insights into the most suitable architecture for the automatic detection of depressed skull fractures.

Conclusion

The deep learning model developed in this study demonstrates very good diagnostic performance in detecting depressed skull fractures involving more than one cortex, which require surgical management. External validation showed that greater CT slice thickness and the use of a filter reduced model performance. This tool has the potential to assist in the automatic detection of depressed skull fractures, helping to prioritize patients for radiologist evaluation and enabling timely and appropriate treatment.
