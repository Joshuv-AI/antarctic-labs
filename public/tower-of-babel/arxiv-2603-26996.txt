FormalProofBench: Can Models Write Graduate Level Math Proofs That Are Formally Verified?
Nikil Ravi Kexing Ying Vasilii Nesterov Rayan Krishnan Elif Uskuplu Bingyu Xia Janitha Aswedige Langston Nashold, [2em] Vals AI École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne (EPFL) Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology Indiana University, Bloomington Independent Researcher

Abstract
We present FormalProofBench , a private 1 1 1 The benchmark is kept private to prevent data contamination. For transparency, we provide sample problems in Appendix A.1 ; in addition, the evaluation prompt and all harness code are available at this https url . benchmark designed to evaluate whether AI models can produce formally verified mathematical proofs at the graduate level. Each task pairs a natural-language problem with a Lean 4 formal statement, and a model must output a Lean proof accepted by the Lean 4 checker. FormalProofBench targets advanced undergraduate and graduate mathematics, with problems drawn from qualifying exams and standard textbooks across topics including analysis, algebra, probability, and logic. We evaluate a range of frontier models with an agentic harness, and find that the best-performing foundation model achieves 33.5% accuracy, with performance dropping rapidly after that. In addition to the accuracy numbers, we also provide empirical analysis of tool-use, failure modes, cost and latency, thereby providing a thorough evaluation of the formal-theorem proving abilities of frontier models.



1
Introduction

Large language models have made striking progress on mathematical reasoning.
Recent systems achieve high scores on competition-style benchmarks such as
MATH
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
, GSM8K
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
)
, the International Mathematical Olympiad, and more challenging math benchmarks like FrontierMath
(
Besiroglu et al. 2024
)
,
driven by advances in pre-training on mathematical corpora,
post-training with reinforcement learning from human and automated feedback,
and inference-time reasoning.

However, natural-language solutions can hide subtle logical errors that are difficult to audit by both human and LLM-based graders. A model may produce a plausible-sounding argument that omits a critical case or invokes a non-existent lemma, errors that even expert reviewers can overlook. This gap between
apparent
and
verified
mathematical competence motivates the use of formal verification as an evaluation methodology: a proof either type-checks or it does not.

Independently, the mathematics community has increasingly adopted interactive theorem provers. Lean 4
(
de Moura et al. 2021
)
and its mathematical library Mathlib
(
mathlib Community 2020
)
have experienced rapid growth, and formalization efforts such as the Liquid Tensor Experiment
(
Commelin & Topaz 2023
)
and the ongoing formalization of Fermat’s Last Theorem
(
Buzzard & Taylor 2025
)
have demonstrated that modern proof assistants can scale to deep, research-level mathematics. Concurrently, several recent works have explored the intersection of AI and formal mathematics, including Google DeepMind’s AlphaProof
(
Hubert et al. 2025
)
and the Aristotle system for auto-formalization
(
Achim et al. 2025
)
. These developments underscore both the opportunity and the need for rigorous benchmarks that measure whether language models can produce machine-checkable proofs for non-trivial mathematics.

We present
FormalProofBench
, a private benchmark designed to evaluate formal theorem-proving capability at the advanced undergraduate and graduate level. Each task pairs a natural-language problem statement with a corresponding formal statement in Lean 4; models must produce a proof term accepted by the Lean kernel. Problems are drawn from qualifying exams and standard textbooks spanning measure theory, real and functional analysis, algebra and commutative algebra, algebraic geometry, number theory, probability and stochastic analysis, and logic. All formalizations were written by experienced Lean users, including PhD students and domain experts, and underwent a secondary review process to guard against misformalization.

Unlike informal mathematics benchmarks, FormalProofBench provides a
binary
and
fully automated
correctness signal: a proof either compiles or it does not. This eliminates the need for model-graded or human-graded evaluation of solution correctness and ensures that no credit is awarded for plausible but flawed reasoning.

We evaluate a range of frontier models within an agent harness, providing each model with tools for searching Mathlib, executing Lean code, and submitting proofs, with up to 40 turns of interaction per problem.

Related Work

Informal mathematics benchmarking

Natural language mathematics (which we refer to as “informal”) have been widely used in evaluating and guiding LLM training. Two widely adopted benchmarks are MATH
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
, which consists of 12,500 competition-mathematics problems, and AIME, a popular benchmark measuring how well models do on American Invitational Mathematics Competition problems. More recently, benchmarks such as IMProofBench and FrontierMath have begun evaluating models on harder, research-level tasks.

Formal mathematics benchmarks

Several benchmarks evaluate neural theorem provers at different difficulty levels.
MiniF2F
(
Zheng et al. 2022
)
is a cross-system benchmark containing olympiad-level problems formalized in multiple proof assistants.
PutnamBench
(
Tsoukalas et al. 2024
)
extends this to collegiate competition mathematics, providing Putnam problems in Lean, Coq, and Isabelle.
ProofNet
(
Azerbayev et al. 2023
)
targets undergraduate-level theorem proving and autoformalization in Lean 3.
FormalMATH
(
Yu et al. 2025
)
offers a larger-scale benchmark that also addresses undergraduate mathematics.

Domain-specific benchmarks address particular mathematical subfields.
IneqComp
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
tests compositional reasoning with mathematical inequalities.
CombiBench
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
focuses on combinatorial mathematics with 100 competition problems formalized in Lean 4.
MATPBench
(
He et al. 2025
)
evaluates the capability of models in multi-modal reasoning in a formal setting.

Formal theorem proving

On the methods side, autoformalization with LLMs
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
demonstrated that large language models can translate natural-language mathematics into formal specifications.
GoedelProver
(
Lin et al. 2025
)
builds on this by training iteratively on autoformalized proofs, achieving strong results on MiniF2F and PutnamBench.
LeanDojo v2
(
Hsiang et al. 2025
)
provides an end-to-end framework for AI-assisted theorem proving in Lean 4, unifying repository extraction, LLM fine-tuning, and proof search.
Lean Workbook
(
Ying et al. 2025
)
provides approximately 57,000 formal-informal problem pairs, including IMO questions, generated via an iterative translation pipeline.
SorryDB
(
Letson et al. 2026
)
indexes incomplete proofs (“sorries”) from public Lean repositories, serving as a live benchmark based on publicly available unresolved sorries.

FormalProofBench aims to evaluate models’ formal theorem-proving abilities on tasks that are at the advanced undergraduate or graduate level, as opposed to high-school problems. In addition, we evaluate models with an agentic scaffold, instead of evaluating their ability to generate the proof in one single forward pass.

3
Benchmark overview

Each task in FormalProofBench consists of
(i) a mathematical problem in natural-language, and
(ii) the corresponding formal statement in Lean 4.
Given both, a model must construct a Lean proof accepted by the proof checker.

3.1
Data Curation

Problems were selected and formalized by a total of six formalizers who were PhD students or experts experienced in writing Lean 4 code. All of them had significant prior experience writing Lean, either via merged contributions to Mathlib, or prior large-scale formalization effort that they contributed to, and had varied mathematical interests, enabling us to formalize problems from different fields. Our problem-collection process used the following criteria:

•

Problems that required knowledge beyond olympiad-style / high-school style questions.

•

Problems that could be formalized using current Mathlib, and where the proof could also be written using current Mathlib.

•

Problems that would challenge a reasonably capable model as of late 2025, while still being relevant to mathematical work in that domain.

•

A diversity of mathematical domains.

Data quality and misformalization checks
While Lean 4 can act as a checker of whether a proof indeed proves a statement, a common problem in formal math benchmarks is misformalization of the
problem statement
, due to misaligned definitions, or incorrect translation to Lean 4. In other words, it is not guaranteed whether the formal statement faithfully expresses the intent of the informal statement. In addition, it is well-known that formal benchmarks often have issues, including misformalized problems and omitted side conditions
(
Ammanamanchi & Bhat 2025
)
.

Anticipating such issues, our data collection process included a secondary review phase of human review and revisions to problem statements. The secondary review phase was performed using standard code review processes on GitHub, and involved a review of the informal mathematics statement, natural language proof, and the formal statement by one or more people other than the original contributor of the problem.

Necessity of a search tool
Some problems in the benchmark have solutions that depend on a theorem that was recently added to
mathlib
. Although models could in principle reprove any needed lemmas, we include these problems to test a crucial capability for real-world formalization: reliably looking up relevant theorems.

3.2
Evaluation settings

A common problem with evaluations is that different models have different settings, such as reasoning effort, temperature,
top_p
, inference provider, etc. We standardize our evaluation settings by using the default sampling settings from each model provider, and by using the native model provider rather than a third-party inference provider.

4
Evaluation harness and tools

We provide models with the natural language problem statement, and the formalization of this statement, along with the necessary headers and context. We also provide the following tools:

•

lean_loogle
: A tool that models can use to search for theorems and definitions in Mathlib, via https://loogle.lean-lang.org.

•

lean_run_code
: Models can use this tool to execute any Lean code and get feedback, including whether it succeeded or failed, error descriptions, unsolved goals, type mismatches, warnings,
sorry
s, syntax errors, missing definitions/lemmas, and more.

•

submit_proof
: Used to indicate final submission of proof, starting with
by
.

In order to allow models to iterate on their solution before submitting, each problem allows up to 40 turns. A solution is considered valid if and only if the model calls
submit_proof
with a proof that successfully passes the Lean 4 kernel. We use Lean
v4.25.2
and Mathlib (at commit
c98ae54af00eaefe79c51b2b278361ca94e59bfb
). We also reject any attempt containing
axiom
or
local_instance
, since that would circumvent formal verification.

SYSTEM_PROMPT = """
You are a Lean 4 theorem proving expert and an expert in graduate level
mathematics. You will be given a graduate level mathematics theorem statement
and its Lean 4 formalization. You must output the Lean 4 proof (your output
must only have the final proof; just the part starting with ’by’).
Pay attention to the way the theorem statement is formalized; there may be
specific nuances in the way the statement has been formalized. This will be
important for you to understand the statement and prove it formally.
You will have access to a set of tools to help you prove the theorem. You
may rely on these tools if needed; be smart about how and when you use them.
You may use these tools however you please within the constraints of the
budget and turns, but you must call submit_proof with the final proof (the
part starting with ’by’) when done.
Use valid, correct Lean 4 syntax; the tools may be helpful if in doubt.
Key Lean 4 syntax rules:
- Use CamelCase for lemmas (e.g., Nat.dvd_mul, not nat.dvd_mul)
- Chain tactics with ’;’ not ’,’
- Rewrites require brackets: rw [h] or rw [step1, step2]
- Use ’constructor’ for conjunctions, not ’split’
- Use ’ring’ for ring axioms, ’linarith’ for linear arithmetic
- Existential witnesses: use 1, 2, 3 not use [1, 2, 3]
- No indentation on tactics
- In your final submission, never use ’sorry’, even in comments
or in the last step.
"""

5.1
Tool-use analysis

Figure 4:
Total tool calls (search vs. code execution) aggregated across
all 200 problems per model. Models vary widely in their tool-use strategies.

A common failure mode is excessive search: models sometimes spiral into repeated

lean_loogle
tool calls while attempting to find non-existent lemmas.
In contrast, stronger models tend to start executing code early and use search to fill gaps mid-proof.
We observe that higher rates of code execution feedback correlate with stronger
overall success. The qualitative reason for this is that models that tend to execute code get more feedback, and can therefore iterate towards the correct solution, only using the search tool when they actually need it (see Figure
4
).

Figure 5:
We observe a positive but modest correlation between the number of code execution calls and accuracy across models. Notable outliers include GPT 5, which achieves
relatively high accuracy with very few code execution calls, and Grok 4.1 Fast, which executes substantial code but
achieves low accuracy.

5.2
Latency and Cost

In addition to measuring accuracy, it is important to also measure the cost and latency associated with each model’s accuracy. Latency is measured as wall-clock time for the entire query execution (including retries), and cost is calculated from token usage and model-specific pricing rules; we also incorporate caching in our cost calculation. The average cost per test ranges from around 10 cents to more than a dollar. The latency in Table
2
indicates that models often take more than an hour (per-test average) when attempting to arrive at their final submission in this benchmark.

6
Conclusion and future work

We present FormalProofBench, a benchmark evaluating whether AI models can produce formally verified mathematical proofs at the graduate level. Our evaluation of frontier models reveals that the best-performing system, Claude Opus 4.5 (Thinking), achieves 33.5% accuracy, with performance dropping rapidly thereafter. Through detailed analysis of tool use patterns, we find that frequent iteration via code execution correlates strongly with success, while excessive search without execution often indicates a failing trajectory.

A benchmark at the intersection of multiple desiderata.

FormalProofBench occupies a unique position in the landscape of AI evaluation. Recent benchmarks such as FrontierMath prioritize including challenging problems of real world significance. FormalProofBench does this but also offers several additional advantages that are difficult to achieve simultaneously: (i) deterministic evaluation via the Lean 4 kernel eliminates the need for model-graded or human-graded correctness checks, (ii) contamination resistance, as the specific Lean formalizations we created are unlikely to appear in training data, (iii) practical evaluation costs, with per-problem evaluation completing in minutes to hours rather than days, and (iv) solvability, since we chose problems that can be correctly stated/solved in Lean using current Mathlib. This combination makes FormalProofBench particularly valuable for tracking progress as models continue to improve.

Implications for mathematical practice.

The gap between informal and formal mathematical competence reveals that formalization remains a significant bottleneck. However, the rapid pace of improvement across recent model releases suggests we may be approaching a threshold where AI systems become practical tools for working mathematicians. Success at formal verification could enable rapid exploration of proof strategies with immediate correctness feedback, democratize access to formal methods beyond the small community of proof assistant experts, and accelerate the formalization of research-level mathematics. As performance on benchmarks like FormalProofBench continues to improve, formal verification may transition from a specialized technique to a standard component of the mathematical research toolkit.

Future directions.

Several extensions would deepen our understanding of model capabilities:

1.

Exploring alternative tool configurations:
The
lean_loogle
tool was significantly less useful to models than the
lean_run_code
tool, and we observed that there were a non-trivial number of trajectories where models unsuccessfully over-used the Loogle tool. Alternative configurations including semantic search, the ability to
grep
local codebases, and tactic-level tools may reveal whether the current search interface is the bottleneck or whether deeper reasoning limitations persist.

2.

Ablating the informal problem statement:
We currently provide both the natural language statement and formal statement to models. Analyzing how performance changes with and without informal proof access would clarify whether models struggle primarily with proof generation or with translating mathematical ideas into Lean syntax.

3.

Broader diversity and coverage:
Our benchmark consists of 200 problems. Expanding coverage to additional mathematical domains and increasing problem count would improve statistical power and enable domain-specific analysis.

4.

Evaluating research-level formal theorem proving:
While FormalProofBench considers advanced undergraduate and graduate level mathematics, we were constrained by the current contents of
Mathlib
in what we could plausibly formalize. As more mathematics becomes formalized and available in
Mathlib
, future iterations of this benchmark could target research-level theorems, providing a pathway to evaluate AI systems on problems at the frontier of mathematical knowledge.
