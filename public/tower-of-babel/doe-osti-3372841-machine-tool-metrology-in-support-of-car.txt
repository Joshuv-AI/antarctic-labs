Introduction | Scope | Purpose
In the context of lab modernization, careful consideration must be given to existing technologies,
processes, and knowledge to support the ongoing production, maintenance, and improvement
of products for the nuclear enterprise. This consideration often involves analyzing manufacturing
processes both to pursue modernization or automation and to ensure the ability to maintain
manufacturing capability.

When critical components are involved, there is a delicate balance between modernization and
maintaining the ability to produce these components. Often, resistance to improving
manufacturing processes arises because specific knowledge or technical expertise about the
process has been lost over time. Sometimes, improving a legacy manufacturing process begins
with understanding why and how the process works in the first place. It is the responsibility of the
modern manufacturing engineer to thoroughly describe, document, and communicate the
mechanics of legacy manufacturing processes to guide modernization and operational
supervision efforts.

The purpose of this investigation is to support modernization and ensure the continued
manufacture of carbide inserts for special materials manufacturing by fully documenting the
legacy Micropoint Tool grinders used in their production. The primary task is to describe the
analysis, methods, and fixtures or tools used (or proposed) to quantify the performance of the
Micropoint Tool grinders with respect to insert grinding from a machine geometry perspective.
As a secondary goal, this report serves to inform methodology for the comparison of the
Micropoint Tool Grinders to other, perhaps more modern, machine tools.

Micropoint Tool Grinders are prime examples of classical manual machine tools and, as such,
cannot be examined with the same specification and verification practices as modern numerically
controlled machine tools. For instance, the Micropoint grinders lack linear scales and rotary
encoders to report axis positions. Consequently, special considerations must be made to
accommodate the modeling of geometric errors in the Micropoint machines, and indeed in
manual machines more generally. These considerations are discussed more fully in the Axes of
Motion, Adjustments, and Error Motion sections of this document. Manual machine process
considerations are also discussed.

Given these differences in machine design and process differences, it becomes clear why
meticulous documentation and performance analysis are essential. The careful documentation

Investigations into carbide insert production for SMMF (Special Materials Manufacturing) have
been ongoing. This report is aware of at least three endeavors in support of insert production,
below they are listed in rough chronological order. 1

 1. The Coborn CNC Grinder was purchased to replace and/or complement the Micropoint
 Tool Grinders. Anecdotally, it has been reported that the Coborn was purchased because
 it had a similar structure to that of a Micropoint Grinder. To this report’s knowledge no
 rigorous2 machine specification nor verification was completed during the Coborn
 Purchase. Subsequently, the inserts produced on the Coborn were deemed unsatisfactory.

 carbide insert geometry.

 lacked the depth of knowledge necessary for the team to understand the insert
 grinding inconsistencies.

 more robust script was created for measuring 55 Deg inserts with a 0.01 and 0.02

 been deployed for product inspection.

 3. An Insert Grinding comparison study was conducted. Inserts produced by Micropoint Tool
 Grinders, Shape-Master (vendor), and the Coborn were compared using the previously
 developed Micro-Vu script. 3

 a. Confirmed that the Micropoint inserts exhibited better geometry than the vendor

Inserts and Process Considerations
The inserts to be ground are made from a special carbide formulation designed for turning specific
materials. They are rhombic in shape, with a 55° included angle and two cutting edges. The inserts
are typically ground with edge radii of 0.010” or 0.020”, although other radii are occasionally
specified. Production volume varies from month to month; however, anecdotal estimates suggest
that a few hundred inserts are produced per year, though this figure remains unconfirmed. Inserts
are typically measured using Micro Vu vison systems. SMMF maintains a stock of ground inserts,
which are ultimately used for turning precision thin-walled parts.

Since the entire cutting edge of the insert is used, the form (or circular profile) of the insert is
considered the most critical functional specification and is quoted with a tolerance of 0.00005”.
The edge radius is regarded as the second most important feature, as it can be adjusted in the
tool path or by selectively choosing inserts for use. Tool rake is also specified but seems to be of
little concern to users and producers. However, the form specification proves to be the most
difficult specification to meet reportedly due to machine tool (grinder) error motion.

It should be noted that during production, Micropoint operators inspect every insert using a
Starret optical comparator and a Micro Vu system, adjusting the Micropoint as necessary. This
inspection process appears to be operator-dependent4 and represents a key process difference
compared to CNC processes such as those performed with the Coborn and is likely the ‘first-order’
reason the Micropoint outperforms the Coborn.

SMMF machinists inspect5 the inserts prior to use and determine their suitability as either
“roughing” inserts or “finishing” inserts based on their assessment of insert quality. However, no
quality data - such as pass/fail rates - is tracked.

This report recommends creating a part quality system to develop and track key metrics of the
carbide insert fabrication process and inventory.

Micropoint Tool Grinders
Brief History, Models in Use, Modifications Made
The Micropoint tool grinders were manufactured by DeVlieg Machine Co6 and date to the late
50s and early 60’s. There are two models of Micropoint Grinders in use – the primary difference is
the variant badged, “Gildemeister”, was originally equipped with a hydraulic motor for driving the
machine’s rotary axis. The other variant simply badged, “DeVlieg Micropoint’, had a manually
driven rotary axis.

 Figure 1. Micropoint Tool Grinder Variants
 DeVlieg Micropoint (Left), Gildemeister Micropoint (Right)

Of the three grinders currently in service (G3, G59, G175), the two DeVlieg Micropoints (G3, G59)
have in-line spindle drives, while the Gildemeister (G175) has a motor-over-spindle drive.

In the early 90’s MTS retrofit all Micropoints with electric rotary drives consisting of an electric
motor (various types have been used) and G2 pullies. The electric motors driving the rotary axis
(servos, PMDC, steppers) have been replaced a few times due to damage. They are often described
as being underpowered due to the low speed (1 RPM), size, and torque requirements. It’s possible
that an investigation resulting in recommendations to improve this arrangement will become
necessary to ensure continuous operation of the Micropoint grinders.7

Adjustments
Due to modern manufacturing practices, the current standardized machine tool metrology is
largely concerned with the specification and verification of computer-numerically-controlled, or
CNC, machine tools. However, the fundamentals of the stacked carriage machine tool model still
apply to manually operated machines, like the Micropoint tool grinders. It is important, though,

 to distinguish between axes of motion (such as spindles, linear stages, and rotary tables) and
 machine tool adjustments (such as offsets and angular adjustments).

 Axes contribute to the motion of the machine tool while work is being done on a work piece.
 Adjustments, on the other hand, affect the alignment of axes, tool and work piece offsets, and
 clearances - usually before the machining process. For example, on a traditional manual engine
 lathe, the angular adjustment of the compound (sometimes called the top-slide) sets the angular
 relationship between the linear D axis (compound infeed) and the other axes of the lathe (the
 saddle and cross-feed axes). The compound itself is considered an axis of motion, but the
 adjustment it sits upon, the compound angle, is an adjustment. See Figure 5. Compound Angle
 Adjustment on a Lathe. While it is permissible to infeed the compound during operation, adjusting

 Modern CNC machine tools often lack adjustment – adjustments have evolved into additional
 CNC axes. For example, the head tilt adjustment on knee-style manual milling machines has
 evolved into the A (or B, depending on configuration) axis on a modern 5-axis CNC milling
 machine. This is advantageous in the sense that it allows for manufacture of geometrically
 complex parts. However, the addition of axes comes at the expense of complexity and additional
 error motion.

When defining a stacked carriage model, there is a temptation to define every degree of freedom
as an axis of motion, as is often the case with modern CNC machine tools. However, since
adjustments typically do not move during the cut, they do not contribute to error motion in the
same way as axes of motion; instead, adjustments often affect the offset of the workpiece or tool,
or change the alignment of the machine’s axes, thereby influencing error motion sensitivity and
general carriage offsets.

For example, consider the compound angle adjustment from above in Figure 5. Compound Angle
Adjustment on a Lathe. If the compound angle was adjusted so that the D axis was parallel to the
Z axis of the lathe the tool tip motion would be more sensitive to X roll motion. That is, since the
tool tip is now further from the X axis, some angular errors are amplified at the tool tip. In terms
of machine tool error modeling, it’s tempting characterizing the compound angle adjustment as
a rotary axis or spindle. However, the compound adjustment cannot be repeatably indexed (with
precision), is not usually on bearings, and is a rigid9 joint during operation.

To negotiate an adjustment with a stacked carriage machine error model, it must be applied like a
fixed linear transform. Just as a tool offset may be added as a position vector, the compound
angular adjustment can be considered an angular offset between the X and D axis in the above
example10. In the case of the lathe compound example, a parallelism specification for the top of
the saddle to the ways could be used to approximate the contribution of (constant) angular error
of the compound motion to D-axis movement. That is, since the compound angular adjustment
is not considered an axis of motion, the error contribution could be accounted for in the (fixed)
geometry of the machine11 either by direct measurement of mating surfaces, reversal methods13,
or by measurement of the tool tip at discrete compound positions12. In general, each
adjustment must be considered and its impact on the tool/workpiece positioning must be
analyzed.

Cutting Angle Adjustments
Both the Lead Cutting Angle Adjustment (LCAA) and the End Cutting Angle Adjustment (ECAA)
are positional stops that control the rotational limit of the B axis. Functionally, these stops
determine the angles of the workpiece’s insert or cutting tool flanks - that is, the included angle
of the cutting tool. Since these are both stops (physical and limit switches), and since the B axis’s
rotational position is not otherwise relevant to (positional) errors in the workpiece, these
adjustments are not relevant for study.14

Relief Angle Adjustments
Relief angle adjustments control the front and side rake of the tool or workpiece to be ground.
The Constant Relief Angle Adjustment (CRAA), also called the conical relief angle adjustment,
allows for a conical grind by adjusting the angle of the B-axis (Y direction) with respect to the C-
axis (Z direction). The Variable Relief Angle Adjustment (VRAA), sometimes called the cylindrical

Tool Point Offset Centering Adjustment
The Tool Point Offset Centering Adjustment (TPOCA) moves the workpiece with respect to the
B-Axis along the x direction. In other words, the TPOCA centers the tool such that the centerline
of the tool coincides with the B-Axis. This adjustment is set for a given insert and is not traversed
during grinding. As a result, this adjustment only affects the workpiece offset.

amount. Therefore, both the TPOIA and the TPRA are considered adjustments.

Error Motion Effects
The machine tool metrology discipline dictates that every axis, also known as a carriage (the actual
physical body), exhibits six degrees of freedom (DOF) of motion, one of which is the desired or
‘correct’ motion. For example, a linear stage or carriage has an intended direction of motion that
ideally follows a perfectly straight line (the stage’s axis) but, in reality, moves with some error.
Generally, when characterizing a machine tool’s error motion, the goal is to determine the position
of the tool relative to the workpiece within a common coordinate system, taking into account all
error motions of the machine tool for every axis, in all six degrees of freedom. Typically, this
characterization involves defining a stacked carriage error model.

Each error term, such as straightness or angular error, is typically measured with respect to the
position along an axis. For example, straightness error is measured at discrete positions along a
linear axis and is usually defined as the range of deviation in a position versus deviation plot.
Positioning error is measured in both directions of travel and is typically assessed at specified
incremental positions. All errors are combined using the stacked carriage model and are
propagated through to a unified coordinate system as a function of commanded position.
However, machines that are not equipped with linear scales or rotary encoders, and are moved
manually rather than by CNC, present a unique challenge: How can errors be described and
combined if axis positions cannot be accurately (and repeatably) determined?

In the case of the Micropoint tool grinders axes positioning is not tracked. That is, a Micropoint
grinder has no linear scales or rotary encoders. However, each axis of motion, the traverse, the
rotary axis, and the spindle all move in a periodic fashion. The traverse axis oscillates; the grinding
spindle and rotary axis have rotational periodicity. Subsequently the error motion is more fully
described in a periodic fashion, for example in a deviation vs. time or deviations cycle perspective.
Subsequently, considerations of the periodic interactions of error motions could be considered.

Furthermore, since the primary interest is in the form error (and radius) of the inserts, rather than
in error motion compensation (such as updates to CNC compensation tables), a fully defined

would only be helpful in the case that the machine’s error motion could be programmatically
compensated for, or the position of each axis could be determined. Instead, a simplified error
model that considers periodic error motion will be presented, and the effect of each of the
Micropoint error motions (in isolation) on the insert geometry will be described in the sensitivity

Periodic Systematic Error Motion

Since the Micropoints lack encoders or positional feedback, each error motion cannot be defined
relative to a nominal (or commanded) position. For example, a linear positioning error term such
as ᵃᵆᵆ (position of the X-axis along the x direction) is typically expressed as a function of the
commanded X position, ᵃᵆᵆ ( ᵄᵅ ). However, for the Micropoint grinders, this investigation
proposes that each error term is fundamentally a function of time, such as ᵃᵆᵆ (ᵆ).

As previously shown, a sensitivity analysis examined the effects of each error motion on the radius
of the tool to be ground. Functionally, this means the relationship between each individual error
motion (in isolation) and the functional point is known. It is now reasonable to consider the
stacking, or combined effects, of the error motions. Since our error motions are functions of time,
we will now consider the error motion stack-up using a time-based approach.

synchronized. This mismatch results in a phase shift in the interactions between the tool and the
workpiece – meaning that the resultant form error in inserts is likely periodic18. This effect is
best described as the ‘blinker effect’19. Further, by varying the frequency of the B-Axis, more
consistent form errors in shorter spark-out periods might be obtainable20.

A final observation is that the machine’s travel is limited to only half a rotation of the B-axis and
just 1–2 inches of X-axis movement, which means error motions are likely to be smaller within this
restricted region of each axis – in the same a way that a board gets ‘straighter’ if cut into smaller
segments.

Overall, looking at how these error motions stack up over time within the grinder’s limited range,
we find that the resulting form errors are likely both periodic and minimized. Since the B-axis and
X-axis only move within a small area, these errors stay localized, helping maintain more consistent
grinding results.

Instrumentation & Fixture Design
One of the main challenges when designing measurement fixtures for the Micropoint (and many
older machines) is the lack of flat or ‘normal’ surfaces to work with. While it can be tempting to
overcomplicate the process or design overly specialized fixtures, the initial concepts for the
Micropoint are intentionally simple and do not require an extensive design effort at this stage.

It is important to note that, since this work was completed as part of an internship with limited
equipment and time, no actual measurements were taken, and no fixtures were fabricated. The
ideas presented here are proposed approaches only; to implement them successfully, a
thorough walkthrough of all relevant dimensions and detailed design considerations would
be necessary.

Below, each axis is discussed in turn, outlining the conceptual fixture and instrumentation
requirements for measurement, along with key factors to consider for each setup.

X-Axis Measurement Notes
  Measuring X-axis error motion is largely unnecessary. Inserts are already produced
 accurately, so additional error data will not be used to improve insert grinding.
 Additionally, the X-axis drive, housed inside the machine frame, must run
 continuously to prevent warmup periods. As a result, measuring the thermal stability
 of the X-axis is unlikely to provide operational benefits, since its warmup period is
 long and wear on components cannot be reduced by limiting running hours.

  The API xD Laser System must remain stationary relative to the Micropoint’s frame. If the
 laser is mounted on a tripod on the floor, ensure it shares the same inertial frame as the
 machine frame, or be reasonably confident that the machine is not moving or rocking.

  The 5DOF API ‘optics package’ should be mounted in-line with the definition of the X-Axis
 coordinate system’s origin, oX, to avoid abbe error in obtained error motion terms. In
 general ensure consistency with error term directionality (sign +/-).

  It may be necessary to ‘trick’ the API software into taking many samples (~<1mm travel
 moves manually) or to try and obtain time-based error motion terms

B-Axis Measurement Notes
  The primary reason to measure the B-Axis is to assess its warm-up period, as the
 modified rotary axis drive is vulnerable. Specifically, measuring the rotary warm-up
 period can help avoid the need for continuous 24/7 operation. This approach is
 feasible because, due to the machine’s construction, the B-Axis is likely to have a
 much shorter warm-up period.

  The X-axis should be rigidly locked (some immobilizer might be needed. Shims, clamps
 etc.) when using this measurement setup.

  The ‘centering fixture’ proposed has oversized mounting holes to facilitate centering the
 fixture to the axis of rotation by means of dial indicator.

  The proposed hanging fixture does not allow for measurement of axial (Eyb) errors, as this
 type of error does not significantly affect changes in insert radius. However, the fixture
 should be modified to include axial (Eyb) thermal growth measurements as part of the
 warm-up period assessment (or simply put measure axial displacement with a cap gauge
 arrangement aimed at the spindle nose itself)
