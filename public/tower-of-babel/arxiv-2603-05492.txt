Ansatz-Free Learning of Lindbladian Dynamics In Situ
Petr Ivashkov, Nikita Romanov, Weiyuan Gong, Andi Gu, Hong-Ye Hu, Susanne F. Yelin

Abstract
Characterizing the dynamics of open quantum systems at the level of microscopic interactions and error mechanisms is essential for calibrating quantum hardware, designing robust simulation protocols, and developing tailored error-correction methods. Under Markovian noise/dissipation, a natural characterization approach is to identify the full Lindbladian generator that gives rise to both coherent (Hamiltonian) and dissipative dynamics. Prior protocols for learning Lindbladians from dynamical data assumed pre-specified interaction structure, which can be restrictive when the relevant noise channels or control imperfections are not known in advance. In this paper, we present the first sample-efficient protocol for learning sparse Lindbladians without assuming any a priori structure or locality. Our protocol is ancilla-free, uses only product-state preparations and Pauli-basis measurements, and achieves near-optimal time resolution, making it compatible with near-term experimental capabilities. The final sample complexity depends on linear-system conditioning, which we find empirically to be moderate for a broad class of physically motivated models. Together, this provides a systematic route to scalable characterization of open-system quantum dynamics, especially in settings where the error mechanisms of interest are unknown.

III
Structure learning

The core challenge of structure learning is that even if the Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
is sparse, the time-evolution channel
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
can become dense once
t
is sufficiently large. To reveal the Lindbladian structure, we therefore focus on the short-time regime, where the growth of the operator structure of
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
is governed by the first few terms of the Taylor expansion

e^{\mathcal{L}t}(\rho)\;=\;\rho\;+\;t\mathcal{L}(\rho)\;+\;\tfrac{t^{2}}{2}\mathcal{L}^{2}(\rho)\;+\;\mathcal{O}(t^{3}).

(9)

It is instructive to consider the
\chi
-matrix representation of
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
,

e^{\mathcal{L}t}(\rho)\;=\;\sum_{i,j}\chi_{ij}(t)\,P_{i}\rho P_{j}.

(10)

The diagonal entries of the (time-dependent)
\chi
-matrix are known as the Pauli error rates
\{\chi_{ii}(t)\}_{i}
and form a probability distribution. At
t=0
, all rates are
0
except
\chi_{00}(0)=1
, corresponding to the identity channel. As
t
increases, the weight of
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{2}
in the Taylor expansion grows, giving rise to new Pauli error rates at the expense of the decreasing identity component, as shown in
Figure

1
(b). The key observation that enables our Lindbladian structure learning algorithm is that the first two derivatives of these Pauli error rates at
t=0
can be directly related to the Lindbladian terms with non-vanishing coefficients. Indeed, by comparing terms in
Equations

9
and
10
, we can notice that
\mathcal{L}(\rho)\;=\;\sum_{i,j}\chi_{ij}^{(1)}\,P_{i}\rho P_{j}
, where we defined
\chi_{ij}^{(1)}=\derivative{t}\chi_{ij}(0)
. Similarly,
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\rho)\;=\;\sum_{i,j}\chi_{ij}^{(2)}\,P_{i}\rho P_{j}
with
\chi_{ij}^{(2)}=\derivative[2]{t}\chi_{ij}(0)
. In
Appendix

D
we show that

\displaystyle\chi_{ii}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=a_{ii},

\displaystyle\text{if }a_{ii}>0,

(11)

\displaystyle\chi_{ii}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\chi_{ii}^{(2)}\geq 2h_{i}^{2},

\displaystyle\text{if }a_{ii}=0.

(12)

Therefore, the presence of a Pauli
P_{i}
in the dissipator structure
\mathcal{S}_{D}
leads to a non-vanishing first derivative
\chi_{ii}^{(1)}
, while the presence of a Pauli
P_{i}
in the Hamiltonian-only structure
\mathcal{S}_{H}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{D}
leads to a non-vanishing second derivative
\chi_{ii}^{(2)}
. Hence, by identifying all first and second derivatives above appropriate thresholds, one is guaranteed to identify a superset of all Pauli terms present in the Lindbladian, as shown in
Figure

1
(c,d).

The key subroutine that allows the estimation of Pauli rate derivatives is the protocol of
35
(see
Figure

1
(a)), who showed that, nontrivially, all Pauli rates can be estimated efficiently using the classical population recovery algorithm. The protocol uses only product-state preparations and single-qubit Pauli measurements to estimate all diagonal rates simultaneously, producing estimators
\widehat{\chi_{ii}}(t)
satisfying

\bigl|\widehat{\chi_{ii}}(t)-\chi_{ii}(t)\bigr|\leq\varepsilon_{s},\qquad\forall\,i,

(13)

with probability at least
1-\delta
, using

\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon_{s}^{-2}\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon_{s}\delta}\right)
queries to the
n
-qubit channel
\mathcal{E}_{t}
. Importantly, the protocol outputs at most
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon_{s})
nonzero estimates
\widehat{\chi}_{ii}(t)
and treats all other rates as zero.

Naively, one could estimate the derivatives
\chi^{(1)}_{ii}
and
\chi^{(2)}_{ii}
via finite differences by
\chi^{(1)}_{ii}\;\approx\;\tfrac{1}{t}\bigl[\chi_{ii}(t)-\chi_{ii}(0)\bigr]
, and similarly for
\chi^{(2)}_{ii}
using a second-order stencil. However, identifying whether
\chi^{(1)}_{ii}\gtrsim\eta
requires estimating
\chi^{(1)}_{ii}
to additive precision
\Theta(\eta)
, and controlling the finite-difference bias forces
t\propto\eta
. In other words, it would require resolving vanishingly short dynamics as the desired precision increases, leading to infeasible experimental requirements.

To alleviate impractical time resolution, we use Chebyshev interpolation, which achieves the desired derivative estimates with exponentially better time resolution in terms of
\eta
. Specifically, we fix a set of
r{+}1
carefully chosen time points
\{t_{m}\}_{m=0}^{r}
within a short-time window
[0,\tau_{\max}]
. At each time
t_{m}
, we estimate all Pauli error rates to a fixed accuracy
\varepsilon_{s}
using the population recovery protocol. Finally, for each observed rate, we fit a low-degree Chebyshev interpolant to the noisy time series
\{\widehat{\chi}_{ii}(t_{m})\}_{m=0}^{r}
and then take the first and second derivatives of the fitted polynomial at
t=0
as the estimators
\widehat{\chi}^{(1)}_{ii}
and
\widehat{\chi}^{(2)}_{ii}
, as shown in
Figure

1
(c).

The resulting derivative-estimation error has two contributions: a systematic interpolation bias controlled by higher-order derivatives of
\chi_{ii}(t)
, and the nodewise sampling noise
\varepsilon_{s}
. For an
M
-sparse Lindbladian, as in

1
, we show in
Section

C.1
the uniform derivative bound

\Big|\frac{d^{\ell}}{dt^{\ell}}\chi_{ii}(t)\Big|\;\leq\;(2M)^{\ell},\qquad\ell\geq 0,

(14)

which implies that the Pauli error rates are uniformly smooth on time scales of order
1/M
. Using this bound, the interpolation bias can be kept below the target scale by choosing the time window
\tau_{\max}=\Theta(1/M)
and polynomial degree
r=\Theta(\log(M/\eta))
. To control the contribution of sampling noise, we enforce that the Pauli-rate estimates returned by population recovery satisfy

\varepsilon_{s}=\widetilde{\Theta}(\eta^{2}/M^{2})
.

With the choice of parameters above, the differentiation step returns
\widehat{\chi}^{(1)}_{ii}
to additive accuracy
\eta/2
and
\widehat{\chi}^{(2)}_{ii}
to additive accuracy
\eta^{2}
, which suffices to resolve the nonzero Lindbladian terms according to
Equations

11
and
12
. In particular,
Equation

11
guarantees that

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\;:=\;\bigl\{\,P_{i}:\ \widehat{\chi}^{(1)}_{ii}\geq\eta/2\,\bigr\}

(15)

includes all (and only) dissipator terms with
a_{ii}\geq\eta
. Similarly, according to
Equation

12
,

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}\;:=\;\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\ \cup\ \bigl\{\,P_{i}:\ \widehat{\chi}^{(2)}_{ii}\geq\eta^{2}\,\bigr\}

(16)

contains all Hamiltonian terms with
|h_{i}|\geq\eta
, as shown in
Figure

1
(d). Evidently,
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
may contain dissipator terms that are not present in the Hamiltonian. Moreover, it may further include spurious Pauli terms arising from second-order dissipative couplings that induce
\widehat{\chi}^{(2)}_{ii}>\eta^{2}
. However, the worst-case number of such false positives is bounded by the total number of Pauli-rate estimates returned across all sampled times, i.e.,
|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}|\;=\;\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(M^{2}/\eta^{2}\right)
, and therefore remains polynomial in the problem parameters. Moreover, such false positives will be eliminated in the subsequent coefficient-learning stage, which accurately estimates all coefficients corresponding to
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
and filters out any below the desired threshold
\eta
.

IV
Coefficient learning

Having identified supersets
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
that contain all nonvanishing Hamiltonian and dissipative Pauli terms, the remaining task is to estimate their associated coefficients to a target accuracy
\varepsilon
.

Our coefficient-learning protocol builds on a standard linear-response relation: short-time derivatives of expectation values provide linear access to the Lindbladian coefficients
44
;
43
;
82
. For any observable
O
and initial state
\rho
, the Heisenberg evolution satisfies

\left.\derivative{t}\expectationvalue{O(t)}\right|_{t=0}=\tr\left(\rho\,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\right),

(17)

which is linear in the coefficients
\{h_{i}\}_{P_{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}}
and
\{a_{ij}\}_{P_{i},P_{j}\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}}
. Substituting the Pauli expansion of the adjoint Lindbladian makes this linear dependence explicit:

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\expectationvalue{O(t)}\bigg|_{t=0}=\sum_{P_{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}}h_{i}\,\tr{i[P_i,O]\rho}

(18)

\displaystyle+\sum_{P_{i},P_{j}\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}}a_{ij}\Bigl(\tr{P_j O P_i\rho}-\tfrac{1}{2}\tr{\acomm{P_j P_i}{O}\rho}\Bigr).

Fixing a probe
(\rho,O)
therefore yields a single linear constraint on the unknown coefficients {
h_{i},a_{ij}
}. Obtaining such constraints over multiple probes gives a linear system
\mathbf{d}=C\mathbf{x}
, where
\mathbf{x}
collects all the unknown coefficients,
\mathbf{d}
collects the measured observable derivatives, and each row of the design matrix
C
is fixed classically according to
Equation

18
. We consider Pauli probes, meaning that
O\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a Pauli observable and
\rho
is a Pauli eigenstate of the form
\rho_{\pm Q}=\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}(I\pm Q),\,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
Then, the entries of
C
can be determined efficiently using Pauli algebra. Finally, if
C
has full rank, the coefficients can be uniquely recovered by inversion.

To select an informationally complete set of probes, we introduce
Lindbladian patchwise Pauli tomography
.
Within this framework, we restrict attention to probes supported on subsystems (“patches”) induced by the candidate Lindbladian terms (
Figure

2
(a)). Note that multiple terms can induce the same patch if their supports are identical. Concretely, we consider the following family of patches:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{\,\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{i}\right):P_{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}\,\bigr\}

(19)

\displaystyle\cup\bigl\{\,\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{i}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{j}\right):P_{i},P_{j}\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\,\bigr\}.

For each patch
T\in\mathcal{T}
, let
\mathcal{P}_{T}
denote the Pauli operators supported on
T
, and collect all probes
(\rho_{\pm Q},O)
with
O,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{T}
across all
T\in\mathcal{T}
. In
Section

E.2
we show that the resulting collection of derivatives

\tr\left(\rho_{\pm Q}\,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\right)

uniquely determines all Lindbladian coefficients consistent with the supports
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
.
Equivalently, patchwise Pauli probes yield a tomographically complete linear system of dimension

\widehat{M}\;=\;|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}|+|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}|^{2},

(20)

whose associated design matrix
C
is invertible. Enumerating all such patchwise probes requires classical time
\mathcal{O}(16^{k}\,\widehat{M})
, which could be far more tractable than a brute-force search over all
k
-local Pauli probes that scales as
\mathcal{O}(16^{k}n^{k})
.

Having identified a sufficient set of probes, we now estimate the corresponding time derivatives
(cf.
Equation

17
) from quantum queries. Similarly to the derivative estimation for
structure-learning (
Section

III
), we measure

f(t)\coloneq\tr\bigl(\rho\,e^{t\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}}(O)\bigr)
at several time points, fit the data using Chebyshev
polynomial interpolation, and compute the required derivatives via numerical differentiation in classical post-processing, as shown in
Figure

2
(c). The key additional input is that, for Pauli probes, the smoothness of
f(t)
can be controlled via the
dual interaction graph
of
\mathcal{L}

(a Lindbladian analogue of the Hamiltonian dual graph
43
;
44
). Concretely, we form a graph whose vertices correspond to the individual Pauli-basis summands of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
, and connect two vertices whenever
their qubit supports overlap, as illustrated in
Figure

2
(b); let
\mathfrak{d}
denote its maximum degree (see
Section

C.2
for a formal definition and representative physical models that have
\mathfrak{d}=O(1)
). Intuitively,
\mathfrak{d}
quantifies how quickly the
support of
(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})^{\ell}(O)
can grow: each application of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
only mixes
O
with Pauli components whose supports overlap it, and
\mathfrak{d}
bounds the number of such neighbors at each step.
Concretely, in
Section

C.2
, we show that for our patchwise Pauli probes,

\Big|\derivative[\ell]{t}f(t)\Big|\;\leq\;\bigl\|(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})^{\ell}(O)\bigr\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;(2\mathfrak{d})^{\ell}\,\ell!,

(21)

implying that
f(t)
is well approximated by a low-degree polynomial for
t\in[0,\tau_{\max}]
with
\tau_{\max}=\Theta(1/\mathfrak{d})
.
Thus, using Chebyshev interpolation, one can estimate each required derivative to accuracy
\varepsilon_{d}
with

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathfrak{d}^{2}/\varepsilon_{d}^{2})
queries per probe. Applying this procedure probe-by-probe yields a naive coefficient learning algorithm with a quantum query complexity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{M}\,\mathfrak{d}^{2}/\varepsilon_{d}^{2})
, saturating the standard quantum limit up to polylogarithmic factors.

The derivative-estimation step can be parallelized using the shadow process tomography method of

k
is sufficiently small. Because every patch in
\mathcal{T}
has a size of at most

k

Finally, after estimating the required derivatives, we assemble the square linear system

.

V
Optimality of time resolution

The minimum evolution time required by our Lindbladian learning algorithm is
t_{\min}=\widetilde{\Theta}(M^{-1})
. A natural question is whether one can work with larger minimum times (i.e., a coarser time resolution), ideally approaching a constant, which would be more experimentally convenient. Unfortunately, we show that this is impossible even for the simpler task of learning only the dissipator support
\mathcal{S}_{D}
. In particular, for dissipator-structure learning, enforcing a time resolution
t\gtrsim\Theta(M^{2/\kappa-1})
(for integer
\kappa\geq 2
) can incur an exponential-in-
n
sample-complexity overhead (see
Appendix

F
for more details).

To show the
\Omega(\exp(n))
sample-complexity lower bound for dissipator-structure learning when

t\gtrsim\Theta(M^{2/\kappa-1})
, we first specify the access model. We consider protocols that perform experiments of the following form:
(i) prepare an arbitrary single-copy
n
-qubit input state
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
,
(ii) evolve it under the unknown Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
for some time
t\geq t_{0}=\Theta(M^{2/\kappa-1})
(where
t_{0}
is the assumed time resolution),
and (iii) perform an arbitrary single-copy measurement on the output state

\rho_{\mathrm{out}}=e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\rho_{\mathrm{in}})
. This access model strictly strengthens the experimental capabilities assumed in

Theorem

D.10
(product-state preparations and single-qubit Pauli measurements).
Hence, the lower bound proved here also holds for our structure-learning setting.

The high-level idea behind our lower bound is as follows. We construct two purely dissipative
Lindbladians,
\mathcal{L}_{0,\kappa}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1,\kappa}
, with distinct dissipator supports.
Each instance has
M=\Theta(n^{\kappa})
nonzero terms of magnitude at least
\eta=1/4
.
Since any procedure that learns the dissipator support would, in particular, distinguish between these
two cases, it suffices to lower bound the sample complexity of the corresponding Lindbladian
distinguishing task. A key feature of our construction is rapid mixing. For any input state
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
and any

t\geq t_{0}=\Theta(M^{2/\kappa-1})
, the evolution under either hypothesis drives the system
exponentially close to the maximally mixed state. Specifically, for all
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
and both

\mathcal{L}\in\{\mathcal{L}_{0,\kappa},\mathcal{L}_{1,\kappa}\}
,

\left\|e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\rho_{\mathrm{in}})-\frac{I}{2^{n}}\right\|_{1}\leq 2^{-n}.

(22)

By the triangle inequality, the two hypotheses therefore produce output states whose trace distance is
at most
\delta_{\mathrm{tr}}\coloneq\mathcal{O}(2^{-n})
, regardless of the chosen inputs. Standard bounds from quantum hypothesis testing imply that any
m
-sample (possibly adaptive) protocol can achieve a distinguishing advantage of at most
\mathcal{O}(m\delta_{\mathrm{tr}})

48
;
47
; equivalently, constant success probability
requires
m=\Omega(1/\delta_{\mathrm{tr}})=\Omega(2^{n})
samples. Consequently, at time resolution
t_{0}\geq\Theta(M^{2/\kappa-1})
(for any integer
\kappa\geq 2
), there exists a
family of Lindbladians with
M=\Theta(n^{\kappa})
nonzero dissipator terms for which dissipator-structure learning
requires an exponential number of samples.

VI
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, we presented the first ansatz-free Lindbladian learning algorithm that assumes no prior knowledge about the generator’s locality or structure. Our protocol efficiently reconstructs both the Hamiltonian and dissipative components of an
M
-sparse Lindbladian in an experimentally motivated, ancilla-free setting restricted to product-state preparations, time evolution, and Pauli-basis measurements. This provides a practical tool for identifying unknown error mechanisms and controlling non-idealities, with potential relevance to tailored error correction/mitigation, noisy quantum metrology, and analog simulation.

Our results highlight several open theoretical questions. First, the overall sample-complexity dependence on the resolution
\eta
of our end-to-end protocol is currently bottlenecked by Hamiltonian structure learning, scaling as
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\eta^{-4})
. This quartic dependence stems from the fact that Hamiltonian terms contribute to the relevant Pauli-error rates (
\{\chi_{ii}\}
) only at second order in time. This raises the question of whether Hamiltonian-support identification—and, by extension, Lindbladian structure learning—can be achieved with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\eta^{-2})
samples in comparable access models. If so, combining such a structure-learning improvement with the coefficient-learning stage developed here would yield an ansatz-free Lindbladian learning protocol with overall
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\eta^{-2})
sample complexity, matching the standard-quantum-limit scaling. Second, our coefficient-learning stage depends on a conditioning factor
\nu
that controls the stability of the final linear system; while we present numerical evidence that this factor remains moderate for a wide family of lattice models, it will be important to prove that it remains bounded in the general case.

Beyond the in situ regime, it is also important to understand the power of additional control and quantum memory in ansatz-free Lindbladian learning. In the follow-up work
75
, we show that adaptive quantum error correction can recover Heisenberg-limited scaling for learning the Hamiltonian component (up to overlap with dissipator terms) while achieving standard-quantum-limited scaling for end-to-end Lindbladian learning in the ansatz-free setting.

Chebyshev interpolation is instrumental to our structure and coefficient learning algorithms. We therefore provide a brief overview based on standard references

B.1
Overview and intuition

Given a function
f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
, we would like to approximate it by a polynomial. The Weierstrass approximation theorem guarantees that any continuous function on a bounded interval can be approximated arbitrarily well by a polynomial. The question is, how do we construct such a polynomial systematically and with good numerical properties.

A natural idea is to pick
r{+}1
points
t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{r}\in[a,b]
, evaluate
f(t)
at those points, and then find a polynomial
p(t)
of degree at most
r
that exactly fits the data:

p(t_{m})=f(t_{m}),\qquad m=0,\dots,r.

(41)

Such a polynomial, called the interpolant, always exists and is unique. It can be written explicitly in the Lagrange form

p(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{r}f(t_{m})\,\ell_{m}(t),\qquad\ell_{m}(t)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\neq m\end{subarray}}^{r}\frac{t-t_{n}}{t_{m}-t_{n}},

(42)

where
\ell_{m}(t)
are the cardinal basis polynomials satisfying
\ell_{m}(t_{n})=\delta_{mn}
. If
f
is
(r{+}1)
-times continuously differentiable, the remainder theorem guarantees that the pointwise interpolation error can be bounded as follows:

|f(t)-p(t)|\;\leq\;\frac{\|f^{(r{+}1)}\|_{\infty}}{(r+1)!}\;\prod_{m=0}^{r}|t-t_{m}|,

(43)

where
\|f^{(r{+}1)}\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{t\in[a,b]}|f^{(r+1)}(t)|
. The second term,
\prod_{m=0}^{r}|t-t_{m}|
, depends entirely on the choice of nodes. For example, the naïve choice of equispaced nodes often leads to large oscillations near the boundaries–the Runge phenomenon–and a rapidly growing error term. To obtain the best possible set of nodes for a fixed degree
r
, one can minimize the worst-case size of this node-dependent product. On the standard interval
[-1,1]
, the unique minimizer is given by the set of Gauss-Chebyshev nodes

z_{m}=\cos\left(\frac{2m+1}{r+1}\,\frac{\pi}{2}\right),\qquad m=0,\dots,r,

(44)

which are precisely the roots of the
(r{+}1)
-st Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind
T_{r+1}(z)
. Chebyshev polynomials can be defined recursively as

T_{0}(z)=1,\qquad T_{1}(z)=z,\qquad T_{r+1}(z)=2zT_{r}(z)-T_{r-1}(z).

(45)

Gauss-Chebyshev nodes
\{Z_{m}\}_{m=0}^{r}
minimize the node-dependent factor for a fixed
r
. Mapping back to the physical interval
[a,b]
via
t_{m}:=\tfrac{b-a}{2}\,z_{m}+\tfrac{a+b}{2}
, the polynomial
p(t)
fitted through the data
\{(t_{m},f(t_{m})\}_{m=0}^{r}
obeys

\max_{t\in[a,b]}|f(t)-p(t)|\;\leq\;\frac{2\|f^{(r{+}1)}\|_{\infty}}{(r+1)!}\,\Big(\frac{b-a}{4}\Big)^{r+1}.

(46)

Although the resulting polynomial has guaranteed approximation accuracy, the Lagrange form (
42
) is numerically unstable to evaluate
p(t)
for large
r
. An equivalent but more stable and computationally efficient representation is obtained by expanding
p(t)
in the Chebyshev basis,

p(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{r}c_{n}\,T_{n}\left(z(t)\right),

(47)

where we map
[a,b]\mapsto[-1,1]
via
z(t)=\tfrac{2t-(a+b)}{b-a}
and the coefficients
\{c_{n}\}
are called the Chebyshev coefficients. These coefficients can readily be computed in
O(r\log r)
time by applying the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to the function values
\{f(t_{m})\}
. Equivalently, by the discrete orthogonality of the Chebyshev polynomials on Chebyshev nodes, the coefficients admit an explicit form

c_{0}\;=\;\frac{1}{r+1}\sum_{m=0}^{r}f(t_{m}),\qquad c_{n}\;=\;\frac{2}{r+1}\sum_{m=0}^{r}f(t_{m})\,T_{n}(z_{m}),\quad n=1,\ldots,r.

(48)

If our only goal were to approximate
f(t)
on
[a,b]
, we would be done here. However, we are interested not in
f(t)
itself, but in its first and second derivatives at
x=0
. In addition, the data points
\{f(t_{i})\}
are obtained from noisy measurements with an uncertainty
\pm\varepsilon_{s}
. In the following, we will show that the Chebyshev interpolant can be used to estimate the first and second derivatives of
f
at
t=0
from noisy function evaluations. The analysis follows the approach of
43
, which we generalize to include the estimation of the second derivative.

B.2
Derivative analysis of the Chebyshev interpolant

From now on, the interval of interest corresponds to a finite time window
[0,\tau_{\max}]
, and the map to the standard domain
[-1,1]
is given by
z(t)=\frac{2t}{\tau_{\max}}-1
. Let

\widehat{p}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{r}\widehat{c}_{n}\,T_{n}\big(z(t)\big)

(49)

denote the degree-
r
Chebyshev interpolant constructed from the noisy function estimates

\{\widehat{f}(t_{m})\}_{m=0}^{r}
at the Gauss-Chebyshev nodes
\{t_{m}\}_{m=0}^{r}
. We assume that the measurement noise is uniformly bounded and controllable:

|\widehat{f}(t_{m})-f(t_{m})|\leq\varepsilon_{s}.

(50)

The corresponding derivative estimators at
t=0
(i.e.
z=-1
) are

\displaystyle\widehat{p}^{\,\,\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{r}\widehat{c}_{n}\,\derivative{t}T_{n}(z(t))\Big|_{t=0}=\frac{2}{\tau_{\max}}\sum_{n=1}^{r}(-1)^{n+1}n^{2}\,\widehat{c}_{n},

(51)

\displaystyle\widehat{p}^{\,\,\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{r}\widehat{c}_{n}\,\derivative[2]{t}T_{n}(z(t))\Big|_{t=0}=\frac{4}{3\tau_{\max}^{2}}\sum_{n=2}^{r}(-1)^{n}n^{2}(n^{2}-1)\,\widehat{c}_{n},

(52)

where we used
T_{n}^{\prime}(-1)=(-1)^{n+1}n^{2}
and
T_{n}^{\prime\prime}(-1)=(-1)^{n}n^{2}(n^{2}-1)/3
(see, e.g.,
(
64
, §2.4.5)
). By substituting the explicit form of the Chebyshev coefficients from
Equation

48
, the derivative estimators can be expressed as weighted sums of the sampled function values:

\widehat{p}^{\,\prime}(0)\;=\;\sum_{m=0}^{r}\alpha^{(1)}_{m}\,\widehat{f}(t_{m}),\qquad\widehat{p}^{\,\prime\prime}(0)\;=\;\sum_{m=0}^{r}\alpha^{(2)}_{m}\,\widehat{f}(t_{m}),

(53)

where the weights
\{\alpha^{(1)}_{m}\}
and
\{\alpha^{(2)}_{m}\}
can be precomputed and reused for all derivative estimators on the same Chebyshev grid:

\displaystyle\alpha^{(1)}_{m}\;

\displaystyle=\;\frac{4}{\tau_{\max}(r{+}1)}\,\sum_{n=1}^{r}(-1)^{n+1}n^{2}\,T_{n}(z_{m})

(54)

\displaystyle\alpha^{(2)}_{m}\;

\displaystyle=\;\frac{8}{3\tau_{\max}^{2}(r{+}1)}\,\sum_{n=2}^{r}(-1)^{n}n^{2}(n^{2}{-}1)\,T_{n}(z_{m}),

(55)

Then, the following result quantifies the total error of the derivative estimators defined above.

Theorem B.1

(Noisy derivative estimators)
.

Let
\tau_{\max}>0
and
f
be
(r{+}1)
-times continuously differentiable on
[0,\tau_{\max}]
. Suppose the available noisy samples
\{\widehat{f}(t_{m})\}_{m=0}^{r}
on the set of Gauss-Chebyshev nodes
\{t_{m}\}_{m=0}^{r}\subset[0,\tau_{\max}]
have bounded uniform measurement noise:
|\widehat{f}(t_{m})-f(t_{m})|\leq\varepsilon_{s}
. Then, the estimators
\widehat{p}^{\,\prime}(0)
and
\widehat{p}^{\,\prime\prime}(0)
defined in
Equation

51
and
Equation

52

\displaystyle\bigl|\widehat{p}^{\,\prime}(0)-f^{\prime}(0)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{5r^{3}}{2\tau_{\max}}\,\varepsilon_{s}}_{\text{noise term}}\;+\;\underbrace{2(r+1)^{2}\tau_{\max}^{r}\frac{\|f^{(r+1)}\|_{\infty}}{r!}}_{\text{bias term}},

(56)

\displaystyle\bigl|\widehat{p}^{\,\prime\prime}(0)-f^{\prime\prime}(0)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{r^{5}}{\tau_{\max}^{2}}\,\varepsilon_{s}}_{\text{noise term}}\;+\;\underbrace{\tfrac{2}{3}(r+1)^{2}((r+1)^{2}-1)\tau_{\max}^{r-1}\frac{\|f^{(r+1)}\|_{\infty}}{(r-1)!}}_{\text{bias term}}.

(57)

where
\|f^{(r+1)}\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{t\in[0,\tau_{\max}]}|f^{(r+1)}(t)|
upper bounds the
(r+1)
-st derivative of
f
on
[0,\tau_{\max}]
.

Proof.

The total error can be decomposed into bias and noise:

|\widehat{p}^{\,\prime}(0)-f^{\prime}(0)|\;\leq\;|p^{\,\prime}(0)-f^{\prime}(0)|+|\widehat{p}^{\,\prime}(0)-p^{\,\prime}(0)|.

(58)

The decomposition for the second derivative is analogous. We first bound the bias term. By construction, the Chebyshev interpolant exactly fits
r+1
data points
\{(t_{m},f(t_{m}))\}_{m=0}^{r}
. Therefore, it classifies as a Lagrange interpolating polynomial, and we can import derivative bounds for Lagrange interpolants:

Theorem B.2

(Derivative error bound for Lagrange interpolants
(
64
, Theorem 3)
)
.

Let
f
be
(r{+}1)
-times continuously differentiable on
[a,b]
and let
p(t)
be the degree-

Lagrange interpolant of

at distinct nodes

.
Then, for any

and

(59)

The terms

and

\|\omega^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;\frac{2(r+1)^{2}}{\tau_{\max}}\|\omega\|_{\infty}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|\omega^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;\frac{4(r+1)^{2}((r+1)^{2}-1)}{3\tau_{\max}^{2}}\|\omega\|_{\infty}

(60)

For

, a straightforward upper bound is
\|\omega\|_{\infty}\leq\tau_{\max}^{r+1}
since
|t-t_{m}|\leq\tau_{\max}
. Putting everything together, we obtain the claimed upper bounds for the two bias terms
\bigl|{p}^{\,\prime}(0)-f^{\prime}(0)\bigr|
and
\bigl|{p}^{\,\prime\prime}(0)-f^{\prime\prime}(0)\bigr|
.

To bound the noise term, consider the explicit form of the derivative estimator in
Equation

51
and
Equation

52
and plug in the expansion of Chebyshev coefficients in
Equation

\displaystyle\widehat{p}^{\,\,\prime}(0)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{2}{\tau_{\max}}\frac{2}{r+1}\sum_{m=1}^{r}(-1)^{m+1}m^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{r}\widehat{f}(t_{i})\,T_{m}(z_{i})

(61)

\displaystyle\widehat{p}^{\,\,\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{4}{3\tau_{\max}^{2}}\frac{2}{r+1}\sum_{m=2}^{r}(-1)^{m}m^{2}(m^{2}-1)\sum_{i=0}^{r}\widehat{f}(t_{i})\,T_{m}(z_{i})

(62)

Then, the noise can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle|\widehat{p}^{\,\prime}(0)-p^{\,\prime}(0)|\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\frac{2}{\tau_{\max}}\frac{2}{r+1}\sum_{m=1}^{r}m^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{r}|\widehat{f}(t_{i})-f(t_{i})|\,|T_{m}(z_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq\;\frac{5r^{3}}{2\tau_{\max}}\,\varepsilon_{s}

(64)

\displaystyle|\widehat{p}^{\,\prime\prime}(0)-p^{\,\prime\prime}(0)|\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\frac{4}{3\tau_{\max}^{2}}\frac{2}{r+1}\sum_{m=2}^{r}m^{2}(m^{2}-1)\sum_{i=0}^{r}|\widehat{f}(t_{i})-f(t_{i})|\,|T_{m}(z_{i})|

(66)

where we used
|\widehat{f}(t_{i})-f(t_{i})|\leq\varepsilon_{s}
by assumption, and
|T_{m}(z_{i})|\leq 1
. We also applied the following bounds for

and
\sum_{m=2}^{r}m^{2}(m^{2}-1)\leq 3r^{5}/8

, we obtain the claimed bounds.
∎

B.3
Time-resolution of sampling times

Finally, we prove that the spacing between the Gauss–Chebyshev is not too small. This is important for experimental feasibility, where one might have a bounded time-resolution:

Lemma B.6
.

Let
r\geq 1
. Given
r+1
Gauss–Chebyshev nodes
z_{i}=\cos\big(\tfrac{(2i+1)\pi}{2(r+1)}\big)
for
i=0,\dots,r
and the corresponding sampling times
t_{i}=\frac{z_{i}+1}{2}\tau_{\max}
, the smallest time resolution satisfies:

\min_{0\leq i\leq r-1}(t_{i}-t_{i+1})\geq\frac{2\tau_{\max}}{(r+1)^{2}}.

(75)

Proof.

\frac{2(t_{i}-t_{i+1})}{\tau_{\max}}=\cos\Big(\frac{(2i+1)\pi}{2(r+1)}\Big)-\cos\Big(\frac{(2i+3)\pi}{2(r+1)}\Big)=-2\sin\Big(\frac{(2i+2)\pi}{2(r+1)}\Big)\sin\Big(\frac{-\pi}{2(r+1)}\Big)

(76)

Note the sequence
\{z_{i}\}
is decreasing. For
r\geq 1
and for any
0\leq i\leq r-1
:

\frac{2(t_{i}-t_{i+1})}{\tau_{\max}}\;\geq\;2\sin\Big(\frac{\pi}{r+1}\Big)\sin\Big(\frac{\pi}{2(r+1)}\Big)\;\geq\;\frac{4}{(r+1)^{2}},

(77)

where we used
\sin{x}\geq 2x/\pi
on
[0,\pi/2]
. Since this holds for any nearest sampling times, it also holds for the minimum. Rearranging concludes the proof.
∎

C.1
Derivative bounds of Pauli error rates

In the structure learning stage (
Appendix

D
), we estimate derivatives of the Pauli error rates
\chi_{ii}(t)
, hence the following lemma:

Lemma C.1

(Uniform bound on
k
-th derivatives of
\chi
-entries)
.

Let
t\geq 0
and let the
n
-qubit channel
\mathcal{E}_{t}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}
have
\chi
-matrix representation

\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho)=\sum_{i,j}\chi_{ij}(t)\,P_{i}\rho P_{j}
. Then for all
k\geq 0
,

\absolutevalue{\frac{d^k\chi_{ij}(t)}{dt^k}}\equiv|\chi_{ij}^{(k)}(t)|\;\leq\;\|\mathcal{L}\|_{2\rightarrow 2}^{\,k},

(79)

where
\|\mathcal{L}\|_{2\rightarrow 2}:=\sup_{X\neq 0}\|\mathcal{L}(X)\|_{2}/\|X\|_{2}

denotes the induced Schatten-
2
norm of the superoperator
\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

Let
|\psi_{0}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{x}|x\rangle\otimes|x\rangle
with
d=2^{n}
, and define
|\psi_{i}\rangle=(\mathbb{I}\otimes P_{i})|\psi_{0}\rangle
. Let
\Lambda_{t}=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{t})\big(|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|\big)
be the Choi state. Then

\chi_{ij}(t)=\langle\psi_{i}|\Lambda_{t}|\psi_{j}\rangle\;=\;\tr[\,|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|\,\Lambda_{t}\,\big].

(80)

Since
\derivative*{t}\mathcal{E}_{t}=\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{E}_{t}
for a time-independent generator, we have

\derivative[k]{t}\Lambda_{t}\;=\;(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathcal{L}^{k})(\Lambda_{t}),\qquad\chi_{ij}^{(k)}(t)\;=\;\tr[\,|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|\,(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathcal{L}^{k})(\Lambda_{t})\,\Big].

(81)

Apply Cauchy–Schwarz for the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product:

\big|\chi_{ij}^{(k)}(t)\big|\;\leq\;\big\||\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|\big\|_{2}\;\big\|(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathcal{L}^{k})(\Lambda_{t})\big\|_{2}\;=\;\big\|(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathcal{L}^{k})(\Lambda_{t})\big\|_{2},

(82)

since
\big\||\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|\big\|_{2}=1
. Using the definition of the induced norm
\norm{\cdot}_{2\rightarrow 2}
,

\big\|(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathcal{L}^{k})(\Lambda_{t})\big\|_{2}\;\leq\;\norm{\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathcal{L}^k}_{2\rightarrow 2}\;\|\Lambda_{t}\|_{2}.

(83)

By stability of the induced Schatten-2 norm under tensoring with the identity,
\norm{\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathcal{L}^k}_{2\rightarrow 2}=\norm{\mathcal{L}^k}_{2\rightarrow 2}
. Moreover,
\Lambda_{t}
is a density operator, so
\|\Lambda_{t}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\tr\{\Lambda_{t}^{2}\}}\leq 1
. Therefore,

\big|\chi_{ij}^{(k)}(t)\big|\;\leq\;\norm{\mathcal{L}^k}_{2\rightarrow 2}\;\leq\;\norm{\mathcal{L}}^{k}_{2\rightarrow 2},

(84)

by submultiplicativity of the induced norm.
∎

(
\chi
-derivative bound for

\big|\chi_{ij}^{(k)}(t)\big|\;\leq\;\|\mathcal{L}\|_{2\to 2}^{\,k}\;\leq\;\left(\Delta(H)\;+\;2\sum_{m,n}|a_{mn}|\right)^{k}\;\leq\;(2M)^{k},

(85)

where
H:=\sum_{P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}h_{k}P_{k}
and
\Delta(H)=E_{\max}-E_{\min}
is the spectral range of
H
. The second and third inequalities above follow directly from
Section

C.3
.

C.2
Dual interaction graph and derivative bounds of Pauli observables

To provide performance guarantees for the polynomial interpolation used in the coefficient-learning stage
(
Appendix

E
), we upper bound the time derivatives of Pauli observables in

Theorem

C.9
. In contrast to Pauli error rates, which encode global channel-level information, coefficient learning
accesses derivatives of selected observables. When the interaction pattern is taken into account, this
permits derivative bounds that improve over worst-case sparsity-based estimates (e.g.,
Section

C.1
). To formalize this, we generalize the dual interaction
graph introduced for Hamiltonians in
44
to the Lindbladian setting.

Here, we use the adjoint master equation

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)=i\sum_{P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}h_{k}\commutator{P_k}{O}+\sum_{P_{k},P_{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}}a_{km}\!\left(P_{m}OP_{k}-\tfrac{1}{2}\anticommutator{P_mP_k}{O}\right),

(86)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
is the adjoint of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product:
\tr\!\big(O^{\dagger}\mathcal{L}(Q)\big)=\tr\!\big((\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O))^{\dagger}Q\big)
for all
O,Q
.

Definition C.3

(Pauli Lindbladian components)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
be an adjoint Lindbladian super-operator expanded in the Pauli basis from
Equation

86
. For each
P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}
with
h_{k}\neq 0
, define the Pauli Hamiltonian component

\mathcal{A}^{H}_{k}(O):=ih_{k}\,[P_{k},O].

For each pair
(k,m)
with
P_{k},P_{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}
and
a_{km}\neq 0
, define the Pauli dissipative component

\mathcal{A}^{D}_{km}(O):=a_{km}\Big(P_{m}OP_{k}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{P_{m}P_{k},O\}\Big).

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the collection of all such components:

\mathcal{A}:=\{\mathcal{A}^{H}_{k}:P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H},\;h_{k}\neq 0\}\ \cup\ \{\mathcal{A}^{D}_{km}:P_{k},P_{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{D},\;a_{km}\neq 0\}.

(87)

This allows to re-write
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
as:

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)=\sum_{\mathcal{A}_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)

Definition C.4

(Pauli component support)
.

For a Pauli Hamiltonian component
\mathcal{A}^{H}_{k}
, define its support as

\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}^{H}_{k}\right):=\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{k}\right).

(88)

For a Pauli dissipative component
\mathcal{A}^{D}_{km}
, define its support as

\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}^{D}_{km}\right):=\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{k}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{m}\right).

(89)

where
\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P\right)
denotes the set of sites on which the Pauli
P
acts nontrivially;

Definition C.5

(Dual interaction graph via Pauli components)
.

The
dual interaction graph
associated with
\mathcal{L}
is the simple graph
\mathfrak{G}=(V,E)
with vertex set representing the collection of all Pauli components

V:=\mathcal{A},

and whose edge set is defined by overlap of component supports:

E\;:=\;\Bigl\{\{v_{1},v_{2}\}\subseteq V\;:\;v_{1}\neq v_{2},\ \operatorname{supp}\!\left(v_{1}\right)\cap\operatorname{supp}\!\left(v_{2}\right)\neq\emptyset\Bigr\}.

That is, vertices correspond to Lindbladian Pauli components, and two vertices are adjacent if and only if their component supports overlap. Denote the maximum degree of this dual interaction graph by

\mathfrak{d}:=\max_{v\in V}\deg_{\mathfrak{G}}(v).

(90)

Definition C.6

(Sparsely interacting Lindbladian)
.

A Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
of the form (
78
) is
sparsely interacting

if the maximum degree
\mathfrak{d}
of the dual interaction graph
\mathfrak{G}
(
Section

C.2
)
satisfies
\mathfrak{d}=O(1)
, i.e. is bounded by a constant independent of the system size
n
.

Although our results (
Theorems

C.9
and
E.6
) hold for general
maximum degree
\mathfrak{d}
, of particular interest is the case of
sparsely interacting
Lindbladians
(
Section

C.2
), where
\mathfrak{d}=O(1)
is independent of the system size
n
.
This captures, for example, a broad class of
locally generated
Markovian dynamics admitting a GKLS
representation

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)=i\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}[E_{k},O]+\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}\!\left(L_{j}^{\dagger}OL_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j},O\}\right),

(91)

in which each Hamiltonian term
E_{k}
is at most
k_{H}
-local and each jump operator
L_{j}
is at most
k_{D}
-local,
and moreover each qubit participates in at most
q_{H}
of the
E_{k}
’s and at most
q_{D}
of the
L_{j}
’s. Assume in addition that each
E_{k}
is at most
m_{H}
-sparse in the Pauli basis and each
L_{j}
is at most
m_{D}
-sparse in the Pauli basis. Such locality/bounded-participation assumptions are standard in local noise models (e.g. dephasing or amplitude
damping), locally monitored or reservoir-engineered systems, and local-contact open-system models. To bound the maximum degree
\mathfrak{d}
of the dual interaction graph
\mathfrak{G}

(
Section

C.2
), consider the qubit hypergraph
G
with vertex set
[n]
and one hyperedge

e(\mathcal{C}):=\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{C}\right)
for each component
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{A}

(
Section

C.2
). In (
91
), each
E_{k}
contributes at most
m_{H}
Pauli strings and hence at most
m_{H}
Hamiltonian components supported on
\operatorname{supp}\!\left(E_{k}\right)
, so each qubit participates in at most
q_{H}m_{H}
Hamiltonian components. Likewise, each
L_{j}
contributes at most
m_{D}
Pauli strings, and hence yields at most
m_{D}^{2}
dissipative components
\mathcal{A}^{D}_{km}
supported on
\operatorname{supp}\!\left(L_{j}\right)
(cf.
Equation

89
), so each qubit participates in at most
q_{D}m_{D}^{2}
dissipative components. Thus each qubit is contained in at most
q_{H}m_{H}+q_{D}m_{D}^{2}
component supports (and thus hyper-edges). Any component
\mathcal{C}
satisfies
|\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{C}\right)|\leq\max(k_{H},k_{D})
, and for each qubit in
\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{C}\right)
there are at most
(q_{H}m_{H}+q_{D}m_{D}^{2}-1)
other components overlapping it. Therefore

\mathfrak{d}\leq\max(k_{H},k_{D})\bigl(q_{H}m_{H}+q_{D}m_{D}^{2}-1\bigr)\leq\max(k_{H},k_{D})\bigl(q_{H}4^{k_{H}}+q_{D}16^{k_{D}}-1\bigr).

(92)

Another important example is the class of Lindbladians that are geometrically local on Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Assume a constant number of qubits per lattice site of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and that each Hamiltonian term

E_{k}
and jump operator
L_{j}
is supported within a single unit hypercube.
Additionally, assume that each
E_{k}
is at most
m_{H}
-sparse in the Pauli basis and each
L_{j}
is at most
m_{D}
-sparse in
the Pauli basis. Since a unit hypercube intersects only hypercubes within

-distance

(at most

and each hypercube contributes at most

\mathfrak{d}\;\leq\;3^{d}\,(m_{H}+m_{D}^{2})-1.

(93)

Lemma C.7

(Single-step Pauli growth on the dual interaction graph)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
be an
n
-qubit adjoint Lindbladian of the form (
86
) where
\max(|h_{k}|,|a_{jm}|)\leq 1
. Let

\mathfrak{G}=(V,E)
be its dual interaction graph from

Section

C.2
with maximum degree
\mathfrak{d}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the collection of adjoint Lindbladian components from

Section

C.2
, so that

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)=\sum_{\mathcal{A}_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{A}_{k}(O).

Let
O
be an
n
-qubit Pauli observable and define its neighborhood

\mathcal{N}(O):=\{\mathcal{A}_{k}\in V:\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}_{k}\right)\cap\operatorname{supp}\!\left(O\right)\neq\emptyset\},\qquad\mathfrak{d}_{O}:=|\mathcal{N}(O)|.

(94)

Then
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)
admits a decomposition

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)=\sum_{\mathcal{A}_{k}\in\mathcal{N}(O)}\mathcal{A}_{k}(O),

(95)

with the following properties:

1.

(
Number of summands
)
The decomposition (
95
) contains at most
\mathfrak{d}_{O}
summands; each a scalar multiple of a Pauli.

2.

(
Summand magnitude
)
Each non-zero summand
\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)
satisfies
\|\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)\|_{\infty}\leq 2

3.

(
Neighborhood growth
)
For each non-zero summand
\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)
, the following holds:
|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A}_{k}(O))|\leq\mathfrak{d}_{O}+\mathfrak{d}.

Proof.

We prove each item in turn.

Decomposition and number of summands.

By definition of the neighborhood,
if
\mathcal{A}_{k}\notin\mathcal{N}(O)
, then
\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}_{k}\right)\cap\operatorname{supp}\!\left(O\right)=\emptyset
.
In this case
\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)=0
:
for a Hamiltonian component
\mathcal{A}^{H}_{k}(O)=-ih_{k}[P_{k},O]
, disjoint support implies
[P_{k},O]=0
; for a dissipative component

\mathcal{A}^{D}_{km}(O)=a_{km}\big(P_{m}OP_{k}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{P_{m}P_{k},O\}\big)
, disjoint support implies
P_{k}
and
P_{m}
both commute with
O
, and hence

P_{m}OP_{k}=OP_{m}P_{k}
and
\{P_{m}P_{k},O\}=2OP_{m}P_{k}
, so the bracket cancels and
\mathcal{A}^{D}_{km}(O)=0
.
Therefore,

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)=\sum_{\mathcal{A}_{k}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)=\sum_{\mathcal{A}_{k}\in\mathcal{N}(O)}\mathcal{A}_{k}(O),

which is the decomposition (
95
).
The number of summands is at most
|\mathcal{N}(O)|=\mathfrak{d}_{O}
by construction.

Now, since a product of Paulis is a Pauli operator,
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{H}(O)\propto P_{k}O
and
\mathcal{A}_{km}^{D}(O)\propto P_{k}P_{m}O
, we get that each summand is indeed a scalar multiple of a Pauli.

Summand magnitude.

We bound the operator norm of each nonzero summand. Since
O
is Pauli,
\|O\|_{\infty}=1
and
\|P\|_{\infty}=1
for all Paulis
P
.
First consider a Hamiltonian component:

\|\mathcal{A}^{H}_{k}(O)\|_{\infty}=|h_{k}|\,\|[P_{k},O]\|_{\infty}\leq|h_{k}|\big(\|P_{k}O\|_{\infty}+\|OP_{k}\|_{\infty}\big)=2|h_{k}|\leq 2.

Next consider a dissipative component:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}^{D}_{km}(O)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq|a_{km}|\left(\|P_{m}OP_{k}\|_{\infty}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|P_{m}P_{k}O\|_{\infty}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|OP_{m}P_{k}\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle=|a_{km}|\left(1+\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)=2|a_{km}|\leq 2,

using
\max(|h_{k}|,|a_{km}|)\leq 1
. This proves the claimed bound
\|\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)\|_{\infty}\leq 2
for every nonzero summand.

Neighborhood growth.

Fix a nonzero summand
\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)
(either Hamiltonian or dissipative). We first show that its Pauli support is contained in

\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}_{k}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(O\right)
using
Section

C.2
.

If
\mathcal{A}_{k}=\mathcal{A}^{H}_{\ell}
, then
\mathcal{A}^{H}_{\ell}(O)
is proportional to
P_{\ell}O
, hence

\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}^{H}_{\ell}(O)\right)=\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{\ell}O\right)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{\ell}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(O\right)=\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}^{H}_{\ell}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(O\right).

If
\mathcal{A}_{k}=\mathcal{A}^{D}_{\ell m}
, then each term in
\mathcal{A}^{D}_{\ell m}(O)
is proportional to either
P_{m}OP_{\ell}
or
P_{m}P_{\ell}O
(or
OP_{m}P_{\ell}
),
so in all cases

\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}^{D}_{\ell m}(O)\right)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{m}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{\ell}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(O\right)=\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}^{D}_{\ell m}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(O\right),

Now let
v\in V
satisfy
\operatorname{supp}\!\left(v\right)\cap\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)\right)\neq\emptyset
, i.e.,
v\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A}_{k}(O))
.
Since
\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}_{k}(O)\right)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}_{k}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(O\right)
, any such
v
must overlap either
\operatorname{supp}\!\left(O\right)
or
\operatorname{supp}\!\left(\mathcal{A}_{k}\right)
.
Therefore,

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A}_{k}(O))\subseteq\mathcal{N}(O)\ \cup\ \mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A}_{k}).

Taking cardinalities and using
|\mathcal{N}(O)|=\mathfrak{d}_{O}
and
|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A}_{k})|\leq\mathfrak{d}
(by definition of maximum degree) gives

|\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A}_{k}(O))|\leq\mathfrak{d}_{O}+\mathfrak{d},

which proves the neighborhood growth claim.
∎

Corollary C.8

(Iterated Pauli growth)
.

Using the setting of
Section

C.2
, for any integer
k\geq 1
we may write

(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})^{k}(O)=\sum_{\mathcal{A}_{j_{1}}\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\mathcal{A}_{j_{2}}\in\mathcal{A}}\cdots\sum_{\mathcal{A}_{j_{k}}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{A}_{j_{1}}\!\big(\mathcal{A}_{j_{2}}(\cdots\mathcal{A}_{j_{k}}(O))\big),

(96)

This uncollected decomposition contains at most

\mathfrak{d}_{O}\,(\mathfrak{d}_{O}+\mathfrak{d})\cdots(\mathfrak{d}_{O}+(k-1)\mathfrak{d})

nonzero Pauli summands, and each summand has operator norm at most
2^{k}
.

Proof.

The decomposition follows by iterating the expansion of
Section

C.2
.
At the first step there are at most
\mathfrak{d}_{O}
summands, and by the neighborhood-growth property of the lemma,
after
\ell
steps each summand has at most
\mathfrak{d}_{O}+\ell\mathfrak{d}
descendants.
Multiplying these bounds yields the stated count.
Finally, each application of a component increases the operator norm by at most a factor of
2
,
so every
k
-fold nested term has norm at most
2^{k}
.
∎

Theorem C.9

(Uniform bound on Pauli observable derivatives)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be an
n
-qubit Lindbladian of the form (
78
) and let

\mathfrak{G}=(V,E)
be its dual interaction graph from
Section

C.2
with maximum degree
\mathfrak{d}
.
Let
O
be an
n
-qubit Pauli observable with neighborhood size
\mathfrak{d}_{O}:=|\mathcal{N}(O)|\leq\mathfrak{d}
(cf.
Equation

94
).
For an arbitrary initial state
\rho
, define
\rho_{t}:=e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\rho)
and
\langle O(t)\rangle:=\tr(O\,\rho_t)
.
Then for every integer
k\geq 0
and all
t\geq 0
,

\left|\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\langle O(t)\rangle\right|\;\leq\;\bigl(2\mathfrak{d}\bigr)^{k}\,(k!).

(97)

Proof.

Write
\rho_{t}:=e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\rho)
so that
\|\rho_{t}\|_{1}=1
.
Using
\frac{d}{dt}e^{t\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{L}\circ e^{t\mathcal{L}}
and the adjoint relation

\tr\!\big(O^{\dagger}\mathcal{L}(Q)\big)=\tr\!\big((\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O))^{\dagger}Q\big)
, we have for all
k\geq 0
and
t\geq 0
,

\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\langle O(t)\rangle=\tr\!\big(O\,\mathcal{L}^{k}(\rho_{t})\big)=\tr\!\big((\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})^{k}(O)\,\rho_{t}\big),

(98)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
is given explicitly by the adjoint master equation (
86
).
Hölder’s inequality implies

\left|\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\langle O(t)\rangle\right|\leq\bigl\|(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})^{k}(O)\bigr\|_{\infty}\,\|\rho_{t}\|_{1}=\bigl\|(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})^{k}(O)\bigr\|_{\infty}.

(99)

By
Section

C.2
,
(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})^{k}(O)
admits an uncollected decomposition into at most

\mathfrak{d}_{O}(\mathfrak{d}_{O}+\mathfrak{d})\cdots(\mathfrak{d}_{O}+(k-1)\mathfrak{d})

(nonzero) Pauli summands, each of operator norm at most
2^{k}
. Applying the triangle inequality and using
\|P\|_{\infty}=1
for any Pauli
P
yields

\bigl\|(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger})^{k}(O)\bigr\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{k}\,\mathfrak{d}_{O}(\mathfrak{d}_{O}+\mathfrak{d})\cdots(\mathfrak{d}_{O}+(k-1)\mathfrak{d}).

(100)

Since
\mathfrak{d}_{O}\leq\mathfrak{d}
, we further bound each factor as

\mathfrak{d}_{O}+\ell\mathfrak{d}\leq(\ell+1)\mathfrak{d}
for

\mathfrak{d}_{O}(\mathfrak{d}_{O}+\mathfrak{d})\cdots(\mathfrak{d}_{O}+(k-1)\mathfrak{d})\leq\mathfrak{d}^{k}\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}(\ell+1)=\mathfrak{d}^{k}\,(k!).

(101)

Combining (
99
), (
100
), and (
101
) yields

\left|\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\langle O(t)\rangle\right|\leq(2\mathfrak{d})^{k}\,(k!).

∎

C.3
Lindbladian Induced Schatten-2 norm bound

In this subsection, we upper bound the induced Lindbladian Schatten-
2
norm
\|\mathcal{L}\|_{2\to 2}
as defined in
Appendix

A
. This norm is used by the Pauli error rates derivative bound in
Section

C.1
.

We briefly recall the operator–vector correspondence (“vectorization”) following
(
96
, Eq. (1.127))
, except we use a tensor-factor ordering corresponding to column-stacking (
Equation

105
) rather than row-stacking.
Let
\{|a\rangle\}_{a=1}^{d}
be the computational basis and
E_{ab}:=|a\rangle\langle b|
the matrix units.
We define the linear map
\operatorname{vec}\!\left(\cdot\right):\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
by

\operatorname{vec}\!\left(E_{ab}\right):=|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle,\qquad\text{extended by linearity.}

(105)

With this choice one has, for all
A,X,B\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
,

\operatorname{vec}\!\left(AXB\right)\;=\;(B^{T}\otimes A)\,\operatorname{vec}\!\left(X\right).

(106)

Moreover,
\operatorname{vec}\!\left(\cdot\right)
is an isometry for the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product:

\langle\operatorname{vec}\!\left(X\right),\operatorname{vec}\!\left(Y\right)\rangle\;=\;\tr(X^\dagger Y),\qquad\text{so in particular}\qquad\|\operatorname{vec}\!\left(X\right)\|_{2}=\|X\|_{2}.

(107)

Here
\|\cdot\|_{2}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
denotes the Euclidean (
\ell_{2}
) norm, while
\|X\|_{2}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
denotes the Schatten-
2
(Hilbert–Schmidt) norm.

Given a linear map (superoperator)
\Phi
, we define its matrix (Liouville) representation
\widehat{\Phi}
by

\operatorname{vec}\!\left(\Phi(X)\right)\;=\;\widehat{\Phi}\,\operatorname{vec}\!\left(X\right)\qquad\forall\,X.

(108)

Then the induced Schatten-
2
norm of
\Phi
coincides with the operator norm of its Liouville matrix representation, i.e., the Schatten-
\infty
norm:

\|\Phi\|_{2\to 2}:=\sup_{X\neq 0}\frac{\|\Phi(X)\|_{2}}{\|X\|_{2}}\;=\;\sup_{v\neq 0}\frac{\|\widehat{\Phi}v\|_{2}}{\|v\|_{2}}\;=\;\|\widehat{\Phi}\|_{\infty}.

(109)

In particular, for the Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
we upper bound
\|\mathcal{L}\|_{2\to 2}
via
\|\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\|_{\infty}
.

Lemma C.12

(Induced Schatten-
2
norm bound for Lindbladians)
.

Recall the Lindbladian expansion in the Pauli basis from
Equation

78
and write

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{D}
for its Hamiltonian and dissipative parts, respectively.
Assume the coefficients have magnitude less than
1
as in
Equation

78
,
and recall the sparsity parameter
M:=|\mathcal{S}_{H}|+|\mathcal{S}_{D}|^{2}
from
Equation

118
.

Then

\|\mathcal{L}\|_{2\to 2}\;\leq\;\Delta(H)\;+\;2\sum_{m,n}|a_{mn}|\;\leq\;2|\mathcal{S}_{H}|+2|\mathcal{S}_{D}|^{2}\;=\;2M,

(110)

where
H:=\sum_{P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}h_{k}P_{k}
and
\Delta(H)=E_{\max}-E_{\min}
is the spectral range of
H
.

Proof.

By
Equation

109
, for any superoperator
\Phi
we have

\|\Phi\|_{2\to 2}=\|\widehat{\Phi}\|_{\infty},

where
\widehat{\Phi}
is the Liouville matrix defined by

\operatorname{vec}\!\left(\Phi(X)\right)=\widehat{\Phi}\,\operatorname{vec}\!\left(X\right)
.
Using subadditivity of the Schatten-
\infty
norm,

\|\mathcal{L}\|_{2\to 2}=\|\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\widehat{\mathcal{H}}\|_{\infty}+\|\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\|_{\infty}.

(111)

Hamiltonian part.

In an eigenbasis
H\ket{j}=E_{j}\ket{j}
,

\mathcal{H}(\outerproduct{j}{k})=-i(E_{j}-E_{k})\outerproduct{j}{k}.

Since
\{\outerproduct{j}{k}\}_{j,k}
is orthonormal in the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product,
\mathcal{H}
is diagonal in this orthonormal operator basis and hence

\|\widehat{\mathcal{H}}\|_{\infty}=\|\mathcal{H}\|_{2\to 2}=\max_{j,k}|E_{j}-E_{k}|=\Delta(H).

(112)

Dissipator part.

Using
Equation

\operatorname{vec}\!\left(P_{m}XP_{n}\right)=(P_{n}^{T}\otimes P_{m})\operatorname{vec}\!\left(X\right),\qquad\operatorname{vec}\!\left((P_{n}P_{m})X\right)=(I\otimes P_{n}P_{m})\operatorname{vec}\!\left(X\right),\qquad\operatorname{vec}\!\left(X(P_{n}P_{m})\right)=((P_{n}P_{m})^{T}\otimes I)\operatorname{vec}\!\left(X\right).

Therefore the Liouville representation of

is

\widehat{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{m,n}a_{mn}\!\left(P_{n}^{T}\otimes P_{m}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,I\otimes P_{n}P_{m}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,(P_{n}P_{m})^{T}\otimes I\right).

Taking the Schatten-

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m,n}|a_{mn}|\,\left\|P_{n}^{T}\otimes P_{m}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,I\otimes P_{n}P_{m}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,(P_{n}P_{m})^{T}\otimes I\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m,n}|a_{mn}|\left(\|P_{n}^{T}\otimes P_{m}\|_{\infty}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|I\otimes P_{n}P_{m}\|_{\infty}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|(P_{n}P_{m})^{T}\otimes I\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}|a_{mn}|\left(1+\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)=2\sum_{m,n}|a_{mn}|.

(114)

Here we used that Paulis are unitary, hence have Schatten-
\infty
norm
1
, and

\|A\otimes B\|_{\infty}=\|A\|_{\infty}\|B\|_{\infty}
.

Combining
Equations

,
112
and
114

Equation

110
.

Crude sparsity bound.

For the Hamiltonian part, use the Liouville picture: for
\mathcal{H}_{k}(X):=-i[P_{k},X]
one has

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{k}=-i\big(I\otimes P_{k}-P_{k}^{T}\otimes I\big),

so
\|\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\|I\otimes P_{k}\|_{\infty}+\|P_{k}^{T}\otimes I\|_{\infty}=2

Hence, writing
\mathcal{H}=\sum_{P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}h_{k}\,\mathcal{H}_{k}

\|\mathcal{H}\|_{2\to 2}=\|\widehat{\mathcal{H}}\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}|h_{k}|\,\|\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq 2|\mathcal{S}_{H}|.

\sum_{m,n}|a_{mn}|\leq|\mathcal{S}_{D}|^{2}

Substituting these into the first inequality in

\|\mathcal{L}\|_{2\to 2}\leq 2|\mathcal{S}_{H}|+2|\mathcal{S}_{D}|^{2}=2M

Appendix D
Structure learning

In this section we describe the Lindbladian structure learning algorithm – the primitive to identify the operator content of both the Hamiltonian and the dissipator. First, we set up the problem. Express the Lindbladian in the Pauli basis:

\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-i\underbrace{\sum_{P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}h_{k}\commutator{P_k}{\rho}}_{\mathrm{Hamiltonian}}+\underbrace{\sum_{P_{k},P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}}a_{kj}\!\left(P_{k}\rho P_{j}-\tfrac{1}{2}\anticommutator{P_j P_k}{\rho}\right)}_{\mathrm{Dissipator}},

(115)

where
a\succeq 0
is commonly called a Kossakowski matrix. We denote by
\eta_{H}:=\min_{P_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}|h_{i}|
and

\eta_{D}:=\min_{P_{i},P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}}|a_{ij}|
the smallest nonzero Hamiltonian and dissipator
coefficients, respectively, and set
\eta:=\min\{\eta_{H},\eta_{D}\}
. In practice, one may treat
\eta
as a
user-chosen resolution threshold satisfying

\eta\leq\min\!\left(\min_{h_{i}\neq 0}|h_{i}|,\ \min_{a_{ij}\neq 0}|a_{ij}|\right)
.
See
Section

D.4
for when
\eta
is chosen to be larger than the true minimum nonzero magnitude.

WLOG, we rescale
\mathcal{L}
such that the coefficients are normalized:
\max\Big\{\max_{P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}|h_{k}|,\ \max_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{m},P_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}\end{subarray}}|a_{mn}|\Big\}=1.

Definition D.1

(Hamiltonian structure)
.

The Hamiltonian structure is the set
\mathcal{S}_{H}
of all Pauli terms with nonzero coefficients in the Hamiltonian expansion:

\mathcal{S}_{H}:=\{\,P_{i}\;\;:\;\;h_{i}\neq 0\,\}.

(116)

Definition D.2

(Dissipator structure)
.

The dissipator structure is the set
\mathcal{S}_{D}
of all Pauli terms with nonzero diagonal Kossakowski entries
3
3

3

For a positive semidefinite matrix
a
, one has

(117)

Let

and

(118)

since each Hamiltonian term contributes one real coefficient, while the dissipator is specified by the

(Structure learning problem)

Given black-box access to the dynamics

D.1
Pauli error rates derivative identities

The diagonal entries
\{\chi_{ii}(t)\}
of the
\chi

To see why (i) holds, consider the Taylor expansion of the channel:

\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho)\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}^{k}(\rho)\,\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\;=\;\sum_{i,j}\chi_{ij}(t)\,P_{i}\rho P_{j}\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i,j}\chi_{ij}^{(k)}\,P_{i}\rho P_{j}\right)\frac{t^{k}}{k!},

where in the last equality we have expanded the coefficients as
\chi_{ij}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\chi_{ij}^{(k)}t^{k}/k!
, and defined
\chi_{ij}^{(k)}=\derivative[k]{t}\chi_{ij}(0)
. By comparing terms, we see that
\mathcal{L}^{k}(\rho)\;=\;\sum_{i,j}\chi_{ij}^{(k)}\,P_{i}\rho P_{j}
, and in particular

\mathcal{L}(\rho)\;=\;\sum_{i,j}\chi_{ij}^{(1)}\,P_{i}\rho P_{j},\qquad\mathcal{L}^{2}(\rho)\;=\;\sum_{i,j}\chi_{ij}^{(2)}\,P_{i}\rho P_{j}.

(124)

Comparing with the Lindblad form (
Equation

\chi_{ii}^{(1)}\;=\;a_{ii}
. Thus, if we could estimate all nonzero

efficiently, we could immediately determine the dissipator structure.

The Hamiltonian structure is less immediate. Because
-i\commutator{H}{\rho}
only acts on one side of
\rho
, the Hamiltonian does not appear in the first derivatives of the diagonals. To “see” the Hamiltonian contributions, we need to go to the second order. The explicit form of the second time-derivative
\chi_{ii}^{(2)}
is quite involved, as it includes quadratic terms in

and

Lemma D.3

(Time-derivatives of Pauli error rates of

)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{t}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}

n

\mathcal{L}

\chi

\displaystyle\chi_{ii}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=a_{ii},

\displaystyle\text{if }a_{ii}>0,

(125)

\displaystyle\chi_{ii}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\chi_{ii}^{(2)}\;\geq\;2h_{i}^{2},

\displaystyle\text{if }a_{ii}=0.

(126)

Proof.

Expanding the channel,

\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{r}(\rho)\,t^{r}}{r!}=\sum_{i,j}\chi_{ij}(t)\,P_{i}\rho P_{j},

\chi_{ij}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\chi_{ij}^{(r)}t^{r}/r!

Throughout this proof we repeatedly match coefficients between the left- and right-hand sides of

We now consider the term
P_{k}\rho P_{k}
and determine the first two derivatives:
\chi_{kk}^{(1)}
and
\chi_{kk}^{(2)}

Notice that the Hamiltonian terms
P_{k}\rho

\rho

\chi_{kk}^{(1)}

\chi_{kk}^{(1)}=a_{kk}\quad(k\neq 0),\qquad\chi_{00}^{(1)}=-\sum_{i\neq 0}a_{ii}.

To “see” the Hamiltonian, we need to go to the second order in

\mathcal{L}^{2}(\rho)=-\underbrace{\commutator{H}{\comm{H}{\rho}}}_{\scalebox{0.7}{\boxed{1}}\;:\;\text{Hamiltonian}}\;-\;i\,\underbrace{\big(\commutator{H}{\mathcal{D}(\rho)}+\mathcal{D}(\commutator{H}{\rho})\big)}_{\scalebox{0.7}{\boxed{2}}\;:\;\text{Mixed terms}}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{D}(\rho))}_{\scalebox{0.7}{\boxed{3}}\;:\;\text{Dissipator}}.

\displaystyle\boxed{1}\;:\;-\commutator{H}{\comm{H}{\rho}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}h_{i}\left(P_{i}\commutator{H}{\rho}-\commutator{H}{\rho}P_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,i^{\prime}}h_{i}h_{i^{\prime}}\left(P_{i}P_{i^{\prime}}\rho-P_{i}\rho P_{i^{\prime}}-P_{i^{\prime}}\rho P_{i}+\rho P_{i^{\prime}}P_{i}\right).

Among these, diagonal terms of the form

\chi_{kk}^{(2)}\leftarrow 2h_{k}^{2}\quad(k\neq 0),\qquad\chi_{00}^{(2)}\leftarrow-2\sum_{i}h_{i}^{2}.

(133)

Thus the Hamiltonian structure, which is invisible at first order, becomes, at least in principle, detectable from the second-order expansion of the error rates. Unfortunately,
\chi_{kk}^{(2)}

The key observation is that if

is not part of the dissipator structure, then any spurious contributions either vanish or only increase the magnitude of
\chi_{kk}^{(2)}
. The crucial point is that whenever
a_{kk}=0
, we also have
a_{ik}=a_{ki}=0\>\forall i

a

Let us first look at the mixed terms:

\displaystyle\boxed{2.1}\quad-i\,\commutator{H}{\mathcal{D}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{i}h_{i}\Big(P_{i}\mathcal{D}(\rho)-\mathcal{D}(\rho)P_{i}\Big)

(134)

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{i,m,n}h_{i}\,a_{mn}\Big(P_{i}P_{m}\rho P_{n}-P_{m}\rho P_{n}P_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}P_{i}P_{n}P_{m}\rho

(135)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt-\tfrac{1}{2}P_{i}\rho P_{n}P_{m}+\tfrac{1}{2}P_{n}P_{m}\rho P_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\rho P_{n}P_{m}P_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{m,n}a_{mn}\left(P_{m}\commutator{H}{\rho}P_{n}-\tfrac{1}{2}\anticommutator{P_n P_m}{\comm{H}{\rho}}\right)

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{i,m,n}h_{i}\,a_{mn}\Big(P_{m}P_{i}\rho P_{n}-P_{m}\rho P_{i}P_{n}-\tfrac{1}{2}P_{n}P_{m}P_{i}\rho

Now consider the second-order dissipator terms

[formula]

Now let’s consider the

P_{j}P_{i}=\omega_{ji}P_{j\oplus i},\qquad\omega_{ji}\in\{\pm 1,\pm i\},\qquad\omega_{ji}=\omega_{ij}^{*},

\Phi^{2}(\rho)\;=\;\sum_{i,j,m,n}a_{ij}a_{mn}P_{i}P_{m}\rho P_{n}P_{j}

D.2
Estimating Pauli error rates

Section

D.1
establishes that the operator content of the Lindbladian is encoded in the time derivatives of
\{\chi_{ii}(t)\}
. In this section, we therefore focus on estimating the Pauli error rates
\{\chi_{ii}\}
as a precursor to estimating the derivatives
\{\chi_{ii}^{(1)}(t=0),\chi_{ii}^{(2)}(t=0)\}

The Pauli error rates of any quantum channel form a valid probability distribution:
\chi_{ii}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}\chi_{ii}=1
. Our goal is to learn the distribution
{\chi_{ii}}
to accuracy

\mathcal{E}
in the Bell basis, one obtains i.i.d. samples from
{\chi_{ii}}
directly, and standard distribution-learning results imply a sample complexity of
\Theta(\varepsilon^{-2})
, independent of system size.

By contrast, the entanglement-free setting is substantially more challenging. Without ancillas, one cannot directly sample from
{\chi_{ii}}
. (Indeed, if system-only operations allowed sampling
k
with probability
\chi_{ii}
, then Pauli eigenvalues could be estimated with
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2})
measurements independent of
n
, contradicting known entanglement-free lower bounds of
\Omega(2^{n}/\varepsilon^{2})

(
18
;
19
)
.)
Nevertheless,
35

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an
n
-qubit channel with Pauli error rates
\{\chi_{ii}\}

For any

(152)

With probability at least

, it outputs a hypothesis
\widehat{\chi}
such that

\max_{i}|\widehat{\chi}_{ii}-\chi_{ii}|\;\leq\;\varepsilon.

(153)

The hypothesis
\widehat{\chi}
is supported on a set
\widehat{S}\subseteq\{0,\dots,4^{n}-1\}

of size
|\widehat{S}|\;\leq\;\frac{4}{\varepsilon}
(i.e.,
\widehat{\chi}_{ii}=0
for
i\notin\widehat{S}
)

The classical post-processing runs in time
\mathcal{O}\!\left(mn/\varepsilon\right)=\widetilde{O}(n/\varepsilon^{3})
, where
\widetilde{O}(\cdot)
omits the
\operatorname{polylog}()\,
factors in
(n,1/\varepsilon,1/\delta)
.

Remark D.5

(Operational form)
.

One may view the procedure in
Theorem

D.4
as first applying Pauli twirling so that the effective channel is a Pauli channel
(i.e. only the diagonal rates
\{\chi_{ii}\}
of the channel survive), then collecting
mn
single-qubit Pauli-basis measurement outcomes from product inputs and performing a population-recovery
post-processing step; see
35
for details.

D.3
Estimating time-derivatives of Pauli error rates

Having established how to estimate the Pauli error rates
\{\chi_{ii}(t)\}
, we now turn to estimating their first and second time-derivatives at
t=0
, which are required to apply
Section

D.1
to structure learning.
Our approach is based on Chebyshev interpolation; see
Appendix

B
. Specifically, we evaluate
\widehat{\chi}_{ii}(t)
at a sequence of nodes
\{t_{m}\}_{m=0}^{r}\subset[0,\tau_{\max}]
, fit a degree-
r
Chebyshev interpolant, and use its closed-form derivatives at
t=0
as estimators for
\chi_{ii}^{(1)}(0)
and
\chi_{ii}^{(2)}(0)
. In
Appendix

B
we show that these estimators achieve provably small bias and variance when the underlying function is sufficiently smooth (see
Section

C.1
).

The following lemmas formalize the sample complexities associated with estimating the derivatives of the Pauli error rates.

Lemma D.6

(First derivative estimation of Pauli error rates)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{t}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}
be an
n
-qubit channel generated by a Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
with at most
M
nonzero Pauli coefficients.
Choose
\tau_{\max}=\Theta(1/M)
and
r=\Theta(\log(M/\epsilon_1))
, and obtain estimates
\{\widehat{\chi}_{ii}(t_{m})\}
of the Pauli error rates of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
at the
r{+}1
Gauss–Chebyshev nodes
\{t_{m}\}_{m=0}^{r}\subset[0,\tau_{\max}]
using a total of

m=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{M^{2}}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\Big(n,\tfrac{M}{\epsilon_{1}},\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Big)\right)

(154)

applications of the channel
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
.
Then, from these estimates, one can construct a Chebyshev-interpolated estimator
\{\widehat{\chi}_{ii}^{(1)}\}
for the first time-derivatives of all Pauli error rates such that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\big|\widehat{\chi}_{ii}^{(1)}-\chi_{ii}^{(1)}\big|\leq\epsilon_{1},\qquad\forall\,i.

(155)

Proof.

By the Pauli-rate derivative bound
Section

C.1
, the function

f(t)=\chi_{ii}(t)
satisfies

\|f^{(k)}\|_{\infty}\leq(2M)^{k}\qquad(k\geq 1),

so
Section

B.2
applies with
B=1
and
\Lambda=2M
.
With the resulting choice of parameters,

\tau_{\max}=\frac{1}{4M},\qquad r=\Big\lceil\log\Big(\frac{36M}{\epsilon_1}\Big)\Big\rceil,\qquad\varepsilon_{s}=\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{20Mr^{3}},

(156)

the Chebyshev estimator
\widehat{\chi}^{(1)}_{ii}
satisfies

|\widehat{\chi}^{(1)}_{ii}-\chi^{(1)}_{ii}|\leq\epsilon_{1}
provided

|\widehat{\chi}_{ii}(t_{m})-\chi_{ii}(t_{m})|\leq\varepsilon_{s}
uniformly over all nodes
t_{m}
.

Such estimates are guaranteed by the population-recovery procedure of
Theorem

D.4
, except with a failure probability of at most
\delta_{s}
per node. Setting the per-node failure probability to
\delta_{s}=\delta/(r+1)
and applying the union bound ensures the total failure probability across all nodes is at most
\delta
. Substituting the parameters from (
156
), we get that each time node requires

\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon_{s}^{-2}\,\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon_{s}\delta_{s}}\right)

(157)

applications of
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
, hence the total number of channel applications across all
r{+}1
nodes is:

m=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{M^{2}}{\epsilon_{1}^{2}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\Big(n,\tfrac{M}{\epsilon_{1}},\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Big)\right)

(158)

concluding the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma D.7

(Second derivative estimation of Pauli error rates)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{t}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}
be an
n

applications of the channel
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
.

Proof.

By the Pauli-rate derivative bound
Section

C.1
, the function

f(t)=\chi_{ii}(t)

Section

B.2

the Chebyshev estimator
\widehat{\chi}^{(2)}_{ii}

D.3

m=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{M^{4}}{\epsilon_{2}^{2}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\Big(n,\tfrac{M^{2}}{\epsilon_{2}},\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Big)\right).

Section

D.3
.
∎

Remark D.8

(First-derivative accuracy under second-derivative parameters)

. Then these parameters also meet the sufficient conditions of

Remark D.9

and

(Ancilla-free structure learning)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{t}=e^{t\mathcal{L}}
be the channel generated by an
n
-qubit Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
whose Pauli expansion
is
M
-sparse with the smallest coefficient magnitude
\geq\eta
(cf.
Equations

115
and
118
), and with Hamiltonian and dissipator structures
\mathcal{S}_{H}
and
\mathcal{S}_{D}

as in
Appendices

D
and
D
. Then there is an ancilla-free procedure
(
Sections

D.4
and
D.4
) which, given parameters

0<\eta,\delta<1
, outputs candidate sets
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
such that the
following guarantees hold:

1.

(Coverage and size.) With probability
\geq 1-\delta
, the output sets satisfy:

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}=\mathcal{S}_{D}
and
\mathcal{S}_{H}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H},
and

|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}|=|\mathcal{S}_{D}|,\qquad|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}|=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{M^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\Big(\tfrac{M}{\eta}\Big)\right).

2.

(Number of experiments.) The total number of channel uses is

N_{\exp}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{M^{4}}{\eta^{4}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\Big(n,\tfrac{M}{\eta},\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Big)\right).

and is dominated by identifying the Hamiltonian set
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
. Identifying the
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
uses nearly quadratically fewer queries.

3.

(Time resolution.) It only ever applies
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
for
t\geq t_{\mathrm{res}}
, where

t_{\mathrm{res}}=\Omega\Bigl(M^{-1}\,\mathrm{polylog}(M/\eta)^{-1}\Bigr).

4.

(Classical overhead.) The total classical post-processing time is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(\frac{n\,M^{6}}{\eta^{6}}\right),

where the
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
hides polylogarithmic factors in
(M,n,1/\eta,1/\delta)
.

5.

(Total evolution time.) The procedure only queries
\mathcal{E}_{t}
at times

with
\tau_{\max}=\Theta(1/M)
, and therefore the total evolution time satisfies

t_{\mathrm{tot}}:=\sum_{\text{calls}}t\;\leq\;N_{\exp}\,\tau_{\max}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{M^{3}}{\eta^{4}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\Big(n,\tfrac{M}{\eta},\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Big)\right).

and is also dominated by identifying the Hamiltonian set
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
. Identifying the
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
takes total time
\widetilde{O}(M/\eta^{2})
.

The procedure prepares only
product

n
-qubit input states in which each qubit is a Pauli eigenstate, and measures each output qubit
in a single-qubit Pauli eigenbasis.

Proof.

Both
Sections

D.4
and
D.4
follow the same template.
First, they invoke the ancilla-free population-recovery procedure
(
Theorem

D.4
) to estimate the Pauli error rates
\{\chi_{ii}(t)\}

of
\mathcal{E}_{t}=e^{t\mathcal{L}}
at a set of Gauss–Chebyshev nodes
\{t_{m}\}_{m=0}^{r}\subset(0,\tau_{\max}]
.
Second, they apply Chebyshev interpolation to these nodewise estimates to obtain estimators for the short-time derivatives

\{\chi_{ii}^{(1)}(0)\}
and
\{\chi_{ii}^{(2)}(0)\}
, with accuracy guarantees provided by

Sections

D.3
and
D.3
.
Finally, they use the structural identities linking Lindbladian coefficients to Pauli-rate derivatives
(
Section

D.1
) to prune the initial candidate pools and output the supersets

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
.

Correctness.

The Pauli error rates derivative guarantees are conditioned on the event that the population-recovery subroutine succeeds at every Gauss–Chebyshev node of the algoritms. In
Section

D.4
the schedule parameters
(\tau_{\max},r,\varepsilon_{s},\delta_{s})
are chosen as in

Section

D.3
, so with probability at least
1-\delta/2
the Chebyshev estimator satisfies

\bigl|\widehat{\chi}^{(2)}_{ii}-\chi^{(2)}_{ii}\bigr|\leq\eta^{2},\qquad\forall\,i.

(163)

In
Section

D.4
the schedule parameters are chosen as in

Section

D.3
, so with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\bigl|\widehat{\chi}^{(1)}_{ii}-\chi^{(1)}_{ii}\bigr|\leq\frac{\eta}{2},\qquad\forall\,i.

(164)

The same bound (
164
) also holds for
Section

D.4

(by
Section

D.3
under its parameter choice),
again with probability
\geq 1-\delta/2
.

By a union bound, (
163
) and (
164
) hold for both
Sections

D.4
and
D.4
with probability at least
1-\delta
.
Conditioning on this event, we now prove coverage for
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
.

Dissipator coverage.

Fix
P_{k}
. If
P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}
, then by
Section

D.1
we have

\chi^{(1)}_{kk}=a_{kk}\geq\eta
.
Combined with (
164
), this implies
\widehat{\chi}^{(1)}_{kk}\geq\chi^{(1)}_{kk}-\eta/2\geq\eta/2
,
so the selection rule in
Section

D.4
includes
P_{k}
. Conversely, if
P_{k}\notin\mathcal{S}_{D}
, then
\chi^{(1)}_{kk}=a_{kk}=0
, and (
164
) gives

\widehat{\chi}^{(1)}_{kk}\leq\eta/2,

so the selection rule excludes
P_{k}
.
Therefore,
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}=\mathcal{S}_{D}
.

Hamiltonian coverage.

Fix
P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}
.
If
P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{D}
, then
a_{kk}=0
and
|h_{k}|\geq\eta
.
By
Section

D.1
,

\chi^{(2)}_{kk}\geq 2h_{k}^{2}\geq 2\eta^{2}
.
Combining with (
163
) yields

\widehat{\chi}^{(2)}_{kk}\geq 2\eta^{2}-\eta^{2}=\eta^{2}
,
so the second-derivative selection rule of
Section

D.4
includes
P_{k}
.
If instead
P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}\cap\mathcal{S}_{D}
, then
a_{kk}\geq\eta
implies

\chi^{(1)}_{kk}=a_{kk}\geq\eta
by
Section

D.1
.
Combining with (
164
) gives

\widehat{\chi}^{(1)}_{kk}\geq\eta-\eta/2=\eta/2
,
so
P_{k}
is included by the first-derivative selection rule.
Therefore
\mathcal{S}_{H}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
.

Sample complexity.

The total number of channel uses needed to estimate all values
\{\widehat{\chi}_{ii}(t_{m})\}
at the Gauss–Chebyshev nodes
to the uniform accuracy
\varepsilon_{s}
is determined by the derivative-accuracy requirement. For dissipator structure learning, only first-derivative estimates are needed. Taking
\epsilon_{1}\leq\eta/2
in

Section

D.3
(so that (
164
) holds) yields

m_{D}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{M^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\Big(n,\tfrac{M}{\eta},\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Big)\right).

(165)

For Hamiltonian structure learning, second-derivative estimates are required (and under the same schedule the
first-derivative accuracy condition also holds). Taking
\epsilon_{2}\leq\eta^{2}
in

Section

D.3
(so that (
163
) and (
164
) hold) gives

m_{H}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{M^{4}}{\eta^{4}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\Big(n,\tfrac{M}{\eta},\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Big)\right).

(166)

Superset sizes.
Since
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}=\mathcal{S}_{D}
, clearly
|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}|=|\mathcal{S}_{D}|
.
The size of the recovered Hamiltonian superset is determined by the maximal number of nonzero terms returned by the population-recovery routine (
Theorem

D.4
) at each sampling node.
Since each call outputs at most
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon_{s})
entries, and the algorithms aggregate results over
r{+}1
nodes, the total number of distinct terms is upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}(r/\varepsilon_{s})
.
For the Hamiltonian step (
Equation

161
), this gives

|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{M^{2}r^{6}}{\eta^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{M^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\,\mathrm{polylog}\Big(\tfrac{M}{\eta}\Big)\right).

(167)

Time resolution.

Per
Section

B.3
, the smallest time resolution (as dictated by the Gauss-Chebyshev nodes) satisfies:

t_{\mathrm{res}}\coloneq\min_{0\leq m<r}\bigl|t_{m+1}-t_{m}\bigr|\;=\;\Omega\left(\tau_{\max}/r^{2}\right)\;=\;\Omega\left(M^{-1}\,\mathrm{polylog}(M/\eta)^{-1}\right).

since
\tau_{\max}=\Theta(1/M)
and
r=\mathrm{polylog}(M/\eta)
in both schedules.

The classical post-processing time is dictated by the population recovery algorithm. By
Theorem

D.4
, the classical cost per node is
\widetilde{O}(n/\varepsilon_{s}^{3})
, hence the total classical cost is upper bounded by
O(nr/\varepsilon_{s}^{3})
and is dominated by the Hamiltonian structure learning. Plugging
\varepsilon_{s}
from
Equation

161
corresponding to
\epsilon_{2}=\eta^{2}
, the total classical cost is:

O(nr/\varepsilon_{s}^{3})=\widetilde{O}(nM^{6}/\eta^{6})

This completes the proof of
Theorem

D.10
(the total evolution time is evident from the theorem statement).
∎

Remark D.11

(False positives)
.

Evidently, the Hamiltonian candidate set
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
may include dissipator terms that are not
present in the true Hamiltonian support. Moreover,
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
can contain additional spurious
Pauli strings arising from higher-order effects in the short-time expansion—most notably, second-order
dissipative couplings that can induce
\widehat{\chi}^{(2)}_{ii}>\eta^{2}
even when the corresponding Pauli
does not appear in the Hamiltonian. Such false positives are benign: in the coefficient-learning stage they
simply enter the linear system as extra variables and are assigned (with high probability) near-zero
estimated coefficients, effectively pruning them.

Remark D.12

(Obtaining
\eta
-heavy support)
.

If
\eta
is chosen larger than the true minimum nonzero magnitude, the structure-learning routine
recovers the support of the
\eta
-heavy part of the dissipator. Under the same scheduling, it also
identifies (almost all of) the
\eta
-heavy Hamiltonian support, returning a superset

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
that contains every
P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}
with
|h_{j}|\geq\eta
, except possibly in
the narrow regime where the associated diagonal dissipator coefficient satisfies

0<a_{jj}<\Theta(\eta^{2})
(as implied by
Section

D.1
). We leave a finer
analysis of this corner case for future work.

This
\eta
-heavy guarantee is sufficient when one only seeks the dominant terms. In contrast, our
coefficient-learning guarantees (
Section

E.6
,
Appendix

E
)
assume that the returned supports are supersets of the true ones, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}_{D}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
and
\mathcal{S}_{H}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
.

Remark D.13

(Reduced resource requirements via tighter operator norm bound)
.

are stated in terms of the crude smoothness scale

, coming from the uniform derivative bound

and

.
More generally, the same analysis goes through with any

.

Coefficient learning

Recall the adjoint Lindbladian in the Pauli basis:

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)=i\sum_{P_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}h_{k}\commutator{P_k}{O}+\sum_{P_{k},P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}}a_{kj}\left(P_{j}OP_{k}-\tfrac{1}{2}\anticommutator{P_j P_k}{O}\right).

(168)

In
Appendix

D
we identified candidate supports for the Hamiltonian and dissipator, yielding supersets

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
that contain the true supports
\mathcal{S}_{H}
and
\mathcal{S}_{D}
with high probability.
This appendix addresses the remaining estimation step: given these candidate supports, learn the corresponding numerical coefficients.

Problem 3

(Coefficient learning problem)
.

Given black-box access to the dynamics
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
for arbitrary
t\geq 0
and given supersets

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
such that

\mathcal{S}_{H}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H},\qquad\mathcal{S}_{D}\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D},

(169)

output estimators

\{\widehat{h}_{i}\}_{P_{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}}
and

\{\widehat{a}_{ij}\}_{(P_{i},P_{j})\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}}

such that, for all coefficients,

|\widehat{h}_{i}-h_{i}|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad|\widehat{a}_{ij}-a_{ij}|\leq\varepsilon,

(170)

with high probability.

The key observation is that short-time derivatives provide
linear
access to the Lindbladian coefficients through a common bilinear probe. For any Hermitian observable
O
and Hermitian input operator
X
(not necessarily a state), define

f_{O,X}(t)\coloneq\tr\bigl(O\,e^{t\mathcal{L}}(X)\bigr).

(171)

Differentiating at
t=0
and using the definition of the adjoint gives

\left.\derivative{t}\,f_{O,X}(t)\right|_{t=0}=\tr\bigl(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\,X\bigr),

(172)

which becomes an explicit linear functional of the unknown parameters
(h_{i},a_{ij})
upon substituting
Equation

168
. Throughout we restrict to
Pauli probes
, where
O\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
X
is chosen from one of the following two experimentally convenient families:

(i) Pauli-eigenstate probes.
Taking
X=\rho
to be a Pauli-eigenstate input recovers

\left.\derivative{t}\,\expectationvalue{O(t)}\right|_{t=0}=\tr\bigl(\rho\,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\bigr).

(173)

(ii) Pauli-input probes.
Taking
X=2^{-n}Q
for
Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
yields

\left.\derivative{t}\,\tr\bigl(O\,e^{t\mathcal{L}}(2^{-n}Q)\bigr)\right|_{t=0}=2^{-n}\tr\bigl(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\,Q\bigr).

(174)

Although
2^{-n}Q
is not a density operator, the scalar
f_{O,2^{-n}Q}(t)
is still experimentally accessible: since

\frac{Q}{2^{n}}=\frac{I+Q}{2^{n}}-\frac{I-Q}{2^{n}}
, one can estimate
f_{O,2^{-n}Q}(t)
as the difference of two expectation values obtained by preparing the Pauli eigenstates
\rho_{\pm}=(I\pm Q)/2^{n}
, evolving under
e^{t\mathcal{L}}
, and measuring
O
.

In both cases, coefficient learning reduces to estimating a collection of derivatives of the form (
172
) and solving the resulting linear system; which probe family is used will be clear from context.

The appendix proceeds in five steps:

•

Linear system from short-time derivatives.

In
Section

E.1
, we derive a real-valued linear system
\mathbf{d}=C\mathbf{x}
, where
\mathbf{d}
is the vector of measured derivatives,
\mathbf{x}
collects unknown
(h_{i},a_{ij})
and each row of the design matrix
C
is determined by a Pauli probe (either (
173
) or (
174
)).

•

Classical construction of the design matrix.

To efficiently choose Pauli probes that make the matrix
C
invertible, we introduce
Lindbladian patchwise Pauli tomography
(
Section

E.2
). We show that the family of patches induced by the candidate supports—namely,
\{\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P\right):P\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}\}
and
\{\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{i}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{j}\right):(P_{i},P_{j})\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\times\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\}
—defines an injective coefficient-to-data map. Concretely, the patch-restricted Pauli data

2^{-n}\tr\bigl(Q\,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\bigr)
, collected over Pauli observables
O,Q
supported within these patches, uniquely determine all Lindbladian coefficients
(h_{i},a_{ij})
(
Section

E.2
). As a consequence, if all candidate Pauli terms are at most
k
-local, one can distill a set of
\widehat{M}:=|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}|+|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}|^{2}
Pauli probes that yield an invertible matrix
C
, with classical preprocessing time
\mathcal{O}(16^{k}\,\widehat{M})
(
Section

E.2
).

•

Derivative estimation by Chebyshev interpolation.

Once the design matrix
C
is constructed, for each Pauli probe, we estimate the required derivative from short-time evolution under
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
using Gauss–Chebyshev interpolation (
Appendix

B
), with smoothness controlled by the dual interaction graph degree
\mathfrak{d}
(
Theorems

C.9
and
E.3
). Performing probe-by-probe derivative estimation to accuracy
\varepsilon_{d}
in the style of
Section

E.3
requires
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{M}\,\mathfrak{d}^{2}/\varepsilon_{d}^{2})
quantum channel queries (
Section

E.3
).

•

Parallelization via shadow process tomography.

If the selected probes Paulis are at most
k
-local, the derivative estimation can be parallelized and achieve smaller query complexity for sufficiently small
k
.
At each Gauss–Chebyshev time node, we estimate all required Pauli expectation values in parallel using the shadow process tomography protocol of
82
(
Section

E.4
) and then combine the node values into derivative estimates. The quantum channel query complexity of this method is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(9^{k}\,\mathfrak{d}^{2}/\varepsilon_{d}^{2})
(
Section

E.4
).

•

Solving the resulting linear system

After the design matrix
C
is constructed and all probe derivatives
\mathbf{d}
have been measured, we classically solve the resulting square linear system
\mathbf{d}=C\mathbf{x}
. The resulting accuracy in estimating the coefficients
\mathbf{x}
is bounded using conditioning factor
\nu=\|C^{-1}\|_{\infty\to\infty}
(
Section

E.5
and
Section

E.7
).

Together, these steps yield an ancilla-free
Section

E.6
that solves the
Problem 3

(Coefficient learning problem)
.
by reconstructing all coefficients
(h_{i},a_{ij})
to additive accuracy
\varepsilon
with high probability. We prove the algorithm guarantees and resource bounds in
Theorem

E.10
.

E.1
Linear relation between Lindbladian coefficients and Pauli expectation values

For any observable
O
and Hermitian input operator
X
(not necessarily a state), the short-time derivative of the
bilinear probe
f_{O,X}(t)\coloneq\tr\bigl(O\,e^{\mathcal{L}t}(X)\bigr)
at
t=0
is determined by the adjoint
Lindbladian acting on
O
:

\left.\derivative{t}\,\tr\bigl(O\,e^{\mathcal{L}t}(X)\bigr)\right|_{t=0}=\tr\bigl(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\,X\bigr).

(175)

Throughout the manuscript, we restrict to Pauli probes: the observable
O
is taken to be a Pauli operator and
X

is chosen from the probe families introduced in

Equations

173
and
174

(either
X=\rho
a Pauli eigenstate, or
X=2^{-n}Q
for
Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
). Substituting the Pauli-basis
expansion of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
and restricting to the candidate supports
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
and

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
, we obtain a linear relation between the measurable quantity

\left.\derivative{t}\,\tr\bigl(O\,e^{\mathcal{L}t}(X)\bigr)\right|_{t=0}
and the unknown coefficients
\{h_{i}\}
and

\{a_{ij}\}
:

\left.\derivative{t}\,\tr\bigl(O\,e^{\mathcal{L}t}(X)\bigr)\right|_{t=0}=\sum_{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}}h_{i}\,\tr{i[P_i,O]\,X}+\sum_{i,j\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}}a_{ij}\Bigl(\tr{P_j O P_i\,X}-\tfrac{1}{2}\tr{\acomm{P_j P_i}{O}\,X}\Bigr).

(176)

The short-time derivative of a Pauli probe is therefore a linear function of all Hamiltonian and dissipative
coefficients, and the prefactors
\tr{i[P_i,O]\,X}
and

\tr{P_j O P_i\,X}-\frac{1}{2}\tr{\acomm{P_j P_i}{O}\,X}

depend only on the probe
(X,O)
and can be computed efficiently.

Evaluating
Equation

176
for a collection of probe settings
(X,O)
yields a
system of linear equations

\mathbf{d}=C\,\mathbf{x},

(177)

where
\mathbf{d}
is the vector of measured derivatives,
\mathbf{x}
collects all unknown coefficients
(h_{i},a_{ij})
,
and each row of the design matrix
C
is determined by the corresponding probe.
If the chosen probes yield linearly independent rows, the coefficients are uniquely determined.
When
C
has full column rank, the coefficients can be recovered by least squares

\mathbf{x}=(C^{\top}C)^{-1}C^{\top}\mathbf{d}.

(178)

Thus, the coefficient-learning problem reduces to a classical linear inversion task, with design matrix
C
fixed by
Pauli algebra. In
Section

E.6
, we construct a square invertible
C
. In practice, one
could also use an overdetermined system by including additional probe settings. Such oversampling can improve
stability in favorable instances, but it can also leave the conditioning unchanged or worse depending on the added
rows. We do not analyze this effect.

We now make the linear structure explicit and derive the real-valued system used for coefficient estimation.
Define the shorthand coefficients

c_{H,i}^{X,O}:=\tr{i[P_i,O]\,X},\qquad c_{D,ij}^{X,O}:=\tr{P_j O P_i\,X}-\tfrac{1}{2}\tr{\acomm{P_j P_i}{O}\,X},

(179)

so that the derivative for each probe
(X,O)
can be written as

d(X,O)=\sum_{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}}h_{i}\,c_{H,i}^{X,O}+\sum_{i,j\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}}a_{ij}\,c_{D,ij}^{X,O}.

(180)

For Pauli observables and Pauli probes
X
from

Equations

173
and
174
,
all coefficients
c_{H,i}^{X,O}
and
c_{D,ij}^{X,O}
are computable directly from Pauli commutation relations.
To obtain a real-valued linear system, we separate diagonal and off-diagonal dissipator terms,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}}a_{ij}\,c_{D,ij}^{X,O}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}}a_{ii}\,c_{D,ii}^{X,O}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\\
j<i\end{subarray}}\Bigl(2\real\{a_{ij}\}\,\real\{c_{D,ij}^{X,O}\}-2\imaginary\{a_{ij}\}\,\imaginary\{c_{D,ij}^{X,O}\}\Bigr),

(181)

where we used the Hermiticity of both
a
and
c_{D}^{X,O}
.
All unknown parameters are grouped into real vectors

\mathbf{h},\quad\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{diag}},\quad\mathbf{a}_{\real},\quad\mathbf{a}_{\imaginary},

(182)

with corresponding design vectors

\mathbf{c}_{H}^{X,O},\quad\mathbf{c}_{D,\mathrm{diag}}^{X,O},\quad\mathbf{c}_{D,\real}^{X,O},\quad\mathbf{c}_{D,\imaginary}^{X,O}.

(183)

Combining these contributions yields

d(X,O)=\mathbf{c}_{H}^{X,O}\dotproduct\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{c}_{D,\mathrm{diag}}^{X,O}\dotproduct\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{diag}}+\mathbf{c}_{D,\real}^{X,O}\dotproduct\mathbf{a}_{\real}+\mathbf{c}_{D,\imaginary}^{X,O}\dotproduct\mathbf{a}_{\imaginary}.

(184)

Finally, we concatenate all parameters into a single unknown vector
\mathbf{x}
and define the corresponding design
row
\mathbf{c}^{\,X,O}
,

\mathbf{x}:=\mathbf{h}\oplus\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{diag}}\oplus\mathbf{a}_{\real}\oplus\mathbf{a}_{\imaginary},\qquad\mathbf{c}^{\,X,O}:=\mathbf{c}_{H}^{X,O}\oplus\mathbf{c}_{D,\mathrm{diag}}^{X,O}\oplus\mathbf{c}_{D,\real}^{X,O}\oplus\mathbf{c}_{D,\imaginary}^{X,O},

(185)

leading to the compact linear system

d(X,O)=\mathbf{c}^{\,X,O}\dotproduct\mathbf{x},\qquad\mathbf{d}=C\mathbf{x}.

(186)

C

E.2
Lindbladian patchwise Pauli tomography

A central step in coefficient learning is to
classically
choose a set of Pauli probes whose resulting design matrix
C
is invertible.
Given that all candidate Pauli terms are at most
k
-local, a naive approach is to keep adding all
\Theta(k)
-local Pauli probe settings on
n
qubits until
C
has full rank, which can incur classical overhead scaling as
\mathcal{O}(n^{k})
.
Instead, we introduce
Lindbladian patchwise Pauli tomography
, which exploits the candidate structure to reduce this preprocessing to a cost that depends only on the sizes and locality of the Pauli terms in the candidate sets
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H},\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
(see
Section

E.2
).

We now formalize the identifiability statement underlying patchwise Pauli tomography in
Section

E.2
.

Lemma E.1

(Injectivity of patchwise Pauli tomography)
.

Consider a Lindbladian with adjoint action expressed in the Pauli basis as

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)=i\sum_{P_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}h_{i}[P_{i},O]\;+\;\sum_{P_{i},P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}}a_{ij}\Bigl(P_{j}OP_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{P_{j}P_{i},O\}\Bigr),

(188)

where
\mathcal{S}_{H},\mathcal{S}_{D}\subset\mathcal{P}_{n}\setminus\{I\}
are known index sets. Define
\mathcal{T}
to be the family of Pauli support sets (patches):

\mathcal{T}:=\bigl\{\mathrm{supp}(P_{i}):P_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}\bigr\}\;\cup\;\bigl\{\mathrm{supp}(P_{i})\cup\mathrm{supp}(P_{j}):P_{i},P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}\bigr\}.

(189)

where
\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P\right)
denotes the set of sites on which the Pauli
P
acts non-trivially.

For each patch
T\in\mathcal{T}
, define the corresponding set of Hermitian Paulis:
\mathcal{P}_{T}:=\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}:\mathrm{supp}(P)\subseteq T\}
and suppose that the full set of linear data

m_{T}(O,Q):=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathrm{tr}\Bigl(Q\,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\Bigr),\qquad\forall\,O,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{T},

(190)

is available. Then the mapping from the coefficients

\{h_{i}\}_{P_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}
and
\{a_{ij}\}_{P_{i},P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}}

to the collection of data

\{m_{T}(O,Q)\}_{T\in\mathcal{T},\;O,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{T}}

is injective.

Equivalently,
Section

E.2
is saying that there cannot exist two distinct Lindbladians supported on the same Pauli structure that give rise to identical patchwise Pauli tomography data. Hence, the coefficients can be uniquely reconstructed given the data.

Proof.

The proof is by contradiction. Suppose that the claim of
Section

E.2
is false. Then there exist two distinct Lindbladians
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
of the above form, with coefficient sets

\{h_{i}^{(1)}\},\{a_{ij}^{(1)}\}
and
\{h_{i}^{(2)}\},\{a_{ij}^{(2)}\}
, such that at least one coefficient differs,
h_{i}^{(1)}\neq h_{i}^{(2)}
or
a_{ij}^{(1)}\neq a_{ij}^{(2)}
, while the induced patchwise data coincide,

m_{T}^{(1)}(O,Q)=m_{T}^{(2)}(O,Q),\qquad\forall\,T\in\mathcal{T},\;\forall\,O,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{T}.

(191)

Define the difference Lindbladian
\Delta\mathcal{L}:=\mathcal{L}_{1}-\mathcal{L}_{2}
. The above assumptions are then equivalent to the simultaneous validity of the two conditions

\Delta\mathcal{L}\neq 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathrm{tr}\Bigl(Q\,\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\Bigr)=0,\qquad\forall\,T\in\mathcal{T},\;\forall\,O,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{T}.

(192)

The remainder of the proof shows that these two conditions cannot hold simultaneously. In particular, the second condition implies
\Delta\mathcal{L}=0
, yielding a contradiction.

Preliminaries.

Let
T\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
be a subset of qubits. We call
T
a patch. We denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})_{T}:=\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{P}_{T})
the space of
n
-qubit operators supported entirely within
T
, where
\mathcal{P}_{T}:=\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}:\mathrm{supp}(P)\subseteq T\}
. The unrestricted space of
n
-qubit operators is denoted by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

Let
\Pi_{T}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})_{T}
denote the corresponding orthogonal projector, given by

\Pi_{T}(X):=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{T}}\langle Q,X\rangle\,Q,

(193)

where
\langle Q,X\rangle:=2^{-n}\mathrm{tr}(Q^{\dagger}X)
.
If
X=\sum_{P_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}x_{i}P_{i}
is the Pauli expansion of an arbitrary operator, then
\Pi_{T}(X)=\sum_{\mathrm{supp}(P_{i})\subseteq T}x_{i}P_{i}
. Thus,
\Pi_{T}
removes all Pauli components of
X
that have nontrivial support outside of
T
.

Given a linear map
\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we define its restriction to the patch
T
by

\Delta\mathcal{L}_{T}^{\dagger}:=\Pi_{T}\circ\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\circ\Pi_{T}.

(194)

When restricted to the domain
B(\mathcal{H})_{T}
, this map captures the action of
\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
visible within the patch
T
. Therefore, it can be expanded in a basis of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})_{T})
. We use the elementary basis maps

E_{P_{n},P_{m}}(X):=P_{n}XP_{m},\qquad P_{n},P_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{T}.

(195)

Accordingly, there exist unique coefficients
c^{(T)}_{mn}
such that:

\Delta\mathcal{L}_{T}^{\dagger}=\sum_{P_{m},P_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{T}}c^{(T)}_{mn}\,E_{P_{n},P_{m}}.

(196)

Similarly, on the full space
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
P_{i},P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, the difference adjoint Lindbladian can be expressed as

\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(X)=i\sum_{P_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}\Delta h_{i}\bigl(E_{P_{i},I}(X)-E_{I,P_{i}}(X)\bigr)+\sum_{P_{i},P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}}\Delta a_{ij}\Bigl(E_{P_{j},P_{i}}(X)-\tfrac{1}{2}E_{P_{j}P_{i},I}(X)-\tfrac{1}{2}E_{I,P_{j}P_{i}}(X)\Bigr).

(197)

Projecting onto
T
gives an explicit representation:

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{L}_{T}^{\dagger}(X)

\displaystyle=i\sum_{P_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}\Delta h_{i}\Bigl((\Pi_{T}\circ E_{P_{i},I})(X_{T})-(\Pi_{T}\circ E_{I,P_{i}})(X_{T})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{P_{i},P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}}\Delta a_{ij}\Bigl((\Pi_{T}\circ E_{P_{j},P_{i}})(X_{T})-\tfrac{1}{2}(\Pi_{T}\circ E_{P_{j}P_{i},I})(X_{T})-\tfrac{1}{2}(\Pi_{T}\circ E_{I,P_{j}P_{i}})(X_{T})\Bigr),

(198)

where
X_{T}:=\Pi_{T}(X)
and
(\Pi_{T}\circ E_{P_{j},P_{i}})(X_{T})=\Pi_{T}(P_{j}X_{T}P_{i})
.

Matching coefficients.

We first show that all of the dissipative differences
\{\Delta a_{ij}\}_{P_{i},P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}}
vanish. Let

\mathcal{T}_{D}:=\bigl\{\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{i}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{j}\right):\;P_{i},P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}\bigr\}

(199)

be the family of dissipator patches. The argument proceeds by an iterative “peeling” over these patches: at each step we pick a patch that is maximal among those not yet handled, prove that
all
coefficient differences whose union-support equals that patch are zero, and then remove that patch and continue.

Formally, let
\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(0)}:=\mathcal{T}_{D}
. At stage
\ell
, choose any patch

T\in\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell)}
that is maximal under inclusion (i.e., no
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell)}
satisfies

T\subsetneq T^{\prime}
). Let

\mathcal{I}^{(\ell)}(T):=\{(u,v):\ P_{u},P_{v}\in\mathcal{S}_{D},\ \operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{u}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{v}\right)=T\}

denote the set of
active pairs
realizing
T
. We will show that
\Delta a_{uv}=0
for every

(u,v)\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell)}(T)
. We then set
\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell+1)}:=\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell)}\setminus\{T\}

and proceed to the next stage, repeating until
\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell)}
is empty. Throughout the peeling we maintain the invariant that, at the beginning of each stage
\ell
,

\Delta a_{uv}=0\qquad\text{whenever}\qquad\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{u}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{v}\right)\notin\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell)}.

This invariant holds trivially at
\ell=0
. The argument below shows that the chosen maximal patch
T

has
\Delta a_{uv}=0
for all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell)}(T)
, so removing
T
preserves the invariant
when passing from
\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell)}
to
\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell+1)}
.

Consider any active pair realizing this patch, for example,
(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell)}(T)
. We show that the corresponding coefficient
\Delta a_{ij}
must vanish. By the second assumption in (
192
), all patchwise Pauli data on
T
is zero:

\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathrm{tr}\Bigl(Q\,\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\Bigr)=0,\qquad\forall\,O,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{T}

(200)

Hence, for any

O\in\mathcal{P}_{T}
,

\Delta\mathcal{L}_{T}^{\dagger}(O)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{T}}\langle Q,\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\rangle\,Q=0,

(201)

which implies
\Delta\mathcal{L}_{T}^{\dagger}=0
as a map on

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})_{T}
. Consequently, all coefficients

c^{(T)}_{mn}
in
Equation

196
must vanish.

We now match these coefficients to the Lindbladian parameters. Consider the
coefficient
c^{(T)}_{ij}
in front of
E_{P_{j},P_{i}}
in
Equation

196
. From the projected form of the Lindbladian in
Section

E.2
, by linear independence of maps
E_{P_{v},P_{u}}
, we get

c^{(T)}_{ij}=\sum_{(P_{u},P_{v})\sim(P_{i},P_{j})}\Delta a_{uv},

(202)

where the sum is over
P_{u},P_{v}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}
and the condition
(P_{u},P_{v})\sim(P_{i},P_{j})
means that
\Pi_{T}\circ E_{P_{v},P_{u}}=E_{P_{j},P_{i}}
as maps on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})_{T}
.

In the above sum, the pair
(P_{i},P_{j})
contributes the term
\Delta a_{ij}E_{P_{j},P_{i}}
directly. The only other way to obtain the same
projected basis element within the sum in (
202
) after applying
\Pi_{T}
is if
P_{u}
and
P_{v}

coincide with
P_{i}
and
P_{j}
on the patch
T
and share a common nontrivial Pauli
factor outside
T
. That is, if
P_{u}=\omega_{u}P_{i}\,(I_{T}\otimes R)
and
P_{v}=\omega_{v}P_{j}\,(I_{T}\otimes R)
for some nontrivial Pauli
R
supported on the complement of
T
and some phases
\omega_{u},\omega_{v}\in\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
. However, in this case,
T_{uv}\in\mathcal{T}_{D}
strictly contains
T
:

T_{uv}=\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{u}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{v}\right)=T\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(R\right)\supsetneq T.

(203)

Since
T
is maximal in
\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell)}
, this implies that
T_{uv}\notin\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell)}
and, therefore, by the maintained invariant has
\Delta a_{uv}=0
. It follows that the right-hand sum in (
202
) reduces to a single term. Since
\Delta\mathcal{L}_{T}^{\dagger}=0
, we obtain
c^{(T)}_{ij}=\Delta a_{ij}=0
.

The above argument applies verbatim to any pair
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell)}(T)
upon considering
c^{(T)}_{u^{\prime}v^{\prime}}
of
E_{P_{v}^{\prime},P_{u}^{\prime}}
. Hence, all dissipative coefficient differences associated with the maximal patch in
T\in\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell)}
are zero, and proceeding to
\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell+1)}:=\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell)}\setminus\{T\}
maintains the invariant.

Iterating this peeling until
\mathcal{T}_{D}^{(\ell)}=\emptyset
yields

\Delta a_{ij}=0\qquad\forall\,P_{i},P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{D},

and hence the dissipative part of
\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
vanishes. Since every dissipator coefficient
\Delta a_{uv}
is indexed by a pair
(u,v)
and hence has a patch
\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{u}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{v}\right)\in\mathcal{T}_{D}
, restricting the peeling argument to
\mathcal{T}_{D}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
is sufficient.

Since we have already established
\Delta a_{uv}=0
for all
P_{u},P_{v}\in\mathcal{S}_{D}
, the difference map
\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
is purely Hamiltonian,

\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)=i\sum_{P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}}\Delta h_{j}[P_{j},O].

(204)

It remains to show that the Hamiltonian coefficients vanish as well. Let

\mathcal{T}_{H}:=\bigl\{\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{i}\right)\>\;P_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}\bigr\}

(205)

be the family of Hamiltonian patches. Consider any patch
T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
and let:

\mathcal{I}_{H}(T):=\{j:\ P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{H},\ \operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{j}\right)=T\}

denote the set of Pauli indices realizing
T
. Consider any index
i\in\mathcal{I}_{H}(T)
. As above, vanishing patchwise data on
T
implies
\Delta\mathcal{L}_{T}^{\dagger}=0
, and hence all coefficients

c^{(T)}_{mn}
in
Equation

196
are zero. We now match these coefficients to the Hamiltonian parameters. Projecting (
204
) into the patch
T
and matching the coefficients in front of
E_{P_{i},I}
with (
196
) yields:

c^{(T)}_{0i}=i\,\Delta h_{i}

(206)

Since
\Delta\mathcal{L}_{T}^{\dagger}=0
, and thus
c^{(T)}_{0i}=0
, this implies
\Delta h_{i}=0
.

The above argument applies verbatim to any patch
T\in\mathcal{T}_{H}
and any corresponding Pauli index
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{H}(T)
, therefore, concluding that:

\Delta h_{i}=0\qquad\forall\,P_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{H}.

(207)

Combined with
\Delta a_{ij}=0
, this yields
\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}=0
. This contradicts the assumption that
\Delta\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\neq 0
. We conclude that no two distinct Lindbladians supported on the fixed index sets
\mathcal{S}_{H}
and
\mathcal{S}_{D}
can produce the same patchwise Pauli tomography data. Equivalently, the coefficient-to-data map is injective. This completes the proof.
∎

In the coefficient–learning stage we are given candidate structure supersets

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
.
We define the Hamiltonian locality
k_{H}
and dissipator locality
k_{D}
as

k_{H}:=\max_{P\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}}|\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P\right)|,\qquad k_{D}:=\max_{(P_{i},P_{j})\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}}|\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{i}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{j}\right)|.

(208)

These quantities correspond to the largest patch sizes that can appear in

Section

E.2
.
They are known at this stage, since
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
are outputs of the structure learning procedure.

Corollary E.2

(Classical cost of constructing an invertible design matrix)
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H},\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
be candidate Hamiltonian and dissipator Pauli supports, and let
k_{H}
and
k_{D}
be Hamiltonian and dissipator localities from
Equation

208
. Let

\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{H}:=\{\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P\right):P\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}\},\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{D}:=\{\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{i}\right)\cup\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P_{j}\right):(P_{i},P_{j})\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\times\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}\},

and
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}:=\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{H}\cup\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{D}
.
Then the full patchwise Pauli dataset

\Bigl\{\,m_{T}(O,Q)=2^{-n}\tr\bigl(Q\,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\bigr):\;T\in\widehat{\mathcal{T}},\ \forall\,O,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{T}\,\Bigr\}

has an associated design matrix of full column rank (hence uniquely identifies all Lindbladian coefficients), and contains at most

16^{k_{H}}\,|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}|\;+\;16^{k_{D}}\,|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}|^{2}

Pauli probes. Consequently, enumerating this probe set and forming the corresponding design matrix
can be done in classical time

\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(16^{k_{H}}|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}|+16^{k_{D}}|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}|^{2}\bigr)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
E.2
, the full patchwise dataset over

T\in\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
and
O,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{T}
uniquely determines the Lindbladian coefficients
supported on
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
; equivalently, the design matrix
indexed by triples
(T,O,Q)
has full column rank.
For each Hamiltonian patch
T\in\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{H}
we have
|T|\leq k_{H}
and thus

|\mathcal{P}_{T}|=4^{|T|}\leq 4^{k_{H}}
, so enumerating all pairs
(O,Q)\in\mathcal{P}_{T}\times\mathcal{P}_{T}

produces at most
16^{k_{H}}
probes per such patch (and by the proof of
Section

E.2
identifies all Hamiltonian coefficients) . Since
|\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{H}|\leq|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}|
,
this contributes at most
16^{k_{H}}|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}|
probes.
Similarly, for each dissipator patch
T\in\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{D}
we have
|T|\leq k_{D}
, so there are at most

16^{k_{D}}
probe pairs per patch, and
|\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{D}|\leq|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}|^{2}
, contributing at most

16^{k_{D}}|\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}|^{2}
probes. Summing the two contributions gives the claimed bound.
∎

E.3
Estimating time-derivatives of Pauli expectation values

Having established that the short-time derivatives of Pauli expectation values determine the Lindbladian coefficients through a linear system and that an appropriate choice of Pauli probes yields a full-rank design matrix
C
, it remains to estimate the required derivatives from experimental data. A simple approach is to treat the probes independently: for each probe, we estimate the derivative
f^{\prime}(0)
of a Pauli expectation value of the form

f(t)\;:=\;\tr\bigl(O\,\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho)\bigr),\qquad\mathcal{E}_{t}=e^{t\mathcal{L}},

where
O\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the measured Pauli observable and
\rho
is a Pauli-eigenstate input. We estimate
f^{\prime}(0)
by sampling
f(t)
at a small set of short times, fitting a Gauss–Chebyshev interpolant, and analytically differentiating the interpolant at
t=0
.
For probes defined via Pauli inputs
X=2^{-n}Q
(i.e., quantities of the form
\tr(O\,\mathcal E_t(2^{-n}Q))
), one can reduce their estimation to the same primitive
\tr(O\,\mathcal E_t(\rho))
using Pauli-eigenstate preparations with only constant overhead (see the discussion following
Equation

174
).

Lemma E.4

(Estimation of Pauli–expectation derivatives)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{t}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}
be an
n
-qubit channel generated by a Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
.
Let
\mathfrak{d}
be the maximum degree of the dual interaction graph of
\mathcal{L}
as in
Section

C.2
. Let

O
be a Pauli observable with neighborhood size
\mathfrak{d}_{O}\leq\mathfrak{d}
(cf.
Equation

94
).
Define
f(t)=\tr{O\,\mathcal{E}_t(\rho)}
.
Choose
\tau_{\max}=\Theta(1/\mathfrak{d})
and

r=\Theta(\log(\mathfrak d/\varepsilon))
, and obtain estimates

\{\widehat{f}(t_{m})\}
at the
r{+}1
Gauss–Chebyshev nodes

\{t_{m}\}_{m=0}^{r}\subset[0,\tau_{\max}]
using a total of

m\;=\;\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{d}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\Big(\mathfrak{d},\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon},\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Big)\right)

(209)

applications of the channel
e^{\mathcal{L}t}
.
Then one can construct an estimator
\widehat{f}^{\,\prime}(0)
such that,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\big|\widehat{f}^{\,\prime}(0)-f^{\prime}(0)\big|\;\leq\;\varepsilon.

(210)

Proof.

We estimate derivatives at
t=0
using Chebyshev interpolation applied to

f(t)=\langle O(t)\rangle=\tr{O\,e^{\mathcal{L}t}(\rho)}.

(211)

If
f(t)
were available exactly, the only source of error would be interpolation bias.
In practice,
f(t)
is estimated from
m_{s}
independent measurements. For a fixed time
t
, perform
m_{s}
independent measurements of the Pauli observable
O
on independently prepared copies of

\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho)
. Let
O_{i}(t)\in\{-1,1\}
denote the outcome of the
i
th measurement (so that

\Pr[O_{i}(t)=\pm 1]=\tfrac{1\pm\langle O(t)\rangle}{2}
). Then
\mathbb{E}[O_{i}(t)]=\langle O(t)\rangle
, and we estimate it by the sample mean

\langle O(t)\rangle_{m_{s}}=\frac{1}{m_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{s}}O_{i}(t),

(212)

and Hoeffding’s inequality implies

\mathrm{P}\Big[\,\big|\langle O(t)\rangle_{m_{s}}-\langle O(t)\rangle\big|\geq\varepsilon_{s}\,\Big]\;\leq\;2\,e^{-m_{s}\varepsilon_{s}^{2}/2}.

(213)

Thus
\big|\langle O(t)\rangle_{m_{s}}-\langle O(t)\rangle\big|\leq\varepsilon_{s}

with probability at least
1-\delta_{s}
whenever

m_{s}\;\geq\;\frac{2}{\varepsilon_{s}^{2}}\log\frac{2}{\delta_{s}}.

(214)

To apply the Chebyshev error bounds, it remains to control higher-order derivatives of
\langle O(t)\rangle
.
By
Theorem

C.9
, the derivatives satisfy the uniform bound

\Big|\derivative[k]{t}\langle O(t)\rangle\Big|\;\leq\;(2\mathfrak{d})^{k}\,(k!),\qquad k\geq 0,

(215)

uniformly in
t
and independent of the initial state
\rho
.
Thus the assumptions of
Section

B.2
hold with

B=1
and
\Lambda=2\mathfrak{d}
.

Applying
Section

B.2
yields the parameter choices

\tau_{\max}\;=\;\frac{1}{4\mathfrak{d}},\qquad r\;=\;\max\left\{16,\;\Big\lceil 4\log\Big(\tfrac{16\,\mathfrak d}{\varepsilon}\Big)\Big\rceil\right\},\qquad\varepsilon_{s}\;=\;\frac{\varepsilon}{20\,\mathfrak{d}\,r^{3}}.

(216)

With
\{t_{m}\}_{m=0}^{r}
chosen as Gauss–Chebyshev nodes on
[0,\tau_{\max}]
,
Section

B.2
guarantees that the Chebyshev-based estimator constructed from noisy samples
\{\widehat{f}(t_{m})\}
obeys
\big|\widehat{f}^{\,\prime}(0)-f^{\prime}(0)\big|\;\leq\;\varepsilon
, provided that the node-wise noise is uniformly bounded by

\big|\widehat{f}(t_{m})-f(t_{m})\big|\;\leq\;\varepsilon_{s},\qquad\forall\,m\in\{0,\dots,r\}.

(217)

By
Equation

214
, achieving
\big|\widehat{f}(t_{m})-f(t_{m})\big|\leq\varepsilon_{s}
with failure probability at most
\delta_{s}
requires

m_{s}\;=\;\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon_{s}^{-2}\,\log\frac{1}{\delta_{s}}\right)\;=\;\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{d}^{2}\,r^{6}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\log\frac{1}{\delta_{s}}\right),

(218)

using
\varepsilon_{s}
from (
216
). Setting
\delta_{s}=\delta/(r{+}1)
and applying a union bound over all
r{+}1
nodes ensures the joint failure probability is at most
\delta
. Summing over nodes, the total number of channel uses is

m\;=\;(r{+}1)\,m_{s}\;=\;\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{d}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\;r^{7}\;\log\frac{r}{\delta}\right)\;=\;\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{d}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\Big(\mathfrak{d},\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon},\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Big)\right),

(219)

since
r=\Theta\big(\log(\mathfrak d/\varepsilon)\big)
. Combining this sample bound with the schedule parameters from
Equation

216
concludes the proof of
Section

E.3
.
∎

Remark E.5

(Sparsity-only schedule)
.

If one only knows that
\mathcal{L}
is
M
-sparse, then
Section

C.2
gives

\bigl|\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\langle O(t)\rangle\bigr|\leq(2M)^{k}
for all
k\geq 0
.
Thus, the same Chebyshev argument as in
Section

E.3
goes through with

\mathfrak{d}
replaced by
M
(equivalently
\Lambda=2M
), i.e. it suffices to take

\tau_{\max}=\Theta(1/M)
and

and use

A naive algorithm for derivative estimation of all probes follows directly:

Corollary E.6

(Estimating all probe derivatives (probe-by-probe))
.

Let
C\in\mathbb{R}^{\widehat{M}\times\widehat{M}}
be a square full-rank design matrix built from
\widehat{M}
Pauli probes, and let
\mathfrak{d}
be the dual-interaction-graph degree from

. Then for any

.

E.4
Parallel estimation of many
k
-local derivatives

When the selected Pauli probes are sufficiently local, the derivative estimation can be
parallelized
to reduce the query complexity.
Concretely, let
k_{H}
and
k_{D}
denote the Hamiltonian and dissipator localities extracted from the candidate sets
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{D}
as in
Equation

208
, and set
k:=\max\{k_{H},k_{D}\}
.

In this section we leverage the shadow process tomography protocol of
82
to estimate, at each Gauss–Chebyshev time node,
all
Pauli expectation values needed for the patchwise derivative data of
Section

E.2
in parallel, and then combine the node-wise estimates into derivative estimates.
This yields overall parallel query complexity

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(9^{k}\,\mathfrak{d}^{2}/\varepsilon_{d}^{2}\right)

(see
Section

E.4
).

First, we import the following lemma:

Lemma E.7

(Shadow process tomography,
82
)
.

Let
\Phi
be a quantum channel on
n
qubits.
Let
\{P^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{K_{1}}
and
\{Q^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{K_{2}}
be Pauli strings with

|\operatorname{supp}\!\left(P^{(m)}\right)|\leq\omega_{P}
and
|\operatorname{supp}\!\left(Q^{(l)}\right)|\leq\omega_{Q}
.
Then there exist estimators
\hat{e}_{m,l}
such that

\bigl|2^{-n}\tr(P^{(m)}\Phi(Q^{(l)}))-\hat{e}_{m,l}\bigr|\leq\varepsilon

(220)

for all
m,l
simultaneously, with probability at least
1-\delta
, using

\mathcal{O}\left(3^{\omega_{P}+\omega_{Q}}\varepsilon^{-2}\log(K_1K_2\delta^{-1})\right)

(221)

samples.
The protocol requires only random product Pauli state preparations and Pauli measurements.

Proof.

This result was proven in Supplementary Note 8 of
82
. The authors introduce a shadow process tomography protocol in which each experimental run prepares a random product Pauli eigenstate, applies
\Phi
, and measures in a random Pauli basis. For each Pauli pair
(P,Q)
they define a classical random variable
X_{P,Q}
from the measurement record. They show that
X_{P,Q}
is an unbiased estimator,
\mathbb{E}[X_{P,Q}]=2^{-n}\tr(P\Phi(Q))
, and that its second moment satisfies
\mathbb{E}[X_{P,Q}^{2}]\leq 3^{\omega(P)+\omega(Q)}
. Using a median-of-means estimator together with a union bound over all
K_{1}K_{2}
Pauli pairs yields simultaneous error
\varepsilon
with failure probability at most
\delta
and the stated sample complexity.
∎

Remark E.8

(Classical cost of shadow process tomography)
.

The shadow process tomography protocol outputs, for each sample, a classical record from which one can compute the per-pair estimators
\hat{e}_{m,l}
. A direct implementation that updates all
K_{1}K_{2}
pairs per sample costs
\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(K_{1}K_{2}(\omega_{P}+\omega_{Q})\bigr)
classical time per sample (using sparse Pauli representations). Combining with the sample complexity in
Section

E.4
gives total classical postprocessing time

\mathcal{O}\!\left(K_{1}K_{2}(\omega_{P}+\omega_{Q})\,3^{\omega_{P}+\omega_{Q}}\varepsilon^{-2}\log(K_1K_2\delta^{-1})\right).

We now apply shadow process tomography (
Section

E.4
) to parallelize the estimation of the Pauli-expectation derivatives required by the patchwise probes in
Section

E.2
.

(Parallel estimation of tomographically complete derivative data)

applications of the channel

Proof.

The total number of unknown coefficients to be reconstructed is

\widehat{M}:=\widehat{M}_{H}+\widehat{M}_{D}^{2}.

(224)

By

(225)

for each patch

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{H}
and

\widehat{M}_{H}

E.5
Stability of least squares under noise

The Chebyshev interpolation procedure provides approximate estimates of the short-time derivatives
d(\rho,O)
, subject to both interpolation bias and sampling noise. Whether we do probe-by-probe estimation (
Section

E.3
) or the parallelized shadow tomography (
Section

E.4
), each entry of the data vector
\mathbf{d}
is perturbed by an additive error of at most
\varepsilon_{d}
, with an overall failure probability at most
\delta
.
We denote these perturbations collectively by a noise vector
\mathbf{e}
and analyze their effect on the least-squares reconstruction of the coefficient vector
\mathbf{x}
.

Assuming
C
has full column rank and
\mathbf{d}=C\mathbf{x}
, the measured data can be written as

\widehat{\mathbf{d}}=\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{e},\qquad\|\mathbf{e}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{d}.

(230)

The least-squares estimator is

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}=(C^{\top}C)^{-1}C^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{d}}.

(231)

Subtracting the true coefficient vector and using the induced
\ell_{\infty}
matrix norm yields

\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\|C^{+}\|_{\infty\to\infty}\,\|\mathbf{e}\|_{\infty}\leq\|C^{+}\|_{\infty\to\infty}\,\varepsilon_{d},

(232)

where
C^{+}=(C^{\top}C)^{-1}C^{\top}
is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse and
\|C^{+}\|_{\infty\to\infty}
is the
\ell_{\infty}
-induced norm, equal to the largest absolute row sum of
C^{+}
.
This quantity measures how strongly entrywise errors in
\mathbf{d}
are amplified in the reconstructed coefficients.

To achieve a target accuracy
\varepsilon
in each recovered parameter, i.e.,
\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
, it suffices to estimate the derivatives with precision

\varepsilon_{d}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\|C^{+}\|_{\infty\to\infty}}.

(233)

The induced norm
\|C^{+}\|_{\infty\to\infty}
thus quantifies the numerical conditioning of the reconstruction.
Its value depends on the structure of
C
, determined by the chosen measurement settings and by the candidate Hamiltonian and dissipator supports.
Once
C
is fixed,
\|C^{+}\|_{\infty\to\infty}
can be computed efficiently and used to set the required accuracy of the derivative estimates. In
Section

E.7
, we numerically show that the conditioning factor
\nu
remains moderate (typically on the order of
10
–
30
) in a physically motivated lattice model up to
n=42
.

Proof.

(ii) estimates the required short-time derivatives using Gauss–Chebyshev sampling, and

Correctness.

Let
\widehat{M}=\widehat{M}_{H}+\widehat{M}_{D}^{2}
. Consider the (rectangular) design matrix

whose rows are indexed by all patchwise Pauli pairs

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{all}}=\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{P}_{T}\times\mathcal{P}_{T}

Section

E.1

\widehat{M}

E.6
selects such a minimal subset and forms the square invertible matrix

with probability

and to bound the resulting channel complexity.

E.6
uses parallel estimation of all Pauli probe derivatives.

E.4

Remark E.12

(Serialized derivative estimation)
.

If the candidate supports are highly nonlocal, then the locality parameters
k_{H},k_{D}
are large and the shadow-process parallelization can be counterproductive. In this regime it can be preferable to estimate derivatives
probe-by-probe
once a full-rank design matrix
C
has been fixed.

Concretely, the parallelized query complexity scales as

m_{\mathrm{par}}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\bigl(9^{k_{H}}+9^{k_{D}}\bigr)\,\frac{\mathfrak{d}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\!\Big(\widehat{M}_{H},\widehat{M}_{D},\mathfrak{d},\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon},\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Big)\right),

whereas estimating the
\widehat{M}
required derivatives independently (via
Section

E.3
) yields

m_{\mathrm{ser}}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\widehat{M}\cdot\frac{\mathfrak{d}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\;\mathrm{polylog}\!\Big(\mathfrak{d},\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon},\tfrac{\widehat{M}}{\delta}\Big)\right).

Thus, whenever
\widehat{M}\lesssim 9^{k_{H}}+9^{k_{D}}
, serialization is at least as favorable in channel-query scaling. Operationally, this corresponds to replacing the call to

\texttt{ShadowProcessTomography}(\mathcal{E}_{t_{m}},\mathcal{R},\varepsilon_{s},\delta_{s})

by probe-by-probe estimation at each node (
Section

E.3
).
All resource bounds in

E.10
continue to hold after the substitution

.

The corresponding classical runtime becomes

Evidently, for highly non-local candidate Paulis, the classical construction of the full rank design matrix

in this regime remains an open question.

Remark E.13

(Sparsity-only schedules)
.

The guarantees in
Theorem

E.10
are stated in terms of an upper bound

on the dual-interaction-graph degree, which governs the Gauss–Chebyshev schedule used for derivative estimation. If such a bound is unavailable, one may instead use a sparsity bound. In particular, the candidate structure size

F.1
Overview of the proof

We now explain the high-level idea behind the

in

The core idea is to construct two purely dissipative Lindbladians

and

\mathcal{L}_{1,\kappa}
with distinct dissipator supports, such that once the evolution time is restricted

This shows that enforcing a coarser time resolution can render structure learning exponentially hard.

•

Reduction to distinguishing.

Any protocol that learns the dissipator structure with threshold

can in particular distinguish
between the following two cases:

1.

The underlying Lindbladian is the alternative

Appendix G
SPAM robustness

In realistic experimental platforms, noise associated with state preparation and measurement (SPAM) is often a substantial source of error. For the learning guarantees derived above to be experimentally meaningful, our algorithm must therefore be robust to SPAM noise. Since the algorithm consists of a structure–learning stage followed by coefficient learning, both stages must tolerate such imperfections.

We consider a standard and widely used SPAM model in which errors are described by single–qubit depolarizing channels acting independently on each qubit. Concretely, for an intended single-qubit state
\rho
, the actual prepared state is

\rho\;\longmapsto\;\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{P}}(\rho):=r_{\mathrm{P}}\rho+(1-r_{\mathrm{P}})\tfrac{I}{2},

(267)

and immediately before the measurement, the single-qubit state undergoes

\rho\;\longmapsto\;\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{M}}(\rho):=r_{\mathrm{M}}\rho+(1-r_{\mathrm{M}})\tfrac{I}{2}.

(268)

We assume the retention parameters
r_{\mathrm{P}}
and
r_{\mathrm{M}}
are known from prior calibration and write

r=r_{\mathrm{P}}r_{\mathrm{M}}
. In our access model, an experiment therefore implements the sequence

\rho\;\longmapsto\;\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{M}}^{\otimes n}\circ e^{t\mathcal{L}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\otimes n}(\rho),

(269)

after which the measurement is performed.

First, consider the SPAM-robustness of the structure–learning step, which relies on Pauli channel estimation via population recovery. It was shown recently that Pauli error estimation can be made robust to SPAM noise of the above form
69
. Writing
\delta_{s}:=1-r
to quantify the magnitude of the overall SPAM error, the SPAM–robust algorithm operates in two regimes. In the low–SPAM regime
\delta_{s}\ll\tfrac{1}{n}\log{\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon_s}}
, the sample complexity matches the SPAM–free setting up to a polynomial factor
\operatorname{poly}\bigl(1/\varepsilon_{s}\bigr)
in terms of the per-node measurement accuracy
\varepsilon_{s}
. Consequently, since the structure learning guarantees of
Result

1
rely on
\varepsilon_{s}\leq\widetilde{\Theta}(\eta^{2}/M^{2})
, our algorithm would continue to hold with an additional
\operatorname{poly}\bigl(M/\eta\bigr)

We now turn to the coefficient–learning stage. Under the depolarizing SPAM model above, the preparation and measurement noise channels act diagonally in the Pauli basis. In particular, for any
n

P
,

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{P}}(P)=r_{\mathrm{P}}^{w(P)}P,\qquad\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{M}}^{\dagger}(P)=r_{\mathrm{M}}^{w(P)}P,

(270)

where
w(P)
denotes the Pauli weight. As a consequence, the short–time Pauli derivative data accessed by the
algorithm are damped relative to the noiseless case. Concretely, instead of
\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}\tr\bigl(Q\,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\bigr)
, we obtain

\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}\tr\Bigl(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{P}}(Q)\,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{M}}^{\dagger}(O)\bigr)\Bigr)=r_{\mathrm{M}}^{w(Q)}\,r_{\mathrm{P}}^{w(O)}\,\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}\tr\bigl(Q\,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(O)\bigr).

(271)

Recovering the noiseless derivative therefore amounts to rescaling the measured data by the known factor
r_{\mathrm{M}}^{-w(Q)}r_{\mathrm{P}}^{-w(O)}

w(Q),w(O)\leq k

k

r^{-2k}
with
