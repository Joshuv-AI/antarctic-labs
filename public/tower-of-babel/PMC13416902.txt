Standardized perioperative terminology for emergency cervical cerclage: A framework for feasibility and procedural complexity assessment
Authors: Yong‐Jin Park, Moon‐Il Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEmergency cervical cerclage (ECC) is performed for advanced cervical dilation with exposed or prolapsed membranes and represents the most technically demanding end of the cerclage spectrum. Existing classification systems categorize cerclage by indication but do not characterize operative cervical state at presentation or separate determinants of technical feasibility from modifiers of procedural complexity. The objective was to develop standardized perioperative terminology for describing operative cervical state in ECC and to propose a feasibility–complexity framework based on bulging extent, membrane reducibility, and cervical rim adequacy.Material and MethodsThis conceptual framework development study was informed by contemporary ECC literature and tertiary obstetric surgical practice. Operative descriptors recurrently described in ECC studies were synthesized into a structured model comprising three components: bulging extent (B), reducibility of prolapsed membranes (R), and cervical rim adequacy (C). Reducibility and cervical rim adequacy were defined as primary determinants of technical feasibility, whereas bulging extent was designated as a modifier of procedural complexity once feasibility is established. The framework supports structured perioperative assessment at presentation and immediately before or during the procedure.ResultsIntegration of the B–R–C components provides standardized perioperative descriptors and defines three feasibility‐based operative categories: low‐complexity, high‐complexity, and technically non‐feasible ECC. The framework explicitly separates technical feasibility from procedural complexity and supports consistent perioperative assessment, inter‐operator communication, and comparison across clinical settings and studies.ConclusionsThis state‐based framework aligns ECC assessment with the operative cervical state encountered at presentation and around the time of surgery, rather than indication alone. By distinguishing indication‐based eligibility, technical feasibility, and procedural complexity, it provides standardized perioperative terminology that may improve interpretation of existing evidence, support structured feasibility assessment, and inform future validation studies in ECC.

INTRODUCTION

Preterm birth remains a leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality worldwide, and cervical insufficiency is a major pathway to mid‐trimester loss and early preterm birth. Cervical cerclage is widely used as a preventive or therapeutic intervention and is categorized according to indication—history‐, ultrasound‐, or examination‐indicated—based on obstetric history, cervical length, or physical examination findings. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 While this classification provides a shared language for eligibility and reporting, it does not address real‐time operative considerations, particularly in advanced or rapidly evolving clinical scenarios.

Emergency cervical cerclage (ECC), performed in the setting of advanced cervical dilation with exposed or prolapsed membranes, represents the most technically challenging end of the cerclage spectrum. Unlike prophylactic or ultrasound‐indicated cerclage, ECC is undertaken under conditions of acute cervical failure rather than anticipated risk. At presentation, the clinical situation is inherently unstable, with a narrow and time‐sensitive operative window in which technical feasibility may change rapidly. This dynamic and unstable operative context is not adequately captured by existing indication‐based classification systems. 5

Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses suggest that ECC may prolong pregnancy compared with expectant management in selected cases; however, they consistently demonstrate substantial heterogeneity in reported outcomes. 6 , 7 This variability reflects not only quantitative differences but also structural limitations in how ECC is currently classified and interpreted. Even large contemporary reviews aggregate cases primarily according to indication or degree of dilation, without standardized characterization of operative feasibility. As a result, key technical factors that determine whether cerclage can be performed—such as membrane reducibility or cervical rim adequacy—are rarely incorporated into comparative analyses.

Reviews of ECC consistently identify membrane status, degree of dilation, and infection as important factors influencing procedural success; however, these variables are typically reported descriptively rather than integrated into a structured operative framework. 6 , 8 Consequently, cases with fundamentally different technical demands are often grouped together in clinical series and comparative analyses. Even advanced analytic approaches, including individual patient data meta‐analyses and umbrella reviews, remain constrained by the absence of standardized descriptors of operative cervical state. 9 As a result, critical intraoperative variables that determine whether cerclage can be performed are rarely captured in primary studies and cannot be systematically incorporated into secondary analyses.

Recent efforts to introduce staging systems based on cervical dilation and membrane prolapse represent an important step toward improved clinical phenotyping. 10 , 11 These systems consistently demonstrate worsening outcomes with increasing severity at presentation. However, such severity‐based staging functions primarily as a prognostic tool and does not resolve the operative question faced by the surgeon: Whether intervention is technically feasible and, if so, at what level of procedural complexity. Accordingly, staging based on dilation or prolapse alone does not define the feasibility thresholds that determine whether ECC can be safely performed.

This limitation reflects a broader shift in understanding cervical insufficiency as a dynamic, time‐dependent process rather than a fixed structural defect. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 In the context of ECC, delay may rapidly convert a technically feasible situation into one of greater complexity or non‐feasibility. The absence of a shared operative framework to capture this dynamic state may contribute to variability in guideline interpretation and leave clinicians without structured guidance at critical decision points.

Accordingly, there is a need for standardized perioperative terminology that characterizes ECC according to the operative cervical state encountered at presentation and around the time of surgery, rather than indication alone. The proposed framework is intended to distinguish indication‐based eligibility for ECC from the subsequent assessment of technical feasibility and expected procedural complexity. In this pathway, indication‐based assessment precedes the B–R–C evaluation, whereas the B–R–C framework addresses whether the procedure is technically feasible and, if feasible, how complex it is expected to be. Such an approach may support concise reporting, inter‐operator communication, operative planning, and comparison across studies.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and conceptual framework development

The proposed framework characterizes ECC according to three operative dimensions assessable at presentation and immediately before or during the procedure: bulging extent (B), reducibility of prolapsed membranes (R), and cervical rim adequacy (C). These components are not intended as diagnostic labels or prognostic scores, but as perioperative state descriptors that capture the operative cervical state relevant to technical feasibility and procedural complexity. Together, they provide standardized perioperative terminology to bridge the gap between indication‐based classification and feasibility–complexity assessment. The framework is intended to standardize the description of the operative cervical state and to support candidate selection, referral, operative planning, and surgeon selection, rather than to replace clinical judgment or function as a prognostic model.

Each component of the B–R–C framework captures a distinct aspect of the operative state in ECC. Bulging extent (B) defines the spatial position of prolapsed membranes and establishes the anatomical context of the operative field. Reducibility (R) reflects the technical feasibility of membrane reduction and directly informs whether cerclage can be performed. Cervical rim adequacy (C) determines whether sufficient residual tissue is available for secure suture anchoring. Although these dimensions often coexist and interact clinically, they are defined separately to permit systematic assessment and clear communication of operative conditions.

Operational definitions of framework components

Bulging extent (B): Spatial definition of membrane prolapse

Bulging extent (B) describes the location and degree of fetal membrane protrusion relative to the cervical canal and external os at presentation. This dimension defines the anatomical context of the operative field and influences exposure and initial technical conditions in ECC.

Figure 1 illustrates the grading of bulging extent based on progressive displacement of the fetal membranes. Lower grades indicate membranes confined within the cervical canal, whereas higher grades reflect descent to or beyond the external os, including vaginal prolapse. Importantly, this positional progression does not determine technical feasibility but defines the anatomical context that shapes subsequent technical challenges in ECC.

Advanced bulging does not preclude ECC, nor does limited bulging guarantee technical simplicity. Instead, B functions as a modifier of procedural complexity and should be interpreted in conjunction with reducibility (R) and cervical rim adequacy (C), rather than in isolation.

Reducibility (R): Technical feasibility of membrane reduction

Reducibility (R) describes the feasibility of repositioning prolapsed fetal membranes into the uterine cavity at presentation. Unlike bulging extent, which defines anatomical position, reducibility reflects the technical difficulty of membrane reduction and directly informs whether cerclage can be performed.

Figure 2 illustrates the grading of reducibility from easily reducible membranes to irreducible prolapse. Lower grades indicate membranes that can be repositioned with minimal manipulation, whereas higher grades require increasing technical effort or may remain irreducible. This grading reflects the technical effort required for membrane reduction rather than the anatomical extent of prolapse.

Grading of membrane reducibility (R) in emergency cervical cerclage. The figure depicts the spectrum of membrane reducibility from easily repositioned membranes to irreducible prolapse. Lower grades indicate minimal technical difficulty, whereas higher grades reflect increasing resistance to reduction. Reducibility serves as a primary determinant of operative feasibility within the B–R–C framework.

Within the B–R–C framework, reducibility is a primary determinant of technical feasibility in ECC. Successful membrane reduction is a prerequisite for safe suture placement, whereas irreducible membranes preclude safe cerclage. Accordingly, R grading directly informs whether ECC is technically feasible or should be deferred, independent of the positional severity defined by bulging extent.

Reducibility is a dynamic property that may change over short time intervals. Consequently, R grading represents a time‐sensitive operative assessment rather than a fixed attribute, underscoring the importance of timely evaluation in ECC.

Reducibility should be assessed using standardized, minimally traumatic maneuvers appropriate to the clinical setting. The framework does not prescribe specific techniques but emphasizes consistent documentation of the response to reduction. Cervical rim adequacy (C): Feasibility of suture anchoring

Cervical rim adequacy (C) refers to the amount and structural integrity of residual cervical tissue available to support secure suture anchoring at presentation. Unlike bulging extent or reducibility, which relate to membrane position and handling, cervical rim adequacy determines whether cerclage can be securely anchored.

Figure 3 illustrates the grading of cervical rim adequacy based on the continuity and structural integrity of the remaining cervical tissue. Lower grades indicate a well‐defined cervical rim that allows secure suture placement, whereas higher grades reflect progressive effacement or loss of tissue, rendering anchoring non‐feasible.

Grading of cervical rim adequacy (C) for suture anchoring. The figure illustrates progressive reduction in circumferential continuity and structural integrity of the cervical rim. Lower grades indicate sufficient tissue for secure suture placement, whereas higher grades represent marked effacement or fragmentation that may compromise anchoring. Cervical rim adequacy functions as a feasibility determinant in emergency cervical cerclage.

Within the B–R–C framework, cervical rim adequacy is a key determinant of technical feasibility in ECC. Even when membranes can be successfully reduced, inadequate or compromised cervical tissue may preclude safe cerclage. Accordingly, C grading directly determines whether ECC is technically feasible, independent of bulging extent or reducibility.

Cervical rim adequacy should be assessed under appropriate conditions with attention to tissue integrity. The framework does not prescribe specific examination techniques but emphasizes consistent evaluation of whether sufficient cervical tissue is present for secure anchoring. As with reducibility, cervical rim adequacy represents a dynamic operative state that may change over time, underscoring the importance of timely assessment in ECC.

RESULTS

The purpose of the B–R–C classification is not to stratify risk or predict outcome, but to translate observed cervical states into operative feasibility and procedural complexity. Although each component captures a distinct aspect of the operative state, their clinical value emerges when integrated into a coherent operative framework. This integration is illustrated in Figure 4, which links the B–R–C components to feasibility‐based operative categories based on standardized perioperative assessment.

Perioperative decision pathway for emergency cervical cerclage using the B–R–C framework. The pathway first distinguishes indication‐based assessment from operative feasibility assessment. Once ECC is considered clinically indicated, the patient is evaluated as an ECC candidate with membrane bulging. Reducibility of prolapsed membranes (R) is assessed first, followed by cervical rim adequacy (C), which together serve as the primary determinants of technical feasibility. Cases with reducible membranes and adequate cervical tissue are categorized as lower‐complexity or high‐complexity ECC according to reduction difficulty and anchoring conditions, whereas any R4 or C4 state indicates technically non‐feasible ECC. The final operative category may then inform operative planning, including whether, where, and by whom ECC should be performed.

Within this framework, reducibility (R) and cervical rim adequacy (C) function as the primary determinants of operative feasibility. Successful membrane reduction and the presence of sufficient cervical tissue for secure anchoring are prerequisite conditions for cerclage placement. When either condition is not met, ECC is not technically feasible, regardless of the extent of membrane bulging. Accordingly, R and C define the threshold of technical feasibility for ECC.

In contrast, bulging extent (B) does not determine operative feasibility but modifies procedural complexity once feasibility has been established. Advanced bulging increases technical difficulty and may require additional intraoperative measures. However, bulging alone does not preclude ECC if membranes are reducible and a sufficient cervical rim remains. Thus, B functions as a modifier of complexity rather than a determinant of feasibility.

Figure 4 depicts this logic as a perioperative decision pathway. Indication‐based assessment first determines whether ECC is clinically indicated. Once indicated, the B–R–C framework addresses whether the procedure is technically feasible and, if feasible, how complex it is expected to be. Reducibility and cervical rim adequacy are primary feasibility determinants, whereas bulging extent and reduction or anchoring difficulty contribute to procedural complexity. The resulting feasibility–complexity assessment may inform operative planning, including whether ECC should be attempted and, if so, where and by whom.

The low‐complexity category includes cases in which membrane reduction is achievable and adequate cervical tissue is present, allowing standard operative techniques. The high‐complexity category includes cases that remain feasible but require advanced skills or increased operative effort. The technically non‐feasible category includes cases in which irreducible membranes or insufficient cervical rim preclude cerclage.

These feasibility categories are not intended to mandate specific actions or operative techniques. Rather, they provide a shared operative language that distinguishes feasible, technically demanding, and technically non‐feasible states. By explicitly separating feasibility from procedural complexity, the B–R–C framework helps explain variability in outcomes among cases grouped under the same indication and the inconsistency observed in comparisons of surgical techniques.

DISCUSSION

In ECC, operative assessment extends beyond technical feasibility to include ethical responsibility. Once feasibility and procedural complexity have been defined through the B–R–C framework, the surgeon must interpret these findings within the clinical context to determine appropriate management. This transition—from defining what is technically feasible to determining what is clinically appropriate—represents the ethical dimension of ECC practice. 16 An important boundary is that technical feasibility should not be equated with clinical usefulness. Some cases may be technically straightforward by B–R–C assessment but only marginally indicated clinically. In such cases, ease of suture placement may overestimate the value of ECC unless indication‐based eligibility, gestational age, inflammatory status, symptoms, and patient‐level risks are considered. Therefore, the B–R–C framework should be applied only after an indication for ECC has been established and should not justify cerclage when the indication is weak or uncertain.

Figure 5 illustrates this transition by linking feasibility categories to corresponding operative considerations. Feasible states are generally associated with a window in which intervention may be considered, as delay can lead to progression toward greater complexity or non‐feasibility. Within this context, the framework highlights the clinical implications of both action and non‐intervention without prescribing specific responses.

In high‐complexity cases, operative considerations extend beyond technical feasibility. Although ECC may remain feasible, such cases often require advanced expertise or additional resources. Assessment of procedural risk and institutional capability, including consideration of referral when appropriate, becomes particularly relevant. Within this context, both proceeding without adequate preparation and declining intervention may carry clinical implications that require careful evaluation.

In technically non‐feasible states, where membrane reduction or secure anchoring cannot be achieved, the framework highlights the limits of surgical intervention. In such cases, attempts at cerclage are unlikely to achieve stabilization, and non‐intervention may be considered within the clinical context. Clear communication of these limitations supports transparent and accountable clinical care.

Ultimately, operative judgment in ECC is closely linked to ethical accountability. A state‐based approach does not eliminate uncertainty but provides a transparent framework for interpreting operative feasibility and procedural complexity under time‐sensitive conditions.

Several limitations of the present framework should be acknowledged. First, the B–R–C classification is a conceptual operative framework rather than a prognostic model. It does not provide quantitative estimates of pregnancy prolongation or neonatal outcome, nor is it intended to replace existing evidence regarding the effectiveness of ECC. Instead, it is designed to clarify operative feasibility and procedural complexity at presentation, domains that remain largely unaddressed by current classification systems.

Second, although the framework is grounded in operative practice and informed by existing descriptive literature, it has not yet undergone prospective validation. The grading of bulging extent, reducibility, and cervical rim adequacy relies on clinical assessment and is therefore subject to inter‐operator variability. Further standardization of examination and reporting will be required to improve reproducibility and support broader adoption.

Third, the framework does not incorporate biochemical, microbiological, or imaging markers that may influence ECC outcomes, such as inflammatory status or subclinical infection. These factors likely interact with cervical state and may modify both operative feasibility and risk. They were intentionally excluded to preserve simplicity and bedside applicability. Future work may explore how such variables can be integrated into the model without compromising its operative clarity.

Despite these limitations, the B–R–C framework provides a basis for future research and clinical development. Prospective studies documenting ECC cases according to B–R–C components may enable more refined stratification of outcomes and clarify which operative states derive the greatest benefit from intervention. This approach may also help reconcile inconsistencies in the literature by distinguishing technical non‐feasibility from apparent lack of efficacy.

In addition, the framework may serve as a foundation for procedure‐oriented refinement of clinical guidance. Rather than relying solely on indication labels or gestational age thresholds, future recommendations may incorporate state‐based feasibility assessments to provide more practical support in emergency clinical settings. Integration of the B–R–C framework into training, quality registries, and multicenter collaborations may further promote a shared operative language and improve consistency in practice.

The ethical dimension of operative judgment highlighted in this framework warrants further conceptual and empirical development. By framing the transition from “Can I operate?” to “Should I operate?” as an ethical consideration, the B–R–C approach emphasizes the need to interpret technical feasibility within a broader clinical context. Future work may explore how state‐based ethical reasoning can be incorporated into training, peer discussion, and guideline development, particularly in high‐stakes emergency settings where both action and non‐intervention carry important implications. Engagement with surgical ethics scholarship may further support the development of transparent and consistent approaches to operative judgment under uncertainty.

Finally, the framework is intended to support continued conceptual refinement. ECC represents a severe manifestation of cervical failure within a dynamic preterm birth continuum. As understanding of cervical biology, biomechanics, and inflammation evolves, a state‐based classification may provide a structured foundation for integrating emerging insights while maintaining applicability across diverse clinical settings.

CONCLUSION

We propose a surgeon‐oriented, state‐based framework that clarifies when ECC is technically feasible, when greater expertise may be required, and when intervention may not be appropriate. The framework organizes ECC assessment around three observable operative dimensions: bulging extent, reducibility of prolapsed membranes, and cervical rim adequacy. By distinguishing operative feasibility from procedural complexity, it provides a structured language that aligns classification with perioperative assessment and supports interpretation in high‐stakes emergency contexts. It may also enhance interpretation of existing evidence, inform future study design, and contribute to procedure‐oriented refinement of clinical guidance.
