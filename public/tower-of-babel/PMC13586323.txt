Fibroblast growth factors in ischemic stroke: Therapeutic potential and clinical challenges
Authors: Confidence Dordoe, Kai-ming Guo, Canol Bwalya, Wen-ting Huang, Shi-hao Chen, Jun Chen, Xue Wang, Peng Wang, Xiao-kun Li, Lin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ischemic stroke is a leading cause of global mortality and long-term disability, with limited therapeutic options. Increasing evidence from experimental studies suggests that fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) play important roles in regulating key biological processes involved in brain repair following ischemia. This review examines the existing evidence to understand the functional roles of FGFs in ischemic stroke, highlighting findings from in vivo and in vitro models, as well as outcomes from clinical investigations. FGFs contribute to neuroprotection by supporting neuronal survival, modulating inflammatory responses, preserving blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, and enhancing angiogenesis. Despite promising data from experimental models, clinical translation of FGF-based therapies has proven challenging. Clinical trials have encountered issues such as safety concerns, particularly regarding optimal dosing and the risk of adverse effects, which complicates treatment response. These limitations highlight the complexity of translating FGF-based therapies into clinical practice in stroke treatment. Additionally, emerging FGF biomarkers may help predict therapeutic responses and guide patient selection and diagnosis. Future research should focus on optimizing delivery systems, identifying therapeutic windows, and improving clinical trial design, as successful clinical translation of FGF therapies relies on resolving subtype-specificity and refining delivery strategies to ensure targeted and effective treatment. Therefore, gaining a deeper understanding of the timing and context of FGF activity following ischemic stroke could pave the way for successful clinical applications, such as early neuroprotection, preservation of BBB integrity, and reduction of neuroinflammation. This understanding could also support the development of more targeted and effective interventions for ischemic stroke.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke occurs by the interruption of blood flow to the brain, which is caused by thrombosis or embolism [1, 2]. Recent treatments for ischemic stroke are limited because of the short therapeutic window period, which leaves many affected patients being unable to receive treatment [3]. However, over the years, scientists have committed to make impactful research studies at both the molecular and cellular stages following ischemic stroke occurrences [4]. This scientific success has identified various risk factors for stroke, which includes diabetes, cigarette smoking, lack of exercise, hypertension, and high cholesterol [5]. The management of stroke generally involves three major approaches: preventive measures, acute management, and post-stroke rehabilitation, and over the years, several approaches were made to improve stroke outcome after the initial onset [6, 7]. The only approved drug for ischemic stroke treatment by the FDA and EU is alteplase, which must be administered within a 3–4.5 h window period [8–10]. However, patients often suffer from side effects after treatment, and since its discovery, there has not been a better alternative for acute ischemic stroke [11]. Therefore, the urgency to explore potential pharmacological methods against ischemic stroke is very needful.

Fibroblast growth factor (FGF) family is made up of cell-signaling proteins produced by diverse types of cells [12]. They are known to affect the proliferation of multiple cells such as neurons, chondrocytes, smooth-muscle cells, melanocytes, endothelial cells, and adipocytes [13], and participate in different biological processes including survival, metabolism, cellular proliferation, differentiation, embryonic development, angiogenesis, tissue repair, and regeneration [14–16]. To date, 22 FGF ligands have been identified, of which 18 are known to interact with and induce the dimerization of FGF receptors (FGFRs) [17]. Given their involvement in cellular processes such as survival, proliferation, and differentiation, FGFs are critical in the pathogenesis of ischemic stroke [18]. This review summarizes the therapeutic potential of FGFs in ischemic stroke, highlighting both the experimental successes and the challenges faced in clinical translation. Experimental studies have shown that FGFs exert neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and angiogenic effects, as well as contributing to blood-brain barrier (BBB) restoration. However, the clinical application of FGFs is impeded by several challenges. Moreover, the need for reliable biomarkers to predict patient response to FGF-based therapies is discussed, alongside innovative delivery strategies that may improve therapeutic efficacy. Ultimately, this review offers insights into how FGFs can be refined as therapeutic candidates in ischemic stroke and outlines future directions for bridging the gap between promising preclinical results and clinical applications, with the goal of improving outcomes for stroke patients.

The FGF family

FGFs represent one of the largest polypeptide growth family having mesodermal and neuroectodermal cells origin [19]. They have a broad-spectrum of mitogens that regulate different cellular functions including proliferation, differentiation, and survival [20]. A common characteristic of this family is their ability to bind to heparin and heparin-sulfates, which protect them from degradation [21]. They are grouped into different subfamilies based on their properties and sequences [22, 23]. It has been documented well that the participation of the FGF signaling occurs in development, metabolism, and tissue homeostasis [23, 24]. The FGF family is one of the most unique groups of growth factors in vertebrates, consisting of 22 ligands identified in both mice and humans. According to studies, the mammalian FGFs are divided into subfamilies with FGF1 (FGF1 and FGF2) [25], FGF4 (FGF4, FGF5, and FGF6) [26], FGF7 (FGF3, FGF7, FGF10, and FGF22) [27], FGF8 (FGF8, FGF17, and FGF18) [26], and FGF9 (FGF9, FGF16, and FGF20) [28] identified as the paracrine FGF subfamilies. The FGF15/19 subfamily consisting of FGF15/19, FGF21, and FGF23 constitute the endocrine FGFs, because they respond to biological processes via an FGFR-dependent manner [22] and have low affinity binding for heparin/HS [29, 30]. In addition, the FGF11 subfamily also called the intracellular or homologous subfamily, consist of FGF11, FGF12, FGF13, and FGF14 [31]. Based on their biochemical activities and similarity in sequence, they have the ability to interact with intracellular domains of voltage gated sodium channels in an FGFR-dependent manner [32]. FGFs bind to their four receptors such as FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR3, and FGFR4 [33]. Physiologically, fundamental processes in adult organisms occur when FGF ligands bind to their high-affinity receptors [13, 16]. The binding of FGFs to their receptors exerts regulatory abilities by controlling proliferation, differentiation, migration, survival and metabolism of target cells [16, 24]. FGFRs contain a heparin-binding site that facilitates interaction with heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs), which act as cofactors for ligand binding. Upon FGF binding, receptor dimerization allows the intracellular juxtamembrane region and split tyrosine kinase domains to autophosphorylate and initiate downstream signaling [24, 34]. Ischemic stroke

Ischemic stroke is a major global health concern, representing the second leading cause of death and the primary cause of disability worldwide. It not only contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality, but also imposes a substantial economic burden, highlighting its impact on public health [7, 35]. Stroke accounts for millions of deaths annually and millions of disability-adjusted life years lost, with an estimated global cost of 74 billion USD [5, 7, 36]. Following an ischemic stroke, various cells such as microglia, astrocytes, and neurons undergo distinct morphological changes at the ischemic site, with neuronal cell bodies and axons undergoing degeneration [37, 38]. These changes lead to key mechanisms such as neuroinflammation [39], disruption of the BBB [40], and cell death [41], all of which contribute to ischemic stroke damage. As a result, cerebral blood flow is reduced, leading to brain edema, neuronal injury, and significant neurological deficits. A deeper understanding of the molecular and cellular processes in the ischemic penumbra is essential for the development of potential therapies for ischemic stroke, underscoring the urgent need for pharmacological interventions to improve stroke outcomes. After an ischemic event, the cells surrounding the ischemic core begin to undergo cell death, which becomes a primary target for therapeutic intervention [7]. Both microglia and astrocytes undergo morphological changes after ischemic injury. Activated microglia transition from their resting state to an activated form, secreting inflammatory mediators such as cytokines, chemokines, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [42]. In contrast, astrocytes undergo reactive changes aimed at protecting neurons in the peri-infarct region [43, 44]. A hallmark of ischemic stroke is the interruption of blood flow, leading to oxygen and glucose deprivation in the brain. This results in energy failure, brain edema, inflammation, neuronal cell damage, as well as acid-base imbalance and ion homeostasis impairment [45, 46], all of which contribute to severe neurological deficits [47]. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of ischemic stroke, it is essential to combine both in vitro and in vivo models. This approach minimizes the limitations inherent in single-model studies and provides a more holistic view of stroke pathology [48].

Therapeutic potential of FGF-mediated mechanisms in ischemic stroke models

FGFs have shown significant therapeutic potential in ischemic stroke models, primarily due to their ability to regulate multiple biological processes crucial for recovery. Following an ischemic stroke, neuronal injury, BBB disruption, and neuroinflammation contribute to long-term neurological deficits. Experimental models such as middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) [48, 49], photothrombosis [50, 51], and oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) [50] have provided researchers valuable insights into how FGFs influence stroke outcomes. The MCAO model in particular, is widely used for studying BBB disruption following cerebral ischemia [52, 53]. FGFs confer protection by activating FGFR-dependent signaling pathways that enhance neuronal survival, support angiogenesis, and modulate inflammatory responses. They also help stabilize the BBB, reducing excessive immune cell infiltration and limiting secondary neuronal injury. Additionally, FGFs can suppress proinflammatory signaling pathways and promote anti-inflammatory microglial phenotype, thereby supporting tissue repair and functional recovery. As such, FGFs represent a promising therapeutic avenue for improving outcomes in ischemic stroke. FGFs in neuroprotection in ischemic stroke

FGF1, also known as acidic FGF (aFGF), is widely distributed in various tissues and organs, including the brain, heart, bone, retina, and peripheral nerves [50, 54]. In the central nervous system (CNS), it is recognized as a neuroprotective and neuroregenerative factor, being highly expressed in sensory and motor neurons [55]. Several studies have shown that FGF1 promotes regeneration of dorsal root ganglion neurons in both humans and mice following spinal cord injury [56, 57]. In ischemic brain injury, FGF1 also exerts beneficial effects. Cheng et al. reported that intranasal delivery of FGF1 after ischemia improved neurological function, although it did not significantly reduce lesion size [54]. In contrast, Tsai et al. demonstrated that FGF1 combined with fibrin glue reduced infarct volume, preserved cortical and hippocampal neurons, and enhanced behavioral recovery in rats subjected to ischemic injury [55]. Previous report showed that FGF1 is a potent trophic factor that supports neuronal survival across various brain regions, with its expression increasing in response to brain injury [58]. It has been demonstrated that continuous infusion of FGF1 into the lateral ventricles, either before ischemia or after the onset of ischemia, prevents the death of CA1 pyramidal neurons in the hippocampus of gerbils and delays ischemia-induced elevations in intracellular calcium levels [59, 60]. Cuevas et al. further demonstrated that FGF can mitigate cerebral ischemia following subarachnoid hemorrhage by exerting a spasmolytic effect on early cerebral vasospasm in rats [56, 61]. Additionally, they found that exogenous FGF1 has neuroprotective effects both in vitro and in vivo, enhancing neuronal survival and protecting against injuries induced by anoxia, toxins, and other harmful conditions. FGF2, also known as basic FGF (bFGF), is the prototypical and most extensively studied member of the FGF superfamily. It is highly expressed in the hippocampus [62] and has been shown to exert neuroprotective effects in both in vivo and in vitro models [63–65]. In the CNS, FGF2 is recognized as a potent neurotrophic and neuroprotective factor, and its upregulation following injury is often associated with endogenous repair and protective responses [19, 66, 67]. In the context of ischemic stroke, numerous experimental models have shown that exogenous FGF2 administration reduced infarct volume and promoted neuronal survival [19, 68, 69]. Mechanistically, these effects occur through FGFR binding, which induces receptor dimerization and tyrosine kinase autophosphorylation [23, 70]. Several in vivo studies support these mechanisms. For example, cells transfected with a herpes simplex virus type I (HSV-1) vector expressing FGF2 promoted functional recovery and reduced infarct volume in MCAO rat model [71]. Transgenic mice expressing bovine FGF2 displayed increased resistance to hypoxemic-ischemic cerebral injury [72], and further evidence demonstrated that FGF2 can cross the adult BBB, highlighting its clinical potential [61, 73]. In addition to its neuroprotective effects in ischemic conditions, FGF2 plays a vital role in cortical development and supports neuronal survival in several brain regions, including the cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, and striatum [74, 75]. Collectively, these findings establish FGF2 as a central regulator of neuroprotection and cell survival in ischemic stroke to reduce infarct volume, enhance functional recovery, and protect neurons from apoptotic injury (Fig. 1a).

FGF10 exhibits both biochemical and biological activities, regulating diverse developmental processes, maintaining tissue homeostasis, and promoting regeneration and wound repair [76, 77]. Its roles have been primarily studied in organ development, particularly in the lung, limb, adipose tissue, and prostate, where it contributes to cell migration and differentiation [78–80]. While the developmental functions of FGF10 are well established, only a few studies have investigated its role in the CNS after injury. Recent work showed that FGF10 is produced by neurons in the brain, and its expression increases after MCAO [81]. Exogenous administration of FGF10 in this model reduced cerebral injury and neuronal apoptosis (Fig. 1a) [78]. Similarly, treatment with recombinant FGF10 protected cortical neurons against cell death following OGD [82]. The above studies indicate that FGF10 is implicated in neuroprotection following ischemic stroke.

FGF18 is a novel FGF that is highly expressed within brain tissue both during development and in the adult brain [83–85]. A study revealed FGF18 promoted the survival of neurons in serum-free cell culture and stimulated neurite outgrowth in PC12 cells [86]. In MCAO models, FGF18 exhibited neuroprotective effects by improving motor function and increasing cerebral blood flow [83, 86]. These neurotrophic effects indicate that FGF18 may be neuroprotective after stroke. Taken together, these findings highlight the unique role of FGF18 in brain development and its potential to modulate the cellular response to ischemic injury (Fig. 1a) [87].

FGF20, a member of the FGF9 subfamily, has paracrine functions and has been implicated in various cellular processes, including apoptosis, aging, inflammation, and autophagy [88, 89]. It also plays a critical role in the survival of neurons and brain homeostasis, with its expression primarily restricted to specific regions of the CNS, such as the cerebellum and substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) [90, 91]. Several studies have shown that FGF20 protects cultured ventral mesencephalic (VM) dopaminergic neurons from serum withdrawal, glutamate, and 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA)-induced toxicity [92, 93]. In a Parkinson’s disease (PD) rat model, intraparenchymal administration of FGF20 protected dopaminergic neurons in the SNc and significantly alleviated motor deficits [94]. While the neurotrophic potential of FGF20 in PD is well-established, its therapeutic role in ischemic stroke remains unexplored. Recently, a study demonstrated the neuroprotective effects of HP-rhFGF20, showing that FGF20 attenuated neurobehavioral deficits and reduced infarct volume in ischemic stroke rats [95], supporting its neuroprotective potential as a therapeutic agent for ischemic stroke (Fig. 1a). Further studies are still needed to investigate its potential therapeutic role in ischemic stroke.

FGFs in microglial inflammation in ischemic stroke

FGF1 also plays a crucial role in inflammatory signaling. Inflammatory response plays a crucial role in ischemic stroke, influencing both the progression of damage and the potential for recovery [96]. Following ischemic insult, the brain undergoes an inflammatory response characterized by the activation of microglia, which release proinflammatory cytokines that exacerbate neuronal injury. A study reported that the topical application of a mixture of FGF1 and fibrin glue in the CNS reduced cerebral infarction, which resulted to decreased microglial infiltration, and subsequently inflammation [55]. Our recent study investigated the protective effects of nmFGF1 on microglia following ischemic injury in mice, specifically examining whether it could alleviate inflammation [50]. In this study, we observed that the intranasal administration of nmFGF1 improved neurological deficits in behavioral tests and regulated the secretion of both proinflammatory (TNF-α, iNOS, IL-6, and IL-1β) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IGF-1, IL-10, and TGF-β). Additionally, these cytokines were significantly modulated in in vitro experiments using primary microglia [50]. A previous report also stated that FGF1 regulated apoptosis and regeneration [97], showing high anti-inflammatory effects, which suggest its potential as a candidate for microglia-targeted inflammatory therapy in ischemic stroke [98]. Mechanistic investigations further demonstrated that FGF1 exerts anti-inflammatory effects by modulating microglial polarization and attenuating inflammatory responses through inhibition of nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) activation and activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway, both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 1b, (1)) [19, 50]. NF-κB plays a central role in inflammation by regulating the expression of inflammatory mediators across various diseases [99, 100]. Upon activation, NF-κB induces the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and COX-2. In its inactive state, NF-κB dimers are sequestered in the cytoplasm by IκBα. However, under anoxic conditions, IκBα undergoes phosphorylation and ubiquitination, leading to its degradation, which allows NF-κB dimers to translocate to the nucleus and activate inflammatory gene expression [101, 102]. Therefore, modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway offers a promising therapeutic strategy for ischemic stroke, with substances that influence or interfere with this pathway potentially serving as novel approaches for treatment. In this study, we found that non-mitogenic FGF1 (nmFGF1) effectively inhibited NF-κB activation both in vivo and in vitro, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent in ischemic stroke [50]. Nrf2 is a key transcription factor that regulates the endogenous antioxidant defense system, controlling the expression of detoxifying enzymes [103, 104]. While inflammation is often mediated through NF-κB, Nrf2 has gained significant attention for its role in cellular defense mechanisms [105]. Under normal conditions, Nrf2 is sequestered by Keap1 in the cytoplasm. However, upon oxidative stress, Nrf2 is released from Keap1 and translocate into the nucleus, and binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs), thereby regulating the expression of Nrf2-responsive genes. Additionally, numerous studies have highlighted Nrf2’s involvement in the inflammatory response, where it can inhibit NF-κB activation [106, 107]. In this research findings, our results indicate that the administration of nmFGF1 significantly enhanced Nrf2 expression, suggesting that nmFGF1 activates the Nrf2 pathway following ischemic injury, thereby contributing to its protective effects against ischemic stroke [50]. These findings highlight FGF1 as a promising therapeutic agent. However, further research is needed to address the current limitations of FGF1 application in ischemic stroke and evaluate its potential for clinical translation.

Similarly, FGF2 has been shown to promote various microglial functions such as migration and phagocytosis of neuronal debris, primarily through FGFR1 and FGFR3 signaling [108–110]. While there is no direct evidence confirming whether FGF2 modulates microglial inflammatory activation, it is important to note the functional heterogeneity of microglia, which play a crucial role in ischemic stroke [111]. Notably, a recent study highlighted the antidepressant-like effects of FGF2 in neuroinflammation-associated depression, suggesting a potential influence on microglial activity in the brain [112]. Furthermore, in vitro studies have explored the significant influence of FGF2 on glial cell responses to injury, supporting its involvement in neuroinflammation and tissue repair following ischemic events [111, 113]. FGF2 and its receptor FGFR1 are upregulated in both MCAO rat models and in PC12 cells subjected to OGD/R. This upregulation suggests that FGF2 interacts with FGFR1 to reduce inflammation and enhance the expression of neuroprotective genes [110, 114]. In addition, the enhancement of microglia-mediated neuronal survival by FGF2 occurs via activation of the FGFR3-ERK pathway [19, 111, 115], which plays a crucial role in cellular survival and repair mechanisms. Furthermore, FGF2 increases ERK phosphorylation and activates Wnt signaling pathway, which promotes microglial migration processes essential for tissue repair following ischemic stroke. These findings suggest that FGF2 acts as a signaling mediator fostering crosstalk between microglia and degenerating neurons, with the FGFR3/ERK/Wnt axis playing a central role in microglial protection [111]. Several studies have also demonstrated that the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating microglial protection, cell metabolism, and proliferation [116, 117]. In ischemic stroke, FGF2 activation is known to enhance this pathway, exerting neuroprotective effects by modulating neuroinflammation, specifically in microglia. FGF2-mediated CREB activation has been shown to regulate microglial activation and influence the inflammatory response (TNF-α, iNOS, IL-6, and IL-1β) in ischemic brain injury [114]. These findings suggest that targeting FGF2 signaling could provide a promising therapeutic strategy for ischemic stroke by controlling microglial functions and limiting inflammation (Fig. 1b, (2)). Further investigation is needed to better understand the precise mechanisms by which FGF2 modulates microglial activation and the M1/M2 polarization, as well as its broader effects on neuroinflammation. These studies could ultimately lead to novel treatments that enhance recovery and minimize long-term damage following ischemic events.

FGF10 levels have been reported to be elevated in the brains of MCAO models, suggesting its potential involvement in ischemic injury [19]. Similarly, a study reported that treatment with FGF10 in cultured cortical neurons prevented cell death in an in vitro OGD model [81]. A study further reported that the anti-inflammatory effects of FGF10 after brain injury may be attributed to its anti-apoptotic activity, with post-ischemic inflammation playing a critical role in determining outcomes [118]. In this study, FGF10 treatment significantly reduced both protein and mRNA expression levels of proinflammatory mediators (TNF-α and IL-6) in the brains of ischemic mice. This was associated with marked attenuation of microglial activation within the ischemic region [118]. Furthermore, neuron-derived FGF10 appeared to interact with FGFR2, which resulted in the suppression of NF-κB activation and inflammatory cytokines suggesting a direct modulatory effect on microglial activity as well [110]. These findings indicate that FGF10 plays an important role in regulating post-ischemic inflammation and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target to mitigate the inflammatory response following stroke (Fig. 1b, (3)). However, in-depth studies are warranted to clarify its precise mechanisms and therapeutic value.

FGF21 is expressed in multiple tissues and organs and plays an important role in glucose and lipid metabolism, particularly in the context of obesity [119, 120]. It regulates endocrine processes by binding to FGFRs in complex with the coreceptor β-Klotho (KLB) [121, 122]. Under physiological conditions, circulating FGF21 is primarily derived from the liver [123], where it activates FGF signaling by binding to FGFR1c and KLB in the brain, adipose tissue, and liver [124]. Through interaction with FGFR1 in the presence of β-Klotho, FGF21 modulates energy expenditure, oxidative stress, and tissue repair. Recent studies have highlighted the potential impact of FGF21 on microglial inflammation. FGFR1 and its coreceptor β-Klotho have been detected in brain tissue [125, 126], suggesting a direct role for FGF21 in the CNS inflammation. In vitro, administration of FGF21 upregulated the downstream effector peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ) in human bone marrow endothelial cells, potentially linking this pathway to microglial activation and inflammatory responses in the CNS [127, 128]. Collectively, these findings indicate that FGF21 may act as a regulatory factor modulating macrophage and microglial activity to promote functional recovery. In support of this, Wang et al. reported that recombinant human FGF21 (rhFGF21) attenuated microglial polarization toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype during the acute stage of ischemic stroke, while sparing the M2 phenotype. This anti-inflammatory effect appeared to occur through regulation of microglia and peripheral macrophages via NF-κB and PPARγ signaling pathways [129]. Thus, FGF21 exhibits anti-inflammatory properties that may ameliorate ischemic injury by modulating microglial activity (Fig. 1b, (4)), highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for future treatment of ischemic stroke.

FGFs in BBB integrity and angiogenesis in ischemic stroke

FGF1 exerts potent regulatory effects on cell proliferation, BBB integrity, and angiogenesis [130]. However, FGF1 cannot naturally cross the BBB, which serves as a selective permeability barrier formed by endothelial cells. The BBB protects the brain by blocking harmful substances while allowing essential nutrients to pass, thereby maintaining a stable internal environment [131]. Despite some limitations, FGF1 contributes to BBB stabilization by modulating endothelial cell function and promoting the expression of tight junction proteins, which are crucial for maintaining barrier integrity [132]. In addition to its role in BBB integrity, FGF1 is also pivotal in angiogenesis, the process by which new blood vessels are formed from existing vasculature, facilitating tissue repair and recovery [133]. Furthermore, FGF1 can be delivered into the CNS through noninvasive approaches, such as intranasal administration, offering potential therapeutic advantages [134]. In a study, treatment with FGF1 following ischemia preserved the integrity of the BBB by regulating the expression of tight junctions and adherens junctions [66]. Additionally, FGF1 exhibited protective effects in brain cells by upregulating tight junction proteins and inhibiting RhoA, which is mediated through the PI3K-Akt-Rac1 pathway, contributing to BBB protection [134]. Within the brain, FGF1 exerts a range of effects such as promoting of angiogenesis, repair of injury, and neuroprotection through trophic support [54]. In MCAO models, intranasal administration of nmFGF1 for 10 consecutive days post-ischemia enhanced angiogenesis via activation of the sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 1 (S1P1) signaling pathway [135]. FGF1/FGFR signaling is also crucial in angiogenesis and tissue regeneration [136, 137]. An in vitro study further confirmed this mechanism, showing that treatment with nmFGF1 following OGD significantly increased the phosphorylation of FGFR1 (p-FGFR1/FGFR1 ratio). However, this effect was abolished by the FGFR1 inhibitor PD173074 [138]. The results indicate that nmFGF1 induces angiogenesis through FGFR1 activation after OGD. Similarly, S1P1 signaling was suppressed after OGD/R, but nmFGF1 treatment rescued S1P1 expression, an effect reversed by FGFR1 inhibition, suggesting that nmFGF1 upregulates S1P1 expression through FGFR1-mediated signaling [138]. In addition, intranasal FGF1 enhance vascular remodeling after ischemic injury, suggesting its potential in promoting angiogenesis CNS [134]. This effect is mediated through the activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway, a critical signaling mechanism downstream of FGF/FGFR signaling [66, 97]. Collectively, these findings position FGF1 as a promising therapeutic candidate for preserving the BBB and enhancing angiogenesis following ischemic brain injury (Fig. 1c, (1)).

FGF2 plays a critical role in maintaining BBB integrity and promoting angiogenesis. Notably, exogenous FGF2 administration following OGD/R in vitro prevents BBB disruption by upregulating S1P1 protein expression [139]. Activation of S1P1 has been reported to mitigate ischemic injury, and when combined with S1P1 modulators, it restored circulation to affected brain regions [140]. Lin et al. demonstrated that FGF2 protects the BBB integrity under hypoxic conditions via FGFR1 activation, leading to the upregulation of tight junction proteins, including ZO-1, occludin, and VE-cadherin [140]. Moreover, in cultured endothelial cells, FGF2 induces an angiogenic phenotype, characterized by increased proliferation, migration, proteinase production, and upregulation of specific integrins [141]. In vivo, FGF2 induces angiogenesis and exerts pleiotropic effects on development and differentiation in the brain through S1P1 signaling [142]. The presence of FGFRs and FGF2 in developing brain is essential, as these are upregulated in the adult brain following CNS injury [143, 144]. These findings indicate the dual role of FGF2 in protecting the BBB integrity and promoting angiogenesis while highlighting the importance of precise regulation to avoid adverse effects on CNS development and repair (Fig. 1c, (2)).

FGF17 is a brain-enriched member of the FGF8 subfamily, acting as a paracrine signaling growth factor that binds to various FGFRs, including FGFR2c, FGFR4, and with the highest activity toward FGFR3c [23, 30, 145]. FGF17 signaling plays a critical role in embryonic development, particularly within the patterning centers of the rostral forebrain and the midbrain-hindbrain boundary [146]. Recently, FGF17 has been reported to protect against cerebral ischemia-reperfusion-induced BBB disruption via FGFR3-mediated PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, highlighting its potential role in pathological conditions such as ischemic stroke [147]. This suggests that FGF17 may serve as a protective factor in maintaining BBB integrity and promoting neurovascular health during ischemic events (Fig. 1c, (3)). Nonetheless, more studies are needed to further substantiate the potential of FGF17 in preserving the BBB in ischemic stroke.

FGF21 is known to exert neuroprotective effects, and owing to its negligible mitogenic activity and weak binding affinity for heparin, it is capable of crossing the BBB to act within the CNS [19, 126, 129]. Research findings demonstrated that rhFGF21 confers strong protective effects against acute BBB disruption following diabetic stroke. This protection appears to be mediated through enhanced PPARγ DNA-binding activity and the upregulation of mRNA expression of BBB junctional complex genes [122]. Consistently, at 24 h after stroke, a significant reduction in PPARγ DNA-binding activity was observed within the brain tissue, along with decreased mRNA expression of BBB junctional genes and the PPARγ target genes like CD36 and FABP4 in the cerebral microvasculature [122]. These findings suggest that FGF21 plays a critical role in BBB integrity following ischemic stroke, partly by modulating PPARγ activity. Enhancing the expression of junctional complex proteins may offer a potential therapeutic strategy for protecting the BBB and promoting recovery following stroke. In another study, the therapeutic effects of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) overexpressing FGF21 (MSCs-FGF21) were investigated in an ischemia-induced model of neurological deficits and BBB disruption. The authors found that MSCs-FGF21 administered 24 h after MCAO via the intracerebroventricular route, significantly attenuated BBB breakdown. Additionally, MSCs-FGF21 treatment mitigated the loss of tight junction proteins, including ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-5 in the ischemic brain [148]. These results highlight FGF21 as a promising therapeutic strategy for protecting BBB integrity and reducing ischemia-induced damage. Furthermore, in an in vitro study, rhFGF21 was tested for its ability to promote angiogenesis in human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs). rhFGF21 stimulated angiogenesis by forming the FGF21/FGFR1/β-klotho complex and activating PPARγ [149]. These findings suggest that rhFGF21 may serve as a potential therapeutic agent for promoting angiogenesis in brain injury, particularly in ischemic stroke models (Fig. 1c, (4)).

Barriers and challenges for FGF clinical trials in ischemic stroke

FGFs have shown significant promise in ischemic models due to their neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and tissue-reparative properties. These properties position FGFs as potential therapeutic agents for ischemic stroke. Despite these encouraging results, translating these findings into clinical therapies has been met with challenges. Early-phase clinical trials of FGF-based therapies have demonstrated some evidence of safety and efficacy, including functional recovery and reductions in infarct size [66]. However, translating the results into consistent clinical outcomes remains difficult. This section examines the challenges hindering clinical translation, reviews the progress of FGF clinical trials in ischemic stroke, and outlines key factors that may influence the success of FGF-based therapies in human patients.

Over the past decade, extensive research has focused on developing neuroprotective therapies aimed at rescuing the ischemic penumbra, thus, the region of brain tissue that remains viable after stroke and could potentially be preserved with timely intervention [150, 151]. Numerous therapeutic strategies have been tested in acute stroke clinical trials, including glutamate receptor antagonists [152, 153], inhibitors of glutamate release or nitric oxide production [154], phospholipid-based agents [155], free radical scavengers [156], antibodies targeting adhesion molecules [157], and partial agonists of GABA receptors [158]. Despite these efforts, none of these approaches have achieved successful clinical translation.

FGF2 (recombinant human FGF2, trafermin) is a protein abundantly expressed in adult brain and plays key roles in neuronal differentiation, survival, and supports the maintenance and proliferation of glial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and various other cell types [159].

Extensive preclinical works demonstrated the neuroprotective effects of FGF2 in rodent focal ischemia models. For example, in a permanent MCAO models, intravenous infusion of trafermin administered up to 3 h after arterial occlusion significantly reduced infarct volume [160, 161], showing the protective efficacy in transient ischemia/reperfusion models [162]. Notably, intravenous administration of FGF2 after cerebral ischemia allows the protein to cross the BBB [160]. Further studies have shown that FGF2 remains effective even when delivered in a delayed therapeutic window. Additionally, in acute ischemic stroke models, delayed administration enhanced functional recovery, and improvements were observed in limb placing, spontaneous forelimb use, and performance on the balance beam, despite the absence of changes in infarct size [163]. These findings indicate that trafermin may promote neurological recovery not only through acute neuroprotection but also by enhancing neuronal plasticity and synaptic remodeling following ischemic injury, which formed the basis for subsequent clinical trials. In a phase I trial, trafermin or placebo was administered intravenously to 66 patients within 12 h of acute ischemic stroke in an ascending dose regimen. The treatment was generally well tolerated, though higher doses were associated with incidence of nausea, vomiting, and transient asymptomatic leukocytosis [159]. Importantly, there was no evidence of adverse effects on neurological function. Based on the neuroprotective effects observed in preclinical studies following ischemic stroke [164, 165], and the fact that trafermin was well tolerated during a 3 h administration period, two concurrent safety and efficacy trials were subsequently conducted in larger patient cohorts [159]. Phase II/III trials have shown that FGF2 can be administered safely to stroke patients. However, the ideal therapeutic window for FGF2 extends beyond 5 h after ischemic injury [159], which contrasts with the FDA and EU approved alteplase treatment window of 3–4.5 h [8–10]. This difference in therapeutic windows may be a limitation in the clinical utility of FGF2. Notably, a North American trial was terminated early by an independent safety monitoring committee due to adverse outcomes, including higher mortality in the treatment group compared to controls [159]. Reported side effects associated with FGF2 infusion included leukocyte activation, fever, hypokalemia, and vomiting. Similarly, a European-Australian phase II/III trial involving 289 acute stroke patients found no significant protective effect of FGF2 after 24 h, but observed hypotension and increased mortality in the treated individuals [159]. These clinical failures highlight the importance of overcoming BBB-related challenges to minimize peripheral side effects. One potential strategy is intranasal delivery, which bypasses the BBB non-invasively, allowing direct delivery of therapeutics into the CNS [66, 166]. Although FGF2 trials have demonstrated significant adverse effects, they also highlight the need for further research to refine safe and effective delivery strategies.

Future preclinical studies should focus on optimizing administration routes and timing to achieve therapeutic levels in the brain while minimizing systemic toxicity. Despite promising results in preclinical models, clinical trials with FGF2 have not yielded consistent outcomes, partly due to challenges in translating dosages from animal models to humans and overcoming the BBB [66]. Notably, due to the unsuccessful outcomes of clinical trials with FGF2, no other FGFs have been tested in patients with acute stroke. Continued research is needed to address these challenges, particularly by identifying safe and effective methods for human use.

Current progress in FGFS as circulating biomarkers in ischemic stroke

FGF20 is predominantly expressed in the brain, where it exhibits neurotrophic activity and has been shown to protect dopaminergic neurons and motor function in Parkinson’s disease [167, 168]. Recently, the first study exploring the role of FGF20 in ischemic stroke prognosis was conducted. In a prospective multicenter cohort study, elevated serum of FGF20 levels associated with a reduced risk of both short-term and long-term adverse outcomes were observed in ischemic stroke patients. This association demonstrates serum FGF20 levels to traditional risk factors, which slightly improves predictive accuracy, as reflected by net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination index indices [89]. Further mediation analysis revealed that estimated glomerular filtration rate and white blood cells counts partially mediated the relationship between FGF20 levels and stroke prognosis. Though studies on the effects of FGF20 in CNS diseases are limited, these findings suggest that low serum of FGF20 levels may identify patients at higher risk of poor outcomes and mortality [89]. This supports the proposal that early monitoring and intervention should be considered for ischemic stroke patients with low FGF20 levels. Additionally, this study extends the prognostic value of FGF20 to acute ischemic stroke, suggesting that incorporating FGF20 into existing risk models can enhance risk stratification, thereby improving treatment planning and resource allocation. In Chinese patients, elevated FGF20 levels were observed to improve prognosis, indicating that FGF20 might serve as a potential biomarker for ischemic stroke prognosis [89]. This was concluded that serum FGF20 levels at stroke onset could serve as a predictive biomarker, offering better identification of patients at risk for adverse outcomes.

FGF21, a circulating metabolic fibroblast growth factor, has been reported as a biomarker associated with poor prognosis in ischemic stroke [169]. Due to the absence of heparin-binding domains, FGF21 is secreted into the bloodstream, where it exerts its endocrine effects. As a key regulator of glucose and lipid metabolism, it has been proposed as a potential therapeutic target for various metabolic disorders [170]. Additionally, several epidemiological studies have highlighted FGF21 as a valuable biomarker for predicting prognosis in cardiovascular disease and atherosclerosis [66, 169]. In the context of ischemic stroke, the use of blood biomarkers has gained increasing acceptance, as they can aid in evaluating the severity and prognosis of stroke patients. Notably, Guo et al. demonstrated that FGF21 levels remain elevated for up to two weeks following a stroke, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for monitoring stroke progression [171]. Patients with ischemic stroke have been shown to exhibit elevated plasma levels of FGF21, which has been independently associated with both the severity of ischemic stroke and functional outcomes [172]. Further studies have demonstrated that serum FGF21 levels are linked to the prognosis of various cardiovascular diseases [173, 174]. In a cohort of Chinese patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), higher levels of FGF21 were strongly associated with poorer long-term outcomes, even after adjusting for established risk factors [169]. Despite these findings, the relationship between serum FGF21 and adverse outcomes in ICH patients is still being explored in a prospective, multicenter cohort study. Further proteomic analyses of patients have revealed that FGF21 is expressed at lower levels in intracranial blood compared to systemic arterial blood [66, 175]. These results suggest a potential role for FGF21 in influencing outcomes following acute ischemic stroke, but the precise mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear. This gap in understanding may be attributed to several limitations in existing studies, such as the lack of diversity in patient populations, which may affect the generalizability of the findings [172]. Nonetheless, balancing the inclusion and exclusion of patients based on baseline characteristics from the China National Stroke Registry [176] proved beneficial, as the individuals in this study were also part of the China Antihypertensive Trial in Acute Ischemic Stroke (CATIS). However, no significant changes were observed from the time of hospitalization, as FGF21 levels and blood pressure were only measured once at baseline. This limited data collection makes it difficult to establish a clear association between FGF21 levels and acute ischemic stroke [176]. Moreover, the inclusion of patients hospitalized for more than 24 h raises the possibility of FGF21 level inaccuracies due to the time lapse from stroke onset. However, previous reports indicate that FGFs remain detectable for up to three days post-ischemia [66]. These studies mentioned suggest that elevated serum levels of FGF21 are linked to a poor prognosis in ischemic stroke patients. Therefore, serum FGF21 could serve as a potential prognostic marker for risk stratification in stroke management.

The prognostic value of elevated FGF23 as a biomarker for increased stroke risk is still under investigation. A meta-analysis suggested a possible association between high circulating FGF23 levels and the risk of stroke, although further clarification is needed [177]. Mechanistically, FGF23 has been reported to exert direct toxic effects on the cardiovascular system, promoting activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, which contributes to vascular dysfunction and may lead to hypertension and stroke [178]. Clinical evidence also showed that individuals with higher FGF23 concentrations have a greater incidence of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), a known risk factor for stroke due to its association with arterial stiffness and atherosclerosis [179]. However, methodological variability in how FGF23 is quantified across studies may introduce bias, limiting the interpretation of its association with ischemic stroke and its subtypes [180]. Since LVH, increased arterial stiffness, and vessel fragility are interrelated, FGF23-induced vascular changes may partially explain the increased risk of both ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage [181]. Thus, more comprehensive clinical studies comparing FGF23 levels in stroke patients versus healthy populations are necessary to validate its prognostic significance. In addition, while several studies have reported an association between FGF23 and stroke, the findings are not consistently observed across ischemic stroke subtypes [180, 182]. For example, Kendrick et al. found no significant relationship between FGF23 and stroke in chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients [183], while the Heart and Soul study suggested that elevated FGF23 levels are associated with a greater stroke risk [182]. The Northern Manhattan Study (NOMAS), involving 212 patients, found no link between FGF23 and ischemic stroke after adjusting for known risk factors [181]. Conversely, Panwar et al. reported no association between FGF23 and total stroke incidence, except for cardioembolic stroke, which was significantly linked to higher FGF23 levels [182]. This finding aligns with Zheng et al., who also observed an association between FGF23 and cardioembolic ischemic stroke [180]. Taken together, these findings highlight the controversial and subtype-specific role of FGF23 in ischemic stroke. Future trials should focus on functional recovery in acute ischemic stroke patients to better understand the role of FGFs in stroke outcomes [184].

Conclusion and future perspectives

Ischemic stroke results from the obstruction of cerebral blood flow, leading to neuronal injury, neurological deficits, and long-term disability [185]. Although significant advances have been made in understanding stroke pathophysiology, the condition remains highly complex, involving neuroinflammation, BBB disruption, and impaired tissue repair. This review highlights the growing evidence that FGFs play in the role of ischemic stroke. Over the past decades, FGFs have been shown to participate in essential processes related to stroke recovery, including neuroprotection, suppression of inflammatory responses, preservation of the BBB, enhancement of angiogenesis, and promotion of functional recovery. By integrating findings from preclinical and clinical studies, this review provides a comprehensive perspective on the therapeutic potential of FGFs, which may contribute to the development of novel and effective treatment strategies for ischemic stroke.

FGFs represent emerging therapeutic agents for ischemic stroke due to their pleiotropic biological functions and their capacity to act on multiple cell types and signaling pathways involved in injury progression and recovery. Accumulative evidence from experimental models indicates that FGFs exert neuroprotective effects by promoting neuronal survival, enhancing angiogenesis, stabilizing the BBB, and modulating neuroinflammation, including microglial activation [140, 186]. Unlike most current stroke treatments that target a single mechanism, FGFs possess broad multitarget actions, making them particularly suitable for a complex disorder such as ischemic stroke. Among the FGF family, FGF1 and FGF2 are the most extensively studied in the context of stroke [187]. Both FGFs demonstrate strong neuroprotective effects by reducing infarct volume, improving neurological scores, and enhancing functional recovery in preclinical stroke models [50, 140]. However, clinical applicability of FGF1 is limited due to poor BBB permeability, short half-life, and concerns related to tumorigenesis [19, 50, 66]. Similarly, FGF2 plays a crucial role in promoting angiogenesis, protecting endothelial integrity, and regulating inflammatory signaling. Nevertheless, FGF2 also raises concerns regarding potential systemic effects and challenges related to achieving optimal dosing and targeted delivery across the BBB. These barriers explain why, despite decades of promising experimental data, FGFs have not yet been successfully translated into clinical stroke treatment.

Studies show that combination therapies involving FGFs may provide synergistic benefits. For example, combining FGF signals with cell-based therapies, biomaterial scaffolds, or other neuroprotective agents has demonstrated enhanced tissue repair and functional recovery in animal models of stroke [58, 130, 188]. However, conventional drug delivery methods such as oral or injectable routes, often fail to effectively target specific sites within the nervous system due to limitations in their targeting efficiency, particularly within the BBB [110]. The BBB acts as a major obstacle, restricting the access of FGF-related therapies to the CNS. To overcome these difficulties, scientists have developed innovative drug delivery systems that encapsulate FGF within modified carriers, facilitating precise targeting to lesions in the nervous system. This approach not only addresses the delivery constraints of FGF-based drugs but also opens new avenues for developing effective therapeutics for ischemic stroke. For instance, hydrogels, which are versatile biomaterials, have found widespread applications in cell and drug delivery due to their ability to provide controlled release and support cellular viability. Biodegradable hydrogels, made from natural and synthetic polymers, have been used to deliver therapeutic cells like neural progenitor cells (NPCs) or MSCs, as well as FGFs, to damaged areas in the brain. By releasing essential nutrients, modulating the immune response, and providing neuroprotection, these hydrogels have shown promise in promoting functional recovery after ischemic injury [189, 190]. In addition to hydrogels, nanoparticles represent another class of biomaterials with significant potential for targeted drug delivery in stroke therapy [191]. For example, cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO2NPs), known for their strong free radical scavenging properties, can be loaded with dl-3-n-butylphthalide (NBP) to clear ROS, protects mitochondrial function, preserves BBB integrity, inhibits neuroinflammation, and reduces neuronal apoptosis, thus limiting cerebral infarction [192]. Furthermore, magnetic nanovesicles composed of iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) have been employed to deliver MSCs, promote polarization of macrophages from the proinflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, ultimately reducing neuronal damage in infarcted brain regions [193]. Additionally, liposomes have been shown to effectively deliver bFGF to the brain via intranasal administration, bypassing the BBB, reducing infarct volume, and promoting functional recovery [194]. Despite these promising advances, these delivery strategies remain primarily at the preclinical stage, and no FGF-based therapies have yet been successfully translated into routine clinical use for ischemic stroke. For clinical translation, challenges related to dose optimization, safety, treatment windows, and delivery systems need to be overcome. An emerging area of research is the role of FGFs in microglial modulation following ischemic injury. Microglia are the principal immune cells of the CNS that play a decisive role in both injury propagation and recovery following ischemic injury. Activated microglia are polarized into proinflammatory (M1-like) or anti-inflammatory/tissue-repairing (M2-like) phenotypes. M1 microglia exacerbate neuronal injury through cytokine release, oxidative stress, and NF-κB pathway activation, while M2 microglia promote tissue repair, debris clearance, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling. While much of the research on FGFs has focused on neurons and endothelial cells, there has been less exploration of how FGFs modulate microglial activity. Recent studies suggest that FGFs such as FGF1, FGF2, FGF10, FGF18, and FGF21 can influence microglial activation by suppressing proinflammatory pathways and reducing NF-κB activation, thus limiting secondary neuronal injury [19]. Further research is needed to explore FGF-mediated microglial phenotype switching, autophagic regulation, and interactions with other brain-resident cells, such as endothelial cells and astrocytes. Another promising research direction involves the use of FGF-derived dekafin peptides and other FGFR stimulants, which show potential in promoting tissue repair [195]. These peptides may also offer new opportunities for targeting and modulating cognitive and social behavior by influencing the inhibition balance in the brain. Additionally, FGF-dendrimer-based targeted drug delivery systems, leveraging FGFR signaling, could allow for selective targeting of FGFR-expressing cells and tissues, providing an exciting avenue for therapeutic development [58]. FGF biomarkers offer a promising avenue for enhancing diagnosis, treatment monitoring, and personalized care in ischemic stroke. Certain FGFs, such as FGF20, FGF21, and FGF23 have been identified as potential biomarkers for ischemic injury and recovery. Their levels in serum may provide insights into the extent of neuronal damage, BBB disruption, and neuroinflammation. Moreover, as our understanding of the roles of FGFs in ischemic stroke progresses, the use of FGFs as biomarkers could play a central role in early intervention and outcome prediction, contributing to more effective stroke therapies and improved patient survival rates. The integration of FGF-based biomarkers with advanced imaging techniques could also revolutionize early diagnosis and individualized stroke treatment plans.

The therapeutic potential of FGFs is further limited by their instability in circulation, which reduces bioavailability. To overcome these challenges, advanced strategies for targeted and conditional manipulation of FGFs are required. Approaches such as spatiotemporal manipulation, single-cell analyses, and multi-omics profiling can provide more precise mapping of FGF signaling dynamics during ischemic injury. Ultimately, combining conditional knockout models with high-resolution profiling will be essential to fully delineate the functions of individual FGFs across different phases of ischemic stroke and guide the development of effective and targeted therapies.

In conclusion, FGFs represent a promising therapeutic strategy for ischemic stroke, owing to their ability to modulate multiple pathological processes, especially neuroinflammation, BBB integrity, neuronal survival, and angiogenesis. Increasing attention toward microglial modulation presents a novel and promising research direction. Moreover, the use of FGF biomarkers to monitor disease progression and therapy response holds significant promise in personalizing stroke treatment. Although challenges in delivery and clinical safety persist, continued mechanistic research, improved animal modeling, and well-designed preclinical-to-clinical translational studies are expected to drive FGFs toward becoming viable therapeutic agents. Additionally, the potential of FGF-based combination therapies remains largely unexplored and warrants further investigation, particularly in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors or neuroprotective agents. Future discoveries, along with better integration of biomarkers, may ultimately lead to FGF-based therapies that can significantly improve neurological recovery and functional outcomes in ischemic stroke patients.
