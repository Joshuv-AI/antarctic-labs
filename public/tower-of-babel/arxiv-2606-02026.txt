Abstract
. The aim of the paper is to survey results on Jordan types for pairs of nilpotent commuting matrices. We also review recent proof of the Box Conjecture on Jordan types that have equal dense orbit in the nilpotent commutator.

1.
Introduction

We survey results on possible pairs of integer partitions that occur as Jordan types of two commuting nilpotent matrices. We call such pairs
commuting Jordan types
and the corresponding partitions
commuting partitions
. Precise description of all possible commuting Jordan types appears to be a difficult task. The fact that it is field dependent was proved for finite fields by Britnell and Wildon
[
15
, Prop. 4.12]
. We assume that the underlying field is an infinite field. The survey is a complement to Khatami’s survey
[
32
]
.

The nilpotent commutator of a nilpotent matrix of Jordan type
P
is an irreducible variety and therefore one of the nilpotent orbits has a dense intersection with it. We denote the Jordan type of the dense orbit by
\mathcal{D}(P)
following Panyushev
[
46
]
, who introduced map
\mathcal{D}
. Recently, the Box Conjecture formulated by Iarrobino, Khatami, Van Steirtenghem, and Zhao
[
27
]
on Jordan types in the inverse image
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(P)
was proved in
[
29
]
. The central part of the survey is dedicated to an outline of the proof. But first, we state some positive and some negative results on commuting Jordan types that are scattered in the literature. They were proved using tools of linear algebra and combinatorics. Section
4
is dedicated to the dense orbit map
\mathcal{D}
and its properties. We include also an observation that seems to be new: Two non-equal Rogers-Ramanujan partitions are not commuting. A partition is
super-distinct
or
Rogers-Ramanujan
if its parts differ by at least
2
. We conclude the survey with a short list of open questions.

Early references on commuting matrices can be traced to the end of the 19th century and the beginning of 20th century, e.g.,
[
19
,
20
,
47
,
49
,
54
,
55
,
56
]
. For a more detailed discussion of the early developments we refer to
[
40
, pp. 93-94]
. The study of varieties of commuting matrices can be traced to Motzkin and Taussky
[
41
]
and Gerstenhaber
[
22
]
. See Guralnick
[
25
]
for a discussion of the irreducibility of varieties of commuting pairs of matrices. Some interesting results on algebras of commuting matrices are found in
[
53
]
.

I met the problem of commuting Jordan types while working on my PhD thesis
[
36
]
. First results on the nilpotent commutator were proved by Basili
[
7
,
8
]
and Baranovsky
[
6
]
. Later, Oblak studied the problem in her PhD thesis
[
42
]
and published her results in
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
. Independently, results related to the problem of commuting Jordan types were published by Panyushev
[
46
]
, Premet
[
48
]
, and Basili, Iarrobino, and Khatami
[
10
,
11
,
26
,
30
,
31
]
. Recently, the topic of commuting Jordan types in artinian algebras has attracted substantial interest (see
[
1
]
for a survey).

The focus of Khatami’s survey
[
32
]
is on the results for the dense orbit map
\mathcal{D}
before the proof of the Box Conjecture. Here we survey also other results on commuting pairs scattered in the literature and provide a review of the proof of the Box Conjecture.

The Box Conjecture was communicated to me by Tony Iarrobino most likely by 2012. It represents a natural formulation of the structures that one gained understanding of through work of Basili, Iarrobino, Khatami, Oblak, Panyushev, and others. During her work on the PhD thesis
[
42
]
, Oblak noticed that the inverse image
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(Q)
for
Q=(p,q)
has
(p-q-1)q
elements (see
[
45
, Prop. 4.4]
for publication of the result). Honors student Zhao, who worked with Iarrobino in 2011/12 on a student research project, proposed to arrange partitions in
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(p,q)
in a rectangular array. The authors of
[
27
]
developed these ideas further and published them as the Table Theorem
[
27
, Thms. 3.12 & 3.19]
and as the Box Conjecture
[
27
, Conj. 4.11]
. The first version of the paper
[
27
]
was put on arXiv repository in 2014, four years prior to the publication of the paper.

The Box Conjecture stands out as the central part of our current understanding of commuting Jordan types for pairs of nilpotent matrices. The Burge correspondence gives a natural connection with the semigroup theory as described later in the paper. The examples found recently in the joint work with Khatami
[
33
]
indicate that the Burge correspondence is likely not sufficient for further progress on the problems of commuting Jordan types. Definitely, it is a useful tool to obtain new results. It was one of the tools used to prove the main theorem of
[
13
, Thm. 1.3]
and it was used to find examples of pairs of Jordan types in
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(p,q)
that do not commute in
[
33
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

We denote by
\mathcal{P}
the set of all partitions of natural numbers and by
\mathcal{P}(n)
the set of all partitions of natural number
n
. A partition
P\in\mathcal{P}
is written as
P=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k})
, where
p_{i}
are arranged in nonincreasing order and
p_{k}>0
. We adjoin an arbitrary finite number of
0
to
P
on the right if needed. We also use the ’power’ notation
P=(p_{1}^{m_{1}},p_{2}^{m_{2}},\ldots,p_{r}^{m_{r}})
, where
p_{i}
are strictly decreasing,
p_{r}>0
, and all multiplicities
m_{i}
are positive integers.

We assume that
F
is an infinite field. We denote by
F[x]
the algebra of all polynomials with coefficients in
F
and by
M_{n}(F)
the algebra of all
n\times n
matrices over
F
.

Suppose
B\in M_{n}(F)
is a nilpotent matrix and
P=P_{B}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k})\in\mathcal{P}
is its Jordan type, i.e.,
p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\ldots\geq p_{k}>0
are sizes of its Jordan blocks. The nilpotent commutator
\mathcal{N}_{B}
of
B
is defined by

\mathcal{N}_{B}=\{A\in M_{n}(F):\,BA=AB,A\ \text{nilpotent}\}.

We are interested in pairs of partitions
(P,Q)\in\mathcal{P}^{2}
that are Jordan types of two commuting nilpotent matrices. We use notation
P\sim_{\mathcal{C}}Q
for such pairs of partitions, i.e., we write
P\sim_{\mathcal{C}}Q
if there exist two commuting nilpotent matrices
A
and
B
such that
P_{A}=P
and
P_{B}=Q
. We say that
P
and
Q
are
commuting partitions
if
P\sim_{\mathcal{C}}Q
. Relation
\sim_{\mathcal{C}}
is symmetric and reflexive, but it is not transitive.

In connection with commuting Jordan types two classes of partitions play important role. A partition
Q=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k})
is called
super-distinct or Rogers-Ramanujan
(RR for short) if its parts differ by at least
2
:
q_{i}-q_{i+1}\geq 2
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k-1
. We denote the set of all RR partitions by
\mathcal{Q}
.

On the opposite side a partition
P=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k})
is called
almost rectangular
(AR for short) if its parts differ by at most
1
:
p_{1}-p_{k}\leq 1
.

3.
Some miscellaneous results

In this section we present a collection of known results on Jordan types for commuting nilpotent matrices. Their proofs involve tools of linear algebra or combinatorics.

3.1.
One Jordan block

We begin with the simplest case when
B
is nonderogatory, i.e.,
\dim\ker B=1
. Then
B
is similar to a nilpotent Jordan block
J_{n}
and its Jordan type is the one part partition
(n)
. The nilpotent commutator
\mathcal{N}_{B}
is equal to the nil-algebra

F_{0}[B]=\{p(B);\,p\in F[x],\,p(0)=0\}

(see
[
24
, Thm. 9.1.4]
), i.e,
F_{0}(B)
is the algebra of all polynomial functions in
B
with the zero constant coefficient. Jordan types of
A\in\mathcal{N}_{B}
are equal to Jordan types of powers
B^{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,n
. The corresponding partitions are exactly all almost rectangular partitions of
n
. Then, we write
P_{B^{k}}=[n]^{k}
. We summarize this case in the following statement.

Proposition 3.1
.

A partition
P\in\mathcal{P}(n)
is commuting with partition
(n)
if and only if
P=[n]^{k}
for some
k
. So, partitions commuting with
(n)
are exactly all almost rectangular partitions of
n
. There are
n
of them.

This result implies that in general, with increasing
n
, most partitions do not commute with the one part partition
(n)
, since the number of partitions grows as an exponential function of the square root of
n

[
2
, p. 69]
. The smallest pair of non-commuting partitions occurs for
n=4
. Then partitions
(3,1)
and
(4)
are not commuting.

3.2.
Universally commuting partitions

Definition 3.2
.

A partition
P\in\mathcal{P}(n)
is called
universally commuting
if
P\sim_{\mathcal{C}}Q
for any partition
Q\in\mathcal{P}(n)
.

Oblak
[
45
, Thm. 2.4]
characterized all universally commuting partitions. The same result was proved independently by Britnell and Wildon
[
15
, Thm. 4.11]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

For
n\leq 3
all partitions are universally commuting, while for
n\geq 4
only partitions of the form
(2^{k},1^{l})
are universally commuting.

3.3.
Conjugate partitions are commuting

Given a partition
P=(p_{1}
,
p_{2}
,
\ldots,p_{k})
, then the part
p_{i}^{\prime}
of its
conjugate partition

P^{T}=(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,p_{p_{1}}^{\prime})
is equal to the number of parts of
P
that are greater or equal to
i
. Baranovsky proved in the proof of
[
6
, Lem. 3]
the following fact.

Proposition 3.4
.

For each partition
P
we have
P\sim_{\mathcal{C}}P^{T}
, where
P^{T}
is the conjugate partition of
P
.

Observe, that conjugate partitions of two commuting partitions might not be commuting. For example, partitions
(2,2,1,1)
and
(2,1,1,1,1)
are commuting by Theorem
3.3
, while their conjugate partitions
(4,2)
and
(5,1)
are distinct RR partitions and therefore not commuting by Theorem
4.3
.

3.4.
Two parts partitions and commutativity

Oblak
[
45
, Thm. 3.1]
proved that:

Proposition 3.5
.

Two distinct partitions of
n
that have exactly two parts each are commuting if and only if
n
is even, say
n=2k
with
k\geq 2
, and one of partitions is equal to
(k,k)
and the other is equal to
(k+1,k-1)
.

Oblak
[
45
, Prop. 3.10]
also proved:

Proposition 3.6
.

If
Q=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots)
is a partition commuting with partition
P=(k,k)
then either
Q=(2k)
or
q_{1}\leq k+1
.

Recently, authors in
[
12
, Thm. 3]
characterized all partitions of
n=2k
that commute with
(k,k)
.

3.5.
Refinements

A
subpartition
of a partition
P
is a subsequence of
P
. The sum of a subpartition is the sum of its parts. If a subsequence
Q
is an almost rectangular partition then we call it
almost rectangular subpartition
(AR subpartition for short).

Definition 3.7
.

If
P
and
Q
are two partitions of
n
then
P
is a
refinement
of
Q
if each part of
Q
is a sum of a subpartition of
P
and the subpartitions involved cover exactly once all of the parts of
P
. So,
P
is a refinement of
Q
if
Q=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k})
and
P
is the disjoint union of partitions
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
,…,
P_{k}
such that the sum of all parts of
P_{i}
is equal to
q_{i}
for all
i
.

We say that
P
is an
almost rectangular refinement (an AR refinement)
of
Q
if all subpartitions
P_{i}
involved in the refinement are almost rectangular.

Note that an AR refinement itself is not an AR partition in general. It is a union of AR partitions. Any partition is trivially an (AR) refinement of itself.

For instance,
P=(5,4,4,2,2,1,1,1)
is an AR refinement of
(13,7)
. One has
P=[13]^{3}\cup[7]^{5}
. It is also AR refinement of
(9,5,4,2)
since
P
is the disjoint union of AR subpartitions
(5,4)
,
(2,1,1,1)
,
(4)
and
(2)
. On the other hand, since
P=(4,4,2,2)\cup(5,1,1,1)
it follows that
P
is a refinement of
(12,8)
. The fact that
P
is not an AR refinement of
(12,8)
follows since
P
contains no AR subpartition of
12
.

Britnell and Wildon
[
15
, Prop. 4.5]
proved the following result.

Theorem 3.8
.

If
P
and
Q
are two almost rectangular refinements of a partition
R\in\mathcal{P}
then
P\sim_{\mathcal{C}}Q
.

Remark 3.9
.

Theorem
3.8
can be easily deduced also from the theory of the symmetric inverse semigroups
\IS(n)

[
21
,
39
]
. The semigroup
\IS(n)
is represented by all
0
-
1
square matrices of size
n
that have at most one nonzero entry in each row and column
[
52
]
. These matrices are also called rook matrices. For further results on centralizers of nilpotent elements in
\IS(n)
see
[
34
,
35
,
39
]
.

Remark 3.10
.

Notice that an RR partition is not an AR refinement of any other partition then itself. Furthermore, it is not enough to consider only pairs of AR refinements of RR partitions to obtain all pairs of AR refinements as in Theorem
3.8
. For instance,
P=(8,3^{3})
and
Q=(5,4,2^{4})
are both AR refinements of
R=(9,8)
that is not an RR partition. Theorem
3.8
implies that partitions
(8,3^{3})
and
(5,4,2^{4})
are commuting.

3.6.
Hook partitions and commutativity

Authors of
[
33
]
described all partitions that commute with a hook partition
(m,1^{n})
for
m\geq 3
and
n\geq 1
. The special case
n=1
was resolved earlier in
[
15
, Prop. 4.9]
. The main result of
[
33
]
states that any partition commuting with a hook partition
(n,1^{m})
contains as a subpartition an AR partition of either
n
,
n-1
or
n-2
. In the case when the AR subpartition is an AR partition of
n
, the remaining subpartition is an arbitrary partition of
m
. In the other two cases, there are some technical conditions on the largest parts of the remaining subpartition of
m+1
or
m+2
.

3.7.
Union of commuting partitions is commuting

Using direct sums of matrices we obtain also the following result.

Proposition 3.11
.

If
(P_{1},P_{2})
and
(Q_{1},Q_{2})
are two pairs of commuting partitions then also
(P_{1}\cup Q_{1},P_{2}\cup Q_{2})
is a pair of commuting partitions.

For instance, Proposition
3.5
implies that
(5,3)
and
(4,4)
are commuting partitions, and also that
(4,2)
and
(3,3)
are commuting. Then partitions
(5,4,3,2)
and
(4,4,3,3)
are commuting. More generally, one proves using Proposition
3.5
that
(k+3,k+2,k+1,k)
and
(k+2,k+2,k+1,k+1)
are commuting partitions for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

4.
The dominant partition

The set
\mathcal{N}_{B}
of all nilpotent matrices commuting with
B
is an irreducible algebraic variety
[
8
, Lem. 2.3]
. Therefore, intersection of
\mathcal{N}_{B}
with one of the nilpotent orbits under
Gl_{n}(F)
conjugation is a dense open subset in the Zariski topology on
M_{n}(F)
. The Jordan type of the dense orbit is denoted by
\mathcal{D}(P)
, where
P
is the Jordan type of
B
. The map
\mathcal{D}
was introduced by Panyushev
[
46
]
. Here the choice of letter can refer to the dense orbit. By Proposition
4.1
,
\mathcal{D}(P)
is also the dominant partition (in the dominance order) among all partitions that are commuting with
P
. For each partition
P
we obviously have
P\sim_{\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{D}(P)
.

We write
P\succeq Q
for the
dominance order
(also called the Bruhat order) on partitions. For partitions
P=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots)
and
Q=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots)
we have
P\succeq Q
if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}

for all
k
.

The image of the map
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}
is equal to the set
\mathcal{Q}
of all RR partitions, i.e., partitions whose parts differ by at least
2

[
10
, Thm. 1.12]
. The map
\mathcal{D}
is idempotent
[
37
, Thm. 6]
.

The following is a known fact. It is stated e.g. by Khatami
[
32
, Prop. 3.2]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that
P
and
Q
are partitions of
n
. If
P\sim_{\mathcal{C}}Q
then
\mathcal{D}(P)\succeq Q
.

Proof.

The intersection of
\mathcal{N}_{B}
with the nilpotent orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{D}(P)}
is a dense open subset of
\mathcal{N}_{B}
. Since
\mathcal{N}_{B}
is irreducible we have

\mathcal{N}_{B}=\overline{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{D}(P)}\cap\mathcal{N}_{B}}\subset\overline{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{D}(P)}}\cap\mathcal{N}_{B}\subset\mathcal{N}_{B}.

Here
\overline{X}
is the Zariski closure of the set
X
. The above chain of containments is in fact a chain of equalities, and so

\mathcal{N}_{B}=\overline{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{D}(P)}\cap\mathcal{N}_{B}}=\overline{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{D}(P)}}\cap\mathcal{N}_{B}.

This implies that if
\mathcal{O}_{Q}
is any orbit such that
\mathcal{N}_{B}\cap\mathcal{O}_{Q}\neq\emptyset
, then
\mathcal{O}_{Q}\subset\overline{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{D}(P)}}
. By the Gerstenhaber-Hesselink Theorem
[
18
, Thm. 6.2.5]
we have
\mathcal{D}(P)\succeq Q
.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

For each partition
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
we have
Q\succeq P
for any
P\in\mathcal{D}^{-1}(Q)
.

Among negative results, i.e., which pairs of partitions are not pairs of Jordan types of two commuting matrices, we state first an easy consequence of Proposition
4.1
.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
Q
and
R
are two distinct partitions in
\mathcal{Q}
, then
Q\not\sim_{\mathcal{C}}R
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{D}
is idempotent and
Q,R\in\im\mathcal{D}
, we have
Q=\mathcal{D}(Q)
and
R=\mathcal{D}(R)
.

We assume that the nilpotent orbit
\mathcal{O}_{Q}
intersects
\mathcal{N}_{B}
, where
R
is the Jordan type of
B
. We want to obtain a contradiction. By Proposition
4.1
our assumption implies that
R=\mathcal{D}(R)\succeq Q
. By reversing the roles of
R
and
Q
we also obtain that
Q=\mathcal{D}(Q)\succeq R
, which is possible if and only if
Q=R
. This contradicts the assumption that
Q
and
R
are distinct.
∎

A partition
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
is the element with the least number of parts among all partitions in the inverse image
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(Q)
. For each
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
there is also a unique partition with the maximal number of parts in
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(Q)
. We denote this unique partition by
Q_{\textrm{mx}}
. Example 3 in
[
29
]
shows that in general the partition
Q_{\textrm{mx}}
is not the minimal partition in
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(Q)
in the dominance order. (The same can be observed also in Table 1 below where partitions
(8,4,1^{5})
and
(8,3^{2},1^{3})
are incomparable in the dominance order.) So,
Q_{\textrm{mx}}
is not in general the minimal element of
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(Q)
in the Bruhat order. An example in
[
33
, Example 6]
also shows that in general
Q_{mx}
does not commute with all other partitions in
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(Q)
.

5.
The Box Theorem

A conjecture regarding the structure of the inverse images
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(Q)
for
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
was proposed in
[
27
, Conj. 4.11]
. The name the
Box Conjecture
was given to the conjecture since it was conjectured that the elements of
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(Q)
for a partition
Q=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k})\in\mathcal{Q}
can be arranged in a box (i.e. an array) of sizes
q_{k}\times(q_{k-1}-q_{k}-1)\times\cdots\times(q_{1}-q_{2}-1)
such that the partition in the
(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{k})
-th position has exactly
\sum_{j=1}^{k}i_{j}
parts. The conjecture was proved in
[
27
]
for partitions with at most
2
parts.
In
[
29
]
, the authors provide a proof of the conjecture for a general
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
. The main tools in the proof are the Burge correspondence
[
16
,
17
]
(cf. also
[
4
,
14
]
) between the set of all partitions
\mathcal{P}
and binary words, and Shayman’s description
[
50
,
51
]
of all invariant subspaces of a nilpotent matrix. Here we provide an overview of the proof. For the precise statement of The Box Theorem proceed to Theorem
5.22
.

5.1.
The Burge correspondence

To introduce the correspondence in
[
29
]
representation of partitions by their
frequency vectors
is used. Here we briefly review this correspondence. It is very natural to use the frequency representation of partitions in connection with the Burge correspondence. However, when working with Jordan type, it is more common to represent a partition with its parts since they represent sizes of Jordan blocks. To accommodate both views we briefly review the Burge correspondence on frequency vectors and then also include a longer discussion using the parts representation of partitions. Thus, we complement the proof in
[
29
]
.

For a partition
P
we write
f_{j}=f_{j}(P)
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, for the number of parts in
P
that are equal to
j
. The
frequency vector of

P
is then the sequence
f=f(P)=(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots)
. Here
f
could be limited to the first
p_{1}
elements, but for convenience we may augment it with an arbitrary finite sequence of zeros. We denote the set of all frequency vectors by
\mathcal{F}
. Equivalently,
\mathcal{F}
is the set of all finite sequences of nonnegative integers. For an
f\in\mathcal{F}
we denote by
P(f)
the corresponding partition in
\mathcal{P}
, i.e., the partition whose frequency vector is equal to
f
. When working with
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we adopt the convention that
f_{0}=0
.

Next we introduce notation for some important invariants (statistics) of a partition
P\in\mathcal{P}
using both types of representation of a partition – by its parts and by its frequency vector.
The size of a partition

P=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k})
or
of its frequency vector

f=f(P)=(f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{p_{1}})
is equal to the sum of its parts

|P|=|f|=\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{1}}jf_{j},

the length of a partition or of its frequency vector
is the number of its parts

\ell(P)=\ell(f)=k=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{1}}f_{j}.

The
empty partition
is the unique partition
\varepsilon\in\mathcal{P}
of size (and length)
0
. The
2
-
measure

\mu_{2}(P)=\mu_{2}(f)
of
P
or of
f=f(P)
is the maximum length of an RR subpartition of

is also the minimal number of AR partitions needed to cover

The concept of

to study sets of partitions. See

Let us illustrate the notation with an example.

Example 5.1

Suppose

The

A

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathsf{L}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{L}(P)=\bigcup\{i,i+2,\ldots,i+2\lfloor\tfrac{j-i}{2}\rfloor\}

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}(P)=\bigcup\{j,j-2,\ldots,j-2\lfloor\tfrac{j-i}{2}\rfloor\},

5.3.
The Theorem

The proof of the Box Theorem is now completed by showing that the descent map and

First, we use an easy consequence of Shayman’s results.

Proposition 5.20

[

Each invariant subspace of

that commutes with

This allows us to describe the Jordan type of a restriction of

Theorem 5.21

[

Suppose that

This result implies the equality

Theorem 5.22

[

, Cor. 2]

Let

6.
Some Open Questions

Despite recent progress made in understanding commuting Jordan types, several fundamental questions remain open:

•

Complete Characterization of Commuting Jordan Types:
The central open problem is to determine all pairs of commuting Jordan types
(P,Q)
. While specific cases and properties have been identified, a general, explicit characterization or an algorithm to test commutativity for arbitrary pairs of partitions is still elusive.

•

Asymptotic proportion of commuting pairs:
No results are known on the asymptotic behaviour of the proportion of commuting partitions with increasing
n
. For
n
small, one can count all pairs of commuting partitions using the results of section
3
and
4
. If we denote by
C(n)
the number of all pairs of commuting partitions of
n
and by
A(n)
the number of all pairs of partitions of
n
, what can one say about the behaviour of the quotient
\frac{C(n)}{A(n)}
as
n
grows?

•

Algebraic structure behind the Burge correspondence and more generally,
behind the commuting Jordan types:
The Burge correspondence has proven effective in describing the structure of
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(Q)
as a “box”. In
[
33
]
the authors give examples of two partitions in the same box
\mathcal{D}^{-1}(Q)
that do not commute. For instance, partitions
P_{1}=(7,1^{2})
and
P_{2}=(3^{2},1^{3})
do not commute but we have
\mathcal{D}(P_{1})=\mathcal{D}(P_{2})=(7,2)
. Moreover, another example in
[
33
]
shows that even partition
P_{mx}
does not have to commute with all other partitions
P
with
\mathcal{D}(P)=\mathcal{D}(P_{mx})
. So, partitions with equal descent set need not commute. Can one decide from the Burge correspondence which pairs of partitions in a ”box” are not commuting?
Are there any results in (inverse) semigroup theory that explain this phenomenon? And more generally, are there other algebraic constructions that govern the commutativity of Jordan types?

•

Stratification of orbits for pairs of commuting nilpotent matrices:
The previous problem has its geometric counterpart. Now, the more specific open problem is a generalization of the Gerstenhaber-Hesselink Theorem
[
18
, Thm. 6.2.5]
to the nilpotent commutator of a nilpotent matrix. Specifically, the question is which orbits are in the closure of the intersection of a given nilpotent orbit with the nilpotent commutator. In this direction, a conjecture on the stratification for a nilpotent commutator of a nilpotent matrix whose Jordan type is an RR partition was formulated by Boij, Iarrobino, and Khatami
[
13
, Conj. 4.2]
. They provide the stratification for the case of an RR partition with two parts
[
13
, Thm. 1.3 and Cor. 3.8]
. A more general question is to provide the Jordan types stratification of the variety of pairs of nilpotent commuting matrices.

•

Commuting Jordan types for other simple Lie algebras:
The study of commuting nilpotent elements and their Jordan types is primarily developed for the general linear Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
(type A). An intriguing question is whether there exists an analogous “Burge correspondence” or a similar combinatorial framework for describing commuting nilpotent elements in other simple Lie algebras (e.g., types B, C, D, etc.). This would extend the rich interplay between combinatorics and representation theory to a broader class of algebraic structures. Note however, that Premet
[
48
]
showed that the variety of pairs of nilpotent elements in other simple Lie algebras is no longer irreducible. Some results on universally commuting Jordan types for other simple Lie algebras are given in Goddard
[
23
, Ch. 4]
.

These open questions and recent results, e.g. in
[
12
,
33
]
