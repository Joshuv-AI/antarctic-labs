Abstract
We propose a task-oriented multiuser wireless communication framework for distributed classification based on a MAP-driven system design under wireless channel impairments. By deriving a tractable class-mean separation objective, the proposed approach enables low-complexity design of both learning-based feature extraction and precoding strategies. Unlike existing covariance-based and reconstruction-oriented methods, the proposed formulation avoids repeated covariance inversions and eigen-decomposition operations while directly improving class separability after channel distortion. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves higher classification accuracy than existing schemes, while simultaneously reducing computational complexity.



I
Introduction

Task-oriented and semantic communication has recently emerged as a promising paradigm for next-generation wireless systems, where the goal is to reliably convey task-relevant semantic information rather than reconstructing raw transmitted signals
[
1
]
. In contrast to conventional communication systems that primarily focus on minimizing symbol or signal distortion, semantic and task-oriented frameworks aim to optimize end-task performance such as classification or inference accuracy at the receiver. This shift has motivated the integration of machine learning techniques with wireless communication design, enabling joint optimization of feature representation, transmission strategies, and decision-making under channel impairments. As a result, new system designs are required that account for both learning-based feature extraction and channel-aware signal processing in a unified framework

.

Earlier works in task-oriented communication focus on learning-based feature transmission, where neural networks extract compact representations and transmit them over wireless channels for downstream tasks such as classification or retrieval
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. These methods mainly optimize task performance indirectly through representation learning frameworks such as deep joint source–channel coding (JSCC) or information bottleneck models. More recent approaches further shape the learned feature space to enhance post-channel inference performance, rather than solely compressing representations for transmission
[
7
,
8
,
5
,
6
]
. Although achieving promising performance, these methods still rely on conventional communication objectives that are not explicitly aligned with end-task inference. For instance,
[
5
]
targets accurate signal reconstruction via the linear minimum mean-squared error criterion, while
[
6
,
7
]
employ mutual information as the optimization objective, focusing on semantic-aware representation learning rather than task-specific discrimination. The method in
[
8
]
employs maximal coding rate reduction (MCR
2
) to enhance class separability in the learned feature space. This reliance on criteria that are not explicitly aligned with task-specific inference objectives limits the effectiveness of existing task-oriented communication systems.

To address this issue, this paper proposes a task-oriented wireless communication framework based on an approximate MAP error formulation in the channel-affected feature space, which is more aligned with the end-task objective than conventional approaches in the literature. Unlike existing approaches relying on reconstruction-based or surrogate coding-rate objectives, the proposed formulation directly targets post-channel MAP decision performance while avoiding expensive covariance and matrix decomposition operations. Simulation results demonstrate improved classification accuracy and more stable performance compared to existing methods, while significantly reducing computational complexity.

II
System Model

We consider a distributed wireless classification system consisting of
K
edge devices (workers) and a central server (fusion center). Each worker
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
is equipped with
N_{k}
transmit antennas, while the server is equipped with
M
receive antennas. The observed data at each worker belongs to one of
C
classes
\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{C}\}
. The
k
th worker processes its high-dimensional observation
\mathbf{s}_{k}
using a feature extraction function
\mathbf{x}_{k}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{D_{k}\times 1}
, where
f_{\theta}(\cdot)
denotes a parameterized nonlinear mapping (e.g., a neural network with parameters
\theta
) that transforms the input sample into a lower-dimensional task-relevant feature vector. The resulting feature vector
\mathbf{x}_{k}
is transmitted to the server through a wireless channel. The ultimate goal of the system is to correctly infer the class label
\mathcal{C}_{j}
from the received signal at the fusion center. The received signal at the edge server corresponding to the
t
th channel use is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{V}_{k,t}\mathbf{x}_{k}+\mathbf{z}_{t},

(1)

where
\mathbf{H}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N_{k}}
is the wireless channel between worker
k
and the server,
\mathbf{V}_{k,t}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{k}\times D_{k}}
is the precoding matrix at the
t
th channel-use of the
k
th edge device, and
\mathbf{z}_{t}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{M})
is the additive complex symmetric Gaussian noise. The received signal in (
1
) can be written in the following matrix form

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{V}_{t}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z}_{t},

(2)

where
\mathbf{H}=[\mathbf{H}_{1},\mathbf{H}_{2},...,\mathbf{H}_{K}]\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
,
\mathbf{x}=[\mathbf{x}_{1}^{T},\mathbf{x}_{2}^{T},....,\mathbf{x}_{K}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times 1}
,
\mathbf{V}_{t}=\mathrm{blkdiag}(\mathbf{V}_{1,t},\mathbf{V}_{2,t},\dots,\mathbf{V}_{K,t})
with
N=\sum_{k=1}^{K}N_{k}
and
D=\sum_{k=1}^{K}D_{k}
. Stacking the received signals over
T
channel uses, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z},

(3)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{TM\times TN}
,
\mathbf{y}=[\mathbf{y}_{1}^{T},\mathbf{y}_{2}^{T},....,\mathbf{y}_{T}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{TM\times 1}
,
\mathbf{z}=[\mathbf{z}_{1}^{T},\mathbf{z}_{2}^{T},....,\mathbf{z}_{T}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{TM\times 1}
,
\mathbf{V}=\left[\mathbf{V}_{1}^{T},\mathbf{V}_{2}^{T},\dots,\mathbf{V}_{T}^{T}\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{TN\times D}
. In (
3
),
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
denotes the effective channel matrix and
\mathbf{V}\mathbf{x}
is the transmitted signal. To satisfy the transmit power constraint, we impose

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\|\mathbf{V}\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\}\leq PT,

(4)

where
P
denotes the total transmit power budget across all users.

III
MAP-Based Task-Oriented System Design

The system design is formulated under a MAP detection framework, where the feature extractor
f_{\theta}(\cdot)
and precoder
\mathbf{V}
are designed to minimize the MAP-induced classification error probability.

III-A

MAP Receiver and Error Probability Analysis

The maximum a posteriori (MAP) detector is given by

\displaystyle\hat{c}=\arg\max_{j}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathcal{C}_{j})\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{j}),

(5)

where
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{j})
denotes the class prior probability. Assuming class-conditional Gaussian feature distributions, i.e.,

\mathbf{x}|\mathcal{C}_{j}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mu_{j},\Sigma_{j})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}\mid\mathcal{C}_{j}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mu_{j},\mathbf{K}_{j}),

(6)

where
\mathbf{K}_{j}=\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\Sigma_{j}\mathbf{V}^{H}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{H}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{MT}
. To obtain a tractable detector structure and avoid repeated covariance matrix inversions, we employ the approximation
\mathbf{K}_{j}\approx\frac{1}{MT}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{K}_{j})\mathbf{I}_{MT}
, which gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{j}\approx\frac{1}{MT}\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\Sigma_{j}\mathbf{V}^{H}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{H})\mathbf{I}_{MT}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{MT}.

(7)

Assuming weak coupling between the channel and feature covariance matrices, we have
\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\Sigma_{j}\mathbf{V}^{H}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{H})\approx\dfrac{1}{MT}\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{H}\tilde{\mathbf{H}})\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{V}\Sigma_{j}\mathbf{V}^{H})
. Then, motivated by (
4
), we can rewrite (
7
) as

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{j}

\displaystyle\approx\delta_{H}\mathbf{I}_{MT},

(8)

where
\delta_{H}=\sigma^{2}+\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{H}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}){P}/({M^{2}T})
. Using (
6
) and (
8
), the MAP detector in (
5
) can be simplified as

\displaystyle\hat{c}=\arg\min_{j}\frac{\|\mathbf{y}-\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mu_{j}\|^{2}}{\delta_{H}}-\log\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{j}),

(9)

where the determinant term is omitted since
\delta_{H}
is independent of the class index.

The error probability of the MAP detector can be upper-bounded using a pairwise union bound as

\displaystyle P_{e}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{C}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{j})P_{jk},

(10)

where

P_{jk}=\mathbb{P}\left(\|\mathbf{y}-\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mu_{k}\|^{2}+a_{jk}<\|\mathbf{y}-\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mu_{j}\|^{2}\big|\mathcal{C}_{j}\right)
with
a_{jk}=\delta_{H}(\log\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{j})-\log\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{k}))
.
We can write
\mathbf{y}|\mathcal{C}_{j}=\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mu_{j}+\mathbf{k}_{j}
, where
\mathbf{k}_{j}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\delta_{H}\mathbf{I}_{MT})
. Accordingly, the pairwise error probability
P_{jk}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle P_{jk}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\|\mathbf{k}_{j}+\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mu_{jk}\|^{2}+a_{jk}<\|\mathbf{k}_{j}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=Q\left(\sqrt{\frac{\|\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mu_{jk}\|^{2}+\delta_{H}\log\left(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{j})/\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\right)}{2\delta_{H}}}\right),

(11)

where
\mu_{jk}=\mu_{j}-\mu_{k}
.

III-B

Feature Extraction and Precoder Design

In this subsection, we present the proposed transmitter design, which consists of feature extraction and a linear precoder. Both components are optimized based on the MAP error probability derived in the previous subsection to improve task performance under power constraints.

III-B
1
Neural Network Training and Feature Learning

In this part, we describe the feature learning module used to generate class-discriminative representations at each user. Specifically, the
k
th user employs a neural network parameterized by
\theta_{k}
, denoted by
f_{\theta_{k}}(\cdot)
, to map its high-dimensional observation into a real-valued latent representation
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2D_{k}}
. The resulting vector is converted into a complex representation
\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{D_{k}\times 1}
as
\mathbf{x}_{k}=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k,1:D_{k}}+j\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k,D_{k}+1:2D_{k}}
, followed by normalization such that
\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|^{2}=1
. For each class, the feature vectors within a minibatch are averaged to obtain the class-dependent mean representations
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{C}
.

To obtain the learning objective, we use the error term in (
10
) under a nominal noiseless and identity-equivalent channel assumption, i.e.,
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\approx\mathbf{I}_{D}
,
\sigma^{2}=0
, and set
\delta_{H}
to a fixed constant value, i.e.,
\delta_{H}=0.5
. These approximations normalize the loss to remove scale ambiguity induced by channel statistics, while preserving the structure of the MAP decision rule. In addition, we assume uniform class priors, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{j})=1/C
, which corresponds to the case where no prior information about class distribution is available. Under these assumptions, the resulting loss function is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{C}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\frac{1}{C}Q\left(\|\mu_{jk}\|\right).

(12)

This loss promotes separation between class-dependent feature means in the learned representation space. The neural network parameters are optimized using standard backpropagation to minimize this objective over the training dataset. After convergence, the learned class means are computed from the trained feature extractor and used in the subsequent precoder design stage, where the wireless transmission matrix is optimized based on these statistics.

III-B
2
Precoder Design

To design the precoder matrix
\mathbf{V}
, we minimize the derived pairwise MAP error approximation in (
10
), under the assumption of uniform class priors, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{j})=1/C
, which yields the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{V}}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{C}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\dfrac{1}{C}\mathcal{L}_{jk}(\mathbf{V}),\ \text{s.t.}\ \|\mathbf{V}\mu_{j}\|^{2}\leq PT,

(13)

where
\mathcal{L}_{jk}(\mathbf{V})=Q\left(\sqrt{0.5{\|\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mu_{jk}\|^{2}}/{\delta_{H}}}\right)
.
To obtain a tractable optimization problem, we approximate the Gaussian Q-function using an exponential surrogate of the form
Q(x)\approx\exp(-\tau x^{2})
, where
\tau>0
is a tuning parameter controlling the tightness of approximation. Defining
d_{jk}(\mathbf{V})=0.5{\|\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mu_{jk}\|^{2}}/{\delta_{H}}
, we have

\mathcal{L}_{jk}(\mathbf{V})=e^{-\tau d_{jk}(\mathbf{V})}
. Using a projected gradient descent/ascent scheme on the surrogate objective, the precoder update rule is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)}=\alpha_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\mathbf{V}^{(t)}+\dfrac{\eta}{C}\sum_{j=1}^{C}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{jk}\left(\mathbf{V}^{(t)}\right)\right),

(14)

where
\eta
is the step size,
\alpha_{\mathcal{P}}
is a simple normalization scalar that rescales the precoder such that the maximum class-wise transmit power satisfies the constraint, i.e.,
\max_{j}\|\mathbf{V}^{(t+1)}\mu_{j}\|_{F}^{2}\leq PT
, and
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{jk}(\mathbf{V})
is the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{jk}(\mathbf{V})
with respect to
\mathbf{V}
, given by

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{jk}(\mathbf{V})=-\frac{\tau}{\delta_{H}}\sum_{j=1}^{C}\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{H}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mu_{jk}\mu_{jk}^{H}e^{-\tau d_{jk}(\mathbf{V})}.

(15)

The precoder matrix is updated using (
14
) over
I_{0}
iterations, and the final updated matrix
\mathbf{V}^{(I_{1})}
is used as the precoding matrix for transmission.

III-B
3
Computational Complexity

In this subsection, we analyze the computational complexity of the proposed feature extractor and precoder and compare them with existing approaches. Specifically, we measure the complexity in terms of the number of complex multiplications required by each algorithm.

Feature Extractor Complexity
: The proposed objective in (
12
) requires computing pairwise distances between class-mean feature vectors across
C
classes in a
D
-dimensional space, which results in a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(DC^{2})
. In contrast, the MCR
2
-based objective in
[
8
]
requires the computation of empirical covariance matrices over a minibatch of size
B
, together with the evaluation of log-determinant terms over
C+1
covariance matrices of size
D\times D
. This leads to a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(BD^{2}+CD^{3})
, which is significantly higher than the proposed approach in high-dimensional settings. We note that, to analyze the feature extractor complexity, we ignore the computational cost of the neural network layers, as they exhibit standard complexity in both the proposed method and MCR
2
and are not the focus of this work. Thus, we only consider the complexity of the loss functions.

Precoder Complexity
: The proposed gradient-based precoder optimization requires evaluating the pairwise separation term
\|\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{V}\mu_{jk}\|^{2}
for all class pairs
(j,k)
. For each pair, the computation of
\mathbf{V}\mu_{jk}
has complexity
\mathcal{O}(N_{k}KTD_{k})
, followed by multiplication with
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
, which requires
\mathcal{O}(MN_{k}KT)
operations, and the computation of the squared norm with complexity
\mathcal{O}(MT)
. Since the number of class pairs scales as
\mathcal{O}(C^{2})
, the total forward complexity scales as
\mathcal{O}\big(C^{2}N_{k}KT(D_{k}+M)\big)
, which also determines the overall computational complexity since the gradient computation involves the same set of pairwise operations and does not change the order. In contrast, the MCR
2
-based precoder in
[
8
]
relies on a block coordinate ascent framework involving matrix inversions, singular value decompositions, and iterative bisection-based power allocation updates, which results in a per-iteration computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(D^{3}+CM^{3}T^{3}+I_{1}KT^{3}N_{k}^{3}D_{k}^{3}\right)
, where
I_{1}
denotes the number of iterations for the bisection-based updates. For the LMMSE precoder in
[
5
]
(with modifications detailed in Appendix A of
[
8
]
), the per-iteration computational complexity is given by
\mathcal{O}\left(M^{3}T^{3}+I_{1}KT^{3}N_{k}^{3}\right)
.

IV
Numerical Results

The performance of the proposed task-oriented communication framework is evaluated using our implementation available on GitHub
[
9
]
. Unless stated otherwise, the following settings are used throughout the simulations. We consider a distributed wireless classification system using the CIFAR-10 dataset with
K=2
views per sample, where each view is generated using stochastic data augmentation including random resized cropping, horizontal flipping, and color jittering, resulting in two independently augmented observations per input image. Each view is encoded using a dedicated ResNet-18 backbone initialized with ImageNet pretrained weights, followed by a two-layer fully connected projection head that maps the 512-dimensional feature representation to a
2D_{k}
-dimensional latent embedding. For performing the update equation in (
14
), we select
\eta=0.1
,
\tau=0.7
, and
I_{0}=30
. The wireless channel coefficients are generated independently according to
\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
, corresponding to a Rayleigh fading environment, with transmit power
P=2
, noise variance
\sigma^{2}=1
, and system parameters
N_{k}=6
and
T=1
.

In Fig.
1
, we plot the test accuracy versus the number of training epochs for the proposed feature extractor and MCR
2
in
[
8
]
with different values of
\epsilon
. For a fair comparison, both methods use the same network architecture. It can be observed that the proposed method closely matches the performance and convergence behavior of MCR
2
across training epochs, while requiring significantly lower computational complexity as discussed in Sec.
III-B3
, due to its independence from covariance estimation and matrix decomposition operations. Moreover, the results indicate that MCR
2
exhibits strong sensitivity to the choice of
\epsilon
, where the best performance is achieved at
\epsilon=5
, while both smaller and larger values lead to noticeable performance degradation. In contrast, the proposed method does not rely on such sensitive tuning parameters, which improves its robustness.

We compare the performance of different precoding schemes in Fig.
2
, which illustrates the MAP detection error performance with respect to the number of receive antennas. The baselines are the MCR
2
precoder proposed in
[
8
]
and the LMMSE baseline in
[
5
]
(with modifications detailed in Appendix A of
[
8
]
). For a fair comparison focused solely on precoder design, all considered schemes employ the same pretrained MCR
2
-based feature extractor
[
8
]
. It is observed that the proposed precoder consistently achieves lower MAP error compared to both the LMMSE and MCR
2
precoders. This superior performance stems from the fact that the proposed method directly minimizes an approximation of the MAP detection error. In contrast, the LMMSE precoder is designed to minimize the mean-squared error of feature reconstruction at the receiver, while the MCR
2
precoder focuses on maximizing rate-related objectives that are not directly aligned with classification error minimization.

In addition, it is observed in Fig.

that the computational complexity of the MCR
2
precoder is significantly higher than that of both the proposed and LMMSE precoders. This is mainly due to the repeated matrix inversions, SVD operations, and inner iterative updates required by the block coordinate ascent framework, whereas the proposed method relies only on gradient-based updates and simple matrix multiplications. Overall, the results demonstrate that the proposed precoder achieves a favorable trade-off between classification accuracy and computational efficiency compared to existing baselines.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed a MAP-driven task-oriented communication framework for distributed wireless classification. A low-complexity feature learning objective and a gradient-based precoder were derived from an approximation of the MAP detection error. Numerical results show that the proposed method achieves higher classification accuracy, lower computational complexity, and improved robustness compared to conventional precoding schemes.
