Bound States and Resonance Analysis of One-Dimensional Relativistic Parity-Symmetric Two Point Interactions
Carlos A. Bonin, Manuel Gadella, José T. Lunardi, Luiz A. Manzoni

Abstract
We consider the one-dimensional Dirac equation with the most general relativistic contact interaction supported on two points symmetrically located with respect to the origin. In order to determine the shape of the interaction, we use a distributional method, which in the present case is equivalent to the standard method of defining contact interactions by self-adjoint extensions of symmetric operators. The interaction on each of these two points depends on four parameters, each one having a clear physical meaning. We are interested in the scattering and confining properties of this model. We focus our attention on even or odd interactions under parity transformations and investigate the existence of critical and supercritical states, bound states, confinement and scattering resonances for some particular interactions of special interest.

VI
Concluding remarks

We have investigated a relativistic, one-dimensional model with two contact interactions located symmetrically with respect to the origin, using the mathematically rigorous distributional method developed by some of the authors. Taking advantage of this approach, each of the contact interactions depends on four independent parameters with a well-defined physical meaning: scalar, electrostatic, magnetic, and pseudoscalar interactions. These interactions can be equivalently characterized by some matching conditions for the wave function at the points supporting the interaction. We discuss the form of the coefficients and matching conditions for two particular cases of special interest: even and odd interactions. We have also considered the limit of one barrier, in which both barriers are supported at the same point, for both even and odd barriers. Some interesting results have emerged after this limit: while the one-point limit of an even arrangement is always an even interaction, the
\ell\to 0^{+}
limit of an odd arrangement does not always have a well-defined symmetry.

Using the form of the wave function outside the interaction points and the matching conditions at these points, we constructed the transmission matrix and, hence, the scattering matrix. Then, we provided a detailed analysis of the structure of critical, supercritical, and bound states, as well as of the resonances for even and odd arrangements of interactions for all the main relativistic, point interactions of physical interest in the literature, namely, equal and inverted mixtures of electrostatic and scalar interactions, pseudoscalar, scalar, magnetostatic and electrostatic interactions.

A possible extension of this work would be to consider interactions with
N
contact interactions, i.e., interaction supported at
N
points, and investigate the transmission bands.
This requires the construction of the transfer matrix for the system of
N
interactions, for an even number of interactions the results obtained here can be directly applied, since the comb can be seen as
N/2
cells of two interactions. This will provide reflection and transmission coefficients and, hence, bound states and scattering features such as resonances.
Additionally, as was demonstrated, in order to confine a particle or an anti-particle on one side of the interaction (
i.e.
, to have an impermeable interaction), the single-point interactions
must
include a scalar and/or a pseudoscalar component, since electrostatic interactions are non-confining (see Figures
7
and
14
). This could be related to the Klein effect and deserves further study.

Appendix A
The Strengths of the Interactions

Here we briefly reproduce the relationships between the physical parameters (potential strengths) and the parameters of the
\Lambda
-matrix – for details on the derivation of these results, we refer the reader to
10
(see also
36
;
6
). At each point supporting the interaction, the physical parameters can be obtained from the elements of each
\Lambda
-matrix in (
4
) through:

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2(c+b)}{2\cos\varphi+d+a}\,,

(78)

\displaystyle A_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2(c-b)}{2\cos\varphi+d+a}\,,

(79)

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-4\sin\varphi}{2\cos\varphi+d+a}\,,

(80)

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2(d-a)}{2\cos\varphi+d+a}\,.

(81)

The inverse relationships are given by

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{4A_{1}}{B^{2}+W^{2}-4-A_{0}^{2}+A_{1}^{2}}\right)\,\qquad\varphi\in[0,\pi)\,,

(82)

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\,\frac{A_{0}^{2}-A_{1}^{2}-B^{2}-(W-2)^{2}}{\sqrt{\left(B^{2}+W^{2}-4-A_{0}^{2}+A_{1}^{2}\right)^{2}+16A_{1}^{2}}}\,,

(83)

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\,\frac{4(A_{0}-B)}{\sqrt{\left(B^{2}+W^{2}-4-A_{0}^{2}+A_{1}^{2}\right)^{2}+16A_{1}^{2}}}\,,

(84)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\,\frac{-4(A_{0}+B)}{\sqrt{\left(B^{2}+W^{2}-4-A_{0}^{2}+A_{1}^{2}\right)^{2}+16A_{1}^{2}}}\,,

(85)

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\,\frac{A_{0}^{2}-A_{1}^{2}-B^{2}-(W+2)^{2}}{\sqrt{\left(B^{2}+W^{2}-4-A_{0}^{2}+A_{1}^{2}\right)^{2}+16A_{1}^{2}}}\,,

(86)

where
\eta=\mathrm{sign}\,A_{1}
, if
A_{1}\neq 0
and
\eta=\mathrm{sign}\,\left(B^{2}+W^{2}-4-A_{0}^{2}\right)
otherwise.

Above, we may consider the
\mathbb{\Lambda}
-matrix parameters as being all finite or (some of them) infinite. The point interaction is said to be
permeable
(or
penetrable
) if the
\mathbb{\Lambda}
-matrix parameters are all finite; in this case equation (
3
) completely determines
\psi(x_{j}^{+})
from the knowledge of
\psi(x_{j}^{-})
, and vice-versa. If any of the
\mathbb{\Lambda}
-matrix parameters were infinite, the point interaction is said to be
impermeable
(or
impenetrable
); in this case
\psi(x_{j}^{+})

is not
completely determined from
\psi(x_{j}^{-})
, and the solutions in both sides of the origin are independent from each other (see
10
for details). The physical strengths
B,A_{\mu}
and
W
may either be all finite or (at least some of them) infinite; in any case, the permeability condition implies that

A_{1}\neq 0\qquad\text{or}\qquad B^{2}+W^{2}-A_{0}^{2}-4\neq 0\,.

(87)

If the physical parameters are all finite, condition (
87
) is
equivalent
to the permeability condition. Finally, if any the physical strengths are infinite and the point interaction is impermeable, condition (
87
) may or may not be satisfied. For example, the interaction with
B=A_{0},\,A_{1}=W=0
(which corresponds to the “delta interaction” in non-relativistic limit), with
A_{0}
(and
B
) infinite, is impermeable at the singular point, but the physical parameters satisfy (
87
). On the other hand, the interaction with
B=A_{0},\,A_{1}=0
, but
W=2
is impermeable and violates (
87
), regardless of whether
B=A_{0}
is finite or infinite.

Appendix B
On the non-relativistic limit

In the present appendix, and for the sake of completeness, we study the non-relativistic limit of the Dirac equation (
1
) with (
). As a matter of fact, this task has been performed in
10
, so that we here just recall the results in that paper adapted to our particular situation.

As is well known, the solution of the Dirac equation (
1
) is a two component spinor of the form
\psi(x)=(u(x),v(x))^{T}
, where the superindex
T
denotes the transpose. Then, equation (
1
) with (
) may be split into two equations both in
u(x)
and
v(x)
in the following form (the prime means derivative with respect to the variable
x
:

\displaystyle(E-m)u(x)+iv^{\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle\qquad=\left[(B^{(1)}+A_{0}^{(1)})\left(\frac{u_{-}^{+}+u_{-}^{-}}{2}\right)+(iW^{(1)}+A_{1}^{(1)})\left(\frac{v_{-}^{+}+v_{-}^{-}}{2}\right)\right]\delta(x-\ell/2)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left[(B^{(2)}+A_{0}^{(2)})\left(\frac{u_{+}^{+}+u_{+}^{-}}{2}\right)+(iW^{(2)}+A_{1}^{(2)})\left(\frac{v_{+}^{+}+v_{+}^{-}}{2}\right)\right]\delta(x+\ell/2)\,,

(88)

and

\displaystyle-(E+m)v(x)-iu^{\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle\qquad=\left[(iW^{(1)}-A_{1}^{(1)})\left(\frac{u_{-}^{+}+u_{-}^{-}}{2}\right)+(B^{(1)}-A_{0}^{(1)})\left(\frac{v_{-}^{+}+v_{-}^{-}}{2}\right)\right]\delta(x-\ell/2)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left[(iW^{(2)}-A_{1}^{(2)})\left(\frac{u_{+}^{+}+u_{+}^{-}}{2}\right)+(B^{(2)}-A_{0}^{(2)})\left(\frac{v_{+}^{+}+v_{+}^{-}}{2}\right)\right]\delta(x+\ell/2)\,,

(89)

where
u_{-}^{+}:=u(-\ell^{+}/2)
,
u_{-}^{-}:=u(-\ell^{-}/2))
,
u_{+}^{+}:=u(\ell^{+}/2)
,
u_{+}^{-}:=u(\ell^{-}/2)
and the same notation for
v
instead of
u
. We recall that
u(\alpha^{\pm}):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}u(\alpha\pm\varepsilon)
.

In order to have the non-relativistic limit, we first note that

E=m+\varepsilon\,,\qquad E+m\approx 2m\,,

(90)

where
\varepsilon
is the non-relativistic energy. Outside the origin, the spinor
\psi(x)
satisfies the free equation and its “small” component
v(x)
is proportional to the “large” component
u(x)
as may be seen from (
B
), so that

v(x)=-\frac{i}{2m}\,u^{\prime}(x)\Longrightarrow v^{\pm}_{\pm}=-\frac{i}{2m}\,u^{\prime\pm}_{\pm}\,.

(91)

Then, take the expression of
v(x)
obtained from (
B
) in terms of
u^{\prime}(x)
and all the other terms, use this expression in (
B
) and take the non-relativistic limit using (
90
). We obtain the following equation:

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2m}\,u^{\prime\prime}(x)-\varepsilon\,u(x)

\displaystyle=\left[-(B^{(1)}+A_{0}^{(1)})\left(\frac{u_{-}^{+}+u_{-}^{-}}{2}\right)-\frac{W^{(1)}-iA_{1}^{(1)}}{2m}\left(\frac{u_{-}^{\prime+}+u_{-}^{\prime-}}{2}\right)\right]\delta(x-\ell/2)

\displaystyle+\left[\left(\frac{W^{(1)}+iA_{1}^{(1)}}{2m}\right)\left(\frac{u_{-}^{+}+u_{-}^{-}}{2}\right)-\left(\frac{B^{(1)}-A_{0}^{(1)}}{4m^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{u_{-}^{\prime+}+u_{-}^{\prime-}}{2}\right)\right]\delta^{\prime}(x-\ell/2)+T\,,

(92)

where
T
is a term that is similar to the two terms on the right hand side of (
B
) with the following modifications: i) the superscript “
(1)
” has to be replaced by the superscript “
(2)

u_{-}^{\pm}\longmapsto u_{+}^{\pm}
and
u_{-}^{\prime\pm}\longmapsto u_{+}^{\prime\pm}

-\ell/2\longmapsto+\ell/2
. In addition,
\delta^{\prime}(x\pm\ell/2)
is the distributional derivative of
\delta(x\pm\ell/2)
.

Now, we discuss some particular cases that we consider as of special interest:

•

Delta interactions
.

From (
B
), a delta interaction appears at the point
\pm\ell/2
only when (
i=1,2
):

W^{(i)}=A_{1}^{(i)}=0\,,\qquad B^{(i)}=A_{0}^{(i)}\neq 0\,.

(93)

This interaction consists of an equal mixture of electrical and scalar point interactions. In this case, the Schrödinger equation reads as

-\frac{1}{2m}\,u^{\prime\prime}(x)-\varepsilon\,u(x)=-2B^{(1)}\,\left(\frac{u_{-}^{+}+u_{-}^{-}}{2}\right)\delta(x-\ell/2)-2B^{(2)}\,\left(\frac{u_{+}^{+}+u_{+}^{-}}{2}\right)\delta(x+\ell/2)\,.

(94)

In the non-relativistic case, we can define for each point
\pm\ell/2
a matrix similar to (
4
), which in the present case takes the form:

\Lambda_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\[8.61108pt]
4mB^{(i)}&1\end{array}\right)\,,\qquad i=1,2\,.

(95)

•

Non-local
\delta^{\prime}
interactions.

At each point, this interaction appears as an inverted mixture of electrostatic and scalar point interactions defined as (
i=1,2,
):

W^{(i)}=A_{1}^{(i)}=0\,,\qquad B^{(i)}=-A_{0}^{(i)}\neq 0\,.

(96)

The Schrödinger equation now reads:

-\frac{1}{2m}\,u^{\prime\prime}(x)-\varepsilon\,u(x)=-\frac{2B^{(1)}}{4m^{2}}\,\frac{u_{-}^{\prime+}+u_{-}^{\prime-}}{2}\,\delta^{\prime}(x-\ell/2)+-\frac{2B^{(2)}}{4m^{2}}\,\frac{u_{+}^{\prime+}+u_{+}^{\prime-}}{2}\,\delta^{\prime}(x+\ell/2)\,,

\Lambda_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\displaystyle\frac{2B^{(i)}}{2m}\\[8.61108pt]

Local
\delta^{\prime}

B^{(i)}=A_{0}^{(i)}=A_{1}^{(i)}=0\,,\qquad W^{(i)}\neq 0\,.

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2m}\,u^{\prime\prime}(x)-\varepsilon\,u(x)=\frac{W^{(1)}}{2m}\left[-\frac{u_{-}^{\prime+}+u_{-}^{\prime-}}{2}\,\delta(x-\ell/2)+\frac{u_{-}^{+}+u_{-}^{-}}{2}\,\delta^{\prime}(x-\ell/2)\right]
