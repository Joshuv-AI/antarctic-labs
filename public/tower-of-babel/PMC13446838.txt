Clinical Comparison of School‐Based Dental Sealant Programme Among Different Caries‐Risk School Children: A 5‐Year Follow‐Up Study
Authors: Yodsapat Paemanukornruk, Piyada Gaewkhiew, Yaowaluk Ngoenwiwatkul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLimited evidence supports the sealant application for children.AimTo assess the association between dental sealant and caries occurrence on first permanent molars among children with different caries risks after 5‐year follow‐up.DesignThis ambispective (retrospective‐prospective) cohort study retrieved data from dental sealant programmes conducted in 2019 and 2024 in Bangkok. Students who participated in the programme during first grade (P1) in 2019 and sixth grade (P6) in 2024 and had at least one sound first permanent molar at baseline were included and categorised based on the number of untreated carious teeth in P1: high‐risk (> 2 teeth), moderate‐risk (1–2 teeth) and low‐risk (no carious teeth). Logistic regression analyses were used to compare caries occurrence between students with at least one sealed first permanent molar and those without for each caries risk group.ResultsSix‐hundred‐ninety‐five students with 2198 sound first permanent molars were analysed. Sealants did not show a significant caries‐preventive effect in low‐and‐moderate‐caries‐risk groups. The high‐risk group, those with at least one sealed tooth, had a significantly lower risk of developing caries (adjusted OR = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.29–0.80; p = 0.005).ConclusionDental sealants are significantly associated with lower caries occurrence, particularly in high‐risk children.

Introduction

Oral diseases are a widespread global public health challenge, irrespective of a country's income level. Untreated dental caries constitutes a significant portion of the oral health burden affecting populations across all age groups, accounting for approximately 2 billion cases in 2019 [1]. Caries‐experienced individuals often suffer from a diminished quality of life due to pain and discomfort [2, 3].

In the case of occlusal caries prevention, dental sealants have been demonstrated to be effective based on clinical evidence, including systematic reviews and evidence‐based guidelines [4], although the extent of caries reduction varies depending on factors such as caries risk, tooth condition, position and the retention rate of the sealant [5, 6, 7]. Currently, school‐based sealant programmes in Thailand focus on the number of students receiving sealants without considering their individual caries risk status, resulting in substantial annual resource demands. In 2019, data from Thailand's Health Data Centre revealed that 662,092 children aged 6–12 years received dental sealants [8]. Similarly, the Bangkok Health Department reported providing sealants to 26602 first‐ and second‐grade students in Bangkok's primary schools [9].

Although guidelines and reviews recommend applying sealants primarily to high caries‐risk children [10, 11, 12], the majority of supporting studies have been conducted in high‐income countries [13, 14, 15, 16]. In contrast, evidence from low‐ to middle‐income countries remains limited, making the effectiveness of implementing a high‐risk‐based sealant strategy in Thailand uncertain.

To address this gap, this retrospective–prospective cohort study aims to assess the association between dental sealant placement and caries occurrence on first permanent molars among children with different caries risks after 5 years of follow‐up, in order to help determine the appropriate strategy for school‐based dental sealant programmes in Thailand.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study design was an ambispective (retrospective–prospective) cohort study using data from the dental sealant programmes conducted in 2019 and 2024 by the Dental Public Health Divisions, Department of Health, and Bangkok Metropolitan Administration (BMA). The reporting of this study was conducted in accordance with the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Ethical approval for this research was obtained. Permission to access and use the dataset was obtained from BMA. Individual informed consent from participants was waived by the ethics committee.

Participant

The Dental Public Health Division's data comprises six dental public health subdivisions, each containing public health centres responsible for implementing preventive programmes within their respective districts. A two‐stage random sampling method was employed, beginning with the random selection of public health centres from each subdivision, followed by the random selection of schools within the responsibility area of each centre.

Inclusion criteria were first‐grade elementary students (P1) who participated in the dental sealant programme during the 2019 academic year. Students were excluded if they did not have fully erupted, sound first permanent molars in 2019 or if data were missing.

Data Collection Process

Each public health centre was requested to deidentify student data by assigning a sequence number to each participant instead of using names. Data were then digitised using Microsoft Excel. Information was collected from the sealant programme conducted in 2019 (baseline) and 2024 (follow‐up), including the tooth status of first permanent molars, sealant application status on first permanent molars, total number of untreated caries, age, sex, frequency of tooth brushing and frequency of snack or sugar‐sweetened beverage consumption. During the programme, sealants were applied by dental professionals, specifically dentists and dental hygienists who were trained prior to working in the public health centre. A standardised resin‐based sealant (Fisseal; WP Dental, Germany) was utilised across all centres.

After data collection, students were divided into two groups based on sealant status at baseline: those who had received a sealant on at least one first permanent molar, and those who had not. They were also categorised into three caries‐risk groups according to the number of untreated carious teeth (including both permanent and deciduous teeth) at baseline: high‐risk (three or more untreated caries), moderate‐risk (one or two) and low‐risk (none).

Evaluation Process

The primary outcome of this study is the occurrence of new caries at the individual level. Students were classified as having caries if at least one of their first permanent molars developed new caries at follow‐up; otherwise, they were considered caries‐free. For students who received sealants at baseline, only sealed molars were assessed. For those without sealants, all first permanent molars were evaluated.

The status of the first permanent molars was recorded in accordance with the WHO Oral Health Survey Basic Methods—5th edition [17]. Given the retrospective nature of the study using secondary data, formal retrospective calibration of the examiners could not be conducted; however, all clinicians were guided by the standardised criteria outlined in the WHO manual. Only first permanent molars recorded as sound (code 0) at baseline were included in the analysis. Molars were classified as having new caries if they changed from sound at baseline to caries (codes 1 or 2), restoration (codes 3 or 7) or were missing due to caries (code 4) at follow‐up. Molars that remained sound (code 0) or had a fissure sealant (code 6) at follow‐up were considered caries‐free. Molars recorded as missing due to other reasons (code 5) at follow‐up were excluded from the analysis.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was calculated using the formula for comparing proportions of two independent population groups, based on previous research reporting caries incidences of 20% in non‐sealed and 13.4% in sealed molars [18], with a significance level of 0.05% and 80% power. This yielded a required sample of 500 sound first permanent molars per group. Assuming each student had at least one sound first permanent molar with a sealant, 500 students were required. To account for an estimated 50% attrition rate, the target sample size was increased to at least 1000 students.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to demonstrate basic characteristics in frequency and percentage. The chi‐square test and Kruskal–Wallis test were employed to compare categorical and continuous basic characteristics among different caries‐risk groups, respectively.

Logistic regression analyses were performed for each caries‐risk group. Each analysis evaluated the association of sealant application, age, sex, brushing frequency and snacking frequency with caries occurrence using both simple and multiple logistic regression models. All confounders were reported as risk factors for dental caries [10, 11, 12]. To address missing data, individuals with incomplete records for primary outcomes or key variables were excluded from the analysis prior to statistical testing. The outcome was binary (caries or caries‐free), with crude and adjusted odds ratios reported for each variable. Adjusted odds ratios were obtained from a multivariable model that included all variables entered into the model. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

At baseline, 1389 students from 19 schools across nine public health centres were included in the study. During the 5‐year follow‐up, 567 students (40.8%) were lost due to school changing or absence on the day of examination, and 127 students (9.1%) were excluded due to missing data. As a result, 695 students were available for analysis. These participants were distributed across the three baseline caries‐risk groups as follows: 158 in the low‐risk group, 90 in the moderate‐risk group and 447 in the high‐risk group (Figure 1).

Flow diagram of participants' data (SFPM, sound first permanent molars).

The mean age of students at baseline was 6.93 years (SD±0.49). The cohort consisted of 334 female students (48.06%) and 361 male students (51.94%). Across all participants, a total of 2332 first permanent molars were fully erupted. Of these, 118 (5.06%) were carious and 16 (0.69%) had existing sealants or restorations. Consequently, 2198 sound first permanent molars were available for caries occurrence analysis, comprising 1282 sealed molars and 916 non‐sealed molars.

At the follow‐up examination, the mean follow‐up duration was 4.80 years (SD±0.31), and the mean age of the students was 11.73 years (SD±0.61). After 5 years, 248 first permanent molars (19.34%) retained their dental sealants. Among these students, 369 (53.09%) had experienced caries (DMFT > 0), resulting in an overall mean DMFT score of 1.38 (SD±1.83). The mean DMFT scores for the low‐risk, moderate‐risk and high‐risk groups were 0.50 (SD±1.00), 0.77 (SD±1.08) and 1.80 (SD±2.02), respectively.

There were no significant differences in the students' basic characteristics, including sex, age, sealant status, brushing frequency and frequency of snack or sweetened beverage consumption among the low, moderate and high caries risk groups (Table 1).

Discussion

This study, based on real‐world data from a school‐based dental sealant programme, found that the presence of at least one sealed molar was associated with a significantly reduced risk of caries. However, this protective effect was observed only among high caries‐risk students, with no significant associations found in the moderate and low caries‐risk groups.

Some limitations are inherent to the study due to its retrospective cohort design, the nature of data collection and the analytical approach used to define caries incidence. This design, in particular, has a limited ability to assess the reliability of sealant application and oral examinations, as operator variability may have influenced data quality. Additionally, tooth status records were based on the WHO Oral Health Survey: Basic Methods (5th edition) [17], which lacked surface‐specific information. Consequently, caries on sealed teeth may have originated from unsealed surfaces (e.g., proximal areas), potentially underestimating the sealant's effect. Another limitation is that each student contributed a different number of first permanent molars (ranging from one to four) to the analysis. In our classification, if at least one of these teeth developed caries, the student was categorised as having caries, even if the other teeth remained sound. This individual level approach may show higher caries incidence than tooth‐level analysis. However, we adopted this approach to avoid duplicating individual‐level variables across multiple teeth in tooth‐level analysis, which could artificially inflate statistical power and mislead interpretation.

One of the major strengths of this study is its large sample size, which was drawn from multiple geographic areas within Bangkok. This enhances the representativeness of the participants and generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, a 5‐year follow‐up period provides long‐term insights into the effects of sealant application on caries incidence. Importantly, the study utilised real‐world data collected through routine oral health services, which reflects actual clinical practices rather than controlled trial conditions. As a result, the findings provide practical insights that can directly inform and guide public oral health strategies for school‐based dental sealant programmes in Thailand and may be applicable to other similar settings. In terms of generalisability, these findings are highly applicable to similar urban, middle‐income settings with high caries burdens, but should be extrapolated with caution to populations with fundamentally different healthcare systems.

Although this study was conducted in a middle‐income country, the findings were aligned with previous studies conducted in high‐income countries that sealant showed its preventive effect primarily in participants at elevated risk or with carious lesions at baseline [5, 19, 20]. This consistency may be due to baseline caries prevalence being a strong predictor for caries occurrence [21], making it a practical and reliable criterion for identifying individuals' caries risk status, regardless of socio‐economic variation. Additionally, children at lower risk of caries are generally less likely to develop caries, so the benefit of sealants in this group is limited and may not reach statistical significance.

The findings suggest that prioritising high caries‐risk students is a more effective strategy for school‐based dental sealant programme. To capture the gradient of disease severity, we adapted a single clinical criterion from the ADA risk assessment, stratifying students into high (≥ 3 untreated caries), moderate (1, 2) and low (0)‐risk groups [22]. We relied exclusively on baseline caries prevalence to reflect the real‐world screening constraints of large‐scale public health programmes, where conducting a comprehensive caries risk assessment is often precluded by time limitations. Furthermore, maintaining these three distinct groups was crucial. In a secondary analysis where the moderate‐ and high‐risk groups were merged, the overall preventive effect remained statistically significant (aOR = 0.52; 95% CI: 0.33–0.84; p = 0.007). However, reporting this combined result would misleadingly imply that sealants are equally beneficial for moderate‐risk children, potentially driving overtreatment and straining limited resources. The absence of a significant preventive effect in the moderate‐ and low‐risk groups confirms that targeted application in high‐risk populations maximises both clinical impact and operational efficiency, aligning with recommendations from multiple guidelines [10, 11, 12].

In conclusion, dental sealants are significantly associated with a reduction in caries incidence, particularly among the high caries‐risk group. To maximise resource efficiency and improve oral health outcomes, policymakers should adopt a targeted approach to school‐based dental sealant programmes as part of national public health policy, with a focus on reaching underserved and high‐risk populations.
